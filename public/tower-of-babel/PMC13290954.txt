Electrospun biodegradable polycaprolactone filter media for filtering facepiece respirators
Authors: Nathan Ewell, Sophie Fleishman, Kristen Mulherin, Gregory C Rutledge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFiltering facepiece respirators (FFRs) protect against disease transmission and other harmful air pollutants through the collection of incident aerosolized particulates. Conventional disposable FFRs, featuring meltblown polypropylene filter layers, rely on electrostatic charges to achieve N95 performance and are not biodegradable. Electrospun filters can achieve high filtration performance without electrostatic charging due to their small fiber size and may be fabricated from environmentally degradable materials. In this work, we design filter media made of biodegradable poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) nanofibers electrospun from a relatively benign solvent system. These media meet N95 performance targets for filtration efficiency and inhalation and exhalation resistance under laboratory test conditions. Moreover, we demonstrate the fabrication of media having average fiber diameters between 60 nm and 300 nm, and we propose combinations of fiber diameter, solidity, and basis weight that produce filters meeting N95 targets. Our results indicate that selecting electrospun media with particularly small diameter fibers leads to better filtration performance with less material and enhanced rates of degradation. Using these insights, we fabricate prototype duckbill-style respirators featuring an electrospun PCL filter layer sandwiched between polylactic acid spunbond support layers. These prototypes meet the N95 criteria for filtration efficiency and breathing resistance under the tested conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44493-026-00021-6.Keywords: Electrospinning, Nanofibers, Filtration, Biodegradability, Respirator, Aerosol

Introduction

Filtering facepiece respirators (FFRs) are important components of personal protective equipment (PPE) that mitigate disease transmission via aerosolized respiratory droplets (Stutt et al. 2020; Bazant and Bush 2021; Offeddu et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2020) and protect against other harmful airborne contaminants such as smoke and pollution (Brook et al. 2024; Kodros et al. 2021). The performance of an FFR is typically evaluated based on its filtration efficiency (FE), the fraction of incident particulates collected (alternatively penetration, P = 1 – FE), and the pressure drop (Δp) across it when subjected to air flow. With increasing filter thickness, there is a direct tradeoff between filtration efficiency and pressure drop, which is quantified by the quality factor, . For a specified filtration challenge (e.g., flow velocity, aerosol size distribution, aerosol charge) QF depends on the intrinsic structure of a filter (e.g., fiber size, electrostatic charging, porosity) (Brown 1993; Hinds 1999).

A widely accepted performance standard for FFRs in the United States is the N95 designation given by the National Institute of Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH). An N95 FFR must exhibit greater than 95% filtration efficiency (FE > 0.95) for a specified challenge aerosol distribution and a pressure drop less than 25 mmH2O or 35 mmH2O (245.2 Pa or 343.2 Pa) at a volumetric air flow rate of 85 L/min, for exhalation or inhalation, respectively, as specified in NIOSH standard test procedures TEB-APR-STP-0059 (NIOSH 2019), TEB-APR-STP-0007 (NIOSH 2023a) and TEB-APR-STP-0003 (NIOSH 2023b). It is comparable to the FFP2 designation in Europe and KN95 designation in China (3M 2021; Corbin et al. 2021).

Typical commercially available N95s feature a filtration layer comprised of electrostatically charged meltblown polypropylene fibers 1–10 μm in diameter (Ullah et al. 2020; Hao et al. 2021). The electrostatic charge enhances the collection of particles, increasing the QF and enabling filters with fibers of this size to meet N95 standards. However, this technology has important limitations. Firstly, the performance of electret meltblown filters is limited by the durability of surface charges, which may decay with use and exposure to heat, moisture, and liquids (Ullah et al. 2020; Walsh and Stenhouse 1998; Wang et al. 2024). Consequently, many N95 manufacturers recommend limited shelf-life under specified storage conditions, as well as limited use life. Furthermore, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the performance of pristine commercial N95s/KN95s varied greatly due to inconsistencies in the application and retention of charges on the fibers during production, transportation and distribution, often falling below standard performance specifications (Hao et al. 2021). Secondly, polypropylene is not environmentally degradable, so the necessary disposal of an FFR after its effective use life can contribute to the environmental accumulation of plastic (Alsabri et al. 2022; Ajaj et al. 2023).

Electrospun nanofiber filters are an attractive replacement for meltblown polypropylene electret filters. Numerous studies have shown that electrospun fibers, being an order of magnitude smaller than typical meltblown fibers, exhibit a good tradeoff between filtration efficiency and air resistance (Zhu et al. 2017; Shen et al. 2022). Moreover, it has been shown that electrospun nanofibers can achieve N95 targets for filtration efficiency and pressure drop without electrostatic surface charges (Ewell et al. 2024). Without the reliance on electrostatic charging, the performance of such filters depends less on chemistry than fiber size and morphology, and therefore, electrospinnable polymers with other attractive properties may be used.

One such property is biodegradability. Numerous biodegradable or bio-based polymers have been electrospun, and several have been investigated as filters including polylactic acid (Zhang et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2022), cellulose acetate (Chattopadhyay et al. 2015), chitosan (Zhang et al. 2017) and silk (Gao et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2016). Here we develop air filters electrospun from poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), a commercially available synthetic polyester that is used for a wide variety of applications (Woodruff and Hutmacher 2010). It has been shown to have superior degradability in soil and compost compared to other degradable synthetic polyesters such as polylactic acid, polyhydroxybutyrate and polybutylene succinate (Al Hosni et al. 2019). It has also been shown to degrade in natural aquatic conditions (Lu et al. 2018), and biodegradable PCL nanofiber scaffolds have been fabricated for biomedical applications (Janmohammadi and Nourbakhsh 2019). Further, it is also generally considered to be biocompatible (Woodruff and Hutmacher 2010). Some studies have reported the fabrication of electrospun PCL air filters, but none have specifically tested or designed them to meet NIOSH N95 performance targets (Tian et al. 2022; Khandaker et al. 2021; Rao et al. 2017). Moreover, there is need to study systematically how the structure (e.g., fiber size, solidity, and thickness) of electrospun PCL filters, and electrospun filters more generally, affects their performance for FFR applications. Also of interest is how the structure impacts biodegradability.

In this study, we report the design of PCL filter media, electrospun from a relatively benign acetic acid/formic acid solvent system, that meet N95 performance targets for filtration efficiency and pressure drop under laboratory conditions. We explore how their filtration efficiency and air resistance vary as a function of fiber diameter, solidity, and basis weight. Using a simple graphical design method, we suggest combinations of these properties to create filters meeting N95 performance targets for filtration efficiency and inhalation and exhalation pressure drop. We also study how the selection of fiber diameter impacts the rate of enzymatic degradation of these filters. Lastly, we demonstrate the fabrication of prototype duckbill-style respirators that include an electrospun PCL filter layer and meet NIOSH N95 performance standards.

Methods

Materials

Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL, CAS 24980-41-4, Mn = 80,000 g/mol), acetic acid (AA, CAS 64-19-7, glacial), formic acid (FA, CAS 64-18-6, ≥ 96%), ethanol (CAS 64-17-5), sodium chloride (NaCl, CAS 7647-14-5), and lipase from Rhizopus oryzae (CAS 9001-62-1, 34.1 U/mg) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further modification. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was obtained from Fisher Scientific.

Preparation of electrospinning solutions

Solutions containing 6–14 wt% PCL in 1:1 AA/FA (m/m) were prepared by first dissolving PCL into AA with gentle magnetic stirring at 50 °C for several hours. Immediately before electrospinning, FA was added to the solution and magnetically stirred for 10 min. This procedure was employed to minimize the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of the PCL molecules, which can occur due to the significant quantity of water present in the FA. This hydrolysis has been previously observed in other studies (Van der Schueren et al. 2011), and it can impact the electrospinning process via the reduction of polymer molecular weight and consequently solution elasticity. The solution was used within 2 h of adding the FA. Standard-grade reagents were used deliberately to demonstrate practical relevance. Higher-purity FA is available but remains hygroscopic and commands a cost premium.

Electrospinning

Electrospun materials were produced using a lab-scale electrospinning apparatus (Nanospinner 24, Inovenso). The electrospinning precursor solution was fed to a 15-gauge stainless steel needle positioned using a syringe pump at rates of 0.5–0.8 mL/h. The tip of the needle was positioned 17 cm below a grounded flat or rolling drum collector (12 cm diameter, 100 RPM), and a voltage of 16–19 kV was applied to the needle. Electrospun fibers were deposited onto aluminum foil, copper mesh (100 mesh openings per linear inch x 100 mesh openings per linear inch), or polylactic acid (PLA) spunbond (30 g/m2, Ahlstrom). The enclosure was maintained at 23 ± 3 °C and 30 ± 10% relative humidity.

Characterization of electrospun media

A scanning electron microscope (SEM, SNE-4500 M Plus, SEC) was used to observe the microscopic structure of the electrospun media. Prior to observation, the samples were sputter-coated (MCM-100P, SEC) with an 8–10 nm thick layer of gold. At least 50 fibers in micrographs of each sample were measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) to obtain a mean and standard deviation in fiber diameter.

To determine the basis weight (W), samples of known area were weighed with an analytical balance (XP2U, Mettler-Toledo), and the basis weight is reported as the sample mass divided by the sample area. Solidity was estimated from select samples spun onto aluminum foil. SEM was used to measure the thickness (h) of the cross-section after cutting with a sharp rotary fabric cutter. Filtration and air resistance testing of electrospun media

The filtration performance of electrospun filter samples (circular disks with 0.8 cm2 of available area during testing) was evaluated in the laboratory using the apparatus previously reported and described in more detail by Chattopadhyay, et al. (Chattopadhyay et al. 2015); additional details and a schematic of the apparatus can be found there. The objective was to assess filter media performance relative to the NIOSH N95 filtration efficiency and air resistance benchmarks in a format suitable for systematic material design studies. As described below, the method approximates the NIOSH protocol, but does not reproduce it in detail.

The challenge aerosol comprising solid NaCl particles was generated using a condensation monodisperse aerosol generator (CMAG, Model 3475, TSI Inc.), whereby a 0.5 wt% NaCl solution was atomized with 2.5 L/min of pressurized air. The resulting droplets were dried in a desiccant column, diluted with 20 L/min of air, and charge-equilibrated with a Kr-85 aerosol neutralizer (TSI 3077 S, TSI Inc.). The generated challenge aerosol had a number concentration (approximately 2 × 106 #/cm3) and size distribution (count mean mobility diameter (CMD) 75 ± 20 nm, geometric standard deviation (GSD) < 1.86, charge-neutralized) in accordance with the NIOSH protocol.

The size-resolved distribution was measured with a scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS), comprising a differential mobility analyzer (DMA, Model 3081, TSI Inc.) and a condensation particle counter (CPC, Model 3775, TSI Inc.). The number concentration of particles within 106 logarithmically distributed mobility diameter (dp) bins from 0.020 μm to 0.85 μm was measured. The count-based measurement employed by the SMPS differs from the photometric measurement employed by the TSI 8130 A, specified in the NIOSH protocol. Because light scattering intensity follows Mie scattering theory and scales strongly with particle diameter, the photometric signal is dominated by the larger particles in the aerosol distribution and is essentially insensitive to particles smaller than approximately 100 nm, which constitute the majority of the challenge aerosol by count (Corbin et al. 2021; Rengasamy et al. 2011). Previous work (Rengasamy et al. 2011) has shown that, for filters with a most penetrating particle size (MPPS) below 100 nm, the count-based measurement, such as that used in this work, provides a more stringent assessment of filtration efficiency than photometry, because it gives equal weight to the smallest, most-penetrating particles. Moreover, count-based penetration values for N95 FFRs can be significantly higher than photometric values for penetration when tested with the NIOSH N95 aerosol. Consequently, media that achieve ≥ 95% FE by count are expected to meet or exceed this threshold when measured photometrically.

The NIOSH protocol specifies a volumetric flow rate of 85 L/min for testing full respirators, which can vary in size and form. However, a representative face velocity must be chosen for testing small filter samples and for quantitative comparisons between different materials. Assuming a typical FFR surface area of 150 cm2 (Corbin et al. 2021; Rengasamy et al. 2017), the corresponding face velocity is 9.44 cm/s, hydrodynamic effects notwithstanding, which is the face velocity used in this work. Three filter disks cut from the same electrospun sample were tested in each case, and the average and standard deviation are reported. Each test consisted of a downstream scan of the aerosol distribution with the filter in place, followed by an upstream scan with the filter holder empty. Before each test, the feed to the SMPS was flushed with HEPA (9900-05-BK, Parker) filtered air for at least 5 min to eliminate residual aerosols in the sample chamber. The penetration fraction of each mobility diameter bin, P(dp), was computed as the quotient of the downstream and upstream concentrations. Similarly, the total penetration fraction, P, was computed by dividing the total downstream number concentration by the total upstream number concentration. A digital manometer (HD750, Extech Instruments) was used to monitor the pressure drop across the filter, and the pressure drop is reported as the average over the duration of the experiment.

The NIOSH test protocol for determining filtration efficiency (TEB-APR-STP-0059) requires pre-conditioning of candidate respirators at 85 ± 5% relative humidity, and 38 ± 2.5 °C for 25 ± 1 h. We observed no statistically significant change in pressure drop or filtration efficiency for a set of electrospun PCL filters conditioned in this manner (Supporting Information, Table S1); therefore, the filter performance here is reported for unconditioned samples.

Production of prototype duckbill-style respirators

Filter media was electrospun from a precursor solution of 8 wt% PCL in 1:1 AA/FA (m/m) onto PLA spunbond affixed to a rotating drum collector using the above electrospinning protocol to produce a spunbond-nanofiber (SN) sheet approximately 20 cm by 25 cm in size. Spin time was varied to control filter basis weight, and sheets of each basis weight were produced in duplicate. Several grades of PLA are also biodegradable (Kolstad et al. 2012; Farah et al. 2016), but the degradability of the PLA spunbond used in this work was not examined.

A second layer of spunbond was added to form a spunbond-nanofiber-spunbond (SNS) stack. A laser cutter (VLS 4.75, Universal Laser Systems) was used to cut two panels forming the duckbill shape, the design of which is provided in the Supporting Information, Fig. S4. The panels were subsequently ultrasonically welded together (Chase FS-90) along the outer edge to form the respirator.

Filtration and air resistance testing of prototype respirators

The filtration efficiency and pressure drop of the duckbill style respirators were tested using an automated filter tester, TSI 8130 A, as specified in the NIOSH standard test protocols, with a flow rate of 85 L/min and an NaCl aerosol with a CMD of 75 ± 20 nm and GSD of less than 1.86. Simultaneous upstream and downstream manometer and photometer measurements were used to compute the pressure drop and filtration efficiency, respectively. The TSI 8130 A does not measure size-resolved penetration but instead reports a single filtration efficiency for the entire challenge aerosol, based on the ratio of downstream to upstream scattered-light intensity (Corbin et al. 2021; Rengasamy et al. 2011). While the same automated filter tester and challenge conditions were used, the number of prototypes prepared in this work was insufficient to replicate the NIOSH protocol in its entirety.

Enzymatic degradation of electrospun media

The extent of reaction during enzyme-catalyzed hydrolysis of electrospun PCL samples with different fiber diameters was measured as a function of time. Electrospun PCL samples (30–60 mg) were initially prewetted with ethanol, which was diluted with DI water over several hours until the samples were transferred to a beaker of unadulterated DI water and shaken overnight. The purpose of this procedure was to ensure repeatable surface wetting, since PCL is hydrophobic and incompletely wetted by water, but better wetted by ethanol. The samples were then incubated in 150 mL solution of 0.2 mg/mL lipase in PBS on an orbital shaker operating at 100 RPM. This enzyme concentration was chosen based on a preliminary screening to enable full degradation of electrospun samples in several hours. The duration of experiments was up to 5 h, and an additional test indicated that the enzyme solution had no noticeable loss of activity 24 h after preparation (Supporting Information, Fig. S5). A nylon mesh bag was inserted into the mouth of the bottle to contain degrading plastic fragments. Throughout the experiment, 1 mL aliquots were periodically extracted using a micropipette. These samples were subsequently diluted by a factor of 10 and filtered with a micron filter. Then their non-purgeable organic carbon (NPOC) content was measured with a total organic carbon analyzer (Shimadzu).

A mass balance on NPOC was used to estimate the extent of reaction. The total NPOC concentration in the reaction solution due to the enzyme itself was measured prior to the experiment. The concentration of NPOC in each aliquot and the total reaction volume at each time point were used to estimate the additional NPOC contributed by conversion of non-soluble polymer to soluble monomer or oligomer. Reaction extents are reported as the quotient of the NPOC contributed by the reaction and the theoretical mass of NPOC contributed by the total conversion of the PCL sample, which was taken to be 63.1% of the mass of the electrospun sample based on the carbon content in the PCL repeat unit.

For comparison, oblate spheroid-shaped PCL pellets with major diameter approximately 3–4 mm and minor diameter of approximately 1 mm were incubated in 0.2 mg/mL lipase solution at the same conditions over a period of 12 days. Every 24 h, the enzyme solution was replaced. The pellets were removed periodically over the course of the experiment, washed with DI water, dried at room temperature under vacuum, and weighed to determine mass loss to track degradation.

Results and Discussion

Electrospinning PCL nanofibers

Smooth, mostly bead-free PCL fiber mats ranging in average fiber diameter from 60 nm to 300 nm were produced, examples of which are pictured in Fig. 1. We observe a systematic increase in average fiber diameter with increasing polymer concentration in the electrospinning solution. Table S2 in the Supporting Information lists the fiber diameter (average and standard deviation) of samples produced for each portion of this study.

SEMs of PCL nanofibers electrospun from (a) 6 wt% (b) 8 wt%, (c) 10 wt%, (d) 12 wt%, and (e) 14 wt% solutions in 1:1 AA/FA (m/m)

The solvent system used here was chosen primarily for its ability to produce relatively small diameter fibers compared to other solvent combinations (Luo et al. 2012), which is critical for air filtration applications. The AA/FA solvent system is also attractive for several additional reasons. Both acetic acid and formic acid are classified as FDA Q3C Class 3 solvents, which are considered biologically benign with permitted daily exposures orders of magnitude greater than Class 2 solvents such as dimethylformamide (DMF) and dichloromethane (DCM) that are prevalent in the electrospinning literature (Mosher et al. 2021). Further, both AA and FA can be considered “green” according to the GSK Green Solvent guide, with only minor concerns related to their acidic nature (Avossa et al. 2022).

The reported method of adding the FA to the electrospinning solution immediately prior to spinning was effective; fibers consistent in size and morphology were formed for up to several hours with the same electrospinning settings before hydrolysis of the PCL molecules impacted the process (Supporting Information, Fig. S1). This method of producing a stable solution may be readily adapted to a continuous process using inline mixing.

Filtration efficiency and pressure drop of electrospun samples

Figure 2a shows the penetration fraction and pressure drop for six sets of electrospun PCL samples varying by basis weight, each electrospun from a different precursor solution concentration, resulting in media with a different average fiber diameter and solidity for each set. For each set, basis weight was modulated by spin time to create filters spanning a range of pressure drops and penetration fractions. The green and blue regions in Fig. 2a correspond respectively to FE and pressure drop meeting NIOSH specifications for an N95 FFR. Samples that fall within the overlap of these boxes (i.e., the upper left quadrant) meet both specifications simultaneously. The x-axis and y-axis quantities, pressure drop and the negative logarithm of penetration, correspond to the denominator and numerator of QF. Plotted this way, one might expect that for a single particle diameter, each basis weight series should fall onto a line intercepting the origin with slope equal to the QF. Indeed, even though the challenge aerosol is not monodisperse, these data do fall onto such lines, within experimental uncertainty. Plotting data in this manner enables facile graphical design of filter media, as demonstrated previously for electrospun polyacrylonitrile (PAN) filters (Ewell et al. 2024). Changing the fiber diameter or solidity through solution composition or spin conditions controls the slope (i.e., QF) of the set, enabling their selection such that the line intersects the target region, while changing the spin time (i.e., basis weight) allows for moving along the QF line.

The green region corresponds to the N95 FE target of at least 95%, and the light and dark blue regions correspond to the N95 inhalation and exhalation pressure drop limits, respectively. (b) Quality factors computed from least squares regression of the penetration and pressure drop data to . We observe that filters electrospun from 6 wt%, 8 wt%, 9 wt%, 10 wt%, and 12 wt% PCL fall along lines crossing through the target region, and each set has at least one sample that exceeds 95% FE and falls below the exhalation pressure drop limit. The set electrospun from 14 wt% precursor solution appears to cross near the intersection of these limits, but no sample lies in the target region. All sets have at least one sample that exceeds 95% FE and falls below the less stringent inhalation pressure drop limit. Figure 2b shows the quality factor of each set computed via least squares regression of the data in Fig. 2a. To simultaneously exceed 95% FE and fall below the exhalation and inhalation pressure drop limits, quality factors of at least 12.2 and 8.7 kPa− 1 are needed, respectively. Reflective of the previous observations, all but the 14 wt% filters exceed the exhalation QF, and all exceed the inhalation QF. As has been previously observed and is theoretically expected, smaller fibers tend to exhibit higher quality factors, with a steep increase in QF observed for fibers with a diameter less than 100 nm.

The systematic dependence of quality factor on fiber diameter and solidity, and the low scatter in the data, are consistent with mechanical (i.e., in the absence of electrostatic) filtration. While it is possible that electrospun PCL fibers possess residual charges, preliminary experiments to remove such charges using isopropanol, following the protocol successfully used to discharge electrospun PAN fibers in previous work (Ewell et al. 2024), caused an increase in pressure drop, which we attribute to capillary-driven densification of the compliant nanofiber network. Nevertheless, no decrease in filtration efficiency was observed after such treatment.

The solidities reported here are lower than values often found in the literature. However, such values have been shown to be consistent with both filtration and pressure drop measurements (Hao et al. 2021; Ewell et al. 2024). Mechanical contact methods such as micrometer gauges must exert a small force to take a measurement; unless considerable care is taken, this force may compress the mat, leading to artificially small thickness values and overestimated solidities. For this reason, we rely on SEM cross-sections for thickness measurement. Similarly, Leung (Leung and Hung 2026) has performed careful profilometry measurements of thickness and reported solidities as low as 0.005 for electrospun nanofiber filters. Given the low values and difficulty of measurement, variations in solidity among samples with different diameters should be interpreted with caution.

For a filter material with an acceptable QF for N95 performance, an appropriate basis weight is required such that minimum filtration efficiency is achieved and maximum pressure drop is not exceeded. Figure 3a shows the range of basis weights in which filters are predicted to meet N95 requirements for filtration efficiency and either inhalation or exhalation pressure drop limits. These ranges were estimated in the following way. First, a proportionality constant between basis weight and pressure drop for the data in Fig. 2 was fit by least squares regression (Supporting Information, Fig. S2 and Table S3). For low Reynolds number flows, the pressure drop across the filter is expected to obey Darcy’s law, such that pressure drop is proportional to thickness (Brown 1993), which is proportional to basis weight by Eq. (1) for a homogeneous filter. The data support this relationship. The maximum allowable basis weight is the basis weight at which the best fit of pressure drop to basis weight equals either the inhalation or exhalation limit. The minimum allowable basis weight is that at which the filter exhibits 95% FE, estimated similarly, but using the additional relationship that –ln(P) is proportional to the pressure drop by the quality factor.

(a) Absolute basis weight ranges versus solution concentration, with total bars (filled plus unfilled) representing the inhalation requirement and filled bars representing the exhalation pressure drop requirement. (b) Relative tolerances in basis weight for the inhalation limit (open markers) and exhalation limit (closed markers), computed as the absolute range divided by the mean of the upper and lower limit

We see in Fig. 3a that, generally speaking, the basis weights falling between the minimum and maximum allowable values increase with increasing fiber diameter, indicating that thicker filter layers are generally required for filters with larger fibers. However, the range of acceptable basis weights for meeting the exhalation pressure drop becomes smaller with increasing fiber diameter, as the QF line more narrowly passes through the target region. This is consistent with the observation that QF decays with larger fiber diameter, with the QF of the 14 wt% filters falling below the exhalation minimum.

Of practical concern is the allowable tolerance for deviation from a target basis weight when manufacturing filters such that the resulting filter performs within the targeted limits. We calculate a measure of this tolerance as the ratio of the range of basis weights divided by the mean value of the upper and lower limits. This tolerance is a relative measure of the maximum allowable variations in spatial and temporal deposition of fibers during manufacturing. In Fig. 3b, the basis weight tolerance systematically decreases with increasing fiber size for both the inhalation and exhalation requirements. Moreover, even though the magnitude of the acceptable basis weights for the less strict inhalation pressure drop increases with larger fiber diameter in Fig. 3a., we still observe the tolerance decreases with increasing fiber diameter in Fig. 3b. This is also readily visualized in the design diagram in Fig. 2, where filters with higher QF cross through the upper left quadrant over a wider range of pressure drop values, with pressure drop being proportional to basis weight. These results further highlight the advantages of using small fibers in filters: less material is required to produce a viable filter, and more variability, relatively speaking, during production can be sustained without loss of ultimate performance.

Figure 4a shows quality factor as a function of particle mobility diameter, dp, which was estimated by performing a linear-least squares regression of -ln(P(dp)) and Δp for size-resolved penetrations measured for the samples in Fig. 2, which span a range of basis weights. We observe that the resulting QF(dp) is a strong function of particle size, spanning a range of up to 30 kPa− 1. Each filter exhibits a single QF minimum, corresponding to the MPPS. Filters with larger diameter fibers tend to shift the QF curve down and toward larger particle sizes. As shown in Fig. 4b, the MPPS of the filters increases systematically with fiber diameter, from around 60 nm for the smallest fibers up to just under 80 nm for the largest fibers studied here. Notably, for the case of the NIOSH N95 challenge aerosol employed here, all but the 14 wt% filters exhibit a MPPS smaller than the CMD of the particle size distribution. The total filtration efficiencies reported in Fig. 2 are based on particle count. However, other common metrics for filtration efficiency like those based on mass or scattered light intensity (such as the TSI 8130) tend to weigh the filtration performance for larger particles more heavily. Consequently, when the MPPS shifts toward smaller diameters, as occurs for filters composed of finer fibers, the overall filtration efficiency appears higher when evaluated using these metrics. Furthermore, we also see that the QF of the MPPS itself, plotted in Fig. 4c., increases with decreasing fiber diameter, with a profile that is qualitatively similar to the overall QF in Fig. 2b.

Importantly, Pich’s model was derived for the slip regime, which extends approximately from Kn = 10− 3 to Kn = 10− 1; larger values of Kn are conventionally considered to belong to the transition regime to free molecular flow (Zhang et al. 2012). The slip flow approximation diminishes in accuracy for higher Knudsen numbers that fall in the transition regime (Karniadakis et al. 2005). However, there is no clear consensus on where to make the cut-off, and slip flow models continue to be used even at moderately high Knudsen numbers. Importantly, for the smallest fibers studied in this work (average diameter of 59 nm), Kn = 2.2, well beyond the conventional slip regime cutoff.

For the larger fibers used in this study, the slip flow-modified Kuwabara model provides an accurate estimate of the pressure drop. However, for smaller fibers (larger Knudsen numbers), it increasingly overpredicts pressure drop and airflow resistance. Qualitatively, this is the expected behavior for flows at high Knudsen number, as there is less drag on the fiber as compared to continuum flow. Other authors have observed similar decreases in air resistance across filters through experiments in which they increased the Knudsen number by decreasing the operating pressure, thereby increasing the mean free path of air molecules (Robinson and Franklin 1972; Zhang and Liu 1992).

We emphasize that this power-law relationship is an empirical observation of the trend in the data and limited to the range of Kn and solidities here; it is not based on any theoretical model for the transition regime. Currently, there are a few empirical models proposed for pressure drop in this regime (Leung and Hung 2026; Kwak et al. 2023; Bian et al. 2018) but, to our knowledge, there is no model for filtration efficiency. However, as these experimental data show, the performance of filters with fibers smaller than 100 nm has considerable practical value. For this reason, the development of models for this transition regime would be of great interest, but is beyond the scope of this work.

Filtration efficiency and pressure drop of fabricated prototype respirators

Three SNS duckbill-style respirators such as the one pictured in Fig. 6a., featuring electrospun PCL as the active filtration layer, were assembled. The adhesion to the spunbond substrate appeared to be adequate without the use of any additional adhesive; there were no visible signs of peeling during fabrication or subsequent handling. Peel tests to quantify the adhesion of the PCL to the substrate were inconclusive, due to decohesion of the PCL layer itself. No residual organic solvent in the fibers was detected in the fibers (Supporting Information, Fig. S3). The average fiber diameter of the electrospun filter layer in these samples was measured to be 103 ± 18 nm.

Filled circles represent SMPS measurements on disks cut from the material used to produce the respirator, and open circles represent measurements on full respirators using the TSI 8130 automated filter tester. The dashed line indicates the fitted QF of the 8 wt% data in Fig. 2a. Annotations indicate the basis weight of electrospun PCL

Figure 6b shows the filtration efficiency and pressure drop of the respirators assessed with both an automated filter tester, which is specified by the NIOSH standard test methods and used for testing full respirators, as well as of disks cut from the SNS material used for the respirators using the SMPS procedure. The SMPS measurements of FE and pressure drop of the respirator materials fall along the QF = 16.6 kPa− 1 line, which is the fitted value of the 8 wt% filters in Fig. 2. All three respirators exceeded 95% FE on both instruments and exhibited pressure drops below the inhalation limit; two respirators have pressure drops below the exhalation limit. The MPPS of these filters, as measured by SMPS, was 65 nm. The basis weights of the filter layers fall in the range estimated in Fig. 3a for filters electrospun from 8 wt% PCL.

The pressure drops across the respirators measured on the automated filter tester are on average 7% lower than those measured on the SMPS. A larger available surface area of the duckbill respirator when mounted onto the machine can result in proportionally lower face velocity, and therefore lower pressure drop. The available surface area of the duckbill respirators studied here was estimated to be 165 cm2, which would result in an average face velocity of 8.6 cm/s. This area is 10% larger than the typical FFR area assumed in the SMPS test, and results in a 10% lower velocity, which is consistent with the observed difference in pressure drop. The filtration efficiency measurements between the two instruments are not directly comparable. The FE from the SMPS measurement is based on particle counts, while the FE measured by the automated filter tester is based on signal from scattered light, which is heavily weighted towards larger particles via Mie scattering.

The filtration performance reported in this work reflects the behavior of the pristine electrospun PCL media. Under the NIOSH certification protocol for filtration efficiency (TEB-APR-STP-0059), respirators are subjected to loading with up to 200 mg of NaCl aerosol, and any decrease in filtration efficiency relative to the pristine state is noted. For electret meltblown filters, particle loading can screen or neutralize fiber surface charges, leading to a decrease in filtration efficiency that may cause an initially passing filter to fail. For mechanical filters such as the electrospun PCL media studied here, particle loading generally increases filtration efficiency, as deposited particles reduce effective pore sizes and contribute additional collection surfaces (Leung et al. 2018). Thus, with regard to filtration efficiency, the pristine filters in this work are believed to represent the minimum in filtration efficiency. However, the improvement in filtration comes at the cost of increasing pressure drop, which may be a particular concern for nanofiber media. Thus, loading studies would be important to establish service life for respirators based on the media reported in this work.

Enzymatic degradation of electrospun PCL media

Figure 7a. shows the extent of enzymatic degradation versus time for electrospun PCL media having different average fiber diameters, prepared from five different precursor solution concentrations. Media with smaller average fiber diameters degraded faster than those with larger fibers. Additionally, we observe that the smallest fibers degrade to 100% soluble content in the experimental time window (approximately 8 h), while the largest fibers do not reach 100% soluble content. However, observations made 24 h after the beginning of the experiment confirmed that all samples degraded to 100% under the prescribed experimental conditions. A rigorous mechanistic kinetic model for this process would potentially need to account for such factors as enzyme adsorption kinetics, catalytic autoacceleration due to roughening and shrinking of fiber surfaces, enzyme deactivation, and mass transport. As shown in Fig. 7b, the fitted apparent rate constant increases with decreasing average fiber diameter. The scaling relationship of these variables is visualized on a log-log scale in Fig. 7c. The apparent rate constants measured here appear to be proportional to the inverse of fiber diameter. This behavior is consistent with a reaction that is limited to the fiber surface, since specific surface area is proportional to df−1. This conclusion agrees with observations by Torabi, et al. (Torabi et al. 2023) in experiments observing the enzymatic degradation of PCL microbeads, from which they concluded that the hydrolysis of PCL occurred as a shrinking particle as opposed to a shrinking core.

Notably, the largest fiber diameter samples do not degrade to 100%. A detailed examination of the fiber size distributions (Supporting Information, Fig. S6) reveals that the distributions for the larger fiber samples are wider and contain outlier fibers whose diameters are much larger than the mean. Figure 8 shows the cumulative mass fraction of fibers as a function of fiber diameter for each sample. In both the 12 wt% and 14 wt% samples, much of the mass fraction is accounted for by fibers that are several times larger than the mean. Since the rate of degradation is controlled by surface area, these large fibers both contribute a large mass fraction and represent a slow degradation mode. Since PCL is semicrystalline, it is also possible that the larger fibers contain larger crystallites, which degrade more slowly than noncrystalline regions on average.

Cumulative mass fraction by fiber size of the electrospun samples tested in the enzymatic degradation experiments. The square markers indicate the average fiber diameter. The inset is an SEM of the 14 wt% sample, displaying an outlier large fiber surrounded by smaller fibers

That degradation rate is controlled by fiber size has important implications for the design of biodegradable electrospun filters. Firstly, it highlights enhanced biodegradability as yet another advantage of small diameter fibers, in addition to enhanced filtration performance (smaller basis weight requirement and greater basis weight tolerance). Secondly, the degradation of filters with large microstructural defects or inclusions may be limited by these large features. Some authors have suggested that the inclusion of beads (Yousefi and Tafreshi 2020; Gungor and Kilic 2025) or the mixing of large and small fibers (Wang et al. 2022; Mei et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2015; Gungor et al. 2022) may enhance filtration performance. Since the degradation rate depends on the inverse of the feature diameter, but the mass depends on the diameter squared, inclusion of these features may reduce the overall rate of degradation. Thirdly, from a practical point of view, the overall degradation of a complete FFR will likely be limited by the non-electrospun components. The typical spunbond outer layers of the SNS composite have basis weights an order of magnitude larger than that of the electrospun filter layer and contain fibers that are an order of magnitude larger in diameter.

The degradation of PCL in bulk form under more natural conditions is well-studied. For comparison to the current work, we measured the enzymatic degradation of PCL pellets under the same conditions as the nanofiber media; the results are plotted in Fig. 9. The pellets, having several orders of magnitude lower specific surface area, degrade much slower, losing only 13% of their mass over the course of 12 days incubation in the enzyme bath. From the best fit of Eq. (8) to these data, we estimate a rate constant of . Normalizing this rate by the area per unit volume of the pellets yields a rate constant of 2.6 ± 0.4 nm/min. The corresponding normalized rate constant averaged over all of the nanofiber media in Fig. 7 is 0.51 ± 0.08 nm/min. These values are similar in magnitude but differ by a factor of around 5. This difference could be due to our assumption that the surfaces of fibers and pellets are smooth, or to differences in crystallinity between fibers and pellets.

Enzymatic degradation of PCL pellets estimated by weight loss. Dashed line is best fit to Eq. (8)

Based on the foregoing analysis, we conclude that the enzymatic degradation of pellets here provides an order of magnitude estimate of the speedup of our enzymatic degradation test compared to those using more realistic degradation environments. Experiments conducted by Al Hosni, et al. (Al Hosni et al. 2019) on PCL strips of dimensions 6 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.05 cm showed 13% reduction in mass after 65 days buried in compost at 37 °C and 121 days buried in soil at 37 °C. Given the similar length scale of these strips compared to the pellets, we estimate that our experiment provides a speedup in degradation of 5.5 and 10 times compared to compost and soil, respectively.

We emphasize that these estimates are approximate and should be interpreted with caution. Degradation in soil or compost involves complex interactions among microbial populations, multiple enzymes and degradation pathways, variable temperature and moisture, and potential UV exposure, all of which may alter the rate and extent of degradation relative to the controlled enzyme bath used here. The accelerated enzymatic test does not replicate these environmental conditions. The high specific surface area of electrospun nanofibers could potentially accelerate hydrolytic degradation during long-term storage. Packaging in sealed, moisture-barrier containers under cool, dry conditions, consistent with standard practice for commercial FFRs, would be advisable. A systematic shelf-life study for electrospun PCL media is an important direction for future work.

Conclusions

We report the fabrication of degradable electrospun PCL filters designed to meet N95 performance specifications for filtration efficiency and air resistance. Moreover, based on our experiments, we provide combinations of fiber diameter, basis weight, and solidity, along with associated electrospinning protocols, for which electrospun PCL filter media meet NIOSH N95 requirements for filtration efficiency and pressure drop under both inhalation and exhalation. We also demonstrate a useful graphical design method based on scaling relationships for the design of aerosol filters that can be used for other applications as well. Our data strongly support the use of media with small fibers for the FFR application, as they exhibit higher quality factors, have a smaller MPPS, require less material, and have a larger manufacturing tolerance to meet specifications. Enzymatic degradation experiments also suggest that smaller fibers degrade faster, as degradation is limited by surface area. Using these design insights, we fabricated several prototype SNS duckbill-style FFRs featuring an electrospun PCL filtration layer that meets N95 performance standards for filtration efficiency and pressure drop when tested by standard automated filter testing equipment.
