Understanding regional differences in MASLD: the Indian perspective
Authors: Karthik Shree Harini, Devaraj Ezhilarasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in India demonstrates marked regional variation influenced by metabolic risk, lifestyle transition, and population-specific susceptibility. This commentary highlights the growing burden of lean MASLD, the importance of visceral adiposity and cardiometabolic dysfunction, and population-specific genetic influences in Indian populations. Given the close association between MASLD and type 2 diabetes, region-specific prevention, early risk assessment, and integrated metabolic care strategies are increasingly important.Subject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Gastroenterology

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has rapidly emerged as the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and is a major contributor to the global burden of metabolic diseases. Rather than a liver-specific disorder, MASLD is recognized as a systemic condition, driven by insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), etc. The MASLD spectrum ranges from steatosis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), progressive fibrosis, and cirrhosis, which might even set off hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)1. According to a meta-analysis, global pooled prevalence of MASLD increased from ~25% to over 30.05% between 2016 and 2019. As of 2019, the highest MASLD prevalence was in Latin America, followed by the Middle East and North Africa, South Asia, South-East Asia, North America, East Asia, Asia Pacific, and Western Europe2. Current estimates suggest that MASLD now affects 38% of the adult population and 7–14% of children and adolescents worldwide, with considerable variation across regions3. The MASLD burden is now prominent in low- and middle-income countries that are going through a rapid epidemiological shift, including rapid urban growth, evolving dietary habits, modification in lifestyle, and population-specific genetic predisposition4. Population-level assessments also indicate that the impact of MASLD on global health has been steadily growing over time, as evidenced by rising prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and mortality.

Regionally, Asia contributes substantially to the global burden of MASLD. With China and India together accounting for 35% of the world’s population, more than half of MASLD cases are estimated to occur in the Asia-Pacific region. The overall occurrence in this region was reported to be ~33.9% in 2017, which is projected to increase in the coming decades. Furthermore, advanced stages such as cirrhosis and HCC, and MASLD-related mortality are expected to rise considerably across several Asian populations5.

In particular, India accounts for a significant proportion of MASLD-associated DALYs and deaths6. Considering its large population, diverse metabolic risk profile, rapid lifestyle transitions, and population-specific genetic susceptibility, understanding MASLD in the Indian context may provide important insights into regional disease heterogeneity. This commentary illustrates the epidemiological, metabolic, lifestyle, genetic, and cardiometabolic factors that may contribute to these differences and discusses their implications for risk stratification and targeted prevention strategies.

Literature search

Literature for this commentary was identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using keywords including “MASLD”, “NAFLD ”, “India”, “Asia”, “lean MASLD”, “metabolic dysfunction”, “prevalence”, and “genetic susceptibility”. Relevant recent epidemiological studies, meta-analyses, multicentre investigations, and review articles focusing on Indian and Asian populations were considered to provide an overview of regional and population-level differences in MASLD.

MASLD prevalence in India

Although estimates vary according to the study design and population factors, epidemiological evidence suggests that MASLD is very common in India. A recent multicenter retrospective cross-sectional study demonstrated that prevalence ranges widely from ~9.7% to over 50.3% across different parts of India7. A pooled analysis further highlights this burden, with MASLD prevalence in Indian adults estimated at 38.6%, rising to over 52.8% in high-risk groups. Further, a pooled prevalence of 35.4% was reported in children, especially among those with obesity8. Evidence from region-specific studies supports these findings. A cross-sectional study from South India (Chennai, Tamil Nadu) reported a prevalence of ~61.5% among young adults. Moreover, increasing age and male sex were also associated with greater susceptibility to MASLD9.

Using controlled attenuation parameter measurements, nationwide MASLD prevalence has been reported to exceed 65%. With respect to regional differences, higher rates are in the northern (73.2%) and central zones (69.4%), followed by Southern and Western regions, while relatively lower prevalence has been observed in the Eastern and Northeastern parts of India. Beyond steatosis, fibrosis burden is also significant across the country. Nearly 33.7% of individuals exhibit liver fibrosis, with higher prevalence observed in the northern and central regions compared to other parts of India7.

These regional differences are likely multifactorial and may reflect variations in dietary practices, socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, urbanization, and underlying metabolic risk profiles. Higher prevalence estimates reported in Northern and Central regions may be influenced by these factors; however, differences in study populations, sampling strategies, urban–rural distribution, diagnostic approaches, and referral patterns should also be considered. Similarly, lower prevalence estimates reported in some Eastern regions may reflect regional variation in disease burden, methodological factors, differences in healthcare access, or underdiagnosis7.

Age and sex-related variations further define the epidemiology of MASLD in India. Several studies indicate the burden of MASLD in India increases with age, with higher rates observed in middle-aged and older adults. In contrast, Asian populations tend to develop MASLD at relatively younger ages compared to Western populations. Additionally, younger males are predominantly affected by MASLD, whereas it is more common in older women, likely due to hormonal changes like menopause. A similar trend was also reported with the Asian population10.

Metabolic phenotype

The “Asian metabolic phenotype,” which is defined by higher visceral adiposity, enhanced insulin resistance, and the presence of ectopic fat at a lower body mass index (BMI) compared to Western populations, is a major contributing factor to regional differences. This remains a key determinant for the development of metabolic disorders, including MASLD, even in individuals with normal body weight. Lean MASLD is one such prominent manifestation of this phenotype. Based on Asian-specific BMI thresholds, lean MASLD is generally defined as MASLD occurring in individuals with a BMI < 23 kg/m², whereas non-obese MASLD includes those with a BMI < 25 kg/m². Individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² are categorized as overweight or obese. These classifications are particularly relevant in Asian populations, where metabolic abnormalities frequently occur at lower BMI levels than those observed in Western populations. In India, a significant proportion of MASLD patients are neither obese nor overweight. This challenges the traditional belief that fatty liver and MASLD are primarily linked to obesity8. Although robust nationwide estimates for lean MASLD in India remain limited, available studies indicate that a clinically relevant proportion of MASLD occurs in individuals who do not meet conventional obesity criteria. This observation is consistent with evidence from Asian populations showing a substantial burden of lean MASLD11. Together, these findings indicate that susceptibility to MASLD in Indian populations cannot be determined by body weight alone. However, differences in body fat distribution, particularly increased visceral adiposity, together with metabolic phenotype and environmental exposures, may influence disease development, even among individuals who do not meet conventional obesity criteria. Therefore, BMI alone may not be sufficient to assess MASLD risk in Indian populations, where visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and ectopic fat may be more relevant determinants of disease susceptibility.

Lifestyle factors

Rapid lifestyle changes by urbanization and economic progress are directly related to India’s rising MASLD burden. Traditional diets high in fiber and whole grains have been replaced by diets high in refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, and highly processed foods. Hepatic fat buildup and metabolic imbalance are caused by high consumption of polished rice, sugar-sweetened drinks, and foods high in calories. These dietary patterns further contribute to metabolic syndrome, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and obesity, which is closely related to MASLD12. Consistent with this, the higher burden of MASLD observed in North India in a regional analysis by Mohan et al.7 may be linked to dietary patterns characterized by greater intake of refined carbohydrates and saturated fats.

Simultaneously, sedentary occupations, mechanization, and changes in transportation decreased the overall physical activity level and increased stress. These changes collectively reduce daily energy expenditure, promoting visceral fat accumulation and sustained insulin resistance, thereby driving MASLD13. Globally, similar lifestyle transitions have been related to a rising burden of metabolic diseases, particularly in regions undergoing rapid economic development.

Genetic factors

Genetic predisposition plays an important role in MASLD susceptibility and progression. One of the most commonly studied genes in the Indian population is the patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing protein 3 (PNPLA3). The PNPLA3 148 M variant is among the most consistently reported genetic factors associated with hepatic fat accumulation and progression to advanced liver disease, including MASH, cirrhosis, and HCC. Other variants, including transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2 (TM6SF2), lyso-phospholipase-like 1 (LYPLAL1), and tribble homolog 1 (TRIB1), have also been implicated in influencing disease progression14.

Emerging evidence from Indian populations highlights the importance of population-specific genetic influences in MASLD. Variants in genes such as TRIB1 rs2954029 have been associated with increased susceptibility to hepatic steatosis, suggesting a role in early disease development15. Among the Indian population, ethnic variation in genetic susceptibility to MASLD was observed. Differential association of PNPLA3 rs2281135 and TM6SF2 rs58542926 with MASLD has been reported in studies comparing ancestral South Indian and North-East Indian populations16. While associations involving PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 have been consistently reported across multiple populations, evidence for other variants remains limited and requires further validation in larger and ethnically diverse cohorts. Notably, genetic predisposition to MASLD shows considerable overlap across several populations with PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 variants; Asian and Indian populations seem to differ in the magnitude of their effect and their interaction with environmental and metabolic factors. Furthermore, although genetic variants may improve our understanding of MASLD susceptibility and progression, their routine use for clinical risk stratification remains limited and is not currently recommended in standard clinical practice.

MASLD and cardiometabolic risk

MASLD is strongly linked with metabolic syndrome, such as central obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, insulin resistance, and particularly T2DM17. Globally, MASLD has been observed in up to 57.5% of obese individuals18. In contrast, Indian populations exhibit a distinct pattern, with MASLD frequently occurring even among individuals with normal body weight (lean MASLD). Studies have also shown that MASLD patients from different parts of India frequently experienced hypercholesterolemia19. Further, MASLD shows a strong bidirectional association with T2DM. The risk of MASLD development is 2-fold greater in T2DM patients. Similarly, T2DM promotes the progression of MASLD to advanced fibrosis, contributing to liver-related mortality. Several studies demonstrated MASLD occurrence in more than half of diabetic populations worldwide20. Indian cohort data showed a higher prevalence of T2DM in individuals with MASLD (34.5%). Notably, the diabetes and hypertension burden is even greater in lean MASLD, reaching ~42.5% and 80.5%, respectively21. Together, these observations highlight that MASLD in India is associated with metabolic comorbidities. Given the substantial burden of diabetes in India, this overlap significantly increases cardiovascular risk22. Moreover, MASLD is now recognized as an independent contributor to cardiovascular disease.

Future directions

The increasing prevalence of MASLD in India has significant implications for public health and healthcare systems. Addressing modifiable risk factors, including unhealthy diet, sedentary behavior, and obesity, is essential, alongside promoting lifestyle changes to reduce disease burden. At the diagnostic level, instead of relying on BMI, incorporating measures like the body roundness index, which integrates waist circumference and better reflects visceral adiposity, may improve early identification of MASLD, especially in India. Given the close association between MASLD and cardiometabolic disorders, individuals with metabolic risk factors may benefit from earlier identification and longitudinal monitoring to prevent progression to advanced liver disease. Incorporating validated non-invasive fibrosis assessment tools into clinical evaluation may facilitate risk-based clinical decision-making. Furthermore, preventive strategies should account for regional variation in dietary habits, lifestyle patterns, and metabolic risk profiles across India7,22.

In conclusion, as the worldwide MASLD prevalence is expected to reach 55% by 20403, it represents an urgent global health threat. Particularly in India, this increasing burden and marked regional heterogeneity underscore the complex interplay of metabolic, lifestyle, genetic, and cardiometabolic factors in disease susceptibility and progression. Recognizing these population-specific differences will be important for developing more effective approaches to disease prevention and management.
