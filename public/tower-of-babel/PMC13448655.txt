Dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic dual-sensing electroluminescent display
Authors: Jong Woong Park, Donyoung Kang, Jihye Jang, Byungjun Kang, Seung Won Lee, Jioh Yoo, Chanyeong Jeon, Kaiying Zhao, Hyungsuk Lee, Cheolmin Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the significant development of technologies based on dielectrophoretic or acoustophoretic forces, the dynamic visualization of these forces is important. Here, we present an electroluminescent display that enables comparative visualization of dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic forces. Our display utilizes alternating current-driven electroluminescence in combination with conductive microparticle self-assembly controlled by dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic forces. When an alternating current electric field is applied across the split-electrode configuration, the self-assembly of the conductive microparticles originating from the dielectrophoretic force generates a dielectrophoresis-dependent electroluminescence from the display. Alternatively, when an acoustic wave is transmitted to the display, the electroluminescence developed by dielectrophoresis is deactivated by acoustophoresis. The electroluminescent responses are modulated by the frequency and intensity of the dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic forces, which enable force-sensitive visualization across both regimes and thereby a rewritable dual-mode sensing display. With this versatile display, visualization of temperature changes, noninvasive monitoring of microfluidic channels, and dynamic motion tracking are demonstrated. Our dual-mode electroluminescent display, responsive to forces across the piconewton to nanonewton range, holds significant potential for various applications in biomedical and physical sensing systems involving dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic stimuli.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Mechanical engineering, Electronic devices, Sensors and biosensors, Fluidics

Introduction

Stimuli-interactive displays that visualize pressure1,2, temperature3,4, and humidity5 have received significant interest because of their potential applications in electronic skins and the Internet of Things6. However, the realization of stimuli-interactive displays via conventional device platforms involves a physical connection between the sensors and displays via microprocessors, making the systems bulky and energy-inefficient1,6. To address this issue, many researchers have developed stimuli-interactive displays based on alternating current (AC)-driven electroluminescence (EL), which enables the direct visualization of stimuli without the aid of microprocessors7,8. Yet most stimuli-interactive displays have emphasized the visualization of sensible information1–5,7, while more potential lies in the dynamic visualization of stimuli that humans cannot perceive such as electric and magnetic fields9, ultrasound10,11, and infrared light12.

Dielectrophoretic (DEP) forces induced by AC electric fields have been employed for the manipulation13,14, separation15, and assembly16,17 of particles, as they provide opportunities for scalable, high-throughput, and contact-free methodologies. From this viewpoint, numerous techniques controlling the DEP force have been developed for various applications including sensors18, nanotweezers19,20, field-effect transistors21,22, photon emitters23,24, and light-emitting diodes25,26. Because the quantification of the DEP-driven self-assembly process requires a complex readout of electrical signals18 or post-processing of microscopic images19, the development of a sensing display enabling instantaneous human-readable output of DEP is imperative. This would provide a convenient method for fine control of the processes involved in DEP14. Furthermore, to realize a rewritable DEP display (where the information written in the display is erased and then the display is rewritten), a material strategy should be developed for an efficient disassembly of the DEP-driven particles with strong particle–particle attractions such as van der Waals and polar attractions13,15,27. Employing another technologically effective sensing force for efficiently disassembling particles, the display can be rewritable in DEP by additionally utilizing the sensing force.

An emerging field of acoustics includes various ultrasound transducers and surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices that operate in the megahertz frequency regime, suitable for sensing28,29, nondestructive testing30, and dynamic particle manipulation31,32,33,34. In particular, the controlled acoustophoretic force (ACP) of an acoustic wave readily traps particles in acoustic pressure nodes or antinodes with high precision over a large spatial region. This yields various applications such as patterning35, acoustic tweezers36,37, and holography38. We envisioned that acoustic waves capable of particle manipulation could be integrated into a DEP-sensing display, where conductive bridges driven by DEP are readily disassembled with ACP, making the display rewritable. It would be more advantageous if the ACP employed in the DEP-sensing display is also visualized with the disassembly of conductive bridges. This would yield a rewritable dual-mode sensing display of DEP and ACP.

Here, we present a dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic dual-sensing electroluminescent (DUET) display that enables dynamic visualization of both DEP and ACP forces. It consists of a split-electrode pair, an emissive layer with inorganic EL phosphors, and a chamber with carbon microspheres suspended in a nonpolar medium. The display is placed on a SAW device consisting of two interdigital transducers (IDTs) patterned on a piezoelectric substrate. When an AC field is applied across the split-electrode configuration of the display, the induced DEP force causes the self-assembly of the microspheres to form dendrites of conductive bridges between the two electrodes, which generates EL from the emissive layer (on-mode). On the other hand, the ACP force from the transmitted acoustic wave breaks the conductive bridges, which deactivates the EL emission (off-mode). The EL intensifies with the DEP force in the on-mode, whereas the EL intensity decreases with the ACP force in the off-mode of our display. This enables the quantification of both DEP and ACP in EL. Utilizing the change in the temperature-sensitive resonant frequency of a standing acoustic wave developed with periodically assembled microspheres, the temperature change is visualized using the DUET display. Moreover, air bubbles in a microfluidic system are noninvasively monitored and visualized with arrays of DUET display in real time. Our display represents a substantial advancement in direct dual-force visualization in a single device, further enhancing the capabilities of DEP and ACP technologies.

Results

Device architecture and dual-mode operation of the DUET display

The device structure of the DUET display, in conjunction with that of the SAW device, is illustrated in Fig. 1a. A 38-μm-thick emissive layer consisting of ZnS:Cu phosphors (diameter, 25 ± 5 μm) embedded in poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-trifluoroethylene-co-chlorofluoroethylene) [P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)] was fabricated by spin coating a composite solution on a split-electrode configuration (gap, 1 mm) of transparent indium tin oxide (ITO) on a 25 mm × 25 mm glass substrate (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). The high density of the ZnS:Cu phosphors caused the composite to exhibit a porous structure, as verified by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). The pores of the emissive layer were filled by spin coating a diluted polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) solution onto the emissive layer, as verified by SEM and energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping of the constituent atomic elements (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Moreover, a 120-nm-thick poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) layer was added by spin coating a PVA solution on the emissive layer. The PVA layer facilitated physical protection of the PDMS coating from n-hexadecane, which is a nonpolar medium. A 400-μm-thick rectangular silicone chamber attached to a cover glass (10.5 mm × 30 mm) was bonded to the spin-coated glass substrate which functions as a top cover of the chamber, by applying PDMS at the junction of the two components (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). The chamber was filled with a 4.5 wt% conductive fluid of carbon black (diameter, 2–12 μm) in n-hexadecane (Supplementary Fig. 5) and sealed with cover glasses. Here, n-hexadecane was chosen as the suspending medium to avoid the strong odors caused by higher volatility and the increased acute inhalation toxicity associated with shorter-chain alkanes. Prior to percolation, the uniformly dispersed suspension exhibited a baseline electrical conductivity of 1.95 × 10−8 S cm−1 (see Methods). The structure and components of the top cover were visualized by the SEM image shown in Fig. 1b. The SAW device consists of 2-μm-thick aluminum IDTs deposited on a 500-μm-thick 128° Y-cut lithium niobate substrate, with a finger width and spacing of 70 μm each (see Methods). Figure 1c shows photographs of a fabricated DUET display mounted on a SAW device (top) and the IDT pattern of the SAW device (bottom).

The modes of the DUET display are controlled by the DEP and ACP forces induced by external AC and acoustic pressure fields, respectively (Fig. 1d). When the DEP force induced across the split-electrode configuration is the dominant force acting on the carbon black microspheres, the microspheres migrate towards the gap between the electrodes and form conductive bridges below the top cover of the chamber, as shown in Fig. 1e (middle). In this situation, the total electrical impedance of the DUET display is lowered due to the high electrical conductivity of the bridges (Fig. 1d, left). In this state, the electric field distribution is converted from in-plane to out-of-plane (concentrated in the emissive layer)9, and field-driven EL from the ZnS:Cu phosphors appears (on-mode) (Supplementary Fig. 6, left). The intensity of the EL emission has a qualitative correlation with the percolation network density of the microspheres, which is determined by the strength of the DEP force. Therefore, the DUET display can detect and visualize the magnitude of the DEP force.

On the other hand, when the ACP force generated by the SAW device exceeds the strength of the DEP force, the microspheres are pushed towards the pressure nodes of the standing SAW (Fig. 1e, right). Here, the chamber of the DUET display is equivalent to a hybrid acoustic device with the presence of both SAW and bulk acoustic wave (BAW), which generates 3D patterns of the microspheres35. The pressure nodes created by the standing BAW are at a certain distance from the top cover of the chamber. Therefore, the microspheres detach from the gap between the electrodes, which leads to the destruction of the conductive bridges. This, in turn, causes the electric field distribution to concentrate at the in-plane gap between the ITO electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 6, right)9. As a result, the EL is deactivated by the ACP (off-mode). As the intensity of the ACP force increases, the total electrical impedance of the display increases, which causes the EL of the device to decrease with the ACP (Fig. 1d, right). Prior to investigating the detailed dual-mode operation mechanisms of the DUET display, we optimized the performance of a DUET display under an AC field (DEP force) (Supplementary Note 1, and Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). In addition, various parameters of the display components including the chamber height, electrode gap, and conductive fluid concentration were examined to understand how these parameters affect the DUET display performance (Supplementary Note 2, and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

Working principle and characteristics of the on-mode DEP-sensing display

Figure 2a illustrates the proposed states of particle self-assembly under different DEP forces proportional to the AC voltage at a given frequency. In the initial state, random Brownian forces act on the suspended microspheres, and self-assembly does not occur near the electrode gap (leftmost of Fig. 2a)18. When a certain AC voltage is applied to the electrodes, the microspheres gather towards the edges of the electrodes, where the electric field is maximized9,19. In addition, the induced dipole moment of the microspheres generates electrostatic attraction between these. This forms dendrites (chains of microspheres) underneath the top cover of the chamber16,39. As the AC voltage increases, more particles are attached to the chains, and conductive bridges are formed between the two electrodes. In this state, the electric field is concentrated in the out-of-plane direction9. Therefore, a further increase in the voltage causes the microspheres to form dendrites underneath the ITO electrodes39, and EL emission occurs (rightmost of Fig. 2a).

The microspheres of the DUET display only experience positive DEP within the typical AC-driven EL operating range of 102–105 Hz3, migrating towards the increasing electric field gradient27,40. To validate the positive DEP characteristic of the DUET display, we first calculated the real part of the Clausius–Mossotti factor (Re[ fCM*(ω)]), which indicates the effective polarizability of the particle in a medium. Here, fCM*(ω) is expressed as

where ω is the angular frequency of the AC signal, and εp*(ω) and εm*(ω) are the complex dielectric permittivities of the particle and medium, respectively (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Table 1)18. Particles experience a positive DEP when Re[fCM*(ω)] has a positive value. Meanwhile, negative DEP occurs for a negative value of Re[fCM*(ω)], wherein the particles are repelled towards the opposite direction. Because the electrical conductivity of the carbon microspheres is several orders of magnitude larger than that of n-hexadecane, Re[fCM*(ω)] showed a value near unity (unitless) at the lower end of the AC-driven EL operating range, around 102 Hz (Fig. 2b). As the frequency increased above 103 Hz, Re[fCM*(ω)] showed a slight decrease to 0.85, a value identical to the lower limit of Re[fCM*(ω)]. This indicates that Re[fCM*(ω)] is always positive (Supplementary Note 3). These results signify that positive DEP occurs in carbon microspheres regardless of the direction, intensity, and frequency of the external AC field. That is, the microsphere dendrites formed by DEP are hardly destroyed, and turning off the DUET display using an AC field is highly unlikely.

The formation of self-assembled microsphere dendrites was observed using real-time in situ optical microscopy (OM) (Supplementary Fig. 11a and Methods). To visualize the dendrite development under OM, an emissive layer with a lower phosphor content was used to increase the transmittance by up to 95.8% (Supplementary Fig. 11b,c). The leftmost OM image in Fig. 2c shows the initial state of the microspheres suspended in n-hexadecane (0 V) (Supplementary Movie 1). At 70 V, dendrites were observed at the edge of the right electrode, which further extended to form a conductive bridge at 140 V (Supplementary Fig. 12). A further increase in the voltage increased the percolation network density of the conductive bridges, as shown in Fig. 2d (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Methods). In addition, dendrites were observed below the ITO electrodes above 140 V (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 12). As shown in the series of photographs in Fig. 2e, the magnitude of the DEP force was quantified by the brightness and area of the EL emission of the DUET display at a given frequency (10 kHz).

The decrease in the electrical impedance of the DUET display with DEP force verified the proposed mechanism, wherein the external electric field increases the percolation network density of the microspheres (Fig. 2f). Here, the electrical impedance change is defined as ΔZE/ZE0 × 100, where ZE0 is the baseline electrical impedance without DEP force and ΔZE is the difference between ZE0 and the electrical impedance at a given DEP force. As the DEP force increased, the impedance sensitivity (SZ = Δ[ΔZE/ZE0 × 100]/ΔFDEP, which is the slope of the linear fit) decreased in magnitude from −0.57% to −0.34% pN−1, reflecting the saturation behavior of the percolation network density9. The corresponding EL change of the display (ΔL/L0 × 100) as a function of DEP force is shown in Fig. 2g. Here, L0 is the lowest detectable luminance and ΔL is the difference between L0 and the luminance at a given DEP force. As the DEP force increased, the EL sensitivity (SL = Δ[ΔL/L0 × 100]/ΔFDEP) increased from 92.38% to 237.40% pN−1. In the EL spectra of the DUET display in Fig. 2h, the EL intensity increased as a function of the AC voltage without a spectral shift, showing a maximum emission wavelength at 450 nm. When the voltage increased from 105 to 175 V, the time constant (defined as the time required for the intensity to reach 1 − 1/e of its maximum value) of the DUET display at 10 kHz decreased from 5.1 to 1.1 s (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Increasing the DEP frequency from 1 to 100 kHz caused an increase in the luminance (Fig. 2i). The luminance (L) of thin-film AC-driven EL displays can be expressed as

where η is the efficiency, C is the effective capacitance, f and V are the operating frequency and voltage, respectively, Vth is the threshold voltage across the device, and VEL,th is the threshold voltage across the phosphor layer42. Because L is proportional to f, higher frequencies drive the initial increase in brightness. Meanwhile, the EL light-emitting area of the DUET display decreased, as shown in Fig. 2j (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). This spatial reduction stems from the nonuniform electric field distribution across the display at a given voltage. Because regions with stronger electric fields (edges of the ITO electrodes) possess a lower Vth, they allow for easier carrier injection. Therefore, as the frequency increases, effective charge transfer and light emission become increasingly restricted to these concentrated, low-Vth regions. However, further increasing the frequency to 200 kHz resulted in insufficient time for carrier injection during each cycle43. The inadequate charge transfer, in turn, caused a significant decrease in both the overall luminance and EL area. It is also worth noting that driving the display at higher voltages amplified the dynamic nature of the particle percolation, which naturally broadened the device-to-device variation. Therefore, to ensure reliable reproducibility alongside moderate brightness and sufficient EL area in the on-mode, the DUET display was mostly operated at 10 kHz, 140 V (where a luminance of 11.76 cd m−2 is achieved). The time constant of the DUET display at 140 V remained similar at lower frequencies, measuring 3.5 s at 1 kHz and 3.7 s at 10 kHz. However, as the frequency further increased to 100 kHz, the time constant significantly increased to 9.7 s (Fig. 2k).

Working principle and characteristics of the off-mode ACP-sensing display

Figure 3a illustrates a dual-mode operating DUET display mounted on a SAW device. When the resonant acoustic frequency (fr, 13.830 MHz) of the SAW device is applied to the IDTs, the interference between the two opposing waves produces a standing SAW that leaks into the chamber of the DUET display (Fig. 1e, right)36. The SAW wavelength (280 μm) is determined by dividing the speed of sound in lithium niobate (3900 m s−1) by fr (Supplementary Table 2)35. Simultaneously, a standing BAW is generated by waves reflecting off the top glass substrate of the DUET display44. This strong reflection is driven by a significant acoustic impedance (ZA = ρc) mismatch at the glass/n-hexadecane interface, where ρ and c are the density and speed of sound in the material, respectively (Supplementary Table 2)45. The resulting BAW has a wavelength of 96 μm, corresponding to the speed of sound in n-hexadecane (1339 m s−1) at the applied fr46. Together, the SAW and BAW create a comprehensive 3D acoustic pressure field within the chamber. Because pressure nodes form every half-wavelength, the field yields spatial intervals of 140 μm and 48 μm along the x and z axes, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Supplementary Fig. 19 shows the proposed states of particle self-assembly under different ACP intensities of the SAW device. Because the ACP force is visualized by the number of microspheres dragged away from the top cover, the DEP force should be initially applied to the DUET display (on-mode) prior to applying the ACP force (rightmost of Fig. 2a and leftmost of Supplementary Fig. 19). When a certain voltage is applied to the IDTs to develop an ACP force, the dendrites below the ITO electrodes disassemble, and the corresponding EL emission at the edge (far from the electrode gap) is erased. As previously described, because the electric field is maximized near the electrode gap, the microspheres farther away from the electrode gap experience a weaker DEP force, which makes it easier to detach using the ACP force. As the input voltage of the IDTs increases, the microspheres near the electrode edges begin to detach, and the EL emission further decreases. An increase in the ACP force up to a few orders of magnitude larger than that of the DEP force causes the conductive bridges to collapse (rightmost of Supplementary Fig. 19). Here, the electric field distribution shifts from out-of-plane to in-plane, which completely deactivates the EL emission.

The OM images obtained by real-time in situ observations verified the proposed principle, as shown in Fig. 3b (Supplementary Movie 2). Because the focal plane of the images was at the top cover of the chamber, the microspheres became out-of-focus as these moved along the z axis with the ACP force50. When the ACP force increased while a constant DEP force (10 kHz, 140 V) was applied (Fig. 3c,d), the conductive bridges gradually collapsed, and the microspheres gathered towards the pressure nodes of the standing wave (Supplementary Fig. 20). This resulted in the visual quantification of the ACP force by monitoring the decrease in EL brightness and area, as shown in the photographs in Fig. 3e. A further increase in the SAW voltage to 22 V induced an FACP of approximately 1041.8 pN. It should be noted that the theoretical expressions for the DEP and ACP forces are based on isolated, single-particle models (Eqs. (2) and (6)). In the highly concentrated chamber of the DUET display, however, local particle–particle interactions become significant, and thus the calculated force values should be interpreted as scaling estimates rather than exact quantitative predictions. Electric field distortions between closely spaced particles induce dipole–dipole interactions that can locally surpass the primary DEP force, accelerating dendrite formation (see Supplementary Note 4)51. Likewise, at short interparticle distances, secondary acoustic radiation forces become comparable in magnitude to the primary acoustic radiation force, driving localized agglomeration52. Nevertheless, the idealized single-particle equations effectively characterize the macroscale onset of particle migration and the baseline force scaling required to operate the DUET display.

As shown in Fig. 3f, the electrical impedance change of the DUET display increased with the applied ACP force, as expected. The electrical impedance change (ΔZE/ZE0 × 100) is defined equivalently as the DEP case; however, ZE0 now represents the baseline impedance under a constant 24.7 pN DEP force (prior to applying the ACP force), and ΔZE is the subsequent impedance change induced by the ACP force. When the ACP force exceeded 115.8 pN, the impedance sensitivity (SZ = Δ[ΔZE/ZE0 × 100]/ΔFACP) decreased from 0.161% to 0.004% pN−1, which aligns with the sharp decrease in percolation network density observed primarily below 115.8 pN (Fig. 3d). Correspondingly, the EL change (ΔL/L0 × 100) decreased with increasing ACP force, where L0 is the initial luminance of the on-mode prior to ACP application (Fig. 3g). Similar to the impedance response, when the ACP force surpassed 115.8 pN, the EL sensitivity (SL = Δ[ΔL/L0 × 100]/ΔFACP) decreased in magnitude from −0.55% to −0.02% pN−1. It is noteworthy that because the DUET display is responsive to the piconewton to nanonewton-scale forces of DEP (6.2 pN–154.4 pN) and ACP (4.6 pN–1.0 nN), it is compatible with various microsystems in which these minute forces are controlled for the patterning14,35, sensing18, and tweezing19,37 of micro and nanoparticles53. The EL spectra of the DUET display shown in Fig. 3h exhibited a decrease in intensity with the ACP force while maintaining the maximum emission wavelength at 450 nm. This was similar to the emission spectral behavior observed in the on-mode DEP sensing display (Fig. 2h).

The interplay between the DEP and ACP forces of the DUET display was explicitly evaluated by examining how the response to the ACP force varied under different DEP intensities and frequencies, chamber heights, and conductive fluid concentrations of the DUET display (Supplementary Note 5, and Supplementary Figs. 22–25). We also examined the response of the DUET display when the acoustic wave phase control or frequency modulation was applied. Because the phase control causes a shift in the pressure nodes within the xy plane, it had a minimal effect on the EL emission of the DUET display. This verifies that the ACP force sensing of the display is governed by the particle movement along the z axis (Supplementary Fig. 26). Furthermore, the DUET display proved compatible with a higher-frequency SAW device (fr, 27.54 MHz) utilizing a 140 μm wavelength (Supplementary Fig. 27). Operating at the second harmonic of the original design, the system maintained its acoustic resonance conditions. This demonstrates the expandability of the DUET display, allowing for tunable spatial resolution and frequency-dependent particle manipulation35. The conductive fluid successfully maintained its responsiveness to DEP and ACP forces across a variety of carbon-based micro- and nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 28). In addition, although a wide range of suspending media also proved effective for the DUET display, n-hexadecane emerged as the superior choice, showing the darkest and most stable off-mode (Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Fig. 29).

The DUET display is rewritable because the conductive bridges destroyed by the ACP force can be reconstructed by the DEP force (shifting from the off-mode back to the on-mode), and consecutive switching between the two modes can be accomplished. To evaluate its rewritability, OM images were obtained during consecutive switching between the two modes (Supplementary Fig. 30 and Supplementary Movie 3). After the collapse of the dendrites with the acoustic wave transmitted from the SAW device (off-mode), new dendrites were formed when the DEP force acted on the microspheres (on-mode). The variation in the EL intensity of the DUET display when various acoustic-wave intensities were applied further verified its rewritability, as shown in Fig. 3i. Initially, both time constants of the rise and fall decreased as the SAW voltage increased, with the rise time constant dropping to 0.12 s at 7 V and the fall time constant reaching a minimum of 0.06 s at 10 V. At higher voltages (>10 V), the rise time constant remained relatively stable, whereas the fall time constant fluctuated, jumping to 0.21 s at 13 V before decreasing again (Supplementary Movie 4). We note that even with the higher viscosity (3.5 mPa s) of n-hexadecane among the available alkane options54, the DUET display exhibited rapid, sub-second time constants9. Figure 3j shows the reliability of the dual mode (on-off cycle), where 10 cycles were performed without significant alternation in the EL intensity of each mode.

Visualization of temperature change via SAW frequency control

In addition to the direct EL visualization of the ACP forces controlled by the input voltages of the SAW device (Fig. 3), the EL of the DUET display is modulated by the different input frequencies of the SAW device. In particular, because characteristic frequencies such as the antiresonant frequency (fa) of the SAW device are sensitive to temperature, this enables a direct visualization of the temperature change in the DUET display, and the results are shown in Fig. 4. The temperature of the DUET display mounted on the SAW device was controlled using a thermoelectric liquid chiller connected to a cold plate placed underneath the lithium niobate substrate, as schematically shown in Fig. 4a (see Methods). To visualize the temperature change, we utilized the frequency shift of fa of the SAW device originating from the softening of the resonator material (lithium niobate)55,56. The frequency shift of the SAW device modifies the ACP force applied to the DUET display. This gives rise to a temperature-dependent EL in the display, as schematically shown in Fig. 4a.

First, we measured the magnitude of the scattering parameter (|S11|) of the SAW device from 13 to 15 MHz at room temperature (23 °C), as shown in Fig. 4b. The |S11| indicates the return loss of a radio frequency signal system, which is closely related to the electromechanical coupling characteristics of lithium niobate in SAW devices56. Therefore, the frequencies at which the |S11| exhibited minimum and maximum values correspond to the fr and fa of our SAW device which are approximately 13.830 and 14.615 MHz, respectively. The EL change of the DUET display under different SAW frequencies showed a trend similar to that of the |S11| of the SAW device. The maximum change was −78.8% near fr, and the minimum change was −6.1% near fa, (Fig. 4b). Figure 4c shows the frequency dependence of the |S11| for SAW devices at different temperatures. As the temperature increased from 20 to 40 °C, the |S11| curve shifted to the left, which caused a decrease in fa from approximately 14.620 to 14.582 MHz. The correlation between the temperature change and fa shift was further investigated, as shown in Fig. 4d. The results show a temperature sensitivity of −2 kHz °C−1, derived from the slope of the linear fit, and a temperature accuracy of ±1.5 °C56. The effect of temperature on the frequency shift can also be described by the temperature coefficient of frequency (TCF), which is defined as the relative change in frequency with temperature:

where v and λ are the speed of sound and wavelength of the SAW in the resonator material, respectively, and T is the temperature of the SAW device56. For our SAW device, the TCF in the 15–40 °C regime was calculated to be −137 ppm °C−1.

Because the EL change of the DUET display was most sensitive near fa (Fig. 4b), we observed significant EL change sensitive to temperatures ranging from 20 to 40 °C, as shown in Fig. 4e. It should be noted that dodecane (rather than n-hexadecane) was used as the medium to ensure stable operation of the DUET display at 20 °C—which is near the freezing point of n-hexadecane (18 °C)—while remaining above the volatility and toxicity thresholds of much shorter alkanes57. It was verified that the frequency-dependent response of the DUET display with dodecane was similar to that with n-hexadecane (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 31). As the temperature increased, the curve of the EL change exhibited an overall shift to the left (Fig. 4e). This reflected the frequency shift of the SAW device with the increase in temperature (Fig. 4c). At each temperature, the frequency at which the DUET display exhibited minimum EL change matched with the fa of the SAW device. Therefore, at a given temperature, the fa of the SAW device was visually identified by the frequency at which the EL of the DUET display exhibited minimum change (Fig. 4f). In addition, by quantifying the shift of fa from 14.615 MHz (fa at room temperature), the temperature change of the system can be determined conveniently without complex equipment. Because the DUET display is directly compatible with existing SAW sensors, it is also potentially suitable for visualizing other stimuli such as strain, ultraviolet light, and ion concentration28.

Noninvasive visual monitoring of microfluidic channels and dynamic motion tracking

To practically realize the advantages of this versatile display, the DUET display was demonstrated for the noninvasive monitoring of microfluidic channels. As schematically shown in Fig. 5a, a 100-μm-thick silicone microfluidic channel (width, 3 mm) was inserted between a DUET display and SAW device. Water flowed through the microfluidic channel (flow rate, 5.39 μL min−1) and the presence of an air bubble inside the channel was monitored by the EL change of the display. The movement of the air bubble was regulated using a syringe pump connected to the inlet and outlet of the microfluidic channels (Fig. 5b and “Methods”). Owing to the large ZA difference between water (1.49 MRayl) and air (443 Rayl), the R at the water/air interface is almost 0.99 (Eq. (4) and Supplementary Table 2). Therefore, air blocks the acoustic wave effectively such that the acoustic wave generated from the SAW device was negligibly transmitted to the DUET display when the air bubble was below the display (“during” state). Here, the transmitted wave energy could be quantified by the transmission coefficient (T = 1 − R)45. This caused DEP to be the dominant force acting on the carbon microspheres inside the chamber of the DUET display. Moreover, EL occurred (on-mode) with a negligible effect of the acoustic energy when a bubble was inserted into the microfluidic channel, as shown in Fig. 5c (also see Fig. 5a, middle). Meanwhile, the absence of the air bubble (‘before’ or ‘after’ state) enabled a high T by water inside the microfluidic channel. This caused the DUET display to maintain an off-mode dominated by the ACP force, as shown in Fig. 5c (Fig. 5a, left and right). To further confirm the potential of the DUET display for broader applications in biomedicine, the detection of air bubbles was demonstrated within a microfluidic channel flowing with 0.1× Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline—a standard physiological buffer ubiquitous in cell culture and biological assays (Supplementary Fig. 32)58. Similar to the monitoring of the water-flowing channel, the display exhibited a clear distinction between the three states (“before”, “during”, and “after”). It should be noted that while the demonstration validates the extension of the DUET display to monitor physiological fluids, acoustic properties may vary with ionic compositions, and further studies would be needed to fully characterize the detection performance across various ionic media.

In addition, the feasibility of the DUET display for noninvasive monitoring was evaluated based on its spatial resolution, design constraints, and compatibility with existing microfluidic systems (Supplementary Notes 7 and 8). Because the monitoring mechanism relies on the target blocking the upward-propagating SAW from destroying the conductive bridges of the DUET display, the detectable object must be larger than the ITO electrode gap (see Fig. 5a). This constraint was verified by numerical simulations, which demonstrated that when a target bubble is smaller than the ITO gap (1 mm), the detachment velocity of the carbon microspheres increases, approaching that of a bubble-free scenario (Supplementary Fig. 33 and Methods). Consequently, the display would remain in the off-mode even in the presence of the target. However, the DUET display can still detect targets smaller than the ITO gap—such as lipid-coated microbubbles (diameter, 3.6 ± 0.1 μm)—provided their concentration is sufficiently high to collectively block the propagating SAW (Supplementary Fig. 34 and Methods). Together with analyses of different electrode gap sizes, these results indicate that the spatial resolution of the DUET display is determined by the interplay between the characteristic size of the target and the ITO gap size (Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36). Beyond spatial resolution, sufficient acoustic contrast between the target and the surrounding medium is required for detection. Numerical simulations show that as the ZA of the target increases above 0.9 MRayl (R, 0.06) and approaches that of the surrounding medium (water, 1.49 MRayl), the detachment velocity of the microspheres increases significantly, diminishing the detectability of the target (Supplementary Fig. 37). Therefore, sub-micron biological vesicles, whose ZA is nearly identical to that of their surrounding biofluids (~1.52 MRayl), need to be labeled with acoustic tags such as gold nanoparticles (~62.6 MRayl) to generate sufficient acoustic reflection for detection59,60.

In addition to its potential application in noninvasive monitoring, our multifunctional display provides dynamic motion tracking of acoustic wave sources (SAW devices) based on dual-mode DEP and ACP characteristics. As schematically shown in Fig. 5d, 3 × 3 arrays of DUET display (pixel size, 7 mm × 7 mm) was fabricated (Supplementary Fig. 38). Below the display arrays, we mounted a SAW device on an xyz motorized stage capable of moving from one pixel to another (see Methods). First, without the SAW device, the DUET display arrays in the initial state showed an on-mode for all the pixels driven by the DEP force (Fig. 5e). Introducing the SAW device at pixel (1,1) caused the pixel to shift from the on-mode to the off-mode because of the transmitted acoustic wave. Meanwhile, all the other pixels remained in the on-mode. When the SAW device moved from pixel (1,1) to pixel (2,1), pixel (2,1) showed an off-mode, whereas pixel (1,1) switched back to the on-mode. Because the crosstalk between adjacent pixels was negligible, individual pixels could be controlled by the SAW device, and the location of the SAW device could be detected by the pixel which exhibited the off-mode. Therefore, as the SAW device moved underneath pixels (1,1), (2,1), (3,1), (3,2), and (3,3), the pixels sequentially shifted from the on-mode to the off-mode and then back to the on-mode. The dynamic motion of the SAW device was monitored from the DUET display arrays, and by recording a series of images, we could detect the path of the SAW device which generated the letter “L” (Fig. 5e)2.

The application of the DUET display arrays shown in Fig. 5d was further expanded to detect air bubbles trapped inside the microfluidic channels by combining the air bubble detection capabilities of the DUET display (Fig. 5a–c), as shown in the top of Fig. 6a. Three-lane silicone microfluidic channels with a thickness of 100 μm (width, 7 mm) were inserted between the DUET display arrays and SAW device. This is similar to the design of the dynamic bubble detection shown in Fig. 5a. However, unlike Fig. 5a, the air bubbles were at a fixed position underneath pixels (1,3) and (2,2) in our scenario (Fig. 6a, bottom). The SAW device was moved underneath the microfluidic channels using an xyz motorized stage to determine the positions of the air bubbles trapped in the system. This method is identical to the dynamic motion tracking shown in Fig. 5d (Fig. 6b and Methods). When the air-bubble detection system was flipped over, two trapped air bubbles were evident, as shown in the rightmost photograph in Fig. 6b.

a Schematic illustration of the trapped bubble detection system (top) and the position of the bubbles inside the microfluidic channels (bottom). b Photographs of the setup (left), DUET display arrays (middle), and the microfluidic channels underneath the display (right). Acoustic wave transmission coefficient (T) (c), EL photograph (the white dotted squares indicate the pixels where the bubbles are positioned) (d), and the corresponding EL intensity of each pixel during the detection (e). As shown in Fig. 6c, the values of T were almost zero at the microfluidic channels underneath pixels (1,3) and (2,2) owing to the air bubbles. Meanwhile, the other channels showed a value near unity. As the SAW device scanned the microfluidic channels, all pixels except pixels (1,3) and (2,2) shifted from the on-mode to the off-mode when the SAW device was placed underneath, as shown in Fig. 6d. However, pixels (1,3) and (2,2) maintained the on-mode even when the SAW device was underneath the pixels. This enabled the identification of the locations of the air bubbles. Therefore, although dynamic motion tracking was possible with the DUET display arrays from the information of the pixels that displayed the off-mode, the information of the trapped air bubble locations was displayed by the pixels that maintained the on-mode, as shown in Fig. 6e. Therefore, the DUET display arrays can noninvasively detect and visualize air bubbles inside microfluidic channels, making them useful for fail-safe architectures in biomedical technologies and industries where the early detection of these defects is crucial61. Notably, unlike conventional optical methods62, the acoustic-based sensing of the DUET display enables reliable operation even within opaque systems. Furthermore, compared to capacitive and ultrasonic sensors that require external microprocessing units for spatial mapping63,64, our platform achieves direct, hardware-level 2D visualization without the need for complex signal processing. This eliminates the need for bulky equipment, offering a simple, compact, and low-cost monitoring system for various applications in biomedical engineering.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a dielectrophoretic and acoustophoretic dual-sensing electroluminescent display that enables the comparative visualization of the two forces through the precise manipulation of conductive particle self-assembly. By regulating the DEP and ACP forces within a single platform comprising a split-electrode pair, an emissive layer, and a suspension of conductive microparticles, we achieved dynamic and responsive visualization capabilities. The induced DEP force facilitated the migration of microparticles to form conductive bridges that activated EL, whose intensity scaled with the applied force. The introduction of the ACP force disrupted the bridges, providing a unique approach to modulating the display function based on acoustic parameters. This dual-sensing approach provides a method for utilizing complex forces and extends its applicability to tasks ranging from temperature visualization to dynamic motion tracking and noninvasive monitoring of microfluidic systems. The integration of our dual-sensing display, sensitive across the piconewton to nanonewton force range, provides a robust and versatile tool for the visualization and manipulation of conventionally available acoustic microsystems for sensing, medical imaging, nondestructive testing, and acoustic holography28,30,38,65.

Methods

Materials

Blue fluorescent phosphor microparticles (ZnS:Cu, LP-6864, M-17964-1) were purchased from Lonco Co. Ltd., Hong Kong. n-Hexadecane (≥99.0%) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-trifluoroethylene-co-chlorofluoroethylene) [P(VDF-TrFE-CFE)] was purchased from PIEZOTECH Inc., France. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and curing agent (Sylgard 184) were purchased from Dowhitech Co. Ltd. Silicone films (ELASTOSIL Film 2030 250) were purchased from Silex LTD, United Kingdom. 1-Decanol (150584, 98%), Deionized water (H2O, 38796), dodecane (anhydrous, ≥99.0%), 1-dodecanol (8.03462, ≥98.0%), Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, 59331 C), ethanol (≥99.5%), glycerol (G5516, ≥99.0%), oleic acid (≥99.0%), Pluronic F-68 (P1300), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, Mw = 130,000 g mol−1), mineral oil (M5094), methyl ethyl ketone (MEK, ≥99.0%), carbon black microspheres (diameter, 2–12 μm), and carbon nanoparticles (<100 nm) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 1,2-Dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC, 850355C) and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)−2000] (PEG2000 PE, 880120C) were purchased from Avanti Research, USA. Carbon nanofibers (>95%, diameter: 200–600 nm, length: 20–50 μm) were purchased from US Research Nanomaterials, Inc., USA. Carbon microfibers (Grade: XN-100-15M, diameter: 7–8 μm, length: 100–300 μm) were purchased from Nippon Graphite Fiber Corporation, Japan. All other organic solvents including acetone (≥99.5%), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, ≥94.0%), and n-hexane (≥95.0%) were purchased from Duksan Reagents and were used as received.

Fabrication of the DUET display

Indium tin oxide (ITO) was sputtered onto a 25 mm × 25 mm glass substrate through a patterned shadow mask to form a split-electrode configuration with a 1 mm gap. The thickness and sheet resistance of the ITO electrodes were 100 nm and 30 Ω cm−2, respectively. The ITO-patterned substrate was sequentially cleaned with acetone and IPA in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min each and then dried in an oven at 65 °C. For the emissive layer, a 10 wt% P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) solution in MEK was prepared by adding ZnS:Cu and P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) powders in the weight ratio 12.7:1. The mixture was spin-coated on the ITO-patterned substrate at 500 rpm for 60 s, which was then annealed at 80 °C for 8 h, forming a ZnS:Cu/P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) composite film. A PDMS solution of 33 wt% in n-hexane was prepared by mixing n-hexane, PDMS liquid, and curing agent in the weight ratio 20:10:1. The PDMS solution was spin coated on the ZnS:Cu/P(VDF-TrFE-CFE) composite film at 500 rpm for 60 s, which was then annealed at 80 °C for 4 h. For the protective layer, a 2.5 wt% PVA solution in deionized water was prepared by stirring the solution for 2 h at 70 °C. After UV-ozone surface treatment of the PDMS-coated emissive layer for 30 min, the PVA solution was spin coated onto the emissive layer at 1000 rpm for 60 s and annealed at 110 °C for 10 min. The chamber for the conductive fluid was prepared by bonding a 400-μm-thick silicone film on a 10.5 mm × 30 mm cover glass, assisted by O2 plasma surface treatment (CIONE4, Femto Science, Republic of Korea), which was then annealed in an oven at 60 °C for 10 min. The silicone films were prepared using a cutting plotter (CE7000-60, Graphtec, Japan). The chamber was attached to the spin coated top cover by applying PDMS at the junction of the two components. A 4.5 wt% conductive fluid was prepared by suspension of carbon black microspheres in n-hexadecane. The fluid was placed in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min to ensure a uniform suspension of the microspheres. The conductive fluid was then poured into the chamber, and the chamber was sealed with O2 plasma-treated cover glasses. The DUET display was operated at an alternating current (AC) of 10 kHz, 140 V using a function generator (33622A, Keysight, USA) connected to an amplifier (623B, Trek, USA).

Fabrication of the SAW device

A surface acoustic wave (SAW) device consisted of two aluminum interdigital transducers (IDTs) patterned on a 500-μm-thick 128° Y-cut lithium niobate substrate through conventional photolithography. The thickness of the IDTs was 2 μm, and the width of each IDT and the interval between the IDT fingers were both 70 μm, designed to produce SAW of 280 μm wavelength. The SAW device was bonded to a flat aluminum chip using conductive epoxy to minimize the effects of bulk acoustic wave (BAW) inside the lithium niobate substrate. The SAW device was mounted on a cold plate (P-400S, Takagi, Japan), connected to a thermoelectric liquid chiller (LK220, Thorlabs, USA) to maintain a constant temperature of 23 °C during the experiment. For the coupling liquid, deionized water was inserted between the SAW device and DUET display to ensure a high transmission of the generated acoustic wave. Two 100-μm-thick silicone films were used as a spacer between the SAW device and DUET display, which acted as a barrier of the coupling liquid. The radio-frequency signal applied to the IDTs was generated using a function generator (33622A, Keysight, USA) and passed through an amplifier (LZY-22+, Mini-Circuits, USA).

Characterization of scattering parameter and temperature-dependent properties

The magnitude of the scattering parameter (|S11|) of the SAW device was obtained by conducting an input frequency sweep from 13 to 15 MHz, with a step resolution of 2 kHz, and measuring the corresponding electrical signal using a SAW generator (BSG F20, Belektronig, Germany). The temperature-dependent characteristics of the SAW device and DUET display were measured by modulating the set temperature of the thermoelectric liquid chiller from 15 to 40 °C.

Dynamic bubble detection

A microfluidic channel was fabricated by attaching a 100-μm-thick silicone film (channel width, 3 mm) on a 10.5 mm × 50 mm cover glass using O2 plasma surface treatment and annealing at 60 °C for 10 min. Silicone films with a thickness of 200 µm were attached on the edges of the channels for tubing connection, also using plasma-activated bonding. 3D-printed thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPU) tube fittings were connected to the inlet and outlet of the microfluidic channels, and M2 screws were used to clamp the TPU tube fitting, microfluidic channel, and TPU tube support to prevent leakage. To combine the SAW device, microfluidic channel, and DUET display, two pairs of 100-μm-thick silicone films were used as a spacer between the SAW device/microfluidic channel and microfluidic channel/DUET display, and deionized water was injected into the spacers, acting as a coupling liquid. Using a syringe pump (flow rate, 5.39 μL min−1), air bubble containing deionized water flowed into the inlet of the microfluidic channel, passed underneath the DUET display, and left through the outlet. For the DPBS flowing microfluidic channel, 0.1× DPBS was prepared by mixing deionized water and 10× DPBS in the volume ratio 100:1. During the experiment, an AC of 1 kHz, 70 V was continuously applied to the DUET display, and the SAW device was operated at 13.830 MHz, 16 V.

Microbubble detection

To prepare the lipid-coated microbubbles, solutions of DPPC and PEG2000 PE in chloroform were mixed in specific quantities to yield final aqueous concentrations of 9.0 and 3.8 mg mL−1, respectively, upon subsequent rehydration. The chloroform was evaporated using a nitrogen gun, and the resulting lipid film was further dried in a desiccator for 2 h. The film was then rehydrated in deionized water, and Pluronic F-68 was added at 20 mol% of the total lipid concentration. To generate the microbubbles, the mixture was first treated in an ultrasonic bath at 55 °C for 20 min, followed by probe sonication (Q125-220, Qsonica, USA) using a 2 mm tip at 100% amplitude for 10 s. The synthesized microbubbles remained stable for over two weeks and resisted structural damage during acoustic-wave excitation. For microbubble detection, the aqueous dispersion of microbubbles was injected via tubing into a microfluidic channel identical to the one used for dynamic bubble detection. Control measurements were performed using a microfluidic channel containing only deionized water. The DUET display was operated at 1 kHz and 105 V, alongside the SAW device driven at 13.830 MHz, 16 V.

Dynamic motion tracking

A 3 × 3 array of DUET displays (pixel size, 7 mm × 7 mm) was fabricated using an ITO-patterned 30 mm × 30 mm glass substrate and a 400-μm-thick three-lane silicone film on a 30 mm × 36 mm cover glass. The specific fabrication conditions were equivalent to those used for the DUET display. The array of DUET displays was clamped to the side frames using a 3D-printed polylactic acid (PLA) holder and immobilized using an aluminum cylinder rod fixed on an optical breadboard. A SAW device (resonant frequency, 15.9 MHz) attached to an xyz motorized stage was fixed on the optical breadboard, underneath the DUET display arrays. To ensure the stable retention of the coupling liquid during the experiment, glycerol was used instead of deionized water. To prevent glycerol from sliding away, a 100-μm-thick square silicone film was used as a barrier. Prior to the experiment, glycerol was sandwiched between the SAW device and the DUET display arrays by moving the motorized stage along the z axis. As the motorized stage moved within the xy plane during the experiment, the mounted SAW device interacted with each pixel. The array of DUET displays was operated at AC 1 kHz, 88 V, and the input voltage of the SAW device was 17.5 V.

Trapped bubble detection

A three-lane microfluidic channel was fabricated by attaching a 100-μm-thick parallel three-lane silicone film (channel width, 7 mm) to a 30 mm × 41 mm polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrate. The edges of the channels were sealed by attaching inlet/outlet holed 400-µm-thick silicone films on the top, assisted by O2 plasma surface treatment and heat treatment at 60 °C for 10 min. The microfluidic channel was attached to the bottom (cover glass) of the DUET display arrays by plasma-activated bonding. Deionized water was injected into the microfluidic channels through the inlet/outlet holes, while two air bubbles were created and positioned underneath pixels (1,3) and (2,2). All other experimental designs were identical to the dynamic motion tracking demonstration described above.

Characterization of electrical properties

The electrical conductivities and dielectric constants of the materials comprising the conductive fluid were calculated by measuring the resistance and capacitance, respectively, of the fluid within a custom chamber using an LCR meter (E4980A, Agilent, USA)57,66. The chamber functioned as a parallel-plate capacitor and was fabricated by arranging two ITO-deposited glass substrates (area, 22 mm × 25 mm) in a face-to-face configuration. To secure a 2 mm gap between the ITO electrodes, the sides of the chamber were blocked with cut microscope slides and the outer edges were sealed with UV epoxy. The electrical impedances of the DUET display were measured using Agilent E4980A and a handheld LCR meter (U1733C, Keysight, USA).

Characterization of OM and SEM imaging

Optical microscopy (OM) imaging of the carbon black microspheres and in situ observation of particle self-assembly were performed using a Ni-U system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (DS-Q1Mc, Nikon). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterizations were performed using a JSM-7610F-Plus system at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. The cross-sectional morphologies of the top cover of the DUET display were characterized by preparing samples using an ion-beam-assisted cross-sectional polisher.

Image analysis

Images of particle self-assembly were processed using Fiji/ImageJ to obtain a topology-preserving quantitative description of arbor morphology. Frames were background-corrected via rolling-ball subtraction and binarized to generate dendritic masks, which were then skeletonized to produce one-pixel-wide centerlines. The skeletons were analyzed using the Analyze Skeleton plugin to classify pixels into endpoints, junctions, and branch segments, yielding branch-level morphometrics such as dendritic number and length. A dendritic length of 100 μm was defined as the percolation threshold for calculating the network density within a 2.87 mm × 2.87 mm area. The electroluminescence (EL) photographs of the DUET display were taken using a USB microscope (AM4113TL, Dino-Lite, Taiwan) operating at a 20× magnification, corresponding to a spatial resolution of 15.3 µm per pixel. The captured photographs were categorized into three states: (1) both DUET display and SAW device being off (off/off state), (2) DUET display being on while the SAW device is off (on/off state), and (3) both DUET display and SAW device being on (on/on state). The background intensity was calculated using an average intensity of the ‘off/off state’ images and the dark regions of the ‘on/off state’ images. Intensity histograms of the ‘on/off state’ images were displayed using MATLAB and were fitted to a bimodal Gaussian distribution using ‘fitgmdist’ function. The first and second modes of the Gaussian distribution were categorized as background and light-emitting area, respectively. The light-emitting area and average intensity of the EL emissions were calculated using a threshold by the bimodal Gaussian fitting.

Characterization of lipid-coated microbubbles

Particle candidates were identified using a two-stage, template-guided segmentation pipeline. First, one or more representative particles were manually selected as grayscale templates, and the target image was scanned via normalized cross-correlation to detect candidate matches. Locations exceeding a predefined similarity threshold were retained, and spatially redundant detections were removed via center distance-based suppression to generate a set of candidate bounding boxes. These bounding boxes were subsequently used as box prompts for Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM 2), which refined each proposal into a pixel-resolved segmentation mask. Final detections were verified by filtering based on the SAM 2 confidence score and, when necessary, mask circularity (4πA/P2, where A is the mask area and P is the contour perimeter) to suppress irregular false positives. For each accepted mask, the particle area was computed from the segmented pixel region, converted to physical units using the image-specific pixel size, and expressed as the equivalent circular diameter.

Simulation of DEP and ACP forces

Computational modeling was performed using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1. DEP force simulations were conducted without modeling the SAW device, and the PDMS coating and PVA protective layer of the DUET display were omitted. The thickness of the ITO electrodes was modeled as 500 nm, while all other dimensions were equal to those of the experimental device. The AC/DC module was used to apply AC currents to the ITO electrodes, and calculate the electric field distribution in the chamber. For the ACP force simulations, a cover glass was used as the top cover of the chamber rather than a spin-coated glass substrate. All other dimensions were equal to those of the experimental devices. The AC/DC module was used for applying electrical AC signals to the IDTs of the SAW device. The acoustics module was used for transferring the acoustic wave from the SAW device to the DUET display, and to estimate the forces in the chamber. The parameters not adopted from the material library of the software are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Simulations evaluating the effects of bubble and electrode gap size variations were conducted using a three-dimensional model consisting of three domains from top to bottom: conductive fluid, cover glass, and microfluidic channel. To simplify the model, the emissive and PVA layers were excluded, and the microfluidic channel domain acted simultaneously as the pathway for bubble transport and the coupling liquid for acoustic transmission. The conductive fluid domain was composed of n-hexadecane and carbon microspheres, and its top boundary reflected the ITO split-electrode configuration. To simulate the acoustic wave generated by the SAW device, a prescribed vibration was applied to the bottom boundary of the microfluidic channel domain. The vibration was implemented in the Pressure Acoustics interface as a normal displacement to emulate the standing SAW produced by the IDTs. The spatial dependence of the displacement was defined as 2ucos(2πx/λ), where u is the displacement amplitude, x is the lateral distance from the device center along the x axis, and λ is the acoustic wavelength. The response of the carbon microspheres was then quantified by tracking their z axis displacement under the coupled electrical and acoustic fields.
