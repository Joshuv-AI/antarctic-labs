A sharp logarithmic condition for the Hardy operator on L 1 ​ ( 0 , ∞ ) L^{1}(0,\infty) and ℓ 1 \ell^{1}
Samson Owusu-Ensaw, Benoît F. Sehba, Ransford T. Tweneboanah

Abstract
. The Hardy operator is not bounded on the space of integrable functions on the positive half-line and its discrete counterpart on summable sequences. we introduce a modified Hardy operator obtained by subtracting a natural corrective term, and characterize the largest subspace of integrable functions on which this modified operator maps into integrable functions. The sharp condition is a logarithmic integrability (summability) requirement whose weight reflects obstructions on both small and large scales.



1.
Introduction

The operator that bears Hardy’s name arose in his 1920 paper

Note on a theorem of Hilbert

[
5
]
, in which Hardy
gave an elegant proof of a result on double series due to Hilbert.
The continuous analogue appeared in explicit form in a 1925 paper

[
6
]
, where Hardy proved:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt\right)^{p}\!\,dx\;\leq\;\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)^{p}\,dx,\qquad 1<p<\infty,\;f\geq 0.

The constant
(p/(p-1))^{p}
is sharp. Hardy attributed the problem to Landau and Riesz;
the result was refined and extended by Hardy,
Littlewood, and Pólya in their celebrated monograph
[
8
]
. The above inequality is known as Hardy’s inequality, for more on this inequality, its discrete version, and related topics, we refer the interested reader to
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

The continuous Hardy operator is defined for measurable functions
f

by

(1)

\mathcal{Q}f(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt,\qquad x>0.

We recall that for
1\leq p<\infty
, the Lebesgue space
L^{p}(0,\infty)
consists of all measurable functions
f
on
(0,\infty)
such that

\|f\|_{L^{p}}:=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty.

The operator
\mathcal{Q}
is not bounded from
L^{1}(0,\infty)
into itself. In effect, consider for example the function

f_{0}(x)=\frac{\chi_{[1,2]}(x)-\chi_{[3,4]}(x)}{x}.

It is easy to see that
f_{0}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
and that

\mathcal{Q}f_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }0<x\leq 1\\
\frac{\ln x}{x}&\text{if }1<x\leq 2\\
\frac{\ln 2}{x}&\text{if }2<x\leq 3\\
\frac{\ln 6-\ln x}{x}&\text{if }3<x\leq 4\\
\frac{\ln(3/2)}{x}&\text{if }x>4.\end{cases}

It clearly follows that
\mathcal{Q}f_{0}\notin L^{1}(0,\infty)
.
In
[
7
]
, Hardy and Littlewood made the same observation.
They then proved that nevertheless, the following local result holds.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
7
, Theorem 11]

Let
a>0
. Assume that
f
is a positive measurable function defined on
(0,\infty)
. Then the following hold.

(i)

If
\int_{0}^{a}f(x)\log^{+}f(x)dx<\infty
, then

\int_{0}^{a}\mathcal{Q}f(x)dx\lesssim 1+\int_{0}^{a}f(x)\log^{+}f(x)dx.

(ii)

If
f
is decreasing and
K:=\int_{0}^{a}\mathcal{Q}f(x)dx<\infty
, then

\int_{0}^{a}f(x)\log^{+}f(x)dx\lesssim 1+K\log^{+}K.

We recall that for
x>0
,
\log^{+}x=\max\{0,\log x\}
. The notation
\log
is used for function while
\ln
is used when the argument is a number.

We aim in this note to consider a discussion of the case where the function under consideration is supported throughout the interval
(0,\infty)
and the integration of its transform is also considered throughout
(0,\infty)
. We also discuss its discrete analogue.

To be more precise on the problem in consideration, knowing that
\mathcal{Q}
is not bounded from
L^{1}((0,\infty))
to itself, we ask the following question: what is the maximal subspace of
L^{1}(0,\infty)
in which we can choose
f
so that
\mathcal{Q}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.

Our first observation will be that for an integrable function
f
,
\mathcal{Q}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
only if the integral of
f
is zero. This means in particular that for a non-negative integrable function
f
, we cannot have
\mathcal{Q}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Thus, for the problem to still be considered for functions
f
in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
, there is a need to perturb
f
to obtain a function
\tilde{f}
with the properties:
\tilde{f}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
and
\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}(x)dx=0
. We will see that this is equivalent (for an appropriate perturbation) to considering the boundedness on
L^{1}(0,\infty)
of the operator

\mathcal{H}f(x)=\mathcal{Q}f(x)-\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)dt.

Our result can be stated as follows:

If
f
is non negative and integrable, then
\mathcal{H}f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
if and only if

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\ln\left(2+x+\frac{1}{x}\right)dx<\infty,

with equivalence of the norms.

One may ask if there is any relation between the Hardy and Littlewood local result and ours. The answer is quite simple: their result is not only local but also, we are not considering the same operator. Also, because of the necessity for an integrable function to have mean zero for its image by the Hardy’s operator to be in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
, one cannot expect Theorem
1.1
to be global.

Let us say a few words about the discrete results. The discrete Hardy
operator
\Gamma
acts on a sequence
a=(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of real
numbers by forming its running Cesàro means,

(\Gamma a)_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k},\qquad n\geq 1.

The operator
\Gamma
is bounded on
\ell^{p}
(the space of
p
-summable sequences) for every
1<p<\infty
with
the sharp constant
(p/(p-1))^{p}
(the discrete counterpart of Hardy’s
classical inequality). As in the continuous case, the boundedness at the endpoint
p=1
fails: the sequence

(1/(k(k+1)))_{k}
is summable, yet its image under
\Gamma
has general
term
1/(n+1)
, which is not summable. The first discrete result of
this paper shows that this failure is
not an accident of the example: if a summable sequence
a
has a
summable image
\Gamma a
, then the sum of
a
must be zero.
This necessary condition motivates the introduction of the
discrete modified operator

(\widetilde{\Gamma}a)_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}-\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k},\qquad n\geq 1,

obtained by subtracting the corrective term

\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}
, in exact analogy with the
continuous construction. The discrete main result
identifies the sharp condition under which

\widetilde{\Gamma}a\in\ell^{1}
: for a sequence of positive terms,
this holds if and only if
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\ln(k+1)<\infty
.
The logarithmic weight
\ln(k+1)
is the discrete counterpart of the
weight
\ln(1+t)
that governs the large-scale behavior in the
continuous result; note that, unlike the continuous case, there is no
small-scale logarithmic obstruction for sequences, since the index
k

is bounded away from zero. The proof relies on a splitting of

\widetilde{\Gamma}a
into two parts, analogous to the decomposition
used in the continuous case, and on a comparison between partial
harmonic sums and their logarithmic asymptotic via the
Euler–Mascheroni constant. As a consequence, the
\ell^{1}
-norm of

\widetilde{\Gamma}a
is equivalent to

\gamma\sum_{k}a_{k}+\sum_{k}a_{k}\ln(k+1)
, providing a quantitative
version of the characterization.

We are motivated not only by Hardy-Littlewood local result Theorem
1.1
, but also by some recent and old results of the same vein obtained for some other operators: the Hilbert’s integral operator, the Bergman projection, and the maximal functions (see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
13
]
).

In the next section, we study the problem for the continuous Hardy operator while in Section 3, we discuss the discrete analogue results.

Throughout this text, for two positive quantities
A
and
B
, the notation
A\lesssim B
(resp.
A\gtrsim B
) means that
A\leq CB
(
A\geq CB
) for some universal constant
C>0
. If both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
hold, we write
A\approx B
.

2.
The result for the continuous Hardy operator

The key observation is that
\mathcal{Q}f\notin L^{1}((0,\infty))
whenever

\int_{0}^{\infty}f\,dt\neq 0
, because the integrand behaves like a
positive constant divided by
x
near infinity. The following
proposition makes this precise.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{Q}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.
Then

(2)

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt=0.

Proof.

For
x>0
, multiply both sides of (
1
) by
x
:

(3)

x\,\mathcal{Q}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt.

Since
f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
, the right-hand side of (
3
)
converges to
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt
as
x\to\infty
. In particular,

(4)

\lim_{x\to\infty}x\,|\mathcal{Q}f(x)|=\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt\right|=:\ell.

Suppose for contradiction that
\ell\neq 0
; without loss of generality
assume
\ell=1
(replace
f
by
\frac{f}{\ell}
if necessary).
By (
4
) for
\varepsilon=\tfrac{1}{2}
, there exists

M>0
such that for all
x>M
,

\bigl|x\,|\mathcal{Q}f(x)|-1\bigr|<\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad x\,|\mathcal{Q}f(x)|>\tfrac{1}{2}.

It follows that for all
x>M
,

(5)

\mathcal{Q}f(x)>\frac{1}{2x},

whence

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{Q}f(x)|\,dx\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}^{\infty}\frac{\,dx}{x}=\infty.

This contradicts
\mathcal{Q}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Hence, necessarily
\ell=0
,
which is (
2
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The condition
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt=0
is
necessary
but not
sufficient for
\mathcal{Q}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. For instance,

f_{e}(t)=\frac{1}{t(\ln t)^{2}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{(0,e^{-1})}(t)-\mathbf{1}_{(e,\infty)}(t)\right]
has integral zero,
but

\mathcal{Q}f_{e}(x)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{x\ln x}&\text{if }0<x\leq e^{-1}\\
\frac{1}{x}&\text{if }e^{-1}<x<e\\
\frac{1}{x\ln x}&\text{if }x>e\end{cases}

is not integrable.

It follows from the above proposition that the integrability of
\mathcal{Q}f
can only be considered for functions
f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt=0
. An alternative being that one can replace
f
by
f-\theta\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt
, where
\theta
is a smooth function with unit integral.

Consider
\theta(t)=\tfrac{1}{(1+t)^{2}}
which is smooth on
(0,\infty)
. Then we have

\mathcal{Q}\theta(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\theta(t)dt=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dt}{(1+t)^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+x},

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\theta(t)dt=1.

If we choose
\theta
as above, then we are led to study the following operator in place of
\mathcal{Q}
:

(6)

\mathcal{H}f(x)=\mathcal{Q}\left(f-\theta\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)dt\right)\right)(x)=\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt-\frac{1}{1+x}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)dt,

for
f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
.

Our main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
. Then
\mathcal{H}f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
if
f
satisfies

(7)

\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)+\ln\left(1+t\right)\right]<\infty.

Conversely, if
f
is non-negative, then
\mathcal{H}f\in L^{1}((0,\infty))
implies that
f
satisfies (
7
).

Obviously, we have
\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)+\ln\left(1+t\right)=\ln\left(2+t+\frac{1}{t}\right)
but we choose to write it as above to mark the fact that only one of the logarithms matters whenever we are dealing with small or large variables. We also note that this weight has the same behavior near zero and at infinity.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Sufficiency.

Assume
f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
satisfies (
7
).
Using the decomposition

(8)

\mathcal{H}f(x)=\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt-\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)dt,

we bound

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{H}f(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\int_{0}^{x}|f(t)|\,dt\,\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}|f(t)|\,dt\,\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}

where

I_{1}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\int_{0}^{x}|f(t)|\,dt\,\,dx

and

I_{2}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}|f(t)|\,dt\,\,dx.

Estimate of
I_{1}
.

By Fubini–Tonelli (exchanging the order of integration over the region

0<t<x<\infty
),

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|\int_{t}^{\infty}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\,dx\,\,dt

(9)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|\,\ln\!\left(\frac{t+1}{t}\right)\,dt\;<\;\infty.

Estimate of
I_{2}
.

By Fubini–Tonelli (region
0<x<t<\infty
),

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\,dx}{x+1}\,dt

(10)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|\,\ln(t+1)\,dt\;<\;\infty.

Hence
\mathcal{H}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
.

Necessity.

Conversely, assume
f
is a non-negative integrable function such that
\mathcal{H}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. As
f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\left[\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt\right]dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left[\int_{\max\{1,t\}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)dx\right]dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left[\int_{1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)dx\right]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left(\ln(2)\right)dt

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty.

We deduce that

\left|\int_{1}^{\infty}\left[\mathcal{H}f(x)-\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt\right]dx\right|<\infty.

Hence

\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)dtdx<\infty.

That is

\infty>\int_{1}^{\infty}f(t)\left(\int_{1}^{t}\frac{1}{x+1}dx\right)dt=\int_{1}^{\infty}f(t)\left[\ln(1+t)-\ln 2\right]dt.

Thus

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\ln(1+t)dt<\infty.

It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)dt\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{x+1}dx\right)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\ln(1+t)dt

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty.

We deduce that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\mathcal{H}f(x)+\frac{1}{x+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)dt\right]dx<\infty.

Hence

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt\right]dx<\infty.

That is

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left[\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+1}\right)dx\right]dt<\infty.

Thus

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)dt<\infty.

The proof is complete.
∎

In particular, we have the following.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
be non negative. Then
\mathcal{H}f\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
if and only
f
satisfies (
7
). Moreover,

(11)

\|\mathcal{H}f\|_{L^{1}}\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\left[\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)+\ln\left(1+t\right)\right]dt.

3.
The case of the discrete Hardy operator

The Hardy operator has a natural discrete counterpart that predates
the continuous version and retains independent importance in sequence
spaces and summability theory. We recall the following definition of sequence spaces.

Definition 3.1
.

For
1\leq p<\infty
,

\ell^{p}:=\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})=\left\{(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}:\|(a_{k})_{k}\|_{\ell^{p}}^{p}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|^{p}<\infty\right\}.

Let
a=(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of reals.
The
discrete Hardy operator
is

(12)

(\Gamma a)_{n}=A_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k},\qquad n\geq 1,

the running Cesàro mean of the sequence.

The boundedness of
\Gamma
on the discrete space
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{N})
(
1<p<\infty
) is a classic in the literature.

Theorem 3.2

(Hardy, 1920)
.

For
1<p<\infty
and
(a_{n})\in\ell^{p}
with
a_{n}\geq 0
,

(13)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\right)^{p}\;\leq\;\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{p}.

The constant
(p/(p-1))^{p}
is sharp.

Inequality (
13
) fails when
p=1
, as constant

p/(p-1)\to\infty
. In fact, it is easy to see that in this limit case,
\Gamma
is not bounded: take
a_{k}=\frac{1}{k(k+1)}
. Then
\|(a_{n})_{n}\|_{\ell^{1}}=1
while the sequence with general term
\left(\Gamma a\right)_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k(k+1)}=\frac{1}{n+1}
is not in
\ell^{1}
.

The discrete analogue of Proposition
2.1

is the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
a=(a_{n})\in\ell^{1}
. If
\Gamma(a)\in\ell^{1}
,
then
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=0
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\lambda:=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\neq 0
. Since
nA_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\rightarrow\lambda
as
n\to\infty
, we have
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n|A_{n}|=|\lambda|
. Thus, for
\epsilon=\frac{|\lambda|}{2}
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all integers
n\geq N
:
\left|n|A_{n}|-|\lambda|\right|<\frac{|\lambda|}{2}
. This gives
|A_{n}|>\frac{|\lambda|}{2n}
for all
n\geq N
. Therefore,

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|A_{n}|>\frac{|\lambda|}{2}\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}=\infty

contradicting
\Gamma(a)\in\ell^{1}
.
∎

In analogy with the continuous operator
\mathcal{H}
, if we let
\lambda=(\lambda_{n})_{n}
be the sequence defined by
\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}
, then the
discrete modified operator
is defined as

(15)

(\tilde{\Gamma}a)_{n}=\left(\Gamma(a-\lambda)\right)_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}-\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k},\qquad n\geq 1.

The following is the discrete analogue of Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
a=(a_{n})\in\ell^{1}
. Then
(\tilde{\Gamma}a)\in\ell^{1}
if

(16)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\ln(k+1)<\infty.

Conversely, if
a=(a_{n})_{n}
is sequence of positive terms and
(\tilde{\Gamma}a)\in\ell^{1}
, then
a=(a_{n})_{n}
satisfies (
16
).

Proof.

Decompose
(\tilde{\Gamma}a)_{n}=J_{1}(n)-J_{2}(n)
where

(17)

J_{1}(n)=\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k},\qquad J_{2}(n)=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}a_{k}.

For the
\ell^{1}
-norm of
J_{1}
, we easily obtain
we obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|J_{1}(n)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\cdot\frac{1}{k},

which is finite since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|<\infty
.

Using Fubini’s theorem and the equivalence

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}-\ln(n)\approx\gamma

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni’s constant, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|J_{2}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\left[\left(\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n}-\ln k\right)+\ln(k+1)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\ln(k+1)<\infty.

Conversely, assume
a=(a_{n})_{n}
is such that
a_{k}>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and that
\tilde{\Gamma}a\in\ell^{1}
. We still write
\tilde{\Gamma}a=J_{1}+J_{2}
where
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are defined by (
17
). We have seen that the
\ell^{1}
-norm of
J_{1}
is controlled by
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}
. Thus, we necessarily have that the sequence
J_{2}
belongs to
\ell^{1}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}a_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{n+1}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}a_{k}\sum_{n=2}^{k-1}\frac{1}{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}a_{k}\left[\left(\sum_{n=2}^{k-1}\frac{1}{n}-\ln(k-1)\right)+\ln(k-1)\right]

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\gamma\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}a_{k}+\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}a_{k}\ln(k-1).

Thus
\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}a_{k}\ln(k-1)<\infty
. This implies that
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\ln(k+1)<\infty
. The proof is complete.
∎

In particular, we have the following.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
a=(a_{n})\in\ell^{1}
be a sequence of positive reals. Then
(\tilde{\Gamma}a)\in\ell^{1}
if and only if

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\ln(k+1)<\infty.

Moreover,

\|\tilde{\Gamma}a\|_{\ell^{1}}\approx\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\ln(k+1).

4.
Conclusion

We have shown that the failure of the Hardy operator to be bounded
on
L^{1}(0,\infty)
is not merely a defect of the endpoint, but
carries a precise structural obstruction: the integrability of the image requires that the function have mean zero. Once this is taken
into account via a natural corrective perturbation, the resulting modified
operator
\mathcal{H}
admits a sharp characterization of its
domain of integrability in terms of the logarithmic weight

\ln(2+t+1/t)
, which simultaneously captures the behavior near
the origin and at infinity. The discrete analogue is fully parallel,
with the single logarithmic weight
\ln(k+1)
playing the
corresponding role, the absence of a small-scale obstruction
reflecting the discreteness of the index. Both results fit into
a broader program of identifying sharp substitutes for

L^{1}
-boundedness of classical operators, alongside analogous
results for the Hilbert-Hardy operator, the Bergman projection, and
maximal functions. Finally, it would be interesting to consider the case of Hardy operator in a higher dimension and other operators of the literature that are bounded on
L^{p}
for
1<p<\infty
, but not on
L^{1}
.
