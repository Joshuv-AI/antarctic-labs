Abstract

The purpose of this paper is to prove a conjecture that was given by Pelekis and Vlasák in 2020
[

Let
\text{{\bf x}}=(\text{{\bf x}}_{1},\text{{\bf x}}_{2},\ldots,\text{{\bf x}}_{n})

For a subset

We then set

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{s}(A):=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi^{\frac{s}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}+1\right)}\left(\frac{\operatorname{diam}\,C_{j}}{2}\right)^{s}:A\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j},\,\operatorname{diam}\,C_{j}\leq\delta\right\}

[
3

. By then setting

The definition in (

Throughout this paper,

agrees with the

As suggested by Pelekis and Vlasák

In this direction, a remarkable result from the discipline of extremal set theory

2.1.
The subgraph perimeter formula for
BV
functions

We make use of a standard perimeter formula for subgraphs of
BV
functions.
This formula is central to the proof of our main result, and we provide a derivation based
on a text by Giaquinta et al.
[
4
, §4.1.5]
.
A closely related subgraph characterization of
BV
functions traces back
to Miranda’s work on generalized Cartesian surfaces and sets of locally finite perimeter on Cartesian products
[
6
]
,
but we use a more modern approach based on the cited Giaquinta–Modica–Souček text

[
4
, §4.1.5]
.
The main result cited from Giaquinta–Modica–Souček is stated for base
dimension
m\geq 2
, and this is the only range in which we shall use the integral formula in (
14
) below.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open set, and let
u
be a Lebesgue-measurable representative of a function in
BV(\Omega)
. Being consistent with the notation of
Giaquinta–Modica–Souček
[
4
, p. 371]
, we define the subgraph of
u
by

\mathcal{S}_{u}=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}:y<u(x)\}
,
and we let

\pi:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\Omega

denote the orthogonal projection onto the first factor.
If
\eta
is a finite signed Radon measure on

\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, its
pushforward
under
\pi
, denoted

\pi_{\#}\eta
, is the finite signed Radon measure on
\Omega
defined by

(\pi_{\#}\eta)(B)=\eta(\pi^{-1}(B))=\eta(B\times\mathbb{R})

for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
. For vector-valued measures, the
pushforward is understood componentwise.

Again following Giaquinta–Modica–Souček
[
4
, p. 371]
,
we define the
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
-valued measure

(8)

\mu_{u}=\mu(Du):=(D_{1}u,D_{2}u,\ldots,D_{m}u,-\mathcal{L}^{m}).

From the same Giaquinta–Modica–Souček text
[
4
, §4.1.5, Theorem 1]
, we have the biconditional equivalence
such that
u\in BV(\Omega)

if and only if
\mathcal{S}_{u}
has finite perimeter in

\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
. In this case,

(9)

\pi_{\#}|D\chi_{\mathcal{S}_{u}}|=|\mu_{u}|.

Equivalently, the equality

(10)

|D\chi_{\mathcal{S}_{u}}|(B\times\mathbb{R})=|\mu_{u}|(B)

holds for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
.
In particular, taking
B=\Omega
, we obtain

(11)

P(\mathcal{S}_{u};\Omega\times\mathbb{R})=|\mu_{u}|(\Omega).

We now rewrite the right-hand side of
(
11
) in the usual
BV
notation. As above, the
Lebesgue decomposition theorem gives us that (
6
) holds,
again with (
7
) holding.
So, from (
8
), we obtain that

(12)

\mu_{u}=(\nabla u,-1)\,\mathcal{L}^{m}+(D^{\operatorname{s}}u,0).

The two measures on the right-hand side of (
12
) are mutually singular. So,
their total variations add, and we thus derive

(13)

|\mu_{u}|=\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\,\mathcal{L}^{m}+|D^{\operatorname{s}}u|

from (
12
).
Combining (
11
) and (
13
) then gives

(14)

P(\mathcal{S}_{u};\Omega\times\mathbb{R})=\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\,d\mathcal{L}^{m}+|D^{\operatorname{s}}u|(\Omega).

We emphasize that (
14
) is a perimeter formula for the subgraph, not a direct Hausdorff-measure formula for the graph. However, if
u
is continuous, then
the relative topological boundary of

\mathcal{S}_{u}
in
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
is precisely

\operatorname{graph}u=\{(x,u(x)):x\in\Omega\}
.
Since the reduced boundary is contained in the topological boundary, the
standard structure theorem for sets of finite perimeter gives

P(\mathcal{S}_{u};\Omega\times\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{H}^{m}(\partial^{*}\mathcal{S}_{u}\cap(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}))\leq\mathcal{H}^{m}(\operatorname{graph}u)
.
Thus, in the continuous case, the subgraph perimeter formula gives the lower
bound

(15)

\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\,d\mathcal{L}^{m}+|D^{\operatorname{s}}u|(\Omega)\leq\mathcal{H}^{m}(\operatorname{graph}u).

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open, and let
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
.
Suppose that, for each
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, the distributional partial
derivative
D_{i}u
is represented by a finite signed Radon measure on

\Omega
. Then
u\in BV(\Omega)
and

Du=(D_{1}u,D_{2}u,\ldots,D_{m}u)

is a finite
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued Borel measure on
\Omega
, and

V(u,\Omega)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|D_{i}u|(\Omega)<\infty.

Proof.

Let
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{m})\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

satisfy
|\varphi(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in\Omega
.
The distributional identity, initially stated for
functions in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, extends to
C_{c}^{1}(\Omega)
, by approximating
each
\varphi_{i}
in
C^{1}
by a smooth function with support contained in a
fixed compact subset of
\Omega
, and by then using
the finiteness of
D_{i}u
together with
u
being in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.
The definition of a distributional partial derivative gives us the equality

(16)

\int_{\Omega}u\,\operatorname{div}\varphi\,d\mathcal{L}^{m}=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{i}\,dD_{i}u.

Since
|\varphi_{i}|\leq 1
for
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, it follows from (
16
) that

(17)

\left|\int_{\Omega}u\,\operatorname{div}\varphi\,d\mathcal{L}^{m}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|D_{i}u|(\Omega).

Taking the supremum on the left-hand side of (
17
)
for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
we obtain that

V(u,\Omega)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|D_{i}u|(\Omega)<\infty,

i.e., so that
u\in BV(\Omega)
. Since each component
D_{i}u
is a finite signed
Radon measure, the tuple
Du=(D_{1}u,D_{2}u,\ldots,D_{m}u)
is a finite

\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued Borel measure.

A full definition of the
Cantor–Lebesgue function

\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]

involved in the below construction may be found in a number of texts in measure theory and real analysis

This provides a standard example of a nondecreasing, continuous, singular function on

Lemma 2
.

There exists a strictly increasing homeomorphism
\sigma:[0,1]\to[0,1]

such that

\sigma(0)=0
and
\sigma(1)=1
and
whose associated Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure is singular with respect to

Proof.

As above, we let
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be the Cantor–Lebesgue function. Now, let

K\subseteq[0,1]
be the middle-thirds Cantor set. Enumerate all nondegenerate
closed intervals with rational endpoints contained in
(0,1)
as

(18)

\{[a_{r},b_{r}]\}_{r=1}^{\infty}.

For each
r
, define

(19)

\gamma_{r}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq a_{r},\\[4.0pt]
\gamma\!\left(\dfrac{x-a_{r}}{b_{r}-a_{r}}\right),&a_{r}\leq x\leq b_{r},\\[10.0pt]
1,&x\geq b_{r}.\end{cases}

Set

(20)

\sigma(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}2^{-r}\gamma_{r}(x)

for all
x\in[0,1]
.
The series converges uniformly, giving us that
\sigma
is continuous. Since each

\gamma_{r}
is nondecreasing, it follows that
\sigma
is nondecreasing. Moreover,
the definition in (
20
)
gives that

\sigma(0)=0
and that
\sigma(1)=1
in a direct way.

Letting
0\leq x<y\leq 1
, we proceed to fix an index

r
satisfying
x<a_{r}<b_{r}<y
, so that (
19
) then gives us that

\gamma_{r}(x)=0
and
\gamma_{r}(y)=1
. From (
20
),
we then have that

\sigma(y)-\sigma(x)\geq 2^{-r}\bigl(\gamma_{r}(y)-\gamma_{r}(x)\bigr)=2^{-r}>0
, i.e., so that
\sigma
is strictly increasing. Since it is continuous and maps

[0,1]
onto
[0,1]
, it is a homeomorphism.

It remains to prove that the associated Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure is singular. Let

(21)

K_{r}=a_{r}+(b_{r}-a_{r})K.

The Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure associated with
\gamma_{r}
is supported on

K_{r}
. Moreover,
the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure
\mu_{\sigma}
associated with

\sigma
is such that: If

, then

(22)

\mu_{\sigma}((s,t])=\sigma(t)-\sigma(s)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}2^{-r}\bigl(\gamma_{r}(t)-\gamma_{r}(s)\bigr)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}2^{-r}\mu_{\gamma_{r}}((s,t]).

From (
22
), we find that

(23)

\mu_{\sigma}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}2^{-r}\mu_{\gamma_{r}}.

Now, define

(24)

E=\bigcup_{r=1}^{\infty}K_{r}.

From (
21
), we find that

K_{r}
has Lebesgue measure zero for each positive integer
r
.
Consequently, from (
24
)
together with the countable subadditivity of measures, the vanishing

\mathcal{L}^{1}(E)=0
holds. On the other hand, each measure
\mu_{\gamma_{r}}
is supported on
K_{r}
, and (using the expansion in (
23

we can then deduce that
\mu_{\sigma}
is supported on
E
. This gives us that

\mu_{\sigma}([0,1]\setminus E)=0
.
This together with the vanishing of
\mathcal{L}^{1}(E)
together give us that
\mu_{\sigma}\perp\mathcal{L}^{1}
.
∎

Z=(0,1)^{m-1}
, with the convention whereby
Z=(0,1)^{0}
is interpreted as a
one-point measure space of mass
1
. For
\text{{\bf z}}=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{m-1})\in Z

and
s\in(0,1)
, define

(29)

\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z})=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{i-1})\cdot(s)\cdot(\text{{\bf z}}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i+1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{m-1}),

omitting empty tuples for
i=1\wedge i=m
or

m=1
. For a Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
, define

B_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}=\{s\in(0,1):\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z})\in B\}
.
Let
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
. Define

(30)

w_{\mathbf{z}}(s)=u(\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z})).

Suppose that there exists a Borel set

Z_{0}\subseteq Z
such that
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(Z\setminus Z_{0})=0
and such that: For each
\mathbf{z}\in Z_{0}
, we have that

w_{\mathbf{z}}(s)

belongs to
BV((0,1))
, and its distributional derivative
Dw_{\mathbf{z}}

is a nonpositive finite signed Radon measure on
(0,1)
.
For
\mathbf{z}\in Z_{0}
, set

\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}=Dw_{\mathbf{z}}
,
and, for
\text{{\bf z}}\in Z\setminus Z_{0}
, set

\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}=0
.
Assume that the family
\{\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}\}_{\mathbf{z}\in Z}
is
measurable in the sense that, for every Borel set
A\subseteq(0,1)
, the map

\mathbf{z}\mapsto\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}(A)

is
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}
-measurable. Assume, moreover, that

(31)

\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|((0,1))\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})<\infty.

Then

-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\,d\mathcal{L}^{m}(x)=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,d\nu_{i}

for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and

(32)

\nu_{i}(B)=\int_{Z}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

for every Borel set

B\subseteq\Omega
. Moreover,

(33)

|\nu_{i}|(B)=\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

Equivalently, after identifying
D_{i}u
with the measure
\nu_{i}
, one may
write
D_{i}u(B)
in place of
\nu_{i}(B)
in
(
32
).

Proof.

For
\text{{\bf z}}\in Z_{0}
, we have
that
\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}=Dw_{\mathbf{z}}
, while for

\mathbf{z}\in Z\setminus Z_{0}
, we have that
\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}=0
.
For a Borel set

B\subseteq\Omega
, the section

B_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}=\{s\in(0,1):\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z})\in B\}

is a Borel subset of
(0,1)
. Moreover, by the measurability assumption on
the family
\{\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}\}_{\mathbf{z}\in Z}
, together with the
usual monotone class argument starting from Borel rectangles in
\Omega
, the
maps

\mathbf{z}\mapsto\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}(B_{\mathbf{z}}^{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{z}\mapsto|\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}|(B_{\mathbf{z}}^{i})

are
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}
-measurable.

Define a signed set function
\nu_{i}
on the Borel subsets of
\Omega
by

\nu_{i}(B)=\int_{Z}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

Since each
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is nonpositive,
\nu_{i}
is also
nonpositive, and

-\nu_{i}(B)=\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
.

We next check countable additivity. Let
\{B_{r}\}_{r=1}^{\infty}
be pairwise
disjoint Borel subsets of
\Omega
. Then, for each

\boldsymbol{\eta}\in Z
,

\left(\bigcup_{r=1}^{\infty}B_{r}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i}=\bigcup_{r=1}^{\infty}(B_{r})_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i},

and the union on the right is pairwise disjoint. Since

|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|
is a positive measure, monotone convergence
gives

\displaystyle-\nu_{i}\left(\bigcup_{r=1}^{\infty}B_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|\left(\left(\bigcup_{r=1}^{\infty}B_{r}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i}\right)\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\int_{Z}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|((B_{r})_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|((B_{r})_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(-\nu_{i}(B_{r})).

Thus
-\nu_{i}
is a positive Borel measure, and hence
\nu_{i}
is a signed
Radon measure. Also,

-\nu_{i}(\Omega)=\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|((0,1))\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})<\infty

by the assumed integrability condition. Therefore
\nu_{i}
is a finite signed
Radon measure. Since
\Omega
is an open subset of Euclidean space, the
standard regularity theorem for finite Borel measures gives that
\nu_{i}
is a
finite signed Radon measure.

Because
\nu_{i}
is nonpositive, its total variation is
|\nu_{i}|=-\nu_{i}
.
Consequently,
the relation

|\nu_{i}|(B)=-\nu_{i}(B)=\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

holds for each Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
.
This proves the asserted total variation formula for
\nu_{i}
.

It remains to identify
\nu_{i}
with the distributional partial derivative

D_{i}u
. Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. By the definition of

\nu_{i}
, first for simple functions and then by approximation using the
integrability of

\boldsymbol{\eta}\mapsto|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|((0,1))
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,d\nu_{i}=\int_{Z}\int_{(0,1)}\varphi(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\,d\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(s)\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

Since
Z\setminus Z_{0}
has
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}
-measure zero and

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=0
on
Z\setminus Z_{0}
, this equals

\int_{Z_{0}}\int_{(0,1)}\varphi(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\,dDw_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(s)\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

For each
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in Z_{0}
, the function

s\mapsto\varphi(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))

belongs to
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,1))
. Hence, by the one-dimensional definition of
the distributional derivative of
w_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,d\nu_{i}

\displaystyle=-\int_{Z_{0}}\int_{(0,1)}w_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\bigl(\varphi(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\bigr)\,ds\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=-\int_{Z_{0}}\int_{(0,1)}u(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\,ds\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

Since
Z\setminus Z_{0}
has
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}
-measure zero, Fubini’s theorem
then gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,d\nu_{i}

\displaystyle=-\int_{Z}\int_{(0,1)}u(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}(\iota_{i}(s,\boldsymbol{\eta}))\,ds\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m}(\mathbf{x}).

The final expression is
D_{i}u(\varphi)
. Therefore

D_{i}u(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,d\nu_{i}\qquad\text{for every }\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Thus
D_{i}u=\nu_{i}
as distributions. Hence
D_{i}u
is represented by the
finite signed Radon measure
\nu_{i}
, and the signed and total variation
formulas are precisely

\nu_{i}(B)=\int_{Z}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

and

|\nu_{i}|(B)=\int_{Z}|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}|(B_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{i})\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta}),

for every Borel set
B\subseteq\Omega
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

For each
a>0
, the function
u_{a}
belongs to
BV((0,1)^{m})
, the measure

Du_{a}
is singular with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{m}
, and

|D^{\operatorname{s}}u_{a}|\big((0,1)^{m}\big)=m.

Proof.

We again let
u=u_{a}\colon(0,1)^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as in
(
26

(34)

\text{{\bf x}}=(\text{{\bf x}}_{1},\text{{\bf x}}_{2},\ldots,\text{{\bf x}}_{m})

be an element in
(0,1)^{m}
, and let

.
We then define

\text{{\bf z}}=\text{{\bf z}}(\text{{\bf x}},i)=(\text{{\bf x}}_{1},\text{{\bf x}}_{2},\ldots,\text{{\bf x}}_{i-1},\text{{\bf x}}_{i+1},\text{{\bf x}}_{i+2},\ldots,\text{{\bf x}}_{m}).

Now, define

(36)

\Lambda_{i,\mathbf{x}}\colon(0,1)\to(0,1)

so that, for
s\in(0,1)
,

(37)

\Lambda_{i,\mathbf{x}}(s)=u_{a}\big(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{i-1},s,\mathbf{x}_{i+1},\mathbf{x}_{i+2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{m}\big).

The definitions in (

(
37
) together give us that
\Lambda_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(s)

=

\Phi_{a}\big(\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{1}\right)
,
\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{2}\right)
,
\ldots
,

\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{i-1}\right)

\sigma\left(s\right)
,
\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{i+1}\right)

\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{i+2}\right)

\ldots
,
\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{m}\right)\big)
,
again for
\sigma
as in Lemma
2
. In a similar spirit, we define

(38)

\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}\colon(0,1)\to(0,1)

so that: For a member
t
of the domain in (
38

(39)

\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(t)=\Phi_{a}\left(\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{1}\right),\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{2}\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{i-1}\right),t,\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{i+1}\right),\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{i+2}\right),\ldots,\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{m}\right)\right).

By then setting

(40)

A=A_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}=a\prod_{j\neq i}\left(1-\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{j}\right)\right)>0

and

(41)

B=B_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}=\prod_{j\neq i}\sigma\left(\text{{\bf x}}_{j}\right)>0,

a combined application of the definitions among (
25
), (
39
),
(
40
), and (
41

(42)

\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(t)=\frac{A(1-t)}{A(1-t)+Bt}.

From (
42

(43)

\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(t)^{\prime}=-\frac{AB}{\left(A(1-t)+Bt\right)^{2}}<0.

We then find that
\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}

is strictly decreasing on its domain, from (
43
).
So, since
\sigma
is strictly increasing and continuous,
we find that

\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(\sigma(s))
is continuous and strictly decreasing in
s
,
i.e., so that
\Lambda_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(s)
is continuous and strictly decreasing in
s
.
Also observe that
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(t)=1

and that
\lim_{t\to 1^{-}}\psi_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(t)=0
, with
\sigma(0)=0

and
\sigma(1)=1
, so that

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\Lambda_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(s)=1

and
\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}\Lambda_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}(s)=0
.

This gives that the one-dimensional total variation for
\Lambda_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}
over its domain
satisfies

\left|D\Lambda_{i,\text{{\bf x}}}\right|\big((0,1)\big)=1.

Now, write
\mu=d\sigma
. For each

\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{m-1})\in(0,1)^{m-1}
, define

\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}\colon(0,1)\to(0,1)
(cf. (
39
))
by

\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}(t)=\Phi_{a}\left(\sigma(\mathbf{z}_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(\mathbf{z}_{i-1})\right)\cdot\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\sigma(\mathbf{z}_{i}),\ldots,\sigma(\mathbf{z}_{m-1})\right),

omitting empty tuples in the manner suggested previously.
We also define

(45)

\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}(s)=u_{a}(\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z}))

for
s\in(0,1)
. We may rewrite the composition on the right-hand side of (
45
) so that

(46)

\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}=\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}\circ\sigma.

Moreover, if
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x},i)

\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}=\psi_{i,\mathbf{x}}
and
\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}=\Lambda_{i,\mathbf{x}}
.
Now, from (
46
),
the one-dimensional chain rule for Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures gives

D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}=(\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\circ\sigma)\,\mu.

Indeed, for
0<s<t<1
,

\int_{(s,t]}\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}(\sigma(r))\,d\mu(r)=\int_{\sigma(s)}^{\sigma(t)}\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}(q)\,dq=\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}(\sigma(t))-\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}(\sigma(s)),

and equality on intervals determines the corresponding Lebesgue–Stieltjes
measure.

Thus, for every Borel set
A\subseteq(0,1)
,

D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}(A)=\int_{A}\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}(\sigma(s))\,d\mu(s).

By the displayed formula for
\psi_{i,\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
, and by the analogous
formula for
\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}
, the map

(\mathbf{z},s)\mapsto\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}(\sigma(s))

is Borel measurable.
Since
\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}<0
, we also have that

|D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}|=-(\mathord{\pitchfork}_{i,\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\circ\sigma)\,\mu
.
Consequently, the maps

\mathbf{z}\mapsto D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}(A)
and
\mathbf{z}\mapsto|D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}|(A)

are
\mathcal{L}^{m-1}
-measurable for every Borel set

A\subseteq(0,1)
.

The preceding discussion shows that: For each
\text{{\bf z}}\in(0,1)^{m-1}
, we have that

\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}(s)=u_{a}(\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z}))

is continuous and decreasing. Hence
D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}
is a nonpositive finite Radon measure on

(0,1)
.

Given
\mathbf{z}\in(0,1)^{m-1}
, choose any

\mathbf{x}\in(0,1)^{m}
such that

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x},i)
.
Then

\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}=\Lambda_{i,\mathbf{x}}
. Now, by (
44
),

(48)

|D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}|((0,1))=1.

for every
\mathbf{z}\in(0,1)^{m-1}
.
Moreover,

\int_{(0,1)^{m-1}}|D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}|((0,1))\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\mathbf{z})=\int_{(0,1)^{m-1}}1\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\mathbf{z})=1,

with the convention that
(0,1)^{0}
has mass
1
when
m=1
.

Since
0<u_{a}<1
, we have that
u_{a}
is in
L^{1}((0,1)^{m})
. Consequently, Lemma
4
applies to
u=u_{a}
, with

Z_{0}=(0,1)^{m-1},\qquad w_{\mathbf{z}}=\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}},\qquad\lambda_{\mathbf{z}}=D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}.

Consequently, the equality

(49)

|D_{i}u_{a}|(B)=\int_{(0,1)^{m-1}}|D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}|\bigl(\{s\in(0,1):\iota_{i}(s,\mathbf{z})\in B\}\bigr)\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\mathbf{z}).

holds for every Borel set
B\subseteq(0,1)^{m}
. Taking

(50)

\left|D_{i}u_{a}\right|\big((0,1)^{m}\big)=\int_{(0,1)^{m-1}}|D\omega_{i,\mathbf{z}}|((0,1))\,d\mathcal{L}^{m-1}(\mathbf{z})=1.

In particular, for each

, the distributional partial derivative

(0,1)^{m}
. Also,

. Lemma
1
thus implies that

u_{a}
is in
BV\big((0,1)^{m}\big)
. Consequently, the distributional derivative

is a finite
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued Borel measure on
(0,1)^{m}
.

We write
\mu=d\sigma
in place of the Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure corresponding to

. From Lemma
2
, we have that

. So, there

Lemma 6

\Gamma_{a}
is an antichain in
[0,1]^{n}
. Hence the antichain upper bound (
3
) gives

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma_{a})\leq n
. Since
m=n-1
, this is the same as

(60)

\mathcal{H}^{m}(\Gamma_{a})\leq n.

From (
59
) and (
60
), we obtain that
\mathcal{H}^{m}(\Gamma_{a})=n
, recalling that
m=n-1
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

For all positive integers
n
and
k
, there exists a
k
-antichain
A\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
such that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A)=kn
.

Proof.

If
n=1
, then the order relation imposed on
[0,1]^{n}
is linear. So, by choosing a set
A\subseteq[0,1]
consisting of any
k
points in
[0,1]
, every chain in
[0,1]
meets
A

in at most
k
points, so that
\mathcal{H}^{0}(A)=k=kn
.

For
n=2
, this two-dimensional case is proved by Pelekis–Vlasák
[
7
]
.

Now, let
n\geq 3
, again with
m=n-1
. We then choose distinct positive numbers
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
\ldots
,
a_{k}
, and we proceed to define

(61)

A=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma_{a_{j}}.

From the definition in (
25
), we find that: If
\text{{\bf x}}\in(0,1)^{m}
is fixed, then
u_{a}(\text{{\bf x}})
is strictly increasing as a function of
a>0
. This gives us,
recalling (
27
), that the graphs
\Gamma_{a_{1}}
,
\Gamma_{a_{2}}
,
\ldots
,
\Gamma_{a_{k}}
are pairwise disjoint. Also observe that: Since
\Gamma_{a}
is the graph
of a continuous function, we have that
\Gamma_{a}
is a Borel set. So, since each of
\Gamma_{a_{1}}
,
\Gamma_{a_{2}}
,
\ldots
,
\Gamma_{a_{k}}
is a Borel set, the Hausdorff
measure
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
is additive on the finite disjoint union of the given Borel sets. So, the definition in (
61
) together with Lemma
6
give
us that

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma_{a_{j}})=kn.

From Lemma
3
, since each expression of the form
\Gamma_{a_{j}}

is an antichain, we find that: If
C\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
is a chain, then
C\cap\Gamma_{a_{j}}

contains at most one point, for an arbitrary index
j
. Consequently, the relations

\operatorname{card}(A\cap C)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\operatorname{card}(C\cap\Gamma_{a_{j}})\leq k

hold. So, since
A\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
is such that

\operatorname{card}(A\cap C)\leq k
for every chain
C

and is such that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A)=kn
, we obtain the desired result.
∎
