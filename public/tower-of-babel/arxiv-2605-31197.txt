Surface lone-pair polarization probed by quantum-geometric transport in tellurium
Nathanael N. Batista, Wendel S. Paz, Manuel Suárez-Rodríguez, Pierpaolo Fontana, Victor Velasco, Marcus V. O. Moutinho, Chang Niu, Peide D. Ye, Marco Gobbi, Fèlix Casanova, Luis E. Hueso, Caio Lewenkopf, Marcello B. Silva Neto

Abstract
Stereochemically active lone pairs are ubiquitous microscopic sources of polarity in molecules and solids, but their collective behavior in crystals is often hidden by symmetry or confined to surfaces. Here we show that quantum-geometry transport provides a sensitive probe of surface lone-pair polarization in trigonal tellurium. This surface polarization appears microscopically as an inversion-odd dipolar component of the crystal potential, which shifts the center of mass of Bloch wavepackets and produces quantum-geometric corrections to their velocity. We describe this lone-pair polar texture through a minimal three-component lattice model, and we show that the resulting linear and nonlinear transport coefficients probe, respectively, the second and first moments of the net polarization field. Because rectified voltages in tellurium flakes are directly proportional to the surface lone-pair polarization, our results provide a microscopic route to understanding and engineering polarization-driven, quantum-geometric electronic devices based on tellurium allotropes.



Introduction.–

Lone pairs of electrons are fundamental microscopic sources of polarity
13
;
26
;
27
.
In molecules, they determine geometry, enable directional bonding, and control the electric response through the stereochemical activity of nonbonding electronic states
13
. In solids lone pairs can remain stereochemically active and strongly influence bonding and structural stability
4
;
17
. However, their collective thermodynamics is often difficult to access experimentally, as the polar distortions may be hidden in local or dynamic disorder, constrained by crystalline symmetry, or reshaped by surfaces and interfaces
9
;
17
;
12
;
39
.

A paradigmatic example is trigonal tellurium
29
,
consisting of helical chains whose interchain interactions are mediated by stereochemically active lone pairs
41
.
Viewed in the basal plane, each unit cell hosts three symmetry-related in-plane lone-pair polar axes, whose directions are connected by the threefold rotational symmetry of the trigonal lattice
41
;
7
.
In the bulk, this symmetry enforces an exact cancellation of the in-plane dipoles, leading to zero net in-plane polarization.
In films or flakes, however, the loss of coordination at the surfaces breaks this
C_{3}
equivalence, allowing
an
in-plane polarization near the boundaries
3
.
Because both exposed surfaces select the same in-plane imbalance, their contributions add rather than cancel, making the surface polarization experimentally accessible when the corresponding bulk response is forbidden by symmetry
32
;
8
;
3
.

Recent transport experiments in tellurium flakes reported a giant anisotropy in the
linear resistivity

\rho_{xx}
and a large longitudinal second-harmonic response,
\chi_{xxx}

at zero magnetic field, featuring a characteristic in-plane angular dependence of the applied current with respect to the crystallographic direction, see Fig.
1
(a), as well as a strong dependence on
gating
35
.
Within the standard framework of intrinsic nonlinear transport in time-reversal-invariant metals, the Berry-curvature dipole is often identified as the dominant mechanism and is typically associated with Hall-like responses
32
;
20
;
33
.
The observed dominance of
\rho_{xx}
and
\chi_{xxx}
, together with their unique gate-voltage and temperature dependences, therefore raises a fundamental question: what microscopic degree of freedom is being probed by these linear and nonlinear transport signals?

In this work, we demonstrate that a net surface lone-pair polarization texture in tellurium, represented by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, produces a quantum-geometry
correction to the semiclassical wavepacket velocity whose leading inversion-odd contribution is proportional to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}

31
;
15
;
38
;
11
.
Within Boltzmann transport theory, this term generates a dominant longitudinal nonlinear conductivity
\chi_{xxx}<0
(for
{\cal E}_{0}>0
), that determines the second-harmonic response
V_{\parallel}^{2\omega}/(I^{\omega})^{2}\propto-\chi_{xxx}/\sigma_{D}^{3}

, where
\sigma_{D}
is the Drude conductivity and
I^{\omega}
is the driving current.
Concurrently,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
produces an inversion-even geometric correction
\propto\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{2}
to the linear conductivity,
\delta\sigma^{geo}_{xx}<0
, which governs the first-harmonic response
V^{\omega}/I^{\omega}\propto-\delta\sigma^{geo}_{xx}/\sigma_{D}^{2}

.
By comparing their temperature dependences with experiments, using a minimal lattice model, we demonstrate that
\chi_{xxx}
and
\delta\sigma^{geo}_{xx}
probe the first
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\rangle_{T}
and second
\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{2}\rangle_{T}

Finally, since
\chi_{xxx}
controls the DC rectification in tellurium (
V_{\rm DC}\propto\chi_{xxx}\propto\langle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\rangle_{T}

paving the way for the design of controllable, radio-frequency quantum-geometric (QG) rectifiers based on tellurium nanostructures.

Inversion-odd wavepacket dynamics.–

The surface lone-pair polarization enters the microscopic Hamiltonian as an inversion-odd polar component of the crystal potential,
V(\mathbf{r})
, producing interband dipolar mixing and a positional shift of the wavepacket center of mass.
We therefore start with a multi-band,
{\bf k}\cdot{\bf p}
Hamiltonian for the valence-band (VB) structure near the
H
point
5

\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{2}}{2m}+V(\mathbf{r})+\frac{\hbar}{m}\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{p}}+\frac{\hbar}{4m^{2}c^{2}}\left[\nabla V(\mathbf{r})\times\hat{\mathbf{p}}\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(s)},

(1)

where
m
is the electron mass,
c
is the speed of light, and
\sigma^{(s)}
are spin Pauli matrices.
The crystal potential is decomposed as
V(\mathbf{r})=V_{\rm even}(\mathbf{r})+V_{\rm odd}(\mathbf{r})
, where the inversion-odd component
V_{\rm odd}(\mathbf{r})
models the lone-pair polar field
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, and we neglect
k
-dependent spin-orbit terms.
Next, we project Eq. (
1
) onto the two uppermost valence bands,
H_{4}
and
H_{5}

14
.
Denoting the projector onto this subspace as
P_{45}
and its complement as
Q=1-P_{45}
, the effective VB Hamiltonian is obtained via Löwdin downfolding,

\hat{H}^{\rm eff}_{45}(\mathbf{k})=P_{45}\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})P_{45}+P_{45}\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})Q\frac{1}{\Delta E_{P-Q}}Q\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})P_{45},

(2)

where
\Delta E_{P-Q}=\varepsilon_{45}-Q\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})Q
, with
\varepsilon_{45}
denoting the unperturbed energy of the doublet.
The detailed derivation of this projection is given in the Supplemental Material
1
;
2
;
23
;
21
;
22
;
25
;
24
;
28
;
18
;
16
;
30
.
In this projected language, the local gradient of
V_{\rm odd}
manifests as a projected dipolar matrix element
P_{45}\left[\nabla V_{\rm odd}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r}_{c})\right]P_{45}
, defined in terms of the wavepacket center-of-mass coordinate
\mathbf{r}_{c}
and the internal coordinate
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
.
This allows us to express the polarization contribution within the projected subspace as
\hat{H}_{\rm pol}=-e\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r}_{c})
, where
\left.\nabla V_{\rm odd}\right|_{\mathbf{r}_{c}\simeq\mathbf{r}_{0}}\equiv-e\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
represents the local slope of the inversion-odd crystal potential at the ionic position
\mathbf{r}_{0}
(see Fig.
2
).
For a crystal with more than one atom in the unit cell, this definition generalizes to each atomic position; the polar scale entering transport is its corresponding unit-cell average, as discussed below.

The coupling to an applied electric field has the usual electric-dipole form,
\hat{H}_{E}=-e\,\mathbf{E}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r}_{c})

38
;
40
.
Since the inversion-odd polar crystal potential
\hat{H}_{\rm pol}
has the same operator structure as
\hat{H}_{E}
, the total dipolar operator responsible for interband mixing is simply

\hat{W}_{\rm dip}=-e[\mathbf{E}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}]\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r}_{c}).

(3)

An electric-dipole perturbation such as Eq. (
3
) admixes remote bands into the wavepacket and produces a gauge-invariant positional shift of its center of mass, denoted by
\delta r_{c,i}\equiv a^{\prime}_{n,i}=eG^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})[E_{j}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0,j}]
, where
G^{(n)}_{ij}
is a cross-gap tensor built from interband matrix elements
15
;
11
.

The same interband reconstruction also yields a second-order quantum-geometric correction to the wavepacket energy

\delta\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\rm geo}_{n}(\mathbf{k})=e^{2}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})[E_{i}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0,i}][E_{j}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0,j}],

(4)

where
\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
is the energy-normalized Berry connection susceptibility
11
;
15
.
While distinct from the standard intrinsic quantum metric
33
, its numerator is constructed from the same real interband Berry-connection matrix elements, whereas its energy denominator accounts for the spectral cost of virtual transitions to remote bands
11
;
15
.
Thus,
\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
is a quantum-metric-related response tensor whose derivative
\Gamma^{(n)}_{\alpha ij}(\mathbf{k})\equiv\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})
defines what we refer to as the QG correction to the velocity,
v^{\rm geo}_{n,\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=(1/\hbar)\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\delta\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\rm geo}_{n}(\mathbf{k})
,

v^{\rm geo}_{n,\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\Gamma^{(n)}_{\alpha ij}(\mathbf{k})[E_{i}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0,i}][E_{j}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0,j}].

(5)

Quantum-geometric transport.–

The calculation of conductivities starts with the definition of current
42

\mathbf{j}(t)=-e\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{k},t)\,f_{n}(\mathbf{k},t),\quad\int_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}.

(6)

Within the relaxation-time approximation,
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}=\tau
, and in the low-frequency regime
\omega\tau\ll 1
, the linear correction to the carrier distribution function induced by the field-driven acceleration
\dot{\mathbf{k}}=-(e/\hbar)\mathbf{E}
is
42

f_{n}^{(1)}(\mathbf{k},t)\simeq\frac{e\tau}{\hbar}\mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}f_{n}^{(0)},

(7)

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}f_{n}^{(0)}=\hbar\mathbf{v}_{n}(\mathbf{k})\partial_{\varepsilon}f^{(0)}
.

Trigonal tellurium contains three inequivalent Te-atoms in the unit cell
29
;
41
.
Therefore, the dipolar scale in
the long-wavelength transport theory, Eq. (
5
), is the unit-cell average of the three local polar slopes

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{a=1}^{3}{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}_{a},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}}_{a}\equiv-\frac{1}{e}\nabla V_{\rm odd}\Big|_{\mathbf{r}_{c}\simeq\mathbf{r}_{a}},

(8)

where
\mathbf{r}_{a}
labels the three Te positions inside the unit cell. Our DFT calculations, shown in Fig.
3
,
provide a direct microscopic visualization of these local polar slopes.
In the bulk, the three local gradients of the Hartree potential associated with the three
Te sites are related by
C_{3}
structure and cancel in the unit-cell average, so that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}=0
, despite the presence of strong local dipolar textures.
Near the two surfaces, however, the loss of interchain coordination breaks the equivalence of the three local environments promoting a surface redistribution of charge.
The cancellation is then incomplete, producing a finite surface contribution
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\parallel\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, in full agreement with earlier experimental observations
19
;
3
;
10
.
In what follows, we therefore parametrize the external drive relative to the special
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
axis as
\mathbf{E}(t)=E^{\omega}\sin\theta\cos\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}
,
where
\theta
denotes the angle relative to the polarization direction.

First-harmonic response.–

The first-harmonic current contains the usual Drude contribution plus the geometric correction proportional to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}^{2}
from Eq. (
5
), namely

j_{x}^{\omega}(t)=-e\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\left[v_{x,n}(\mathbf{k})+\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\Gamma^{(n)}_{xxx}(\mathbf{k})\mathcal{E}_{0}^{2}\right]f_{n}^{(1)}(\mathbf{k},t).

(9)

From Ohm’s law,
j_{x}^{\omega}(t)=\sigma_{xx}E^{\omega}\sin\theta\cos\omega t
, the conductivity along the polar axis is
\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{xx}^{D}+\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}
.
Here, the Drude term is
\sigma_{xx}^{D}=e^{2}\tau\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}v_{x,n}^{2}(\mathbf{k})(-\partial_{\varepsilon}f^{(0)})
and

\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}=\frac{e^{4}\tau\,\mathcal{E}_{0}^{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\Gamma^{(n)}_{xxx}(\mathbf{k})v_{x,n}(\mathbf{k})\left(-\partial_{\varepsilon}f^{(0)}\right).

(10)

Equation (
10
) shows that the linear longitudinal correction is inversion-even (
\propto\mathcal{E}_{0}^{2}
) and is nonzero because because the Fermi-surface average
\langle\Gamma^{(n)}_{xxx}(\mathbf{k})v_{x,n}(\mathbf{k})\rangle_{FS}\neq 0
.
The resulting conductivity tensor is diagonal, with principal components
\sigma_{xx}
and
\sigma_{zz}
.
Inverting
this tensor gives the resistivity
\rho(\theta)=\rho_{zz}\cos^{2}\theta+\rho_{xx}\sin^{2}\theta,

with
\rho_{ii}=1/\sigma_{ii}
, which precisely reproduces the angular dependence observed experimentally in the first-harmonic resistance shown in Fig.
1
(b).

\displaystyle E^{2\omega}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle E^{2\omega}_{\perp}

The angular dependence predicted by Eq. (

Finally, the quantum-geometric framework also predicts the absence of a nonlinear longitudinal response for currents flowing along the helical chains, that is, at

Here, since

To demonstrate that

Model for surface polarization.–

We now turn to the microscopic model of the polar scale
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
in tellurium.
We take the three local polar directions entering Eq. (
8
) as

\hat{\bf u}_{1}=(-1,0),\;\;\hat{\bf u}_{2}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right),\;\;\hat{\bf u}_{3}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right),

(16)

which satisfy
\hat{\bf u}_{1}+\hat{\bf u}_{2}+\hat{\bf u}_{3}=0
.
This constraint introduces a geometric frustration at the local level:
although the local dipoles at each triangular motif
t
develop a finite magnitude,
w_{t,a}
, the unit-cell polarization
{\bf P}_{t}=p_{0}\sum_{a=1}^{3}w_{t,i}\,\hat{\bf u}_{a}
vanishes in the bulk, when all three local slopes are equivalent,
w_{t,1}=w_{t,2}=w_{t,3}
.

\displaystyle E=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{t,a}(w_{t,a}-1)^{2}+\frac{J}{2}\sum_{\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle,a}(w_{t,a}-w_{t^{\prime},a})^{2}

\displaystyle-\sum_{t}h(j_{t})\,w_{t,1}.

Here

enforces restoration towards uniform dipole weights,

penalizes spatial variations via inter-triangle coupling between adjacent layers

is a surface-induced symmetry-breaking field,

The competition between

P_{x}(j)\sim e^{-j/\xi}

, shown as surface red arrows in Fig.

.
The sole outlier is sample S1, which was removed from the cryostat in between measurements, causing variations in doping level, thereby reducing the measured signal.

Temperature dependence.–

Finally, we relate the temperature dependence of the harmonic signals to the thermodynamics of the surface lone-pair polarization.
The surface breaks the threefold equivalence of the local lone-pair directions, while preserving the residual reflection symmetry
2\leftrightarrow 3
.
We can therefore write
w_{t,1}=1+2m_{t}/3
and
w_{t,2}=w_{t,3}=1-m_{t}/3
, so that the net polarization becomes,
\mathbf{P}_{t}=p_{0}\sum_{a=1}^{3}w_{t,a}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}=p_{0}m_{t}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
.
Here
p_{0}
is the microscopic dipole scale, and
m_{t}
the
local imbalance between the surface-selected direction and the two remaining
equivalent directions. Keeping the three dominant local sectors

m_{t}=+1,0,-1
, the single-triangle partition function is

Z_{t}=1+e^{x(T)}+e^{-x(T)},\qquad x(T)=\frac{h_{\rm eff}(T)}{k_{B}T}.

(18)

Here,
h_{\rm eff}(T)
is the effective surface-induced polar field. It represents the boundary bias
created by the loss of coordination at the surface, dressed by correlations
between neighboring triangles and thermal fluctuations (see
1
for details).
The first two thermal moments of the local polarization are given by

\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}=p_{0}\frac{e^{x(T)}-e^{-x(T)}}{Z_{t}},\quad\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}=p_{0}^{2}\frac{e^{x(T)}+e^{-x(T)}}{Z_{t}}.

(19)

While
\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}
measures the net inversion-odd surface polarization,
\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}
quantifies the even polar strength, incorporating local polar fluctuations of either sign.
The connection to transport directly follows from the quantum-geometric velocity framework.
Since the effective surface polar field is proportional to the lone-pair polarization
(
\mathcal{E}_{0}\propto P_{x}
), the first-harmonic correction
\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}\propto\langle\mathcal{E}_{0}^{2}\rangle_{T}
is even in the polarization and scales with
\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}
.
Conversely, the second-harmonic response
\chi_{xxx}^{\rm geo}\propto\langle\mathcal{E}_{0}\rangle_{T}
is inversion-odd and tracks
\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}
.
As a result, quantum-geometric transport serves as a dual probe of both the fluctuating polar strength and the net inversion-odd surface order via distinct, measurable harmonic voltages (see Fig.
6
).

Conclusion.–

The framework developed here opens several experimentally testable directions that extend beyond the interpretation of existing data.
First, Eq. (
5
) implies the existence of a higher-order, inversion-even nonlinear response that has not yet been reported, that is a third-harmonic longitudinal conductivity of the form

\frac{V^{3\omega}}{(I^{\omega})^{3}}\;\propto\;\left(\frac{2\chi^{2}_{xxx}}{\sigma_{D}^{5}}-\frac{\zeta_{xxxx}}{\sigma_{D}^{4}}\right),\qquad\zeta_{xxxx}<0,

(20)

which leads to a characteristic gate-voltage dependence combining distinct power laws,
\propto\mu^{-6}
and
\propto\mu^{-9/2}
.
A second key direction concerns rectification.
The nonlinear conductivity responsible for the second-harmonic response must also generate a rectified DC voltage under a driving AC or radiofrequency field
E_{x}(t)=E_{\rm RF}\cos\omega t
.
Under open-circuit conditions, the induced voltage
V_{x}^{\rm DC}
satisfies
36

V_{x}^{\rm DC}\propto-\frac{\chi_{xxx}}{\sigma_{D}}\,E_{\rm RF}^{2}\propto\mathcal{E}_{0}\propto\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T},

(21)

up to device-geometry and impedance-matching factors.
This mechanism can be generalized to other materials featuring frustrated polar structures, surface-confined dipole textures, or geometrically constrained order parameters,
Such systems represent ideal platforms for engineering QG rectifiers that can be tuned via strain, sample thickness, gate voltage, temperature, and surface design.

Harmonics generated by

in

For reference, let us first consider electric-field bias. If

\displaystyle j_{a}^{(2)}(t)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{abc}E_{b}^{\omega}E_{c}^{\omega}\cos^{2}\omega t

S1.1
Current-bias inversion: from
\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{E})
to
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{j})

Let
\rho_{ab}

\rho_{ac}\sigma_{cb}=\delta_{ab}.

We seek the perturbative inverse of Eq. (
S2

E_{a}=\rho_{ab}j_{b}+\eta_{abc}j_{b}j_{c}+O(j^{3}).

Substituting Eq. (

\displaystyle j_{a}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{ab}\left(\rho_{bc}j_{c}+\eta_{bcd}j_{c}j_{d}\right)+\chi_{abc}\left(\rho_{bd}j_{d}\right)\left(\rho_{ce}j_{e}\right)+O(j^{3})

(S8)

The quadratic term must vanish identically, which gives

\eta_{ade}=-\rho_{ab}\chi_{bmn}\rho_{md}\rho_{ne}.

For a scalar isotropic conductor, Eq. (

(S13)

\displaystyle E(t)=

\displaystyle\rho j^{\omega}\cos\omega t-\frac{1}{2}\chi\rho^{3}(j^{\omega})^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\chi\rho^{3}(j^{\omega})^{2}\cos 2\omega t+O[(j^{\omega})^{3}].

(S14)

Thus the rectified and second-harmonic electric-field amplitudes under current bias are

E^{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\chi\rho^{3}(j^{\omega})^{2},\qquad E^{2\omega}=-\frac{1}{2}\chi\rho^{3}(j^{\omega})^{2}.

(S15)

The minus sign is important: a positive nonlinear conductivity produces a negative

(S18)

Since

\frac{V^{2\omega}}{(I^{\omega})^{2}}=-\frac{L}{2A^{2}}\frac{C_{\chi}C_{\sigma}^{2}}{C_{\sigma}^{3}}\rho^{2}=-\frac{L}{2A^{2}}\frac{C_{\chi}}{C_{\sigma}}\rho^{2}.

(S19)

S1.3
Relation to the quantum-geometric correction

In the quantum-geometric mechanism discussed in the main text, the surface lone-pair
polarization selects
\mathbf{E}_{0}=E_{0}\hat{\mathbf{x}}
and generates a dominant nonlinear
conductivity
\chi_{xxx}^{\rm geo}
. Within the low-frequency relaxation-time
approximation,

\chi_{xxx}^{\rm geo}=\frac{2e^{4}\tau E_{0}}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\Gamma_{xxx}^{(n)}(\mathbf{k})v_{x,n}(\mathbf{k})\left[-\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{n}^{(0)}\right].

(S37)

Substituting Eq. (
S37

Eqs. (
S34
) and
(
S35

\displaystyle\frac{V_{\parallel}^{2\omega}}{(I^{\omega})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L_{\parallel}}{A^{2}}\frac{e^{4}\tau E_{0}}{\hbar}\rho_{xx}^{3}\left[\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\Gamma_{xxx}^{(n)}(\mathbf{k})v_{x,n}(\mathbf{k})\left(-\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{n}^{(0)}\right)\right]\sin^{3}\theta,

(S38)

\displaystyle\frac{V_{\perp}^{2\omega}}{(I^{\omega})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{L_{\perp}}{A^{2}}\frac{e^{4}\tau E_{0}}{\hbar}\rho_{xx}^{3}\left[\sum_{n}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\Gamma_{xxx}^{(n)}(\mathbf{k})v_{x,n}(\mathbf{k})\left(-\partial_{\varepsilon}f_{n}^{(0)}\right)\right]\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta.

(S39)

These expressions show explicitly that the odd second-harmonic voltage is linear in the
surface polar scale
E_{0}
. Under inversion,
E_{0}\rightarrow-E_{0}
and

\chi_{xxx}^{\rm geo}\rightarrow-\chi_{xxx}^{\rm geo}

second-harmonic voltage changes sign. By contrast, the linear quantum-geometric

If one writes the polar-axis conductivity as

\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{D}+\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo},\qquad|\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}|\ll\sigma_{D},

(S40)

then the corresponding resistivity is

\displaystyle\rho_{xx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{D}+\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}}=\rho_{D}-\frac{\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}}{\sigma_{D}^{2}}+O[(\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo})^{2}],

\displaystyle\rho_{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{D}}.

(S41)

The first-harmonic longitudinal response therefore contains

\frac{V_{\parallel}^{\omega}}{I^{\omega}}=\frac{L_{\parallel}}{A}\left[\rho_{zz}\cos^{2}\theta+\left(\rho_{D}-\frac{\delta\sigma_{xx}^{\rm geo}}{\sigma_{D}^{2}}\right)\sin^{2}\theta\right],

(S42)

to first order in

signals are controlled by

-\chi_{xxx}\sin^{3}\theta/(2\sigma_{D}^{3})

channels, respectively.

S2
Parity-odd semiclassical dynamics

To clarify the role of the inversion-odd structure in the semiclassical description, we distinguish two distinct physical effects:

•

the external electric field, which controls the motion of the wavepacket in reciprocal space, and

•

the intrinsic inversion-odd crystal potential, which does not act as a macroscopic force but instead modifies the internal structure of the Bloch wavepackets through inderband mixing effects.

Accordingly, only the external field enters the semiclassical equation of motion for the crystal momentum of the wavepacket, i.e.

\hbar\dot{\mathbf{k}}_{c}=-e\,\mathbf{E}(t).

(S43)

S2.1
Microscopic origin of the polar scale

We write the microscopic Hamiltonian as

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}^{\rm even}+\hat{H}_{0}^{\rm odd}-e\,\mathbf{E}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}},

(S44)

where
\hat{H}_{0}^{\rm even}
contains the inversion-even part of the lattice
Hamiltonian,
\hat{H}_{0}^{\rm odd}
contains the inversion-odd polar part of the crystal potential, and
\hat{H}_{E}(t)=-e\,\mathbf{E}(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}
is the coupling to the externally applied voltage field (here
e>0
denotes the magnitude of the electron charge).

We denote by
\mathbf{R}_{\ell}
the generic ionic position and let

\boldsymbol{\delta}{\bf r}=\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{R}_{\ell}

(S45)

be the local coordinate measured from that site. The inversion-odd part of the crystal potential can be expanded locally as

V_{\rm odd}(\mathbf{R}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\delta}{\bf r})=V_{\rm odd}(\mathbf{R}_{\ell})+\left.\nabla V_{\rm odd}\right|_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}{\bf r}+\cdots.

(S46)

In a centrosymmetric environment the linear term would be absent at the ionic
site. In a polar, inversion-odd environment, the local slope does not need to vanish,
\left.\nabla V_{\rm odd}\right|_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\neq 0
. It is therefore useful to define a local polar dipolar scale
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
as

\left.\nabla V_{\rm odd}\right|_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}{\bf r}\equiv-e\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}{\bf r}.

(S47)

Equivalently,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}=-\frac{1}{e}\left.\nabla V_{\rm odd}\right|_{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}}.

(S48)

Therefore, the leading dipolar contribution defines an intrinsic polar scale, which does not act as a macroscopic electric field, but as a compact parametrization of inversion-odd interband matrix elements.

S2.2
Unified dipolar coupling

Looking at Eqs. (
S44
) and (
S47
), both the external electric field and the intrinsic polar structure enter the wavepacket dynamics through the dipolar operator acting on the internal coordinate:

\hat{W}_{\rm dip}=-e\left[\mathbf{E}(t)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\right]\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{r}}-\mathbf{r}_{c}).

(S49)

However,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is not a macroscopic electrostatic field and thus does not contribute to the semiclassical force equation.

We therefore define the effective dipolar field

\mathbf{E}_{\rm eff}=\mathbf{E}(t)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}

(S50)

which controls all interband mixing effects.

S2.3
Wavepacket geometry

It has been shown that the first-order correction to a Bloch state due to an electromagnetic perturbation produces a gauge-invariant displacement of the wavepacket center of mass
11
. On general ground, this positional shift reads

\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\prime}=\langle u_{n}|i\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{n}^{\prime}\rangle+\langle u_{n}^{\prime}|i\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}|u_{n}\rangle,

(S51)

where
\ket{u_n}
is the unperturbed cell-periodic Bloch function and

\ket{u_n'}
is its first-order correction due to the effective dipolar field, given by

\ket{u_n'}=\sum_{m\neq n}\ket{u_m}\frac{\bra{u_m}\hat{W}_{\rm dip}\ket{u_n}}{\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m}}.

(S52)

Using the interband connections

\mathcal{A}^{i}_{mn}=\langle u_{m}|i\partial_{k_{i}}|u_{n}\rangle,\qquad m\neq n,

(S53)

we can write the matrix element of the effective dipolar operator as

\bra{u_m}\hat{W}_{\rm dip}\ket{u_n}=-e\left[E_{i}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{0,i}\right]\mathcal{A}^{i}_{mn}.

(S54)

For an external electric field and in the absence of magnetic field, this shift has the specific form

a^{\prime}_{n,i}=e\,\mathcal{G}^{(n)}_{ij}E_{j},

(S55)

where
\mathcal{G}^{(n)}_{ij}
is the cross-gap geometric tensor

\mathcal{G}^{(n)}_{ij}=2\,{\rm Re}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{(v_{i})_{nm}(v_{j})_{mn}}{(\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m})^{3}}.

(S56)

Here,
(v_{i})_{nm}
are interband velocity matrix elements. In a two-band model this tensor is directly proportional to the quantum metric with an additional energy-denominator factor. Thus, the positional shift is the real-space manifestation of quantum-geometric interband mixing.

In analogy with Eq. (
S55
), the positional shift in the present case becomes

a^{\prime}_{n,i}=e\,\mathcal{G}^{(n)}_{ij}\left[E_{j}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{0,j}\right].

(S57)

This expression shows that the internal polar scale
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
enters the real-space position of the wavepacket through the same cross-gap geometric tensor that governs the response to an external electric field.

S2.4
Energy correction

By means of second order perturbation theory one can derive a correction to the unperturbed band energy. For a single isolated band
n
, it reads

\Delta\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}^{(2)}=e^{2}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}E_{\rm eff,i}E_{\rm eff,j},

(S58)

with
\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}
the quantum-geometric susceptibility of the band

\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{{\rm Re}\left[\mathcal{A}^{i}_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{mn}\right]}{\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m}}.

(S59)

This susceptibility is closely related to the quantum metric

g^{(n)}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})={\rm Re}\sum_{m\neq n}\mathcal{A}^{i}_{nm}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{mn},

(S60)

with an additional interband energy denominator. In an effective
two-band description,
\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}
reduces to the quantum metric divided by the band gap
15
.

Expanding Eq. (
S58
) in terms of the external electric field and the polarization scale gives

\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=e^{2}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}E_{i}(t)E_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+2e^{2}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}E_{i}(t)\mathcal{E}_{0,j}

\displaystyle\quad+e^{2}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{0,i}\mathcal{E}_{0,j}.

(S61)

The three contributions have distinct roles.
The first is the usual second-order Stark correction to the wavepacket energy, the last renormalizes the equilibrium polar band structure, and the mixed term encodes the key effect of interest: a response linear in the external field enabled by the intrinsic inversion-odd nontrivial structure.

S2.5
Semiclassical dynamics

The semiclassical equations of motion retain their standard form
11

\displaystyle\hbar\dot{\mathbf{k}}_{c}

\displaystyle=-e\,\mathbf{E}(t),

(S62)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\varepsilon}_{n}-\dot{\mathbf{k}}_{c}\times\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{n},

(S63)

where

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}+\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}^{\prime},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{n}^{\prime}=\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\prime},

(S64)

with
\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\prime}
is given by Eq. (
S57
). The quantum-geometric contribution to the band velocity is

\delta v_{n,\alpha}=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}E_{\rm eff,i}E_{\rm eff,j},

(S65)

which can be expanded on the same lines of Eq. (
S61
) to get the separate contributions

\displaystyle\delta v_{n,\alpha}^{\rm geom}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}E_{i}(t)E_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2e^{2}}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}E_{i}(t)\mathcal{E}_{0,j}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{\alpha}}\Lambda^{(n)}_{ij}\mathcal{E}_{0,i}\mathcal{E}_{0,j}.

(S66)

The physical interpretation of the present construction is the following: the external electric field
\mathbf{E}(t)
governs momentum-space transport via
\dot{\mathbf{k}}_{c}
, while the intrinsic polar structure acts only through interband mixing, modifying the wavepacket geometry, energy, and velocity.
It does not introduce an additional semiclassical force, but instead enters transport observables through quantum-geometric response functions.

S3
Löwdin downfolding and Christoffel symbols in the valence sector

In this Section we derive the effective quantum-geometric structure of the
upper valence sector of tellurene. The goal is to obtain the Christoffel
symbols that enter the Fermi-surface average

\left\langle v_{a}\Gamma_{bcd}\right\rangle_{\rm FS}=\sum_{n}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left(-\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon_{n}}\right)v^{n}_{a}(\mathbf{k})\,\Gamma^{n}_{bcd}(\mathbf{k}),

(S67)

where the motion is restricted to the experimentally relevant
k_{x}
–
k_{z}

plane, so that
k_{y}=0
. We shall focus on the two components relevant for the

\Gamma_{xxx},\qquad\Gamma_{zxz}.

(S68)

The first one controls the longitudinal geodesic velocity generated by an

S3.1
Bare
H_{4}
–
H_{5}

We begin from the upper valence block

\mathcal{H}_{45}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{45}(\mathbf{k})-S_{1}k_{z}-\Delta_{2}&-2\Delta_{1}+u_{45}(\mathbf{k})\\
-2\Delta_{1}+u^{*}_{45}(\mathbf{k})&\varepsilon_{45}(\mathbf{k})+S_{1}k_{z}-\Delta_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(S69)

with

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{45}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=(A-r)k_{\perp}^{2}+Bk_{z}^{2},

(S70)

\displaystyle u_{45}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-tk_{\perp}^{2}+2uk_{z}^{2}-2iw_{1}k_{z}.

(S71)

For the leading geometric mechanism we may set
t=w_{1}=0

are negligible in the valence-band parameter set

. The resulting two-band

\mathcal{H}_{45}=d_{0}^{(45)}(\mathbf{k})\,\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{d}_{45}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(S72)

\mathbf{d}_{45}(\mathbf{k})=\left(-2\Delta_{1}+2uk_{z}^{2},\,0,\,-S_{1}k_{z}\right).

(S73)

Thus the bare

–
H_{5}

in its pseudospin texture.
Consequently, although the quantum metric is finite, it has no dependence on

In particular,

\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{xx}^{(45)}=\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{zz}^{(45)}=0,

(S74)

\Gamma_{xxx}^{(45)}=0,\qquad\Gamma_{zxz}^{(45)}=0.

(S75)

This shows that an isolated

sector
14

S3.2
Löwdin downfolding of the
H_{6},H_{6^{\prime}}

The full valence Hamiltonian is written in block form as

\mathcal{H}_{4\times 4}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{H}_{45}(\mathbf{k})&\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{k})\\
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})&\mathcal{H}_{66^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\end{pmatrix}.

(S76)

Since the

Löwdin expression

\mathcal{H}_{45}^{\rm eff}=\mathcal{H}_{45}-\mathcal{T}\left(\mathcal{H}_{66^{\prime}}-\varepsilon\right)^{-1}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}.

(S77)

To leading order in

\left(\mathcal{H}_{66^{\prime}}-\varepsilon\right)^{-1}\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta_{2}}\,\mathbb{I},

(S78)

so that

\mathcal{H}_{45}^{\rm eff}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{45}-\frac{1}{\Delta_{2}}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}.

(S79)

Keeping the leading hybridization terms that generate a new pseudospin

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}
contains an identity

pseudospin component in the low-energy
H_{4}
–
H_{5}
subspace. Restricting to

k_{x}
–

\mathcal{H}_{45}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})=d_{0}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})\,\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{d}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma},

(S80)

with

\displaystyle d_{x}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=M(k_{z})\equiv-2\Delta_{1}+2uk_{z}^{2},

(S81)

\displaystyle d_{y}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-\beta\,k_{x}^{2}k_{z},

(S82)

\displaystyle d_{z}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-S_{1}k_{z},

(S83)

where

\beta\equiv\frac{2Rv}{\Delta_{2}}

(S84)

for real

The essential result is Eq. (
S82

pseudospin texture in the otherwise trivial
H_{4}
–
H_{5}

This term is even in

and odd in

responsible for the finite gradient of the quantum metric in the low-energy valence manifold.

For compactness, we define

D(\mathbf{k})\equiv|\mathbf{d}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})|=\left[M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right]^{1/2},

(S85)

where, in the last expression, the weak

dependence of

simplest analytic formulas below are written for slowly varying

S3.3
Energy-normalized metric

For a two-level Hamiltonian the quantum metric is

g_{ij}=\frac{1}{4D^{4}}\left[D^{2}\,\partial_{k_{i}}\mathbf{d}\cdot\partial_{k_{j}}\mathbf{d}-\left(\mathbf{d}\cdot\partial_{k_{i}}\mathbf{d}\right)\left(\mathbf{d}\cdot\partial_{k_{j}}\mathbf{d}\right)\right].

(S86)

The geometric velocity entering the nonlinear response is controlled not by
g_{ij}
itself, but by the energy-normalized metric

\tilde{g}_{ij}=\frac{g_{ij}}{2D}.

(S87)

This normalization accounts for the interband energy denominator associated
with the virtual transition between the two effective valence bands.

Using Eq. (
S86
) with the downfolded vector above, and
setting
M
slowly varying, one finds

\displaystyle g_{xx}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}k_{x}^{2}k_{z}^{2}\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}\right)}{\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{2}},

(S88)

\displaystyle g_{zz}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{2}\left(S_{1}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}\right)}{4\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{2}},

(S89)

\displaystyle g_{xz}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{2}\beta^{2}k_{x}^{3}k_{z}}{2\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{2}}.

(S90)

These metric components are even under
\mathbf{k}\rightarrow-\mathbf{k}
, as required by time-reversal symmetry, but
they now carry a nontrivial
k_{x}
dependence inherited from the downfolded
remote bands.

S3.4
Christoffel symbols
\Gamma_{xxx}
and
\Gamma_{zxz}

The Christoffel symbols constructed from the energy-normalized quantum metric
are defined as

\Gamma_{abc}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{k_{b}}\tilde{g}_{ac}+\partial_{k_{c}}\tilde{g}_{ab}-\partial_{k_{a}}\tilde{g}_{bc}\right).

(S91)

For the longitudinal symbol we obtain

\Gamma_{xxx}=\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{xx}.

(S92)

Substituting Eq. (
S88
) into Eq. (
S87
) results that

\Gamma_{xxx}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{\beta^{2}k_{x}k_{z}^{2}\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}\right)\left[M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}-4\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right]}{2\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{7/2}}.

(S93)

This component is odd in
k_{x}
and even in
k_{z}
.
Consequently, the product
v_{x}\Gamma_{xxx}
is even on the Fermi surface, because
v_{x}
is also odd in
k_{x}
in the parabolic valence-band approximation.

The second component required in the present geometry is

\Gamma_{zxz}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{zz}+\partial_{k_{z}}\tilde{g}_{zx}-\partial_{k_{z}}\tilde{g}_{xz}\right).

(S94)

Since
\tilde{g}_{xz}=\tilde{g}_{zx}
, the last two terms identically cancel

\Gamma_{zxz}=\frac{1}{2}\,\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{zz}.

(S95)

Using Eq. (
S89
), one obtains

\Gamma_{zxz}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{M^{2}\beta^{2}k_{x}^{3}\left[2M^{2}-3S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}-3\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right]}{8\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{7/2}}.

(S96)

This component is also odd in
k_{x}
.
Thus,
v_{z}\Gamma_{zxz}
is odd in
k_{z}
and does not contribute to a strictly inversion-symmetric Fermi-surface average unless the remaining factors in the nonlinear transport kernel supply
the required odd-in-
k_{z}
structure.
In the experimental tensor projection, however,
\Gamma_{zxz}
remains the natural Christoffel component associated with a
z
-directed geodesic response driven by mixed
x
-
z
acceleration.

S3.5
Scaling of the Fermi-surface average

We now extract the chemical-potential scaling of the Fermi-surface average that enters the geometric nonlinear response.
The starting point is the full electric magnetochiral anisotropy tensor written as

G_{ijk\ell}\sim\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\,\Gamma_{ak\ell}(\mathbf{k})\,\partial_{a}v_{j}(\mathbf{k})\,\left(-\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right),

(S97)

where
\partial_{a}v_{j}
is constant within the parabolic-band approximation.
The scaling is controlled by the product
v\,\Gamma
evaluated at the Fermi surface.
Therefore, we treat as the relevant object the two-dimensional momentum integral defined in Eq. (
S67
),
up to tensorial prefactors, velocity derivatives, Levi-Civita symbols, and the polarization field
\mathcal{E}_{0,i}
, which do not affect the scaling argument.

We set the zero of energy at the upper valence-band maximum and define the positive hole energy

\varepsilon\equiv|\mu|=-\mu>0.

(S98)

For the upper valence band, we use the parabolic approximation

\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}k_{x}^{2}}{2m_{x}}-\frac{\hbar^{2}k_{z}^{2}}{2m_{z}}.

(S99)

Equivalently, after rescaling momenta to absorb the mass anisotropy, the
Fermi surface is a circle defined by

\bar{k}^{2}=k_{F}^{2},\qquad k_{F}^{2}\propto\varepsilon.

(S100)

Therefore
k_{F}\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}
. At low temperature,

-\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\longrightarrow\delta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu).

(S101)

Using polar coordinates in the rescaled two-dimensional momentum plane,

d^{2}k=k\,dk\,d\varphi,

(S102)

the Fermi-surface integral becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle v\,\Gamma\right\rangle_{\rm FS}

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_{0}^{\infty}k\,dk\,v_{i}(k,\varphi)\,\Gamma_{abc}(k,\varphi)\,\delta\!\left(Ak^{2}-\varepsilon\right),

(S103)

where
A
is a positive constant determined by the effective masses. The
radial integral is then evaluated using

\delta(Ak^{2}-\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2Ak_{F}}\,\delta(k-k_{F}),

(S104)

which gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}k\,dk\,F(k,\varphi)\,\delta(Ak^{2}-\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2A}F(k_{F},\varphi).

(S105)

Here,
F(k,\varphi)
denotes the combination of momentum-dependent factors in the integrand, namely
F(k,\varphi)=v_{i}(k,\varphi)\Gamma_{abc}(k,\varphi)
.
Thus, the explicit factor of
k
from the two-dimensional measure is canceled by the Jacobian of the delta function.
The scaling is
obtained by evaluating the remaining integrand directly on the Fermi surface.

The velocity scales as

v_{i}(k_{F})=\frac{1}{\hbar}\partial_{k_{i}}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\sim k_{F}\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}.

(S106)

The nontrivial part is represented by the Christoffel symbol. From the downfolded
Hamiltonian,

\mathbf{d}^{\rm eff}(\mathbf{k})=\left(M,\,-\beta k_{x}^{2}k_{z},\,-S_{1}k_{z}\right),

(S107)

[with Eqs. (
S93
) and (
S96
)] one sees that the Christoffel symbols have the common denominator

D^{7}=\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{7/2}.

(S108)

For the scaling regime relevant to the asymptotic hole-doped valence band, we consider momenta large enough that the constant gap scale is no longer the dominant term,

S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}\gg M^{2},

(S109)

while the higher-order
\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}
term only modifies the angular
dependence and numerical prefactor. In this regime
D^{7}\sim k_{F}^{7}
.

The numerator of the relevant Christoffel symbols contains five powers of momentum in the same asymptotic regime.
More explicitly, for the longitudinal component in Eq. (
S93
),
dropping the gap scale
M
for power counting, the leading part behaves as

\Gamma_{xxx}(k_{F},\varphi)\sim\frac{k_{F}^{5}}{k_{F}^{7}}=k_{F}^{-2},

(S110)

up to an angular function of
\varphi
.
The same power counting applies to the transverse Christoffel component, given by Eq. (
S96
), namely

\Gamma_{zxz}(k_{F},\varphi)\sim\frac{k_{F}^{5}}{k_{F}^{7}}=k_{F}^{-2},

(S111)

again up to angular factors and possible parity cancellations depending on the tensor component being integrated.

Combining Eqs. (
S105
),
(
S106
), and (
S110
), the
Fermi-surface average scales as

\left\langle v\,\Gamma\right\rangle_{\rm FS}\sim v(k_{F})\,\Gamma(k_{F})\sim k_{F}^{-1},

(S112)

and, since
k_{F}\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}
, this gives

\left\langle v\,\Gamma\right\rangle_{\rm FS}\propto\varepsilon^{-1/2}=|\mu|^{-1/2}.

(S113)

S3.6
Symbols relevant for the transverse (Hall-like) nonlinear response

The transverse second-harmonic voltage measured in the
x-z
plane can arise from two distinct mechanisms. The first is a genuine transverse nonlinear conductivity channel, e. g. a microscopic contribution to
\chi_{zxx}
. The second is purely geometric at the level of the measured voltage: even if the intrinsic nonlinear conductivity is dominated by
\chi_{xxx}
, tensor inversion under current bias and projection onto the transverse voltage probes generate a transverse signal with the angular form

\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta
.

The genuine transverse channel is controlled by Christoffel symbols that generate a
z
-directed geodesic velocity under in-plane driving.
In the present
k_{y}=0
geometry, the relevant components are
\Gamma_{zxx}
and
\Gamma_{zxz}
, together with symmetry-related components.
Recalling Eq. (
S91
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{zxx}

\displaystyle=\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{zx}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k_{z}}\tilde{g}_{xx},

(S114)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{zxz}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{zz}+\partial_{k_{z}}\tilde{g}_{zx}-\partial_{k_{z}}\tilde{g}_{xz}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k_{x}}\tilde{g}_{zz},

(S115)

where we have used that
\tilde{g}_{xz}=\tilde{g}_{zx}
.

Substituting the quantum-metric components given by Eqs. (
S88
), (
S89
), and (
S90
) into the Löwdin-downfolded effective Hamiltonian in the
k_{x}
-
k_{z}
plane yields the compact result

\Gamma_{zxz}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{M^{2}\beta^{2}k_{x}^{3}\left[2M^{2}-3S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}-3\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right]}{8\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{7/2}}.

(S116)

This symbol is odd in
k_{x}
, hence it contributes to a Fermi-surface average only when multiplied by another odd-in-
k_{x}
factor, or when the experimental projection selects the corresponding parity-allowed tensor component.

The other transverse symbol is

\Gamma_{zxx}(\mathbf{k})=\partial_{k_{x}}\left[\frac{M^{2}\beta^{2}k_{x}^{3}k_{z}}{4\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k_{z}}\left[\frac{\beta^{2}k_{x}^{2}k_{z}^{2}\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}\right)}{2\left(M^{2}+S_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}+\beta^{2}k_{x}^{4}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}\right].

(S117)

We leave Eq. (
S117

x
-gradient of the mixed metric
\tilde{g}_{xz}
, while the second term is the
z
-gradient of the longitudinal metric
\tilde{g}_{xx}

d_{y}^{\rm eff}\propto k_{x}^{2}k_{z}
texture.

It is important to emphasize that in the minimal cylindrically symmetric approximation, the intrinsic transverse Fermi-surface average associated with
\Gamma_{zxx}
or
\Gamma_{zxz}
can be
strongly suppressed, or even vanish, by parity after integration over the closed Fermi contour. This does not imply that the measured transverse second-harmonic voltage must vanish. When the dominant microscopic nonlinear conductivity is
\chi_{xxx}
, the current-bias inversion and the projection of the resulting nonlinear electric field onto the transverse probe direction produce

V_{\perp}^{2\omega}\propto\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta,

(S118)

even in the absence of a large intrinsic
\chi_{zxx}

the transverse voltage primarily as a projection diagnostic of the dominant

\chi_{xxx}
channel, while
\Gamma_{zxx}
and
\Gamma_{zxz}
describe
additional intrinsic Hall-like channels that may become visible when
crystalline anisotropies beyond the simplest
k_{\perp}^{2}
model are retained.

S3.7
Summary: interband origin of quantum geometry in
H_{45}

The bare
H_{4}
–
H_{5}
valence block is not sufficient to generate the
quantum-geometric nonlinear response required here. In the minimal
approximation, its pseudospin texture depends only on
k_{z}
, and therefore its energy-normalized quantum metric does not have a leading
k_{x}
-gradient. As a result, the relevant Christoffel symbols are
\Gamma_{xxx}^{(45)}=0,
and
\Gamma_{zxz}^{(45)}=0

14
. Therefore, the isolated
H_{45}
sector cannot produce the desired Fermi-surface average
\langle v\Gamma\rangle_{\rm FS}
.

The essential physics enters through virtual transitions to the remote
H_{6},H_{6^{\prime}}
bands, since Löwdin downfolding generates an effective pseudospin texture and the low-energy valence band inherits a quantum geometry from these remote Weyl-sector bands. The term
d_{y}^{\rm eff}\propto k_{x}^{2}k_{z}
in Eq. (
S82
) is the minimal ingredient that produces finite metric gradients and, therefore, finite Christoffel symbols.

The two most relevant Christoffel symbols for the present nonlinear response are the ones written in Eqs. (
S93
)) and (
S96
). The first one controls the dominant longitudinal nonlinear channel
\chi_{xxx}^{\rm geo}
, whereas the other component enters the possible intrinsic transverse, Hall-like channel. In the experimental geometry,
however, a transverse second-harmonic voltage can also be generated by tensor inversion and projection of the dominant
\chi_{xxx}
response, giving the robust angular dependence

V_{\parallel}^{2\omega}\propto\sin^{3}\theta,\qquad V_{\perp}^{2\omega}\propto\sin^{2}\theta\cos\theta.

(S119)

Finally, within the parabolic approximation for the valence band, the
Fermi-surface momentum scales as
k_{F}\sim|\mu|^{1/2}
, while the downfolded
Christoffel symbols carry inverse powers of the same momentum scale in the
large-hole-density regime. Combining this with the band velocity

v\sim k_{F}
, one obtains

\left\langle v\Gamma\right\rangle_{\rm FS}\sim|\mu|^{-1/2}.

(S120)

This scaling is the analytic signature of the present valence-band mechanism: the nonlinear response is generated not by a Weyl node at the Fermi surface, but by the quantum geometry inherited through interband Löwdin dressing of the otherwise trivial
H_{45}
valence manifold.

S4
Derivation of the first and second thermal moments from the coarse-grained polar lattice model

In this Section we derive the expressions used in the main text for the first and second thermal moments of the surface-induced lone-pair polarization.

S4.1
Reduction of the coarse-grained energy to a single imbalance variable

We begin from the coarse-grained lattice energy

\displaystyle E=

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{t,a}(w_{t,a}-1)^{2}+\frac{J}{2}\sum_{(t\rightarrow n,a)}(w_{t,a}-w_{n,a})^{2}

\displaystyle-\sum_{t}h(j_{t})\,w_{t,1},

(S121)

where
t
labels triangles,
a=1,2,3
labels the three local lone-pair directions,
J
is the inter-triangle matching energy,
\kappa
restores bulk equivalence, and
h(j_{t})
is the boundary-induced bias favoring the
a=1
direction.

As emphasized in the main text, close to the surfaces the residual symmetry between directions
2
and
3
implies

w_{t,2}=w_{t,3}.

(S122)

It is then convenient to parametrize the three weights by a single scalar imbalance variable
m_{t}
:

w_{t,1}=1+\frac{2m_{t}}{3},\qquad w_{t,2}=w_{t,3}=1-\frac{m_{t}}{3}.

(S123)

This choice preserves the average weight

\frac{w_{t,1}+w_{t,2}+w_{t,3}}{3}=1

(S124)

while allowing an imbalance between direction
1
and the pair
(2,3)
. Using the identity

\hat{u}_{1}+\hat{u}_{2}+\hat{u}_{3}=0,

(S125)

the triangle dipole moment becomes

\mathbf{P}_{t}=p_{0}\sum_{a=1}^{3}w_{t,a}\hat{u}_{a}=p_{0}(w_{t,1}-w_{t,2})\hat{u}_{1}=p_{0}m_{t}\hat{u}_{1}.

(S126)

Thus the polarization is completely controlled by the scalar variable
m_{t}
.

S4.2
Effective local three-sector description

To obtain a minimal thermodynamic description
2
, we retain the three dominant local sectors

m_{t}=+1,\qquad m_{t}=0,\qquad m_{t}=-1,

(S127)

corresponding respectively to:

•

a locally positive polarization along
\hat{x}
,

•

a bulk-like locally compensated state,

•

a locally negative polarization along
\hat{x}
.

In terms of Eq. (
S126
), these three sectors carry local polarization

P_{x,t}=P_{0}s_{t},\qquad s_{t}\in\{+1,0,-1\},

(S128)

The role of the interaction terms in Eq. (
S121
) is twofold:

1.

they penalize local deviations from bulk equivalence (
\propto\kappa
),

2.

they renormalize the response to the boundary field through inter-triangle correlations (
\propto J
).

At the level of a single-site mean-field treatment, these effects can be absorbed into an effective local field
h_{\rm eff}(T)
, so that the effective local energy reads

E_{\rm loc}(s)=-h_{\rm eff}(T)\,s,\qquad s\in\{+1,0,-1\}.

(S129)

We define the corresponding dimensionless field

x(T)\equiv\beta h_{\rm eff}(T),\qquad\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}.

(S130)

More generally, to incorporate Weiss-like interaction effects, one may regard
x(T)
as the dimensionless ratio

x(T)\propto\frac{h(T)}{T+\theta},

(S131)

where
\theta
encodes the correlation-induced renormalization of the local response.

Finally, the local partition function for the considered three-state statistical model is written as

Z=\sum_{s=0,\pm 1}e^{-\beta E_{\rm loc}(s)}=1+e^{x(T)}+e^{-x(T)}.

(S132)

S4.3
First moment: thermal average polarization

The thermal average of the local polarization is

\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{s=0,\pm 1}(P_{0}s)\,e^{-\beta E_{\rm loc}(s)}.

(S133)

Substituting Eq. (
S129
) gives

\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}=P_{0}\,\frac{e^{x(T)}-e^{-x(T)}}{1+e^{x(T)}+e^{-x(T)}}.

(S134)

This is the odd moment of the polarization, which changes sign under inversion of the polar axis,
x(T)\to-x(T)
.

S4.4
Second moment: even polar sector

The second moment is

\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{s=0,\pm 1}(P_{0}^{2}s^{2})\,e^{-\beta E_{\rm loc}(s)}.

(S135)

Since
s^{2}=1
for
s=\pm 1
and
s^{2}=0
for
s=0
, this becomes

\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}=P_{0}^{2}\,\frac{e^{x(T)}+e^{-x(T)}}{1+e^{x(T)}+e^{-x(T)}}.

(S136)

This is the even sector of the polarization, as it is invariant under inversion,
x(T)\to-x(T)
.

S4.5
High-temperature (weak-bias) limit and Curie–Weiss form

For weak bias, i.e.
x(T)\ll 1
, we expand the exponential weights in Eq. (
S134
) to obtain

\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}=P_{0}\frac{2x(T)+O(x^{3})}{3+O(x^{2})}\simeq\frac{2P_{0}}{3}x(T).

(S137)

Thus, using Eq. (
S131
), we recover the Curie–Weiss form

\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}\propto\frac{h(T)}{T+\theta}.

(S138)

This is the origin of the fitting form used in the main text,

h(T)=h_{0}\left(1-e^{-T/\tau}\right),\qquad\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T}\propto\frac{1-e^{-T/\tau}}{T+\theta}.

(S139)

S4.6
High-temperature limit of the second moment

In the same weak-bias limit,

\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}=P_{0}^{2}\frac{2+O(x^{2})}{3+O(x^{2})}\simeq\frac{2}{3}P_{0}^{2}+O(x^{2}).

(S140)

Therefore the raw second moment approaches a finite constant as
T\to\infty
.

This is not a contradiction: the first-harmonic transport does not necessarily couple to the absolute second moment, but rather to its temperature-dependent excess over the isotropic background. Accordingly, the quantity relevant for fitting the experimental anisotropy is

\Delta\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}\equiv\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}-\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{x=0},

(S141)

or, equivalently, the same expression supplemented by an additive offset parameter in the fitting function.

S4.7
Transport interpretation

The two moments derived above map directly onto the two experimentally observed channels:

\frac{V^{2\omega}}{(I^{\omega})^{2}}\propto\langle P_{x}\rangle_{T},

(S142)

\frac{V^{\omega}}{I^{\omega}}\propto\Delta\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}\quad(\text{or }\langle P_{x}^{2}\rangle_{T}\text{ plus a constant offset}).

(S143)

Thus the odd nonlinear response probes the first moment of the boundary-induced polarization, whereas the even linear anisotropy probes its second moment.

S5
Computational Methods

S5.1
Density Functional Theory Calculations

All slab calculations were performed within density functional theory
(DFT) using norm-conserving pseudopotentials and pseudo-atomic
localized basis functions (LCPAO), as implemented in the OpenMX
package
23
;
21
;
22
. Exchange and
correlation effects were treated within the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof
generalized gradient approximation
(PBE-GGA)
28
. The supercell used for each
slab has the form
3\times N\times 3
, where
N
is the number of
layers composing the slab. A Monkhorst-Pack
mesh
18
of
3\times 1\times 3
was used
to sample the Brillouin zone, with a single
k
-point along the
non-periodic slab direction. Tellurium atoms were described using a
fully relativistic norm-conserving pseudopotential and an optimized
pseudo-atomic basis set with a confinement radius of
7.0
Bohr,
comprising three
s
, three
p
, two
d
, and one
f
radial
functions per atom, both taken from the OpenMX 2019
database
23
;
21
. Calculations were performed
in a non-spin-polarized, scalar-relativistic setting, since the
present analysis focuses on charge redistribution rather than on
spin- or spin-orbit-induced features. The real-space cutoff energy
was set to
150
Ha (
300
Ry), corresponding to a grid spacing of
approximately
0.10
Å. This grid was used to represent both the
Hartree potential associated with the difference charge density,

\delta V_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{r})
, and the difference charge
density
\delta\rho(\mathbf{r})
itself, which serve as inputs to the
local field analysis described below.

S5.2
Local Electric Field at Atomic Sites

The vectors
\mathbf{E}_{i}
shown at each atomic site in
Fig. 3 were computed in three steps from the
self-consistent Hartree potential of the difference charge density,

\delta V_{\mathrm{H}}
, written by OpenMX in
.cube
format.
We use
\delta V_{\mathrm{H}}
rather than the full Hartree potential
because the spherical neutral-atom contribution produces no net field
at the sites
25
, so

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})=-\nabla\,\delta V_{\mathrm{H}}(\mathbf{r})

(S144)

isolates the field associated with bonding-induced charge
redistribution.

First, we differentiate the potential on the
.cube
grid,
which is regular only in fractional coordinates. We use a hybrid
scheme: spectral (FFT) differentiation along the two periodic axes,
exploiting

\partial_{\alpha}\tilde{V}\;\longrightarrow\;(2\pi i\,k_{\alpha})\,\hat{\tilde{V}},

(S145)

and a centered sixth-order finite-difference stencil along the slab
direction, with one-sided formulas near the vacuum boundaries.

Second, we evaluate the gradient at each atomic position
\mathbf{p}_{i}

without interpolating the precomputed grid. A two-dimensional FFT of

\tilde{V}
over the periodic axes yields the corresponding derivatives
along the entire slab-direction column through the atom,

\partial_{\alpha}\tilde{V}_{k}(\mathbf{u}_{0})=\frac{1}{n_{x}n_{z}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}(2\pi i\,k_{\alpha})\,\hat{V}_{\mathbf{q},k}\,e^{2\pi i\,\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{0}},

(S146)

where
\mathbf{u}_{0}=(u_{0},w_{0})
is the in-plane projection of the
atomic position and
\mathbf{q}=(p,q)
runs over the in-plane
Fourier modes, to machine precision; a quintic spline along that
column then provides the remaining derivative at the atomic ordinate,
with residual error
\mathcal{O}(h^{5})
.

Third, we convert the fractional gradient to Cartesian components
through

\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\,\delta V_{\mathrm{H}}=M^{-\mathsf{T}}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{s}}\tilde{V},

(S147)

where
M
is the cell matrix. The off-diagonal entries of

M^{-\mathsf{T}}
are essential for the oblique helical-Te cell;
omitting them produces errors of order unity in the
y
component.
Applying the Hartree-to-eV conversion then yields
\mathbf{E}_{i}
in
V/Å. The result was cross-checked against an independent tricubic
interpolation of the precomputed gradient and against the residual

C_{3}
symmetry of the helical axis.

S5.3
Polarization as a Function of the Number of Layers

The macroscopic polarization along the
x
direction, perpendicular
to the Te helices, was computed within the modern theory of
polarization
16
;
30
, as implemented
in OpenMX
24
. The electronic contribution is
given by the Berry phase

\mathbf{G}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{P}^{\mathrm{el}}=-\frac{e}{(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}d^{3}k\;\mathbf{G}_{i}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\eta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right)_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}=\mathbf{k}},

(S148)

where
\mathbf{G}_{i}
are the reciprocal-lattice vectors and

\eta_{\sigma}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})=\mathrm{Im}\,\ln\det\langle u^{(\mathbf{k})}_{\sigma\mu}|u^{(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}_{\sigma\nu}\rangle
is
the gauge-invariant phase of the overlap matrix between the
cell-periodic parts of the occupied Bloch states. The Brillouin-zone
integral was evaluated through the discretized
King-Smith–Vanderbilt formula on a string of
N_{1}

k
-points along

\mathbf{G}_{1}
, sampled at
N_{2}\times N_{3}
points in the transverse
plane.

We repeated this calculation for slabs of
N=1,2,\ldots,11

layers, keeping the in-plane lattice parameter, vacuum thickness, and
self-consistency convergence criteria identical to those of the
single-layer calculation. The resulting electronic polarization
component
|P_{x}^{\mathrm{elec}}(N)|
is shown in
Fig. S
S1
. The data are well described by an
inverse-layer dependence,
|P_{x}^{\mathrm{elec}}|\approx a/N
with

a\approx 14.5\penalty\ \mu\mathrm{C\,cm^{-2}}
(
R^{2}=0.999
); a
free-exponent fit yields
b=0.97
, confirming that the deviation
from a pure
1/N
law is negligible. This
1/N
scaling is the slab
analogue of the
\propto 1/t
thickness dependence observed
experimentally in the main text.
