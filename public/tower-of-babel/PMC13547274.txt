Abdominal fat distribution phenotypes and metabolic risk in premenopausal Asian women: insights beyond general adiposity measures
Authors: Yeshe M Kway, Navin Michael, Narasimhan Kothandaraman, Jadegoud Yaligar, Mya Thway Tint, Kok Hian Tan, Keith M Godfrey, Peter D Gluckman, Yap Seng Chong, Melvin Khee-Shing Leow, Jerry K Y Chan, Shiao-Yng Chan, Marielle V Fortier, Johan G Eriksson, Jonathan Huang, Yung Seng Lee, Mengling Feng, S Sendhil Velan, Suresh Anand Sadananthan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rising prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in women of reproductive age highlights their growing susceptibility to future cardiometabolic diseases. Both conditions signal early metabolic dysfunction tied to abdominal adiposity. We investigated whether relative abdominal adipose tissue (AAT) distribution characterises metabolic risk beyond general adiposity. Using magnetic resonance imaging, we quantified superficial (SSAT) and deep (DSAT) subcutaneous, intraperitoneal (IPAT), and retroperitoneal (RPAT) adipose tissue compartments to derive AAT distribution phenotypes from their relative contributions. These phenotypes were characterized with respect to MetS, GDM risk, ectopic fat accumulation, and metabolic profiles. The relative AAT distribution of higher %IPAT and lower %DSAT was a significant determinant for MetS. Women with both lower %IPAT and higher %DSAT exhibited the most favourable metabolic profiles, whereas women with higher %IPAT and lower %DSAT showed increased prevalence of MetS, hepatic steatosis, elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, and 5.36-fold higher odds of developing GDM (95% CI 1.12–27.85). Notably, metabolic differences were observed despite similar overall adiposity including total AAT, body fat percentage, body mass index, and waist circumference. Introduction

Risk assessment of obesity and its associated cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) has been challenged by obesity’s heterogeneous manifestation. Obesity’s cardiometabolic heterogeneity can be partly explained by differences in adipose tissue distribution. Specifically, accumulation of abdominal adipose tissue (AAT) is a major contributor to the development of CMDs1,2. AAT itself is highly heterogeneous and anatomically distinct AAT depots exhibit differences in physiological and cellular properties, including their secretome, insulin sensitivity, and proliferative and adipogenic potential1–4. Consequently, with increasing adiposity, these depot-specific differences can manifest as distinct AAT distribution phenotypes with varying cardiometabolic risk profiles.

Conventional differentiation of AAT, through quantitative CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), into subcutaneous (SAT) and visceral (VAT) adipose tissue has revealed that the expansion of SAT is generally regarded as benign, compared to the expansion of VAT, which is strongly associated with CMDs1,2,4. Studies have revealed that the preferential expansion of SAT and VAT is sex-specific2,4. Premenopausal women preferably expand SAT, while men preferably expand VAT. From an adipocentric viewpoint, maintaining metabolic homeostasis and preventing VAT and ectopic fat accumulation in liver, muscle, and pancreas, rely on the expandability of SAT to maintain “healthy” storage of excess energy, as proposed by the expandability hypothesis5,6. Further investigations of anatomically distinct AAT depots revealed that SAT sub-depots, superficial (SSAT) and deep (DSAT) subcutaneous adipose tissue, and VAT sub-depots, intraperitoneal (IPAT) and retroperitoneal (RPAT) adipose tissue, exhibit differences with respect to their cellular composition, function, and association with CMDs4,7–16. Prior imaging studies have predominantly relied on cross-sectional areas as surrogates for volumetric assessment of these AAT depots and have investigated their relationships with clinical outcomes individually. Acknowledging the importance of relative AAT distribution over absolute volumes in assessing metabolic health, this study employs volumetric MRI to comprehensively study AAT distribution across SSAT, DSAT, IPAT, and RPAT.

The metabolic syndrome (MetS) represents a key clinical expression of metabolic dysfunction that is closely associated with abdominal adiposity17. In women of reproductive age, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is another important manifestation of underlying metabolic vulnerability, often driven by obesity and excess abdominal fat18. Both MetS and GDM are strongly associated with an elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes (T2D) and cardiovascular diseases, highlighting their value as early indicators of future cardiometabolic disease burden18,19. Notably, the rising prevalence of MetS, specifically in young women, stresses their increasing vulnerability, which emphasizes the need to better understand how distinct AAT distribution patterns contribute to early metabolic risk19–21.

This study aims to identify unique AAT distribution phenotypes that can characterize metabolic risk beyond general obesity measurements, such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and total body fat %.

Results

This study comprised 389 women from the Singapore PREconception Study of long-Term maternal and child Outcomes (S-PRESTO) cohort (median age [Q1, Q3]: 31.3 [29, 34] years)22. All participants with available MRI data were selected (Fig. S1 [Supplement]). Participants’ characteristics are shown in Table 1. The prevalence of MetS was 9.5% (36/379). High waist circumference and low HDL-C were the predominant components of MetS (Supplementary Table S1).

Distinct associations of absolute and relative abdominal adipose tissue depots with total abdominal and ectopic fat

SSAT (47.1%) constituted the largest proportion of total AAT (TAAT), followed by DSAT (31.1%), IPAT (12.1%), and RPAT (8.9%). Figure 1a shows the relationship between absolute and relative AAT measurements and TAAT. While all absolute measurements showed strong positive correlation with TAAT (all P < 0.001), relative measurements (% of TAAT) showed varying relationships with TAAT. %IPAT correlated positively, whereas %RPAT and %SSAT correlated negatively with TAAT (all P < 0.001). Notably, there was no correlation between %DSAT and TAAT (r = − 0.03, P = 0.600). All absolute AAT volumes correlated positively with ectopic fat measurements (Fig. 1c, all P < 0.05), whereas, among the relative measurements, only %IPAT correlated positively with ectopic fat measurements, liver fat, pancreatic fat and intramyocellular lipids (IMCL) (Fig. 1c). Both, %SSAT and %DSAT correlated negatively (all P < 0.05) with ectopic fat depots.

Deep subcutaneous (%DSAT) and intraperitoneal (%IPAT) adipose tissue depots stratify metabolic syndrome risk in opposite directions

Absolute and relative AAT measurements and ectopic fat depots exhibited heterogeneous associations with MetS (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table S2). Intra-abdominal volumes (VAT, Odds ratio [95% confidence interval]: OR 4.6 [3.2, 7.2]; IPAT OR: 4.4 [3.0, 6.7]; and RPAT OR: 4.8 [3.2, 7.5]), and %IPAT (OR: 4.2 [2.8, 6.5]) showed strongest positive associations with MetS, whereas %SSAT (OR: 0.6 [0.4, 0.8]) and %DSAT (OR: 0.3 [0.2, 0.4]) were negatively associated with MetS. In addition to these associations, we evaluated the discriminative performance of relative AAT depots for MetS. Table 2 shows the depot-specific discriminative values for MetS classification, using optimal cut-offs that maximized the Youden’s index (YI). AUC values were highest for %DSAT (AUC: 79.5%) and %IPAT (85.6%), compared with %SSAT (AUC: 64.6%) and %RPAT (AUC: 66.9%) (Fig. 2a). Only %DSAT and %IPAT were identified as informative for MetS stratification based on the predefined criterion of YI > 50%. The optimal cut-off values determined using YI were 27.3% for DSAT and 14.5% for IPAT. Interestingly, higher %IPAT classified women with MetS, whereas higher %DSAT classified women without MetS (Fig. 2b).

Abdominal fat phenotypes defined by %DSAT and %IPAT strata reveal metabolic risk heterogeneity beyond general adiposity

Using depot-specific optimal cut-off values of informative adipose depots (YI > 50%) for MetS classification (Table 2), participants were dichotomized into %IPAT-specific high (↑; ≥ 14.5%) and low (↓; < 14.5%) categories in combination with %DSAT-specific high (↑; ≥ 27.3%) and low (↓; < 27.3%) categories, resulting in four adiposity phenotype strata: P1 (↑%IPAT↓%DSAT), P2 (↑%IPAT↑%DSAT), P3 (↓%IPAT↑%DSAT), and P4 (↓%IPAT↓%DSAT). Figure 2b illustrates the stratification and Figure 2c further highlights the phenotype-specific distinctive AAT distribution patterns in axial magnetic resonance images across waist circumference- and BMI-matched participants.

Table 3 shows the phenotype-specific participants’ characteristics (see Supplementary Table S3 for available data). Most participants were characterized by P3, hence P3 was used as reference group in subsequent analysis. Figure 3 illustrates the differences in general obesity, ectopic fat, and clinical measurements across the individual phenotypes. Because P4 defined a minority group (10/389) of lean individuals (Fig. 3a) with clinical characteristics like P3 (Fig. 3b), P4 was excluded from subsequent analysis. Phenotypes with high %IPAT (P1 and P2) showed greater BMI, waist circumference, body fat %, TAAT (Fig. 3a), and increased ectopic fat accumulation (IMCL, liver and pancreatic fat) compared to P3 (Fig. 3b). While P3 showed lowest prevalence of MetS (2.0%, Table 3), the inverse phenotype (P1) showed the highest prevalence of MetS (37.1%). Notably, despite sharing similar levels of BMI, waist circumference, TAAT, and body fat % with P2 (Fig. 3a), P1 exhibited a ~ four-fold increased relative risk and prevalence for MetS (prevalence: P1 = 37.1% vs. P2 = 9.3%). These findings were further supported by additional stratified analyses across BMI, TAAT, and body fat percentage categories, demonstrating a consistent gradient in MetS prevalence across AAT distribution phenotypes (P1 highest, P2 intermediate, P3 lowest) within general adiposity categories (Fig. 4). While, as expected, MetS prevalences were concentrated in the highest VAT tertile, P1 also showed higher MetS prevalence than P2 and P3 within the same high VAT stratum.

The elevated metabolic risk profile in P1 was characterised by significantly higher liver fat, insulin resistance (homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance [HOMA-IR]), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), and dyslipidemia (Fig. 3b). Moreover, P1, but not P2, showed higher hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HOMA-IR, and hsCRP compared to P3. Consequently, P1 exhibited the highest prevalence of prediabetes/ T2D. No difference was found in systolic and diastolic blood pressure across all phenotypes.

Abdominal fat distribution phenotypes characterize risk for gestational diabetes mellitus beyond BMI

To assess the relevance of the identified adiposity phenotypes with respect to future metabolic events, we assessed their odds for developing GDM. Amongst the 389 study participants who underwent MRI at pre-conception, 92 were longitudinally followed during pregnancy, out of which 17 (18.5%) developed GDM. With respect to the reference phenotype (P3), P2 was not associated with GDM. However, P1 showed 5.36-fold (1.12, 27.85) increased odds for developing GDM, independent of pre-pregnancy BMI (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Functional and morphological variations among AAT depots could manifest as metabolically distinct distribution phenotypes. To characterize these phenotypes, we studied the relative distribution (% of TAAT) across SSAT, DSAT, IPAT, and RPAT with respect to MetS. Thereby, we identified distribution phenotypes that provide additional characterization of metabolic risk heterogeneity not fully captured by general obesity measurements such as BMI, waist circumference, body fat %, TAAT, as well as VAT, in premenopausal Asian women. Overall, our findings show that relative AAT measures provide complementary and distinct information to absolute volumes by capturing qualitative differences in fat distribution relevant to metabolic risk.

Aligning with the adipose tissue expandability hypothesis, our findings provide new insights into potential depot-specific expansion patterns5. The hypothesis states that it is not the overall accumulation of fat mass, but instead it is the limited storage capacity of SAT, or exceeding this capacity, that leads to accumulation of VAT and ectopic fat, causing lipotoxicity and insulin resistance5. In our study, women with lower %DSAT and higher %IPAT exhibit higher prevalence of MetS compared to women with similar levels of overall adiposity. In addition, across tertiles of TAAT, BMI, and body fat percentage, P1 consistently showed higher prevalence of MetS compared with phenotypes characterised by higher %DSAT. Moreover, P1 also showed higher MetS prevalence within VAT strata, supporting that this fat distribution pattern captures metabolic variation not fully reflected by general adiposity or absolute VAT volume. Collectively, these observations might suggest a potential synergistic effect of DSAT and IPAT expansion on metabolic health in women, with %DSAT expansion potentially offering safer sequester of excess lipids in this population group, and %IPAT linked to higher metabolic risk. Since IPAT shows increased lipolysis and secretion of pro-inflammatory factors14–16, increased %IPAT would indicate a pathogenic AAT expansion. Unlike other AAT depots, IPAT drains into the portal circulation and could promote development of liver lipotoxicity23,24. Consistent with this theory, amongst all AAT depots, only %IPAT correlated positively with liver fat, and AAT phenotypes with higher %IPAT exhibited increased liver fat compared to the other phenotypes. However, further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms linking %IPAT and %DSAT with MetS and to determine whether their interplay influences metabolic health.

Previous research has linked DSAT to CMD risk, but mainly in men and postmenopausal women using absolute measurements, without considering effect modification by sex10,25–28. By examining relative AAT distribution, we found that as TAAT increases, premenopausal women predominantly accumulate a higher proportion of %IPAT. Notably, increased %DSAT correlated negatively with liver fat and identified women without MetS. Several sex-stratified studies have reported strong associations between DSAT and liver fat, glycemic traits, and reduced HDL-C in men but not in women8,9,29,30. Furthermore, while Marinou et al. reported overall increased lipolytic, lipogenic, and adipogenic activity in DSAT, they also noted heightened pro-inflammatory expression in men’s DSAT compared to SSAT8. Hence, unlike in men, where DSAT has been linked closely to metabolic impairments, under normal physiology, women may benefit from DSAT’s enhanced plasticity, promoting metabolic flexibility during weight gain.

During rapid weight gain like gestation, impaired adipose tissue plasticity could result in reduced metabolic flexibility and might manifest as gestational metabolic complications like GDM18. In our study, only women with lower %DSAT and higher %IPAT had 5.36-fold (95% CI: 1.12, 27.85) increased odds for developing GDM. This AAT distribution pattern may reflect a reduced capacity for metabolically healthy fat storage, which may be associated with an increased risk for GDM,18. Interestingly, normal-weight but not overweight and obese Caucasian women showed significant increase in DSAT thickness during gestational weight gain31. This supports our hypothesis that DSAT expansion is a vital adaptive mechanism, potentially compromised in women with increased adiposity.

Collectively, we proposed a hypothesis linking decreased %DSAT and increased %IPAT to MetS and GDM. Given the close relationship between both MetS and GDM with the development of T2D and cardiovascular diseases, our results emphasize the significance of AAT distribution in early metabolic risk characterization in premenopausal Asian women18,19. Moreover, the prevalence of MetS has been rising globally, specifically in young women19,21. Our findings provide insights for future studies aimed at elucidating the mechanisms linking abdominal fat distribution to MetS and GDM.

Future studies are warranted to explore the prognostic value of the proposed AAT distribution phenotypes for cardiometabolic outcomes, such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. In addition, integrating multi-omics approaches may enhance understanding of the observed relationship between phenotypic fat distribution and metabolic perturbations and disease risk. Furthermore, advanced imaging methods may enable more detailed characterization of adipose tissue beyond volumetric assessment, including emerging exploratory concepts such as tissue-level metabolic and isotope-related signatures (e.g., deuterium-related variability), perfusion and diffusion properties, and MR spectroscopy–based lipid composition.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Precise differentiation of SSAT, DSAT, IPAT, and RPAT enabled highly granular assessment of AAT distribution. By studying the relative distribution of these depots, rather than isolated absolute volumes, we introduced a more holistic approach to characterize abdominal adipose tissue distribution phenotypes with respect to CMD risk. This methodology enables precise and standardized assessment of AAT distribution and depot-specific expansion, improving comparability across individuals and groups. Considering the rigorous process of delineating individual depot-specific volumes, this study comprised a sizable cohort for metabolic phenotyping. Furthermore, the young participants’ characteristics of this study presents a critical window for early intervention for CMDs, making it ideal for profiling obesity-related CMD risk. The study’s multi-ethnic composition encompassing Chinese, Malays, and Indians enhances its generalizability to Singapore’s population and other Asian countries. A primary limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, limiting causal inference. Consequently, experimental studies and longitudinal tracking are needed to validate the suggested mechanisms. The study’s focus on Asian premenopausal women limits the generalizability to other demographic and ethnic groups. Given known sex-, menopause-, age-, and ethnicity-related differences in fat distribution and metabolic risk, future studies involving men, postmenopausal women, and other ethnic groups are required to validate the generalizability and comparative performance of these phenotypes across diverse populations. Replication of the findings in different cohorts will strengthen the translational relevance of the proposed AAT distribution framework.

Conclusion

By examining the relative distribution of anatomically distinct AAT sub-depots (SSAT, DSAT, IPAT, RPAT), we identified distinct metabolic risk profiles in premenopausal Asian women, revealing a clinically relevant dimension of metabolic heterogeneity that would be masked by absolute fat volumes alone. These findings enhance the metabolic characterization of obesity and may contribute to advances in cardiometabolic risk stratification, potentially opening new avenues for more personalized assessment and management strategies.

Methods

Study participants

Data were retrospectively collected from participants with available MRI data from the Singapore PREconception Study of long-Term maternal and child Outcomes (S-PRESTO, enrollment from 2015–2017)22. Participant characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Singapore PREconception Study of long-Term maternal and child Outcomes (S-PRESTO)

S-PRESTO is a preconception longitudinal mother–offspring birth cohort, which recruited 1032 women aged 18–45 years from Chinese, Malay, Indian (or any mixed combination) ethnicities at preconception and followed them throughout pregnancy and postpartum22. During preconception (< 1 year prior to pregnancy), all participants were approached to undergo MRI and clinical measurements. Abdominal MRI was successfully acquired on 389 participants (Figure S1 [Supplement]). Amongst these 389 women, 92 women conceived and were screened for GDM at 26–28 weeks of gestation.

Clinical measurements

Demographics including age, ethnicity, and educational status were obtained through questionnaires. Parity status (nulliparous or parous) was inquired during recruitment and subsequently confirmed by cross-referencing the individual’s medical records during delivery. Height was measured using a stadiometer and weight using a digital scale (SECA, model 803). BMI was calculated by dividing an individual’s weight by the square of their height in meters. WHO-suggested Asian-specific cut-offs for BMI were used to categorize participants into discrete BMI categories (Underweight: , Normal weight: , Overweight: , Obese: )32. Body fat % was measured using Air Displacement Plethysmography (BODPOD). Diastolic and systolic blood pressure were averaged across two repeated readings. If two readings differed by more than 5 mmHg, an additional reading was taken, and the average across all readings was computed.

Biochemical analyses were performed at the accredited clinical laboratory of National University Hospital, endorsed by the College of American Pathologists. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and insulin were used to calculate the HOMA-IR33. Participants underwent oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at preconception and at 26–28 weeks of gestation. FPG and insulin concentrations were measured after 8–14 h overnight fast. 2-h postprandial glucose (2hPG) concentrations were measured after ingesting 75g of glucose. FPG and HbA1c measurements were conducted using the ARCHITECT c8000 Clinical Chemistry Analyser (Abbott Laboratories) and insulin was assessed via immunoassays (Backman DxI 800, RRID: AB_2756878). Serum total cholesterol, Triacylglycerols (TGs), and HDL-C were measured at the clinical laboratory at the National University Hospital, using enzymatic colorimetric methods (Beckman AU5800 analyser, Beckman Coulter). The Friedewald equation was used to compute low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)34. Prediabetes was diagnosed based on either impaired FPG (5.6mmol/L≤ FPG<7.0mmol/L) or impaired glucose tolerance (7.8mmol/L≤ 2hPG<11.1mmol/L) and T2D by FPG≥ 7.0mmol/L and/or 2hPG≥ 11.1mmol/L. International Association of the Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria (IADPSGC) was used to diagnose GDM from OGTT at 26–28 weeks of gestation35. MetS was defined by NCEP ATP III harmonized consensus criteria (≥ 3 out of 5 metabolic abnormalities), using Asian specific cut-off values for waist circumference (women: ≥ 80)36.

Ectopic fat measurements

Liver fat and IMCL were determined by proton MR spectroscopy. MR spectra were acquired from 8 cm3 voxels, with both water-suppressed and non-suppressed acquisitions, using point-resolved spectroscopy (PRESS) sequence (Siemens Skyra, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 33 ms). The spectra were fitted using the linear combination of model spectra software. The muscle spectrum was acquired from the soleus muscle and the amount of IMCL was expressed as % of water signal. Liver spectra were acquired from carefully selected voxels, to avoid blood vessels, in both the left and right liver lobes. Both readings were corrected for T2 losses and subsequently averaged. The area of the water peak at 4.7 ppm, in the water-unsuppressed spectrum, and the sum of lipid peaks within 0–3 ppm, in the water-suppressed spectrum, were quantified. These measurements were used to express liver fat %, standardized by the liver’s weight. Pancreatic fat content was assessed using multi-echo Dixon fat–water imaging sequence (TR = 15 ms, 8 echoes, TE1 = 1.23 ms, ΔTE = 1.24 ms) and a body matrix coil. Within the fat-fraction image, multiple regions of interest were manually drawn across the head, body, and tail of the pancreas. Pancreatic fat was quantified as the mean proton density fat fraction within the selected regions of interest.

Abdominal adipose tissue quantification

Axial abdominal MRI was performed to quantify AAT volumes using 2-point Dixon sequence (TR = 3.86ms, TE1 = 1.23ms, TE2 = 2.46ms). AAT depots between L1-L5 lumbar vertebrae were differentiated using established anatomical landmarks (Fig. S2 [Supplement]). SAT voxels located above the fascia superficialis were labelled as SSAT, while those below the fascia were labelled as DSAT. VAT voxels located within the intraperitoneal space were labelled as IPAT and VAT voxels within the retroperitoneal space as RPAT. Figure S2 (Supplement) illustrates a visual segmentation example and detailed delineation criteria. A convolutional neural network aided in segmenting the respective AAT depots37. Network-generated segmentation masks were meticulously reviewed and, if needed, refined using ITK-SNAP by a trained MRI reader (YMK) under supervision of a radiologist (MVF), with 5 years and > 20 years of experience, respectively. TAAT was defined as the sum of all measured AAT depots.

Data analysis

Phenotyping of abdominal adiposity

The rationale for the phenotyping methodology was that, with increasing adiposity, AAT depot-specific differences in metabolic function and expansion potential give rise to distinct distribution phenotypes with varying cardiometabolic risk profiles. To identify these phenotypes, we studied the relative distribution across SSAT, DSAT, IPAT, and RPAT, and their ability to identify individuals with MetS. Importantly, unlike studying absolute measurements in isolation, relative assessment enables holistic, precise, and standardized evaluation of AAT distribution and depot-specific expansion. Moreover, by studying the relative distribution, we sought to investigate the intricate interactions between the various AAT depots and thereby incorporate the adipose tissue expandability hypothesis into the phenotype methodology5,6.

First, absolute AAT measurements were converted into relative depot-specific proportions (% of TAAT) – denoted as %SSAT, %DSAT, %IPAT, and %RPAT. Next, the metabolic relevance of each AAT depot was evaluated using receiver-operating-characteristic-curve analysis for MetS classification, with performance characteristics derived from the confusion matrix. AAT depot-specific optimal cut-off values to classify MetS were defined as the optimal threshold that maximized the Youden’s index (YI=sensitivity+specificity-1). Only AAT depots with YI > 50% were considered as informative biomarkers for MetS (metabolic risk) assessment and further used to create distinct AAT distribution-specific phenotypes. The identified optimal cut-off values were then used to dichotomize participants into depot-specific high and low categories for each informative AAT depot. These depot-specific classifications were subsequently combined to derive distinct AAT distribution-specific phenotypes reflecting different patterns of abdominal fat partitioning. The resulting phenotypes were then characterized by profiling differences in clinical and ectopic fat measurements.

Statistical analysis

Relationships between AATs and TAAT, as well as ectopic fat measurements, were assessed by Spearman correlation coefficients. Associations of adipose and ectopic fat measurements with MetS were assessed using univariate logistic regression models, with all adiposity-related variables standardized to SD-scaled units to enable direct comparison of effect sizes across measurements. Mann–Whitney U statistic was used to compare BMI, waist circumference, body fat %, and TAAT between adiposity phenotypes. Differences in clinical measurements across the adiposity phenotypes were assessed using estimated marginal means from general linear models, adjusted for confounding of age, ethnicity, educational status, parity, and BMI. To assess whether adipose tissue distribution phenotypes provide metabolic risk information beyond conventional adiposity measures, stratified analyses were performed across BMI categories, TAAT tertiles, and body fat percentage tertiles, and extended to VAT tertiles given its established relevance to metabolic risk. Within each stratum, MetS prevalence was calculated across AAT distribution phenotypes, and results were visualised using heatmaps. To evaluate associations with GDM, multivariable binary logistic regression models were used with adjustment for the same covariates (age, ethnicity, educational status, parity, and BMI). Relative risks for MetS and prediabetes/T2D were assessed by comparing the prevalence of each clinical outcome within individual adiposity phenotypes relative to the prevalence observed in the reference phenotype. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R (4.1.1) and Python (3.6.0).
