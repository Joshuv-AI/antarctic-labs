Bilateral corpus cavernosum thrombosis revealing penile synovial sarcoma
Authors: Álvaro Andrés Saavedra, Diego Scherpenisse, Álvaro Encinas, Andrés Labra, Biserka Spralja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corpus cavernosum thrombosis is rare, and its association with penile malignancy is exceptionally uncommon. Although most penile cancers are squamous cell carcinomas, primary synovial sarcoma is exceedingly rare. We report a 36-year-old man with bilateral corpus cavernosum thrombosis and persistent perineal pain, managed conservatively for two years, during which imaging failed to identify a neoplasm. He later developed a rapidly enlarging penile mass with nodal involvement; total penectomy with lymphadenectomy confirmed synovial sarcoma. Early recurrence with metastatic progression required palliative therapy, and he died eight months postoperatively. Persistent or unexplained corpus cavernosum thrombosis should prompt consideration of malignancy.

Introduction

Thrombosis of the corpus cavernosum is an uncommon clinical entity, most frequently described in young men with a mean age of approximately 30 years.1,2 It typically presents with perineal or pelvic pain, tenderness in the proximal penis, and occasionally a palpable painful mass. Painful erections and ejaculation may also occur.1,3 In most cases, the thrombosis is unilateral and located in the proximal segment of the corpora cavernosa, whereas bilateral involvement is rare, accounting for approximately 7% of reported cases.1, 2, 3.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a central role in confirming the diagnosis and distinguishing corpus cavernosum thrombosis from other conditions with similar clinical presentations, including penile neoplasms, Peyronie's disease, abscesses, penile fracture, or intracavernosal hematoma.1, 2, 3.

Although the exact etiology remains uncertain, several predisposing factors have been described, including hematologic disorders such as sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis, and hyperhomocysteinemia; recreational drug use; vigorous sexual activity; repetitive perineal microtrauma such as prolonged cycling; and hypercoagulable states associated with systemic diseases or malignancy.1, 2, 3 However, evidence linking corpus cavernosum thrombosis with primary penile malignancies remains extremely limited in the available literature.

We report a rare case of penile synovial sarcoma initially presenting as bilateral corpus cavernosum thrombosis.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old man with a history of human immunodeficiency virus infection on antiretroviral therapy with undetectable viral load presented in October 2022 with a five-month history of perianal pain radiating to the scrotum. The pain occurred predominantly at night and was not associated with erectile dysfunction. His medical history included active smoking (1–2 cigarettes per day), occasional alcohol consumption, and previous laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Physical examination revealed a normal-appearing penis and glans, with a firm induration of the proximal third of both corpora cavernosa extending from the penoscrotal junction toward the perineum, without signs of Peyronie's disease. The patient did not report a sustained erection. The penile shaft was not diffusely rigid or markedly tender to palpation; instead, induration was limited to the proximal third of the corpora cavernosa, and pain was predominantly perineal rather than shaft-centered. There was also no history of an acute painful episode suggestive of ischemic priapism, or of a partially rigid, usually painless or minimally painful erection suggestive of high-flow priapism. MRI demonstrated bilateral thrombosis of the proximal corpora cavernosa, more prominent on the right side, with a longitudinal extent of 53 mm (Fig. 1). Follow-up MRI in May 2023 showed progression of the bilateral thrombosis and a focal discontinuity of the dorsal tunica albuginea with extension of the hematoma into adjacent soft tissues, and without evident malignancy (Fig. 1). Further evaluation including thrombophilia testing and vascular studies was considered; however, the patient was lost to follow-up during 2023.

In October 2024, he returned with a rapidly enlarging pubic mass associated with increasing pain (Fig. 2). MRI revealed a large penile mass measuring 133 x 120 × 110 mm, arising from the base of the penis with extensive involvement of the corpora cavernosa and corpus spongiosum, with trans-compartmental extension beyond the tunica albuginea and possible extension toward the right hemiscrotum (Fig. 1). A suspicious left internal iliac lymph node was also identified. Positron emission tomography (PET) confirmed a hypermetabolic penile mass consistent with a primary tumor and a hypermetabolic left internal iliac lymph node (Fig. 3). Considering the rapid local progression, potential bone, cutaneous, and urethral involvement could have led to a locally unmanageable scenario, including infiltration of the ischiopubic rami, non-healing genital ulceration, and urinary obstruction requiring suprapubic catheterization or upper urinary diversion in a patient with poor prognosis. This concern was further supported by rapidly worsening local pain. Because the disease was initially classified as locally advanced with regional lymph node involvement and no evidence of distant metastasis, upfront surgery was chosen for local control and staging rather than induction systemic therapy. Comprehensive molecular profiling was not performed prior to surgery, as synovial sarcomas typically lack actionable targets, and initial treatment aimed to address the patient's severe local symptoms. In January 2025, after multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, total penectomy with laparoscopic pelvic lymphadenectomy was offered as the preferred strategy for local control and staging. The patient underwent total penectomy with complete resection of the corporal bodies, urethrectomy with perineal urethrostomy, wide tumor excision with negative margins, and laparoscopic extended left ilio-obturator lymphadenectomy (Fig. 2).

Histopathological examination revealed a hypercellular spindle cell neoplasm with necrosis (30%) and mitotic activity (14 mitoses/10 HPF). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for CD99, BCL2, and TLE1, findings consistent with monophasic synovial sarcoma (Fig. 4). The tumor was classified by Fédération Nationale des Centres de Lutte Contre le Cancer (FNCLCC) as grade 3. Surgical margins were negative, and metastatic involvement was identified in 3 of 18 lymph nodes with extranodal extension. Molecular confirmation of the SS18 gene rearrangement was not available. Postoperatively, the patient recovered well without significant pain or urinary complications. During follow-up, the patient developed gluteal pain initially treated as neuropathic pain and a superficial surgical site infection that resolved with local wound care and oral antibiotics.

Forty-four days after surgery, he presented to the emergency department with perineal pain and new onset lower urinary symptoms including hesitancy and post-void dribbling from the perineal urethrostomy. Computed tomography revealed a mass adjacent to the prostate, multiple peritoneal and iliac lymphadenopathies, and a pulmonary nodule suspicious for metastatic disease. PET imaging confirmed local recurrence with nodal, peritoneal, bone, and pulmonary metastases (Fig. 3). The treatment strategy was therefore changed from a curative intent to a palliative approach, with initiation of palliative chemotherapy using ifosfamide at 3000 mg/m2, administered as a three-dose regimen every 21 days.

Two weeks later, he was admitted again due to worsening pelvic pain, abdominal discomfort, constipation, and reduced urine output. Imaging showed persistent disease without major interval changes, and he was hospitalized for pain control.

A follow-up PET scan two months later, obtained after completion of three cycles of chemotherapy, demonstrated partial response to systemic therapy with reduction in the size and metabolic activity of the pelvic tumor, iliac lymph nodes, and distant metastases; accordingly, the same chemotherapy regimen was continued for an additional three cycles.

In July 2025, the patient presented again with acute abdominal pain, distension, nausea, and constipation. Laboratory tests revealed elevated inflammatory markers and acute renal failure. Imaging demonstrated progression of local and metastatic disease with ipsilateral hydronephrosis, so an antegrade double-J ureteral stent was placed.

During hospitalization he developed progressive disease-related complications including deep vein thrombosis of the left lower limb due to pelvic venous compression, pulmonary embolism, and severe cancer-related pain requiring continuous morphine infusion. He also developed recurrent infections, including sepsis caused by Enterococcus faecalis, and progressive tumor burden despite treatment. Subsequent comprehensive molecular profiling using a 501-gene panel (NGS ONCO-501) was performed but did not identify any actionable or targetable genomic alterations. Given disease progression after six cycles and the absence of actionable mutations, the treatment regimen was adjusted by adding pegylated doxorubicin at 50 mg/m2, administered as a single dose every 28 days; additionally, due to uncontrolled pain, palliative radiotherapy was initiated.

After completing one cycle of the modified chemotherapy regimen, the patient experienced continued disease progression and clinical deterioration to an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 4, subsequently developing tumor lysis syndrome and episodes of confusion. Therefore, best supportive care was initiated, consisting of exclusive palliative measures and pain management; the patient died in September 2025.

Discussion

Corpus cavernosum thrombosis is an uncommon condition that predominantly affects young men1, 2, 3 and usually presents with perineal pain, penile induration, and occasionally a palpable mass.1,3 In most reported cases the thrombosis is unilateral and located in the proximal corpora cavernosa, while bilateral involvement is uncommon. The condition is generally considered benign and is most often managed conservatively with anticoagulation, analgesia, and clinical surveillance.1, 2, 3.

Although its exact etiology remains unclear, several predisposing factors have been described, including hematologic disorders, hypercoagulable states, prior episodes of priapism, recreational drug use, vigorous sexual activity, and repetitive perineal microtrauma such as prolonged cycling. Malignancy has rarely been reported as an underlying cause of corpus cavernosum thrombosis, and the literature linking this condition to primary penile tumors is extremely limited.1, 2, 3.

Penile cancer itself is a rare malignancy worldwide, with an estimated incidence of approximately .9 cases per 100,000 men.4 The vast majority of penile tumors are squamous cell carcinomas, accounting for nearly 95% of cases. Synovial sarcoma is a malignant mesenchymal tumor characterized by a specific chromosomal translocation involving the SS18 gene on chromosome 18, most commonly resulting in SS18–SSX fusion genes. Histologically, it may present in biphasic or monophasic forms, the latter being composed predominantly of spindle cells.10,12,13 Diagnosis relies on histopathological examination supported by immunohistochemistry, with TLE1 being one of the most sensitive markers, while BCL2 and CD99 are frequently positive but less specific. Molecular confirmation of the SS18 translocation can further support the diagnosis when available.13

Most synovial sarcomas arise in the extremities of adolescents and young adults,10, 11, 12, 13 whereas involvement of the penis is exceptionally rare. The few cases reported to date typically describe young patients presenting with a painless or mildly painful penile mass, most commonly located at the base of the penis.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 In contrast, our patient initially presented with bilateral corpus cavernosum thrombosis without evidence of malignancy on imaging, and the tumor became clinically evident only after two years of follow-up. To our knowledge, no prior cases have reported corpus cavernosum thrombosis as the initial presentation of penile synovial sarcoma.

For localized synovial sarcoma, complete surgical excision with negative margins remains the cornerstone of treatment, often combined with radiotherapy in intermediate- or high-grade tumors.10,12 The role of systemic chemotherapy remains controversial but is frequently considered in high-risk disease, particularly in large or high-grade tumors.12,14

Prognosis in synovial sarcoma depends on several factors, including tumor size, histologic grade, patient age, and the presence of metastases. Tumors larger than 5 cm, high FNCLCC grade, high mitotic activity, and the presence of necrosis are all associated with poorer outcomes.9,11,12 In the present case, the tumor measured 13.3 cm in maximum dimension, was classified as FNCLCC grade 3, and showed high mitotic activity and significant necrosis, all of which are recognized adverse prognostic factors. These features likely contributed to the rapid recurrence and metastatic progression observed despite multimodal treatment.

This case underscores an important diagnostic consideration: persistent or atypical corpus cavernosum thrombosis, particularly when bilateral or progressive, should raise clinical suspicion and prompt careful imaging surveillance, with consideration of an underlying malignancy. In selected cases, early biopsy may be warranted to avoid delayed diagnosis of aggressive tumors.

Conclusion

Persistent or atypical corpus cavernosum thrombosis should prompt careful imaging follow-up and a higher consideration of underlying malignancy. Early biopsy may help avoid delayed diagnosis of aggressive tumors such as penile synovial sarcoma.
