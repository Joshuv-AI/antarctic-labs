Lingua Franca of Ideology? Common and Country-Specific Measures of Symbolic and Operational Ideology in Asian and Western Countries
Authors: Ikuma Ogura, Hirofumi Miwa, Tetsuro Kobayashi, Yuko Kasuya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComparative public opinion surveys, including the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems (CSES), often rely on identical labels and question items to measure respondents’ symbolic and operational ideology across countries. However, it remains unclear whether common symbolic measures of ideology carry the same meaning across contexts, or whether the issue items used to construct common operational measures are equally relevant in national settings. To address these concerns, we compare symbolic and operational ideology as measured using common cross-national items and country-specific items. We conducted public opinion surveys before and after national elections in seven Asian and Western countries. Our analyses reveal that (i) country-specific measures of ideology are generally more stable over time than common measures, (ii) country-specific symbolic and operational ideology are more strongly associated with each other than their common counterparts, (iii) respondents tend to have a greater understanding of country-specific ideological labels than common ones, (iv) country-specific measures are generally more predictive of vote choice in elections, and (v) ideological extremity, as measured by these two types of indicators, shows different correlations with respondents’ attitudes toward democratic values. These findings suggest that researchers should use country-specific measures of ideology in comparative public opinion research.

Introduction

Many political scientists consider ideology among the most fundamental concepts in the field. The central concept of ideology that many empirical scholars accept comes from Converse (1964) and his definition of a belief system, “a configuration of ideas and attitudes in which the elements are bound together by some form of constraint or functional interdependence” (207). That is, ideology allows us to predict one attitude from another that is believed to be bundled with it. Here, attitudes include opinions on policy issues as well as preferences for political actors. Since this foundational work, scholars have examined which attitudes are intertwined, to what extent, and in which directions across a wide range of countries (e.g., Studlar and Welch 1981; Converse and Pierce 1986; Jennings 1992; van Noord et al. 2025).

Converse (1964) also noted that scholars can use a “yardstick” composed of simple terms, such as liberal–conservative, to provide a summary description of the contents of a belief system. Although he demonstrated that ordinary citizens do not necessarily share a common understanding of such concepts, scholars have found that many voters have been able to locate themselves on these scales and that such placements have summarized their attitudes toward issues and their political behaviors to some extent (e.g., Levitin and Miller 1979). Similar survey items using left–right labels were also adopted in European public opinion research (Inglehart and Klingemann 1976). These items came to be understood as measures of voters’ self-identification with ideological labels (e.g., Conover and Feldman 1981).

Drawing on this line of research, empirical studies frequently employ ideology to explain various political phenomena, especially voter behavior, treating it as a settled construct. However, despite the varied aspects of ideology that Converse (1964) and others have articulated, these studies often rely on markedly different definitions and measurements, frequently without fully acknowledging the associated conceptual and methodological issues.

One such issue concerns the distinction between symbolic and operational ideologies, increasingly recognized through US-based studies (e.g., Stimson 1991; Popp and Rudolph 2011; Ellis and Stimson 2012). Symbolic ideology refers to people’s self-identified, or subjective, ideological positions using labels, or “yardstick” terms, such as “left–right” and “liberal–conservative,” whereas operational ideology denotes their substantive, or objective, ideological orientations constructed by synthesizing their positions on individual policy issues and measured in terms of constraint in the Conversian terminology. For example, researchers emphasize this distinction by demonstrating that many self-identified conservatives are operationally liberal in the United States (Ellis and Stimson 2012) and that partisan ideological sorting is inflated when surveys rely on symbolic labels (Yeung and Quek 2025).

Building on this framework, our study contributes to the literature by addressing two often-overlooked issues in comparative political behavior. First, regarding symbolic ideology, prior cross-national research frequently adopts the labels “left” and “right” without critically examining their meanings or contextual relevance. Second, most studies have neglected the variation in the policy content underlying ideological conflicts when measuring citizens’ operational ideology. Both the terminology used to express ideological positions and the nature of associated policy debates vary significantly across countries. Given that a growing number of studies have attempted to measure voter ideology in globally comparable ways, addressing these two issues is of critical importance.

In this context, we conducted public opinion surveys in seven countries, including both Western and non-Western cases: India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Our surveys included both common measures of symbolic and operational ideology based on items from the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems (CSES) and country-specific measures tailored to national contexts. The country-specific items were developed through expert surveys conducted in each country. We compare the common and country-specific measures and demonstrate the superior validity of the latter.

This paper offers two key contributions to the literature. First, it identifies country-specific ideological labels, where applicable, that are more commonly used than the conventional “left” and “right” labels, and proposes their use in measuring symbolic ideology. Second, it develops country-specific sets of survey items that capture individuals’ positions on ideologically salient issues, which are then synthesized into measures of operational ideology. We argue that identifying country-specific symbolic and operational ideology scales and measuring voter ideology using such scales warrants scrutiny because they tend to vary across nations, reflecting a country’s history, party systems, and elite discourse, among other factors (e.g., Caprara and Vecchione 2018).

Previous Literature and Its Limitations

We begin by clarifying our position that ideology is pervasive across diverse political contexts worldwide and that the core concepts articulated by Converse (1964) and subsequent studies, including symbolic and operational ideologies, are broadly applicable across countries. There are several reasons why ideology is almost inevitable. It functions as an informational shortcut for individuals making decisions in policy spaces with multiple issue dimensions, and political elites mobilize it to communicate and promote their positions in electoral competition (Downs 1957). Ideology further facilitates collective decision-making by reducing political conflict to a unidimensional space, thereby mitigating the instability that arises in multidimensional settings (McKelvey 1979). In addition, psychological research suggests that individuals’ underlying motivations tend to align their policy positions and partisan preferences systematically (e.g., Jost et al. 2009).

Consistent with these arguments, scholars have shown that in many countries, including those in this study (Hix and Jun 2009; Miwa and Taniguchi 2017; Chhibber and Verma 2018; Fossati et al. 2020), policy conflict can be represented by few dimensions, though not necessarily a single one (e.g., Benoit and Laver 2006). These dimensions are often summarized using simple labels, some of which are indigenous to particular national or regional contexts (as shown later in this study). This is not a call to abandon ideological labels in non-Western contexts, but rather an emphasis on the need for scholars to be more cautious about assuming cross-national comparability in both the ideological terms they use and the issue content those terms are meant to reflect.

From this standpoint, we are concerned that much of the existing cross-national research on ideology has relied too heavily on common measurement strategies that insufficiently account for country-specific political contexts. The structure and meaning of ideology are shaped by institutional and discursive contexts. For example, how a policy space is structured depends on which issues political parties see as salient and bundle together. This bundling is shaped by party systems (Taagepera and Grofman 1985; Stoll 2011) and historical pathways (Hinich and Munger 1993; Noel 2012). Moreover, elite discourse plays a critical role in making such structures intelligible and shared among ordinary citizens (Zaller 1992; Sniderman and Bullock 2004), and this process varies across countries. Although examining how these factors determine each country’s ideological structure and dominant labels lies beyond the scope of the current study, taken together, these studies suggest that measurement strategies tailored to national contexts may outperform unified cross-national measures. Below, we identify two issues in the measurement of ideology in cross-national studies: one concerning symbolic ideology and the other concerning operational ideology.

The first issue is that scholars tend to adopt the “left” and “right” labels without critical reflection in cross-national studies. In early comparative studies focusing on established Western democracies, the use of the left–right scale was largely taken for granted (Inglehart and Klingemann 1976), even though some citizens failed to grasp its meaning (Klingemann 1979; Fuchs and Klingemann 1990). As cross-national surveys expanded to include non-Western countries, scholars began to explore cross-cultural variation in the meanings of “left” and “right” (Dalton 2006; Freire and Kivistik 2013a, 2013b; Freire 2015; Noël et al. 2021). Nonetheless, many continued to assume that these labels retained their validity across most national contexts. Against this backdrop, scholars sometimes use the left–right measures in multicountry surveys uncritically, as if they captured the same meaning, typically related to economic issues, across countries (Hellwig 2008; Gingrich 2014; Fortunato et al. 2025).

To be sure, a survey of experts conducted more than 30 years ago in 42 countries, including non-Western and nondemocratic ones, demonstrated that “left” and “right” were widely used and primarily associated with similar issues (specifically, economic or class conflict) in most of the surveyed countries (Huber and Inglehart 1995). Reflecting this ubiquity, the left–right labels have been likened to a “political Esperanto” (Laponce 1981). That said, this survey also identified several countries where other labels were more prevalent than “left” and “right,” and where these terms signified conflicts in areas other than the economy. Moreover, even in countries where left–right labels represented primarily economic or class conflict, their secondary meanings varied across countries, and the relative importance of economic issues differed widely. More recent country-specific studies directly raise concerns about the uncritical use of left–right terms in cross-national research: These labels are hardly relevant in China (Beattie et al. 2022; Wu 2023) or Taiwan (Hsiao, Cheng, and Achen 2017); they are less common in the United States (Caprara and Vecchione 2018); and in Japan they are not widely understood as denoting economic cleavages (Miwa et al. 2023). In India (Chhibber and Verma 2018) and Indonesia (Fossati et al. 2020), major ideological divisions center on the role of the state in religious affairs.

As the scope of the literature has expanded beyond Western Europe and North America, research on comparative political behavior has increasingly examined the validity of left–right labels and the issues associated with them in other regions. A typical approach is to examine the relationships between people’s left–right placements and their issue attitudes; such studies have been conducted in Eastern Europe (Pop-Eleches and Tucker 2020; Lindqvist 2025), Latin America (Wiesehomeier and Doyle 2012; Harbers et al. 2013; Zechmeister and Corral 2013), and Asia (Dalton and Tanaka 2007; Jou 2010, 2011). Comparative studies on voter perceptions of parties along the left–right spectrum (or the absence thereof) have also emerged (Fortunato et al. 2016; Meyer and Wagner 2020; Nasr 2020; Carroll and Kubo 2021). Although limited to countries with Western cultural heritages, efforts to assess the comparability of left–right labels have been made using new approaches such as anchoring vignettes (Lin and Lee 2024) and quantitative text analysis of open-ended questions regarding respondents’ understanding of the labels (Lindqvist and Dornschneider-Elkink 2024).

Although these prior studies offer valuable insights into the scope and limitations of left–right labels outside established Western democracies, they do not explore alternative labels and thus do not inform which labels suit countries where the left–right scale is less appropriate. Some may worry that using different labels to measure people’s symbolic ideology would undermine cross-national comparability. However, imposing the left–right labels on countries where they are unfamiliar or have different meanings may be more detrimental, because it may lead readers to assume that measured self-identifications are comparable across contexts. Importantly, using context-specific indicators to operationalize abstract concepts is standard practice in social science research: For example, comparativists commonly measure vote choice using country-specific party lists and then construct analytically comparable variables such as government- versus opposition-party voting. Similarly, if researchers wish to measure symbolic ideology in multicountry surveys and analyze it with full awareness of variation in ideological components across countries, it is more transparent and productive to employ country-specific terminologies, ensuring comparability through methods such as standardizing the resulting variables. Therefore, exploring country-specific ideological labels and comparing their utility to that of “left” and “right” is essential for advancing comparative public opinion research.

The second issue is that cross-country variations in the substantive components of ideological conflict—that is, which issues are related to ideology and the directions in which issue positions are correlated—also tend to be overlooked. Public opinion researchers often make the common components assumption to compare public opinion across countries (e.g., Caughey et al. 2019) and to measure individuals’ ideological orientations (e.g., Krishnarajan 2023).

Admittedly, depending on the research purpose, it may be valuable to apply a common definition of ideological components across countries to locate parties, politicians, or voters within a globally comparable ideological space. The most prominent example of this approach is the RILE scale offered by the Manifesto Research on Political Representation (originally the Manifesto Research Group; Laver and Budge 1992), which measures parties’ left–right positions based on how often manifestos refer to predefined policy categories. Although the predefined categories are drawn largely from topics relevant to the economy and liberal democracy, and were almost exclusively tailored to Cold War–era Western democracies, this scale and its variants have been applied to party manifestos in other regions, including Africa (Elischer 2012) as well as Central and Eastern Europe (Jahn 2023). Among such studies, some scholars recognize the limitations of this Western-oriented measure and propose country-specific indicators of parties’ operational ideology (Aydogan and Slapin 2015; Winkler and Taniguchi 2022).

While a prespecified common coding scheme may be useful for locating parties or political elites across countries, employing such a strategy to measure voters’ operational ideology in cross-country surveys seems less justifiable. When focusing on voter ideology from a comparative perspective, scholars tend to focus on how the relationships between voter ideology and other variables differ across countries, rather than locating voters’ ideal points within a common ideological space. If the application of a common coding scheme reveals that voters’ operational ideology is uncorrelated with a certain variable of interest in a given country, we cannot determine whether ideology is truly unrelated to that variable in that context, or whether the Western-centered operationalization fails to capture voters’ actual ideological orientations. Under these conditions, operational ideology may still be relevant if measured using a battery of issue positions that accurately reflects elite-level ideological conflict in that country.

This problem is illustrated by empirical evidence. Analyses of cross-national voter surveys indicate that cultural and economic conservatism are negatively correlated in many countries outside the developed West (Malka et al. 2019), suggesting that Western-centric approaches are inappropriate for measuring voters’ operational ideology in these countries (see also Malka et al. 2014). Recent efforts using novel methods have sought to explore heterogeneous belief system structures (van Noord et al. 2025) and citizens’ perceptions of economic and cultural conflict in domestic politics (Gidron and Tichelbaecker 2025) across European countries. Nonetheless, most of these studies rely on existing survey data, limiting their usefulness for developing country-specific batteries of issue items to measure operational ideology. Gidron and Tichelbaecker (2025), as an exception, employ an original survey with unique open-ended questions, but the ideological content they detect is based on ordinary citizens’ perceptions and may not accurately reflect elite-level ideological conflict. Moreover, their study is limited to Western democracies.

Data and Methods

To address these two issues, we conducted online public opinion surveys measuring symbolic and operational ideology using both common cross-national items and country-specific adaptations.1 Below, we describe the details of the data collection, the measurement of the key variables, and the analyses conducted for the purposes of this paper.

Data Collection

The surveys were administered in five Asian countries and regions (India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan) and two Western countries (the United Kingdom and the United States). We focused primarily on the Asian cases because prior research has shown that the meanings of ideological labels and the content of ideological conflict there often differ markedly from those in Western contexts (e.g., Miwa et al. 2023). Therefore, if measurements of symbolic and operational ideology based on self-placement on the left–right scale or common policy issue batteries yield results comparable to those obtained using country-specific indicators even in Asian countries, this would offer strong evidence supporting the validity of the common measures of symbolic and operational ideology.

In each country or region, we conducted two-wave panel public opinion surveys with individuals of voting age, both before and after national legislative and/or presidential elections. The pre-election surveys began approximately ten days before election day and concluded either upon reaching 4,000 completed responses or when voting commenced. Respondents who completed the pre-election survey were subsequently invited to participate in the post-election wave, which began the day after the election and remained open for one week. To ensure respondent attentiveness, we included a simple attention check item at the start of the survey. Only those who selected the correct response proceeded. Because we relied on nonprobability convenience samples, we implemented quotas based on gender, age, and geographic region to approximate the demographic composition of the electorate in each country.2 The details of the surveys are summarized in table 1.3

Measuring Symbolic and Operational Ideology

To assess similarity and comparative validity between common and country-specific measures of ideology, in both the pre- and post-election surveys in each country, we measured four types of ideology for each respondent: symbolic ideology using the common left–right scale, symbolic ideology using a country-specific ideological scale, operational ideology based on a common issue battery, and operational ideology based on an issue battery tailored to each country.

The common items were adapted from the CSES, one of the most widely used cross-national survey programs in research on citizens’ ideological orientations. The CSES measures serve as a useful benchmark for evaluating country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology. Specifically, symbolic ideology was measured by asking respondents to locate themselves on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (“left”) to 10 (“right”). For operational ideology, respondents were asked about their preferred levels of public spending in eight policy areas (health, education, unemployment benefits, defense, old-age pensions, business and industry, police and law enforcement, and welfare benefits) using a five-point scale (much more than now, somewhat more than now, the same as now, somewhat less than now, much less than now).4 Although this particular battery was taken from CSES Module 4, similar items measuring respondents’ preferences on redistribution and the size of government are also included in other CSES modules and in other prominent cross-national public opinion surveys, such as the World Values Survey (WVS).5

To ensure that the country-specific measures capture the theoretically relevant construct, namely the ideological labels citizens identify with and their objective ideological orientations, the labels and policy issues used in the surveys needed to reflect the terms and conflicts most salient within each national context. Thus, prior to developing the country-specific questionnaires, we consulted experts on public opinion or electoral politics in each country, with an average of 5.4 experts per country. These experts were selected based on having at least one academic publication on political behavior in their respective countries. We asked them to (i) identify ideological labels widely used in their country, specifying which terms represent left- and right-leaning positions, and (ii) list up to five policy issues that are central to ideological conflict in their country.6

Using expert-identified terms, we developed country-specific ideological self-placement scales for each country, again presented on an 11-point scale with country-relevant labels at each pole. The United Kingdom was the only exception: Based on the expert survey, the conventional terms “left” and “right” were identified as the most widely used ideological labels in the country. Therefore, we did not include a country-specific symbolic ideology item in the UK survey. For operational ideology, we selected five country-specific policy issues. Respondents were presented with a pair of opposing statements (A and B) for each issue and asked to indicate which was closer to their own view on a five-point scale (close to A, somewhat close to A, neither close to A nor B, somewhat close to B, close to B). To generate continuous measures of operational ideology, we applied the graded response model (GRM), an item response theory model designed for ordinal outcome variables, to responses from both the common and country-specific item batteries. All measures were scaled such that higher values indicate more right-leaning positions.

Analysis

To compare the common and country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology, we conducted five analyses.

First, we examined the temporal stability of both the common and country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology around national elections. Although political events such as elections can influence citizens’ ideological identification and policy preferences, symbolic and operational ideology should remain relatively stable if respondents are familiar with the ideological labels used in the survey and the included issues reflect salient policy conflicts in their country. To assess temporal stability for each of the four types of ideology, we computed the correlation between respondents’ pre-election and post-election scores. This approach corresponds to evaluating the test–retest reliability of the common and country-specific ideological measures.7

Second, we examined the correlations between the symbolic and operational ideologies using the common and country-specific batteries. Although citizens do not always choose ideological labels that align with their actual policy preferences to describe themselves, if they are sufficiently familiar with the terminology, their ideological self-placements should be positively associated with their operational ideology. To assess the validity of the common left–right scale in each country, we calculated, for both the pre- and post-election surveys, the correlations between the common symbolic ideology measure and the common and country-specific measures of operational ideology. We compared these with the corresponding correlations based on respondents’ self-placements using the country-specific ideological scales.

Third, we examined how well respondents in each country understood the left–right ideological labels compared to their country-specific counterparts. If the left–right labels capture symbolic ideology across diverse contexts, respondents should demonstrate a similar level of comprehension for both sets of labels. To assess this, we compared, separately for the pre- and post-election surveys, the proportion of respondents in each country who selected “don’t know” or “prefer not to answer,” as well as the proportion who chose the midpoint (5) on both the left–right and the country-specific ideological scales.

Fourth, we compared how well the common and country-specific measures of ideology predict voting behavior in national elections. If the left–right scale and government spending preferences are valid indicators of symbolic and operational ideology across countries, they should predict vote choice at least as well as their country-specific counterparts. To evaluate this hypothesis, we applied a repeated random subsampling validation procedure to the data pooled from all seven countries, separately for the pre- and post-election survey data. Specifically, we predicted respondents’ vote choices reported in the post-election survey using either the common or country-specific ideology measure, together with demographic covariates and country fixed effects, based on multinomial logistic regression models trained on the remaining data. Vote choice was recoded into four or five categories: votes for one of the top two or three candidates (for presidential elections) or parties (for legislative elections) by vote share, votes for other candidates or parties, and abstention. We repeated this procedure 100 times and evaluated predictive performance using accuracy (the proportion of correctly predicted cases) and the mean F1 score.8 We also report McFadden pseudo R2 as a measure of model fit.9

Fifth, we examined whether the relationship between ideological extremity and preferences for democratic procedures varies across common and country-specific measures of ideology. Research suggests that as ideological extremity increases, individuals may become more likely to prioritize partisan interests and devalue democratic procedures (Torcal and Magalhães 2022). We analyzed whether the relationship between these two variables remains the same regardless of how symbolic and operational ideology are measured.

We measured ideological extremity as follows. For symbolic ideology, we calculated the absolute distance from the midpoint (5) on both the common and country-specific scales. For operational ideology, we used the absolute value of the ability scores estimated via the GRM for both common and country-specific measures. To measure preferences for democratic procedures, we adopted items from table 1 of Kingzette et al. (2021), with minor modifications. We estimated a two-dimensional confirmatory factor analysis model, with the first four items from table 1 of Kingzette et al. (2021) loading on the first dimension and the remaining items loading on the second. For the analysis, we used the factor scores from the first dimension, scaled so that higher values indicate a stronger preference for politics to follow democratic procedures.10 For each of the four measures of ideology, we estimated a linear regression model that also included the same covariates used in the vote choice prediction models. For the regression analysis, we standardized both the outcome variable and the key independent variables (i.e., the common and country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology) so that the estimated coefficients are comparable across models. We used only the pre-election survey data, as the items measuring preferences for democratic procedures were included exclusively in the pre-election surveys.

Correlation Between Common and Country-Specific Measures

Table 4 summarizes the correlation coefficients between the common and country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology for each country based on data from pre-election surveys.11 Across the six countries that included country-specific ideological labels, country-specific symbolic ideology is positively correlated with country-specific operational ideology. By contrast, in these six countries, the correlation between symbolic and operational ideology is weaker when using the common indicators than when using the country-specific ones. In Taiwan, Indonesia, and India, the correlation between the two constructs is negative when the common indicators are used. Furthermore, in these three countries, respondents’ self-placements on the common left–right scale are more weakly associated with their country-specific operational ideology than their placements on the country-specific ideological scale. These findings suggest that while country-specific symbolic ideology tends to reflect individuals’ positions on salient policy conflicts within each country, placement on the conventional left–right scale does not necessarily capture respondents’ preferences on government expenditure, even in the UK and the United States, nor does it necessarily capture their preferences on country-specific policy issues.

This finding indicates that symbolic and operational ideology measures developed using expert surveys and tailored to each country’s political context are more strongly associated with vote choice than the common measures adopted from the CSES.

Ideological Extremity and Preferences on Democratic Procedures

Figure 2 presents the results of linear regression analyses of attitudes toward democratic procedures on the degree of ideological extremity.12 The circles and crosses in the figure indicate the point estimates of the regression coefficients for the extremity variables, while the horizontal lines represent the corresponding 95 percent confidence intervals. As in figure 1, point estimates based on symbolic ideology are depicted with circles, and those based on operational ideology are shown with crosses. Results based on common measures are displayed in gray, while those based on country-specific measures are shown in black.

Ideological extremity and preferences on democratic procedures. Gray circles, black circles, gray crosses, and black crosses represent the point estimates of the regression coefficients for ideological extremity, operationalized using common symbolic, country-specific symbolic, common operational, and country-specific operational measures of ideology, respectively. The horizontal segments represent the corresponding 95 percent confidence intervals. The regression coefficients are reported in Supplementary Material section D.4, table S.11.

The figure shows that the relationship between ideological extremity and attitudes toward democratic procedures depends on whether common or country-specific measures are used. When extremity is measured using common indicators, we observe a negative and statistically significant association between operational ideological extremity and support for democratic procedures across all countries in our study. In Indonesia, South Korea, India, and the United States, respondents who place themselves at the extremes of the left–right scale tend to show weaker support for democratic procedures, which is consistent with previous related research (Torcal and Magalhães 2022).

By contrast, using the country-specific measures, operational ideological extremity is positively associated with support for democratic procedures in six of the seven countries (with Indonesia as the exception). In Taiwan and Japan, extreme symbolic ideology is positively associated with attitudes toward democratic procedures at the 10 percent level (p-values equal to 0.051 and 0.055, respectively). In contrast, the association is negative and statistically significant at 5 percent in South Korea (p = 0.038).

These findings suggest that common and country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology capture distinct constructs, highlighting that measurement choices carry important implications for conclusions drawn in cross-national research on political behavior. Although we cannot definitively explain why operationalizing extremity using country-specific measures yields patterns opposite to those reported in previous research, we conjecture that individuals who score at the extremes of country-specific symbolic or operational ideology are likely to be more politically sophisticated: Such individuals may position themselves at the ideological poles because they are familiar with the specific terms used to describe the ideological spectrum. Likewise, prior studies have shown that cross-policy preference scores derived from methods like the GRM reflect ideological consistency across issues (Broockman 2016), and that politically sophisticated citizens are more likely to hold ideologically consistent policy preferences (e.g., Barber and Pope 2018). Additionally, earlier research has found that politically sophisticated individuals tend to place greater value on democratic procedures (e.g., Malka and Costello 2023). However, these explanations remain speculative, and we leave further investigation of the relationship between ideological extremity and preferences for democratic procedures to future research.

Conclusion

This paper highlights two overlooked issues in the measurement of ideology in comparative political behavior research: the uncritical use of left–right labels to capture symbolic ideology, and the frequent neglect of country-specific variation in the substantive content of ideological conflict. To address these issues, we conducted public opinion surveys in five Asian and two Western countries, where we measured symbolic and operational ideology using both common items and country-specific questions. We then empirically assessed the validity of these two types of indicators. Our analysis yields five key findings. First, country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology are more temporally stable than their common counterparts. Second, country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology are also more strongly associated with each other than their common counterparts. Third, respondents tend to demonstrate a greater understanding of country-specific ideological labels than the common left–right terminology. Fourth, country-specific measures of both symbolic and operational ideology more accurately predict vote choice in national elections. Fifth, measures of ideological extremity based on common versus country-specific indicators show divergent associations with respondents’ support for democratic values.

Admittedly, this study demonstrates the broad applicability of the concept of ideology to non-Western contexts. In particular, the fact that ideology measures—especially country-specific ones—predict vote choice in Asian countries as effectively as in the UK and the United States suggests that the notion of ideological constraint (Converse 1964) extends beyond Western democracies. At the same time, our findings indicate that country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology have greater validity than common measures. Although common measures allow researchers to capture these concepts in a uniform way across countries, they do not necessarily measure them equally well. The meanings of “left” and “right” vary across contexts and may not be widely understood as political identifiers, and the issues structuring ideological conflict also differ across countries. For these reasons, even in cross-national research, employing country-specific items is likely to yield more accurate results while better preserving the conceptual comparability that the ideology literature seeks to achieve.

The findings of this study suggest that results from prior cross-national research relying on common measures of ideology may need to be reconsidered. If common indicators of symbolic and operational ideology capture different constructs than their country-specific counterparts, analyses that use them as outcomes or explanatory variables may yield different conclusions. Our study shows that ideological extremity is negatively associated with support for democratic procedures when measured using common indicators, replicating findings from previous research. By contrast, the opposite pattern emerges when extremity is measured with country-specific indicators. Future research should further examine how different approaches to measuring ideology may shape the results of cross-national research.

Finally, we note three limitations of this study and directions for future research. First, due to resource constraints, we conducted our surveys in only seven countries. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether similar findings would emerge in other Western and non-Western contexts. Future studies should replicate this study’s survey design in other countries and explore which ideological labels are most appropriate given each country’s political context, which policy issues best capture operational ideology based on prevailing political conflicts, and how common and country-specific measures of ideology relate to one another.

Second, this study was unable to clarify what the common measures of symbolic and operational ideology capture within each country. If individuals’ self-placement on the left–right scale or their responses to the common issue battery reflect something different from what country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology capture, what do these common measures represent? Exploring this question would help interpret the findings of previous research that relies on common ideology indicators. Future studies should investigate how voters understand left–right ideological labels and what their responses to common issue batteries, such as those used in the CSES, actually reflect.

Third, future research should investigate why common measures perform better in some countries than in others. One possibility is whether a country belongs to the Western world, although this raises the broader question of why ideological labels and belief systems are more widely shared across Western countries. Factors such as party systems, historical trajectories, and elite discourse likely play a role, but their relative importance remains unclear. Addressing this question will therefore require broader comparable survey data to clarify how these factors shape the public’s understanding of ideological labels and belief structures.

Appendix A. AAPOR-Required Disclosure Elements

Data Sources

Public opinion survey and expert survey data collected by the authors.

Data Collection Strategy

We conducted expert surveys in Taiwan, Indonesia, South Korea, India, the United Kingdom, Japan, and the United States to develop country-specific questionnaires for the public opinion surveys. We then conducted online public opinion surveys both before and after national elections in each of the seven countries.

Research Sponsor and Conductor

The research is supported by the Japan Society for the Promotion of Science (JP19H00584, JP21K13233, JP25H00532, and JPJS00123811919), Keio University Next Generation Research Program Fund, and Hitotsubashi University HIAS Brain Research Center. Respondents to the public opinion surveys were recruited through participant pools provided by Rakuten Insight for the Taiwan, Indonesia, South Korea, India, and Japan surveys, and through PureSpectrum’s respondent marketplace for the UK and US surveys. The survey instruments were programmed by the authors using Qualtrics software.

Measurement Tools/Instruments

The wording of all public opinion survey items used in this paper, in the relevant local languages, is provided in Supplementary Material section A. The complete questionnaire for the expert surveys is provided in Supplementary Material Section B.2.

Population Under Study

The public opinion surveys targeted residents of voting age in each of the seven countries. Specifically, we surveyed individuals who were (i) 20 years or older in Taiwan, (ii) 17 years or older in Indonesia, and (iii) 18 years or older in South Korea, India, and the United Kingdom. For the Japan and US surveys, we limited the sample to individuals between the ages of 18 and 79.

For the expert surveys, we targeted scholars who had published at least one paper on political behavior in their country.

Methods Used to Generate and Recruit the Sample

The samples for both the public opinion and expert surveys were selected using nonprobability methods. Respondents to the public opinion surveys were recruited through participant pools provided by Rakuten Insight for the surveys conducted in Taiwan, Indonesia, South Korea, India, and Japan, and through PureSpectrum’s respondent marketplace for the United Kingdom and United States. Respondents were compensated for their participation according to the policies of the respective survey companies with which they were registered. We set quotas for gender, age, and region of residence. In the US survey, we also set quotas for race and ethnicity. For details on the quota settings for each country’s survey and any discrepancies from the quotas, please refer to Supplementary Material section C.

For the expert surveys, we invited scholars who had published at least one paper on political behavior in their respective countries via email.

Methods and Modes of Data Collection

In both the public opinion and expert surveys, respondents completed the surveys online.

Dates of Data Collection

In each country, we conducted the expert survey a few weeks prior to the corresponding pre-election public opinion survey. The pre-election survey began approximately ten days prior to the election. The post-election survey commenced the day after the election and remained open for one week. Specific data collection periods for the public opinion surveys are provided in table 1, and those for the expert surveys are summarized in Supplementary Material section B, table S.1.

Dispositions or Response or Participation Rates

Since we relied on nonprobability, convenience samples, we cannot calculate response rates for the public opinion surveys according to the AAPOR Standard Definitions. In addition, because we did not receive precise information from the survey companies on which panel registrants were invited to participate, we are also unable to compute cooperation rates.

For the expert surveys, we invited eight to fourteen scholars to participate and received three to eight responses. The cooperation rates are summarized in Supplementary Material section B, table S.1.

Sample Sizes

The target sample size for each pre-election public opinion survey was 4,000 respondents. For the post-election surveys, we recontacted all individuals who had completed the corresponding pre-election survey. The exact number of respondents for both the pre- and post-election surveys in each country is reported in table 1, and the corresponding figures for the expert surveys are summarized in Supplementary Material section B, table S.1.

Whether and How the Data Were Weighted

We did not use weights in this paper.

How the Data Were Processed and Procedures to Ensure Data Quality

Respondents to the pre-election public opinion surveys were allowed to complete the survey only if they selected the correct response to the attention check item (adopted from Ternovski and Orr 2022) placed at the beginning of the survey. Although we also included a directed question item (Maniaci and Rogge 2014) in the matrix measuring attitudes toward democratic procedures, we did not use the item to further screen respondents in the present analysis.

Measurement and Model Specification

See subsections “Measuring Symbolic and Operational Ideology” and “Analysis” under “Data and Methods” for details on the operationalization of key independent and outcome variables used in this study (i.e., common and country-specific measures of symbolic and operational ideology, vote choice in the national elections, and attitudes toward democratic procedures) as well as the specifications of the statistical models employed. For information on how the covariates (respondents’ gender, age, and education) used in the regression analyses were operationalized, see Supplementary Material section D. With regard to missing data, when estimating the graded response models (GRMs), we retained observations containing item nonresponse (arising from respondents choosing the “Don’t know” or “Prefer not to say” options) and estimated each respondent’s ability score using their nonmissing responses. In contrast, when computing correlation coefficients or estimating the regression models reported in the “Results” section and Supplementary Material section E, we deleted observations with missing values in any of the variables used in the analyses. The data and R scripts necessary to reproduce all analyses in this paper are available on POQ’s Dataverse at https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/AZELGM.

Because we relied on nonprobability sampling methods in this study, the findings presented here may not fully reflect the broader population patterns in each country. Furthermore, in some countries, we had to relax quota enforcement partway through data collection because of constraints associated with the survey company’s opt-in panels.
