Mediating Role of Plasma Biomarkers Associated with Metabolic Syndrome in Cognitive Decline Among Older Adults
Authors: Binjin Zhao, Kalavathy Ramasamy, Siong Meng Lim, Wei Lim Chong, Maw Pin Tan, Hasniza Zaman Huri, Baharudin Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMetabolic syndrome (MetS), characterized by central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and cognitive decline in older adults. As the global population continues to age, elucidating the metabolic mechanisms linking MetS to cognitive decline is crucial.Patients and methodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study to profile plasma metabolites of 42 MetS patients and 42 healthy controls (median age 69.0 and 68.5 years, respectively) using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) prior to mediation analysis. Cognitive function was assessed with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Digit Span Tests (Forward and Reverse).ResultsMetabolomic analysis (PLS-DA: Q2 = 0.41, R2 = 0.50, accuracy 78.3%) identified 13 differentially abundant metabolites, including amino acids (isoleucine, alanine, serine), fatty acid chains, lactate, glycoprotein acetylation (GlycA), acetone, and creatine. Mediation analysis showed that fatty acid chains, GlycA, acetone, lactate, and isoleucine exhibited significant competitive mediation effects between MetS and delayed recall function. Furthermore, lactate levels fully mediated the effects between MetS and attention.ConclusionMetabolite biomarkers associated with cognitive decline in older adults with MetS were identified, with lactate, isoleucine, GlycA, acetone, and fatty acid being associated with better delayed recall performance, whereas elevated lactate levels were associated with poorer attention. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to confirm these pathways and clarify their implications for cognitive health in this population.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS), a cluster of metabolic abnormalities including central obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, affects 40–70% of older adults globally1,2 and is strongly associated with Alzheimer’s disease.3 Notably, the presence of MetS further accelerates the progression from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer’s disease.4 In Malaysia, the prevalence of MetS reaches 44.2% among individuals aged 55 years and older,5 posing a significant public health challenge. MetS is linked to cognitive decline6 and specific cognitive domains impairment, such as memory, executive functions, attention, visuoconstructional, language, naming, and orientation.7,8 However, some studies report no significant associations9 or even potential protective effects on executive functions.10 These inconsistencies highlight the need for deeper mechanistic insights into MetS-related cognitive decline.

Emerging evidence suggests that MetS contributes to cognitive decline through chronic inflammation,11 gut dysbiosis,12 Apolipoprotein E epsilon 4 (APOE-ε4) allele,13 and region-specific brain damage14 affecting areas like the prefrontal cortex, insula, cingulate cortex, and temporal lobe. Meanwhile, regions involved in visuospatial processing, including the parietal and occipital lobes, may show compensatory cortical thickening.15 These changes may underlie the domain-specific cognitive deficits observed in older adults with MetS.16

In addition to these established mechanisms, MetS may contribute to cognitive decline through metabolic alterations. Metabolomic studies on individuals with MetS exhibit abnormal levels of branched-chain amino acids (eg leucine and isoleucine),17 neurotransmitter-related amino acids (eg tyrosine, tryptophan, glutamate, aspartate, and histidine),18 lactate levels,19 and sphingomyelin.20 Interestingly, metabolomic studies on cognitive decline have likewise identified similar metabolic alterations, including plasma amino acid (eg proline, serine, lysine, glycine, glutamine, histidine, and alanine),21,22 phospholipids such as diacyl-phosphatidylcholine C40:6,23 and elevated lactate levels.22 Accumulating evidence suggest associations between certain metabolites and cognitive functions, such as leucine, sphingomyelin C24:1, and nervonic acid are linked to executive function, memory,24 and attention,25 respectively. Nevertheless, studies exploring how MetS-related metabolic disturbances influence specific cognitive domains remain limited, thereby constraining the development of early diagnostic biomarkers and targeted interventions for MetS-related cognitive decline. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap.

In this study, we aim to integrate metabolomics with mediation analysis to identify metabolites that mediate the effect of MetS on cognitive decline. This approach quantifies the effects of MetS on cognition that operates through intermediate metabolic pathways and provides insight into the underlying mechanisms.26 By identifying these metabolic pathways, the analysis clarifies how specific metabolites are associated with cognitive decline. It may inform more targeted strategies to slow or prevent cognitive decline in elderly population.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics Approval

This study conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 84 participants (Figure 1) assessed between 2020 and 2022 from the Transforming Cognitive Frailty to Later Life Self-Sufficiency (AGELESS) Longitudinal Study of Ageing. Participants without fasting blood samples or with incomplete data on key variables (demographics, cognitive scores, anthropometrics, blood tests, and medication use) were excluded. The AGELESS study is a longitudinal cohort study that enrolled community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above, primarily from Kuala Lumpur, Negeri Sembilan, Selangor, Perak, and Sarawak in Malaysia. This longitudinal study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Received ethical approval from the Medical Research Ethics Committee, University Malaya Medical Centre (Ethics approval ID: 20191231–8121). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. The detailed characteristics of this cohort have been described previously by Aravindhan et al.27

Diagnosis

MetS was diagnosed under the Joint Interim Statement (JIS) criteria when any three of the following five conditions are present: waist circumference ≥90 cm in men or ≥80 cm in women (Asian criteria); TG level ≥150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or receiving treatment for elevated TG levels; HDL-C level <40 mg/dL in men or <50 mg/dL in women or receiving treatment for low HDL-levels; systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥85 mmHg or receiving antihypertensive treatment; fasting glucose level ≥100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or previously diagnosed type 2 diabetes.28

Data Collection and Anthropometric Assessments

Trained researchers obtained information on basic demographics such as sex, age, ethnicity, and education level from the respondents. The BMI was calculated by dividing each participant’s weight (kg) by height (m2). Participants’ weight and height were measured using a calibrated stadiometer (SECA 220; Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany) and a floor weighing scale (SECA 769; Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany) to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm. The participants’ waist circumference was measured in centimeters at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the upper margin of the anterior superior iliac spine. Blood pressure was measured in mmHg using an automated sphygmomanometer (Omron HEM-7143T; Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd. Kyoto, Japan) with patients. In a seated position and remained at rest for at least 5 minutes in a quiet environment. Indicators including BMI, waist circumference, and blood pressure were used to determine the presence of MetS.

Biochemical Assessments

A 5mL blood sample from the median forearm vein was collected into an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tube by a trained phlebotomist after the participant had fasted for 12 hours and did not engage in strenuous exercise. The samples were kept in a freezer and then sent to the laboratory for further analysis to determine participant blood glucose levels, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and blood lipid profiles (HDL-C and TG). These measurements were used to evaluate the presence and severity of MetS, a key exposure in our analysis.

Cognitive Decline

Cognitive decline was assessed using the MoCA. The test was administered by trained researchers fluent in each language (English, Chinese, and Malay). The MoCA measures cognitive performance in the domains of visuospatial/executive, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation. The total score is obtained by summing the scores on each domain, with a maximum score of 30. Higher or lower scores reflect changes in the level of cognitive function.

The Digit Span Test is used to measure attention and working memory and consists of two parts: the DSF test and the DSR test. In the DSF test, participants are asked to repeat numbers ranging from 2 to 7 digits in the same order as presented, with two trials per length of sequence and a total score of 16 points. The DSF is primarily used to assess attention and short-term memory. In contrast, in the DSR test, participants are required to repeat numbers in reverse order, ranging from 2 to 6 digits, with two trials per length of sequence and a total score of 14 points. The DSR places greater demands on working memory and executive function than does the DSF.

Sampling for NMR-Based Metabolomics

Plasma samples (350 µL) were mixed with an equal volume of phosphate buffer (28.85g Na2HPO4, 5.25g Na2H2PO4, 1mM sodium 3-(trimethylsilyl) propionate-2,2,3,3-d4 (TSP), 3nM sodium azide, 200mL deuterium oxide), vortexed and transferred into a 5 mm NMR tube before performing the NMR measurement.

NMR Analysis

Proton spectroscopy (Carr‒Purcell‒Meiboom‒Gill, CPMG) was performed using a BRUKERAscendTM 600 MHz (Rheinstetten, Germany). NMR spectra were processed using TopSpin 4.1.3 (Bruker BioSpin, Germany). All NMR experiments were performed at 310.3 K. One dimension of each 1H CPMG NMR spectrum with a 20 ppm spectral width consisted of 64 scans of 65536 data points, 4 dummy scans, a 2.7-s acquisition time, and a 4-s relaxation delay. This protocol generated a free induction decay (FID) with a duration of 8 min and 14s per sample. The FIDs were processed with 0.3 Hz line broadening and zero filling to 125,000 points and then manually phased and autobaseline corrected by TopSpin 4.1.3.

Spectrum Preprocessing

MestReNova 14.0.2 software (Mestrelab Research SL, Santiago de Compostela, Spain) was used to perform peak alignment, scaling, bucketing, and integration. We used the doublet of alanine (1.465 ppm) as a reference to calibrate the spectra. The water peak (4.5–5.1 ppm), free-EDTA peak (3.19–3.23 ppm, 3.6–3.63 ppm), Ca-EDTA peak (3.05–3.18 ppm, 2.525–2.58 ppm), and Mg-EDTA peak (2.67–2.71 ppm) were excluded. The spectra were binned with a width of 0.04 ppm for further analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most variables did not follow a normal distribution, all baseline continuous data were uniformly presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (n, %). Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test as a non-parametric test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion, and only complete cases were included in the analysis.

MetaboAnalyst version 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) was used in this study to complete data scaling (Pareto scaling) and normalization by sum. As no standard reference samples were included, metabolite features were filtered using a relative standard deviation (RSD) threshold of 10% and an abundance threshold of 5% based on the default recommended parameters of the MetaboAnalyst platform to ensure data quality and comparability.29 A model (statistical classifier) was trained through partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) to distinguish between the MetS and healthy control groups. The validity and predictive ability of the established models were expressed as the Q2 and R2 values, respectively. Variables with importance for projection (VIP) values exceeding 1 with a p-value<0.05 were considered to have a significant contribution to the model for group discrimination. Metabolites were confirmed based on reference spectra from databases, including the Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank (https://bmrb.io/) and the Human Metabolome Database (https://hmdb.ca/). The 3D NMR spectra were processed and visualized using OriginPro 2023 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Heatmap Visualization

Metabolite concentration data were extracted and preprocessed using R version 4.4.0. Autoscaling (z-score normalization) was applied to each metabolite variable to ensure comparability across samples. The samples were sorted by group (healthy control vs. MetS) prior to visualization. A heatmap was generated using the pheatmap package to display the standardized metabolite levels. Metabolites (columns) were clustered using Euclidean distance and Ward’s method, whereas samples (rows) were grouped without clustering.

Partial correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations between cognitive function domain scores and serum metabolite levels in the MetS and healthy control groups separately. The analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.0, and the ppcor package was employed to compute partial correlation coefficients while adjusting for relevant covariates, including age, sex, ethnicity, and education level. Prior to analysis, rank transformation was applied to all variables to reduce the impact of nonnormal distributions. The resulting correlation matrices were visualized using the pheatmap package as heatmaps, with statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) indicated by asterisks.

Mediation Analyses

To investigate whether MetS-related differentially abundant metabolites mediate cognitive decline under MetS conditions, we conducted the mediation analysis proposed by Imai et al (2010) using the R-based mediation analysis tool developed by Tingley et al (2014) Prior to the statistical analysis, all the data, including the cognitive function scores and differentially abundant metabolite signal intensities, were standardized using z-score transformation. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion, and only complete cases were included in the analysis. The covariates included age, sex, race, and education level. Using a model with MetS status as the independent variable (X), cognitive function scores as the outcome variable (Y), and differentially abundant metabolites as candidate mediators (M), we constructed 140 single mediation (X–M–Y) models. Nonparametric bootstrapping with 5000 simulations was performed to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), and total effect, along with pvalues and bias-corrected confidence intervals.

Metabolomics Results for MetS

To investigate the metabolic alterations associated with MetS, we first applied supervised PLS-DA analysis (Figure 2). The results showed a good separation between the MetS and healthy control groups, with Q2 = 0.41, R2 = 0.50, with an accuracy of 78.3% (Figure 2A). The Spectral bins with a VIP > 1 and a nonparametric test p-value < 0.05 were considered differentially abundant metabolites and indicative of metabolic disturbances related to MetS and selected for further analysis (Figure 2B). Analysis of the z-score normalized heatmap of metabolite levels revealed distinct clustering patterns between healthy controls and patients with MetS (Figure 3B). In the MetS group, signals corresponding to acetone, lactate, GlycA, and various fatty acid chains were increased compared with those in healthy controls. These alterations reflect a metabolic shift in patients with MetS toward increased lipid utilization and altered amino acid metabolism.

Association Between MetS and Cognitive Decline

To preliminarily explore the relationship between MetS-related metabolic changes and cognitive function, partial correlation analysis was performed to illustrate the associations between MetS and cognitive decline (Figure 4). After adjusting for covariates, including age, sex, ethnicity, and education level, we observed that in the healthy control group, levels of (CH2-C=C)-fatty acid chains were significantly positively correlated with DSF (r = 0.35, p = 0.0314). In contrast, among MetS participants, lactate (r = 0.385, p = 0.0171) and GlcNAc (r = –0.329, p = 0.0436) levels were significantly associated with visuospatial/executive function scores, whereas isoleucine levels were positively correlated with delayed recall scores (r = 0.361, p = 0.0258). Mediation Effect

Discussion

Our metabolomics findings indicate that elderly patients with MetS exhibit dysregulated glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, accompanied by chronic inflammation. Our mediation analysis revealed several metabolites (lactate, isoleucine, acetone, fatty acids, and GlycA) as potential modulators of cognitive functions in MetS. Baseline Characteristics Finding

The baseline data reflected pronounced metabolic disturbances in participants with MetS. Cognitive assessments indicated that MetS may selectively affect brain regions involved in language processing and spatial orientation, while global cognitive function remains largely intact in the early stages of the syndrome. This pattern aligns with previous reports by Wu et al8, providing further evidence that MetS is associated with domain-specific cognitive impairment rather than generalized cognitive decline.

Metabolic Dysregulation in MetS

Our findings indicate that elderly patients with MetS exhibit dysregulated glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, accompanied by chronic inflammation. Regarding glucose metabolism, we observed elevated lactate and reduced serine and creatine levels, suggesting a shift toward anaerobic glycolysis due to impaired aerobic metabolism.30 The reduction in serine and creatine synthesis, an alternative pathway of glucose metabolism, further reflects an upregulation of anaerobic glycolysis. Similar increases in plasma lactate among MetS patients have been reported by Terry et al.30 This shift may result from suppressed glycolytic enzyme activity under hyperglycemic conditions, where inhibition of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and pyruvate dehydrogenase promotes anaerobic glycolysis.31 Impaired serine biosynthesis may also disrupt the synthesis of phosphatidylserine and cause lipid metabolic imbalance.32

In terms of amino acid metabolism, we observed elevated isoleucine levels, which were consistent with previous findings.18 As an essential branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) derived mainly from diet, increased isoleucine reflects impaired BCAA catabolism and is associated with insulin resistance.33 For fatty acid metabolism, elevated fatty acids and acetone point to enhanced adipose tissue lipolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation, resulting in excess acetyl-CoA, and greater acetone formation.34 Elevated GlycA levels, a composite biomarker of systemic low-grade inflammation, have also been associated with increased adipose tissue lipolysis.35 Overall, elderly patients with MetS exhibit enhanced anaerobic glycolysis, dysregulated amino acid metabolism, increased fatty acid β-oxidation, and chronic inflammation.

Metabolites as Mediators Between MetS and Cognition in Aging

Lactate, produced by astrocytic glucose metabolism and converted to pyruvate in neurons to enter the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is a key neuronal energy substrate supporting learning and memory.36 In our study, lactate demonstrated a dual role, preserving delayed recall while potentially impairing attention, consistent with previous reports.37,38 The protective effect of lactate on delayed recall may be mediated by promoting the expression of immediate early genes closely involved in memory,39 or influencing adult neurogenesis via monocarboxylate transporter 2.40 Moreover, lactate can also elevate glutamate and brain-derived neurotrophic factor in hippocampal neurons, promoting learning and memory functions.41 Conversely, lactate may impair attention by inducing neuronal hyperexcitability in the medial prefrontal cortex through monocarboxylate transporter 2 mediated inhibition of calcium-activated potassium channels.42,43 Given this dual role, moderate regulation via exercise intervention and lactate administration could enhance cognitive function. Evidence shows that moderate-intensity aerobic exercises (such as running, cycling)44 or resistance training (eg weight machines, elastic bands)45 performed 2–3 times per week increase lactate and improve memory. Furthermore, oral lactate supplementation appears to improve memory.46 However, since excess lactate may disrupt attention, maintaining blood lactate levels below 4 mmol/L appears important.47

Fatty acids also play essential roles in brain energy metabolism,48 membrane structure,49 and neurotransmission.50 In our study, fatty acid appeared to protect delayed recall, possibly by mitigating the cognitive decline associated with MetS-related cognitive decline. Polyunsaturated fatty acids may enhance synaptic signaling by upregulating glutamatergic receptors and GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission.51,52 Studies have shown that they reverse hippocampal structural degeneration.52 Furthermore, they may exert anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects.53 Taken together with existing evidence, our results suggest that supplementation with polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as omega-3 and omega-6,54 serve as potential metabolic targets for improving delayed recall in MetS.

Isoleucine supports neuronal energy through the TCA cycle,55 modulates neurotransmitters like serotonin,56 participates in the glutamate–glutamine cycle,57 and regulates neuroinflammatory via AMPK/mTOR signaling.58 Our results suggest that moderately elevated isoleucine may benefit delayed recall in older adults with MetS. Reduced isoleucine has been linked to impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation and fatty acid metabolism, insulin resistance, neuroinflammation, and cognitive decline,59 whereas isoleucine supplementation can enhance fatty acid β-oxidation,60 replenish hippocampal glutamate pools, and protect memory.61 While evidence supporting the protective effects of isoleucine remains limited, our findings provide preliminary evidence support for its beneficial role, though caution is advised to avoid excessive intake.62

Acetone and other ketone bodies serve as minor brain energy substrates, entering the brain and contributing to neuronal TCA cycle metabolism after conversion to acetyl-CoA.63 In our study, acetone represents a metabolite associated with delayed recall. Limited evidence suggests that elevated acetone or its precursor acetoacetate can promote glutamate recovery in the hippocampus,64 reduce neuronal excitability,65 and enhance expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor.66 These findings suggest that moderately increasing acetone levels such as ketogenic diets67 may help slow cognitive decline in MetS.

GlycA, a composite biomarker of systemic low-grade inflammation reflecting glycoproteins, including α1-acid glycoprotein and haptoglobin, is associated with cognitive decline and increased brain atrophy in Alzheimer’s disease (AD).68 However, inflammation may have stage-dependent effects. For example, Jay et al reported that early inflammation in AD mouse model reduced amyloid plaque deposition, while late inflammation worsens brain damage69 This shift may suggest a change in microglial behavior that transitions from neuroprotective to neurotoxic over time.70 Given that our participants exhibited MoCA scores ranging from 20 to 30 (normal to mildly impaired), it is possible that systemic inflammation at this stage may exert a protective or compensatory neuromodulatory effect. Future studies are needed to examine how inflammatory signaling evolves across cognitive decline stages in older adults with MetS to identify optimal intervention windows.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged, including a relatively small sample size (n = 84), incomplete disease history, and insufficient sensitivity for detecting low-abundance metabolites, which may affect the reliability and interpretability of the results. Despite these limitations, this study combines untargeted metabolomics and mediation analysis to identify promising early diagnostic biomarkers and metabolic targets for cognitive interventions in aging populations with MetS.

Conclusion

We identify metabolite biomarkers associated with cognitive decline in older adults with MetS. Metabolomic profiling identified 13 key metabolites, including amino acids (Isoleucine, L-alanine, and serine), 5 fatty acid-related compounds, and other metabolites (Lactate, GlycA, acetone, and creatine). Mediation analysis further revealed that lactate, isoleucine, GlycA, acetone, and fatty acid were associated with better delayed recall performance, whereas elevated lactate levels were associated with poorer attention. These findings indicate alterations in anaerobic glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, fatty acid β-oxidation, and chronic inflammation among individuals with MetS. Interventions including moderate-intensity exercise and dietary strategy such as long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids intake, isoleucine supplementation, and ketogenic diet may potentially protect cognitive performance.
