Human Exposure to Spirochete-Infected Ornithodoros turicata in a Residential Setting in the Austin, Texas Metropolitan Area
Authors: Serhii Filatov, Bonny Mayes, Sarah M Gunter, Alexander R Kneubehl, Job E Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA central Texas resident found an engorged Ornithodoros turicata tick attached to their ankle while at home. The tick tested positive for Borrelia turicatae, an agent for soft tick-borne relapsing fever. Three weeks earlier, the patient started experiencing febrile episodes, rash, and nausea, yet the etiological agent went undiagnosed.Keywords: Borrelia, soft tick, tick-borne relapsing fever, urban exposure, vector-borne disease

Borrelia turicatae is the primary causative agent of soft tick relapsing fever (STRF) in the southern United States. These pathogenic spirochetes predominantly circulate among wild vertebrates and cavity-dwelling Ornithodoros turicata ticks. Individuals that enter the natural habitat of infected O. turicata and experience bites will typically develop symptoms including recurrent febrile episodes, rigors, facial paralysis, and nausea within 3–7 days [1, 2]. Exposure to O. turicata often goes unnoticed because the ticks feed rapidly and are rarely found attached to their hosts.

Historically, human cases of B. turicatae have been linked to caves and rural areas such as cabins and outbuildings frequented by peridomestic mammals [3], but recent reports indicate a larger distribution than previously considered [4]. Spirochete-infected O. turicata ticks that have been associated with human exposure have been collected in public parks and green spaces within urban centers in Texas [4–6]. In this current study, we report that in early March of 2024, a resident of an apartment building in the Austin metropolitan area noticed a tick attached to their ankle while sitting on their couch. The individual sent the tick to the Texas Department of State Health Services Zoonosis Control Branch (ZCB), and follow-up testing was performed. Our findings underscore the potential for household exposure to B. turicatae-infected ticks.

METHODS

When ZCB staff received the tick, they noted that it was a live engorged argasid and contacted the patient to inform them that it was a potential vector of STRF spirochetes. The ZCB sent the tick to Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) where it was examined morphologically and tested to determine if it was infected. Using a stereomicroscope, the dorsal and ventral surfaces were visualized to identify morphological and developmental features.

To determine the infectious status of the tick, transmission studies were performed using a mouse model. All animal work was approved through BCM's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The tick fed on a 6-week-old Institute of Cancer Research mouse, and a drop of blood was collected from the animal daily and inspected for spirochetes by dark-field microscopy. Upon spirochete visualization, the animal was sedated, exsanguinated, and the collected blood was centrifuged at 500 × g for 20 minutes. The spirochete-containing serum was inoculated into 4 mL of the modified Barbour–Stoenner–Kelly (mBSK) medium [7]. Once spirochetes reached midexponential growth, 40 ml of the fresh mBSK medium was inoculated, and glycerol stocks were made. The 40 mL culture was centrifuged at 11 000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C to pellet spirochetes, and the total DNA was extracted using the phenol–chloroform method [8]. Using an established approach of Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies [9], the isolate genome was sequenced and assembled for phylogenomic analysis.

RESULTS

Identification and Characterization of the Attached Tick

Physical examination determined the tick's developmental stage. When the tick was received by BCM from ZCB, it was still engorged. The morphology indicated that the specimen was a nymph (8 legs and the genital aperture was absent). Over the following 3 weeks, the tick molted, and a follow-up morphological examination identified the presence of a genital aperture characteristic of an adult male O. turicata tick (Supplementary Figure 1). These findings indicated that the individual was fed upon by a nymphal O. turicata that molted into an adult male.

Evaluation of the Infection Status of the Ornithodoros turicata Tick

After feeding the tick on an uninfected laboratory mouse, spirochetes were visualized in the blood 4 days later. The spirochetes were successfully cultured from mouse serum in mBSK and designated as isolate ZLK. These findings confirmed that the tick collected in the individual's residence was infected with STRF spirochetes.

Genomic Assessment of the Spirochete Isolate

Using short- and long-read sequencing technology, the genome assembly of ZLK was generated. The merqury tool (v1.3) determined that the assembly was 99.7% complete and had a consensus quality of “+inf”. This indicated that no error k-mers were detected in the assembly relative to the sequencing data [10], and thus the estimated error rate approached zero. The genome assembly was 1 670 619 base pairs in length, and a phylogenomic analysis confirmed that the ZLK isolate was B. turicatae (Figure 1A). ZLK grouped most closely with a human (BTE5EL, GCA_023035415.1) and tick (91E135, GCA_023035855.1) isolate from Texas and a tick isolate from Mexico (BTCAM1, GCA_036362965.1) [9, 11]. Clinical History of the Patient

The individual reported that prior to finding the attached tick, they were recently hospitalized, were recovering from Bell's palsy, and had tested positive for Lyme disease. ZCB staff searched the Texas National Electronic Disease Surveillance System for electronic lab reports and requested medical records, which indicated that the patient experienced intermittent fevers for 10 days. Electronic lab reports suggested that exposure to STRF spirochetes was not considered by healthcare providers. Antigen tests for influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and group A streptococcal pharyngitis were negative as was the nucleic acid amplification test for human immunodeficiency virus. Serology for syphilis, West Nile virus, flea-borne typhus (day 5 and 11 serum samples), and spotted fever rickettsiosis (day 5 serum sample) were also negative. The patient tested positive on the first tier of the Lyme disease serology test (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay), while the second tier (immunoblot) was negative.

Given the electronic lab records and medical history, personnel at ZCB suspected that the patient was infected with STRF spirochetes. ZCB staff had a serum sample that was collected 11 days after symptom onset forwarded to CDC for immunoblot testing. The sample was positive for the STRF Borrelia diagnostic antigen, recombinant glycerophosphodiester phosphodiesterase (rGlpQ); however, antibodies to a 17 kDa diagnostic antigen were undetectable. Unfortunately, attempts to re-contact and obtain another serum sample from the patient were unsuccessful.

DISCUSSION

The clinical presentation and absence of an alternative explanation for the patient's syndrome suggest infection with STRF spirochetes. They did not report travel outside of Texas in the previous 3 months, yet regularly visited Austin city parks. Exposure may have occurred at a park, but they reported no known tick attachment on their body prior to disease onset. A tick may have also attached to the patient's clothing and was introduced into their home. Evidence for intrahousehold exposure included the discovery of an engorged O. turicata tick 17 days after their symptoms began (Table 1). If this tick was responsible for infecting the patient, there was sufficient time for it to molt and subsequently feed again 17 days later.

While it remains unclear how the tick was introduced into the patient's apartment or if there was an underlying infestation of O. turicata, prior work indicates a close association between argasid ticks and human dwellings. In the United States, this has been observed with Ornithodoros hermsi [12]. Investigations by Schwan et al demonstrated that O. hermsi colonizes human dwellings, particularly rodent-infested cabins, where ticks reside in structural voids, such as walls, floors, and attics. In an investigation in Montana, O. hermsi ticks were collected directly from cabins associated with human infection, confirming intradomiciliary transmission of STRF spirochetes [12].

Our findings highlight the need for increased awareness among clinicians and a better understanding of the diagnostic assays used to determine exposure. For example, the identity of the 17 kDa antigen and the serological characterization of this protein are unknown. While we were unable to obtain information regarding this antigen, CDC noted that false-negative results may occur from serum samples collected <14 days after symptom onset. Importantly, detection of anti-rGlpQ IgG antibodies is noteworthy. rGlpQ is highly antigenic and serologically differentiates exposure to relapsing fever group Borrelia and Lyme disease-causing spirochetes [13]. When serological results are questionable, microscopy, polymerase chain reaction, and bacterial culture can support a more conclusive diagnosis.

There have been increasing reports of the collection of B. turicatae-infected ticks and human exposure to STRF spirochetes in urban areas of Austin, Texas (Supplementary Figure 2) [4, 5, 14]. With greater ecological overlap between humans, O. turicata, and vertebrates, ticks are likely to enter domestic environments and feed on humans. Once introduced, an endemic focus of infected O. turicata can persist for decades because these ticks live over 10 years, can feed over 15 times throughout their lifespan, and vertically transmit STRF spirochetes [15]. Consequently, we recommend increased surveillance efforts in green spaces, neighborhoods, and parks to identify O. turicata and the vertebrates that aid in maintaining B. turicatae.
