Impact of professional type on job satisfaction and job burnout among physicians in oncology-specialty hospital: the mediating and moderating roles of perceived organizational support and career adaptability
Authors: Nan Xie, Wang Guan, Yuehong Tang, Haiyan Guo, Bingli Zuo, Yujing Li, Xiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs cancer incidence continues to rise globally, oncology-specialty hospitals have assumed an increasingly central role in modern healthcare systems. Within these institutions, differentiation in professional roles may be associated with variations in physicians’ occupational experiences. This study examines the association between professional type (oncological vs. non-oncological physicians) and job satisfaction and job burnout, and explores the mediating and moderating roles of perceived organizational support and career adaptability.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 383 physicians from five provincial-level oncology-specialty hospitals in China. Physicians were classified according to departmental function as oncological physicians directly involved in cancer diagnosis and treatment, and hospital-based non-oncological specialist physicians providing diagnostic, perioperative, imaging, pathological, or other supportive services within oncology-specialty hospitals. The latter group did not include general physicians practicing outside oncology-specialty hospitals. Standardized instruments were used to assess job satisfaction, job burnout, perceived organizational support, and career adaptability. Available demographic and work-related covariates, including sex, age, education level, years of clinical experience, and weekly working hours, were considered in the adjusted analyses. Group comparisons, bootstrapped mediation analyses, and joint moderation models were performed using SPSS and Stata.ResultsOncological physicians reported significantly higher job satisfaction than non-oncological physicians. However, they also reported higher overall levels of job burnout, a pattern primarily characterized by elevated emotional exhaustion and depersonalization. Perceived organizational support and career adaptability were both associated with job satisfaction and job burnout and served as mediating variables linking professional type with these outcomes. In addition, both resources functioned as moderators, conditioning the magnitude of professional-type differences. Career adaptability was particularly relevant for non-oncological physicians, showing a buffering pattern in relation to job burnout.ConclusionsDifferences in professional roles within oncology-specialty hospitals are closely associated with physicians’ job satisfaction and job burnout. Perceived organizational support and career adaptability play complementary process and boundary roles in shaping these associations.Keywords: Oncological physicians, Oncology-specialty, Job satisfaction, Job burnout, Organizational support, Career adaptability

Introduction

Cancer incidence continues to rise globally. According to the 2022 Global Cancer Statistics released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were an estimated 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths worldwide, with approximately one in five individuals expected to develop cancer during their lifetime [1]. Oncology-specialty hospitals, therefore, have become increasingly vital within the modern healthcare systems. These specialized hospitals play a key role in managing complex conditions, fostering multidisciplinary collaboration, and promoting precision medicine. At the same time, they provide highly intensive and demanding platforms for medical talents. However, the sustained high workload, risk exposure, and psychological pressure that characterize such institutions have also been accompanied by growing concerns about declining job satisfaction and elevated burnout among physicians, which have become increasingly central to the agenda of healthcare workforce management [2–3].

Although increasing attention has been paid to physicians’ mental health, the role of professional type in shaping occupational experience within oncology-specialty hospitals remains underexplored. In this study, physicians were analytically classified into two groups according to their departmental functions within oncology-specialty hospitals. Oncological physicians refer to those directly involved in cancer diagnosis and treatment, including physicians from medical oncology, surgical oncology, radiation oncology, and related treatment-oriented departments. Non-oncological physicians refer specifically to hospital-based specialist physicians working in oncology-specialty hospitals who provide diagnostic, perioperative, imaging, pathological, or other clinically supportive services, such as anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, and ultrasound. This group does not include general physicians practicing outside oncology-specialty hospitals, such as primary care physicians, cardiologists, or physicians working in general hospitals. The classification was adopted to capture differences in institutional role centrality within oncology-specialty hospitals rather than to imply homogeneity within either group or to compare technical competencies across specialties. While both groups are integral to the effective delivery of cancer care, they may differ in access to organizational resources, research opportunities, promotion pathways, and academic recognition [4–5]. Such professional segmentation may be associated with lower career fulfillment, reduced sense of belonging, and diminished job satisfaction among physicians in less central institutional roles, thereby increasing their susceptibility to burnout [6–7].

Job satisfaction and job burnout are widely recognized as key psychological indicators of physicians’ professional experience, and their close associations with organizational performance, patient safety, and turnover intention have been demonstrated by extensive empirical evidence [8–10]. Physicians who report higher levels of job satisfaction are more likely to maintain a positive professional state and engage in effective communication with patients. In contrast, job burnout is typically characterized by emotional exhaustion and depersonalization and has been associated with impaired professional functioning and withdrawal-related attitudes in healthcare settings [11–12]. Therefore, understanding the antecedents of these two outcomes, particularly the influence of professional type within oncology-specialty hospitals, offers valuable insights into improving physician well-being and enhancing healthcare system performance.

Two theoretical frameworks, Social Exchange Theory and Career Development Theory, offer valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying these professional outcomes. Social Exchange Theory emphasizes that perceptions of fair treatment and emotional reciprocity from the organization are fundamental to eliciting positive employee attitudes and behaviors [13–14]. When physicians perceive that their contributions are recognized and that they receive adequate institutional support, they are more likely to experience higher job satisfaction and lower levels of job burnout. Meanwhile, Career Development Theory posits that career growth is a multi-stage, context-dependent process in which individuals construct and integrate their professional roles through both cognitive regulation and adaptation to their environment [15–16].

Within this theoretical framework, two key variables are identified as psychological mechanisms influencing physicians’ professional experience: Perceived Organizational Support (POS) and Career Adaptability (CA). POS reflects physicians’ subjective perceptions of the extent to which their organization values their contributions and provides support in terms of resources, opportunities, and care. CA refers to the psychological resources physicians draw upon when facing environmental stress, role conflicts, and career challenges, including concern, control, curiosity, and confidence [17–18]. In recent years, both constructs have been examined as mediating or moderating variables within the job stress-outcome pathway. However, most of these researches has focused on nurses or general clinical physicians [19–20], with limited exploration in the context of oncology-specialty hospitals, where complex organizational structures and professional stratification are particularly pronounced.

Importantly, this study does not aim to test the general applicability of Career Development Theory or Social Exchange Theory. Instead, it seeks to examine how established organizational and career-related mechanisms operate under conditions of high professional specialization and pronounced role differentiation, as exemplified by oncology-specialty hospitals. Building on the above theoretical and empirical gaps, this study focuses systematically on how physicians’ professional type, i.e. oncological physicians versus non-oncological physicians, influences their levels of job satisfaction and job burnout in oncology-specialty hospitals. Two key psychological mechanisms, POS and CA, are introduced to explain these effects. Based on Career Development Theory and Social Exchange Theory, we construct a theoretical model incorporating both mediating and moderating pathways and test it empirically using structural equation modeling (SEM). In contrast to previous studies, this research is the first to conduct a systematic comparison between physician subgroups within oncology-specialty hospitals, which are highly specialized organizational context. Moreover, this study reveals the interactive mechanisms through which POS and CA shape physicians’ professional experiences by applying four validated measurement scales. The findings aim to provide both theoretical insight and practical guidance for promoting equitable career development among diverse physician groups in specialized healthcare settings.

This study focuses on differences in occupational experience between physician groups occupying different institutional roles within oncology-specialty hospitals. The distinction between oncological and non-oncological physicians is employed as an analytical device to reflect structural positioning in this specific hospital context. The contrast captures differences in institutional role centrality—namely, treatment-oriented decision-making roles versus primarily diagnostic, perioperative, imaging, pathological, or other supportive roles—rather than implying homogeneity within either professional category. The aim is to examine how organizational positioning within oncology-specialty hospitals is associated with physicians’ job satisfaction and job burnout, rather than to compare technical competencies across medical specialties or to generalize to physicians outside oncology-specialty hospitals. Drawing on Career Development Theory and Social Exchange Theory, we propose four types of mechanism pathways: the main effects of professional type, mediation mechanisms, and moderation mechanisms. These theoretical considerations form the basis for the research hypotheses and the conceptual model developed in this study.

Theoretical framework and research hypotheses

Influence of professional type on physicians’ work experience: a Career Development Theory perspective

Career Development Theory emphasizes that individuals do not passively occupy professional roles; rather, they continuously construct and adjust their career trajectories through exploration, adaptation, and interaction with organizational environments [21–22]. From this perspective, job satisfaction reflects the extent to which individuals experience meaning, value realization, and person–environment fit at a given career stage, whereas job burnout can be understood as a psychological response to sustained barriers in career development, role conflict, and resource imbalance [23].

In oncology-specialty hospitals, physicians are commonly classified into oncological physicians and non-oncological physicians. In practice, this distinction is not merely technical but also organizational. Oncological physicians typically serve as the core clinical workforce, assuming primary responsibility for treatment decision-making and longitudinal cancer management. As a result, they often gain greater access to promotion opportunities, research platforms, and institutional support. By contrast, non-oncological physicians—although indispensable to diagnosis, perioperative care, and treatment support—are more frequently positioned in supporting roles within organizational hierarchies and may receive comparatively limited recognition and developmental resources [24].

A growing body of research suggests that such differences in professional centrality are closely related to individuals’ career-related experiences. Employees occupying core roles tend to report stronger career confidence, organizational identification, and perceived control over their work environment, whereas those in more peripheral positions are more likely to face ambiguous career pathways and constrained access to key resources [25–26]. These structural differences have often been linked to variations in job satisfaction and job burnout across occupational groups, rather than reflecting purely individual-level characteristics [27].

Taken together, these observations suggest that professional type is likely to be reflected in distinct patterns of job satisfaction and job burnout among physicians working in oncology-specialty hospitals. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1a

There are differences in job satisfaction between oncological and non-oncological physicians.

H1b

There are differences in job burnout between oncological and non-oncological physicians.

The mediating role of perceived organizational support: a Social Exchange Theory perspective

Social Exchange Theory posits that employees’ work-related attitudes are shaped by ongoing evaluations of fairness, trust, and reciprocity in their relationship with the organization [28]. When individuals perceive that their organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being, they are more likely to maintain positive attitudes toward their work and remain psychologically engaged.

Building on this theoretical foundation, POS was introduced to capture employees’ beliefs regarding the extent to which the organization appreciates their efforts and is willing to invest in their welfare and development [29]. Prior empirical work in healthcare and organizational contexts has consistently linked POS to job satisfaction and job burnout, with lower perceived support often accompanying emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [30–31].

Within hospital settings, perceptions of organizational support are not evenly distributed. Oncological physicians are often regarded as core contributors and tend to be more visible within institutional evaluation systems, particularly with respect to research performance, promotion criteria, and reward allocation. In contrast, the contributions of non-oncological physicians—especially those related to diagnostic accuracy and technical support—are frequently less observable in formal appraisal processes. Notably, this invisibility does not diminish their clinical importance, but it may translate into lower perceived recognition and fewer opportunities for professional development. Such differences in perceived organizational support may help explain why professional type is associated with divergent work experiences.

For this reason, perceived organizational support is conceptualized as a key psychological mechanism linking professional type to physicians’ occupational outcomes. The following hypotheses are proposed:

H2a

Perceived organizational support mediates the association between professional type and job satisfaction.

H2b

Perceived organizational support mediates the association between professional type and job burnout.

Career adaptability as a psychological mediator in Career Development

Career adaptability represents a central construct in Career Development Theory and refers to individuals’ psychosocial resources for managing career-related tasks, transitions, and uncertainties [32]. Savickas and colleagues conceptualized career adaptability as comprising four interrelated dimensions—concern, control, curiosity, and confidence—which together support individuals’ capacity to navigate changing career demands and role expectations [33].

Prior empirical studies have linked career adaptability to a range of positive occupational outcomes, including job satisfaction, psychological well-being, and motivation for professional development, particularly in work environments characterized by uncertainty or limited support [34–35]. In oncology-specialty hospitals, non-oncological physicians may face greater ambiguity regarding career advancement and professional identity, which makes career adaptability especially relevant to how they interpret and cope with workplace challenges.

Rather than operating independently, professional roles may shape physicians’ opportunities to develop and utilize adaptive career resources. Differences in career adaptability may therefore help explain why professional type is associated with variation in job satisfaction and job burnout. Based on this reasoning, career adaptability is expected to function as an important mediating mechanism in physicians’ work experiences. The following hypotheses are proposed:

H3a

Career adaptability mediates the association between professional type and job satisfaction.

H3b

Career adaptability mediates the association between professional type and job burnout.

The moderating roles of perceived organizational support and career adaptability

POS and CA may operate not only as mediating mechanisms but also as boundary conditions shaping how professional-type differences are reflected in occupational outcomes. As mediators, these constructs represent pathways through which structural role positioning is associated with job satisfaction and job burnout. As moderators, they define conditions under which such differences become amplified or attenuated. This distinction clarifies that their dual specification reflects analytically complementary explanatory levels rather than conceptual redundancy.

In highly specialized hospital settings characterized by role differentiation, both organizational and individual resources may condition how professional positioning translates into work-related experiences. Consistent with resource-based perspectives in occupational health research, individuals operating in high-demand environments are more vulnerable to burnout when adequate buffering resources are limited [36]. POS may reduce the negative impact of structural inequalities by enhancing perceptions of fairness, support, and belonging [37]. Similarly, CA represents a self-regulatory psychological resource that enables individuals to manage uncertainty and career constraints through adaptive coping processes [38–39]. Prior studies have documented the moderating roles of both constructs among occupational groups exposed to elevated work demands [40–41].

Accordingly, perceived organizational support and career adaptability may alter the strength of the associations between professional type and physicians’ job satisfaction and job burnout. The following hypotheses are proposed:

H4a

Perceived organizational support moderates the association between professional type and job satisfaction.

H4b

Perceived organizational support moderates the association between professional type and job burnout.

H5a

Career adaptability moderates the association between professional type and job satisfaction.

H5b

Career adaptability moderates the association between professional type and job burnout.

Research methods

Participants and data collection

This study employed a cross-sectional design to investigate clinical physicians working in five provincial-level oncology-specialty hospitals across China. Data were collected via an electronic questionnaire distributed through the “Wenjuanxing” platform (www.wjx.cn) using cluster sampling method. The aim was to compare physicians of different professional types in terms of job satisfaction, job burnout, POS, and CA, and to further explore the mediating and moderating mechanisms underlying these relationships.

403 physicians were recruited from five hospitals located in distinct geographic regions of China, including Sichuan Cancer Hospital (n = 102, Southwest), Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (n = 95, Northwest), Yunnan Cancer Hospital (n = 76, Southwest), Shandong Cancer Hospital (n = 73, North), and Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (n = 57, East). Physicians were categorized into two groups according to the primary clinical function of their departments within oncology-specialty hospitals. Oncological physicians were defined as physicians working in departments directly involved in cancer diagnosis and treatment, including surgical oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, and other treatment-oriented oncology departments. Non-oncological physicians were defined as hospital-based specialist physicians employed in the same oncology-specialty hospitals but working in departments that mainly provide diagnostic, perioperative, imaging, pathological, or other supportive clinical services, including anesthesiology, radiology, pathology, and ultrasound. Therefore, the non-oncological physician group in this study should not be interpreted as representing general physicians outside oncology-specialty hospitals, such as primary care physicians, cardiologists, or physicians from general hospitals. The distinction was used to reflect differences in institutional role centrality and clinical specialization within oncology-specialty hospitals. Demographic and professional background variables were also collected, including sex, age, educational background, professional title, years of clinical experience, and weekly working hours. These variables were used to describe the sample characteristics, while available covariates were incorporated into the adjusted analyses as described below.

The survey was administered via the Wenjuanxing online platform (https://www.wjx.cn), a widely used tool for electronic data collection in China. Data were collected during the second half of 2024. A total of 403 questionnaires were distributed based on the sampling design. After removing incomplete and invalid responses, 383 valid questionnaires were retained, resulting in a response rate of 95.0%.

All participants took part voluntarily and provided informed consent. All personal data and responses were treated with strict confidentiality. This study was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Sichuan Cancer Hospital (Approval No. SCCSMC-01-2025-07) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

All participating hospitals are leading provincial oncology-specialty institutions located in provincial capital cities. These hospitals provide high-volume oncology services and comparable clinical and training environments, supporting robust comparisons of physicians’ occupational experiences across sites.

Measures

The survey instrument in this study was developed based on four internationally validated scales, covering the key constructs of job satisfaction, job burnout, POS, and career adaptability. In addition, the questionnaire also collected basic demographic information, including sex, age, educational background, professional title, years of work experience, participation in further training, and attendance in professional development programs. These variables were used for descriptive analysis, and as covariates in the subsequent regression models.

All measurement tools were adapted from well-established English-language scales with well demonstrated structural validity and cross-cultural applicability. Either existing validated Chinese versions were used or the original instruments were translated and back-translated following standard procedures. The internal consistency of each scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and average scores were computed for analysis.

Job satisfaction

Job satisfaction was assessed using the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire-Short Form (MSQ-SF), developed by Weiss et al. in 1967 [42]. This instrument consists of 20 items designed to measure respondents’ satisfaction with various aspects of their job. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), with higher scores indicating greater job satisfaction. The MSQ-SF has been widely applied in organizational and occupational behavior researches with well demonstrated reliability and structural validity.

Job burnout

Job burnout was measured using the Maslach Burnout Inventory-General Survey (MBI-GS) developed by Maslach, Jackson, and Leiter in 1996 [43], which is applicable to professionals across a wide range of industries. The Chinese version of this scale consists of 15 items, which are grouped into three dimensions, i.e. emotional exhaustion (5 items), depersonalization (4 items), and reduced personal accomplishment (6 items). Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (every day), with some items reverse-scored.

In this study, the average scores for each dimension were calculated to describe the specific manifestations of job burnout among physicians. A composite mean score was also computed to reflect overall burnout. Subscale analysis follows the standard practice recommended by the MBI-GS and is widely adopted in international research.

POS

POS was measured using the short version of the Perceived Organizational Support Scale (POSS) developed by Eisenberger et al. in 1986 [17], which consists 13 items. This scale assesses employees’ subjective perceptions on whether and to what extent their organization values their contributions, and cares about their well-being and the fair distribution of resources. Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The POSS has been widely applied in studies of organizational behavior across healthcare, education, and corporate sectors.

Career adaptability

Career adaptability was assessed using the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (CAAS) developed by Savickas and Porfeli in 2012 [18]. The scale contains 24 items, which are divided into four dimensions: Concern, Control, Curiosity, and Confidence, with six items per dimension. Respondents were asked to rate the extent to which each statement reflected their personal ability to cope with career-related challenges and plan their career paths. Responses were given on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all true) to 7 (completely true). The CAAS has undergone cross-cultural validation in multiple countries and is widely recognized as a robust instrument in career development research.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 26.0 and Stata MP 16.0. Data cleaning and scale reliability were first examined using Cronbach’s α coefficients. Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were then performed to summarize the study variables and assess bivariate associations. Independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare job satisfaction, job burnout, POS, and CA between oncological and non-oncological physicians.

To assess construct validity, we first conducted the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Subsequently, to rigorously examine the discriminant validity among the four core constructs—job satisfaction, job burnout, POS, and career adaptability—we performed a series of Confirmatory Factor Analyses (CFA) using Mplus 8.3. We tested a hypothesized four-factor model and compared it against several alternative models: a three-factor model combining POS and CA (as an alternative specification), a three-factor model combining job satisfaction and job burnout (as an alternative specification), and a single-factor model where all items were loaded onto one factor. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic (χ²), degrees of freedom (df), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).

To assess the robustness of group differences, multiple regression models were estimated with adjustment for available demographic and work-related covariates, including sex, age, education level, years of clinical experience, and weekly working hours. Years of clinical experience was used as an available indicator of clinical seniority, while weekly working hours was used as an approximate indicator of clinical workload. Professional title was collected and reported descriptively, but it was not included in the adjusted models because of its conceptual overlap with years of clinical experience and potential collinearity with career stage. More detailed workload and family-responsibility indicators, such as patient caseload, shift frequency, night-duty frequency, case complexity, department-level workload arrangements, and childcare responsibilities, were not collected and are therefore acknowledged as limitations. Mediation effects were examined using bootstrapped indirect effects (1,000 resamples). In addition, SEM) was conducted using Stata’s SEM module to validate the hypothesized mediation pathways, with results reported in both tabular and graphical formats.

Moderation effects were examined using multiple regression models in Stata MP 16.0. To test the unique moderating contributions of POS and career adaptability CA, we specified joint moderation models for each outcome (job satisfaction and job burnout) that included professional type (PT), POS, CA, and the interaction terms PT×POS and PT×CA simultaneously, together with all covariates. This specification allows assessment of whether each interaction explains incremental variance beyond the other moderator. Continuous predictors involved in interaction terms were mean-centered prior to model estimation. Multicollinearity diagnostics were examined; variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all predictors were below commonly used thresholds, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 383 valid responses were collected from physicians working in five provincial oncology-specialty hospitals located in Sichuan, Xinjiang, Yunnan, Shandong, and Zhejiang Provinces. Among the respondents, 217 (56.7%) were oncological physicians, and 166 (43.3%) were non-oncological physicians. The sample included 166 male physicians (43.3%) and 217 female physicians (56.7%), with a mean age of 37.9 years (SD = 6.8).

In terms of education level, 72 participants (18.8%) held a bachelor’s degree or lower, 199 (52.0%) held a master’s degree, and 112 (29.2%) held a doctor’s degree. The average length of work experience was 11.6 years (SD = 7.3). The most common professional title was associate chief physician, held by 122 participants (31.9%), followed by attending physician, held by 120 participants (31.3%). Reliability and validity testing

In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were used to evaluate the internal consistency of the measurement instruments. Cronbach’s α reflects the degree of consistency among items within each scale, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A value above 0.70 is generally considered acceptable, while values above 0.80 indicate high reliability [44].

To assess construct validity, the KMO measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were conducted. KMO value indicates the suitability of data for factor analysis, with values closer to 1 suggesting stronger partial correlations among variables, and a value above 0.70 is considered acceptable. Bartlett’s test determines whether the correlation matrix significantly differs from an identity matrix, and a p-value less than 0.05 suggests that the data are appropriate for factor analysis.

In this study, all four core scales demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.85. The KMO values were also greater than 0.85 for each scale, and all Bartlett’s tests were statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating strong structural validity. These results confirmed that the measurement instruments were suitable for subsequent statistical analysis and structural modeling. Detailed results are presented in Table 2.

Group differences in job satisfaction and job burnout

Clear differences emerged between the two physician groups in terms of job satisfaction. Oncological physicians reported substantially higher job satisfaction scores than non-oncological physicians (88.29 ± 8.27 vs. 68.49 ± 8.20, t = 23.303, p < 0.001), supporting Hypothesis H1a. The two groups also differed significantly in overall job burnout. Oncological physicians showed higher burnout scores compared with their non-oncological counterparts (30.40 ± 14.02 vs. 27.17 ± 16.80, t = 2.046, p = 0.041), providing support for Hypothesis H1b. This between-group difference should be interpreted alongside the subdimension results, indicating that higher overall burnout in the oncological physician group was driven primarily by emotional exhaustion and depersonalization rather than a uniform elevation across all dimensions. In line with the multidimensional structure of the MBI-GS, overall burnout scores are reported alongside subdimension analyses to distinguish between aggregate burnout levels and specific manifestations across dimensions.

When job burnout was examined at the dimensional level, oncological physicians scored higher on emotional exhaustion (t = 2.749, p = 0.006) and depersonalization (t = 3.064, p = 0.002). No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in reduced personal accomplishment (p = 0.676). This pattern suggests that higher job satisfaction among oncological physicians should not be interpreted as indicating lower occupational strain. Rather, the results indicate the coexistence of stronger professional affirmation and greater emotional or interpersonal strain among physicians occupying treatment-centered roles in oncology-specialty hospitals.

Group differences in POS and CA

Differences were also observed for perceived organizational support and career adaptability. Oncological physicians reported higher levels of POS than non-oncological physicians (54.57 ± 7.70 vs. 44.87 ± 11.71; t = 9.756, p < 0.001). A similar pattern was found for career adaptability, with oncological physicians scoring markedly higher than non-oncological physicians (152.34 ± 9.70 vs. 122.10 ± 15.08; t = 23.794, p < 0.001).

Group differences in categorical variables

Chi-square analyses of categorical levels of job satisfaction, overall job burnout, and burnout subdimensions revealed significant between-group differences. Specifically, the distributions of job satisfaction level (χ² = 314.592, p < 0.001), job burnout level (χ² = 34.219, p < 0.001), emotional exhaustion (χ² = 12.150, p = 0.002), depersonalization (χ² = 25.344, p < 0.001), and reduced personal accomplishment (χ² = 15.478, p < 0.001) differed significantly between oncological and non-oncological physicians.

Taken together, oncological physicians reported higher levels of job satisfaction, perceived organizational support, and career adaptability than non-oncological physicians. At the same time, they also exhibited higher overall job burnout, particularly with respect to emotional exhaustion and depersonalization. These descriptive findings provide a foundation for the subsequent mediation and moderation analyses.

To further assess the robustness of the observed group differences, subsequent regression models incorporating available demographic and work-related covariates were estimated. These covariates included sex, age, education level, years of clinical experience, and weekly working hours. The results indicate that professional type remained associated with both job satisfaction and job burnout after adjustment, providing additional support for Hypotheses H1a and H1b.

Discussion and implications

Summary of key findings

Drawing on a multi-center sample of 383 physicians from oncology-specialty hospitals across five provinces in China (Sichuan, Xinjiang, Yunnan, Shandong, and Zhejiang), this study examined whether professional type (oncological vs. non-oncological physicians) is associated with physicians’ job satisfaction and job burnout, and whether POS and CA are associated with these outcomes and condition the magnitude of the observed group differences.

Three findings are particularly salient. First, oncological physicians reported higher job satisfaction than non-oncological physicians, while also exhibiting higher overall job burnout, a pattern primarily characterized by elevated emotional exhaustion and depersonalization rather than consistent differences across all burnout dimensions. In other words, favorable job attitudes and elevated strain co-occurred within the oncological physician group, underscoring the need to consider both motivational and stress-related facets of occupational experience when interpreting “core role” advantages in specialized settings. Related evidence in specialized hospital contexts has also suggested that patterns of satisfaction and burnout do not always move in tandem across clinical groups and institutional types [46].

Second, POS and CA were not merely correlated with the two outcomes; they also emerged as key variables through which professional-type differences were reflected in satisfaction and burnout and through which these differences varied in magnitude. These findings extend broader organizational research by showing that the relevance of perceived support and enabling conditions for well-being outcomes is contingent on physicians’ professional positioning within highly specialized hospital settings [47, 48].

Third, CA appeared particularly consequential for non-oncological physicians. Across analyses, higher CA was linked to higher job satisfaction and lower burnout, consistent with a body of research that views career adaptability as a set of psychosocial resources relevant to work engagement, satisfaction, and stress-related outcomes [49–52].

Differences in professional type and physician occupational experience

The present study identified clear differences in occupational experience between oncological and non-oncological physicians working in oncology-specialty hospitals. Oncological physicians reported higher job satisfaction than their non-oncological counterparts, while also exhibiting higher levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization.

The higher satisfaction observed among oncological physicians may be associated with their central positioning in treatment decision-making and greater visibility within institutional evaluation systems. Core treatment roles often provide more direct access to research opportunities, promotion pathways, and professional recognition. Institutional context and role positioning have similarly been shown to shape patterns of both satisfaction and burnout [47].

At the same time, oncological physicians demonstrated elevated burnout. In highly specialized clinical settings, central treatment roles may combine professional meaning with intensified psychological demands. While such positions can strengthen identity affirmation and perceived impact, they also involve sustained responsibility for complex decisions and emotionally demanding patient care. The coexistence of higher satisfaction and higher burnout therefore reflects the dual psychological consequences of structural role centrality rather than a contradiction between positive and negative occupational states.

Although the non-oncological physician group comprises diverse specialties, including radiology, pathology, intensive care, and general internal medicine, the analytical contrast adopted in this study is intended to capture structural role centrality within oncology-specialty hospitals. The distinction reflects differences in institutional visibility, decision-making authority, and access to organizational resources, rather than specialty-specific clinical variation. Career construction theory suggests that satisfaction depends not only on task characteristics but also on perceived opportunity structures and professional recognition [53, 54]. In highly specialized cancer centers, such role-based differentiation may become particularly salient.

At the same time, the present study did not further disaggregate the non-oncological physician group by specialty in the main analyses, which remains an important limitation. More fine-grained specialty-based analyses would be valuable in future research to further assess within-group differences. Another issue that should be considered is the institutional specificity of oncology-specialty hospitals. Because the clinical functions, research platforms, performance evaluation systems, and professional development pathways of such hospitals are generally organized around cancer diagnosis and treatment, these settings may be structurally more aligned with the work of oncological physicians. Non-oncological physicians employed in oncology-specialty hospitals may therefore differ from physicians in similar specialties working in general hospitals, both in terms of employment selection and the scope of clinical responsibility. In addition, supportive departments may have different patterns of workload, patient contact, case complexity, and decision-making responsibility compared with treatment-oriented oncology departments. These facility-level and clinical-function characteristics may have contributed to the observed group differences and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The mediating and moderating roles of POS

POS played a central role in the relationships examined in this study. Non-oncological physicians reported lower POS on average, which is compatible with their comparatively lower organizational visibility and fewer perceived opportunities for advancement in many specialized hospital settings. POS has been consistently linked to job-related outcomes and psychological states in healthcare workforces, including work attitudes and indicators relevant to strain and well-being [46, 48].

Importantly, POS was also involved in the moderation patterns. The interaction results were consistent with the idea that higher organizational support is associated with a weaker relationship between professional type and burnout—an interaction form often described as a buffering pattern. In practical terms, organizational support may be most consequential when physicians face structural constraints: where role disadvantages exist, supportive organizational climates and empowerment-related conditions appear to matter more for mitigating adverse experiences. It should be noted that, however, organizational support does not remove underlying structural inequalities between professional groups; rather, it conditions how such inequalities are perceived and experienced at the individual level. This interpretation fits well with evidence showing that support and empowerment mechanisms are related to burnout and turnover intentions among healthcare workers.

Taken together, these findings add nuance to social exchange interpretations in a specialized hospital context: perceptions of being valued and supported are not simply “nice-to-have” correlates, but appear to be closely tied to how occupational disparities are experienced and how strongly they translate into strain-related outcomes [48].

The mediating and moderating roles of CA

CA emerged as another key resource linked to physicians’ occupational experiences. Oncological physicians reported higher CA than non-oncological physicians, and higher CA was associated with higher job satisfaction and lower levels of burnout, particularly with respect to emotional exhaustion and depersonalization. This pattern builds on prior research linking career adaptability to work engagement and positive job-related outcomes by illustrating how such associations manifest under conditions of constrained and differentiated career structures [49–51].

The relevance of CA is especially evident for non-oncological physicians. In settings where career pathways may appear less linear or less rewarded within centralized systems, adaptability-related capacities—such as maintaining future orientation, exercising control over career decisions, exploring alternatives, and sustaining confidence—may be closely related to how physicians interpret constraints and maintain occupational well-being. Evidence from career adaptability research supports such associations with satisfaction and job stress, and meta-analytic work also indicates robust links between adaptability and adaptive outcomes across occupational groups [50–52].

The moderation results further suggest that CA shapes the extent to which professional-type differences are reflected in job satisfaction and burnout. At higher levels of CA, between-group differences in job satisfaction tended to be more pronounced, whereas the association between professional type and burnout was weaker, consistent with a buffering pattern for burnout. Conceptually, this implies that adaptability does not erase structural disadvantages rooted in role differentiation or institutional arrangements; rather, it shapes how physicians interpret, respond to, and navigate these constraints, especially in rapidly evolving healthcare contexts where specialty differentiation and reform pressures continue to reshape professional trajectories [55].

Regional sample characteristics and international implications

A further strength of this study lies in its multi-region design. Despite differences in resources and management practices across provinces, the overall pattern of associations involving professional type, POS, CA, and occupational outcomes was detectable across regions. This provides preliminary support for the stability of the proposed mechanisms in oncology-specialty hospital systems.

The findings are also consistent with international evidence highlighting the importance of organizational and psychosocial resources for healthcare workers’ well-being. For example, research in European hospital settings has reported that organizational support conditions are relevant to how burnout translates into turnover intention among nurses [4]. Similarly, work focusing on leadership quality and social support has emphasized their links to well-being and psychological distress outcomes in nursing populations [56]. Although these studies differ in population and setting, the parallels strengthen the argument that organizational support and psychosocial resources are broadly relevant protective factors across healthcare systems.

Theoretical and practical implications

Theoretical implications

Grounded in career construction theory and social exchange perspectives, this study contributes to a more differentiated understanding of how role structures within specialized healthcare organizations relate to physicians’ job satisfaction and job burnout. Rather than treating physicians as a homogeneous professional group, the findings highlight how role centrality within oncology-specialty hospitals is associated with distinct profiles of satisfaction and strain [47–48]. Beyond documenting these differences, the present study further clarifies two complementary theoretical mechanisms. First, POS and CA operate as mediators, indicating that professional positioning is associated with differential access to organizational support and adaptive career resources, which partly explains why job satisfaction and burnout vary across groups. Second, POS and CA also function as moderators, suggesting that these resources define the conditions under which professional-type differences become more pronounced (for job satisfaction) or are buffered (for job burnout). Distinguishing these “process” and “boundary” roles helps integrate social exchange and career development perspectives in a specialized hospital context, where role-based resource structures are salient. Under these organizational conditions, physicians’ professional positions are closely linked to differential access to decision-making authority, career opportunities, and institutional recognition. Consequently, social exchange processes and career development mechanisms are more likely to be experienced unevenly across professional groups, rather than operating uniformly within the organization.

The results also extend the relevance of POS and CA in this context. POS appears closely related to occupational outcomes and helps clarify how structural differences are psychologically experienced within organizations [46, 50]. Meanwhile, CA contributes an individual resource perspective, suggesting that adaptability-related capacities are linked to both job satisfaction and burnout outcomes and may condition how professional-type differences manifest. This is consistent with the broader career adaptability literature and its documented associations with adapting responses and adaptation results [52–54].

Practical implications for hospital management

From a management standpoint, the findings suggest that improving physicians’ occupational experiences in oncology-specialty hospitals requires attention to both organizational conditions and individual resources.

First, strengthening organizational support—through more transparent recognition systems, more equitable promotion and evaluation pathways, and stronger developmental and emotional support mechanisms—may be especially important for hospital-based non-oncological specialist physicians whose contributions are essential but less visible in oncology-centered institutional systems.

Second, hospitals may consider structured interventions that support CA, particularly for non-oncological physicians who face more constrained career opportunity structures. Career planning support, professional development programs, and skills training that enhance perceived control and confidence may be useful, given the consistent associations between CA and job satisfaction/job stress in prior studies [51–54]. Such measures are likely to be increasingly relevant in China’s ongoing healthcare reform environment, where specialty differentiation and institutional performance pressures continue to intensify [55–56]. Beyond individual-level resources, structural and institutional factors likely shape both perceived organizational support and career adaptability. Workload intensity, performance evaluation systems, departmental autonomy, and access to research platforms may influence how physicians perceive recognition and construct their career trajectories. POS and CA should therefore not be viewed solely as personal attributes but as partially embedded in broader institutional arrangements. Future research should incorporate structural indicators to further disentangle institutional determinants from psychological mechanisms.

Limitations and directions for future research

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although mediation terminology was used for analytical clarity, the indirect associations identified in this study should be interpreted as statistical relationships consistent with the proposed theoretical framework rather than as temporally verified process pathways. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships among professional type, organizational resources, psychological resources, and occupational outcomes. In addition, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce common-method bias and social desirability effects. Future research could incorporate multi-source assessments, objective indicators, or mixed-method designs to strengthen validity.

Second, the classification of physicians into oncological and non-oncological groups represents an analytical simplification. In this study, “non-oncological physicians” refers only to hospital-based specialist physicians working in oncology-specialty hospitals who provide diagnostic, perioperative, imaging, pathological, or other supportive services. It should not be interpreted as referring to general physician groups outside oncology-specialty hospitals, such as primary care physicians, cardiologists, or physicians working in general hospitals. Although this classification captures differences in role centrality within oncology-specialty hospitals, it does not fully reflect heterogeneity within either group. Future studies should adopt more fine-grained specialty-based classifications to examine whether radiologists, pathologists, anesthesiologists, ultrasound physicians, and other supportive specialists experience different patterns of job satisfaction, burnout, organizational support, and career adaptability.

Third, the institutional specificity of oncology-specialty hospitals should be considered when interpreting the findings. Because their clinical functions, research platforms, performance evaluation systems, and professional development pathways are largely organized around cancer diagnosis and treatment, these hospitals may be structurally more aligned with the work of oncological physicians. Non-oncological physicians employed in such hospitals may therefore differ from physicians in similar specialties working in general hospitals in terms of employment selection, clinical responsibility, workload patterns, patient contact, and case complexity. Although years of clinical experience and weekly working hours were included as available covariates, more detailed indicators—such as patient caseload, shift frequency, night-duty frequency, department-level workload arrangements, professional title, childcare responsibilities, and family-care demands—were not fully incorporated into the adjusted models. These factors may be relevant to burnout and should be considered in future studies.

Conclusions

Within oncology-specialty hospitals, differences in professional roles are closely associated with physicians’ job satisfaction and job burnout. In shaping these associations, POS and CA played complementary process and boundary roles. These findings should be interpreted within the specific context of oncology-specialty hospitals, where institutional systems are largely organized around cancer diagnosis and treatment. The results provide insight into how organizational and psychological resources may help address occupational disparities among multidisciplinary physicians in highly specialized hospital settings.
