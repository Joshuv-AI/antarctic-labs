Numerical simulation of deformation and hole position shift in extrusion process of four-hole propellant
Authors: Hao Liang, Baolu Song, Bo Xu, Yu Pang, Pin Hu, Zengjie Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Four-hole propellant were prepared by solvent extrusion. The polyflow module of ANSYS software was used to simulate the hole position deviation and shape change in the extrusion process of four-hole propellants. The extrusion quality of four-hole propellants was simulated and optimized. The results show that the deformation and the hole position deviation of the simulated four-hole propellants after extrusion are basically consistent with those obtained by the experiment, and the flow rate of the propellant decreases gradually from the center to the wall or the surface of the pins during extrusion. Properly reducing the volume flow rate of the inlet is beneficial to reduce the deformation and hole offset of the propellants. The simulation results show that the designed mold can greatly reduce the bad deformation in the extrusion process of four-hole propellant.

Introduction

The shape and size of propellants are key factors affecting the combustion regularity of propellants.

The suitable structure of mold is an important factor to ensure the quality of propellants. With the development of modern military, in order to meet the needs of different weapons, the research on the modification of propellants is gradually deepened, and the structure of extrusion die needs to be changed. When the mold design is unreasonable, the extruded propellants will produce eccentric holes, uneven density, unstable product quality, mold damage and so on.

With the development of computer hardware and software, numerical simulation technology has been widely used in polymerization research, which shortens the development time of mold and improves the accuracy of design1.

Mutlu et al.2 simulated the polymer melt flow problem driven by free extrusion pressure and cable traction during cable coating extrusion. Hitkara N R et al.3 pair asymmetric T type. The extrusion molding process of the material was studied. The curvature of the product and the deformation of the grid are predicted.

In this paper, four-hole propellant were prepared by solvent extrusion. ANSYS polyflow (version 2022 R1, part of the ANSYS 2022 software suite) was used to simulate the hole position deviation and shape change in the extrusion process of four-hole propellants (ANSYS polyflow 2022 R1, https://www.ansys.com/products/fluids/ansys-polyflow). The extrusion quality of four-hole propellants was simulated and optimized.

Numerical simulation

Basic assumptions

In order to simulate one model well, two factors should be considered. The first one is in accordance with actual condition, and another one is the convenience of calculation. In view of the complexity between solvent extrusion process and all kinds of parameters, three assumptions for the characteristics of materials and technology features were listed as follows4,5.

(1)The charge is an incompressible steady laminar flow; the heat transfer in the extrusion process is not considered;

(2)There is no slip on the flow channel wall for the initial baseline simulation, meaning each velocity component is zero; however, a non-linear wall-slip model is subsequently introduced in the optimization stage to investigate high-shear effects.

(3)The influence of gravity and inertia force of the charge is not taken into account.

The model presented in Fig. 1 depicts the original process design, drawn at a 1:1 scale. The flow channel geometry, generated via Boolean operations, represents the legacy configuration. As shown in Fig. 1, the channel is divided into three sections: the compression section, the forming section, and the free section. A hybrid mesh was generated automatically, employing a tetrahedral grid for the compression and forming sections, and a hexahedral grid for the free section. This model serves as the baseline for simulating and analyzing the existing forming issues.

Numerical method details

Mesh generation and independence verification

The computational domain was meshed using a hybrid strategy, employing tetrahedral elements for the complex compression and forming sections, and hexahedral elements for the free section. To ensure that the numerical results are independent of the grid resolution, a mesh-independence study was conducted using three different mesh densities: a coarse mesh (95,000 elements), a medium mesh (185,000 elements), and a fine mesh (350,000 elements). The maximum inlet pressure was chosen as the evaluation metric. The results indicated that the relative difference in the calculated pressure between the medium and fine meshes was merely 1.8%. Therefore, to optimize computational efficiency while preserving high numerical accuracy, the medium mesh was selected for all subsequent simulations.

Solver settings and boundary conditions

The numerical simulations were executed using the finite element method based ANSYS Polyflow solver. Due to the highly non-linear nature of the pseudoplastic fluid described by the Bird-Carreau model, the Picard iterative scheme was employed to solve the coupled mass and momentum conservation equations. Inlet: A specified volume flow rate was applied.

Theoretical analysis of deformation and hole position shift

Flow rate distribution

The flow rate distribution of the diagonal plane and the section on the the propellant extrusion channel was analyzed, as shown in Figs. 5 and 6, respectively. It can be seen that the flow rate in the center of the flow channel is the fastest, the flow rate of materials on the surface of the wall and pins is the slowest, and the flow rate of material decreases along the center to the wall or the surface of the pins. From the analysis of the flow rate distribution diagram on the section at the exit, it can be seen that the flow rate at the center of the exit is the fastest, which leads to the expansion from the center to the edge after extrusion, which is the main reason for the deformation of the propellant and the deviation of the hole position.

Pressure distribution

Figure 8 is a cloud view of the pressure distribution on the diagonal plane of the flow channel. It can be seen that the pressure in the runner decreases along the extrusion direction, and the pressure at the entrance is the highest, which is 51,300 Pa. The outlet pressure is the lowest, 18,100 Pa.

Fig. 8Cloud image of pressure distribution on diagonal plane.

Cloud image of pressure distribution on diagonal plane.

Based on a comprehensive analysis integrating the pressure distribution contour plot and velocity vector results, the underlying physical mechanism can be fully articulated as follows. Within the die flow channel, a sudden geometric contraction forces the material velocity to increase sharply to satisfy mass conservation. In accordance with the Bernoulli principle, this localized acceleration causes a corresponding significant drop in static pressure, giving rise to the low and negativepressure zones observed in the simulation. This coupled state of high velocity and low pressure has two critical consequences. Firstly, the material experiences extreme shear rates, generating substantial recoverable elastic strain energy. Secondly, it forms a highly non-uniform velocity profile across the channel section. Upon exiting the die into the free section, the abrupt disappearance of the die wall constraints triggers a dual response: the release of the contained elastic energy and the redistribution of momentum to equilibrate the uneven velocity profile. The synergistic action of these two factors manifests macroscopically as the non-uniform radial swelling and subsequent shape distortion of the extrudate. This elucidated mechanism directly explains the root cause of the experimental defects, such as pore misalignment and cross-sectional deformation, confirming the diagnostic accuracy of the simulation model.

Wall slip

In simulating the extrusion process under high-shear conditions, significant wall slip may occur between the material and the die wall. The traditional no-slip boundary condition tends to overestimate the shear rate and flow resistance in such scenarios, leading to inaccurate predictions of the pressure field, velocity field, and final extrudate morphology. To accurately describe the nonlinear, continuous relationship between wall-slip velocity and shear stress, this study employs a wall-slip constitutive model based on an S-shaped function. This model can physically characterize the smooth transition from an adhesion state below a critical stress to a slip state when the stress is exceeded. Its expression is:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = \frac{{{A_1} - {A_2}}}{{1 + {{\rm{e}}^{(x - {x_0})/dx}}}} + {A_2}$$\end{document}

Here, y represents the relative slip velocity at the wall, and x denotes the shear stress acting on the wall. The model parameters have clear physical meanings: A1 = 2.39749, A2 = 2.79693, the asymptotic values of the slip velocity in the low-stress and high-stress regions, respectively; X0 = 9.65665 is the critical shear stress threshold where slip behavior becomes significant; and DX = 0.59469, controls the smoothness and rate of the transition from the low-slip plateau to the high-slip plateau, a smaller value indicates a sharper transition, while a larger value indicates a more gradual one. Substituting the specific parameters gives the concrete form of the model:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = \frac{{ - 0.39944}}{{1 + {{\rm{e}}^{\frac{{x - 9.65665}}{{0.59469}}}}}} + 2.79693$$\end{document}

In the simulations of this study, this wall-slip model is applied as a critical boundary condition on all solid wall surfaces of the flow channel. Its introduction enables the simulation to more realistically capture the flow characteristics in high-shear regions, thereby more accurately predicting various effects induced by wall slip. These effects include, but are not limited to, a reduction in apparent viscosity, changes in overall pressure drop, alterations in the extrudate velocity profile, and most importantly, flow instabilities caused by local slip. Such instabilities are a significant contributor to defects in the extrudate, such as surface flaws, shape distortion, and internal pore misalignment. Therefore, simulations incorporating the wall-slip model provide a more complete mechanical framework for accurately tracing the root causes of defects like hole deformation and “hole position offset” and for guiding optimization efforts.

To accurately obtain the specific parameters for the wall-slip model, a parametric calibration approach was employed. As shown in Fig. 12, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically varying the logarithm of the slip coefficient, log(k), and monitoring the resulting extrudate side length. The discrete simulation results (orange dots) were then used to establish a continuous S-shaped fitting equation (dashed line). By matching the experimental side length with this fitting curve, the critical wall-slip parameters applied in the optimized simulation were successfully calibrated, ensuring that the defined boundary conditions physically reflect the actual high-shear extrusion process.

Experimental verification

Materials

Double-based absorbed powder was provided by Luzhou North Chemical Industry limited company. Acetone and ethanol are purchased from the Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.,Ltd.

Preparation methods

Extrusion process

Mix solvent of acetone and ethanol in ratio of 1:1 in reserve. Double-based absorbed powder was added into kneader, pour mixed solvent in with the dose of 0.6 mL per gram of absorbed powder. The absorbed powder was gradually plasticized in mixed solvent under stirring for an hour, thus plasticized propellant was prepared. The plasticized propellant was extruded with a specific mold under action of the hydraulic press. The mold diagram is shown in Fig. 13. Dry naturally, and cut the extruded propellant into smaller grains with a length to diameter ratio of 1:1 after the it is slightly hardened. Then put the grains in a 50 °C oven for 7 days to remove the solvent.

Experimental results

As shown in Fig. 14, the initial four-hole propellant underwent serious deformation at the exit. The four sides changed from a straight line to an arc, and the hole position offset radially, changing the hole shape from circular to oval. To quantitatively validate the numerical model, both the hole spacing on the diagonal lines (representing internal offset) and the cross-sectional area (representing macroscopic die swell) were measured from the experimental extrudate photographs via digital image analysis, and then compared with the simulation results (Table 5). The experimental diagonal hole spacing for the original extrudate was 2.485 mm, and the cross-sectional area was 8.243 mm², which are highly consistent with the simulated values of 2.516 mm and 7.910 mm². The resulting relative errors are merely 1.25% and 4.04%, respectively. This robust quantitative agreement proves that the proposed numerical model accurately captures the complex viscoelastic deformation behavior.

Furthermore, the application of the simulation-optimized die successfully yielded a four-hole propellant with significantly enhanced dimensional fidelity. The extrudate exhibits straight side profiles and maintains true circular holes without severe radial offset. As quantitatively detailed in Table 5, the optimized experimental hole spacing was reduced to 1.402 mm, and the cross-sectional area was tightly controlled at 6.415 mm². These experimental measurements closely match the simulation predictions (1.484 mm and 6.501 mm²), with relative errors contained within 5.85% and 1.34%. This fully validates that the flow uniformity and pressure distribution achieved through the optimized design effectively mitigate the die swell and flow imbalance that caused the prior defects.

Conclusions

(1) The ANSYS Polyflow module was employed to simulate the extrusion process of four-hole propellants. The results show a reasonable agreement between simulation and experiment in terms of deformation and hole position deviation, supporting the usefulness of the model within its assumed framework.

(2) After optimization, the edge distance increased by 23.04% and the hole distance by 42.07%, indicating improved dimensional consistency compared to the initial design, though some variability remains.

(3) The simulation reveals that the flow rate decreases from the center toward the wall or pin surfaces, which is a key factor contributing to the observed deformation and hole position deviation.

(4) Reducing the inlet volume flow rate is shown to mitigate deformation and misalignment. The optimized mold design demonstrates a reduction in undesirable deformation under the studied extrusion conditions.

Limitaions

Although this study provides useful insights into the extrusion behavior of four-hole propellants, certain limitations exist. The simulation assumed isothermal and incompressible flow, which may not fully represent actual processing conditions. Additionally, the material model, while capturing shear-thinning behavior, may simplify certain viscoelastic effects. Future work could consider non-isothermal and more advanced constitutive models to further improve predictive accuracy.
