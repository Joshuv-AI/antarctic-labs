DS-ARO: a multi-strategy improved artificial rabbits optimization algorithm for global optimization and corporate bankruptcy prediction
Authors: Xiran Bai, Yuchi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the limitations of the Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO) algorithm in solving complex high-dimensional and multimodal optimization problems—such as insufficient convergence guidance, weak balance between exploration and exploitation, and lack of adaptive search control—this paper proposes a novel dual-strategy enhanced ARO algorithm, termed DS-ARO. The proposed method integrates three complementary mechanisms: (1) a convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy based on current-to-pbest/1 to simultaneously enhance convergence pressure and population diversity; (2) an adaptive elite-guided search mechanism to improve the reliability of search directions and convergence stability through elite learning and adaptive perturbation control; and (3) a success-rate-based strategy selection mechanism to dynamically adjust the usage probability of different search strategies according to real-time optimization performance. To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm, extensive experiments are conducted on the CEC2017 (30-dimensional) and CEC2022 (10- and 20-dimensional) benchmark test suites, and compared with several state-of-the-art metaheuristic algorithms. Statistical analyses, including the Friedman test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test, are further employed to verify the significance of performance improvements. In addition, ablation studies are performed to quantify the contribution of each proposed strategy. The experimental results demonstrate that DS-ARO consistently achieves superior optimization accuracy, faster convergence speed, and stronger robustness compared with the competing algorithms. Furthermore, the proposed DS-ARO is applied to the hyperparameter optimization of the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) model for corporate bankruptcy prediction. Experimental results on the Wieslaw financial dataset show that the DS-ARO-KNN model outperforms traditional KNN and several mainstream machine learning models, achieving higher accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, while effectively reducing misclassification risks.

Introduction

With the rapid development of the digital economy and the increasing complexity of global financial markets, enterprises are facing highly dynamic market, policy, and capital environments. As a result, accurately predicting financial distress and bankruptcy risk has become a critical requirement for financial institutions in risk management, for investors in decision-making, and for enterprises in strategic adjustment of their operations1,2. Corporate bankruptcy prediction is essentially a binary classification problem based on multidimensional financial indicators. The modeling process involves several complex optimization stages, including feature selection, model selection, and hyperparameter optimization. Moreover, financial datasets typically exhibit characteristics such as high dimensionality, nonlinearity, nonconvexity, and imbalanced sample distributions3,4. Consequently, traditional deterministic optimization methods, which rely heavily on the mathematical properties of objective functions and possess limited global search capability, often struggle to achieve satisfactory modeling performance in such scenarios.

Meanwhile, in many engineering applications and scientific research fields—such as mechanical structure design, resource scheduling, deep learning parameter tuning, and wireless sensor network deployment—various high-dimensional, multimodal, and nonconvex optimization problems frequently arise. The solution spaces of these problems usually contain numerous local optima, which impose stringent requirements on optimization algorithms in terms of global exploration ability, local exploitation accuracy, and convergence stability5–7. Metaheuristic algorithms, inspired by biological swarm behaviors, physical phenomena, and social mechanisms observed in nature, have become mainstream tools for solving complex optimization problems due to their gradient-free characteristics, strong robustness, and simple implementation. These algorithms have been widely applied in fields such as financial engineering, intelligent manufacturing, and artificial intelligence.

Among various intelligent optimization techniques, swarm intelligence optimization algorithms have attracted extensive attention because they simulate the cooperative behavior of biological populations in nature8,9. Representative algorithms include Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)10, which simulates the foraging behavior of bird flocks; Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)11, inspired by the hunting strategy of wolf packs; Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO)12, which mimics the cooperative hunting strategy of hawks; and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)13. In addition, several recently proposed algorithms have also demonstrated promising performance, such as the Griffon Vultures Optimization Algorithm (GVOA)14, the Farthest Better or Nearest Worse Optimizer15, the Cuckoo Catfish Optimizer (CCO)16, the Secretary Bird Optimization Algorithm (SBOA)17, the Birds of Prey-Based Optimization (BPBO)18, the Graduate Student Evolutionary Algorithm (GSEA)19, Preschool Education Optimization Algorithm (PEOA)20, the Red-billed Blue Magpie Optimizer (RBMO)21, Bounty Hunter Optimizer (BHO)22, and the Sled Dog Optimizer (SDO)23. These algorithms employ population-based collaborative search mechanisms to continuously update individual positions within complex search spaces, thereby achieving a certain balance between global exploration and local exploitation. Owing to their simple structures, high computational efficiency, and wide applicability, these algorithms have been widely applied in engineering optimization24, path planning, energy scheduling, and machine learning25.

However, according to the No Free Lunch (NFL) theorem, no single optimization algorithm can consistently outperform all others across every optimization problem26. Therefore, researchers continuously attempt to improve existing algorithms from different perspectives to enhance their performance in complex optimization scenarios. For instance, some studies introduce chaotic mappings or differential evolution strategies to enhance the global exploration capability of algorithms27–29, while others improve convergence speed and stability through adaptive parameter control or multi-strategy fusion mechanisms30. Additionally, techniques such as elite learning mechanisms, Q-learning, historical information feedback, and multi-stage search strategies have also been employed to enhance search efficiency in high-dimensional complex problems31,32. These studies indicate that properly designed strategies and algorithmic hybridization can effectively improve the performance of swarm intelligence algorithms in solving complex optimization problems.

Recently, the Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO) algorithm33, a newly proposed swarm intelligence algorithm, has attracted increasing attention from researchers. The algorithm is inspired by the foraging and hiding behaviors of rabbits under predation pressure in nature. It simulates two behavioral mechanisms—detour foraging and random hiding—to balance exploration and exploitation during the search process. In ARO, each individual represents a “rabbit” whose position corresponds to a candidate solution, and the population evolves by continuously updating individual positions to search for the optimal solution. Due to its simple algorithmic structure and inherent stochastic search capability, ARO has achieved promising results in certain engineering optimization problems. However, when dealing with complex high-dimensional optimization problems, the original ARO algorithm still suffers from several limitations. For example, its search process mainly relies on randomly selected learning individuals and stochastic perturbations, lacking an explicit convergence guidance mechanism. Moreover, the perturbation scale is not effectively adjusted during different stages of the iteration process, resulting in excessive randomness in the later search stages and thus affecting convergence accuracy34–36. In addition, a single search strategy is often insufficient to adapt to complex and dynamic search environments, which may lead to slow convergence or premature stagnation in local optima when solving challenging optimization problems.

On the other hand, in practical application domains, corporate bankruptcy prediction has become an increasingly important research topic in financial risk management. Bankruptcy prediction is typically formulated as a binary classification problem aimed at determining whether a firm is likely to become bankrupt based on its financial indicators. Early studies mainly relied on statistical models for analysis. However, as data scale and complexity continue to increase, traditional statistical models have shown limitations in capturing nonlinear relationships within financial data. In recent years, machine learning methods have been widely introduced into bankruptcy prediction research. Algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)37, Random Forest (RF)38, Extreme Learning Machine (ELM)2, and Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT)39 have demonstrated strong classification capabilities in bankruptcy prediction tasks. Furthermore, with the rapid development of deep learning techniques, some studies have begun to employ deep neural network models to capture complex patterns in financial datasets40,41. Although these models can improve prediction accuracy to some extent, their performance is often highly dependent on the selection and optimization of model parameters. Consequently, employing intelligent optimization algorithms to optimize model parameters has become an important research direction.

Motivated by the above research background, this study proposes an improved Artificial Rabbits Optimization algorithm named DS-ARO and applies it to the hyperparameter optimization of the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classification model. By introducing a convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy, an adaptive elite-guided search strategy, and a success-rate-based adaptive strategy selection mechanism, the proposed algorithm can achieve a more effective balance between global exploration and local exploitation during the search process, thereby improving optimization efficiency and convergence accuracy. Subsequently, DS-ARO is applied to optimize the parameters of the KNN model to construct a DS-ARO-KNN corporate bankruptcy prediction model, and its effectiveness in financial risk prediction tasks is experimentally verified.

Despite the effectiveness of existing ARO variants, most improvements focus on single-strategy enhancement, lacking a systematic framework to coordinate multiple search mechanisms. In particular, three key challenges remain unresolved: (1) the absence of an explicit mechanism to balance convergence pressure and population diversity, (2) insufficient guidance from high-quality solutions during the search process, and (3) the lack of adaptive strategy selection mechanisms that can dynamically adjust search behaviors according to real-time performance feedback.

To address these limitations, this paper proposes a novel dual-strategy collaborative optimization framework, termed DS-ARO. The main innovation of this work lies in the integration of three complementary mechanisms: a convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy to regulate exploration–exploitation trade-off, an adaptive elite-guided search strategy to enhance directional learning reliability, and a success-rate-based strategy selection mechanism to dynamically allocate computational resources. These strategies are not only independently effective but also collaboratively improve the overall optimization performance in complex search environments.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, extensive numerical experiments are first conducted on the CEC2017 and CEC2022 benchmark functions, where the DS-ARO algorithm is compared with several classical and recently developed intelligent optimization algorithms. Experimental results demonstrate that DS-ARO exhibits significant advantages in terms of solution accuracy, convergence speed, and stability. Furthermore, the proposed algorithm is applied to the corporate bankruptcy prediction problem using real financial datasets. The results indicate that the KNN model optimized by DS-ARO outperforms several traditional machine learning models across multiple evaluation metrics.

Improved strategies in DS-ARO

Through the alternating execution of detour foraging and random hiding behaviors, ARO attempts to balance exploration and exploitation. However, since both behaviors rely heavily on random learning sources and stochastic perturbations, the algorithm lacks explicit convergence guidance and adaptive control over search intensity. As a result, ARO may exhibit unstable convergence behavior or premature stagnation when applied to complex, multimodal, or high-dimensional optimization problems.

These inherent limitations motivate the proposed DS-ARO, which introduces a convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy, a success-rate–based adaptive strategy selection mechanism, and an Adaptive Elite-Guided ARO Search to enhance robustness and optimization efficiency.

Current-to-pbest/1-based convergence-diversity balanced mutation strategy

In the standard ARO algorithm, individual updates mainly rely on random masks and perturbation terms, and the search direction lacks explicit convergence guidance. As a result, the algorithm may suffer from insufficient convergence speed or unstable search efficiency when solving complex, multimodal, or high-dimensional optimization problems.

To strengthen the convergence pressure during the search process while avoiding premature convergence to local optima, DS-ARO incorporates the current-to-pbest/1 mutation strategy inspired by differential evolution (DE). This strategy aims to dynamically balance convergence and population diversity.

Specifically, at the t-th iteration, the current population is first sorted in ascending order according to fitness values. Adaptive elite-guided ARO search (AEG-ARO)

Although the original ARO algorithm possesses strong stochastic exploration capability, its update process involves large perturbation scales and randomly selected learning sources. This may lead to unstable search behavior, especially in the middle and later stages of the optimization process, potentially affecting convergence accuracy.

To address this issue, DS-ARO proposes an Adaptive Elite-Guided ARO Search (AEG-ARO) strategy. This strategy preserves the original search framework of ARO while enhancing search effectiveness and stability through elite guidance and adaptive control mechanisms.

Success-rate-based adaptive strategy selection mechanism

A single search strategy often fails to maintain consistent superiority across different optimization stages or problem landscapes. To enhance the adaptability and robustness of the algorithm in complex optimization environments, DS-ARO constructs a success-rate–based adaptive dual-strategy selection mechanism, which dynamically allocates the usage probabilities of different strategies during the search process.

Based on the relative success rates of each strategy, the strategy selection probability vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbf{P}=[{P}_{1},{P}_{2}]$$\end{document} is adaptively updated according to:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{P}_{s}^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha\:)\cdot\:{P}_{s}^{\left(t\right)}+\alpha\:\cdot\:\frac{suc{c}_{s}}{\sum\:_{k=1}^{2}\:suc{c}_{k}}\end{array}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} is a smoothing factor, set to 0.2.

To prevent any strategy from being completely eliminated due to temporary inefficiency, DS-ARO imposes a lower bound constraint \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{s}\ge\:\epsilon\:$$\end{document} on the strategy probabilities. After updating, the probability vector is normalized.

Through this mechanism, the algorithm dynamically adjusts the frequency of strategy usage based on real-time feedback from the search process, allowing more effective strategies at the current stage to obtain more computational resources, thereby improving the overall optimization performance.

Complexity analysis

The computational complexity of the original Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO) algorithm is primarily determined by the population size N, problem dimension D, and maximum number of iterations T. During each iteration, the position update and fitness evaluation processes require \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(N\:\times\:\:D)$$\end{document} operations. Therefore, the overall time complexity of the standard ARO can be expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(T\:\times\:\:N\:\times\:\:D)$$\end{document}, which is typical for population-based metaheuristic algorithms.

In the proposed DS-ARO algorithm, additional operations are introduced, including elite selection, current-to-pbest mutation, and the success-rate-based strategy selection mechanism. Specifically, the sorting operation for elite selection incurs a cost of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(N\:log\:N\right)$$\end{document}, while the mutation and adaptive strategy update processes require \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(N\:\times\:\:D)\:$$\end{document}operations. Consequently, the overall time complexity of DS-ARO can be approximated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(T\:\times\:\:(N\:\times\:\:D\:+\:N\:log\:N\left)\right)$$\end{document}. Considering that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\:\times\:\:D$$\end{document} generally dominates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\:log\:N$$\end{document} in practical optimization scenarios, the overall complexity of DS-ARO remains approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O(T\:\times\:\:N\:\times\:\:D)$$\end{document}, which is consistent with that of the original ARO. This indicates that the proposed improvements do not significantly increase the computational burden while effectively enhancing optimization performance.

Figure 2 illustrates the convergence curves of the four algorithms on representative benchmark functions from the CEC2017 test suite. The original ARO algorithm exhibits evident premature convergence and search stagnation in the stage of the iteration process, characterized by a slow decrease in fitness values and ultimately low convergence accuracy. By contrast, the variants incorporating a single strategy, namely ARO + MS and ARO + AEG, both demonstrate improved convergence performance compared to the original ARO. Specifically, ARO + MS enhances the convergence pressure through an elite-guided mutation mechanism, resulting in a faster fitness decline during the early iterations. Meanwhile, ARO + AEG improves the reliability of the search direction by leveraging adaptive perturbation and elite learning mechanisms, leading to reduced oscillations and stronger stability in the later stages. Notably, the complete DS-ARO consistently achieves the best convergence behavior across all test functions: it exhibits a rapid decrease in fitness values during the early iterations, maintains a steady and continuous downward trend without noticeable fluctuations in the middle stage, and ultimately converges to the lowest fitness value among all four algorithms. These results clearly indicate that the collaborative integration of the three strategies effectively balances global exploration and local exploitation, thereby significantly enhancing both convergence speed and solution accuracy.

Figure 3 presents the average ranking results of the four algorithms on all CEC2017 benchmark functions at 30 dimensions, where a smaller ranking value indicates better overall performance. The average rankings of the four algorithms, from worst to best, are as follows: ARO (3.83) > ARO+MS (2.93) > ARO+AEG (2.13) > DS-ARO (1.10). The original ARO obtains the highest average rank, indicating the weakest overall optimization performance. After incorporating the convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy, the average rank of ARO + MS decreases to 2.93, reflecting a clear performance improvement. With the further introduction of the adaptive elite-guided search strategy, ARO + AEG achieves an improved average rank of 2.13, demonstrating that this strategy significantly enhances both search stability and directional reliability. In contrast, DS-ARO, which integrates all proposed strategies, achieves a substantially lower average rank of 1.10, markedly outperforming the other three variants and attaining the best overall performance. These statistical results convincingly demonstrate that each of the three core strategies plays an indispensable role in performance enhancement. Their synergistic integration enables DS-ARO to overcome the inherent limitations of the original ARO, exhibiting superior stability, robustness, and optimization efficiency in complex high-dimensional optimization problems.

Performance comparison on the CEC2017 benchmark in 30 dimensions.

Table 2 presents the numerical experimental results of all algorithms on the 30-dimensional CEC2017 benchmark functions. The mean value reflects the solution accuracy of an algorithm, whereas the standard deviation indicates its robustness, with smaller values representing better performance. From the perspective of overall solution accuracy, DS-ARO achieves the best mean values on the majority of benchmark functions, demonstrating significant performance advantages. For the unimodal functions F1–F2, the mean values obtained by DS-ARO are 1.9520E + 03 and 2.3350E + 13, respectively, which are substantially lower than those of the other compared algorithms. Compared with the second-best algorithm RLTARO, the reductions reach 47.0% and 95.6%, respectively, indicating strong global optimization capability. For the multimodal functions F5–F9 and F14–F19, the superiority of DS-ARO becomes even more prominent. For instance, on F9, DS-ARO achieves a mean value of 9.1542E + 02, representing a 67.7% reduction compared with ARO, while on F19, the mean value is 2.0028E + 03, which is 71.6% lower than the second-best RLTARO. These results demonstrate that DS-ARO can effectively escape local optima and accurately locate the global optimum in highly multimodal landscapes. For the hybrid and composition functions F20–F30, DS-ARO still maintains superior solution accuracy. For example, the mean values on F21, F24, and F27 are 2.3752E + 03, 2.9001E + 03, and 3.2095E + 03, respectively, all of which are the smallest among the compared algorithms, verifying its adaptability to complex composite optimization problems.

In terms of robustness, DS-ARO achieves the best or second-best standard deviation values on all test functions, with values significantly smaller than those of other algorithms. For example, the standard deviations for F6, F8, and F25 are 1.3452E-02, 8.7869E + 00, and 6.8726E + 00, respectively, which are one or more orders of magnitude lower than those of other algorithms. This indicates that DS-ARO maintains stable performance across multiple independent runs and is less affected by random factors. Although DS-ARO does not achieve the best mean value on a few functions (such as F3 and F10), it still maintains relatively low standard deviations, demonstrating strong stability. The deviation observed on these functions also suggests that there remains some room for further refinement of local exploitation in certain unimodal and hybrid optimization problems.

Comparison of convergence speeds of various algorithms on the CEC2017 benchmark functions.

Figure 4 illustrates the convergence curves of representative functions in the 30-dimensional CEC2017 benchmark suite, which intuitively reflect the decreasing trend of the objective function values and the convergence efficiency of each algorithm during the iterative process. Overall, the convergence curves of DS-ARO consistently lie at the lowest position across all representative functions, and its convergence speed is significantly faster than that of the other algorithms, exhibiting the characteristics of “rapid descent, early convergence, and high precision.” For unimodal and composition functions such as F1, F12, and F30, the objective function value of DS-ARO decreases rapidly during the early iterations (within the first 50 iterations), becoming substantially lower than those of other algorithms at the same stage. The algorithm typically approaches convergence around 200 iterations, after which the solution accuracy continues to improve slightly until the global optimum is finally reached. For multimodal and hybrid functions such as F9, F18, and F27, other algorithms often exhibit premature stagnation or oscillatory convergence, whereas the convergence curve of DS-ARO maintains a smooth downward trend without significant fluctuations. This indicates that the proposed convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy and adaptive elite-guided search strategy effectively balance global exploration and local exploitation, preventing the algorithm from being trapped in local optima and enabling continuous optimization and stable convergence.

Compared with other improved algorithms in the ARO family (such as IMARO and RLTARO), the convergence advantage of DS-ARO is also evident. The original ARO and IMARO tend to exhibit premature stagnation in the middle stage of iterations, making it difficult for the objective function value to further decrease. Although RLTARO shows certain improvements, both its convergence speed and final solution accuracy remain inferior to those of DS-ARO. These results indicate that the adaptive strategy selection mechanism and elite-guided search mechanism proposed in DS-ARO effectively compensate for the shortcomings of the original ARO algorithm, such as lack of guidance in search direction and fixed perturbation scale, thereby significantly improving convergence efficiency and optimization accuracy. Furthermore, for low-dimensional composition functions such as F21 and F28, DS-ARO still maintains a slow but continuous optimization trend in the later stages of convergence, further verifying the effectiveness of its local exploitation capability and ensuring that the algorithm can continue refining the optimal solution after convergence.

In summary, both the numerical results and the convergence curves on the 30-dimensional CEC2017 benchmark test suite demonstrate that the proposed DS-ARO algorithm, through the integration of convergence–diversity balanced mutation, adaptive elite-guided search, and a success-rate-based strategy selection mechanism, effectively enhances global exploration capability, local exploitation accuracy, and convergence stability. Consequently, DS-ARO exhibits superior overall performance compared with existing representative intelligent optimization algorithms when solving complex high-dimensional optimization problems.

Corporate bankruptcy prediction can be formulated as a binary classification problem in which a learning model aims to distinguish financially distressed companies from healthy ones based on their financial indicators. Suppose the dataset contains N firms, each represented by a d-dimensional financial feature vector. Optimization strategy for KNN hyperparameter selection

Although KNN is conceptually simple, its predictive performance is highly sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly the number of neighbors K and the distance metric. Improper parameter settings may lead to unstable decision boundaries or reduced classification accuracy. To overcome this limitation, this study employs swarm-based optimization algorithms to automatically search for the optimal parameter combination.

In the optimization process, each candidate solution is represented by a two-dimensional vector X=(K,d), where K denotes the number of nearest neighbors and d represents the index of the distance metric.

The search space is defined as:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}K\in\:\left[\mathrm{1,20}\right],d\in\:\left[\mathrm{1,4}\right] \end{array}$$\end{document} where the four distance indices correspond to Euclidean, Cityblock, Chebyshev, and Minkowski distances, respectively.

The optimization algorithms iteratively update a population of candidate solutions to explore the parameter space. During each iteration, the fitness of each candidate solution is evaluated by training a KNN classifier using the corresponding parameter configuration and measuring its prediction performance.

To ensure reliable evaluation and avoid overfitting, a k-fold cross-validation strategy is employed. In this work, k=10 is used. The dataset is randomly partitioned into ten equal subsets. In each fold, nine subsets are used for training and the remaining subset is used for testing. The process is repeated ten times so that every sample is used once for testing.

The overall classification accuracy is computed as:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}ACC=\frac{1}{k}\sum\:_{i=1}^{k}\:AC{C}_{i} \end{array}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:AC{C}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the classification accuracy obtained in the i-th fold.

Since most optimization algorithms are formulated as minimization problems, the fitness function is defined as:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}Fitness=1-ACC \end{array}$$\end{document}

Therefore, minimizing the fitness value corresponds to maximizing the classification accuracy.

Through iterative evolution, the optimization algorithm gradually improves the candidate solutions and eventually obtains the optimal parameter combination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:({K}^{*},{d}^{*})$$\end{document}, which is then used to construct the final KNN bankruptcy prediction model.

Conclusion and future work

This study addresses several limitations of the Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO) algorithm when solving complex optimization problems, including slow convergence speed, insufficient search direction guidance, and susceptibility to local optima. To overcome these issues, an improved algorithm named DS-ARO is proposed. The proposed method systematically enhances the original ARO algorithm by integrating multiple cooperative optimization strategies. First, a current-to-pbest/1-based convergence–diversity balanced mutation strategy is introduced. This strategy strengthens convergence pressure while maintaining population diversity, thereby improving the global exploration capability of the algorithm. Second, an adaptive elite-guided search strategy is proposed, which enhances search directionality and stability through an elite learning mechanism and an adaptive perturbation control mechanism. Finally, a success-rate-based adaptive strategy selection mechanism is constructed, allowing different search strategies to dynamically adjust their selection probabilities according to their real-time performance during the optimization process. This mechanism enables a more effective balance between global exploration and local exploitation.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm, extensive numerical experiments were conducted on the CEC2017 and CEC2022 benchmark functions, and the results were compared with several classical and recently developed intelligent optimization algorithms. The experimental results demonstrate that DS-ARO achieves higher solution accuracy on most benchmark functions and exhibits superior stability and robustness across multiple independent runs. Furthermore, the Friedman statistical test was employed to evaluate the overall performance of the algorithms. The results show that DS-ARO consistently ranks first under different dimensional settings and benchmark environments, further confirming its superior optimization capability in complex optimization problems.

In terms of practical applications, the proposed DS-ARO algorithm was applied to the hyperparameter optimization of the K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) classification model, and a DS-ARO-KNN corporate bankruptcy prediction model was constructed. Experimental results on the Wieslaw financial dataset indicate that the optimized KNN model achieves significant improvements in accuracy, recall, and F1-score, particularly demonstrating stronger capability in identifying bankrupt enterprises. These results indicate that DS-ARO can effectively improve the performance of numerical optimization tasks and also shows strong potential in machine learning hyperparameter optimization and real-world prediction problems.

Despite the promising results achieved in this study, the proposed DS-ARO still exhibits several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the bankruptcy prediction experiments were conducted on a single financial dataset (Wieslaw dataset), which may restrict the generalizability of the obtained conclusions. Moreover, the current study evaluates the proposed model only on this single dataset, further limiting the generalization capability of the findings. Second, the experimental validation is primarily based on the KNN classifier, and the performance of DS-ARO has not yet been comprehensively assessed across a broader range of machine learning and deep learning models. In addition, although the incorporated enhancement strategies improve the overall optimization capability, they inevitably introduce a certain degree of computational overhead, and the algorithm may exhibit sensitivity to parameter settings in certain scenarios. Furthermore, in high-dimensional optimization problems, the computational efficiency of DS-ARO still has room for improvement.

Future research can be extended in several directions. First, more large-scale and cross-domain financial datasets, as well as additional public benchmarks, will be considered to further verify the robustness and general applicability of the proposed method. Second, DS-ARO can be employed for hyperparameter optimization of various machine learning models, such as support vector machines, ensemble learning approaches, and deep neural networks, to further explore its potential in data-driven applications. Third, integrating feature selection or dimensionality reduction techniques with DS-ARO may further enhance prediction performance when handling high-dimensional financial data. Finally, parallel computing or distributed optimization strategies can be introduced to accelerate the DS-ARO algorithm, thereby improving its computational efficiency and scalability for large-scale optimization problems.

Overall, the proposed DS-ARO algorithm demonstrates strong performance in both complex optimization problems and machine learning hyperparameter optimization tasks, providing a promising approach for applying swarm intelligence optimization methods to practical problems such as financial risk prediction and decision support systems.
