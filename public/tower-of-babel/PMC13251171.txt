Partnering with people with lived experience in eating disorder research: conceptual and practical considerations
Authors: Rosiel Elwyn, Kai Schweizer, Laurence Cobbaert, Alana Gall, Zyana Gall, Mia L Pellizzer, Jeannie Park, Matthew Jackman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcross medical and mental health research, lived experience (LE) collaboration and partnership is increasingly being prioritised and embedded into research methods and considered as an essential ethical approach. In eating disorder (ED) research, however, LE involvement is still emerging, and ED researchers have identified a need for guidance on ethical collaboration with people with LE. Written by a team of LE researchers and advocates, this article outlines core principles for ethical and meaningful partnership for people with ED in research, including: (1) Recognise lived experience as expertise, (2) Share power and decision-making, (3) Support autonomy, readiness, and dignity of risk, (4) Clarify roles, expectations, and disclosure choices, (5) Provide fair remuneration, authorship, and recognition, (6) Build emotionally safe and sustainable ways of working, (7) Practice reflexivity and transparency, (8) Ensure mutual benefit and capacity building. Conceptual clarification is also provided regarding types of LE involvement in ED research, with examples (LE-led research, co-production, co-design, consultation). Using the core principles, practical guidance is provided for clarifying roles and expectations, supporting disclosure choices, planning remuneration and authorship, developing emotionally safe ways of working, and addressing institutional barriers to meaningful partnership. This article aims to support the ED research community in shared learning and growth opportunities for ethical collaboration with people with LE in ED research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01670-2.

Background

In the context of mental health research, lived experience refers to the knowledge of mental health concerns gained from personal experience [1]. Increasingly, national research bodies, including Australia’s National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) and the United Kingdom’s Medical Research Council, recommend LE involvement in every aspect and phase of health and medical research [2, 3]. Despite this, Lived Experience involvement in eating disorder1(ED) research has been limited to date [4–7]. The purpose of ED research is not only to produce knowledge, but to improve prevention, treatment, and quality of life for people living with ED. This cannot be meaningfully achieved without the involvement of those intended to benefit from this research. This requires attention to not only whether Lived Experience partners are included, but also whose knowledge is centred, how power is shared, and whether research frameworks are meaningful for diverse communities. Current barriers in the ED field may not reflect a lack of willingness, but rather a desire for clearer guidance on ensuring ethical collaboration [8]. Therefore, the purpose of this Lived Experience-led commentary is to provide conceptual clarification and practical guidance for ethical Lived Experience partnership in ED research, drawing on the authors’ collective experiential knowledge, advocacy, professional experience, and critical engagement with relevant literature.

Methods

Aims

This article aimed to develop a Lived Experience-led framework to guide ethical and meaningful partnership with people with Lived Experience in ED research. Specifically, the article sought to: (1) clarify different forms of Lived Experience involvement in ED research, including Lived Experience-led research, co-production, co-design, advisory and governance roles, and consultation; (2) identify core principles for ethical Lived Experience partnership; and (3) provide practical considerations to support researchers in planning, conducting, and sustaining Lived Experience partnership across ED research contexts.

Approach

This article used a conceptual and interpretive approach. Rather than involving participant recruitment or data collection at a single clinical or research site, the framework was developed by an interdisciplinary authorship team with lived, professional, research, policy, advocacy, governance, and peer support expertise. It was informed by the authors’ collective experiential knowledge and purposively selected peer-reviewed and grey literature relevant to Lived Experience involvement, co-production, co-design, epistemic justice, disability justice, First Nations Peoples’ health and wellbeing, ED research, neurodivergence research, Mad Studies, and mental health research. Literature was selected to support conceptual clarification, ethical analysis, methodological development, and practice recommendations.

Framework development and analysis

The framework was developed through an iterative conceptual and interpretive process that brought the authors’ collective expertise into dialogue with relevant literature and examples of Lived Experience involvement in ED and mental health research. This process focused on recurring ethical, conceptual, and practical issues, including epistemic justice, power-sharing, dignity of risk, readiness, disclosure, role clarity, remuneration, authorship, emotional labour, reflexivity, sustainability, mutual benefit, and capacity building. These considerations were organised into eight core principles: (1) recognise Lived Experience as expertise; (2) share power and decision-making; (3) support autonomy, readiness, and dignity of risk; (4) clarify roles, expectations, and disclosure choices; (5) provide fair remuneration, authorship, and recognition; (6) build emotionally safe and sustainable ways of working; (7) practice reflexivity and transparency; and (8) ensure mutual benefit and capacity building. Practical resources were then developed to support application of the framework, including a typology of Lived Experience involvement in ED research, examples of how different forms of involvement may be enacted, and suggested steps for researchers considering Lived Experience partnership.

Benefits of LE involvement in ED research

Lived Experience involvement in ED research can strengthen the relevance, quality, and impact of research by ensuring that research questions, methods, outcomes, and interpretations are informed by the people most affected by the work. Lived Experience partners can help identify priorities that may be overlooked when research is shaped primarily by clinical, academic, or service-system perspectives, including priorities related to treatment acceptability, access, identity, embodiment, safety, recovery, and quality of life [8, 9, 13, 18, 20, 33, 46–48]. In this way, Lived Experience involvement may broaden the forms of knowledge that inform ED research, challenge narrow or taken-for-granted assumptions, and support the development of research questions and outcomes that are meaningful, relevant, and practically useful for people living with EDs [10, 14, 28]. By directing attention toward questions, materials, and resources that are more likely to be useful and acceptable, Lived Experience involvement may also reduce research waste and support more responsible use of funding, time, and participant labour [8, 36, 38, 47, 49–52].

Lived Experience involvement can also strengthen study design, conduct, and interpretation. Partners with Lived Experience may provide insight into how recruitment materials, consent processes, study procedures, interview questions, measures, interventions, and dissemination materials are likely to be experienced by participants [14, 31, 42, 45, 47, 53]. This can help research teams identify potential barriers to recruitment, participation, retention, and engagement, and can support more sensitive, accessible, and contextually appropriate approaches to study design [8, 31, 36, 42]. Lived Experience partners may also contribute to richer interpretation of findings by identifying gaps, patterns, tensions, and plausible explanations that may not be visible through academic or clinical interpretation alone [14, 31, 32, 42, 53, 54]. This is particularly important in ED research, where findings may be interpreted differently depending on assumptions about diagnosis, recovery, risk, embodiment, neurodivergence, culture, trauma, weight stigma, gender, and systems of care.

Finally, Lived Experience involvement may improve implementation, acceptability, and knowledge translation by helping research teams consider how findings will be received, used, and communicated by the communities they are intended to benefit. Lived Experience partners can support translation of findings into accessible, sensitive, and practically meaningful language, and can help develop outputs beyond peer-reviewed publications, such as community summaries, resources, videos, training materials, policy briefings, and practice recommendations [8, 20, 42, 55, 56]. Involvement may also support trust, epistemic justice, autonomy, empowerment, and healing when it is undertaken ethically and with appropriate attention to power, recognition, remuneration, emotional labour, and mutual benefit [8, 54, 57–59]. These benefits should not be assumed to occur simply because Lived Experience contributors are present; rather, they depend on whether involvement is meaningful, adequately supported, appropriately recognised, and able to influence the research process.

It is also important to note that the benefits of Lived Experience involvement in research cannot be measured, quantified, or made to fit within already existing neolibertarian frameworks. Lived Experience is deeply personal and relational, and Lived Experience work has roots in political and social activism connected with epistemic and social injustice. The need for Lived Experience involvement in ED research reflects ethical and epistemic justice values, and its impact reflects historical injustice in knowledge systems, stigma and discrimination, social and systemic power imbalances, and ongoing need for restorative practices across multiple domains; these impacts cannot be captured in quantified measures of research methodology, collaboration, or output.

What meaningful involvement looks like

Lived Experience involvement in research can occur in many forms, with varying levels of engagement including consultation, advisory groups, co-design, and co-production. All of these forms of involvement can be valuable when they are appropriately and ethically undertaken. However, they differ in terms of levels of Lived Experience decision-making, influence, and recognition. With this in mind, researchers should carefully consider which method/s are best suited to their project and can be ethically undertaken with appropriate levels of support, recognition, and remuneration. Table 1 provides some common examples of Lived Experience involvement in research, with examples of how this may look in the context of ED research. A key factor to approaching Lived Experience partnership while respecting these different forms of Lived Experience involvement is transparency; researchers should become familiar with the different forms of involvement, communicating with potential Lived Experience partners about the intended roles, recognition, and remuneration, and logistics involved for the project, and avoid overstating the level of involvement or influence being offered.

While these factors can sound daunting from the outset, as with any learning process, many Lived Experience partners understand the need to learn and discover together, and to be able to make mistakes and learn from them. Transparency and open communication are key to the process (see Sect. "Build emotionally safe and sustainable ways of working"). A ‘flattened, equal, power’ dynamic that recognises mutual expertise acknowledges that everyone is learning through the process and that the process is imperfect - this can be particularly meaningful for PWLE of EDs.

ED-specific considerations

Lived Experience partnership in ED research occurs within a context shaped by stigma, paternalism, and entrenched epistemic hierarchies (hierarchies of what is perceived as valued and credible knowledge). Within mental health research broadly, people with Lived Experience are frequently positioned as vulnerable subjects rather than credible knowers, leading to their exclusion or marginalisation in research design and decision-making [60–62]. In ED contexts, these dynamics are often intensified due to heightened concerns about risk and safety, which can reinforce paternalistic practices and limit meaningful involvement. Such approaches risk reproducing testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, where Lived Experience knowledge is discounted and discredited or rendered unintelligible within dominant research frameworks [9, 10, 57, 63–65].

Core constructs within ED research, including diagnosis and recovery, are also contested. ED research has historically been shaped by biomedical and cognitive-behavioural frameworks, which have defined illness, treatment, and recovery in relatively narrow ways [11]. However, Lived Experience research demonstrates that individuals may conceptualise eating distress through alternative frameworks, including socio-cultural, intersectional, neurodivergent, and trauma-informed perspectives [10, 11]. Recovery, in particular, is not a universally agreed construct; it may be understood in terms of meaning, autonomy, or quality of life rather than symptom improvement and/or elimination (see 1.6. Reflexivity and Transparency: Conceptualisations of recovery) [11, 12, 66]. These differences reflect fundamentally distinct epistemological positions, requiring researchers to engage with, rather than resolve, conceptual and epistemic plurality.

Conceptual and epistemic plurality also requires attention to the structure, cultural, and community contexts that shape eating distress, help-seeking, recovery, and research participation. This includes considering how racism, colonisation, cultural disconnection, food insecurity, intergenerational trauma, health system mistrust, and other social determinants may shape experiences of EDs and the meanings attached to them [13, 46, 67]. For First Nations peoples, eating distress may need to be understood in relation to holistic understandings of health and wellbeing [68], including social and emotional wellbeing, connection to Country, kinship, culture, community, spirituality, and systemic determinants of health. Lived Experience partnership in ED research should therefore avoid assuming that dominant ED concepts, language, and recovery models are universally meaningful, acceptable, or culturally safe.

Finally, there is a risk of privileging only certain Lived Experience narratives within ED research. Research partnerships may unintentionally favour perspectives that align with dominant clinical paradigms or are perceived as more acceptable within status quo-reinforcing academic contexts. This can exclude more complex, critical, or marginalised experiences, including those shaped by intersectionality [10, 14, 20]. Evidence from Lived Experience-informed ED research highlights the importance of including diverse and underrepresented perspectives to avoid narrowing the scope of knowledge production [5, 15, 44, 57, 63]. It is important for researchers to consider these factors in LE partnerships - whose voices may be missing from the table? What parameters are being intentionally or unintentionally placed on how lived experience is shared, or influencing the lens in which it is understood? How might these parameters influence who can share their lived experience and in what ways they can share it? Acknowledging limitations, biases and challenges to partnership with Lived Experience partnership is important (for example, if there is only funding for one Lived Experience researcher or advisor on a project, or budget constraints). As part of committing to values that support Lived Experience involvement in ED research, it is important to consider impact on a Lived Experience partner when involved in a project when circumstances are less than ideal. Even if the Lived Experience partner consents to involvement, it is essential for them to hear acknowledgement that this needs to change. A first step in restorative justice is acknowledgement. While epistemic justice and institutional change takes time, authentic allyship requires action, demand for change, support, values commitment, and acknowledgement.

Recognise lived experience as expertise (epistemic justice)

Epistemic justice concerns fairness in people’s capacity as knowers, including how credibility is assigned and how shared meaning is produced in social contexts [69]. In mental health research and practice, these dynamics shape whose voices are heard, what knowledge is legitimised, and how findings are interpreted and applied [60–62].

Experiential knowledge offers distinct, situated, and embodied insights that cannot be replicated through education and training alone [70, 71]. Across mental health and ED research and practice, people with lived experience (PWLE) have historically been systemically discredited and excluded as knowers due to stigma, credibility undermining, and dominant biomedical frameworks [60, 61, 72]. This has constrained which concepts, questions, and outcomes are prioritised, and produced gaps in understanding the experiences, treatment, and outcomes of those directly affected [10, 11, 44, 63]; reflecting which knowledge has been legitimised within research systems [5, 57, 62].

Epistemic justice requires researchers to examine how credibility is allocated and to recognise Lived Experience contributors as legitimate knowers whose insights shape research design, interpretation, and translation [57, 61, 73]. Treating Lived Experience knowledge as requiring academic validation reinforces epistemic hierarchies and limits meaningful involvement [57, 60, 62, 74]. It is important to engage in reflexive practice to recognise and challenge assumptions about different forms of expertise [74, 75]. While consultation is also a valid form of Lived Experience engagement, it is also important to move beyond consultation toward involvement that carries genuine influence, particularly given evidence that current involvement in ED research remains inconsistent in depth, reporting, and reflexivity [5].

Inclusion alone does not guarantee epistemic justice. Risks such as ‘elite/narrow capture’ and epistemic exploitation (where only certain voices are included - such as individuals at a certain state of recovery, or where Lived Experience contributors are expected to share personal experiences without corresponding influence) demonstrate how existing inequities can persist if underlying hierarchies remain unchanged [61, 62, 72]. It is therefore important to consider Lived Experience partnerships with people who may have experience in research, and how to reach additional voices. Can existing partnerships with PWLE who have research experience support this reach (i.e., through co-produced recruitment efforts, community engagement and relationships, collaborative mentorship)?

In many contexts, ED research has limited infrastructure to support Lived Experience involvement, particularly in leadership or decision-making roles [12, 57, 66]. Stigma, lack of funding, and unsupportive research cultures restrict opportunities for meaningful engagement and reinforce epistemic exclusion [5, 57, 62]. A precursor to enable Lived Experience involvement may first require building enabling conditions including supportive infrastructures, collaborative cultures, and pathways for Lived Experience leadership and partnership [76–78]. The readiness of an organisation and its leaders to embrace change, a shift in traditional power dynamics, willingness to meaningfully address structural inequalities in research, and take on a trauma-informed approach are important factors [74, 79, 80]. The development of infastructure for ongoing Lived Experience partnership can be supported by organisational and worker strategic vision and commitment [79] (including the value of lived experience knowledge and leadership and co-development of a standard operating procedure for ethical partnership). Advocating for training on the value of Lived Experience research may support organisational and value shifts [81]. Supportive relationships and providing space and support for PWLE to build capacity in research and mentoring skills are also key [79, 80]. Are there opportunities to discuss the development of Lived Experience institutional roles (i.e., Lived Experience Engagement Advisory Committee; Lived Experience Adjunct Academic/Researcher)? When applying for grants, what opportunities exist to invite Lived Experience partners to co-lead applications, co-produce and co-design research as co-investigators or contribute as associate investigators? Requesting training and resources for skills development in co-production and co-design can put Lived Experience partnership at the forefront of institutional priorities for professional development. Requesting that universities or institutions include training and resources on co-production and co-design within their resource libraries can also support organisational shifts toward infrastructure development. International collaboration and partnerships may also help support shared learning, building resources, infrastructure and develop capacity in settings where Lived Experience involvement in ED research is less established. For PWLE from countries where Lived Experience collaboration is less recognised, having Lived Experience research partners acting in research mentor roles may provide support for inclusion.

Embedding epistemic justice in ED research therefore involves recognising Lived Experience as authoritative knowledge, critically examining how power operates within research processes, redistributing epistemic authority within research, and ensuring involvement translates into meaningful influence rather than symbolic inclusion [5, 61, 62]. Co‑production can support this when involving meaningful power‑sharing across research stages, including agenda setting, design, analysis, and dissemination [4, 5, 25]. However, these approaches require structural support, including time, funding, and institutional commitment, and cannot be assumed to occur without deliberate planning [25, 62]. The principle of epistemic justice underpins subsequent sections on dignity of risk, role clarity, remuneration, and reflexivity; which addresses dimensions of how epistemic justice can be enacted within research partnerships.

Support autonomy, readiness, and dignity of risk

Dignity of Risk to be involved in research should be considered as a foundational research principle. Dignity of Risk ‘the principle of allowing an individual the dignity afforded by risk-taking, with subsequent enhancement of personal growth and quality of life’ [82](p. 189) is based on the value of respect and the right to self-determination, including that all human endeavour involves risk and potential for harm, benefit, growth, success, and failure. As part of addressing epistemic injustice, PWLE of EDs must be recognised as experts in their own risk, capacity, and readiness for research engagement, rather than being reduced to wholly vulnerable subjects that require researcher and clinician protection. Readiness should also be understood in relation to the Lived Experience partner’s broader context, including cultural obligations, community relationships, prior experiences of research or health systems, and the potential personal, professional, or relational impacts of involvement. In order to move beyond safety management by researchers-by-training toward meaningful inclusion of Lived Experience-led, co-produced and co-designed research in ED literature, dignity of risk must be understood as an ethical imperative, whereby collaborative person-led assessment of risk and readiness is utilised as a foundation principle rather than a practical consideration. Professional concerns for safety and ethical care can lead to risk aversion [8], however this can contribute to epistemic injustice by excluding Lived Experience voices from research. It is important, therefore, that perceived or feared safety risk is not conflated with actual risk, particularly where stigma and paternalism may be conflating risk aversion. Further, researchers may be more anxious for the safety of Lived Experience partners due to not having enough training or infrastructure support to partner with Lived Experience collaborators [76], becoming more risk averse and concerned about liability - this reflects a structural issue, rather than the vulnerability of the Lived Experience partners themselves.

It is equally important to acknowledge that needs for safety and safety planning as part of all research partnerships, and specific support needs that may be required in Lived Experience partnership. Thus far, this is inconsistently applied. In the context of intervention studies that include Lived Experience mentoring, a systematic review of 11 studies found only 3 assessed mentor outcomes [83]. Of those that did, no significant increases in ED psychopathology, quality of life, or mood were found [83] highlighting that from a clinical perspective, people with Lived Experience of EDs can safely participate in this type of research. Subsequent research has since supported this (e.g., [84]). Studies included in the review varied considerably in the level of training and supervision provided to Lived Experience mentors, and reporting of remuneration; it was recommended future studies ensure adequate training, supervision, reimbursement, and supervision are provided [83]. However, although these studies assessed potential clinical impacts, the potential for Lived Experience involvement to support empowerment, healing and recovery processes has been less explored [54, 58, 59]. Critically, it is important that Lived Experience experiential knowledge is not viewed solely from a pathologising framework, and Lived Experience partners are not positioned from a paternalistic lens. When approaching Lived Experience partners, Dignity of Risk and readiness to be involved in research as key principles can inform the approach to respect, safety, and support.

It may be helpful to discuss with Lived Experience partners possible approaches for their role should they become unwell or need to step back from the project; this can prevent feelings of uncertainty, fear of stigma [8], distress and heightened feelings of vulnerability regarding disclosure should the need for support arise. As an example from one of the authors’ own experience, during a project, one of the authors (a researcher with Lived Experience) was uncertain about disclosing having elected to go to hospital for refeeding, due to concern about stigma and worry that they would be perceived as impaired and incapable of continuing with their role; they also did not want malnutrition effects (i.e., brain fog) or the emotional recency of treatment to impact their contribution to data collection (i.e., co-facilitating participant interviews) or later thematic analysis. Other members of the research team were understanding and discussed checking in over email. Together they agreed on the researcher with Lived Experience stepping back from data collection, contributing to the later thematic analysis when discharged, and reviewing literature if capacity allowed. The team held no expectation of the researcher with Lived Experience continuing work during treatment, however the researcher with Lived Experience worked on reviewing literature in the meantime during inaptient treatment, as they found this helpful - other team members could review or edit this work if needed, and when discharged, contributed to thematic analysis and broader manuscript writing. Project timelines may not always afford the flexibility for a Lived Experience partner to continue with their original role if they become unwell or need to step back; therefore discussing different avenues for contribution at the outset and agreeing on contingency plans can support flexible engagement. For example, contributing to analysis vs. sense-checking of thematic analysis or synthesis of results.

Supporting Readiness to be Involved in Research through Co-assessment

Co-assessment means that the Lived Experience individual who is considering research partnership’s own assessment of their readiness to be involved in research carries equal or greater weight than the assessment of other researcher team members. Readiness is commonly assessed by gatekeepers (i.e., ethics committees, clinicians, researchers-by-training) rather than genuinely negotiated. In order to deconstruct these epistemic barriers, research methods should explicitly utilise co-assessed readiness, and if the researcher and Lived Experience partner disagree about readiness, the Lived Experience partner’s assessment of readiness should be considered primary. In a survey of PWLE of ED, clinicians and researchers by Neill and colleagues [8], there was agreement that individuals with Lived Experience and researchers should co-assess readiness to be engaged in research and co-monitor the individual with Lived Experience’s wellbeing throughout their involvement in the research project [8]. In the survey by Neil et al. [8], concerns were raised about illness stage or recovery level and research involvement, and stability and support; however no specific solutions were proposed, highlighting the complexity with these tensions. Recovery from an ED is difficult to define, and there is no clear consensus on ED recovery; further, Lived Experience recovery definitions may differ from clinical definitions, and research on recovery has highlighted these limitations [11, 85, 86]. This illustrates epistemic issues in requiring recovery for Lived Experience engagement in research; whose definitions of recovery should be prioritised? Which voices may be excluded if some definitions and recovery durations are required? For instance, in the systematic review by Pellizzer and Wade [83] all 11 included studies required Lived Experience mentors to be recovered from their ED, with varying guidelines of how long recovered a Lived Experience mentor should be prior to participating. This illustrates arbitrariness and gatekeeping for Lived Experience participation; which leads to exclusion. As part of collaborative assessment of readiness, important factors for research engagement should be discussed. Such as the Lived Experience partner considering potential impacts of involvement and visibility in the work; being identified as a peer by other people with lived experience; the potential disclosure of ED by others due to peer recognition; and the types of lived experience knowledge they draw on or disclose (the emotional impact, identity and relationship considerations, and considerations for navigating systems and life domains such as employment and housing).

When considering dignity of risk, safety considerations, and ethics committee requirements, there are a number of approaches to balancing safety and readiness for involvement - these considerations should be discussed with Lived Experience partners and other research team members. The Roses in the Ocean reflective tool for readiness to be involved in suicide prevention [87] has been used in research with PWLE of suicide [47, 88, 89]. Other approaches have included the preparation of a Wellness Plan [90, 91], prohibiting mention of weight and BMI in conversation [91], and potentially involving treatment teams and carers in the support of PWLE involved in research, although how to engage treatment teams and carers and what to do if external support is not available was not explicitly suggested [8]. Others included no specific requirements for Lived Experience involvement in sensitive research [92]. In order to support potential Lived Experience partners with self-assessment of readiness for involvement in ED research, the Roses in the Ocean reflective tool could be adapted for ED research contexts [87]. Alternative or additional options include Wellness Plans, health practitioner engagement, and emergency contacts; however it’s also important to consider that Lived Experience partners should be afforded the autonomy to decide how and when they can contribute to research and if it is safe for them to do so. A co-assessment of readiness could also look like a meeting with a member or members of the research team and shared discussion of type/s of Lived Experience engagement in the project and considerations for any meaning and life impacts.

Clarity roles, expectations and disclosure choices

Members of the research team should provide clear communication about Lived Experience involvement in the research, and any expectations about Lived Experience roles (see Table 1; Fig. 1). This may involve discussion about various Lived Experience roles and preferences during research conceptualisation and grant application and recruitment, and different methodological approaches.

It is important from the outset to clearly collaborate with Lived Experience partners about the ways that they can draw on their Lived Experience expertise and preferences, and how this information will be used to inform decisions. For example, drawing on their Lived Experience to inform the research questions, protocol, hypotheses, methodology, data collection, analysis and translation; disclosure of personal experiences. Conversely, researchers should avoid tokenism and approaching Lived Experience involvement as a tick box exercise. Clear discussions around decision making and how Lived Experience information will be used is essential.

Expectations

It is important to clarify with Lived Experience collaborators if their role in the project is to draw on their Lived Experience to inform their understanding of a research question, approach to the methods, and interpretation of results, rather than to make a Lived Experience disclosure. For many people new to Lived Experience research, this distinction may be unfamiliar, and they may feel as though it is unclear how they are to contribute to the project. It is important to name that the critical insider lens that they bring is the explicit contribution, and can help to identify and deepen aspects of the research that may otherwise be missed.

Lived Experience contributions as a ‘point in time’ has important and specific implications for ED research: healing and recovery is not linear, Lived Experience conceptualisations of healing and recovery may change, a person's sense of identity, identification with, and/or relationships to ED shift, and a person who contributed to research during a particular phase of their experience may later feel differently about the research itself, including their role and contribution. It is important to consider what may be needed if Lived Experience contributors want to revisit or revise their contributions after the fact - including in published work. Collaborative discussion about the different approaches should be included within the co-development of ‘ways of working’ at the outset of the project, including in grant and ethics applications.

A person’s relationship with their experiences may change over time, and it is important to recognise and name that Lived Experience contribution made at one point in time does not permanently define a person with Lived Experience’s understanding, perspective, and meaning of their experiences. For example, a person with Lived Experience may understand their experiences within biomedical, pathology-based frameworks of illness; over time, they may perceive their ED as situated within socio-cultural-political structures, and may resist biomedical framing. Accordingly, it may be helpful for the research team to discuss if they or the Lived Experience co-author would like to refer to their position as a point-in-time perspective in a positionality statement, or if they feel that this is implied.

Disclosure

Concerns about disclosure and safety are especially salient in ED research. Lived Experience partnership may involve navigating stigma, visibility, and potential personal or professional considerations, particularly within relatively small and interconnected ED communities. While attention to safety is essential, conflating perceived risk with actual harm can lead to unnecessary restriction of participation and reinforce exclusion [62, 76]. It is therefore important to distinguish between disclosures that pose genuine risk for Lived Experience partners and disclosures that are politically and analytically rich and draw on structural, political, and embodied knowledge that challenge dominant frameworks, create discomfort for researchers, or may be outside of the scope of a project. Failure to make this distinction risks silencing critical experiential knowledge and perpetuating epistemic injustice [60, 61, 69]. Collaborative discussion of disclosures that may put Lived Experience partners at risk should involve development of a plan for compassionate support if needed after such disclosures. Similarly, researchers and Lived Experience partners can discuss what may be needed if researchers are uncomfortable with disclosures, or there is a mismatch with the research priorities of the Lived Experience partners (such as collaborative reflection, researcher self-reflection, sharing resources regarding the content, plan for future projects, supported networking). However, research teams should also avoid placing sole responsibility on Lived Experience partners to educate others about their community, culture, or structural experiences unless this is clearly agreed, supported, and recognised as part of their role.

As part of considering disclosure and concerns for Lived Experience safety and risk when managing disclosure, research team members may consider autoethnography and collective/collaborative autoethnography as methodological approaches. It is important to acknowledge that autoethnography and collective/collaborative autoethnography are scholarly methodologies with their own epistemological traditions; they are not simply vehicles for Lived Experience disclosure management, and should not be positioned as such. Mad Studies, Disability Studies, Queer Studies and Critical Race scholarship for example have a rich autoethnographic tradition. Research that involves autoethnographic and collective/collaborative autoethnographic material should honour these approaches as full research methods, and not position them primarily as a means of managing risks of Lived Experience contribution. Accordingly, research teams that wish to engage an Lived Experience co-author to share their lived experience as part of the manuscript, and/or to share their clinical or research expertise, should familiarise themselves with these methods and engage in reflexive practices in consideration of the use of these methods.

Provide fair remuneration, authorship, and recognition

Remuneration

Equitable, fair remuneration for Lived Experience work is needed, and is a common and frequent issue of inequity in mental health work faced by people with lived experience. Again, this is despite the issue of fair remuneration being highlighted and recommended in national guidelines such as Australian National Medical Research guidelines [3]. For instance, in the field of Lived Experience peer work there are inconsistencies in remuneration, either not being paid or being paid a low and inequitable amount, which can further exacerbate feelings of disempowerment [93, 94].

Researchers have identified concern that they will not be able to adequately remunerate Lived Experience partners in Lived Experience research collaboration [8]. A common difficulty faced by researchers is that many grant applications need to include budgeting for Lived Experience partnership, however until the grant is won, the funding is not received, and Lived Experience input is also needed for the grant. University research committees need to identify costs associated with Lived Experience partnership as part of grant application early on in order to ensure that Lived Experience partners can be supported and a working partnership can be developed and maintained throughout the project.

As with other aspects of Lived Experience and researcher collaboration, transparency and clear communication are critical. This includes the nature and amount of remuneration, timing of remuneration (for example, there can be university or organisation delays in paying invoices or contracting), how long remuneration is available and what other options are there should the budget run out. Efforts should be made to limit administrative work for Lived Experience partners where possible (i.e., needing to complete invoices after each meeting, having to repeatedly follow-up payment). This can lead to Lived Experience partners feeling alienated or devalued compared to non-Lived Experience team members. Research teams may consider partnering with local mental health organisations to discuss collaboration and funding support for Lived Experience partners as a potential option for remuneration for Lived Experience partners and developing ongoing industry relationships. In addition to, and not as a substitute for payment, researchers should also consider other offers of support and recognition including training and development opportunities, references and letters to support, other employment options, supervision and mentoring.

Co-authorship and acknowledgement

The authors of this paper have experienced circumstances where they were invited as Lived Experience partners into projects, only to have their contributions removed, overwritten, and dismissed - yet the team was later praised for having engaged Lived Experience partners. The authors have also experienced having their work being unacknowledged and having credit taken for their work. Poor engagement and exploitation of Lived Experience work is unfortunately very common, and can contribute to burnout, lack of trust, feelings of betrayal, and epistemic injustice. It is important, therefore, to be transparent and acknowledge and celebrate Lived Experience contributions, and advocate for equitable practice. Because PWLE may have experienced significant past exploitation and co-option, trust may need to be built; Lived Experience partners may need clear choice and reassurance that they will be provided with co-authorship or other forms of acknowledgement [19]. This represents ongoing needs for reparation, and underscores the importance of Lived Experience involvement in this work, and its acknowledgement. Acknowledgement is important not only on an individual level, but can also repair and restore trust for PWLE in the broader community, by demonstrating that PWLE have been equitably and meaningfully engaged in research. This may be particularly important for PWLE of ED who have experienced negative or harmful (iatrogenic) treatment experiences and marginalised communities who are particularly vulnerable, isolated, and alienated. Acknowledgement of Lived Experience partnership and contribution may be an early foundational step in restoring trust when research is translated by demonstrated efforts to hear and respond to need.

Non-Lived Experience co-authors should consider how to support Lived Experience co-authors who may not have academic writing experience without overwriting their voice. For example, can quotes or partial quotes be used? Can the team members find agreed-upon language to ensure meaning is retained? It is important to maintain a reflexive approach and awareness of biases, and to consider how Lived Experience co-authors can contribute to research framing as well as content.

The research team should consider how to support an Lived Experience co-author if a contribution could identify them, or has implications for their safety, relationships, or employment (current or future). It’s also important to consider the person’s dignity of risk. It may be that before providing any personal Lived Experience contributions (if relevant), the Lived Experience co-author can be prompted to consider the safety, longevity, vulnerability, and potential impacts for a personal contribution.

In many circumstances, Lived Experience contributors are not offered co-authorship. Co-authorship should be considered as an opportunity for meaningful engagement in ED research, particularly for individuals with research interest, and an opportunity to support skills development. This includes offering a PWLE co-authorship opportunities with choice of their full name, or use of full or partial pseudonyms which also represent their contribution e.g., [95].

Build emotionally safe and sustainable ways of working

Emotional labour

Lived Experience partners provide intellectual, emotional, and political labour when engaging in research, including drawing on personal experiences, engaging with potentially distressing material, and navigating power dynamics within research teams. This work can be emotionally demanding and requires recognition, support, and appropriate recovery time [96–99]. User-led and co-produced research further shows that experiential knowledge is central to research on sensitive topics, and that this work can be both demanding and meaningful when undertaken in respectful and supportive conditions [100].

To safely partner with PWLE of ED, research principles that acknowledge emotional labour must be embedded within project design and methods. This includes proactively discussing how Lived Experience partners can step back, step out, or re-engage at different points, ensuring that participation remains flexible and responsive to individual needs, and building contingency plans into timelines and team structures so that no one person carries the burden alone [96, 99], and so that Lived Experience partners are not pressured to work in ways that do not feel safe or supported. These needs are reinforced by literature on Lived Experience integration in academia, which highlights both the value of Lived Experience in improving research and the risks of disclosure burden, tokenism, and emotional strain when support structures are inadequate [76].

Importantly, stepping back from research engagement should not be interpreted as fragility or lack of capacity. Rather, it may reflect the intensity and significance of the work, alongside appropriate boundary-setting and self-care. It could also represent a need for restorative justice or repair, or post-traumatic healing and growth. At the same time, changes in engagement should be collaboratively explored, as they may indicate inequity, exclusion, or harm within the research partnership. Lived Experience involvement can involve epistemic and relational risks, including exploitation and misrecognition, particularly where power imbalances are not adequately addressed [62, 69]. This is consistent with broader arguments that Lived Experience knowledge must be treated as credible and legitimate within mental health research, rather than marginal or merely consultative [100–103].

Developing ways of working together

At the outset, it is important for the research team members to develop their ways of working together, such as developing their shared values [19, 47, 75], and identifying language they may use, the methodological approach to the research, and plan. This may include the conceptualisation of the issue/s themselves and how language and framing may differ (e.g., eating ‘disorder’ or eating ‘distress’, ‘adaptive eating’, ‘purging’, ‘being sick’). This is especially important for intersectional considerations (i.e., neurodivergent sensory-based adaptive eating vs disordered eating; cultural and linguistic considerations). It could also include shared values such as respect; curiosity and openness; assuming good intentions; ‘inviting’ learning and feedback and ‘calling in’ shared learning rather than leading with judgement, blame and ‘calling out’; accountability and responsibility; collective care and reparation. Ways of working should also be revisited throughout the project, in particular when team dynamics, project scope, emotional demands, or cultural and relational considerations shift over time.

The methodological approach should be co-developed, co-selected, or introduced as part of working together. For example, Yarning methods [104–106], Double Diamond Model [107, 108] and Evolved Double Diamond Model [109, 110], Experience-Based Co-Design [31, 111, 112], Participatory Action Research [102, 113, 114], Collective/Collaborative Autoethnography [54, 115], PhotoVoice [116–118], Group Model Building [119], and Nominal Group Technique [120, 121].

Photovoice may be a meaningful approach in ED research because it can enable participants to represent embodied, social, relational, environmental, and cultural dimensions of ED experience that may be difficult to capture through interviews or surveys alone [116–118]. However, photographic methods also raise specific ethical and methodological considerations in ED contexts. Images of bodies, food, exercise, treatment environments, clothing, domestic spaces, or objects associated with illness and recovery may unintentionally reproduce narrow or triggering representations of ED, reinforce comparison, invite surveillance of bodies or behaviours, or expose participants to privacy and identification risks. Photographs should therefore not be treated as neutral documentary evidence of illness, recovery, or Lived Experience. If Photovoice is used, decisions about what may be photographed, what should not be photographed, how images will be interpreted, who controls image selection, how images may be used in dissemination, and how participant safety, privacy, cultural meaning, and consent will be protected should be co-developed with Lived Experience partners and participants. This is one example of why methodological decisions in Lived Experience partnership should be made reflexively and collaboratively, rather than selecting participatory methods as inherently ethical or appropriate.

As a practical example of Ways of Working Together, at the end of research meetings and sessions, for all research team members, it may be helpful to share a quick intention on how each member plans to move forward with their day and one thing they are looking forward to. This can help to shift from difficult rumination, if it occurs, and identify if any team members need to debrief.

‘Small World’ considerations

When partnering with PWLE, it may be important to consider who is involved in the research (e.g., clinician members of the team), and the setting of any work together (e.g., online, local hospital/health services). For example, member/s of the research team may include health practitioners (i.e., GPs, psychologists, psychiatrists, dietitians etc.). With consideration to the country, state, or community the research concerns, a health practitioner in the research team may have been involved in the multidisciplinary treatment of a PWLE. Many PWLE may also have travelled across state borders and across countries to receive treatment. This may also be a consideration for PWLE who are working professionals in the ED space, who may have particular considerations or conflicts of interest with colleagues (patient-practitioner relationships). Prior to confirming engagement in the project, inform the PWLE about the clinician and researcher members of the team. The PWLE should not be required to disclose if they have had treatment from any of the clinician members, but should be allowed space to consider if it would feel comfortable for them to be part of the project, while being given the opportunity to be aware of who the team members consist of.

As an additional consideration, ED research and treatment involving ‘small worlds’ may mean that many Lived Experience partners come to know each other through peer support networks, advocacy communities, online spaces, prior treatment, and prior work. This can create complex dynamics of solidarity, intra-community competition in underpaid and limited work environments and need for survival, trauma exposure and vicarious trauma, and burnout related to community mentorship or complex prior experiences (such as stigma, exploitation, or miscommunication). In small Lived Experience communities, PWLE often advocate for each other and support each others’ work, however miscommunication and misinterpretation can occur in these circumstances when involved in different projects, which can lead to conflicts of interest, the appearance of competition or lack of transparency, or undue stress and burden on the Lived Experience partner about how to manage multiple relationships and roles. Where Lived Experience work has been limited in ED research, Lived Experience partners may also have had their actions assumed to be those of other people with Lived Experience, or be assumed to represent all PWLE or researchers with Lived Experience, which may lead to isolation, burnout, and negative career impacts. Lived Experience contributors may therefore need to consider what project teams will work for them, and if the right dynamics and levels of support exist. LE partners should be involved in designing support and conflict management considerations rather than just being informed about them.

Language considerations

Referring to team members

Consider how the members of your research team will be referred to, and check with Lived Experience research partners if this language feels appropriate and accurate. Language does not simply describe roles within a research team; it actively shapes how different forms of knowledge are recognised, valued, and legitimised within research processes [122]. For example, you might refer to Lived Experience research partners as having ‘Expertise by Lived Experience’, and research team members who do not have direct or disclosed Lived Experience as having ‘Expertise by Education and Training’. Rather than being parallel but distinct forms of knowledge, these positions can intersect. Many Lived Experience researchers have both forms of expertise, and individuals may move between or occupy multiple positions across contexts [123]. Lived Experience expertise does not become more credible when combined with academic credentials; rather, Lived Experience expertise without formal research training remains a valuable epistemology on its own [124, 125].

As an example description of the research team which involves Lived Experience partners and formally trained researchers: “Our co-design group consisted of 4 individuals with Expertise by Lived Experience, 4 individuals with Expertise by Education and Training (two formally trained researchers, a psychologist, and a dietitian) and 2 individuals with both Expertise by Lived Experience and Education and Training (one psychologist with Lived Experience, and one Lived Experience researcher with formal research training).”

Using such language acknowledges the equal value of Lived Experience expertise and expertise by education and training, while also acknowledging differences and intersections in these forms of knowledge. Explicitly naming these positions can support transparency and help mitigate the risk of tokenistic inclusion by clarifying the roles and contributions of different team members [76, 126].

Positionality statements (or reflexivity statements) contextualise research by outlining how worldview, sociopolitical context, social identities and Lived Experience influence research questions, data collection, analysis, and interpretation [127–129]. Used across qualitative and quantitative research, they strengthen ethical and inclusive practice by increasing transparency, challenging assumptions, and clarifying how power operates in knowledge production [76, 130]. Statements may be individual or collective and should form part of ongoing reflexive practice in which researchers critically examine their assumptions and methodological grounding e.g., [10, 21, 57, 131–133]. However, they risk becoming performative if not accompanied by deeper ideological and structural change [134–136].

For Lived Experience researchers, positionality statements should not require disclosure of diagnosis or treatment history. Lived Experience contributors should choose what to share, rather than be driven by expectation to support perceived credibility of the research project. Expectations of disclosure can reinforce power imbalances and burden Lived Experience partners, particularly where Lived Experience is treated as something to be demonstrated or verified [76, 123].

Positionality statements from researchers without disclosed Lived Experience are equally important. The absence of disclosed Lived Experience is itself shaped by relationships to psychiatric systems, biomedical frameworks, institutional systems and dominant knowledge paradigms [126]. Researchers should therefore name their relationship to these systems, and frameworks and values informing their work (e.g., social justice, Mad Studies, disability justice, feminist approaches), rather than relying solely on demographics or professional credentials. Recognising both the presence and absence of Lived Experience as meaningful positionalities supports more equitable, reflexive research and helps redistribute epistemic authority within research teams [75, 122].

Practice reflexivity and transparency

Clear communication regarding parameters of research

Discussing what parameters and limitations may exist for the project is important (i.e., scope, funding limitations, team member capacities). This is helpful for transparency and particularly important for Lived Experience partners who may be new to or less familiar with research engagement, such as the time needed and processes for multiple project phases or project types (i.e., pilot studies, feasibility studies, RCTs). It can be helpful to provide background information regarding how the project is situated within wider pragmatic constraints and broader aims; such as subpopulations, mental health research and equity. Many PWLE are critically aware of and sensitive to socio-cultural and political factors that impact their work [19], and open discussion of these issues can support sensitive engagement and enriched discussion. It is equally important to consider that PWLE may not be engaged with politics that impact research, and to consider how to invite people in by discussing research processes and factors that influence research; such as how research methods are conducted, and why. This may also help in role clarity and discussing parameters, but also in discussing research biases and limitations, gaps, and areas that may be challenged and expanded through Lived Experience engagement [18, 19].

Diagnosis and research framing

Rather than assuming biomedical framing and diagnostic terms as the default perspective and language of the research, this should be collaboratively determined. If some team members differ in their understanding and positionality, this can also be noted in positionality statements if wished, and can inform how the research was understood and conducted. Biomedical framing is one framework among many, and is not a neutral and assumed default ground for the research. This is particularly important where ED research involves First Nations peoples, as culturally narrow framing may persist even when Lived Experience involvement is included. Social model frameworks, human rights frameworks, Mad Studies frameworks, trauma-informed frameworks, historical frameworks, psychological frameworks, existential frameworks, and cultural and spiritual frameworks are also ways of understanding ED (see Table 2).

Example of frameworks in mental health, medical and social research

More broadly, Western or biomedical research approaches should not be treated as the default frame through which ED is understood. All research traditions bring particular assumptions about what health is, what counts as evidence, how problems are defined, and which forms of knowledge are recognised as legitimate [137–140]. These assumptions shape not only interpretation, but also the questions asked, the methods chosen, and the kinds of responses that are deemed appropriate. Reflexive engagement is therefore needed. For instance, important background factors, such as disciplinary training, cultural positioning, institutional norms/contexts and other aspects of positionality, all have an impact on the work. Western approaches tend to privilege categorisation, measurement, reductionism and individualisation, whereas others may centre relationality, culture, community, spirituality and Lived Experience. Through taking an approach of humility, curiosity, reflexivity and openness to learning together, research can remain open to multiple, interconnected, or sometimes disparate ways of understanding ED. Where appropriate, research may draw on approaches that intentionally hold different knowledge systems together without collapsing one into the other. For example, Two-Eyed Seeing (Etuaptmumk in Mi’kmaw) and Two-Ways Knowing have been used to support work that learns from both Indigenous and Western ways of knowing (epistemologies), recognising that each offers important insights [141, 142]. Such approaches require care, reflexivity, and accountability, rather than simple inclusion or tokenism.

Conceptualisation of recovery

The concept of recovery itself is contested within eating distress communities and knowledge schools (i.e., Mad Studies, Neurodivergent and disabled communities). For some people, recovery as a goal reproduces the idea that there is a normative state to return to. For others, the focus is on meaning and quality of life rather than symptom elimination. For people who understand their ED as a response to structural conditions (poverty, trauma, sanism, racism, ableism, cisheteronormativity, transphobia and homophobia), recovery language can position ED as an individual, micro-level problem rather than a socio-cultural-political problem. This is not a matter of definition preference, but represents deeper conceptualisation of ED. Researchers should resist trying to select Lived Experience partners that solely align with their conceptualisation of ED; this may reproduce epistemic injustice by excluding other understandings of the context and contributors to ED that could contribute to rich interpretations of data and knowledge advancement [143–145]. For example, deepening understanding of holistic models of health, Indigenous Social and Emotional Wellbeing models, and diverse perspectives on contributions to ED should be collaboratively discussed in order to consider how they may be acknowledged and integrated into research [46, 67, 146].

Ensure mutual benefit and capacity building

In research partnerships, benefits are rarely equal for people with lived experience. Researchers gain data, publications, grant outcomes, career opportunities, and career advancement. Lived Experience contributors may gain skills and the satisfaction of contributing and, in rarer circumstances, formal skills training, letters of recommendation, and co-authorship; however, their contribution often occurs at significant personal cost. It is important to consider what more equal, mutual benefit could look like (e.g., [147] ). If Lived Experience contributors had equal power to define what counted as beneficial and equal access to the structural rewards produced in research processes, what might the goals be in research partnerships? What opportunities exist to further mutual benefit (e.g., benefit to individuals, communities, health system improvement)? Mutual benefit may therefore include outcomes beyond academic publication, such as relationship-building, community relevance, accessible outputs, cultural safety, skills development, or changes to practice and policy [147, 148].

Training, up-skilling, letters of recommendation

For your project and in grant applications, plan from the outset how you aim to support Lived Experience partners with training, upskilling, and considerations of mutual benefit. This puts into practice and demonstrates principles of mutuality, reciprocity, equity, and a commitment to epistemic justice. This can also help to demonstrate consideration for how the research may benefit people with Lived Experience in the short and long-term over the stages of the research. There are multiple avenues for research training that can be used to plan for budgeting. There can also be multiple opportunities to partner with Lived Experience organisations that offer training, including organisational training, group training (i.e., for research teams), and individual training for Lived Experience partners in building research skills (e.g., Lived Experience Australia, n.d.; Lived Experience Training, n.d.). The Australian Eating Disorder Research and Translation Centre (AEDRTC) for example, has a quickstart guide for co-production in ED research [26, 149] and a co-production checklist [149]. The Australian ED charity the Butterfly Foundation co-produced resources as part of the Lived Experience Collective Education Hub [49], which included introductory training on types of Lived Experience work and pathways into Lived Experience work [38, 50, 51]. Other ways to support the career development of Lived Experience partners include offering to provide letters of recommendation and letters describing specific research engagement, roles, and skills used by a Lived Experience partner during their involvement in a research project. While many frameworks and guides exist on Lived Experience research involvement, helpful resources include [148, 150–157].

Limitations

This manuscript is not intended to be a comprehensive guideline for Lived Experience engagement in ED research; rather it is intended to outline key considerations for meaningful, inclusive, accountable, and ethical partnership, with practical examples. The authorship team resides predominantly in countries where Lived Experience knowledge is increasingly recognised as a critical pillar of mental health research and a distinct epistemology, and where Lived Experience partnership in ED research may be sought. In countries of the global north and global south where Lived Experience knowledge is less recognised, the creation of infrastructure and further resources may be required for equitable partnership.

Summary of recommendations

It is suggested that researchers familiarise themselves with types of Lived Experience partnership (Table 1) and key principles outlined in this article, as part of considering Lived Experience collaboration (Fig. 2). Namely, the core principles for ethical and meaningful partnership: [1] Recognise lived experience as expertise [2], Share power and decision-making [3], Support autonomy, readiness, and dignity of risk [4], Clarify roles, expectations, and disclosure choices [5], Provide fair remuneration, authorship, and recognition [8], Build emotionally safe and sustainable ways of working [60], Practice reflexivity and transparency [61], Ensure mutual benefit and capacity building. It is recommended that researchers are clear about what kind of partnership they would like to develop, and begin developing plans for engagement. This may include involving Lived Experience partners as part of designing recruitment or types of engagement with additional Lived Experience partners (i.e., Lived Experience-led research, coproduction), or may involve planning for Lived Experience involvement at later stages of a project (i.e., codesign, consultation).

Researchers should also consider whose Lived Experience voices and perspectives may be absent or underrepresented, and whether the project’s framing, language, methods, and outcomes are culturally safe and meaningful for the communities the research seeks to benefit.

It is recommended that researchers are clear about what kind of partnership they would like to develop, avoid overstating more limited involvement, and build collaboration on values of equity, respect, transparency, and meaningful engagement.

Conclusion

ED researchers increasingly want to work with lived experience partners, but often need clearer guidance on what different forms of partnership actually mean (see Table 1; Fig. 1) and what principles should guide them. Clarifying these terms may support more ethical, equitable, and meaningful collaboration. This article therefore provides a concise overview of key forms of Lived Experience partnership in ED research, and outlines core principles and practical considerations for Lived Experience collaboration in ED research.

Our team also have experience across multiple sectors including: LE-led ED research (RE, KS, LC, AG, ZG), LE-led mental health research, Mad Studies, and Neurodivergence research (RE, KS, LC, MJ), co-produced and co-designed ED and mental health research (RE, KS, AG, ZG, MP), First Peoples health and wellbeing research (AG, ZG) and Cultural Medicines (AG, ZG), experience in ED and other mental health policy work (RE, KS, LC, AG, ZG, MJ), ED and other mental health governance, and advisory roles (RE, KS, LC, AG, ZG, MJ), mental health peer support work (RE, KS), lived experience research mentorship (MJ, RE), mentorship on lived experience research partnership (MJ, RE), and advocacy work (RE, KS, LC, AG, ZG, JP, MJ), including advocating for the need for lived experience work in countries with limited eating disorder care infrastructure (JP). This paper is informed by our collective lived experiences and professional expertise, our wish to continue learning and growing, to collaborate and support the field in this space.
