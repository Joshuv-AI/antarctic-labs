ABSTRACT
The thermodynamic equilibrium code known as TIGER has been in use since the 1970s [1–3] and is
designed to calculate the performance of energetic materials during detonation at high temperatures
and pressures. The original TIGER code utilized a limited database consisting of 12 gaseous and 3
condensed constituents, which were composed of the elements carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen
(N), oxygen (O), and aluminum (Al).
In contrast, the more modern JCZS3 database [4] features a significantly larger dataset, including
756 gaseous species, 189 positive and negative ions, and 496 condensed constituents derived from
62 different elements.
While this expanded product species database allows for the exploration of a broader range of
problems relevant to our laboratory, it also introduces challenges related to convergence, particularly
when both liquid and solid species coexist under a vapor dome. In this work, we present an improved
TIGER solution technique aimed at accurately determining the equilibrium state for systems that
can produce a diverse array of products, including both solid and liquid condensed species.
This report presents the status of the TIGER solver as of the end of FY25. Ongoing efforts
are focused on enhancing the solver, and the report outlines several planned improvements. We
anticipate that further developments will require additional documentation in the future.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents a comprehensive overview of the advancements made in the TIGER solver, a
critical tool for analyzing complex thermodynamic equilibria in high-energy scenarios. Building on
decades of foundational research, the enhanced TIGER solver introduces innovative convergence
methods and novel derivations that significantly improve computational performance, particularly
in high-pressure and high-temperature environments.
The report details several case studies, including the analysis of adiabatic flame behavior in py-
rotechnic ignitors and the performance of fireworks under explosive conditions. These examples
not only showcase the versatility of the updated TIGER code but also validate its improved predic-
tive capabilities, demonstrating strong alignment with experimental observations and established
ignition models.
Key enhancements include the incorporation of a robust stoichiometric framework that rigorously
enforces elemental conservation, as well as a refined component selection methodology that ac-
celerates convergence and streamlines calculations. The report also outlines future directions for
TIGER, such as the integration of deep web crawling techniques to expand the equation-of-state
database and the transition to a relational database structure. These improvements are expected to
enhance data management, reduce uncertainty in experimental inputs, and ultimately increase the
reliability and adaptability of the TIGER solver across various applications in shock physics and
thermochemistry.
The advancements detailed in this report position TIGER as a powerful asset for addressing com-
plex equilibrium problems, paving the way for future studies that will deepen our understanding
of thermodynamic behaviors in high-energy systems. Funding this work will not only support the
continued development of the TIGER solver but also contribute to the broader scientific commu-
nity’s efforts to enhance safety and performance in high-stakes applications, such as aerospace and
defense.
In conclusion, the enhanced capabilities of the TIGER solver represent a significant leap forward in
computational thermodynamics, making it an invaluable tool for researchers and engineers alike.
Continued investment in this area promises to yield substantial returns in both scientific knowledge
and practical applications.

1. INTRODUCTION

A brief history of the TIGER code was given at the 15th Detonation Symposium [5] to show the
origins of the Sandia National Laboratories (SNLs) TIGER code and Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory’s (LLNLs) CHEETAH code. Other codes also have roots originating with the TIGER
code. For example, BLAKE [6] is a thermodynamics code based on TIGER as well as JAGUAR [7]
which is a Munitions Safety Information Analysis Center (MSIAC) web-based application.
The original TIGER code used a small product species database and did not perform well when
numerous condensed products were used in complex problems that involved multiple unnecessary
solver loops. In the current report, we present a more robust solver that can be used for complex
mixtures containing multiple condensed products. Although the intent of this report is to document
the new and improved solution strategy for TIGER, we also provide a few examples of TIGER
calculations in this introduction that show the versatility of the code and give future research
directions.
The documentation for the TIGER solver hasn’t been updated since the original work of Wiebenson
[8–10] and Cowperthwaite [1, 2]. Furthermore, the JCZ3 equation of state (EOS) documentation
hasn’t been updated since Cowperthwaite’s conference paper [3]. New equations for the JCZ3 EOS
with a new set of independent variables are needed with our new solution technique. Because we
are using a new set of variables, all partial derivatives must be reevaluated.
This report will present new derivations and convergence methods developed for the TIGER code.
However, it will not cover the derivations of the individual equations of state in detail. For those
specific derivatives, readers are encouraged to consult the original documentation [1–3] for the
necessary definitions. Alternatively, a second report will be available [11] that includes the new
derivations for TIGER’s equations of state, with a particular focus on the JCZ3 equation of state.

1.2. Future work

We propose that the new code necessitates the development of a new database and database format,
incorporating intermolecular potential parameters derived from Hugoniot data and overdriven
detonation states. The legacy version of TIGER encountered conversion challenges under high-
pressure and high-temperature conditions, where ion formation is prevalent.
To enhance the size and comprehensiveness of the Equation of State (EOS) database, we recommend
utilizing deep web crawling tools to identify and retrieve data from existing European and Russian
databases. This approach will significantly expand the available data resources.
Furthermore, the current EOS database should be migrated to a relational database format. This
transition will allow for the integration of various forms of data present in the literature and en-
sure that the database is extensible—facilitating easy updates and revisions as new data becomes
available. A thermodynamic database inherently involves numerous interrelated parameters, which
should be codified within the database. This structure will enable automatic adjustments of depen-
dent parameters when one set of parameters is modified due to new information or experimental
findings. Such functionality is best achieved through a relational database format.
A thermochemical database can be viewed as a network of interrelated thermochemical data. The
accuracy of this network can be significantly enhanced by reducing the combined uncertainty
associated with the experiments that generated the data [15]. Therefore, we advocate for the
creation of a relational database, such as PostgreSQL (www.postgresql.org), which can effectively
represent the internal network of thermodynamic dependencies. A good example of a PostgreSQL
thermodynamic database is discussed by Belov et al. [16].
Additionally, the new TIGER solver can be utilized to generate a set of EOS product databases (e.g.,
SESAME) that can be employed by shock physics codes for various analyses. We have integrated
an automatic capability into the new TIGER to create SESAME tables for use in these codes.

2. GENERAL ALGORITHMS WITHIN TIGER

The original formulation of TIGER incorporates several inherent assumptions that are critical to
its functionality. One fundamental assumption is that a gas phase must always be present. This
condition is essential for the current implementation of the algorithm. However, there are scenarios
and input configurations where the gas phase may not be stable compared to a set of fully condensed
phases. In such cases, TIGER encounters a limitation that prevents it from accurately calculating
the appropriate state. To address this issue, it is recommended to add a small amount of argon (Ar)
to the input composition card. Since argon does not exist in any condensed phase, this addition
ensures the presence of a gas phase under varying thermodynamic conditions.

Currently, TIGER operates exclusively with stoichiometric solid phases and does not accommodate
solid or liquid solutions. A typical TIGER calculation begins with the specification of the mixture’s
composition, followed by the selection of the type of equilibrium to be solved. TIGER is capable
of solving a variety of equilibrium cases, including those related to shock propagation. For point
calculations, two ensemble variables are required to fully specify the thermodynamic equilibrium,
given fixed values of element abundances. The following pairs of ensemble variables can be
specified and solved by TIGER:

     • (𝑃, 𝑇); (𝑃, 𝐻); (𝑃, 𝑆); (𝑃, 𝑉)

     • (𝑉, 𝑇); (𝑉, 𝐸); (𝑉, 𝑆); (𝑆, 𝑇)

In addition to these capabilities, TIGER can perform various detonation calculations pertinent
to shock physics. It can calculate conditions along a Chapman-Jouguet detonation wave and
determine the properties of a heterogeneous mixture of detonation products along Hugoniot curves
and isentropes. Furthermore, partial equilibrium can be employed to simulate nonreactive species,
such as aluminum being passivated in the reaction zone via the formation of Al2 O3 . This is achieved
by designating a portion of the aluminum in the reactant as inert, allowing only solid, liquid, and
gaseous aluminum to form. This approach prevents the inert aluminum from further reacting to
produce Al2 O3 .

In this report, we will focus on a new solver implementation that enhances a subset of these
capabilities: the (𝑃, 𝑇) and (𝑉, 𝑇) solutions. This new solver offers a more direct and globally
convergent approach. The improved convergence results from the need to solve only one equation
system instead of two, as required by the original solver. While the solution of other variable sets,
as well as the Chapman-Jouguet and Hugoniot solutions, can still be achieved using the previous
method, we anticipate that the entire suite of capabilities will be enabled through the new method
in the next version of TIGER.

less likely to approach zero, thus reducing the need for replacement. Solid phase species are
also preferred as components unless they exist in small concentrations, in which case they must
be discarded as components. Solid species must be classified as constituents before they can be
eliminated as stable phases.

2.2. Specification of Composition

In the TIGER code, a fixed elemental composition is specified via the COM card, with the total weight
fixed at 100 g. The COM card defines the molar composition of a mixture. Based on this, the total
mixture weight is calculated using molecular weights, and the molar composition is then scaled to
ensure a total weight of exactly 100 g.
A significant enhancement to TIGER’s capabilities would be the implementation of continuation
strategies for molar composition. However, this is challenging because the 100 g total weight is an
ingrained specification within the code, making it difficult to implement a strategy where the moles
of a species were iteratively titrated and the results recomputed.

3.3. Charge as a Special Case

The specification of charge is a special case of the equilibrium problem. Within TIGER, the overall
system must be charge neutral, though the presence of ions can be turned on or off with the ion
keyword. Currently, each molecule in the system is assigned one electron per atom as part of its
elemental composition. The electron composition is then tracked just like any other elemental
composition within the element. This method ensures that the element composition for the electron
is always positive. However, this abstract treatment is equivalent to one where the element number
is associated with the charge of the element, and it may be changed to this alternative treatment in
the future.
The selection of major and minor species has a significant effect on the efficiency of the algorithm, a
topic that will be discussed in later parts of this report. The selection is based on a relative criterion
for each element in the mixture, specifically the species with the highest mole numbers. Major and
minor species are selected based on their relative rankings for each element, irrespective of their

 28

total mole number rankings within the species list. Major and minor species are differentiated by
the fact that their rows and columns in the matrix solve are fully formed. In contrast, other small
species have a diagonal entry, and tiny species are solved using a different algorithm altogether.

For the case of the electron, these relative rankings are based on the absolute value of the charge of
the species. Therefore, the major positive and negative charged species in the mixture are typically
assigned as major species. Additionally, the sign of the charge is taken into account when making
these major and minor species type determinations.

3.4. Solving the Stoichiometric Equations

The new algorithm is stoichiometric. The stoichiometric solve rarely requires updates to satisfy
the elemental abundances once they have been initially calculated. However, at the start of the
calculation, after the initial mole numbers are estimated, the element abundance equations must be
calculated in a dedicated solve step.

TIGER carries out this element abundance solve using both a steepest descent algorithm and a
heuristic iterative technique that maintains the positivity of all species, which has proven sufficient
in practice. Both algorithms are exclusively applied to the initial constitutive species vector when
a better method is not available to solve the stoichiometric equations. Although there are superior
alternatives, such as those based on linear simplex solves, this approach has been found to be
adequate for finding an acceptable starting point for typical TIGER calculations with a specified
temperature and pressure (e.g., 1 atm and 3000 K).

These two algorithms are executed in an alternating fashion until the element abundance equations
are solved. After each attempt, a new component basis is calculated if necessary. Convergence
typically occurs within a few iterations.

4. SELECTION OF NEW COMPONENTS

One of the most important aspects of the algorithm is the selection of components. The work of
Smith and Missen has shown that a similar matrix problem, associated with the equilibrium of
ideal gases using the Villars-Cruise-Smith (VCS) stoichiometric equilibrium method, demonstrates
that the selection of the components can significantly affect the convergence rate [18]. In their
algorithm for a (𝑇, 𝑃) solve, they actively clear all the non-diagonal entries of the Hessian matrix.
This ensures that the resulting Newton direction in reaction coordinate space is guaranteed to start
out reducing the total Gibbs free energy of the system. Therefore, their algorithm is guaranteed to
converge without actually inverting a matrix.
Our algorithm is related to the VCS algorithm, but it is not the same. It also uses a full Hessian
approach for (𝑇, 𝑃) problems and a full Newton equation for other thermodynamic pairs for major
and minor species’ non-diagonal interaction terms.
The algorithm for choosing components has been modified from the original TIGER code in
several important ways. Non-component condensed phase species with positive mole numbers are
"birthed" or "killed-off" and are assigned an IDType of 5. All species are classified by their IDType.
Condensed phase species with small concentrations are demoted from being components (changing
their IDType from 1 to 5) when their mole number falls below a threshold (e.g., 1 × 10−11 gmol),
which occurs at the lower end of the minor species category. This approach allows species to be
birthed or killed-off at concentrations small enough not to significantly change the overall activities
of other gas-phase species. This allows the moles of a condensed-phase species to be temporarily
greater than the Gibbs Phase Rule would typically permit. When conditions change and the moles
of a condensed-phase species are "birthed" or "grown," the moles of a condensed-phase component
species decreases until it switches IDTypes, and the "birthing" phase becomes a component. At a
converged solution, the Gibbs Phase Rule always holds.
This change essentially eliminates a great deal of the complicated logic associated with phase-
flipping situations in the old TIGER code, which would have had to keep track of combinations of
phases to prevent infinite loops when convergence breaks down.
The second major change is that the component choice now favors species with the largest mole
number, regardless of whether the species is a condensed phase. This modification is based on
the evidence from Smith and Missen and eliminates the need to use condensed-phase species as
components when they have relatively small mole numbers. The selection of the basis species may
be fairly transient during the algorithm. When small species are chosen as components, the step
lengths may have to be small due to numerical damping to keep the mole numbers positive. This
forces the algorithm to select a different set of component species to recalculate the reaction matrix,
leading to wasted time. To improve efficiency, it is best to select a set of components so that larger
step lengths can be taken to enter the Newton convergence region more quickly.

The algorithm does not test for phase stability problems with the gas equation of state, i.e.,
whether the gas phase will be unstable to breaking up into multiple phases itself. In order to
carry out that analysis, tangent plane stability criteria would need to be studied, as is done in flash
calculations [19]. This type of analysis is a large field of work [20]. Within TIGER, the algorithm
only tests for the satisfaction of the gas phase being on the correct side of its spinodal line, where
it does not spontaneously fall apart into two phases. We have found empirically that many global
equilibrium failures experienced by TIGER have occurred because the local solution state drifts
into the forbidden region delineated by the spinodal line during the relaxation of the equilibrium
problem.

9. GIBBS MINIMIZATION DAMPING STRATEGY

At each step (m), the minimization strategy at constant temperature (T) and pressure (P) involves
calculating a new set of mole numbers. The direction of this update is determined by inverting the
Hessian matrix or using its diagonal.

mization problem. 𝜹𝝃 (𝑚) is the step chosen by the inversion of the matrix and represents the full
change in mole numbers suggested by the Hessian. 𝜹𝝃 (𝑚+1) is the new set of reaction states after
the current step.
To ensure that the mole numbers of all species remain positive, the update vector is checked. This
is accomplished first by "cropping" the update reaction vector, and then by applying a damping
strategy to the combined update vector using a damping coefficient.
The initial damping strategy, which was based on ensuring the total Gibbs free energy always
decreased, was ultimately unsuccessful due to roundoff errors. The current damping strategy
is based on a robust method from Zuzax [26], originally developed by Smith and Missen [18].
This approach relies on a reliable evaluation of the slope of the minimization path, which can be
accurately estimated even for difficult problems. The step length is then determined by estimating
the distance at which this slope equals zero.

11. COMMENTS REGARDING THE (T,P) SOLUTION PROCEDURE

The new (𝑇, 𝑝) solver algorithm is generally globally convergent, with few serious convergence
issues. Recent tuning has improved convergence at high-pressure, dense conditions by adjusting
the criteria for categorizing major and minor species.
A general convergence problem occurs when the solution state enters the spinodal region, also
known as the dome region of the (𝑇, 𝑉) space. In this region, the gas phase becomes spontaneously
unstable (where 𝑑𝑝/𝑑𝑉 > 0), causing the code to terminate with an error. This issue often arises
when species concentrations from a previous solution or initial guess are not close enough to
equilibrium; for example, if solid-phase species concentrations are too low or ion concentrations
are too high. This problem can be circumvented by solving the system at intermediate (𝑇, 𝑝)
conditions to guide the species concentrations closer to their equilibrium values along a stable
trajectory.
While a global continuation method—a technique used with the legacy TIGER code and other
thermodynamic variable combinations—could be implemented, the (𝑇, 𝑝) algorithm’s robustness
generally makes this unnecessary.
There is significant room to tune the algorithm for speed, as efficiency has not been a priority.
Additional tuning of the major, minor, and small species specification could yield substantial
efficiency gains. For example, solving just for the major species at the beginning could greatly
reduce solve times. Many calculations are also currently superfluous; the full Jacobian, for instance,
is calculated at every step even when it may not be necessary. Finally, a better initial guess, perhaps
derived from a linear simplex programming method using only standard state Gibbs free energy
values, could significantly reduce the initial solve time.

12.1. Sidelight on Efficiency

Solving Eqn. (12.4) by treating this as a dense equation with the full species set at every step and
then inverting a full matrix is an obvious source of inefficiency in the algorithm. In fact, there are
probably only a few species whose concentrations actually affect the values of𝛾𝑖 for other species.
We have identified these species as having an ID_TYPE of Major Species. These are probably
species whose mole fractions are above 1.0E-3. The rest of the gas phase species are minor species
or small species. These are species whose mole fractions are small enough that their values don’t
affect the solution of Eqn. (12.1) for species other than themselves.
Then, an algorithm where only the major species concentrations are solved for via a matrix inversion,
while minor species concentrations are solved by assuming the matrix is diagonal for these entries,
may achieve a dramatic speed-up in execution. For example, if there are roughly 50 gas phase
species with only 4 major species, the expected speed-up could be a factor of 2000, assuming the
calculation of the LU decomposition of the Jacobian is the dominant CPU-time process.
An algorithm where the off-diagonal matrix is reduced to Major Species and Condensed Species
(ID_TYPEs 2 and 5, see Table 4-1) has been shown to be very efficient and convergent. Minor species
(i.e., ID_TYPE 3) are handled via a diagonal approximation to the matrix entries of Eqn. (12.1),
while Small species (i.e., ID_TYPE 4) are handled via their own alternate equation formulation.

This means that for most steps, the matrix inversion process is only handled on a small subset of the
total equations in the system (major species, condensed constituents, and the two thermodynamic
relations). If a step fails to reduce the residual, a full matrix—including the off-diagonal Jacobian
rows and columns for the minor species—is then incorporated.

13. DAMPING STRATEGY FOR A GENERAL NEWTON PROBLEM

The initial damping strategy was based on ensuring the total Gibbs free energy always decreased.
This method was ultimately unsuccessful due to round-off error. For problems that are not strictly
Gibbs free energy minimization problems (where temperature and pressure are held constant), the
solution of the equilibrium system uses a standard Newton’s method. A key change in the solution
procedure was moving the scaling of solution unknowns from a logarithmic scale to a linear scale.
This is a necessary condition for stoichiometric methods, where solution unknowns refer to delta
quantities of species mole numbers, making it illogical to treat them as logarithmic variables.
The numerical method closely follows the approach of Dennis and Schnabel (1996) [27], where a
minimization problem is created by solving for the dot product of the original residual vector. This
method relies heavily on an important scaling procedure for both the residual equations and the
solution unknowns.
While several different scaling methods for the residual terms and solution unknowns have been
tested, their effects remain a topic of debate. Scaling is an important topic because while it does not
affect the Newton direction, it does influence the steepest descent direction and, consequently, the
global convergence properties of the method. The final decision was to treat the residual weights
using a characteristic value of a chemical potential. This ensures that the residual norms equally
reflect the convergence of each species’ characteristic equation. This strategy may give small
species a more significant role in the residual than is strictly necessary, but this is mitigated by the
approach of solving for the major constituents.
Although the current strategy has a large area of global convergence, it is not foolproof. If the
solution vector enters the wrong side of the spinoidal boundary, the algorithm cannot automatically
correct itself. The best solution strategy in this case is to back up and take a smaller step size in the
thermodynamic variables. This reduces the number of unstable species that create additional non-
ideal interactions within the gas phase and affect the 𝑑𝑝/𝑑𝑣 value. In other words, when attempting
to take large jumps in the thermodynamic variables, the trajectory of the iterations is kept closer to
the equilibrium species trajectory. This is the method that the old TIGER algorithm relied on heavily.
Using the old algorithm, it was not possible to change the thermodynamic variables appreciably
without losing local convergence. The new method has a larger range of convergence but, in some
cases, still needs to employ the old method of reducing the thermodynamic step size.

15. GLOBAL EQUILIBRIUM DERIVATIVES

For problems involving derivatives of an equilibrated system with respect to a system variable, such
as the temperature or system volume, a linear matrix problem must be solved in a post-processing
step once the initial system is solved for as a function of 𝑇 and 𝑉𝑡𝑜𝑡 . This is a new matrix problem,
where the derivative of the mole numbers of the species with respect to the system variable must
be solved for. We will refer to these derivatives as “equilibrated” derivatives and indicate the type
by using the superscript E. We will also indicate what system variable is held constant by putting
that variable as a subscript on the derivative specification. Note that in these derivatives, the
species moles are not held constant, though the system’s element abundance vector is kept constant.
Therefore, these derivatives are not related to the more common single-phase based thermodynamic
functions. We will, however, require that the reaction chemical potential equations are satisfied
along the derivative direction.

helpful. 𝐶𝑣𝐸 is calculated from the expression involving the derivative of the entropy.

16. NEW SOLVER ALGORITHM FOR MELTING

When considering an equilibrium case where a solid is partially melted, several key conditions
must be met. The liquid and solid phases are only stable along a specific line in the 𝑇-𝑃 space.
The liquid phase is stable for all pressures below this line, while the solid phase is stable for all
pressures above it, assuming that the molar volume of the liquid is greater than that of the solid.
If the problem is solved under the assumption that the liquid is stable, the resulting pressure
determined from the overall problem will lie above the 𝑇-𝑃 line, indicating that the liquid is
unstable with respect to the solid transformation. Conversely, if the problem is solved assuming the
solid is stable, the resulting pressure will fall below the line, indicating that the solid is unstable with
respect to the liquid phase of the compound. Consequently, the algorithm may oscillate indefinitely
between the solid and liquid phases without reaching a definitive solution.
The resolution to this problem is to allow both phases to coexist within the problem space. This
approach will yield the correct answer. It may be beneficial for the algorithm to designate one of
the phases—either solid or liquid—as a component in the problem. By doing so, the change in
Gibbs free energy (Δ𝐺) associated with the melting process can be expressed explicitly, leading
to a numerically more stable solution. A direct calculation of the 𝑇-𝑃 line will then be possible.
When the solution deviates from the 𝑇-𝑃 line due to changes in parameter space, one or both of the
condensed phases can be zeroed out as necessary.
A known challenge arises when solving for 𝑇-𝑃 conditions: the driving force for phase conversion
approaches zero as the 𝑃(𝑇) condition is approached. For simple phase change problems, this can
be mitigated by performing an explicit Δ𝐺 check on the phase change and subsequently eliminating
the phase with the higher Gibbs free energy. However, in eutectic problems, we have observed that
the number of iterations required for convergence may approach infinity as the 𝑇-𝑃 eutectic point
is approached, due to the driving force nearing zero.
Several conditions must be satisfied for the proposed algorithm change to be successfully imple-
mented. First, the ability to formulate the equation set with both solid and liquid forms of a species
must be developed and verified. This may involve an unknown amount of work, as this approach
has not been previously undertaken. Additionally, an indexing system must be established to
track which condensed phases are being monitored, allowing for reversal when they are no longer
needed.
A potentially more effective solution would be to keep each phase separate from the outset,
permitting only one phase with the same atomic structure—or a factor of the atomic structure—to
be stable at any given time unless specific criteria are met (e.g., proximity to the 𝑇-𝑃 line, such as
within 10% of the pressure along the 𝑃(𝑇) curve). This would apply when the algorithm is not
solving a 𝑇-𝑃 problem or is unable to resolve the problem due to oscillating solid-liquid criteria,
as previously mentioned.

If condensed phases exist under the conditions where Δ𝐻 = 0 and Δ𝑉 = 0, the system is considered
fully degenerate and cannot be solved in general. If degeneracy occurs at a single temperature
point, the use of multiple phases of that condensed product must be prohibited in the vicinity of

A similar condition, albeit in a more complex scenario, arises at eutectic points. Eutectic conditions
have not yet been encountered within TIGER. In such cases, the driving force for the solution
approaches zero as the eutectic temperature is approached. The appropriate approach for this
situation is to solve for the eutectic condition exactly in terms of (𝑇, 𝑃) using a modified form of
the equations.
To implement this, the parameter space should be divided into quadrants using the temperature
and constituent axes. Depending on which side of the constituent axis the system is located,
one of the solid equations should be eliminated from the solution if the temperature is above the
eutectic point. Conversely, if the temperature is below the axis, the liquid equation should be
eliminated. This method effectively reduces the null space of the problem to zero as the eutectic
point is approached from all directions. While this requires considerable special handling to ensure
accuracy, it fundamentally employs the same algorithm that TIGER currently uses for phases that
can exist as either liquid or solid.
Let us delve into the specifics of the new implementation.
For the (𝑇, 𝑃) algorithm, the only requirement is to recognize that Δ𝐺 ≤ 0 indicates that one phase
is stable while the other is not.
In the case of the (𝑇, 𝑉) algorithm, if both a solid and a liquid constituent are included, and one of
the phases is a component, the equation of formation for the constituent will essentially state that
the pressure must satisfy 𝑃(𝑇) = 𝑃0 (𝑇), meaning the pressure must lie on the melting 𝑝 versus
𝑇 curve. All other aspects will be handled automatically. The solid component may be reduced
sufficiently so that both phases become constituents, allowing for one or both phases to be zeroed
out during subsequent iterations. This functionality is already feasible within the existing (𝑇, 𝑉)
algorithm.
We have introduced a vector called InMeltingRegion(KSC), which spans the index of condensed
constituent species. This vector is typically set to 0 for all species; however, if it is set to 1, it
indicates that both the liquid and solid phases can coexist. The goal is to expand the calculations so
that the thermodynamic derivatives for both phases are available for use, and to extend the equation
set to include both phases simultaneously.

17. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This report presents a comprehensive exploration of advanced computational methods for modeling
thermodynamic systems, specifically within the context of the TIGER code. It focuses on the
behavior of phases in equilibrium conditions, particularly addressing the challenges associated
with phase transitions in systems where solid and liquid phases coexist, such as during partial
melting and eutectic conditions.
Key highlights of the report include:

 1. Equilibrium Modeling in TIGER: The report emphasizes the importance of accurately
 defining the stability of solid and liquid phases within the 𝑇-𝑃 space as implemented in the
 TIGER code. It outlines how stability conditions can lead to oscillations in the algorithm if
 not properly managed.
 2. Algorithm Enhancements for TIGER: Proposed modifications to the TIGER computa-
 tional algorithms aim to allow for the coexistence of solid and liquid phases, thereby im-
 proving numerical stability and convergence. The introduction of a vector to track phase
 coexistence is a notable advancement that enhances the code’s functionality.
 3. Handling Eutectic Points: The report discusses the complexities of modeling eutectic
 conditions within the TIGER framework, where the driving force for phase conversion
 approaches zero. It suggests quadrant-based strategies to simplify calculations and ensure
 accurate solutions in these critical scenarios.
 4. Thermodynamic Derivatives: The need for accurate calculations of thermodynamic deriva-
 tives, such as Gibbs free energy changes, is highlighted as essential for diagnosing phase
 stability and ensuring the robustness of the TIGER algorithm.
 5. Implementation Considerations: The report outlines the necessary steps for implementing
 the proposed changes within the TIGER code, including the formulation of new equation sets
 and the establishment of indexing systems to monitor phase behavior.

In conclusion, this report provides a thorough framework for enhancing the TIGER code’s model-
ing capabilities regarding thermodynamic systems involving phase transitions. By addressing the
challenges of phase coexistence and proposing innovative algorithmic solutions, it lays the ground-
work for more reliable and accurate predictions in complex thermodynamic scenarios. These
advancements are crucial for improving operational efficiency and decision-making in research and
development within the field, specifically for users of the TIGER code.
