Thomassen’s proof and Filippov’s proof of the Weak Jordan Theorem
M. Klazar (KAM MFF UK, Praha)

Abstract
We present, in detail and with a modern rigor, the two title proofs. The Weak Jordan Theorem (WJT) states that the complement of any topological circuit in the plane is disconnected.



1
JT, WJT and AT. Auxiliary results

JT, WJT and AT.
Let us begin with a few necessary definitions.
We denote by
I
any compact real interval
I=[a,b]
with
a<b
. If

f\colon I\to X
is a map, we set
f[I]=\{f(x)\colon\;x\in I\}
,
f^{0}=\{f(x)\colon\;a<x<b\}
and

\mathrm{ep}(f)=\{f(a),f(b)\}
.
We also say that
f
joins
f(a)
to
f(b)
in
X
. If
f(a)=f(b)
, we call
f

closed
. If
g\colon A\to B
is any map and
C
is
any
set, we define

g[C]=\{g(x)\colon\;x\in A\cap C\}\,\text{ and }g^{-1}[C]=\{x\in A\colon\;g(x)\in C\}\,.

An
arc
is a continuous injection
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A
circuit
is
a continuous map
f\colon I=[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
f(x)=f(y)
for
x\neq y
if and
only if
\{x,y\}=\{a,b\}
. The following
Jordan Theorem
, or JT,
is a fundamental result in plane topology. It is due to C. Jordan
[
14
, pp. 587–594]
in 1887.

Theorem 1.1
(Jordan, 1887)

For any circuit
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the complement

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]=A\cup B

is a disjoint union of two nonempty open connected sets
A
and
B
such that
A
is bounded,
B
is unbounded, and
\partial A=\partial B=f[I]
.

We explain the statement. We work in the
Euclidean plane
, which is
a metric space

\langle\mathbb{R}^{2},d_{2}\rangle
with the
metric

d_{2}(a,\,b)=\sqrt{(a_{x}-b_{x})^{2}+(a_{y}-b_{y})^{2}}\,,

and in its subspaces. We count among them
\mathbb{R}
and
I
. A set
X\subset M
in a metric space
\langle M,d\rangle
is
disconnected
if there exist open (equivalently, closed) sets
A,B\subset M
such that

A\cup B\supset X\;\&\;A\cap X\neq\emptyset\neq B\cap X\;\&\;A\cap B\cap X=\emptyset

— we say that
A
and
B
cut
X
. Else, if such sets
A
and
B
do not exist,
X
is
connected
.
We take for granted that every real interval (not necessarily compact) is connected. A set

X\subset M
is
bounded
if
X\subset B=B(a,r)
for a ball
B
in
\langle M,d\rangle
. Else, if
X
is not contained in any ball,

X
is
unbounded
. Finally, the
boundary of
X

is the set

\partial X=\{a\in M\colon\;\forall r\,B(a,r)\cap X\neq\emptyset\neq B(a,r)\cap(M\setminus X)\}\,.

In this article, we will not prove the JT, but we present two proofs for
the weaker version, the
Weak Jordan Theorem
, or WJT.

Theorem 1.2
(WJT)

For any circuit
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the complement

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]

is disconnected.

JT
\Rightarrow
WJT because the disjoint union of two nonempty open
sets is disconnected.
The proof of the JT is harder than that of the WJT because it requires the following “lemma”, the
Arc Theorem
, or AT.

Theorem 1.3
(AT)

For any arc
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the complement

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]

is connected.

We write about this overlooked cornerstone of the theory of planar
graphs in a separate contribution
[
15
]
, where we also complete
the presentation of a proof of the JT.

Thomassen’s proof of the WJT.
In 1992, C. Thomassen found in
[
17
]
an
interesting proof
of the
JT based on planar graphs. He actually obtained combinatorial
proofs of the more general
Jordan–Schönflies theorem and of the theorem on classification of surfaces. In
Section
2
we present only a part of
[
17
]
,
Thomassen’s proof of the WJT. It employs the following virtual
configurations.
A
K_{3,3}
-configuration
has two disjoint three-element sets

U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and nine
arcs
f_{u,v}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
for
u\in U
and
v\in V
such that

f_{u,v}
joins
u
to
v
and

f_{u,\,v}^{0}\cap f_{u^{\prime},\,v^{\prime}}^{0}=\emptyset=f_{u,\,v}^{0}\cap(U\cup V)

for every two pairs
\langle u,v\rangle\neq\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle
. It
is a plane drawing, without crossings, of the complete bipartite
graph
K_{3,3}
; these configurations do not exist. A PL
map
is a map
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
for a partition
a=a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}=b
of
[a,b]
, all restrictions
f\,|\,[a_{i-1},a_{i}]
for
i=1,2,\dots,k
are non-constant linear maps. Every PL map is continuous. The straight segments

f[\,[a_{i-1},a_{i}]\,]

(
\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
)
are the
segments of
f[I]
. The points
f(a_{i})
(
\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
) are
the
corners of
f[I]
.
In a PL
K_{3,3}
-configuration
, the nine arcs are PL maps. Thomassen’s proof of the WJT in

[
17
]
, that we present in Section
2
, is the sequence claim 1
\Rightarrow

claim 2
\Rightarrow
claim 3
\Rightarrow
claim 4, where the four claims are as follows.

1.

The Intermediate JT holds for PL circuits: the complement of any PL
circuit is a disjoint union of two nonempty open connected sets.

2.

PL
K_{3,3}
-configurations do not exist.

3.

K_{3,3}
-configurations do not exist.

4.

The WJT holds.

Thomassen’s main invention is the last implication claim 3
\Rightarrow
claim 4.

Filippov’s proof of the WJT.
Another proof of the WJT is contained in the
proof of the JT given by A. F. Filippov in
[
5
]
in 1950. We
present this proof in Section
3
. We divide it in three steps.

1.

Let
f,g\colon I=[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be PL maps such that

f[I]\cap g[I]=\emptyset
and that either
f
is closed or

g[I]
lies between the two vertical lines going through the points
f(a)

and
f(b)
. Consider the function

N=N(c)=N(c,\,f)\colon g[I]\to\{0,1\}

such that

N(c)
is the parity of the number of simple intersections of the vertical
half-line going up from the point
c
with
f[I]
(we provide a precise
definition later). Then
N
is constant.

2.

The function
N(c,f)
is additive in the variable
f
. If
f
is not closed
and the point
c
lies between the two vertical lines and below all
intersections of the line
x=c_{x}
with
f[I]
, then
N(c)=1
.

3.

Let
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a circuit. The results in steps 1
and 2 are used to obtain two distinct points
c,d\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]
such
that for every PL map
g
joining
c
to
d
we have
g[I]\cap f[I]\neq\emptyset
. This means that the
complement
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]
is disconnected and the WJT holds.

Why this presentation of the two proofs?
They are beautiful,
but they are not rigorous by modern standards. Since we like them, we
decided to
elevate them to the present level of rigor. See also the discussion in
Section
4
.

Auxiliary results.
In the rest of Section
1
we present,
with proofs, auxiliary results that will be needed in Sections
2

and
3
. Since they are mostly standard, the impatient reader may
now jump to the two proofs and check
any auxiliary result only if the need arises.

We begin with three constructions related to maps. Let
I=[a,b]
and
J=[c,d]

be real intervals with
a<b
and
c<d
, and let
f\colon I\to X
be
a map. We can parametrize

f[I]
by
J

via the map
g=f(h)\colon J\to X
, where
h\colon J\to I
is
the linear homeomorphism
h(x)=\frac{b-a}{d-c}(x-c)+a
. Clearly,
g

joins
g(c)=f(a)
to
g(d)=f(b)
in
X
. If
f
is a PL map, then so is
g
, and
the same holds for being a continuous map, an arc, or a circuit. Thus we
can use any compact interval
I=[\alpha,\beta]
,
\alpha<\beta
, as the
common definition domain of PL maps, (PL) arcs, and (PL) circuits.

Let
f,g\colon I=[a,b]\to X
be two maps such that
f(b)=g(a)
. We write

f+g
for their concatenation
h=f+g\colon I\to X

given by
h(x)=f(2x-a)
for
a\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}(a+b)
, and by
h(x)=g(2x-b)
for
\frac{1}{2}(a+b)\leq x\leq b
.
Clearly,
h
joins
h(a)=f(a)
to
h(b)=g(b)
in
X
. If
f

and
g
are PL maps, then so is
h
, and the same holds for continuity. Let
in addition
f[I]\cap g[\,(a,b]\,]=\emptyset
, and let

f
and
g
be arcs. Then
f+g
is an arc. Let in addition
f[I]\cap g^{0}=\emptyset
,
g(b)=f(a)
, and let
f
and
g
be arcs. Then
f+g
is a circuit.

Finally, let
U
,
V
, and

\{f_{u,v}\colon\;u\in V,v\in V\}
be a
K_{3,3}
-configuration. For
any of the arcs
f_{u,v}\colon I=[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we denote by
f_{v,u}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
the reverse arc

f_{v,\,u}(x)=f_{u,\,v}(a+b-x)\,.

The first two auxiliary results concern closed sets in metric spaces.
Recall that a set
X\subset M
in a metric space

\langle M,d\rangle
is
closed
if the complement
M\setminus X
is
an open set, that is, if for every point
a\in M\setminus X
there exists an
r>0

such that the ball
B(a,r)\subset X\setminus M
. It is well known that
X\subset M
is closed if and only if for every sequence
(a_{n})\subset X
with
\lim a_{n}=a\in M
we have
a\in X
.

Proposition 1.4

In any metric space, the union of two closed sets is a closed set.

Proof.
Let
X,Y\subset M
be closed sets in a metric space
\langle M,d\rangle
,
and let

a\in M\setminus(X\cup Y)=(M\setminus X)\cap(M\setminus Y)\,.

We have
B(a,r)\subset M\setminus X
and
B(a,r^{\prime})\subset M\setminus Y
for some
r,r^{\prime}>0
. Thus

B(a,\,r^{\prime\prime})\subset(M\setminus X)\cap(M\setminus Y)

for
r^{\prime\prime}=\min(r,r^{\prime})
(
>0
). We see that
X\cup Y
is closed.

\Box

Proposition 1.5

Let
\ell\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a vertical half-line going up or down from a point

c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then
\ell
is a closed set.

Proof.
Suppose that
\ell
goes down (if it goes up, we argue similarly) and that

a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\ell
. If
a_{x}=c_{x}
, we define
r=a_{y}-c_{y}

(
>0
), and if
a_{x}\neq c_{x}
, we set
r=|a_{x}-c_{x}|
(
>0
). Then

d_{2}(a,b)\geq r
for every point
b\in\ell
, and therefore

B(a,r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\ell
.

\Box

The use of the positivity of the distance between two disjoint plane
sets, one
of which is compact and the other is closed but
non-compact (for example, a half-line), is an interesting maneuver in
Filippov’s proof.

The second group of auxiliary results concerns compactness in metric spaces.
Recall that a set
X\subset M
in a metric space

\langle M,d\rangle
is
compact
if every sequence in
X
has a convergent
subsequence with a limit in
X
. We take for granted the compactness of
intervals
I=[a,b]
(
\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
).

Proposition 1.6

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
and
\langle N,e\rangle
be metric spaces,
f\colon M\to N
be a continuous map, and let
X\subset M
be a compact set. Then
f[X]
is a compact subset of
N
.

Proof.
Let
(b_{n})\subset f[X]
be a sequence. Using the axiom of choice, we select
elements
a_{n}\in X
such that
f(a_{n})=b_{n}
. By
the compactness of
X
, there is a subsequence
(a_{m_{n}})
of
(a_{n})

with
\lim a_{m_{n}}=a\in X
. Since
f
is continuous,

\lim b_{m_{n}}=\lim f(a_{m_{n}})=f(\lim a_{m_{n}})=f(a)\in f[X]\,.

\Box

Proposition 1.7

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
be a metric space and
X\subset M
be a compact set. Then
X
is a closed and bounded set.

Proof.
We show that if
X
is not closed or not bounded, then it is not compact.
If
X
is not closed, then there is a sequence
(a_{n})\subset X
such that
\lim a_{n}=a\in M\setminus X
.
Such
(a_{n})
does not have any subsequence with a limit in
X
. If

X
is not bounded, then there exists a sequence
(a_{n})\subset X
such that

d(a_{m},a_{n})\geq 1
for every
m<n
. Such
(a_{n})
does not have any convergent
subsequence.

\Box

Proposition 1.8

For any continuous map
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the complement

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]
is an open set.

Proof.
By Proposition
1.6
, the image
f[I]
is a compact subset of

\mathbb{R}^{2}
. By Proposition
1.7
, this
image is a closed set.

\Box

Proposition 1.9

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
be a metric space,
X\subset M
be a closed set, and
let
f\colon I=[a,b]\to M
be a continuous map.
If
f[I]\cap X\neq\emptyset
, then the subset
f^{-1}[X]
of
I
has both
minimum and maximum.

Proof.
Since the nonempty set
f^{-1}[X]
is closed and bounded,
\inf(f^{-1}[X])

and
\sup(f^{-1}[X])
are elements of it.

\Box

Recall that two sets
X,Y\subset M
in a metric space

\langle M,d\rangle
have distance

d(X,\,Y)=\inf(\{d(x,\,y)\colon\;x\in X,\,y\in Y\})\ \ (\in[0,\,+\infty))\,.

For
X=\{x\}
we write just
d(x,Y)
instead of
d(\{x\},Y)
.

Proposition 1.10

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
be a metric space and let

X,Y\subset M
be nonempty compact sets that are disjoint. Then
d(X,Y)>0
.

We omit the proof because we prove a stronger result.

Proposition 1.11

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
be a metric space and let

X,Y\subset M
be nonempty sets such that
X
is compact,
Y
is closed and
X\cap Y=\emptyset
. Then
d(X,Y)>0
.

Proof.
For the contrary, let
d(X,Y)=0
. Then there exist
sequences
(x_{n})\subset X
and
(y_{n})\subset Y
such that
\lim d(x_{n},y_{n})=0
.
Since
X
is compact, we may assume that
\lim x_{n}=x\in X
. Using the
triangle inequality, we see that also

\lim y_{n}=x
. Since
Y
is closed,
x\in Y
. Thus
x\in X\cap Y
, which
contradicts the disjointness of
X
and
Y
.

\Box

In Thomassen’s proof, Proposition
1.10

suffices. Filippov’s proof uses also
Proposition
1.11
.

Recall that a map
f\colon M\to N
between metric spaces
\langle M,d\rangle
and
\langle N,e\rangle
is
continuous at a point
a\in M
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that
f[B(a,\delta)]\subset B(f(a),\varepsilon)
. The map
f
is
continuous
if it is continuous at
every point in
M
. The map
f
is
uniformly continuous
if for
every
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that
d(x,y)\leq\delta

\Rightarrow

e(f(x),f(y))\leq\varepsilon
for every
x,y\in M
. It is easy to show that for every
continuous map
f
and every open (closed) set
X\subset N
the preimage
f^{-1}[X]
is
an open (closed) set in
M
. If
f
is continuous at
a
then for
every sequence
(a_{n})\subset M
with
\lim a_{n}=a
we have
\lim f(a_{n})=f(a)
.

Proposition 1.12

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
and
\langle N,e\rangle
be metric spaces, the
former being compact, and let
f\colon M\to N
be a continuous map. Then
f

is uniformly continuous.

Proof.
Suppose, for the contrary, that
f
is not uniformly continuous. Then there
exist
\varepsilon>0
and sequences
(a_{n}),(b_{n})\subset M
such that
\lim d(a_{n},b_{n})=0
, but
e(f(a_{n}),f(b_{n}))\geq\varepsilon
for every

n
. Due to the compactness of
M
, we may assume that
\lim a_{n}=\lim b_{n}=a

(
\in M
). But
\lim f(a_{n})=\lim f(b_{n})=f(a)
(
\in N
) does not hold, in
contradiction with the continuity of
f
at
a
.

\Box

The third group of auxiliary results concerns connectedness.

Proposition 1.13

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
and
\langle N,e\rangle
be metric spaces,
X\subset M
be a connected set and
f\colon M\to N
be
a continuous map. Then
f[X]
is a connected subset of
N
.

Proof.
We show that if
f[X]
is disconnected, then so is
X
. Let

A,B\subset N
be open sets cutting
f[X]
. Since
f
is continuous, the
subsets
f^{-1}[A]
and
f^{-1}[B]
of
M
are open. It is easy to see that
they cut
X
.

\Box

A graph
G=\langle V,E\rangle
(
E\subset\binom{V}{2}
) is usually defined as
connected
if every two vertices
u,v\in V
can be joined in

G
by a path (equivalently, by a walk). Else
G
is
disconnected
. Recall that a
partition
of a set
X
is a set
Y
such that
the elements of
Y
are nonempty and mutually disjoint sets such that
\bigcup Y=X
.

Proposition 1.14

A graph
G=\langle V,E\rangle
is disconnected if and only if there exists a partition
\{A,B\}
of
V
such that no edge
e\in E
joins
A
and
B
.

Proof.
Suppose that
G
is disconnected and that
u,v\in V
cannot be joined in
G
by any path. We set

A=\{w\in V\colon\;\text{a~path in $G$ joins $u$ to $w$}\}\,\text{ and }\,B=V\setminus A\,.

Then
u\in A
,
v\in B
and no edge
e\in E
joins
A
and
B
. So
\{A,B\}
is the desired partition of
V
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are as stated, and take any
u\in A

and
v\in B
. Any path in
G
joining
u
and
v
would contain an edge

e\in E
joining
A
and
B
, which means that there is no such path. We see that
G
is disconnected.

\Box

The
intersection graph
corresponding to a set system
X_{v}
,
v\in V
, is the graph
\langle V,E\rangle

with the edges
E=\{\{u,v\}\colon\;u,v\in V,u\neq v,X_{u}\cap X_{v}\neq\emptyset\}
.

Proposition 1.15

Let
\langle M,d\rangle
be a metric space, let

X_{v}\subset M
,
v\in V
, be a set system of connected sets
and let the intersection graph
G=\langle V,E\rangle
be connected. Then the set

X=\bigcup_{v\in V}X_{v}\ \ (\subset M)

is connected.

Proof.
Suppose that
X
is disconnected and that the sets
A,B\subset M
cut
X
. Every set
X_{v}
is completely contained either in
A
or
in
B
, and we get the partition
\{V_{A},V_{B}\}
of
V
by the sets

V_{A}=\{v\in V\colon\;X_{v}\subset A\}\,\text{ and }\,V_{B}=\{v\in V\colon\;X_{v}\subset B\}\,.

Since
A\cap B\cap X=\emptyset
, there is no edge
e\in E
joining
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
. Hence
G
is disconnected by Proposition
1.14
.

\Box

We prove, as an application, that balls in the plane are connected.

Proposition 1.16

Every ball in the metric space
\langle\mathbb{R}^{2},d_{2}\rangle
is connected.

Proof.
Let
B=B(b,r)=\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\;d_{2}(a,b)<r\}
be a ball in the Euclidean
plane. For any point
a\in B\setminus\{b\}
we denote by
S_{a}
the straight
segment joining
b
and
a
. Every
S_{a}
is connected by
Proposition
1.13
, as a continuous image of an interval.
Thus

{\textstyle B=\bigcup_{a\in B\setminus\{b\}}S_{a}}

is connected by Proposition
1.15
(the intersection graph is complete).

\Box

In general, balls in metric spaces need not be connected.

The following proposition is the most intricate of the auxiliary results.
We need a lemma for it.

Lemma 1.17

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open set, let
u,v\in X
with
u\neq v
, and let there be a PL arc joining
u
to
v
in
X
. Then there exists
a
v
-centered ball
B\subset X
such that for every point
w\in B
there
exists a PL arc joining
u
to
w
in
X
.

Proof.
Let
f\colon I=[a,b]\to X
be a PL arc
joining
u
to
v
in
X
, let
a=a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}=b
be the
partition of
[a,b]
, and let
s_{i}=f[\,[a_{i-1},a_{i}]\,]
for

i=1,2,\dots,k
be the segments of
f[I]
. Since
d_{2}(s_{i},v)>0
for

i=1,2,\dots,k-1
(Propositions
1.6
and
1.10
), we can take small enough
r>0
such
that
B=B(v,r)\subset X
and
s_{i}\cap B=\emptyset
for
i=1,2,\dots,k-1
. We
define for every point
w\in B
a PL arc
f_{w}
joining
u
to
w
in
X
.
If
w\in B\cap s_{k}
, then
f_{w}
is the initial part of
f
ending at
w
. If

w\in B\setminus s_{k}
, then
f_{w}
is the extension of
f
by the
straight segment joining
v
to
w
. It is easy to see that
f_{w}
is as
claimed.

\Box

Proposition 1.18

Let

s_{i}\subset\ell(c)
. It
follows that

P(c)=P(c,\,f):=f^{-1}[\ell(c)\cap f[I]\,]\ \ (\subset[a,\,b])

is a finite disjoint union of
components
z
such that every
z
is either a subinterval
[a_{i},a_{j}]
(
\subset[a,b]
) with
0\leq i<j\leq k
, where for non-closed
f
the first and last inequality is
strict, or a point in
(a,b)
. We call
z
a
simple component
if the values
f(t)
for
t
immediately before and after
z
lie on different
sides of
\ell(c)
. Else, if they lie on the same side of
\ell(c)
, we call

z
a
double component
. We define

\text{$N(c)=$ the parity of the number of simple components of $P(c)$}\ \ (\in\{0,\,1\})\,.

We prove that the parity map
N
is continuous on
S(f)
. Let
c\in S(f)
. We take
a
\delta>0
small enough such that the ball
B=B(c,\delta)\subset S(f)
(Proposition
1.8
) and that no
vertical line that intersects
B
contains a corner of
f[I]

distinct from those whose preimages appear in
P(c)
. Let
c^{\prime}\in B
with
c^{\prime}\neq c
.
As we move
c
to
c^{\prime}
, every simple component of
P(c)
is preserved
and every double component either does not change or disappears or
decays in two
simple (point) components. It follows that
N(c)=N(c^{\prime})
.
So
N
is continuous at
c
and on
S(f)
. We obtain the main result of
Step 1:

\text{the parity map $N$ is constant on the set $g[I]$}\,.

Indeed, since
g[I]\subset S(f)
, the map
N(g)\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and
N(g)[I]\subset\{0,1\}
. If
N(g)[I]=\{0,1\}
, we contradict Proposition
1.13
.

Step 2. Two properties of the parity map
N
.
The first property is
the additivity of
N(c,f)
in the variable
f
. Let
f_{1},f_{2}\colon I=[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be two non-closed PL maps such that
f_{1}(b)=f_{2}(a)
. Let
f_{3}=f_{1}+f_{2}
and let

c\in S(f_{1})\cap S(f_{2})\cap S(f_{3})
. Then

N(c,\,f_{3})=N(c,\,f_{1})+N(c,\,f_{2})\ \ (\mathrm{mod}\;2)\,.

Here is an outline of the proof of this equality: if
S_{i}
is the set of simple components in
P(c,f_{i})
,

i=1,2,3
, then there is a bijection

\beta\colon S_{3}\to(S_{1}\times\{0\})\cup(S_{2}\times\{1\})\,.

We leave the details of
\beta
to the reader as an exercise.

We proceed to the second property of
N
. Let
f\colon I=[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a non-closed PL map
and let
c\in S(f)
lie below all intersections of the line
x=c_{x}
with

f[I]
. Then

N(c,\,f)=1\,.

To prove it, we consider
the partition
a=a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}=b
of the domain of
f
and the components

z_{1}<z_{2}<\dots<z_{l}
of

P(c,f)=f^{-1}[\ell(c)\cap f[I]\,]=f^{-1}[(x=c_{x})\cap f[I]\,]\,.

As we know, every component
z_{i}
is an interval
[a_{r_{i}},a_{s_{i}}]
(
\subset[a,b]
) with

0<r_{i}<s_{i}<k
or a point
\{t_{i}\}
with
t_{i}\in(a,b)
. It is clear that

P(c,f)\neq\emptyset
and
l\geq 1
(Proposition
1.22
). We
call the
l+1
open subintervals

g_{0}=(a_{0},\min(z_{1})),\,g_{1}=(\max(z_{1}),\min(z_{2})),\,\dots,\,g_{l}=(\max(z_{l}),\,,a_{k})

of
(a,b)

gaps
. It follows from the assumption on
c
that for every
gap
g_{i}
and every
t\in g_{i}
, all values
f(t)
lie on only one side of

\ell(c)
, the left side
L
or the right side
R
.
We accordingly assign to
P(c,f)
a word
w_{0}w_{1}\dots w_{l}\in\{L,R\}^{l+1}
. For
i=1,2,\dots,l
, the transition
w_{i-1}w_{i}
with

w_{i-1}=w_{i}

corresponds to a double component of
P(c,f)
, and that with

w_{i-1}\neq w_{i}
to a simple component.
Since
w_{0}\neq w_{l}
, the number of the latter transitions is odd.

Step 3. Some two points in the complement of any circuit are separated.
Let
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be
a circuit and
D=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]
. We may assume that
f[I]
lies to the
right of the
y
-axis (Propositions
1.6
and
1.7
). Let
l\in f[I]
and
p\in f[I]
be, respectively,
a leftmost and a rightmost point in
f[I]
(Propositions
1.6
and
1.19
). These points divide
f
in two arcs
f_{1},f_{2}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
f_{1}^{0}\cap f_{2}^{0}=\emptyset
,
f_{1}[I]\cup f_{2}[I]=f[I]
and
\mathrm{ep}(f_{1})=\mathrm{ep}(f_{2})=\{l,p\}
(Proposition
1.21
). Clearly,
l_{x}<p_{x}
(Proposition
1.20
).
Let
\ell
be any vertical line with
x
-coordinate
in
(l_{x},p_{x})
. Let
a\in f[I]\cap\ell
be the highest point of this intersection
(Proposition
1.19
). We
may assume that
a\in f_{1}^{0}
. Similarly, let
b\in f_{1}^{0}\cap\ell

be the lowest point of this intersection (Proposition
1.19
). We take a point
c\in\ell
below
b
but such that

0<d_{2}(c,\,b)<d_{2}(b,f_{2}[I])

— then
c\in D
. The point
c
exists by Propositions
1.6
and
1.10
,
by which
d_{2}(b,f_{2}[I])>0
.
We show that for every PL map
g\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
that joins
c\in D
to the origin

\overline{0}=\langle 0,0\rangle\in D
(
f[I]
lies to the right of the
y
-axis),
g[I]\cap f[I]\neq\emptyset
.
In view of Propositions
1.8
and
1.18
it means that
the complement
D=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus f[I]
is disconnected.

Suppose for the contrary that
g\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a PL map joining
c
to

\overline{0}
in
D
, so that
g[I]\cap f[I]=\emptyset
. Let

\kappa
(
\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
) be the half-line going down from

and let

\lambda=c\,b\cup f_{3}[I]\cup\ell(a)\ \ (\subset\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,

where
cb
is the straight segment joining these two points,
f_{3}
is
a subarc of
f_{1}
joining
b
to
a
(if
b=a
, we omit
f_{3}[I]
) and, as
before,
\ell(a)
is the half-line going up from
a
. Let

be the
minimum of the three positive distances

d_{2}(g[I],\,f[I])>0,\ d_{2}(\kappa,\,f_{1}[I])>0\,\text{ and }\,d_{2}(\lambda,\,f_{2}[I])>0\,.

The first distance is positive by the assumption that

and by
Propositions
1.6
and
1.10
. The latter two distances are positive by

1.4
,
1.5
,

1.6
,
1.7
, and

.

We approximate the given circuit
f\colon I=[\alpha,\beta]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
sufficiently

exists a partition

\alpha=a_{0}<a_{1}<\dots<a_{k}=\beta

\{a,b,l,p\}\subset\{f(a_{i})\colon\;i=0,1,\dots,k\}

d_{2}(f(a_{i-1}),\,f(a_{i}))<h\,\text{ for every }\,i=1,\,2,\,\dots,\,k\,.

We join for

and include the four points

,

f_{4}[I]\cap g[I]=\emptyset,\ f_{5}[I]\cap\kappa=\emptyset,\,\text{ and }\,f_{6}[I]\cap(cb\cup f_{7}[I]\cup\ell(a))=\emptyset\,.

The parity function

The first equality follows from the fact that since

4
Concluding remarks

Jordan’s proof of his theorem in
[
14
]
was often questioned in the past, and it was claimed that the first correct proof of the JT was given by
Veblen
[
18
]
. Hales
[
8
]
reversed these
assessments and showed that Jordan’s proof is basically correct when some gaps are
filled, but that Veblen’s proof has serious problems. Filippov’s article

[
5
]
is preceded by the article
[
19
]
by A. I. Vol’pert, which
contains a similar proof of the JT. The footnote on the first page of

[
5
]
reads “After this work was completed, the author learned
that a proof of Jordan’s theorem, very close to the one in this article,
was somewhat earlier found by A. I. Vol’pert (Lviv)”. Authors of these two proofs,
Aleksei F. Filippov (1923–2006)
and
Aizik I. Volpert (Vol’pert)
(1923–2006)
, were excellent mathematicians — see
[
2
,
1
]
.

In recent years, the ways in which we (mathematicians and
philosophers) think about the links between informal and formal
proofs, and about the
role of formalized mathematics, have changed considerably and dramatically
compared to the times before, say, 2000: see, for example,

[
3
,
4
,
6
,
7
]
. What should be the results of the work of
a research mathematician? A possible answer is: rigorous proofs of
mathematical theorems. An informal proof is rigorous if it can be routinely
translated into a formal proof (
[
11
]
), formal in the sense of
the previously mentioned developments; see also
[
9
,
10
]
. In this
article, we wanted to lift the beautiful proofs of Thomassen and
Filippov to the modern level of rigor, and we hopefully succeeded. The
preprint
[
3
]
reports on the process of autoformalization of
Munkres’ textbook
[
16
]
on topology. It is an impressive
achievement; it seems that very soon AI will do everything for us. But does this
autoformalization help a student interested in topology learn and
understand (
[
12
,
13
]
) the proofs of theorems in
[
16
]
?
Not much. In contrast, we hope that our article will be
helpful to anyone wishing to understand the two proofs of the WJT.

I lectured on Thomassen’s proof of the WJT in the course
Mathematical
Analysis 3
in the years 2023–2026. For the next year, I plan to switch to
the proofs of the JT in
[
5
,
19
]
.
