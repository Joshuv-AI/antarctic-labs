Facial nerve schwannoma of the tympanic segment with geniculate ganglion involvement: Diagnostic and therapeutic challenges
Authors: Ayman Elmahjoubi, Younes Banana, Saad Bouchlarhem, Mohamed Boutahar, Mohammed Amine Rabhi, Achraf Amine Sbai, Drissia Benfadil, Azeddine Lachkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFacial nerve schwannoma is a rare benign tumor arising from Schwann cells of the facial nerve, most commonly involving the geniculate ganglion and intratemporal segments, and often presenting with nonspecific clinical features that may delay diagnosis. We report the case of a 35-year-old woman presenting with progressive left-sided facial nerve palsy evolving over 15 months, ultimately classified as House–Brackmann grade VI. High-resolution computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a well-defined lesion centered on the tympanic segment of the facial nerve with extension to the geniculate ganglion, showing characteristic enhancement without vascular flow voids. A transcanal endoscopic biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of schwannoma with positive S100 and SOX10 immunostaining. Given the advanced facial nerve dysfunction, a staged management strategy including delayed hypoglossal–facial nerve anastomosis was adopted. This case highlights the importance of early radiological evaluation in atypical facial nerve palsy and emphasizes that management should be individualized based on preoperative facial nerve function, which remains the main prognostic factor.Keywords: Facial nerve schwannoma, Tympanic segment, Geniculate ganglion, Magnetic resonance imaging, Facial nerve palsy

Introduction

Facial nerve schwannoma is a rare benign tumor arising from Schwann cells of the facial nerve, accounting for approximately 0.15%-0.8% of intracranial schwannomas. It may involve any segment of the nerve, with a predilection for the geniculate ganglion and intratemporal portions, resulting in a wide spectrum of clinical presentations. Because of its slow growth and the complex anatomy of the facial nerve, diagnosis is often delayed, and symptoms may remain nonspecific or misleading for long periods [1].

Clinical manifestations are variable and depend on tumor location and extent. Progressive facial nerve dysfunction is the most common presentation, although hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, or otalgia may also occur. Importantly, facial nerve function may be preserved even in advanced stages, making early diagnosis challenging [2].

Imaging plays a pivotal role in the diagnostic approach. High-resolution computed tomography is useful for detecting smooth expansion of the facial canal, while magnetic resonance imaging allows precise characterization of the lesion and its extension, as well as differentiation from other pathologies. Management remains controversial and should be individualized, with treatment decisions primarily guided by preoperative facial nerve function. We report a case of intratemporal facial nerve schwannoma involving the tympanic segment and geniculate ganglion, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic considerations [3].

Case presentation

The patient was a 35-year-old woman with no significant past medical or surgical history who was referred for evaluation of a progressive left-sided peripheral facial nerve palsy evolving over a 15-month period. The facial paralysis had gradually worsened and was classified as House–Brackmann grade VI at presentation, indicating complete facial nerve dysfunction. No constitutional symptoms or signs of systemic disease were reported.

On otologic examination, otoscopy revealed a retrotympanic mass occupying the left middle ear cavity, partially obscuring normal landmarks, without associated otorrhea or signs of middle ear infection. Flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy showed no abnormalities.

High-resolution computed tomography of the temporal bones demonstrated a well-circumscribed soft-tissue lesion filling the left tympanic cavity (Fig. 1). The lesion extended along the course of the facial nerve toward the geniculate ganglion and was associated with smooth expansion of the facial canal, without aggressive bone destruction. The lesion was in close contact with the dura of the middle cranial fossa, with preserved contours and no evidence of intracranial invasion.

Axial computed tomography scan of the temporal bones demonstrating a soft-tissue mass occupying the left middle ear cavity (white arrow), associated with smooth expansile remodeling of the facial canal (arrowhead), suggestive of involvement of the tympanic segment of the facial nerve.

Magnetic resonance imaging further characterized the lesion as isointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with strong and homogeneous post-contrast enhancement (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The absence of flow voids and the presence of smooth bony remodeling favored the diagnosis of a facial nerve schwannoma, while vascular lesions such as paraganglioma and facial nerve hemangioma were considered unlikely.

Axial contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating a well-defined enhancing lesion along the left tympanic segment of the facial nerve (white arrow), without evidence of vascular flow voids.

Coronal T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging after gadolinium administration showing a well-circumscribed enhancing lesion involving the left tympanic segment of the facial nerve and extending toward the geniculate ganglion (white arrow).

Given the progressive deterioration of facial nerve function and the imaging findings, a minimally invasive transcanal endoscopic approach was performed to obtain a diagnostic biopsy while minimizing additional neural injury. Intraoperatively, a friable, vascularized mass occupying the tympanic cavity was identified. Histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed typical Antoni A and Antoni B patterns, and immunohistochemistry showed strong positivity for S100 and SOX10 proteins, confirming the diagnosis of schwannoma (Fig. 4).

Histopathological examination (hematoxylin and eosin staining) showing intersecting fascicles with alternating hypercellular and hypocellular areas corresponding to Antoni A and Antoni B patterns (white arrows).

Considering the long-standing complete facial nerve paralysis and the high risk of further morbidity associated with radical tumor resection, a conservative staged management strategy was adopted. The patient was scheduled for hypoglossal–facial nerve anastomosis three months after the initial procedure to optimize facial reanimation outcomes. The postoperative course was uneventful. Clinical and radiological follow-up has been planned at 3 and 6 months to assess facial nerve function and monitor tumor stability.

Discussion

Facial nerve schwannoma is a benign neoplasm arising from Schwann cells that envelop the facial nerve fibers. Despite its indolent and non-invasive nature, it represents a complex clinical entity due to the functional importance of the facial nerve and the anatomical intricacy of its course. The tumor may remain clinically silent for years or present with progressive neurological deficits, making early diagnosis challenging. The primary objective in managing facial nerve schwannoma is to achieve adequate tumor control while preserving facial nerve function, which has a profound impact on patients’ quality of life [4].

Facial nerve schwannoma is exceedingly rare, accounting for approximately 0.15%-0.8% of all intracranial schwannomas. It typically affects adults in the third to fifth decades of life and may arise along any segment of the facial nerve, with a predilection for the geniculate ganglion and intratemporal segments. The rarity of the disease and the heterogeneity of reported cases have limited the establishment of standardized management guidelines [5].

From a pathophysiological perspective, these tumors exhibit slow, expansile growth leading to progressive nerve compression rather than direct neural invasion. Intratemporal lesions are more likely to produce early and severe facial nerve dysfunction due to the confined anatomy of the fallopian canal, whereas extratemporal lesions may remain asymptomatic for longer periods [6].

Clinically, presentation is highly variable and depends on tumor location and extent. Progressive facial nerve palsy is the most common manifestation, although associated symptoms such as hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, or otalgia may occur. Imaging plays a central role in diagnosis, with computed tomography demonstrating smooth bony expansion and magnetic resonance imaging showing a well-defined enhancing lesion without vascular flow voids. Histopathological confirmation typically reveals Antoni A and B patterns with strong S100 and SOX10 immunoreactivity [7].

The differential diagnosis includes vestibular schwannoma, paraganglioma, facial nerve hemangioma, meningioma, cholesteatoma, and malignant tumors with perineural spread. In our case, the absence of flow voids and the presence of smooth bony remodeling favored schwannoma over vascular lesions such as paraganglioma or hemangioma, which typically exhibit more aggressive bone changes or characteristic imaging patterns [8].

Management remains controversial and should be individualized according to tumor characteristics and, most importantly, preoperative facial nerve function. Observation is generally recommended for small tumors with preserved function, whereas surgical intervention is considered in cases of progressive symptoms or advanced dysfunction. In the present case, radical tumor resection was not pursued due to the presence of long-standing complete facial paralysis (House–Brackmann grade VI) and the high risk of additional morbidity. Instead, a minimally invasive diagnostic biopsy followed by a planned hypoglossal–facial nerve anastomosis was considered the most appropriate strategy to optimize functional recovery [9].

This case highlights several important clinical considerations. First, it illustrates the potential for delayed diagnosis in facial nerve schwannoma, even in the presence of progressive neurological symptoms. Second, it emphasizes the key role of imaging in guiding diagnosis and excluding differential diagnoses. Finally, it underlines the importance of a functional-oriented management strategy, particularly in advanced cases where facial nerve preservation is no longer feasible.

The natural history of facial nerve schwannoma is characterized by slow progression and gradual functional deterioration. Prognosis is primarily determined by preoperative facial nerve function, with better outcomes observed in patients treated before severe dysfunction occurs. Following nerve reconstruction procedures, the best achievable functional outcome is typically House–Brackmann grade III. Early diagnosis and individualized management remain essential to optimize long-term outcomes and quality of life [10].

Conclusion

Facial nerve schwannoma is a rare entity that may present with nonspecific and progressive symptoms, leading to delayed diagnosis. This case illustrates the key role of imaging in identifying characteristic features and guiding diagnostic orientation, particularly in atypical presentations of facial nerve palsy. It also highlights the importance of individualized, function-oriented management, especially in advanced stages with complete facial paralysis, where reconstructive strategies may offer better functional outcomes than radical tumor resection. Early recognition and appropriate therapeutic decision-making remain essential to optimize patient prognosis and quality of life.
