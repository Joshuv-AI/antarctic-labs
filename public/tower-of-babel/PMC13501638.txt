Awareness and clinical practices of pulmonologists regarding the periodontal–pulmonary link: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Seval Ceylan Şen, Özlem Saraç Atagün, Gülbahar Ustaoğlu, Şeyma Çardakci Bahar, Tuğçe Paksoy, Burcu Şahi̇n Dedeoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis and chronic respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, are chronic inflammatory conditions that share common risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms. Accumulating evidence indicates a bidirectional relationship between periodontal inflammation and respiratory disease outcomes; however, the extent to which pulmonologists recognize and incorporate this association into clinical practice remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate pulmonologists’ awareness, perspectives, and clinical practice approaches regarding the relationship between periodontal disease and COPD/asthma.MethodsA questionnaire-based, cross-sectional study was conducted among 111 pulmonologists. The survey assessed demographic characteristics, oral health awareness, knowledge of periodontal–pulmonary interactions, and clinical behaviors related to oral health inquiry and dental referral. Data was analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests to examine associations between responses and participant characteristics.ResultsMost participants (91.9%) lacked formal education on the periodontal–pulmonary link, yet a significant ‘desire-knowledge gap’ emerged, with 68.5% were willing to attend structured training. Although 61.3% reported that patients referring to dentists, 54.1% never inquired about oral symptoms. While clinical awareness was high for inhaler side effects, 66.7% remained uncertain about shared inflammatory mechanisms and the impact of periodontal treatment on COPD exacerbations. Notably, female pulmonologists and those in university settings demonstrated significantly higher knowledge and proactive inquiry patterns.ConclusionPulmonologists exhibit a significant ‘desire-knowledge gap’, characterized by a lack of formal education despite high receptivity to interdisciplinary training. Awareness remains fragmented and largely confined to local inhaler effects, leaving systemic inflammatory risks and clinical inquiry neglected. Integrating oral–systemic health into medical curricula and fostering collaborative referral cultures—particularly in non-academic settings—are essential to bridge this clinical gap and optimize the holistic management of patients with COPD and asthma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04408-7.Keywords: Periodontitis, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), Asthma, Pulmonologists' awareness, Periodontal–pulmonary link, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the progressive destruction of the tooth-supporting tissues, arising from complex interactions between bacterial dental plaque and the host immune response [1]. Although bacterial plaques are recognized as the primary etiological factor, periodontitis is widely accepted as a multifactorial disease influenced by a broad range of local, systemic, and environmental determinants. In addition to regional factors such as dental plaque and calculus, disease onset and progression are modulated by genetic susceptibility, systemic health conditions, advancing age, lifestyle-related behaviors, and various social determinants. Importantly, the pathogenic effects of periodontopathogen microorganisms are not limited to the periodontium but may also contribute to systemic inflammatory burden, thereby adversely affecting overall health [2].

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable, and treatable respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation that is usually progressive and associated with a chronic inflammatory response of the airways and lungs to harmful particles or gases [3]. Respiratory comorbidities commonly associated with COPD include bronchiectasis, asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, and lung cancer; however, extra-thoracic chronic inflammatory diseases are also frequently observed in affected individuals [3, 4]. Globally, COPD affects approximately 9–10% of adults aged over 40 years and is responsible for nearly 3 million deaths annually [5]. Cigarette smoking is well established as the primary risk factor for both COPD and asthma [5, 6]; nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that periodontitis may also be associated with an increased risk of COPD development [7].

A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that periodontal diseases affect not only oral health but also systemic health [8–10]. Periodontal diseases have been linked to several systemic conditions, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease, primarily through mechanisms involving low-grade systemic inflammation [7]. Periodontitis and COPD/asthma are chronic, progressive inflammatory diseases that share common risk factors, such as cigarette smoking, advancing age, and low socioeconomic status [6, 11]. Previous studies have consistently reported an association between COPD/asthma, oral health, and periodontal disease [12]. A meta-analysis of 14 observational studies demonstrated that periodontitis was associated with a 2.08-fold increase in the odds of developing COPD (OR = 2.08) [13]. Conversely, compared with healthy individuals, patients with COPD exhibit a 1.2-fold higher risk of developing periodontal disease, which increases to 3.17 among those with a history of hospitalization [14]. Furthermore, the presence of periodontal disease has been associated with poorer pulmonary function in patients with COPD [10, 15].

In this context, it is of considerable importance that physicians, particularly pulmonologists, are aware of the systemic inflammatory burden associated with periodontal diseases. The presence of periodontal disease in individuals with COPD or asthma may not only adversely influence the course of the respiratory condition but also modify the response to treatment. Therefore, routine assessment of periodontal status by pulmonologists and timely referral for dental consultation when indicated are essential components of multidisciplinary patient management. Such an integrated approach may improve control of systemic inflammation and, ultimately, lead to better overall health outcomes in this patient population.

Despite the growing number of studies exploring medical specialists’ awareness of oral–systemic links, there remains a critical shortage of data specifically analyzing the clinical translation of this knowledge in pulmonology [16–18]. Most existing research focuses on broad awareness levels, rather than the specific behavioral barriers that prevent integrating oral health into respiratory care. This study aims to bridge this gap by evaluating pulmonologists’ awareness and examining the discrepancy between their referral behaviors and routine inquiry practices. By identifying a significant ‘desire-knowledge gap’—characterized by a lack of formal education (91.9%) despite high receptivity to training (68.5%)—this research provides a unique justification for systemic curriculum reforms and the establishment of structured interprofessional collaboration cultures.

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate pulmonologists’ awareness, perspectives, and clinical practices regarding the relationship between periodontal disease and the two most prevalent chronic obstructive airway diseases, COPD and asthma. By evaluating these conditions together, the study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of how physicians manage the periodontal–pulmonary link in their routine clinical workload.

Materials and methods

The study focused on COPD and asthma as they represent the primary patient groups for pulmonologists and share common inflammatory pathways and risk factors (such as smoking and systemic inflammation) with periodontitis. This dual focus allows for a broader assessment of interdisciplinary clinical integration. This questionnaire-based study was approved by the Hamidiye Non-Interventional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025/16–26 / Date:19.08.2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki revised in 2013.

The questionnaire was developed methodologically, following survey studies [19, 20], and a comprehensive review of the existing literature on the relationship between chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and periodontitis [3, 4, 19, 20]. To ensure content validity, the initial draft was reviewed and refined by an expert panel of experienced periodontists (SCŞ, GU, ŞÇB, TP, and OSA) for clarity, relevance, and clinical accuracy. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted with 10 pulmonologists (not included in the final analysis) to evaluate the survey’s comprehensibility and feasibility; minor linguistic adjustments were made based on their feedback.

The questionnaire was designed using a deductive approach, in which items were derived directly from established theoretical frameworks and clinical evidence on the periodontal–pulmonary link. To ensure structural validity without the need for preliminary qualitative thematic analysis, an expert-led mapping process was conducted. During this process, the expert panel mapped each survey item to specific domains—Personal Awareness, Clinical Approach, and Knowledge Levels—based on their clinical intent and relevance to the study’s title. This ensured that the instrument specifically addressed the bidirectional relationship between periodontitis and chronic respiratory diseases while maintaining a clear focus on the routine clinical workload of pulmonologists.

The study instrument was validated using a multi-step process to ensure its psychometric robustness. First, content validity was established through an expert panel review (SCŞ, GU, ŞÇB, TP, and OSA), where items were assessed for clinical accuracy and relevance until a unanimous consensus was reached. Second, face validity was evaluated through a pilot study with 10 pulmonologists to ensure the instrument’s comprehensibility and feasibility. Finally, the internal consistency and reliability of the categorical response format were statistically verified using the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), which yielded a coefficient of 0.76. This methodological approach confirms that the questionnaire provided a stable and valid measure of awareness and clinical practices for the target population.

The questionnaire was structured into two main sections. The first section comprised 5 items focused on participants’ demographic and professional characteristics. The second section consisted of a 20-item scale specifically developed to evaluate awareness, knowledge, and clinical behaviors related to oral–pulmonary interactions. To ensure the reliability of this newly constructed scale, internal consistency was assessed using the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20). This statistical method was selected as the most appropriate measure for the instrument’s categorical response format, providing a more robust assessment of the scale’s stability than Cronbach’s alpha. The KR-20 analysis confirmed the scale’s reliability for assessing awareness, knowledge levels, and clinical practices within the study population.

All developed questionnaire items were uploaded to the Google Forms platform (https://docs.google.com/forms) and subsequently distributed via a WhatsApp group. Participants were selected using convenience sampling through a nationwide professional WhatsApp network comprising approximately 200 pulmonologists. The BŞD is a member of this group, which also serves as a platform for exchanging clinical and academic knowledge among experts in the field. A total of 111 pulmonologists voluntarily participated in and completed the survey, resulting in a 55.5% response rate.

Before dissemination, the pulmonologists in the WhatsApp group were informed about the study. After being provided with detailed information on the study’s aim, methodology, and scientific contribution, participants were given informed, voluntary consent forms. Participation was entirely voluntary; pulmonologists who declined were not required to participate. The questionnaire was administered only to those pulmonologists who agreed to participate and completed the informed consent forms. Informed consent to participate was voluntarily obtained from all individual participants in written electronic format. In addition to basic demographic questions (age and sex), the survey included items designed to assess participants’ awareness, perspectives, and clinical practice approaches regarding the relationship between periodontal disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma. The questionnaire included basic demographic questions (e.g., age and gender) as well as items designed to assess participants’ awareness, perspectives, and clinical practice approaches regarding the relationship between periodontal disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study population was selected based on the following specific criteria:

Inclusion Criteria: 1. Being a medical doctor specializing in Pulmonology. 2. Actively managing and following up with patients diagnosed with COPD and/or Asthma in clinical practice. 3. Providing voluntary informed consent to participate in the digital survey.

Inclusion Criteria: 1. Being a medical doctor specializing in Pulmonology. 2. Actively managing and following up with patients diagnosed with COPD and/or Asthma in clinical practice. 3. Providing voluntary informed consent to participate in the digital survey.

Exclusion Criteria: 1. Medical residents or specialists from other disciplines (e.g., Internal Medicine, Cardiology) who do not primarily manage pulmonary cases. 2. Participants who submitted incomplete or inconsistent questionnaire responses. 3. Pulmonologists who are not currently active in clinical practice (e.g., retired or working solely in administrative roles).

Exclusion Criteria: 1. Medical residents or specialists from other disciplines (e.g., Internal Medicine, Cardiology) who do not primarily manage pulmonary cases. 2. Participants who submitted incomplete or inconsistent questionnaire responses. 3. Pulmonologists who are not currently active in clinical practice (e.g., retired or working solely in administrative roles).

Sample size and power analysis

The required sample size was determined using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7). The calculation was based on the primary outcome variable, the pulmonologists’ level of knowledge regarding oral–pulmonary interactions [21]. Accounting for the effect of professional experience (≤ 10 years vs. >10 years) and using a similar study as a reference, we assumed a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.65). With a Type I error rate (α) of 0.05 and a target statistical power (1-β) of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 80 participants [22]. To compensate for potential non-response or incomplete survey submissions, recruitment continued until 111 pulmonologists participated in the study, thereby exceeding the required power.

Statistical analysis

In the calculations, the statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05. Following data collection, all analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages in tables. The questionnaire data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, with results presented as frequencies and relevancies for each categorical response. To ensure the internal consistency and stability of the response patterns across the 20-item survey, the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) was utilized, yielding a coefficient of 0.76. This confirms that the instrument provided a reliable measure of awareness and clinical practices within the study population. The patterns of these responses were further analyzed by examining the percentage distributions listed in the tables, while associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Pearson chi-square test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test, as appropriate. The selection criteria for the test were based on the expected cell counts in the contingency tables: Pearson chi-square was used when the expected frequency in at least 80% of the cells was ≥ 5; otherwise, the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was applied to maintain statistical accuracy. Prior to analysis, all statistical assumptions, including the independence of observations and the adequacy of expected frequencies, were formally tested. To address concerns regarding multiple comparisons and mitigate the risk of Type I error inflation, we adopted a more stringent significance threshold and quantified the strength of significant associations using Cramer’s V (V). Cramer’s V values were interpreted as small (V ≈ 0.1), medium (V ≈ 0.3), or large (V ≈ 0.5). This methodology ensured that our findings represent robust clinical associations rather than incidental statistical differences. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the simultaneous testing of 15 survey items per demographic variable, Bonferroni correction was applied (adjusted α = 0.003). Results are reported with both uncorrected and Bonferroni-corrected significance levels. All associations identified at p < 0.05 (uncorrected) are presented with their effect sizes to facilitate interpretation; however, none met the Bonferroni-corrected threshold, and findings should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Results

Demographic and professional profile of participants

Table 1 presents the distribution of personal and professional characteristics of the 111 study participants. The largest age group was 35–44 years (36.9%), followed by 25–34 years (35.1%), while participants aged ≥ 55 years accounted for 8.1%. Gender distribution was nearly equal, with women comprising 52.3% and men 47.7% of the sample. Regarding professional experience, most participants had 11–20 years of specialization (42.3%), followed by 0–5 years (33.3%) and ≥ 21 years (11.7%). State hospitals were the most common workplace (40.5%), followed by university hospitals (31.5%) and training and research hospitals (13.5%), whereas private practice was the least frequent (3.6%). Most participants reported following 21–100 patients with COPD/asthma per month, with 41.4% managing 21–50 patients and 35.1% managing 51–100 patients. Smaller proportions were observed in ≥ 100 patients (9.0%) and in 0–20 patients (14.4%).

Age-related differences

Within the Personal Awareness domain, participants aged 35–44 years reported the highest prevalence of self-reported gum disease (59.3%, p = 0.016, V = 0.31). In clinical observations, this same age group (35–44) was significantly more likely to endorse the association between periodontitis and increased COPD exacerbations (84.6%, p = 0.008, V = 0.28). Conversely, younger pulmonologists (25–34 years) showed higher awareness regarding the contribution of inhaler medications to oral candidiasis (40.0%, p = 0.029, V = 0.25) and the infection risk resulting from dental plaque aspiration (51.3%, p = 0.049, V = 0.24). Notably, participants aged 55 years and older demonstrated higher agreement with the existence of shared inflammatory mechanisms (22.2%, p = 0.008, V = 0.28) and the necessity of collaboration with a dentist (13.5%, p = 0.049, V = 0.24).

Gender-based variations

Statistical analysis revealed that gender was a significant factor in clinical knowledge levels. Females demonstrated significantly higher clinical alertness regarding the potential for oral flora to increase pulmonary inflammation (p = 0.006, V = 0.30), with 67.9% of those responding “Yes” being female. Furthermore, 66.7% of participants who were aware of infection risks from plaque aspiration were female (p = 0.015, V = 0.28). Additionally, women were more likely to “occasionally” observe oral symptoms in their patients than men (64.9% vs. 35.1%, p = 0.033, V = 0.28).

Impact of professional experience

Clinical experience significantly influenced referral behaviors and personal health habits. Pulmonologists with 11–20 years of specialization had the highest rate of definitive dental referrals (51.5%), while those with 0–5 years of experience often referred patients only “sometimes” (51.9%, p = 0.005, V = 0.29). As with age-related findings, senior physicians with 21 + years of experience showed significantly higher awareness of shared inflammatory mechanisms than those who were “not sure” (25.0% vs. 5.4%, p = 0.048, V = 0.24). Regarding personal health, younger specialists (0–5 years) were more likely to have visited a dentist within the last year (45.7%, p = 0.029, V = 0.24), and physicians with 6–10 years of experience reported a lower prevalence of gum disease compared to other groups (p = 0.022, V = 0.30).

Comparative analysis by institutional setting

Table 3 presents the distribution and associations between participants’ workplace settings and questionnaire items assessing awareness, knowledge, and clinical practices related to oral–dental health and COPD. Among the survey items, seven questions showed statistically significant differences across workplace settings. Within the Personal Awareness and Perception domain, responses to the question “Do you have gum disease?” differed significantly by workplace (p = 0.002). Among participants reporting periodontal disease, 63.0% were employed at university hospitals, whereas the highest proportion of participants without periodontal disease was observed in state hospitals (46.4%). In the Clinical Approach and Observation domain, four items showed significant differences. For the question “How often do you observe the following symptoms in your COPD/asthma patients?”, 40.4% of those reporting “occasionally” were working in university hospitals (p = 0.024). Regarding institutional settings, a significant, medium-strength association was observed between facility type and awareness of inhaler-related oral candidiasis (p = 0.001, V = 0.38). Specifically, while 40.0% of respondents in university hospitals confirmed this association, 37.0% of those in private hospitals remained uncertain. Similarly, for the question “Do you recommend rinsing the mouth after inhaler use?”, 37.5% of participants who did not make this recommendation were employed in private hospitals, while 37.8% of those who did were working in university hospitals (p = 0.001). Within the Knowledge Level and Awareness domain, two questions demonstrated significant differences. For the question “Do COPD patients have a higher risk of periodontal disease?”, 47.5% of participants responding “Yes” were employed in university hospitals (p = 0.049). Likewise, 48.7% of respondents who answered “Yes” to the question “Does aspiration of dental plaque cause infection?” were employed at university hospitals (p = 0.011).

Education gaps and personal oral health awareness

Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the distribution of responses in the Personal Awareness and Perception domain. This domain was characterized by a profound lack of formal education, with 91.9% (n = 102) of participants reporting no prior training in oral health. This deficiency correlated with the self-perceived knowledge levels of the participants, as the majority (70.3%; n = 78) rated their competence as only ‘partially adequate’. Regarding personal health behaviors, 41.4% (n = 46) reported a dental visit within the past year, while 24.3% (n = 27) acknowledged having a periodontal condition. When formal education related to oral and dental health was assessed, most participants (91.9%, n = 102) reported not having received any formal training in this area, whereas only 8.1% (n = 9) indicated having received such education. In terms of self-perceived knowledge level regarding oral and dental health, most participants (70.3%, n = 78) rated themselves as “Partially adequate.” In comparison, 15.3% (n = 17) considered their knowledge “Adequate,” and 14.4% (n = 16) perceived it as “Inadequate’’.

Clinical practice approaches and patient observations

Supplementary Table 2 summarizes the distribution of responses in the Clinical Approach and Observation domain. Analysis of this domain revealed a significant discrepancy between proactive referral and routine inquiry practices, indicating predominantly reactive clinical behavior. While more than half of the participants (54.1%; n = 60) reported never inquiring about oral–dental symptoms, a more favorable pattern was observed regarding referral practices, with 61.3% (n = 68) reporting that they refer their patients to a dentist. In contrast, 41.4% (n = 46) reported inquiring “sometimes,” and only 4.5% (n = 5) reported doing so on a regular basis. In comparison, a more favorable pattern was observed regarding referral practices. A total of 61.3% of participants (n = 68) reported referring their patients to a dentist, 24.3% (n = 27) reported referring patients “sometimes,” and 14.4% (n = 16) reported not referring patients. Regarding the frequency of oral symptom observation in patients with COPD/asthma, the most common response was “occasionally” (51.4%, n = 57). This was followed by “rarely” (37.8%, n = 42), “frequently” (8.1%, n = 9), and “never” (2.7%, n = 3). When asked whether clinical observations suggest that periodontitis increases the frequency of COPD exacerbations, nearly half of participants (43.2%, n = 48) reported being uncertain, and 45.0% (n = 50) reported no such observation. Only 11.7% (n = 13) indicated that they had observed such an association. With respect to the contribution of inhaler medications to the development of oral candidiasis, most participants (72.1%, n = 80) believed that inhalers contribute to this condition, while 24.3% (n = 27) were unsure, and a small proportion (3.6%, n = 4) believed there was no such contribution. Regarding recommendations for mouth rinsing after inhaler use, two-thirds of the participants (66.7%, n = 74) reported routinely providing this recommendation. In contrast, 21.6% (n = 24) reported they did not recommend mouth rinsing, and 11.7% (n = 13) indicated that they did so only sometimes.

Knowledge levels regarding periodontal–pulmonary interactions

Supplementary Table 3 summarizes the overall distribution of responses from participating physicians to questions in the Knowledge Level and Awareness domain. In this domain, a widespread pattern of uncertainty was observed, with over 60% of participants reporting being ‘unsure’ about the pathophysiological links between periodontitis and COPD. Specifically, the majority of physicians were uncertain regarding the increased risk of periodontal disease in COPD patients (64.0%; n = 71) and its potential impact on disease progression (64.9%; n = 72). Responses regarding the potential role of oral flora bacteria in increasing pulmonary inflammation were nearly evenly distributed. Half of the participants (50.5%, n = 56) reported hearing of this association but were not confident, whereas 47.7% (n = 53) confirmed the relationship. Only a small proportion (1.8%, n = 2) believed that no such association exists. A similar pattern was observed for the perceived impact of periodontal disease on COPD progression. While 64.9% of participants (n = 72) were uncertain, 32.4% (n = 36) believed that periodontal disease could influence disease progression. Regarding the role of periodontal pathogens in COPD exacerbations, 62.2% of participants (n = 69) reported uncertainty. In contrast, 36.9% (n = 41) believed that periodontal pathogens play a role, whereas only 0.9% (n = 1) did not. With respect to the risk of infection associated with aspiration of dental plaque, 61.3% of participants (n = 68) reported being unsure, 35.1% (n = 39) acknowledged this risk, and 3.6% (n = 4) did not consider it a risk.

Professional attitudes and receptivity to interdisciplinary education

Supplementary Table 4 summarizes the overall distribution of responses from participating physicians to questions within the Education, Attitudes, and Clinical Referral domains. Regarding the association between COPD and periodontitis via shared inflammatory mechanisms, 66.7% (n = 74) of participants reported being unsure. The proportion of participants who acknowledged this association was 32.4% (n = 36), while only 0.9% (n = 1) rejected it. When asked about the necessity of oral examination during COPD follow-up, 63.1% of participants (n = 70) reported uncertainty. In contrast, 31.5% (n = 35) considered oral examination necessary, whereas 5.4% (n = 6) regarded it as unnecessary. A similar distribution was observed regarding the potential of periodontal treatment to reduce COPD exacerbations. While 63.1% of participants (n = 70) reported hearing of this possibility but remaining unsure, 29.7% (n = 33) believed that periodontal treatment could reduce exacerbations, and 7.2% (n = 8) did not. Responses regarding the necessity of collaboration with a dentist were nearly evenly divided. A total of 50.5% of participants (n = 56) were undecided, whereas 46.8% (n = 52) considered interdisciplinary collaboration necessary. Only 2.7% (n = 3) considered such collaboration unnecessary. The clearest and most positive response was observed for willingness to participate in an educational program on this topic. Most participants (68.5%, n = 76) expressed interest in attending such training, while 26.1% (n = 29) were undecided, and only 5.4% (n = 6) were not interested.

Discussion

The present study provides novel insights into pulmonologists’ awareness, knowledge, and clinical practice approaches regarding the relationship between periodontal disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)and/or asthma. Although a growing body of evidence supports a bidirectional association between periodontal inflammation and chronic respiratory diseases, our findings demonstrate that uncertainty remains prevalent among pulmonologists, particularly concerning shared inflammatory mechanisms, the impact of periodontal disease on COPD progression and exacerbations, and the clinical relevance of routine oral examinations [10, 15]. While the widespread uncertainty regarding common inflammatory mechanisms and the potential for periodontal treatment to reduce COPD exacerbations primarily indicates a knowledge deficit, these findings are also fundamental indicators of pulmonologists’ professional attitudes and subsequent interdisciplinary education needs. Consequently, these items were utilized to evaluate how the presence or absence of specific knowledge shapes the professional mindset and dictates the requirement for structured educational interventions. Saito et al. reported that, in a cohort of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease followed for eight years, the presence of periodontitis was positively correlated with the severity of COPD symptoms, independent of smoking status. This finding suggests that periodontal inflammation may contribute to respiratory symptom burden through mechanisms beyond shared risk factors such as tobacco exposure [19]. Both Zhou et al. [20] and Küçükcoşkun et al. [23] demonstrated that periodontal treatment was associated with improvements in pulmonary function parameters and a reduction in the frequency of COPD exacerbations. Notably, enhanced oral hygiene and reduced dental plaque levels following periodontal therapy appeared to parallel these respiratory benefits, underscoring the potential role of oral health optimization in the comprehensive management of COPD. These findings underscore the need for targeted educational initiatives and interdisciplinary collaboration to improve holistic management of patients with COPD and asthma.

The higher awareness and clinical alertness observed among female pulmonologists and those employed in university hospitals warrant further exploration. In our study, female participants demonstrated significantly greater knowledge of oral–pulmonary interactions, such as the role of oral flora in lung inflammation. This finding is consistent with previous research suggesting that female physicians may adopt a more holistic and preventive-oriented approach to patient care, leading to higher rates of interdisciplinary inquiry [24, 25]. Our observation that female pulmonologists have greater awareness is consistent with the findings of Tsugawa et al., who suggested that female physicians often adhere more strictly to clinical guidelines and emphasize preventive strategies [25]. Furthermore, the superiority of awareness levels in university settings reinforces the importance of ‘interprofessional education’ (IPE). As noted by Wilder et al., academic environments provide a unique infrastructure for informal consultations, effectively bridging the knowledge gap that remains prevalent in private or state facilities [26]. Regarding institutional settings, the significantly higher awareness of the periodontal–pulmonary link in university hospitals compared to state or private facilities could be attributed to several factors [26–28]. First, inter-departmental proximity in academic environments often facilitates informal and formal consultations between periodontologists and pulmonologists, fostering a ‘culture of referral’ that may be less prevalent in resource-limited or non-academic settings. Second, physicians in university hospitals are often more exposed to recent medical curriculum changes and ongoing academic research, which increasingly emphasize the systemic impact of low-grade oral inflammation. This academic environment likely bridges the ‘knowledge gap’ more effectively through integrated clinical grand rounds and multi-disciplinary collaboration, emphasizing the need for structured interprofessional education across all healthcare settings.

The lack of formal oral health education within the medical curriculum appears to be a consistent barrier to interdisciplinary care. While dentists receive extensive training on the oral-systemic axis, the extent to which medical providers are exposed to periodontal-systemic research remains critically low [29, 30]. Our findings align with previous literature, such as the study by Mosley et al., which reported that a significant majority of cardiologists in North Carolina had received no formal education regarding oral healthcare [31]. This systemic deficiency is echoed in our cohort, where an overwhelming 91.9% of pulmonologists reported a total absence of education on the periodontal–pulmonary link during their medical or specialty training. However, a pivotal finding of the present study—which distinguishes our results from mere documentation of a ‘knowledge gap’—is the identification of a significant ‘desire-knowledge gap.’ Despite the lack of prior training, a substantial proportion (68.5%) of participants expressed a clear willingness to engage in structured educational programs. This high level of receptivity suggests that the current lack of awareness is rooted in systemic curriculum deficiencies rather than professional indifference. Furthermore, the effectiveness of such educational interventions is well-documented; for instance, Domínguez-Ortega et al. [32] demonstrated that 91.1% of clinicians were well-acquainted with the Spanish consensus on asthma management, and repeated educational initiatives significantly modified their prescribing behaviors to optimize patient care. In correlation with these findings, our study observed a high level of clinical consciousness among pulmonologists regarding the risk of oral candidiasis associated with inhaler use, leading them to routinely recommend oral rinsing. This suggests that like the clinicians in the study by Domínguez-Ortega et al. [32], the specialists in our cohort demonstrate a strong adherence to established safety protocols within their primary domain. Importantly, bridging this ‘desire-knowledge gap’ through interdisciplinary seminars and undergraduate updates could significantly improve patient prognosis. By integrating periodontal evaluations into routine respiratory care, clinicians can mitigate local inflammatory burdens, which may in turn reduce the frequency of exacerbations and enhance the overall quality of life for patients with COPD and asthma.

Recent evidence indicates persistent gaps in physicians’ understanding of oral–systemic health, with general practitioners demonstrating moderate to low awareness and limited referral behavior across multiple settings, underscoring the ongoing need for enhanced interprofessional training. Stojilković et al. found that although overall awareness of the association between periodontitis and systemic diseases was high, physicians demonstrated significantly lower knowledge than dentists, and higher knowledge, particularly among female participants and dental professionals, was associated with more proactive clinical practices, including patient inquiry and referral behaviors [33]. Patil et al. reported that general physicians demonstrated predominantly moderate awareness of oral–systemic health relationships, with higher knowledge levels observed among those with longer clinical experience, while routine oral health inquiries and dental referral practices remained limited, underscoring the need for mandatory interdisciplinary education [34]. Badrov et al. demonstrated that physicians in Croatian primary healthcare exhibited limited knowledge and self-confidence regarding oral–systemic health links—particularly the association between periodontitis and systemic conditions—compared with dentists, highlighting insufficient education and experience as major barriers and emphasizing the need for enhanced interprofessional collaboration and integration of oral–systemic health topics into medical education [35]. Al-Habib et al. [36] reported that most physicians were aware of links between oral and systemic diseases, and higher awareness was significantly associated with increased patient referrals to dentists and greater belief in patients’ access to oral care services. Sarumathi et al. demonstrated that primary care physicians exhibited only moderate awareness of common oral diseases, with notable deficiencies in knowledge regarding specific conditions and their management, despite a high self-reported rate of routine oral examinations, underscoring the need for targeted oral health education in medical curricula [37]. Ustaoğlu et al. reported that although cardiologists and cardiovascular surgeons largely acknowledged the potential role of periodontal disease treatment in reducing cardiovascular disease risk, their overall knowledge and clinical practices regarding periodontal disease and its systemic effects were limited, highlighting the need for improved oral health education in cardiology training programs [38]. In addition to the lack of information in all these examples from the literature, in our study, the high willingness observed among pulmonologists suggests a potential for improved patient prognosis; by integrating periodontal evaluations into routine respiratory care, clinicians can mitigate local inflammatory burdens, which may in turn reduce the frequency of exacerbations and enhance the overall quality of life for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma. Consequently, to transition from this theoretical interest to proactive clinical integration, it is essential to bridge this gap through interdisciplinary seminars, integrated clinical guidelines, and undergraduate medical education updates, fostering a collaborative environment between dentistry and respiratory medicine.

In this study, the observation that 91.9% of pulmonologists had not received formal education on oral–systemic health, 54.1% did not routinely inquire about oral health, and approximately two-thirds were uncertain about periodontal–pulmonary pathophysiological links, while awareness was largely confined to inhaler-related local adverse effects, further reinforces the evidence that oral–systemic health remains inadequately integrated into specialty medical training and highlights the urgent need for structured, interprofessional educational interventions in pulmonology practice. The observation that more than half of the participating pulmonologists (54.1%) never inquire about oral or dental health symptoms represents a significant ‘missed opportunity’ in the comprehensive management of respiratory patients. Periodontal disease serves as a persistent reservoir for systemic inflammatory mediators and respiratory pathogens. By failing to incorporate basic oral health screening into routine clinical practice, pulmonologists may overlook a modifiable risk factor for pulmonary deterioration. This clinical gap is particularly critical given the existing evidence supporting the benefits of periodontal intervention. For instance, studies by Zhou et al. [20]and Küçükcoşkun et al. [23] have demonstrated that effective periodontal therapy can lead to improved pulmonary function and, more importantly, a reduction in the frequency of COPD exacerbations. Therefore, the lack of routine inquiry reported in our study directly impacts the ability to provide early dental referrals, which could otherwise mitigate the systemic inflammatory burden and improve the long-term prognosis of patients with COPD and asthma. Moving forward, clinical protocols should emphasize the importance of oral health inquiry as a simple yet effective tool for preventing exacerbations.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire that relied on participants’ self-reported knowledge, attitudes, and clinical practices, which may have introduced subjective reporting and social desirability bias. The absence of direct observation or objective assessment prevented verification of actual clinical behaviors and competencies related to oral health inquiry and referral practices. Another primary limitation of this study is the use of convenience sampling via a professional WhatsApp group for survey distribution. While this approach facilitated rapid data collection among specialists, it inherently introduces selection bias, as pulmonologists who are more tech-savvy or have a pre-existing interest in interdisciplinary oral health may have been more likely to participate. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the diverse perspectives of all pulmonologists, particularly those in different geographical regions or those not active in digital professional networks. Furthermore, the sample size and the specific platform used may limit the generalizability of the results to the wider pulmonology community. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study limits causal inference and precludes assessing changes in awareness or practice patterns over time. Consequently, the findings reflect only the current state of pulmonologists’ knowledge and attitudes toward periodontal disease and its relationship with COPD and asthma. Third, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as physicians with a greater interest in oral–systemic health might have been more inclined to participate. Additionally, nonresponse bias cannot be excluded, and the results may not fully represent all pulmonologists, particularly those working in non-academic or resource-limited settings. The predominance of certain demographic characteristics among respondents may further limit the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, the questionnaire consisted primarily of closed-ended items, which, although practical for time-constrained clinicians, may have limited the depth of responses and the ability to capture nuanced perspectives, clinical reasoning, and contextual barriers. Moreover, for several knowledge-based questions, the structure of the response options may have led to an overestimation of knowledge levels. Finally, to ensure feasibility and acceptable completion time, the number of survey items was necessarily limited, restricting in-depth exploration of specific domains. Future studies employing mixed-method approaches, including qualitative interviews or focus group discussions, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of pulmonologists’ perceptions, educational needs, and real-world clinical practices regarding oral–systemic health.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant ‘desire-knowledge gap’ among pulmonologists: while formal education on the periodontal–pulmonary link is critically lacking (91.9%), there is a high receptivity to interdisciplinary training (68.5%). Awareness is currently fragmented, being largely confined to local inhaler side effects rather than systemic inflammatory risks. The superior awareness observed in university settings and among female physicians underscores the vital role of academic collaboration and holistic clinical approaches. To prevent ‘missed opportunities’ in COPD and asthma management, oral–systemic health must be integrated into medical curricula and routine clinical protocols, transitioning from theoretical interest to proactive interdisciplinary care to improve patient prognosis.
