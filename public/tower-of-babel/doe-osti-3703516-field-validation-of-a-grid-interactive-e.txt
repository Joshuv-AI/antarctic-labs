 Executive Summary
 In this pilot study, the U.S. General Services Administration (GSA) and the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)
 evaluated an energy management information system (EMIS) with grid-interactive efficient building (GEB)
 features. This software technology was evaluated in partnership with the National Laboratory of the Rockies
 (NLR) at a federal testbed facility in Las Vegas, Nevada.

 Multiple features were evaluated, including (a) continuous demand management to avoid setting high
 monthly kilowatt (kW) peaks, (b) energy efficiency for reduction of kilowatt-hours (kWh) and natural gas
 consumption, and (c) automated demand response (ADR) for purposes of lowering demand during a utility-
 called demand response event.

 The testbed facility was the Foley Federal Building and U.S. Courthouse (“Foley Federal Building”) located in
 Las Vegas, Nevada. This is a 209,496 ft2 building constructed in the 1960s with major renovations in 2004.
 The facility was a good candidate due to the prevalence of office and courthouse space in GSA’s building
 portfolio. It also has many features that enable integration with and control of the building, as well as a
 strong facilities team to assist with the study.

 Quantitative and qualitative performance objectives were developed using GSA’s Center for Emerging
 Building Technologies (CEBT) GEB measurement and verification guidance (Langner, Granderson, and
 Crowe 2022) along with input from the vendor and building facility staff. Table 1 outlines the results for the
 quantitative performance objectives, and Table 2 outlines the results for the qualitative objectives. The
 quantitative objectives focused on continuous demand management, energy efficiency, and ADR. The
 qualitative performance objectives focused on the ease of installation and commissioning, as well as the
 operability of the GEB software. Other reported performance metrics include cost-effectiveness, and
 occupant acceptance.

 Introduction
 Background
 The U.S. General Services Administration’s (GSA’s) Center for Emerging Building Technologies (CEBT), in
 partnership with the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), completed a field study of a grid-interactive
 efficient building (GEB) software operating system technology (“GEB software”). The study focused on a
 single testbed facility to evaluate the GEB functionality of the software operating system technology, along
 with other features. The testbed facility—a courthouse—is a common building type in GSA’s vast building
 portfolio, offering potentially impactful findings on a scalable level.

 The study evaluated Nantum AI, previously known as Prescriptive Data’s technology, Nantum OS, a
 connected building operating system. Nantum OS aggregates multiple sources of previously siloed building
 data and combines that data with external sources, such as weather information or utility signals, into a
 single integrated platform. The GEB software is a type of energy management information system (EMIS).
 An EMIS is defined as a system of devices, data services, and software applications that communicates
 with any building system or third-party data source to aggregate and transform data into new capabilities to
 aid in the optimization of energy use at the building, campus, or agency level (Pachuta et al. 2022). This
 specific GEB software includes automated system optimization that offers supervisory control enabling
 demand and consumption energy conservation measures with certain aspects of the building automation
 system.

 The GEB software can provide grid-responsive load flexibility and continuous demand management through
 the following strategies:

 ● Energy efficiency: Reduction in energy use and improved building function
 ● Load shedding: The ability to reduce electricity use for a short period of time and, typically, on short

 The GEB software that was evaluated aggregates multiple sources of previously siloed building data and
 combines that data with external sources, such as weather information or utility signals, into a single,
 integrated platform. It is a type of energy management information system. An energy management
 information system is a system of devices, data services, and software applications that communicates
 with any building system or third-party data source, aggregates and transforms data into new capabilities,
 and optimizes energy use at the building, campus, or agency level. Figure 1 shows an example of a GEB
 software’s connectivity and integration.

 GEBs offer significant opportunities to reduce energy consumption, energy costs, and emissions and to
 improve the resilience of buildings. A recent study by the Rocky Mountain Institute (RMI) estimated the
 implementation of GEB strategies across GSA’s building portfolio could result in an estimated $50 million in
 annual cost savings, $206 million in net present value over 8 years, and a payback under 4 years
 (Carmichael et al. 2019). These savings were attributed to five GEB capabilities with potential direct value
 for GSA, including:

 ● Demand-charge management
 ● Demand response revenue
 ● GEB-specific rebates and incentives
 ● Ability to access flexible time-of-use tariffs
 ● Cost savings from enhanced energy efficiency.

 Additionally, RMI noted these potential added benefits for GSA:
 ● Better building control
 ● Deeper savings in energy savings performance contracts and utility energy savings contracts
 ● Continued leadership by the federal government and real estate industry.

 Because of these potential benefits, in July 2020, GSA and DOE’s Building Technologies Office selected four
 GEB operating system software technologies for field demonstrations through CEBT. The National
 Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) and three other DOE national laboratories were selected to lead
 measurement and verification (M&V) efforts for the installations in federal and private sector buildings.

 Technology Description
 The GEB software evaluated in this study was developed by a real estate company to manage its portfolio
 of buildings. Its platform integrates all building functions and pulls real-time data from a building’s building
 automation system (BAS), external data providers, and other third-party software application technologies.
 It then uses machine learning algorithms to optimize energy use and tenant comfort. The solution includes
 automated system optimization to enable energy conservation measures (ECMs). It has the following
 product features:

 ● Data visualization: Water; gas; electricity; steam; heating, ventilating, and air conditioning
 (HVAC); occupancy; indoor air quality; weather; and power quality across the building and
 tenant spaces.
 ● Predictive and prescriptive analytics: Machine learning to correlate and discern trends in
 disparate data streams, providing predictive and prescriptive insights. The GEB software can be
 applied to demand response and load-shedding programs, as well as supporting energy-related
 technologies, including cogeneration, solar photovoltaics, and battery storage.
 ● Automated optimization process: As part of the evaluation, a third-party whole-building
 occupancy counting technology was installed at each building entrance. The GEB software
 used this occupancy data, along with thermal modeling and machine learning algorithms, to
 recommend start-up times, midday ramps, and end-of-day ramp-downs to building operators.
 ● Reporting and data export: Strategic insights that allow operators to understand how, where,
 and when energy and water are being consumed and which systems, processes, and facilities
 are driving consumption.
 The GEB software uses a cloud-based platform that connects multiple data streams (such as weather data,
 advanced metering infrastructure, BAS, and internet of things (IoT)). It performs the capabilities listed
 previously to centralize, normalize, and visualize data, utility bill management, interval meter analytics, M&V,

 automated fault detection and diagnostics, and supervisory control for automated demand response (ADR)
 and continuous demand management.

 Measurement and Verification Plan
 Testbed Site
 The Foley Federal Building and United States Courthouse (Figure 2) is a 209,496 ft2 federally owned building
 under GSA’s jurisdiction, custody, and control in Las Vegas, Nevada. Built in the early 1960s, the building
 has nine occupant agencies, with the Bankruptcy Court being the primary occupant. Before COVID-19, the
 maximum occupancy was 184 federal employees and contractors, and there were 150 to 200 daily visitors.
 Table 3 outlines the site characteristics.

 Several characteristics of the building make it an ideal candidate to evaluate the software technology in this
 study. For one, the building has an updated building automation system (BAS) and supervisory control
 ability, and the technology needs to connect to the BAS to optimize building equipment performance. The
 building is similar to other buildings in GSA’s vast portfolio, which includes many courthouses and office
 buildings, offering potential opportunity to apply the results broadly. Also important was the fact that the
 building has areas with noncritical loads, as the software needs to be able to customize set points and
 schedules. The building has more than one air handler unit (AHU) rather than smaller split units found in
 residential buildings or smaller office facilities. This enabled potential energy savings from supervisory
 control due to the AHU and zone controllability.

 Methodology
 The facility has whole-building advanced meters, including one electric meter, one gas meter, and three
 water submeters. These meters collect 15-minute interval data that are integrated into the BAS. The whole-
 building energy savings and peak demand savings in the project were measured using the Option C–Whole
 Facility approach described in the International Performance Measurement and Verification Protocol
 (IPMVP) (American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers [ASHRAE] 2014). This
 involved comparing the M&V period’s consumption to consumption in the baseline period (2021). The
 difference between these two values was normalized with multilinear regression, accounting for
 independent variables such as weather, facility operation, occupancy, BAS trend points, submetered energy
 usage, and facility modifications. This enabled an estimation of how much energy would have been used in
 the M&V period without the GEB software.

 Where applicable, subsystem energy and demand reductions were evaluated using the approach described
 in IPMVP Option B–Retrofit Isolation. Subsystems include HVAC fans, condenser plant, and the chiller plant.
 Although these evaluations are not related to any performance objective, they provide additional visibility
 into how subsystems respond to the GEB software. The energy and demand savings were calculated using
 multilinear regression for differences in independent variables such as BAS trend logs, weather data, facility
 operation, and facility modifications.

 During evaluation of building automation systems that use machine learning, it can be difficult to
 understand exactly how the algorithms optimize energy savings, especially if the algorithms are stochastic.
 To provide better insight into the GEB software’s impact on HVAC system behavior, electrical submeters
 were installed on all major HVAC components, including chillers, air handlers, fan coil units, cooling towers,
 boilers, and associated pumps.

 The submeters monitor voltage (at the individual panel level) and current (at the individual circuit level) to
 provide 1-minute and 15-minute resolution averages of:

 • Current
 • Voltage
 • Power factor
 • Energy
 • Power.

 This submetered data was not fed into or used by the GEB software. Rather, the study team used it to
 evaluate HVAC system behavior at the component level.

 In addition to the submetering data, additional data were collected from all devices and subsystems that are
 connected to the GEB software, including:

 ● Advanced metering infrastructure (i.e., electricity meters, natural gas meters, and water submeters)

 ● Occupancy counting sensors
 ● BAS
 ● Weather station at McCarran (Harry Reid) International Airport (National Oceanic and Atmospheric
 Administration [NOAA] 2024).

 Quantitative Objectives
 Continuous Demand Management

 Continuous demand management, also known as monthly peak management, is a term used in other GSA
 studies. The Foley Federal Building’s utility bill includes a monthly demand charge based on the highest
 peak building demand during the previous billing year. In each billing period, the GEB software uses a
 machine learning model to predict the building's peak demand based on historical building energy, HVAC,
 occupancy, and weather data. The software then provides these values to building operators showing
 realistic peak demand reduction targets. These targets seek to minimize the impact on occupants’ comfort.
 The software automatically activates different stages of supervisory control to meet the demand required to
 avoid a predicted peak. Table 6 shows the software estimated demand limits and potential savings for the
 Foley Federal Building based on 2020 data. These estimated demand savings were determined by the
 vendor following early morning testing.

 The GEB software reduces the building demand (kW) by staging various controls to adjust HVAC
 temperature set points. An algorithm monitors building data and projects demand based on that data. When
 demand is projected to exceed the current billing period’s demand limit, the algorithm triggers a demand
 event. During the event, the AHUs’ supply air temperature is reduced to the minimum or maximum value
 depending on the building’s geographical location and season. Additional stages are implemented

 ADR is a method for a dispatcher to broadcast a demand response signal continuously over the internet to a
 customer, using a common language called OpenADR 2.0. The ADR aspects of this study used the same
 demand response stages as in continuous demand management. The GEB software vendor used its “Virtual
 Top Node” server to send simulated utility ADR signals and specify the required curtailment. A “Virtual End

 signals and implements them in the building.

 To the extent possible, the simulated demand response events reflected the local utility’s historical pattern
 of event season, time of day, and duration. Between June and September 2020, the utility called 20 events
 between 3 p.m. and 5 p.m. based on its load projections. Customers are notified 24 hours in advance and
 can opt out. They are requested to reduce power for the entire period. Since 2020, the utility has shifted the
 events later in the day, with the start time ranging from 5 p.m. to 7 p.m., outside of normal facility operating
 hours.

 Nevada Energy’s ADR events are not ideal for the Foley Federal Building because they begin after 5 p.m.,
 when building demand decreases significantly. As a result, the study team implemented three simulated
 ADR events in the 2022 summer peak billing period to demonstrate the GEB software’s ability to receive and
 respond to ADR signals with load shedding. To calculate the results of these events, the team used the

Field Evaluation of a Grid-Interactive Efficient Building Software Solution Page 29

 IPMVP Option C–Whole Facility approach along with whole-building, 15-minute interval meter data (ASHRAE
 2014). Table 11 provides the metrics for the ADR/load shed performance objective.

 The calculation of the ADR-driven demand reduction was based on Nevada Energy’s protocol for calculating
 demand savings. Event average power (P_ave_event) was calculated by averaging the power during the
 event (e.g., 8/18/22, from 3 p.m. to 5 p.m.). For baseline days, the team selected the 3 days with the
 highest maximum, outdoor-air, dry-bulb temperature from the 10 days immediately before the event,
 excluding other event days, weekends, and holidays. These temperatures were calculated based on actual
 meteorological year weather data. Baseline average power (P_ave_base) was calculated as the average
 power across the 3 baseline days during the same time frame as the event (i.e., 3 p.m. to 5 p.m.).

 The event preceding power (P_pre_event) was calculated by averaging the power before the precooling
 period of the event (e.g., 8/18/2022, from 12 p.m. to 2 p.m.). The baseline preceding power (P_pre_base)
 was calculated by averaging the power during the same time frame in the 3 baseline days (12 p.m. to 2
 p.m.). To compare the load curve on the baseline and event days, an adjustment factor was calculated as:

 f_adj = P_pre_event / P_pre_base.

 Adjusted baseline power was calculated as:

 P_adj_base = P_ave_base * f_adj.

 The demand response savings (P_savings) was then calculated as:

 P_savings = P_adj_base – P_ave_event.

 The savings were calculated with this approach for each of the simulated demand response events. The
 three savings values were averaged and normalized by the baseline average power, yielding a final
 percentage savings.

 The GEB software used real-time courtroom- and building-level occupancy data from sensors installed at

 To evaluate the effectiveness of these ECMs, the team compared the building’s pre- and postretrofit
 performance. Table 12 lists the metrics for the energy efficiency performance objective. For the preretrofit
 period, the team identified a period (2021) with a wide range of outdoor air temperature conditions. The
 postretrofit period includes a similarly wide range of outdoor air conditions when the GEB software was on.

 The evaluation method consists of IPMVP Option C – Whole Facility.

 To account for different weather conditions in the pre- and postretrofit periods, normalized daily electricity
 consumption (kWh/day) was regressed against average daily outdoor-air, dry-bulb temperatures (°F) using
 weather data from McCarran International Airport (NOAA 2024). Separate regressions were calculated for
 the pre- and postretrofit periods. The regressions employed three-parameter change-point models, which
 are used to model seasonal weather-sensitive energy use with one cooling mode (ASHRAE 2014). The
 change-point temperature is the outside air temperature above which the HVAC system is providing cooling,
 and below which the HVAC system is not providing cooling. The change-point temperatures for each period
 were determined via Python's Sklearn module, 1 which is a collection of tools for predictive data analysis.
 The change-point temperature of a building is the point at which heat gains are equal to the losses. At
 temperatures above the change-point temperatures, a linear regression captures the slope of the cooling-
 dependent electricity consumption. At temperatures below the change-point temperature, a constant
 electricity consumption was calculated, as visual inspection of the data shows that this building exhibits no
 heating-dependent electricity consumption. This constant electricity consumption was calculated as the
 average consumption of all weekdays with average daily temperatures below the change-point temperature.

 Note these regressions were performed for weekdays. For weekends and holidays, a constant electricity
 consumption was calculated, as visual inspection of the data shows that the building exhibits no
 temperature dependence on weekends or holidays. This constant electricity consumption was calculated as
 the average consumption of all weekends and holidays.

 The weekday pre- and postretrofit regression equations were applied to typical meteorological year daily
 average temperatures obtained from the McCarran International Airport (Climate.OneBuilding.Org n.d.).
 This results in weather-normalized daily electricity consumption for the weekdays for both the pre- and
 postretrofit periods. The weekday consumption values were summed across the typical year. A similar
 summation of weekend/holiday consumption values was undertaken. Combining the weekday and

 Cost-Effectiveness

 In this study, the cost-effectiveness metric was not intended to provide a comprehensive market
 assessment. Rather, it was meant to serve as a rough indicator of potential savings and provide the basis
 for assessing cost-effectiveness at other locations.

 The cost-effectiveness calculations only include savings from the energy efficiency performance objective.
 Savings from the continuous demand management performance objective are not included because of the
 inconclusive findings. The GEB software was not observed to consider the timing of the utility’s peak
 periods. The summer midpeak hours are 10 a.m. to 1 p.m., and the peak hours are 1 p.m. to 7 p.m.

 With a lack of data points on how the continuous demand management changes the building’s behavior, it
 is unclear whether the GEB software pushed the peak demand into a high-priced time-of-use period.

 The cost-effectiveness is determined based on a simple payback calculation. This calculation includes:

 ● The various costs provided by GSA and the vendor for the GEB software (Table 35). Additional BAS
 integration costs associated with an automated GEB software are site specific and not included in
 this analysis.
 ● Annual cost savings based on a blended utility rate—in other words, annual total energy costs
 divided by annual total energy use in kWh (Table 36). The Foley Federal Building blended utility rate
 was $0.08/kWh, and the GSA average blended utility rate was $0.13/kWh.

 Occupant Acceptance

 Using data from facility staff and the focus groups, the study team tracked impacts on occupants, such as
 hot calls (notifications from staff or occupants reporting an area is too hot), cold calls (notifications
 reporting an area is too cold), and cases when the software was disabled due to comfort issues. It should
 be noted there were supplemental cooling issues prior to the GEB software implementation.

 There were 11 hot calls in a 10-month preproject/baseline period (6/1/21–4/7/22) and 28 hot calls in a 9-
 month, 1-week project period (6/1/22–4/7/23; includes removal of a software-off period). That translates
 into a 154% increase in hot calls when the software was deployed. Notably, during the software-off period
 (8/25/22–9/22/22), there were zero reported hot calls.

 During the same preproject/baseline and project periods, there were 20 and 30 cold calls, respectively—a
 50% increase. The two cold calls in the software-off period are not included in the project numbers.

 The facility management team said the increase of hot and cold calls was so significant they were not able
 to track all complaints accurately. The occupants and agencies were all informed of the study, which can
 result in a bias in which occupants are more aware of comfort and either more or less tolerant of
 uncomfortable conditions. One focus group participant reported that occupants have been “very helpful
 working with us by internally tolerating less comfortable, warmer circumstances than they were used to in
 years prior.”

 On the last day of the evaluation dates, facilities staff turned off the software due to high temperatures
 (nearly 80°F) in multiple offices around 10 a.m. The set points for static pressure and temperature were
 increased to a level that resulted in numerous complaints about uncomfortable conditions. The evaluation
 for all savings metrics ended March 23, 2023, to include a full year of data.

 Conclusions
 Overall Technology Assessment at Testbed Facility
 In this study, the GEB software demonstrated functionality in several performance objectives, although
 some results were inconclusive. The software was successfully integrated into the Foley Federal Building,
 offering a supervisory control solution. Integration and commissioning took 17 weeks. This is a reasonable
 amount of time, considering these types of software require constant calibration and maintenance.

 With time-series data from the BAS to inform the GEB software, whole-building energy savings of 4.3% were
 realized during the evaluation period. The continuous demand response results were inconclusive because
 the software did not result in any measurable demand reduction compared to the baseline period. In three
 simulated ADR events at predetermined times, the GEB software succeeded with an average kilowatt
 demand reduction of 8%. Simulated events were used because utility-called events occur after operating
 hours.

 The GEB software was not found to be cost-effective in this application. High installation costs and SaaS
 fees combined with low site utility rates and realized energy savings resulted in costs being higher than the
 savings.

 In focus groups and surveys, building staff expressed mixed reviews of the GEB software. Regarding ease
 of installation and commissioning, the GEB software did successfully engage and worked in some aspects,
 while others offered lessons learned. There were operability challenges, such as temperature control
 problems. With respect to occupant acceptance, there was a significant increase in complaints (such as hot
 and cold calls) during the evaluation period. Software commissioning occurred throughout the evaluation,
 with the GEB software being turned off early near the end of the project due to occupant comfort issues.

 Although all these factors should be considered when selecting different building energy management
 systems, the GEB software evaluated in this study could be suitable for different applications throughout
 the GSA building portfolio, depending on energy prices, building systems, and many other factors that can
 contribute to a software’s success.

 Lessons Learned and Best Practices
 ● Technology requirements: GEB software can interact with the existing BAS infrastructure in
 different ways. It is important to understand the methodology and confirm that the BAS hardware
 and software are compatible with software.
 ● Site/facility selection: It is challenging to conduct field evaluations of advanced building energy
 management technologies in courthouses, particularly technologies that impact occupant comfort.
 This is due to security guidelines in such buildings, as well as the strict temperature requirements
 associated with the occupants and courtrooms. Results in this study may have varied if climate

 control of the courtrooms were permitted. GEB software solutions are best suited for buildings with
 flexibility in operating temperatures.
 ● Factor in utility billing structure: Not all building energy management technologies consider time-
 of-use demand pricing. The operation of these technologies may push buildings into more
 expensive time-of-use pricing brackets. Locations with time-of-use rates ideally should have high
 rates during all operational hours. Annual peak demand could be a more important consideration in
 reducing energy costs than monthly peak demand and potential impacts on occupant comfort.
 ● Verify privacy guidelines for occupancy sensors: Before installation, it is essential to verify that
 occupancy counters and other hardware meet all federal security guidelines.
 ● Occupancy sensor installation: Ensure occupancy sensors are installed at all entrances and exits
 to maximize occupancy tracking. Verify occupancy sensors are properly oriented to track enter and
 exit movements.
 ● Commissioning: Knowing most critical zones in each building, test continuous demand
 management and demand response events at the same time as when they would be implemented in
 the evaluation period, considering details such as seasonal changes, temperature, pressure, and
 humidity. Updating equipment operation sequences for demand management can cause changes in
 other building parameters, such as the lowering of pressure that spiked humidity in this study. Plan
 ahead for ongoing testing and adjustments, including a human-in-the-loop until automated
 strategies are perfected.
 ● Demand response: Set up low, moderate, and high load-shedding events to allow for the least
 amount of operational interference with the available GEB technologies, if desired.
 ● Granular data: Use 5-minute data intervals for automated demand management for faster response
 times to changing demand. This evaluation used 15-minute data.
 ● BAS: Use a local BAS subcontractor to install GEB software. Local companies in areas with
 government-operated buildings are more likely to be familiar with government standards and BAS
 mapping. It may be beneficial to set up notifications for when automated GEB software is actively
 impacting the building.
 ● Customize continuous demand management and demand response strategies for each building:
 Potential energy savings will depend on the strategies developed. It’s helpful to calculate estimated
 savings before deploying the technology.
 ● Tracking of costs: To accurately assess cost-effectiveness, ensure all project costs are tracked
 from start to finish.
 ● Empower O&M team: Provide appropriate training and time to ensure building managers are
 comfortable with the software. Operators can turn the system, ramps, and demand management on
 and off and adjust static pressure minimum and maximum set points.

 ● Energy storage: Incorporating on-site storage and renewables can minimize impact on occupant
 comfort and strengthen resilience.
 ● Strategic goal setting: Balance objectives (cost, comfort, energy) and adapt automation strategies
 over time.
 ● Known challenges: First-generation automated GEB software has not yet resolved challenges with
 occupant comfort and fluctuating building performance. Additional evaluations are recommended.

 Deployment Recommendations
 The GEB software tested in this project has deployment potential, though its cost-effectiveness will likely be
 location dependent. It is best to consider this technology for areas that have favorable ADR programs, such
 as high financial incentives or actionable time windows. Successful deployment will also depend heavily on
 the pricing of demand (i.e., cost of demand charges in $/kW). With inconclusive kilowatt savings in this
 study, however, it is difficult to recommend a suitable pricing structure.

 The presence of time-of-use rates could be an important consideration in where and how to deploy GEB
 software. The technology will be more cost-effective with a utility tariff with highly priced demand during all
 hours when the building is occupied. This is because the GEB software may not consider time-of-use price
 changes during the operational hours of the day unless manually customized to implement demand
 management events in certain time windows.

 Sites with high energy rates ($/kWh) may be good candidates for cost-effective deployments driven by
 efficiency savings (reduced kilowatt-hours).

 Facilities with a more sophisticated BAS will be important from an integration standpoint, as well as
 commissioning. For example, a more sophisticated BAS might have better controllability and more
 customization available. Buildings that are able to accommodate operational flexibility, such as office
 buildings, are more likely to be suitable candidates for deployment. Facilities such as courtrooms or
 hospitals with strict operational requirements for temperature, humidity, and other parameters are less
 favorable candidates.
