Post pneumonectomy syndrome: a narrative review of current management strategies
Authors: Ethyn G Loreno, Min P Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivePost pneumonectomy syndrome (PPS) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication following pneumonectomy. Progressive mediastinal shift into the post-resection space results in distortion and compression of mediastinal structures, including the tracheobronchial tree, esophagus, and vasculature. Patients often present with dyspnea and recurrent pulmonary infections, with imaging with computed tomography (CT) scan classically showing compression of the distal trachea or remaining bronchus. Diagnosis is confirmed with bronchoscopy demonstrating dynamic airway obstruction with extrinsic compression and airway displacement. While surgical mediastinal repositioning remains the standard of care, multiple operative and minimally invasive strategies exist, and long-term outcomes are poorly reported with relatively high complication and reoperation rates. This review evaluates the current management strategies, compares surgical and endoscopic approaches, and highlights emerging techniques in PPS treatment.MethodsA narrative review was performed of existing literature on PPS diagnosis and management options, including stenting and surgical interventions. A PubMed search was performed of the key terms: “post pneumonectomy syndrome”, “post-pneumonectomy syndrome”, “postpneumonectomy syndrome”, “management of post-pneumonectomy syndrome”, “implant-based treatment of post pneumonectomy syndrome”, “endobronchial stenting for postpneumonectomy syndrome”, and “classic post pneumonectomy syndrome presentation”. Novel approaches, reviews that reported long-term results, and those with comparative analysis of current treatment options were prioritized in the literature evaluation.Key Content and FindingsFor those patients who are surgical candidates, the most common treatment involves mediastinal repositioning with fixed-volume or tissue expander prostheses. For patients who are not surgical candidates, airway stenting is required to palliate symptoms. Overall, mediastinal repositioning with prosthetic implantation is the current standard of care and provides reasonable short-term and long-term resolution of PPS, though a personalized approach to surgical management remains paramount.ConclusionsThough rare, PPS is a potentially life-threatening complication of pneumonectomy. Identification and diagnosis may be difficult due to the dynamic nature of mediastinal shifting and wide range of symptomatology. Surgical intervention remains the best definitive management to correct mediastinal shifting, though endobronchial stenting may be indicated in some cases. Surgical management should be reviewed on a case-by-case basis with a multi-disciplinary evaluation, usually in conjunction with plastic surgery.Keywords: Post pneumonectomy syndrome (PPS), pneumonectomy complications, mediastinal shift, airway compression, tissue expanders

Introduction

Post pneumonectomy syndrome (PPS) is a rare complication of pneumonectomy. It is characterized by an excessive mediastinal shift toward the pneumonectomy space, resulting in compression of the mediastinal structures. This displacement can produce significant respiratory effects, most notably dynamic airway obstruction, as well as vascular compromise and esophageal compression (1,2). The classic presentation occurs after a right-sided pneumonectomy, in which the mediastinal shift stretches the left main and lower lobe bronchus over the descending aorta. The result is the characteristic “kinking” of dynamic airway obstruction, often presenting as progressive dyspnea, stridor, or recurrent pulmonary infections (1). PPS after left pneumonectomy is rare (2). The current standard of care involves surgical correction with mediastinal repositioning, usually with the use of implant-based prostheses into the post pneumonectomy space. However, the large variability in the patient population and severity of illness and symptoms may necessitate a more personalized approach to care. Although surgical mediastinal repositioning continues to be the primary treatment for PPS, a variety of operative and minimally invasive approaches have been described, with limited long-term outcome data and relatively high rates of complications and reintervention. This review examines current management options, compares surgical and endoscopic techniques, and discusses emerging therapies in the treatment of PPS. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0702/rc).

Methods

A narrative review of PPS and current treatment strategies was performed. Keywords including “post pneumonectomy syndrome”, “post-pneumonectomy syndrome”, “postpneumonectomy syndrome”, “management of post-pneumonectomy syndrome”, “implant-based treatment of post pneumonectomy syndrome”, “endobronchial stenting for postpneumonectomy syndrome”, and “classic post pneumonectomy syndrome presentation” were searched in the PubMed database. All publication types, from all locations, and in all languages were utilized if found to be contributory to the historical significance, identification, diagnosis, and/or management of PPS. Special emphasis was given to articles that demonstrated a degree of novelty to the topic, reviews that analyzed long-term outcomes, and articles that performed comparative analyses of different treatment types. Additionally, topics that demonstrated exceptionally good or poor outcomes were screened for inclusion (Table 1).

Epidemiology and risk factors

PPS is a rare but clinically significant late complication of pneumonectomy, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.16% to 2% (3). However, the true frequency is likely underrecognized due to delayed presentation and diagnostic variability (3). It often occurs within the first few years following resection, although cases have been reported decades later (4). PPS occurs more frequently after right-sided pneumonectomy, as right-sided resection permits pronounced mediastinal shift and rotation with subsequent compression of the left main bronchus between the aorta and pulmonary artery (5). Young age, particularly in pediatric and young adult populations, is also associated with increased risk of PPS, likely due to increased mediastinal elasticity and thoracic compliance (5). Several series suggest a female predominance, potentially related to smaller thoracic dimensions and airway caliber, although this association remains observational (4,6,7). Collectively, these epidemiologic and anatomic factors promote progressive mediastinal shift and rotation, resulting in dynamic airway compression and the characteristic clinical manifestations of PPS (4). One retrospective study suggests that increased anterior mediastinal fat content may be protective against PPS (8).

Several studies have examined preventive measures following pneumonectomy. Nikulin et al. published a technique for the prophylactic prevention of postpneumonectomy syndrome, which involves the surgical fixation of mesh into the pneumonectomy space by suturing the mesh to the ribs, effectively isolating the hemithoracic space, in an attempt to prevent mediastinal herniation into the empty space (9). This rigid fixation was reported to be a reliable method of preventing the onset of PPS, preventing displacement during a “cough shock”, and decreasing fluid formation in the paramediastinal space (9). This case report does not have long-term follow-up. Prophylactic tissue expander placement in children was also demonstrated to be effective in a case series of four pediatric patients; however, one of the four patients developed expander rupture requiring reoperation (10). Prophylactic measures for PPS, especially with implanted prostheses, may subject individuals to further complications due to foreign material introduction and the need for further operations. This may provide a higher risk of complications and reoperative risk than necessary for an already rare condition. Therefore, the risk of PPS should be analyzed and compared to that of prophylactic interventions in an individualized approach as the overall risk of developing PPS remains quite low (10).

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

PPS most commonly presents with progressive shortness of breath, stridor, and recurrent pneumonias, reflecting the etiology of PPS, which is a mediastinal shift causing dynamic compression of the airway (5). If untreated, PPS may progress to tracheomalacia and respiratory failure (1). Rarely, the mediastinal shift may impact the esophagus, which can cause dysphagia, and the mediastinal vasculature, which can create hemodynamic compromise (2,11,12).

Key diagnostic tests for PPS include computed tomography (CT) scan and bronchoscopy as chest X-ray with mediastinal and cardiac shift toward the pneumonectomy side, loss of post pneumonectomy space, and overexpansion of the non-resected lung is insufficient to formulate a definitive diagnosis. A high-resolution CT scan should be performed to evaluate tracheobronchial compression by adjacent vascular structures. It also facilitates precise localization of airway obstruction, which most commonly involves the left mainstem bronchus, typically compressed between the pulmonary artery and aorta or against the vertebral column (5). As this disease is classically right-sided, the typical CT scan findings will show tracheal or left bronchus compression with rightward deviation of the mediastinum, counterclockwise rotation of the great vessels, and overexpanded left lung (13).

Once CT confirms airway compression, bronchoscopy should be performed to confirm the diagnosis of PPS. Classic findings demonstrate dynamic, extrinsic airway compression resulting in a slit-like airway lumen, likely pulsatile compression due to vascular impingement, and collapse with respiration. Grillo et al. described characteristic bronchoscopy findings as “displacement of the trachea to the right or left, depending on the location of the aortic arch, and generally a marked lateral wall impression of the arch on the lower trachea, sometimes with a degree of compression of the lower trachea. The carina was rotated. The left main bronchus was compressed anteroposteriorly after right pneumonectomy, with a variable increase in obstruction, often nearly complete, upon cough or deep expiration” (14). Once chest CT and bronchoscopy confirm the diagnosis of PPS in patients with classic symptoms, they should be evaluated for surgical treatment.

Management

The management of PPS typically relies on several factors. The first and possibly most important step is the restoration of the normal anatomy. Herniation of mediastinal structures into the post pneumonectomy space creates compressive symptoms due to impingement by the surrounding structures. Restoration of normal anatomy will relieve extrinsic compression on mediastinal structures and restore patency; however, this relies on surgical intervention. Airway blockage may be relieved with a less invasive approach through endobronchial stenting, with mixed results reported.

Airway stents can be inserted in patients who are not surgical candidates to correct airway abnormalities (15). According to an analysis by Christodoulides et al., in cases of endobronchial stenting as the primary management strategy, half of the patients had complications in the short-term postprocedural period, including stent migration, obstruction, and recurrent lower respiratory tract infection (6). Thirty-three percent required repeat stenting, and fifty percent died within 1 year of follow-up. One study with a long-term 12-year follow-up of a patient who received two nitinol stents demonstrated good durability; however, the patient suffered from recurrent multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas episodes believed to be associated with the stent. The patient ultimately required repeat hospitalization and succumbed to the infection (16). The high complication and mortality rates likely reflect both the severity of airway compromise and the selection of higher-risk surgical candidates, emphasizing that stenting as a primary intervention should be reserved for patients who are unsuitable for definitive mediastinal repositioning.

However, there are further indications for stent placement in PPS. A stent can be placed prior to a scheduled surgery, especially in patients with tracheomalacia, as a therapeutic bridge to surgical intervention; notably, care needs to be taken during the intubation process as it can lead to stent migration (14,17). Stent placement may also be indicated postoperatively in patients with tracheomalacia. Mediastinal shifting during PPS can create prolonged compression of the cartilaginous rings of the bronchus, which can ultimately lead to softening of the rings and subsequent airway collapse. In these instances, merely shifting the mediastinum with prosthetic implantation and surgical fixation may not adequately address the patient’s symptoms. Placing a stent in the collapsible region may provide a good response and is therefore indicated in such cases (18). Long-term outcomes regarding the effectiveness of stents as adjunctive interventions to surgery are poorly reported, although their use in tracheomalacia both before surgery (as a bridge to definitive management) and postoperatively is likely to provide benefit. Although clinically inferior to surgical management, patients unfit for surgery would benefit from stent placement despite the relatively high complication rates.

The severity and etiology of the presenting symptoms may also necessitate adjunctive care. The rare instance of pulmonary vein compression due to mediastinal shift may necessitate extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support to bridge the time until a patient can be brought to the operating room. The etiology in this case was not due to poor ventilation but was instead due to the inability to fill the left heart appropriately, creating hemodynamic instability; therefore, this case would not be adequately controlled with intubation and mechanical ventilation alone. Rzyman et al. described a case in which a 55-year-old female developed “severe hypoxemic and circulatory failure”, which was temporized with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) support and ultimately treated with tissue expander implantation (12). Notably, this implant required replacement just 1 year later due to a leak. This case highlights the potential severity of the broad clinical spectrum of PPS, while also noting the limitations of current management strategies (12).

For patients who are surgical candidates, the gold standard for the primary management of PPS involves mediastinal repositioning using a prosthesis (5). The best prosthesis may be a saline-filled expander, as it allows real-time adjustment in the operating room. Regardless of the prosthesis placed in the chest cavity, high-resolution CT with three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction should be performed before surgery to determine the volume of the chest cavity, providing an estimate of the volume needed to fill the space and how many prostheses may be needed. Most cardiothoracic surgeons work with plastic surgeons to help with the selection and management of saline tissue expanders. In a single-institution analysis of 18 patients (15 females and 3 males) with PPS from January 1992 to June 2006, all 18 underwent mediastinal repositioning by reopening the operative site, lysing adhesions to allow the mediastinum to assume its anatomical location, and filling the space with saline-filled breast prostheses (4). Nine of these patients also underwent pericardial suturing of the sternal fascia during surgery (4). Complications reported included death in one patient with multisystem organ failure after aortic division and bypass in a middle-aged female who developed recurrent PPS 4 years after the initial treatment, pneumonia in three patients, and acute respiratory distress in two patients (4). Their median follow-up was 32 months, and only one patient required reoperation, believed to be due to contralateral lobar compression from the implants (4). These outcomes, though not without complications, demonstrate good long-term functional and practical results from saline breast prostheses.

In the operating room, the patient should undergo single-lumen intubation and central line placement to assess the impact of the prosthesis on correcting airway compression and its impact on the cardiovascular system. The patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position with the previous pneumonectomy side facing up. The patient should undergo posterolateral thoracotomy to gain access to the pneumonectomy space. Subsequently, extensive lysis of adhesions is performed to facilitate mediastinal mobilization, allowing for implant placement. For most adults, two saline tissue expanders are placed: one at the apex and the other over the diaphragm, and 100–200 mL of saline is incrementally instilled into each expander. The target volume should be based on the volume of the chest cavity, calculated using high-resolution CT with 3D reconstruction. Underfilling the prosthesis may not correct PPS; however, overfilling may lead to cardiovascular collapse by preventing venous return. Therefore, careful monitoring must be performed during this portion of the operation. Continuous bronchoscopy should be performed with each saline fill to determine the point at which there is a fully patent airway, and perioperative central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring is important to ensure that the saline expander does not impede venous return. If the CVP increases by >5 mmHg or is >10 mmHg, saline should be removed from the prosthesis to correct the blood pressure (5). The endpoint of saline expansion is when the airway lumen is fully patent and the trachea is near the midline with stable hemodynamics. There are several variations in the literature regarding how these steps may occur. Jansen et al. describe two cases in which expandable prostheses were implanted and incorporated with a subcutaneous port that allowed for external volume adjustment more easily postoperatively (19). Chang et al. used saline implants that were filled once implanted into the thoracic cavity, allowing for smaller incisions, with the degree of repositioning confirmed by bronchoscopy and echo (20). Ultimately, one’s goal should be to restore anatomical positioning of the mediastinum while taking care to ensure hemodynamic stability and patency of the airway.

Despite the need for occasional reintervention, mediastinal repositioning with prosthetic implantation remains the most durable and physiologically corrective strategy, as it directly addresses the underlying anatomic distortion rather than temporizing airway patency alone. The so-called “best” implant appears to be situationally dependent. The capacity for incremental expansion of the prosthesis is particularly advantageous in pediatric patients, as it allows adjustment to progressive increases in intrathoracic dimensions associated with growth. Fluid volume can also be adjusted in adults if the thoracic cavity size changes. A systematic review of the PubMed and Embase databases by Hancock et al. found no difference in efficacy or failure rates in the type of prosthesis used (saline vs. silicone vs. tissue expander), indicating that the best prosthetic is likely the one that best suits the patient at that specific time and addresses any potential growth this individual may undergo (21). Although the efficacy was the same, they found that overfilling complications were more common with saline implants, and leaking was more common with tissue expanders; additionally, a larger implant volume was significantly correlated with the complication rate (21). In this review, they found that children overwhelmingly received tissue expanders (93%), whereas adults received an “expandable prosthesis” only 54% of the time, supporting the idea that many children receive expanders due to the ability to increase expansion with growth (21). A long-term analysis of expandable prosthetic implantation in seven children was performed with a mean follow-up of 6 years, where all cases reported adequate symptom control; this demonstrates that an expandable prosthesis allowed for progressive mediastinal repositioning with growth that is well tolerated and effective in the long term (22). The most effective strategy to determine hemithoracic volume was found to be image-based volume estimation with CT scans and intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring, as previously described (21), Although generally well tolerated, implants are not without risk, and patients may present with intolerance to the implant material. Merlo et al. describe the case of a 16-year-old female who presented with fevers, night sweats, and arthralgias 2 years after undergoing silicone-based implant-based treatment of PPS. Her workup was ultimately negative, and her symptoms improved after implant removal. Her symptoms were attributed to intolerance to the silica-based material of the implant (23).

In 85 patients who underwent mediastinal repositioning, Christodoulides et al. demonstrated that short-term outcomes were generally favorable. Sixty-four (75.3%) patients experienced no complications, although 9.4% experienced symptom recurrence due to improper prosthesis filling, and a few required volume adjustments or repeat surgeries (6). Intraoperative hemodynamic compromise occurred in four patients (4.6%), and three deaths (3.4%) were reported within 1 month postoperatively (6). The median follow-up was 1 year, and long-term data showed that 15.9% of patients required repeat surgical intervention, most commonly for prosthesis leakage (8.9%) (6). Some patients required custom implants, redo thoracotomy, or endobronchial stenting to address complications such as prosthesis failure or airway collapse (6). Overall, PPS correction demonstrates generally favorable short- and long-term outcomes, though careful prosthesis sizing and monitoring remain critical (6).

Other techniques have also been reported to achieve mediastinal repositioning successfully. These include anchoring the pericardium to the posterior sternum with sutures, performing surgical or chemical phrenectomy, and inserting prosthetic materials to occupy the vacant hemithorax. Materials used for treatment have included Lucite spheres, silicone implants, and intrathoracic injection of sulfur hexafluoride gas (5). Recently, there was a report on the use of a carbon fiber printed prosthesis (24). Although novel and with good short-term outcomes at 1 year, there is concern that the rigidity of such a structure may eventually lead to vascular damage over time, necessitating longer-term follow-up to prove safe use in young individuals (25). Many individuals have also combined several of these techniques, notably performing mediastinal correction and cardiopexy in conjunction with the implantation of prostheses (14).

In general, the long-term outcomes of PPS and repair remain poorly reported. Based on an extensive literature review, the best surgical management is one that is tailored specifically to the patient. The tissue expander prosthesis provides the most customizable experience over time, which is largely beneficial for growing individuals. However, they tend to exhibit higher leak rates. Saline prostheses provide good long-term outcomes, especially in adult populations; however, caution must be exercised to avoid overfilling, as this leads to significant postoperative morbidity and reoperation rates. Silica-based implants also have good outcomes but are limited in their ability to adapt to growth as their size is fixed and may be complicated by intolerance to the material. In general, stenting should be reserved for those who are not surgical candidates and/or suffer from persistent tracheomalacia postoperatively. For individuals with severe hemodynamic compromise, VV-ECMO may provide a bridge to surgery if hypoxia is believed to be due to poor pulmonary venous return rather than airway obstruction.

Conclusions

PPS remains a rare but serious complication of total lung resection, most commonly following right-sided pneumonectomy and in younger patients with greater mediastinal mobility. Although its incidence is low, the physiological consequences of progressive mediastinal shift and airway compression can be significant and life-threatening, necessitating timely recognition and intervention.

Current evidence supports mediastinal repositioning with fixed-volume prostheses or saline-filled tissue expanders as the primary and most durable corrective strategy, with generally favorable short- and long-term outcomes despite a measurable risk of prosthesis-related complications. Endobronchial stenting may provide temporary or palliative relief in selected patients who are not candidates for surgery. Additionally, stenting may be indicated in cases of tracheomalacia, both preoperatively as a bridge to surgical intervention and in cases where dynamic airway collapse persists due to tracheomalacia despite surgical repair of the mediastinal shift. Given the rarity of PPS, high-quality comparative data are limited, and management decisions are frequently guided by institutional experience. However, with the introduction of novel techniques and technologies, and as minimally invasive thoracic surgery continues to grow, approaches to surgical management will continue to evolve. The ability to adjust prosthetics from subcutaneous ports, the introduction of expandable prosthetics through small incisions (or even port sites), and the ability to directly customize an implant with 3D printing technology may all provide advancements in care in the near future. Future efforts should focus on the standardized reporting of outcomes and refinement of prosthetic designs. Further studies analyzing long-term outcomes, variations in technique, and multidisciplinary reviews would significantly contribute to future surgical innovations in this disease process.
