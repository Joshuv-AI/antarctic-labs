Self-sufficient killing of malignant tumors by an engineered cancer-selective gene circuit
Authors: Qi Liu, Qiqi Xiong, Guanglei Xie, Qidi Chen, Shujie Yang, Yuhang Wu, Liuqi Zhao, Ting Gao, Xinyuan Qiu, Jian Jiang, Xi Wang, Jiawei Shao, Mingqi Xie, Songmin Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGene therapies that selectively eliminate tumor cells within a heterogenous population remain an elusive goal in oncology, largely due to the difficulty to achieve cancer specificity by strict definition. Here, we engineer a therapeutic gene circuit capable of identifying cells that harbor oncogenic gain-of-function aberrations in E26 transformation-specific (ETS) transcription factors. Using a machine learning-guided random forest framework, we develop a cancer-selective promoter PETS∗ that only activates during ETS overexpression and/or gene fusion events, while remaining inactive under physiological RAF-MEK-ERK signaling or in rapidly proliferating healthy tissues. When delivered using adenoviral vectors, PETS∗ enables tumor-restricted viral replication in vivo as well as long-lasting tumor suppression and complete survival of treated mice. Specifically, intratracheal delivery of PETS∗-driven adenoviruses achieves sustained control of metastatic lung tumors for over 140 days. This work overcomes key barriers of synthetic biology and oncolytic virotherapy and could open up important avenues for future cancer treatment.

Introduction

Cancer afflicts several million patients per year worldwide with mortality rates remaining above 30% even in developed countries such as the United States.1 At present, treatment efficacy is predominantly impeded by the lack of technologies than can strictly distinguish malignant cells from healthy tissues.2,3,4 Although suicide gene therapies form a theoretically ideal treatment regimen by using cancer/tumor-specific promoters to drive the expression of toxic gene products and selectively eliminate malignant subpopulations upon delivery into a heterogenous tumor mass,5,6,7,8 none of such promoters developed to date are truly selective to cancer in its strictest sense.9 For example, mainstream cancer-specific promoters derived from human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT)10 or survivin genes (BIRC5)11 or E2F-responsive cell-cycle programs12 are applicable to a wide range of tumors but are often also active in healthy proliferating cells and could, therefore, show typical limitations of conventional chemotherapies.13,14 In contrast, tumor-specific promoters based on alpha-fetoprotein protein (AFP) or carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) are indeed specific to the tumorigenesis process but are only available for a limited number of cancer types while showing relatively weak induction folds of gene expression.8,15 As a consequence, an indirect implementation strategy also known as “gene-directed enzyme prodrug therapy (GDEPT)” has evolved in the field, in which the inducible promoter controls the expression of a prodrug-converting enzyme within the transduced tissue.16 This allows the administration of a prodrug to induce lethality to cells that express the therapeutic transgene as well as to its vicinity through a phenomenon known as bystander effects.17,18 However, the clinical success of GDEPTs remains below expectation, as suicide gene therapies based on HSV-TK could neither improve tumor progression nor improve the overall survival rates of patients19 and those built on the CPG2/NM system show substantial toxicity and/or immunogenicity issues potentially caused by leakage of xenogeneic enzymes into the circulation.16 Thus, technologies allowing for improved (1) cancer-selective detection, (2) efficiency of transgene delivery, and/or (3) safety and efficacy of tumor lysis are urgently required to unlock the full treatment potential of cancer gene therapies.3,4,8

To close this gap, we present a synthetic gene circuit that can specifically detect the aberrant transcriptional activities of E26 transformation-specific (ETS) family members in human cells. The ETS family comprises 28 transcription factors responsive to key signaling pathways involved in proliferation (e.g., MAPK/ERK) or survival (e.g., PI3K/AKT) and may, therefore, function as central nodes of tumor initiation and development.20,21 Under normal conditions, ETS family transcription factors (ETS-TFs) govern critical cellular processes such as cell cycle control, differentiation, and apoptosis,22,23 yet their aberrant regulation—often through chromosomal translocations, gene amplifications, or overexpression—profoundly alters transcriptional networks,24,25 thereby driving tumorigenesis, metastasis, and immune evasion.20,26 Thus, we engineered a synthetic promoter PETS∗ with the capability to respond to such oncogenic gain-of-function ETS aberrations known to be involved in most human cancers,26 eventually enabling accurate detection of pathological ETS signaling without showing responsiveness to physiological ERK/MAPK activation at baseline ETS-TF levels in primary immune cells. Also, in contrast to conventional cancer-specific promoters, we specifically demonstrate that PETS∗ was neither activated in mammalian cells nor activated in tissues during normal fast proliferation in vivo. Then, we went on to engineer an oncolytic adenovirus (ETS∗-selective oncolytic adenovirus [ADETS∗]) where PETS∗ was used to control tumor-specific virus replication and cell killing. Because adenoviruses typically lyse their host cells during proliferation,27 ADETS∗ administration was effective in self-sufficiently triggering “iterative” cycles of tumor transduction, cancer-selective detection, and cell lysis in vivo. When tested in mice using xenograft models, we show that ADETS∗ could suppress the growth of tumors of up to 150 mm3 in mass and maintain a 100% survival rate following repeated injection into tissues of abnormal ETS-TF signatures. To describe another clinically relevant treatment option, we show that intratracheal ADETS∗ delivery could effectively suppress the growth of metastatic lung tumors over the course of 140 days, whereas control groups not constantly receiving the ADETS∗-mediated gene therapy only survived for 74 ± 6.7 days. In all experiments, we consistently validate strict tumor-specific virus replication, inactivity of engineered PETS∗ promoters in healthy tissues, as well as the absence of systemic toxicity and adverse effects on body weight potentially caused by the means of virus administration. Taken together, this work presents a rational strategy to design tumor-selective gene circuits, alleviates the restriction of oncolytic virotherapies to rely on intratumoral injection as the sole administration route, and could offer an attractive solution to overcome tumor heterogeneity and resistance.

Results

ETS family members as potential cancer-selective targets

Emerging hallmarks of cancer—including genome instability and mutation, sustained proliferative signaling, and immune evasion mechanisms—highlight the intriguing possibility of developing therapeutic strategies that target universal oncogenic traits across diverse cancer types.28 Although pan-cancer analyses have consistently identified high-frequency driver mutations in genes such as ARID1A, TP53, KRAS, PIK3CA, TERT, CDKN2A, MYC, and PTEN,28,29 emerging evidence suggests that targeting dysregulated signaling-specific transcription networks could provide a more comprehensive therapeutic approach by capturing critical cellular information spanning genomic aberrations to oncogenic transformation.29,30,31 Notably, the ETS family transcription factors (ETS-TFs) has emerged as a key oncogenic driver showing aberrant activation throughout tumorigenesis and demonstrating pathogenic significance in various solid tumors26,32 (Figure S1A). This transcription factor family represents a critical phosphorylation target of the RTK/MAPK/RAS cascade21—a predominant oncogenic pathway exhibiting the highest median frequency of genetic alterations across malignancies.29,33 Consistent with these findings, our bioinformatic analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas transcriptomic datasets34 revealed significant ETS-TF overexpression in multiple cancer types (Figure S1B). Notably, this overexpression persisted in both tumors with common genetic alterations (Figure S1C) and those without such mutations (Figure S1D), underscoring the fundamental role of ETS-TFs as universal oncogenic mediators independent of specific mutational contexts (Figure S1A). A gene circuit programmed for selective detection and elimination of cells with oncogenic gain-of-function ETS aberrations

Abnormal ETS-TF activities are typically manifested through chromosomal translocations, genetic overexpression, and impaired protein degradation.26 Thus, a synthetic gene circuit engineered for cancer-selective therapeutic transgene expression should ideally respond to all different types of abnormal ETS-TF activities (ETS∗), while remaining silent in cells with a normal ETS signature (Figure 1A). Each ETS-TF uses a highly conserved DNA-binding domain of 85 amino acids in length to bind a cognate purine-rich response element containing a consensus GGA(A/T) core sequence18 (Figure 1A). To create a universal synthetic ETS∗-selective promoter, we first used a cell-based reporter assay based on human placental secreted alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) to test different combinations and copy numbers of ETS response elements in their specific capability to drive ETS-TF-dependent gene expression (Figure 1A; Figures S2A–S2H). In particular, we first compared the performances between multimerized consensus repeats and other TF-specific ETS-binding sequences, such as the native ETV4-binding site (CCGGAAGT) and the EHF-binding site (ACTTCCTG) in mediating ETV4-dependent transcription (Figure S2A). Results show that consecutive consensus repeats exhibited the most pronounced and dose-dependent increase in reporter gene expression in response to ETV4 overexpression (Figure S2A). The same phenomenon was observed for most other ETS family members, in which a promoter construct containing up to seven tandem consensus repeats appeared to account for superior fold-changes of target gene expression when directly compared with isogenic controls engineered with native response elements of each specific ETS member (Figures S2B–S2H). Notably, this result remained consistent across different cancer-related ETS-TF members that activate transcription either by ectopic overexpression (ETV4, Figure S2A; ETS2, Figure S2F) or by experimental MAPK-dependent phosphorylation (ELK1, Figure S2B; SPIB, Figure S2C; EHF, Figure S2D; ELK3, Figure S2E; ELF5, Figure S2G; ELF3, Figure S2H).

Based on these experimental data, we went on to establish a quantitative relationship between binding site candidates (CCGGAAGT, ACTTCCTG, or GGAA), copy numbers (1–23), and ultimate fold-changes of gene expression (Figures S2A–S2H) for delineation of sequence features that may positively affect the performance of promoter elements with a preference to respond to ETS family members with known roles in malignant transformations26 (ETV4, ETV1, ELF4, FLI1, ETS1, ERG, ETV5, ETV2, ETS2, ELF5, EHF, ELF3, SPIB, ELK1, SPI1, and ELK3; Figures S2I and S2J). Then, we applied a random forest model in which promoter sequences were vectorized using a combination of one-hot and 4-mer codes, thereby capturing both positional nucleotide information and short motif patterns (Figure 1B, left panel). Feature importance analysis of the trained model revealed that the GGAA motif was the dominant contributor to transcriptional activation (Figure 1B, right panel). Consistent with this prediction, cell-based assays demonstrated that promoters containing tandem GGAA repeats exhibited robust activation under diverse ETS-abnormal conditions. Hence, a synthetic configuration PETS∗ containing 21 tandem consensus GGAA motifs placed upstream of a minimal cytomegalovirus-derived promoter (PhCMVmin) was identified as the best-in-class candidate to detect abnormal ETS-TF levels produced by genetic overexpression (Figure S2I), phosphorylation (Figure S2J), and/or gene fusion (Figure 2A). Killing efficacy and tumor selectivity of PETS∗-driven gene circuits

After confirming that PETS∗ could dose-dependently detect various types of aberrant ETS-TF signatures produced by ectopic overexpression (Figure 2A), we tested the efficacy of such gene circuit in distinguishing various cancer cell lines with differential endogenous ETS signatures (Figure S3A). To normalize the efficiency of gene circuit delivery, we produced lentiviral particles containing either a constitutive (PhCMV) or PETS∗-driven mCherry expression cassette (Figure 2B). Then, upon lentiviral transduction of cell lines showing normal (e.g., HEK-293, RD, HeLa, and MCF-7; Figure S3A) or aberrant ETS-TF expression levels (A549, NCL-H1299, MCF-7/ADR, LoVo, and 786-0; Figure S3A), a strict cancer-related responsiveness to oncogenic ETS aberrations was observed for all cell lines tested (Figure 2B; Figure S3B). Thus, after confirming that the engineered PETS∗-driven circuit was equally responsive to both human (Figure 2A) and murine ETS-TF members with high relevance for cancer (Figure S3C), and that activation of PETS∗-dependent gene expression required both overexpression and continuous activation of MAPK/ERK targets for over 4 h (Figure S3D), we evaluated the safety profile of cancer-selective PETS∗ detection in vivo (Figures 2C–2E). For this purpose, we loaded a PETS∗-dependent firefly luciferase (FLuc) expression unit into adeno-associated virus (AAV) particles of the serotypes 2/9 and PHP.eB that are known to show a broad tropism after systemic administration in vivo.35,36 Following intravenous injection into healthy C57BL/6 mice, no PETS∗-mediated FLuc expression was observed in various organs by bioluminescence analysis of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain (Figure 2C; Figure S3E). Though, AAV particles containing a constitutive FLuc expression unit confirm that these organs were indeed effectively transduced (Figure 2C). In contrast, we observed off-target FLuc expression in healthy organs following systemic injection of isogenic AAV particles engineered with conventional tumor-specific (e.g., AFP-driven) or tissue-specific promoters (e.g., PhGfaABC1D) (Figure 2C). Then, to assess therapeutically relevant and PETS∗-compatible killing strategies, we created lentivirus particles in which PETS∗ was used to control the expression of pro-apoptotic hBax protein, herpes simplex virus-derived thymidine kinase (HSV-TK), or the adenovirus replication gene E1A as three types of effector proteins typically used in state-of-the-art suicide gene therapies37 and/or oncolytic virotherapies38 (Figure 2D). Upon transduction of ETS∗-positive A549, cells were supplemented with either ganciclovir (to activate HSV-TK) or replication-deficient adenoviruses (to activate E1A), with results showing that a therapeutic gene circuit built upon PETS∗-driven adenovirus replication achieved superior killing efficacies than either indirect (HSV-TK-based) or direct (hBax-based) suicide gene therapy approaches. Using the same killing assay, we also confirmed that PETS∗-mediated detection of malignancy was not dependent on the cells’ proliferation status (Figure S4A) and that PETS∗ appeared to remain inactive in non-tumorigenic human retinal pigment epithelial cells showing fast proliferation rates (Figure S4B). Instead, our experimental results suggest that conventional cancer-specific promoters derived from human telomerase (hTERT),10 human survivin (hSurv),11 and human E2F (hE2F)12 may be less efficient in distinguishing cancer cells from healthy proliferating tissues in vitro (Figure S4B) and in mice (Figure 2E; Figure S3F). Though, PhSurv appeared to be capable of killing HeLa cells representative for a different set of tumors, for which PETS∗ showed limited activity (Figure S4C). However, we showed that PETS∗ remained strictly insensitive to physiological activation of ERK/MAPK signaling and/or expression of ETS genes under normal cellular conditions, such as during GM-CSF-driven myeloid differentiation of murine bone marrow cells (Figures S5A–S5C) or activation of primary peripheral blood mononuclear cell-derived T cells (Figures S5D and S5E). Thus, in direct comparison to conventional cancer-specific promoters such as PhSurv, PETS∗ may be applicable to a smaller set of cancer cells, but could provide enhanced safety layers by ensuring inactivity during various physiological and/or cancer-unrelated conditions. Furthermore, we show that PETS∗ cannot be activated by the endogenous nuclear factor of endogenous T cells (NFAT) that is known to bind a very similar GGAAA consensus motif (Figure S3G), further suggesting that PETS∗ may fulfill the expectation for cancer-/tumor-selective detection at a high standard without compromising the overall efficacy of therapeutic target gene expression.

ADETS∗ for gene therapy applications

After evaluating the safety and efficacy of PETS∗-mediated detection from the viewpoint of cancer-specific target gene expression (Figure 2; Figures S3–S5), we engineered “one-component” oncolytic adenoviruses ADETS∗ by integrating the synthetic PETS∗-driven E1A transcription unit into the viral genome. Thus, direct administration of ADETS∗ to cancer cells is expected to trigger repeated and self-sufficient cycles of ETS∗ detection, tumor-specific virus replication and cell lysis, as well as tissue (re-)infection in closed loop (Figure 3A). Experimental results show that ADETS∗ produced not only ETS∗-selective killing efficacies (Figures 3B and 3C; Figures S6A and S6B) that were comparable to those previously achieved with lentiviral gene circuit delivery (Figure 2D; Figure S4) but also a clear correlation between adenovirus replication and cell lysis (Figure 3D). To test the applicability of ADETS∗ in identifying target cells within heterogenous populations, we established a cell-based assay based on co-cultivation of ETS∗-positive A549 stably expressing mCherry with ETS∗-negative human rhabdomyosarcoma cells (Figure 3E). Four days after exposure of the cell mixture to ADETS∗, A549 cells producing red fluorescence was no longer observed, with the surviving cell population consisting of unaffected rhabdomyosarcoma cells showing healthy morphologies as revealed by both microscopic and flow cytometric analysis (Figure 3E). In contrast, no significant cytotoxic effects were observed when the cell mixture was treated with a replication-deficient control strain ADΔE1A (Figure 3E). Such precisely coordinated killing mechanism could form an important basis to move toward therapeutic applications, where normal tissues within a heterogenous cell population should ideally remain intact during the course of treatment.39 To demonstrate potential efficacy in cancer treatment, we established tumor xenograft models based on ETS∗-positive A549 and MCF-7/ADR cells, as well as RD cells showing a normal signature of ETS-TF expression (Figure 4A). When tumors grew into a volume of at least 50 mm3, therapeutically active ADETS∗ or replication-deficient ADΔE1A (negative control) were intratumorally injected once per week. Results show that administration of ADETS∗ could indeed suppress the growth of A549- (Figures 4B–4D) and MCF-7/ADR-derived tumors (Figure S7), but did not affect the development of tumors harvested from RD cells (Figure S8); this again confirms strict responsiveness to oncogenic ETS aberrations of the therapeutic gene circuit. Furthermore, we show that there were no changes in the body weight of mice treated with either ADETS∗ or ADΔE1A (Figure 4E; Figures S7D and S8D) and that virus replication could not be detected in various healthy tissues outside the tumor in vivo (Figure 4F). These results suggest that the safety and efficacy profiles of an ADETS∗-mediated gene therapy are generally acceptable at the current preclinical stage. We also show that ADETS∗ remained effective even after injection of viruses into ETS∗-positive tumors that have already reached a relatively high volume of 150 mm3, not only showing side effect-free suppression of tumor progression (Figures 4G and 4H) but also maintaining a 100% survival rate for mice that were receiving the cancer gene therapy for over 100 days (Figure 4I). In contrast, mice injected with therapeutically inactive ADΔE1A did not survive for more than 60 days (Figure 4I), indicating the applicability of using ADETS∗ as a treatment alternative to control advanced-stage cancers. Importantly, histological analysis of dissected tumors after the treatment (42 days; Figures 4G–4I) demonstrated that viral replication, as indicated by GFP fluorescence, remained spatially restricted to discrete tumor regions (Figure S9A). Notably, ADETS∗ treatment groups showed stronger and more disseminated GFP signals than ADΔE1A-treated tumors, indicating the onset of active virus replication during the killing process (Figure S9A). Furthermore, after extraction and digestion of these residual tumors into single cells, an ADETS∗-dependent killing efficacy was still retained after administration of 10 MOI of ADETS∗ in culture (Figure S9B), suggesting that the survived tumor population remains responsive to the therapy without evolving resistance mechanisms. Treatment potential for metastatic lung cancer by tracheal ADETS∗ administration

Currently, intratumor injection is the major administration route of oncolytic virotherapy, which restricts applicability to cancers that have accessible lesions and/or to follow-up treatments of unresectable tumors after initial surgery.40 In recent years, aerosol-mediated gene delivery has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of lung cancer, a leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide.18 Specifically, around 40% of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are diagnosed at stages III or IV, for which effective treatment strategies are currently lacking.41,42 Thus, we speculated whether intratracheal ADETS∗ administration could become an effective alternative for NSCLC treatment, not only describing a potentially attractive delivery route for oncolytic adenoviruses but also proposing a gene therapy approach for a hitherto intractable disease such as lung cancer. To establish a corresponding disease model, we systemically injected FLuc-transgenic A549 cells into NSG mice (Figure 5A). After 7 days, mice were exposed to different ADETS∗ doses administered through a vaporizer device, administered every other day for a total of seven treatments, allowing for ETS∗-specific lysis of metastatic tumors formed in the lungs (Figure 5A). Remarkably, bioluminescence imaging revealed a dose-dependent suppression of pulmonary tumor growth after a month, with a high dose of 2 × 109 VP applied every second day accounting for a profound reduction in tumor burden when compared with controls (Figure 5B). Histological analysis via H&E staining corroborated these findings, revealing a significant reduction in the size and number of tumor nodules (Figure 5C). Once again, quantitative PCR analysis confirmed selective ADETS∗ replication within pulmonary tissues, achieving a more than 1,000-fold enrichment when compared with controls with no detectable systemic dissemination (Figure 5D). To test the long-term efficacy of an intratracheally administered ADETS∗ therapy, we divided mice into two treatment groups (Figure 6A). While one group received aerosol delivery of ADETS∗ over the course of 140 days (denoted ADETS∗(L) for “long-term”), ADETS∗ administration was discontinued at day 19 for another group to form a “short-term” treatment regimen (denoted as ADETS∗(S)) (Figure 6A). As expected, while the ADETS∗(L) group constantly receiving ADETS∗ aerosols exhibited effective tumor suppression throughout the entire experimental time span, tumor relapse was observed in the ADETS∗(S) group after 41 days of treatment cessation as indicated by bioluminescence signals (Figures 6B and 6C). This suggests that metastatic tumors can remain sensitive to a cancer-selective gene therapy over an extended treatment window, but the oncolytic ADETS∗ may fail to “eradicate” all cancer cells in vivo. Nevertheless, ADETS∗ could account for a 100% survival rate while remaining efficacious, whereas mice receiving therapeutically inactive ADΔE1A did not survive for more than 76 days (Figure 6D). A similar pattern was also observed in a histological analysis performed at day 60, where similar profiles of lung metastases were observed for ADΔE1A and ADETS∗(S), but the ADETS∗(L) treatment group showed markedly reduced numbers of tumor nodules (Figure 6E). Furthermore, no changes in body weight were observed across all groups (Figure S10A) as well as in healthy C57BL/6 mice receiving either type of AD particles (Figure S10B). Similarly, histopathological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain, revealed no overt tissue damage attributable to tracheal ADETS∗ administration (Figure S10C). Consistent with these findings, no detectable physiological or histopathological abnormalities across major organs were observed with systemic administration of ADETS∗ either (Figures S10D and S10E). Discussion

On average, each malignant cell may contain up to five mutations across distinct genomic elements (e.g., TP53, KRAS, or PTEN),29 which could complicate the development of mutation-specific therapies. Also, the double-edged role of p53 in serving both as a tumor suppressor and a context-dependent guardian of the cell may raise further safety concerns when directly targeting such “pan-cancer” genes.43,44 In contrast, the role of ETS family transcription factors (ETS-TFs) in malignant transformation is widely established, as ETS factors are known to drive oncogenic kinase signaling, co-regulate p53 expression, manipulate cellular longevity through TERT, and modulate genomic instability as well as the chromatin landscape.26 Also, disruption of various cancer-related signaling pathways (e.g., MAPK/ERK or PI3K/AKT) commonly leads to ETS-TF overexpression in various solid tumors26 (Figure S1). However, even though the targeting of ETS family members is indeed regarded as an effective therapeutic strategy to treat cancer,26 the success of ETS-specific drug discovery has remained limited by the putatively “undruggable” nature of transcriptional regulators according to conventional mindsets.31,32,45 Here, we use a synthetic gene circuit as an unconventional approach to target oncogenic gain-of-function ETS aberrations by directly linking aberrant transcriptional activities (ETS∗) to the initiation of customized therapeutic outcomes. In this manner, therapeutic killing programs can be flexibly designed to “decouple” from specific mutational contexts and, therefore, counteract the evolution of drug resistances in a highly personalized and effective manner, as it is relatively unlikely that any one transcription factor functioning as a definitive oncogenic driver can be completely replaced by another.31 Therefore, this approach of directly targeting intracellular signaling-specific transcription factors may set the stage to an alternative mindset of drug discovery, in which the modular architecture of the synthetic gene circuit could allow flexible targeting of other seemingly “undruggable” transcription factors. For example, by replacing the ETS-TF-specific consensus motif with other signaling-specific response elements—such as E-box motifs for MYC-driven malignancies46 or NF-κB-mediated lymphoma47—this framework could be extended to a wide array of oncogenic networks that have hitherto been inaccessible to conventional pharmacology. In parallel, recent therapeutic efforts have begun to directly exploit amplified genomic loci as vulnerabilities (e.g., TFO-based strategies that induce copy-number-dependent DNA damage), underscoring the broader concept of using amplification status for patient stratification and for designing rational combinations with programmable gene circuits.48,49 Similarly, the versatility of our delivery strategy could be adapted for other anatomical compartments; intrapleural administration for mesothelioma or intravesical dosing for bladder cancer are potential extensions that capitalize on tissue-specific barriers to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Using a machine learning-guided optimization framework to establish quantitative relationships between putative binding sites, promoter sizes, and experimental results of gene expression performance (Figure 1B; Figure S2), we engineered PETS∗ as a putative cancer-selective promoter capable of preferentially responding to aberrant levels of ETS transcription factors that play major roles in malignant transformations26 (i.e., ETV4, ETV1, ELF4, FLI1, ETS1, ERG, ETV5, ETV2, ETS2, ELF5, EHF, ELF3, SPIB, ELK1, SPI1, and ELK3; Figure S3C). As prominent examples of physiological activation of ETS family gene expression, we show that PETS∗ remained consistently inactive during key cellular processes of GM-CSF-driven hematopoietic differentiation50,51 and CD3/CD28-mediated T cell activation52 (Figure S5), suggesting that physiological ETS-TF induction in proliferating normal cells (e.g., SPI1) is insufficient to activate PETS∗. For the specific case of SPI1, our data suggest that activation of PETS∗ requires not only overexpression but also persistent phosphorylation of the corresponding ETS family transcription factor (Figure S2J), such as by the activation of upstream MAPK/ERK signaling for more than 4 h (Figure S3D)—constraints that may be only fulfilled in malignant cancers.53 Furthermore, with our results also showing that PETS∗ may remain insensitive to mTOR signaling (Figure S4A), we would hypothesize that PETS∗ does not primarily identify cancer cells by their proliferation rates, but by other factors such as distinct transcriptional signatures (i.e., the types of ETS abnormalities as outlined in Figure 2A). Thus, this work also solves a long-standing issue of cancer gene therapies, where current strategies typically fail to achieve true tumor specificity in its strictest sense.4 Regardless of the currently available chemotherapies, suicide gene therapies, or even oncolytic virotherapies, cancer cells are primarily identified by their substantially higher proliferation rate when compared with healthy non-dividing cells, rather than by directly targeting signals that are related to oncogenesis.54 For example, most oncolytic viruses capitalize on the natural tropism of certain strains to selectively infect and replicate in actively dividing cells,8,41,55 as well as a partially defective antiviral machinery of tumors,56 to induce cytotoxicity to the transduced tissues following constitutive expression of specific effector genes in vivo.57 Although the modular nature of adenoviruses permits the use of tumor-specific promoters and/or oncogenic miRNA signatures to increase specificity of viral replication and transduction,58 tumor/cancer selectivity of currently available promoters is probably not sufficiently strict.59 Although hTERT and survivin-derived promoters have already progressed into clinical trials,8,56 we experimentally show that both promoters may also be active in healthy fast-proliferating cells (Figure S4B). Furthermore, human E2F and survivin promoters appear to be incapable of distinguishing tumors from natural regeneration processes such as wound healing (Figure 2E). Similarly, we show that an AFP-derived promoter could produce background expression levels in healthy livers (Figure 2C) and that a putative brain-specific promoter (PhGfaABC1D) also showed significant activity in various peripheral organs (Figure 2C). None of these issues were observed for the PETS∗ promoter (Figures 2C and 2E; Figure S4), which was designed to remain silent during physiological MAPK activation in cells with a normal ETS-TF signature (Figure S5), while being specific for well-defined malignancy states of gene amplification, overexpression, and gene fusion (Figure 2A). Although PETS∗ may be inferior to other putative cancer-selective promoters (e.g., PhSurv) in killing various ETS∗-negative tumors (e.g., rhabdomyosarcoma, HeLa, or MCF7; Figures S4C and S8) or loss-of-function mutations/alterations of ETS transcription factors,60 this strategy will be capable of capturing a sufficiently large set of cell states that are most likely cancerous and ensure strict inactivity in healthy fast-proliferating conditions in vivo. These features may substantially advance the safety of future cancer therapies.

In terms of therapeutic efficacy, we show that the engineered cancer selectivity of PETS∗ can be coupled with the natural ability of adenoviruses in coordinating replication and cell lysis to constitute a therapeutic gene circuit for self-sufficient cancer-selective detection and killing of various intractable tumors. In particular, solid tumors are characterized by clonal heterogeneity, the presence of immunosuppressive niches, and significant anatomical barriers that impede effective drug delivery.61,62 To this end, oncolytic viruses programmed to combine local self-amplification and anatomical targeting may be an attractive treatment strategy.63 Experimentally, we use co-culture models to show how ADETS∗ could selectively lyse ETS∗-positive cells, while leaving adjacent ETS∗-negative populations intact (Figure 3E). Although gene circuit-driven adenoviruses have been described for clinically eligible treatment of liver cancer,58 a remaining key challenge of current oncolytic virotherapies is the restriction to intratumor injection in vivo. Though oncolytic virotherapy has toxicity profiles that may compare favorably over conventional anticancer therapies,56 therapeutic efficiency of systemic virus injection remains below satisfaction.56 Here, we show that aerosol delivery of oncolytic ADETS∗ was effective in suppressing the onset of metastatic lung cancer via a non-invasive and patient-compliant administration route (Figures 5 and 6). In a preclinical pulmonary tumor model, we show that repeated ADETS∗ inhalation could achieve a 97% reduction in the metastatic tumor burden (Figures 5B and 5C), with viral replication being exclusively observed in the malignant lung tissue (Figure 5D). Furthermore, we show that ADETS∗ remained efficacious over the entire experimental course of 140 days, suggesting that resistance mechanisms did not evolve during this time span (Figure 6D). Consistently, tumors extracted and analyzed at 42 days after the treatment showed preserved ETS-TF expression patterns and sensitivity to ADETS∗ in vitro (Figure S9B), with histopathological evaluation revealing that viral replication remained spatially confined to discrete tumor regions (Figure S9A), suggesting that incomplete intratumoral dissemination—rather than acquired resistance—may limit complete eradication and explain relapse upon treatment discontinuation (Figure 6). For future clinical development, we, therefore, anticipate that strategies enhancing intratumoral viral spread—such as by the incorporation of matrix-degrading enzymes such as hyaluronidase64 or other strategies to remodel the tumor extracellular matrix (ECM)—would represent promising avenues to improve killing efficacy, particularly given that dense ECM and limited vascular perfusion may also constrain viral spread even with repeated adenovirus administration. Nevertheless, the type of efficiency achieved in this work offers a prospect of “relegating” cancer to a controllable chronic disorder with markedly reduced morbidity or mortality rates.18 Though, a major uncertainty of this work is how the findings from this study may translate into clinically relevant settings, as most currently available animal models based on immune-deficient mice can only hardly predict the efficacy of oncolytic viruses in humans.56 Although our data suggest that ADETS∗ may not elicit organ-level toxicity in an immunocompetent host (Figure S10), this would not exclude the possibility that such safety profiles were the result of a host antiviral response that would attenuate oncolytic activity. Another open question is whether and how other viral strains are as accessible to engineering as adenoviruses, since the prevalence of pre-existing immunity to the adenovirus serotype 5 is found in over 60% of adults.65 Patient stratification represents another critical avenue for optimizing the clinical impact of ADETS∗. Combining ETS immunohistochemistry with genomic analyses could identify NSCLC subsets and other ETS-related cancers (e.g., ERG-positive prostate or ETS1/ETV4-positive breast cancers32) that could most likely benefit from this approach. Such biomarker-driven selection not only enhances therapeutic precision but also aligns with the broader movement toward personalized medicine in oncology. In conclusion, this work describes fundamental design principles and application scopes of a cancer-selective gene therapy, overcomes important hurdles of cancer biology and oncolytic virotherapy, and could pave the way to the development of next-generation precision medicines for various intractable cancers.

Limitations of the study

Although PETS∗ was engineered to decode oncogenic gain-of-function ETS aberrations, its design intrinsically confines applicability to biomarker-defined malignancies that would necessitate well-known expression profiles of ETS family transcription factors, reflecting a trade-off between circuit specificity and breadth of coverage. While the promoter exhibits stringent transcriptional gating in the examined physiological contexts, the stability of this input-output behavior across diverse tissue states and dynamically fluctuating signaling environments remains to be fully established. In addition, despite the absence of detectable resistance under our experimental settings, the full therapeutic potential may be further constrained by limited intratumoral propagation of the viral circuit, pointing to spatial dissemination rather than circuit failure as a potential bottleneck. Also, the adherence to immune-deficient mice as the experimental model to establish xenografts further complicates extrapolation of therapeutic efficacy in immune-competent hosts such as humans. Therefore, as with many other oncolytic adenoviral platforms at such preclinical stages, systemic deployment remains challenged by host-virus interactions, including immune clearance and restricted biodistribution, highlighting the need to further optimize delivery strategies to fully leverage the programmability of this synthetic gene circuit.

Experimental model and study participant details

8-weeks old male NSG and C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Laboratory Animal Resources Center of Westlake University (Hangzhou, China). Animal experiments were performed according to the protocol (Protocol ID: 23-089-XMQ) approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Westlake University and in accordance with the Animal Care Guidelines of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China.

Xenograft tumor model. 3 × 106 human A549 or RD cells resuspended in 0.2mL sterile PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right lower back of 8-weeks old male NSG mice. After the tumor volume exceeded 50mm3, mice were randomly assigned into different treatment groups each receiving 3 × 109 VP of ADETS∗ or ADΔE1A by intratumor injection. Tumor size was monitored until reaching a maximal volume of 1500 mm3 or for 30 days after the first adenovirus injection.

Metastatic lung cancer model. 2 × 106 A549FLuc cells resuspended in 0.1mL sterile PBS were intravenously injected into 8-weeks old male NSG mice. Tumor growth was followed by monitoring luminescence signals in lungs using a PhotonIMAGER Optima real-time imaging device. Intratracheal instillation of ADETS∗ or ADΔE1A was performed using a Intratracheal Pulmonary Delivery Device (Yuyan Instruments, Shanghai, China, Cat# YAN30012).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Lung tissues were collected and fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde overnight, and dehydrated using an Ethanol gradient. Following clearing in xylene, tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 μm slices using a Leica RM2235 Microtome. Sections were stained with H&E Staining Kit and examined under an Olympus BX53 Digital Upright Microscope.

Excisional wound healing model. The murine wound healing model66 was established by clipping and shaving the hair from the back skin of anesthetized mice, followed by pinching skin tissues along the dorsal midline to allow a full-thickness cutaneous incision using a scalpel to create a 1cm diameter wound in the back. Plasmids were intradermally injected at 5 days after the natural wound healing process using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. Tissues were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy at 48h post injection following DAPI staining.

Tumor extraction and isolation of tumor-derived cells. Tumors were excised from mice and rinsed in ice-cold PBS, followed by dissection into ∼1–2 mm fragments using sterile scissors (Figure S9). Tissue fragments were then enzymatically dissociated in RPMI-1640 containing Collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (100 U/mL) by gentle agitation at 37°C for 1.5 h. After digestion, the suspension was quenched by adding an equal volume of complete cell culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were then filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to remove undigested tissue fragments and debris, followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. The cell pellet was washed once with PBS or complete medium and resuspended in appropriate growth medium.

Method details

Differential expression gene analysis

Bulk RNA-Seq data of the GDC TCGA portal were retrieved from the UCSC XENA database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/), and the STAR pipeline67 was used to quantify and normalize expression levels into TPM (transcript per million) measures. The number of TCGA samples and cancer types included in this analysis is summarized in Table S1. The data was then transformed using the log2(TPM+1) strategy. Differential expression levels of ETS-family associated genes between normal tissue adjacent to the tumor (NAT) and tumors (included cancer types: BLCA, BRCA, COAD, CHOL, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PAAD, PRAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, THCA and UCEC) were visualized using ggplot2 and ggpubr packages. Statistical analysis was performed by unpaired, two-sided Wilcox test. Similarly, differential expression levels of ETS family–associated genes between tumor samples harboring mutations in KRAS, TP53, ARID1A, PIK3CA, or PTEN (at least one gene mutated) and normal samples were compared. Mutations of KRAS, TP53, ARID1A, PIK3CA, or PTEN genes were extracted from the somatic mutations downloaded from the UCSC XENA database. TCGA samples with matched transcriptomic and somatic mutation data were used to assess expression differences between the mutation and normal groups. In addition, tumor samples without mutations in these genes were also compared to normal samples, as summarized in Table S2.

Cell Culture and transfection

Human cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) or Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 Medium (RPMI 1640) supplemented with 10% (v/v) Gibco Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), Australia (lot. no.2177370) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin solution under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37°C as described above. Doxycycline-resistant MCF7/ADR cells were cultivated in cell culture medium supplemented with 1 μg/mL Doxycycline. For passaging, cells of pre-confluent cultures were detached by incubation in 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China; cat no. A610629-0050; lot no. F319BA0030) for 3 min at 37°C, collected in 10 mL of cell culture medium, centrifuged for 2 min at 1000 rpm, and resuspended in fresh culture medium at 1.5 × 105 cells/mL, and then seeded into new tissue culture plates (Wuxi NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China). All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air. Cell number and viability were quantified using an Invitrogen Countess II AMQAX1000 Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfection was performed at 18h after seeding 60′000 mammalian cells into each well of a 24-well plate (Wuxi NEST Biotechnology). The cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium (not containing transfection reagents) at 6 h after transfection. HEK-293 cells were transfected using a PEI-based protocol at a PEI:DNA ratio of 3:1 (w/w) and in a transfection volume of 50 μL native serum-free DMEM per well. A549 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfection agents and nucleic acids were incubated for 15 min at 25°C before dropwise addition to cells. Unless indicated otherwise, cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium (not containing transfection reagents) at 6 h after transfection.

Lentivirus production

Recombinant replication-deficient lentivirus particles were generated by transfecting 3 × 106 native HEK-293 cells (cultured in a 10cm dish) with 5μg psPAX2 (Addgene no. 12260), 2.5μg pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene no. 12259) and 5μg of different transfer plasmids (pQL80, pQL116, pQL84, pQL85, pQL112, pQL163, pQL261, pQD699 and pYW483) carrying the desired gene expression cassette (Table S3). At 48h after transfection, culture supernatants containing viral particles was harvested, followed by centrifugation at 1000g for 5 min to remove cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.45μm filter for experimental use or storage at −80°C. Viral titers were determined using a BD FACSCanto II Flow Cytometry System or qRT-PCR.

AAV2/9-PETS∗-FLuc, AAV2/9-PMusAFP-Fluc, AAV2/9-PhCMV-FLuc, AAV2/9-PhCMVmin-FLuc, AAV-PHP.eB-PETS∗-FLuc, AAV-PHP.eB-PhCMV-Fluc, AAV-PHP.eB-PhCMVmin-Fluc and AAV-PHP.eB-PhGfaABC1D-Fluc were produced by PackGene Biotech (Guangzhou, China) using the transfer plasmids pQL120, pQL90, pYW475, pQL121 and pYW470 (Table S3), respectively.

Generation of stable cell lines

Polyclonal A549mCherry cells stably transgenic for constitutive mCherry expression were constructed by incubation of 5 × 104 A549 cells with supernatants containing lentiviral particles created with pQL80 as transfer plasmid. Following flow cytometric sorting using the MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter (Sony Biotechnology; San Jose, CA), the cell population showing strongest red fluorescence were harvested. Monoclonal A549FLuc cells stably transgenic for constitutive luciferase expression were constructed by transduction of 5 × 104 A549 cells with supernatants containing lentiviral particles created with pYW483 as transfer plasmid. After selection with 100 mg/mL puromycin for 10 days, the surviving cell population was subjected to FACS-mediated cell sorting followed by harvesting and selection of single cell clones.

Adenovirus production

The vector of oncolytic adenovirus showing ETS∗-selective replication (ADETS∗) was obtained by subcloning the corresponding expression cassette ((GGAA)21-PhCMVmini-E1A) from pQL83 into the NotI/BglII sites of pAdTrack (Addgene no.16404). The vector of non-replicating adenovirus (ADΔE1A) served in the mock group was pAdTrack (Addgene no.16404). Following linearization of these shuttle vectors by PmeI restriction, fragments were transformed into Super Competent BJ5183 bacterial strains carrying the pAdEasy-1 system (Addgene no.16398) for recombination into adenoviral vectors that show typical large (∼30kb) and small (∼4kb) fragments upon digestion with PacI. To collect viral particles, 4μg of validated and purified adenoviral vectors were linearized and transfected into 1 × 106 HEK-293T cells seeded onto 60 mm tissue culture plates, before cells were lysed via three consecutive ‘freeze-thaw’ cycles at one week after transfection. For virus amplication, the lysate is transferred to 1 × 106 HEK-293T cells, followed by concentration of supernatants using PEG8000 to reach a final volume of 5 mL, as well as ultracentrifugation with CsCl gradients (1.4 and 1.2 g/mL) using an Optima XPN-100 device (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Indianapolis, IN) at 100,000 × g for 8 h. Viral titers were calculated using the formula VP = A260 × 20 (dilution factor) × 1.1 × 1012. Absorbance (A260) was determined using a Fluoroskan FL plate reader (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Assessment of cytotoxicity

4 days after exposure of mammalian cells (8 × 104 cells seeded in a 24-well plate) to various cytotoxic agents, viability scores were measured by crystal violet staining upon fixing the cells in cold formaldehyde, or using a CCK8 assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorescent imaging

Fluorescence microscopy was performed with a Nikon ECLIPSE Ts2-FL Fluorescence Microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY) with excitation and emission filter set to 587/610 nm (mCherry). Images were acquired by OPLENIC software (version × 64,10.1.14643.20190511).

Myeloid differentiation of murine bone marrow cells

Bone marrow cells extracted from C57BL/6 mice were cultivated in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Myeloid differentiation was induced by addition of recombinant murine GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) over 6 days. On day 2, cells were transduced with lentiviral particles created for PETS∗-, PhCMV- or PhCMVmin-driven mCherry expression. After another 96h, adherent cells were enriched and analyzed by flow cytometry or qRT-PCR.

Stimulation of human PBMC-derived T-cells

Primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were purchased from Milecell Biotechnologies (Shanghai, China) and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% PenStrep and L-glutamine at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. For T cell activation, cells were stimulated with recombinant anti-CD3 (1 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 antibodies (1 μg/mL). After 48h, cells were transduced with lentiviral particles created for PETS∗-, PhCMV- or PhCMVmin-driven mCherry expression. On day 5, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry or qRT-PCR.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mammalian cells or animal tissues using a Trizol-based RNA extraction method. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China; cat. no. R223-01). Organ and tissue samples of mice were collected and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. Adenoviral genomic DNA within tissue lysates was isolated utilizing TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit after homogenization. For quantitative analysis, PCR reaction was carried out with an initial step of 95°C for 30 s followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific) using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech; cat. no. Q711-02) and the primers listed in Table S4. Relative cycle thresholds (CT) were normalized against the expression level of endogenous human glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

SEAP assay

Expression levels of human placental secreted alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) in culture supernatants were quantified based on p-nitrophenyl phosphate-derived light absorbance at 415 nm. In brief, 80 μL of heat-inactivated supernatants (30 min at 65°C) were transferred to a 96-well plate containing 100 μL of 2× SEAP assay buffer [20 mM L-homoarginine, 1 mM MgCl2, 21% (v/v) diethanolamine, pH 9.8] per well. Immediately after addition of 20 μL pNPP solution [120mM stock solution in 2× SEAP assay buffer] per well, time-dependent increase of light absorbance at 405 nm was followed over 30 min using a Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) set at 37°C.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA were used to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between two groups or more than three groups, respectively. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ns, not significant; ∗, 0.01 < p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.0001. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (Graph Pad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
