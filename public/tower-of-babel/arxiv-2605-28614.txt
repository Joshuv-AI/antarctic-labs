Equidistribution of CM points and RM curves
Erick Ross, Hui Xue

Abstract
. In 1988, William Duke showed that CM points of fundamental discriminant D D are equidistributed in the complex upper half-plane ℋ \mathcal{H} as D → − ∞ D\to-\infty . He also showed a similar result for RM curves (a positive discriminant analog of CM points). In this paper, we investigate analogous problems concerning the distribution of CM points and RM curves along fixed geodesics in ℋ \mathcal{H} , and around fixed points in ℋ \mathcal{H} . Specifically, we show that CM points and RM curves are equidistributed along every fixed rational geodesic in ℋ \mathcal{H} , and around every fixed CM point in ℋ \mathcal{H} . To prove these results, we solve the aggregate Linnik problem for arbitrary binary quadratic forms.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}:=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}~:~y>0\right\}
denote the complex upper half plane and
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
denote the modular surface.
Then let

denote the hyperbolic measure on
\mathcal{H}
and
ds_{\text{hyp}}=\frac{\sqrt{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}}{y}
denote the hyperbolic metric on
\mathcal{H}
.

In
[
8
]
, Duke studied the distribution of CM points over
\mathcal{H}
.
Specifically, let

\mathcal{F}:=\left\{z=x+iy\in\mathcal{H}~:~|z|>1,~-\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

denote the standard fundamental domain for
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
.
Then Duke showed that CM points of fundamental discriminant
D
are
d\mu_{\text{hyp}}
-equidistributed over
\mathcal{F}
as
D\to-\infty
.
Duke also showed a similar result for RM curves in
\mathcal{F}
(a positive discriminant analog of CM points). We note here that all the relevant objects are well-defined both in
\mathcal{H}
, as well as in the quotient
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
. Hence Duke’s work can equivalently be interpreted as an equidistribution result over all of
\mathcal{H}
, or as an equidistribution result over
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
.

Now, observe that Duke’s result concerns the distribution of CM points and RM curves in fixed
2
-dimensional regions in
\mathcal{H}
.
In this paper, we investigate the analogous problem for

Before stating our results precisely, we first give several definitions.

(Binary quadratic forms)

A
binary quadratic form
is any bivariate polynomial of the form
Q(x,y)=Ax^{2}+Bxy+Cy^{2}
where
A,B,C\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In this paper, we will mainly just be concerned with the corresponding univariate polynomial
Q(x,1)=Ax^{2}+Bx+C
and its roots.
We will usually denote a binary quadratic form by its corresponding triple
(A,B,C)
.
The
discriminant
of
(A,B,C)
is
D:=B^{2}-4AC
. We say that a binary quadratic form is
normalized
if
\gcd(A,B,C)=1
and the first non-zero entry of the triple
(A,B,C)
is positive. Note that any nonzero binary quadratic form can be normalized by scaling by an appropriate constant (and this normalization does not change, for example, the roots of
Ax^{2}+Bx+C
).

(Geodesics)
The
geodesics
in
\mathcal{H}
with respect to the hyperbolic metric are precisely the half-lines
G_{x}:=\{x+iy~:~y\in(0,\infty)\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the semicircles
G_{q,r}:=\{q+re^{i\theta}~:~\theta\in(0,\pi)\}
for
q\in\mathbb{R},r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Throughout this entire paper, the word “half-line” always refers to a vertical half-line in
\mathcal{H}
starting at the real axis, and the word “semicircle” always refers to a semicircle in
\mathcal{H}
centered at a point on the real axis.
These geodesics in
\mathcal{H}
are precisely the preimages of geodesics in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
.

(Rational geodesics)

We say that a geodesic
G_{x}
or
G_{q,r}
is
rational
if its real part
x
is rational, or if its center
q
and squared radius
r^{2}
are rational, respectively.
Note that rational geodesics are defined in this way because, for example, a geodesic contains more than one CM point if and only if it is rational.
It turns out that one can parametrize rational geodesics by normalized binary quadratic forms of positive discriminant. In particular, for any normalized binary quadratic form
(A,B,C)
of positive discriminant, we define
G_{(A,B,C)}
to be the geodesic with endpoints the root(s) of
Ax^{2}+Bx+C
. Explicitly, this means that

\displaystyle G_{(A,B,C)}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathcal{H}~:~A|z|^{2}+B\,\mathrm{Re}(z)+C=0\}

(1.1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\{\left(\frac{-C}{B}\right)+iy~:~y\in(0,\infty)\}&\text{if }A=0,\\
\{\left(\frac{-B}{2A}\right)+\left(\frac{\sqrt{B^{2}-4AC}}{2A}\right)e^{i\theta}~:~\theta\in(0,\pi)\}&\text{if }A>0.\end{cases}

(1.2)

This map defines a bijection between normalized binary quadratic forms of positive discriminant and rational geodesics. We also define the discriminant of
G_{(A,B,C)}
to be
D:=B^{2}-4AC
.

(CM points)
We define a
CM point
to be the root
z\in\mathcal{H}
of
az^{2}+bz+c
, where
(a,b,c)
is a normalized binary quadratic form of negative discriminant. We denote this CM point by
[a,b,c]:=\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}
. This parametrization gives a bijection between CM points and normalized binary quadratic forms of negative discriminant. Additionally, we define the discriminant of
[a,b,c]
to be
the discriminant of the corresponding binary quadratic form.

(RM curves)
RM curves are a positive discriminant analog of CM points.
Note that if
(a,b,c)
has positive discriminant with
a\neq 0
, then the roots of
az^{2}+bz+c
both lie on the real axis. So we define an
RM curve
to be the semicircle in
\mathcal{H}
connecting the roots of
az^{2}+bz+c
, where
(a,b,c)
is a normalized binary quadratic form of positive discriminant with
a\neq 0
. We denote this RM curve by

\langle a,b,c\rangle:=\left\{\text{the semicircle connecting }\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}\right\},

and define the discriminant of
\langle a,b,c\rangle
to be
the discriminant of the corresponding binary quadratic form.
We also note here that RM curves happen to be precisely the set of all semicircle rational geodesics in
\mathcal{H}
(i.e. the rational geodesics
G_{(a,b,c)}
with
a\neq 0
).

(CM points and RM curves along a geodesic)
In this paper, we are interested in studying the distribution of CM points and RM curves
along
a fixed rational geodesic
G
. For CM points, it is obvious what this means; we are interested in the CM points that happen to lie on
G
. However, it is slightly less obvious how one should define RM curves along
G
. It turns out that the natural objects to study are the RM curves that have perpendicular-intersection with
G
. So to be precise, when we discuss the distribution of RM curves
along

G
, we are referring to the distribution of RM curve perpendicular-intersection points on
G
.

(Equidistribution)

Let
I
be an interval and
\mu
be a Radon measure on
I
. Then a sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subseteq I
is said to be
equidistributed over
I
with respect to the measure
\mu
if

\displaystyle\frac{\#\,I_{1}\cap\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\leq N}}{\#\,I_{2}\cap\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\leq N}}\longrightarrow\frac{\mu(I_{1})}{\mu(I_{2})}\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty

(1.3)

for all compact subintervals
I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}\subseteq I
with
\mu(I_{2})\neq 0
.

(Hyperbolic equidistribution over a geodesic)

In this paper, we show
ds_{\text{hyp}}
-equidistribution of certain sequences over a given geodesic. We note here how the hyperbolic metric restricts to each type of geodesic.
Points on half-line geodesics
G_{x}
are parametrized by their imaginary part,
y=\mathrm{Im}(x+iy)
. In this case, the hyperbolic metric on
G_{x}
restricts to
ds_{\text{hyp}}=\frac{1}{y}dy
.
Points on semicircle geodesics
G_{q,r}
, on the other hand, are parametrized by their arguments (around
q
),
\theta=\mathrm{arg}_{q}(q+re^{i\theta})
. In this case, the hyperbolic metric on
G_{q,r}
restricts to
ds_{\text{hyp}}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}d\theta
. Hyperbolic equidistribution over a geodesic is then defined via this parametrization, using the above definition.

(Angle equidistribution around a point)

For a fixed point
p\in\mathcal{H}
, we also study the distribution of certain points and geodesics around
p
. The geodesics
G
passing through
p
can be parametrized by
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)
: the angle at which they pass through
p
, where
\theta=0
represents the angle pointing straight down towards the real axis.
Note that
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)
can alternatively be interpreted as the argument of
p
as a point on
G
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G_{q,r})=\arg_{q}(p)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G_{x})=0.

(1.4)

Similarly, for points
z
around
p
, their corresponding angle from
p
can be computed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{ang}_{p}(z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)&\text{if}\quad\mathrm{Re}(z)>\mathrm{Re}(p)\text{ or }\mathrm{Re}(z)=\mathrm{Re}(p),\mathrm{Im}(z)<\mathrm{Im}(p)\\
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)+\pi&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.5)

\displaystyle\text{where $G$ is the unique geodesic connecting $p$ and $z$}.

(1.6)

See Figure
1.1
for an illustration of these notions of
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)
and
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(z)
.
We will show in Theorem
1.3
that the RM curves passing though CM points
p
are equidistributed with respect to the uniform angle measure
d\theta
(recall that RM curves are just special types of geodesics).
And we will also show in Theorem
1.4
that CM points around
p
are equidistributed with respect to the uniform angle measure
d\theta
.

Figure 1.1.
The angles
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(z)=\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)
and
\mathrm{ang}_{p}(z^{\prime})=\mathrm{ang}_{p}(G)+\pi
.

We are now finally able to state our results precisely.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix a rational geodesic
G
, and let

\textnormal{CM}^{G}_{\Delta}
denote the set of CM points along
G
with discriminant
|D|\leq\Delta
.
Then as
\Delta\to\infty
,
\textnormal{CM}^{G}_{\Delta}
is equidistributed along
G
with respect to the hyperbolic metric.

We make three remarks about Theorem
1.1
.
First, we remark that we had previously shown in
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
that CM points are
ds_{\text{hyp}}
-equidistributed on the boundary
\partial\mathcal{F}
of the fundamental domain for
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
. That result can be viewed as a special case of Theorem
1.1
.
Second, observe that this equidistribution theorem differs from Duke’s result
[
8
, Theorem 1]
in that we are considering
CM
points for all discriminants
|D|\leq\Delta
, instead of for individual discriminants
D
. This is necessary because the sets of CM points for individual discriminants
D
do not even become dense along a given geodesic.
Third, we remark that our result shows equidistribution of CM points of general discriminant, whereas
[
8
, Theorem 1]
shows equidistribution just for CM points of fundamental discriminant.

Next, we show the equidistribution of RM curves along a fixed rational geodesic.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix a rational geodesic
G
, and let

\textnormal{RM}^{G}_{\Delta}
denote the set of RM curves along
G
with discriminant
D\leq\Delta
.
Then as
\Delta\to\infty
,
\textnormal{RM}^{G}_{\Delta}
is equidistributed along
G
with respect to the hyperbolic metric.

Note that this theorem claims the equidistribution of RM curves along
G
.
To be precise, recall that this terminology is referring to the equidistribution along
G
of the intersection points for RM curves that have perpendicular intersection with
G
.

Next, we show equidistribution of RM curves around a fixed CM point.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix a CM point
p
, and let

\textnormal{RM}^{p}_{\Delta}
denote the set of RM curves passing through
p
with discriminant
D\leq\Delta
.
Then as
\Delta\to\infty
, the angles
\mathrm{ang}_{p}\,\textnormal{RM}^{p}_{\Delta}
are equidistributed with respect to the uniform angle measure.

Finally, in Theorem
1.4
, we prove equidistribution of CM points around any fixed point in
\mathcal{H}
. It turns out that this theorem follows from Duke’s original equidistribution result. For this reason, we have chosen to state Theorem
1.4
in terms of CM points of fundamental discriminant
D
. However, we remark here that in symmetry with Theorems
1.1
-
1.3
, the same result also holds for CM points of discriminant
|D|\leq\Delta
. This follows from an aggregate version of Duke’s result; specifically the aggregate Linnik problem for
Q(a,b,c)=4ac-b^{2}
(see the discussion above Theorem
1.5
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix
z_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and for negative fundamental discriminants
D
, let

\textnormal{CM}^{\overline{B}(z_{0},s_{0})}_{D}
denote the set of CM points of discriminant
D

within hyperbolic distance
s_{0}
of
z_{0}
.
Then as
D\to-\infty
, the angles
\mathrm{ang}_{z_{0}}\,\textnormal{CM}^{\overline{B}(z_{0},s_{0})}_{D}
are equidistributed with respect to the uniform angle measure.

Near the beginning of the introduction, we gave a bulleted list showing the symmetry between
[
8
, Theorem 1]
and Theorems
1.1
-

. We would also like to point out four additional symmetries between Theorems

-

.

(1)

Theorems

and

show that CM points/RM curves are equidistributed along each fixed rational geodesic. From the (in some sense) inverse perspective, Theorems
1.2
and

show that rational geodesics are equidistributed around each fixed CM point/RM curve. Note that as stated, Theorems

and

just show the equidistribution of RM curves. However, these results can also be viewed as showing equidistribution of rational geodesics since there is at most one half-line geodesic around each fixed CM point/RM curve.

(2)

Theorems
1.1
-
1.3
show that CM points are equidistributed along every fixed RM curve, RM curves are equidistributed along every fixed rational geodesic, and rational geodesics are equidistributed around every fixed CM point.

(3)

The CM points/RM curves in Theorems

-

are all governed by the same Diophantine equation (see Lemma

(4)

Theorems

-

To show the above results, we solve the aggregate Linnik problem for arbitrary binary quadratic forms. We give a brief overview of what exactly this terminology means.

Fix a homogeneous integer polynomial

[(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})]_{Q}:=\frac{(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})}{Q(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})^{1/\mathrm{deg}\,Q}}

S_{Q}:=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Q(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})=1\right\}.

Then the

\displaystyle\Big\{[(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})]_{Q}:(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},\ Q(x_{1},\ldots x_{n})=d\Big\}

(1.7)

over
S_{Q}
as

2.
A Diophantine equation governing the relevant CM points/RM curves

In this section, we prove Lemma
2.1
. This lemma shows that the CM points/RM curves in Theorems
1.1
-
1.3
are all governed by the Diophantine equation
2aC+2cA=bB
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(1)

Fix a rational geodesic
G_{(A,B,C)}
. Then
[a,b,c]
lies on
G_{(A,B,C)}
iff

\displaystyle 2aC+2cA=bB.

(2.1)

(2)

Fix a rational geodesic
G_{(A,B,C)}
. Then
\langle a,b,c\rangle
has perpendicular intersection with
G_{(A,B,C)}
iff

\displaystyle 2aC+2cA=bB.

(2.2)

(3)

Fix a CM point
[A,B,C]
. Then
\langle a,b,c\rangle
passes through
[A,B,C]
iff

\displaystyle 2aC+2cA=bB.

(2.3)

Proof.

For part (1), recall that
G_{(A,B,C)}=\left\{z\in\mathcal{H}~:~A|z|^{2}+B\,\mathrm{Re}(z)+C=0\right\}
. For
z=[a,b,c]
, observe that

\displaystyle A|z|^{2}+B\,\mathrm{Re}(z)+C

\displaystyle=A\left|\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}\right|^{2}+B\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left(\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}\right)+C

(2.4)

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{b^{2}}{4a^{2}}+\frac{4ac-b^{2}}{4a^{2}}\right)+B\left(\frac{-b}{2a}\right)+C

(2.5)

\displaystyle=A\frac{c}{a}-B\frac{b}{2a}+C

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{2aC+2cA-bB}{2a}.

(2.7)

Hence
z=[a,b,c]
lies on
G_{(A,B,C)}
iff
2aC+2cA=bB
.

For part (2) for half-line rational geodesics, recall that
G_{(0,B,C)}
is the half-line with real part
\frac{-C}{B}
. Then it is easy to see that
\langle a,b,c\rangle
has perpendicular intersection with
G_{(0,B,C)}
iff
\langle a,b,c\rangle
is centered at
\frac{-C}{B}
. This occurs precisely when
\frac{-b}{2a}=\frac{-C}{B}
, i.e. when
2aC+2c(0)=bB
.

For part (2) for semicircle rational geodesics, let
L
denote the line segment connecting the centers
G_{(A,B,C)}
and
\langle a,b,c\rangle
. Then note that
\langle a,b,c\rangle
has perpendicular intersection with
G_{(A,B,C)}
precisely when
L
is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs the radii
G_{(A,B,C)}
and
\langle a,b,c\rangle
. Now, we have that

\displaystyle\text{radius}(G_{(A,B,C)})^{2}+\text{radius}(\langle a,b,c\rangle)^{2}-|L|^{2}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{B^{2}-4AC}{4A^{2}}+\frac{b^{2}-4ac}{4a^{2}}-\left(\frac{-b}{2a}-\frac{-B}{2A}\right)^{2}

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{4a^{2}(B^{2}-4AC)}{16a^{2}A^{2}}+\frac{4A^{2}(b^{2}-4ac)}{16a^{2}A^{2}}-\left(\frac{2aB-2bA}{4aA}\right)^{2}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{4a^{2}(B^{2}-4AC)+4A^{2}(b^{2}-4ac)-4a^{2}B^{2}-4b^{2}A^{2}+8abAB}{16a^{2}A^{2}}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{8abAB-16a^{2}AC-16acA^{2}}{16a^{2}A^{2}}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{bB-2aC-2cA}{2aA}.

(2.13)

Hence by the Pythagorean theorem,
\langle a,b,c\rangle
has perpendicular intersection with
G_{(A,B,C)}
precisely when
2aC+2cA=bB
.

Part (3) follows immediately from part (1).
∎

We remark here that Case 1 of the above proof (for half-line geodesics) could also be shown as a corollary of Case 2 (for semicircle geodesics); see the paragraph below for explanation. We have chosen here to prove both parts directly for sake of exposition. The argument for Case 1 turns out to be much easier, so we gave that argument first. Then the argument for Case 2 generalizes the ideas from Case 1.

The reason that Case 1 follows from Case 2 is that every half-line rational geodesic can be written as an
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
transformation of a semicircle rational geodesic.
Given a half-line rational geodesic
G_{x}
, choose a natural number
n>x
and let

.
Then it is straightforward to verify that the
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
transformation
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}n&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right):z\mapsto\frac{nz-1}{z}
bijectively maps

\displaystyle\gamma~:~G_{q,r}=\{q+re^{i\theta}~:~\theta\in(0,\pi)\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow G_{x}=\{x+iy~:~y\in(0,\infty)\}.

(3.31)

Under this mapping, we have that
\theta\mapsto y(\theta)=\frac{1}{2r}\frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}
, and so the metric
ds_{\text{hyp}}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}d\theta
becomes
ds_{\text{hyp}}=\frac{1}{y}dy
.
As an
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
transformation,
\gamma
preserves discriminants of CM points.
Hence applying
\gamma
to
\textnormal{CM}^{G_{q,r}}_{|D|\leq\Delta}
, we have by Case 2 that
\textnormal{Im\,}\textnormal{CM}^{G_{x}}_{|D|\leq\Delta}
is equidistributed with respect to
\frac{1}{y}dy
, verifying Case 1 for
G_{x}
.

To prove Theorem

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{hyp}}(x+iy,x_{0}+iy_{0})=\mathrm{arccosh}\left(\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}+y^{2}+y_{0}^{2}}{2yy_{0}}\right).

Moreover, if

to

is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{ang}_{x_{0}+iy_{0}}(x+iy)=\arccos\left(\frac{q-x_{0}}{r}\right),

(4.2)

where

\displaystyle\quad q:=\frac{x+x_{0}}{2}+\frac{y^{2}-y_{0}^{2}}{2(x-x_{0})}\quad\text{and}\quad r:=\sqrt{(x_{0}-q)^{2}+y_{0}^{2}}.

(4.3)

Proof.

The distance formula (
4.1
) is well known. Here, we prove (
4.2
).

Let
G=G_{q,r}
denote the geodesic connecting
x_{0}+iy_{0}
and
x_{i}y
. Then

\displaystyle(x_{0}-q)^{2}+y_{0}^{2}=r^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(x-q)^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}

(4.4)

Solving this system for

and

yields the claimed formulas (
4.3
).

Now, since
x>x_{0}
, recall that
\mathrm{ang}_{x_{0}+iy_{0}}(x+iy)=\mathrm{ang}_{x_{0}+iy_{0}}(G)
, and that

as a point on

). Hence

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ang}_{x_{0}+iy_{0}}(x+iy)=\mathrm{ang}_{x_{0}+iy_{0}}(G)=\mathrm{arg}_{G}(x_{0}+iy_{0}),

(4.5)

\displaystyle\text{so }\text{that}\quad x_{0}=\text{Re}(q+r\,e^{i\theta})=q+r\cos(\theta).

(4.6)

This then implies (
4.2
), completing the proof.
∎

Now, Theorem
1.4
concerns the behavior of CM points in the (hyperbolic) closed ball
\overline{B}(z_{0},s_{0})
. Writing

with center

Lemma 4.2
.

,

, and

within hyperbolic distance

9.
Discussion

We discuss some applications of our results. First, we observe that our work yields an asymptotic formula for the number of CM points on closed geodesics in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 9.1
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Then for any closed geodesic
\overline{G}
in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
of discriminant
D
, the number of CM points on
\overline{G}
is given by

\displaystyle\#\{\textnormal{CM points on $\overline{G}$ with discriminant of magnitude $\leq\Delta$}\}

(9.1)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{3\gcd(D,2)\,\mathrm{length}(\overline{G})}{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{D}}\Delta+O(\Delta^{1/2+\varepsilon}).

(9.2)

Let
\overline{G}
be an arbitrary closed geodesic in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
. It is well known that
\overline{G}
can be written as the image of a geodesic
G=G_{(A,B,C)}
in
\mathcal{H}
, where
(A,B,C)
is an indefinite primitive binary quadratic form such that
D=B^{2}-4AC>0
is a non-square. In particular, this means that
G
is a semicircle rational geodesic in
\mathcal{H}
. Now, in the quotient space
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
,
G
wraps around several times on itself (i.e. the quotient map from
G
to
\overline{G}
is not injective). However, it turns out that one can exactly represent
\overline{G}
in
\mathcal{H}
as a subinterval along
G
(see, for example,
[
14
, Theorem 13.1.6]
).
In particular, let

\gamma:=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{t_{0}-Bu_{0}}{2}&-Cu_{0}\\
Au_{0}&\frac{t_{0}+Bu_{0}}{2}\end{matrix}\right),

where
t_{0},u_{0}
are the smallest positive integer solutions to the Pell equation
t^{2}-Du^{2}=4
. It turns out that this
\gamma
is a generator for
\Gamma_{(A,B,C)}
: the stabilizer of
(A,B,C)
in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. This then means that for any
z_{0}\in G
, the closed geodesic
\overline{G}=\Gamma_{(A,B,C)}\backslash G
in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
can be represented exactly in
\mathcal{H}
by the arc along
G
connecting
z_{0}
and
\gamma z_{0}
.

One can compute the length of
\overline{G}
explicity as

\displaystyle\mathrm{length}(\overline{G})=\int_{\overline{G}}ds_{\text{hyp}}=2\log\varepsilon_{D},

(9.3)

where
\varepsilon_{D}=\frac{1}{2}(t_{0}+u_{0}\sqrt{D})

[
16
]
.
The integral over
\overline{G}=\Gamma_{(A,B,C)}\backslash G
here is called a
cycle integral
, and is understood to run over the arc along
G
connecting
z_{0}
and
\gamma z_{0}
for some
z_{0}\in G
. See, for example,
[
3
, Section 2]
for precise details about cycle integrals.

On the other hand, the results of this paper imply that the cycle integral of (
9.3
) (over a geodesic arc of
G=G_{(A,B,C)}
) can be replaced with counting the number of CM points (over the same interval). Specifically, by the argument of Theorem
1.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\#\{\text{CM points on $\overline{G}$ with discriminant of magnitude $\leq\Delta$}\}

(9.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\Delta}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\overline{G}}d\mu(t)+O(\Delta^{1/2+\varepsilon})\qquad\qquad\ \ \text{(evident range for $t$)}

(9.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\Delta}{\pi^{2}}\int_{\overline{G}}\frac{2}{\sqrt{16\gcd(D,2)^{-2}D}}\frac{1}{\sin\theta}d\theta+O(\Delta^{1/2+\varepsilon})\qquad\text{(evident range for $\theta$)}

(9.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\gcd(D,2)\Delta}{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{D}}\int_{\overline{G}}\frac{1}{\sin\theta}d\theta+O(\Delta^{1/2+\varepsilon})

(9.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\gcd(D,2)\,\mathrm{length}(\overline{G})}{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{D}}\Delta+O(\Delta^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

(9.8)

as claimed.

The same method can also be used to compute more general cycle integrals. Let
f
be a modular function for
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. For every real quadratic irrationality
w\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, one can associate the semicircle rational geodesic
G=G_{(A,B,C)}
connecting
w
and its conjugate
w^{\prime}
. One defines the “value” of
f
at
w
to be the cycle integral

\displaystyle f(w):=\int_{\overline{G}}f(z)ds_{\mathrm{hyp}},

(9.9)

where as above
\overline{G}
denotes the image of
G
in
\text{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathcal{H}
.
These “values” have been studied in various contexts. For example,
[
7
]
relates the “values” of the
j
-function to the Fourier coefficients of a weight
1/2
weakly harmonic modular form, and
[
3
]
establishes a certain convergence property of these “values” conjectured by
[
11
]
.

We would like to point out here that our work yields an alternative formula for these “values” of
f
. Specifically, by a similar argument as before, we have the following.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
w
be a real quadratic irrationality with associated geodesic
G=G_{(A,B,C)}
of discriminant
D
. Denote
\textnormal{CM}^{*\overline{G}}_{\Delta}
to be the set of all CM points on
\overline{G}=\Gamma_{(A,B,C)}\backslash G_{(A,B,C)}
with discriminant of magnitude
\leq\Delta
.
Then the “value” of
f
at
w
is given by

\displaystyle f(w)=\lim_{\Delta\to\infty}\frac{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{D}}{3\gcd(D,2)\Delta}\sum_{p\in\textnormal{CM}^{*\overline{G}}_{\Delta}}f(p).

(9.10)

This proposition means that the “value” of
f
at a real quadratic irrationality
w
can be interpreted as the average value of
f
over the CM points on the geodesic associated to
w
. It is standard to refer to
f(w)
as the “value” of
f
at
w
. However, we remark here that
f(w)
could perhaps more naturally be understood as the “value” of
f
at the RM curve
\langle A,B,C\rangle=G_{(A,B,C)}
.

Finally, we comment on an interesting open problem.
The results of this paper show that CM points are dense (and moreover equidistributed) along all rational geodesics in
\mathcal{H}
. CM points are not dense along irrational geodesics, on the other hand, since each such geodesic can contain at most one CM point. We also give an example of a non-geodesic curve containing a dense (and moreover equidistributed) subset of CM points.

Example 9.3
.

Let
L
denote the horizontal line in
\mathcal{H}
of imaginary part
1
, and
\mathrm{CM}^{L}_{\Delta}
denote the set of all CM points on
L
with discriminant
|D|\leq\Delta
. Then it is straighforward to verify that

\displaystyle\mathrm{CM}^{L}_{\Delta}=\{[n^{2},-2mn,n^{2}+m^{2}]=\tfrac{m}{n}+i~:~1\leq n\leq(\Delta/4)^{1/4},~m\in\mathbb{Z},~\gcd(m,n)=1\}.

(9.11)

In particular, this means that
\textnormal{CM}^{L}_{\Delta}
is equidistributed over
L
with respect to the uniform measure
dx
(or equivalently with respect to the hyperbolic metric
ds_{\mathrm{hyp}}
) as
\Delta\to\infty
.

It would be a very interesting problem to classify all algebraic curves in
\mathcal{H}
containing a dense (or equidistributed) subset of CM points. This would be an analog of the famous André-Oort conjecture
[
17
]
, which gives a complete classification of subvarieties of Shimura varieties containing a dense subset of special points.
