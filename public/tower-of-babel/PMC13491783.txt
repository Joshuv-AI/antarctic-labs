Maternal cytokine profile and anti-bacterial specific antibody response associated with neonatal infection by E. coli and K. pneumoniae
Authors: Azeddine Chakroun, Narjis Amar, Hanane Ben-Taleb, Karima Mohtadi, Amine Lamrissi, Amal Badre, Norddine Habti, Hanane Salih Alj, Rachid Saïle, Mustapha Chamekh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe transmission of pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae from mother to child during pregnancy contributes significantly to neonatal morbidity and mortality, particularly in low and middle-income countries. Identifying maternal risk factors associated with vertical transmission is crucial. This prospective study aimed to investigate the maternal cytokine profile and anti-LPS-specific antibodies in pregnant mothers with positive cultures for E. coli and K. pneumoniae, exploring their potential association with neonatal invasive disease.MethodsThis prospective study examined groups of pregnant women with a positive culture for E. coli or K. pneumoniae, who had or had not given birth to newborns with invasive diseases. A group of age-matched mothers negative for E coli and K. pneumoniae was used as a control. A panel of circulating inflammatory cytokines was measured in mother-infant dyads using Luminex multiplex assays or ELISA in maternal and cord blood, as well as in the supernatants of blood leucocytes stimulated with TLR4 and TLR1/2 ligands. The serum levels of specific IgG anti-LPS E. coli and anti-LPS K. pneumoniae were quantified using ELISA.ResultsMothers with a positive culture for E. coli and K. pneumoniae and their newborns exhibited elevated plasma levels of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α compared to healthy mothers and their newborns. Similar results were observed for IL-6 and TNF-α when blood leucocytes were stimulated by TLR4 and TLR1/2 ligands. Mothers who gave birth to newborns with severe infectious diseases had higher IL-6 levels and lower specific IgG anti-LPS E. coli or anti-LPS K. pneumoniae levels compared to those with healthy newborns. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated a strong association between maternal IL-6 and specific IgG levels and perinatal bacterial transmission.ConclusionPregnant women with positive cultures for E. coli or K. pneumoniae, as well as higher plasma levels of IL-6 and lower levels of specific anti-bacterial IgG, are at increased risk of perinatal bacterial transmission. This data could help improve the monitoring of mother-child dyads and optimize prenatal care.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13766-w.

Introduction

Pregnant women can develop ascending infections caused by various bacteria colonizing the rectovaginal tract. Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli and K. pneumoniae are the most frequent etiological agents of various pathological situations ranging from urinary tract infections to chorioamnionitis. These bacteria contribute significantly to maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in low-income countries [1–5].

The incidence rate of neonatal invasive E. coli and K. pneumoniae infections varies across different countries and regions, with a notable paucity of data from low-income countries. For instance, the incidence of neonatal early-onset disease (EOD) caused by E. coli is 0.39 per 1,000 live births in the USA [6], 0.69 per 1,000 live births in Spain [7], and 2.36 per 1,000 live births in China [8]. Studies in North African regions remain scarce. For instance, a single study conducted in 2012 on a cohort of infants in Morocco indicated a predominance of E. coli (64.8%) and K. pneumoniae (15.6%) in urinary tract infections [9].

The outcomes of E. coli and K. pneumoniae infections in pregnant women and neonates are critically influenced by not only the virulence factors of the pathogens but also the mother’s immune status, which is shaped by previous exposure and the transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies [6]. Notably, the immune-inflammatory response triggered during infection in pregnant mothers plays a crucial role in controlling the infection. However, the type and magnitude of this response can significantly impact maternal and neonatal outcomes, including early labor, pregnancy loss, maternal sepsis, and preterm birth [10–13].

Vertical transmission of bacteria has been reported in several studies, with a rate of 30.2% when the etiological agent, E. coli, was isolated from a vaginal swab and 100% when it was isolated from blood culture [14]. Another study in Ethiopia reported vertical transmission rates of 57.7% for E. coli and 51.7% for K. pneumoniae [15]. Among the factors influencing vertical transmission, the maternal humoral immune response plays a key role in protecting the fetus and newborn during the perinatal period. Maternal transfer of specific IgG through the placenta via the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) is a fundamental mechanism of passive protection for the newborn [16]. Several studies have reported that specific maternal antibody levels correlate with a reduced risk of vertical transmission of various pathogens, such as human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) [17], human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [18], or GBS [19]. In the case of E. coli, transplacental transfer of maternal-specific IgG has been demonstrated in healthy pregnant women and their newborns [20]. Another study further showed that maternal transfer of anti-E. coli and anti-K. pneumoniae IgG is influenced by gestational age at delivery [21].

To date, the predictive factors for perinatal bacterial transmission remain insufficiently characterized, especially regarding maternal inflammatory cytokine response and anti-bacterial specific antibody response. To address this gap, particularly in low and middle-income countries, we conducted a prospective study in mother-newborn dyads in Morocco to investigate the risk factors associated with maternal inflammation due to bacterial colonization or infection. More recently, we reported that maternal colonization by Gram-positive bacteria, group B streptococcus, can trigger a cytokine inflammatory response, with IL-17 emerging as a significant prognostic biomarker for assessing the risk of GBS transmission to neonates [22]. In this study, we focus on the screening of two major Gram-negative bacteria, E. coli and K. pneumoniae, in a cohort of mother-newborn dyads and investigate whether maternal cytokine inflammatory pattern and/or specific anti-bacterial antibody response could be associated with neonatal invasive disease.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

This is a prospective study conducted in a single center at the maternity unit of the Abderrahim Harouchi Mother-Child Hospital, Averroes University Hospital Center of Casablanca, Morocco. It is a reference hospital center that receives a high flux of pregnant women from the region of Casablanca, the biggest city in the country. Pregnant women were recruited between April 2021 and July 2024. The consort diagram of the study population is shown in Figure S1. The inclusion criteria were women over 18 years at the third trimester of pregnancy with positive culture for E. coli or K. pneumoniae and presenting high risk for perinatal bacterial transmission linked to pregnancy complications such as premature rupture of membranes (defined as the spontaneous rupture of the fetal membranes before the onset of labor) or threatened preterm labor (defined as the occurrence of uterine contraction associated with cervical changes in pregnant women between 22 and 37 weeks of gestation). This group of mothers was referred to as “suffering mothers”. Pregnant women with recent anti-infective treatment (within the preceding 15 days), tuberculosis, herpes, viral infections, or sexually transmitted infections were excluded from the study. A group of age-matched healthy pregnant women, negative for E. coli and K. pneumoniae, was used as a control.

The neonates born to suffering mothers or healthy mothers were included in the study. The exclusion criteria were congenital abnormalities or congenital immunosuppression. The neonates born to suffering mothers were clustered based on clinical and biological inflammatory markers: Symptomatic newborns (38.46%, 15/39) who exhibited at least two or more inflammatory symptoms: respiratory distress, neurological manifestations, hemodynamic manifestations, abnormal biological parameters such as CRP and abnormal leucocyte count; and asymptomatic newborns (61.53%, 24/39) with no apparent clinical or biological marker of inflammation. Neonatal invasive disease found among symptomatic newborns (53.33%, 8/15) was confirmed by positive blood or urinary culture for E. coli or K. pneumoniae. Mother-to-child transmission of bacteria was suspected when the detection of E. coli or K. pneumoniae was confirmed in cultures from both mothers and neonates.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association’s Code of Ethics (Declaration of Helsinki). Before enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The protocol of the study was approved by the local hospital Ethics Committee (N° 21/2020) and by the National Commission of the Control of Personal Data Protection (N° A-RS-248/2021).

Bacterial screening

The bacterial screening for E. coli or K. pneumoniae was performed using routine methods [23]. Urine samples were cultured on two ready-to-use media, CLED and MacConkey (BioMérieux France). Vaginal swabs were directly seeded on MacConkey agar medium. Identification of isolated colonies was performed using several complementary methods, including Gram staining and microscopic observation, the API 20E gallery, and confirmation by MALDI-TOF.

Samples processing

Peripheral maternal blood samples were collected at recruitment by venipuncture into EDTA vacutainers within 5–7 days before delivery. Cord blood samples were collected just after delivery. Blood samples were processed within 4 h to extract plasma or for the stimulation experiments. The plasma was stored at -80 °C until use.

TLR stimulation

Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with sterile prewarmed RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA. Cat #11875093), penicillin G (Biobasic, Markham, Canada. Cat #PB0135), and streptomycin (100ug/ml) (Biobasic, Markham, Canada. Cat #SB0494) and stimulated with LPS (10ng/ml) (List biological laboratories, Campbell, CA, USA. Cat #LL-203 A), or with Pam3CSK4 (1ug/ml) (APExBIO technology, Houston TX, USA. Cat #B5662). Unstimulated cells were used as a control. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 5000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant, consisting of a mixture of plasma and medium, was collected carefully and stored at -80 °C until use.

Cytokine Luminex / ELISA assays

The ProcartaPlex™ Human Cytokine Storm Panel (ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC, Vienna, Austria. Cat EPX210-15850-901) was used to measure 21 analytes (G-CSF, GM-CSF, INF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-17 A, IL-18, IP-10, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, TNF-α and TNF-β) in sera according to manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were tested in duplicate in the same assay plate and were acquired on a Luminex MAGPIX instrument. A minimum of 50 microspheres per analyte per well were acquired, and the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was recorded for each analyte. Results were expressed in pg/ml.

Sandwich ELISA Kits were used, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, to determine the levels of IL-6 (ThermoFisher, Vienna, Austria. Cat #88-50625-86), TNF-α (ThermoFisher, Vienna, Austria. Cat #88-7346-88), and IL-8 (ThermoFisher, Vienna, Austria. Cat #88-8086-86) in plasma samples. The assay sensitivities for IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-8 were 2 pg/ml, 2 pg/ml, and 4 pg/ml, respectively.

Antibody ELISA assay

The quantification of specific anti-LPS IgG was determined in serum samples by ELISA as previously described [24]. Briefly, three sources of LPS antigens were used, including two derived from E. coli strains O26:B6 (Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA. Cat #L8274) and O111:B4 (Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA. Cat #L2630). These serotypes were selected because they are associated with pathogenic E. coli strains and are widely used in immunological studies. The third one is derived from K. pneumoniae (Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA. Cat #L4268). Plates were incubated with LPS (10 ug/ml PBS) overnight at 4 °C, followed by blocking reagent (Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA. Cat #11112589001). Sera samples diluted in buffer (Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA. Cat RABELADE) were incubated in duplicate for 2 h at room temperature. The reactions were visualized using HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (ThermoFisher, Vienna, Austria. Cat #62-8420), Tetramethylbenzidine (Sigma Aldrich, Missouri, USA. Cat #T4444), and the addition of 100 µL of ELISA stop solution (ThermoFisher, Vienna, Austria. Cat #SS04). Absorbance was measured at 492 nm (Agilent BioTek Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer). The concentrations of anti-lipopolysaccharide IgG were determined using the standard curve performed by a reference human serum sample in each assay according to the procedure described by Fomsgaard [24].

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of biological, clinical, and demographic characteristics, as well as cytokine and antibody levels among study groups, were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2. Statistical tests were selected according to data distribution and the number of groups compared. Continuous variables were analyzed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test and one-way Welch ANOVA, while categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. For multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Holm–Šidák correction. Continuous data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and categorical data as percentages. Correlations between maternal and neonatal cytokines were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation. Optimal cut-off values were determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the mother-newborn study population

Demographic, biological, and clinical characteristics of pregnant women and their respective newborns are shown in Table 1. The first group concerns mothers with positive culture for E. coli or K. pneumoniae and suffering from pregnancy complications that represent significant risk factors for neonates, such as premature rupture of membranes and threatened preterm delivery (n = 39). This group, referred to as “suffering mothers,” was compared to a group of age-matched healthy pregnant women as a control (n = 38) (Figure S1). There was no significant difference in the maternal age between the two groups. In suffering mothers, most positive bacterial cultures (94,8%; n = 37/39) were detected in urine or in both urine and vaginal samples, associated with abnormal urinalysis, including cloudy urine (74.35%, 29/39), leukocyturia (82.05%, 32/39), and hematuria (69.23%, 27/39). Moreover, this group had higher inflammatory symptoms as shown by increased levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cells, neutrophils, and monocytes, compared to healthy mothers.

Regarding the newborns, the preterm births (< 37 weeks of gestation) were significantly higher in those born to suffering mothers (79.48%, n = 31/39) compared to those born to healthy mothers (10.52%, n = 4/38). A significant percentage of newborns from suffering mothers (51.28%, n = 20/39) required hospitalization, and 20,5% (n = 8/39) had a confirmed positive bacterial culture, confirming neonatal invasive disease. Moreover, neonates born to suffering mothers had higher inflammatory symptoms as indicated by a significant increase in CRP concentrations (58.82%, 10/17) and elevated monocyte counts (52.94%, 9/17) compared to normal reference ranges.

Cytokine profile in the mother-newborn dyads

To further assess the inflammatory status of the mother-newborn dyads, we measured circulating levels of cytokines in plasma derived from the mother’s peripheral blood and the newborn’s cord blood. We first performed multiplex dosage using the Luminex test in randomly selected samples of the cohort. This allowed the quantification of a panel of 21 cytokines, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cytokines and chemokines. The data revealed significantly higher levels of IL-6, IL-8, and a trend toward increased production of TNF-α in suffering mothers compared to healthy mothers (Fig. S2). Next, we specifically measured these three cytokines by ELISA across the entire cohort of mothers and their newborns. In line with the Luminex assay results, we observed significantly higher levels of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α in suffering mothers compared to healthy mothers (Fig. 1A, B, and C).

We performed similar measurements of cytokines by ELISA in cord blood to evaluate the inflammatory state in neonates. As shown in Fig. 2, significantly high levels of IL-6, IL-8, and TNFα were also detected in newborns of suffering mothers compared to those born to healthy mothers. These results indicate that neonates born to mothers with a high inflammatory state associated with positive bacterial culture are at risk and more likely to be affected.

TLR-induced cytokine production in blood cells from suffering mothers and their newborns

Next, we further examined the inflammatory cytokine response by performing ex vivo stimulation of the mother’s peripheral blood and the newborn’s cord blood cells with two pathogen-associated molecular patterns: Bacterial LPS (TLR4 ligand) and Pam3CSK4 (a synthetic ligand for TLR1/2). Medium alone served as an unstimulated control. Figure 3 illustrates the measurement of normalized cytokine levels relative to the total number of leukocytes in each sample. Blood cells from suffering mothers produced significantly higher levels of IL-6, IL-8, and TNFα upon stimulation with LPS and Pam3CSK4 compared to unstimulated controls (Fig. 3A, B, and C; SM LPS vs. SM PAM vs. SM CTR). A moderate, but significant, production of IL-6 was observed in blood cells from healthy mothers upon stimulation with LPS and Pam3CSK4 as compared to unstimulated controls. We also detected a moderate increase of IL-8 in healthy mothers upon stimulation with Pam3CSK4 but not LPS, as compared to unstimulated controls. Both stimulations yielded a significant production of TNF-α in blood cells from healthy mothers, as compared to unstimulated controls (Fig. 3A, B, and C; HM LPS vs. HM PAM vs. HM CTR). Notably, the production of IL-6 and TNF-α remained constantly and significantly stronger in the supernatant of blood cells from suffering mothers upon stimulation with LPS and Pam3CSK4, compared to stimulated cells from healthy mothers, while the level of IL-8 was similar in both groups, regardless of the stimulus. The levels of the three cytokines were similar between the unstimulated groups (CTR) (Fig. 3, B, and C; SM vs. HM). This indicates that the elevated plasmatic levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in suffering mothers are associated with the higher functional capacity of circulating leukocytes to produce these cytokines upon TLR4 and TLR2/1 ligation. Taken together, these functional analysis results indicate significant priming of blood immune cells in suffering mothers, which probably occurs in the context of exposure to bacteria.

Maternal cytokine profile and its association with neonatal invasive disease

Because newborns of suffering mothers had an increased risk of bacterial infection, we sought to determine whether a specific profile of circulating cytokines in the mother could be associated with neonatal invasive disease. We stratified the suffering mothers into two groups: Those who gave birth to newborns with invasive disease, as confirmed by positive bacterial culture, and those giving birth to asymptomatic non-infected newborns. Interestingly, we found that suffering mothers with infected newborns had significantly higher IL-6 and lower TNF-α levels, compared to the mothers giving birth to non-infected newborns (Fig. 4A and C). Circulating levels of IL-8, however, remained similar in both groups (Fig. 4B).

We also measured these cytokines in the cord blood of infected and non-infected newborns and analyzed their correlation with the levels observed in mothers. We found a significant correlation of the plasmatic level of IL-6 in the neonates and their mothers (ρ = 0.3546, p < 0.05) (Table S1). Furthermore, we determined whether maternal cytokine levels could be linked with the susceptibility of the neonates to invasive bacterial diseases by using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis. As shown in Fig. 5, the area under the curve (AUC) of IL-6 was 0.9427 (p = 0.0002), which is higher than the AUC obtained with other cytokines and with the conventional inflammatory marker CRP (Table S2). This suggests that IL-6 level in suffering mothers has a significant predictive value for an increased risk to the newborn.

Evaluation of specific antibody response in mother-newborn dyads

Next, we examined the antibody response using specific antigens from E. coli and K. pneumoniae. In suffering mothers with a positive culture for E. coli, we observed a trend toward higher levels of IgG anti-LPS-E. coli O111:B4, compared to healthy mothers, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.090) (Fig. 6A). For IgG anti-LPS E. coli O26:B6, no difference was observed between the two groups (p = 0.191) (Fig. 6B). In contrast, in suffering mothers with a positive culture for K. pneumoniae, the levels of IgG anti-LPS-K. pneumoniae were significantly higher, compared to healthy mothers (p = 0.0097) (Fig. 6C).

To confirm the association of maternal specific IgG levels with the neonatal invasive disease outcomes, we performed ROC analysis comparing suffering mothers with infected newborns and those with non-infected newborns (Fig. 8 and Table S3). The AUC was 0.9394 (p = 0.024) for IgG anti-LPS E. coli O111:B4 and 0.9697 (p = 0.015) for IgG anti-LPS E. coli O26:B6. Similar ROC analysis yielded a significant AUC level of 0.95 (p = 0.027) for IgG anti-LPS K. pneumoniae. This clearly indicates the association of the low level of specific IgG anti-LPS E coli and anti-LPS K. pneumoniae in suffering mothers with the high risk of bacterial transmission to newborns.

Discussion

Maternal and neonatal infections are a major health challenge worldwide, especially in low and middle-income countries, where few studies have included both mothers and their newborns to better unravel the risk of perinatal microbial transmission and characterize the correlates of susceptibility. To fill this gap in the North Africa region, we implemented a prospective study on pregnant women in Morocco with a focus on major bacterial pathogens, Gram-negative E. coli and K. pneumoniae, and Gram-positive Group B Streptococcus. We recently reported that the circulating maternal level of IL-17 in pregnant mothers carrying group B streptococcus has proven to be a key marker for identifying at-risk newborns [22]. Here, we focus on mothers suffering from pregnancy complications with positive culture for E. coli or K. pneumoniae, with the aim of analyzing a panel of circulating inflammatory cytokines together with specific antibody response, and to see whether a specific profile could be associated with neonatal invasive disease.

The study revealed that among a large panel of tested inflammatory cytokines, plasma levels of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α were significantly higher in suffering mothers with positive culture for E. coli and K. pneumoniae as compared to healthy mothers. We observed a clear correlation between plasmatic levels of IL-6 and TNF-α and the functional capacity of circulating leucocytes to produce these cytokines upon TLR4 and TLR2/1 ligation, highlighting the triggering of the immune-inflammatory response in the context of exposure to E. coli and K. pneumoniae. This is in line with previous studies reporting higher expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in the bloodstream of non-pregnant women infected with gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli and K. pneumoniae [25]. In addition to their essential role in controlling infection, elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines can negatively impact pregnancy and lead to serious clinical consequences for the fetus. Indeed, elevated levels of circulating cytokines in pregnant women were shown to be associated with gestational complications such as preterm rupture of membranes and preterm birth. It has been shown that levels of IL-6 and IL-8 in maternal serum, amniotic fluid, and cord blood serum were significantly higher in pregnant women exhibiting preterm rupture of membranes [26], which increases the risk of the exposure of the fetus to bacteria present in the uterine environment. Other studies reported the association of increased serum levels of IL-6 and IL-8 in pregnant women with preterm birth [27, 28]. Consistent with these studies, a significant proportion of our study cohort with a high inflammatory cytokine profile suffered from preterm rupture of membranes and preterm birth, hence increasing the risk for neonatal invasive disease.

Our study revealed higher serum levels of IL-6 in suffering mothers who gave birth to infected newborns compared to those who did not. The association between maternal IL-6 levels and bacterial transmission was further supported by an ROC analysis highlighting the predictive value of IL-6 for neonatal outcomes regarding perinatal transmission of E. coli and K. pneumoniae. IL-6, a well-known early inflammatory mediator, which is produced by a wide range of immune cells, has been shown to be a valuable marker for differentiating bacterial and fungal infections in pregnant women [29]. Few studies have explored the potential of using maternal serum cytokine levels to predict neonatal outcomes. It has been shown that maternal levels of IL-6 were associated with preterm birth, neonatal sepsis, and death [30]. The elevated level of IL-6 found in suffering mothers is likely contributing to preterm rupture of membranes and, therefore, to increased risk for neonatal infection. However, the lower levels of TNF-α found in mothers who gave birth to infected newborns compared to those who did not initially suggest a reduced host immune defense against bacteria. Nevertheless, the elevation of IL-6 argues against a globally impaired immune response. Instead, this profile may reflect differences in cytokine kinetics, as TNF-α is an early and transient inflammatory mediator, while IL-6 is often sustained for longer periods during infections [31].

In neonates born to suffering mothers, the plasma level of IL-6 and IL-8 in umbilical cord blood correlates with the functional capacity of umbilical cord blood leucocytes to produce these cytokines upon stimulation by bacterial ligands LPS and Pam3CS4. Our results are in line with previous studies showing increased cord blood levels of IL-6 and IL-8 and their biomarker value in early-onset infections or sepsis [32–35, 36]. In addition to direct exposure of the fetus to microbial antigens, maternal immune activation following contact with bacterial antigens may also have an impact on the postnatal inflammatory response. Indeed, studies in mouse models reported that maternal exposure to LPS during the prenatal period increases the baseline of IL-6 and IL-1β levels in offspring [37].

Regarding specific antibody response, our results revealed that although there was a trend toward a higher IgG anti-LPS-E. coli O111:B4 levels in suffering mothers compared to healthy mothers, the level of IgG anti-LPS-E. coli O26:B6 remained similar in both groups. This suggests the occurrence of naturally acquired humoral immunity, which is likely resulting from prior exposure to E. coli strains, as has been reported in other studies [38–40].

Interestingly, when looking specifically at the group of suffering mothers, we observed a significant association between anti-bacterial IgG antibodies in maternal blood and neonatal invasive disease. The mothers whose newborns were infected with E. coli or K. pneumoniae had lower levels of specific IgG antibodies. The association was further supported by an ROC analysis, hence suggesting the potential role of maternal-specific IgG in preventing bacterial transmission. Consistent with our findings, a recent study identified reduced levels of vertically transferred natural maternal IgG anti-E. coli as a risk factor for neonatal sepsis. Moreover, newborns with neonatal sepsis showed consistently lower levels of anti-E. coli IgG, primarily IgG2, than in control newborns without sepsis. Similar observations were also reported for anti-OmpA IgG [41].

The mechanisms involved in reducing the risk of perinatal transmission of E. coli and K. pneumoniae were not investigated in this study, yet it is well known that specific antibodies act through multiple mechanisms. This may occur by the neutralization of bacterial surface antigens and virulence factors such as lipopolysaccharides and adhesins, thereby inhibiting bacterial adhesion to the host’s membrane and limiting colonization [42] or by the opsonization through complement activation and phagocytic killing of E. coli and K. pneumoniae, as evidenced by the early studies [43, 44]. The latter mechanism appears to play a particularly important role, as shown by Raymond E. Diep et al., who reported reduced anti-E. coli opsonophagocytic activity in macrophages and neutrophils from neonates with E. coli sepsis [41].

To our knowledge, this is the first investigation conducted on a cohort of mother-infant pairs within the North Africa region, with the objective to elucidate the risk factors of perinatal bacteria transmission with regard to maternal inflammatory cytokine profile and specific anti-bacteria antibody response. We found that elevated plasma levels of IL-6 and low concentrations of specific anti-LPS IgG antibodies were significantly linked to an increased risk of neonatal invasive disease.

Despite the strengths of this dyad-based study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, some recruited mothers were lost to follow-up because they gave birth in other hospitals, hence reducing the size of the cord blood samples and, consequently, the number of samples available for mother-infant pairs. Further multicentric studies would be of value to support these observations. Second, residual confounding may have independently influenced inflammatory cytokine profiles, including factors such as pregnancy complications and differences in gestational age. However, this seems unlikely because functional assays showed that maternal white blood cells were primed to produce higher cytokine levels upon stimulation compared to controls. Third, vertical transmission was inferred based on the simultaneous isolation of bacteria species E. coli and K. pneumoniae from the mother and newborn. However, strain-level confirmation was not conducted. Such approaches would be necessary to unequivocally confirm the transmission of the same isolate and strengthen this assumption. We recently reported in another cohort study the characterization of circulating Gram-positive group B streptococcus isolates among pregnant mothers [45]. The same strategy is being employed for the molecular characterization of circulating E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolates in the present study cohort (Ben-Taleb et al. manuscript under preparation). This complementary study is also an important step in exploring whether the triggered inflammatory response varies based on the bacterial genotype and how differences in virulence between strains affect the level of specific antibodies. Additionally, further research is necessary to investigate the functional antibody response in mother-newborn pairs and unravel the mechanisms by which antibodies provide protection against perinatal bacterial transmission.

Conclusion

This study, conducted on a North African cohort, provides valuable insights into the transmission of E. coli and K. pneumoniae from mother to newborn. It emphasizes the significance of predictive factors related to the maternal inflammatory profile and the level of specific antibacterial antibody response.
