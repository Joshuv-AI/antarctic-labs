Chronic proton pump inhibitor exposure aggravates intestinal injury by impairing intestinal stem cell self-renewal through the microbiota-7-ketolithocholic acid Axis
Authors: Xinyuan Wang, Li Cheng, Kehan Yin, Bo Wang, Xiujuan Yan, Shengliang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use is associated with increased intestinal disease risk, but its damaging mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsMice were administered rabeprazole (Rab) for 4 weeks before dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) or ionizing radiation (IR) injury. We employed RNA sequencing, metabolomics, and metagenomics, evaluated intestinal stem cell (ISC) function, and used organoids for validation.ResultsLong-term Rab induced small intestinal mucosal injury and exacerbated DSS/IR-induced damage, manifesting as crypt/villus atrophy and reduced ISC numbers. Mechanistically, Rab downregulated the Wnt pathway and impaired mucosal defense and regeneration. Microbiota involvement was indicated by fecal transplantation. Integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that Rab induced intestinal dysbiosis and reduced ileal bile acids, particularly 7-ketolithocholic acid (7KLCA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA). Faecalibaculum rodentium supplementation restored ISC self-renewal by converting CDCA to 7KLCA. In vitro, 7KLCA activated Wnt signaling to rescue Rab-induced stem cell impairment. In vivo, both 7KLCA and Gly-β-MCA (intestinal FXR antagonists) suppressed the FXR-FGF15 axis, restored the expression of hepatic bile acid synthesis enzymes, and promoted epithelial repair, thereby mitigating DSS-induced injury.ConclusionsChronic PPI use impairs ISC self-renewal by disrupting the microbiota-7KLCA-Wnt axis. F. rodentium or 7KLCA supplementation ameliorates PPI-induced effects, highlighting a microbe-metabolite axis as a pivotal mechanism and potential therapies for PPI-associated intestinal damage.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08448-9.

Introduction

As the most widely used acid-suppressive agents, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) have been recommended as the first-line medication for acid-related diseases such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), peptic ulcer, and Helicobacter pylori eradication [1], [2–4]. By irreversibly inhibiting the activity of H+-K+-ATPase in gastric parietal cells, they effectively reduce gastric acid secretion to facilitate mucosal repair [5, 6]. Despite the growing application of potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs) due to the advantages of faster onset, stronger acid inhibition, and longer duration of action, PPIs, with more than 30 years of clinical validation, affordability, and broad indications, are still commonly used for treatment of gastrointestinal diseases [7–10]. PPIs are among the top 10 most commonly prescribed drugs in the United States and even globally [11]. However, the potential health risks associated with PPI use have raised growing concerns.

Existing evidence has shown that long-term PPI use is not only associated with adverse effects such as electrolyte disorders [12], fracture risk [13], and cardiovascular events [14], but may also cause gastrointestinal responses such as enteric infections (Clostridium difficile infection [15, 16]) and diarrhea [17, 18]. Animal studies have demonstrated that long-term PPI use impairs the colonic barrier in experimental colitis [19]. Short-term application of PPIs also increases intestinal permeability in stressed mice [20]. Notably, clinical evidence suggests that PPI use is associated with an increased risk of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [21–23]. Moreover, PPIs may compromise remission on infliximab therapy in IBD patients, particularly those with CD [24]. Although these findings may be attributable to facilitation of enteric infections associated with alterations of gut microbiota by PPIs, the mechanism by which PPI disturbs the intestinal repair process remains unclear.

Microbial dysbiosis has been suggested as a potential contributor to PPI-associated enteric infections. Long-term PPI use was reported to facilitate Clostridium difficile infection [15, 16, 25], which may be associated with the alteration of fecal flora. PPIs can promote oral-to-gut microbial transmission leading to increased fecal Streptococcus abundance and intestinal permeability [26]. These data indicate that PPIs may impair intestinal repair by affecting the microbiome. However, which species of the gut microbiota mediates the PPI-related disruption of host intestinal homeostasis and the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

Metabolites are critical mediators for gut microbiota action on the host. Emerging evidence has shown that the microbiota-derived metabolites modulate host intestinal homeostasis and the function of intestinal stem cells (ISCs) [27]. The homeostasis of ISCs, which is disrupted in intestinal diseases such as IBD and colorectal cancer, is essential for epithelial regeneration and mucosal barrier function [28, 29]. It is still unclear whether and how PPIs disturb microbiota-host interactions through bacterial metabolites and thus disrupt intestinal homeostasis and the repair process.

Therefore, the present study was designed to explore how chronic PPI exposure, via affecting gut microbiota and the associated metabolites, disrupts ISCs-mediated intestinal repair upon dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)- and ionizing radiation (IR)-induced intestinal injury [30–34]. We report that PPI exposure impaired the self-renewal of ISCs and niche homeostasis through ileal Faecalibaculum rodentium (F. rodentium)-7-ketolithocholic acid (7KLCA; a bile acid metabolite of F. rodentium)-Wnt signaling axis. Furthermore, we evaluated the rescue effects of supplementation with F. rodentium and 7KLCA, so as to provide potential translational solutions for the prevention and treatment of PPI-associated intestinal complications.

Methods

Mice and in vivo treatment

Six-week-old C57BL/6J mice were housed at the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. They were maintained in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) environment at 22–25 °C under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to diet and water. Following a 7-day acclimation period, the mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Mice were orally gavaged (200 μL) with either rabeprazole (20 mg/kg) or a vehicle control (sterilized water) every other day for 4 weeks. The mice were euthanized on day 28, and samples of the small intestine (SI), colon, small intestinal contents, liver, and serum were collected.

To establish the intestinal injury animal models, two distinct protocols were employed in this study. For the DSS-induced colitis model, mice were provided with 2.5% (w/v) DSS dissolved in sterile drinking water ad libitum for 7 consecutive days, while control mice received standard drinking water. Daily monitoring was strictly conducted to record water consumption, body weight, stool consistency, and the presence of gross rectal bleeding. On day 8, the DSS solution was replaced with standard drinking water to clear residual DSS polymer from the intestinal lumen and mitigate chemical artifacts prior to tissue collection. After 3 days, the mice were euthanized, and intestinal tissues were harvested for further analysis [35, 36]. For the radiation-induced intestinal injury model, mice were subjected to a single dose of total body irradiation using an RS 1800Q irradiator (Rad Source Technologies, Buford, GA, USA). The radiation was delivered at a total dose of 11 Gy with a dose rate of 0.98 Gy/min. At 4 days post-irradiation, the mice were euthanized, and SI tissues were collected for further experimental evaluation.

For bile acid intervention, mice were gavaged every other day for 4 weeks with 7KLCA (50 mg/kg), Gly-β-MCA (10 mg/kg), or a vehicle control (10% dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO). For the F. rodentium gavage, mice were gavaged with approximately 2 × 108 CFU of F. rodentium or sterile modified PYG medium every other day for 4 weeks.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Donor ileal content was collected from either control or Rab-treated mice, immediately diluted in sterile PBS, and homogenized. The homogenate was then centrifuged, filtered, and the resulting supernatant was resuspended in 40% glycerol for storage at −80 °C until transplantation.

Recipient mice were first subjected to a 10-day antibiotic cocktail (ABX) regimen (including 1 mg/ml ampicillin, 1 mg/ml neomycin, 1 mg/ml metronidazole, and 0.5 mg/ml vancomycin) to deplete the intestinal microbiota. This was followed by oral gavage with 200 μL of the bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL) every other day for 28 days.

Bacteria

Faecalibaculum rodentium was purchased from the BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC, Cat#BNCC363015). It was cultured in an anaerobic environment consisting of 5% hydrogen, 15% carbon dioxide, and 80% nitrogen at 37 °C in modified PYG medium (DSMZ Medium 104) supplemented with hemin, vitamin K1, and L-cysteine.

Reagents

Rabeprazole (HY-B0656A), 7-Ketolithocholic acid (HY-W018512), and Gly-β-MCA (HY-114392) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Beijing, China). Chenodeoxycholic acid (C9377) and dimethyl sulfoxide (D2650) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Tween 80 (S6702) and PEG300 (S6704) were purchased from Selleck (Houston, TX, USA). Anti-Ki67 antibody (ab15580) and anti-Lysozyme antibody (ab108508) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). OLFM4 antibody (D6Y5A) and β-Catenin antibody (D10A8) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti-SOX9 antibody (AB5535) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Anti-MUC2 (A14659) was purchased from Abclonal (Wuhan, China). Mouse Fibroblast Growth Factor 15 (FGF15) ELISA Kit (CSB-EL522052MO) was purchased from CUSABIO (Wuhan, China). Mouse Defensin Alpha 3 ELISA Kit (JL40213) was purchased from Jonln (Shanghai, China). One-step TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (CX108), EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (CX002), and Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (CX006) were purchased from Epizyme Biotech (Shanghai, China). Small intestinal organoids were cultured using IntestiCult™ Organoid Growth Medium (Cat#06005, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) and DMEM/F-12 (Cat#36254, STEMCELL Technologies). Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS) was purchased from MP Biomedicals (California, USA). Matrigel® (Cat#356231) was purchased from Corning (New York, USA). DMEM and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Massachusetts, USA).

Organoids and cell culture

Mouse small intestinal tissues were collected and thoroughly cleansed. After mincing, crypts were isolated through a mild cell dissociation reagent, filtration, and centrifugation. The crypts were resuspended in cold DMEM/F-12. Afterwards, they were counted and resuspended in Complete IntestiCultTM Organoid Growth Medium, embedded with Matrigel. Complete IntestiCultTM Organoid Growth Medium was added, and the cultures were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The MODE-K cell line was purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 10 units/mL penicillin, and 10 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For experiments, the organoids were treated with DMSO, 7KLCA (20 μM), or CDCA (20 μM) for 72 hours, and MODE-K cells were treated for 48 hours. For irradiation, a total dose of 8 Gy was delivered to cells using an RS 1800Q irradiator (Rad Source Technologies, Inc., Buford, GA, USA) at 0.98 Gy/min.

Metagenomic sequencing

We collected mouse ileal content and extracted microbial DNA using a kit from LC-Bio (Hangzhou, China). Whole-genome shotgun metagenomic sequencing was then carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Following preprocessing and filtering of the raw data, the sequences were mapped to the NR_meta database to obtain taxonomic profiles and enable downstream analysis. The sequencing process was conducted by LC-Bio Technologies Co., Ltd. in Hangzhou, China.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) untargeted metabolomics

Mouse ileal contents were collected for metabolomics profiling. Briefly, contents were collected immediately after ileum excision and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Metabolites were extracted from homogenized samples using methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). In addition, the supernatant collected after 24 hours of bacterial culture was also subjected to metabolomic analysis. After centrifugation, the supernatant was dried and reconstituted in acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v) for UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS analysis (Agilent 1290 LC coupled to SCIEX TripleTOF 6600). Metabolites were identified by matching accurate mass (mass error < 10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra to an in-house standard database. Metabolomic data were analyzed via PCA and OPLS-DA using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Metabolites with significant differential expression were screened based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 and a p value < 0.05 from Student’s t-test [37]. Differential metabolite heatmaps were plotted using the R package “pheatmap”.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA was extracted from mouse terminal ileal tissue and organoids for sequencing. Ileal tissue was collected post-euthanasia after removing intestinal contents. Organoids were washed three times with cold PBS containing 0.1% BSA via centrifugation (500 g, 5 min, 4°C). Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Quality control was performed using NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA), Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA). Samples with concentrations > 50 ng/μL, RIN values > 7.0, OD260/280 > 1.8, and total RNA > 1 μg were used for subsequent experiments. For library preparation, mRNA was captured with Dynabeads oligo(dT) beads, fragmented, reverse-transcribed, and constructed into libraries for paired-end sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq™ 6000.

16S rDNA amplicon sequencing

Mouse ileal contents were collected for 16S rDNA sequencing. Briefly, ileal contents were stored at −80 °C immediately after being obtained and proceeded to the next experiments as quickly as possible. Genomic DNA from the samples was extracted using the OMEGA Mag-bind soil DNA kit, and PCR amplification of DNA from the V3–V4 variable region was performed. The recovered products were used to construct libraries by TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (Illumina), and the libraries were quality checked by Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and Promega QuantiFluor, and then sequenced by Illumina Novaseq 6000 sequencing platform. Primer sequences were cut off from the raw data using cutadapt software, followed by quality control analysis, including filtering low-quality sequences, denoised, merging, and detecting and cutting off the chimeric reads using DADA2 with the default parameters of QIIME2. The obtained representative sequences and ASVs were annotated for species comparison through the Silva 138 database.

Community diversity and difference analyses

The α-diversity index was used to compare differences in microbial community richness within-community in each group. Chao1α-diversity index was calculated using QIIME2 software. β-diversity was used to compare differences in species composition between samples. Principal Co-ordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance was used for conducting β-diversity analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence

Mouse ileal tissues were fixed in formalin for over 24 hours, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. The sections were baked at 70 °C for 40 minutes and then underwent dewaxing, rehydration, blocking, antigen retrieval, and serum blocking. For immunohistochemistry, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 or OLFM4 at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin before the sections were mounted and examined under a microscope. For immunofluorescence, after overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Lysozyme, MUC2, β-Catenin, or SOX9, the sections were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies and mounted with DAPI Fluoromount-G® (Southern Biotech).

For cultured organoids, they were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated with PBS containing 1% Triton-X-100 with 5% donkey serum for 1 h, respectively. Afterwards, they were incubated with Lysozyme or SOX9 primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature away from light for 1 h. The nuclei were restained with DAPI. The images were captured under a Nikon AX with NSPARC confocal microscope.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Mouse small intestinal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were then deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Following this, the sections were stained with hematoxylin to visualize nuclei and with eosin to highlight the cytoplasm, before being mounted with neutral resin. Histopathological scoring of the SI was performed based on the evaluation of inflammatory cell infiltration, crypt injury, epithelial loss, and goblet cell loss [38].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Fresh mouse terminal ileum tissues were dissected and trimmed into small pieces (approximately 3 × 1 × 1 mm3). The tissue specimens were fixed in pre-cooled glutaraldehyde at 4 °C. Subsequently, the fixed samples were embedded in resin and processed into 70-nm ultrathin sections, which were then subjected to staining. The ultrastructural morphology of the sections was finally observed and analyzed using the HT-7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA from mouse terminal ileum, liver, or cultured organoids was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of extracted RNA were measured by NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). Eligible RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Quantitative RT-PCR was then conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Actb mRNA was used as the internal control. Supplementary table 1 contains the sequences of all primers used in the experiment.

Flow cytometry apoptosis assay

MODE-K cells were treated with DMSO or 7KLCA, and apoptosis was induced by IR, with the cells being collected 48 hours later. Afterwards, the cells were resuspended in binding buffer according to the manufacturer’s procedure and incubated with Annexin V-FITC and PI Staining Solution. The proportion of early apoptotic (Annexin V-FITC+/PI-) and late apoptotic cells (Annexin V-FITC+/PI+) was recorded using a flow cytometry assay.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse serum FGF15 was detected using Mouse Fibroblast Growth Factor 15 (FGF15) ELISA Kit, and fecal DEFA3 was detected using Mouse Defensin Alpha 3 ELISA Kit. Feces were processed by homogenization in PBS, followed by centrifugation. According to the manufacturer’s recommendation, 100 μl of the samples was incubated in pre-coated plates. After sequential incubations with biotinylated antibody and enzyme conjugate, and subsequent washing steps, TMB substrate was added for color development. The reaction was terminated, and OD450 was measured for concentration calculation against a standard curve.

EdU proliferation assay

For in vivo EdU labeling in mice, a solution of EdU (50 mg/kg body weight) was administered via intraperitoneal injection 6 hours before euthanasia. For organoids, the organoids were incubated with 50 μM EdU at 37 °C for 2 hours, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 1 hour, and washed with PBS containing 1% Triton-X-100 for 30 minutes. Click reaction solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at room temperature away from light, and then incubated with DAPI for 20 min. Images were captured on the Z-stack using the Nikon AX with NSPARC confocal microscope.

TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

The organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 1 hour and washed with PBS containing 1% Triton-X-100 for 20 minutes. After being equilibrated with equilibration buffer for 20 minutes, the organoids were incubated with TdT incubation buffer at 37 °C away from light for 1 hour, and the nucleus of the cells was restained by DAPI. A Nikon AX with NSPARC confocal microscope was used for photography.

Statistics

All mouse experiments were performed in at least 2 independent experiments, and in vitro experiments were performed in at least 3 independent experiments. Differences were considered significant if p < 0.05. All experimental data were first tested for normality, and for those with satisfying normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, Student’s t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and one-way or two-way ANOVA was used for comparison among multiple (≥3) groups. Otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparison between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparison of multiple groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad 9.3.1. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.4.0.

Results

Long-term use of rabeprazole impairs the intestinal mucosa of mice

To study the effects of long-term PPI use on the bowel, mice were gavaged with rabeprazole (Rab) every other day for 4 weeks. Compared to the control group, Rab-exposed mice showed significantly shorter SI length (Fig. 1A), whereas colon length remained unchanged (Fig. 1B). Moreover, Rab exposure did not significantly alter body weight gain in mice (Fig. 1C). H&E staining revealed that Rab-treated mice showed significantly decreased depth of crypts and shortened villus height in the ileum (Fig. 1D–F). Concurrently, the histological score of the SI in Rab–treated mice was higher than that in the control group (Fig. 1D and G). Moreover, Rab induced disruption and loss of intestinal microvilli, as revealed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1H). These findings collectively indicate that Rab impairs the integrity of the SI mucosa. In addition, Rab induced a reduction in EdU+ cells and Ki67+ proliferative cells in intestinal epithelium, indicating that Rab suppressed intestinal epithelial cell renewal (Fig. 1I–J). This prompted us to investigate whether intestinal stem cells (ISCs), the key cells maintaining intestinal epithelial homeostasis, are affected by Rab. It was found that Rab reduced the number of OLFM4+ ISCs in the mouse ileum (Fig. 1K). Mice subjected to DSS treatment exhibited a markedly reduced body weight and a significant shortening of the SI. Furthermore, the intestinal architecture was severely impaired, as evidenced by decreased crypt depth and villus height, along with notable histological damage (Fig. 2B–F). Mice treated with DSS+Rab displayed a more pronounced body weight loss compared with those receiving DSS alone (Fig. 2B). Besides, they had significantly shorter lengths of the SI than mice receiving DSS (Fig. 2C). Notably, crypt depth and villus height were markedly shorter in the DSS+Rab-treated mice than those in DSS (Fig. 2D–E). The histological score of the SI was significantly elevated in the DSS+Rab-treated mice, indicative of more severe intestinal tissue damage (Fig. 2F). In vivo labeling with EdU and Ki67 staining revealed a significant suppression of ileal crypt cell proliferation in mice treated with DSS+Rab (Fig 2G-H). Moreover, the number of OLFM4+ ISCs in the ileum was significantly lower in the DSS+Rab-treated mice compared with mice given DSS alone (Fig. 2I). Taken together, these results indicate that Rab impaired intestinal mucosal repair in DSS-induced enteritis.

In the radiation-induced injury model, mice exposed to IR+Rab exhibited higher mortality and significantly shorter SI length than those subjected to IR alone (Supplementary figures 1B-1C). Mice subjected to IR+Rab demonstrated more severe disruption of the intestinal epithelium, characterized by extensive loss of villi and crypts (Supplementary figure 1D). The number of residual EdU+ and Ki67+ proliferating cells in the SI crypts was significantly reduced in the IR+Rab-treated mice (Supplementary figures 1E-1F). Moreover, the depletion of OLFM4+ ISCs was observed in IR+Rab-treated mice (Supplementary figure 1 G). These findings indicate that Rab aggravates both DSS- and radiation-induced intestinal injury by impairing ISCs.

Rab inhibits stem cell regeneration and differentiation by suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

We subsequently investigated the effect of Rab on intestinal homeostasis under inflammatory conditions. Terminal ileal tissues were collected from mice receiving DSS or DSS+Rab treatment for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. PCA revealed significant differences between DSS and DSS+Rab groups (Supplementary figure 2A). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to annotate the biological functions of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were significantly enriched in biological process terms defense response to bacteria and humoral immune response, with DEGs within these terms significantly downregulated in the DSS+Rab group versus the DSS group (Fig. 3A). In parallel, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also demonstrated that defense response and innate immune response in the mucosa were significantly down-regulated in the ileum of DSS+Rab-treated mice (Fig. 3B–C), indicating that Rab impairs mucosal defense. We therefore investigated the intestinal epithelial cell subtypes. Consistent with the RNA-seq data, we subsequently observed that long-term use of Rab exacerbated DSS-induced injury to intestinal stem cells in the mouse ileum, as evidenced by significantly reduced mRNA levels of stem cell marker genes (Lgr5, Olfm4, Ascl2, and Sox9) in the mice of DSS+Rab-treated compared to those of DSS-treated alone (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, Rab treatment impaired the function of PCs in the ileal mucosa of DSS-treated mice, as indicated by significantly decreased ileal mRNA levels of lysozyme (Lyz1) and α-defensin (Defa3 and Defa23) and decreased fecal DEFA3 protein level (Fig. 3F and supplementary figure 2C). The ileal levels of Lyz1, Defa3, and Defa23 in Rab-treated mice were also reduced compared to the control (Fig. 3F). TEM analysis revealed morphologically abnormal and disintegrated endocrine granules in ileal PCs of mice receiving DSS+Rab treatment (Fig. 3G). Notably, compared to mice receiving DSS alone, mice receiving DSS+Rab treatment exhibited a significant reduction in the number of PCs (Lysozyme+ cells) and their differentiation-associated stem cells (SOX9+ cells) in ileal crypts, while MUC2+ goblet cells (GCs) remained unaffected (Fig. 3Hand supplementary figures 2E-2H). Similarly, Rab reduced the number of IR-induced LYZ+ PCs but did not affect the number of GCs (Supplementary figure 2I-2K). These findings indicate that Rab not only exacerbates damage to ISCs but also compromises their differentiation into PCs.

To further explore the mechanism of Rab in DSS-induced mouse injury, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis based on DEGs screened from RNA-seq data. The results showed that DEGs between the DSS+Rab group and DSS treatment group were significantly enriched in the Wnt signaling pathway, as well as antibacterial defense-related pathways including the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and IL-17 signaling pathway. Integration of the enrichment results with the gene expression profiles revealed downregulation of the aforementioned pathways in DSS-treated mice receiving Rab administration (Supplementary figure 2D). GSEA further confirmed that, compared with the DSS group, the activity of the Wnt signaling pathway was significantly reduced in the DSS+Rab group (Supplementary figure 2B). qPCR confirmed that mRNA levels of Wnt signaling pathway marker genes (Ccnd1, Myc, and Axin2) were downregulated in the mice treated with DSS+Rab compared to those with DSS (Fig. 3I). Importantly, Rab treatment reduced the number of crypt cells with β-catenin nuclear distribution in DSS-preconditioned mice (Fig. 3J), further indicating the reduced activity of the Wnt signaling pathway. The above results suggest that long-term use of Rab suppressed the Wnt signaling pathway and impaired the function of ISCs and PCs in the SI.

Intestinal microbiota mediates Rab-induced damage to intestinal regeneration

Intestinal microbes and their metabolites are engaged in the functional regulation of ISCs [39]. Previous research has demonstrated that the use of PPI affects the intestinal microbiota [40]. To investigate whether the intestinal microbiota contributes to Rab-induced ISCs damage, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). To this end, antibiotic cocktail (ABX)-treated mice were orally gavaged with intestinal contents from Rab donors to determine if the transplanted microbiota could recapitulate the Rab phenotype (Fig. 4A). ABX treatment significantly reduced the abundance of 16S rRNA in mouse feces (Supplementary Figure 3A). Mice receiving Rab microbiota transplantation (DSS+FmtRab) exhibited more severe intestinal damage than the DSS+FmtCon control, as indicated by aggravated body weight loss and shortened small intestinal length (Fig. 4B–C). Specifically, histological analysis revealed exacerbated tissue injury with significantly shortened crypts and villi (Fig. 4D–E). At the cellular level, we observed a notable reduction in EdU+ cells and OLFM4+ ISCs, alongside a decrease in LYZ+ PCs (Fig. 4F–H). Multi-omics identifies Rab-induced microbial dysbiosis and bile acid suppression

Next, we investigated the effects of Rab on the intestinal microbiota and its metabolites in mice. In this regard, we performed metagenomic and metabolomic sequencing on the intestinal contents of mice treated with DSS and DSS+Rab. Complementary evidence from multi-omics analysis consistently indicated that Rab induced comprehensive alterations in the intestinal environment. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed significant disruption of intestinal microbiota structure (Bray-Curtis, PERMANOVA p = 0.025), while PCA also indicated corresponding reprogramming of the metabolic profile (PERMANOVA p = 0.031) (Supplementary figures 3B and 3E). These findings suggest that Rab significantly remodels both the structure and function of the mouse intestinal microbiota.

On this basis, we further assessed the α diversity of the microbial community. Compared with the DSS group, the Chao1 index was significantly reduced in the DSS+Rab group, indicating that Rab treatment markedly decreased the species richness of the intestinal microbiota (Supplementary figure 3C). Significant alterations in the microbial composition at the phylum level were observed following Rab treatment. Specifically, we found that the relative abundances of Bacteroidota and Verrucomicrobiota were significantly decreased, while those of Actinomycetota and Pseudomonadota were increased in mice subjected to DSS+Rab compared to the control (Fig. 4I). To identify key taxa contributing to intergroup differences, we performed a LEfSe analysis. At the genus level, the ileum of the mice subjected to DSS was significantly enriched with Clostridium, Schaedlerella, and Akkermansia. In contrast, the DSS+Rab treatment showed significant enrichment of Streptococcus and Enterobacter. At the species level, significant enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila, Schaedlerella arabinosiphila, Bacteroides acidifaciens, Flintibacter muris, Muribaculum intestinale, and Faecalibaculum rodentium was observed in DSS treatment. Conversely, species such as Streptococcus danieliae and Streptococcus hyointestinalis were identified as significantly enriched biomarkers in the mice treated with DSS+Rab (Fig. 4J and supplementary figure 3D).

We then investigated how Rab influences the composition of microbiota-derived metabolites in the SI. Compared to mice treated with DSS, those treated with DSS+Rab exhibited a significantly reduced bile acid pool in the ileum (Fig. 4K and supplementary figure 3F). In particular, ileal levels of 7-ketolithocholic acid (7keto-LCA, 7KLCA), deoxycholic acid (DCA), chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), β-muricholic acid (β-MCA), tyrosine conjugated cholic acid, and cholic acid (CA) in DSS-treated mice were significantly repressed by Rab (Fig. 4L). To determine if Rab directly modulates bile acid metabolism, we examined its effect on AML12 hepatocytes. Our results demonstrated that Rab treatment had no direct impact on the expression of hepatic bile acid synthetic enzymes (Supplementary figure 3 G). We concluded that Rab is unlikely to act directly on hepatocytes. Instead, we hypothesized that it modulates bile acid metabolism indirectly via the intestine-liver axis. We therefore investigated whether Rab influences bile acid homeostasis through the intestinal microbiota. To identify bacteria potentially linked to the observed changes in bile acid metabolism, we performed a correlation analysis integrating metagenomic and non-targeted metabolomics data. Analysis of correlations between the top LEfSe bacteria and key bile acids revealed that Faecalibaculum rodentium was most strongly and positively associated with 7KLCA (r = 0.98, FDR = 0.006) (Fig. 4M).

To dissect whether the suppression of Faecalibaculum rodentium was directly driven by Rab or secondary to intestinal injury, we analyzed intestinal microbiota by 16S rDNA sequencing across four groups: Con, Rab, DSS, and DSS+Rab (Fig. 2A). Intriguingly, Rab administration, regardless of DSS co-treatment, significantly reduced the abundance of Faecalibaculum in the SI (Supplementary figures 3 H and 3J). This indicates that the suppression is directly induced by Rab, rather than being a consequence of intestinal damage. Additionally, irrespective of the concurrent administration of DSS, Rab increased Lactobacillus abundance and reduced Bifidobacterium levels (Supplementary figure 3J). Furthermore, LEfSe analysis identified Faecalibaculum and Akkermansia as significantly enriched in the Con group, whereas Lactobacillus was enriched in the Rab group (Supplementary figure 3K). It is noteworthy that Faecalibaculum rodentium is the only validly published species within the genus Faecalibaculum (Supplementary figure 3I) [41]. This Rab-specific suppression of Faecalibaculum rodentium was conclusively confirmed by qPCR in the ileum (Fig. 4N).

Mice receiving F. rodentium supplementation exhibited markedly attenuated enteritis, as evidenced by a more moderate body weight, loss of histological score, and a significant increase in EdU+ proliferative crypt cells (Fig. 5C–E). Moreover, F. rodentium administration restored the numbers of ISCs and PCs, and concurrently increased the expression levels of stem cell marker genes in the ileum (Fig. 5F–H). We next sought to elucidate the specific mechanism by which F. rodentium modulates bile acid homeostasis. Given that the gene hdhA encodes 7α-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (7α-HSDH), an enzyme that catalyzes the NAD+ -dependent oxidation of the 7α-hydroxyl of primary bile acids to form 7-oxo steroids (e.g., CDCA to 7KLCA) [42], we asked whether F. rodentium could convert CDCA to 7KLCA in vitro. Remarkably, CDCA treatment significantly accelerated bacterial growth, suggesting that F. rodentium utilizes CDCA as an energy source (Fig. 5I). Subsequently, we performed untargeted metabolomics analysis on bacterial culture supernatants to explore whether F. rodentium could transform CDCA to other bile acids. Statistically, the concentration of 7KLCA was significantly increased in the supernatant of the CDCA-treated F. rodentium (Fig. 5J), suggesting that F. rodentium catalyzes CDCA into 7KLCA. To support the functional assignment of 7α-HSDH in F. rodentium, we compared its protein sequence with known 7α-HSDH enzymes from other bacteria, such as Paraclostridium sordellii, Clostridium sardiniense, Bacteroides fragilis, and Escherichia coli (Supplementary figure 4). The combined data suggest that F. rodentium restores self-renewal of ISCs in the SI of mice treated with DSS+Rab via synthesizing 7KLCA.

7KLCA activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling to restore stem cell self-renewal in vitro

To validate the functional role of 7KLCA, we employed both the murine small intestinal epithelial cell line MODE-K and murine SI-derived organoids. We first treated MODE-K cells with 7KLCA and found that it suppressed irradiation-induced cell death in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6A). Based on this cytoprotective effect, a concentration of 20 μM 7KLCA was selected for subsequent experiments. We next utilized the organoid model to assess regeneration. Compared to organoids derived from DSS-treated control mice (Con), those from DSS+Rab mice (Rab) exhibited reduced formation efficiency, smaller size, and decreased budding. Supplementation with 20 μM 7KLCA significantly rescued these defects, leading to increased organoid number, size, and budding efficiency. In contrast, the potent FXR agonist CDCA had no significant effect on organoid growth (Fig. 6B and C). PCA based on organoid RNA-seq data showed significant differences between Con, Rab, and Rab combined 7KLCA (RK) (Fig. 6D). Of importance, the Lgr5-Ascl2 signature was down-regulated by Rab, whereas 7KLCA significantly increased the expression levels of several related genes (Fig. 6E). GSEA revealed that the Wnt signaling pathway was downregulated by Rab, and 7KLCA reversed this effect (Fig. 6F). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that Rab impaired organoid homeostasis by inhibiting proliferation (EdU assay), promoting apoptosis (TUNEL assay), and reducing the numbers of PCs (LYZ+) and SOX9+ cells. Conversely, 7KLCA treatment reversed these effects by restoring the proliferative capacity, suppressing apoptosis, and increasing the expression of both LYZ and SOX9. In contrast, CDCA did not exhibit such rescue effects (Fig. 6G–J). Strikingly, 7KLCA treatment markedly attenuated the body weight loss and prevented the SI length shortening induced by DSS+Rab (Fig. 7B and C). Histological analysis further demonstrated that 7KLCA significantly ameliorated the intestinal damage induced by DSS+Rab and promoted crypt cell proliferation (Fig. 7D and E). In the radiation injury model, supplementation with 7KLCA significantly reduced the mortality induced by IR+Rab and strikingly improved the histological score of SI (Supplementary figures 5A-4C). Research has shown that 7KLCA, a secondary bile acid, is an antagonist of FXR and participates in the regulation of hepatic synthesis of bile acids [43, 44]. Similarly, we gavaged the DSS combined with Rab-treated mice for 4 weeks with glycine-β-muricholic acid (Gly-β-MCA; Fig. 7A), an intestine-specific FXR antagonist. We found that weight loss in mice treated with DSS+Rab+Gly-β-MCA was comparable to those with DSS+Rab+7KLCA and significantly lower than that of those with DSS+Rab (Fig. 7B), and their SI length was also longer than that of those with DSS+Rab (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, similar to 7-KLCA, Gly-β-MCA improved small intestinal damage, restored crypt cell proliferation, and rescued stem cell self-renewal and differentiation (Fig. 7D–I). Mechanistically, the restorative effects of 7KLCA or Gly-β-MCA correlate with their suppression of the aberrantly activated FXR-FGF15 signaling axis in the ileum (Fig. 7J). This was accompanied by a corresponding decrease in circulating FGF15 level, which in turn alleviated the negative feedback on bile acid synthesis, leading to upregulated expression of key synthetic enzymes Cyp7a1 and Cyp8b1, and ultimately rebalancing bile acid homeostasis (Fig. 7K and L) [45]. Collectively, these cascading events indicate that administration of the FXR antagonists 7KLCA or Gly-β-MCA restores intestinal homeostasis, thereby rescuing the self-renewal and niche microenvironment of ISCs.

Discussion

PPI-related intestinal damage is of growing concern. Recent clinical evidence shows that PPIs may increase the risks of intestinal disorders, such as infections and IBD [46]. Notably, PPIs may also retard remission on biologic therapy in CD patients [24]. However, the mechanism by which PPI causes or aggravates intestinal damage is still unclear. To address this, we utilized mouse models combining long-term PPI exposure with intestinal injury induced by DSS or IR [31–34]. We provide the first evidence that Rab reduces the abundance of Faecalibaculum rodentium and its bile acid metabolite 7KLCA. This reduction impairs the self-renewal of ISCs and the defensive function of PCs, thereby hindering intestinal repair. Notably, supplementation with 7KLCA could improve the regeneration of ISCs and PCs-related niche homeostasis by activating the intestinal Wnt signaling pathway, effectively ameliorating PPI-induced impairment of intestinal mucosal repair.

While the association between PPI use and gut dysbiosis is recognized, the mechanisms driving this microbiota-host disruption require further elucidation. Consistent with previous clinical observations with fecal samples [47], our data demonstrated that Rab significantly increased Streptococcus and Lactobacillus abundance while reducing Bifidobacterium levels in the SI. Notably, Rab markedly depleted Faecalibaculum rodentium in the SI, irrespective of the concurrent administration of DSS. F. rodentium belongs to the family Erysipelotrichaceae and primarily colonizes the murine intestine. Its human intestinal bacterial homologue is Holdemanella biformis [48]. Pairwise analyses based on identical twins showed that the intestinal Erysipelotrichaceae was significantly negatively correlated with PPI exposure [49]. This suggests that Erysipelotrichaceae, especially F. rodentium, is strongly associated with PPI exposure. We subsequently investigated whether F. rodentium supplementation could rescue the PPI-induced impairment of ISCs-mediated intestinal repair. Oral gavage of F. rodentium indeed protected ISCs and promoted intestinal repair.

Emerging evidence suggests that microbial bile acid metabolites modulate the proliferation of ISCs [44]. In our study, metabolomic analyses of SI contents revealed that Rab disrupted intestinal bile acid homeostasis. Specifically, Rab exposure significantly reduced the ileal levels of multiple bile acids, with 7KLCA exhibiting the most pronounced depletion. Further analysis revealed a strong correlation (r = 0.98) between Rab-induced ileal F. rodentium depletion and reduced 7KLCA levels. Although this near-perfect correlation must be contextualized within the limited sample size (n = 8) and is likely driven by the robust bimodal suppression of both the microbe and metabolite, these findings prompted us to hypothesize whether 7KLCA is a bile acid metabolite of F. rodentium that mediates microbiota-host interactions. Previous studies have established that the hdhA-encoded 7α-HSDH transforms CDCA to 7KLCA [42]. Notably, our in vitro experiments showed that CDCA treatment remarkably increased 7KLCA levels in the F. rodentium culture supernatant. Additionally, we identified the potential homologous protein in the F. rodentium genome that encodes 7α-HSDH function. These observations align with our in vivo findings that Rab decreases ileal F. rodentium abundance. Together, they strongly indicate that Rab suppresses 7KLCA production by reducing F. rodentium-mediated synthesis. Consequently, F. rodentium exerts a rescue effect by restoring 7KLCA levels. A recent study showed that F. rodentium promotes epithelial turnover and stem cell proliferation via reducing retinoic acid required for eosinophil production by duodenal epithelial cells [50]. Thus, F. rodentium seems to be a key species that regulates intestinal homeostasis through various pathways, including metabolism and immunity.

Next, we tested the effect of oral 7KLCA supplementation. 7KLCA rescued stem cell self-renewal capacity and intestinal repair. This aligns with a recent report that microbiota-derived 7KLCA promotes mucosal repair [44]. Our in vitro experiments utilizing SI-derived organoids and MODE-K cells further validated that 7KLCA activates the Wnt signaling pathway and restores organoid growth. Previous studies demonstrate that FXR activation inhibits Wnt signaling, thereby regulating tumor progression in colorectal and hepatocellular carcinomas [29, 51–54]. Furthermore, 7KLCA has been identified as an FXR antagonist [44]. We observed a similar protective effect on stem cell self-renewal and intestinal repair using Gly-β-MCA, an intestine-specific FXR antagonist, known to improve metabolic dysfunction [55]. Therefore, the rescue effect of 7KLCA may partially rely on its FXR antagonism. Furthermore, 7KLCA treatment rescued the Rab-activated FXR-FGF15 signaling pathway in the ileum of DSS-pretreated mice and elevated hepatic bile acid synthase expression. These data indicated that 7KLCA not only improves stem cell function by recovering the Rab-induced downregulation of the Wnt pathway, but also contributes to restoring intestinal bile acid homeostasis by inhibiting ileal FXR-FGF15 signaling axis, a negative modulator of hepatic bile acid synthesis [45].

Recent epidemiological studies have reported that the use of PPI is associated with an increased risk of IBD [46] and significantly reduces the response to infliximab in CD patients [24]. Our study shows that long-term PPI exposure specifically compromises ileal ISCs and PCs function, with minimal effect on the colon. Although our findings, together with previous studies [56], demonstrate that long-term PPI exposure did not overtly alter body weight or absolute food intake during the experimental window, the resulting localized villous atrophy significantly impairs the structural integrity of the absorptive mucosa, which may ultimately suppress digestive capabilities and reduce effective energy intake over time. Furthermore, this specific disruption of the ileal niche directly mirrors the Paneth cell-specific α-defensin deficiency seen in ileal CD, implying that PPIs could worsen infliximab non-response by aggravating their pre-existing ileal PCs dysfunction [57–59]. Moreover, Rab treatment significantly reduced ileal α-defensin levels in untreated mice, suggesting that PPIs may disrupt the function of PCs, potentially predisposing individuals to ileal-predominant IBD. Therefore, targeting the gut microbiota-bile acid axis, such as through specific microbial supplementation or FXR modulation, may represent a promising adjunct therapeutic strategy to restore mucosal homeostasis and optimize biologic efficacy in these patients.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study establishes that chronic PPI exposure disrupts the microbiota-7KLCA-Wnt axis, thereby impairing ISC function and repair. Specifically, PPI reduces Faecalibaculum rodentium abundance, leading to decreased 7KLCA production. This metabolite depletion aberrantly activates the FXR-FGF15 signaling pathway, which subsequently inhibits hepatic bile acid synthesis. Concurrently, this enhanced FXR activation ISC self-renewal and niche homeostasis by suppressing Wnt signaling and antimicrobial defenses. Importantly, 7KLCA supplementation improves stem cell self-renewal and restores bile acid pool homeostasis. Overall, this study uncovers a pivotal microbiome-derived metabolite that mediates PPI-induced disruption of stem cell function, providing potential therapeutic approaches for PPI-associated intestinal damage.
