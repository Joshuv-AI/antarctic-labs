The application of welfare technology in preventing solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child: A qualitative study based on the experiences of home help service providers
Authors: Jixiang Peng, Khadijah Alavi, Azahah Abu Hassan Shaari, Jing Li, Lulu Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrail older adults who have lost their only child face elevated risks of solitary death due to the combined impacts of physical vulnerability and lack of family support. Welfare technology has been promoted to enhance safety in home-based care, yet little is known about its practical role and challenges from the perspective of community service providers.AimTo explore home help service providers' perceptions of welfare technology in preventing solitary death and the challenges in its implementation.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological design was employed. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 16 community workers and caregivers and one focus group with 7 volunteers across two urban communities in Chongqing, China. Data were transcribed verbatim and analysed using thematic analysis.FindingsThree themes were identified: (i) welfare technology enhanced safety and reassurance through more timely risk monitoring and response; (ii) practical difficulties limited sustained use, including operational complexity, low acceptance among older users, technical instability, and added workload for providers; (iii) welfare technology could not fulfil emotional support needs or replace relational aspects of care, particularly for those lacking family support.ConclusionWelfare technology can support physical safety in home care for frail older adults who have lost their only child. However, its effectiveness is constrained by usability issues and its inability to address emotional and relational dimensions of care. Integrating technological solutions with person-centred relational care may be critical for improving overall well-being in this vulnerable group.Keywords: Welfare technology, Frail older adults, Solitary death, Home help services, Community care, Relational support

Background

China is experiencing rapid population ageing, and safety risks associated with ageing in place have become increasingly visible. Between 1979 and 2016, China implemented the one-child policy, which resulted in a large number of one-child families. Some of these families later became Shidu families (In Chinese) after the death of their only child due to illness or accident [1], [2]. As these parents grow older, they often face a sudden and substantial gap in care provision [3], [4]. Within this population, frailty is particularly prevalent. Frailty reflects the cumulative decline of multiple physiological systems and makes older adults highly vulnerable to even minor stressors，such as a common cold, a fall, or emotional disturbance，which may lead to marked functional deterioration [5], [6]. For frail older adults who have lost their only child, the breakdown of family support combined with physical vulnerability creates a structural risk in which acute incidents may remain unnoticed when they live alone. As a result, their risk of solitary death is significantly higher than that of the general older adult population [7].

In the global response to population ageing, welfare technology has been widely regarded as an important means of enhancing safety in home-based care. Such technologies include emergency call systems, fall detection devices, telemedicine, and wearable equipment [8], [9]. Existing studies suggest that these technologies can improve the timeliness of risk alerts, strengthen older adults' sense of autonomy and security [10], [11], and optimise care processes while improving service efficiency [12]. At the same time, however, the practical use of welfare technology is constrained by multiple factors, including operational complexity, equipment malfunction, reliance on network infrastructure, learning burden, and concerns about privacy [13], [14], [15]. For frail older adults with physical and cognitive limitations, these barriers are often more pronounced. Their effective use of technology frequently depends on explanations, hands-on assistance, and emotional support from service providers [16], [17]. This body of literature suggests that the benefits of welfare technology do not automatically materialise through device provision alone, but rather depend on technological suitability and the level of support available within the community care environment.

Although prior research has examined the functions and ethical implications of welfare technology from the perspectives of older adults or family caregivers, relatively little attention has been paid to the experiences and judgements of different types of service providers, such as community workers, family caregivers, and volunteers, in the process of technology use. This gap is particularly evident for frail older adults who have lost their only child, a high-risk group with limited family support. In this context, service providers often assume key responsibilities, including device installation, monitoring and feedback, risk identification, and emotional companionship, yet empirical evidence on their experiences remains scarce. To date, the literature provides limited insight into how welfare technology contributes to the prevention of solitary death, as well as into the practical difficulties faced by service providers in the use and dissemination of these technologies.

Against this backdrop, the present study focuses on three groups of home help service providers, community workers, caregivers, and volunteers, to explore the role of welfare technology in preventing solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child, as well as the limitations encountered in its use. Specifically, the study addresses two questions: (i) how do service providers understand the role of welfare technology in reducing the risk of solitary death, and (ii) what practical challenges do they face in the processes of use, maintenance, and dissemination? By focusing on the perspectives of home help service providers, this study extends current discussions on welfare technology and solitary death prevention. Their experiences offer a valuable lens through which to understand how technology is introduced, supported, and sustained within community care settings. By examining both the perceived benefits and the challenges of implementation, the study contributes to a more grounded understanding of the role of welfare technology in supporting frail older adults who have lost their only child and informs efforts to strengthen community-based responses to care risk and social isolation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a descriptive phenomenological approach, aiming to understand how service providers perceive, use, and interpret welfare technology in the prevention of solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child, drawing on participants' first-hand experiences [18], [19], [20]. Descriptive phenomenology places emphasis on careful description of lived experience, which is well suited to illuminating how service providers make sense of technology use and translate it into everyday care practices within specific care contexts.

The study participants were home help service providers working in community-based settings, including community workers, family caregivers, and volunteers. To capture both shared and role-specific experiences of welfare technology use, a mixed interview strategy was employed. Community workers (n = 6) and family caregivers (n = 10) participated in semi-structured individual interviews lasting approximately 45–60 min. Volunteers (n = 7) took part in one focus group discussion lasting around 90 min. The focus group format facilitated interaction and collective reflection, allowing volunteers to elaborate their experiences through shared discussion and mutual comparison [21].

The study was conducted in two urban communities in Chongqing, China. Both communities operate community-based elderly care service centres and have stable volunteer teams. A range of welfare technologies had already been integrated into routine home-based services, including smart wristbands, monitoring devices, emergency call systems, smoke detectors, and fall detection equipment.

Data collection procedures

Inclusion criteria

Purposive sampling was used to ensure that participants were able to provide information-rich accounts of everyday home help service delivery [22], [23]. All participants were recruited from the two selected communities and were directly involved in daily assistance and safety monitoring for frail older adults who have lost their only child, making them key actors within the community-based home help service system.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:(i)Aged 18 years or older;(ii)At least six months of experience in community-based home help, caregiving, or volunteer services; (iii)Direct experience in providing services to frail older adults who have lost their only child; (iv)Exposure to or use of at least one form of welfare technology in practice (e.g., smart wristbands, emergency call systems, monitoring devices, or smoke detectors); (v)Ability to communicate in Mandarin or the Chongqing dialect. Based on these criteria, the researcher worked collaboratively with community leaders and relevant organisations to identify and preliminarily screen eligible participants.

Respondent recruitment

Participant recruitment was carried out in the two urban communities in Chongqing. The researcher first contacted community leaders to introduce the study aims, interview content, and ethical arrangements, and obtained institutional support and permission. Community leaders then recommended potential participants who met the inclusion criteria. Three groups of service providers were invited to participate: community workers, family caregivers, and volunteers. These groups undertake distinct yet complementary roles within the home-based care system, including care coordination, daily assistance, and emotional companionship. Their combined perspectives offered a multi-layered understanding of the application and challenges of welfare technology in preventing solitary death.

When approaching each potential participant, the researcher provided detailed information about the study purpose, interview format, audio recording procedures, and confidentiality measures. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection, after which interview times and locations were arranged through mutual agreement. In total, 23 participants met the inclusion criteria and completed the study, including six community workers, ten family caregivers, and seven volunteers.

Data collection process

Data collection took place between May and July 2025. Sixteen individual interviews were conducted with community workers and family caregivers, with interview durations ranging from 27 to 62 min. The volunteer focus group discussion lasted approximately 79 min. The interview guide focused on participants' experiences of using or observing welfare technology in home-based services, with particular attention to its perceived role in preventing solitary death, practical difficulties and limitations in use, and tensions between technological support and emotional care. All interviews were conducted in quiet and familiar settings, such as community activity rooms or service centre offices, to enable participants to speak freely about their experiences. With participants' permission, all interviews and the focus group discussion were audio-recorded. Following each session, the researcher wrote brief field notes to capture contextual observations and non-verbal cues. Preliminary comparative analysis was conducted alongside data collection. When subsequent interviews no longer generated new information or themes, data saturation was considered to have been reached [24]. Participants' characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Data processing and analysis

All individual interviews and the focus group discussion were audio-recorded with participants' informed consent and transcribed verbatim by the researcher. The transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data and to gain an overall sense of the material. Initial impressions and reflexive notes were recorded to capture salient meanings and contextual information.

Consistent with a descriptive phenomenological orientation, the analysis focused on how home help service providers experienced and understood the role and limitations of welfare technology in preventing solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child. An inductive thematic analysis was conducted, informed by the analytic steps outlined by Braun and Clarke [25]. First, meaning units were identified through close reading of the transcripts and coded inductively. Codes with conceptual similarity were then grouped into potential themes, which were repeatedly compared against the original data to ensure that they accurately reflected participants' accounts. Throughout the analytic process, members of the research team discussed coding differences and thematic boundaries, and the thematic structure was revised iteratively to enhance credibility and analytic coherence. The final themes captured shared experiences related to perceived safety, barriers to technology use, and the absence of emotional responsiveness in technology-mediated care. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the National University of Malaysia (Approval No. JEP-2025-143). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. Prior to data collection, all participants were fully informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, as well as their rights, including the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants also consented to the audio recording of interviews and focus group discussions. All data were anonymized, and strict measures were taken to ensure confidentiality and privacy. The data were used solely for academic research purposes.

Findings

Across interviews, participants consistently noted that a range of welfare technologies had been gradually introduced in their communities in recent years to enhance home-based safety for frail older adults who have lost their only child. These technologies included smart wristbands, monitoring devices, emergency call systems, smoke detectors, and door and window sensors. In addition, some communities had adopted internet-plus elder care platforms to coordinate services such as home nursing, meal delivery, and domestic assistance. Service providers generally considered these technologies to have improved service efficiency to some extent and to have contributed to reducing potential safety risks for older adults living alone. At the same time, participants emphasised that welfare technology did not always function smoothly in everyday practice. Common challenges included limited digital literacy among older adults, difficulties with device maintenance, unstable data transmission, and the inability of technology to respond to emotional needs.

Based on the analysis of interview data from 23 home help service providers, three main themes were identified, experiencing safety and reassurance in technology-assisted care, Negotiating welfare technology in everyday care practice, and Emotional needs beyond technology-assisted care. Theme 1: reassurance through technology

Home help service providers generally viewed welfare technology as playing a supportive role in preventing solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child, particularly in relation to safety monitoring, risk alerts, and emergency response. These technologies were described as offering practical support within home-based care rather than functioning as independent solutions. Several participants noted that emergency call systems, fall detection devices, smoke detectors, and wearable equipment enabled service providers to gain a basic understanding of older adults' safety status during periods when no in-person visits were taking place. In this sense, technology was perceived as a continuous auxiliary resource that helped reduce the likelihood of incidents remaining unnoticed over time.

“Previously, we mainly relied on regular home visits. Now, with these devices, we can at least know straight away if something abnormal happens.”

(CW1)

In responding to sudden incidents, welfare technology was also regarded as having particular value. Participants pointed out that many frail older adults who have lost their only child live alone on a long-term basis and often lack immediate channels for seeking help in the event of a fall or acute illness. Technology was seen as partially addressing this gap by enabling earlier awareness and response.

“If the system indicates that an older person may have fallen, we will contact them or go to check immediately.”

(V2)

In addition, welfare technology was described as enhancing the manageability of daily service work. Compared with relying solely on manual visits, technological tools were perceived as providing ongoing reference points that helped clarify responsibilities and support service planning.

“For us, these devices are more like an additional way of observing things, which makes our work feel more secure.”

(CW4)

Some service providers also observed that welfare technology brought a degree of psychological comfort to certain frail older adults who have lost their only child and, in some cases, to their family members. Even when older adults did not fully understand the technical details, the awareness that “something would be noticed if a problem occurred” contributed to a sense of reassurance in daily life.

“Some older adults say that after the devices are installed, they feel more at ease when sleeping at night.”

(V3)

Overall, in service providers' accounts, the role of welfare technology in preventing solitary death was primarily understood as reducing uncertainty around whether sudden risks would go unnoticed, thereby offering a form of ongoing safety support within solitary living arrangements.

Theme 2: negotiating technology

Although most participants acknowledged the supportive role of welfare technology in safety protection, they repeatedly highlighted a range of practical challenges encountered during implementation and everyday use. These challenges were seen as shaping, and at times constraining, the effectiveness of technology in practice. One frequently mentioned issue concerned device instability and network-related problems. Service providers reported that when systems generated alerts, it was often unclear whether these reflected equipment malfunction or temporary network delays, necessitating additional home visits for verification.

“Sometimes the system gives an alert, but we can't tell whether it's a device problem or a network issue, so we have to go and check in person.”

(CW5)

Another challenge involved resistance or distrust among some frail older adults who have lost their only child. Participants described instances in which older adults felt uncomfortable with monitoring devices or wearable equipment, perceiving them as intrusive or as a form of surveillance.

“Some older adults feel that installing these devices means they are being watched, and they are not very willing to accept them.”

(V2; CW2)

The tension between technological complexity and age-related functional decline was also commonly discussed. Difficulties such as memory loss, reduced vision or hearing, and impaired manual dexterity made it hard for some older adults to operate devices independently.

“Some older adults use them quite well at the beginning, but later they find them troublesome and gradually stop using them.”

(V-FG)

In addition, service providers pointed out that devices require regular charging, stable internet connections, and ongoing maintenance. For frail older adults who have lost their only child and lack family support, these requirements often proved challenging, resulting in increased reliance on service staff.

“When something goes wrong with the equipment, it usually means we have to go back again and again to fix it.”

(V1)

Participants' accounts reflect that the role of welfare technology in preventing solitary death is closely shaped by everyday care contexts and support conditions, rather than determined by the technology itself.

Theme 3: human connection beyond technology

While welfare technology was widely regarded as helpful for safety monitoring, its limitations in addressing emotional needs were clearly articulated by most participants. Service providers emphasised that although technology can assist with monitoring and reminders, it cannot offer emotional companionship or psychological comfort. Several participants noted that some frail older adults who have lost their only child continued to experience loneliness even when multiple devices were in place, particularly in the absence of regular family contact.

“The device reminds him what to do, but it doesn't talk to him.”

(CW4)

Some service providers shared observations of older adults attempting to interact verbally with smart devices, which they interpreted as expressions of unmet emotional needs.

“One older woman would talk to the smart speaker, just because she wanted someone to chat with.”

(V3)

Participants also pointed out that, following the introduction of welfare technology, certain interactions that previously occurred through home visits were partially replaced by technical monitoring, leading to fewer opportunities for face-to-face communication.

“You can see the data in the system, but you can't tell whether she is in a good or bad mood.”

(V-FG)

According to service providers, emotional changes and psychological distress are difficult to capture through devices, yet these are often the aspects of greatest concern for frail older adults who have lost their only child.

“Sometimes what an older person needs are a few words of comfort, not another data point.”

(CW5)

Participants' experiences highlight that while welfare technology can support safety monitoring, it cannot replace the relational and emotional dimensions of care that remain central to preventing isolation and vulnerability in later life.

Discussions

Drawing on the lived experiences of home help service providers, this study offers an in-depth examination of the roles and limitations of welfare technology in preventing solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child. The findings suggest that while welfare technology can contribute positively to safety protection, notable shortcomings remain in terms of usability, maintenance systems, acceptance, and the provision of emotional support.

Reconsidering reassurance in home-based care

Previous studies have widely suggested that welfare technology can enhance older adults' sense of safety through risk monitoring and emergency response mechanisms [10], [26], [27]. Building on this literature, the present study indicates that, within the care context of frail older adults who have lost their only child, reassurance does not arise directly from technological functions alone. Rather, it is gradually formed through the ongoing verification of technology as reliable and through the coordinated support provided by service personnel.

From a care practice perspective, welfare technology appears to translate into stable safety support only when combined with service providers' professional judgement, routine follow-up, and context-sensitive use. This finding resonates with Pajalic et al.'s [15] and Cuesta et al.'s [28] argument that a sense of safety is socially constructed, while further highlighting that, in the absence of family support, service providers play a particularly central role in shaping reassurance. For frail older adults who have lost their only child, the possibility of “being noticed in time” itself constitutes a crucial form of psychological support, one that depends on the joint operation of technological systems and human care [10], [29].

In addition, this study suggests that the perceived reduction in service providers' work burden may help explain how reassurance is strengthened. When risk identification and information recording become clearer and more manageable, uncertainty in care-related decision-making is reduced, thereby supporting more stable care relationships. This perspective adds nuance to existing research that has primarily focused on older adults' subjective feelings, and it broadens understanding of how welfare technology may indirectly shape care interactions.

The structural gap between being able to use and being willing to use welfare technology

Although welfare technology is widely regarded as an important tool for reducing risks in home-based care [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], the present findings show that its practical effects are constrained by multiple structural factors. Operational complexity, dependence on maintenance, network instability, and psychological resistance to monitoring may all undermine sustained use. These challenges are consistent with previous studies highlighting how limited technological fit affects willingness to use technology [13], [16]. Among frail older adults who have lost their only child, this gap between “being able to use” and “being willing to use” technology appears particularly pronounced. Age-related physical and cognitive decline, combined with the absence of support from adult children, raises barriers to learning, daily operation, and problem-solving. At the same time, emotional vulnerability and heightened safety-related anxiety may amplify perceptions of technological risk, making some older adults more likely to resist or misunderstand technological devices. Such contextual vulnerability has received limited attention in previous research focusing on general older adult populations.

In contrast to studies such as Thunberg et al. [35], which primarily examine technological stress among healthcare professionals, the Chinese community context examined in this study reveals that frontline service providers often assume multiple roles, including device operation, maintenance, and explanation. This expansion of responsibilities suggests that the introduction of welfare technology is accompanied by an increase in hidden workloads, while corresponding institutional support remains insufficient. In line with discussions by Choi et al. [7] and Zander et al. [36] concerning privacy, autonomy, and capability, the present study emphasises that the sustainable use of welfare technology among frail older adults who have lost their only child requires the parallel development of technological adaptation, capability support, and emotional accompaniment, rather than reliance on device provision alone.

The irreplaceable human warmth in care

This study further demonstrates that, despite improvements in monitoring efficiency and risk responsiveness, welfare technology cannot replace the emotional support provided through interpersonal interaction. For frail older adults who have lost their only child, who already experience limited family support, the absence of this emotional dimension is particularly salient, and their reliance on emotional connections with communities and service providers is correspondingly greater. These findings are consistent with arguments by Pajalic et al. [15] and Glomsås et al. [8] that a sense of safety is grounded not only in technological functionality but also in relationships and interaction. At the same time, the present study suggests that an overreliance on technological monitoring may inadvertently reduce opportunities for face-to-face contact, gradually shifting care practices from “seeing the person” to “seeing the data.” In the context of losing an only child, such a shift may further intensify older adults' experiences of loneliness [27], [37].

Accordingly, this study argues that welfare technology should be understood as a supportive tool within interpersonal care, rather than as a substitute for it. Genuine reassurance arises not only from risks being monitored and events being recorded, but also from sustained attention, understanding, and responsiveness. For frail older adults who have lost their only child, the experience of being cared for emotionally is as important as being protected physically, an essential dimension of care that welfare technology alone cannot fulfil.

Policy and practice implications

The findings indicate that the effectiveness of welfare technology in home-based care for frail older adults who have lost their only child depends on whether it is meaningfully embedded within community care contexts. Technology provision alone is unlikely to generate stable experiences of safety and reassurance. Community-based, integrated service models, linking device installation, user guidance, ongoing follow-up, and routine care, are more likely to foster sustained trust and continuity of care. From a practice perspective, welfare technology should therefore be implemented as part of integrated community care platforms rather than as isolated technical initiatives. This study also shows that for highly vulnerable groups such as frail older adults who have lost their only child, technological solutions alone are insufficient to address emotional and social needs. In the absence of family support, emotional care and psychological comfort constitute essential elements of quality home-based care, rather than optional additions. Community care practices should thus incorporate stable arrangements for interpersonal companionship and emotional support alongside technological interventions, in order to reduce the risk of emotional absence.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The sample was drawn from urban communities in Chongqing, China. Community service systems, the availability of welfare technology, and care resources may vary across regions, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other settings. Future research could undertake comparative studies across different regions or between urban and rural contexts to further examine the applicability of the conclusions. Additionally, this study primarily reflects the perspectives of home help service providers, focusing on their experiences and judgements in the use and promotion of welfare technology. The subjective experiences and emotional responses of frail older adults who have lost their only child were not systematically incorporated. Given the differences in roles and priorities between service providers and older adults, their understandings of the value and limitations of welfare technology may not fully align. Future studies could adopt a multi-perspective design that integrates the views of older adults, service providers, and relevant family members to achieve a more comprehensive understanding.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important empirical insights into how welfare technology operates in practice among highly vulnerable older populations. It also lays a foundation for further exploration of how technological support and relational care can be developed in a coordinated manner at the community level.

Conclusions

This study explored the role and limitations of welfare technology in preventing solitary death among frail older adults who have lost their only child, drawing on the experiences of home help service providers. The findings suggest that welfare technology contributes to safety monitoring, risk alerts, and emergency response, supporting both older adults' safety and service providers' everyday care work. However, its practical use is constrained by challenges related to usability, maintenance, acceptance, and the limited capacity of technology to address emotional needs, particularly within this highly vulnerable group.

Overall, the value of welfare technology lies primarily in supporting physical safety rather than replacing relational care. For frail older adults who have lost their only child, a sense of safety is shaped not only by technological monitoring but also by sustained interpersonal attention and companionship from service providers, such as, community workers, their caregivers and volunteers. Welfare technology should therefore be understood as a complementary element within community-based home care systems, whose effectiveness depends on its integration with relational care practices that uphold older adults' dignity, emotional well-being, and quality of life.
