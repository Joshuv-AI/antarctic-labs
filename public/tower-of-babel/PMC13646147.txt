Effect of stress inoculation on reducing performance anxiety among physical education students

Rawan Abdul Mahdi Neyef Al-Saliti, Hamed Mohammed Ali Doum, Mohammad A. Tashtoush, Alaaeldin Ahmed Hamid, Sultan Mubarak Almughyirah, Abdelrahim Fathy Ismail

Abstract

ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of stress inoculation on reducing performance anxiety among university students enrolled in Physical Education.MethodologyThe study sample consisted of 30 s-year university students enrolled in the Physical Education program at Ajloun National University in Jordan. A quasi-experimental design was used to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention program. Pre- and post-tests were administered to both the experimental and control groups, followed by a follow-up assessment. A performance anxiety scale, specifically developed for this study, was used for the assessments. The stress inoculation program was designed to provide students with cognitive and behavioral coping skills through three main stages: preparation and psychoeducation, acquisition and practice of coping skills, and application of these skills to performance-related situations.ResultsThe results showed that the designed program was effective in reducing performance anxiety among students in the experimental group compared with the control group. The results also indicated no statistically significant differences in the experimental group's performance anxiety scores between the post-test and follow-up assessments, suggesting that the reduction in anxiety was maintained during the follow-up period.ConclusionThe findings suggest that stress inoculation is an effective method for reducing performance anxiety among university students enrolled in Physical Education and may help them cope more effectively with performance-related pressures.

Introduction

In the highly competitive world of sports, performance is a fundamental and indispensable element. It serves as a crucial criterion for judging an athlete's competence and progress (–). As competition intensifies and the spotlight shifts to achievement, performance anxiety emerges as a hidden adversary that accompanies athletes and can limit their ability to realize their full potential (). A range of psychological factors intertwine with performance anxiety, most notably perceived training patterns. These training patterns shape behaviors and experiences within the sporting environment. Therefore, they may have a positive or negative impact on his performance level depending on the nature of this interaction (), whereas anxiety constitutes an internal state that affects the athlete's cognitive and emotional responses (–). Performance anxiety is a common psychological phenomenon in sports. It appears in athletes at all levels, especially in situations requiring evaluation or direct competition (, ). This anxiety manifests as a state of tension and excessive preoccupation linked to the fear of failure or negative evaluation. It directly affects concentration, decision-making, and the ability to execute skills efficiently (). Therefore, this concept has received considerable attention in the sports psychology literature due to its close connection to performance quality and achievement ().

Among university students majoring in physical education, performance anxiety may emerge from the specific demands of practical physical education courses and the evaluative nature of motor performance. Students are frequently required to demonstrate physical and motor skills in front of university instructors and peers, which may increase concerns about making mistakes, receiving negative evaluation, or failing to meet expected performance standards. Research involving university physical education students has demonstrated that cognitive and somatic anxiety are important dimensions of performance-related anxiety and are associated with emotional and psychological factors relevant to sporting performance (). Such evaluative situations may contribute to somatic anxiety through physiological arousal, including increased heart rate, muscle tension, sweating, and physical discomfort. At the cognitive level, students may experience worry about failure, negative expectations, and excessive concern about their performance, which may interfere with concentration and task execution. Evidence from sport and performance research indicates that cognitive and somatic anxiety are meaningful components of performance anxiety and may influence performance under pressure (, ). In addition, anxiety-related self-monitoring and concern about performance may interfere with attentional control and increase distractibility during practical activities. The difficulty and complexity of motor tasks may further contribute to anxiety when students perceive the required skills as exceeding their current abilities or when they have limited confidence in their capacity to perform them successfully. Moreover, the instructional and evaluative environment, including the nature of feedback provided by university instructors, opportunities for practice, perceived evaluation, and the social context of performing in front of peers, may shape students' emotional and cognitive responses (, ). Therefore, performance anxiety among university physical education students may result from the interaction of physiological arousal, performance-related cognitions, attentional difficulties, perceived task difficulty, self-confidence, and characteristics of the instructional and evaluative environment (, ).

Studies have focused on interpreting performance anxiety through its physiological and psychological dimensions, such as arousal levels, stress, and cognitive interference (, , ). These approaches have contributed to building the theoretical foundations for understanding the nature of anxiety and its effects. With the development of scientific research, performance anxiety is no longer viewed as a one-dimensional phenomenon. Rather, it is now understood within a multidimensional framework of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral aspects, such as emotion regulation, self-confidence levels, and the coping strategies athletes employ to control stress.

Therefore, performance anxiety is considered a negative factor affecting athletes' performance levels. It causes psychological stress, impaired concentration, and decreased efficiency during competitive situations (). Hence, the need arose to design specialized counseling programs aimed at reducing levels of this anxiety and helping athletes control their emotional responses. Stress inoculation is a modern and effective method in sports psychology counseling. It aims to equip athletes with psychological skills that enable them to cope with competitive pressures and minimize their negative effects. This, in turn, contributes to improved psychological adaptation and enhanced performance (). In this context, coping skills reflect the pivotal role of cognitive processes in determining an individual's response to different situations, especially under high levels of stress and anxiety. These skills maintain and improve performance levels during competition (). Stress inoculation is considered one of the most prominent applied methods in the sports field. This approach aims to train athletes to develop effective coping skills and manage competitive pressures in a structured and gradual manner (). It is based on a progressive principle of training coping skills first in low-stress situations, then transferring them to more stressful ones. This method enhances the athlete's ability to regulate their emotional and cognitive responses during competition (, ). Stress inoculation consists of three overlapping phases: preparation or education, skill acquisition, and application or transfer. The transition between these phases is integrated to ensure the consolidation of acquired skills (). Practical applications of this approach indicate its effectiveness in helping athletes who struggle to perform under pressure. Training them to use effective coping strategies in situations to simulate real-world competition would reduce their anxiety levels and improve their performance.

A literature review indicates a growing interest in performance anxiety among physical education students. However, most studies have focused on measuring anxiety levels or correlating them with other variables. At the same time, the use of stress desensitization programs as a direct therapeutic approach to reducing performance anxiety remains relatively limited. In addition, studies addressing this type of program have often been conducted within general anxiety frameworks, lacking sufficient specificity for performance anxiety among physical education students. Moreover, the experimental application of stress desensitization programs to this group remains limited, despite the nature of performance-related stressors among them. Therefore, the current study aims to bridge this gap by investigating the effectiveness of stress desensitization programs in reducing performance anxiety among physical education students.

This study aims to test the following two hypotheses:
Hypothesis 1: There are no statistically significant differences at (α=0.05) between the mean scores of the experimental group and the control group on the athletes' performance anxiety scale attributable to the training program.Hypothesis 2: There are no statistically significant differences at (α=0.05) between the mean scores of the experimental group in the post-test and follow-up on the athletes' performance anxiety scale attributable to the persistence of the training program's effect.

Methods

The current study utilized a quasi-experimental design to investigate the effect of stress inoculation on reducing performance anxiety among physical education students. The design included two groups: an experimental group that participated in the stress inoculation-based intervention program, and a control group that did not receive this intervention during the implementation period. Pre-and post-tests were administered to both the experimental and control groups to measure performance anxiety levels before and after the intervention. To achieve the study's objectives, a performance anxiety scale was used to assess the performance anxiety levels of the physical education students. Students in the experimental group participated in stress inoculation sessions. Pre-and post-tests verified the effectiveness of the intervention program.

Participants

A sample of 30 s-year students majoring in Physical Education at Ajloun National University in Jordan was selected using a performance anxiety scale. Students with high performance anxiety scores were deemed eligible to participate in the study, as this aligned with the intervention objectives. After obtaining informed consent from the participants, they were randomly assigned to two groups: an experimental group that underwent a stress inoculation program and a control group that received no intervention during the program's implementation. Each group consisted of 15 students. Data collection

Performance anxiety scale: The performance anxiety scale used in the study by () is a psychological tool used to measure athletic performance anxiety in individuals. It is based on a cognitive-affective theory model that posits anxiety as composed of several interrelated dimensions. The scale consisted of 15 items distributed across three dimensions. The first dimension, Somatic Symptoms, included items 1–5 and refers to the physiological symptoms associated with anxiety, such as rapid heartbeat, muscle tension, and stomach upset. The second dimension, Cognitive Anxiety, included items 6–10 and reflects negative thoughts and mental preoccupation related to performance, such as fear of failure or anxiety about negative outcomes. The third dimension, Distractibility, included items 11–15 and expresses difficulty concentrating on the task or distractibility during athletic performance.

Validity and reliability of the original scale: The Sports Anxiety Scale (SAS-2) exhibits high levels of validity and reliability. Construct validity was confirmed using confirmatory factor analysis. The results affirmed the suitability of the three-dimensional model, which comprises somatic anxiety, cognitive anxiety (preoccupation), and distractibility. The fit indices showed good values (CFI = 0.95–0.97, NNFI = 0.95–0.96, RMSEA = 0.05–0.065), indicating the scale's construct quality. The scale also demonstrated high internal reliability, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.73 to 0.86 for the subdimensions and 0.88 for the overall score. Furthermore, test-retest reliability was confirmed, with values ranging from 0.73 to 0.80, indicating the stability of the measurement over time. Therefore, the scale is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring multidimensional sports performance anxiety.

Data analysis

Appropriate non-parametric statistical methods were used to examine the effect of the training program on reducing performance anxiety among physical education students. Also, they verified the sustainability of its effect over time. In addition, suitable statistical tests were used to determine the significance of differences between the experimental and control groups in the post-test and to compare the experimental group's performance between the post-test and follow-up tests. To test the first hypothesis, the means, ranks, and standard deviations of the physical education students' performance anxiety scores in the post-test were calculated according to the group variable (experimental and control). Moreover, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to determine the significance of differences between the two groups. To test the second hypothesis, the means, ranks, and standard deviations of the experimental group's performance anxiety scores in the post-test and follow-up tests were calculated. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to determine the significance of differences between the two applications.

Intervention package: A counseling program based on stress inoculation therapy

A counseling program based on stress inoculation therapy was designed to reduce performance anxiety among physical education students. The design of the therapy program in this study was based on recent literature (–). The program was implemented with second-year students majoring in Physical Education at Ajloun National University who had high levels of performance anxiety. The intervention was conducted in the Psychological Counseling Laboratory, specifically in the Group Counseling Room, and was delivered by a faculty member with a PhD in Psychological Counseling, who was one of the researchers in the present study, is licensed by the Jordanian Ministry of Health to practice as a psychological counseling specialist, and is qualified and experienced in psychological counseling and in delivering structured counseling interventions.

The program provided a structured psychological intervention focused on developing cognitive and behavioral coping skills to help students deal with stressful sports situations more effectively. It included training in relaxation, cognitive restructuring, positive self-talk, visualization, and other stress-management techniques. The program also enhanced students' awareness of the relationship between thoughts, emotions, and performance. Particular emphasis was placed on modifying negative beliefs associated with fear of failure and concerns about being judged by others, and replacing them with more realistic and positive thoughts that foster self-confidence and support improved athletic performance.

The program consisted of 13 structured counseling sessions, conducted twice a week, with each session lasting approximately two hours (). The sessions followed a planned sequence of stress inoculation components, including preparation and identification of stressors, development and rehearsal of coping skills, and application of these skills in situations simulating the real sports environment, such as competitions and practical examinations. The sessions also emphasized managing physiological and emotional stress responses, reducing distractions, improving concentration, and restructuring negative cognitive and emotional experiences associated with performance anxiety. Through repeated practice and application, students were trained to use these coping skills when facing sports-related pressures, thereby strengthening their perceived competence and confidence and supporting more consistent and effective performance.

Discussion

The findings of the present study revealed statistically significant differences between the experimental and control groups in performance anxiety, with the differences favoring the experimental group that received the stress inoculation training program for athletes. These findings suggest that the program addressed multiple dimensions of performance anxiety, including somatic anxiety, cognitive anxiety, and distractibility, reflecting a comprehensive approach to managing anxiety in sports contexts.

At the cognitive level, the reduction in cognitive anxiety among members of the experimental group may be attributed to the program's emphasis on cognitive restructuring and coping skills. These components may have helped students reinterpret performance situations as manageable challenges rather than threatening situations, thereby reducing negative expectations, fear of failure, and excessive concerns about performance (). This finding is consistent with cognitive theories that emphasize that how an individual interprets a situation, rather than the situation itself, is a determining factor in the intensity of anxiety. Furthermore, the use of techniques such as cognitive restructuring may have reduced rumination and expectations of failure while replacing them with more realistic and positive thoughts ().

At the physiological level, the findings indicate a decrease in the level of somatic symptoms associated with performance anxiety among members of the experimental group. This finding demonstrates the effectiveness of the training program in reducing the physiological manifestations of anxiety. Stress inoculation relies on gradual and systematic exposure to stressors combined with training in relaxation techniques, such as deep breathing and muscle relaxation. These techniques may reduce physiological arousal associated with anxiety, including muscle tension and increased heart rate. Consequently, athletes may become better able to regulate their physiological responses during competition rather than allowing these responses to interfere with their performance (, ).

Regarding distractibility, the significant reduction observed in the experimental group may be explained by the training provided to help students direct their attention, remain focused on the present moment, and manage both internal distractions, such as negative thoughts, and external distractions. Anxiety often consumes a significant portion of cognitive capacity, which may interfere with focusing on the primary task. The present findings are consistent with processing efficiency models, which suggest that anxiety can reduce attentional efficiency and that psychological training may help improve the effective allocation of attentional resources ().

Overall, the findings can be interpreted in light of the program's gradual exposure to stressful situations while simultaneously equipping students with coping skills. This combination may have strengthened their ability to adapt to future stressors and enhanced their perceived control over stressful performance situations. In addition, the program may have contributed to enhancing students' self-efficacy, as mastering anxiety-management skills can provide a greater sense of control, which may reduce stress and increase self-confidence, an important factor in athletic performance

Conclusion

This study highlights the effectiveness of a stress-proofing program as a successful intervention for reducing performance anxiety among physical education students. The results showed a clear positive effect that persisted even after the program's completion. This persistence suggests the potential for incorporating this type of program into ongoing and effective psychological interventions to address performance anxiety.

Limitations

The limitations of this study lie in the generalizability of its findings, due to its quasi-experimental design, which may restrict complete control over all influencing variables. Furthermore, it did not address several other factors and variables associated with performance anxiety, such as prior experiences or individual differences among students, which may limit the comprehensive interpretation of the results.

Recommendations

- Future longitudinal studies are recommended to examine the sustainability of the effectiveness of the stress inoculation program in reducing performance anxiety over longer periods.
- The stress inoculation program is recommended to be implemented under real-world field conditions and across different physical education contexts to further examine its effectiveness and applicability.
- Future studies are recommended to use larger and more diverse samples to determine the generalizability of the program's findings to different groups of physical education students.
- Future research is recommended to examine the role of individual factors and students' previous experiences in their responses to stress inoculation programs.
