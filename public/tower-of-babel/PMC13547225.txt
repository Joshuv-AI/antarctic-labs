Exploring biohydrogen producing potential of Arctic ice and water through metagenomics and dark fermentation kinetics
Authors: Shwetakshi Mishra, Srikanth Mutnuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryospheric ecosystems in the high Arctic harbor largely unexplored microbiomes with significant biotechnological potential. The present study evaluates the biohydrogen production capabilities of the indigenous microbiome of Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard, using glacial ice and surface water samples. Dark fermentation batch assays were performed at 4 °C and 20 °C with 2-bromoethanesulfonate (BES), a methanogenic inhibitor, to track the succession of metabolic and taxonomic diversity. Metagenomic and functional analyses revealed that under 20 °C and BES conditions, psychrotolerant microbial communities maximize biohydrogen production to 85% of the total biogas produced, with an acetate-dominant fermentation pathway, as inferred from volatile fatty acid (VFA) analysis. This evolves into a highly coordinated system utilizing a coupled Rnf-nitrogenase route alongside Formate Hydrogenlyase and [FeFe]-hydrogenase pathways. Kinetic modelling using the Modified Gompertz equation, along with Q10 temperature-sensitivity indices, demonstrated a very high latent catalytic potential in these cold-adapted microbiomes. This study indicates that Arctic microbiomes are highly elastic thermodynamically and could serve as highly efficient, manipulatable biocatalysts for the environmental recovery of bioenergy through engineered low-temperature systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57926-z.Keywords: Biohydrogen, Arctic microbiome, Dark fermentation, 2-bromoethanesulfonate (BES), MetagenomicsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

The global trend toward sustainable energy transitions to address the rapidly declining state of climate health has propelled biofuels, specifically bio-hydrogen, into a critical alternative fuel source, given their environmentally friendly combustion properties1. Hydrogen has an unmatched gravimetric energy density (approximately 120 MJ/kg), which is approximately five times higher than coal, and produces only water vapor upon combustion, making it a “fuel of the future” for the heavy industrial, maritime shipping, and long-distance aviation sectors2. However, in the current industrial environment, hydrogen is produced by steam methane reforming or electrolysis, which require complex infrastructure, energy inputs, and high operational costs, yet remain carbon-positive3. On the other hand, utilizing biological processes such as photofermentation, dark fermentation, or biophotolysis to create biohydrogen can help support the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of the United Nations; in particular, SDGs 7 and 13, which refer to creating energy that is both affordably priced and environmentally responsible, as well as combating climate change4. Also, biohydrogen production can complement various national/international frameworks, including India’s National Green Hydrogen Mission, which targets the production of 5 million metric tons of biohydrogen by 2030 and aims to become a leading global exporter of hydrogen energy5. Furthermore, targeting dark fermentation for biohydrogen production, it is an anaerobic fermentation system that can be considered a decentralized, low-carbon solution that doesn’t require complex electrochemical systems, but only organic substrates and microbial consortia, and thus can also be clubbed with waste valorization strategies6.

Although producing biohydrogen is a very promising process, the amount that can be produced and the conditions required to produce hydrogen constrain its global production potential. High-yield hydrogen-producing bacteria such as Thermotoga maritima, Thermoanaerobacter tengcongensis, Clostridium butyricum, Enterobacter aerogenes, etc., mostly work in mesophilic and thermophilic environments and require a biochemical reactor temperature range of 35–60 °C to produce hydrogen7. This thermal requirement presents a formidable barrier for a large portion of the population living in cold-climate countries, where maintaining mesophilic growth conditions for biohydrogen production is not feasible, as such reactor operating requirements would result in a negative net energy process8. Thus, it would become an impediment to biohydrogen production plants for populations living in such cold areas covering around 10% of the global land area, including the Arctic, Canada, Scandinavia, and towns in and around the Himalayan region, among others. Therefore, there is a need to identify psychrophilic and psychro-tolerant indigenous microbial communities that have evolved metabolic efficiencies, such as generating cold-adapted FeFe and NiFe hydrogenases for hydrogen production at lower temperatures, thereby eliminating the high-temperature hurdle in setting up biohydrogen reactors. Furthermore, because cold-adapted methanogens naturally co-exist with these fermentative bacteria in cryobiotic environments and act as the primary biological sink for hydrogen, it is necessary to examine the combined effects of psychrophilic hydrogen-producing microbes and methanogens.

One such dynamic biome could be the ‘Arctic cryosphere’, with glacial ice, seasonal snowpacks, and meltwater streams, supporting a wide array of psychrophilic and psychrotolerant microbial communities that display unique physiological adaptations to sub-zero temperatures, limited nutrient availability, and exposure to strong ultraviolet radiation9. Furthermore, since the Arctic is experiencing Arctic amplification, which has accelerated heating by about 4 times the global average, it may be crucial to determine whether long-stored organic carbon released from receding glaciers and thawing permafrost can support hydrogen gas production in future biogeochemical feedback loops or will only produce potent greenhouse gases10.

Though it’s more likely that, in a natural scenario of mixed bacterial populations, methanogens might take over the hydrogen produced as an electron donor to form methane. Though much literature already exists for mesophilic and thermophilic bacteria, few empirical studies have addressed the production of hydrogen by psychrophiles and at low temperatures. For example, some research has shown successful dark fermentative production of hydrogen from psychrotolerant isolates, such as Klebsiella sp. and Polaromonas sp. obtained from Antarctic seawater and soil with impressive metabolisms compared to those produced by mesophiles11. Likewise, the possibility of using room temperatures and below to achieve dark fermentation through particular organic matter has proven psychro-bacteria to be suitable for biohydrogen production12.

However, when maintaining indigenous microbes in a controlled fermenter, various approaches to select hydrogen-producers and inhibit methanogens can be considered. For instance, exposure to chloroform or heat shock can enrich hydrogen-producing microbes. But they may offer a low degree of reproducibility as well as an indiscriminate elimination of the sensitive psychrophilic hydrogen-producing microbes. Contrary to non-directed approaches, the use of chemical agents like 2-bromoethanesulfonate (BES) makes it possible to isolate and characterise the top-performing microbial consortia for biohydrogen production. BES, being a structural analogue of coenzyme M, competitively inhibits the methanogenic pathway and thus may harness the biohydrogen production capacity of Arctic consortia13. But literature on whether crude indigenous microbial communities from the High Arctic can drive dark fermentation pathways to produce biohydrogen naturally or under BES enrichments is very limited. Thus, there also exists a significant knowledge gap in understanding the production kinetics of biohydrogen-producing microbial consortia and their metagenomic and functional profiling from the High Arctic.

BES may cause a significant rerouting of metabolic pathways towards biohydrogen production, which inevitably results in the formation of carbon dioxide under controlled conditions within a bioreactor system, but it successfully precludes the formation of methane, a much more potent greenhouse gas. This carbon dioxide, formed by dark fermentation, can be harnessed and incorporated within carbon capture and utilisation (CCU) systems. Furthermore, concerning the use of BES, although a one-off dosage may be quite effective for short-term experiments, long-term inhibition is difficult to achieve. Over time, certain syntrophic anaerobic bacteria may desulfonate BES, converting it into bromide, acetate, and sulfide. Ultimately, this biological degradation significantly reduces the efficacy of the compound as an inhibitor, thus making frequent dosages necessary for continuous operation. Nevertheless, BES action-based knowledge will be useful for future work aimed at isolating these hardy bacteria for use in biohydrogen co-cultures without relying on chemicals.

In this regard, the current study aims to provide a detailed assessment of the potential of microbial consortia collected from the ice and surface waters of Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard, to produce biohydrogen under crude and BES-enriched conditions at 4 °C and 20 °C, respectively. The nativity of this study lies in investigating uncultured cryobiological microbial consortia that occur exclusively in the High Arctic—a very underexplored biotope from the perspective of bioenergy research. The present study differs from previous studies in its use of natural indigenous microbial consortia, whereas previous studies had utilized either individual cryospheric strains or artificially designed cold-adapted microbial communities. This study aims to identify strong, cold-adapted microorganisms by examining their genomic signatures and metabolic outputs in experiments, with the primary goal to develop blueprints for new low-energy bioreactors that leverage the Arctic’s unique biological resources to support the global circular bioeconomy.

Materials and methods

Study area

The sampling was carried out during the June 2025 Summer Batch of the Indian Arctic Expedition to Ny- Ålesund, located on the Brøgger Peninsula in Spitsbergen of the Svalbard archipelago, as shown in Fig. 1a. The study area portrays Arctic polar ecosystem at high latitudes, where glacial meltwater from Kongsfjorden affects the microbially inhabited zones14. Microbial communities were documented in the area using 10 sampling locations: 5 for ice samples and 5 for water samples, as shown in Fig. 1b and listed in Table 1 with their respective coordinates. The surface water sampling was designed to capture the salinity gradient from glacial meltwater to coastal brackish water. While a few of the ice samples were collected near water sampling sites, this allows for comparison between two adjacent liquid and solid matrices. All the surface samples were collected using aseptic terrestrial sampling protocols to ensure the integrity of the indigenous microbial communities. Surface ice was collected by removing the top layer with a sterilized hand shovel and transferring the material to sterile, leakproof containers. Surface water samples were collected directly into pre-sterilized screw-capped bottles15. All activities adhered to the regulations mandated by the Governor of Svalbard and the Himadri Indian Arctic Station. To maintain community diversity and the function of individual microbial populations, including community structure and functional enzymes, all samples were quickly transported back to the field laboratory for storage at −20 °C, followed by transport in a cold chain (−20 °C) for experimentation.

Sample processing and physicochemical characterization

Once the samples arrived at the laboratory via − 20 °C transport, all crude ice and water samples were allowed to thaw slowly in the dark at 4 °C, maintaining their microbial and chemical integrity. These crude environmental samples, along with the Biohydrogen Potential (BHP) experimental samples at Day 0 (initial) and Day 30 (after BHP), were then subjected to extensive physicochemical analysis to establish an environmental baseline and to evaluate changes in microbial metabolism at the start and end of the dark fermentation process. The physicochemical parameters were performed as per protocols mentioned in Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater (APHA, 2017) and were inclusive of Total Solids (TS), Volatile Solids (VS), Ash, and Moisture content via gravimetry, electrochemical parameters (pH and electrical conductivity (EC)) using Oakton PC450 pH meter and a Contech conductivity meter, Carbon and nitrogen fractions in the form of Total Organic Carbon (TOC), Total Carbon (TC), Inorganic Carbon (IC), and Total Nitrogen (TN) using Shimadzu Liquid TOC Analyzer and spectrophotometry based nutrient profiling including ammonia and ortho-phosphates via Nessler and ascorbic acid method respectively. All measurements were performed against BHP endogenous blanks and/or distilled water to ensure baseline accuracy16.

Biohydrogen potential (BHP) experimental design

Using indigenous microbial consortia (i.e., 90% v/v matrix filling with thawed crude ice and water samples), batch assays of dark fermentation were conducted in triplicate in sterilized 125 ml serum bottles with a working volume of 90 ml to assess the biohydrogen production potential of the native microbial community in the samples. A mixed microbial consortium approach was deliberately chosen over pure culture isolation. Mixed consortia strategy ensures operational reliability, obviates the necessity for costly sterile procedures, and allows for syntrophy, which enables different metabolic communities to work together to break down substrates into biohydrogen, thereby making them very attractive from a practical standpoint.

The protocol was modified based on the method described by Ergal et al., (2022)17. Each of the serum bottles was provided 2% of macronutrient (NH4Cl, KH2PO4, MgCl2.6H2O and CaCl2. 2H2O at 26.6, 10, 6 and 3 g/L, respectively) and micronutrient solutions (FeCl2.4H2O at 2 mg/L, CoCl2.6H2O at 0.5 mg/L, MnCl2.4H2O and NiCl2.6H2O at 0.1 mg/L, ZnCl2, H3BO3, and Na2SeO3 at 50 mg/L, CuCl2.2H2O at 40 mg/L and Na2MoO4.2H2O at 10 mg/L) respectively. At the beginning of fermentation, pH and salinity were carefully adjusted to match those of the crude samples. However, adding macro- and micronutrients contributed to an approximate 0.1% increase in ionic strength. This raised the overall EC in freshwater and ice samples, but was needed to ensure that nutrients are always present in excess throughout the BHP setup.

The batch assay was then categorized into the BES-treated and the test group without BES. As is evident, the BES-treated group was enriched with 50 mM 2-bromoethanesulfonate (BES) to selectively inhibit methane producers, while the test group without BES was set up to assess the natural potential of microbes to produce biohydrogen without external chemical means. In both groups, 1% glucose was added as the sole carbon source for fermentation. Subsequently, substrate-negative endogenous control groups with and without BES were also prepared as a matrix background to eliminate endogenous respiration and provide a baseline blank for chemical analyses. All serum bottles were then closed with butyl rubber septa and sealed with aluminium seals. Ultra-high-purity nitrogen gas was purged for 5 min to eliminate oxygen, maintaining anaerobic conditions. One set of samples was incubated at 4 °C in the dark, and another at 20 °C, for a total of 30 days. A portion of the sets was immediately sacrificed after establishing the BHP to establish an initial Day 0 comparison.

Analytical monitoring of biogas and metabolic profiling

Both the gas and liquid fractions of the fermentation were tracked throughout the fermentation process. Biogas production from fermentation was measured using volume measurements every 48 h using the water-displacement method. Gas chromatograph (GC) was used to measure the composition of headspace gas (H2, CO2, and CH4) using thermal conductivity detection (TCD) with a molecular sieve 5 A column, where nitrogen was utilized as the carrier gas. The GC method was similar to mentioned in Agilent technologies with modifications18. Method includes GC model: Thermo Trace 1110 equipped with TCD detector, propack Q column with oven, injector and detector temperatures of 40 °C, 100 °C and 150 °C respectively and nitrogen at 30 ml/min as carrier gas with six minutes run time at 8 V bridge voltage and 1 ml sample injection.

The soluble amount of glucose remaining in the fermentation liquid following completion of fermentation was quantified via the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) colorimetric assay to quantify the amount of glucose that remained within the fermentation medium. Centrifugal separation of the fermentation liquid (10,000 rpm for 10 min) was performed to remove any turbidity caused by bacteria prior to the DNS assay. The supernatant collected following centrifugation was mixed with the DNS reagent, incubated at 100 °C for 8 min, and the absorbance of the sample at 540 nm was compared with that of a reagent blank to determine the residual glucose concentration.

Profiling of volatile fatty acids (VFA) was performed only on the final Day 30 samples to determine whether metabolic partitioning had occurred. The amount of VFA in the samples was determined using the modified Montgomery method by spectroscopy19, while their composition was determined by gas chromatography with DB-FFAP capillary columns. The carrier gas flow rate during analysis was maintained at 1.2 mL/min. The injector and detector temperatures were set to 250 °C and 280 °C, respectively. The oven temperature program began at 60 °C for a period of 3 min, then increased in temperature at 15 °C/min to a final temperature of 160 °C; this was then raised again at a rate of 5 °C/min to a total maximum temperature of 200 °C and held constant for 5 min at that maximum temperature. Prior to the injection all samples were acidified to ensure that the VFA present would be volatilized, then 0.5 µL of each sample was injected into the column20.

Metagenomic DNA extraction and nanopore sequencing

Using the manufacturer’s instructions, we extracted total environmental DNA (eDNA) from crude environmental samples and Day 30 BHP samples with the Promega Wizard® Enviro Total Nucleic Acid Kit (Cat.# A2991). Following extraction, the concentration and purity of the genomic DNA were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer with the dsDNA High Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). We also used the Monarch® Spin PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (Cat. # T1130L) to process the extracted nucleic acids to achieve sequencing-grade purity. Sequencing libraries were constructed with the Oxford Nanopore Rapid Barcoding Kit 96 with V14 chemistry (Cat.# SQK-RBK114.96). The multiplexed libraries were loaded onto a MinION R10.4.1 flow cell (FLO-MIN114) and high-throughput long-read sequencing was performed using a MinION nanopore sequencer. Raw electrical signal data were converted into sequencing reads via high-accuracy basecalling using the Dorado basecaller (HAC model) integrated within the MinKNOW software.

Bioinformatic processing

A customized, sequential workflow on Galaxy (usegalaxy.org) was used to bioinformatically process the raw sequencing reads. Initially, raw sequence cleanup was performed using Cutadapt to remove barcode and adapter sequences, and Falco to remove sequences that failed quality control. To eliminate any potential host (human) contamination (due to movement of samples and during handling), reads were mapped to the NCBI hg38 human reference database using Minimap2, and any matching host reads were discarded. Following this, the microbial community’s composition was taxonomically profiled using Kraken2, which matched reads to the 2024 pre-built RefSeq core nucleotide collection. MAPseq was used to improve the resolution of unidentified fungal and rare (taxa) reads concurrently with taxa profiling, and eggNOG-mapper was used to annotate the functional profiles of all sequences, specifically to identify genes and metabolic pathways associated with biohydrogen production.

All plots were made using R software. Using the vegan package, microbial community diversity was assessed by calculating alpha diversity (Shannon, Simpson, Fisher alpha, Chao1, and ACE indices) to measure species richness and evenness, and beta diversity (nonmetric multidimensional scaling [NMDS] based on Bray-Curtis, Jaccard, and Morisita-Horn distance metrics) to examine differences between communities. Community trajectories and structural shifts of the samples were analyzed using PCoA from Bray-Curtis distance. In order to understand what type of ecological assembly process is responsible for such structural shifts, the null model analysis was performed using Raup-Crick (RC) dissimilarity index (nsimul = 999), where RC value less than 0.05 signifies deterministic homogeneous selection while 0.05 to 0.95 denotes stochastic drift. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to reduce dimensionality and identify major patterns among samples, while canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) was used to evaluate interactions among environmental drivers including temperature, BES application, physicochemical parameters, and functional gene profiles. To make sure that the CCA is reliable, the multicollinearity between the environmental drivers was determined through calculation of the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) using the vegan package on R. The stepwise selection eliminated those variables that had high correlation, and those left were pH, Volatile Fatty Acids (VFA), TOC, and IC (VIF < 10). Databases, along with graphing tools such as the tidyverse, ggplot2, and patchwork, enabled extensive manipulation and figure plotting for data visualization.

Results and discussion

Microbial community diversity and taxonomic composition of crude samples

Metagenomic sequencing of raw ice and surface water samples collected at Ny-Ålesund sampling locations has been used to measure the environmental microbiome baseline. The alpha diversity metrics (Shannon, Fisher alpha, Chao1, and ACE) indicate that richness and evenness patterns differ across sample types (Fig. 2a). Water 3, Ice 1, and Ice 4 samples have high Chao1 and Shannon indices, indicating an even community structure with complex richness and diversity within those communities, whereas several samples (such as Water 1, Water 4, and Ice 2) were very low in both diversity (evenness) and richness (Chao1) and likely dominated by a small number of well-adapted taxonomic groups. Beta diversity analysis revealed variable degrees of community clustering (Fig. 2a) when Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) was performed using Bray-Curtis, Jaccard, and Morisita-Horn distance metrics. Water and ice community structure is likely overlapping partially attributing to the local hydrological mixing of ice melt and surface water21.

Further, taxonomic profiling provided by the data in Fig. 2b (ice samples) and 1c (ice and water samples) and supplementary Figure S1 and S2 supports the differences between the two communities. Although both ice and water matrices contained members of Proteobacteria, nevertheless, different composition was detected among the two matrixes. The samples from the ice matrix were found to be dominated by Gammaproteobacteria, while water samples had higher abundance of Chromadorea, major class of roundworms, common in Arctic habitats with extreme cryoprotective survival strategies, along with Betaproteobacteria among the microbial groups. This result is in line with the known community distribution in high latitude ecosystems where Gammaproteobacteria occur abundantly in both marine and brackish waters and glacial ice22.

Physicochemical profiling and functional metabolic linkages

Microbial diversity is influenced by the physical and chemical properties of the substrates analyzed, as reported in Tables 2 and 3 for ice and water samples respectively. All ice samples have a mildly acidic character (pH between 6.44 and 6.82), and their electrical conductivity was relatively low (8–15 mV). These properties are consistent with water bodies in unpolluted glacial environments23. The water samples indicate that slightly alkaline conditions (pH between 7.23 and 7.99) were present, coupled with a wide range of salinity. The first three water samples had relatively high electrical conductivity (EC) measurements (up to 6792 mV), indicating they were influenced by brackish Kongsfjorden waters. In contrast, Water 4 and Water 5 exhibited lower EC (91 and 75 mV, respectively), indicating that these were influenced primarily by fresh glacial meltwaters. Additionally, the amounts of total organic carbon (TOC) and total carbon (TC) were much higher in the water samples than in the ice samples, providing a greater supply of nutrients to support large numbers of microorganisms. These baseline data align well with the available literature for Arctic coastal margins undergoing active glacier retreat24. Crucially, these physicochemical profiles strongly influence the functional potential of the resident microbiomes. As shown in Fig. 2d by the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) functional analysis, broad functional classifications indicate that “Metabolism” represents the highest relative proportion. For instance, when examining specific subcategories such as amino acid transport and metabolism, energy production/conversion, and carbohydrate transport/metabolism in the COG heat map, it is evident that both the individual sample communities exhibit high genetic potential. This enables the efficient scavenging of carbon and maintenance of cell energy gradients with high rates of metabolic adaptation to extreme cold and variable salinities.

Biohydrogen production dynamics under thermal and chemical constraints

Following the crude samples’ physicochemical, metagenomic, and functional analysis, the fermentative capacity of samples to convert glucose to biohydrogen was assessed using a 30-day biohydrogen potential (BHP) assay. Figure 3 comprehensively details the outcomes of the BHP assay, in which Fig. 3a suggests that temperature is majorly driving of overall metabolic kinetics. The total biogas and biohydrogen increased significantly at 20 °C compared to 4 °C nearly across all ice and water samples. This also indicates that there is a thermodynamic limit to the metabolic rates of psychrotolerant organisms, given current temperature ranges. However, one remarkable anomaly was seen in the Ice 2 sample community, which had a greater yield of biohydrogen and total gases at 4 °C compared to the 20 °C sample. This anomaly suggests that while the vast majority of the samples analyzed had communities of microorganisms that were psychrotolerant and optimized their metabolism in warmer conditions, the Ice 2 sample probably had strictly psychrophilic species that were thermally inhibited at 20 °C. Consequently, bringing the Ice 2 community temperatures to 20 °C may likely have induced thermal stress and enzymatic inhibition, resulting in a lower yield compared to its optimum performance at 4 °C. Furthermore, the addition of BES, a methanogenic inhibitor, significantly affects hydrogen production. The addition of BES to the fermentation process significantly altered the composition of the resulting biogas, yielding a maximum biohydrogen fraction of 84.8% from the Ice 4 sample and 85.0% from the Water 5 sample at 20 °C.

The relationship illustrated in Fig. 3b, which shows a comparison between total biogas production against absolute biohydrogen yield, suggesting a positive linear trend across all sample types, i.e., biohydrogen gas increases with an increase in biogas production. Notably, within the samples, the addition of BES slightly alters the slope of the linear relation with BES-treated samples, as represented in triangles in Fig. 3b, consistently falling above the 50% hydrogen fraction threshold (dashed line). This indicates they are producing more hydrogen gas from each unit of total biogas compared to those without BES addition. Examples of this can be seen for specific samples like Water 5, Water 1, and Ice 4 that have great fermentation capacities when incubated at 20 °C in the presence of BES, resulting in them being the top three samples based on volume biohydrogen producers with their respective matrices.

Impact of temperature and BES on biohydrogen production kinetics

The cumulative and daily biohydrogen production profiles display the dynamics of these processes over time (Figs. 3c and d). The cumulative gas curves show the classic sigmoidal relationship of classical growth kinetics: a lag phase (adaptation), an exponential phase (rapid growth), and a stationary phase when all the maximum glucose has been consumed. Compared with the 4 °C groups, the 20 °C BES-treated groups produced the highest cumulative volumes and reached their stationary phase sooner. As mentioned, an exception to this trend was seen in the Ice 2 sample, which had a greater of biohydrogen and total gases at 4 °C compared to the 20 °C sample. However, at ice 2 samples with BES still produced significantly more hydrogen compared to 4 °C sample without BES additions. The statistical significance of these increased yields under this optimal treatment was determined by independent Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference post hoc analysis (p < 0.05). The distinct alphabetical groupings shown in Fig. 3c indicate that, except Ice 2, treatment at 20 °C differs significantly from the 4 °C conditions. Daily production rates, as shown in Fig. 3d, indicate a peak in hydrogen generation at 20 °C between days 4 and 10, characterized by sharp, high-amplitude spikes in gas evolution. Hydrogen production from 4 °C incubations exhibited relatively flat, wide peaks, characteristic of delayed, prolonged metabolic activity resulting from adaptation to cold stress. The BES-treated samples do exhibit relatively increased effectiveness in producing biohydrogen. The reason can be justified based on the specific mode of BES action. In dark fermentation, biohydrogen is an intermediary product that is quickly taken up by hydrogenotrophic methanogens and converted to methane, providing a thermodynamically favourable route. Coenzyme M is a crucial cofactor required for the final stage of methanogenesis. Because BES is structurally similar to Coenzyme M, it acts as a competitive inhibitor of the methanogenesis pathway25. Despite clear knowledge of BES’s biochemical effects, its use in this case, i.e. within cryogenic Arctic consortia, marks innovation. BES addition compels the communities to reroute carbon flows away from methanogenic pathways. On the other hand, regarding chemical stability, although BES is highly stable and maintains strictly inhibitory properties throughout the 30 days of the batch experiment, according to the literature, some specific syntrophic bacteria can desulfonate the molecule under continuous reactor conditions.

Substrate utilization and acidogenic pH shifts

To elucidate how biogas and biohydrogen are produced by biochemical processes, the liquid metabolites from the 30-day BHP experiments were characterized for substrate utilization using DNSA. Details to the entire physicochemical profiling of BHP sampling are mentioned in Supplementary Table 1. The residual glucose concentrations by experimental matrix over time are illustrated in Fig. 4a. Based on the Statistical analysis via independent one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc comparisons (p < 0.05), a clear trend in temperature-dependent substrate assimilation can be observed in Fig. 4a: moderate glucose utilization in 4 °C incubations and a significant reduction in residual glucose levels during 20 °C incubations for majority of samples. The enhancement of glycolytic processes occurring at 20 °C correlates perfectly with the increased volume of biogas produced at this temperature, according to previously reported biogas production data. Notably, consistent with the volumetric gas anomaly discussed previously, the strictly psychrophilic Ice 2 community exhibited the inverse trend, showing greater glucose assimilation at 4 °C. However, reduced residual glucose was consistent with all samples compared to the Day 0 baseline.

Glucose catabolism leads to hydrolysis, acidogenesis, and acetogenesis, which inherently accumulate organic acids, as reflected in the terminal pH profiles shown in Fig. 4b. The initial Day 0 pH was adjusted to near neutrality, and upon post-incubation, the pH was acidified, with the extent dependent mainly on incubation temperature. Robust acidogenesis is readily recognized by the rapid and extensive acidification observed in 20 °C samples, where pH frequently fell below 5.5. It indicates that there was a rapid fermentation of glucose into soluble metabolites by the microbial consortia, and therefore, that the high biohydrogen yield associated with these conditions can be attributed to active acidogenic pathways26.

Volatile fatty acid (VFA) partitioning and metabolic pathways

Acidogenesis is further characterized by the relative VFA concentration and composition profiles seen in Figs. 4c & d. The bubble plot in Fig. 4c indicates that the total absolute concentration of VFA in the 20 °C treatment was majorly higher, except in Ice 2, which also correlated with decreasing pH and increasing glucose utilization. Figure 4d illustrates that Arctic consortia from samples predominantly utilize the acetic acid metabolic pathway regardless of BES influence (comprising roughly 50%–60% total VFA fraction). Relative contributions to the total VFA profile of butyric and propionic acids appear to vary with the application of BES. The data in Fig. 3d indicate that specific inhibition of methanogens by application of BES directed flux to predominantly produce butyric acid. The acetic acid/butyric acid fermentation pathway predominates both thermodynamically and stoichiometrically, allowing for the theoretical maximum potential of biohydrogen production (up to 4 moles of hydrogen per mole of glucose via complete acetogenesis)27. The presence of acetic acid in the Day 30 liquid fraction explains why these particular microbial communities from high-performing samples (e.g., Water 5, Water 4, water 1, and Ice 4) produced the highest biohydrogen gas yields from the provided glucose substrate. This also correlates directly with previous observations referred in Fig. 3b of extremely high fermentative capacity in these samples, indicating that psychrotolerant baseline communities adapted efficiently, when thermodynamically favorable metabolic conditionals were provided with BES enrichments.

Carbon mineralization and nutrient assimilation dynamics

The physicochemical properties of BHP samples were also monitored throughout the incubation period to assess overall mass balances of carbon and nutrients (i.e., TOC, TC, and IC as shown in Fig. 4e and f for ice and water samples). Total Organic Carbon (TOC) decreased considerably from initial levels, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the 20 °C treatments, indicating successful transformation of the substrate. Conversely, Total Carbon (TC) majorly remained higher at 20 °C compared to 4 °C treatments, especially for water samples, because of a sharp, simultaneous increase in Inorganic Carbon (IC) concentration. The increase in Inorganic Carbon (IC) concentration across all experimental conditions was due to microbial metabolism producing carbon dioxide in the liquid phase, which dissolved to form bicarbonate ions, which served as a partial buffer against the accumulation of VFAs28. The constant growth and enzyme production by the microbial consortium required a continuous supply of essential macronutrients, as evidenced by the loss of Total Nitrogen (TN) and Ortho-phosphates from Day 0 to Day 30. Nutrient assimilation was more at 20 °C, which confirms that the faster kinetic turnover rate at the higher temperature supported increased yields of microbial biomass. Nevertheless, one unique feature could be observed about the effect of the chemical inhibitor: the BES-treated samples almost in all instance had reduced nutrient assimilation (high residual TN and ortho-phosphate values) than those of the non-BES group regardless of the temperature. Such a phenomenon is indicative of the synergistic decoupling effect exerted by the BES treatment on the ecosystem. In the case of non-BES systems, both fermentative bacteria and hydrogen-consumers methanogens grow in great numbers, which results in high biological demand for nitrogen and phosphorus. However, with the suppression of methanogenic archaea, fewer organisms were involved in the process of biomass synthesis. Thus, the physical-chemical profiles provide strong evidence that these syntrophic microorganisms operated in a highly active environment, coupling rapid carbon mineralization and nutrient assimilation with robust biohydrogen production.

Community trajectories and diversity shifts during enrichment

For identifying the mechanisms behind biohydrogen formation, the metagenomic successions of both crude and 30-day BHP samples were analyzed. The analysis is provided below in Fig. 5a in terms of a Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. As seen clearly from the figure, the changes have caused significant restructurings in the composition of the microbiomes. Both the water and the ice samples can be observed moving towards new steady states far apart from the original “Initial” state (on the left end of the graph). These new states account for 30.7% of the variance on the PCo1 axis, followed by 22.7% on the PCo2 axis. For determining statistically whether the observed trend resulted from stochastic drift or deterministic environmental pressure, a null-model approach to assembling the communities was undertaken using the Raup-Crick (RC) dissimilarity index (see Fig. 5a, lower right panel). RC values within treatments in the case of enriched samples were found to be significantly less than 0.05. This clearly indicates that the environmental pressure was strongly deterministic (environmental filtering), leading to convergence of the community structure into a specialist fermentative network.

The deterministic restructuring of the microbial community can be seen from the analysis of the Shannon diversity index (Fig. 5a, bottom left). Although the starting diversity was initially higher for the ice compared to the water samples, the diversity of the community in most of the replicates decreased upon 30 days’ incubation period. This indicates a selective enrichment of specialized fermentative bacteria in the microbial community. On the other hand, several replicates of water sample (such as Water 1 and Water 4) and also some 20 °C ice treatment samples showed an increase in diversity. Furthermore, a combination of lower temperature and methanogenesis inhibition (4 °C + BES) resulted in high restriction of active members within certain water samples such as Water 2, Water 3 and Water 5. As a result, the Shannon index does not show the collapse of the community but rather indicates complex community restructuring and its stimulation in some cases.

Taxonomic succession and suppression of hydrogen consumers

The relative abundance heat map of the enriched taxa is shown in Fig. 5b as a heatmap, which illustrates their specific identities. The selective pressure was able to enhance previously known potent biohydrogen producers, which also comprised the genera Clostridium, Bacillus, Enterobacter, and Klebsiella29. In particular, such genera prevail within the scope of dark fermentation processes owing to their unique metabolic characteristics. Spore-forming bacteria from Firmicutes, including species of Clostridium and Bacillus, are extremely resistant to harsh cryobiotic conditions. Under the favorable anaerobic conditions, they employ very active [FeFe]-hydrogenase enzymes for obligatory production of hydrogen gas via the acetate and butyrate fermentation routes, perfectly explaining the observed dominant VFA compositions of the samples30. Additionally, facultative anaerobes from genera Enterobacter and Klebsiella have been known for their fast adaptation and cleavage of formate molecules into hydrogen and carbon dioxide using Formate Hydrogenlyase (FHL). Such psychrotolerant representatives of these genera have already been shown to maintain their high yield of biohydrogen along with the high rate of glycolysis even in low-temperature environments, allowing them to quickly outcompete non-fermentative taxa for available glucose when methanogenesis is synthetically blocked11. Moreover, the evidence captured in the heat map provides insights into the mechanism by which BES accomplishes its effects on the hydrogen consumers. In the BES-treated samples, the relative abundances of archaeal consumers varied significantly. Since BES acts as a metabolic inhibitor rather than a bactericidal, BES inhibits the production of methanogenic bacteria rather than killing them off31. For this reason, even though sensitive types such as Methanotorris, Methanomassiliicoccus from Ice 1, Methanolobus from Ice 5 and Acetobacterium from Water 5 were drastically decreased, certain resilient taxa like Methanosphaera, Desulfotomaculum, and Shweanella sustained or increased their relative sequence shares in specific tracks. This observed increase represents a mathematical percentage shift within the remaining archaeal community profile rather than absolute biomass growth. This shift in the community structure relates directly to the chemical data obtained from the hydrogen (Fig. 3) and methane analyses, respectively, and demonstrates that BES effectively decoupled the syntrophic hydrogen-producer and hydrogen-consuming network.

Functional gene reconfiguration and biohydrogen pathways

Beyond taxonomy, the metagenomic profile tracked not only the succession of functional genes within optimized communities, but also their associated metabolic pathways. The overall functional categorization of genes using COG, as in Fig. 5c, shows that throughout the entire BHP period, the community is perpetually dominated by the functional categories “Metabolism” and “Cellular Processes,” underscoring a community entirely geared toward rapid substrate turnover and energy conversion. Targeted functional profiling reveals that there is a large number of specific genes involved in the evolution of biohydrogen, such as the rnf, and nif operons, that are abundant in communities that produced higher biohydrogen, such as Water 1, Water 4, and Ice 5.

The way these different genetic pathways are strategically arranged is clearly shown in the PCA of functional gene profiles shown in Fig. 5e. There is a large split in how hydrogen is produced by both communities, which is associated with temperature and BES application. The vectors pointing toward the high-performing 20 °C clusters are primarily comprised of rnf complex genes and nif genes like nifH, nifD, along with hydA (encoding highly active [FeFe]-hydrogenases) as well as hyf, hyc, and the maturation factor hypB (which together comprise and mature the Formate Hydrogenlyase [FHL] complex)32. This further implies that, under optimal heat and inhibitory conditions, rather than thermodynamically favouring solely the most efficient dark fermentation pathways, the community may rely heavily on the nitrogenase pathway (likely coupled with the Rnf system) as the core contributor to hydrogen production in this unique Arctic context. Although this nitrogenase pathway is extremely ATP-dependent and traditionally thought to not produce greater volumes of hydrogen than the [FeFe]-hydrogenase pathway, it is clearly not abandoned by the community33. Therefore, the PCA plot demonstrates that increasing the temperature and adding BES is associated with both the enrichment of specific taxonomic groups and a restructuring of functional gene abundances. Under these conditions, the community utilize a coupled Rnf-nitrogenase route for hydrogen production, rather than strictly favouring the hyper-efficient, rapid-turnover hydrogenase pathways as initially presumed.

As demonstrated by Fig. 6a, all the determined kinetic parameters corroborate the previous qualitative findings. The maximal hydrogen production rate (Rmax) and the Volumetric Hydrogen Production Rate (VHPR) were greatly increased at 20 °C, but these rates often showed greater peaks when BES was not added to the cultures. On the contrary, the final potential production (P) was vastly better in with BES cultures at both temperatures. Such kinetic discrepancy underlines the biological separation: although mixed cultures without BES show a quick metabolic activation (maximal Rmax), the methanogens quickly consume all the accumulated hydrogen. In contrast, BES cultures achieve lower but stable maxima, yet retain all the hydrogen produced. Moreover, at 20 °C, the lag phase was actually prolonged compared to 4 °C. This extended lag phase may reflect acclimation period of psychrotolerant microbes to reconfigure their cold-adapted metabolisms to use substrate. Finally, this system efficiency directly relates to the specific biohydrogen yield as shown in Fig. 6b, expressed as mmol hydrogen per gram of glucose consumed. The addition of BES resulted in statistically significant increases in specific yield, as determined by an independent Tukey HSD analysis (p < 0.05). The chemical blocking of the methanogenic sink created a massive carbon flux toward biohydrogen accumulation. Indeed, although incubation at 20 °C resulted in high peak rates and volumes, maximum specific yields seemed to be obtained at 4 °C. This is evident through the superior performance of 4 °C samples like Water 2 and Ice 1 compared to those at 20 °C such as Ice 2 and Water 4 in terms of specific yield. From a biological standpoint, this implies that when operating under their natural cold conditions, these microbial populations channel more of their acquired carbon towards fermentative reactions that generate energy instead of using it for biomass production and thermoregulation.

Furthermore, to provide a quantitative baseline, the volumetric hydrogen production rate (VHPR) of our highest-performing 20 °C Arctic consortium (216.0 mL/L/day) was evaluated against existing literature. While this yield is predictably lower than the massive volumetric outputs of highly optimized, pure mesophilic isolates operating at 37 °C such as 5460 mLH2/L/day with Rmax of 91 Nml/h (2184 ml/day) as reported by Muri et al. (2016)35, it remains highly competitive with other psychrotolerant cultures. For instance, compared to study by Mohammed et al., (2018) using same 1% glucose substrate with Antarctic Psychrotolerant Klebsiella sp. at 30 °C, they obtained Rmax of 20.67 ml/day (reported as 0.8 mol/L/h) with potential (P) value of 41.5 ml (38.55 mol/L)11, while we reported our BES-enriched consortia at 4 °C with 11 ml/day and 154 ml as Rmax and hydrogen potential respectively. This demonstrates that unsterilized, native Arctic consortia possess substantial metabolic elasticity, offering a viable bioenergy pathway that circumvents the heating and sterilization costs required to maintain standard mesophilic reactors.

Environmental drivers and functional gene co-variation

In order to establish the independent environmental factors that influence the genetic capability for biohydrogen production, a variance inflation factor (VIF)-corrected canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) was performed (Fig. 6c). After exclusion of the collinear factors, pH, VFA, TOC, and IC emerged as the main independent constraints. Biplots show specific environment-associated evolutionary patterns. For instance, in the water environment, the cluster formed by nif genes (nifK, nifN, nifD, anfK) showed significant correlation with the VFA axis, especially in the case of high-efficient biohydrogen production in 20 °C BES samples. Thus, the acidogenic fermentation and subsequent biohydrogen production through the nitrogenase pathway appear tightly associated in aqueous systems. However, in the ice environment, hydrogenase genes (hydA, hypB, hupA) were found to be uncorrelated with main acidic parameters (VFA, pH). This may suggest that these specific pathways may be optimized under different thermodynamic or buffering conditions within the glacial consortia.

Carbon mass balance and thermal sensitivity

A detailed carbon mass balance-based analysis was developed (Fig. 6d) to determine the overall fate of the initial substrate used in bioprocesses. The relative carbon recovery calculations indicated that at 4 °C, a substantial fraction of the carbon in the system remained trapped as residual glucose. Whereas in 20 °C samples, there was a shift in carbon partitioning. Most of the total carbon was successfully directed towards VFAs. Furthermore, the thermodynamic elasticity of microbial communities in the Arctic was measured through calculation of the Temperature Coefficient (Q10), which calculates the factor by which the reaction rate (R) multiplies for every 10-degree rise in temperature36:

As is evident from Fig. 6e, the thermal sensitivity (Q10) was highly diverse among the samples in question, with the vast majority having Q10 readings falling beneath the established biological reference point of 2.0 under both treatments. The relative lower thermal sensitivity implies that the selected psychrotolerant communities carry a specially evolved enzymatic mechanism, which ensures high efficiency of metabolism even at low temperatures (4 °C) without the need for substantial thermal activation. In contrast, certain exceptions, such as Ice 1, Water 2, and Water 5, showed Q10 readings far greater than 2.0. High thermal sensitivity, in turn, means that some communities have a relatively high level of latent catalytic potential, which is highly responsive to thermal activation. Thus, they become a highly valuable biocatalytic resources for engineered energy-recovery systems operating at ambient temperatures like 20 °C.

Conclusion

The current study provides a comprehensive overview of the biohydrogen production capacity of native Arctic microbes from the pristine environmental baseline of Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard. Using an engineered dark-fermentation system, we explored the thermodynamic flexibility of the cryobiotic consortia and characterized their transition from the pristine environment in Ny-Ålesund to the engineered anaerobic system. Baseline communities demonstrated spatial and matrix-dependent heterogeneities with various nutrient-foraging taxa that utilize a broad range of metabolic strategies. However, the communities also exhibited rapid responses to thermal and chemical perturbations. In addition, the use of BES (a methanogenic inhibitor) decoupled hydrogen-producing and consuming networks, resulting in the greater specific biohydrogen production yields, particularly at 4 °C, and triggering genetic reprogramming of the microbial system. Rather than completely abandoning baseline nif-operon linked metabolic pathways, the community shifted to a synergistic network combining a coupled Rnf-nitrogenase route with highly efficient, rapid-turnover dark fermentation using Formate Hydrogenlyase and [FeFe]-hydrogenase pathways based on the accumulation of high levels of acetic acid. Finally, predicting kinetic parameters, such as diverse Q10 thermal-sensitivity coefficients, suggests that these organisms are not simply surviving cold stress; rather, their predominantly low Q10 values indicate specially evolved enzymatic efficiency at low temperatures, while certain outliers possess latent hyper-responsive catalytic capacity. The present work bridges polar microbial ecology and the practical uses of biotechnology, demonstrating that latent genetic reservoirs in unexploited extreme, high-latitude ecosystems can be expeditiously rewired within precise environmental constraints to facilitate efficient, sustainable bioenergy resources.
