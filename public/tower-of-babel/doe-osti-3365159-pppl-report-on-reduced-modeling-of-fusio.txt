1 Executive summary
We are reporting on the modeling of fusion alpha particle transport in the planned ARC fusion device being designed
by the CFS (Commonwealth Fusion Systems: https://cfs.energy). The ARC tokamak is designed to operate in a
burning-plasma regime characterized by a substantial population of fusion-born alpha particles.
 Alfvén eigenmode (AE) stability is assessed both analytically and numerically, incorporating alpha-particle drive,
ion Landau, and radiative damping from thermal species and collisional damping from trapped electrons. Regions
of unstable and near-threshold AE activity are mapped across ARC’s operational parameter space. Linear stability
analysis with NOVA indicates multiple, often marginally unstable AEs, extending to toroidal mode numbers up to
n = 30. The present report focuses on the ARC flat-top operating point prior to the sawtooth event.
 Alpha-particle transport on timescales exceeding the neoclassical slowing-down time is assessed using the NUBEAM
module [1][2] of the TRANSP code [3], employing transport coefficients derived from the RBQ quasilinear modeling
(cf. Appendix B). These global simulations identify favorable and unfavorable operating regimes with respect to
alpha confinement, pressure redistribution, and overall alpha-heating efficiency.
 We also evaluate additional transport mechanisms—including neoclassical tearing mode (TM)–induced stochas-
ticity, sawtooth-driven redistribution, and toroidal-field ripple using the kick model (cf. Appendix C) which makes
use of the guiding-center code ORBIT, see Section 5. The kick model is integrated into TRANSP to enable self-
consistent predictions of alpha-driven current formation and sustainment within the ARC scenario. Sensitivity scans
are performed over the mode frequency, rational-surface alignment, island width, mode amplitude, and proximity of
the limiter to the plasma.
 Our study provides an initial, physics-based guidance for machine design, operational planning, and equilibrium
control, ensuring adequate alpha confinement and robust self-heating performance in ARC.
 Our simulations mostly targeted worst case scenarios, e.g. for TMs and sawteeth. Overall, we expect benign
effects for the ARC scenario investigated in this work on fusion alpha confinement and losses in the presence of AEs,
tearing modes and sawteeth.
 This report addresses three thrusts identified at the outset. The first thrust focuses on analytic estimates of the
parametric dependencies of EP relaxation based on local AE stability simulations (Section 3). The second thrust
involves global evaluations of AE stability using the NOVA, RBQ, and NUBEAM codes (Section 4). Finally, we
investigate alpha-particle transport driven by low-frequency instabilities associated with sawteeth and tearing modes
(Section 5).

4.2 TAE stability in drift kinetic approximations
The TAE stability analysis is refined using the global kinetic extension of the ideal MHD NOVA code — namely,
NOVA-C — which computes the energetic-particle (EP) drive arising from gradients in the constants of motion
phase space. The rich kinetic physics incorporated in NOVA-C, including several damping mechanisms, results in
the figure 5 (see Appendix A for additional details). The dominant contributions to the net growth rate include
the finite–orbit–width (FOW) and finite–Larmor–radius (FLR)–corrected alpha-particle drive, together with the
deuterium (D) and tritium (T) thermal-ion Landau dampings.
 A noteworthy feature of calculated TAE growth rates is their clear rollover behavior as a function of the toroidal
mode number n. NOVA-C captures this trend numerically, showing that the growth rate maximizes near k⊥ ρα = 1
and decreases for higher n values, consistent with earlier theoretical and numerical expectations [4, 10].

4.4 NUBEAM predictions of fast ion saturation
Ideally, EP relaxation used in whole-device modeling simulations should incorporate the evolution of the EP distri-
bution function in the constant-of-motion space over a timescale that spans the three key characteristic times: the
inverse linear AE growth rate, the inverse damping rate, and the inverse effective pitch-angle scattering rate [13]. Such
dynamics have been demonstrated and are well captured by the QL theory as implemented in RBQ simulations [14].
With this approach in mind, our long-term goal is to enable steady-state plasma-operation regimes, while ignoring
intermittent behavior.
 In the present work, however, we employ NUBEAM simulations [15], which use the alpha and H-minority diffusion
coefficients generated by the RBQ runs as shown in Fig. 6. These RBQ calculations include the contribution of H-
minority ions to the TAE growth rates.

to capture fast-ion slowing-down effects as well as the alpha source. This behavior is clearly reflected in the NUBEAM

5 Alpha particle transport by low-frequency instabilities
Alpha particle transport by low-frequency plasma instabilities has been investigated for the target ARC scenario.
Considering the scenario provided by CFS, the main target instabilities are sawteeth and Neoclassical Tearing Modes
(TMs). The q-profile for the reference TRANSP run crosses q = 1 around t = 1.47s and an internal kink mode is
expected to be destabilized. A sawtooth crash at t = 1.74s is indeed included in the reference TRANSP run using the
Porcelli mmodel implemented in TRANSP. At the same time, other instabilities such as Tearing Modes or neoclassical
TMs (NTMs) may also be destabilized. For this initial study, 2/1 and 3/2 TMs have been considered, as they are
expected to be the most detrimental in terms of alpha particle transport and losses.
 For this study, an approach to evaluate worst case scenarios has been followed to assess the expected transport
and loss of alpha particles by either sawteeth, (N)TMs, or a combination of both. For sawteeth, full reconnection is
assumed. For TMs, the amplitude is varied up to a maximum ”reasonable” value, i.e. up to 20cm island width.
 Modeling has been performed with the ORBIT and TRANSP codes, see Appendix C. ORBIT is used for
both stand-alone, single time-slice analysis, and for computing the transport coefficients that can then be used in
TRANSP/NUBEAM for time-dependent simulations. The advantage of ORBIT is the possibility of performing scans
of parameters efficiently. However, several physics phenomena are not included in the code, e.g. varying plasma
equilibrium and source terms. That motivated refinements of the analysis leveraging the full capabilities provided by
TRANSP.
 The main results on alpha particle transport and losses caused by sawteeth and TMs are discussed in the following

5.2 Projections of alpha transport by sawteeth
Following the method discussed in Ref. [20], ORBIT is first used to determine the expected peak amplitude of the
sawtooth, assuming complete reconnection. The mode structure has been calculated1 through the extended-MHD
code M3D-C1 [21][22]. The resulting displacement is shown in Fig. 15(a) for the dominant poloidal harmonics,
m = 1...5. Once processed in ORBIT to estimate the peak amplitude, the corresponding solution results in a
maximum perturbation of the magnetic field of δBr /B0 ≈ 4 × 10−3 .
 The sawtooth in ORBIT is simulated with a time-dependent amplitude, shown in Fig. 15(b). The crash is
mimicked by a fast decrease of the amplitude, although - as mentioned above - no changes in the background
magnetic equilibrium are included.
 Figure 16 shows ORBIT results in terms of modifications of the alpha density profile. Particles are considered as
lost if they intersect the machine limiter, which in this case was assumed to be the V3A limiter. Overall, a modest
redistribution is observed in the core for normalized toroidal flux inside ρ ∼ 0.3, i.e. inside the q = 1 surface. The
edge alpha density remains essentially unchanged. Losses are small, < 1% in terms of both number of markers and
of relative power lost to the wall.
 Some of the sawtooth parameters can be modified in ORBIT, for example the number of poloidal harmonics
retained in the simulation and a finite frequency associated with the mode. Scans have been performed over those
parameters. Increasing the number of harmonics up to 20 does not result in appreciable changes in the ORBIT
results. That can be understood from Fig. 15, where it is clear that the amplitude of harmonics decreases quickly for

 In addition to the stand-alone simulations discussed above, ORBIT also provides transport coefficients that can

Fig. 16) can be further analyzed to look at the amount of lost power and the spatial distribution of lost alpha
particles, as shown in Fig. 17. TRANSP calculates < 1% prompt alpha losses, mostly near the alpha born energy
of 3.5MeV and near the trapped-lost boundary at lower energies. The addition of the sawtooth through the kick
model transiently increases the losses by a factor 2 − 3. Most of the sawtooth-induced losses concentrate around the
midplane, both at the LFS and at the HFS. In contrast, prompt losses are distributed over a broader region of the
vessel.
 To summarize the main results from this Section, sawteeth transiently increase alpha losses by x2 − 3, but only
transiently. In the absence of other instabilities (or TF ripple effects), prompt losses remain the main loss channel.

5.3 Combined effects of sawteeth and TMs
Combining the analysis from Secs. 5.1-5.2, this Section explores the effects of simultaneous destabilization of Tearing
Modes and sawteeth on alpha confinement. The analysis is only performed through TRANSP simulations. The effects
of sawteeth are included as presented in Sec. 5.2. Four additional sets of transport coefficients are added to include
2/1 and 3/2 tearing modes before and after the sawtooth crash. (Two sets are needed for each mode because of the
changes in the magnetic equilibrium after the crash). The radial mode structure for TMs is modeled as in Sec. 5.1,
based on Ref. [16]. Since no quantitative simulations of the TM stability and saturation amplitude are available, a
scan is performed over the TM island width. The latter is varied independently for each TM in subsequent TRANSP
runs, to collect a matrix of alpha losses vs. TM island width. Values range from 5cm up to 20cm.
 Figure 18 shows a summary of the losses vs. TM island width at three times during the TRANSP simulation -
before, during and after the sawtooth crash. As a reference, prompt losses account for ≈ 1% of lost alpha power.
If small amplitude TMs with wisland = 5cm are included in the simulation, the overall effects on alpha losses are
negligible compared to prompt losses. Losses double for wisland = 10cm and start exceeding 5% for wisland = 15cm
for both 2/1 and 3/2 modes. That number is more significant during the sawtooth crash, when losses can exceed
10% of the fusion power, although only transiently.
 The alpha density radial profiles for the TM amplitude scan are shown in Fig. 19. Compared to the reference
case without TMs and to the wisland = 5cm case, a peaking in the alpha profile is observed as wisland > 5cm.

Based on previous work on other devices, as well as on analysis of the wisland scan for ARC, fast ion profile peaking

a stabilizing mechanism that would further limit the modes’ growth. This observation implies that - based on the
reduced-model approach adopted in this work - TM growth might be limited to wisland = 5 − 10cm at most, thus
resulting in modest impact on the overall transport and loss of alpha particles.
 The spatial distribution of lost alpha particles is shown in Fig. 20 for different values of the TMs island width
and for the pre-sawtooth and sawtooth phases. (The phase post-sawtooth, not shown, is virtually the same as the
pre-sawtooth phase).
 Losses are limited to the bottom half of the limiter. As observed previously, the covered area increases rapidly for
wisland > 5cm. Only for wisland = 20cm are losses observed near the entrance of the lower divertor. The distribution
is very similar for the pre-sawtooth and sawtooth phases, indicating that TMs are mostly responsible for the losses
and that the sawtooth mostly adds an offset to those losses.
 A similar behavior is observed in the distribution of lost alphas with respect to their energy E and pitch p, see
Fig. 21. Prompt losses are fairly localized in (E, p), as seen from the pre-sawtooth case at zero TM island width
(top-left panel in Fig. 21). Two bands are seen, one at the alpha birth energy of 3.5MeV and another one more
localized in pitch near the trapped/lost boundary. The introduction of a sawtooth in the simulation leads to a more
scattered distribution of lost alphas in (E, p). Adding the TMs further broadens the distribution in both energy and
pitch, with a quick increase already when moving from wisland = 5cm to wisland = 10cm.

6 Discussion and Conclusions
Reduced modeling has been used to ascertain AE presence and stability and alpha particle transport and confinement
in ARC V3A burning plasmas. The reduced models permit a rapid analysis based on strong physical principles.
Preliminary assessments indicate that ARC will almost certainly exhibit a range of MHD instabilities, including
AEs, TMs, and sawtooth crashes. Initial reduced, single–time-slice modeling shows only moderate alpha particle
redistribution and relatively small power losses (∼ 1–2% above prompt losses) across all scenarios examined. This is
an encouraging result with minimal impact on overall device performance and integrity. This is largely due to ARC’s
naturally low beta (both thermal and fast ion) from its high-temperature superconducting magnets as well as the
q-profile currently constructed. The large, radial separation of the core-localized alpha particles from the locations
of the AE modes, TM rational surfaces, and q = 1 surface mitigate overall transport to local redistribution.
 When viewed in the context of the full set of results presented in this manuscript, these findings suggest that

Appendices
A NOVA and NOVA-C codes
NOVA/NOVA-C codes are linear MHD/drift kinetic codes for EP driven instabilities studies. NOVA solves ideal
MHD equations and finds eigenmodes, such as TAEs [25], including such effects as plasma compressibility and
realistic geometry. NOVA-C (earlier known as NOVA-K) evaluates the wave particle interaction of the eigenmode
such as TAE or RSAE by employing the quadratic form with the perturbed distribution function coming from the
drift kinetic equation [10]. NOVA-C also prepares the wave-particle interaction matrices which are used by the RBQ
code [12, 11]. NOVA-C is able to predict various kinetic growth and damping rates perturbatively, such as the phase
space gradient drive from energetic particles, radiative damping, ion/electron Landau damping and trapped electron
collisional damping. NOVA is routinely used for AE structure computations and comparisons with the experimentally
observed instabilities [26, 27]. The main limitations of NOVA code are caused by neglecting thermal ion FLR, toroidal
rotation, and drift effects in the eigenmode computations. Therefore it relies on the analytic expressions the radiative
damping [28]. Finite element methods are used in NOVA in radial direction and Fourier harmonics are used in
poloidal and toroidal directions. In the particular results reported here we used uniform in ψ radial grid with 201
and 256 in radial and poloidal directions, and poloidal harmonics ranging from m = n to m = nqedge .

B RBQ quasi-linear approach
The recently developed, numerically efficient, self-consistent quasi-linear (QL) code RBQ is designed to model ener-
getic particle (EP) confinement in the presence of multiple Alfvénic modes. RBQ follows the time evolution of mode
amplitudes and advances the EP distribution function in canonical orbital momentum (COM) space. This can be
done internally within RBQ or by exporting the corresponding diffusion and convective coefficients for subsequent
processing by Whole Device Modeling (WDM) packages such as NUBEAM [15]. In addition, RBQ is interfaced
with the WDM code TRANSP, and preliminary applications to DIII-D critical-gradient experiments have shown
encouraging agreement [11].
 RBQ solves the drift-kinetic equation for the fast-ion distribution function in the presence of several AEs [29, 30].
The code also computes the instantaneous linear growth rate of each mode at every time step—a quantity essential
for a self-consistent QL treatment.
 Recent RBQ simulations for ITER steady-state plasmas have been carried out for WDM processing by TRANSP
[9]. In these cases, both beam ions and alpha particles contribute to the AE drive. The resulting AE amplitudes
were found to be relatively small, with typical values δBθ /B ∼ 10−5 –10−3 . Consequently, no significant beam-ion

C ORBIT code and kick-model
In recent years, the NUBEAM module [1][2] implemented in TRANSP has been updated to include a physics-based,
reduced model to account for resonant fast ion transport induced by Alfvénic and other MHD instabilities. The
new model, known as kick model [23], introduces a kick probability matrix in NUBEAM. The matrix is defined over
phase-space constant of motion variables E, Pϕ and µ that define the fast ion energy, toroidal angular momentum
and magnetic moment [31]. For each (E, Pϕ , µ) region in phase space, the matrix contains a probability p(∆E, ∆Pϕ )
which represent the probability of correlated energy and Pϕ kicks experienced by a fast ion as a result of its interaction
with instabilities. Each probability matrix is associated with either a single mode or a set of modes.
 The kick matrix is computed via particle following codes such as ORBIT [32]. The mode structure used in
ORBIT to represent the perturbation can be computed through MHD codes such as NOVA and its kinetic extensions
NOVA-K and NOVA-C (see Appendix A), or M3D-C1 or analytic theory as discussed in this report. Details on how
the matrix is populated for a given set of mode structure, frequency and mode number spectrum can be found in
Refs. [23][24]. Details on matrix computation for time-dependent modes such as sawteeth are given in Ref. [20]
 ORBIT is a guiding-center code and relies on the guiding-center approximation. Temporally, this is easily achieved
as all modes of interest are at frequencies much less than the alpha gyrofrequency on the order of MHz. Spatially, the
Lamor radius of a typical 3.5 MeV alpha particle in ARC V3A is around 1-3 cm. This is certainly smaller than the
device size and the radial extent of the modes consider. Thus, the guiding-center approximation is considered valid.
To verify this, the global loss fraction of alpha markers due to TF-ripple was computed with ORBIT and compared
against those by A. LeViness at CFS who utilized the ASCOT5 code. The values were found to be 0.54% and 0.57%
respectively. Additionally, the spatial distribution of losses was in agreement. This further confirmed the validity of
ORBIT in our framework.
