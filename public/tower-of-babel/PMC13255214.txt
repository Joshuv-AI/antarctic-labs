Exertional heat stroke-related acute kidney injury: novel animal and cellular models
Authors: Jiaona Liu, Jing Hao, Pengfei Kang, Yingjie Zhang, Wanjun Shen, Pu Chen, Jingjing Chen, Xuefeng Sun, Xiangmei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExertional heat stroke (EHS) is a life-threatening condition frequently complicated by acute kidney injury (AKI), yet standardized experimental models remain limited. This study aimed to establish reproducible in vivo and in vitro models specifically for EHS-induced AKI (EHS-AKI). Male C57BL/6J mice underwent exercise in a heat stress environment. We evaluated the effects of dehydration before heat exposure (12 h without water) and different relative humidity levels (55%, 65%, 75%) on renal outcomes. The results showed that compared with the free drinking water control group, water deprivation significantly exacerbated renal dysfunction (manifested as increased serum creatinine and urea nitrogen levels) and aggravated renal pathological changes. Research has determined that an environmental condition of 39.5 °C combined with 65% relative humidity is the optimal condition for inducing severe but non lethal AKI. In addition, we constructed an in vitro model using HK2 cells exposed to high temperature (42.7 °C). Research has found that when the concentration ratio of myoglobin to L-ascorbic acid (Mb: Vit C) is 1:2, it can induce the maximum upregulation of the renal injury marker neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin and lead to a significant decrease in cell viability, thus simulating thermal injury related to rhabdomyolysis. In summary, this study validated a dehydrated mouse model and optimized cell experiment protocol, providing an important tool for exploring the pathophysiological mechanisms and potential therapies of exertional heat stroke associated acute kidney injury.

Introduction

As global warming intensifies, heat stress has emerged as a significant public health concern [1]. Heatstroke is a life-threatening disorder characterized by a core body temperature exceeding 40 °C accompanied by central nervous system dysfunction [2]. It can cause multiple organ injury, particularly disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute kidney injury (AKI), liver dysfunction, and rhabdomyolysis [2,3]. Heatstroke is classified into two types: classic heatstroke and exertional heatstroke [2–4]. Classic heat stroke primarily affects older adults and is associated with a mortality rate of up to 50% [5]. Conversely, exertional heat stroke (EHS) typically occurs during athletic events and military training [3], with an incidence as high as 80% [6]. Ambient temperature and relative humidity significantly influence the incidence of heat stroke [7].

AKI is one of the most common and clinically important complications of EHS [3,8]. Its development involves multiple mechanisms, including hemodynamic alterations [9–12], rhabdomyolysis and myoglobin-mediated toxicity [13–15], inflammation and coagulation abnormalities [16–19], as well as electrolyte disturbances [20]. Although a number of experimental models of EHS have previously been reported [7,21,22], studies specifically focusing on EHS-associated AKI (EHS-AKI) remain limited and have largely concentrated on early renal injury within 24 h after heat exposure [23,24]. Some existing models are also constrained by high mortality rates [23] or by reliance on electrically stimulated exercise [24], which may introduce additional stress-related confounding and thus hinder the stable reproduction of the key features of EHS-AKI. Meanwhile, it remains unclear whether pre-exposure dehydration exacerbates EHS-related renal injury, and there has been a lack of systematic optimization of appropriate humidity conditions for model establishment. In addition, in vitro studies integrating heat stress with rhabdomyolysis-related factors are still scarce. Therefore, the present study aimed to establish a reproducible in vivo model of EHS-AKI and to develop an in vitro injury model combining heat stress and myoglobin exposure, in order to more faithfully recapitulate the pathogenesis of EHS-AKI.

Methods

Mouse housing conditions

Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) grade male C57BL/6J mice, 12 weeks old, were procured from Beijing SPF Biotechnology Company. The animals were housed at the Medical Experimental Animal Center of the Chinese People’s Liberation Army General Hospital. Housing conditions were maintained with five mice per cage, a room temperature of 25 °C, relative humidity of 35% to 45%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. The control group was maintained under these environmental conditions. Animal experiments were carried out in accordance with a protocol approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army (2023-X19-40). All experimental methods and protocols were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) and strictly adhered to international laws and regulations.

Core temperature measurement

Core temperature was measured using a capsule thermometer (FMG, China). After the mice were anesthetized with 0.5% sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 60 mg/kg, a 1 cm midline incision was made under sterile conditions in the abdominal area. The thermometer was positioned between the abdominal aorta and the mesentery, and the incision was subsequently closed with sutures. Following surgery, the mice were monitored for 48 h, and buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously every 12 h for analgesia. After one week, the wounds had healed, and the mice had recovered to a normal body weight, food and water intake, and stable diurnal and activity rhythms. Experiments were conducted one week after the wound had healed (Figure 1a). The implanted capsule thermometer, capable of monitoring core temperature every second, was programmed to record core temperature every 5 min. Core body temperature was recorded from the time of implantation until explantation after euthanasia.

Heat exposure protocol

A small animal treadmill (ZS-PY-III, Zhongshi, China) was positioned within a temperature-controlled chamber (LTH-575N-01, Longyue, China). The chamber temperature was set to 39.5 °C, and the humidity level was adjusted to 65%. After the temperature and humidity stabilized, the mice were placed on the treadmill to commence exercise and heat exposure (Figure 1a). The exercise protocol comprised the following stages: 5 m/min (5 s acceleration) for 600 s; 7 m/min (10 s acceleration) for 1200 s; and 9 m/min (10 s acceleration) maintained until the heat exposure endpoint. The heat exposure endpoint was defined as reaching a core temperature (Tc) of 42.7 °C or the onset of physical exhaustion. Exhaustion was characterized by ineffective escape attempts lasting 5 s, loss of righting reflex, loss of consciousness, or convulsions, occurring prior to Tc reaching 42.7 °C. Throughout the experiment, the mice were deprived of food and water, and their core temperature changes were monitored every second via Bluetooth using a mobile device. Furthermore, the researchers continuously monitored the mice for signs of exhaustion. After reaching the endpoint, the mice were immediately removed and returned to their original housing cages in a normal environment to recover with access to food and water. They were allowed to recover undisturbed. Mice were euthanized with an overdose of 0.5% sodium pentobarbital to ensure painless death at 24, 48, and 72 h post-heat exposure.

After the termination of heat exposure, return the animals to the standard feeding environment. For animals assigned to the organizational collection group, planned euthanasia will be performed at predetermined time points (such as 24, 48, and 72 h) after heat stress.

Severity of heat exposure

One group of mice was used to assess survival rates and weight loss rates. Animals were assigned to groups as detailed in the Experimental Design and Animal Grouping section. Mice were weighed both before and after heat exposure, and the rate of weight loss was calculated using the following formula (100 − (weight after heat exposure/weight before heat exposure) × 100) to determine the percentage of weight loss.

Evaluating organ injury

After euthanizing the mice, serum was collected and analyzed using commercial kits to measure blood urea nitrogen (BUN, C013-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China) and serum creatinine (CRE, C011-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng, China). The levels of sodium (Na), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), chloride (Cl), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and creatine kinase (CK) were determined using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Chemray 800, Shenzhen). The kidneys were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 2 µm. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed to observe morphological changes in the kidneys. Two pathology experts (P.C., J.C.) conducted a blinded review of the pathological specimens. The pathological injuries assessed included tubular epithelial cell necrosis, casts, and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration. The renal pathological injury scoring system was as follows: the percentage of the injury area relative to the cortical-medullary junction area: no damage = 0; 1%–25% = 1; 26%–50% = 2; 51%–75% = 3; ≥76% = 4 [25,26].

Experimental design and animal grouping

Validation of Control Conditions (Supplementary Figure S1a): To rule out the potential confounding effect of water deprivation alone on renal injury, a preliminary validation study was conducted. Mice were assigned to either a Control group (maintained at 25 °C with free access to food and water) or a Water Deprivation Control group (deprived of water for 12 h at 25 °C, followed by ad libitum access to water). Both groups of animals underwent the same temperature capsule implantation surgery, but were not exposed to heat stress. We evaluated renal function and pathological changes at 24, 48, and 72 h (n = 6 per group at each time point). Given that confirmatory studies have shown no significant differences between groups (see results section), we used the control group as the unified baseline in the main study. This strategy allowed us to minimize animal usage, adhering to the 3Rs principle (Reduction).

Immunohistochemical staining

To evaluate myoglobin deposition in renal tissue after EHS, myoglobin immunohistochemical staining was performed on paraffin-embedded renal tissue sections. Renal tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (5 mL, Solarbio, P1110), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 2 μm. After routine deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was carried out using EDTA buffer (pH 9.0, 100 mL, Solarbio, C1033). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2 (50 μl, ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9311D) for 10 min, followed by blocking with goat serum (50 μl, ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9022) at room temperature for 30 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-myoglobin primary antibody (30 μl, Proteintech, 16048-1-AP, 1:100). After washing with PBS (100 mL, Biosharp, BL601A), sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (30 μl, ZSGB-Bio, PV-9001) for 30 min. Staining was visualized using DAB chromogen (50 μl, ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9017, 1:20), followed by hematoxylin (50 μl, ZSGB-Bio, ZLI-9610) counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting. Sections were observed and photographed under a light microscope. Semi-quantitative analysis of positive staining signals was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0, MD, USA).

Cell culture and treatment

In order to simulate the pathophysiological process of exertional heat stroke (EHS) combined with rhabdomyolysis in vitro, we established a ‘dual strike’ model that combines heat stress and myoglobin toxicity. This study selected human renal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2 cells) as they represent the main target of myoglobin induced injury - proximal renal tubular epithelial cells. Given that the cytotoxicity of myoglobin is closely related to the redox state of heme iron, we introduce L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C; Vit C) as a reducing agent to convert myoglobin into its reduced ferrous form (Fe2+). The experimental design aims to clarify which redox form of myoglobin is the main driving factor of kidney injury, in order to optimize the in vitro model to more accurately reflect the pathological condition.

HK2 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) medium (500 mL, Gibco, C11330500BT) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (50 mL, Gibco, 10099141C) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). HK2 cells were seeded at a density of 10, 000 cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated for 12 h before changing the culture medium. The cells were treated with human myoglobin (Mb, 882.35 nmol/L, at a concentration of 1.5 mg/d based on the renal threshold for myoglobin, Abcam, ab77876) or L-ascorbic acid (Vitamin C = Vit C, MCE, HY-80166). The groups were categorized as follows: HK2, HK2 + Vit C, HK2 + Mb, HK2 + Mb + Vit C (Mb: Vit C = 1:10), HK2 + Mb + Vit C (Mb: Vit C = 1:5), HK2 + Mb + Vit C (Mb: Vit C = 1:2), and HK2 + Mb + Vit C (Mb: Vit C = 1:1). The cells were exposed to these treatments for 4 h in both a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator and a 42.7 °C, 5% CO2 incubator (simulating severe hyperthermia). Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, CK04, 1:10) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting

After the cells were lysed using Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA, Servicebio, G2002, 1:2) buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Solarbio, P0100, 1:100), the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and the protein concentration was assessed using a Bicinchoninic Acid Assay (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo, 23227). A total of 20 µg of protein was loaded per lane onto a 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel (SDS-PAGE, BIO-RAD, 4561043) and subsequently transferred to a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane. The membrane was blocked at room temperature with a 1× casein solution (50 μL, Thermo, 37582) for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL, 10 mL, Abcam, ab26426, 1:1000) and β-tubulin (10 mL, Proteintech, 66240-1, 1:20, 000). The membrane was then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (10 mL, Beyotime, A0208, 1:2000) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by three washes with tris buffered saline with tween (TBST, Solarbio, T1081, 1:10). Signals were detected using the ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, 2 mL, Amersham Biosciences, GERPN2235, 1:1), and the data were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0, MD, USA).

TUNEL staining

Cells were cultured in 12-well plates. After removal of the culture medium, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (2 mL, Solarbio, P1110) at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS (2 mL, Biosharp, BL601A) and permeabilized with 0.2 mL PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (2 mL, Thermo Scientific, A16046.AE) on ice for 5 min. After three additional washes with PBS, the TUNEL reaction mixture was prepared according to the instructions of the Beyotime C1090 kit (TdT enzyme: fluorescent labeling solution = 1:9). A total of 50 μL of the reaction mixture was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and counterstained with DAPI (50 μl, Abcam, ab285390) at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, images were observed and captured using a Bio-Rad ZOE fluorescence microscope.

LDH release assay

After centrifugation of the cell culture supernatants, the supernatants were collected for LDH measurement using the LDH release Assay Kit (Beyotime, C0019S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples and reaction working solution were added to a 96-well plate, incubated at 37 °C in the dark, and then mixed with stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and LDH release activity was determined according to the standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Data were reported as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0. The Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney test were employed for comparisons between two groups, while two-way and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test and the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test, were utilized for multiple comparisons. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. p < 0.05 were considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Validation of the control conditions: water deprivation alone does not induce renal injury

To determine whether the 12-h water deprivation protocol itself contributes to renal injury independent of heat stress, a preliminary validation study was conducted under standard housing conditions (25 °C). Mice were divided into a Control group (free access to water) and a Water Deprivation group (Control + WD). No significant differences were observed in renal function between the two groups at 24, 48, and 72 h after intervention. Specifically, the serum creatinine (CRE) and urea nitrogen (BUN) levels of the Control + WD group were comparable to those of the control group (Control) at all time points (Supplementary Figure S1b, c). In addition, PAS staining histological examination showed that the structures of the two groups of renal tubules were intact, and no tubular necrosis, tubular formation, or interstitial inflammation was observed (Supplementary Figure S1d). The above results confirm that under non heat stress conditions, simple short-term water deprivation does not induce acute kidney injury. Consequently, the standard Control group was utilized as the baseline for subsequent EHS experiments.

Survival rates and weight loss rates

Survival rates for the NWEHS, HWEHS, and control groups were evaluated 72 h post-endpoint. In the NWEHS group, out of 20 mice, 10 survived, with 6 dying within 3 h after heat exposure ended. In the HWEHS group, 16 mice survived, resulting in a survival rate of 80% (Figure 1b). The survival rate of the two groups at 72 h showed a significant difference (p < 0.05). Regarding the weight loss rates, no significant differences were observed between the NWEHS and HWEHS groups (Figure 1c).

Core temperature

During the heat exposure period, both the NWEHS and HWEHS groups exhibited a hypothermic phase after reaching the experimental endpoint, defined as a core temperature of less than 35 °C in this study. The duration of the hypothermic phase for the NWEHS group was 3 (1.67, 4.58) hours, while for the HWEHS group, it lasted for 2.88 (0.83, 3.51) hours. The figure illustrates the temperature changes during heat exposure, regardless of whether it pertains to the NWEHS group or the HWEHS group (Figure 1d).

Renal histopathology

The pathological changes in the kidneys after heat exposure were mainly characterized by tubular epithelial cell necrosis (indicated by the red arrow), casts (indicated by the yellow arrow), and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration (indicated by the green arrow) (Figure 3a). As shown in the figure, both the NWEHS and HWEHS groups exhibited renal pathological damage (Figure 3b). Notably, the damage was more pronounced in the NWEHS group. When comparing the severity of pathological damage at 48 h, the NWEHS group showed significantly aggravated injury compared to the HWEHS group (p < 0.01) (Figure 3c).

Renal function and kidney injury biomarkers

Both NWEHS and HWEHS exposure caused increases in BUN and CRE (Figure 4a,c), with the most pronounced effects observed at 48 h. At 48 h post-heat exposure, the levels of BUN (p < 0.01) and CRE (p < 0.001) in the NWEHS group were significantly higher than those in the HWEHS group (Figure 4b,d). Similarly, renal NGAL expression and Mb deposition were significantly increased in both groups compared with the control group, peaking at 48 h (Figure 4e,f,i,j). At this time point, both markers were significantly higher in the NWEHS group than in the HWEHS group (p < 0.0001) (Figure 4g,h,k). Together, these results indicate that kidney injury in the NWEHS group is more severe than that in the HWEHS group.

Effects of different humidity levels

Mice subjected to EHS at 55% humidity exhibited a survival rate of 80%, whereas the survival rate at 75% humidity was 20% (Figure 2b). Due to the critically low survival rate observed at 75% humidity, this condition was excluded from subsequent analyses.

Renal injury was assessed at 24, 48, and 72h under 55% and 65% RH. CRE, BUN, and kidney injury scores increased after heat exposure in both groups, reaching peak levels at 48h, with greater increases in the 65% RH group (Figure 2c,d). Under 55% RH, CRE and BUN levels, as well as kidney injury scores, were not significantly different from those of the control group at 24h or 72h. In contrast, under 65% RH, serum CRE (p < 0.05) and BUN (p < 0.01) levels were significantly elevated at 24h compared with the control group. Histological examination (Figure 2d) showed tubular epithelial necrosis, cast formation, and inflammatory cell infiltration, with kidney injury scores significantly higher than those of the control group at both 24h (p < 0.01) and 72h (p < 0.01). These findings indicate that 65% RH induced more pronounced renal injury than 55% RH, with the most severe damage occurring at 48h.

Other biochemical parameters

Levels of Na, K, Ca, Cl, LDH, and CK were measured at 24, 48, and 72 h using the NWEHS model (Supplementary Table 1). Heat exposure following 12 h of water restriction before EHS resulted in alterations in several measured indicators. Compared with controls, Na was significantly decreased at 24 h (p < 0.001) and significantly increased at 48 h relative to the 24 h time point (p < 0.01). At 24 h, K (p < 0.01) and Cl (p < 0.05) were significantly elevated compared with controls. By 48 h after EHS, K (p < 0.001), LDH (p < 0.01), and CK (p < 0.05) levels were significantly elevated compared to the control group.

Cell experiments

To further investigate the direct role of myoglobin in renal tubular injury under hyperthermic conditions, we established an in vitro model using HK-2 cells. HK-2 cells were treated with Mb, Vit C, or Mb + Vit C and incubated at either 37 °C or 42.7 °C in 5% CO2 for 4 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. In the overall comparison, cell viability in the 42.7 °C HK2 + Mb group was significantly lower than that in the 37 °C HK2 + Mb group (p < 0.001), and cell viability in the 42.7 °C HK2 + Mb + Vit C group was significantly lower than that in the corresponding 37 °C HK2 + Mb + Vit C group (p < 0.01). In addition, the 42.7 °C HK2 + Mb + Vit C group showed significantly lower viability than the 42.7 °C HK2 group (p < 0.05) (Figure 5a). We then performed a subset analysis and found that, at 42.7 °C, the viability of the HK2 + Mb + Vit C group was significantly lower than that of the 42.7 °C HK2 group, the 37 °C HK2 group, and the 37 °C HK2 + Mb + Vit C group (p < 0.01) (Figure 5b). Under 42.7 °C, the viability of the HK2 + Mb + Vit C group was significantly lower compared to the HK2, HK2 + Mb, and HK2 + Vit C groups (Figure 5c), suggesting that the combined treatment caused more severe cellular injury than either treatment alone. Furthermore, when the concentration ratio of Mb to Vit C was 1:2, the HK2 cell viability was at its lowest, approximately 54.59 ± 6.373% (Figure 5d).

Discussion

Exertional heat stroke is a major cause of severe organ dysfunction during intense exercise in hot environments, yet kidney-focused models remain limited. In this study, we established an animal model that reproduced key features of EHS-AKI, including significant elevations in CRE and BUN and histopathological evidence of renal tubular injury. Notably, our model’s mortality rate is 50%, which is closer to the mortality rate of EHS in humans [23]. In addition, the model did not require any additional aversive stimuli, thereby reducing potential confounding factors compared with previous approaches [24]. Therefore, this model may serve as a useful platform for mechanistic and interventional studies of EHS-AKI.

Under normothermic conditions, dehydration can often be compensated by antidiuretic hormone, aldosterone, and related mechanisms that help maintain renal perfusion, and therefore is usually insufficient to cause overt AKI [27,28]. In contrast, during exercise heat stress, dehydration exacerbates renal injury through several interacting mechanisms. Heat stress redistributes cardiac output toward the skin and skeletal muscle, markedly reducing renal perfusion [29]. Elevated core temperature can directly injure renal tubules and trigger inflammatory responses [30]. Exercise-induced rhabdomyolysis releases myoglobin, whose nephrotoxicity is enhanced at high temperatures; dehydration further concentrates the urine, promoting cast formation and tubular obstruction [31]. In addition, substantial sodium loss and vasopressin activation further contribute to functional volume depletion [32]. Overall, the contribution of dehydration to AKI is context dependent [28]. The combination of heat stress, renal hypoperfusion due to blood flow redistribution, and increased metabolic burden is a key feature distinguishing exercise-associated AKI from simple dehydration under normothermic conditions. Our research findings suggest that a 12-h water deprivation before the experiment exacerbates kidney injury. Dehydration can lead to perfusion deficiency [33], thereby worsening heat stroke. Furthermore, mice have a lower number of sweat glands and primarily rely on respiration to regulate body temperature [34]. Therefore, the water deprivation before our experiment better simulates the perfusion deficiency that may occur in humans after exercise.

In our experiment, continuous temperature monitoring was utilized, which is consistent with the observations made by Leon LR [35]. We also observed a biphasic thermoregulatory response through the temperature monitoring system, with reports indicating its occurrence in humans as well [36]. Recent studies, such as M Alzahrani et al. [37], have demonstrated that slowing the cooling rate by maintaining animals in a 30 °C environment can prevent this hypothermia, thereby creating a thermal profile that more closely mimics human physiology for metabolic investigations. However, in the present study, we adhered to a standard ambient temperature recovery protocol. This decision was based on previous literature indicating that artificially preventing hypothermia can lead to increased intestinal damage and higher mortality rates [38]. Regarding the implications of this protocol, the decision not to prevent hypothermia allowed us to observe the natural progression of the injury. The phenomenon of hypothermia has been attributed to dysfunction in the thermoregulatory center in some studies, while others suggest that excessive inflammation may be the cause [39]. In our study, the lowest recorded temperature during the hypothermic phase reached 28 °C, with the longest recovery time extending to 15.16 h. Notably, the renal injury in these mice was also the most severe, and the high mortality rate can be attributed to the failure to recover from hypothermia, which aligns with the findings presented in Leon LR’s previous article [40].

Since the mechanisms driving hypothermia (e.g., central dysfunction or profound inflammation) are likely linked to the severity of the heat insult, the duration of hypothermia served as a valuable physiological marker in our study. The observed correlation between prolonged hypothermia, severe renal injury, and mortality implies that the inability to spontaneously rewarm reflects a critical threshold of systemic injury. In terms of translation, while the specific hypothermic phenotype is less common in clinical heat stroke patients, it functionally represents a state of physiological decompensation. Therefore, the phenomenon of hypothermia observed in mice can be interpreted as a substitute indicator for the exhaustion of compensatory mechanisms in severe human cases, thus confirming the applicability of this model in studying mortality and terminal organ damage. Further research is needed in the future to clarify the causal relationship between this hypothermia period and specific organ damage pathways, and to explore corresponding response measures.

Both air temperature and relative humidity are critical factors in the development of heatstroke [41]. Dongyan He et al. established a meteorological threshold system for heatstroke: daily mean temperature ≥ 30 °C and daily mean relative humidity (RH) ≤ 80%, or daily maximum temperature ≥ 35 °C and daily minimum RH ≤ 65% [42]. Consistent with these findings, higher humidity in our model was associated with increased mortality. Although the 75% RH condition may represent a more severe exposure setting, the very low survival rate under this condition limited further time-course analysis. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on the 55% and 65% RH groups across the 24, 48, and 72h time points, in which renal injury was more severe at 65% RH, as evidenced by higher BUN and CRE levels. Mechanistically, elevated ambient relative humidity reduces the water vapor pressure gradient between the skin and the surrounding air [43]. Consequently, even with sustained sweating, evaporative efficiency is markedly impaired. Given that evaporative heat loss represents the predominant cooling mechanism during exertional heat stress, this leads to insufficient heat dissipation, progressive heat accumulation, and a more rapid elevation of core body temperature [43,44]. Simultaneously, ongoing sweating accelerates fluid and electrolyte loss, resulting in dehydration and reduced circulating volume, which further compromises circulatory compensation and thermoregulatory capacity, ultimately exacerbating renal injury [44,45]. Concurrently, the reduction in serum sodium and elevation in serum potassium at 24 h following NWEHS suggest early changes in cell permeability associated with EHS, possibly involving the Na+/K+-ATPase pump-mediated entry of sodium into cells. The subsequent increase in Na at 48h may reflect progressive dehydration and free water loss after heat exposure, leading to relative hemoconcentration. During the 2003 Paris heatwave, hyponatremia was reported in 32% of patients, while hypernatremia was observed in 17%, with hypernatremia identified as an independent risk factor for mortality [46]. As observed in our experiments, renal injury was most pronounced at 48h, coinciding with elevated levels of K, Na, CK, and LDH, indicative of rhabdomyolysis, renal tubular damage, and acidosis.

Current clinical studies indicate that EHS frequently leads to rhabdomyolysis. Elevated myoglobin levels have been directly correlated with both AKI and mortality in patients with rhabdomyolysis following EHS [15]. Moreover, our in vivo findings showed marked myoglobin deposition in renal tissue, supporting the rationale for using myoglobin in vitro to mimic renal tubular injury in EHS-associated rhabdomyolysis. Research on EHS, particularly EHS-AKI, remains limited. In cell experiments, myoglobin is currently used to simulate rhabdomyolysis induced by heat stroke [13]. Our team has previously demonstrated that ferrous myoglobin mediates renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis through endoplasmic reticulum stress in rhabdomyolysis-induced kidney injury, highlighting the critical role of ferrous myoglobin [47]. Some studies also suggest that ferrous myoglobin is more likely to cause kidney cell damage [48–51], possibly due to its central iron ion being ferrous ion (Fe2+). This conformation may lead to increased exposure of the heme [52], thereby heightening the potential for cellular damage. In previous in vitro studies, vitamin C was commonly used in combination with myoglobin to induce ferrous myoglobin formation [47,49]. Yanhua Gong’s research [49] indicated that damage to renal tubules was most pronounced at a ferrous myoglobin concentration of 200 μM. However, the study did not specify the ratio of Mb to Vit C. In our study, we developed a cell model of EHS-AKI under heat exposure using ferrous myoglobin. We found that when the ratio of Mb to Vit C was 1:2, the CCK-8 assay revealed a more pronounced decrease in cell viability after 4 h of exposure at 42.7 °C with 5% CO2. The 1:2 Mb:Vit C ratio also induced the highest LDH release and the strongest TUNEL fluorescence signal, further supporting that this condition caused the most severe HK-2 cell injury and apoptosis. Consistent with this finding, the kidney injury marker NGAL was significantly elevated in HK-2 cells under the same condition, further confirming the enhanced cellular injury. This increase may be due to the high temperature catalyzing reduction reactions. Notably, the elevation of NGAL observed in vitro aligns with the increased NGAL expression detected in our in vivo model, supporting the translational relevance of this cell model. Therefore, this cell model is more suitable for simulating the condition of renal tubular epithelial cells during thermal injury.

However, this study also has its limitations and challenges. First, the experiments were conducted in mice, and the generalizability of the findings should be validated in other animal models. Second, we focused on acute injury within 24–72 h, whereas the pathophysiology of EHS-AKI is complex and its long-term outcomes and progression to chronic kidney injury remain unclear. Third, direct blood pressure measurements were not performed in this study due to the critically ill status of animals after EHS induction; additional hemodynamic monitoring could have imposed extra procedural stress and potentially affected survival. Consequently, our ability to directly assess volume status is limited. Fourth, although the in vitro model is the first to combine ferrous myoglobin with heat stress, it cannot fully recapitulate the complex systemic factors present in vivo. Finally, our mechanistic investigation mainly focused on tubular epithelial cells, and the roles of immune cell infiltration and tubulointerstitial crosstalk require further clarification. Identifying earlier biomarkers and integrating clinical data will be important priorities in future studies.

In summary, our in vivo and in vitro models recapitulate the core pathophysiological processes of EHS-AKI, including ischemic injury, myoglobin-mediated tubular toxicity, and electrolyte imbalance. In vivo, dehydration and heat exposure likely reduced renal perfusion, whereas increased creatine kinase, myoglobin deposition, and NGAL expression supported rhabdomyolysis-associated tubular injury, accompanied by electrolyte disturbances. In vitro, ferrous myoglobin directly injured tubular cells under heat stress, and their combination further supported the synergistic contribution of rhabdomyolysis and thermal injury to epithelial damage. Collectively, these complementary models provide a reliable experimental platform for mechanistic studies and therapeutic exploration in EHS-AKI.

Conclusions

We successfully developed a reproducible mouse model of EHS-AKI through continuous capsule temperature monitoring and a controlled temperature-humidity environment, incorporating assessments of renal function and pathological changes. By combining ferrous myoglobin with heat exposure, we identified a more suitable model for thermal injury in renal tubular epithelial cells. Together, these complementary models provide a robust methodological platform for future mechanistic and interventional studies of EHS-AKI.
