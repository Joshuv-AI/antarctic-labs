Introduction

In this work, we present improved distributed algorithms for degree splitting problems in the LOCAL model. As a corollary we also obtain faster algorithms for classic edge coloring problems.

The LOCAL model
[
30
]
.

We work in the standard LOCAL model of distributed computation. The network is represented as a graph, where each node hosts a processor and communication proceeds in synchronous rounds. In each round, nodes can exchange arbitrarily large messages with their neighbors and perform unbounded local computation. The complexity of an algorithm is measured solely by the number of communication rounds.

Degree splitting.

Degree splitting problems are among the most basic problems in distributed graph algorithms that resist straightforward greedy solutions. Despite their simple formulation, they have received considerable attention due to both their intrinsic combinatorial interest and their applications, e.g.,
[
7
,
23
,
6
,
19
,
5

25
,
16
,
8
,
9
]
. In the
undirected degree splitting
problem, the task is to color the edges of a graph with two colors, red and blue, such that the discrepancy between the number of incident red and blue edges at every node is minimized. The objective in the
directed degree splitting
problem is to orient the edges of a graph such that for each node the difference between its number of incoming and outgoing edges is minimized.

Which discrepancy is possible?

From a centralized perspective, the problem admits a simple solution via an Euler tour argument, and more generally it connects to classical discrepancy theory
[
15
]
. By adding a dummy vertex to connect odd-degree nodes and traversing an Euler tour, alternating the colors along the tour and removing the dummy vertex yields a discrepancy of at most
2
. In the directed variant, orienting edges along such a tour even gives a discrepancy of at most
1
.
In the distributed setting, however, the problem becomes substantially more challenging. The global structure exploited by standard centralized arguments is not directly accessible, and known distributed approaches rely on more involved techniques such as augmenting paths
[
23
]
or bootstrapping techniques
[
19
]
for amplifying balanced orientations.

Degree splittings in the bigger scope of distributed complexity theory.

Although degree splitting problems are easy to state and well understood centrally, they capture a fundamental source of difficulty in distributed computation. The necessity of global coordination inherent in these problems can be formalized: each admits a logarithmic lower bound on the round complexity, which holds even for constant-degree graphs
[
11
,
6
]
.
In contrast to the classical
’big four’
problems—maximal independent set, maximal matching,
(\Delta+1)
-vertex coloring, and
(2\Delta-1)
-edge coloring—which admit
\mathcal{O}(\log^{*}n)

[

They are closely tied to the Lovász Local Lemma (LLL)
[
12

6
,

,

,
8
]

The distributed complexity of problems in the LLL regime is central to the landscape of locally checkable labeling problems and underlies many recent advances in sublogarithmic and randomized distributed algorithms

Degree splitting and the logarithmic time complexity barrier.

The logarithmic barrier already appears in the
sinkless orientation
problem, which can be interpreted as a directed degree splitting with discrepancy
\max(\deg(v)-1,2)
.
The problem is known to have a
\Theta(\log n)
round complexity on general graphs
[
11
,
23
]
, making it one of the few natural problems with a tight intermediate complexity on general graphs.
The same logarithmic lower bound extends to weak (undirected) degree splitting
[
6
]
where the goal is to color edges red and blue so that each node has at least one edge of each color.

Thus, the
\Theta(\log n)
barrier already arises for the largest meaningful discrepancy. Consequently, the goal is not to circumvent this barrier but to understand the trade-offs within it, namely how the round complexity depends on the allowed discrepancy. This dependence is crucial in recursive divide-and-conquer applications, where one requires discrepancy
\varepsilon\cdot\deg(v)
with
\varepsilon=o(1)
and the overall efficiency hinges on how the runtime scales with
\varepsilon

Limitations for Degree Splitting Algorithms.

We have already discussed the inherent logarithmic barrier for degree splitting problems. Our algorithm achieves a runtime of
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
for a discrepancy of
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{deg}(v)
at every vertex
v\in V
. The first distributed algorithm for degree splitting
[
23
]
was based on augmenting paths. We prove that the
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
bound is tight for naïve augmenting-path–based approaches, even though our own algorithm does not rely on augmenting paths.
Formally, we show a

[
4
]

Theorem 4
.

Computing a directed degree splitting with discrepancy at most
\varepsilon\cdot\Delta

\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot\log n)

Open Problem I: Optimality of the runtime.

Our algorithms achieve a round complexity of
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
for a local discrepancy of
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{deg}(v)
. Is this dependence optimal? In particular, can one improve the
\varepsilon^{-1}
factor, or obtain a bound in which the discrepancy-dependent term appears only additively in the runtime? More generally, does a matching lower bound hold beyond the limitations of augmenting path-based techniques?

Open Problem II: Multi-way splitting.

Our edge-coloring results rely on recursively applying

1

Related Work

Degree splitting

The first authors to consider degree splits in the distributed setting were Hanćkowiak, Karoński and Panconesi
[
26
]
.
They used degree splits as a subroutine for their breakthrough result of designing the first polylogarithmic deterministic distributed maximal matching algorithm.
However, to achieve this result they had to work with a relaxed version of the problem, allowing a
1/\mathrm{poly}\log n
fraction of nodes to have arbitrary splits.
The first algorithm solving the degree splitting problem in full generality only came much later, when Ghaffari and Su released their influential paper
[
23
]
.
The paper is best-known for its seminal sinkless orientation algorithm.
Nevertheless, it also shows how to compute more balanced orientations, as well as undirected splits.
Surprisingly, their splitting algorithms do not use sinkless orientation as a subroutine—instead, they rely on an augmenting-path based approach.
As a result, they obtain a deterministic directed degree splitting algorithm in
\mathcal{O}(\log^{7}n/\varepsilon^{3})
rounds and a randomized counterpart in
\mathcal{O}(\log^{4}n/\varepsilon^{3})
rounds.
For the undirected setting, they obtain a deterministic algorithm of complexity
\mathcal{O}(\log^{7}n/\varepsilon^{3})
for a sufficiently large
\Delta
and randomized algorithm of complexity
\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}\log^{4}n/\varepsilon^{2})
, which works for all
\Delta
.
All of their algorithms compute a balanced split with discrepancy at most
\varepsilon\cdot\Delta
.
The next improvement, and the current state-of-the-art, comes from
[
19
]

[
23
]
to amplify balanced orientation.
Their deterministic algorithm relies on a recursive bootstrapping procedure and computes both directed and undirected splits with a discrepancy of

\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\varepsilon^{-1}(\log\log\varepsilon^{-1})^{1.71}\cdot\log n)

Most recently,
[

further generalized the above work by providing an algorithm for mixed directed and undirected problem with the same discrepancy. Moreover, they reduce the additive constant in the discrepancy for undirected splits from

In

1.3
Our Algorithm in a Nutshell

For simplicity’s sake, suppose
G=(V,E)
is a
\Delta
-regular graph and focus on the directed version.

Split and orient.

For each vertex
v\in V
, we create
\lfloor\Delta/2\rfloor
virtual copies of degree
2
and possibly one degree-
1
node if
\Delta
is odd. For directed degree splitting from a centralized perspective we can then simply orient all paths and cycles consistently.
The discrepancy at a vertex
v\in V
can then be bounded by the number of paths originating at a copy of
v
.
Hence, the discrepancy will be
0
for nodes of even degree and
1
for nodes of odd degree.

From centralized to distributed

The main bottleneck with this approach is that consistently orienting paths—for the sake of this overview assume that the split graph has no cycles—in a distributed manner has an overhead that is proportional to the length of the path. The natural solution is to chop each very long path into shorter ones.

Why is standard symmetry breaking not enough?

A commonly used tool to break long paths into short ones in distributed models are ruling sets
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
Ruling sets are a relaxed notion of maximal independent sets and come with a parameter
k
, which prescribes the maximal distance that any vertex can have to the ruling set.
Hence, by chopping the path at every ruling set vertex, we can obtain a sequence of shorter paths of maximum length
k
.
However, these chops come at a price.
At every
chopping point
we introduce new start/endpoints of shorter paths and as a result there is no coordination between the orientation of these paths. As a result, the discrepancy of the vertex of
G
hosting the split may increase by an additive two for every chopping point that it hosts.
Since the classic symmetry breaking tools do not come equipped with any kind of load balancing functionalities, it may happen that we are left with a very imbalanced split for some unlucky vertices.

Balancing chopping points via HSO

Ensuring that we do not chop at too many virtual copies of the same vertex can be seen as a resource allocation problem.
The players in this problem are the nodes in the ruling set
\mathcal{R}
, which want to chop the path in their vicinity.
The resources are the vertices
V
of
G
that offer the chopping points. In order to limit the discrepancy at each node, each vertex
v\in V
divides its virtual copies into
x
buckets of approximately equal sizes.
In each bucket, we allow at most one chop. This yields a discrepancy of at most
2x
for even-degree vertices and
2x+1
for odd-degree vertices.
To each node
r
in the ruling set, we associate a subpath
P(r)
of length
\ell
.
Now we set up a hypergraph sinkless orientation instance
H
.
Each node
r\in\mathcal{R}
will model a vertex in
H
.
Each bucket
B_{1}(v),\dots,B_{x}(v)
of each vertex
v\in V
will model a hyperedge.
The hyperedge corresponding to a bucket
B
contains all ruling set nodes, where the subpath
P(r)
contains at least one node of the bucket.
The maximum rank of
H
is then easily bounded by
\Delta/x
.
Controlling the minimum degree is a bit more tricky, since a subpath might contain multiple nodes from the same bucket aka hyperedge.
Hence, we will need to construct subpaths containing nodes from at least
2\Delta/x
different buckets. Under this condition, HSO can be solved efficiently to determine where to chop paths such each vertex of the graph is at the start/end of few paths effectively limiting the discrepancy.

Towards optimal discrepancy.

The outlined approach results in a discrepancy of
2x+1
. This is perfectly satisfactory if we set
x=\varepsilon\cdot\Delta
. However, for very precisely balanced splits, where we want to set
x=1
, we would like to get the discrepancy for odd nodes down to just the optimal value of one.
To achieve this, we compute a sinkless orientation on another graph, where we merge the three degree-one copies of odd-degree vertices into one virtual node of degree three. A sinkless orientation on this graph finally then the discrepancy from three down to one.

Extension to undirected splitting.

For the unbalanced splitting problem we do not aim to orient paths consistently but to color them red/blue in an alternating manner. Here, additional care has to be taken with short cycles, e.g., a cycle of length three does not allow for a red/blue coloring that is balanced for all of its vertices. Hence, we perform a preprocessing on the graph that effectively changes the way the graph is split to remove all short cycles. Long cycles are not a problem because we will chop them in the middle anyway and thus we can deal with them just like long paths.

, but it is simple enough to restate it here.

Corollary 15
.

Let
s\geq 3
be an integer. An orientation where each node
v
of degree
\deg(v)
has at least
\lfloor\deg(v)/s\rfloor
outgoing and incoming edges can be computed in
O(\log_{s}n)
rounds of deterministic
Local
and with high probability in
O(\log_{s}\log n)
rounds of randomized
Local
.

Proof.

For each vertex
v
of degree
\mathrm{deg}(v)
we create
\lfloor\mathrm{deg}(v)/s\rfloor
copies and divide the edges incident to
v
evenly among the copies.
The virtual graph obtained this way has minimum degree
s
.
Hence, we can apply
Lemma

9
to obtain a sink- and sourceless orientation of this virtual graph within
\mathcal{O}(\log_{s}n)
rounds deterministically or
\mathcal{O}(\log_{s}\log n)
rounds randomized.
Lifting this orientation back to
G
, we get that each vertex
v
has at least
\lfloor\mathrm{deg}(v)/s\rfloor
incoming and outgoing edges.
∎

Since sinkless orientation only guarantees an outgoing edge for nodes of degree at least three, the discrepancy achievable with this approach is capped at
\mathrm{deg}(v)/3
.
Hence, in order to go below this barrier, we need to adopt a more aggressive splitting strategy.
We split each vertex into virtual copies of degree
2
(with one additional degree-one copy for odd-degree vertices).
Our splitting strategy comes with the clear upside that it is now very easy to compute a near-perfect split from a centralized perspective. All nodes on the same path/cycle decide on a consistent orientation of the path/cycle. This procedure clearly produces an orientation where no node of degree two is a source or a sink.
Since the edges of the split graph are in a one-to-one correspondence with the edges of
G
, we can immediately lift the orientation back to
G
.
All even-degree vertices are thus perfectly split, while for odd-degree vertices we get an unavoidable discrepancy of one.

However, there is also a very clear downside of this approach.
Without any additional modifications, it is a very inefficient distributed algorithm.
The maximum length of a path in the split graph might be linear in the number of edges and even the weak diameter in
G
might still be linear in the number of nodes.
Hence, consistently orienting every path and cycle in such a split graph might take up to
\Omega(n)
rounds in
Local
.

Therefore, we need to construct a split graph, where each path or cycle has a small weak diameter in
G
.
We start with an arbitrary split graph of the input graph
G
.

Definition 16

(
G_{\mathrm{split}}
)
.

We define the split graph
G_{\mathrm{split}}=(V_{\mathrm{split}},E)
as follows:
Each vertex
v
obtains a list
N(v)=[w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{\mathrm{deg}(v)}]
of all its neighbors sorted in ascending order of IDs.
Then, it creates
\lceil\mathrm{deg}(v)/2\rceil
virtual copies and assigns the edge
(v,w_{i})
to its
\lceil i/2\rceil
-th virtual copy.
Symmetrically, the node
w_{i}
assigns the edge
(v,w_{i})
to its
\lceil j/2\rceil
-th virtual copy, if
v
is the neighbor with
j
-th lowest ID of
w_{i}
.
We denote the set of all virtual copies of
v
by
\mathrm{split}(v)
.
Further, for any
w\in\mathrm{split}(v)
we call

the
creator
of
w
.

To avoid confusion we will reserve the term
vertex
to refer to vertices of
G
and
node
to refer to nodes of
G_{\mathrm{split}}
.
Note that the construction of
G_{\mathrm{split}}
does not require coordination between the vertices of
G
and further, one round on
G_{\mathrm{split}}
can be simulated in one round of
G
in
Local
.
Furthermore, when we talk about distances in the split graph, we sometimes need to refer back to the communication network

.

Definition 17

(Weak diameter)
.

Let
v,w\in V,v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{split}(v)
and
w^{\prime}\in\mathrm{split}(w)
.
Then, with slight abuse of notation we write

to refer to the distance between

and

in
G
.
Furthermore, for any subgraph

we define the

as the diameter of the subgraph of

induced by the creators of nodes in
H
.

Now we want to modify
G_{\mathrm{split}}
in such a way that all paths and cycles disappear, while chopping at most
x(v)
degree-2 copies of each vertex

.
The first goal could be achieved by computing a maximal independent set
S
on the power graph

.
Chopping the paths at every node

would produce paths of length at most

Hence, we let each vertex split all its virtual copies into

Definition 18

(buckets)
.

Let
v
be a vertex of degree
\mathrm{deg}(v)
in

and

R_{i}(v):=\{w_{j}:(i-1)\cdot\mathrm{deg}(v)/x(v)\leq j<i\cdot\mathrm{deg}(v)/x(v)\}

of

Further, we write

and

.

Definition 19

(Hypergraph for HSO)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of pairwise disjoint subpaths of
G_{\mathrm{split}}
. Then we define a hypergraph

on the vertex set
\mathcal{P}
as follows:
For each bucket
R_{i}(v)\in\mathcal{R}
we add a hyperedge

e(R_{i}(v)):=\{P\in\mathcal{P}:P\cap R_{i}(v)\neq\emptyset\}
.

Recall that solving HSO efficiently requires a hypergraph

, where the minimum degree
\delta
exceeds the maximum rank
r
.
The maximum rank of

is clearly bounded by the maximum size of a bucket

.
For each bucket

we have that
\lvert R_{i}(v)\rvert=\lceil\mathrm{deg}(v)/x(v)\rceil=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
.
Hence, we need to construct a set
\mathcal{P}
such that every path
P\in\mathcal{P}
intersects at least
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1})
buckets.

To construct the set
\mathcal{P}
that we will use to define our hypergraph, we first let each node
v
in

select a subpath
B(v)\ni v
of its own component in
G_{\mathrm{split}}
. The subpath

should contain nodes from at least
\ell
distinct buckets, where
\ell
is a parameter that we can tune to satisfy the degree-rank condition.
For instance, if
x(v)=\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{deg}(v)
for all

, it suffices to choose

.
As the second step, we use ruling sets on the intersection graph

of
\mathcal{B}=\{B(v):v\in V_{A}\}
as a subroutine in order to compute a set of pairwise disjoint paths
\mathcal{P}
such that paths and cycles of

are small in diameter.
Then, we can apply the deterministic HSO algorithm from
[
9
]
to assign one bucket exclusively to each voting block in
\mathcal{P}
.
For each voting block
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\varphi(P)
denote the bucket assigned to

by the HSO procedure.
By construction of

, the voting block

now contains a virtual node of the bucket
\varphi(P)
that it can split into two virtual nodes of degree one.
Denote this new virtual graph obtained this way by
G^{\prime}
.
Now, all paths and cycles in
G^{\prime}
have a small weak diameter in

and we can efficiently compute a consistent orientation of all of them.
Finally, we observe that the discrepancy of any vertex

is at most
2\cdot x(v)+1
.
Each vertex

is split into
x(v)
buckets.
For each bucket, at most one virtual node is split into two degree-one node and all other nodes get one incoming and one outgoing edge.
Since odd-degree vertices have an additional degree-one node, this yields a total discrepancy of at most
2\cdot x(v)+1
.

3.3
Randomized Algorithm

In order to obtain a faster randomized version of our algorithm, we make three crucial changes:
Firstly, we replace the deterministic ruling set algorithm by a faster randomized algorithm.
Secondly, we replace the deterministic HSO algorithm by its faster randomized counterpart.
The randomized HSO algorithm imposes a stricter degree-rank condition on us, which forces us to increase the size

Lemma 33
.

Let
\ell\leq D
be a positive integer, with
D
the diameter of the graph.
Then, there is a deterministic distributed algorithm of round complexity
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
that computes a split of each vertex of degree
\mathrm{deg}(v)
into
\lfloor\mathrm{deg}(v)/2\rfloor
virtual copies of degree two (and one copy of degree one for vertices of odd degree) such that each cycle in the split graph has true length at least
\ell
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a cycle in
G_{\mathrm{split}}
and
X
be a cycle or a path in
G_{\mathrm{split}}
. We define the distance between
C
and
X
as
\mathrm{dist}(C,X):=\min_{v\in C,v^{\prime}\in X}\mathrm{dist}_{G}(v,v^{\prime})
.
We call a cycle
C
in
G_{\mathrm{split}}
short if the true length
\ell^{\star}(C)
of
C
is less than
\ell
and long otherwise. For any short cycle
S
we set
f(S):=\ell^{\star}(S)
, while for any long cycle
C
we set
f(C):=\infty
. We also set
f(P):=\infty
for any path
P
in
G_{\mathrm{split}}
.
Each vertex
v\in V
starts by determining the set of short cycles
\mathcal{S}(v)
containing at least one virtual copy of
v
.
This can be done in
\mathcal{O}(\ell)
rounds of
Local
.
For each short cycle
S\in\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{v\in V}\mathcal{S}(v)
we write
\mathrm{Min}(S)
to denote the the vertex with minimum ID among all creators of nodes in
S
and let
\mathrm{Min}(S)
handle all computations regarding
S
.
Now each cycle
S
finds a nearby path or the largest nearby cycle
\mathrm{Max}(S):=\mathrm{argmax}\{f(X):\mathrm{dist}(X,S)\leq 2\ell\}
. To break ties we use the IDs of
\mathrm{Min}(S)
concatenated with the smaller ID of the two creators of nodes adjacent to
\mathrm{Min}(S)
on
S
.
We write
d(S)=\mathrm{dist}(S,\mathrm{Max}(S))
.
Note that
\mathrm{Min}(S)
only needs to collect its
4\ell
-hop neighborhood in
G
to determine
\mathrm{Max}(S)
. Each path or cycle
X
satisfying
\mathrm{dist}(S,X)\leq 2\ell
has to contain a node
v
such that
\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{Min}(S),v)\leq\ell+\mathrm{dist}(S,X)\leq 3\ell
.
Next, using just the
\ell
-hop neighborhood around the creator of
v
,
\mathrm{Min}(S)
can determine whether
f(X)=\ell^{\star}(X)
or
f(X)=\infty
.
Further, if
\mathrm{Max}(S)\neq S
,
S
identifies a target path or cycle
\mathrm{Tar}(S)
such that
\mathrm{dist}(S,\mathrm{Tar}(S))=0
, that is, there are nodes
v\in S
and
v^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Tar}(S)
that share a common creator, and
\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{Tar}(S),\mathrm{Max}(S))\leq d(S)-1
.
Now, for all short cycles
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S\neq\mathrm{Max}(S)

in parallel, we modify the local vertex splits such that
S
and
\mathrm{Tar}(S)
are joined to form a larger path or cycle according to
Lemmas

31
and
32
.
After executing all those merge operations in parallel, let
\mathrm{New}(S)
denote the new connected component that
S
now a part of. If
\mathrm{New}(S)
is a path, we are done, as our goal is to delete short cycles. We do not need to argue about the true length of
\mathrm{New}(S)
if it is a path. Let us assume in what follows that
\mathrm{New}(S)
is a cycle, that is,
S
only merged with other cycles, and let analyze its length.

We claim that the weak diameter of
\mathrm{New}(S)
is at least
d(S)
, the distance between
S
and
\mathrm{Max}(S)
.
Let
S=S_{0},S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
denote the chain of cycle joins originating from
S
, where
S_{i+1}=\mathrm{Tar}(S_{i})
for all
i=0,\dots,k-1
.
First, we note that these merge chains can never close a cycle, that is, the graph induced by all cycle joins is acyclic itself.
Indeed suppose that
\mathrm{Tar}(S_{i})=S
for some
i<k
.
If
\mathrm{Max}(S_{i})=\mathrm{Max}(S)
, then
S
cannot be the target for
S_{i}
, as
\mathrm{dist}(S_{i},\mathrm{Max}(S))<\mathrm{dist}(S,\mathrm{Max}(S))
.
On the other hand, if
\mathrm{Max}(S_{i})\neq\mathrm{Max}(S)
, then it must hold that

f(\mathrm{Max}(S_{i}))\geq f(\mathrm{Max}(S_{i-1}))\geq\cdots\geq f(\mathrm{Max}(S)).

But
S
is also the target of
S_{i}
, which contradicts with the fact that either
f(\mathrm{Max}(S))<f(\mathrm{Max}(S_{i}))
or
\mathrm{Max}(S)
loses in the tiebreak with
\mathrm{Max}(S_{i})
.
Thus, if we consider a set of cycles merging together, they form a rooted tree if we put a directed edge from
C
to
C^{\prime}
in the set whenever
C^{\prime}=\mathrm{Tar}(C)
.
Executing the merge operations sequentially, from root to leaves, we progressively grow the root cycle with each merge until a single cycle remains, spanning the virtual nodes of all cycles in the rooted tree.

Next, we argue that the weak diameter of
\mathrm{New}(S)
is at least
d(S)
.
This is clear, if
C_{k}=\mathrm{Max}(S)
.
If
C_{k}\neq\mathrm{Max}(S)
, let
i^{\star}<k
be the smallest

such that
\mathrm{Max}(S_{i^{\star}})\neq\mathrm{Max}(S)
. Since
\mathrm{Max}(S_{i^{\star}})
beats
\mathrm{Max}(S)
either in terms of true length or tiebreaks we must have that
\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{Max}(S_{i^{\star}}),S)>\ell
.
Since
f
is monotonically increasing along the chain, this implies that the chain can never terminate at a cycle
C
with
\mathrm{dist}(S,C)\leq\ell
.
Hence, the weak diameter of
\mathrm{New}(S)
is at least
\ell\geq d(S)
.

If we are in the second case, we are already done, as the weak diameter is always a lower bound for the true length of a cycle.
Now suppose there still exists a short cycle
S
, where the true length of
\mathrm{New}(S)
is less than
\ell
. Unless the entire graph itself has small diameter (trivializing all our problems), there must be two cycles
C
and
C_{i}
such that
\mathrm{dist}(C,C_{i})=0
such that
C_{i}\subseteq\mathrm{New}(S)
and but
C\not\subseteq\mathrm{New}(S)
, i.e.,
C
has not been merged with
S
. Since
\mathrm{Tar}(C)\neq C_{i}
and
\mathrm{Tar}(C_{i})\neq\mathrm{Tar}(C)
, we have that
\mathrm{Max}(C_{i})=\mathrm{Max}(S)
. Since
\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{Max}(S),C)\leq 2\ell
, this implies that
\mathrm{dist}(S,\mathrm{Max}(C_{i}))>2\ell
.
Hence, after joining
\mathrm{New}(S)
with
\mathrm{New}(C)
, we get a cycle with weak diameter at least
\ell
(or a path, if
\mathrm{New}(C)
is a path).
Importantly, since
\mathrm{dist}(S,S_{i})<\ell
, this implies that
\mathrm{New}(C)
already had a weak diameter of at least
\ell
on its own.
Since this step only joins cycles of true length less than
\ell
to cycles of weak diameter at least
\ell
(or paths), we get that our graph of cycle joins remains acyclic and we never execute a cycle join on two virtual nodes that are part of the same cycle.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, we represent a partial orientation of the edges of that graph as an assignment
\psi:E\to\{-,+,\bot\}
.
For an edge
e\in E
,
\psi(e)=\bot
corresponds to
e
being unoriented.
For an edge
e=uv\in E
, with
u<v
(i.e.,
v
has a larger identifier than
u
),
\psi(uv)=+
represents that the edge is oriented from
u
to
v
. Conversely
\psi(uv)=-
indicates that the edge is oriented in the opposite way, from
v
to
u
.
When
\psi(e)\neq\bot
for all
e\in E
,
\psi
is said to be a full orientation.
We say that a partial solution
\psi^{\prime}
extends another partial solution
\psi
if for each
e\in E
, either
\psi^{\prime}(e)=\bot\implies\psi(e)=\bot
or
\psi(e)\neq\bot\implies\psi(e)=\psi^{\prime}(e)
.
For two solution
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
, their difference
\psi\triangle\psi^{\prime}
is the set of edges
\{e\in E\mid\psi(e)\neq\psi^{\prime}(e)\}
.

For each vertex
v
, let us partition its neighbors according to whether their identifiers are lower or greater than that of
v
. That is, we define
N^{+}(v)=\{u\in N(v)\mid u>v\}
and
N^{-}(v)=N(v)\setminus N^{+}(v)
.
A partial solution to the problem of computing a directed splitting with discrepancy
\varepsilon\Delta
is an assignment
\psi
satisfying the following properties at each vertex
v

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{-1}(+)\cap N^{+}(v)\rvert+\lvert\psi^{-1}(-)\cap N^{-}(v)\rvert\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\ ,

\displaystyle\lvert\psi^{-1}(-)\cap N^{+}(v)\rvert+\lvert\psi^{-1}(+)\cap N^{-}(v)\rvert\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\ .

Intuitively, the first equation indicates that a vertex
v
does not have too many outgoing edges, while the second indicates that it does not have too many incoming edges.
When these equations hold at a vertex
v
, it is always possible to give an orientation to the unoriented edges incident to
v
such that the discrepancy at
v
is at most
\varepsilon\Delta
.

However this is a very local property. It might not be possible to give an orientation to unoriented edges such that all vertices have discrepancy below
\varepsilon\Delta
.
In fact, we will show that there exist a graph
G=(V,E)
, a partial assignment
\psi
, and an edge
e\in\psi^{-1}(\bot)
, such that for any partial assignment
\psi^{\prime}
extending
\psi
on
e
(i.e.,
\psi^{\prime}(e)\neq\bot
), the graph induced by the edges in
\psi\triangle\psi^{\prime}
has diameter
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
.
The mending radius measures exactly this: given a partial assignment
\psi
, which we extend to a new edge
e
, how large can the diameter of the subgraph induced by the changes made to
\psi
be?

Definition 37

(Mendability, mending radius
[
4
]
)
.

For any integer
T
, a partial solution
\psi
on a graph
\Pi
is
t
-mendable at a vertex
v
if there exists a valid partial solution
\psi^{\prime}
extending
\psi
s.t.
\psi^{\prime}(e)\neq\bot
for all edges
e
incident to
v
and for every edge
uu^{\prime}\in\psi\triangle\psi^{\prime}
,
\max_{w\in\{u,u^{\prime}\}}\dist_{G}(v,w)\leq t
.

A problem
\Pi
on input graph family
\mathcal{G}
is
T
-mendable for a non-decreasing function
T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
if for every graph
G=(V,E)\in\mathcal{G}
, valid partial solution
\psi
on
G
, and vertex of
v
,
\psi
is
T(\lvert V\rvert)
-mendable at
v
.

A problem
\Pi
on input graph family
\mathcal{G}
has mending radius
\Theta(T(n))
for a non-decreasing function
T:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
if there exists two functions
T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}
s.t.
T^{\prime}(n)\in\Theta(T(n))
,
T^{\prime\prime}(n)\in\Theta(T(n))
, and
\Pi
on input graph family
\mathcal{G}
is
T^{\prime}
-mendable but not
T^{\prime\prime}
-mendable.

To show our mending lower bound, at a high level, we build a graph consisting of
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
ordered layers. The lowest layer contains unoriented edges between its vertices, while all other edges connect vertices in different layers and are oriented towards lower layers.
Each layer is only connected to the two layers preceding it and the two layers succeeding it, so that the graph has diameter
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
. Having all edges pointing in the direction of the lower layers makes it impossible to give an orientation to edges in the lowest layer without making changes in the higher layers.
A similar idea was used in
[
10
]
in the context of sub-
4\Delta/3
-edge coloring.

Proof.

For simplicity throughout the proof, we assume
\varepsilon\Delta/2
and
\Delta/2
to be integers, so that a fortiori
(1-\varepsilon)\Delta/2
and
(1+\varepsilon)\Delta/2
are integers.

We build a graph
G=(V,E)
and a partial solution
\psi
on
G

that is not
c\varepsilon^{-1}\log n
-mendable at some vertex
v
for a small enough constant
c\in\Theta(1)
.
At a high level, we construct a graph and a solution in which a lot of edges are oriented towards some unoriented clique.
In oriented parts of the graph, all vertices have the maximum allowed discrepancy, so as to create an imbalance between the number of edges pointing towards the unoriented clique and the number of edges pointing away from it.
In the unoriented clique, the unoriented edges ensure that no vertex has a discrepancy above the allowed threshold, but extending the solution to the clique cannot be done without changing the orientation of some edges at distance
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)
from the clique.

The graph consists of
D\in\Theta(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n/\Delta))
sets of vertices
V_{1},\dots,V_{D}
, forming layers.
For each
i\in[D-1]
,
\lvert V_{i+1}\rvert\approx\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\lvert V_{i}\rvert
.
Also, for each
i\in[3,D-2]
, each vertex
v\in V_{i}
has
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
edges to vertices in
V_{i+1}\sqcup V_{i+2}
, and
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
to vertices in
V_{i-1}\sqcup V_{i-2}

Vertices from the first set
V_{1}
form a
(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta+1)
-clique.
The next layers
V_{2},\dots,V_{D}
are constructed iteratively as follows:

•

Each vertex in
V_{1}
has
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
edges to vertices in
V_{2}\sqcup V_{3}
, for a total of
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{1}\rvert
. Let
V_{2}
be of size
\lfloor\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\lvert V_{1}\rvert\rfloor
, and add
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{2}\rvert
edges between
V_{2}
and
V_{1}
, respecting that each vertex in
V_{2}
is connected exactly
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
vertices in
V_{1}
, and each vertex in
V_{1}
is connected to at most
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
vertices in
V_{2}
.
Vertices in
V_{1}
are missing
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{1}\rvert-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\left\lfloor\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\lvert V_{1}\rvert\right\rfloor
incident edges for all of them to be of degree
\Delta
. Let us denote this number of missing edges by
m_{1}
.
By definition
m_{1}
is between
0
and
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
. We choose edges between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
s.t. exactly on vertex
w_{1}\in V_{1}
is missing
m_{1}
edges to be of degree
\Delta
.

•

When constructing
V_{3}
, we connect
m_{1}
vertices in
V_{3}
to
w_{1}
, before adding edges between
V_{2}
and
V_{3}
.

V_{3}
is chosen to be of size
\lfloor\frac{2}{(1-\varepsilon)\Delta}\cdot(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{2}\rvert+m_{1})\rfloor
.
Again, we add edges between vertices of
V_{2}
and
V_{3}
, such that all vertices in
V_{3}
have exactly
\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
edges to
V_{2}\cup\{w_{1}\}
, and all vertices in
V_{2}
except one have
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta
edges to
V_{3}
. One vertex
w_{2}\in V_{2}
has only
\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta-m_{2}
edges to
V_{3}
, where
m_{2}\in[0,\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\Delta)
.

•

We continue this construction recursively, choosing
\lvert V_{i+2}\rvert=\lfloor\frac{2}{(1-\varepsilon)\Delta}\cdot(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{i+1}\rvert+m_{i})\rfloor
for each
i\in[1,D-2]
, defining a vertex
w_{i}
and a number of missing edges
m_{i}
for each layer.

For
\varepsilon
close to
0
, layers grow by a multiplicative factor of about
1+2\varepsilon
.
As the first layer is of size
\Theta(\Delta)
, the process constructs a graph of size
\leq n
when choosing
D
, the number of layers, to be
c\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n/\Delta)
with a small enough constant
c
.

We now give a partial orientation
\psi
of that graph, and argue that it is not
(D/2-1)
-mendable.
For each edge between two vertices
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
s.t.
i<j
, we orient the edge towards
u
. That is, we orient edges towards lower layers. We do not orient edges in the clique formed by
V_{1}
.

Consider an orientation
\psi^{\prime}
extending
\psi
to edges of the clique formed by
V_{1}
. Let us denote
\bias(v,\psi)=\indeg(v)-\outdeg(v)
the difference between the indegree and outdegree of a vertex in a partial orientation
\psi
.
From all edges between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}\sqcup V_{3}
being oriented towards
V_{1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{1}}\bias(v,\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{1}\rvert\ .

Similarly, if we do the same sum over all layers from
V_{1}
to
V_{D-2}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D-2}\sum_{v\in V_{i}}\bias(v,\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\lvert V_{1}\rvert+\varepsilon\Delta\sum_{i=2}^{D-2}\lvert V_{i}\rvert

\displaystyle>\varepsilon\Delta\sum_{i=1}^{D-2}\lvert V_{i}\rvert.

That is, the average bias of a node
v\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{D-2}V_{i}
is strictly greater than
\varepsilon\Delta
.

Consider now the second orientation
\psi^{\prime}
, extending
\psi
to the clique formed by
V_{1}
. Suppose that
\psi^{\prime}
only differs from
\psi
on edges incident to the vertices in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{D-4}V_{i}
. Any change in that set of edges does not change the sum of biases.
Indeed, when changing the orientation of an edge
uv
in that set, the effect on the bias of
v
is the opposite of the effect on the bias of
u
, and both biases are in the sum. Therefore, if
\psi^{\prime}
only differs with
\psi
on edges within distance
D/2-2
from
V_{1}
, there must exist a vertex of bias greater than
\varepsilon\Delta
, meaning that the orientation does not have discrepancy
\leq\varepsilon\Delta
on all vertices.
Mending the partial solution therefore requires changes at distance
\Omega(D)=\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n/\Delta))
from where we want to extend the solution, and the problem has mending radius
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n/\Delta))
.
∎

An important caveat to our lower bound is that some problems have a mending radius far exceeding their complexity.
For instance, there exists a problem of mending radius
\Theta(n)
but complexity
O(\log^{*}n)
: coloring paths with
5
colors, where the colors are partitioned into a set of size
2
and another one of size
3
, and colors are only compatible within their set
[
4
, Theorem 7.1, arxiv version]
. An even more pathological example is the following LCL with
3
labels:
1
and
2
can only be adjacent to each other, and
3
can only be adjacent to itself. The problem has a trivial
0
round solution (output
3
everywhere) but mending radius
\Omega(n)
.
These examples show what to look out for: the mending radius can be overly pessimistic, because a problem can admit fragile hard-to-mend partial solutions that an efficient algorithm will simply never consider.
In fact, many problems admit randomized algorithms of complexity lower than the mending radius, showing that randomness is one way to avoid bad partial solutions.
