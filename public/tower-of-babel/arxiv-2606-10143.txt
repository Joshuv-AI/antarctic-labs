1
Introduction

The single-eigenvalue problem for stochastic matrices asks, for fixed
n
, which complex numbers can
occur as eigenvalues of
n\times n
row-stochastic matrices. The problem was solved in low dimensions
by Dmitriev and Dynkin and in full generality by Karpelevich

[
2
,
1
]
. The resulting region is now usually called the
Karpelevich region.

Although Karpelevich’s theorem is the accepted answer to the unrestricted problem, its proof is poorly
understood. The original argument is technically opaque, the statement of the theorem is long and
difficult to use directly, and this has motivated a line of work aimed at reformulating the result and
reproving its boundary structure rigorously with modern tools. Ito gave a cleaner formulation of the
boundary in terms of polynomial arcs
[
3
]
; Johnson and Paparella gave a matricial realization
of those arcs
[
4
]
; Kim and Kim formulated further conjectural descriptions

[
5
]
; Kirkland and Šmigoc studied stochastic realizations of the boundary

[
6
]
; and Munger, Nickerson, and Paparella explicitly return to
the problem of putting Karpelevich’s theorem on a clearer and more verifiable footing

[
7
]
. Thus the Karpelevich region is not merely a closed chapter in the
spectral theory of stochastic matrices: understanding its proof and boundary mechanism remains a
contemporary problem. The present paper should be read in this context. We do not use Karpelevich’s
theorem as an input, nor do we obtain the answer by intersecting the Karpelevich region with an
additional constraint. Instead, we give a direct and complete derivation for a natural
support-constrained stochastic family. The looped-cycle model is therefore a transparent setting in
which Karpelevich-type boundary phenomena—root-of-unity endpoints, algebraic arcs, branch structure,
and visibility between competing boundary pieces—can be proved from first principles.

For structured subclasses of stochastic matrices, the situation is more open. Karpelevich’s theorem
gives an ambient constraint, but it rarely describes the exact region once additional structure is
imposed. A basic example is the doubly stochastic single-eigenvalue problem. Perfect and Mirsky
proposed a natural polygonal candidate for the doubly stochastic region; this candidate is correct in
small dimensions, but it fails in dimension five, and the general boundary remains the subject of
further conjectural and computational work

[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Another
recent direction is the study of monotone stochastic matrices, where even low-dimensional eigenvalue
regions and realizing families require a separate analysis

[
14
]
. These examples illustrate a common phenomenon: natural
subclasses of stochastic matrices have their own spectral geometry.

The present paper concerns a prescribed-zero-pattern subclass. We study the row-stochastic matrices
whose directed graph is an
n
-cycle and whose only other allowed nonzero entries are self-loops:

A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&1-\alpha_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\alpha_{2}&1-\alpha_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{n-1}&1-\alpha_{n-1}\\
1-\alpha_{n}&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\alpha_{j}\in[0,1).

Equivalently, each row has exactly two allowed transitions: a self-loop and the next edge of a
directed cycle. Let

\mathcal{C}_{n}:=\{A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}):\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in[0,1)\}.

We write

\Sigma_{n}:=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\sigma(A)

for the corresponding spectral region.

The cases
n=1
and
n=2
are degenerate and can be disposed of explicitly. For
n=1
, the
cycle edge and the self-loop coincide, so the only row-stochastic matrix in the family is
[1]
,
and hence

\Sigma_{1}=\{1\}.

For
n=2
,

A_{2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&1-\alpha_{1}\\
1-\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1),

has eigenvalues
1
and
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-1
. Since
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-1
ranges over

[-1,1)
, while
1
is always an eigenvalue, we obtain

\Sigma_{2}=[-1,1].

Thus the first genuinely nontrivial case is
n=3
, and the rest of the paper concerns the
structure of
\Sigma_{n}
for
n\geq 3
.

This zero pattern is already nontrivial in small dimension. Ran and Teng studied the nonnegative
inverse eigenvalue problem with prescribed zero patterns and gave a full analysis in dimension three,
together with remarks and conjectures in dimension four
[
12
]
. In particular, their

4
-cycle stochastic conjecture was recently resolved in
[
13
]
, where the region was shown
to be bounded by one straight Karpelevich-type edge and one algebraic one-loop edge. The present paper
extends that dimension-four result to arbitrary
n
, and gives a complete all-dimensional
classification for the directed cycle-with-loops pattern.

The dimension-four result is the motivating special case for the present work. It isolates, in the
first nontrivial dimension, the same structural features that appear here in all dimensions: a straight
Karpelevich-type edge, an algebraic one-loop edge, and a visibility mechanism selecting the exposed
boundary. It also points naturally to the question whether the looped-cycle geometry persists for
longer directed cycles. The present paper answers that question completely: for every
n
, we give an
all-dimensional classification of the corresponding spectral region, with the
n=4
picture appearing
as the first instance of the general sector-and-visibility mechanism.

The answer has a simple global form, but only after passing to angular coordinates. For

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, set

m=\Arg\lambda,\qquad M=\Arg(\lambda-1).

The map

\Lambda(m,M)=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}e^{im},\qquad 0<m<M<\pi,

is the inverse parametrization from angular data to the upper half-plane. In these coordinates the
upper-half-plane region is a finite union of vertical sectors. The number of sectors is

K=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

Each sector has a horizontal lower edge and an algebraic upper edge. The horizontal lower edges map
to straight chords from
1
to roots of unity, while the algebraic upper edges are selected components
of the one-loop carrier

\Imop\!\left((\lambda^{n}-1)\overline{(\lambda^{n-1}-1)}\right)=0.

Only part of each sector edge is visible on the boundary of the union: adjacent sectors hide one
another. This produces the alternating upper boundary chain

C_{1},L_{1},C_{2},L_{2},\ldots,C_{K},L_{K},

or, equivalently,

1\to e^{2\pi i/n}\to p_{1}\to e^{4\pi i/n}\to p_{2}\to\cdots\to p_{K-1}\to e^{2\pi iK/n}\to 0.

The lower boundary is the conjugate chain. The real spectral part is

\Sigma_{n}\cap\mathbb{R}=\begin{cases}[-1,1],&n\text{ even},\\
(0,1],&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Thus the full topological boundary consists of the two conjugate nonreal chains, together with the
additional real interval
[-1,0]
exactly when
n
is even.

The proof is independent of the Karpelevich theorem. The key point is that the prescribed cycle
pattern makes the characteristic equation have only two determinant monomials:

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(\lambda-\alpha_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{j}).

Writing
t_{j}=1-\alpha_{j}
and
z=\lambda-1
, this becomes

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z+t_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

For fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, we parametrize

u_{j}=\Arg(z+t_{j}).

The argument constraint selects one of finitely many branches,

u_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}=2\pi k,\qquad 1\leq k\leq K.

The modulus constraint becomes the vanishing of a sum

\Psi_{k,\lambda}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{\lambda}(u_{j})

on the simplex cut out by this argument equation. The function
F_{\lambda}
is strictly convex.
Jensen’s inequality therefore gives the exact minimum of
\Psi_{k,\lambda}
, and a majorization
argument gives the exact maximum. This yields a sharp branchwise membership criterion.

The final step is geometric. The branchwise criterion is translated into the angular
(m,M)
-plane,
where every branch becomes a vertical subgraph. The lower edge is the Jensen equality line

M=\phi_{k}
; the upper edge is an algebraic graph
M=\beta_{k}(m)
. The global boundary is then
obtained by a finite visibility argument: the beginning of the
k
-th algebraic edge is hidden by the

(k+1)
-st sector, and the beginning of the
(k+1)
-st Jensen edge is hidden by the
k
-th sector.
This is the only combinatorial complication that appears when passing from the
4
-cycle case to
arbitrary
n
.

The boundary admits an explicit realization dictionary. The straight Jensen pieces are realized by
the uniform cycle family

U_{n}(q)=(1-q)I+qP_{n},\qquad 0<q\leq 1,

where
P_{n}
is the directed cyclic permutation matrix. The algebraic pieces are realized by the
one-loop family

A_{L,n}(\alpha)=A_{n}(\alpha,0,\ldots,0),\qquad 0\leq\alpha<1,

whose characteristic equation is

\lambda^{n}-\alpha\lambda^{n-1}+\alpha-1=0.

Thus the boundary is not only described set-theoretically; it is realized by two elementary
one-parameter subfamilies. Radial filling from the point
1
then realizes the interior.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 states the main theorem and introduces the angular
notation. Section 3 derives the multiplicative eigenvalue constraint. Section 4 converts this
constraint into an exact argument and logarithmic-modulus criterion. Section 5 proves the branchwise
variational criterion using convexity and majorization. Section 6 translates the criterion into
angular sectors. Section 7 extracts the visible boundary of the sector union. Section 8 treats the
real axis and completes the proof of the main theorem. Section 9 records the boundary-realizing
families.

The matrices and the main theorem

Throughout, indices are read modulo
n
, with
n+1=1
. We write

\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Imop z>0\}.

Definition 2.1

(The
n
-cycle family)
.

For
n\geq 3
and

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in[0,1)^{n},

let
A_{n}(\alpha)=A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
be the row-stochastic matrix with

(A_{n}(\alpha))_{jj}=\alpha_{j},\qquad(A_{n}(\alpha))_{j,j+1}=1-\alpha_{j},

and all other entries equal to
0
. The half-open convention keeps every cyclic edge weight

1-\alpha_{j}
positive; allowing
\alpha_{j}=1
would delete that edge and leave the intended looped-cycle
support class, as well as the parametrization
t_{j}\in(0,1]
used below. Equivalently,

A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&1-\alpha_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\alpha_{2}&1-\alpha_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{n-1}&1-\alpha_{n-1}\\
1-\alpha_{n}&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

The spectral region of the
n
-cycle family is

\Sigma_{n}:=\bigcup_{\alpha\in[0,1)^{n}}\sigma(A_{n}(\alpha)).

Let

K:=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor,\qquad S_{k}:=2\pi k,\qquad\theta_{k}:=\frac{S_{k}}{n}=\frac{2\pi k}{n},

\phi_{k}:=\frac{\pi+\theta_{k}}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{\pi k}{n},\qquad\gamma_{k}:=\frac{S_{k}-\pi}{n-1}=\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{n-1}.

Let

D:=\{(m,M):0<m<M<\pi\}.

For
(m,M)\in D
, define

\Lambda(m,M):=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}e^{im}.

(2.1)

Thus
\Lambda(m,M)
is the unique point
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
satisfying

\Arg\lambda=m,\qquad\Arg(\lambda-1)=M.

For each
k=1,\ldots,K
and
m\in[\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
, let
\rho_{k}(m)\in[0,1]
be the unique solution of

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)=\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m).

(2.2)

The existence and uniqueness of
\rho_{k}(m)
, and the endpoint values

\rho_{k}(\gamma_{k})=0,\qquad\rho_{k}(\theta_{k})=1,

are proved in Section
6
.
Define

\beta_{k}(m):=\Arg(\rho_{k}(m)e^{im}-1),

(2.3)

with the endpoint convention

\beta_{k}(\gamma_{k})=\pi.

Equivalently,

\beta_{k}:[\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]\longrightarrow[\phi_{k},\pi].

Define the
k
-th angular sector by

\displaystyle\Omega_{n,k}:={}

\displaystyle\{(m,M)\in D:0<m\leq\gamma_{k},\ \phi_{k}\leq M<\pi\}

(2.4)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\cup\{(m,M)\in D:\gamma_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k},\ \phi_{k}\leq M\leq\beta_{k}(m)\}.

Set

\Omega_{n}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{n,k}.

(2.5)

For
1\leq k<K
, let
\eta_{k}\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
be the unique point satisfying

\beta_{k}(\eta_{k})=\phi_{k+1}.

(2.6)

Put

p_{k}:=\Lambda(\eta_{k},\phi_{k+1}).

(2.7)

Define the visible straight pieces by

C_{1}:=\{1\}\cup\{\Lambda(m,\phi_{1}):0<m\leq\theta_{1}\},

(2.8)

and, for
2\leq k\leq K
, by

C_{k}:=\{\Lambda(m,\phi_{k}):\eta_{k-1}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\}.

(2.9)

Define the visible algebraic pieces by

L_{k}:=\{\Lambda(m,\beta_{k}(m)):\eta_{k}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\},\qquad 1\leq k<K,

(2.10)

and

L_{K}:=\{0\}\cup\{\Lambda(m,\beta_{K}(m)):\gamma_{K}<m\leq\theta_{K}\}.

(2.11)

Finally set

B_{n}^{+}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(C_{k}\cup L_{k}).

(2.12)

Theorem 2.2

(Spectral region and boundary)
.

For every
n\geq 3
, the functions

\beta_{k}:[\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]\longrightarrow[\phi_{k},\pi],\qquad 1\leq k\leq K,

are continuous and strictly decreasing, with

\beta_{k}(\gamma_{k})=\pi,\qquad\beta_{k}(\theta_{k})=\phi_{k}.

The upper-half-plane spectral region is

\Sigma_{n}\cap\mathbb{C}^{+}=\Lambda(\Omega_{n}).

(2.13)

Equivalently, it is the union of the
K
angular sectors
\Omega_{n,k}
under the angular
homeomorphism
\Lambda:D\to\mathbb{C}^{+}
.
The upper boundary is

B_{n}^{+}=C_{1}\cup L_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup L_{2}\cup\cdots\cup C_{K}\cup L_{K},

(2.14)

If
K=1
, this is the path

1\xrightarrow{\ C_{1}\ }e^{2\pi i/n}\xrightarrow{\ L_{1}\ }0.

If
K\geq 2
, it is the oriented chain

1\xrightarrow{\ C_{1}\ }e^{2\pi i/n}\xrightarrow{\ L_{1}\ }p_{1}\xrightarrow{\ C_{2}\ }e^{4\pi i/n}\xrightarrow{\ L_{2}\ }p_{2}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow p_{K-1}\xrightarrow{\ C_{K}\ }e^{2\pi iK/n}\xrightarrow{\ L_{K}\ }0.

The lower boundary is obtained by complex conjugation.

More precisely, if

\overline{B_{n}^{+}}:=\{\overline{z}:z\in B_{n}^{+}\},

then the full topological boundary is

\partial\Sigma_{n}=B_{n}^{+}\cup\overline{B_{n}^{+}}\cup\begin{cases}[-1,0],&n\text{ even},\\[2.84526pt]
\varnothing,&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(2.15)

The real spectral part is

\Sigma_{n}\cap\mathbb{R}=\begin{cases}[-1,1],&n\text{ even},\\[2.84526pt]
(0,1],&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

(2.16)

Moreover,

(0,1)\subset\interior\Sigma_{n}.

The point
0
is a boundary point for every
n
, and

0\in\Sigma_{n}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\text{ is even}.

For
\lambda=a+ib
, define

H_{n}(\lambda):=\Imop\!\left((\lambda^{n}-1)(\overline{\lambda}^{\,n-1}-1)\right).

(2.17)

Since

\lambda^{n}-1=(\lambda-1)(1+\lambda+\cdots+\lambda^{n-1})

and similarly for
\overline{\lambda}^{\,n-1}-1
, the real polynomial
H_{n}(a+ib)
is divisible by

|\lambda-1|^{2}
. Since it also vanishes on the real axis, it is divisible by
b
. We define the
reduced real polynomial

G_{n}(a,b):=\frac{H_{n}(a+ib)}{b((a-1)^{2}+b^{2})}.

(2.18)

The quotient extends polynomially to all
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and has degree
2n-4
.

Remark 2.3
.

The algebraic equation

H_{n}(\lambda)=0,

or equivalently the reduced equation
G_{n}(a,b)=0
, is only a real algebraic carrier for the one-loop
arcs. Its origin is the realness condition for the one-loop parameter

\alpha(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1};

away from the endpoint singularities, requiring this quotient to be real is exactly the equation

\Imop\!\left((\lambda^{n}-1)(\overline{\lambda}^{\,n-1}-1)\right)=0.

It is not, by itself, a boundary description. The relevant boundary components are selected by
the angular restrictions

\gamma_{k}\leq\Arg\lambda\leq\theta_{k},\qquad\Arg(\lambda-1)=\beta_{k}(\Arg\lambda),

by the stochasticity condition

\alpha(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1}\in[0,1),

and by the visibility truncations in (
2.10
)–(
2.11
).

3
Reformulation of the characteristic equation

Because the matrices are real, it suffices in the nonreal part to work in the upper half-plane and
then reflect by conjugation.

Proposition 3.1

(Multiplicative eigenvalue constraint)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
\lambda\in\sigma(A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))
if and only if

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(\lambda-\alpha_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{j}).

(3.1)

Equivalently, with

t_{j}:=1-\alpha_{j}\in(0,1],\qquad z:=\lambda-1,

one has

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z+t_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

(3.2)

Proof.

The matrix
\lambda I-A_{n}
has diagonal entries
\lambda-\alpha_{j}
and cyclic off-diagonal entries

-(1-\alpha_{j})
. In the determinant expansion the only nonzero permutation terms are the diagonal
term and the full directed-cycle term. The latter has sign

(-1)^{n-1}
from the cycle and a factor
(-1)^{n}
from the entries, so its total sign is negative.
Therefore

\det(\lambda I-A_{n})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(\lambda-\alpha_{j})-\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{j}).

This proves (
3.1
). Substituting
\lambda-\alpha_{j}=\lambda-(1-t_{j})=z+t_{j}

gives (
3.2
).
∎

4
Argument parametrization and the exact criterion

Let

\lambda=a+ib,\qquad b>0,\qquad z=(a-1)+ib.

Set

m:=\Arg\lambda,\qquad M:=\Arg(\lambda-1).

(4.1)

Then
0<m<M<\pi
. Indeed, both points
\lambda
and
\lambda-1
lie in the upper half-plane, so
their arguments lie in
(0,\pi)
. Passing from
\lambda
to
\lambda-1
shifts the point one unit to
the left while keeping the same positive imaginary part, and the argument in the upper half-plane is
strictly increased by such a left shift.

For
t\in(0,1]
, define

u(t):=\Arg(z+t).

Lemma 4.1

(Argument parametrization)
.

The map
t\mapsto u(t)
is a continuous strictly decreasing bijection

(0,1]\longrightarrow[m,M).

Its inverse is

t(u)=b\cot u+1-a,

(4.2)

and, for
u\in[m,M)
,

|z+t(u)|=b\csc u.

(4.3)

Proof.

We have

z+t=(a-1+t)+ib.

For a point
x+ib
in the upper half-plane, the relation
\Arg(x+ib)=u
is equivalent to

\cot u=\frac{x}{b}.

Applying this with
x=a-1+t
gives

\cot u(t)=\frac{a-1+t}{b},

which gives (
4.2
). Also

\frac{d}{dt}\Arg(a-1+t+ib)=-\frac{b}{(a-1+t)^{2}+b^{2}}<0.

Moreover
u(1)=\Arg\lambda=m
and
u(t)\to\Arg(\lambda-1)=M
as
t\downarrow 0
. Finally,

|z+t(u)|^{2}=(b\cot u)^{2}+b^{2}=b^{2}\csc^{2}u.

∎

Define

F_{\lambda}(u):=\log|z+t(u)|-\log t(u)=\log(b\csc u)-\log(b\cot u+1-a).

(4.4)

Lemma 4.2

(Convexity)
.

The function
F_{\lambda}
is strictly convex on
[m,M)
. More precisely,

F_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(u)=\frac{(a-1)^{2}+b^{2}}{(b\cot u+1-a)^{2}\sin^{2}u}>0.

(4.5)

Proof.

This follows by differentiating (
4.4
).
∎

For
k=1,\ldots,K
, put

P_{k}(\lambda):=\left\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in[m,M)^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}=S_{k}\right\},

(4.6)

and

\Psi_{k,\lambda}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{\lambda}(u_{j}).

(4.7)

Proposition 4.3

(Exact branchwise criterion)
.

Let
\lambda=a+ib
with
b>0
. Then
\lambda\in\Sigma_{n}
if and only if for some

k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,

P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\in\Psi_{k,\lambda}(P_{k}(\lambda)).

(4.8)

Moreover, each
P_{k}(\lambda)
is convex and hence connected.

Proof.

We first prove the forward implication. Assume that
\lambda\in\Sigma_{n}
. Then for some

\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in[0,1)
, equivalently for

t_{j}:=1-\alpha_{j}\in(0,1],

the multiplicative equation (
3.2
) holds:

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z+t_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

For each
j
, set

u_{j}:=\Arg(z+t_{j}).

By Lemma
4.1
,
u_{j}\in[m,M)
.

We first compare arguments. The right-hand side
\prod_{j}t_{j}
is positive real, because each

t_{j}>0
. Hence its argument is
0
modulo
2\pi
. Therefore

\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\equiv\Arg\!\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z+t_{j})\right)\equiv 0\pmod{2\pi}.

Since each
u_{j}\in(0,\pi)
, the sum lies in
(0,n\pi)
. Thus

\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}=2\pi k

for some integer
k
satisfying
1\leq k<n/2
. These integers are precisely

k=1,\ldots,K
, where
K=\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
. Hence
u\in P_{k}(\lambda)
for some such
k
.

We next compare moduli. Taking absolute values in (
3.2
) gives

\prod_{j=1}^{n}|z+t_{j}|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j},

and hence, after taking logarithms,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(\log|z+t_{j}|-\log t_{j}\bigr)=0.

Since
u_{j}=\Arg(z+t_{j})
and
t_{j}=t(u_{j})
, this is exactly

\Psi_{k,\lambda}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{\lambda}(u_{j})=0.

Thus
0\in\Psi_{k,\lambda}(P_{k}(\lambda))
.

We now prove the converse implication. Suppose that for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
there is

u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in P_{k}(\lambda)
such that
\Psi_{k,\lambda}(u)=0
. Define

t_{j}:=t(u_{j}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.

Then
t_{j}\in(0,1]
by Lemma
4.1
, and

\Arg(z+t_{j})=u_{j}.

Thus

z+t_{j}=|z+t_{j}|e^{iu_{j}}.

Multiplying over
j
and using
\sum_{j}u_{j}=S_{k}=2\pi k
, we obtain

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z+t_{j})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}|z+t_{j}|\right)e^{i\sum_{j}u_{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}|z+t_{j}|.

On the other hand,
\Psi_{k,\lambda}(u)=0
gives

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(\log|z+t_{j}|-\log t_{j}\bigr)=0,

so

\prod_{j=1}^{n}|z+t_{j}|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

Therefore (
3.2
) holds. Setting
\alpha_{j}:=1-t_{j}
gives

\alpha_{j}\in[0,1)
and
z+t_{j}=\lambda-\alpha_{j}
, so (
3.1
) holds and

\lambda
is realized as an eigenvalue of an admissible matrix.

Finally,

P_{k}(\lambda)=[m,M)^{n}\cap\left\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}=S_{k}\right\}.

It is the intersection of a convex box with an affine hyperplane, hence is convex. Every convex set
is connected.
∎

5
The branchwise variational criterion

Lemma 5.1

(Non-emptiness)
.

For
k=1,\ldots,K
,

P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing\iff nm\leq S_{k}<nM\iff m\leq\theta_{k}<M.

(5.1)

Proof.

If
u\in P_{k}
, then
nm\leq S_{k}<nM
. Conversely, if
m\leq\theta_{k}<M
, then

(\theta_{k},\ldots,\theta_{k})\in P_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2

(Jensen minimum)
.

Assume
P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing
. Then

\min_{P_{k}}\Psi_{k,\lambda}=nF_{\lambda}(\theta_{k}).

(5.2)

Proof.

By Jensen’s inequality and Lemma
4.2
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{\lambda}(u_{j})\geq F_{\lambda}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\right)=F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k}).

Equality is attained at
u_{1}=\cdots=u_{n}=\theta_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Supremum and maximum)
.

Assume
P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing
, and put

U_{k}:=S_{k}-(n-1)m.

(5.3)

If

(n-1)m+M\leq S_{k},

(5.4)

then

\sup_{P_{k}}\Psi_{k,\lambda}=+\infty.

(5.5)

If

(n-1)m+M>S_{k},

(5.6)

then
U_{k}\in[m,M)
and

\max_{P_{k}}\Psi_{k,\lambda}=(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U_{k}).

(5.7)

Proof.

Assume first (
5.4
). Let one coordinate
u_{n}
tend to
M
from below and put

u_{1}=\cdots=u_{n-1}=\frac{S_{k}-u_{n}}{n-1}.

For
u_{n}
sufficiently close to
M
, these coordinates lie in
[m,M)
, so
u\in P_{k}
. As

u_{n}\uparrow M
, one has
t(u_{n})\downarrow 0
, while
|z+t(u_{n})|\to|z|>0
. Hence

F_{\lambda}(u_{n})\to+\infty
, and the remaining terms stay bounded. This proves
(
5.5
).

Now assume (
5.6
). Since
P_{k}\neq\varnothing
,
S_{k}\geq nm
, so
U_{k}\geq m
.
The tight inequality gives
U_{k}<M
. Every coordinate of every
u\in P_{k}
is at most
U_{k}
; otherwise
the remaining
n-1
coordinates, each at least
m
, would force the total sum to exceed
S_{k}
.
Thus
P_{k}\subset[m,U_{k}]^{n}
and is compact.

Order the coordinates decreasingly,
v_{1}\geq\cdots\geq v_{n}
. The vector

w=(U_{k},m,\ldots,m)

majorizes
v
. Indeed, for
1\leq r\leq n-1
,

v_{1}+\cdots+v_{r}\leq S_{k}-(n-r)m=U_{k}+(r-1)m,

and both vectors have total sum
S_{k}
. By Karamata’s inequality and strict convexity,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{\lambda}(v_{j})\leq F_{\lambda}(U_{k})+(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m).

Equality is attained at permutations of
(U_{k},m,\ldots,m)
.
∎

Theorem 5.4

(Branchwise variational criterion)
.

Let
\lambda=a+ib
with
b>0
. Then
\lambda\in\Sigma_{n}
if and only if there exists

k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
such that

P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing,\qquad F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k})\leq 0,

(5.8)

and either (
5.4
) holds, or

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U_{k})\geq 0.

(5.9)

Proof.

By Proposition
4.3
, realization is equivalent to the existence of a branch for
which
0\in\Psi_{k,\lambda}(P_{k})
. The set
P_{k}
is connected and
\Psi_{k,\lambda}
is continuous, so its
image is an interval. Therefore zero lies in this image if and only if the minimum is at most zero
and the supremum is at least zero. Lemmas
5.2
and
5.3
give exactly
(
5.8
) and the two alternatives above.
∎

6
Angular coordinates and sector structure

Let

D:=\{(m,M):0<m<M<\pi\}.

Recall that

\Lambda(m,M):=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}e^{im},\qquad(m,M)\in D.

The map
\Lambda:D\to\{\lambda:\Imop\lambda>0\}
is a homeomorphism. Its inverse is

\lambda\longmapsto(\Arg\lambda,\Arg(\lambda-1)).

Indeed, if
\lambda=a+ib
with
b>0
, then both
\Arg\lambda
and
\Arg(\lambda-1)
lie in

(0,\pi)
, and the second angle is larger than the first because
\lambda-1
is obtained from

\lambda
by shifting left at the same height. Hence

(\Arg\lambda,\Arg(\lambda-1))\in D.

Conversely, fix
(m,M)\in D
. The point
\lambda
with angular data
(m,M)
must lie on the ray from

0
with direction
m
and also on the ray from
1
with direction
M
. Thus

\lambda=\rho e^{im}=1+se^{iM}

for some
\rho,s>0
. Taking imaginary parts gives
\rho\sin m=s\sin M
. Eliminating
s
from the
real-part equation gives

\rho\left(\cos m-\sin m\cot M\right)=1,

and therefore

\rho=|\lambda|=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}.

(6.1)

Thus the unique point with angular data
(m,M)
is
\Lambda(m,M)
. The formula for
\Lambda
is
continuous on
D
, and the inverse argument map is continuous in the upper half-plane. Hence

\Lambda
is a homeomorphism.

We shall repeatedly use the following elementary consequence. If

\lambda=a+ib=\Lambda(m,M)
, then

\cot M=\frac{a-1}{b},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\frac{1-a}{b}=-\cot M.

For the
k
-th branch,
\theta_{k}=S_{k}/n
is the equal-distribution point in the simplex

u_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}=S_{k}
, and it is the point where Jensen’s inequality gives the minimum of

\Psi_{k,\lambda}
.

Lemma 6.1

(Jensen condition in angular form)
.

For
k=1,\ldots,K
,

F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k})\leq 0\iff M\geq\phi_{k}.

(6.2)

Proof.

The inequality
F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k})\leq 0
is equivalent to

b\csc\theta_{k}\leq b\cot\theta_{k}+1-a,

that is,

\frac{1-a}{b}\geq\csc\theta_{k}-\cot\theta_{k}=\tan\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}.

Since
\Arg(\lambda-1)=M
, one has
(1-a)/b=-\cot M
. Hence

-\cot M\geq\tan\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}=-\cot\!\left(\frac{\pi+\theta_{k}}{2}\right).

The function
M\mapsto-\cot M
is strictly increasing on
(0,\pi)
, so this is equivalent to

M\geq\phi_{k}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2

(Algebraic form of the tight maximum)
.

Assume the
k
-th branch is nonempty, so
m\leq\theta_{k}<M
, and that the tight regime

M>U_{k}(m):=S_{k}-(n-1)m
holds. Then
U_{k}(m)\in[m,M)
, and the inequality

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U_{k}(m))\geq 0

is equivalent to

H_{n}(\lambda)\geq 0.

(6.3)

Proof.

Write
\lambda=\rho e^{im}
, where
\rho
is given by (
6.1
). Since
t(m)=1
,

F_{\lambda}(m)=\log|\lambda|=\log\rho.

For
U=U_{k}(m)
, using
b=\rho\sin m
and
a=\rho\cos m
, we get

\displaystyle\exp F_{\lambda}(U)

\displaystyle=\frac{b\csc U}{b\cot U+1-a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho\sin m}{\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)}.

Therefore

\exp\big((n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U)\big)=\frac{\rho^{n}\sin m}{\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)}.

(6.4)

The denominator is positive in the tight regime. Indeed, using

\rho=\sin M/\sin(M-m)
, we compute

\displaystyle\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)

\displaystyle=\sin U-\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}\sin(U-m)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin U\sin(M-m)-\sin M\sin(U-m)}{\sin(M-m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin m\,\sin(M-U)}{\sin(M-m)}.

All sine factors in the last quotient are positive because

0<m<\pi
,
0<M-U<\pi
, and
0<M-m<\pi
. Since
\exp x\geq 1
if and only if
x\geq 0
, and since the
denominator in (
6.4
) is positive, we have

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U)\geq 0\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\rho^{n}\sin m}{\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)}\geq 1.

Clearing the positive denominator, this is equivalent to

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(U-m)-\sin U\geq 0.

(6.5)

Now
U=S_{k}-(n-1)m
and
U-m=S_{k}-nm
. Since
S_{k}=2\pi k
,

\sin(U-m)=-\sin(nm),\qquad\sin U=-\sin((n-1)m).

Thus (
6.5
) becomes

\rho^{n}\sin m-\rho\sin(nm)+\sin((n-1)m)\geq 0.

(6.6)

Multiplying by
\rho^{n-1}>0
gives

\rho^{2n-1}\sin m-\rho^{n}\sin(nm)+\rho^{n-1}\sin((n-1)m)\geq 0.

But

\displaystyle H_{n}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\Imop\!\left((\rho^{n}e^{inm}-1)(\rho^{n-1}e^{-i(n-1)m}-1)\right)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2n-1}\sin m-\rho^{n}\sin(nm)+\rho^{n-1}\sin((n-1)m).

This proves the equivalence.
∎

Proposition 6.3

(Sectorial membership theorem)
.

Let
\lambda=\Lambda(m,M)\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Imop\lambda>0
. Then
\lambda\in\Sigma_{n}
if and only if for
some
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,

m\leq\theta_{k},\qquad M\geq\phi_{k},\qquad\bigl(M\leq U_{k}(m)\quad\text{or}\quad H_{n}(\Lambda(m,M))\geq 0\bigr),

(6.7)

where
U_{k}(m)=S_{k}-(n-1)m
. Equivalently,

\Sigma_{n}\cap\{\Imop z>0\}=\Lambda\!\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{n,k}^{\triangle}\right),

(6.8)

where

\Omega_{n,k}^{\triangle}:=\left\{(m,M)\in D:m\leq\theta_{k},M\geq\phi_{k},\big(M\leq U_{k}(m)\text{ or }H_{n}(\Lambda(m,M))\geq 0\big)\right\}.

(6.9)

Proof.

Fix
\lambda=\Lambda(m,M)
in the upper half-plane. By Theorem
5.4
,

\lambda\in\Sigma_{n}
if and only if, for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the following three conditions hold:

P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing,

F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k})\leq 0,

and either

(n-1)m+M\leq S_{k}

or

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(S_{k}-(n-1)m)\geq 0.

We now translate these requirements into angular coordinates.

First, by Lemma
5.1
,

P_{k}(\lambda)\neq\varnothing\iff m\leq\theta_{k}<M.

Second, by Lemma
6.1
,

F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k})\leq 0\iff M\geq\phi_{k}.

Since
\phi_{k}=(\pi+\theta_{k})/2>\theta_{k}
, the inequality
M\geq\phi_{k}
automatically implies

M>\theta_{k}
. Hence the non-emptiness condition reduces, in the presence of
M\geq\phi_{k}
, to

m\leq\theta_{k}
.

It remains to translate the final alternative. Put

U_{k}(m):=S_{k}-(n-1)m.

Then

(n-1)m+M\leq S_{k}\iff M\leq U_{k}(m).

This is the unbounded-supremum regime, so no further condition is needed. In the complementary tight
regime
M>U_{k}(m)
, Lemma
6.2
gives

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U_{k}(m))\geq 0\iff H_{n}(\lambda)\geq 0.

Since
\lambda=\Lambda(m,M)
, this is the same as
H_{n}(\Lambda(m,M))\geq 0
.

Combining the three translated conditions gives exactly (
6.7
). The set identity
follows by applying the angular homeomorphism
\Lambda:D\to\{\Imop z>0\}
.
∎

We now prove that the sectors have the vertical sections stated in the main theorem.

Lemma 6.4

(The functions
\rho_{k}
and
\beta_{k}
)
.

For
m\in[\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
, the equation

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)=\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m)

has a unique solution
\rho_{k}(m)\in[0,1]
. The resulting function

\beta_{k}(m)=\Arg(\rho_{k}(m)e^{im}-1)

is continuous on
[\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
, strictly decreasing on
(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
, and satisfies

\beta_{k}(\gamma_{k})=\pi,\qquad\beta_{k}(\theta_{k})=\phi_{k}.

More precisely, on
(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
,

\beta_{k}^{\prime}(m)=-\frac{(n-1)\sin(S_{k}-nm)}{n\rho_{k}(m)^{n-1}\sin m+\sin(S_{k}-nm)}<0.

(6.10)

Proof.

Put

U=S_{k}-(n-1)m,\qquad V=S_{k}-nm=U-m.

For
m\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
, one has
m<U<\pi
and
0<V<\pi
. Indeed,

U<\pi\iff m>\frac{S_{k}-\pi}{n-1}=\gamma_{k},\qquad U>m\iff m<\frac{S_{k}}{n}=\theta_{k},

and then
0<V=U-m<\pi
. Repeating the defining equation for
\rho_{k}
explicitly, we need to solve

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)=\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m).

In the notation
U,V
, this is

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin V=\sin U.

(6.11)

The left-hand side is strictly increasing in
\rho
. At
\rho=0
it equals
0<\sin U
. At

\rho=1
it exceeds
\sin U
, because
U=m+V
and

\sin m+\sin V-\sin U=4\sin\frac{m}{2}\sin\frac{V}{2}\sin\frac{U}{2}>0.

Therefore there is a unique solution in
(0,1)
. At
m=\gamma_{k}
, one has
U=\pi
, so
(
6.11
) becomes

\rho^{n}\sin\gamma_{k}+\rho\sin(\pi-\gamma_{k})=0.

The sine factors are positive in the relevant range, and the unique solution in
[0,1]
is

\rho=0
. At
m=\theta_{k}
, one has
V=0
and
U=\theta_{k}
, so (
6.11
) becomes

\rho^{n}\sin\theta_{k}=\sin\theta_{k}.

Since
0<\theta_{k}<\pi
, the unique solution in
[0,1]
is
\rho=1
. This proves existence,
uniqueness, and the endpoint values

\rho_{k}(\gamma_{k})=0,\qquad\rho_{k}(\theta_{k})=1.

Set
\lambda_{k}(m):=\rho_{k}(m)e^{im}
. The point
\lambda_{k}(m)
lies on the ray from the origin with
argument
m
, and

\lambda_{k}(m)-1=\rho_{k}(m)e^{im}-1

is the vector from
1
to
\lambda_{k}(m)
. Thus

\Arg(\rho_{k}(m)e^{im}-1)
is precisely the second angular coordinate of this point. Consequently

\beta_{k}(\gamma_{k})=\Arg(-1)=\pi,\qquad\beta_{k}(\theta_{k})=\Arg(e^{i\theta_{k}}-1)=\frac{\pi+\theta_{k}}{2}=\phi_{k}.

On
(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
, continuity of
\rho_{k}
follows from the implicit function theorem because

\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\bigl(\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)-\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m)\bigr)=n\rho^{n-1}\sin m+\sin(S_{k}-nm)>0.

The endpoint limits are forced by the uniqueness of the endpoint solutions just proved. Therefore

\rho_{k}
is continuous on the closed interval, and continuity of
\beta_{k}
follows from continuity of

(m,\rho)\mapsto\Arg(\rho e^{im}-1)
along this curve.

It remains to prove monotonicity. Define

Q(m,\rho):=\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)-\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m).

Then
Q(m,\rho_{k}(m))=0
, and

Q_{\rho}=n\rho^{n-1}\sin m+\sin(S_{k}-nm)>0.

(6.12)

For brevity write
\rho=\rho_{k}(m)
. Also

Q_{m}=\rho^{n}\cos m-n\rho\cos(S_{k}-nm)+(n-1)\cos(S_{k}-(n-1)m).

Implicit differentiation gives
\rho_{k}^{\prime}(m)=-Q_{m}/Q_{\rho}
. Since

\beta_{k}(m)=\Arg(\rho e^{im}-1),

differentiation gives

\beta_{k}^{\prime}(m)=\frac{\rho^{2}-\rho\cos m-\rho^{\prime}\sin m}{|\rho e^{im}-1|^{2}}.

(6.13)

Substituting
\rho^{\prime}=-Q_{m}/Q_{\rho}
yields

\beta_{k}^{\prime}(m)=\frac{(\rho^{2}-\rho\cos m)Q_{\rho}+Q_{m}\sin m}{Q_{\rho}|\rho e^{im}-1|^{2}}.

(6.14)

A direct simplification using
Q=0
gives

(\rho^{2}-\rho\cos m)Q_{\rho}+Q_{m}\sin m=-(n-1)\sin(S_{k}-nm)|\rho e^{im}-1|^{2}.

(6.15)

Indeed, writing again
U=S_{k}-(n-1)m
and
V=S_{k}-nm
, the equation
Q=0
gives

\rho^{n}\sin m=\sin U-\rho\sin V.

Substituting this relation reduces the left-hand side of
(
6.15
) to

-(n-1)\sin V(\rho^{2}-2\rho\cos m+1).

Combining (
6.12
), (
6.14
), and (
6.15
) gives
(
6.10
). Since
0<S_{k}-nm<\pi
on
(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
, the derivative is negative.
∎

For fixed
k
, set

U_{k}(m):=S_{k}-(n-1)m.

When no confusion is possible we write
U=U_{k}(m)
.

Lemma 6.5

(Vertical monotonicity)
.

Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
m\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
. For

M\in(U_{k}(m),\pi)
, put

E_{k}(m,M):=\exp\bigl((n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U_{k}(m))\bigr),\qquad\lambda=\Lambda(m,M).

Then
M\mapsto E_{k}(m,M)
is continuous and strictly decreasing on
(U_{k}(m),\pi)
. Moreover,

\lim_{M\downarrow U_{k}(m)}E_{k}(m,M)=+\infty,\qquad\lim_{M\uparrow\pi}E_{k}(m,M)=0.

Proof.

Write

\lambda=\rho e^{im},\qquad\rho=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}.

Since
t(m)=1
, we have

F_{\lambda}(m)=\log|\lambda|=\log\rho.

Let
U=U_{k}(m)
. Since
a=\rho\cos m
and
b=\rho\sin m
, we get

|z+t(U)|=b\csc U=\rho\frac{\sin m}{\sin U}.

Also

\displaystyle t(U)

\displaystyle=b\cot U+1-a

\displaystyle=\rho\sin m\frac{\cos U}{\sin U}+1-\rho\cos m

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho\sin m\cos U+\sin U-\rho\cos m\sin U}{\sin U}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)}{\sin U}.

Therefore

\exp F_{\lambda}(U)=\frac{\rho\sin m}{\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)}.

Thus

E_{k}(m,M)=\frac{\rho^{n}\sin m}{\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)}.

(6.16)

In the regime
M>U
, the denominator is positive. Indeed,

\displaystyle\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)

\displaystyle=\sin U-\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}\sin(U-m)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin U\sin(M-m)-\sin M\sin(U-m)}{\sin(M-m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin m\,\sin(M-U)}{\sin(M-m)}>0.

The last inequality follows from

0<m<M<\pi,\qquad M>U,\qquad U\geq m.

For fixed
m
,

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial M}=\frac{\cos M\sin(M-m)-\sin M\cos(M-m)}{\sin^{2}(M-m)}=-\frac{\sin m}{\sin^{2}(M-m)}<0.

(6.17)

Now regard the right-hand side of (
6.16
) as a function of
\rho
:

E(\rho)=\frac{A\rho^{n}}{B-C\rho},

where

A=\sin m>0,\qquad B=\sin U>0,\qquad C=\sin(U-m)\geq 0.

On the positivity domain
B-C\rho>0
,

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{An\rho^{n-1}(B-C\rho)+AC\rho^{n}}{(B-C\rho)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{A\rho^{n-1}\bigl(nB-(n-1)C\rho\bigr)}{(B-C\rho)^{2}}.

But

nB-(n-1)C\rho=n(B-C\rho)+C\rho>0.

Hence
E^{\prime}(\rho)>0
. Since
M\mapsto\rho(M)
is strictly decreasing,
M\mapsto E_{k}(m,M)
is strictly
decreasing.

Finally, as
M\uparrow\pi
, one has
\rho\to 0
, while the denominator in (
6.16
) tends to

\sin U>0
. Hence
E_{k}(m,M)\to 0
. As
M\downarrow U
, if
U>m
, then the denominator

\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m)=\frac{\sin m\,\sin(M-U)}{\sin(M-m)}

tends to
0^{+}
while
\rho
tends to a positive value, so
E_{k}(m,M)\to+\infty
. If
U=m
, then the
denominator is
\sin m>0
and
\rho\to+\infty
, so again
E_{k}(m,M)\to+\infty
.
∎

Lemma 6.6

(The lower edge lies below the tight boundary)
.

For every
m\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
,

\beta_{k}(m)\geq\phi_{k}.

Proof.

Let
U=U_{k}(m)
. By Lemma
6.5
, the equation
E_{k}(m,M)=1
has a unique
solution
M\in(U,\pi)
. We first identify this solution with
\beta_{k}(m)
.

Using (
6.16
), the equation
E_{k}(m,M)=1
is

\rho^{n}\sin m=\sin U-\rho\sin(U-m),

or equivalently

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(U-m)=\sin U.

Since

U=S_{k}-(n-1)m,\qquad U-m=S_{k}-nm,

this becomes

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)=\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m).

This is exactly the defining equation for
\rho_{k}(m)
. Therefore the unique solution of

E_{k}(m,M)=1
is

M=\Arg(\rho_{k}(m)e^{im}-1)=\beta_{k}(m).

We now prove
\beta_{k}(m)\geq\phi_{k}
. If
\phi_{k}\leq U
, then

\beta_{k}(m)>U\geq\phi_{k},

because
\beta_{k}(m)
lies in the tight interval
(U,\pi)
. Assume instead that
\phi_{k}>U
. Then

M=\phi_{k}
lies in the tight regime. By Lemma
6.1
, at
M=\phi_{k}
one has

F_{\lambda}(\theta_{k})=0.

Hence the Jensen point
(\theta_{k},\ldots,\theta_{k})
has
\Psi_{k,\lambda}=0
. In particular, the
tight-regime maximum of
\Psi_{k,\lambda}
is nonnegative. By Lemma
5.3
, this maximum is

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U).

Therefore

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U)\geq 0,

or equivalently

E_{k}(m,\phi_{k})\geq 1.

Since
M\mapsto E_{k}(m,M)
is strictly decreasing and
E_{k}(m,\beta_{k}(m))=1
, it follows that

\phi_{k}\leq\beta_{k}(m).

∎

Proposition 6.7

(Vertical sections)
.

For each
k=1,\ldots,K
, the sector
\Omega_{n,k}^{\triangle}
in (
6.9
) has vertical
sections

\Omega_{n,k}^{\triangle}(m)=\begin{cases}[\phi_{k},\pi),&0<m\leq\gamma_{k},\\[2.84526pt]
[\phi_{k},\beta_{k}(m)],&\gamma_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k},\\[2.84526pt]
\varnothing,&m>\theta_{k}.\end{cases}

In particular,
\Omega_{n,k}^{\triangle}=\Omega_{n,k}
from Theorem
2.2
.

The resulting vertical-sector geometry is shown in Figure

.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.3
,

\Omega^{\triangle}_{n,k}=\Bigl\{(m,M)\in D:m\leq\theta_{k},\ M\geq\phi_{k},\ \bigl(M\leq U_{k}(m)\ \text{or}\ H_{n}(\Lambda(m,M))\geq 0\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Thus no point with
m>\theta_{k}
belongs to
\Omega^{\triangle}_{n,k}
, proving the third line.

Assume first that
0<m\leq\gamma_{k}
. Since

\gamma_{k}=\frac{S_{k}-\pi}{n-1},

we have

U_{k}(m)=S_{k}-(n-1)m\geq\pi.

But
M<\pi
for every
(m,M)\in D
. Hence the unbounded-supremum alternative

M\leq U_{k}(m)

holds automatically. Since
m\leq\gamma_{k}<\theta_{k}
, the condition
m\leq\theta_{k}
also holds.
Therefore the only remaining condition is

Indeed,

and

For

gives

By Lemma

and the identification

Combining the unbounded part

with the tight part gives

M\leq\beta_{k}(m).

Together with the Jensen condition

, and using Lemma

\Omega^{\triangle}_{n,k}(m)=[\phi_{k},\beta_{k}(m)].

∎

Corollary 6.8

(Radial filling and star-convexity)
.

The set
\Omega_{n}
from (
2.5
) is horizontally left-saturated: if

(m,M)\in\Omega_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0<m^{\prime}\leq m,

then

(m^{\prime},M)\in\Omega_{n}.

(6.18)

Consequently,

\Lambda(\Omega_{n})\cup\{1\}

is star-convex with star-centre
1
. More explicitly, if

\lambda\in\Lambda(\Omega_{n})=\Sigma_{n}\cap\{\Imop z>0\}

and
0<s\leq 1
, then

\lambda_{s}:=1+s(\lambda-1)

also belongs to
\Lambda(\Omega_{n})
. The endpoint
s=0
gives
\lambda_{0}=1
.

Moreover, for every
M\in[\phi_{1},\pi)
, the horizontal section

I(M):=\{m\in(0,M):(m,M)\in\Omega_{n}\}

is of the form

I(M)=(0,\mu(M)]

for a uniquely determined number
\mu(M)\in(0,M)
. For
0<M<\phi_{1}
, one has

I(M)=\varnothing.

If

q(M):=\Lambda(\mu(M),M),

then
q(M)
is a relative boundary point of
\Lambda(\Omega_{n})
in the upper half-plane, and

\Lambda(\Omega_{n})=\bigl\{1+s(q(M)-1):\phi_{1}\leq M<\pi,\ 0<s\leq 1\bigr\}.

(6.19)

In particular, every relative interior point of
\Lambda(\Omega_{n})
lies on the open segment from
1

to one of the radial boundary points
q(M)
.

The radial filling described in Corollary
6.8
is illustrated in
Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Radial filling of the upper spectral region from the star-centre
1
, illustrated for

n=9
. For each angular height
M
, the radial boundary point is

q(M)=\Lambda(\mu(M),M)
, and the filled segment is

\lambda=1+s(q(M)-1)
with
0<s\leq 1
.

Proof.

We first prove the left-saturation. It is enough to prove it for each individual sector

\Omega_{n,k}
. Fix
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and suppose
(m,M)\in\Omega_{n,k}
. If

0<m\leq\gamma_{k}
, then Proposition
6.7
gives

M\in[\phi_{k},\pi).

Hence for every
0<m^{\prime}\leq m
, again
0<m^{\prime}\leq\gamma_{k}
, and therefore

(m^{\prime},M)\in\Omega_{n,k}.

Now suppose
\gamma_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}
. Then

M\in[\phi_{k},\beta_{k}(m)].

Let
0<m^{\prime}\leq m
. If
m^{\prime}\leq\gamma_{k}
, then the first part of the vertical-section formula gives

\Omega_{n,k}(m^{\prime})=[\phi_{k},\pi),

so
(m^{\prime},M)\in\Omega_{n,k}
. If instead
\gamma_{k}<m^{\prime}\leq m
, then the monotonicity of
\beta_{k}
gives

\beta_{k}(m^{\prime})\geq\beta_{k}(m).

Thus

\phi_{k}\leq M\leq\beta_{k}(m)\leq\beta_{k}(m^{\prime}),

and again
(m^{\prime},M)\in\Omega_{n,k}
. Hence every
\Omega_{n,k}
is horizontally left-saturated, and so
is their union
\Omega_{n}
. This proves (
6.18
).

We now translate this into the
\lambda
-plane. Let

\lambda=\Lambda(m,M)\in\Lambda(\Omega_{n}),

and write

\lambda-1=Re^{iM},\qquad R>0.

For
0<s\leq 1
, put

\lambda_{s}:=1+s(\lambda-1)=1+sRe^{iM}.

Then

\Arg(\lambda_{s}-1)=M.

If

m_{s}:=\Arg\lambda_{s},

then
0<m_{s}\leq m
. Indeed,

\frac{d}{ds}\Arg(1+sRe^{iM})=\Imop\!\left(\frac{Re^{iM}}{1+sRe^{iM}}\right)=\frac{R\sin M}{|1+sRe^{iM}|^{2}}>0,

because
0<M<\pi
. Since
m_{0}=0
and
m_{1}=m
, this gives
0<m_{s}\leq m
for
0<s\leq 1
. By
left-saturation,

(m_{s},M)\in\Omega_{n}.

Because
\Lambda
is the inverse angular parametrization,

\lambda_{s}=\Lambda(m_{s},M)\in\Lambda(\Omega_{n}).

Thus
\Lambda(\Omega_{n})\cup\{1\}
is star-convex with star-centre
1
.

It remains to prove the radial filling statement. First, if
0<M<\phi_{1}
, then no sector contains a
point with second angular coordinate
M
, since every sector has lower edge
M=\phi_{k}\geq\phi_{1}
.
Hence
I(M)=\varnothing
.

Let now
M\in[\phi_{1},\pi)
. The first sector has vertical section

\Omega_{n,1}(m)=[\phi_{1},\pi)

for all sufficiently small
m>0
, so
I(M)\neq\varnothing
. By the left-saturation just proved,

I(M)
is an initial interval. Also
I(M)
is bounded above, since every point of
\Omega_{n,k}

satisfies
m\leq\theta_{k}
, and an active sector at height
M
must have
\phi_{k}\leq M
. Hence

m\leq\max\{\theta_{k}:\phi_{k}\leq M\}<M
.
Since each
\Omega_{n,k}
is relatively closed in
D
, their finite union
\Omega_{n}
is relatively
closed in
D
. Hence the right endpoint of
I(M)
is attained. Thus

I(M)=(0,\mu(M)]

for a unique
\mu(M)\in(0,M)
.

The point

q(M):=\Lambda(\mu(M),M)

is a relative boundary point: points with the same
M
-coordinate and
m<\mu(M)
lie in
\Omega_{n}
,
while points with the same
M
-coordinate and
m>\mu(M)
, sufficiently close to
\mu(M)
, do not.

Finally, for fixed
M
, points of the form
\Lambda(m,M)
lie on the same ray from
1
, because

\Arg(\Lambda(m,M)-1)=M.

Moreover,

|\Lambda(m,M)-1|=\frac{\sin m}{\sin(M-m)},

and this quantity is strictly increasing in
m
, since

\frac{d}{dm}\log\!\left(\frac{\sin m}{\sin(M-m)}\right)=\cot m+\cot(M-m)=\frac{\sin M}{\sin m\,\sin(M-m)}>0.

Therefore, for every
m\in(0,\mu(M)]
, there is a unique
s\in(0,1]
such that

\Lambda(m,M)=1+s(q(M)-1).

This proves (
6.19
). If
\Lambda(m,M)
is a relative interior point, then
necessarily
m<\mu(M)
, hence the corresponding
s
satisfies
0<s<1
.
∎

Corollary 6.9

(Realization of radial fillings)
.

Let
q\in\Sigma_{n}
be realized as an eigenvalue of

A=A_{n}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}).

Then, for every
0<s\leq 1
, the point

q_{s}:=1+s(q-1)

also belongs to
\Sigma_{n}
. More precisely,
q_{s}
is an eigenvalue of

A^{(s)}:=A_{n}(\alpha_{1}^{(s)},\ldots,\alpha_{n}^{(s)}),\qquad\alpha_{j}^{(s)}:=1-s(1-\alpha_{j}).

Equivalently,

A^{(s)}=(1-s)I+sA.

In particular, if the radial boundary point
q(M)
from Corollary
6.8
is
realized, then the entire radial section

\{1+s(q(M)-1):0<s\leq 1\}

is realized. Hence, once the boundary points
q(M)
are realized, every point in the relative
interior of the upper-half-plane region is realized automatically by the construction

A\longmapsto(1-s)I+sA.

Proof.

Since
\alpha_{j}\in[0,1)
, we have

1-\alpha_{j}\in(0,1].

For
0<s\leq 1
,

1-\alpha_{j}^{(s)}=s(1-\alpha_{j})\in(0,1],

so

\alpha_{j}^{(s)}\in[0,1).

Thus
A^{(s)}
is again an admissible
n
-cycle stochastic matrix.

The diagonal entry in the
j
-th row of
(1-s)I+sA
is

1-s+s\alpha_{j}=1-s(1-\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}^{(s)},

and the cyclic off-diagonal entry is

s(1-\alpha_{j})=1-\alpha_{j}^{(s)}.

Therefore
A^{(s)}=(1-s)I+sA
.

If
v\neq 0
is an eigenvector of
A
with eigenvalue
q
, then

A^{(s)}v=((1-s)I+sA)v=(1-s+sq)v=(1+s(q-1))v=q_{s}v.

Hence
q_{s}\in\sigma(A^{(s)})\subset\Sigma_{n}
.

The case
s=0
would give
A^{(0)}=I
, corresponding formally to
\alpha_{j}=1
, which is outside the
chosen parameter range. The endpoint
q_{0}=1
, however, is already an eigenvalue of every
row-stochastic matrix in the family.
∎

Boundary extraction

In this section we extract the boundary of the union of the vertical subgraphs. The following simple
inequalities are the key; the adjacent-sector visibility mechanism is shown in
Figure

.

. For
m<\eta_{k}
the full upper edge

\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}
is hidden by
\Omega_{n,k+1}
, while the full lower edge

Lemma 7.1

(Separation of branch intervals)
.

For
k<K
,

\gamma_{k}<\theta_{k}<\gamma_{k+1}.

(7.1)

For
k\geq 2
,

\gamma_{k}>\theta_{k-1}.

(7.2)

Proof.

We compute

\theta_{k}-\gamma_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{n}-\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{n-1}=\frac{(n-2k)\pi}{n(n-1)}>0,

because
k\leq K
. Similarly,

\gamma_{k+1}-\theta_{k}=\frac{(2k+1)\pi}{n-1}-\frac{2\pi k}{n}=\frac{(n+2k)\pi}{n(n-1)}>0.

Finally,

\gamma_{k}-\theta_{k-1}=\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{n-1}-\frac{2\pi(k-1)}{n}=\frac{(n+2k-2)\pi}{n(n-1)}>0.

∎

Lemma 7.2

(Adjacent crossing)
.

For each
k=1,\ldots,K-1
, there is a unique point

\eta_{k}\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})

such that

\beta_{k}(\eta_{k})=\phi_{k+1}.

(7.3)

Equivalently,
\eta_{k}
is the unique solution in
(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
of

\sin^{n-1}(\phi_{k+1}-m)\,\sin\!\bigl(\phi_{k+1}-S_{k}+(n-1)m\bigr)=\sin^{n}\phi_{k+1}.

(7.4)

Moreover,

\beta_{k}(m)>\phi_{k+1}\quad(\gamma_{k}\leq m<\eta_{k}),

\beta_{k}(m)=\phi_{k+1}\quad(m=\eta_{k}),

and

\beta_{k}(m)<\phi_{k+1}\quad(\eta_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}).

Proof.

By Lemma
6.4
, the function
\beta_{k}
is continuous and strictly decreasing on

[\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
, with

\beta_{k}(\gamma_{k})=\pi,\qquad\beta_{k}(\theta_{k})=\phi_{k}.

Since

\phi_{k}<\phi_{k+1}<\pi,

the intermediate value theorem gives a point
\eta_{k}\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
satisfying

\beta_{k}(\eta_{k})=\phi_{k+1}.

Strict monotonicity gives uniqueness.

It remains to record the scalar equation satisfied by this crossing point. Put

U_{k}(m):=S_{k}-(n-1)m.

For
M=\beta_{k}(m)
, the defining equation of the algebraic edge can be written as

\sin^{n-1}(M-m)\,\sin\!\bigl(M-U_{k}(m)\bigr)=\sin^{n}M.

(7.5)

Indeed, this is obtained from

\rho^{n}\sin m+\rho\sin(S_{k}-nm)=\sin(S_{k}-(n-1)m),\qquad\rho=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)},

by multiplying by
\sin^{n}(M-m)
and using

\sin U_{k}(m)\sin(M-m)-\sin M\sin(U_{k}(m)-m)=\sin m\,\sin(M-U_{k}(m)).

Substituting
M=\phi_{k+1}
in (
7.5
) gives exactly
(
7.4
). Conversely, if
m\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
satisfies
(
7.4
), then with
M=\phi_{k+1}
the same calculation reverses to the defining
equation for
\rho_{k}(m)
, where
\rho=\sin M/\sin(M-m)
. By the uniqueness in Lemma

6.4
, this forces
M=\beta_{k}(m)
, and hence

\beta_{k}(m)=\phi_{k+1}
. This proves the equivalence.

Finally, because
\beta_{k}
is strictly decreasing and crosses the horizontal level
\phi_{k+1}

exactly at
m=\eta_{k}
, the three inequalities follow immediately.
∎

Proposition 7.3

(Visible upper boundary)
.

Let

\Omega:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{n,k}\subset D.

Define the angular visible lower edges by

\mathcal{C}_{1}:=\{(m,\phi_{1}):0<m\leq\theta_{1}\},

and, for
2\leq k\leq K
,

\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\{(m,\phi_{k}):\eta_{k-1}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\}.

Define the angular visible upper edges by

\mathcal{L}_{k}:=\{(m,\beta_{k}(m)):\eta_{k}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\},\qquad 1\leq k<K,

and

\mathcal{L}_{K}:=\{(m,\beta_{K}(m)):\gamma_{K}<m\leq\theta_{K}\}.

Then the relative boundary of
\Omega
in
D
is

\partial_{D}\Omega=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cup\mathcal{L}_{k}).

Consequently, applying the homeomorphism
\Lambda:D\to\{\Imop z>0\}
, the upper boundary of the
spectral region is

B_{n}^{+}=C_{1}\cup L_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup L_{2}\cup\cdots\cup C_{K}\cup L_{K},

where

C_{1}=\{1\}\cup\Lambda(\mathcal{C}_{1}),\qquad C_{k}=\Lambda(\mathcal{C}_{k})\quad(2\leq k\leq K),

L_{k}=\Lambda(\mathcal{L}_{k})\quad(1\leq k<K),\qquad L_{K}=\{0\}\cup\Lambda(\mathcal{L}_{K}).

Proof.

We work in angular coordinates. Since
\Lambda
is a homeomorphism from
D
onto the upper
half-plane, it suffices first to determine the relative boundary of

\Omega=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{n,k}

inside
D
.

By the vertical-section formula, the full lower and upper edges of the
k
-th sector are

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}:=\{(m,\phi_{k}):0<m\leq\theta_{k}\},

and

\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}:=\{(m,\beta_{k}(m)):\gamma_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}\}.

There is no additional boundary edge at
m=\gamma_{k}
: as
m\downarrow\gamma_{k}
along the upper edge,
one has
\beta_{k}(m)\to\pi
, while
M=\pi
lies outside
D
.

For
1\leq k<K
, the number
\eta_{k}\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k})
is defined by

\beta_{k}(\eta_{k})=\phi_{k+1}.

Since
\beta_{k}
is strictly decreasing,

\beta_{k}(m)>\phi_{k+1}\quad\text{for }\gamma_{k}<m<\eta_{k},

\beta_{k}(m)=\phi_{k+1}\quad\text{for }m=\eta_{k},

and

\beta_{k}(m)<\phi_{k+1}\quad\text{for }\eta_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}.

We first determine the visible lower edges. For
k=1
, no previous sector exists, and all later
sectors have lower edge strictly above
\phi_{1}
. Hence the whole edge

\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\mathrm{full}}=\{(m,\phi_{1}):0<m\leq\theta_{1}\}

is visible.

Let
2\leq k\leq K
. If
0<m<\eta_{k-1}
, then the point
(m,\phi_{k})
lies in the interior of

\Omega_{n,k-1}
. Indeed, if
m\leq\gamma_{k-1}
, then
\Omega_{n,k-1}
has vertical section

[\phi_{k-1},\pi)
, and

\phi_{k-1}<\phi_{k}<\pi.

If
m>\gamma_{k-1}
, then
m<\eta_{k-1}
, hence

\beta_{k-1}(m)>\phi_{k},

so again

\phi_{k-1}<\phi_{k}<\beta_{k-1}(m).

Thus the part
0<m<\eta_{k-1}
of the full
k
-th lower edge is hidden.

Now let
m\geq\eta_{k-1}
. Sectors
1,\ldots,k-2
are absent at this
m
when
k\geq 3
, because

m\geq\eta_{k-1}>\gamma_{k-1}>\theta_{k-2}.

The sector
k-1
is either absent or has upper boundary at most
\phi_{k}
. Later sectors

j\geq k+1
have lower boundary
\phi_{j}>\phi_{k}
. Hence no other sector contains
(m,\phi_{k})
in its
interior. Since vertical points immediately above
(m,\phi_{k})
belong to
\Omega_{n,k}
, while
vertical points immediately below it do not belong to any sector, this part is boundary. Therefore
the visible lower edge is exactly

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{(m,\phi_{k}):\eta_{k-1}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\}.

We now determine the visible upper edges. Let
1\leq k<K
. If
\gamma_{k}<m<\eta_{k}
, then

\beta_{k}(m)>\phi_{k+1}.

Moreover, by Lemma
7.1
,

m\leq\theta_{k}<\gamma_{k+1},

so the
(k+1)
-st sector has vertical section
[\phi_{k+1},\pi)
at this
m
. Hence

(m,\beta_{k}(m))
lies in the interior of
\Omega_{n,k+1}
, and this part of the
k
-th upper edge is
hidden.

If
\eta_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}
, then

\beta_{k}(m)<\phi_{k+1}.

Earlier sectors are absent when
k\geq 2
, because

m>\eta_{k}>\gamma_{k}>\theta_{k-1},

and later sectors have lower boundary at least
\phi_{k+1}
. Thus no other sector contains

(m,\beta_{k}(m))
in its interior. Points immediately above the graph
M=\beta_{k}(m)
lie outside

\Omega_{n,k}
, and also outside all later sectors, while points immediately below it lie in

\Omega_{n,k}
. Hence this part is boundary.

At the transition point
m=\eta_{k}
, the point

(\eta_{k},\beta_{k}(\eta_{k}))=(\eta_{k},\phi_{k+1})

is still a boundary point. Indeed, perturbing
m
slightly to the right gives

\beta_{k}(m)<\phi_{k+1},

so one can choose
M
with

\beta_{k}(m)<M<\phi_{k+1},

which lies outside all sectors, while nearby points of
\Omega
approach the transition point from
below. Therefore the visible part of the
k
-th upper edge is exactly

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\{(m,\beta_{k}(m)):\eta_{k}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\},\qquad 1\leq k<K.

For
k=K
, there is no later sector. Earlier sectors are absent for
m>\gamma_{K}
when
K\geq 2
,
because

\gamma_{K}>\theta_{K-1}.

Hence the whole final upper edge

\mathcal{L}_{K}=\{(m,\beta_{K}(m)):\gamma_{K}<m\leq\theta_{K}\}

is visible. Its missing endpoint in
D
corresponds, after applying
\Lambda
, to the limiting point

0
.

No other boundary pieces occur. Indeed, by the vertical-section description of the sectors, any
point of
\Omega
not lying on one of the lower or upper sector edges has a small neighbourhood
contained in
\Omega
. Conversely, any point outside the closure of the listed visible edges and
outside the vertical sections has a small neighbourhood disjoint from
\Omega
. Therefore

\partial_{D}\Omega=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cup\mathcal{L}_{k}).

Applying the homeomorphism
\Lambda
gives the asserted upper boundary chain in the
\lambda
-plane,
with the endpoint
1=\lim_{m\downarrow 0}\Lambda(m,\phi_{1})
added to
C_{1}
and the endpoint

0=\lim_{m\downarrow\gamma_{K}}\Lambda(m,\beta_{K}(m))
added to
L_{K}
.
∎

Proposition 7.4

(Chord interpretation)
.

The line
M=\phi_{k}
maps under
\Lambda
to the straight segment

C_{k}^{\rm full}:=\{1-s+se^{2\pi ik/n}:0\leq s\leq 1\}.

(7.6)

Thus the visible
C_{k}
is a subsegment of this chord.

Proof.

Let
\omega_{k}=e^{2\pi ik/n}
. If
\lambda=1-s+s\omega_{k}
with
0<s\leq 1
, then

\lambda-1=s(\omega_{k}-1),

so

\Arg(\lambda-1)=\Arg(\omega_{k}-1)=\frac{\pi+\theta_{k}}{2}=\phi_{k}.

Conversely, as
m
runs from
0
to
\theta_{k}
with
M=\phi_{k}
, the point
\Lambda(m,\phi_{k})

runs over the same segment. The endpoint
s=0
is the limiting point
\lambda=1
.
∎

8
Real eigenvalues and the full boundary

Proposition 8.1

(Real eigenvalues)
.

The real eigenvalues realized by the
n
-cycle stochastic matrices are

\Sigma_{n}\cap\mathbb{R}=\begin{cases}[-1,1],&n\text{ even},\\[2.84526pt]
(0,1],&n\text{ odd}.\end{cases}

Moreover
(0,1)\subset\interior\Sigma_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
be an eigenvalue. Since
A_{n}
is nonnegative and row-stochastic,
|r|\leq 1
. The
multiplicative constraint gives

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(r-\alpha_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{j})>0.

(8.1)

If
n
is odd and
r<0
, all factors
r-\alpha_{j}
are negative, so the left-hand side of
(
8.1
) is negative, a contradiction. If
n
is odd and
r=0
, the left-hand side is
nonpositive, again a contradiction. Hence for odd
n
, every real eigenvalue lies in
(0,1]
.

Conversely, every
r\in(0,1)
is realized. Put
s=1-r
and choose

t_{1}=t_{2}=t,\qquad t_{3}=\cdots=t_{n}=1,\qquad t:=\frac{sr^{(n-2)/2}}{1+r^{(n-2)/2}}.

Then
0<t<s<1
and

(s-t)r^{(n-2)/2}=t.

Since
r-\alpha_{j}=r-(1-t_{j})=t_{j}-s
, we get

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(r-\alpha_{j})=(t-s)^{2}r^{n-2}=t^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

Thus the product constraint holds. The point
1
is always the Perron eigenvalue.

If
n
is even, let
P_{n}
be the cyclic permutation matrix and consider the admissible uniform family

U_{n}(q):=(1-q)I+qP_{n},\qquad 0<q\leq 1.

Its eigenvalues are

1-q+qe^{2\pi ij/n},\qquad j=0,\ldots,n-1.

Taking
j=n/2
gives
1-2q
, which runs over
[-1,1)
as
0<q\leq 1
. The missing endpoint
1
is
always the Perron eigenvalue. Thus the real region is
[-1,1]
for even
n
.

It remains to show that
(0,1)
is interior. Fix
r\in(0,1)
. For
\lambda=a+ib
with
a
close
to
r
and
0<b\ll 1
, one has
m=\Arg\lambda
close to
0
and
M=\Arg(\lambda-1)
close to

\pi
. Hence, for the first branch,

m<\theta_{1},\qquad M>\phi_{1},\qquad(n-1)m+M<2\pi.

By the sectorial membership theorem, such upper-half-plane points lie in
\Sigma_{n}
. By conjugation,
the corresponding lower-half-plane points also lie in
\Sigma_{n}
, and nearby real points remain in

(0,1)
and are realized. Therefore
(0,1)\subset\interior\Sigma_{n}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

The upper-half-plane formula (
2.13
) follows from Proposition
6.3

and Proposition
6.7
. Proposition
7.3
gives the upper
boundary
B_{n}^{+}
. Since all matrices under consideration are real, the lower boundary is obtained by
conjugation.

Proposition
8.1
gives the real spectral part and shows that
(0,1)
is interior. For even

n
, the interval
[-1,0]
is realized. It is boundary rather than interior: if
r<0
and

\lambda\to r
from the upper half-plane, then
\Arg\lambda\to\pi
, while every nonreal upper-half-plane
spectral point must satisfy
m\leq\theta_{K}<\pi
. Thus a sufficiently small upper-half-plane neighborhood
of
r<0
contains no spectral points. The same holds below by conjugation. Hence
[-1,0]
is a
boundary segment for even
n
.

For odd
n
, negative real points are neither realized nor limit points of the nonreal spectral region,
again because nonreal spectral points satisfy
m\leq\theta_{K}<\pi
. Thus the negative real axis
contributes no boundary segment. The point
0
is a boundary point for every
n
because

L_{K}
tends to
0
as
m\downarrow\gamma_{K}
. It is an actual eigenvalue exactly when
n
is even,
as follows from the even-
n
uniform family in Proposition
8.1
with
q=1/2
. This proves
the full boundary formula.
∎

9
Boundary-realizing families

The boundary description in Theorem
2.2
is geometric. We now record the corresponding
realizing matrices. Only two one-parameter subfamilies are needed: the uniform cycle family, which
realizes the straight Jensen chords, and the one-loop family, which realizes the algebraic arcs. Since
all matrices are real, the lower-half-plane boundary is realized by the same matrices, using the
conjugate eigenvalue branches.

9.1
The uniform family and the Jensen chords

Let
P_{n}
denote the cyclic permutation matrix

P_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&1\\
1&0&\cdots&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

For
0<q\leq 1
, define

U_{n}(q):=(1-q)I+qP_{n}=A_{n}(1-q,\ldots,1-q).

Thus
U_{n}(q)
belongs to the admissible family precisely for
0<q\leq 1
.

Proposition 9.1

(Uniform realization of the Jensen chords)
.

For
0<q\leq 1
,

\sigma(U_{n}(q))=\{\,1-q+qe^{2\pi ij/n}:j=0,\ldots,n-1\,\}.

Consequently, for
1\leq k\leq K
, the branch

\lambda_{k}(q):=1-q+qe^{2\pi ik/n}

realizes the chord from
1
to
e^{2\pi ik/n}
, with the endpoint
1
realized separately as the
Perron eigenvalue.

More explicitly, if

\lambda=\Lambda(m,\phi_{k}),\qquad 0<m\leq\theta_{k},

then

\lambda=1-q_{k}(m)+q_{k}(m)e^{2\pi ik/n},

where

q_{k}(m)=\frac{\sin m}{2\sin(\theta_{k}/2)\sin(\phi_{k}-m)}.

(9.1)

Hence the visible Jensen pieces in Theorem
2.2
are realized by
U_{n}(q)
as follows:

C_{1}\setminus\{1\}=\{\,\lambda_{1}(q_{1}(m)):0<m\leq\theta_{1}\,\},

and, for
2\leq k\leq K
,

C_{k}=\{\,\lambda_{k}(q_{k}(m)):\eta_{k-1}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\,\}.

The point
1
is realized by every stochastic matrix, in particular by every
U_{n}(q)
, as its Perron
eigenvalue.

If
n
is even, then the additional real boundary segment
[-1,0]
is also realized by the uniform
family. Indeed,

\lambda_{n/2}(q)=1-2q,

so
q\in[1/2,1]
realizes
[-1,0]
.

Proof.

The eigenvalues of
P_{n}
are
e^{2\pi ij/n}
,
j=0,\ldots,n-1
. Therefore the eigenvalues of

(1-q)I+qP_{n}
are

1-q+qe^{2\pi ij/n},\qquad j=0,\ldots,n-1.

Put

\omega_{k}:=e^{2\pi ik/n}=e^{i\theta_{k}}.

Then

\omega_{k}-1=2\sin(\theta_{k}/2)e^{i(\pi+\theta_{k})/2}=2\sin(\theta_{k}/2)e^{i\phi_{k}}.

Hence every point
1-q+q\omega_{k}
lies on the ray from
1
with argument
\phi_{k}
. Conversely, the
line
M=\phi_{k}
in angular coordinates is exactly the chord from
1
to
\omega_{k}
.

For
\lambda=\Lambda(m,\phi_{k})
, the distance from
1
to
\lambda
is

|\lambda-1|=\frac{\sin m}{\sin(\phi_{k}-m)}.

Since

|\omega_{k}-1|=2\sin(\theta_{k}/2),

the chord parameter is

q_{k}(m)=\frac{|\lambda-1|}{|\omega_{k}-1|}=\frac{\sin m}{2\sin(\theta_{k}/2)\sin(\phi_{k}-m)}.

This gives the asserted realization of the chord away from the endpoint
1
and therefore of its
visible subsegments
C_{k}
.

If
n
is even, then
e^{2\pi i(n/2)/n}=-1
. The corresponding branch is

1-q+q(-1)=1-2q.

For
q\in[1/2,1]
, this runs through
[0,-1]
, which is the real boundary segment
[-1,0]
.
∎

9.2
The one-loop family and the algebraic arcs

Define the one-loop family

A_{L,n}(\alpha)=A_{n}(\alpha,0,\ldots,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&1-\alpha&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&1\\
1&0&\cdots&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad 0\leq\alpha<1.

(9.2)

Cyclically moving the unique nonzero self-loop gives a similar matrix, and hence the same spectrum.

For
1\leq k\leq K
, define the full algebraic arc

L_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}:=\{\Lambda(m,\beta_{k}(m)):\gamma_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}\}.

Proposition 9.2

(One-loop realization of the algebraic arcs)
.

The characteristic equation of
A_{L,n}(\alpha)
is

\lambda^{n}-\alpha\lambda^{n-1}+\alpha-1=0,

(9.3)

or equivalently

\lambda^{n-1}(\lambda-\alpha)=1-\alpha.

(9.4)

Solving for
\alpha
gives

\alpha(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1}.

(9.5)

The algebraic carrier
H_{n}(\lambda)=0
is precisely the condition that this quotient be real, away from
points where the denominator vanishes.
For every
1\leq k\leq K
and every
m\in(\gamma_{k},\theta_{k}]
, put

\mu_{k}(m):=\Lambda(m,\beta_{k}(m)).

Then

\alpha_{k}(m):=\frac{\mu_{k}(m)^{n}-1}{\mu_{k}(m)^{\,n-1}-1}

belongs to
[0,1)
, and

\mu_{k}(m)\in\sigma(A_{L,n}(\alpha_{k}(m))).

Moreover,

\alpha_{k}(\theta_{k})=0,\qquad\lim_{m\downarrow\gamma_{k}}\alpha_{k}(m)=1,\qquad\lim_{m\downarrow\gamma_{k}}\mu_{k}(m)=0.

Conversely, every nonreal upper-half-plane eigenvalue of
A_{L,n}(\alpha)
, with
0\leq\alpha<1
,
lies on exactly one of the full arcs
L_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}
. The branch issuing from

e^{2\pi ik/n}
at
\alpha=0
traces
L_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}
as
\alpha\uparrow 1
.

The visible algebraic boundary pieces in Theorem
2.2
are the following restrictions of
these full arcs:

L_{k}=\{\mu_{k}(m):\eta_{k}\leq m\leq\theta_{k}\},\qquad 1\leq k<K,

and

L_{K}=\{0\}\cup\{\mu_{K}(m):\gamma_{K}<m\leq\theta_{K}\}.

The endpoint
0
corresponds to the limiting value
\alpha=1
, which is not allowed in the one-loop
family. Thus
0
is realized by a matrix in the admissible family exactly when
n
is even, in which
case it is realized by
U_{n}(1/2)
.

Proof.

For
A_{L,n}(\alpha)
, the multiplicative eigenvalue equation has one parameter
1-\alpha
and

n-1
parameters equal to
1
. Equivalently, direct expansion gives

\lambda^{n-1}(\lambda-\alpha)=1-\alpha,

which is

\lambda^{n}-\alpha\lambda^{n-1}+\alpha-1=0.

Solving this equation for
\alpha
gives

\alpha(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1}.

Now fix
k
and let

\lambda=\mu_{k}(m)=\Lambda(m,\beta_{k}(m)),\qquad\gamma_{k}<m\leq\theta_{k}.

Set

U:=S_{k}-(n-1)m.

By the definition of the upper algebraic edge
M=\beta_{k}(m)
, equivalently by the equality case in the
tight maximum condition,

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U)=0.

Also

(n-1)m+U=S_{k}.

Therefore the argument vector

(U,m,\ldots,m)

satisfies both the argument equation and the logarithmic modulus equation in Proposition

4.3
. The associated
t
-parameters are

t(U),1,\ldots,1,

because
t(m)=1
. Hence the corresponding matrix is, after possibly cyclically moving the
exceptional coordinate,

A_{L,n}(\alpha),\qquad\alpha=1-t(U).

Since
U\in[m,M)
, where
M=\beta_{k}(m)
, Lemma
4.1
gives

t(U)\in(0,1],

and therefore

\alpha\in[0,1).

Thus
\lambda
is realized by an admissible one-loop matrix. Since
\lambda
also satisfies the
one-loop characteristic equation, the realizing parameter is necessarily

\alpha=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1}.

This proves the asserted formula for
\alpha_{k}(m)
.

At
m=\theta_{k}
, one has

\mu_{k}(\theta_{k})=e^{2\pi ik/n},

and the one-loop equation is realized with
\alpha=0
. Hence

\alpha_{k}(\theta_{k})=0.

As
m\downarrow\gamma_{k}
, Lemma
6.4
gives

\beta_{k}(m)\to\pi,

and the angular representation gives

\mu_{k}(m)=\Lambda(m,\beta_{k}(m))\to 0.

Consequently

\alpha_{k}(m)=\frac{\mu_{k}(m)^{n}-1}{\mu_{k}(m)^{\,n-1}-1}\to\frac{-1}{-1}=1.

Conversely, suppose that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Imop\lambda>0
is a nonreal eigenvalue of

A_{L,n}(\alpha)
,
0\leq\alpha<1
. In the multiplicative formulation, the corresponding

t
-parameters are

1-\alpha,1,\ldots,1.

Thus
n-1
of the associated arguments are equal to

m=\Arg\lambda,

and the remaining argument is

U=\Arg(\lambda-\alpha).

The argument equation gives

(n-1)m+U=S_{k}

for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, hence

U=S_{k}-(n-1)m.

The modulus equation gives

(n-1)F_{\lambda}(m)+F_{\lambda}(U)=0.

By the uniqueness of the solution of the tight vertical equation, this implies

\Arg(\lambda-1)=\beta_{k}(m).

Therefore

\lambda=\Lambda(m,\beta_{k}(m))\in L_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}.

It remains to justify the branch statement. At
\alpha=0
, the equation becomes

\lambda^{n}=1,

so the upper-half-plane nonreal roots are

e^{2\pi ik/n},\qquad 1\leq k\leq K.

For
0\leq\alpha<1
, all roots are simple. Indeed, with

p_{\alpha}(\lambda)=\lambda^{n}-\alpha\lambda^{n-1}+\alpha-1,

one has

p_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\lambda^{n-2}(n\lambda-(n-1)\alpha).

The point
\lambda=0
is not a root for
\alpha<1
, since

p_{\alpha}(0)=\alpha-1\neq 0.

A nonzero multiple root would have

\lambda=\frac{n-1}{n}\alpha.

Substitution gives

p_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\alpha\right)=-\frac{\alpha}{n}\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\alpha\right)^{n-1}+\alpha-1<0

for
0\leq\alpha<1
, which is impossible. Hence no two roots collide on
[0,1)
. Since the
coefficients are real, a nonreal branch cannot cross the real axis without colliding with its conjugate
branch. Therefore the branch issuing from
e^{2\pi ik/n}
remains in the upper half-plane for

0\leq\alpha<1
.

As
\alpha\uparrow 1
, the polynomial tends to

\lambda^{n-1}(\lambda-1),

so every non-Perron branch tends to
0
. The converse part already showed that the upper nonreal
one-loop eigenvalues lie on the full arcs
L_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}
, while the first part showed that every
point of those arcs is realized by the one-loop family. Hence the branch issuing from

e^{2\pi ik/n}
traces
L_{k}^{\mathrm{full}}
.
∎

The real algebraic carrier of the one-loop arcs is obtained by requiring
\alpha(\lambda)
to be real.
Away from
\lambda^{n-1}=1
, this is equivalent to

\Imop\!\left((\lambda^{n}-1)\overline{(\lambda^{n-1}-1)}\right)=0.

Thus, in the notation of Section
6
, the one-loop arcs lie on the carrier

H_{n}(\lambda)=0
. The actual boundary arcs are the components selected by the angular restrictions

\gamma_{k}<\Arg\lambda\leq\theta_{k},\qquad\Arg(\lambda-1)=\beta_{k}(\Arg\lambda),

and by the visibility truncations in Theorem
2.2
.

9.3
Boundary dictionary for fixed n

We now summarize the realizing matrices in a form that can be read off directly for any fixed
n
.
The upper boundary is the alternating chain

C_{1},L_{1},C_{2},L_{2},\ldots,C_{K},L_{K}.

The straight pieces
C_{k}
come from the uniform family
U_{n}(q)
, and the algebraic pieces
L_{k}
come
from the one-loop family
A_{L,n}(\alpha)
. The lower boundary is realized by the same real matrices
through conjugate branches.

Proposition 9.3

(Boundary-realization dictionary)
.

Let
\lambda\in\partial\Sigma_{n}
.

1.

If
\lambda=1
, then
\lambda
is the Perron eigenvalue of every admissible matrix.

2.

If
\lambda\in C_{k}\setminus\{1\}
, then

q=\frac{\lambda-1}{e^{2\pi ik/n}-1}

is real and belongs to
(0,1]
, and
U_{n}(q)
realizes
\lambda
through the branch

1-q+qe^{2\pi ik/n}.

3.

If
\lambda\in L_{k}\setminus\{0\}
, then

\alpha=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1}

is real and belongs to
[0,1)
, and
A_{L,n}(\alpha)
realizes
\lambda
.

4.

If
\lambda\in\overline{C_{k}}\setminus\{1\}
, then

q=\frac{\lambda-1}{e^{-2\pi ik/n}-1}

is real and belongs to
(0,1]
, and
U_{n}(q)
realizes
\lambda
through the conjugate branch

1-q+qe^{-2\pi ik/n}.

5.

If
\lambda\in\overline{L_{k}}\setminus\{0\}
, then

\alpha=\frac{\lambda^{n}-1}{\lambda^{n-1}-1}

is real and belongs to
[0,1)
, and the same one-loop matrix
A_{L,n}(\alpha)
realizes
\lambda

through the conjugate branch.

6.

If
n
is even and
\lambda\in[-1,0]
, then

q=\frac{1-\lambda}{2}

belongs to
[1/2,1]
, and
U_{n}(q)
realizes
\lambda
through the branch

1-2q.

7.

The boundary point
0
is realized if and only if
n
is even. In that case it is realized by

U_{n}(1/2)
. If
n
is odd, then
0\in\partial\Sigma_{n}
but
0\notin\Sigma_{n}
.

Equivalently, the realizing families are given by the following table:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\text{boundary piece}&\text{realizing family}&\text{eigenvalue branch}\\
\hline\cr C_{k}&U_{n}(q)=(1-q)I+qP_{n}&1-q+qe^{2\pi ik/n}\\[2.84526pt]

is
realized both by

and by

, which are both equal to the cyclic permutation matrix

. Likewise, the transition point

is realized both by the one-loop branch

and by the uniform branch corresponding to the chord

.

10
Conclusion

We have determined the exact single-eigenvalue region of row-stochastic matrices supported on a directed
cycle with self-loops, in every dimension
n\geq 1
. The cases
n=1
and
n=2
are elementary and were
recorded at the outset; for
n\geq 3
, the region has the genuinely two-dimensional geometry described in
the main theorem. Thus the looped-cycle stochastic support admits a complete all-dimensional
classification.

Apart from the unrestricted Karpelevich theorem itself, whose proof remains poorly understood and has
motivated recent efforts to reformulate and reprove its boundary structure rigorously with modern
tools, the looped-cycle family gives the first fully transparent all-dimensional classification of a
nontrivial stochastic eigenvalue region with Karpelevich-type boundary geometry.

The proof does not use Karpelevich’s theorem, nor does it obtain the answer by intersecting the
Karpelevich region with an additional constraint. The support pattern itself generates the geometry.
The determinant expansion has only two contributing monomials, giving the characteristic equation

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(\lambda-\alpha_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-\alpha_{j}).

After the change of variables
t_{j}=1-\alpha_{j}
and
z=\lambda-1
, this becomes

\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z+t_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}.

Passing to argument variables turns this equation into a convex variational problem on a simplex.
Jensen’s inequality gives the lower boundary of each branch, majorization gives the upper boundary, and
a finite visibility argument determines which branchwise boundary pieces remain exposed in the global
region.

The resulting geometry is a transparent support-constrained analogue of the Karpelevich picture. The
boundary has root-of-unity endpoints, algebraic arcs, branch structure, and visibility between
competing pieces, but all of these features are derived directly from the two-monomial characteristic
equation. In the upper half-plane, the region is the image of finitely many angular sectors under the
map

\Lambda(m,M)=\frac{\sin M}{\sin(M-m)}e^{im},

and its exposed boundary is the alternating chain

C_{1},L_{1},C_{2},L_{2},\ldots,C_{K},L_{K},\qquad K=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor

The lower boundary is obtained by complex conjugation, and the only additional real boundary segment
occurs in even dimension.

A further feature of the classification is the rigidity of the boundary realization. Although the
family has
n
stochastic parameters, its nonreal boundary is realized by two elementary one-parameter
subfamilies. The uniform cycle family

U_{n}(q)=(1-q)I+qP_{n}

realizes the straight Jensen chords, while the one-loop family

A_{L,n}(\alpha)=A_{n}(\alpha,0,\ldots,0)

realizes the algebraic arcs. The interior is then obtained by radial filling from the Perron root.
Thus the full spectral region is governed by a small number of extremal mechanisms.

The method suggests a broader direction. For a general strongly connected stochastic support, the
determinant expansion contains many monomials, and one should not expect such a direct angular
reduction. But sparse supports with few determinant monomials may admit exact spectral geometries of
the same kind. The looped directed cycle is the first case in which this mechanism can be carried out
completely in all dimensions.
