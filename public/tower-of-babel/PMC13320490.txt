Deep Learning Based on Swin-Transformer and 3D U-Net: Implant Three-Dimensional Position Planning
Authors: Jiajin Shen, Xi Yang, Junbiao Zhang, Xiaohuan Lu, Guangneng Chen, Yin Liu, Bin Ye, Minxian Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsThis study aimed to devise a deep learning–based model for the automated identification of anatomical mandibular lingual concavities in the posterior mandible and the prediction of biologically guided three-dimensional implant positions.MethodsCone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images with single-tooth posterior mandibular edentulism were included. A deep learning framework was constructed utilizing 3D U-Net and Swin-Transformer. This framework was designed to perform automated segmentation of teeth, mandible, and the mandibular nerve canal; to classify morphological types of lingual concavities; and to identify implant key points with coordinate prediction. Model performance was assessed via five-fold cross-validation.ResultsThe proposed model achieved Dice similarity coefficients ranging from 0.87 to 0.91 for dental segmentation. In the classification of mandibular lingual concavities, an accuracy of 0.92 to 0.97 was attained. Regarding the prediction of three-dimensional implant positions, the automatically generated plans maintained a safety margin of 3.20 to 3.89 mm from the mandibular nerve canal. Furthermore, sufficient bone volume was preserved at both cervical and apical implant levels, with buccal/lingual cervical bone widths averaging 4.94 to 5.78 mm.ConclusionThe deep learning model in this experiment performed well across different views and learning tasks in a retrospective internal validation setting. Furthermore, it demonstrated the ability to accurately identify relevant anatomical structures, and the predicted three-dimensional implant positions showed clinically acceptable safety margins in the internal validation cohort.Clinical RelevanceThis model provides a preliminary AI-assisted framework for identifying critical mandibular anatomical structures and generating biologically guided preliminary implant position suggestions, thereby helping to mitigate intraoperative complications and reduce the risk of postoperative mechanical and biological complications in preclinical evaluation.Key words: CBCT, Deep learning, Implant three-dimensional position, Mandibular lingual concavity, Preoperative planning

Introduction

Dental implantology has become a primary modality for modern oral rehabilitation, with its application becoming increasingly widespread. This expansion, however, has been accompanied by a rising incidence of intraoperative and postoperative complications, including nerve injury, alveolar ridge or Schneiderian membrane perforation etc. Notably, the posterior mandibular alveolar ridge frequently presents with varying degrees of lingual concavity,1 with reported prevalence of deep concavities ranging from 36% to 39%.2 This anatomical characteristic elevates the risk of perforating the lingual cortical plate, which occurs in 1.1% to 1.2%.3

In especial, in cases of limited bone volume accompanied by a mandibular lingual concavity, rigid according to the prosthetically guided concept for designing the ideal implant position may readily lead to drill perforation of the lingual cortical plate,4,5 which damaging the lingual nerve and related vasculature and resulting in complications.6,7 Conversely, adopting a purely anatomy-guided approach8 to avoid such risks necessitates compensatory measures such as the use of highly angled abutments, which introduces long-term risks of mechanical complications. The use of abutments with angulations exceeding 15° has been associated with a significant rise in the incidence of screw loosening or fracture, demonstrating a 5-year loosening rate of 3.1% to 10.8%.9 This may be accompanied by abutment fracture,10 exacerbated peri-implant bone loss,11, 12, 13 or failure of osseointegration, ultimately compromising long-term implant stability.14

Therefore, a precise assessment of available bone dimensions (height and width), careful consideration of heterogeneous bone densities, and a thorough evaluation of the influence exerted by mandibular lingual concavities are essential for implant surgery in the posterior mandible. Thus, the critical clinical dilemma resides in reconciling these two paradigms: identifying a biologically guided three-dimensional implant position15,16 that circumvents immediate surgical risks while simultaneously ensuring long-term functional and biomechanical stability. To address clinical complications associated with implant placement in the posterior mandible, this study adopts a biologically guided implant planning concept, which is defined based on established biological and bone regenerative principles.15, 17 Biologically guided planning prioritizes anatomical safety, bone biological integrity, soft tissue phenotype preservation, and long-term biologic stability as primary determinants of 3D implant positioning. It emphasizes strict protection of vital structures including the mandibular canal and lingual concavity, ensures sufficient buccal/lingual bone thickness, and avoids lingual cortical plate perforation. Implant position, angulation, depth, and diameter are determined in accordance with alveolar bone morphology rather than being driven solely by prosthetic demands. This paradigm balances biological soundness with restorative requirements, consistent with the bio-restorative concept that integrates surgical biological principles and prosthetic predictability to minimize short-term surgical risks and long-term biologic and mechanical complications.15 This decision depends heavily on accurate assessment and intelligent preoperative measurement of anatomical structures.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising solution to address this clinical need. Deep Learning (DL), a key enabling technology within AI, was first introduced by Hinton et al18 in 2006. This technique emulates biological nervous systems by simulating the information transmission mechanisms of neurons and neural networks. Currently, deep learning has been extensively applied across various domains of dentistry: in endodontics, it assists in diagnosing caries and periapical diseases19; in oral and maxillofacial surgery, it enables automatic assessment and classification of impacted teeth, and helps differentiate cysts, abscesses and tumors;20, 21, 22 in orthodontics, it enables automatic identification of cephalometric landmarks, skeletal pattern classification, and supports extraction decision-making;23 in pediatric dentistry, it helps determine the number and position of primary and permanent teeth;24 in preventive dentistry, it supports public education and disease surveillance;25 in prosthodontics, it participates in crown design and classification of different arch forms in partially edentulous dentition;26,27 and in implant dentistry, it involves tasks such as implant brand recognition,28 risk assessment,29 and preoperative planning.30, 31, 32

While the prevalence and associated risks of mandibular lingual concavities have been well documented, there remains a lack of deep learning‑based tools capable of providing automated risk stratification and alerting in preoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) analysis. Furthermore, most existing AI‑assisted implant planning research has focused on surgically guided position design, whereas biologically driven implant positioning remains relatively underexplored. This study aimed to developed a deep learning model that integrates the global modeling capability of Transformer with the multiscale hierarchical features of CNN. Using Swin-Transformer33 as the backbone network and incorporating 3D U-Net34 – which exhibits strong feature extraction and fusion abilities in three-dimensional space – the model leverages CBCT images to accomplish multiple objectives. This model delivers high-fidelity anatomical segmentation alongside biologically guided implant position predictions that demonstrate preliminary clinical potential in internal validation.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guiyang Stomatological Hospital (Reference Number: KQYYLL-2025-30). It adhered to the guidelines of the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the Oviedo Convention on Human Rights and Biomedicine. To ensure privacy, imaging data were anonymized.

Data source

A total of 283 CBCT scans from the imaging database of Guiyang Stomatological Hospital, covering the period from January 2020 to August 2025, were selected and divided into training, testing, and validation sets in a 6:2:2 ratio. Inclusion criteria for the scans were: (1) Patients with a single missing premolar or molar in the posterior mandible, (2) The edentulous site had been missing for at least 3 months. Exclusion criteria were: (1) CBCT scans with motion artifacts or of poor quality, (2) CBCT scans involving recently extracted sites, bone augmentation procedures, or pathology. These CBCT scans were exported as Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files. All imaging was performed by the same experienced radiologist using identical scanning parameters. Device: CBCT (KaVo 3DeXam, Germany); Imaging area: 160*130 mm; Voxel size: 0.25 mm; Tube voltage/current: 120 kV, 5.0 mA; Exposure time: 14.7 s.

Data annotation and processing

Manual annotation was performed by a trained implant clinician. To establish a reliable gold standard, the annotator received additional standardized training based on expert consensus and established criteria for ideal implant position. All labelled data were further evaluated and calibrated by another senior attending clinician specializing in oral implantology. The final reference standard was determined by consensus of the two senior specialists, which effectively improved the accuracy and objectivity of the dataset. Intraobserver consistency was evaluated using 30 CBCT scans. The reliability for the segmentation and keypoint identification tasks was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(3,1)), and for the classification task using the Kappa value (Figure 1). CBCT data in DICOM format were imported into 3D Slicer 5.6.2 software (Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA, USA). First, the axial view of the edentulous region was selected to segment and label the left/right mandibular premolars, molars, and the missing tooth. Subsequently, the four mesiodistal axial line angles of the teeth adjacent to the missing tooth were marked. Based on the three-dimensional spatial coordinates provided by these four angles, a coronal view intersecting their spatial midpoint was calculated. In this coronal view, the mandible and mandibular nerve canal were segmented. Key points were marked including the alveolar crest of the mandible, the most prominent point of the mandibular lingual concavity, the lingual cusp tip of the opposing maxillary tooth, and the central fossa. Based on these points, the direction of the implant’s long axis and related line segments were calculated, and the angle and depth of the mandibular lingual concavity were measured.

Deep learning model

The architecture is cantered on a U-Net framework with Swin-Transformer as the backbone. This study adopts a staged training strategy to sequentially complete subtasks including slice selection, segmentation, keypoint localization, candidate slice ranking, and clinical classification. The Swin module receives a 1 × 512 × 512 image input. All 2D segmentation and localization tasks use a unified input size of 512 × 512, while the R-view slice module employs a 256 × 256 input. It first undergoes patch encoding, then is progressively downsampled through three Swin Block layers to obtain low-level patch features (size 512 × 32 × 32), while intermediate features are preserved. Subsequently, symmetric upsampling is performed via dilated convolutions, with skip connections established to the intermediate features, ultimately restoring the original image size for segmentation learning. The low-level patch features are compressed to a 512-dimensional vector via average pooling, then fed into two task-specific branches: one directly inputs into a linear classification head for category prediction; the other concatenates with ROI-embedded features and FDI-embedded features before being input into a linear layer to output keypoint coordinate prediction.

During the inference phase, the original 3D CBCT data is first preprocessed to a size of 1 × 64 × 512 × 512. A 3D convolution and classification head are used to select the depth slice index containing the most missing tooth pixels. Based on this index, the R-view slice is extracted from the original data and input into the framework to complete three tasks: segmenting different teeth and the edentulous region; predicting the four mesiodistal keypoints for the edentulous region; and predicting the optimal G-view slice index. The selected optimal G-view slice is input into a separate, identically structured framework to complete three tasks: segmenting the mandibular alveolar bone and mandibular nerve canal; classifying the alveolar bone category; and identifying four keypoints on the coronal plane. Finally, these four keypoints are used to measure relevant line segments on the coronal plane.

During the training phase, images are normalized using the 1st and 99th percentiles, and data augmentation includes random rotation ±2.0°, random scaling ±0.1, and random flipping, with a fixed random seed of 42. A multitask joint loss function was employed: segmentation uses a weighted combination of cross-entropy, Focal, and Dice losses; keypoint localization uses coordinate regression loss combined with validity BCE loss; ranking and classification use cross-entropy loss. The primary optimizer is AdamW, and only the final C/U/P classification uses Adam, with a learning rate range of 1 × 10−⁴ to 5 × 10−⁴. Training epochs range from 40 to 400 across tasks, with an early stopping patience of 25 to 50. The AdamW optimizer was chosen with an initial learning rate of 1e-4 and weight decay of 1e-5. Training was conducted for a total of 100 epochs, employing an early stopping strategy: monitoring the total loss on the validation set, training was stopped if no decrease was observed for 10 consecutive epochs, and the model with the lowest validation loss was saved (Figure 2). The deep learning model measures the relevant line segments required for implant design through keypoint measurements. Furthermore, the implant design visualization flowchart outputs different results based on the determination of different conditions. Evaluation metrics

The predicted results were compared and evaluated against the ground truth annotations made by the observer. Given the limited size of the dataset, five-fold cross-validation was employed. Model performance was comprehensively assessed through multidimensional criteria, including medical classification metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Sensitivity, Specificity, F1 Score) and pixel-level error (target ≤ 10 pixels), with visual comparisons of predictions also provided.35 In this study, a strict hierarchical validation strategy was adopted to ensure model reliability and unbiased evaluation. All CBCT datasets were randomly divided into training, validation, and independent test sets at a ratio of 6:2:2. Five-fold cross-validation was performed exclusively within the training set to sufficiently optimize model parameters, enhance training stability, and reduce overfitting. In contrast, the independent test set, accounting for 20% of the total data, was completely isolated from cross-validation procedures and only used for final unbiased model assessment. This combined validation framework guarantees both adequate model learning and objective, credible evaluation of generalization performance.

Results

Evaluation results of deep learning segmentation tasks

This study systematically evaluated the deep learning model on medical image segmentation tasks, demonstrating excellent overall performance. For segmentation tasks, the model achieved accuracies of ACC@0 = 0.93 ± 0.025 and ACC@1 = 0.98 ± 0.02 in axial slice selection (Table 1). In the segmentation of different tooth positions on the axial plane, the DSC ranged from 0.87 ± 0.052 to 0.91 ± 0.028, IoU ranged from 0.77 ± 0.068 to 0.83 ± 0.039, Precision and Recall were maintained between 0.89 to 0.93 and 0.86 to 0.88, respectively, and HDmm95 was controlled within a range of 0.75 to 0.90 mm. For the segmentation of the mandible and mandibular nerve canal on the coronal plane, DSC reached 0.82 to 0.88, IoU was 0.70 to 0.78, Precision and Recall were 0.84 to 0.90 and 0.81 to 0.86, respectively, and HDmm95 was 0.86 to 1.19 mm (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7). Furthermore, for the three-category classification task of mandibular lingual concavity, the model achieved a classification accuracy (ACC) of 0.92 to 0.97 (Table 2).

Evaluation of the clinical safety of implant 3D positioning via visualization of deep learning results

This study evaluated the clinical feasibility of the AI model, based on keypoint settings, in predicting the 3D position and selecting the dimensions of virtual implants in different posterior mandibular regions (second premolar, first molar, and second molar). The results in Table 4 show that AI-generated plans achieved reasonable spatial relationships between the implants and surrounding anatomical structures with sufficient safety margins in all regions. In the second premolar region, the buccal and lingual bone widths at the implant cervical were 4.94 ± 0.85 mm and 4.96 ± 0.89 mm, respectively, while at the apical they were 7.95 ± 2.38 mm and 5.79 ± 1.47 mm. The average implant length was 10 ± 1.68 mm, and the distance to the mandibular nerve canal was 3.20 ± 0.72 mm. In the first molar region, buccal and lingual bone widths at the cervical were 5.20 ± 1.16 mm and 5.14 ± 1.17 mm, at the apical they were 9.42 ± 3.18 mm and 6.84 ± 2.55 mm, average implant length was 10.58 ± 1.60 mm, and the distance to the nerve canal was 3.89 ± 1.95 mm. In the second molar region, buccal and lingual bone widths at the cervical were 5.70 ± 1.65 mm and 5.78 ± 1.66 mm, at the apical they reached 10.24 ± 3.60 mm and 8.34 ± 3.19 mm, average implant length was 10.27 ± 1.64 mm, and the distance to the nerve canal was 3.70 ± 1.68 mm. Overall, the AI system automatically selected appropriate implant lengths (approximately 10-10.6 mm) based on anatomical conditions and consistently maintained a safe distance from the mandibular nerve canal (3.20-3.89 mm), demonstrating good clinical safety (Table 4, Figure 11).

Distribution of mandibular lingual concavities

A total of 313 posterior mandibular edentulous regions from 283 CBCT scans were analysed. Based on the morphology of the mandibular lingual concavity observed in the coronal plane, the distribution of C-type and P-type concavities (which do not constitute an implant risk) was as follows: in the second premolar region, 54.17% and 8.33%; in the first molar region, 16.74% and 33.49%; and in the second molar region, 2.7% and 2.7% (Figure 12A). For the U-type concavity, which poses a risk of lingual perforation during implant surgery, the lingual angle varied between 0° and 90°. Overall, the distribution of lingual angles was normal (P > .05), with a peak between 60° and 70° (Figure 12C). Among these, cases with lingual angles > 60° (not constituting a serious implant risk) accounted for 55.56%, 61.68%, and 52.86% in the second premolar, first molar, and second molar regions, respectively. Deep concavities with angles < 60° were considered high-risk factors for lingual perforation. Particularly, in the first and second molar regions, 5.61% and 8.57% of cases, respectively, exhibited extremely deep concavities with angles < 30°, warranting high vigilance (Figure 12B). The relationship between the depth of the mandibular lingual concavity and the bone width 2 mm above the mandibular canal with the concavity angle was also analysed. A moderate negative correlation was found between concavity depth and concavity angle (P < .01), indicating a trend of decreasing depth as the angle increases (Figure 12D). Additionally, it was found that the bone width 2 mm above the mandibular canal was not correlated with the degree of the mandibular lingual concavity angle (Figure 12E). Meanwhile, there exists a stable, moderate inverse correlation between the concavity angle and the angle between the mandibular lingual concavity and the implant long axis (Figure 12F) . When the mandibular lingual concavity angle is larger, heightened vigilance is required as the implant long axis at this location may be very close to or even parallel to the bony concave surface (ie, the intersection angle is small).

Discussion

In dental implant restoration treatment, advancements in digital technology enable clinicians to thoroughly evaluate specific patient conditions and personalize treatment plans and surgical protocols. They can manually simulate virtual implant positioning before surgery and execute it precisely during the procedure. With the progress of artificial intelligence (AI) technology, this study established a hybrid deep learning model based on Swin-Transformer and 3D U-Net, which can achieve automatic recognition of mandibular lingual concavities and biologically guided three-dimensional implant planning. Recent authoritative reviews in dental artificial intelligence have systematically elaborated the transformative role, standardized application frameworks, and ethical guidelines of AI in oral medicine, providing top-level guidance and industrial consensus for the scientific application of AI technology in dental practice.36,37 This study strictly follows the AI application principles and research paradigms proposed in the above reviews, and conforms to cutting-edge standards from model construction, task design, clinical evaluation to limitation analysis, providing a verifiable complete framework for the clinical translation of AI technology in preoperative implant planning.

The deep learning model constructed in this study demonstrated stable and accurate performance in the foundational task of implant 3D position planning: the image segmentation of the edentulous area and adjacent anatomical structures. In tooth segmentation, the model performed excellently across different tooth positions, with Dice similarity coefficients (DSC) ranging from 0.87 to 0.91 and precision ranging from 0.89 to 0.93. This is consistent with the findings of Fontenele et al38 and Lahoud et al39 based on CNN research, confirming its high precision and robustness. Simultaneously, while maintaining a relatively high recall rate, the model controlled the 95% Hausdorff Distance (HDmm95) within 0.75 to 0.90 mm, demonstrating good edge segmentation capability to handle multiple tooth positions and complex anatomical structures.

In the segmentation tasks for the mandible and mandibular nerve canal, the model also achieved reliable results: mandible segmentation DSC was 0.82 to 0.88 and IoU was 0.70 to 0.78. Although its DSC was slightly lower than the hierarchical deep learning reported by Verhelst et al40 and did not reach the high level of consistency shown by Lo Giudice et al,41 this indicates that the model can effectively identify the overall morphology of the mandible. However, there remains room for improvement in segmentation refinement when handling areas of lower bone density or similar densities to adjacent anatomical structures. The mandibular nerve canal segmentation achieved a DSC of 0.82 to 0.88, with both precision and recall maintained above 0.80, and an HDmm95 below 1.2 mm, falling within the clinically acceptable error range. These results are comparable to those of Jindanil et al42 and Lin et al,43 who specifically focused on nerve canal detection, indicating that the model possesses reliable anatomical structure recognition capability, particularly holding clinical significance for locating critical structures like the nerve canal.

According to biologically guided 3D implant position planning, the midpoint of the mesiodistal axial line angles of the teeth adjacent to the edentulous space serves as the mesiodistal spatial positioning of the implant’s long axis. In this study, the pixel error for the four key axial line angles was 2.8 ± 0.86 px. Generally, a pixel error of <3 px indicates that the model has high stability. However, due to situations such as tooth rotation or the tooth cervical cross-section not being near the height of contour, changes in the position of axial line angles can easily affect the training outcome of the deep learning model, leading to slight deficiencies in robustness. This can be addressed by increasing the sample size through methods like rotation and intensity transformations to improve segmentation and classification accuracy, thereby reducing the impact on subsequent design steps.

By evaluating the performance of the deep learning model based on keypoint settings in virtual implant planning for different posterior mandibular regions (second premolar, first molar, and second molar), this study found that the deep learning model can automatically generate implant positions and dimensions meeting clinical safety standards while maintaining sufficient distances from important anatomical structures like the mandibular nerve canal. The average implant length selected by AI in the posterior mandible was 10 to 10.6 mm, which is close to the lengths reported in the literature for AI selection (11.7 ± 1.3 mm) and human expert selection (11.5 ± 1.3 mm), all within clinically acceptable ranges. Furthermore, the AI-planned implants in this study maintained sufficient buccal and lingual bone volume at both the cervical and apical (approximately 5.0-5.8 mm at the cervical, 5.8-10.2 mm at the apical), indicating that this model can perform adaptive 3D position adjustments based on local bone conditions. This aligns with the strategy of Cai et al,44 who used nnU-Net to directly predict implant position and dimensions. Additionally, the average distance between the implant planned by this model and the mandibular nerve canal was 3.20 to 3.89 mm, which is consistent with the results of multiple AI-designed implant studies45 and meets the clinically recommended safety standard of ≥2 mm.46 Moreover, the deep learning model in this study maintained reasonable implant-bone relationships and nerve canal distances in the second premolar, first molar, and second molar regions, indicating that the AI model possesses strong regional generalization capability. However, attention must be paid to clinical safety and implant 3D position deviation issues.47

This study is the first to use a deep learning model to identify and measure lingual concavities in the posterior mandible on CBCT and analyse their morphological distribution and angular characteristics. It found significant differences in concavity types and angles among different tooth positions, with the second molar region having a higher proportion of deep lingual concavities (U-type). This indicates a higher risk of lingual perforation during implant surgery in this area, consistent with the research of Kong et al.48 The analysis of 826 edentulous sites by Bodart et al3 showed that approximately 26% had lingual concavities with angles <60°, suggesting that most concavities are mild. This finding shows some discrepancy with the analysis results of the present study, possibly due to the relatively smaller sample size in this research. The virtual implantation study by Sun et al49 confirmed that all lingual perforations occurred in cross-sections of the concave type, especially in the mandibular second molar region (where U-type accounted for up to 63%). They established linear equations relating mandibular lingual concavity depth, alveolar bone width, and cervical-alveolar bone angle to perforation distance, providing quantitative tools for preoperative risk assessment. However, there was no significant correlation between bone width 2 mm above the mandibular canal and the mandibular lingual concavity angle, suggesting that lingual concavities can still exist even with sufficient bone width. Therefore, intraoperative assessment must rely on cross-sectional imaging for individualization.

Although the results of this study indicate good clinical feasibility of AI in posterior mandibular implant planning, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First and foremost, the present study was based on a single-centre dataset, which limits the generalizability and external validity of the proposed model. Since all CBCT images were obtained from the same imaging device, the same clinical centre, and the same patient population, the model cannot be fully proven to perform robustly across different devices, centres, or populations without external validation. This represents a major methodological constraint, as single-centre designs inherently carry potential selection bias and may restrict the reliability of conclusions when extrapolated to broader clinical scenarios. Furthermore, the present study did not include or predict implant diameter, which represents a critical clinical deficiency. As emphasized by reviewers, implant diameter is a core component of comprehensive implant planning; without it, the automated output cannot be regarded as clinically complete, as diameter directly determines primary stability, surgical selection, and long-term clinical success. In addition, independent external validation was not performed in this study. All model evaluations were based on internal data splitting (6:2:2) without verification on an independent external cohort. This further limits the generalizability and clinical applicability of the model, and multicentre, multidevice, and multipopulation validations are strongly recommended before any clinical translation. Moreover, this study did not conduct comprehensive comparisons between AI-generated plans and manual planning regarding 3D positioning accuracy or time efficiency. Previous studies have demonstrated that AI can significantly reduce planning time; however, such benefits were not formally assessed in the current investigation. Additionally, complex scenarios such as severe bone deficiency and extended edentulous areas were not included, and the model’s performance in such contexts remains unclear. Finally, future investigations should employ multicentre, prospective designs with diverse CBCT devices and patient populations to strengthen external validity. Further integration of implant diameter prediction, automatic planning, and clinical outcome evaluation will help establish a more comprehensive, reliable, and clinically practical AI-driven implant planning system.

Conclusion

The deep learning framework developed in this study demonstrated the ability to automatically identify mandibular lingual concavities and predict preliminarily clinically acceptable three-dimensional implant positions in retrospective internal validation. The system exhibited stable and accurate performance across core tasks, including image segmentation, anatomical landmark identification, and the classification of lingual concavity morphology. Thus, it provides preclinical, objective, quantitative support for preoperative planning in the posterior mandible. Notably, the implant positions generated by the model show adherence to clinical safety margins in internal validation and incorporate an early warning function for high-risk, deep U-shaped concavities, thereby offering a promising preclinical tool for risk mitigation in implant dentistry.
