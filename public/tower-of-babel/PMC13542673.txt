Overview of Rehabilitation in Low- and Middle-Income Countries
Authors: Jean Claude Karimwabo
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I am writing this letter to raise concerns about the under-recognition and limited integration of rehabilitation services in both clinical and policy processes in the vast majority of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).[1] Evidence indicates that rehabilitation helps people regain independence, improve quality of life, and return to either educational or employment pursuits.[2] However, across sub-Saharan Africa, and in Rwanda, rehabilitation remains under-prioritised despite the growing burden of disability from stroke, trauma and non-communicable diseases. Limited government attention, underfunding, shortages of professionals, and inadequate rehabilitation infrastructure restrict access to essential services.[3] Consequently, many individuals experience preventable disability, reduced functional independence, and poor quality of life despite surviving acute illness or injury.[4]

Even though rehabilitation practitioners have proven effective in improving patient outcomes, they routinely do not participate in either policy discussions or care teams that develop treatment plans.[5] For example, patients recovering from strokes, traumatic injuries, or chronic diseases like Type 2 diabetes or COPD are often discharged from the hospital without coordinated rehabilitation referral and follow-up plan, which disrupts the continuity of care and increases the risks of preventable disabilities and poorer health outcomes.[6] The resultant impact of the above-mentioned is not a small matter. Patients, subsequently, develop avoidable disabilities which result in reduced productivity on their part, and additional expenses not only to them but also to the health care system through hospitalisations and additional medical interventions. All of these outcomes directly militate against attaining the goals of Universal Health Coverage (UHC),[7] as set by the World Health Organisation.

To address the gaps, the Ministry of Health should take the following into consideration: Policy integration: Rehabilitation should be explicitly included in health policy, strategic planning and budgeting processes.Multidisciplinary care: Clinical decision-making must routinely include rehabilitation professionals alongside physiciansEducation and training: Physicians and other health professionals require training on the scope and benefits of rehabilitation for patient care.Leadership representation: Rehabilitation professionals should have a voice within ministries of health and policy advisory bodies.

Rehabilitation is not discretionary but a fundamental component of the health system, human rights, and economic development. Recognizing and improving its integration into both policy and clinical practice is not only ethical but crucially highly efficacious.
