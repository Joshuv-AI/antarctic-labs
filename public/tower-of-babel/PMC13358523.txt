Comparison of learning curves between electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy and Ion robotic bronchoscopy for preoperative localization
Authors: Hongjiang Liu, Hecheng Zhang, Xiang Tang, Yong Ge, Xingyu Zhu, Teng Sun, Cheng Zhang, Hao Zhang, Shoujie Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElectromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy (ENB) and Ion robotic bronchoscopy are novel, high-precision techniques with low complication rates for the preoperative localization of peripheral pulmonary nodules (PPNs). Their clinical application is largely influenced by the operator’s learning curve; however, direct, systematic comparisons of the learning curves between these two techniques are scarce. This study therefore aimed to analyze differences in the learning curves between ENB and Ion robotic bronchoscopy for PPN localization.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the perioperative data of 120 patients who underwent preoperative PPN localization (60 consecutive ENB-guided cases and 60 Ion robotic bronchoscopy-guided cases) from September 2024 to June 2025. All procedures were performed by the same thoracic surgeon. The clinical characteristics of the two groups were compared, and the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method was employed to analyze operative time (OT).ResultsPerioperative comparisons revealed that the ENB group had a significantly shorter OT (17.13 vs. 45.62 min in the Ion group), whereas the Ion group exhibited a higher localization success rate (96.67% vs. 86.67% in the ENB group). CUSUM modeling of OT divided the learning curves into learning and proficiency phases, with inflection points at the 19th procedure for ENB and the 34th for Ion. Further analysis confirmed that the OT during the learning phase was significantly longer than that in the proficiency phase for both groups (P<0.05).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that ENB-guided PPN localization has a shorter learning curve than Ion robotic bronchoscopy, while the latter achieves a higher localization success rate.

Key findings

• This study compares learning curves between electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy (ENB) and Ion robotic bronchoscopy for preoperative peripheral pulmonary nodule (PPN) localization. The learning curve inflection point was 19 cases for ENB and 34 cases for Ion robotic bronchoscopy. ENB had shorter operative time, while Ion robotic bronchoscopy had a higher localization success rate.

What is known and what is new?

• ENB and Ion robotic bronchoscopy are safe and effective for PPN localization, and their clinical application depends on the learning curve. However, direct systematic comparisons of their learning curves remain rare.

• This study is the first to directly compare learning curves using the cumulative sum method, confirming that ENB has a shorter learning curve while Ion provides higher localization success.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These results support clinical strategy and surgeon training. Institutions should adopt differentiated training plans according to the learning characteristics of each technique to improve efficiency and clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the malignant tumor with the highest mortality rate worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths annually (1). In China, its incidence and mortality rates both rank first among all malignant tumors (2). With the widespread application of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening, an increasing number of pulmonary nodules (PNs) have been detected, among which ground-glass nodules (GGNs) are particularly common (3). The NELSON Lung Cancer Screening Study further confirms that lung cancer remains persistently high in both incidence and mortality (4). In recent years, the advent of LDCT screening has provided an effective tool for detecting early-stage lung cancer (5). For patients with peripheral pulmonary nodules (PPNs), video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) resection has become the preferred treatment, which significantly reduces the mortality rate of early-stage lung cancer (6). However, during thoracoscopic surgery, thoracic surgeons face substantial challenges in accurately locating certain ground-glass opacities (GGOs) or small subsolid PNs, especially those with a diameter of less than 10 millimeters (mm) (7). In addition, small and deeply located GGNs are also difficult to be effectively identified via VATS. This may necessitate surgeons to resect a larger volume of lung tissue, or even convert to thoracotomy for successful lesion resection through bimanual palpation.

Multiple studies have confirmed that electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy (ENB) is a feasible and effective preoperative localization method, with a success rate as high as 85.6–94.5% (8). ENB is increasingly utilized for preoperative marking of small PPNs. Compared with the commonly used computed tomography (CT)-guided transthoracic localization, ENB-guided localization can effectively reduce the incidence of complications such as pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and embolism (9). Meanwhile, robotic systems have been increasingly applied in the field of surgery in recent years. Among them, the Ion robotic bronchoscope has attracted considerable attention due to its slender catheter that can access peripheral bronchi (10). As is well known, the trachea and bronchi branch continuously downward, forming a tree-like structure where the lumen diameter gradually decreases in peripheral pulmonary bronchi, and more than 70% of lung cancer nodules are concealed in these peripheral bronchi (11). The thinner the bronchoscope, the more peripheral bronchi it can reach; accordingly, the proportion of detectable PNs increases, along with the biopsy reach rate. Conventional bronchoscopes can usually only access segmental bronchi, while the outer diameter of the Ion system catheter is merely 3.5 mm, enabling it to reach all 18 pulmonary segments (12). As the world’s first shape-sensing bronchial navigation and control system, the Ion system adopts optical fiber shape-sensing technology, which significantly improves the accuracy of PPN localization. Integrating high-precision positioning, ultrasound navigation, and minimally invasive operation, the Ion robotic bronchoscope has been successfully applied to the preoperative marking and localization of PPNs (10).

Typically, innovations in surgical techniques can improve surgical outcomes and reduce complications. However, following the introduction of new technologies, the medical team needs to integrate them into daily practice—a process crucial for enhancing surgical efficacy. Therefore, the implementation of new surgical techniques must incorporate a learning curve based on objective and measurable outcomes. Unfortunately, no independent studies have compared the learning curves of preoperative localization between ENB and Ion robotic bronchoscopy to date. In view of this, this study retrospectively analyzed the data of two consecutive types of preoperative PPN localization procedures performed de novo by the same thoracic surgeon in our hospital, and adopted the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method to analyze the learning curve of endoscopically guided preoperative localization, aiming to provide valuable references for clinical practice. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0777/rc).

Methods

Patients and inclusion criteria

This study retrospectively collected perioperative data of 120 cases undergoing preoperative localization for PPNs. The dataset included 60 consecutive cases of preoperative localization via ENB performed from September 2024 to June 2025, as well as 60 consecutive cases of preoperative localization via Ion robotic bronchoscopy conducted during the same period. All localization procedures were completed by the same thoracic surgeon, who had experience in conventional bronchoscopy but no experience in endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS)-guided or ENB-guided biopsy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (No. XYFY2026-KL317-01). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

The inclusion criteria for ENB localization were as follows: (I) lesions were PPNs suspected of carcinoma in situ; (II) pure ground-glass nodules (pGGNs) with a diameter ≤3 cm and mixed ground-glass nodules (mGGNs) with a diameter ≤2 cm; (III) lesions were difficult to detect and impalpable during VATS based on the surgeon’s experience.

The inclusion criteria for Ion localization were as follows: (I) lesions were PPNs suspected of carcinoma in situ; (II) pGGNs with a diameter ≤3 cm and mGGNs with a diameter ≤2 cm; (III) no pleural indentation; (IV) lesions were difficult to detect and impalpable during VATS based on the surgeon’s experience.

Exclusion criteria included: (I) complicated with severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction or hepatorenal insufficiency; (II) complicated with endobronchial lesions; (III) poor lesion visualization or obvious pleural indentation sign; (IV) allergy to anesthetics.

The CUSUM method was adopted in this study to evaluate the learning curves of the above two preoperative localization techniques.

Pathway planning and ENB-guided localization

Path planning and ENB-guided localization were performed in accordance with the following steps (Figure 1):

Path planning: low-dose chest CT was performed with identical scanning parameters (1 mm slice thickness, 120 kVp, 80 mAs) for all patients, and the scan data were stored in DICOM format. The ENB system (LUNGCARE, Suzhou, China) was used for virtual bronchial reconstruction. Based on chest CT images acquired during end-inspiratory breath-hold (INSP) or end-expiratory breath-hold (EXP) phase, the system generated a visualized pathway to the target nodule for the surgeon. If no direct bronchus connected to the target nodule, the nearest site to the nodule was selected via the adjacent bronchus.

Pathway planning and Ion-guided localization

Path planning and Ion-guided localization were performed in accordance with the following steps (Figure 2):

Path planning: the patient’s preoperative thin-section chest CT data acquired using the same LDCT scanning parameters as the ENB group were uploaded to the PlanPoint planning laptop for image segmentation, and a navigation pathway to the target PPN was generated.

Path planning: the patient’s preoperative thin-section chest CT data acquired using the same LDCT scanning parameters as the ENB group were uploaded to the PlanPoint planning laptop for image segmentation, and a navigation pathway to the target PPN was generated.

Ion-guided localization: the patient was placed in a supine position on the operating table. After general anesthesia, endotracheal intubation was performed and mechanical ventilation was initiated. First, a conventional flexible bronchoscope was used to clear airway secretions. Subsequently, the SS-RAB system was connected to the patient’s endotracheal tube (ETT). A peripheral vision probe was inserted into the catheter working channel, followed by placement of the catheter into the patient’s airway via the ETT. The surgeon manipulated the catheter through the airway under direct visualization via the controller, first completing registration of the segmentation model with the subject’s anatomical structure, then advancing the catheter to the target nodule under navigation guidance. During the procedure, the real-time catheter position, based on shape-sensing technology, was displayed on the system monitor. After the catheter was positioned at the target site, the vision probe was removed, and a radial endobronchial ultrasound (r-EBUS) probe (UM-S20-17S, Olympus) was inserted through the catheter to confirm the nodule location. Subsequently, 0.3 mL of diluted ICG (dilution ratio: 1 mL ICG mixed with 3 mL normal saline) or methylene blue was injected, followed by an appropriate amount of air.

Differential use of radial EBUS

Notably, r-EBUS was routinely employed during Ion-guided procedures to confirm tool-in-lesion positioning, as per the standard protocol recommended by the manufacturer. However, r-EBUS was not available or utilized during ENB procedures in this study. Lesion localization in the ENB group relied solely on electromagnetic navigation guidance without additional real-time intraprocedural confirmation. This difference in adjunctive confirmation modalities represents an inherent technical distinction between the two platforms and is acknowledged as a methodological limitation of this comparative analysis.

Operation

General anesthesia was administered using a double-lumen endobronchial tube. Once the patient’s anesthetic state was stable, they were positioned in an appropriate lateral decubitus position, followed by two-port or single-port thoracoscopic surgery. After intraoperative localization of the methylene blue-stained area, wedge resection was performed, with the margin distance between the resection edge and the lesion ensured to be ≥2 cm. Based on the results of intraoperative frozen section pathology, segmentectomy or lobectomy was performed if necessary, along with systematic lymph node dissection.

Operative time (OT)

The starting point of OT is defined as follows: for ENB-guided localization, it refers to the moment of successful registration and matching; for Ion-guided localization, it is marked by the completion of registration between the segmentation model and the subject’s anatomical structure. The endpoint of OT is uniformly defined as the moment when the pleura is reached and methylene blue is injected.

Location success and failure

The criterion for successful localization is defined as follows: visible methylene blue staining on the pleura.

The criteria for localization failure include the following situations: (I) no methylene blue staining was observed on the pleura during thoracoscopy; (II) methylene blue staining was observed on the pleura, but no lesion was found in the resected lung tissue; (III) no methylene blue staining was detected in the lung tissue.

Statistical analysis

In our statistical analysis, we employed the CUSUM technique to plot the learning curves. The formula adopted in this study is as follows:

The CUSUM method was employed to evaluate the learning curve, calculated as the cumulative deviation of each data point from the overall dataset mean. Patients were sequenced chronologically, and OT pertaining to virtual trachea construction and target nodule marking was collected for analysis. Here, xi represents the measured OT for each nodule (i =1,2,…,n), µ denotes the average OT across all nodules, and n denotes the sequential case number. Fluctuations in the learning curve correspond to distinct phases of the surgical learning trajectory.

With OT as the primary outcome measure and the average OT as the target value, the CUSUM of deviations between each patient’s measured OT and the overall mean OT served as the ordinate, while the sequential case number served as the abscissa. Across the entire case series, deviations between individual patient OT and the overall mean OT were calculated; the CUSUM of these deviations was plotted on the Y-axis, followed by fitting a smoothed curve to the resulting plot.

Statistical analyses were conducted with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x¯±s), with intergroup comparisons conducted via t-test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup differences assessed using the Pearson chi-square test. For expected cell frequencies <1, Fisher’s exact test was employed to assess intergroup differences. The significance level was established at α=0.05, with a P value <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

In terms of nodule type distribution, multiple nodules accounted for 28.33% and solitary nodules for 71.67% in the ENB group; in the Ion group, multiple nodules accounted for 68.33% and solitary nodules for 31.67%, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (P=0.003).

There were no statistically significant differences in the incidence of hemorrhage (Ion: 1 case vs. ENB: 2 cases) or pneumothorax (Ion: 2 cases vs. ENB: 4 cases) between the two groups (both P>0.05).

The learning curve of the ENB group was a two-dimensional curve with the equation: CUSUM = y =12.38+12.58x −0.40x2 +0.003x3 (where x denotes the number of surgical cases). The equation for the Ion group’s learning curve was: CUSUM = y =5.72+13.13x +0.01x2 −0.004x3 (where x also denotes the number of surgical cases).

Based on the above equations, the learning curves of both the ENB and Ion groups could be divided into two phases. The first phase was the initial learning period (i.e., novice phase), covering the first 19 cases in the ENB group and the first 34 cases in the Ion group. The second phase was the proficiency phase, reflecting the surgeon’s mastery of surgical procedures, which included the remaining 41 cases in the ENB group and the remaining 26 cases in the Ion group.

In the ENB group, the OT was 24.05±7.24 minutes during the learning phase and 13.93±6.38 minutes during the proficiency phase; in the Ion group, the OT was 55.35±6.12 minutes during the learning phase and 32.88±9.28 minutes during the proficiency phase. Intragroup comparisons showed that the OT was significantly longer in the learning phase than in the proficiency phase for both groups (both P<0.001).

Results of ENB-guided PN marking indicated that with accumulated learning experience, the localization success rate gradually increased across the two phases, reaching 78.95% in the learning phase and 90.24% in the proficiency phase. Additionally, there was a statistically significant difference in gender distribution between the learning phase (male: 57.89%, female: 42.11%) and proficiency phase (male: 29.27%, female: 70.73%) in the ENB group (P=0.03).

Discussion

In recent years, remarkable progress has been made in surgical robotic systems in China. Compared with preoperative localization of PPNs using ENB, shape-sensing bronchoscopic robots demonstrate unique advantages in this scenario (10). Their core innovation lies in embedding shape-sensing fiber optic sensors throughout the catheter, which enables real-time and accurate monitoring of the catheter’s three-dimensional (3D) morphology and spatial position within the complex bronchial tree (13). This effectively overcomes the inherent limitations of traditional bronchoscopes in operational flexibility, positioning stability, and ability to reach deep lesions. Especially for deeply located PPNs, the Ion bronchoscopic robot has proven to be an ideal option for preoperative localization (14,15). Through cumulative analysis, this study clarified the learning curve characteristics of objective and measurable indicators for PPN preoperative localization using ENB and shape-sensing bronchoscopic robots. The findings indicate that combining surgeons’ extensive experience in conventional bronchoscopy with the technical advantages of the robotic system, the Ion bronchoscopic robot achieves a significantly higher success rate in PPN preoperative localization than ENB. To the best of our knowledge, no relevant studies have reported a direct comparison of learning curves between ENB-guided and Ion bronchoscopic robot-guided preoperative localization for PPNs at home and abroad to date.

This study found that surgeons need to perform a cumulative total of 34 procedures using the Ion robotic bronchoscopy system for PPN preoperative localization to move beyond the initial learning phase and achieve proficient operation; in contrast, only 19 procedures are required to master the technique with ENB. This difference may stem from the fact that ENB’s operational mode is more accessible to physicians with bronchoscopy experience, as its core operational logic has certain continuity with traditional bronchoscopy, significantly lowering the initial learning threshold (16). Notably, the inflection point of its learning curve mostly indicates that the surgeon has mastered basic operational procedures; to achieve a stably high localization success rate, more clinical cases are needed to address complex lesion scenarios. Previous studies have shown that surgeons need to complete approximately 40 procedures to overcome the learning curve of the Ion robotic bronchoscope and reach proficiency, which is consistent with the conclusions of this study (17). The Ion system represents an entirely new “robot-assisted operational paradigm”: in the early learning stage, surgeons must shift from direct handheld operation to remote control via a console in both mindset and operational mode. They must not only understand and master the system’s fully automatic registration function and real-time 3D navigation based on shape sensing but also proficiently use the robotic platform’s unique operational interface, force feedback system, and fine control skills for 360° catheter steering. This paradigm shift may prolong the initial adaptation period, characterized by slow reduction in OT and delayed inflection point of the learning curve (18). Furthermore, the routine incorporation of r-EBUS for tool-in-lesion confirmation during Ion procedures adds another dimension to the learning requirements. As the performing operator had no prior experience with r-EBUS technique, the acquisition of r-EBUS interpretation skills—including recognition of characteristic ultrasound patterns and differentiation of lesion signals from surrounding structures—likely contributed to the prolonged initial learning phase and should be considered an integral component of the Ion system’s composite learning curve (19). Therefore, surgeons trained on the Ion system often experience a longer learning curve in PPN localization.

Based on learning curve characteristics, this study divided both the ENB and Ion groups into learning and proficiency phases for intergroup comparative analysis. Results showed that the OT in the proficiency phase was significantly shorter than that in the learning phase in both groups. This indicates that with continuous accumulation of clinical experience, surgeons’ precision in regulating key surgical parameters improves significantly, and they handle various intraoperative challenges more calmly and targeted.

The results confirm that the Ion bronchoscopic robot achieves a significantly higher localization success rate than ENB across all learning phases, which is highly consistent with the study’s pre-hypothesis. Its core advantage derives from the shape-sensing fiber optic sensor integrated into the Ion system. This technology allows operators to obtain real-time, accurate 3D morphological and spatial positional information of the catheter in the complex bronchial tree throughout navigation, technically eliminating the risk of positioning deviation associated with traditional navigation methods (14). Additionally, the Ion system features a 2.0 mm working channel and a 3.5 mm outer diameter catheter, enabling penetration into terminal bronchioles that are inaccessible to ENB and coverage of all 18 anatomical regions of the lung. Meanwhile, the catheter tip can deflect 180° in any direction, providing a structural foundation for precise navigation to lesions in small, curved airways (20). Moreover, the routine use of r-EBUS as an adjunctive confirmation tool during ION procedures provided independent validation of tool-in-lesion positioning, enabling real-time detection and correction of navigation errors arising from CT-to-body divergence. This multimodal confirmation capability—combining shape-sensing navigation with r-EBUS verification—likely contributed to the higher localization success rate observed in the Ion group, particularly during the learning phase when navigation accuracy is still being refined (21). Technical asymmetry in adjunctive confirmation modalities: a critical methodological distinction between the two platforms in this study was the differential availability and utilization of intraprocedural confirmation tools. While the Ion system incorporated r-EBUS as an integral component of its standard workflow for tool-in-lesion verification, r-EBUS was neither available nor employed during ENB procedures at our institution during the study period. Consequently, ENB localization relied solely on electromagnetic navigation guidance and virtual bronchoscopic visualization without additional real-time confirmation. This technical asymmetry may have influenced the observed outcomes in several important respects. First, r-EBUS serves as an independent validation modality that can identify and correct navigation discrepancies caused by respiratory motion, patient positioning changes, or magnetic field interference, thereby potentially enhancing localization success rates (22). Second, the absence of such confirmation in ENB procedures may have resulted in undetected navigation deviations, particularly for deeply located PPNs in small bronchioles where electromagnetic tracking accuracy is most vulnerable. Third, the additional procedural steps required for r-EBUS deployment and interpretation in the Ion group—including probe advancement, image acquisition, and pattern recognition—likely contributed to the longer OTs documented during the learning phase. We acknowledge that this differential use of confirmation modalities represents a methodological limitation that precludes fully equitable comparison of the two systems’ intrinsic navigation capabilities. Future comparative studies should ideally employ standardized confirmation protocols—such as r-EBUS adjunctive use for both platforms, or alternative confirmation modalities including C-arm fluoroscopy or cone-beam CT—to enable more balanced performance evaluation.

In contrast, ENB’s localization efficacy is often limited when dealing with deeply located PPNs that require access through small bronchi due to two main factors: first, navigation deviation caused by magnetic field interference; second, structural limitations of the catheter that prevent entry into terminal bronchioles, necessitating empirical localization based on the operator’s clinical experience in most cases. The absence of adjunctive confirmation tools, such as r-EBUS, further compounds these limitations by depriving operators of independent validation when electromagnetic tracking reliability is most compromised, thereby increasing reliance on subjective judgment and empirical estimation during critical navigation phases. For such deep PPNs in small bronchioles, ENB is not only technically demanding and requires high operator proficiency but also its inherent limitation of insufficient catheter flexibility further reduces the accuracy of navigating to target lesions, ultimately leading to low localization success rates (20,23). Since the introduction of the Ion bronchoscopic robot in our center, surgeons can precisely control robotic arm movement and catheter tip deflection angle via the console’s rollers and trackballs, enabling rapid and accurate access to target lesions (23). This operational mode not only significantly improves the success rate of PPN preoperative localization but also optimizes localization accuracy to further enhance overall surgical outcomes, providing more reliable technical support for the diagnosis and treatment of complex clinical PPNs.

Previous studies have confirmed that the OT of ENB for PPN preoperative localization is comprehensively influenced by multiple factors, not merely the surgeon’s learning phase. Among these, the presence of CT bronchus sign and patient gender are key influencing factors. The average OT for localizing PPNs without CT bronchus sign is significantly longer than that for nodules with CT bronchus sign, and this factor has a higher impact weight on OT than the learning curve (24). Although nodule location showed no statistical difference, clinical practice studies found that localization time for lower lobe nodules is usually shorter than that for middle and upper lobe nodules; ENB poses significantly higher localization difficulty in the upper lobe, especially the apical segment (S1), which is closely related to the steep anatomical angle of upper lobe bronchi that increases operational resistance for catheter navigation (25). In contrast, prospective multicenter clinical studies have shown that the Ion bronchoscopic robot can effectively localize early malignant lesions, which are mostly small, subpleural and peripheral, and its localization efficacy has no significant correlation with CT bronchus sign (26).

Cost-effectiveness considerations: while this study did not collect prospective cost data for formal economic analysis, the observed differences in localization success and OT carry important cost-effectiveness implications that warrant consideration. The Ion system entails substantially higher capital investment and maintenance costs compared with conventional ENB platforms, reflecting the additional robotic hardware, proprietary software, and integrated confirmation technologies including r-EBUS (27). The significantly longer OTs documented during the Ion learning phase may constrain procedural throughput, potentially reducing the number of localization procedures achievable per operative day—a particularly relevant consideration for high-volume centers where resource utilization optimization is paramount. Conversely, the higher localization success rate observed with Ion may translate into reduced need for repeat procedures, salvage percutaneous localization attempts, or unplanned conversions to thoracoscopic exploration, thereby offsetting some incremental costs through avoidance of downstream resource consumption and associated morbidity (28). The balance between upfront technology investment and clinical performance remains a critical decision factor for institutions considering implementation of robotic bronchoscopic platforms. Comprehensive health economic evaluations incorporating equipment depreciation, consumable costs, personnel training expenditure, maintenance contracts, and long-term patient outcomes are currently lacking in the literature and are needed to inform value-based procurement decisions. We recommend that future comparative effectiveness studies integrate formal economic evaluation alongside clinical endpoints to provide actionable guidance for healthcare administrators and policymakers.

There are certain limitations in this study. First, it is a single-center retrospective study with a sample size that needs further expansion. Future research should adopt a multicenter design and incorporate more indicators reflecting operational quality for comprehensive evaluation. Second, the learning curves in this study are based solely on the operational data of one surgeon. Surgeons with conventional bronchoscopy experience may affect the learning curves of ENB and Ion, which limits the generalizability of the conclusions to some extent and thus requires verification by more multicenter studies. Furthermore, inevitable biases in case selection may interfere with learning curve analysis, but such biases are difficult to completely eliminate in clinical practice. Therefore, the unique learning curves presented in this study still reflect clinical reality to a certain extent and hold important reference value. Third, the differential use of r-EBUS between the Ion and ENB groups represents a technical asymmetry that may have influenced the observed localization success rates and OTs, as discussed above. This limitation precludes definitive conclusions regarding the intrinsic navigation capabilities of the two systems independent of adjunctive confirmation modalities. Fourth, the absence of prospective cost data precluded formal economic evaluation, and our cost-effectiveness discussion remains qualitative. Future studies should incorporate standardized confirmation protocols and health economic analyses to enable more robust comparative assessment.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Ion robotic bronchoscope-guided preoperative localization has a longer learning curve than ENB, resulting from the new learning requirements imposed by its entirely new operational paradigm. including the acquisition of r-EBUS interpretation skills. However, it exhibits a higher localization success rate from the learning phase onward, reflecting the inherent advantages of robotic systems in navigation accuracy, stability and integration, further augmented by multimodal confirmation capabilities, which effectively guarantee the core surgical goal. Therefore, for clinical training, it is crucial to fully recognize the upfront investment required to master Ion technology and design targeted training programs. In the long run, this investment will translate into more reliable and efficient precise localization capabilities. Nevertheless, institutions must weigh these clinical benefits against higher acquisition costs, longer initial OTs affecting throughput, and the need for comprehensive training encompassing both robotic operation and adjunctive imaging interpretation.

Conclusions

The CUSUM method exhibits excellent performance in learning curve analysis and can objectively assess the proficiency level of preoperative localization for small PPNs under ENB and Ion bronchoscopic robot guidance. As a reliable quantitative evaluation tool, the learning curve not only objectively measures localization accuracy but also helps surgeons accurately identify deficiencies in their operational processes, thereby providing targeted directions for skill improvement. We anticipate that specialized training programs tailored to the learning curve characteristics of the Ion bronchoscopic robot can be developed in the future. Such programs are expected to accelerate the progression of young surgeons along the learning curve, shortening the initial adaptation period and further reducing potential risks to patients during the learning phase.
