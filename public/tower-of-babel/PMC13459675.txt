Beyond main effects: Age, education, and immigration history shape self-rated health among Latin Americans in Canada
Authors: Keren M. Escobar, Nicolaus Espitia, Peter Felsman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLatin Americans represent one of the fastest growing populations in Canada, yet remain largely absent from national health research. Recent descriptive data suggest Latin Americans report higher SRH than the Canadian average, but little is known about how sociodemographic and migration-related factors shape these outcomes.Design and MethodsThis study used pooled data from the 2015–2018 cycles of the Canadian Community Health Survey (N = 203,754) to examine SRH among Latin Americans (n = 1,799) compared with Whites (n = 168,225) and other racialized groups (n = 33,730). Logistic regression models assessed associations between ethnicity and fair/poor SRH, adjusting for sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and health-related covariates. Interaction terms tested whether associations varied by age, sex, education, income, and immigration history.ResultsAcross all models, Latin Americans did not differ significantly from Whites in odds of reporting fair/poor SRH (Model 4 OR = 0.88, 95% CI [0.65, 1.19]). However, significant interaction effects indicated that the association between ethnicity and SRH varied by age, education, and immigration history. Relative to Whites, older Latin Americans and those with primary education demonstrated higher odds of fair/poor SRH, whereas some more recent immigrant subgroups demonstrated comparatively lower odds.ConclusionsAggregate comparisons may obscure important heterogeneity in the association between ethnicity and SRH across sociodemographic and immigration-related subgroups. Findings highlight the importance of intersectional and disaggregated analyses in understanding health inequities among Latin Americans in Canada and underscore the need for culturally responsive policies that address structural and migration-related inequities.

Background

Despite the rapid growth of the Latin American population in Canada, there remains a striking lack of peer-reviewed evidence on their health outcomes. Latin Americans are among the most understudied racialized groups in Canadian health research, as most large-scale analyses either omit them entirely or subsume them into broad “visible minority” categories,1–4 masking heterogeneity and obscuring health inequities and resiliencies specific to this community. National statistics illustrate the significance of this gap: the Latin American population has more than tripled over the last 25 years, increasing from 176,970 in 1996 to 580,235 in 2021. 5 Of this population, 371,965 were immigrants, 128,515 were non-immigrants, and 79,755 were non-permanent residents. Within the immigrant population, the largest groups originated from Colombia (19.1%), Mexico (15.5%), and El Salvador (12.8%). These figures highlight both the rapid demographic growth and the diversity of Latin Americans in Canada, underscoring the need to examine their health outcomes as a distinct population rather than aggregating them into broader categories.

Racialized, immigrant, and other sociodemographic health inequities in Canada

Canadian research consistently demonstrates that racialized and immigrant groups experience health inequities shaped by structural and systemic factors.6,7 For example, Lacey et al. 6 show that Caribbean Blacks and Caribbean non-Blacks were at higher risk of cardiovascular disease than White Canadians, and Canadian Black and other non-White Canadians were also at higher risk of cardiovascular disease than Canadian Whites, even after controlling for social and demographic factors such as years since immigration and education. They propose several explanations for this, including lower rates of physician visits and health screening among immigrant and minority groups, language and cultural barriers, fear of westernized medicine, lack of knowledge about seeking help, and note that cardiovascular conditions increase with age especially among immigrant and racialized groups. 6 The health of immigrants often follows the “healthy immigrant effect”, whereby newcomers report better health upon arrival than non-immigrants (Vang et al., 2016), in part due to selective migration processes. However, this advantage typically erodes over time as immigrants encounter cumulative socioeconomic disadvantage, discrimination, and barriers to care.7–9

Further, health outcomes differ by other sociodemographic factors, including socioeconomic position, gender, and immigration trajectory, underscoring the need for analyses attentive to the interplay of these determinants.10,11 Language barriers, cultural discordance with providers, socioeconomic disadvantage, and racism all contribute to differences in access to care and health outcomes.12,13 Even within a universal health care system, racialized populations continue to face inequities in both service use and outcomes,6,14,15 suggesting coverage alone does not eliminate barriers to equity. Importantly, consider how these factors may interact. For example, Canadian immigrants have lower SES compared to native-born populations, 16 SES influences educational attainment, likely because social capital increases opportunities for higher SES individuals, 17 and education and income predict nutritional intake, 18 which can mitigate health risks among immigrant populations in Canada. 19 Of these factors, education can be considered especially important in relation to health because it has been linked causally to improved health outcomes in Canada. 20

Self-rated health as an indicator

Self-rated health (SRH) provides an important lens for examining inequities. SRH reflects an individual’s holistic assessment of their health and is strongly predictive of future morbidity, mortality, health care utilization, and quality of life. 21 It is widely used in Canadian and international population health surveys as a summary measure of well-being and captures experiential dimensions that may differ across groups.22–24 Canadian evidence shows consistent variation in SFH by immigration status and racialization. For example, Ali, McDermott, and Gravel 25 reviewed evidence from national surveys and found that immigrants often reported equal or better health than Canadian-born populations, though patterns varied by subgroup. Newbold 26 similarly demonstrated that immigrants initially reported better SRH than Canadian-born individuals, but this advantage diminished with longer residence in Canada. Lu and Ng 27 further showed that differences in SRH erosion varied across immigrant categories, underscoring the importance of considering immigration pathways in health research.

Longitudinal analyses reinforce these findings. Sing Setia et al., 28 using the Longitudinal Survey of Immigrants to Canada, found that SRH declined over time after arrival, with outcomes shaped by country of origin, sex, socioeconomic factors. Fuller-Thomson and colleagues 29 also documented early health decline among recent immigrants, suggesting that the healthy immigrant effect is time-sensitive. Together, this body of work suggests that SRH disparities are not only influenced by immigrant status or duration of residence, but also by systemic racism and broader social determinants of health.

Latin Americans and self-rated health (SRH)

Recent national data shed light on SRH for Latin Americans: in 2023, 61.2% rated their health as very good or excellent, compared with 52.2% of the total Canadian population, while only 8.3% reported fair or poor health, compared with 14.4% nationally. 30 These patterns were particularly pronounced among recent Latin American immigrants, 73.3% of whom reported very good or excellent health, far surpassing the Canadian-born average of 51.3%. 30 While these descriptive figures suggest that Latin Americans perceive themselves as healthier and more satisfied than the broader population, the reasons for these patterns remain unclear. Crucially, descriptive statistics do not account for variation in socioeconomic position, immigration experience, or other determinants strongly associated with health outcomes. Moreover, existing studies of SRH in Canada rarely consider Latin Americans as a distinct population, and instead tend to examine immigrants as a broad category or concentrate on larger racialized groups such as South Asian, Chinese, or Black Canadians.25,31,32 Thus, extant literature has not answered whether the diverse sociodemographic profiles and migration trajectories of Latin Americans shape their health outcomes in similar or unique ways. To address this issue, the present study examines self-rated health among Latin Americans in Canada compared to White and other racialized populations using national data. Specifically, it investigates whether Latin Americans report different odds of poor SRH and explores how these associations are moderated by age, sex, socioeconomic status, education, and immigration history.

Design and Methods

Data source and study sample

This study secondarily analyzed data from the Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey conducted annually by Statistics Canada. 33 The CCHS collects comprehensive data on the health status and determinants of health of Canadians aged 12 and older. For the present analysis, respondents aged 18 or older were included. To examine the health of Latin Americans prior to the disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic and to enhance statistical power given the relatively small Latin American population in Canada, data from four CCHS cycles (2015-2018) were pooled. The total pooled sample included 203,754 respondents, with three subsamples: Latin American (n = 1,799), non-Hispanic White (NHW; n = 168,225), and Other racialized groups (n = 33,730). The lack of data on specific Latin American immigrant ethnic groups residing in Canada prevented us from conducting a within group analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated using the full sample. Missing data for individual variables were low (<2% per variable). However, because listwise deletion was used in multivariable analyses, cases with missing values on any analytic variable were excluded. As a result, the final analytic sample was reduced to 175,496 respondents, reflecting cumulative missingness across variables rather than substantial missingness on any single variable.

Measures

The primary outcome was self-rated health (SRH) and was derived from the question: “In general, how is your health?” with response options of excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor. Following established practice, responses were dichotomized into poor/fair versus good/very good/excellent health.34,35 This categorization is commonly used to distinguish individuals reporting poorer health status from those reporting more favorable health and facilitates comparability across studies. The main predictor was self-reported racialized identity, with respondents categorized as Latin American, White (reference group), or Other racialized groups. Additional predictors included:● Age (18–24, 25–44, 45–64, 65+),● Sex (male or female, based on self-report),● Education (primary or less to graduate degree),● Household income (<$30,000 to $150,000+),● Immigration status (Canadian-born, ≥10 years, 5–10 years, or <5 years since arrival), and● Knowledge of official languages (English and/or French).

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and included marital status, sense of community belonging, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, smoking, and alcohol consumption.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using unweighted data. The CCHS employs a complex, non-self-weighting sampling design with person-level and bootstrap weights to generate population-representative estimates. These weights were not applied in the present analysis due to data access constraints. As such, findings should be interpreted as reflecting patterns within the analytic sample rather than precise population estimates. Prior methodological research suggests that weighting has a greater impact on descriptive statistics than on regression coefficients, 36 and that regression-based modeling approaches may still provide valid inferences under certain conditions even in the absence of weights. 37

All analyses were conducted using SPSS, Version 28.0. Descriptive statistics and chi-square tests examined associations between ethnicity, SRH, and all predictors and covariates. Binary logistic regression was then used to estimate disparities in SRH across groups. To assess whether the association between ethnicity and SRH varied across subgroups, five two-way interaction terms were tested: ethnicity by age, sex, education, income, and immigration status. Interaction terms were evaluated within multivariable logistic regression models, and only statistically significant interactions were retained in the final models presented. Non-significant interactions were tested but are not shown in the final models. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from regression models. A 95% CI excluding the null value of 1.00 indicated statistical significance (p < .05). ORs greater than 1.00 indicated higher odds of reporting poor/fair SRH relative to the reference, while ORs less than 1.00 indicated lower odds. Finally, this study utilized restricted CCH data accessed through the University of Windsor’s Research Data Centre with Statistics Canada approval.

Latin Americans were considerably younger than both Whites and other racialized groups. Over half (51.5%) of Latin Americans were between 25-44 years old, compared to 25.8% of White and 40.9% of other racialized respondents. Only 9.7% of Latin Americans were seniors (65+), compared to one-third (33.2%) of Whites. Sex distributions were similar across groups, with just over half of respondents identifying as female. Marital status varied significantly: Latin Americans had a higher proportion of individuals who were separated or divorced (14.3%) compared to Whites (12.7%) and other racialized groups (10.2%). Differences also emerged in body mass index (BMI). Nearly half of respondents from other racialized groups (48.3%) were in the normal weight range, compared with 42.9% of Latin Americans and 40.6% of Whites. Obesity rates were comparable for Latin Americans (19.6%) and other racialized groups (19.5%), but notably higher among Whites (24.0%).

Further, educational attainment was generally higher among Latin Americans: 25.7% held an undergraduate degree and 11.2% held an advanced degree, compared with 14.8% and 6.7% among Whites, respectively. Other racialized groups also showed higher postsecondary completion rates than Whites. With regards to income, distributions showed greater concentration at the lower end among Latin Americans and other racialized groups. About 20% of Latin Americans reported household incomes below $30,000, compared to 17.9% of Whites. Conversely, only 11.8% of Latin Americans reported household incomes of $150, 000 or more, compared to 14.6% of Whites. Lastly, patterns of immigration status also differed substantially. Only 24.8% of Latin Americans were Canadian-born, compared with 90.9% of Whites and 51.5% of other racialized respondents. Among immigrants, nearly half of Latin Americans (49.5%) had been in Canada for more than 10 years, while 11.2% had arrived within the past 5 years.

Main and interaction effects of self-rated health

Across all models, Latin Americans did not differ significantly from Whites in their odds of reporting fair or poor SRH (Model 1 OR = 0.90, 95% CI [0.79, 1.02]; Model 4 OR = 0.88, 95% CI [0.65, 1.19]). Other racialized groups showed slightly higher odds of poor SRH in Models 2 and 3 (ORs = 1.11 and 1.06, respectively), but this association was no longer significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 4 OR = 0.90, 95% CI [0.78, 1.03]). While these main effects suggest no overall differences between Latin Americans and Whites, they may obscure important heterogeneity across sociodemographic subgroups. Although some interaction-specific odds ratios included confidence intervals crossing the null value, statistically significant interaction terms indicate meaningful effect modification and should not be dismissed solely on the basis of subgroup confidence intervals (Greenland, 2009). Of the five interaction terms tested (ethnicity by age, sex, education, income, and immigration status), interactions with sex and income were not statistically significant and were not retained in the final models.

In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), significant interactions indicated that the association between ethnicity and SRH varied by age (p < .001), education (p = .017), and immigration history (p = .006). Relative to Whites, Latin American seniors demonstrated higher odds of reporting fair or poor SRH (OR = 1.56, 95% CI [1.05, 2.31]), whereas Latin American emerging adults showed comparatively lower odds (OR = 0.89, 95% CI [0.53, 1.51]). Educational gradients also differed by ethnicity. Compared with Whites, Latin Americans with only primary education demonstrated higher odds of fair or poor SRH (OR = 1.97, 95% CI [1.07, 3.64]), whereas those with a high school education showed lower odds (OR = 0.51, 95% CI [0.30, 0.86]). Similarly, the association between immigration history and SRH differed by ethnicity, with Latin Americans residing in Canada for 5–10 years demonstrating comparatively lower odds of fair or poor SRH relative to the reference group (OR = 0.61, 95% CI [0.36, 1.05]). Discussion

This study provides large-scale national survey-based evidence on patterns of self-rated health among Latin Americans in Canada. Findings should be interpreted as reflecting patterns within a large national survey sample rather than definitive population-level estimates. Additionally, given the number of predictors and interaction terms examined, there is an increased risk of Type I error; therefore, results—particularly those from subgroup analyses—should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Using national data, no significant differences between Latin Americans and Whites in the odds of reporting fair or poor SRH were found across all models. At first glance, these results suggest parity. However, it is important to note that this suggested parity does not indicate equity. Similarity in outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence that structural inequities are absent for Latin Americans in Canada. These findings add nuance to recent descriptive evidence showing that Latin Americans report higher levels of very good or excellent SRH compared with the Canadian population overall, with particularly favorable assessments among recent immigrants. 30 While such descriptive patterns suggest advantage, this analysis demonstrates that not all subgroups share equally in this profile. Relative to Whites, older Latin Americans and those with limited education demonstrated higher odds of fair or poor SRH, whereas younger Latin Americans and some more recently arrived immigrant subgroups demonstrated comparatively lower odds.

The observed interaction between ethnicity and age is consistent with broader evidence documenting health decline among immigrants over time and across the life course.26–28 Relative to Whites, older Latin Americans demonstrated higher odds of reporting fair or poor SRH, which may reflect the cumulative effects of structural disadvantage, barriers to culturally responsive care, and socioeconomic inequities experienced across the migration process. Educational gradients also differed by ethnicity. Relative to Whites, Latin Americans with lower educational attainment demonstrated higher odds of fair or poor SRH, whereas those with a high school education demonstrated comparatively lower odds. The finding that Latin Americans with a high school education reported more favorable SRH relative to Whites is noteworthy and may reflect several underlying mechanisms. One possible explanation is the healthy immigrant effect, whereby immigrants are often healthier upon arrival than both populations in their countries of origin and native-born populations in receiving countries due to selective migration processes. 38

In addition, self-rated health is a subjective measure that may be shaped by cultural norms, expectations, and reference-group comparisons that differ across populations. It is also possible that strong family networks, social cohesion, and community-based forms of support provide protective effects that are not fully captured by educational attainment alone. Similar patterns have been described in literature on immigrant and ethnic enclave health advantages. However, these interpretations remain speculative and additional research is needed to better understand how education, migration history, and cultural context interact to shape SRH among Latin Americans in Canada. Immigration history also appeared to modify the association between ethnicity and SRH. Relative to comparison groups, Latin Americans residing in Canada for 5–10 years demonstrated comparatively lower odds of reporting fair or poor SRH, whereas this apparent advantage was less evident among those with longer residence in Canada. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior research suggesting erosion of immigrant health advantages over time. 29 However, given the cross-sectional design and interaction-based nature of the analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of causal or temporal processes.

From a public health perspective, these results have several implications. First, they reinforce the importance of disaggregated analyses. Latin Americans are frequently grouped into broad “visible minority” categories, obscuring population-specific health patterns and perpetuating invisibility in research and policy. Second, the findings illustrate the limits of main effects models. Aggregate parity with Whites masks meaningful differences that emerge only when interactions are considered. As methodological literature emphasizes, statistically significant interaction terms provide evidence of effect modification. 39 Recognizing this complexity is essential for designing interventions that reflect the lived realities of immigrant subgroups. Finally, the findings point to the need for policies and programs that address structural inequities and respond to the diversity of migration trajectories. Interventions that are linguistically and culturally responsive, support educational attainment, and address barriers faced by aging immigrants may be particularly beneficial for Latin Americans in Canada. Each of these steps theoretically could help reduce some of the cumulative disadvantage faced by Latin Americans residing in Canada.

Study limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, limiting the ability to establish temporal relationships between sociodemographic factors and SRH. Second, SRH is a subjective measure that, while widely validated, may be influenced by cultural norms of health perception and reporting, which could vary across immigrant and racialized groups. Third, despite pooling four survey cycles to increase statistical power, the relatively small Latin American subsample limited precision for some subgroup estimates, particularly in the interaction analyses, where wide confidence intervals were observed. Fourth, the analysis did not disaggregate the Latin American population by country of origin, which may mask important within-group heterogeneity. A further limitation is the aggregation of all non-Latin-American racialized groups into a single comparison category. This grouping encompasses diverse populations (e.g., South Asian, Chinese, Black, Arab) with distinct social, cultural, and health profiles. As such, this approach may obscure important heterogeneity across groups. While disaggregating these populations would provide more nuanced insight, this was not feasible in the present study given data access constraints and sample size considerations for interaction analyses. Accordingly, findings for this category should not be interpreted as representing a homogeneous population. Future research should examine these groups separately to better capture population-specific health patterns.

An additional limitation is the use of unweighted analyses. Because the CCHS employs a complex sampling design with survey and bootstrap weights to produce population-representative estimates, failure to apply these weights may affect the generalizability of findings and the precision of variance estimates. While prior research suggests that regression coefficients are often less sensitive to weighting than descriptive estimates, 36 results should nonetheless be interpreted with caution and understood as reflecting patterns within a large national sample rather than definitive population-level estimates. 37 Finally, the reliance on self-reported measures for covariates such as BMI, physical activity, and alcohol use introduces the possibility of reporting bias. Further, self-rated health was dichotomized, which, while consistent with prior research, may reduce variability and obscure more nuanced differences across response categories. This limitation may be particularly relevant in subgroup and interaction analyses, where gradations in health status could provide additional insight. Despite these limitations, the use of nationally representative data provides robust evidence and a critical first step in addressing the longstanding lack of peer-reviewed research on Latin American health in Canada.
