Combined sleep behaviors, mediating biomarkers, and incident vascular complications among individuals with type 2 diabetes: A prospective study in UK Biobank
Authors: Yanqiu Zou, Xia Jiang, Jinyu Zhou, Tao Han, Sirui Zheng, Mingshuang Tang, Yutong Wang, Lin Chen, Lingli Qiu, Xuan Wu, Zhixin Tan, Yunjie Liu, Bowen Lei, Xiaofeng Ma, Di Zhang, Jiaqiang Liao, Jiayuan Li, Ben Zhang, Mengyu Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo prospectively examine how combined sleep behaviors affect vascular complications in type 2 diabetes, whether these associations are modified by disease severity, as well as the mediating roles of clinical biomarkers.MethodsA total of 12,456 participants with type 2 diabetes from the UK Biobank were included. A sleep score was defined by: sleep duration, chronotype, insomnia, snoring, and daytime sleepiness. Cox regression models were used to examine the association between healthy sleep score and risk of vascular complications.ResultsOver 13.5 years, 1,878 macrovascular and 3,066 microvascular complications occurred. Compared with a healthy sleep score of 0–1, the multivariable‐adjusted HRs (95% CIs) of the score 4–5 were 0.75 (0.63, 0.89) for macrovascular complications [coronary artery disease 0.72 (0.57, 0.91), stroke 0.92 (0.66, 1.28), peripheral artery disease 0.79 (0.56, 1.10)]; 0.86 (0.75, 0.99) for microvascular complications [neuropathy 0.62 (0.47, 0.83), nephropathy 1.01 (0.84, 1.21), retinopathy 0.84 (0.68, 1.03)]. The aggregated diabetes severity score (P interaction = 0.037) significantly modified the association with microvascular complications. Total cholesterol, LDL‐C, triglycerides, apolipoprotein B, and CRP collectively explained 11.34% (4.24%, 37.70%) of macrovascular associations [including 11.14% (2.85%, 43.03%) for coronary artery disease], while total bilirubin explained 3.57% (0.43%, 20.63%) of microvascular associations, collectively with triglycerides, GGT, and CRP mediating 9.33% (3.95%, 27.58%) for diabetic neuropathy.ConclusionsA healthy sleep pattern is associated with lower risks of vascular complications in type 2 diabetes, independent of traditional risk factors, and the association with microvascular complications is modified by diabetes severity. Lipid profiles and inflammatory factors partly mediate the favorable association.Keywords: Sleep, Type 2 diabetes, Vascular complications

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes remains a substantial public health issue, and its control represents an ongoing challenge. 1 Poorly managed type 2 diabetes resulting in continuous hyperglycemia affects almost every organ of the human body in the long term, particularly blood vessels, nerves, eyes, and kidneys, yielding complications. The most common of these are macrovascular conditions, such as coronary heart disease (CAD), stroke, and peripheral arterial disease (PAD), as well as microvascular conditions, including diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy. 2 All these complications bring substantial economic loss to families, health systems, and nations. 3 , 4 Therefore, cost‐effective type 2 diabetes management strategies to prevent or delay the development of vascular complications are urgently needed.

Sleep takes up to a third of individuals' everyday lives and represents a fundamental determinant of health. Various sleep behaviors, such as sleep duration, chronotype, snoring, insomnia, and daytime sleepiness, portray the multi‐dimensional characteristics of sleep. These behaviors often cluster together and interact with each other, where alterations in one behavior typically lead to changes in other behaviors. While a series of cohort studies has consistently demonstrated a beneficial role of healthy sleep behaviors in the onset of type 2 diabetes in the general population, 5 , 6 whether it influences the prognosis of type 2 diabetes, particularly the risk of vascular complications, has only recently begun to receive attention. Most early studies have focused on individual sleep behavior, overlooking its complex interplay. For example, several studies indicated that inadequate sleep duration 7 and insomnia 8 increased the risk of incident macrovascular complications among type 2 diabetes patients. A previous literature review reported that insomnia was associated with diabetic retinopathy, and individuals with type 2 diabetes and obstructive sleep apnea, characterized by snoring, were more likely to develop microvascular complications. 9 Three recent studies have extended this line of research to overall sleep patterns; however, these investigations have typically focused on either macrovascular 10 or microvascular outcomes 11 alone, or on composite vascular endpoints, without differentiating between specific complication types. 12 To summarize, sleep behaviors receive much less attention than they deserve in the management of type 2 diabetes.

Progression of type 2 diabetes is often influenced by disease severity, yet it remains unclear whether the association between sleep and vascular complications differs across subpopulations with varying diabetes severity. Moreover, as a metabolically active and dynamic process, insufficient sleep or sleep disorders may activate inflammatory responses, 13 interfere with glucose homeostasis, 14 alter lipid and lipoprotein levels, 15 , 16 and affect the liver 17 and kidney function, 18 all of which may influence the development of macrovascular 19 and microvascular 20 , 21 , 22 complications. However, existing evidence on these mechanisms remains limited. Prior studies have focused exclusively on composite or certain specific outcomes, 11 , 12 leaving the biological pathways linking combined sleep behaviors to distinct vascular complications insufficiently characterized.

Therefore, in the current study, based on a large, prospective cohort of the UK Biobank, we aimed to (1) quantify the associations of combined sleep behaviors, including sleep duration, chronotype, insomnia, snoring, and daytime sleepiness, with the risk of macro‐ and microvascular complications among individuals with type 2 diabetes; (2) examine whether these associations are modified by diabetes severity or other baseline risk factors; and (3) explore the potential mediatory effects of a range of clinical biomarkers on the association between combined sleep behaviors and vascular complications of diabetes.

METHODS

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large population‐based prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants during 2006–2010. Participants provided information on sleep and other health‐related resources through touchscreen questionnaires, verbal interviews, physical measurements, and biological samples. Details have been reported previously. 23 The UK Biobank research received approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethical Committee. All participants provided written informed consent for the study.

In the present study, we enrolled 23,533 participants with type 2 diabetes at baseline, identified through self‐reported medical information or hospital inpatient records. 24 We excluded participants who had outlying sleep durations (i.e., <2 or >12 h/day), those with missing data on sleep behaviors needed to construct the healthy sleep score, those with macrovascular or microvascular complications at baseline, and those with cancer or missing information on cancer at baseline. Finally, a total of 12,456 participants with type 2 diabetes remained in our analysis. A flowchart illustrating participants' inclusion and exclusion criteria is presented in Figure S1.

Assessment of sleep behaviors and definition of the healthy sleep score

All sleep behaviors were self‐reported. Sleep duration was recorded in hours per day by querying participants with “About how many hours sleep do you get in every 24 hours (including naps)?”. Chronotype was classified by asking participants to identify themselves as either definitely a “morning” person, more a “morning” than “evening” person, more an “evening” than “morning” person, or definitely an “evening” person. Insomnia symptoms assessment was conducted by asking participants, “Do you have trouble falling asleep at night or do you wake up in the middle of the night?” with response options of never/rarely, sometimes, or usually. Whether snoring or not was determined based on responses to the question, “Does your partner or a close relative or friend complain about your snoring?” with answers of yes or no. Information on daytime sleepiness was gathered by querying participants. “How likely are you to doze off or fall asleep during the daytime when you don't mean to? (i.e., when working, reading or driving)”, with response answers of never/rarely, sometimes, often, or all of the time.

The healthy sleep score was constructed through these five dimensions of sleep, consistent with a previous study. 25 Briefly, low‐risk sleep behaviors were defined as follows: sleep duration of 7–8 h per day, early chronotype (definitely “morning” or more “morning” than “evening”), never or rarely reporting insomnia symptoms, no self‐reported snoring, and no frequent daytime sleepiness (“never/rarely” or “sometimes”). For each low‐risk sleep behavior, the participants were assigned 1 point if meeting the definition, or 0 points otherwise. The healthy sleep score was computed by summing up all component scores, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 5. A higher score indicates a healthier sleep pattern. This study sets four groups, categorizing the healthy sleep score into 0–1, 2, 3, and 4–5, respectively.

Assessment of biomarkers

Based on the knowledge of potential pathways, five sets of biological biomarkers that might mediate the sleep‐vascular complications relationship among type 2 diabetes participants were selected. These included a total of 21 specific markers: (1) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c); (2) lipid profiles [total cholesterol (TC), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), triglycerides, apolipoprotein A, apolipoprotein B, and lipoprotein A]; (3) biomarkers related to liver function [alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin, total protein, and albumin]; (4) biomarkers related to renal function [cystatin C, creatinine, urate, and urea]; (5) inflammation factors [C‐reactive protein (CRP) and white blood cell count].

Assessment of outcomes

The vital status of each participant was ascertained from hospital admission records and death registry data through linked inpatient data and death certificates. The primary outcomes in our study were macrovascular complications (including the occurrence of CAD, stroke, and PAD) and microvascular complications (including the occurrence of diabetic neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy). Detailed coding information is provided in Table S1.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates included age (continuous), sex (male or female), ethnicity (White, Black, Asian, and mixed/other), Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI, continuous), body mass index (BMI, <25, 25–30 or ≥ 30 kg/m2), total physical activity level (low, moderate, or high IPAQ activity group), sedentary time (continuous), family history of CVD (yes or no), smoking status (never, previous, or current), drinking status (never, previous, or current), healthy diet score (0–1, 2, 3, 4, 5), antihypertensive medication (yes or no), cholesterol‐lowering medication (yes or no), diabetes duration (continuous), HbA1c (continuous), and diabetes medication (yes or no). TDI was an area‐based index, with a lower value indicating a higher socioeconomic status. The healthy diet score was calculated using the following factors 26 : vegetable intake, fruit intake, fish intake, unprocessed red meat intake, and processed meat intake. Each one point was given for each favorable dietary factor, yielding a total diet score ranging from 0 to 5. Diabetes duration was defined as the years between the first diagnosis of diabetes and the UK Biobank baseline assessment. Diabetes severity status was considered as an aggregated measure based on longer duration of diabetes (≥5 years), higher level of HbA1c (≥53 mmol/mol), or use of diabetes medication. Each of these characteristics was treated as one unit of diabetes severity, and their sum was defined as the diabetes severity score (0–3).

Statistical analysis

Person‐time was calculated from the date of enrollment to the date of diagnosis of outcomes, death, loss to follow‐up, or end of follow‐up (October 31, 2022), whichever came first. The proportion of missing data for all covariates is presented in Table S2. Missing values were imputed using the “missForest” package, based on a random forest algorithm. 27

Baseline characteristics of the included participants were described using means or percentages in each category of the healthy sleep score. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the associations between healthy sleep score and macro‐ and micro‐vascular complications, as well as their specific subtypes. Hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated from three models: model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; model 2 was additionally adjusted for ethnicity, TDI, family history of CVD, BMI, total physical activity level, sedentary time, healthy diet score, smoking status and drinking status; model 3 was further adjusted for antihypertensive medication, cholesterol‐lowering medication, diabetes duration, HbA1c and diabetes medication. For component‐level analyses, all sleep behaviors were included simultaneously in the fully adjusted models.

Stratified analysis was conducted by diabetes severity, age at baseline (≤60 or >60 years), sex, TDI (lower or higher according to median), smoking status (never or previous/present), drinking status (never or previous/present), healthy diet score (0–2 or 3–5), total physical activity level (low or moderate/high), BMI (<30 or ≥ 30 kg/m2), diagnosis age of diabetes (≤40 or >40 years). Interaction between the healthy sleep score and stratification factors was performed using the likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without a cross‐product term. Forest plots were utilized to show the associations between healthy sleep scores and macro‐ and microvascular complications among different subgroups.

Mediation analysis was performed to test whether the associations between healthy sleep score (exposure) and risk of vascular complications (outcome) in type 2 diabetes patients were mediated by the selected biological biomarkers (mediators). The mediated proportions were quantified during this process. The “CMAverse” package of R software was used for mediation analysis. 28 Linear regression models were fitted for the exposure‐mediator models, while Cox proportional hazards models were fitted for the exposure‐mediator‐outcome models. The covariates included in these models were the same as those used in the main models.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of findings. First, we restricted the analysis to participants with virtually complete data. Second, we excluded participants who developed vascular complications within the first 2 years of follow‐up. Third, multiple imputation of missing covariates was conducted using the ‘mice’ package. 29 Fourth, a weighted sleep score was constructed by assigning weights to each sleep behavior based on β coefficients derived from fully adjusted Cox models. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 software. The statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics were largely similar before (Table S3) and after imputation (Table 1). Among the 12,456 participants with type 2 diabetes, around 8.2%, 26.0%, 36.9%, and 28.9% had a healthy sleep score of 0–1, 2, 3, and 4–5, respectively. Participants with a higher healthy sleep score tended to be older, white, and have a higher socioeconomic status. They were more likely to be non‐current smokers or drinkers, to eat healthily, to be physically active, to spend less time in sedentary activities, and to keep a healthier BMI. Additionally, these participants tended to have lower HbA1c levels and be less likely to use antihypertensive or diabetes medication.

Combined sleep behaviors and vascular complications of diabetes

During a median follow‐up time of 13.5 years, we observed 1,878 incident macrovascular complications, including 1,099 CAD cases, 536 stroke cases, and 539 PAD cases, as well as 3,066 microvascular complications, including 626 diabetic neuropathy cases, 1,806 nephropathy cases, and 1,394 retinopathy cases.

Table 2 shows the associations of healthy sleep score with macro‐ and microvascular complications among participants with type 2 diabetes. The risk of overall vascular complications decreased significantly as the healthy sleep score increased. Specifically, compared to participants with a healthy sleep score of 0–1, the fully adjusted HRs (95% CIs) of those with a score of 4–5 were 0.75 (0.63, 0.89) for macrovascular complications and 0.86 (0.75, 0.99) for microvascular complications. Treating the healthy sleep score as an ordinal variable, each additional point was associated with a 7% decreased risk of macrovascular complications (HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.88–0.97), whereas the association with microvascular complications was not statistically significant (HR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.94–1.00).

These overall results were most pronounced for CAD and diabetic neuropathy. Specifically, compared to participants with a healthy sleep score of 0–1, the fully adjusted HRs (95%CIs) of those with a score of 4–5 were 0.72 (0.57, 0.91) for CAD and 0.62 (0.47, 0.83) for diabetic neuropathy; treating the healthy sleep score as ordinal, each additional point was associated with a 9% decreased risk of CAD (HR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.85–0.96) and a 10% decreased risk of diabetic neuropathy (HR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.84–0.98).

Unfortunately, we did not find any significant result for healthy sleep score in reducing the risk of stroke (HR = 0.92, 95% CI = 0.66–1.28), PAD (HR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.56–1.10), nephropathy (HR = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.84–1.21), or retinopathy (HR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.68–1.03). Most low‐risk sleep behaviors were associated with lower risks of vascular complications, although some associations did not reach statistical significance (Table S4).

Stratified analysis

As shown in Figure 1, no interaction was observed for healthy sleep score and diabetes severity with incident macrovascular complications (all P interaction >0.05). On the contrary, the aggregated diabetes severity score (P interaction = 0.037) and its two components, HbA1c (P interaction = 0.046) and diabetes duration (P interaction = 0.012), significantly modified the associations between healthy sleep score and microvascular complications. The protective effects of healthy sleep score were more pronounced among type 2 diabetes patients with higher disease severity indices at baseline. Specifically, compared with participants with a healthy sleep score of 0–1, those with a score of 4–5 had an HR of 0.75 (95% CI = 0.57–0.99) among individuals with a diabetes severity score of 3, whereas the corresponding HRs were 1.02 (95% CI = 0.69–1.50) for those with a severity score of 0 and 0.89 (95% CI = 0.74–1.06) for those with scores of 1–2. When the healthy sleep score was analyzed as a continuous variable, each additional point was associated with HRs of 0.91 (95% CI = 0.85–0.98), 1.07 (95% CI = 0.97–1.18), and 0.97 (95% CI = 0.93–1.02), respectively. Additionally, among participants with HbA1c exceeding 53 mmol/mol, the HR was 0.86 (95% CI = 0.70–1.04) for the categorical analysis and 0.94 (95% CI = 0.89–0.99) for the continuous analysis, whereas the corresponding HRs among those with HbA1c lower than 53 mmol/mol were 0.88 (95% CI = 0.73–1.07) and 1.00 (95% CI = 0.95–1.05), respectively. Furthermore, among participants with a diabetes duration longer than 5 years, the HR was 0.75 (95% CI = 0.61–0.92) for the categorical analysis and 0.93 (95% CI = 0.88–0.98) for the continuous analysis, compared with 0.98 (95% CI = 0.81–1.18) and 1.00 (95% CI = 0.96–1.05), respectively, among those with a duration less than 5 years.

The associations of healthy sleep score at baseline with biomarkers at baseline, and of biomarkers at baseline with the risk of vascular complications among participants with type 2 diabetes are shown in (Tables S7, S8).Nine biomarkers were significantly associated with the healthy sleep score, including lipid profiles (TC, LDL‐C, triglycerides, apolipoprotein B), liver function markers (ALT, AST, GGT, total bilirubin), and inflammation markers (CRP).

In summary, seven biomarkers were detected as significant mediators (Table S9). Specifically, CRP and several lipid profiles, including TC, LDL‐C, triglycerides, and apolipoprotein B, mediated the associations between healthy sleep score and macrovascular complications risk, with the proportion of mediation effect ranging from 3.0% to 6.9%. These mediatory effects remained consistent for CAD, with the proportion mediated ranging from 3.8% to 8.0%. Meanwhile, total bilirubin accounted for 3.6% of the proportion mediated for healthy sleep score and the risk of microvascular complications. In addition, triglycerides, GGT, and CRP also showed significant indirect effects for diabetic neuropathy, contributing 2.6%, 3.2%, and 4.5%, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In this large‐scale prospective cohort study, we found that a healthy sleep score, featured as sleep 7–8 h per day, an early chronotype, never or rarely experiencing insomnia, no self‐reported snoring, and no frequent daytime sleepiness, was inversely associated with the risk of both macro‐ and microvascular complications among individuals with type 2 diabetes. Specifically, CAD and diabetic neuropathy showed relatively stronger associations. Moreover, the favorable association between the healthy sleep score and microvascular complications was more pronounced among severe type 2 diabetes patients. In addition, five biomarkers (TC, LDL‐C, triglycerides, apolipoprotein B, and CRP) significantly mediated the association between sleep and macrovascular complications (including CAD), whereas total bilirubin mediated the association with microvascular complications; additional mediating effects of triglycerides, GGT, and CRP were observed for diabetic neuropathy.

Our study contributes to the evidence supporting that a healthy sleep pattern can significantly reduce the incidence risk of macrovascular complications, particularly for CAD. These findings are consistent with earlier prospective studies reporting inverse associations between overall sleep patterns and cardiovascular outcomes in this population. 10 , 12 Importantly, our study extends prior research by simultaneously evaluating both macrovascular and microvascular complications and by examining specific subtypes of these outcomes. In particular, we observed a robust inverse association between a healthy sleep pattern and the risk of diabetic neuropathy. This finding is in line with previous research that reported associations of individual sleep behaviors 9 , 30 , 31 and overall sleep patterns 11 , 12 with microvascular complications. However, we did not observe statistically significant associations for stroke, peripheral arterial disease, nephropathy, or retinopathy, although most estimates suggested a protective direction. Several explanations may account for these findings. First, the relatively limited number of incident cases for some outcomes may have reduced statistical power. Second, substantial heterogeneity exists across vascular beds in diabetes, with endothelial cells from different vascular territories exhibiting distinct molecular signatures, metabolic profiles, and responses to systemic exposures. This may result in differential susceptibility to sleep disturbances. 32 Third, although diabetic complications also share some common systemic mechanisms, their development and progression are organ‐specific and may require different exposure durations or intensities. 33 Nevertheless, through using the overall sleep pattern that comprised various highly correlated sleep behaviors, our study provides a straightforward and comprehensive description of sleep behaviors and facilitates the translation of findings into the public. Given that sleep behavior is a common modifiable lifestyle factor and type 2 diabetes patients are being failed by the lack of effective treatment options, this study indicated that maintaining a healthy sleep pattern could be a key part of type 2 diabetes management. Unfortunately, sleep has received little attention in the current management of type 2 diabetes, according to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline 34 or the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommendation. 35 Therefore, our study might serve as a wake‐up call to physicians, health‐care professionals, and policy makers regarding its importance in the management of type 2 diabetes patients.

Interestingly, we found a moderate interaction between healthy sleep score and severity of diabetes on the risk of microvascular complications. The observed protective association was more pronounced among those with higher HbA1c levels or longer diabetes duration, in line with previous studies supporting the sleep‐severity interaction. Existing evidence indicated that unhealthy sleep characteristics may precipitate type 2 diabetes progression, 9 , 30 , 31 and may influence the severity of diabetes. 36 , 37 Specifically, unhealthy sleep habits could result in poor glycemic control 38 , 39 and prolonged duration of diabetes, both of which are key factors symbolizing the disease severity. Moreover, these signals of diabetes severity were associated with an increased risk of microvascular complications of type 2 diabetes. 40 , 41 Thus, unhealthy sleep and diabetes severity synergistically contribute to a higher risk of subsequent microvascular complications. Although the manifestations of clinical disorders are generally less affected by lifestyle factors when the disease is more severe, we observed that among patients with severe type 2 diabetes, the risk of microvascular complications was more likely to be exacerbated by abnormal sleep patterns, or in other words, attenuated by healthy sleep patterns. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the need to reconsider sleep as an essential means of secondary prevention among patients with type 2 diabetes, especially for those under severe conditions with higher HbA1c levels or longer disease duration.

Importantly, our study introduced mediation analysis to examine the proportional contribution of potential clinical biomarkers to the association between sleep and the progression of type 2 diabetes. We found that lipid‐related profiles and inflammatory factors partly mediated the association. Our results appeared in line with the previous efforts in exploring the potential mediatory role of lipids, lipoproteins, and inflammatory factors in the relationship between sleep and vascular complications. For example, in the general population, a US study showed that inadequate sleep duration was positively associated with abnormal CRP, while sleep disturbance was associated with abnormal triglycerides, which may trigger metabolic imbalance and increase the risk of developing macrovascular events. 42 A previous review also concluded that chronic sleep deficiency disturbed immune homeostasis, leading to inflammatory and other reactions, thereby presumably increasing the risk of developing cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases. 43 Recent cohort studies in individuals with type 2 diabetes have also identified lipid metabolism and inflammatory pathways as potential mediators in the relationship between overall sleep patterns and composite vascular complications 12 or specific microvascular endpoints. 11 Our analysis provides a granular assessment by evaluating multiple biomarker domains in relation to distinct vascular endpoints. Despite this, the magnitude of the observed mediation effects was modest, suggesting that additional biological mechanisms beyond those captured in our biomarker panel may play important roles. First, sleep disturbances are closely linked to neuroendocrine dysregulation, including activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, which may adversely affect metabolic and vascular function. 44 Second, insulin resistance and broader metabolic dysregulation beyond glycemic control may contribute to vascular damage but were not fully captured by HbA1c alone. 32 , 33 Third, oxidative stress, an important mechanism in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications, 32 , 33 , 45 was not directly measured in this study. These unmeasured pathways may partly explain the limited proportion of mediation observed. Our mediation analysis nonetheless corroborated these findings by contributing to a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms in delaying the progression of type 2 diabetes. ADA guidelines have emphasized the significance of lipid management in type 2 diabetes, to prevent and/or to delay the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases and certain microvascular complications, including diabetic neuropathy. 46 , 47 Our findings further suggest that optimizing sleep behaviors may complement existing metabolic interventions, underscoring the need for an integrated approach that jointly targets sleep and biological risk pathways to reduce the burden of vascular complications in type 2 diabetes.

The current study systematically investigated the relationship between combined sleep behaviors and vascular complications in type 2 diabetes patients, and comprehensively explored the possible mediation pathways in a cohort of a large sample size and long follow‐up period. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all sleep behaviors were self‐reported, which may introduce errors in measurement and misclassification of exposures. However, it is likely that this would attenuate the findings towards null and underestimate the effect of the observed associations. Second, all sleep behaviors were assessed only once at baseline, which necessitates exploring the impact of changes in sleep patterns in the future. Third, the study population primarily consisted of individuals of white ethnicity residing in the UK, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. However, the geographical factor does not affect the internal validity of the research. Fourth, although multiple outcomes were examined, the primary analyses did not adjust for multiple comparisons. Using False Discovery Rate correction, associations for continuous sleep score remained consistent, whereas some categorical associations (e.g., with CAD) were attenuated, suggesting that these findings should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, there may still be some residual or unknown confounders that could influence the associations, although we have tried our best to adjust for the potential confounding factors.

CONCLUSIONS

In light of these findings, our results support the potential value of public health programs and clinical guidelines aimed at improving holistic sleep behaviors to reduce the risk of vascular events among type 2 diabetes patients, particularly for those with severe conditions. Lipids and inflammation biomarkers could serve as mediators of sleep patterns on type 2 diabetes progression, supporting the potential utilization of lipid‐lowering and anti‐inflammatory intervention strategies among these patients. Further studies are needed to confirm and to extend our findings.
