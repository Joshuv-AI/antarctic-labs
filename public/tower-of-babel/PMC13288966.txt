Economic evaluation of the Healthy Early Life Moments in Singapore (HELMS) programme: a study protocol for an integrated lifestyle intervention supported by a mobile health tool for mothers and children in Singapore
Authors: Kyaw Zay Ya, Chee Wai Ku, See Ling Loy, Annabel Ngien, Kai Ting Mok, Mei Chien Chua, Fabian Yap, Jerry Kok Yen Chan, Elizabeth Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Healthy Early Life Moments in Singapore (HELMS) programme was developed by a multidisciplinary team to improve metabolic and mental health outcomes in women/mothers and to promote healthy growth and development in their children. Despite increasing interest in preventive life-course care, evidence on the long-term economic impact of these life-course interventions remains limited. This protocol outlines the economic evaluation of the HELMS integrated lifestyle intervention, supported by a mobile health (mHealth) app, across the reproductive continuum from preconception to postpartum.Methods and analysisThe HELMS programme is conducted at KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital, recruiting overweight or obese women and planning to conceive. The economic evaluation comprises a within-trial costing study and a cost-effectiveness analysis. We will develop a Markov model for mothers and children to simulate maternal-child health outcomes over a 10-year horizon from the healthcare provider’s perspective. Primary data will be sourced from the HELMS programme and supplemented by local mother–child cohorts and published literature. The cost analysis will include direct medical, direct non-medical and programme-related costs incurred through the HELMS intervention. We will measure health outcomes in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) using the EuroQol-5 Dimension utility scores. We will conduct both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses to assess model uncertainty. The evaluation will estimate total and incremental costs, QALYs and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios for HELMS versus standard care. We will present cost-effectiveness acceptability curves and perform subgroup analyses to explore heterogeneity by maternal age, ethnicity and body mass index. A willingness-to-pay threshold appropriate for the Singapore context, based on recently published estimates, will be used to determine cost-effectiveness. We will also identify key cost drivers and examine the intervention’s scalability.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Centralised Institutional Review Board of SingHealth (2021/2247). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. The findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and disseminated to international and national policy makers.Trial registration numberNCT05207059.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH; Maternal medicine; Child; Other metabolic, e.g. iron, porphyria; Health Care Costs; HEALTH ECONOMICS

Background

Women of reproductive age with overweight or obesity face significant health challenges, including subfertility, adverse pregnancy outcomes, metabolic disorders and long-term health complications for both themselves and their offspring.1,4 Their children are also more vulnerable to obesity, metabolic diseases and psychosocial problems.2 5 The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) paradigm underscores the influence of early life exposures on long-term health outcomes, emphasising the importance of investing in preventive interventions starting prior to conception.6,9

Early childhood interventions can improve children’s psychological well-being, educational attainment and life-course trajectories, while also reducing healthcare use and societal costs over time.10,12 However, most programmes that begin during pregnancy or after birth have shown limited success or are still being evaluated, in preventing later metabolic or mental health problems in children.13,16 This highlights the need to shift our efforts further upstream in the life-course, where interventions in the preconception period may offer greater potential to improve metabolic health, enhance overall population health and reduce health disparities.16 17

To address this urgent unmet clinical gap, the Healthy Early Life Moments in Singapore (HELMS) programme was developed by a multidisciplinary team comprising obstetricians, neonatologists, paediatricians, dietitians, physiotherapists, psychiatrists and psychologists.18 19 HELMS is a multiphase, holistic lifestyle programme supported by a mobile health (mHealth) application.19 It targets women with overweight or obesity, beginning in the preconception phase and continuing through pregnancy and up to 18 months post partum.19 20 The programme also supports child feeding and growth monitoring, adopting a life-course approach to improve metabolic and mental health in mothers while promoting healthy growth and development in children.19 This model of care is aligned with Singapore’s Ministry of Health (MOH) Healthier SG initiative, launched in 2023 to strengthen primary care with a focus on prevention and healthier behaviours.21 In line with this, and guided by the DOHaD framework, HELMS programme aims to transform maternal–child healthcare through primordial prevention and sustained support.9 18 22 23

Despite the promise of such interventions, a significant gap remains in the health economics literature regarding the long-term economic impact of life-course programmes.24 25 While there is increasing recognition of their value, few studies have rigorously assessed the cost-effectiveness of integrated lifestyle interventions that address both maternal and child health outcomes.26 The evaluation of this full continuum of care presents methodological challenges and remains underexplored, with findings often limited and mixed evaluation of this full continuum of care presents methodological challenges and remains underexplored.26 As a result, the long-term practical economic benefits of these programmes remain unclear, and delayed long-term benefits may be overlooked or undervalued in policy and planning decisions. Closing this evidence gap can offer a more robust data-driven evaluation of intervention outcomes, as well as support the prioritisation of Healthier SG initiatives that provide the greatest value for investment.21

This study protocol details the economic evaluation of HELMS, using mother-child models run in parallel that reflect the distinct clinical pathways of such dyads. The primary objective is to assess the cost-effectiveness of the HELMS intervention across the reproductive continuum: preconception, pregnancy and post partum, compared with standard care, and assess the scalability of HELMS within Singapore’s health system.

Methods and analysis

We will conduct an economic evaluation alongside a single-centre implementation study of the HELMS Programme. The evaluation will adhere to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards and established best practices in health economic reporting.14 27

The HELMS programme

The HELMS Programme adopts a life-course model of care, providing integrated mHealth application support throughout the preconception, pregnancy and postpartum periods.19 The intervention comprises three main components: HELMS Journey, HELMS Model and HELMS Lifestyle.19 The HELMS Journey provides anticipatory guidance across the maternal care continuum, along with study-specific and routine clinical assessments at each stage. The HELMS Model follows the 4S framework, comprising Screening (health risk and biomarker assessments), Size (education and monitoring of weight, diet and activity), Supplementation (tailored micronutrients such as vitamins D and E, calcium and docosahexaenoic acid) and Special considerations tailored to the life-course (sexual health, pregnancy symptom management, postpartum recovery, and infant feeding). The HELMS Lifestyle targets metabolic health through diet and activity modules, guided by the 6P tool, which uses the Theory of Planned Behaviour to foster self-awareness and lasting behaviour change.20 28 The 6Ps are Portion (amount of food intake), Proportion (caloric density of food intake), Pleasure (frequency of snacks and sugary beverages and irregular intake), Period (time of day of food intake), Physicality (physical activity and sedentary behaviour) and Psychology (readiness for change).20 The programme is supported by tailored exercise videos, wearable trackers, and mental health and sleep modules, with engagement reinforced through the SIGN strategy: Support (enabling women to receive timely support from healthcare providers), Inform (informing women of important points of caution from prepregnancy to postpregnancy), Guide (guidance focusing on nutrition, physical activity, mental health and sleep hygiene) and Nudge (personalised messages and reminders).19 Further details of the intervention are available in Chan et al.19

The primary outcomes include pregnancy rate, maternal metabolic health (body mass index, BMI, glucose, lipids, blood pressure) and mental health. The secondary outcomes include reproductive health, gestational weight gain, pregnancy complications, birth outcomes and behavioural factors (diet, activity, sleep). Infant outcomes include feeding practices and growth up to 18 months.

Participants

Participants are recruited through preconception clinics, public health campaigns and healthcare referrals to ensure a diverse sample. Eligible women are aged 21–40 years, of Chinese, Malay or Indian ethnicity (the main ethnic groups in Singapore), have a BMI of 25–40 kg/m² (overweight or obese), plan to conceive within 12 months, intend to reside in Singapore for the next 4 years, have Internet access and are able to provide written informed consent.19 Exclusion criteria include current pregnancy, certain medical conditions (eg, diabetes), recent use of specific medications and limited English proficiency. While English is the main language used in Singapore including a majority of research settings, we acknowledge that this limitation (involving only English-speaking participants) may inhibit generalisability of results across different settings.19 The babies born to women who enrolled in HELMS and gave birth within 1 year of the preconception phase are also being recruited as part of the study.19 Participants who withdraw, are non-compliant, experience adverse outcomes or do not conceive by the end of the preconception phase are removed from the study. Partners are passively involved and monitored throughout HELMS.19

Implementation setting

This single-arm study is being conducted at KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH) in Singapore, which is a leading healthcare institution that supports approximately 12 000 births each year.19 The intervention spans from the preconception phase to 18 months post partum. Participants undergo assessments at multiple time points, with comprehensive follow-up including clinic visits, digital tracking and periodic check-ins. Longitudinal bio-sampling and detailed phenotyping are performed throughout the study to monitor biological and metabolic changes, identify biomarkers of intervention efficacy and explore maternal–infant health pathways across the preconception to postpartum period. Women who become pregnant will continue to undergo the scheduled assessments and will receive personalised support during pregnancy and the postpartum period, including interventions for both mother and infants. Those who do not conceive within 12 months will complete an exit assessment, which includes questionnaires, physical measurements and bio-sampling, and may be referred for a subfertility evaluation. Their health data will still be considered in our analyses to ensure a holistic evaluation of the programme’s effectiveness. In other words, the study captures and investigates outcomes for both women who conceive and those who do not, to derive a comprehensive evaluation of the programme’s impact across the full range of reproductive and metabolic health outcomes.

Patients and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in shaping the analytical approach for this economic evaluation. However, findings from interviews undertaken during the development of the HELMS intervention informed the assumptions related to engagement, delivery and uptake in the model.

Cost-effectiveness modelling

A cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) will be conducted to evaluate the incremental cost and health consequences of the HELMS intervention relative to standard care, with outcomes expressed as the incremental cost per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gained.24 25 Cost-effectiveness will be modelled, rather than only using the study data and time frames. Trial-based CEA will limit the generalisability of the results and the ability to do sensitivity analysis to explore uncertainty of the results. Two Markov models will be developed to simulate the progression of health outcomes and healthcare resource use over time: one for the mother and another for the child (see example models in figures1 2). The use of state-transition Markov models is particularly well suited to this evaluation, as data beyond that sourced from the HELMs study can be used in the model and changes in model parameters over time.29 Furthermore, the two Markov models will enable the modelling of costs and health outcomes forward over a 10-year period. These two models will be designed, populated with data and run in parallel. This means that costs and health outcomes for both the mother and child can be incorporated into the final results.

Distinct health states for each model will be defined, with the probability of transitioning between health states estimated based on health outcomes observed in the HELMS study and supplemented by published literature.30 These health states will capture maternal outcomes and complications such as metabolic and pregnancy status, gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, preterm birth and birth weight categories, as well as infant outcomes including live births, feeding practices and growth trajectories. Modelling mother and child in parallel allows the evaluation to capture intergenerational effects, recognising that improvements in maternal health directly influence child health outcomes and associated costs.31 A utility value, derived from health outcome data, and a cost will be attributed to each health state.30

Resource use

This study will adopt a trial-based costing approach, identifying, measuring and valuing resources used from the healthcare provider perspective.14 As the HELMS study aims to assess outcomes such as pregnancy rates, maternal metabolic and mental health, and infant growth and development, the economic evaluation will estimate the cost of delivering the intervention compared with standard (usual) care.

We will focus on major cost categories including healthcare service utilisation, programme delivery costs and indirect programme-related costs. Examples include consultation fees, diagnostic investigations, hospitalisation, medications and supplements, digital health tools, staff time and administrative overheads. These cost categories reflect the different phases of the intervention: preconception, pregnancy, delivery and post partum and capture resource use associated with female participants, infants and partners. The clinical costs include consultation fees for healthcare providers, investigations (eg, blood tests, ultrasounds, anthropometric assessments) and vaccinations based on the national immunisation schedule. Hospitalisation-related costs such as vaginal or caesarean delivery, surgical fees and postoperative care will also be included. Costs of medications and supplementations (eg, maternal multimicronutrients and infant vitamin D drops) will be considered.

Programme-related costs, such as the operational cost of the mHealth app, educational materials, activity and sleep trackers and lifestyle coaching, will be captured. Partners’ involvement, including one-time bio sampling during pregnancy, will also be included. Indirect costs, such as programme coordination, patient incentives and administrative overheads, are also considered. All cost items related to the HELMS intervention can be seen in table 1.

Estimating costs for the comparator arm, representing standard maternal care in Singapore, will draw on observational data from two large local cohorts: Singapore Preconception Study of Long-Term Maternal and Child Outcomes (S-PRESTO) and Growing Up in Singapore Towards Healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) supplemented by relevant literature and systematic reviews.32 33 The S-PRESTO is a longitudinal cohort that follows women from preconception through pregnancy and postpartum, capturing detailed metabolic, behavioural and offspring health outcomes. The GUSTO study is a pregnancy cohort that tracks women from early pregnancy through their child’s early years, collecting comprehensive maternal, fetal and child health data. Both cohorts reflect routine maternal care without structured preconception support, targeted postpartum metabolic recovery strategies or the digital and lifestyle interventions delivered in HELMS.

Identification of HELMS resource use

Identifying resource use will involve systematically documenting all components required for delivering the HELMS intervention and usual care. The HELMS study protocol outlines the details of the comprehensive HELMS intervention, including the services provided to participants across the preconception, pregnancy and postpartum phases.19 In contrast, usual care is defined using data from GUSTO, S-PRESTO and relevant published literature that reflects standard maternal care, which typically excludes structured preconception care and postpartum strategies to optimise mothers’ metabolic recovery, and does not include the digital and lifestyle interventions provided in the HELMS programme.32 33 The Cost of Illness (COI) framework will guide the cost analysis, enabling relevant cost components to be identified and categorised.34 Cost data will be obtained from various sources, such as patient registries, billing records, questionnaires and KKH’s outpatient and inpatient administrative financial systems. Other sources of data include the HELMS reimbursement summary, tax invoices, HELMS charge forms, the outpatient administration system, hospital records, relevant literature and patient surveys. Where necessary, interviews with clinical and administrative staff will supplement this information to clarify patient flow and actual care delivery.

Measurement of HELMS resource use

Resource use will be measured at the individual participant level to accurately capture utilisation patterns and timing across different phases of the intervention: preconception, pregnancy, delivery and postpartum. Costs will be further classified as start-up (eg, staff training) or recurrent (eg, ongoing consultations, supplementation). Data on healthcare utilisation will be drawn from participant records, digital tracking via the mHealth platform (eg, self-monitoring features) and HELMS-specific forms such as charge sheets and reimbursement summaries. Time spent by healthcare staff on intervention-related activities, coaching and data capture will also be recorded and included. Participant questionnaires will supplement administrative data, especially for patient-reported utilisation (eg, use of sleep/activity trackers or counselling). Only resource use directly attributable to the HELMS intervention will be measured. Services or referrals beyond KKH and unrelated to the intervention will be excluded.

Valuation of HELMS resource use

All resources will be valued in Singapore dollars from the healthcare provider perspective. Unit costs will be obtained from KKH’s outpatient and inpatient administrative financial systems. For programme-specific items, such as coaching, digital tools, patient materials and incentives, valuation will be based on actual expenditure. Staff time will be valued using wage rates for relevant personnel. Costs will be adjusted to current-year values using the Singapore Gross Domestic Product (GDP) deflator published by the World Bank to ensure comparability over time.25

Health outcomes and transition probabilities

The impact of the HELMS intervention will be quantified in terms of QALYs. This is estimated by combining the time participants spend in various health states with appropriate utility weights derived from the EuroQol-5 Dimension (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire, using a Singapore-specific EQ-5D-5L value set.35 Health states will be defined primarily based on key clinical and lifestyle outcomes such as pregnancy and postpartum metabolic status, mental health and overall well-being as outlined in the modelling approach. For the HELMS arm, utility values will be estimated mainly from EQ-5D-5L data. If the study data indicate that the HELMS intervention provides additional quality-of-life benefits within the same clinical state, for example through behavioural, psychosocial or lifestyle support, these differences will be captured through adjusted utility estimates. For the comparator arm (derived from the S-PRESTO and GUSTO cohorts), where EQ-5D-5L data are unavailable, utilities will be estimated indirectly using clinical and demographic variables informed by published literature and assigned to each corresponding health state. Finally, utilities will be combined with the time spent in each health state to calculate QALYs, using the same methodological approach as for the HELMS intervention group. To account for the skewed nature of health utility data, we will apply preference-based index scores and beta regression models to estimate health state utilities where appropriate.36

Primary health outcomes from the HELMS study will inform the transition probabilities in our economic model. Key maternal outcomes, including pregnancy rate, time-to-conception, live birth rate, maternal metabolic indicators (such as BMI, fasting glucose, lipid profiles, blood pressure) and mental health status, will be used to determine how individuals move between health states. While QALYs offer a standardised measure for comparing interventions, they may not fully capture the breadth of outcomes targeted by HELMS.37 Therefore, the transition probabilities between health states in both the mother and infant Markov model structures will be an important way to represent how likely it is that a mother or infant will actually move to a state of health with a high utility value.

Cost-effectiveness modelling analysis

The two Markov models (figures1 2) will be run twice each: once for HELMS and again for usual care in Singapore. The mean values for costs, health outcomes and transition probabilities will be estimated along with SD. At the completion of 10 years in the model, the difference in total mean costs between the HELMS programme and standard care will be divided by the difference in total mean QALYs to calculate an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). To quantify uncertainty in the economic evaluation, both model structure and parameter uncertainty will be systematically assessed.38 Both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses will be employed to explore how variations in key parameters influence the ICER. One-way deterministic sensitivity analyses will be used to evaluate the effect of varying individual parameters on the ICER. Parameters to be tested include discount rates (0%, 1%, 3.5% and 5%), the persistence of intervention effects and the classification of costs as one-time versus recurrent.39 Probabilistic sensitivity analysis will also be conducted to quantify the uncertainty in the model. The SD around the utility, cost and transition probability values will be combined with appropriately allocated distributions for these parameters. This expression of possible data for the model parameters will be used to create a random set of 10 000 ICERs, using Monte Carlo simulation. These will be plotted on a cost-effectiveness plane and compared with a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of SG$15 000 per QALY gained to determine cost-effectiveness.40 HELMS will be deemed cost-effective if more than 50% of the 10 000 possible ICERs sit below the willingness to pay threshold. The simulated distribution of ICER values will also be used to construct cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.41 These curves will depict the probability that the HELMS programme is cost-effective at various WTP thresholds. WTP thresholds will be guided by international benchmarks, including one to three times Singapore’s GDP per capita per QALY gained.

Subgroup analyses will be undertaken to explore heterogeneity in cost-effectiveness across different population subgroups, such as maternal age, ethnicity and BMI. Finally, scenario analyses will be used to test the impact of alternative modelling assumptions, including extended time horizons, delayed health benefits and varying levels of programme coverage and adherence.

The analysis will adopt a healthcare provider perspective and apply a ten-year time horizon to capture the medium- to long-term impacts of the HELMS programme on both mothers and their children. The model will simulate health outcomes starting from study cohort data collected from preconception through 18 months postpartum. For the extended follow-up of children at ages 6, 8 and 10 years, data will be sourced from relevant published literature and cohort studies to inform assumptions about long-term health outcomes and resource use.

Tools and software

All statistical analyses and economic modelling will be conducted using a combination of software tools. STATA/SE V.16 will be used for data management, statistical analyses and regression modelling. Microsoft Excel, together with TreeAge Pro 2023, will be used to construct and analyse decision-analytic models, including decision trees and Markov models for the CEA.

Ethics and dissemination

The intervention is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05207059) and was approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB Ref: 2021/2247). All participants signed a written informed consent form before participating in the study. Identifying information was removed before analysis and all methods followed relevant guidelines and regulations. The findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and disseminated to international and national policy makers.

Discussion

This study presents one of the first extended-horizon economic evaluations of an integrated lifestyle intervention that captures long-term health and cost outcomes for both mothers and their children. We will develop and evaluate two parallel Markov models for mothers and children, which simulate distinct clinical pathways and estimate outcomes related to pregnancy, metabolic and mental health and early childhood development. This structure improves the precision of outcome estimates and enhances the policy relevance of the findings, particularly in light of Singapore’s efforts to address persistently low fertility rates and improving metabolic outcomes.1 18

Singapore’s healthcare system provides a supportive context for implementing and evaluating preventive, integrated lifestyle interventions such as the HELMS programme. It is characterised by a well-integrated public–private model, centralised health governance and a strong maternal and child health (MCH) infrastructure, with coordinated care across primary and specialist services.41 Singapore’s MOH launched the Healthier SG initiative in 2023 which aimed to strengthen primary care through preventive health and healthier behaviours.21 The HELMS programme is well aligned with these national priorities. This setting supports the delivery and potential scalability of integrated care models within polyclinics and primary healthcare settings. Such models may improve access and population reach and have the potential to generate economic benefits through more efficient use of resources and improved MCH outcomes, although these effects require formal evaluation.41 This is where the present economic evaluation will contribute by assessing the potential costs and benefits of implementing the HELMS programme within the healthcare system.

The HELMS programme was developed based on the DOHaD paradigm and the well-established empirical relationship between maternal overweight and obesity and a range of adverse outcomes, including increased risks of chronic disease, poor mental health and the intergenerational transmission of metabolic risk.6 42 Maternal obesity is also associated with multidirectional effects on fetal growth, including low birth weight, fetal growth restriction and macrosomia.43 44 HELMS uniquely offers continuous, participatory care from the preconception period through to postpartum, enabling a life-course, preventive approach to risk reduction across the reproductive continuum.

The HELMS programme provides an opportunity to evaluate the long-term health and economic impact of a lifestyle intervention across both maternal and child outcomes. In addition, HELMS takes a broader perspective by addressing not only maternal health but also child outcomes, with a particular focus on metabolic and mental health. These areas are often overlooked in existing economic evaluations, despite their potential to significantly influence long-term healthcare costs and resource use. To capture these effects for both mothers and children, the HELMS programme uses the two Markov models that allow for simulation of long-term maternal and child health pathways, capturing both the direct and intergenerational effects of the HELMS programme.13 15 25 26 45 This supports a more comprehensive assessment of its potential economic impact, providing evidence to inform policy decisions and population health planning.

HELMS also targets a high-risk population of women with overweight or obesity. This also aligns with public health goals to reduce health disparities and improve outcomes in vulnerable groups.22 The intervention also aims to improve mental health outcomes, which are frequently underrepresented in similar programmes. The inclusion of psychological as well as physical health dimensions will also enhance the complex, multidimensional nature of maternal lifestyle interventions. Subgroup analyses based on maternal age, ethnicity and BMI will further assess heterogeneity in cost-effectiveness, offering insights into equity and generalisability of the study results to other healthcare settings. This study will assess the scalability of the HELMS programme within Singapore’s healthcare system. Findings will provide critical evidence to guide healthcare resource allocation, support programme design and evaluate the potential for scaling HELMS to primary care and polyclinic settings.

However, we acknowledge several limitations. Although trial-based data and transition probabilities are used to model disease progression, outcomes measured in QALYs may not fully capture the broader health and behavioural effects of lifestyle interventions. Long-term evidence on lifestyle interventions remains limited and inconsistent, and in the absence of a comparator arm, costs and effects are estimated using assumptions and external cohort data, introducing uncertainty and limiting transferability. In addition, important pregnancy complications such as preterm birth and fetal growth restriction are clinically relevant outcomes; however, their expected incidence is relatively low, and the study is not powered to assess these endpoints. Future studies should explore the potential impact of the intervention on reducing obstetric complications and the associated cost savings, as well as the potential role of partner or spouse involvement in influencing these outcomes.

Taken together, given the comprehensive scope and evidence-based design of the HELMS programme, it has the potential to shape future funding decisions and guide the integration of MCH services within Singapore’s evolving healthcare landscape. The findings contribute to growing evidence that early investments in MCH yield long-term health and economic returns. While the HELMS model of care is currently tailored to the Singaporean context, this model of care framework can be adapted to other settings with similar healthcare infrastructure and maternal-child health priorities. As the body of evidence for early-life interventions grows, cross-jurisdictional comparisons will be fundamental in guiding MCH policy decisions and maximising the long-term impact on population health.
