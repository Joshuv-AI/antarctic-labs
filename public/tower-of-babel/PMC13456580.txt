A Prospective Multicenter Post-Market Study Evaluating the Safety and Effectiveness of Cochlear Implantation in Adults With Single-Sided Deafness
Authors: Daniel M. Zeitler, Enrique Perez, Jennifer Alyono, Courtney Broadfoot, Olivia Boothby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To assess the safety and efficacy of cochlear implantation in adult patients with single-sided deafness (SSD) through a post-market approval study.Study design:Prospective, nonrandomized, single-arm multicenter post-market clinical investigation.Setting:Five tertiary referral cochlear implant (CI) centers.Patients:Thirty adults older than or equal to 18 years with profound unilateral sensorineural hearing loss (pure-tone average ≥ 80 dB HL and CNC ≤ 5%) and normal/near-normal hearing in the contralateral ear (pure-tone average ≤ 30 dB HL).Intervention(s):Unilateral cochlear implantation with an FDA-approved CI system.Main outcome measure(s):Changes in speech recognition in quiet and noise, binaural hearing in noise, spatial hearing, tinnitus reduction, and quality of life.Results:Significant improvements in speech perception in quiet and noise were demonstrated from the preoperative to the 12-month postoperative timepoint. Target-to-masker separated hearing tasks showed significant improvements for both head shadow and binaural squelch at the 12-month postoperative timepoint. Significant effects of the CI were also seen for tinnitus reduction, quality of life improvement, fatigue reduction, and self-reported benefits.Conclusions:This prospective, multicenter post-market approval study reports on the outcomes of CI for SSD in a cohort of adult patients. Significant improvements in speech in quiet and noise at 12 months were demonstrated. In addition, significant benefit was observed for all patient-reported outcome measures including tinnitus reduction, listening fatigue, quality of life, and spatial hearing. Cochlear implantation is an excellent treatment option for SSD patients and has a positive impact on many aspects of patients’ lives and well-being.

Introduction

Single-sided deafness (SSD), defined as severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) with poor speech recognition in one ear and normal/near-normal hearing in the contralateral ear, has an estimated prevalence of 1.5% in the US population and an annual estimated incidence of 7500 new cases1–4. The listening handicaps caused by SSD include difficulty with speech comprehension in noise5–12, sound localization5,8,9,13,14, increased listening effort15,16, loss of binaural cues17–19, and decreased overall and hearing-related quality of life (QOL)14,20–22. Many with SSD are also affected by tinnitus. In fact, 75% of SNHL patients report tinnitus, and nearly one-third report moderate to severe levels of tinnitus23–25.

Many patients with SSD first attempt to compensate using conservative, noninvasive measures such as head positioning, selective seating, or assistive listening devices. Many individuals report avoiding challenging environments, thereby reducing social interactions and negatively impacting their QOL26. In cases in which these conservative measures fail, several treatment options exist. Until recently, rehabilitative options included only devices that reroute the acoustic signal from the impaired ear to the better hearing ear (eg, contralateral routing of signal (CROS) aid and bone conduction hearing devices [BCD])5,20,27. Rerouting devices show significant benefits for speech perception in noise for specific target-to-masker configurations (eg, head shadow) and can improve signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) by 2.5 dB4,20. Conversely, these devices significantly degrade speech understanding in noise in other situations by up to 3.1 dB SNR (eg, noise rerouted to the better hearing ear). As such, they prove detrimental to the squelch effect and do not reliably improve sound localization20,27,28.

In 2008, Van de Heyning reported on the use of a cochlear implant (CI) to treat SSD patients with incapacitating tinnitus and showed a meaningful reduction in tinnitus in 21 patients up to 2 years post-implantation29. Subsequent research showed CIs not only provided substantial tinnitus reduction in patients with SSD, but also afforded meaningful improvements in speech recognition, hearing in noise, sound localization, self-perceived improvements, and QOL4,30–32. In 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved CIs for adults and children 5 years and older with SSD and asymmetric hearing loss33, and in 2022 for adults and children 5 years and older with SSD34. Since 2008, several reports have highlighted the growing interest in and research on this topic, all showing significant benefits in adults undergoing CI for SSD for both subjective and objective measures4,35,36. The purpose of the current study is to assess the safety and efficacy of cochlear implantation in adult patients with SSD at 12 months postoperatively. Specifically, we evaluated objective and subjective outcomes using several test environments as well as patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).

Materials and methods

The study complied with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from each participant. A master IRB approval was obtained from WCG Clinical (Cary, NC) by the study sponsor (IRB # 20221506) and was used by 4 sites. The fifth site obtained local IRB approval (IRB # 202203685) (ClinicalTrials.gov registration number: NCT05318417).

This was a prospective, nonrandomized, single-arm multicenter post-market clinical investigation after FDA approval for use of the Cochlear Nucleus CI system for the SSD indication. Five investigational sites across the United States participated. Enrollment took place between August 2022 and April 2023 with a maximum enrollment of 30 adults. Participants were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: age older than or equal to 18 years; severe SNHL defined as pure-tone average (PTA) at 0.5, 1, 2, 4 kHz >80 dB HL and aided CNC score ≤5% in the ear to be implanted; normal/near-normal hearing (NH) defined as a PTA of 0.5, 1, 2, 4 kHz ≤30 dB HL in the contralateral ear; previous experience with or 2-week minimum trial with a conventional treatment option (eg, hearing aid, BCD, or CROS aid); English as primary language; and a willingness and ability to provide informed consent. Participants who met any of the following exclusion criteria were deemed ineligible: cochlear ossification, abnormal cochlear nerve or other cochlear anomaly; previous cochlear implantation; hearing loss of neural or central origin; profound SNHL >10 years; active and/or chronic middle-ear infection; conductive hearing loss; medical or psychological condition contraindicating surgery; unrealistic expectations on the part of the participant and/or family; cognitive or developmental disability; inability or unwillingness to comply with the requirements of the investigation.

Surgical procedure

All patients received the Cochlear Nucleus CI System with either the 622 or 632 electrode array (Cochlear Limited, Sydney, Australia). The choice of the electrode was made by the implanting surgeon. All surgeries were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for use.

Outcomes

Pre-implantation, unaided audiometric thresholds were obtained for each ear with insert earphones using standard techniques. After device implantation, participants attended 4 scheduled visits over 12 months (activation, 3, 6, and 12 mo) to undergo objective hearing measures and safety assessments. After implantation, aided sound field audiometric thresholds were obtained using the CI alone with masking in the NH ear. Masking was performed using narrowband noise delivered through an earphone in the NH ear centered at the tested frequency, whereas speech noise was used for speech testing.

The primary effectiveness endpoint was an improvement in speech understanding in noise in the binaural (CI+NH) listening condition compared with the preoperative best listening condition (NH alone or NH+HA) at 12 months post-implantation in 3 target-to-masker separated speaker configurations: speech front/noise front (S0N0; binaural summation); speech front/noise NH ear (S0NNH; head shadow); and speech front/noise CI (S0NCI; binaural squelch). Speech understanding in noise was assessed using the Bamford-Kowal-Bench Speech-in-Noise (BKB-SIN) test given this test allows for adaptive hearing in noise testing37. An SNR improvement of 1.0 dB corresponds to an ~10% improvement, which aligns with the minimal clinically meaningful difference used for CNC words38. Note that these values were accepted by FDA to demonstrate minimal clinically meaningful benefit.

The study included 2 secondary effectiveness endpoints: (1) improvement in patient-reported spatial hearing as quantified by the Speech, Spatial, and Qualities of Hearing (SSQ) Scale,39 (2) the effectiveness of CI alone for speech perception in quiet measured by consonant-nucleus-consonant (CNC) word recognition test at 12 months post-CI in the implanted ear alone40.

Several additional endpoints were assessed using PROMs including: tinnitus (Tinnitus Handicap Inventory [THI])41, QOL (Cochlear Implant Quality of Life [CIQoL-35])42, hearing ability (SSQ)39, and fatigue (Vanderbilt Fatigue Scale for Adults [VFS])43.

The primary safety endpoint was the occurrence of procedural and/or device-related adverse events (AEs). AEs were collected as part of the surgical procedure and at each subsequent encounter and quantified by type, frequency, and severity.

Statistical analysis

Thirty adult participants were enrolled. This sample size was not based on an a priori power analysis but rather on requirements stipulated by the FDA during study approval. In addition, 30 participants would have a 95% probability of observing one or more safety events of interest occurring at a population incidence of 16.8%. Descriptive statistics are presented along with significance testing for relevant comparisons at each interval. Continuous variables are summarized using the mean, SD, minimum, and maximum values. Post-activation performance was compared with preimplant performance using a paired Student t test. This test was 1-sided, conducted at an alpha level of 0.025, and assessed the paired difference between preimplant and postimplant results. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate the assumption of normality. Differences in mean change scores across timepoints were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with an F test. Statistical significance was defined as P< 0.05 for all tests. Data analyses were performed by an external biostatistics consultant using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Primary outcome

Speech perception in noise improved significantly at 12 months in both S0NNH and S0NCI conditions, demonstrating restoration of the head shadow and augmentation of binaural squelch, respectively (Fig. 1). In the S0NNH condition (head shadow), SNR improved by ≥1 dB in 17/29 (59%) of participants, whereas 4/29 (14%) showed no change (one outlier with preoperative SNR −4.5 dB due to lack of masking/insufficient masking to the NH ear was removed from statistical analysis). The mean change in SNR for 29 participants was 2.0 dB SNR (SD: 4.5; P=0.03; 95% CI: [−3.7, −0,3]), whereas the mean improvement in the 17 participants showing improved SNR was 4.82 dB SNR (SD: 3.5; range: −1.5, −12.0; 95% CI: [−6.6, −3.0]). In the S0NCI condition (binaural squelch), SNR loss improved ≥1 dB between in 18/30 (60%) of participants, whereas7/30 (23%) demonstrated no change. The mean change in SNR in these 18 participants was −5.08 dB SNR (SD: 3.55; range: −1, −14.5; 95% CI: [−6.8, −3.3]). The overall improvement in SNR was 2.6 dB SNR (SD: 4.3; P=0.001; 95% CI: [−4.3, −1.1]). In the S0N0 condition (binaural summation), there was no significant improvement in SNR loss; 15/30 (50%) demonstrated SNR improvement ≥1 dB, whereas 4/30 (13%) showed no improvement.

Secondary and exploratory outcomes

Speech perception in quiet using CNC words significantly improved from baseline (mean: 1%; SD: 1.4; range: 0 to 5) at both 6 months (mean: 50.7%; SD: 23.0; range: 8 to 87; 95% CI: [41.2-58.5]) and 12 months (mean:, 50%; SD: 23.8; range: 14 to 89; 95% CI: [40.2, 58.1]). There was no significant improvement on CNC between 6 and 12 months. All participants demonstrated a ≥10-percentage point improvement on CNC at 12 months. At 3 and 6 months 24/30 (80%) and 27/30 (90%) demonstrated at least a 20-percentage point improvement, respectively. 26/30 (87%) demonstrated more than a 20-percentage point improvement at 12 months.

Using the SSQ questionnaire, significant improvements between baseline and both 6-month and 12-month timepoints were observed in all subdomains and overall score (Fig. 2). Between baseline and 12 months, improvement >1 point was observed in 20/29 (69%), 21/29 (72%), and 11/29 (38%) of participants for speech hearing, spatial hearing, and quality domains, respectively, and in 19/29 (66%) for the average score. 9/29 (31%), 7/29 (24%), and 12/29 (41%) of the participants reported no change for speech hearing, spatial hearing, and quality subdomains, respectively, and 9/29 (31%) participants reported no change in average score. One participant (1/29, 9%) and 6/29 participants (21%) reported poorer ratings post-CI on the spatial and quality domains, respectively, and one participant reported an average rating that was poorer at 12 months compared with baseline.

Improvements in tinnitus as measured by the THI were demonstrated at both 6 and 12 months compared with baseline (Fig. 3A, B). Preoperatively, 9/30 (30%) participants reported slight/no handicap and 21/30 (70%) reported mild or worse handicap (mean preoperative score: 38.9; SD: 30.8). By 12 months, 19/30 (63.3%) reported slight/no handicap and 10/30 (33.3%) reported mild or worse handicap (mean 12-month score: 13.6; SD: 17.1). Data from one participant were missing at 12 months; however, this participant reported catastrophic tinnitus at baseline and 6 months so catastrophic tinnitus at 12 months can be assumed. The participant with catastrophic tinnitus was a limited user (2.65 hours/day at 6 mo, 1.85 hours/day at 12 mo). This participant did not complete any patient-reported outcome measures at the 12-month interval and had a CNC word score of 17% at 12 months.

Using the VFS, significant improvements were reported across all 6 fatigue domain scores (Fig. 5). The total mean score reduced significantly from 83.4 (SD: 40.5; range: 7 to 158) at baseline to 51.9 (SD: 33.5; range: 1 to 134) at 12 months (P < 0.001, 95% CI [−42.8, −15.1]).

On the Cognivue mental status test, 4 participants did not have both pre-activation and post-activation scores available. There was a trend towards better performance at 12 months (mean: 84.9, SD: 8.2, range: 68 to 96) compared with baseline (mean: 82.2, SD: 8.1, range: 64 to 97; P=0.05; 95% CI [−0.03, 5.6]). Preoperatively 22/26 (85%) participants obtained a Cognivue score within the normal range (≥75) and 4/26 (15%) scored below 75. Postoperatively, 3/4 of participants who fell outside the normal range moved into the normal range and 2/22 (9%) participants who scored within the normal range preoperatively dropped outside the normal range at 12 months.

Average device use at 3 months was 9.4 hours (SD: 3.9; range: 2.0 to 14). At 12 months, average device use remained similar at 9 hours (SD: 4.2; range: 0.5 to 14.2). 17/30 (56%) and 14/29 (48%) of participants used their device at least 10 hours per day at 3 and 12 months, respectively. 3/29 (10%) of the subjects used their device less than 2 hours per day at the 12-month follow-up and 5/29 (17%) used the device <6 hours. One participant did not have datalogging data available at 12 months but was described as a daily device user.

When comparing performance outcomes between participants with <6 hours/day of device use and participants with more than 10 hours/day, clear differences were demonstrated on objective and subjective measures at 12 months. Limited users demonstrated a median improvement of 17 percentage points on CNC, whereas regular users demonstrated a median improvement of 54 percentage points. Similarly, on SSQ there was a greater perceived benefit among regular users. The limited users reported an overall score median improvement of 0.7, whereas the regular users reported an overall score median improvement of 1.75. Performance on the BKB-SIN also varied between groups depending on the spatial configuration of speech and noise. When speech was presented from the front and noise to the normal-hearing ear, limited users showed a median SNR improvement of 3.5 dB, exceeding the 1.5 dB improvement observed among regular users. In contrast, when noise was directed to the poorer ear, limited users demonstrated a 1.5 dB median improvement, whereas regular users showed a slightly larger improvement of 1.75 dB. When both speech and noise were presented from the front, limited users had a median improvement of 1.0 dB compared with 0.5 dB for regular users.

Nine adverse events (AEs) were reported in 6 participants. All reported AEs were mild and resolved by 12 months. The AEs most commonly reported included incisional pain (2), dizziness (2), new/worsened tinnitus (2), and non-auditory stimuli (2). No patients required revision, explanation, or experienced device failure.

Discussion

Cochlear implantation for SSD was approved by the FDA in 2019; however, post-market outcomes data are limited. Our objective was to assess the safety and efficacy of cochlear implantation in adults with SSD. This real-world post-market study confirms cochlear implantation is safe and effective in this population. We observed significant improvements in speech perception in quiet and noise including specific target-to-masker separated hearing tasks. Specifically, speech understanding in the implanted ear in quiet significantly improved compared with preoperative scores at both 6 and 12 months with no significant improvement between 6 and 12 months. This finding is consistent with pre-market data after CI for SSD4,36. Significant effects of the CI were also seen for tinnitus reduction, QOL improvement, fatigue reduction, and self-reported benefits.

When evaluating how cochlear implantation restored binaural hearing in noise, the current study found some results to be consistent with published findings, whereas others were unexpected. Similar to findings of a recent meta-analysis, our data showed a statistically significant improvement in SNR favoring CI use for a task evaluating head shadow effect (S0NNH)4. Prior SSD studies have used an alternative target-to-masker configuration (SCINNH) to study head shadow effect, which is arguably a more favorable condition to demonstrate head shadow-related SNR improvements46,47. Therefore, our findings indicate implanted SSD patients can have improved speech performance under these listening conditions by taking advantage of the improved SNR in the more favorably positioned ear.

Although head shadow effect allows for improvements in SNR using physical properties, true binaural auditory processing is measured using binaural summation and squelch. Summation is tested using a target-to-masker configuration with noise and speech presented at zero azimuth (S0N0). At 12 months post-implantation we did not observe a significant binaural summation benefit compared with baseline testing, consistent with previous literature46,48–50. Although statistically significant improvements in SNR through binaural summation have been demonstrated in both short-term and long-term post-CI speech-in-noise testing (6 mo and >5 y post-CI, respectively), the clinical significance of this benefit is more difficult to determine given the variability of the pooled data and lack of standardized reporting benchmarks4,10,11.

Binaural squelch refers to the capacity of the central auditory system to process the stimuli received from each ear and to reproduce it with a higher SNR by comparing interaural time and intensity differences between ears. In real-world terms this is the equivalent of a noise-canceling system allowing for improved hearing in noisy environments (eg, a cocktail party). In contrast to prior studies, our 12-month data revealed a statistically significant improvement in SNR when assessing binaural squelch. Apart from a study by Friedmann et al. demonstrating significant improvement in binaural squelch in a small group of 8 SSD patients after CI, most publications have reported nonsignificant improvements when measuring binaural squelch in post-implantation SSD patients6,7,10,12,48–52. This finding suggests a CI, unlike a transcranial rerouting device (eg, CROS or BCD), does not significantly interfere with hearing when noise is directed to the implanted ear48. Similarly, we did not observe any interference by the CI in the S0NCI condition (binaural squelch) for the majority of our cohort, with only 5/30 (17%) showing an SNR decrement. Interestingly, 18/30 participants (60%) showed an improvement of ≥1 dB SNR with an average change in SNR of -5.08 dB (SD: 3.55; range: −1, −14.5). It is not clear why the current study showed a binaural squelch benefit, whereas other similar studies in the literature have not. The answer underlying this discrepancy is likely multifactorial and poorly understood and could be related to factors differing between studies such as duration of device experience, length of deafness in the implanted ear, testing configuration and test measure used, patient demographics, and device type and coding strategies to name a few.

Our study demonstrated significant improvements in speech in noise across multiple listening scenarios at 12 months post-CI. Although individual responses varied, many participants showed improvements in one or more objective measures. Like prior studies, this study verified cochlear implantation is a superior option to transcranial rerouting devices in patients with profound unilateral hearing loss. When compared with transcranial routing devices, which can, in some cases, overcome the head shadow effect, only a CI can provide necessary cues to restore true binaural hearing. This advantage allows CI to demonstrate superiority in complex listening conditions and noisy environments5,32,53. Further studies will be required to understand if factors such as age at hearing loss, duration of deafness, age at implantation, or daily implant use can explain the difference between our results and those of others. Improvements in speech perception in quiet and noise are particularly impactful for individuals with SSD, enabling more effective communication in daily life, reduced listening effort, and better participation in group or social settings.

As previously reported, CI as a treatment for tinnitus related to SSD is particularly successful. Van de Heyning and colleagues, the first to use CI for the treatment of tinnitus in patients with SSD, demonstrated a 95% success rate in tinnitus reduction after CI29. Since then, other studies have shown similar findings54–58. The minimally clinical important difference (MCID) is defined as the smallest change in a PROM that is meaningful to patients59. For the THI, an MCID of 7 points for THI score reduction has been previously reported, with a reduction of ≥17 points considered a superior response45,60. In the current study, for participants reporting at least mild tinnitus at baseline, 95% demonstrated a clinically meaningful improvement in their THI compared with baseline, and 81% reported a superior response. Similar to the work of others, the reduction in tinnitus handicap in this study was durable up to 12 months. Importantly, even among those with less debilitating, milder degrees of tinnitus, the vast majority still demonstrated clinically meaningful improvement. The current study did not assess whether tinnitus reduction was present with the device on versus off as the participants were asked to assess their tinnitus reduction only while using the CI.

Previous studies have shown patients with SSD demonstrate significant improvement in their hearing-related QoL after CI29,61,62. The mean global CIQoL-35 in our cohort significantly improved at 12 months post-CI (45.6, 53.4) as did the mean change score in all 6 subdomains. One patient in each of the communication, environment, and social subscales demonstrated a worsening of their score. These data suggest although a CI may not be a perfect solution for all patients in all QOL domains, global improvement is a reasonable expectation while other benefits may be achievable for some patients.

The majority of participants reported global improvement on the SSQ after CI. These data are consistent with a recent meta-analysis, which examined 17 studies of QOL in adult patients, showing a statistically significant improvement in SSQ36. Although the cutoff for the MCID is debatable for the SSQ, our cohort did not meet an MCID of 2 (used in prior literature), with the mean SSQ score in this study increasing by 1.99 points63. It is important to note, however, the MCID for the SSQ reported previously is taken from individual data, and may not be generalizable to aggregated data such as ours. Although a few individuals noted no change or worsening in specific domains, such outcomes may have reflected variability in device use, auditory rehabilitation, or individual adaptation.

Several studies have shown patients undergoing CI for SSD have increased rates of device non-use compared with recipients with bilateral hearing loss. This trend is even more evident in the pediatric population, where non-use rates have been reported as high as 52%64–66. Encouragingly, average daily device use in this cohort remained stable between 3 and 12 months, suggesting sustained satisfaction among participants. Although definitions of non-use vary in the literature, in this study, 3/29 participants (10%) used their device <2 hours per day, whereas 5/29 (17%) used their device <6 hours per day. These findings mirror studies focusing on non-use among SSD adults (14%)65,66 but are higher than that reported among traditional CI recipients (3% to 5%)67,68. Furthermore, datalogging in our cohort demonstrated a mean use of 9 hours per day at 12 months. It is important to recognize this figure approximates published data on average daily use in SSD-CI recipients, which is known to be somewhat lower than the average 10 to 12 hours per day of device use in recipients with bilateral hearing loss48,69,70.

Our findings reinforce the favorable safety profile of CI for SSD. All 9 reported AEs were mild and resolved by 12 months. The most common AEs of incisional pain, dizziness, new or worsened tinnitus, and non-auditory stimuli are within the anticipated frequency of these events71. Further, no patients required revision surgery or had device failure.

Our study is limited by its small sample size, which may limit generalizability and preclude detection of rare adverse events. In addition, as with any nonrandomized trial, there is an element of selection bias. The patients who were motivated enough to agree to a prospective trial with longer visit times and extra questionnaires compared with typical care may have been more affected by their SSD and committed to practicing with their CIs postoperatively. Although subjectively addressed through the SSQ and CIQOL questionnaires, this study also did not include any objective assessment of sound localization, which has been shown to significantly improve in SSD patients receiving CIs4.

Conclusions

This post-market study confirms the safety and efficacy of cochlear implantation for adults with SSD. Patients demonstrated meaningful improvements across multiple hearing domains and quality of life measures, supporting the continued use of cochlear implantation as a standard treatment option for appropriately selected individuals with SSD. Among QoL measures, assessments evaluating tinnitus demonstrated the most robust and clinically meaningful improvement.
