An adversarial-resilient intrusion detection framework for internet of medical things (IoMT) using digital twin-enabled behavioral threat modeling and federated hybrid ensemble learning
Authors: Khalid Alkhattabi, Salem Belhaj, Julius Selecky, Muhammad Talha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) has transformed healthcare by enabling continuous patient monitoring and remote diagnostics. However, this growth introduces considerable security challenges. This paper examines vulnerabilities in IoMT devices to advanced cyberattacks that jeopardize patient safety and data integrity. We review the limitations of traditional security methods and motivate the need for adaptive defenses. We evaluate machine learning and deep learning models for real-time threat detection and identification of anomalous behavior within IoMT networks. We further propose a security framework that integrates digital twin technology with edge-cloud computing to improve the reliability of IoMT applications. Results show that hybrid and deep-learning models maintain detection performance under the resource constraints typical of medical devices. The proposed XGBoost component achieved a precision of 0.97, a recall of 0.98, and an ROC-AUC of 0.999 on the SmartWard dataset, while the hybrid ensemble showed measurable adversarial robustness under FGSM perturbations. The decision-critical inference path runs in under 0.05 seconds, supporting deployment on resource-constrained medical devices.

Introduction

The proliferation of Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) devices has transformed healthcare delivery by enabling continuous patient monitoring, remote diagnostics, and personalized treatments through connected medical sensors and wearables1,2. IoMT applications such as mobile health platforms and secure record-sharing systems also raise challenges, including privacy protection, sustainable power for battery-operated devices, sensor reliability, and storage efficiency, all of which must be addressed for widespread clinical adoption. While IoMT devices enhance patient care through real-time monitoring and automated medication management, they also introduce vulnerabilities that expose healthcare systems to sophisticated cyberattacks, threatening patient safety, data integrity, and system availability3,4.

Conventional security methods designed for general IoT environments do not meet the requirements of healthcare contexts. Traditional intrusion detection systems that rely on predefined signatures and rule-based approaches are ineffective against the dynamic threat landscape of IoMT networks and therefore require machine-learning and deep-learning methods capable of detecting zero-day attacks and behavioral anomalies in real time5,6. Prior analyses have shown that machine learning techniques, including supervised learning for attack classification, unsupervised learning for anomaly detection, and hybrid ensemble models, improve detection accuracy against evolving threats that conventional systems cannot identify7. Hafid et al.8 reported 0.98 precision on CICIoMT2024 using SHAP-based explainability and a late-fusion ensemble, while keeping the model feasible for resource-constrained medical devices. CNN–LSTM hybrid architectures combine spatial and temporal feature extraction for edge-based detection, addressing the latency and bandwidth constraints inherent to edge-centric IoMT deployments.

Increasingly sophisticated cyberattacks on healthcare infrastructure make IoMT security critical for patient safety and data integrity9. Unauthorized access to patient records and tampering with diagnostic data can lead to misdiagnoses or incorrect treatments. Al Khatib et al10. identify challenges, including privacy protection, sustainable power sources, and sensor resilience, that hinder the widespread adoption of IoMT technologies and expose these systems to malicious threats. The consequences are not theoretical: recent incidents illustrate the operational impact. For example, the high-profile cyberattack on a major healthcare provider in 2021, which exposed millions of patient records, illustrates the need for resilient security frameworks. According to Gheni and Al-Yaseen11, traditional signature-based intrusion detection systems fall short when applied to resource-constrained IoMT devices operating across diverse communication protocols. This gap motivates the development of domain-specific datasets, such as CICIoMT2024, that reflect the traffic patterns and attack vectors in healthcare environments. Addressing these challenges is essential for protecting patient data, ensuring regulatory compliance, and maintaining trust in healthcare systems.

This work proposes an approach to IoMT security combining machine learning with continuous monitoring. By developing a unified security framework that uses digital twin technology and 6G-enabled edge-cloud orchestration, we present a real-time defense system that detects and isolates threats while preserving operational efficiency.

Prior work addresses subsets of the IoMT security problem; to our knowledge, no published framework integrates all components covered by SmartWard. The closest related framework is Hafid et al8., which uses XGBoost with SHAP explainability on the CICIoMT2024 dataset; however, this prior work does not include adversarial robustness testing, behavioral context, or digital twin state tracking. CNN and LSTM models have been proposed in12 for real-time intrusion detection at the network edge, but without trust-based access control or privacy-compliance mechanisms. Gheni and Al-Yaseen11 apply feature selection on CICIoMT2024 but do not incorporate deep-learning autoencoders, federated learning, or post-quantum cryptography. SmartWard contributes the following beyond prior work: (i) digital-twin behavioral scoring covering patient activity, circadian rhythm, and environmental state; (ii) 6G-supported dynamic trust computation; (iii) hybrid ensemble detection combining classical ML with deep-learning autoencoders (LSTM-AE, GRU-AE, CNN-1D-AE); (iv) federated learning with HIPAA/GDPR compliance validation; and (v) FGSM-based adversarial robustness testing within the real-time monitoring loop, together with post-quantum cryptographic readiness, all in a single pipeline with sub-millisecond decision latency. To our knowledge, no prior work integrates all five capabilities on a single dataset.

Related work and background

Nithish et al13. developed CPAC-Net, a deep learning framework designed for classifying cyber-physical attacks in water treatment systems. This framework addresses security vulnerabilities in industrial water infrastructure. CPAC-Net employs data preprocessing, including normalization and outlier detection, and uses Principal Decision Component Tree Analysis (PDCTA) for feature extraction while preserving key attack patterns required for classification. The framework reduces false positives reported in prior IDS for industrial control systems and improves operational continuity in water treatment facilities.

Alabdulatif et al14. developed a real-time machine-learning-based detection system for DoS and DDoS attacks on vulnerable IoT devices. The framework evaluates XGBoost, MLP, and RF algorithms on the CICIoT2023 dataset and employs Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) for feature selection. XGBoost with PSO achieved 99.93% accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score in 491.023 seconds. The system analyzes network traffic and is suitable for resource-constrained IoT environments in sectors such as healthcare and smart cities. Hernandez-Jaimes et al15. developed a machine learning method for detecting ransomware in IoMT environments using N-gram similarity (NILS) fingerprinting to analyze network traffic. This approach addresses challenges such as heterogeneous data and the lack of standardized features by automating detection without manual feature extraction. On the ICE and CICIoMT2024 datasets, the Random Forest algorithm achieved 100% precision and 98.72% F1-score on ICE, and 99.44% precision with 98.59% F1-score on CICIoMT2024. These results outperform prior methods on the same datasets and are suitable for resource-constrained IoMT settings. Tarish et al16. surveyed network security frameworks for IoMT applications, highlighting challenges including heterogeneous devices, resource constraints, and privacy concerns. The review covers machine learning and blockchain technologies for security and analyzes deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods for intrusion and anomaly detection. It highlights blockchain’s role in ensuring data integrity and defending against DDoS attacks. The study calls for an approach combining user education, standardized protocols, and layered defenses to protect sensitive medical information. Balhareth and Ilyas17 developed an optimized intrusion detection system for IoMT networks, integrating tree-based machine learning classifiers and filter-based feature selection to improve detection accuracy while minimizing computational overhead. The framework employs Mutual Information (MI) and XGBoost for feature selection to improve intrusion detection during data transfers among devices such as pacemakers and smartwatches. It addresses security challenges from limited device resources, enabling efficient healthcare data analysis at the network edge. Evaluation on the CICIDS2017 dataset yielded 98.79% accuracy and a false-alarm rate of 0.007. Kulshrestha and Vijay Kumar18 developed a machine-learning-based intrusion detection system for IoMT networks and evaluated 11 classification algorithms on the ToN-IoT dataset. AdaBoost achieved approximately 99% accuracy across reported metrics, including precision, recall, F1-score, false discovery rate (FDR), and false positive rate (FPR), surpassing existing ToN-IoT IDS models in detecting cyberattacks on Electronic Health Record systems. Kikissagbe and Adda19presented a systematic review of machine learning-based intrusion detection methods for IoT systems, analyzing peer-reviewed literature from 2014 to 2024 covering supervised, unsupervised, deep learning, and hybrid approaches for security in dynamic IoT environments. The review evaluates Support Vector Machines, Random Forests, Deep Neural Networks, and other algorithms across applications in smart homes, healthcare, transportation, and industrial automation, identifying limitations and research gaps. Alzubi et al12. developed a blended deep learning intrusion detection framework for edge-centric IoMT applications, combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architectures for real-time intrusion detection in Smart Healthcare Systems. The hybrid approach uses CNN for spatial feature extraction and LSTM for temporal sequence learning. Evaluation on the CSE-CIC-ID dataset confirms its effectiveness. Hafid et al8. developed an interpretable and cost-effective machine learning framework for IoMT intrusion detection, balancing security performance and computational cost using a fine-tuned XGBoost classifier and a regularized Logistic Regression. The framework uses SHAP for interpretability and implements a late-fusion approach with max voting, achieving 0.98 precision and fewer false negatives on the CICIoMT2024 dataset. The study analyzes the security- cost trade-off during training and testing. Almotairi et al20. developed a heterogeneous machine-learning-based stack classifier for IoT intrusion detection that combines K-Best feature selection (top 15 critical features) with an ensemble of Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, Naive Bayes, and K-Nearest Neighbors. The stacking ensemble approach uses a meta-learner to address security challenges in heterogeneous IoT networks. Evaluation on the ToN-IoT dataset achieved 99.99% accuracy, 99.98% precision, 99.99% recall, and 99.99% F1-score.

Berhili et al21. reviewed machine learning-based intrusion detection systems for IoT networks, analyzing how ML techniques address security vulnerabilities arising from limited device resources, diverse communication protocols, and outdated systems across domains such as industry, smart cities, and healthcare. The review covers supervised learning, unsupervised anomaly detection, deep learning, and hybrid models, examining their ability to improve detection accuracy, adapt to new threats, detect zero-day attacks, and reduce false-positive rates. Doménech et al22. demonstrated the importance of domain-specific datasets for IoMT intrusion detection. By comparing machine learning models trained on a general IoT dataset (CICIoT2023) with an IoMT-specific dataset (CICIoMT2024), they reported F1-score drops of up to 66.87% when tested on the IoMT-specific dataset. Their study introduced optimization techniques, including uniform windowing and improved dataset balancing, achieving a model accuracy of 0.9985. These findings indicate that general IoT datasets fail to capture the traffic patterns and security requirements of healthcare environments. Gheni and Al-Yaseen11developed an intrusion detection system that uses feature selection on the CICIoMT2024 dataset, which includes 18 cyberattack types across five categories (DDoS, DoS, Reconnaissance, MQTT-specific attacks, and spoofing) affecting 40 IoMT devices (25 real and 15 simulated) via Wi-Fi, MQTT, and Bluetooth. Their feature selection method identifies key network traffic patterns while minimizing computational demands. Abdullahi et al23. compared AI methods for detecting cyberattacks in cyber-physical systems (CPS), evaluating Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The study analyzes how ensemble methods and recurrent neural networks address temporal dependencies and anomaly patterns in attacks against critical infrastructure, including industrial control systems, smart grids, and healthcare monitoring systems.

Al Khatib et al10. reviewed 134 articles (2004–2024) covering IoMT applications, including mobile health platforms, remote biomarker detection, and machine-learning-based disease prediction. They identified seven main challenges: privacy, power sustainability, sensor intelligence, human adaptation, data transmission speeds, device reliability, and storage efficiency, along with solutions such as network control and cryptography. Their contributions include categorizing emerging technologies, analyzing AI integration, and proposing a framework that connects applications, challenges, and solutions to improve patient safety and data privacy.

Federated Learning for IoMT Security. Centralized aggregation of patient data for IDS training poses privacy risks that conflict with healthcare guidelines. Nguyen et al24. showed that federated learning can achieve detection accuracy comparable to centralized IDS while preserving data locality on IoT traffic datasets, achieving 97.2% accuracy across distributed nodes with non-IID data distributions. Orzikulova et al25. applied this in healthcare settings, showing that federated averaging converges within 5 communication rounds for medical sensor data when clients have similar device profiles. Both studies evaluate federated IDS in isolation, without integrating the learned models into a trust-scoring and access-control pipeline. SmartWard addresses this gap by directly incorporating federated learning into Phase 6 A, in which distributed models from various healthcare institutions are integrated into the ensemble’s anomaly scores without exchanging raw data.

Adversarial Machine Learning Threats to Medical IDS. Adversarial perturbations against ML-based IDS remain under-explored in IoMT. Recent systematic studies of NIDS show that gradient-based attacks such as FGSM, PGD, and decision-based black-box methods substantially degrade detection accuracy across a wide range of ML architectures26,27. In healthcare-specific settings, recent work demonstrates that IoMT IDS models are vulnerable to FGSM and Carlini–Wagner perturbations, motivating manifold-aware and federated defenses28,29. These results motivate explicit adversarial-robustness evaluation as a primary criterion rather than a secondary check. Accordingly, SmartWard implements ε = 0.01 and ε = 0.1 tests within Phase 6B’s real-time monitoring loop, automatically evaluating model robustness as new traffic batches arrive.

Digital Twin Applications in IoT Security. Digital twin technology has emerged as a tool for proactive threat detection in cyber-physical systems. Eckhart and Ekelhart30 used digital twins to detect behavioral deviations in industrial control systems, reporting attack alerts up to 40% earlier than signature-based detection. van der Wal et al.31 presented an innovative security framework designed to address the vulnerabilities inherent in Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystems. They propose a dual-layered methodology that leverages Digital Twin (DT) technology to create a synchronized virtual replica of physical IoT networks, providing a safe environment for rigorous testing. By integrating Automated Adversary Emulation (AAE) into this digital replica, the framework enables continuous, automated simulation of sophisticated cyberattacks without disrupting live operations.

Proposed approach

This section presents the methodology for the SmartWard security framework, a 7-phase architecture for IoMT networks. The approach comprises seven integrated phases: (1) data acquisition and preprocessing using CICIoMT2024 and SmartWard datasets, (2) digital twin simulation creating virtual representations of devices, environment, and patients, (3) behavioral analytics computing risk scores from activity patterns and environmental conditions, (4) 6G-enabled edge-cloud trust computing generating dynamic trust scores for adaptive access control, (5) hybrid ensemble learning combining classical ML algorithms (Isolation Forest, XGBoost) with deep-learning autoencoders (LSTM-AE, GRU-AE, CNN-1D-AE) and federated learning, (6) real-time threat monitoring with FGSM adversarial defense and SHAP explainability, and (7) privacy-compliance validation with post-quantum readiness. Experimental setup and dataset specifications

We document the full experimental setup for reproducibility. The SmartWard dataset includes 4500 patient-hour records from 50 patient devices with 13 numerical physiological and environmental features: Age, Room Temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C), Humidity (%), Noise Level (dB), Air Quality Index, Light Intensity (Lux), Heart Rate (bpm), Body Temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C), Respiration Rate (bpm), Oxygen Saturation SpO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} (%), Pain Level Score, Sleep Quality, and Recovery Speed (days). Attack labels were assigned using a clinically informed rule-based approach: a risk score was computed by counting anomaly flags from the following vital signs (after MinMax normalization): Heart Rate > 0.8, Body Temperature > 0.75, Respiration Rate > 0.7, and SpO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2 < 0.4$$\end{document}, together with environmental sensors: Air Quality Index > 0.6, Noise Level > 0.6, and Pain Level > 0.6. A record with 3 or 4 of these flags was labeled as an attack (Label = 1), producing a class imbalance of approximately 28% attack samples and 72% benign samples. This threshold-based labeling is consistent with clinical anomaly-detection practice and similar to the ground-truth labeling procedure in CICIoMT2024. The CICIoMT2024 dataset contains network traffic from 40 IoMT devices (25 physical and 15 simulated) covering 18 attack categories in five families: DDoS (ARP Spoofing, ICMP Flood, TCP SYN Flood), DoS (HTTP Flood, UDP Flood, SlowLoris), Reconnaissance (Port Scan, OS Fingerprinting, Vulnerability Scan), MQTT (MQTT Brute Force, MQTT DoS), and Spoofing (DNS Spoofing, ARP Spoofing). For all experiments, the data is split 70:15:15 into training, validation, and test sets. Feature standardization was fit only to the training data and then applied to the validation and test partitions to avoid data leakage. All experiments were performed in Python 3.10, TensorFlow 2.15, and XGBoost 2.0 on an NVIDIA A100 GPU via Google Colab Pro. All stochastic operations used a random seed of 42.

Behavioral analytics engine

The behavioral analytics engine quantifies risk across multiple dimensions by analyzing contextual factors beyond network traffic patterns. The algorithm assigns predetermined weights to four behavioral dimensions: activity patterns (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{act}=0.3$$\end{document}), environmental conditions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{env}=0.25$$\end{document}), mood indicators (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{mood}=0.25$$\end{document}), and circadian rhythm compliance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{circ}=0.2$$\end{document}), satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{i} w_i = 1$$\end{document}. At each time step t in the real-time network stream, the system retrieves the current state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t$$\end{document}) from incoming traffic and compares it with the expected state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$dt_t$$\end{document}) maintained in the digital twin registry. Deviations are computed across all four dimensions as shown in Eq. (6):6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \delta _i(t) = |s_i(t) - dt_i(t)|, \quad i \in \{\text {act, env, mood, circ}\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}capturing anomalies in patient mobility patterns, environmental sensor readings, biometric indicators from wearable devices, and adherence to medication schedules. These deviations are aggregated using the weighted scoring mechanism in Eq. (7):7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} B_t = 100 \times \left( 1 - \sum _{i} w_i \cdot \frac{\delta _i(t)}{\delta _{i,max}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}producing a behavioral score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_t \in [0, 100]$$\end{document}, where higher scores indicate normal behavior and lower scores suggest potential security threats or compromised devices.

6G-enabled edge-cloud trust computing

Phase 5 implements dynamic trust management that uses 6G network capabilities for ultra-low-latency security decision-making. The algorithm establishes two thresholds: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{allow}=0.7$$\end{document} for granting full network access and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{quarantine}=0.3$$\end{document} for immediate device isolation. For each device d, three trust components are computed as shown in Eq. (8):8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_B(d) = \frac{B_d}{100}, \quad T_A(d) = \text {Attest}(d), \quad T_C(d) = \text {Comply}(d) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_B$$\end{document} is behavioral trust derived by normalizing the behavioral score to the [0, 1] range, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_A \in [0,1]$$\end{document} is attestation trust obtained through firmware integrity verification and authentication credential validation, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_C \in [0,1]$$\end{document} is compliance trust determined by checking adherence to organizational security policies. These components are fused using the weighted aggregation in Eq. (9):9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {T_d = 0.4 \cdot T_B(d) + 0.35 \cdot T_A(d) + 0.25 \cdot T_C(d)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}producing a dynamic trust score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_d \in [0, 1]$$\end{document}. Based on the computed trust score, the system executes reactive access control decisions as defined in Eq. (10):10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_d = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \text {ALLOW} & \text {if } T_d \ge 0.7 \\ \text {THROTTLE} & \text {if } 0.3< T_d < 0.7 \\ \text {QUARANTINE} & \text {if } T_d \le 0.3 \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}corresponding to full network privileges, rate-limited access, or immediate isolation with alerting. Trust computation and access-control decisions execute at sub-millisecond latency on 6G edge infrastructure, enabling real-time policy enforcement.

SmartWard ML/AI security layer

Phase 6 implements the hybrid ensemble detection layer, combining classical machine learning, deep-learning autoencoders, and federated learning. This phase is subdivided into two components: hybrid ensemble models for multi-stream threat detection, and real-time hybrid threat monitoring for continuous security assessment.

Hybrid ensemble models

Phase 6 A integrates three parallel detection streams. The classical machine-learning stream trains Isolation Forest (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{IF}$$\end{document}) for unsupervised anomaly detection and XGBoost (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{XGB}$$\end{document}) for supervised gradient-boosted classification. The deep-learning autoencoder stream trains LSTM-AE (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{LSTM}$$\end{document}) for temporal dependency modeling in sequential network traffic, GRU-AE (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{GRU}$$\end{document}) for efficient sequence processing with reduced computational complexity, and CNN-1D-AE (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{CNN}$$\end{document}) for spatial feature extraction from packet-level characteristics. In addition, a fully connected Dense Autoencoder (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{AE}$$\end{document}) with the symmetric [64, 32, 16, 32, 64] architecture (Table 2) is included as a fourth autoencoder branch operating on tabular feature vectors.

For autoencoders, the reconstruction error is computed as shown in Eq. (11):11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{AE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum _{i=1}^{N} \Vert x_i - \hat{x}_i\Vert ^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}_i = \text {Decoder}(\text {Encoder}(x_i))$$\end{document}.

SmartWard’s federated learning module (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{FL}$$\end{document}) is a simulated distributed anomaly-detection model that maintains data locality across clinical sites. In this scenario, K = 3 federated clients correspond to independent data partitions obtained by evenly dividing the SmartWard feature space. This is not a real hospital distribution, but it provides a controlled federated environment for testing distributed anomaly-detection behavior. Each client trains an Isolation Forest model for R = 5 rounds of communication using its local data partition. Unlike classical Federated Averaging (FedAvg), which exchanges model parameters, our framework uses a score-level aggregation strategy. Each client generates anomaly scores using its locally trained model, and these scores are fused at the server via mean-based aggregation. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_k^{(t)}$$\end{document} denote the mean anomaly score generated by client k in round t. The global federated score is computed as Eq. 12:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S^{(t)}_{FL} = \frac{1}{K} \sum _{k=1}^{K} s_k^{(t)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where K = 3 is the number of federated clients. This formulation is not a weight-sharing optimization procedure; it is a form of ensemble-based federated aggregation. Communication rounds are tracked to assess convergence behavior. In practice, the system stabilizes after 2–3 rounds with slight oscillations around a steady-state value, attributable to randomness in Isolation Forest training and per-round seed differences across clients. The low amplitude of variability, measured by the standard deviation of global scores across rounds, confirms stability. This shows that distributed anomaly detectors can coordinate global detection behavior using only score-level consensus, without sharing parameters. Overall, this design offers a lightweight, privacy-focused alternative to FedAvg-based learning: patient-level data remains local, and only aggregated statistical outputs are shared globally for decision-making.13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} { \hat{y} = \textbf{1}\!\left[ w_{\textrm{XGB}} \cdot P_{\textrm{XGB}} + w_{\textrm{IF}} \cdot P_{\textrm{IF}} + w_{\textrm{AE}} \cdot P_{\textrm{AE}} + w_{\textrm{FL}} \cdot S_{\textrm{FL}} \ge 0.5 \right] } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}while aggregated anomaly scores provide quantitative threat severity assessments as expressed in Eq. (14):14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} { S = \frac{\sum _{i=1}^{n} w_i \cdot S_i}{\sum _{i=1}^{n} w_i}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Real-time hybrid threat monitor

Phase 6B implements continuous, real-time monitoring with adversarial robustness and explainability. The algorithm initializes a rolling-window buffer (W) to maintain a recent history of traffic and a SHAP explainer for the XGBoost model to provide interpretable threat explanations. As the network stream remains active, incoming traffic batches are processed through three parallel operations: FGSM (Fast Gradient Sign Method) adversarial testing generates perturbed examples as shown in Eq. (15):15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x_{adv} = x + \epsilon \cdot \text {sign}(\nabla _x L(M, x, y)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}With ε =0.01 to validate model robustness against evasion attacks, ensemble-based threat detection produces predictions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_{batch}$$\end{document}) and anomaly scores (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{batch}$$\end{document}). SHAP analysis extracts feature-importance explanations (E) that identify which network characteristics contribute most strongly to each detection decision. When the anomaly score exceeds the alert threshold (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{alert}$$\end{document}), the system generates security alerts (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {A}$$\end{document}) that include timestamps, device identifiers, threat severity scores, and SHAP-based explanations, supporting clinical interpretability and regulatory compliance. For high-severity threats exceeding the quarantine threshold (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{quarantine}$$\end{document}), the algorithm automatically isolates the compromised device from the network. Forensic audit logs are maintained for incident investigation and breach containment.

Privacy, compliance and post-quantum readiness

Phase 7 ensures regulatory compliance and future-proof security through privacy-preserving mechanisms and cryptographic protections. The algorithm applies differential privacy by adding calibrated Laplace noise as expressed in Eq. (16):16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P' = P + \text {Lap}\left( \frac{\Delta f}{\epsilon }\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with privacy budget ε =1.0 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta =10^{-5}$$\end{document}, where Δ f is the sensitivity of function f, producing anonymized data (P') that enables threat-intelligence sharing while protecting individual patient privacy with the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\epsilon , \delta )$$\end{document}-differential privacy guarantee in Eq. (17):17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Pr [\mathcal {M}(P) \in S] \le e^{\epsilon } \cdot \Pr [\mathcal {M}(P') \in S] + \delta \end{aligned}$$\end{document}for all datasets P, P' differing in one record and all sets S. Regulatory compliance validation is conducted in accordance with HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) and GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) standards. HIPAA compliance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{HIPAA}$$\end{document}) is verified through access-control validation, encryption standard checks, and audit logging completeness. GDPR compliance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{GDPR}$$\end{document}) is confirmed through data-minimization verification, consent record validation, and right-to-erasure capability checks. Post-quantum cryptography is implemented using the Kyber lattice-based algorithm for key generation and encryption, ensuring data security against future quantum computing threats. The algorithm generates a compliance report (R) that documents all validation results, audit summaries, and temporal metadata, suitable for regulatory inspection and certification processes.

The output provides actionable intelligence for threat mitigation, an automated response that reduces manual intervention, SHAP-based explanations that support clinical interpretability, and evidence of compliance for regulatory audits.

Experimental analysis and results

Trust-based access control results

Phase 5 implements the multi-factor trust computation in Eq. (9): \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_d = 0.4 \cdot T_B(d) + 0.35 \cdot T_A(d) + 0.25 \cdot T_C(d)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_B$$\end{document} is the normalized behavioral score from Phase 4, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_A$$\end{document} is hardware-attestation status, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_C$$\end{document} is policy compliance. Digital twin detection performance analysis

To quantitatively assess the proposed digital-twin-based behavioral modeling, we evaluate its influence on trust-score sensitivity and anomaly-based decision-making in a controlled simulation. The digital-twin framework uses behavioral signals, attestation status, policy compliance, and anomaly penalties from Isolation Forest predictions to compute the trust score for each device, enabling dynamic adjustments of trust levels based on deviations in physiological and behavioral characteristics. When simulated anomaly conditions are applied, and behavioral scores are artificially lowered for selected devices, the system shows a measurable change in the trust-score distribution, with more devices in the throttle or quarantine ranges. The digital twin is particularly sensitive to behavioral perturbations from devices with lower behavioral consistency, increasing the probability of crossing the decision thresholds (allow at 0.7 and quarantine at 0.3). Statistical analysis of trust-score distributions further shows clear separation between normal and anomalous device populations, as evidenced by group-wise summary statistics computed as functions of anomaly scores. This validates the digital-twin model as an improvement over static trust estimation by integrating contextual behavioral information with system-level security attributes, broadening anomaly awareness and access-control responsiveness.

Adversarial robustness quantification under FGSM attacks

To evaluate model robustness, we measure the loss of detection accuracy under FGSM attacks at perturbation budget ε = 0.1. Three model components, XGBoost, Isolation Forest, and the LSTM Autoencoder, are targeted. Table 8 reports clean and adversarial accuracy for each. The results show practically meaningful differences in adversarial vulnerability across model types. Despite achieving the highest clean accuracy (0.9758), XGBoost is most affected by FGSM perturbation, with an accuracy drop of 18.36% to 0.7923. This reflects an architectural vulnerability: gradient-boosted decision trees use splits that are sensitive to gradient-based perturbations, which can move samples across split boundaries. In contrast, the LSTM Autoencoder is almost unaffected (0.8198 → 0.8116, a drop of only 0.82%). Its detection mechanism is reconstruction-based and therefore robust: the autoencoder does not rely on explicit decision boundaries but flags anomalies via reconstruction error, the difference between input and learned representation. FGSM perturbations tend to make adversarial samples easier rather than harder to detect, since they increase reconstruction error. This finding motivates the hybrid ensemble design in SmartWard: XGBoost and the LSTM Autoencoder have complementary weaknesses. XGBoost is highly accurate under benign conditions but vulnerable to attack, while the autoencoder is less accurate under benign conditions but robust under attack. Combining the autoencoder’s outputs with XGBoost mitigates degradation under adversarial scenarios, providing a defense-in-depth posture. This complementary behavior demonstrates that adversarial robustness can be obtained via heterogeneous model fusion in Phase 6 A without explicit adversarial training.

Second, removing the digital twin or federated-learning components produces a small increase in raw accuracy (0.9710 → 0.9758) and F1-score (0.9032 → 0.9180). Computational analysis and scalability

Real-time clinical monitoring requires bounded inference latency, memory footprint, and federated-communication overhead. Table 10 reports the measured per-component inference latency in the SmartWard pipeline, measured on the SmartWard test set when deployed on Google Colab Pro. The key scalability limitation is the sequential processing required by the LSTM Autoencoder. The current test-set latency is 0.208 s, and linear scaling to 1,000 devices would yield around 4.2 s for LSTM inference alone, exceeding the 1-second-per-update target for real-time monitoring. Two mitigations follow: (i) batched GPU inference on a modern GPU such as the A100: LSTM inference latency can be reduced by approximately 10–15×; and (ii) running the LSTM Autoencoder as an asynchronous background validator while XGBoost and Isolation Forest handle real-time decisions. Together, the latter two require under 0.05 s across all tested scales. SmartWard is therefore designed for an edge-server deployment, with IoMT devices acting as data sources and all machine-learning inference running on a dedicated edge server, fitting the 6G edge-cloud orchestration paradigm in Section 3.6. Cross-institutional federated updates are also feasible over standard clinical network infrastructure, given the FL communication cost of 1.28 MB per training cycle.

Conclusion and future scope

This paper examined limitations of traditional security measures for IoMT and proposed an integrated alternative. We evaluated machine-learning and deep-learning methods for real-time threat detection and anomaly identification. The proposed XGBoost component achieved a precision of 0.97, a recall of 0.98, and an ROC-AUC of 0.999, with the hybrid ensemble delivering measurable adversarial robustness under FGSM perturbations. These results were obtained within a decision-critical inference budget under 0.05 s, fitting the constraints of resource-limited medical devices. The proposed framework integrates digital twins, edge–cloud computing, hybrid ensembles, and federated learning into a single pipeline. Future work will (i) extend evaluation to multi-institution federated deployments, (ii) test on additional IoMT-specific datasets, and (iii) integrate post-quantum primitives end-to-end.
