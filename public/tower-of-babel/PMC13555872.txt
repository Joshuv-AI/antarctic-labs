Intersecting narratives: exploring generational differences in maternal health factors in Vietnamese mothers of children with orofacial clefts
Authors: Quynh Nguyen, Kristina Bridges, Huynh Kim Phuc, Jason Glenn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores generational differences in maternal health practices among Vietnamese mothers of children with orofacial clefts (OFCs) living in rural communities in Vietnam. Through narrative interviews with 21 mothers and five physicians, it highlights significant gaps in prenatal nutrition knowledge, barriers to diagnostic tools, and the conflict between traditional health practices and contemporary medical advice.MethodsThis study employed a narrative research methodology in combination with minimal semi-structured interviews using a topics guided technique to explore individuals’ stories, perceptions, and experiences related to having children with OFCs. Eligibility criteria included Vietnamese mothers aged 45 + years who bore children with OFCs; Vietnamese mothers < 45 years of age who bore children with OFCs; and physicians practicing in Vietnam who provide prenatal care. Participants meeting the criteria were presented with a series of open-ended questions focusing on their folic acid consumption, access to healthcare, physician recommendations, cultural health practices, and the impact of social determinants of health during pregnancy.ResultsThe research brings together insights from mothers and physicians, highlighting complex issues surrounding maternal health and OFCs. Physicians emphasized the importance of folic acid supplementation and early detection, while mothers revealed challenges stemming from poverty, health literacy gaps, and stigma.ConclusionsImproving maternal health and shifting perceptions of OFCs requires more than education and awareness. The findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive health education, expanded healthcare access, and strategies addressing social and structural determinants to improve maternal care and reduce OFC prevalence in Vietnam. Public health campaigns targeting pregnant women on the benefits of nutrients like folic acid, calcium, and vitamin A is crucial, but must be developed in collaboration with members or community service organizations based in those communities. The many traditional community practices that are protective of moms and babies must be strengthened while offering science-based guidance to counter traditional beliefs that are harmful. Expanding affordable healthcare access and outreach for low-income families is essential to address barriers and promote better outcomes.Trial registrationRegisterd through the Kansas University Medical Center Institutional Review Board on May 1, 2024. Background

OFCs are a public health burden, exacerbating health inequities as affected families face financial hardship, food insecurity, limited access to primary care, and increased childhood adversities [1]. The development of OFCs involves a complicated connection between genetic [2] and environmental factors that are not yet fully understood. Notably, OFCs remain highly prevalent in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) recovering from ravages of colonialism, where significant infrastructure for research and healthcare is lacking compared to wealthier nations [3]. Challenges such as limited access to care, cultural practices, and inadequate data records hinder physician awareness of OFC prevalence and research into preventative measures [3]. A literature search revealed only one previous population-based study conducted in Vietnam with a 1-year registry tracking the prevalence of birth defects in 2010. According to that study, OFCs remained one of the most common types of birth defects in Vietnam [4].

Orofacial clefts (OFCs) are a deeply personal issue for children affected by this condition and their parents. This is due to the stigma associated with children with birth defects or deformities, as well as the internalized shame and self-blame experienced by their parents. Typically, children born with OFC will need to undergo multiple surgeries, which can place significant financial, emotional, and social strain on families due to medical costs, travel for care, time away from work, and stigma associated with congenital differences.There are significant social and structural determinants of health (SSDoH) that play a role in the development of OFCs. Orofacial clefts pose significant public health challenges, particularly in nations with economic burdens, high-density populations, and limited access to healthcare and nutritional resources, such as South Asia and Africa [5].

OFCs are increasingly recognized not only as congenital anomalies with genetic and environmental causes, but also as conditions strongly shaped by social and structural determinants of health. Studies across LMICs have shown that maternal nutrition, poverty, limited education, environmental exposures, and restricted access to prenatal care all influence both the risk of OFCs and the outcomes for affected children. These disparities are particularly pronounced in settings where access to folic acid supplementation, prenatal screening, and specialist care is inconsistent or delayed. Global reviews have emphasized that the burden of OFCs is disproportionately experienced in LMICs, where health-system limitations and socioeconomic inequality may increase both incidence and severity of outcomes [6–8].

In specific regions like South Asia, Vietnam, and the Philippines, the prevalence of OFCs is notably severe [9], hinting at a complex interplay of factors contributing to its incidence. This observation highlights the need for a comprehensive examination of the contributors influencing the development of OFCs, including genetics, environmental influences [10], cultural health practices, access to care, and SSDoH. Factors like socioeconomic status, education level, cultural maternal health practices, health literacy, and social support significantly impact OFC development alongside genetic mechanisms. A study conducted in Argentina revealed higher OFC risks among mothers with low socioeconomic status [11], suggesting the need for tailored pregnancy health policies based on individuals’ socioeconomic status to reduce the incidence of OFCs. Additionally, mothers with higher education levels display a reduced risk of certain types of OFCs [12]. This emphasizes the need to explore SSDoH factors in Vietnam, where no such studies have been conducted. Higher OFC rates have been observed in areas with high unemployment and unskilled labor in many countries, calling for standardized screening of socioeconomic status to tailor care for resource-deprived mothers [6]. In Vietnam, unskilled labor remains a primary source of income which poses a possible link to OFCs.

Pregnant women in rural communities of Vietnam face limited healthcare access, economic struggles, and significant challenges seeking medical attention. Often, these communities will have only one clinic, scarce resources, and one doctor. Accessing essential healthcare services often means enduring a 90-minute car drive to the nearest city hospital. These experiences demand attention to improve healthcare conditions and advocacy for the underserved. In rural areas of Vietnam, maternal health care is not provided exclusively by physicians. Women may seek care from a variety of sources including midwives, traditional birth attendants, community elders, family members, and practitioners of traditional medicine. In settings where access to formal medical care is limited, these individuals may play an important role in shaping maternal health practices, beliefs, and decisions regarding pregnancy. Although the present study focused on physicians and mothers, understanding the broader network of maternal health influences is important for interpreting health behaviors in rural communities and represents an important direction for future research.

In exploring maternal health practices among Vietnamese women, it is important to consider their cultural practices related to health and pregnancy, passed through generations. Vietnamese culture often blends Western medicine with herbal Chinese medicine obtained from monks in temples and traditional healing methods such as wind scraping, wind snatching, and cupping [13, 14]. One study conducted interviews with two generations of families, including mothers and daughters, regarding traditional practices potentially harmful to a child’s health. It was found that while some traditional practices have been abandoned due to perceived harm, others continue with modifications, and some persist unchanged. This trend is primarily driven by increased internet access, enabling the newer generation to question traditional practices, along with a higher level of education among mothers in the newer generation compared to the older generation in developing countries [15]. These findings raise questions about potential differences in traditional health practices between older and younger generations of Vietnamese mothers and how these differences may influence the development of Orofacial Clefts.

These traditional practices all influence dietary habits and pregnancy care. More specifically, dietary restrictions based on trimesters and restricted movement during pregnancy play significant roles from a traditionalist viewpoint [16]. However, non-beneficial superstitious practices, such as drinking beer during pregnancy to give the baby a lighter skin tone (seen as more desirable in Vietnamese culture) exist alongside protective traditional practices. Other cultural traditions are practiced alongside evidenced-based preventive care like prenatal ultrasounds - such as extremely restricting the movements of expectant mothers and laying on top of hot coals after giving birth to help mothers heal faster [16]. Other traditional practices include putting warm leaves on an infant’s head to keep bad spirits away and using honey to clear an infant’s tongue, which can cause infant botulism. Understanding and addressing these cultural beliefs can help explain the high prevalence of Orofacial clefts in Vietnamese women.

Maternal nutrition, particularly periconceptional folic acid intake, has been identified as a potentially modifiable factor associated with the risk of OFCs [17]. Several epidemiologic and meta-analytic studies suggest that folic acid–containing supplements taken before conception or during early pregnancy may reduce the risk of cleft lip with or without cleft palate, although the magnitude of the effect varies across populations. In many LMICs, however, supplementation is often started late, inconsistently used, or poorly understood, limiting the effectiveness of preventive strategies. Studies in Vietnam have also reported low rates of appropriate folic acid use during early pregnancy and significant variation in access to prenatal counseling, particularly in rural areas [18–21]. Currently, the Vietnamese health system lacks widely implemented national guidelines recommending folic acid supplementation before pregnancy. Many staple Vietnamese foods lack folic acid fortification, contributing to a global health disparity in OFC prevention [18]. Also, there is confusion in Vietnam between B9, iron saturation (Sat/Fe), and folic acid. The doctors and patients do not always understand what each is used to treat and they are often confused to be the same thing. Investigating folic acid consumption and physician recommendations in Vietnam is urgently needed.

This research project explores the diverse maternal health practices during pregnancy among different generations of Vietnamese mothers with children affected by orofacial clefts in order to plan for future culturally-tailored public health interventions. It also investigates folic acid consumption, healthcare accessibility, and physician recommendations for expectant Vietnamese women. It is observed that younger generations might abandon or alter some traditional health practices compared to older generations due to increased accessibility to information via technology and higher levels of education [15]. Through narrative interviews with mothers and pregnant women, the study delves into generational disparities in maternal health practices while advocating for health equity. The project focuses on understanding the SSDoH most impacting rural communities in Vietnam, particularly how factors like socio-economic status, cultural beliefs, and access to healthcare influence maternal care. The primary research question of this study is: How do generational disparities shape maternal health practices among Vietnamese mothers with children affected by orofacial clefts, considering the social determinants of health and their impact on folic acid consumption, healthcare accessibility, and physician recommendations in prenatal care among expectant mothers in Vietnam?

In understanding the multifaceted factors contributing to the higher prevalence of OFCs in Vietnam, this research will help in the future development of health policies in underserved areas promoting folic acid consumption, screenings for SSDoH, and empowering safe cultural maternal health practices while advocating for improved healthcare access and health education. The aspiration of this work extends beyond national borders, envisioning a global initiative to address epidemiological health burdens in OFCs and inspire fellow healthcare professionals to engage in similar advocacy efforts.

Methods

This study was initiated by researchers in the United states in collaboration with a pediatrician at Ganh Hao Hospital in Vietnam (co-author HKP). We employed a narrative research methodology in combination with minimal semi-structured interviews using topic guided technique to explore individuals’ stories, perceptions, and experiences through in-depth interviews. Data collection for this study took place between May 2024 and August 2024. Eligibility criteria were defined prior to recruitment, and participants were categorized into older and younger maternal generations based on age at the time of interview (> 45 years vs. < 45 years) in order to explore generational differences in maternal health practices across different historical periods of the Vietnamese health system.

Participants who met the criteria and visited local hospitals in Vietnam were informed about the study by on-site physicians and study personnel. Additionally snowball sampling was used with Dr. Phuc to recruit pediatricians for physician interviews. Eligible participants were requested to provide consent before participating in the interviews for this study. Participants meeting the criteria were presented with a series of open-ended questions focusing on their folic acid consumption, access to healthcare, physician recommendations, cultural health practices, and the impact of social and structural determinants of health during pregnancy. The interview guide was developed using Jovchelovitch et al.’s and Anderson et al.’s principles of narrative interviewing [22, 23] (see Appendix 1). Participant interviews were conducted in Vietnamese in a private meeting room within Ganh Hao hospital and documented through audio recordings.

Data collection and analysis

Audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed and translated into English using TurboScribe service for further analysis. Lead-author QN reviewed and corrected the AI-generated transcripts for accuracy. Co-authors QN, KB, and JG independently conducted qualitative analysis on the translated transcripts to identify recurrent themes, patterns, and interconnections prevalent across the narratives to produce a coding system. Co-authors QN, KB, and JG then met to determine a unified coding system. Lead author QN then used the unified coding system to recode all the interviews and group responses into thematic categories.

Discussion

The findings of this study are best understood within an LMIC framework in which maternal nutrition, health literacy, poverty, and uneven access to prenatal services can shape both the risk environment surrounding OFCs and families’ experiences after diagnosis. Global cleft literature has consistently emphasized that OFCs are not only biologically complex congenital conditions, but also conditions whose burden is amplified by social and structural inequities, especially in settings where nutritional supplementation, prenatal screening, specialist referral pathways, and cleft care infrastructure are inconsistently available [6, 7].

The experiences described by Vietnamese mothers in this study reflect many of the challenges reported in other LMIC settings. Participants frequently described poverty, limited education, delayed or absent prenatal care, difficulty obtaining supplements, and restricted access to ultrasound or specialist consultation. These findings align with prior studies showing that antenatal care utilization in Vietnam is marked by significant rural-urban disparities, with women in rural areas more likely to initiate care late, attend fewer visits, and receive fewer recommended services [18, 21]. In addition, studies of maternal nutrition in Vietnam have demonstrated that folic acid supplementation often begins after the first prenatal visit rather than during the preconception period, reducing the potential protective effect against congenital anomalies [20].

The role of maternal nutrition is particularly important when considering OFC risk. Population-based studies and meta-analyses have reported that folic acid supplementation before and during early pregnancy may reduce the risk of cleft lip with or without cleft palate [19]. These findings suggest that inadequate access to supplements, confusion about vitamin use, and lack of counseling, issues repeatedly described by mothers in our interviews, may have meaningful biological as well as social consequences. In LMIC settings where supplementation programs are not universal and counseling resources are limited, preventive strategies that are effective in high-income countries may not translate directly without attention to structural barriers.

Our interviews with physicians also highlighted financial and health-system constraints that limit access to prenatal diagnostics. Although ultrasound and newer prenatal tests are available in Vietnam, cost and travel distance often prevent timely use. This pattern is consistent with international data showing that in many LMICs, access to antenatal care does not guarantee access to high-quality antenatal care. Large multi-country analyses have demonstrated substantial socioeconomic inequality not only in whether women receive antenatal care, but also in whether they receive core components of recommended services once care is initiated [24].

The association between socioeconomic disadvantage and OFC risk has also been observed in both LMIC and high-income settings. Studies in Argentina and the United States have reported higher OFC risk among mothers with lower education, lower income, and reduced access to prenatal care, suggesting that the pathways linking social disadvantage and congenital anomalies may operate across different health-system contexts [11, 12]. The present findings extend this literature by providing qualitative evidence from rural Vietnam showing how these structural factors are experienced at the level of everyday decision-making during pregnancy.

Maternal nutrition and awareness

Both mothers and physicians emphasized the importance of nutrition, especially the use of folic acid, iron and other vitamins in the diet (Quotes 1–3). Despite this, mothers said that they did not know the importance of these supplements while physicians pointed out that women needed to be educated and encouraged to make preparations prior to pregnancy including taking folic acid to prevent neural tube defects. This shows that there is still a lot of work to be done in order to facilitate proper communication and awareness. In order to solve this problem, there should be special educational campaigns targeted to rural communities to raise the awareness of the needs of prenatal nutrition and to make supplements more available and more affordable.

Role of ultrasounds and early detection

While some mothers described having access to at least one ultrasound during their pregnancy, the timing was not consistent with evidence-based recommendations to have them done in the first, second, and third trimesters. Also, many mothers who did have an ultrasound did not have access to an obstetrician to discuss the results. For these mothers, a single ultrasound was often their single point of contact for prenatal care. Many mothers lacked access to or knowledge about ultrasound and genetic testing (Quotes 4–6). Physicians, on the other hand, highlighted advancements in ultrasound technology and genetic testing, such as the Non-Invasive Prenatal (NIP) test, which can detect abnormalities like OFCs as early as 22 weeks (Quotes 27, 28). However, the cost of advanced testing remains a significant barrier for low-income families (Quotes 29, 30). These conclusions point out that there is also a necessity to provide subsidized prenatal diagnostic services and affordable screening tools. Physicians may also fail to educate women on the advantages of and means to access early detection and this needs to be addressed so that patients can be helped to make informed choices on their pregnancies.

Socioeconomic barriers

Both mothers and physicians pointed out that poverty is a major factor that affects the health of mothers. Mothers stated that they had difficulties in accessing healthy foods and medical care while the physicians pointed out that lack of funds meant that essential tests and treatment were delayed or not sought at all. These economic inequalities compound the injustices already experienced by women, especially those in the rural or hard-to-reach areas. To this end, policymakers should ensure that they increase their coverage of financial support programs, subsidize the diagnostic tools and offer prenatal care at little or no cost to overcome these challenges.

The role of empathy in the construction of trust

According to the physicians, it is crucial to gain the trust of the pregnant women so as to be able to counsel and advise them appropriately (Quotes 31, 32). It is, therefore, important that healthcare providers develop empathy for the patients and their needs, be it physical, family or even financial to enhance the patients’ compliance with the medical advice and the care plans. Crucially, this includes understanding the SSDoH that most impact the patients they serve. These findings indicate that there is a need to enhance the training of healthcare providers on structural competency and patient centeredness in order to foster the bond with their patients which would in turn improve the outcomes of maternal health interventions.

Cultural practices and beliefs

Cultural traditions and beliefs surrounding pregnancy remain deeply influential, with mothers often adhering to practices based on folk wisdom rather than medical guidance (Quotes 7–9). Physicians must navigate these traditions sensitively, finding ways to integrate cultural practices with evidence-based recommendations. For example, promoting culturally adapted educational materials and engaging community leaders could help bridge the gap between traditional beliefs and modern healthcare practices.

Medication concerns and stigma

Mothers expressed fears about medication safety during pregnancy, often rooted in misinformation, while physicians emphasized the importance of accurate guidance on medication use (Quotes 19–21). Additionally, social stigma related to OFCs remains a significant emotional burden for families, with mothers reporting feelings of blame or judgment (Quotes 22–24). Addressing these issues requires a dual approach: providing clear, accessible information about safe medication practices during pregnancy and fostering community awareness campaigns to reduce stigma surrounding congenital conditions like OFCs.

The research brings together data from moms and doctors to highlight complex issues surrounding maternal health and OFCs. Some of the most prevalent themes (from food and access to healthcare, to cultural norms and socioeconomic disadvantage) highlight the link between the personal and the systemic.

In addition to structural barriers, our findings illustrate how cultural beliefs and traditional practices interact with limited health-system support. When access to consistent prenatal counseling is restricted, mothers may rely more heavily on family advice, community beliefs, and inherited explanations for pregnancy outcomes. This dynamic does not necessarily represent resistance to biomedical care, but rather reflects the absence of reliable, accessible, and trusted medical guidance. Similar patterns have been described in other LMIC settings, where explanatory models rooted in culture and family experience often coexist with, or replace, formal medical recommendations when health services are difficult to access [6, 7]. These observations suggest that interventions aimed at reducing OFC risk in Vietnam should combine improvements in access to care with culturally responsive education that works within existing community networks rather than attempting to replace them.

Conclusions

Better maternal health and changing perceptions about orofacial clefts are not straightforward issues that can be tackled only through education, accessibility and awareness. Training pregnant women about the benefits of nutrients and vitamins (folic acid, calcium, vitamin A) is key to a healthy pregnancy and avoiding complications. To deal with cultural influences, by offering science-backed advice that is sensitive to folk wisdom, pregnant women can make scientifically informed decisions. There is an urgent need to promote access to healthcare for people who are low on money and may find it difficult to go to the doctor on a regular basis. Providing more affordable care and outreach will fill in the blanks.

It is also a matter of raising consciousness about stigmatization of orofacial clefts. More awareness and education around these conditions will help to diminish guilt and social pressures among many mothers. So too with respect to reducing the anxieties and misperceptions about pregnancy medication so that pregnant women can use prescribed and safe medications without causing anxiety. Health information accumulated in the early years can have lasting effects. Maternal and prenatal health education in schools supplies future generations with this information. Lastly, enhancing early detection tools (including ultrasounds) through community resources helps people who need them to get the right diagnosis at the right time, so the outcomes are better for mothers and children.
