ABSTRACT

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Hereditary angioedema (HAE) guideline goals recommend long-term prophylaxis (LTP) to achieve complete disease control and normalization of patients’ lives.Garadacimab is an anti-activated factor XII monoclonal antibody approved as LTP against HAE attacks in patients aged ≥ 12 years.The pooling of data from multiple studies in integrated analyses enhances the robustness of data, allows tracking of individual patient outcomes across studies where patients have transitioned from one study to another, and provides important insights into the long-term safety and efficacy of a treatment. This is particularly valuable for rare diseases with small patient populations, such as HAE.These integrated analyses of the garadacimab clinical program aimed to report long-term safety and efficacy outcomes with garadacimab, supporting its utility as LTP for HAE. What was learned from the study? Introduction

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare genetic disorder characterized by recurrent, painful, unpredictable, and potentially life-threatening attacks of swelling [1–4]. Activated factor XII (FXIIa) is the principal initiator of the kallikrein–kinin system [1, 5–7]. In HAE, dysregulation of the kallikrein–kinin system leads to the overproduction of bradykinin, the key mediator of HAE attacks [6, 7]. Thus, FXIIa inhibition constitutes an attractive strategy for HAE long-term prophylaxis (LTP), as an upstream alternative to currently available therapies that target prekallikrein, plasma kallikrein, or the bradykinin receptor [8–10].

Owing to the substantial burden of disease placed on patients and their caregivers, the international World Allergy Organization/European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (WAO/EAACI) guidelines recommend the use of LTP to achieve the treatment goals of complete disease control and normalization of patients’ lives [2].

Garadacimab is an FXIIa-targeting monoclonal antibody approved for the routine prevention of recurrent HAE attacks in adults and adolescents aged 12 years and older in Australia, Canada, the European Union, Japan, Switzerland, the United Arab Emirates, the UK, and the USA [11–18]. Garadacimab has been evaluated as LTP for HAE in a comprehensive clinical program, comprising the phase 2 (NCT03712228), pivotal phase 3 (VANGUARD; NCT04656418), and ongoing phase 3 open-label extension (OLE; NCT04739059) studies, and has demonstrated durable efficacy and a favorable long-term safety profile [19–22]. Inhibition of FXIIa represents a novel approach for HAE LTP; therefore, the long-term evaluation of the efficacy and safety of garadacimab is critical.

For diseases that require lifelong treatment, such as HAE, the pooling of data from multiple studies can provide important insights into long-term safety and efficacy by evaluating treatment effects over increased periods of exposure and tracking individual patient outcomes across studies where study transition occurs, enhancing the robustness of findings, and providing an assessment of the consistency of results. In addition, pooled analyses are advantageous in evaluating treatments for rare diseases, which have inherent challenges associated with small patient populations that can make it difficult to detect trends and patterns. The objective of these post hoc analyses was to examine the long-term integrated safety and efficacy of garadacimab for HAE LTP using data pooled from the garadacimab clinical program, which consists of phase 2, pivotal phase 3, and phase 3 OLE studies, representing the longest garadacimab exposure duration reported to date (up to 5.5 years).

Methods

Study Design

The integrated analyses of the safety and efficacy of garadacimab utilized pooled data from the garadacimab clinical program. The designs of the individual studies have been described previously, and an overview of the garadacimab HAE clinical program is shown in Fig. S1 [19, 21, 22]. In brief: the phase 2 study consisted of a 13-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period and a subsequent ≥ 44-week open-label period (n = 44, overall); the pivotal phase 3 study was a 6-month double-blind, placebo-controlled study (n = 64); and the ongoing phase 3 OLE study is evaluating garadacimab for ≥ 12 months (n = 161; 68 newly enrolled patients; 36 and 57 rollover patients from the phase 2 and pivotal phase 3 studies, respectively), totaling 176 unique patients. In the placebo-controlled period of the phase 2 study, patients were randomized to 75 mg, 200 mg, 400 mg, or 600 mg garadacimab Q4W. Patients were then re-randomized to 200 mg or 600 mg for the open-label portion of the study. On the basis of findings from that study, the 200 mg dose was selected for further investigation in the phase 3 and phase 3 OLE studies.

Each patient retained a unique identifier throughout the clinical program allowing tracking from enrollment to final study completion. The published interim analysis of the phase 3 OLE contained data up to the cutoff date of February 13, 2023; additional data up to June 15, 2024 are included here. Patients with HAE due to C1 inhibitor (C1INH) deficiency or dysfunction (HAE-C1INH-Type1/2) were eligible for all studies. Patients with HAE with normal C1 inhibitor (HAE-nC1INH, specifically PLG and FXII mutations) were eligible to enter the phase 2 study and could roll over into the phase 3 OLE. Key eligibility criteria are described in the supplementary materials (Fig. S1 and Tables S1–S3).

Outcome Measures

Endpoints for the individual studies have been described previously [19, 21, 22]. Key safety outcomes for these post hoc analyses were treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), TEAEs related to garadacimab, TEAEs leading to death or treatment discontinuation, TEAEs by severity, serious adverse events, and adverse events of special interest (AESIs) per protocol (defined as abnormal bleeding, thromboembolic events, and severe hypersensitivity including anaphylaxis). Laboratory tests for coagulation were as commonly seen in assessments in the clinical setting and included D-dimer, International Normalized Ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, and prothrombin time. Key efficacy outcomes were time to first attack, the time-normalized number of HAE attacks/month (HAE attack rate), the number of patients experiencing 100% reduction in attack rate (attack-free) by 6-month intervals, and the longest attack-free duration. HAE attacks across the clinical program were assessed and confirmed by the treating physician utilizing information documented by the patient in their eDiary.

Analysis

The two analysis sets used different pooling strategies. The integrated summary of safety (ISS) analysis was conducted using pooled data from patients who received ≥ 1 garadacimab dose (75–600 mg, once monthly) in the phase 2, pivotal phase 3, and phase 3 OLE studies. The integrated summary of efficacy (ISE) analysis focused on the recommended dose of garadacimab and was therefore conducted using data from patients who received garadacimab 200 mg subcutaneously once monthly in the pivotal phase 3 and phase 3 OLE studies. Patients with HAE-nC1INH were included in both ISS (6 patients) and ISE (2 patients) analyses; detailed safety and efficacy data for this patient population are reported in a dedicated manuscript [23].

Patient Characteristics

Race, ethnicity, and sex were collected at screening according to a list of classifications; if racial identity was multiracial, then all applicable races were selected.

Statistical Methods

This integrated analysis of data was primarily descriptive. No statistical tests were planned or applied.

Compliance with Ethics Guidelines

Ethics and consent details for the individual studies in the integrated analyses have been reported previously [19, 21, 22]. For all three studies, clinical trial protocols and informed consent forms were approved by institutional review boards or ethics committees. All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use Good Clinical Practice Guidelines.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Exposure

At data cutoff (June 15, 2024), in the ISS analysis set (garadacimab, n = 172; placebo, n = 33), the median exposure to garadacimab was 2.5 years/30.4 months (interquartile range [IQR] 2.4–3.1 years; range 0.2–5.5 years) with a maximum exposure of 5.5 years (66.4 months). Most patients (83.1%) had received garadacimab for ≥ 2 years, and 90.1% had ≥ 1 year of exposure. The majority of patients (63.4%) had received no LTP prior to receiving garadacimab. Baseline characteristics and exposure of the ISS set are shown in Table 1; in both the garadacimab and placebo groups, the majority of patients were female (64.5% and 54.5%, respectively) and most patients were white (82.6% and 87.9%).

Safety Outcomes

Overall, 155 patients (90.1%) receiving garadacimab (at any dose) experienced ≥ 1 TEAE, and 43 patients (25.0%) experienced TEAEs considered to be garadacimab-related; event rates adjusted by patient-year (PY) were 2.8 and 0.2 for all TEAEs and garadacimab-related TEAEs, respectively (Table 2). No deaths were observed. Four TEAEs led to treatment discontinuation, of which two were related to garadacimab: a mild injection-site urticaria (within 24 h after injection; recovered after 5 days) and an abdomen irritation of moderate severity at the injection site occurring after 4 months of treatment (within 24 h after injection; recovered after 13 days). The patient with the abdomen irritation was subsequently identified by the treating physician to have a delayed hypersensitivity to polysorbate 80, an excipient in the formulations. The TEAEs leading to discontinuation that were not related to garadacimab were breast cancer and mood swings, both of which were of severe intensity. By data cutoff, 1152/1345 of individual TEAEs reported were considered recovered or resolved.

TEAEs were mostly mild (61.2%) or moderate (36.3%) in severity. Eleven serious adverse events were reported, all considered not related to garadacimab: facial/abdominal severe HAE attack (n = 1) in the placebo-controlled period of the phase 2 study; diverticular perforation (n = 1) and asthma (n = 1) in the open-label period of the phase 2 study; laryngeal attack (n = 1) in the pivotal phase 3 study; and drug reaction (n = 1), hand fracture (n = 1), food allergy (n = 1), COVID-19 (n = 2), abdominal HAE attack (n = 1), and breast cancer (n = 1) in the phase 3 OLE.

One AESI per protocol was reported in the phase 2 study: a patient receiving garadacimab 600 mg experienced one episode of mild, unexplained epistaxis, assessed by the investigator as non-serious and not related to garadacimab. The epistaxis resolved, was not related to any medical diagnosis or concomitant medications, and the patient continued to receive treatment with garadacimab for > 5 years with no further abnormal bleeding events reported.

The most common TEAEs (occurring in ≥ 5% of patients) were infections and infestations, injection-site reactions (ISRs), gastrointestinal disorders, headache, arthralgia, back pain, and cough (Table 3). ISRs were all either mild (94.9%) or moderate (5.1%) in severity. The rates of infection and infestation TEAEs in the garadacimab and placebo groups were similar, at 0.853/PY and 0.954/PY, respectively. None of the reported infections in the garadacimab group were considered related to treatment.

Efficacy Outcomes

In the ISE, 98.2% of patients who received garadacimab (n = 161/164) had a ≥ 50% reduction in attack rate and 84.8% (n = 139/164) had a ≥ 90% reduction, versus 33.3% (n = 8/24) and 8.3% (n = 2/24) of patients who received placebo, respectively. When analyzed by 6-month treatment windows, the proportion of attack-free patients in the garadacimab group was 64.0% during months 1–6 (i.e., the first 6 months after starting treatment), increasing to 88.9% during months 31–36 (2.5–3 years) and remaining high (86.7%) in months 37–42 (3–3.5 years) (Fig. 2). The maximum attack-free period for patients who received garadacimab was a median (IQR) of 2.2 (1.2–2.6) years, ranging from 0.1 to 3.1 years versus 0.1 (0.1–0.2) years with placebo.

Discussion

Consistent with findings from the individual studies within the HAE clinical program, these integrated analyses demonstrated that garadacimab had a favorable long-term safety profile with durable and sustained protection against HAE attacks [19, 21, 22, 24]. The evaluation of the long-term safety and efficacy of garadacimab is critical considering the novelty of FXIIa inhibition upstream of other currently available therapeutic targets as a targeted approach for LTP and the necessity of lifelong treatment for patients with HAE. Combining data from multiple studies can provide more robust estimates of TEAE rates, potential risks, and identification of safety signals (especially for rare events). In addition, pooled analyses increase the sample size, allowing detection of trends and patterns, and the use of unique identifiers permits tracking of individual patient outcomes across studies.

Results from these post hoc integrated analyses over a median exposure of 2.5 years (range 0.2–5.5) showed that garadacimab continued to be well tolerated for periods of up to 5.5 years, which is the longest observed exposure in the garadacimab HAE clinical program to date. The safety profile observed in the pooled analysis remains consistent with that observed in the individual studies, with no new safety concerns identified and a positive risk–benefit profile confirmed [19, 21, 22].

Our analysis revealed no garadacimab-related AESIs per protocol throughout the HAE clinical program. Notably, the absence of abnormal bleeding events after a median of 2.5 years of FXIIa inhibition with garadacimab is consistent with data from other studies that demonstrated an absent bleeding phenotype in individuals with a congenital factor XII deficiency [25–27]. Similarly, no thromboembolic events were observed with garadacimab throughout. Further analyses on AESIs from the garadacimab clinical program will be reported elsewhere.

The incidence of ISRs was low throughout the HAE clinical program, with low rates per administration (≤ 0.005). This result is consistent with findings from a phase 1 study comparing garadacimab administered via pre-filled autoinjector/pen and prefilled syringe in healthy volunteers [28]. Importantly, ISR rates decreased across successive 3-month intervals in the integrated safety dataset, possibly suggestive of improved tolerability with continued use.

Garadacimab 200 mg subcutaneously once monthly demonstrated durable efficacy over a maximum exposure of 3.2 years, with a 95% reduction in mean monthly HAE attack rate versus run-in. At each 6-month treatment window, the majority of patients were attack-free, with 64.0% of patients attack-free in the first 6 months of treatment, increasing steadily throughout the study to 86.7% during months 37–42 (3–3.5 years). Furthermore, the median time to first attack after garadacimab initiation was more than 2 years, highlighting the potential of garadacimab in helping patients achieve the goal of complete disease control, per international guidelines [2]. The phase 3 OLE is ongoing and further long-term safety and efficacy data are being collected; we anticipate that these data will reinforce the observations so far.

In terms of strengths, at the time of publication, these analyses report safety and efficacy data for the longest exposure to garadacimab so far, which is important for a new drug intended to be used as LTP. In addition, these analyses contain data reflective of the largest population of patients with HAE treated with garadacimab, enhancing previously published safety and efficacy data by pooling three clinical studies. The use of a unique identifier for each patient across the studies strengthens these analyses by extending the continuous data available for individual patients, allowing for comprehensive safety tracking, increased reliability of efficacy endpoint assessments, and improved risk–benefit evaluation. A limitation is that, similar to the pre-planned analyses of the individual phase 2 and 3 studies, these ISS and ISE analyses were not powered to show statistical differences between garadacimab and placebo in any of the endpoints. A formal sex‑specific subgroup analysis was not prespecified or requested during regulatory review as a priori or empirical reasons for statistic testing were not identified. Additionally, garadacimab prescribing information does not include sex-specific dosing or efficacy information as sex was not identified as a clinically relevant covariate during phase 1 studies [28]. These findings align with clinical experience with other LTPs targeting the kallikrein–kinin pathway, which similarly find no clinically meaningful sex-based differences in response [29, 30]. As no sex-specific analysis was performed, conclusions concerning sex-differences cannot and are not drawn.

Conclusion

These post hoc integrated analyses further corroborate the long-term safety and efficacy of garadacimab over up to 5.5 years of garadacimab exposure, as previously seen in the individual phase 2, pivotal phase 3, and phase 3 OLE studies. These data confirm a favorable safety profile with low rates of ISRs and no abnormal bleeding or thromboembolic events. Additionally, garadacimab showed durable and sustained protection against HAE attacks, and more than half of patients were attack-free for long periods of time. These data demonstrate a positive risk–benefit profile and that garadacimab has the potential to help patients achieve complete disease control.
