Prophylactic use of a compliant occlusion balloon for limited superior vena cava injury: Shifting from rescue to prevention
Authors: Atef Badreddine, Benoît Plourde, Hugo-Pierre Racine, François Philippon
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Superior vena cava perforation is a rare but life-threatening complication that can occur even with passive-fixation leads, especially if unexpected resistance is encountered during lead advancement.•To prevent such injuries, any resistance during lead positioning should prompt an immediate halt of the maneuver and a systematic fluoroscopic verification in multiple projections and contrast injection to document the anatomy and any vascular injury.•Although the compliant occlusion balloon is typically used as a bridge to surgery in emergency rescue situations, its prophylactic deployment in high-risk lead explantations can help prevent catastrophic rebleeding and ensure hemodynamic stability.•For procedures requiring prolonged balloon inflation, continuous monitoring with transesophageal echocardiography is vital to balance the risk of bleeding against the possibility of venous thrombosis caused by stasis.

Introduction

Transvenous lead extraction (TLE) is linked to rare but potentially life-threatening vascular complications, especially involving the superior vena cava (SVC). Since its introduction in 2016, the Bridge occlusion balloon (BOB) has become an essential tool for emergency management of SVC injuries, enabling quick hemodynamic stabilization and acting as a bridge to surgical repair.1, 2, 3, 4 Although complications such as thrombosis can occur, these are generally uncommon with proper use.5

The BOB is a compliant endovascular balloon that can be rapidly deployed over a guidewire to achieve temporary SVC occlusion, significantly reducing blood loss and allowing time for definitive management.4

To date, its use has primarily been described as a rescue strategy in cases of catastrophic vascular injury during TLE. However, its role as a prophylactic measure in high-risk situations remains largely unexplored.

We report a case of cardiac tamponade caused by SVC perforation owing to a passive-fixation atrial lead, successfully managed with a prophylactic BOB strategy during lead removal, thereby preventing rebleeding and avoiding the need for surgical intervention.

Case report

A 79 year-old woman with sick sinus syndrome underwent dual-chamber pacemaker implantation at a referring center involving 2 passive fixation leads [Medtronic 4074 and 4574 (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN)]. During the procedure, severe chest pain and hypotension developed in the patient. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a large pericardial effusion causing cardiac tamponade. Urgent pericardiocentesis removed 200 mL of blood, stabilizing her hemodynamics. The injury was suspected to have occurred during manipulation of the atrial lead. When resistance was encountered, the operator abandoned the lead in situ and closed the pocket. A subsequent computed tomography scan confirmed the extraluminal position of the atrial lead on the posterolateral aspect of the SVC (Figure 1). Notably, there was no active contrast extravasation, indicating a contained intrapericardial SVC perforation. Recognizing the significant risk of rebleeding related to the removal of a perforating passive lead, we adopted a prophylactic approach. The procedure was performed in a hybrid suite under general anesthesia, with invasive hemodynamic monitoring, continuous transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and on-site cardiac surgery backup. After confirming the proper position of the pericardial drain and preparing the patient for potential open-heart surgery, vascular access was established through the right femoral vein for the BOB and the left axillary vein for lead management. An SVC angiogram was performed to locate the perforation site (Figure 1). The BOB was then positioned in the SVC and left deflated, whereas the right ventricular lead was explanted and new atrial and ventricular leads (Medtronic CapSureFix 4076) were implanted. Immediately after removing the perforating atrial lead by manual traction, the BOB was inflated for 10 minutes to achieve hemostasis (Figure 2). Postdeployment TEE indicated no recurrent pericardial effusion or thrombus formation, and venography confirmed the absence of contrast extravasation (Figure 3). The patient’s condition remained hemodynamically stable throughout; the pericardial drain was removed after 24 hours, and the patient was discharged without complications.Figure 2BOB inflated. Discussion

SVC perforation caused by a passive lead is a rare complication. Our review of the literature did not find any previously reported cases, with the only documented instance involving an active fixation lead.6 This highlights the uniqueness of the present observation.

The exact cause of lead perforation in this case remains unknown, given the implantation was performed at a referring center, and detailed procedural records were not available at the time of our assessment. We attempted to retrieve intraoperative imaging, but only limited fluoroscopic recordings had been made. Consequently, we cannot definitively determine whether the perforation was due to stylet-related trauma or another factor. On the basis of the computed tomography findings showing extraluminal lead position on the posterolateral aspect of the SVC, and considering that the operator experienced resistance during the procedure, the most likely explanation is that the lead was inadvertently directed against the SVC wall during atrial placement. This case highlights the importance of systematic fluoroscopic monitoring during lead advancement and the necessity of ceasing any maneuver that encounters unexpected resistance, regardless of lead type or the use of contrast to assess anatomy and identify complications.

In our case, the tamponade was caused by the intrapericardial location of the SVC perforation. In contrast, extrapericardial injuries involving the distal two-thirds of the vessel typically lead to hemothorax with hypovolemia and a new right pleural effusion or mediastinal hematoma. Proximal injuries confined to the pericardial sac present with tamponade, whose onset depends on the size of the perforation. This anatomical distinction is vital for accurate diagnosis and urgent management.

The clinical significance of BOB availability is well supported by evidence.7 A large single-center study involving 4946 patients showed that procedure-related deaths were eliminated during the BOB era, whereas previously, there was a 0.3% mortality rate. Survival after SVC injury reached 100% with its use, compared with 50% without, representing a major shift in how catastrophic SVC injuries are managed.3

The traditional use of the BOB has been primarily reactive, focusing on life-saving deployment after overt SVC laceration. In rare cases, it has been used without surgical repair if the patient’s condition is hemodynamically stable. Our case illustrates a novel prophylactic approach guided by individual risk assessment rather than the presence of active bleeding. Key risk factors in our case included recent tamponade, extraluminal lead position, and an older, frail patient. This proactive strategy shifts the focus from crisis management to prevention: by securing hemostasis before instability occurs, it maintains hemodynamic stability and improves procedural decision-making.

It is also crucial to distinguish the mechanisms of SVC injury during TLE from those caused by lead perforation because this difference significantly affects clinical presentation, management, and prognosis. Extraction-associated injuries usually happen through direct mechanical trauma caused by powered sheaths or laser tools, or from excessive traction, generating sudden vascular damage and uncontrolled bleeding that often needs complex surgical repair. Conversely, SVC perforation by a passive lead, as in our case, tends to be smaller and more confined. This mechanism is less damaging, tends to protect nearby structures, and permits natural compensatory responses such as vascular spasm, the tamponade effect of the lead, and fibrin buildup. These protective responses are the reason bleeding stopped after pericardiocentesis (the lead was clearly sealing the perforation), there was no ongoing leakage on follow-up imaging, and stable blood flow was observed.

Recognizing these distinct pathophysiologies is crucial for risk stratification and procedural planning. Although extraction-related lacerations almost always require immediate surgical intervention, lead perforations may, in carefully selected patients, be managed conservatively or with preventive strategies. One might reasonably wonder whether spontaneous sealing of the perforation could have occurred after lead removal, aided by tissue elasticity, vascular recoil, and clot stabilization once the mobile foreign body was no longer disrupting thrombus formation. We cannot dismiss this possibility. However, several factors argued against an expectant approach in this case: the SVC is a thin-walled, noncontractile vessel with limited intrinsic hemostatic capacity; the perforation involved the intrapericardial segment, where even minor rebleeding would quickly cause hemodynamic compromise; the patient had already shown vulnerability to tamponade; and no published data support reliable self-sealing of SVC perforations in this context, unlike the well-known tendency of right ventricular perforations to seal after lead withdrawal. Prophylactic BOB deployment therefore provided a physiologically sound safety net, enhancing natural hemostatic mechanisms while keeping the option for immediate reinflation available if needed.

The positive outcome highlights the essential role of a dedicated heart team approach within a hybrid setting, combining advanced imaging, interventional skills, and immediate surgical backup. This multidisciplinary approach enhanced patient safety and avoided the need for emergency open repair, reinforcing the idea that procedural success depends as much on cohesive teamwork as on the devices used.

Continuous intraoperative TEE monitoring was performed by a dedicated anesthesiologist throughout the entire procedure, including during and after balloon inflation. TEE confirmed no thrombus formation within the SVC, along the balloon surface, or within the right atrium at all timepoints. Postdeflation TEE revealed no recurrent pericardial effusion, and angiography verified the absence of contrast extravasation, providing objective evidence that hemostasis had been achieved without thrombotic complications.

Beyond this specific case, we have successfully extended this prophylactic occlusion technique to manage other vascular injuries, including an extrapericardial SVC perforation caused by an atrial lead and an internal jugular branch injury induced by a dialysis catheter. Although these subsequent cases had less acute hemodynamic instability owing to their mediastinal location, the prophylactic use of the balloon effectively prevented hematoma expansion and eliminated the need for surgical intervention.8

Conclusion

This case reports a rare SVC perforation with tamponade caused by a passive-fixation lead and what we believe is the first use of BOB to prevent rebleeding during lead explantation. Instead of being solely a rescue tool for uncontrolled lacerations, the balloon was used proactively as a hemostatic aid, supporting natural clotting and stabilizing the patient’s condition. This physiology-based, heart team-guided approach reveals how the customized preventive use of advanced tools can improve safety in rare, higher-risk lead explantation procedures.
