Rong-Hua Wang

Abstract




Polynomial reduction for
q
-holonomic sequences

Abstract.

This paper provides a (Laurent) polynomial reduction to
q
-holonomic sequences
F_{k}(q)
.
We first characterize Laurent polynomials
\tilde{p}(x)
such that the product
\tilde{p}(q^{k})F_{k}(q)
is summable.
Then the reduction framework is given to decompose any given Laurent polynomial into a summable part and a remainder with lower degree.
Finally, we introduce a power-partible reduction for
q
-holonomic sequences of which the recurrence relation satisfies a certain symmetry condition.
The advantage is that it can not only simultaneously eliminate the highest-degree and lowest-degree terms of a Laurent polynomial satisfying a symmetry condition, but also guarantee the symmetry of the remainder.
As applications, we apply the reduction to
q
-central-Delannoy numbers to derive new
q
-identities and
q
-congruences.

Keywords
: polynomial reduction;
q
-holonomic sequence;
q
-power-partible reduction;
q
-central-Delannoy numbers.

1
Introduction

The mechanical proof and discovery of combinatorial identities have been central themes in symbolic computation, a paradigm largely established by the seminal work of Wilf and Zeilberger
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
The method of creative telescoping, as the core of the WZ theory, provides a powerful algorithmic framework for proving hypergeometric identities.
Within this framework, reduction-based methods play a crucial role by separating the computation of telescopers from the potentially costly calculation of certificates
[
8
]
, thereby enabling more efficient algorithm design.
This reduction-based approach traces its roots back to the classical work of Ostrogradsky
[
19
]
and Hermite
[
15
]
on the integration of rational functions, and has been systematically extended to various classes of functions, including multivariate rational functions
[
4
,
7
]
, hyperexponential functions
[
5
]
, algebraic functions
[
12
]
, and D-finite functions
[
9
,
6
]
.

In the discrete setting, Abramov
[
1
]
first studied the summability problem for rational functions, which was extended to the trivariate rational case in
[
10
]
with the key idea being to decompose a given rational function into a summable part and a non-summable remainder. Subsequently, Abramov and Petkovšek
[
2
,
3
]
and van der Hoeven
[
16
]
extended this decomposition to hypergeometric terms and holonomic cases respectively. In recent years, significant advances have been made in reduction-based methods. Chen et al.
[
11
]
proposed a modified Abramov–Petkovšek reduction, which significantly improved algorithmic efficiency by introducing the concept of polynomial reduction, and successfully applied it to the computation of minimal telescopers for bivariate hypergeometric terms.

Of particular interest is the polynomial reduction introduced by Hou, Mu, and Zeilberger
[
18
]
in 2021, which avoids the multiplicative decomposition needed in
[
11
]
. This method not only enables the automated proof of hypergeometric identities but also facilitates the generation of infinite families of supercongruences. This polynomial reduction was utilized by Hou and Li
[
17
]
to obtain new hypergeometric identities.

In the realm of
q
-analogues, Wang and Zhong
[
22
]
developed a
q
-analogue of this reduction, establishing a
q
-rational reduction framework via the
q
-Gosper representation that characterizes the summability of products of rational functions with
q
-hypergeometric terms. As applications, they derived several
q
-analogues of series for
\pi
and provided a new tool for the automated discovery of
q
-identities.

More recently, Wang and Zhong
[
23
]
extended the polynomial reduction framework to the broader class of holonomic sequences, which includes harmonic numbers, Fibonacci numbers, Domb numbers, Franel numbers, and all hypergeometric terms.
By introducing a difference Lagrange identity, they established a polynomial reduction for holonomic sequences and successfully applied it to derive new
\pi
-series involving Domb numbers and Franel numbers, as well as new families of congruences.

A refined property of polynomial reduction, known as power-partibility, was first observed by Hou, Mu, and Zeilberger
[
18
]
in the hypergeometric setting, where the reduced polynomial contains only odd or only even powers of
(k-\gamma)
under a symmetry condition. This property has been proven instrumental in deriving systematically families of supercongruences.
Subsequently, Wang and Zhong
[
24
,
25
]
extended power-partibility to holonomic sequences, establishing a comprehensive theory with applications to Apéry numbers, Legendre polynomials, and related congruences.

The present paper further extends the polynomial reduction to the
q
-holonomic sequences, a broad class that includes
q
-hypergeometric terms and many other important combinatorial sequences.
We first establish a polynomial reduction framework for
q
-holonomic sequences via the adjoint operator and a corresponding Lagrange identity, characterizing the summability of terms of the form
\tilde{p}(q^{k})F_{k}(q)
, where
\tilde{p}(x)
is a Laurent polynomial.
As applications, we apply the developed theory to
q
-central-Delannoy numbers to derive new
q
-identities and
q
-congruences.
Finally, we introduce the notion of
q
-power-partibility for
q
-holonomic sequences. A
q
-holonomic sequence
F_{k}(q)
is called
q
-power-partible if the coefficients of its associated linear recurrence satisfies a certain symmetry condition.
Under this condition, we develop a reduction algorithm for Laurent polynomials
Q([k])
satisfying

Q([\gamma+k])=\pm Q([\gamma-k]),

for some rational number
\gamma
.
The key feature of this algorithm is, by appropriately choosing reduction functions, eliminating the highest and lowest degree terms simultaneously.
The procedure can be repeated until no further reduction is possible.
As an application, we generalize a theorem of Pan
[
20
]
from the linear case to arbitrary odd powers, establishing new
q
-congruences.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the fundamental concepts of
q
-holonomic sequences and establishes the theoretical foundation of polynomial reduction. Section 3 is devoted to
q
-power-partible reduction, with applications in deriving
q
-congruences on
q
-central Delannoy numbers.

2
Polynomial reduction with applications

In this section, we introduce the polynomial reduction for
q
-holonomic sequences and apply it to derive
q
-congruences on
q
-central-Delannoy numbers.

2.1
Polynomial reduction for
q
-holonomic sequences

Let
{\mathbb{K}}
be a field of characteristic zero and
q
an inderterminate.
A sequence
(F_{k}(q))_{k=0}^{\infty}
is called
q
-holonomic
if there exist
J\in{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots,\}
and Laurent polynomials
a_{i}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
,
0\leq i\leq J
, with
a_{J}(x)\neq 0
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})F_{k+i}(q)=0.

(2.1)

Or, equivalently, if we define the
annihilator
of
F_{k}(q)
by

\ann F_{k}(q):=\left\{L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[q^{k},q^{-k}][\sigma]\mid L(F_{k}(q))=0\right\},

where
\sigma
is the shift operator,
that is,
\sigma^{i}F_{k}(q)=F_{k+i}(q)
for any
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then
(F_{k}(q))_{k=0}^{\infty}
is
q
-holonomic if and only if

\ann F_{k}(q)\neq\{0\}
.
We call
J
in (
2.1
) the order of the recurrence relation for
F_{k}(q)
, and the minimum order of all such recurrences is called the
order
of
F_{k}(q)
.

The class of
q
-holonomic sequences covers a great percentage of combinatorial sequences arising in applications.
In particular, all
q
-hypergeometric terms are
q
-holonomic.
Recently, Wang and Zhong
[
22
]
introduced a reduction for
q
-hypergeometric terms which is a
q
-analogue of the Hou–Mu–Zeilberger reduction introduced in 2021.
For a
q
-hypergeometric term
t_{k}(q)
, a key step in the reduction is to characterize such polynomials
\tilde{p}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x]
that the product
\tilde{p}(q^{k})t_{k}(q)
is
q
-Gosper-summable
, that is,

\tilde{p}(q^{k})t_{k}(q)=\Delta(u(q^{k})t_{k}(q)),

for some rational function
u(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
, where
\Delta=\sigma-1
is the difference operator (that is,
\Delta F_{k}(q)=F_{k+1}(q)-F_{k}(q)
).

It is natural to consider a similar problem in the
q
-holonomic case.

Problem 2.1
.

Given a
q
-holonomic sequence
(F_{k}(q))_{k=0}^{\infty}
satisfying (
2.1
),
for which Laurent polynomials
\tilde{p}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
, the product
\tilde{p}(q^{k})F_{k}(q)
can be written as

\tilde{p}(q^{k})F_{k}(q)=\Delta\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(q^{k})F_{k+i}(q)\right)

(2.2)

for some rational functions
u_{0}(x),u_{1}(x),\ldots,u_{J-1}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
?

To proceed, some notations are needed.
Given
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}
with
a_{i}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
,
the
adjoint
of
L
is defined by

L^{\ast}=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k-i})\sigma^{-i}.

Then

L^{\ast}(p(q^{k}))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k-i})p(q^{k-i})

for any
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
.
For simplicity of illustration, we also utilize

L^{\ast}(p(x))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{-i}x)p(q^{-i}x)

when there is no confusion.
The following lemma due to van der Hoeven
[
16
, Proposition 3.2]
is crucial in our polynomial reduction.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}
with
a_{i}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
.
Then for any
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
and sequence
F_{k}(q)
, we have

p(q^{k})L(F_{k}(q))-L^{\ast}(p(q^{k}))F_{k}(q)=\Delta\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(q^{k})F_{k+i}(q)\right),

(2.3)

where

u_{i}(q^{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{J-i}a_{i+j}(q^{k-j})p(q^{k-j}).

(2.4)

When
L\in\ann F_{k}(q)
, identity (
2.3
) reduces to

L^{\ast}(p(q^{k}))F_{k}(q)=\Delta\left(-\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(q^{k})F_{k+i}(q)\right).

(2.5)

If
\tilde{p}(x)=L^{\ast}(p(x))\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
, then
\tilde{p}(x)

is a desired polynomial such that (
2.2
) holds.
From equality (
2.5
) we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(p(q^{k}))F_{k}(q)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(1)F_{i}(q)\right)-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}u_{i}(q^{n})F_{n+i}(q)\right),

(2.6)

where
u_{i}
is defined in (
2.4
).

When the order
J
in (
2.1
) is minimum, it can be directly checked that all rational functions
\tilde{p}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
such that (
2.2
) holds are of the form
L^{\ast}(p(x))
with
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
.
It may happen that
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)\setminus{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
but
L^{\ast}(p(x))\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
.
The following lemma shows that this rarely happens.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}
with
a_{i}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}],\ 0\leq i\leq J
and
a_{0}(x)a_{J}(x)\neq 0
.
If
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)(x)
and

\gcd(a_{0}(x),a_{J}(q^{i}x))=1,\quad\forall i\in{\mathbb{N}},

(2.7)

then
L^{\ast}(p(x))\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
if and only if
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
.

Proof.
Suppose
p(x)=\frac{r(x)}{s(x)}
with
r(x),s(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
and
r(x),s(x)
are coprime in
{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
.
If
\tilde{p}(x)=L^{\ast}(p(x))
is a Laurent polynomial in
{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
, then

\tilde{p}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{-i}x)p(q^{-i}x)=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{-i}x)\frac{r(q^{-i}x)}{s(q^{-i}x)}.

Multiplying
s(x)s(q^{-1}x)\cdots s(q^{-J}x)
on both ends, we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{p}(x)\prod_{i=0}^{J}s(q^{-i}x)=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{-i}x)r(q^{-i}x)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq J\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}s(q^{-j}x).

Apparently,
s(x)
is a divisor of the left hand side, then we must have

\displaystyle s(x)\mid a_{0}(x)r(x)s(q^{-1}x)\cdots s(q^{-J}x).

From
\gcd(r(x),s(x))=1
we know that

s(x)\mid a_{0}(x)s(q^{-1}x)\cdots s(q^{-J}x).

(2.8)

By a similar argument,

s(q^{-J}x)\mid a_{J}(q^{-J}x)s(x)s(q^{-1}x)\cdots s(q^{-J+1}x),

namely,

s(x)\mid a_{J}(x)s(q^{J}x)s(q^{J-1}x)\cdots s(qx).

(2.9)

Suppose that
s(x)
is not invertible in
{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
and
t(x)
is an irreducible factor of
s(x)
.
By (
2.8
), if
t(x)\nmid a_{0}(x)
, we have
t(q^{j_{1}}x)\mid s(x)
for some
j_{1}>0
.
Again, by (
2.8
), if
t(q^{j_{1}}x)\nmid a_{0}(x)
, then
t(q^{j_{1}+j_{2}}x)\mid s(x)
for some
j_{2}>0
.
Since
s(x)
can not have infinitely many distinct irreducible factors, there must exist a
j\geq 0
such that
t(q^{j}x)\mid a_{0}(x)
.
By a similar argument, (
2.9
) guarantees that there exists an
i\geq 0
such that
t(q^{-i}x)\mid a_{J}(x)
.
Then
a_{0}(x)
and
a_{J}(q^{i+j}x)
have a common factor
t(q^{j}x)
, contradicting (
2.7
).
As the converse is clearly true, this completes the proof.

Combining Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
, we have the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
F_{k}(q)
be a
q
-holonomic sequence of order
J
and
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}\in\ann F_{k}(q)
with

\gcd(a_{0}(x),a_{J}(q^{i}x))=1,\quad\forall i\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Then
\tilde{p}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
satisfies the summability condition (
2.2
) if and only if

\tilde{p}(x)=L^{\ast}(p(x))
for some
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
.

Next we will determine the degrees of
L^{\ast}(p(x))
for
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
and
L=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}
with coefficients
a_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{K}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
.

For any Laurent polynomial
a(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
,
denote by
\hdeg a(x)
(resp.
\operatorname{ldeg}a(x)
) the highest (resp. lowest) degree of
x
in
a(x)
and

\deg a(x)=\hdeg a(x)-\ldeg a(x).

The
highest degree
and the
lowest degree
of
L
, denoted by
\hdeg L
and
\ldeg L
respectively, are defined as

\hdeg L=\max_{0\leq i\leq J}\{\hdeg a_{i}(x)\}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{ldeg}L=\min_{0\leq i\leq J}\{\operatorname{ldeg}a_{i}(x)\}.

The
degree
of
L
is defined by

\deg L=\hdeg L-\ldeg L.

Let

f_{L}^{(t)}(s)=\sum_{i=0}^{J}[x^{t}](a_{i}(q^{-i}x))q^{-si},

where
[x^{t}](a(x))
denotes the coefficient of
x^{t}
in
a(x)
.
Define

R_{L}^{t}=\{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}\mid f_{L}^{(t)}(s)=0\}.

Then
L
is called
highest-degree-degenerated
if
R_{L}^{\hdeg L}\neq\emptyset
or
lowest-degree-degenerated
if
R_{L}^{\ldeg L}\neq\emptyset
,
which will be denoted as
HDD
or
LDD
for short.
When
R_{L}^{\hdeg L}=R_{L}^{\ldeg L}=\emptyset
, we say
L
is
nondegenerated
.

Now we are ready to characterize the degrees of
L^{*}(p(x))
for Laurent polynomials
p(x)
, which is crucial in the polynomial reduction.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}
with
a_{i}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
and
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

\hdeg L^{\ast}(p(x))=\hdeg L+\hdeg p(x)

except when
L
is HDD and
\hdeg p(x)\in R_{L}^{\hdeg L}
, in which case we have
\hdeg L^{\ast}(p(x))<\hdeg L+\hdeg p(x)
,
and

\ldeg L^{\ast}(p(x))=\ldeg L+\ldeg p(x)

except when
L
is LDD and
\ldeg p(x)\in R_{L}^{\ldeg L}
, in which case we have
\ldeg L^{\ast}(p(x))>\ldeg L+\ldeg p(x)
.

Proof.
We know by definition that

L^{*}(p(x))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{-i}x)p(q^{-i}x)

and

\hdeg(a_{i}(q^{-i}x)p(q^{-i}x))=\hdeg a_{i}(x)+\hdeg p(x)\leq\hdeg L+\hdeg p(x).

Hence it is clear that

\hdeg L^{*}(p(x))\leq\hdeg L+\hdeg p(x).

Note that
\hdeg\ L^{\ast}(p(x))<\hdeg L+\hdeg p(x)
if and only if

\sum_{i=0}^{J}[x^{\hdeg L}](a_{i}(q^{-i}x))[x^{\hdeg p(x)}](p(q^{-i}x))=0,

which is equivalent to

\sum_{i=0}^{J}[x^{\hdeg L}](a_{i}(q^{-i}x))q^{-i\cdot\hdeg p(x)}=f_{L}^{(\hdeg L)}(\hdeg p(x))=0.

This completes the proof of the first part. The second part can be confirmed similarly and thus omitted.

Let
F_{k}(q)
be a
q
-holonomic sequence and
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}(q^{k})\sigma^{i}\in\ann F_{k}(q)
with
a_{0}(q^{k})a_{J}(q^{k})\neq 0
.
By definition, one can see that
L
is usually nondegenerated.
In this case, we can split any Laurent polynomial
Q(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
as

Q(x)=\tilde{p}(x)+\tilde{Q}(x),

where
\tilde{p}(x)
is a Laurent polynomial satisfying the summability condition (
2.2
) and

\deg\tilde{Q}(x)<\deg L
.
We call
\tilde{p}(x)
the summable part and
\tilde{Q}(x)
the reminder.
This clearly holds when
\deg Q(x)<\deg L
.
In the following, we assume that
\deg Q(x)\geq\deg L
.

First suppose that
L
is not HDD.
We can choose a
p_{0}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
with

\hdeg p_{0}(x)=\hdeg Q(x)-\hdeg L

and

\ldeg p_{0}(x)\geq\ldeg Q(x)-\ldeg L
, for example,
p_{0}(x)=x^{\hdeg Q(x)-\hdeg L}
.
Then by Theorem
2.5
, we have

\hdeg L^{*}(p_{0}(x))=\hdeg Q(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\ldeg L^{*}(p_{0}(x))\geq\ldeg Q(x).

It is clear that there exists a
b_{0}\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)
such that

Q_{1}(x)=Q(x)-b_{0}L^{*}(p_{0}(x))
is a Laurent polynomial satisfying

\hdeg Q_{1}(x)<\hdeg Q(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\ldeg Q_{1}(x)\geq\ldeg Q(x).

(2.10)

Thus
\deg Q_{1}(x)<\deg Q(x)
.
If
\deg Q_{1}(x)<\deg L
, one can see that
Q(x)=L^{*}(b_{0}p_{0}(x))+Q_{1}(x)
is the required decompositoin.
Otherwise, we can replace
Q(x)
by
Q_{1}(x)
, and continue the above process.
After finite steps, one can find
b_{s}\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)
and Laurent polynomials
p_{s}(x),\tilde{Q}_{h}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
such that

Q(x)=\sum_{s}b_{s}L^{*}(p_{s}(x))+\tilde{Q}_{h}(x)

(2.11)

with
\deg\tilde{Q}_{h}(x)<\deg L
,
\hdeg\tilde{Q}_{h}(x)<\hdeg Q(x)
and
\ldeg\tilde{Q}_{h}(x)\geq\ldeg Q(x)
.
The equality (
2.11
) is the required decomposition since

\tilde{p}(x)=\sum_{s}b_{s}L^{*}\left(p_{s}(x)\right)=L^{*}\left(\sum_{s}b_{s}p_{s}(x)\right)

satisfies (
2.2
) by Lemma
2.2
.

When
L
is not LDD,
we can choose a polynomial
p_{0}(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
with

\ldeg p_{0}(x)=\ldeg Q(x)-\ldeg L

and

\hdeg p_{0}(x)\leq\hdeg Q(x)-\hdeg L
,
for example,
p_{0}(x)=x^{\ldeg Q(x)-\ldeg L}
.
Then by Theorem
2.5
, we have

\ldeg L^{*}(p_{0}(x))=\ldeg Q(x)\text{ and }\hdeg L^{*}(p_{0}(x))\leq\hdeg Q(x).

There exists a
b_{0}\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)
such that
Q_{1}(x)=Q(x)-b_{0}L^{*}(p_{0}(x))
is a Laurent polynomial satisfying

\ldeg Q_{1}(x)>\ldeg Q(x)\text{ and }\hdeg Q_{1}(x)\leq\hdeg Q(x).

(2.12)

Thus
\deg Q_{1}(x)<\deg Q(x)
.
A similar reduction to (
2.11
) then follows as

Q(x)=\sum_{s}b_{s}L^{*}p_{s}(x)+\tilde{Q}_{\ell}(x)

(2.13)

with
\deg\tilde{Q}_{\ell}(x)<\deg L
,
\ldeg\tilde{Q}_{\ell}(x)>\ldeg Q(x)
and
\hdeg\tilde{Q}_{\ell}(x)\leq\hdeg Q(x)
.

We call (
2.11
) or (
2.13
) a
(Laurent) polynomial reduction
with respect to
L
.
It is worth to notice that, when
\deg Q(x)>\deg L
and
L
is nongenerated, that is,
L
is neither HDD nor LDD,
we can choose
p_{0}(x)
and
b_{0}
such that both (
2.10
) and (
2.12
) hold.
This accelerates the reduction process.
In Section 3, we will present a natural scene for the application of this acceleration.

2.2
Applications to
q
-central-Delannoy numbers

In this subsection, we take the
q
-central-Delannoy number as an example to illustrate how the polynomial reduction can be utilized to derive arithmetic properties of combinatorial sequences.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
q
-integer
[n]
is defined by

[n]=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}.

Clearly,
\displaystyle\lim_{q\to 1}[n]=n
.
For a nonnegative integer
n
and a positive integer
k
, the
q
-binomial coefficient

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right]=\frac{\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}[n-j+1]}{\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}[j]}.

Moreover, we set
\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
0\end{matrix}\right]=1
and
\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right]=0
if
k<0
.

For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, the Delannoy numbers

D_{m,n}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\binom{m}{k}\binom{n+k}{m}

count lattice paths from
(0,0)
to
(m,n)

in which only east
(1,0)
, north
(0,1)
, and northeast
(1,1)
steps are allowed.
The
n
th
central Delannoy number

D_{n,n}
, denoted by
D_{n}
for short, is then given by

D_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+k}{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{2n-k}{n}.

The arithmetic properties of central Delannoy numbers have been extensively studied.
Let
\varepsilon=\pm 1
and
n
a positive integer.
Sun
[
21
]
proved that

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1)\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

When
p>3
is a prime, Sun further derived that

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)(-1)^{k}D_{k}\equiv p-\frac{7}{12}p^{4}B_{p-3}\pmod{p^{5}}

and

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(2k+1)D_{k}\equiv p+2p^{2}q_{p}(2)-p^{3}q_{p}(2)^{2}\pmod{p^{4}},

where
B_{p}
are the Bernoulli numbers and
q_{p}(2)
denotes the Fermat quotient
(2^{p-1}-1)/p
.
Later Guo and Zeng
[
14
]
showed that

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1)^{2v+1}\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}\equiv 0\pmod{n}

(2.14)

for any
v\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

In the following, we will recall
q
-central-Delannoy numbers and apply the polynomial reduction to obtain their arithmetic properties.
In particular, we will obtain
q
-congruences which reduce to (
2.14
) by taking
q\to 1
.

In 2014, Pan
[
20
]
defined the generalized
q
-Apéry polynomial

A_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x;q)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\alpha\left(\binom{k}{2}-kn\right)}\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right]^{\alpha}\left[\begin{matrix}n+k\\
k\end{matrix}\right]^{\alpha}x^{k}

and then proved

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-k}[2k+1]A_{k}^{(\alpha)}(x;q)^{m}\equiv 0\pmod{[n]},

(2.15)

which confirmed a conjecture of Guo and Zeng
[
13
]
by taking
q=1
.
One can see

D_{n}(q):=A_{n}^{(1)}(1;q)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\binom{k}{2}-kn}\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}n+k\\
k\end{matrix}\right]

(2.16)

is a
q
-analogue of the
n
th central Delannoy number.

Let
\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}
, by
q
-Zeilberger algorithm, we find that

\displaystyle L=

\displaystyle q^{-k}(1+q^{k+1})[k+2]\sigma^{2}+\varepsilon(q+q^{k+2}+q^{2k+4}+3q^{k+3})[-2k-3]\sigma

\displaystyle+q^{-k}(1+q^{k+2})[k+1]\in\ann\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}(q).

(2.17)

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
L
be given by (
2.2
)
and
n
a positive integer.
For any Laurent polynomial
p(x)\in{\mathbb{K}}(q)[x,x^{-1}]
and
n\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(p([k]))\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{n}[n]q^{1-n}\left((1+q^{n+1})p([n-1])\varepsilon D_{n-1}(q)-q(1+q^{n-1})p([n-2])D_{n}(q)\right).

(2.18)

Proof.
By equality (
2.6
) and the easily checked fact that

u_{0}(1)D_{0}(q)+u_{1}(1)\varepsilon D_{1}(q)=0
,
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(p([k]))\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}(q)=-\left(u_{0}(q^{n})\varepsilon^{n}D_{n}(q)+u_{1}(q^{n})\varepsilon^{n+1}D_{n+1}(q)\right),

(2.19)

where

u_{0}(q^{n})=q^{2-n}(1+q^{n-1})[n]p([n-2])-\varepsilon q^{-2n}(1+q^{n}+3q^{n+1}+q^{2n+1})[2n+1]p([n-1])
and

u_{1}(q^{n})=q^{1-n}(1+q^{n})[n+1]p([n-1]).

As
L\in\ann\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}(q)
, one has

\displaystyle-q^{1-n}(1+q^{n})[n+1]\varepsilon^{n+1}D_{n+1}(q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q^{2}+q^{1-n})[n]\varepsilon^{n-1}D_{n-1}(q)+q(1+q^{n}+q^{2n+1}+3q^{n+1})[-2n-1]\varepsilon^{n+1}D_{n}(q)

(2.20)

for any
n\geq 1
.
Substituting (
2.2
) into (
2.19
) derives (
2.6
).

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
L
be given as in (
2.2
).
For any
p(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[q,q^{-1}][x,x^{-1}]
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}L^{\ast}(p([k]))\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}(q)\equiv 0\pmod{[n]}.

Taking different
p(x)
in Corollary
2.7
leads to different
q
-congruences.
This provides a mechanical way to generate new
q
-congruences.
In the following, we show that even when
p(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[q,q^{-1}][x]
, Theorem
2.6
can still give
q
-identities which are
q
-analogues of known congruences.

For
\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}
, let

\tilde{L}=(k+2)\sigma^{2}-3\varepsilon(2k+3)\sigma+(k+1).

It can be checked that
\tilde{L}(\varepsilon^{k}D_{k})=0
.
The adjoint operator
\tilde{L}^{\ast}
is given by

\tilde{L}^{\ast}=k\sigma^{-2}-3\varepsilon(2k+1)\sigma^{-1}+(k+1).

In
[
25
]
, the first and the third authors proved that

\tilde{L}^{\ast}(x_{s}(k))=(1-3\varepsilon)(2k+1)^{s+1}+2\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}e_{j}^{(s)}(2k+1)^{s+1-2j},

(2.21)

where
x_{s}(k)=(2k+3)^{s}
and

e_{j}^{(s)}=\binom{s}{2j}2^{2j-1}+\binom{s}{2j-1}2^{2j-2}\in\mathbb{Z}

for
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
j=1,2,\dotsc,\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor
.
Taking
s=2v
in (
2.21
) shows

(2k+1)^{2v+1}=\frac{1}{1-3\varepsilon}\tilde{L}^{\ast}(x_{2v}(k))+\frac{2}{3\varepsilon-1}\sum_{j=1}^{v}e_{j}^{(2v)}(2k+1)^{2(v-j)+1}.

Then by induction one can see for each
v\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2k+1)^{2v+1}=\tilde{L}^{\ast}\left(p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(k)\right)

for a polynomial
p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(k)
in
k
satisfying

p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(k)=\frac{1}{1-3\varepsilon}x_{2v}(k)+\frac{2}{3\varepsilon-1}\sum_{j=1}^{v}e_{j}^{(2v)}p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v-j}(k).

Clearly
p^{(\varepsilon)}_{0}(k)=\frac{1}{1-3\varepsilon}
.
When
v\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
,
p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(k)
can be determined recursively.
By definitions of
x_{s}(k)
and
e_{j}^{(s)}
, we know

p^{(1)}_{v}(k)=2\sum_{j=1}^{2v}c^{(v)}_{j}k^{j}+\frac{c^{(v)}_{0}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad p^{(-1)}_{v}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{2v}t^{(v)}_{j}k^{j}+\frac{t^{(v)}_{0}}{4},

where
c^{(v)}_{j},\ t^{(v)}_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for
0\leq j\leq 2v
.

Example 2.8
.

Equality (
2.6
) reduces to Guo and Zeng’s congruence (
2.14
) when
p([k])=\frac{1}{2}p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}([k])
and
q\to 1
.

Proof.
Taking
p([k])=\frac{1}{2}p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}([k])
and
q\to 1
in (
2.6
),
the left hand side becomes

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\tilde{L}^{\ast}(p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(k))\varepsilon^{k}D_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1)^{2v+1}\varepsilon^{k}D_{k},

and the right hand side becomes

\varepsilon^{n}n\left(\varepsilon p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(n-1)D_{n-1}-p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(n-2)D_{n}\right).

Then by the definition of
p^{(\varepsilon)}_{v}(k)
, we get

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1)^{2v+1}D_{k}\equiv\frac{c^{(v)}_{0}}{2}n\bigl(D_{n-1}-D_{n}\bigr)\equiv 0\pmod{n}

and

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(2k+1)^{2v+1}(-1)^{k}D_{k}\equiv\frac{t^{(v)}_{0}}{4}(-1)^{n+1}n\bigl(D_{n-1}+D_{n}\bigr)\equiv 0\pmod{n}

since
2\mid(D_{n}-D_{n-1})
and
4\mid(D_{n}+D_{n-1})
.

3
q
-Power-partibility and applications

Power-partible reduction was initiated by Hou, Mu and Zeilberger
[
18
]
in 2021 for hypergeometric terms.
This was later extended by Wang and Zhong
[
24
,
25
]
to holonomic sequences and applied in mechanical discovery and proof of arithmetic properties of combinatorial sequences like Apéry numbers and Delannoy numbers.

In this section, we introduce a
q
-power-partible reduction for
q
-holonomic sequences with annihilators satisfying a symmetry property.
For these
q
-holonomic sequences, we can not only apply the reduction introduced in Section 2 to Laurent polynomials to eliminate simultaneouly the highest and lowest terms, but also characterize the structure of the Laurent polynomial after reduction.

3.1
q
-Power-partible reduction for
q
-holonomic sequences

In the following, we assume that
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{K}}(q^{\frac{1}{n}})
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
.
Define
[\gamma]=\frac{1-q^{\gamma}}{1-q}
if
q^{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{F}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}([k])\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}[q^{k},q^{-k}][\sigma]\in\ann F_{k}(q)
with
a_{0}([k])a_{J}([k])\neq 0
.
If there exist an
\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}
and a
q^{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{F}}
such that

a_{i}([\gamma+k])=\varepsilon\cdot a_{J-i}([\gamma-k-J]),\quad\forall k\in{\mathbb{Z}},i=0,1,\dots,J.

(3.1)

Then
L
or
F_{k}(q)
is called
q
-power-partible with respect to
(\gamma,\varepsilon)
.

Remark 1
.

One only need to check equality (
3.1
) for
i=0,1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{J}{2}\right\rfloor
.
Since for

\left\lfloor\frac{J}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq i\leq J
,
we have
0\leq J-i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{J}{2}\right\rfloor
and thus

a_{J-i}([\gamma+k])=\varepsilon\cdot a_{i}([\gamma-k-J]).

Replacing
k
with
-k-J
in the above equality yields that equality (
3.1
) also holds for
\left\lfloor\frac{J}{2}\right\rfloor+1\leq i\leq J
.

By the symmetry property (
3.1
) in the definition of
q
-power-partibility, it is direct to obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
L
is
q
-power-partible, we have
\hdeg L=-\ldeg L
.

Next, we will show that when
L
is
q
-power-partible, one can find Laurrent polynomials
u([k])
such that
L^{\ast}(u([k]))
satisfy certain symmetry property.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that
L=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}([k])\sigma^{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}[q^{k},q^{-k}][\sigma]
is
q
-power-partible with respect to
(\gamma,\varepsilon)

and for some
\tilde{\varepsilon}\in\{1,-1\}
,
u([k])\in{\mathbb{F}}[q^{k},q^{-k}]
satisfies

u([\gamma+k])=\tilde{\varepsilon}\cdot u([\gamma-k-J]).

(3.2)

Then
v([k])=L^{\ast}(u([k]))
satisfies

v([\gamma+k])=c\cdot v([\gamma-k]),

where
c=\varepsilon\tilde{\varepsilon}\in\{1,-1\}
.

Proof.
Since
L
is
q
-power-partible with respect to
(\gamma,\varepsilon)
, we have

a_{i}([\gamma+k-i])=\varepsilon\cdot a_{J-i}([\gamma-k+i-J]),\quad 0\leq i\leq J.

(3.3)

Clearly
v([k])=L^{\ast}(u([k]))=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}([k-i])u([k-i])
.
Then equalities (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) lead to

\displaystyle v([\gamma+k])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}([\gamma+k-i])u([\gamma+k-i])

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{J}\varepsilon\tilde{\varepsilon}\cdot a_{J-i}([\gamma-k+i-J])u([\gamma-k+i-J])

\displaystyle=c\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{J-i}([\gamma-k-(J-i)])u([\gamma-k-(J-i)])

\displaystyle=c\sum_{i=0}^{J}a_{i}([\gamma-k-i])u([\gamma-k-i])

\displaystyle=c\cdot v([\gamma-k]).

Given
q^{\gamma},q^{\delta}\in{\mathbb{F}}
and
c\in\{1,-1\}
,
denote

S_{\gamma,\delta}^{\langle c\rangle}=\{Q([k])\in{\mathbb{F}}[q^{k},q^{-k}]\mid Q([\gamma+k])=c\cdot Q([\delta-k])\}.

The set
S_{\gamma,\delta}^{\langle c\rangle}
is clearly a linear space over
{\mathbb{F}}
and will be written as
S_{\gamma}^{\langle c\rangle}
for short when
\gamma=\delta
.

An explicit characterization of Laurent polynomials in
S_{\gamma,\delta}^{\langle c\rangle}
is given as follows.

Proposition 3.4
.

For a Laurent polynomial
Q([k])\in{\mathbb{F}}[q^{k},q^{-k}]
,

Q([k])\in S_{\gamma,\delta}^{\langle c\rangle}
if and only if it can be written as

Q([k])=\sum_{i=-I}^{I}e_{i}q^{ki}

for some
I\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
e_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}
and

e_{i}=c\cdot q^{-(\gamma+\delta)i}e_{-i}
,
0\leq i\leq I
.

Proof.
As
Q([k])
is a Laurent polynomial in
q^{k}
, one can write it in the form

Q([k])=\sum_{i=-I_{1}}^{I_{2}}e_{i}q^{ki},\quad I_{1},I_{2}\in\mathbb{N},e_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}},

with
e_{-I_{1}}e_{I_{2}}\neq 0
.
Then it can be seen that

Q([\gamma+k])=\sum_{i=-I_{1}}^{I_{2}}e_{i}q^{\gamma i}q^{ki}

and

Q([\delta-k])=\sum_{i=-I_{1}}^{I_{2}}e_{i}q^{\delta i}q^{-ki}=\sum_{i=-I_{2}}^{I_{1}}e_{-i}q^{-\delta i}q^{ki}.

Then
Q([\gamma+k])=c\cdot Q([\delta-k])
forces
I_{1}=I_{2}=I
for some
I\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

e_{i}q^{\gamma i}=c\cdot e_{-i}q^{-\delta i},\quad-I\leq i\leq I,

and vice versa.

Remark 2
.

If
c=-1
, then
e_{0}(q)=-e_{0}(q)
leads to
e_{0}(q)=0
.

Now we are able to illustrate the process of
q
-power-partible reduction.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose that
Q([k])\in S_{\gamma}^{\langle c\rangle}
and
L\in{\mathbb{F}}[q^{k},q^{-k}][\sigma]
is nondegenerated with order
J
and
q
-power-partible with respect to
(\gamma,\varepsilon)
.
Then there exist
p([k])\in S_{\gamma,\gamma-J}^{\langle c\varepsilon\rangle}
and
\tilde{Q}([k])\in S_{\gamma}^{\langle c\rangle}
with
\deg\tilde{Q}(x)\leq\deg L
such that

Q([k])=L^{\ast}(p([k]))+\tilde{Q}([k]).

(3.4)

When
c\varepsilon=1
, we can further guarantee that
\deg\tilde{Q}(x)<\deg L
.

Proof.
Suppose now
\deg Q(x)>\deg L
.
By Proposition
3.2
and Proposition
3.4
, we can assume that

\hdeg L=-\ldeg L=d\quad\text{and}\quad\hdeg Q(x)=-\ldeg Q(x)=I

for some
d,I\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
I>d
.
Let

p_{0}([k])=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }c\varepsilon=1\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

and

p_{i}([k])=q^{-ki}+c\varepsilon\cdot q^{(J-2\gamma)i}q^{ki},\ i\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}.

Proposition
3.4
shows that
p_{i}([\gamma+k])=c\varepsilon\cdot p_{i}([\gamma-k-J])
, that is
p_{i}([k])\in S_{\gamma,\gamma-J}^{\langle c\varepsilon\rangle}
for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Thus
v_{i}([k])=L^{\ast}(p_{i}([k]))\in S_{\gamma}^{\langle c\rangle}
by Theorem
3.3
and

\hdeg v_{i}(x)=d+i,\quad\ldeg v_{i}(x)=-d-i

by Theorem
2.5
.
Then there exists
b_{I-d}\in{\mathbb{F}}
so that

Q_{0}([k])=Q([k])-b_{I-d}v_{I-d}([k])\in S_{\gamma}^{\langle c\rangle}

and

I_{0}=\hdeg Q_{0}(x)=-\ldeg Q_{0}(x)<I
.

We first consider the case when
c\varepsilon=-1
.
If
I_{0}\leq d
, we are done with the decomposition

Q([k])=L^{\ast}(b_{I-d}p_{I-d}([k]))+Q_{0}([k])
.
Otherwise, we continue the above process to
Q_{0}([k])
to find
b_{I_{0}-d}\in{\mathbb{F}}
so that

Q_{1}([k])=Q_{0}([k])-b_{I_{0}-d}v_{I_{0}-d}([k])\in S_{\gamma}^{\langle c\rangle}

and

I_{1}=\hdeg Q_{1}(x)=-\ldeg Q_{1}(x)<I_{0}
.
If
I_{1}\leq d
, we are done with

Q([k])=L^{\ast}(p([k]))+Q_{1}([k])
, where

p([k])=b_{I-d}p_{I-d}([k])+b_{I_{0}-d}p_{I_{0}-d}([k])
.
Otherwise, we continue the above process to
Q_{1}([k])
.
After finite steps, we find a decomposition satisfying
(
3.4
).

In the case of
c\varepsilon=1
, we can reduce one step further by the Laurent polynomial
v_{0}([k])=L^{\ast}(p_{0}([k]))
of degree
d
to get a
\tilde{Q}([k])
with
\deg\tilde{Q}(x)<\deg L
.
This completes the proof.

3.2
Applications

Recall that Pan’s
q
-congruence (
2.15
) implies that

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^{n-1-k}[2k+1]D_{k}(q)\equiv 0\pmod{[n]},

(3.5)

where
D_{k}(q)
are the
q
-central Delannoy numbers given by (
2.16
).
It is straightforward to check that the annihilator
L
of
D_{k}(q)
given by (
2.2
) with
\varepsilon=1
is not HDD and
q
-power-partible with respect to
(-\frac{1}{2},-1)
.

For any
\gamma,b\in{\mathbb{Q}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the Laurent polynomial

f_{m,b}^{(\gamma)}([k])=q^{m(-k+b)}[2k-2\gamma]^{m}=([k+b-2\gamma]-[-k+b])^{m}.

(3.6)

Note that

f_{m,b}^{(\gamma)}([k])\in S_{\gamma}^{\langle(-1)^{m}\rangle}
and
q^{(n-1-k)(2v+1)}[2k+1]^{2v+1}=f_{2v+1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])
.
This motivates the discovery of the following generalization of (
3.5
).

Theorem 3.6
.

For any
v\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}q^{(n-1-k)(2v+1)}[2k+1]^{2v+1}D_{k}(q)\equiv 0\pmod{[n]}.

For the proof of Theorem
3.6
, we need the following two lemmas.
The first one gives an explicit expansion of
f_{m,b}^{(\gamma)}([k])
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f_{m,b}^{(\gamma)}([k])
be defined as in (
3.6
).
Then

f_{m,b}^{(\gamma)}([k])=\frac{q^{mb}}{(1-q)^{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=-m\\
2\mid(m+i)\end{subarray}}^{m}\binom{m}{\frac{m+i}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{m+i}{2}}q^{-\gamma(m+i)}\ q^{ki}.

Proof.
By definition, one can see

\displaystyle f_{m,b}^{(\gamma)}([k])

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{m(-k+b)}}{(1-q)^{m}}(1-q^{2k-2\gamma})^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{m(-k+b)}}{(1-q)^{m}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(-1)^{j}q^{2j(k-\gamma)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{mb}}{(1-q)^{m}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(-1)^{j}q^{-2\gamma j}\,q^{k(2j-m)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{mb}}{(1-q)^{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=-m\\
2\mid(m+i)\end{subarray}}^{m}\binom{m}{\frac{m+i}{2}}(-1)^{\frac{m+i}{2}}q^{-\gamma(m+i)}\ q^{ki}

by taking
i=2j-m
in the final step.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
v,n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and
L
be given by (
2.2
) with
\varepsilon=1
.
Then there exist
c(q),c_{i}(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}]
such that

f_{2v+1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])=\sum_{i=0}^{2v-1}\frac{c_{i}(q)q^{(2v+1)n}}{(1-q)^{2v}}L^{\ast}(p_{i}([k]))+\frac{c(q)q^{2vn}}{(1-q)^{2v}}f_{1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k]),

(3.7)

where
p_{i}([k])=q^{-ki}+q^{(k+3)i}
for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and
p_{0}([k])=1
.

Proof.
Note that

f_{2v+1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])=q^{(n-1-k)(2v+1)}[2k+1]^{2v+1}\in S_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
and
\hdeg{L}=-\ldeg{L}=2
.
Next, we go through the
q
-power partible reduction given in
the proof of Theorem
3.5
.
By the definition of
p_{i}([k])
, one can see
\tilde{p}_{i}([k])=L^{\ast}(p_{i}([k]))\in S_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\langle-1\rangle}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Moreover, since

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{i}([k])=

\displaystyle q^{-k}(1+q^{k+2})[k+1]p_{i}([k])+q^{-k+2}(1+q^{k-1})[k]p_{i}([k-2])

\displaystyle+(q+q^{k+1}+q^{2k+2}+3q^{k+2})[-2k-1]p_{i}([k-1]).

We have
(1-q)\tilde{p}_{i}([k])\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}][q^{k},q^{-k}]
,

\hdeg\tilde{p}_{i}(x)=-\ldeg\tilde{p}_{i}(x)=i+2
and

[q^{-k(i+2)}]\left((1-q)\tilde{p}_{i}([k])\right)=-q^{i}.

Lemma
3.7
shows that
q^{-(2v+1)n}(1-q)^{2v+1}f_{2v+1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}][q^{k},q^{-k}]
.
So there exists
c_{2v-1}(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}]
such that

Q_{0}([k])=q^{-(2v+1)n}(1-q)^{2v+1}f_{2v+1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])-c_{2v-1}(q)(1-q)\tilde{p}_{2v-1}([k])

satisfies

Q_{0}([k])\in S_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\langle-1\rangle}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}][q^{k},q^{-k}]

and

\hdeg{Q_{0}}(x)=-\ldeg{Q_{0}(x)}\leq 2v.

Then there exists
c_{2v-2}(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}]
such that

Q_{1}([k])=Q_{0}([k])-c_{2v-2}(q)(1-q)\tilde{p}_{2v-2}([k])\in S_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\langle-1\rangle}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}][q^{k},q^{-k}]

with
\hdeg{Q_{1}}(x)=-\ldeg{Q_{1}}(x)\leq 2v-1
.
Continuing the above process, we find

q^{-(2v+1)n}(1-q)^{2v+1}f_{2v+1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])=\sum_{i=0}^{2v-1}c_{i}(q)(1-q)L^{\ast}(p_{i}([k]))+\tilde{Q}([k])

(3.8)

for some
c_{i}(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}]

and
\tilde{Q}([k])\in S_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\langle-1\rangle}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}][q^{-k},q^{k}]

satisfying
\hdeg\tilde{Q}(x)=-\ldeg\tilde{Q}(x)<2
.
By Proposition
3.4
,

\tilde{Q}([k])=\tilde{c}(q)(q^{-k}-q^{k+1})=\tilde{c}(q)(1-q)q^{1-n}f_{1,n-1}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}([k])

(3.9)

for some
c(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}]
.
Substituting (
3.9
) into (
3.8
) and letting
c(q)=\tilde{c}(q)q
completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Multiplying both sides of (
3.7
) with
D_{k}(q)
and then summing over
k
from

to
n-1
.
The conclusion comes directly by Corollary
2.7

).
