The root exudates of wild tomato compared to a modern variety maintain elevated soluble soil phosphorus by interacting with rhizosphere microbiota
Authors: Mary Dixon, Antisar Afkairin, Daniel Manter, Jane Godfrey, Alison Hamm, Nathalie Munoz, Kent Bloodsworth, Vimal Kumar Balasubramanian, Cassidy Buchanan, James A Ippolito, Meagan Burnet, Jorge Vivanco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWild plants are periodically exposed to nutrient flushes, whereas modern cultivars are acclimated to regular nutrition from fertilizers. Phosphorus (P) fertilizers, however, convert into unavailable forms in soil. Here, we assessed plant growth, rhizosphere microbiome, and root exudation of a wild and modern tomato to determine how a wild crop relative responds to a flush of P fertilization. We found that the modern tomato relatively lacked P-mineralizers and grew quickly, depleting soil P. Conversely, wild tomato continued growth and promoted an array of beneficial soil bacteria, resulting in higher bioavailable soil P across development. This microbial strategy was driven by its root exudates, which were subsequently found to be enriched in trehalose and glycerol compounds. In vitro testing showed that these compounds promoted microbial P solubilization. Finally, we grew modern tomato in P-fertilized soils following a previous planting of either wild or modern tomato. Modern tomato grown in wild tomato-conditioned soils increased biomass compared to those grown in modern tomato-conditioned soils. The change may be associated with residual soil P as the wild-induced microbial community changes diminished. Introduction

Phosphorus (P) is an essential but often limiting nutrient for plants, particularly in terrestrial and non-agricultural soils1–5. Phosphorus becomes limited in soil environments through soil retention reactions, including precipitation, immobilization, and sorption6,7. Plants thus employ strategies (e.g., organic acid exudation) to acquire bound P, and these responses differ between wild and cultivated plants8. Cultivated plants tend to emphasize physical responses such as topsoil foraging, whereas wild crop ancestors showcase ecological responses such as enhanced microbial associations9.

To survive in conditions of low P, low-nutrient-adapted undomesticated plants tend to have a reduced nutrient requirement and prioritize processes unrelated to primary growth (e.g., defense)10. For example, native grasses (e.g., Chionochloa crassiuscula, Hordeum leporinum) have similar phosphate uptake capacities but slower growth rates compared to cultivated species, causing a reduction in the annual P requirement11. While the responses of wild crops to a P deficit are well-established, it is less clear how wild crop relatives physiologically respond to a flush of high P. This trend is more established for modern crops, as they are bred in optimal conditions: sufficient water and nutrients12. It is possible that thousands of years of breeding, development, and agricultural events such as the Green Revolution have allowed domesticated crops to have high-nutrient resource-acquisitive strategies9,13. More recently, fertigation has been implemented in many crop production systems; therefore, modern crops are adjusted to growing in conditions of not just high, but frequent applications of nutrients14–16.

In natural ecosystems, wild and native plants are periodically exposed to temporal flushes of nutrients after a disturbance, such as organic deposits from leaf litter on forest floors10,17. However, nutrient requirement studies comparing wild and domesticated crops have been conducted under resource deficit conditions18–21. The mechanisms by which wild and low nutrient adapted plants respond to conditions of high P remain unclear and unexplored.

Until now, the response by wild plants, like wild relatives of tomato, to flushes of P has not been addressed. It is important to investigate mechanisms of P acquisition by wild crop relatives in order to identify means to improve P bioavailability in modern accessions. To elucidate mechanisms by which wild plant species respond to a flush of exogenous phosphate, we cultivated wild tomato in conditions of P fertilization and compared its responses to a modern cultivar. Our overarching aim was to determine if wild tomato showcases increased reserves of soil P relative to a modern counterpart and if the wild tomato could impart benefits to both itself and to future generations of plants.

Results

Divergent growth responses to phosphorus application between wild and modern tomato

Growth patterns differed for the wild variety and modern cultivar in P-fertilized soil (Fig. 1a) (Table S1). Early on, both groups markedly increased dry shoot biomass from the first to the second stage, and during this time, modern tomato shoots were larger (Fig. 1a). Shortly after, the modern representative slowed its rate of biomass accumulation (Fig. 1a). However, after starting off with lower biomass during the first two growth stages, the wild tomato caught up to modern tomato by the later harvest points. Roots of the modern tomato grew fast and accumulated their greatest biomass by the second harvest point (Fig. 1b). Unlike the growth pattern experienced by the modern tomato, the wild tomato roots continued growth, and by the final harvest stage, wild and modern root biomass were not different (Fig. 1b). Conversely, in unfertilized soil, modern tomato had greater shoot (Fig. 1a) and root (Fig. 1b) biomass than wild tomato at every harvest stage (Table S1).

Unlike biomass accumulation, where the wild tomato caught up to the modern tomato by the final harvest point in P-fertilized soil, wild tomato maintained a lower shoot P concentration across time compared to the modern counterpart regardless of P fertilization (Fig. 1c). There was no interactive effect for shoot P concentration, but there was a significant interactive effect for root P concentration (Table S1). The wild tomato stored more of its P in its root tissue, as its root P concentration increased with time and was larger than the modern representative for the final three harvest points (Fig. 1d). In unfertilized soils, these differences between the two domestication groups diminished, and the wild tomato was not different from the modern tomato at any tested harvest point for root P concentration (Fig. 1d) (Table S1).

Increased soil P levels in the wild over the modern tomato

We explored three pools of labile soil P that capture increasingly greater amounts of bioavailable P: water soluble P, Olsen P, and Mehlich-3 P. For water soluble P, there was a significant interactive effect between fertilization, domestication, and harvest stage (Table S2). For Olsen P, there was a significant interaction between domestication and harvest (Table S2), thus suggesting that regardless of fertilization, the tomato domestication groups show different Olsen P values dependent on time. The P-fertilized wild tomato showed greater water-soluble P (Fig. 2a), Olsen P (Fig. 2b), and Mehlich-3 P (Fig. 2c) concentration than modern tomato in the early vegetative stage. Further, there was no relative decline or increase in water soluble P (Fig. 2a) or Mehlich-3 P (Fig. 2c) values with time for P-fertilized wild tomato. In contrast, modern tomato depleted these soil P values relative to its initial concentration. This sharp drop in bioavailable P in modern tomato corresponds to its sharp increase in biomass.

Promotion of rhizosphere microbiota related to P by the wild tomato

The relative abundance of P mineralizing bacteria (i.e., include a pho gene identified from PICRUSt2-predicted data) showed a significant interactive effect between fertilization, domestication, and harvest stage (Table S3). There was a spike in relative abundance of PICRUSt2-predicted P mineralizers that occurred at the early vegetative stage for wild tomatoes planted in fertilized soil, and at this stage, P mineralizer relative abundance was greater in wild compared to modern tomato (Fig. 3a). For P-fertilized modern tomato, there was a drop in relative abundance between the first and second stages of harvest and then a plateau (Fig. 3a).

There were also notable differences in microbial biomass (measured as copies of the 16S gene per g of soil) (Fig. 3b). There was a significant interaction between domestication and harvest (Table S3). The wild tomato accession showed an increase of microbial biomass at the early flower and late flower stages relative to the modern tomato (Fig. 3b).

Bacterial fluctuations related to changes in P application and domestication

Bacterial abundance at each harvest stage for wild and domesticated tomato differed by fertilizer conditions. The P-fertilized modern tomato rhizosphere showed pronounced stabilization by the later stages of development (Fig. 4a). Conversely, wild tomato showed both greater fluctuation than modern at the same stages of development and an increase in bacterial fluctuation between the seedling and early vegetative stage for wild tomato (Fig. 4b). These bacteria that were enriched in the early vegetative stage possessed genes related to soil nutrient cycling, such as P mineralization (phoD) and siderophore production (entA). Although there were clear differences between wild and modern tomato when fertilizer was applied, there were minimum differences when fertilizer was omitted for both modern (Fig. 4c) and wild tomato soils (Fig. 4d).

Certain bacteria were promoted by P-fertilized wild tomato. There were 7, 31, 17, 87, and 17 bacteria that differed between fertilized wild and fertilized modern tomato in the seedling, early vegetative, late vegetative, early flower, and late flower stages (Table S4–S8), respectively. In exploring a separate comparison between fertilized wild and unfertilized wild soils, there were 85 bacteria that differed, with most being enriched in the fertilized soil (Table S9). We identified 31 P-fertilized wild tomato-promoted bacteria (i.e., enhanced in conditions of wild P-fertilized compared to wild unfertilized, and in conditions of wild P-fertilized compared to modern P-fertilized) (Table S10). These bacteria contain an abundance of redundant and beneficial PICRUSt2-identified functions, including P mineralization, P solubilization, and siderophore production (Table S10).

Wild tomato-derived exudates promote bacterial P solubilizing activity

To illuminate the mechanisms by which the modern and wild tomato representatives interact with the soil to acquire P, we carried out a root exudate study. There were clear differences in the composition of root exudates between the tested accessions of wild and modern tomato. The modern tomato representative principally promoted fatty acids and organic acids (e.g., myristic acid, lauric acid, malic acid, 3-hydroxybutyric acid), whereas wild tomato primarily exuded sugar and glycerol-related compounds (e.g., trehalose, 1-stearoylglycerol, glycerol monostearate) (Fig. 5) (Table S11).

Certain root exudates that were highly enriched in either the wild tomato (glycerol monostearate, 1-stearoylglycerol, 1-monopalmitin, trehalose) or the modern tomato (lauric acid, dodecanol) (Fig. 5, Table S11) were selected, and solutions of these individual metabolites were added to plates of NBRIP media with a P solubilizing bacterium (Azospirillum brasilense)22 to evaluate their effect on P solubilizing index (PSI) (calculated as the diameter of the halo divided by the diameter of the colony). Application of wild tomato-derived exudates promoted the PSI of A. brasilense, particularly glycerol monostearate and 1-stearoylglycerol (Fig. 6a, Fig. 6b). Trehalose increased PSI by A. brasilense in tricalcium phosphate (TCP) media (Fig. 6b), but not in phytin media (Fig. 6a) relative to the control. The tested modern tomato exudates resulted in neither solubilization nor colony growth (Fig. 6a, Fig. 6b). Regardless of exudate treatment, A. brasilense showed a greater P solubilization index when grown in phytin media (Fig. 6a) compared to TCP (Fig. 6b). These exudates were also tested alone without the supplementation of A. brasilense, and no compounds produced solubilization directly (data not shown).

Conditioning soils with fertilized wild tomato slightly alters modern tomato growth

In the soil conditioning trial, we found a significant interaction effect between the soil conditioning treatment and the pattern of fertilization for shoot and root biomass (Table S12). However, it is important to note that conditioning soils with wild tomato produced little-to-no differences in biomass compared to the no plant control (Fig. 7a, b). Regardless of whether the second cycle received fertilization, initially fertilized wild-conditioned soils resulted in larger shoots than the initially fertilized modern-tomato conditioned soils (Fig. 7a). Fertilized wild tomato-conditioned soils also increased root biomass of modern tomato compared to fertilized modern-tomato conditioned soils (Fig. 7b). Fertilized modern-conditioned soils showed the greatest root P concentration of the soil conditioning treatments (Fig. 7c).

Modern tomato has a rhizosphere microbiome resilient to change

The wild-conditioned soils had a greater relative abundance of P mineralizers compared to the modern-conditioned soils, but it was no different from the no plant control (Fig. 7d, Table S12). Under low fertility (i.e., when soils were not fertilized in the first planting cycle), the wild conditioned soils did not promote P mineralizing bacterial growth. Further, Olsen P was unaffected by the soil conditioning treatment (Fig. 7e).

A comparison of the highly abundant bacteria of modern tomato grown the wild-conditioned soils (soil conditioning trial) compared to the species promoted by fertilized wild tomato (time course trial) showed some consistency in the abundant bacterial species (Fig. S1, Tables S4–S8). For example, Trichocoleus desertorum and Geitlerinema sp. PCC 7407 were among the top 10 species in wild-conditioned but not the modern-conditioned soils of the soil conditioning trial (Fig. S1), and they were significantly enriched in the P-fertilized wild tomato soils during the time course trial. These species are putatively plant beneficial; T. desertorum and G. sp. PCC 7407 both contain genes associated with C decomposition and N decomposition.

Discussion

Wild plants—more so than their domesticated counterparts—rely on microbial associations to grow in a P deficit18,23,24. To grow in conditions of limited nutrition, wild crop relatives tend to demonstrate insensitivity to low P20,25 and allocate resources for survival, not production10. However, natural soils also have heterogeneously distributed bioavailable phosphate through events such as cadaver decomposition26, faunal fecal deposits27, and treefall occurrences28. In our current study, we observed how a wild tomato representative responded to a similar flush of exogenous nutrition: by interacting with its associated soil microbiota, likely via its root exudates, to promote soil P availability and result in enhanced P acquisition and plant development.

The root exudates of our tested accession of wild tomato increased the P solubilization index of a rhizosphere bacterium, and these chemical dialogues may be promoting beneficial bacterial associations. Our tested modern tomato rapidly accumulated root tissue biomass, thereby exploiting the fertilizer-P reserves. The concentrations of water-soluble, Olsen, and Mehlich-3 P decreased with time for P-fertilized modern tomato. While water soluble P measures only dissolved reactive phosphate29, Olsen P tends to primarily capture orthophosphate, and Mechlich-3 may extract other less soluble forms of P (e.g., orthophosphate mono-/di-esters)30,31. Thus, the modern tomato utilizes and depletes P across a range of labile soil pools to levels that were no different from the unfertilized control.

The divergent P acquisition strategies by the representative wild and modern tomato (i.e., microbially facilitated for the wild variety and root scavenging for the modern variety) are most apparent at the early vegetative harvest stage. It has been shown in tomato that there are dynamic changes occurring during the first 4 weeks of development (equivalent to between the seedling and early vegetative stage in this current study), but after 4 weeks, the rhizosphere stabilizes32. Thus, the wild and modern tomato representatives may be shifting their microbial communities near the early vegetative stage, resulting in distinct communities and P recovery strategies. In both wild tomato and domesticated tomato, growth to this approximate stage was sufficient to produce differences in the rhizosphere bacteriome33. Here, wild tomato promoted plant beneficial P mineralizing bacteria that allowed for elevated levels of P in soils. Other studies that found that wild tomato harbors P mineralizing bacteria to a greater extent than modern tomato18,20. Therefore, the finding of consistently increased soil P in the wild tomato variety may be a result of its ability to culture beneficial microbes. However, it is possible that the ability of wild tomato to associate rhizosphere microbes may vary with species. Although S. pennellii (LA0716) is a useful accession and commonly used for introgressions and experiments on abiotic stress34, there are 13 species of wild tomato35, and it has been shown that there is inter-specific variation in rhizosphere P mineralizers18. Our conclusions that wild and modern tomato diverge in their P acquisition are limited by the two accessions included in this study, and future research could include more wild tomato species and modern varieties to increase robustness.

Through their organic acid and phosphatase activity, P cycling bacteria increase soil P status9,36,37. At the point when PICRUSt2-predicted P mineralizing bacteria increased in the P-fertilized wild tomato soils, we observed a concomitant increase in water-soluble, Olsen, and Mehlich-3 P in the P-fertilized wild compared to the P-fertilized modern tomato soils. Across a variety of soil types, application of P-solubilizing bacteria increased the concentration of Olsen P38,39. Thus, the wild tomato variety may have been capable of enhancing growth of P-cycling organisms in the soil, and this may have resulted in the increased stable reserves of plant-available soil solution phosphate. The concentration of water-soluble, Olsen, and Mehlich-3 P in P-fertilized wild tomato soils did not significantly change with time. The available P is presumably preserved by wild tomato for uptake and biomass accumulation in the later plant growth stages. Conversely, P mineralizing bacteria did not proliferate in soils planted with modern tomato, and available soil P declined.

These changes in bioavailable P may also have been driven by microbial abundance. Soil microbes immobilize P by incorporating it into their biomass40. The microbial biomass in the rhizosphere of our tested accession of wild tomato not only was greater than the modern accession, but it also increased with time. Thus, greater bacterial abundance may have led to an increase in the extractable forms of P as immobilization and mineralization occur in soils.

The wild tomato accession may be increasing soil microbial biomass with time through root exudate activity, as our in vitro testing revealed trehalose (a sugar compound) to be promoted by the wild accession. Sugars have been hypothesized to proliferate soil microbial communities when produced as an exudate32,41. Sugars also tend to be exuded by roots earlier in development42. This trend supports our result in which P-fertilized wild tomato showcased great fluctuations in the rhizosphere microbiome at the early vegetative stage. Trehalose, preferentially exuded by wild tomato, is also an important root exudate that promotes plant growth. In a study with maize, for example, exogenous application of trehalose increased plant growth and tolerance to low P43. The wild tomato also exuded an glycerol compounds, which increased the P solubilizing activity of A. brasilense, a P solubilizing bacterium commonly used as an inoculant for its plant growth promoting properties22. As A. brasilense was present across modern and wild tomato samples in the first trial of our study, the ability of wild tomato exudates to increase the P solubilization index supports the conclusion wild tomato uses microbial associations to acquire P. The Azospirillum genus tends to be slower growing than other rhizosphere genera, like Pseudomonas44. Therefore, there may be a strong indirect solubilizing capacity of our selected glycerol compounds. Glycerol has been shown to be a carbon source for microbes45, further supporting the conclusion that the exudates from our accession of wild tomato may allow for the proliferation of rhizobacteria. However, we pooled replicates for metabolomics analysis, and in future studies, more replicates could be included to increase statistical rigor.

Rather than being enriched in sugars and glycerol compounds, the modern tomato accession promoted exudation of organic acids. There is strong evidence in current literature that organic acids promote direct P solubilization46–49. The primary mechanism by which organic acids promote plant P uptake is by their acidity, so they solubilize precipitated compounds8. Therefore, our tested accession of modern tomato may be engaging in a direct physical strategy to recover soil P. However, the specific tested metabolites from modern tomato (lauric acid and dodecanol) did not increase the measured P solubilization index of A. brasilense, which may be explained by the antimicrobial properties of lauric acid and dodecanol50,51. Root exudates with antimicrobial properties can indicate pathogen suppressive strategies52. Thus, the difference in the bacterial suppression and bacterial promotion between the modern and wild tomato accessions suggests that they showcase different strategies for growth and development. We suggest that while the wild variety enhances intimate associations in the rhizosphere, the modern tomato cultivar, as illustrated through its production of antimicrobial compounds and organic acids, may not as intimately associate with its rhizosphere microbiota.

The results from this root exudate trial are from hydroponically-grown samples using a 3 days collection window. Because hydroponically-grown plants differ physiologically from soil-grown plants53, exudate application to soil conditions may differ from in vitro trials. In their recent comprehensive review of root exudate experiments, McLaughlin et al.54 found a range of collection windows: < 1 h to more than 3 days. While there is a possibility of root damage and leakage from being in still water, passive leakage occurs naturally as roots navigate soil environments55. Traditional hybrid collection methods (initial soil growth followed by hydroponics) tend to result in greater risk of root damage53. Furthermore, the presence of organic compounds and sugars suggests that these compounds originate from active transport of root exudates56,57. While small, inorganic compounds may be exuded via passive diffusion, sugars and organic acids are typically mediated by active transporters to reach the rhizosphere. Moreover, tomato can be grown in deep culture hydroponics58–60, thereby indicating root system integrity in liquid hydroponics. Regardless of potential root damage, the exudates examined in this study significantly differed by imposed treatments; any metabolites resulting from damaged roots would have been present across treatments and would therefore not be used in the root exudate plate assays. Because these compounds were significantly enriched by our imposed treatments, they are likely actively exuded and possibly to tied to stimuli, such as P deficiency.

Possibly as a result of its root exudate activity, the modern tomato was unable to maintain the populations of the wild-promoted bacteria. However, there was some consistency in the bacteria promoted by P-fertilized wild tomato and the bacteria in the wild-conditioned soils in the soil conditioning trial. In particular, two bacteria (T. desertorum and Geitlerinema sp. PCC 7407) were present in these treatments. Trichocoleus desertorum plays a role in the weathering of calcareous stone material61, a process that may be promoting soil P cycling in our tested soils, which are calcareous. Geitlerinema sp. PCC 7407 has also been found to be enriched in the rhizosphere of wild tomato compared to heirloom or modern tomato18. Moreover, both T. desertorum and G. sp. PCC 7407 are members of Cyanobacteria. In a recent study comparing the effects of Cyanobacteria application to different forms of organic fertilizers, researchers found that Cyanobacteria inoculation impacted community composition and increased microbial biomass62. Thus, the wild tomato in this study may be influencing the composition of soil microbiota in the next cycle of planting, and these bacteria may promote soil nutrient cycling. However, these results from the soil conditioning trial are from a single time point. It has been shown that, across plant species, the rhizosphere microbiome shifts with season, year, and stage of development63,64. This shift in the microbiome as a result of time has also been found for tomato32. Therefore, it is possible that we would see differences in the microbiome if samples were taken at other time points in addition to 5 weeks after planting.

He soil was initially autoclave-sterilized in the soil conditioning trial, a process that can alter soil properties, such as nutrition65. Autoclave sterilization substantially reduces soil bacterial abundance, but not eliminate it totally, unless autoclaving is executed more than once66. By reducing live microbial populations and increasing dead microbial biomass, the soil microbiome is more receptive to the host plant root exudates. This phenomenon has been shown in tomato and corn, in which soils were autoclave-sterilized and subsequently, the rhizosphere microbiome was reshaped65,67,68. Thus, bacteria identified in the soil conditioning trial may have originated from microbial species that were not killed off from the autoclave sterilization procedure, or may have been introduced environmentally through vertical transfer from the seedling69, irrigation water70, and air71. Further, because non-sterilized soil has less host selection power than sterilized soil, it is possible that these results observed from the soil conditioning trial may be less apparent in field conditions.

Conclusion

In times of high fertility, wild tomato maintains bioavailable soil P through its activity of the rhizosphere microbiome. In contrast, modern crop relatives, like the modern tomato in this study, express resource acquisitive strategies and take up nutrients rapidly. Domesticated varieties are adapted to grow in highly fertile conditions and rely on quick uptake of nutrients when they are available. Here, we illustrate how P from applied fertilizer is kept bioavailable in the soil for longer time periods via the activity of root exudates and soil microbes. This finding of divergent strategies in wild and modern tomato is valuable because P from fertilizer is easily lost from soil solution from retention reactions72. Thus, results from this study could be used in future research to strategically increase P bioavailability and reduce fertilizer loss. Future directions for research may include applications of these identified root exudates to soils to identify their P solubilization capacity in soil environment and can be validated though soil P fractionation.

Materials and methods

Three trials occurred in parallel: (1) a time course study, (2) a root exudates study, and (3) a soil conditioning study with successive planting cycles.

Trial 1: Time course study

Plant growth conditions

Two varieties of tomato were selected: one representative wild tomato (Solanum pennellii, accession no. LA0716) (henceforth “wild”) and one representative domesticated tomato (S. lycopersicum ‘Quali T 27’) (henceforth “modern”). The ‘Quali T 27’ variety is a commonly grown cultivar in the San Juaquin Valley in California, and this variety was shown to perform similar in plant growth and bacterial associations to other cultivated varieties when exposed to conditions of P deficiency18. Solanum pennellii accession LA0716 is one of the wild ancestors of domesticated tomato that is used for introgression to increase stress tolerance73, the growth and microbiome of this tomato has been shown to be responsive to changes in external P status18,25. These accessions were selected for these experiments because of their representative growth (‘Quali T 27’) and response to P status (LA0716). Wild tomato seeds were obtained from the Tomato Genetics Resource Center at the University of California Davis, and modern tomato seeds were donated by Syngenta (Delaware, United States). Seeds from these accessions were surface sterilized using diluted sodium hypochlorite and germinated on filter paper18.

Seedlings were transferred from filter paper to a low-nutrient potting mixture (PromixBK, Québec, Canada) at the stage of cotyledon emergence. Seedlings were left to establish for 1 week prior to transplanting them to their experimental units. An experimental unit consisted of one seedling in one pot (black plastic greenhouse pot, 11 × 11 × 13 cm) filled with growing media composed of agricultural soil and sand. Plants were grown in a completed randomized design in a greenhouse and had seven replicates per treatment. Agricultural soil was collected from the Colorado State University (CSU) Agricultural Research, Development, and Education Center (ARDEC) in Fort Collins, CO and was classified as a clay loam. Because topsoil in agroecosystems often contains an abundance of nutrients from organic matter accumulation and fertilization74, topsoil was avoided, and soil was collected from 5 to 20 cm below the surface. Soil was filtered through a 1 cm sieve to remove debris and large aggregates. To further reduce bioavailable P concentrations, sieved agricultural soil was mixed at a 1:1 volumetric ratio with sand (Quikrete Play Sand, Georgia, United States). After mixing with sand, the texture of the soil was a sandy loam as determined by the hydrometer method75 (Table 1) .

Chemical and physical properties of soil-sand mixture.

The Olsen phosphorus test was used to determine phosphorus concentration.

The soil-sand mixture had a low plant-available P concentration of P 4.7 mg/kg (Table 1). Half of the plants were left in soils not fertilized with P, and half were fertilized to P sufficiency. Pots in the fertilized treatment received triple superphosphate (TSP) (0–46–0) at rates equivalent to 160 kg/ha P2O5 (194 mg P2O5 per pot). Based on soil testing results, we determined the P amendment rate based on the tomato fertilization guides from the University of California, the University of California Davis, and from the soil testing recommendations by Ward Laboratories (Kearney, NE). To ensure that P recovery strategies were not conflated with N recovery strategies, all pots regardless of treatment received N fertilization using a polymer-coated urea (Environmentally Smart Nitrogen) (44–0–0) at rates equivalent to 100 kg/ha N.

Every 3 weeks for 15 weeks, samples (i.e., tomato plants and soil) were destructively harvested to elucidate soil P acquisition over time. These correspond to five separate developmental stages (seedling, early vegetative, late vegetative, early flower, late flower). We selected these stages prior to fruit following Shamshiri et al.76. Because the modern tomato is determinate, and the wild tomato is indeterminate, the fruiting patterns and timing may vary, and would therefore be difficult to capture a “fruiting” stage at a single time point for these two varieties.

Biomass and soil sampling

At harvest, plants were removed from pots and were gently shaken to remove bulk soil. Bulk soil was collected for soil-P analysis. Soil remaining on the root axis was identified as rhizosphere soil, collected in sterile 15 mL conical tubes, and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. Subsequently, roots were washed to remove any remaining soil-sand mixture. Plants were severed at the root-shoot interface. Root and shoot samples were placed in a drying oven at 70 °C for 4 days, and dry biomass was recorded.

Plant and soil P analysis

Bulk soil samples were immediately air-dried following harvest. Dry bulk soil was sieved (2 mm) and the Olsen-P bicarbonate extraction method was used to determine bioavailable soil P concentrations77. Water soluble P was determined through colorimetric analysis using the ascorbic acid-molybdenum blue method following Rodriguez and Soltanpour78. Mehlich-3 P was measured in addition to Olsen P for fertilized soils only. As our soil pH is high (7.96), the Olsen P test is appropriate31. The Mehlich-3 test has been shown to be accurate along a broad range of pH79,80. Approximately 10 g of dry sieved soil was mailed to Ward laboratories to determine Mehlich-3 P concentration. Briefly, 2 g soil was mixed and agitated with 20 mL Mehlich-3 extraction solution, and this mixture was filtered and analyzed through colorimetric analysis on a flow injection analyzer (Lachat Instruments, Milwaukee WI) at 880 nm81.

Shoot samples and root samples were homogenized by grinding to a powder and then digested with nitric acid following Ippolito and Barbarick82. Following nitric acid digest, P concentrations were determined through inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES).

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Rhizosphere soil samples were stored at − 80 °C and were weighed to 0.25 g. Remaining rhizosphere was weighed to determine soil moisture content. Moisture content was calculated by measuring the fresh weight of soil samples and the oven-dried weight of samples. Total gDNA was extracted using a QIAcube instrument and DNeasy Power Soil Pro DNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following manufacturer’s instructions. Following extraction, DNA concentration (ng/µL) was determined using a Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Illinois, United States).

DNA sequencing of the 16S rRNA bacterial community was conducted using the Oxford Nanopore platform following the methods of Afkairin et al.83. We included a water control and a D6305—ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA Standard (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). The primers used were 27F (27F-Mn, 5′-TTTCTGTTGGTGCTGATATTGC AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (1492R-Mn, 5′-ACTTGCCTGTCGCTCTATC TTC TACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The PCR product was bead-cleaned using lab-made paramagnetic beads84 at a 1:1 ratio and twice cleaned with 70% ethanol. The library was prepared for sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore Ligation Sequencing Kit V14 (SQK-LSK114) following manufacturer instructions. The final library (50 fmol) was sequenced on a MinION Mk1B for 48 h using the MinKNOW platform (ONT, Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, United Kingdom). Raw sequences were base-called, demultiplexed, and assigned q-scores using dorado v0.9.5 (ONT). Data were then filtered by average q-score (> 10) and length (1360–1560 bp) using NanoFilt85 resulting in > 20,000 quality filtered reads per sample. There were 31 million raw reads, and 6.6 million high quality reads after filtering, with an average of 35,872 ± 543 reads per sample. Bacterial classification of sequence reads was performed using Emu pipeline and database86. Resulting Emu taxonomy table was combined with sample data into a Phyloseq87 object for further analysis. Data were run through PICRUSt2 to identify predictive functional gene abundance utilizing KEGG ontologies88, and we selected a list of putatively beneficial genes using PICRUSt2 (e.g., P mineralizing bacteria, siderophore producing bacteria) (Table S13).

Trial 2: Root exudate study

Plant growth conditions

To identify mechanisms by which the wild and modern tomato accessions interact with their surrounding environment to acquire bound P, we carried out a root exudate trial. The root exudate trial had the same tomato accessions as the time course trial. Seedlings were similarly surface sterilized, and at cotyledon expansion, were transferred to sterile test tubes filled with modified Hoagland hydroponic solution (500 μL NaH2PO4, 1500 μL K2SO4, 1000 μL Ca(NO3)2, 250 μL MgSO4, 50 μL KCl, 150 μL Fe-EDTA, 25 μL H3BO3, 0.3 μL MoO3, 2 μL ZnSO4, 2 μL MnSO4, 0.5 μL CuSO4) adjusted to a pH of 7.21 and filled to cover the roots. The caps of the test tubes were covered with parafilm and left in a growth chamber, and plants were arranged in a completely randomized design. The nutrient solution was replenished twice each week, and seedlings were left to grow for 3 weeks. There were 15 wild tomatoes and 15 modern tomatoes grown in hydroponic solution.

Root exudate collection and metabolomics analysis

Exudates were collected after 3 weeks of growth. Following Pantigoso et al.89, plants were gently removed from nutrient solution and triple rinsed in sterile DI water. These cleaned seedlings were placed in new sterile test tubes filled with enough sterile DI water to cover the roots. Seedlings were left in these conditions to exude for 3 days, following Chaparro et al.42. Following root exudation, seedlings were removed from water and discarded. The remaining water was filtered through a 2 μm filter. To ensure enough biomass of metabolites was collected, exudates from five plants were pooled together to equate to one replicate, with three replicates per treatment. The filtrate was lyophilized, and the exudates were sent to the Environmental Molecular Sciences Laboratory at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (Richland, WA) for metabolomics analysis.

Lyophilized exudates were resuspended in 150 µl of 4:1 methanol:water solvent, vortexed and sonicated to dissolve. Samples were filtered through a 96-well 0.2 µm PTFE filter prep (AcroPrep™ Advance 96-well filter plates for solvent filtration, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) using a positive pressure system (Waters, Milford, Massachusetts). Filtrates were transferred into labeled Thermo SureSTART level 3 vials (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, Massachusetts), capped, and stored at − 20 °C until analysis by LC–MS.

For reverse-phase chromatography, 5 µl metabolite extract was injected and compounds separated using a Thermo Hypersil GOLD column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3 μm particle size) with a column temperature of 40 °C and a primary flow rate of 400 μL min−1. Mobile phase A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and B (ACN with 0.1% formic acid) were initially 90% and 10%, respectively. The gradient method continued as 0–2 min at 90% A; 2–11 min 10% A; 11–12 min at 10% A; 12–12.5 min 10% A with an increased flow rate to 500 μL min−1; 12.5–13.5 min at 90% A; 13.5–14 min at 90% A; 14–14.5 min at 90% A with a decreased flow rate to 400 μL min−1; 14.5–16 min held at 90% A. For hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), 5 µL extract was injected and compounds separated using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH HILIC column (Waters, Milford, MA) (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle size) with a column temperature of 50 °C and a primary flow rate of 300 μL min−1. Mobile phase A (5% ACN: 95% 10 mM NH4OAc in H2O with 0.05% NH4OH) and B (100% ACN with 0.05% NH4OH) were initially 5%, 95%, respectively. The gradient method continued as 0–6.0 min 63% A; 6.0–7.0 min at 63% A; 7.0–7.1 min 5% A; 7.1–7.2 min 5% A with an increased flow rate to 500 μL min−1; 7.2–9.5 min at 5% A; 9.5–9.7 min 5% A with a decreased flow rate to 300 μL min−1; 9.7–12.0 min at 5% A.

Samples were analyzed by LC–MS using a Thermo Vanquish Flex UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) interfaced with a Thermo QExactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA). The HESI probe was maintained at 350 °C and the MS inlet at 300 °C with a spray voltage of 3.7 kV for positive and 3.0 kV for negative mode. The sheath gas, aux gas, and spare gas were maintained at flow rates of 30, 8, and 1 arbitrary units, respectively. Full MS scan data were acquired at a resolving power of 120,000 FWHM at m/z 200 with a scanning range of m/z 80–800. The automatic gain control target was set at 3E6 ions, with a maximum injection time of 20 ms. The data-dependent acquisition parameters used to obtain product ion spectra were resolving power 15,000 FWHM at m/z 200, AGC target 1E5 ions with maximum injection time of 100 ms, isolation window of 0.4 m/z, loop count 12, and a higher-energy collision dissociation normalized collision energy of 20, 30, and 40 eV.

Confident metabolite identifications were made using Thermo Compound Discoverer 3.3. Positive and negative mode spectra were aligned using an adaptive curve with a maximum of 0.3 or 0.6 RT shift, respectively, and a 3 ppm mass tolerance. Peaks were selected based on a minimum intensity of 5e4 and a chromatographic S/N of 3. Detected features are grouped based on a mass tolerance of 3 ppm and an RT tolerance of 0.3. Compounds are assigned based on isotopic pattern, RT, MS1, and/or MS2.

After samples were acquired by LC–MS, consistent volumes of each sample (57 µL) were vacuum-dried and chemically derivatized as previously reported90. Extracts were derivatized by methoxyamination and trimethylsilyation, then the samples were analyzed by GC–MS. Samples were acquired in an Agilent GC 7890A using a HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) coupled with a single quadrupole MSD 5975C (Agilent Technologies). One microliter of sample was injected into a splitless port at 250 °C. The GC temperature gradient started at 60 °C, with a hold of temperature for 1 min after injection, followed by an increase to 325 °C at 10 °C/min and a 10-min hold at 325 °C. A fatty acid methyl ester standard mix (C8–28) (Sigma-Aldrich) was analyzed in parallel as standard for retention time calibration.

GC–MS raw data files were processed using the metabolite detector software91. Retention indices were calculated based on the analysis of a FAMEs mixture, followed by their chromatographic alignment across all analyses after deconvolution. Metabolites were identified by matching experimental spectra to a PNNL augmented version of Agilent GC–MS library, containing spectra and retention indices for over 1200 metabolites. Unknown peaks were additionally matched with the NIST20/Wiley11 GC–MS library. Metabolite identifications and quantification ions were validated to reduce deconvolution errors during automated data-processing and to eliminate false identifications. Peak areas of metabolites detected were reported and submitted for statistical analysis.

Root exudate and bacterial application on NBRIP media

From the list of metabolites exuded by the wild tomato accession and modern tomato accession, six were selected: four highly enriched by wild tomato (trehalose, 1-stearoly-rac-glycerol, glycerol monostearate, 1-monopalmitin) and two by modern tomato (lauric acid, dodecanol). These chemicals were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA) (trehalose, 1-stearoly-rac-glycerol, glycerol monostearate, lauric acid, dodecanol) and Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) (1-monopalmitin). Following Pantigoso et al.89, a stock solution of 7 mM was made for each compound. Trehalose was dissolved in sterile Milli-Q water and all other compounds dissolved in 100% ethanol. Azospirillum brasilense was chosen as a representative bacterium for this experiment because it has been shown to be plant-beneficial and P-sensitive83, it was present across samples in this study (thereby minimizing bias by not being enriched by a specific domestication group), and it has been shown to be capable of growth in mediums with ethanol92. Although ethanol has been shown to be a carbon source and its supplementation can increase bacterial cellulose production for some bacteria93, its application should not cause an indirect effect on P pools in this trial because this trial occurs aseptically in vitro, in which the only introduced organism was A. brasilense, and the only pools of P are the tricalcium phosphate (TCP) and phytin added to the media. Moreover, negative controls of solvents (water and ethanol) without application of metabolites to ensure growth of A. brasilense on NBRIP media.

Following Islas-Valdez et al.94, a culture of A. brasilense was grown to late log phase (optical density OD600 = 0.7) in LB media. The culture was then serially diluted with sterile water. The bacteria (100 uL at 5.6 × 10⁸ CFU mL−1) and root exudate (100 μL) were added to National Botanic Research Institute’s Phosphorus (NBRIP) agar plates amended with either phytin (organic P) or TCP (inorganic P) and incubated at 27 °C for 4 days. Subsequently, the phosphate solubilization index (PSI) was calculated by measuring the proportionate difference in the halo diameter to the colony diameter95.

Trial 3: Soil conditioning study

Plant growth conditions

This soil conditioning trial had the same soil-sand mixture (Table 1), fertilization rate, tomato accessions, replicate number, and pot size as the time course trial. Seedlings were surface sterilized and germinated following the same method outlined in the time course trial.

Here, we sought to test the ability of a wild tomato to confer beneficial P acquisition traits in a modern tomato. This trial consisted of two planting cycles: the first cycle was to alter the condition of the soil, and the second cycle was planted with only modern tomato plants to determine if the soil conditioning treatment had an effect on subsequent planting cycles. Soil was initially autoclave-sterilized to better showcase the effects from the host plant. Soil was conditioned in the first cycle by leaving the pots without a plant, planting a wild tomato, or planting a modern tomato. Henceforth, these soil conditioning treatments are referred to as “wild-conditioned”, “modern-conditioned”, and “no plant-conditioned” soils, respectively. The plants were left to grow for 5 weeks in soils that were unfertilized or P-fertilized, and then modern tomato was planted in all the pots. The modern tomato was again either fertilized or left unfertilized. These plants were also left to grow for 5 weeks. This time period was selected following Dixon et al.32, in which the rhizosphere bacteriome for tomato in similar soil conditions showed a marked difference after 5 weeks of growth, thus suggesting sufficient time for the plants to impart change on their rhizosphere microbiome composition.

Plant and soil P analysis

The methodology of biomass and soil sampling followed that of the time course trial. Bulk soil was also air-dried and sieved after harvest using the same procedure as the time course trial. However, for this soil conditioning trial, only Olsen P was measured using Olsen-P bicarbonate extraction77.

Roots and shoots were separated, and dry root tissue was analyzed for P concentration through nitric acid digest82 followed by ICP-AES.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Rhizosphere DNA was extracted and sequenced using the same procedure outlined in the time course trial.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in R (v 4.3.3) and RStudio (v. 2024.12.0 + 467). An interactive analysis of variance (ANOVA) using α = 0.05 was performed on shoot and root biomass, shoot P concentration, root P concentration, Olsen-P concentration, water soluble P concentration, P mineralizing bacterial relative abundance, and microbial biomass using Y ~ Fertilization * Domestication * Harvest (time course trial) and Y ~ Fertilization * Conditioning (soil conditioning trial).

In the time course trial, the factor “Fertilization” indicates the P-fertilization treatment (P-fertilized, unfertilized), “Domestication” indicates the type of plant used (wild, modern), and “Harvest” indicates the stage of destructive harvest (seedling, early vegetative, late vegetative, early flower, late flower). The response variable in time course trial had to be log-transformed to satisfy the assumption of homoscedasticity. After log-transformation, ANOVA assumptions were satisfied using diagnostic plots96. For visualization, data were back-transformed. When the three-way interactive term was significant (i.e., Fertilization * Domestication * Harvest), the letters in the figure would represent the differences in that interactive term. When the three-way interactive was not significant, the letters on the graphs denote differences from the Domestication * Harvest interactive term or the main effect of Domestication if there is no significant interaction found.

In the soil conditioning trial, the factor “Fertilization” indicates if plants were fertilized (P-fertilized, unfertilized) in the second planting cycle. “Conditioning” indicates the condition of the pots in the first cycle (wild, modern, no plant). A Tukey HSD was used for post-hoc comparisons using an α = 0.05.

To compare the bacterial species that changed in abundance between treatments in the time course and soil conditioning trials, a differential abundance analysis was done on bacterial abundance data, indicating the sequence reads per sample. Abundance data were normalized through a relative log expression prior to testing. A Wald test was then performed on the log-twofold change of normalized abundance data using the microbiomeMarker package (v 3.20)97. A 0.05 FDR p adjustment was used for this test. This test was used to compare the bacteria changing in abundance between each harvest point within each domestication and fertilization treatment for time course trial.

To examine metabolite composition in the root exudate trial, a sparsity filter was employed using a robust Mahalanobis distance to look for potential outliers. To determine differences, a two-tailed t test was run to assess if the normalized log2 abundance values were different between the modern and wild tomatoes. An interactive ANOVA was run to determine differences in PSI: Y ~ Exudate * P Source, where “Exudate” indicates the type of compound used, “P Source” indicates whether phytin or TCP were used in NBRIP formulation.
