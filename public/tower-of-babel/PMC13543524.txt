Decoding grapevine elicitation responses through cell culture metabolomics
Authors: Maria Paes, João Proença Pereira, Catarina Paiva-Silva, Nuno Domingues, Jorge Cunha, Jasmine Hertzog, Vincent Carré, Andreia Figueiredo, Marisa Maia, Rita B. Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant cell culture systems offer a reproducible and controlled platform for studying stress responses in grapevine. However, the extent to which grapevine cell cultures capture inducible metabolic features observed in organ-level systems remains insufficiently explored. Here, we established cell suspension cultures (CSCs) from two grapevine cultivars with contrasting reported disease susceptibility backgrounds, Cabernet Sauvignon and Chambourcin, and compared their jasmonic acid (JA)-induced metabolic responses with those observed in detached leaves. CSCs and detached leaves were elicited with JA, and their metabolic profiles were assessed at 2 and 5 days post-elicitation using Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR MS) in negative ion mode. Putatively annotated metabolite candidates revealed cultivar- and time-dependent responses in both systems. Although CSCs and leaves retained distinct global metabolic profiles, a partial functional convergence was observed in selected categories, particularly oxidative stress defense, stress signaling, and secondary metabolism. These results support the use of grapevine CSCs as complementary platforms to investigate selected inducible stress responses, while highlighting the need for further validation in intact plants and targeted metabolite confirmation. Introduction

Grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) is one of the most important crop plants worldwide, with great economic and cultural significance [1]. However, due to domestication events and a lack of co-evolution with some pathogens, grapevine has become susceptible to many diseases, with downy mildew, powdery mildew, and grey mold among the most prevalent. These diseases are currently managed by frequent application of various agrochemicals, a widespread practice that is not only costly to farmers but also detrimental to the environment and human health [2, 3], making it urgent to mitigate their systematic use. The development of novel resistant varieties via interspecific crosses has been used as a solution to combine agronomic and viticultural traits of interest, ultimately increasing tolerance to disease [4, 5]. Although demonstrated to be highly effective, this process is time-consuming and laborious and does not address the need for simple, effective methods for disease control in vineyards.

Plant cell suspension cultures (CSC) combine plant totipotency, cell population homogeneity, and biosynthetic plasticity, making them attractive models for studying specific molecular processes. Specifically, plant CSCs have been used to study immune-related processes such as calcium signaling [6, 7] and programmed cell death [8], as well as responses to chemical stimuli (i.e., elicitation), both pathogen- and plant-borne [6–8]. In grapevine, CSCs have been extensively used to study stilbene production, with particular emphasis on viniferins [9] under chemical elicitation, although other compounds have also received attention [10]. Nonetheless, grapevine cell suspension elicitation still presents challenges, as knowledge of the metabolic response to elicitors and the mechanisms by which cultured cells transmit the signal to induce secondary metabolism remains scarce.

Among the most popular elicitors in grapevine CSCs are jasmonates, such as jasmonic acid (JA) or methyl jasmonate (MeJA), phytohormones that play a central role in plant defense responses [11, 12], including several grapevine diseases [9, 13, 14]. Elicitation with these molecules translated into an accumulation of various secondary metabolites in grapevine CSCs [10, 15–18]. Along the same lines, metabolome reprogramming has also been described in various instances upon jasmonate elicitation in plants, suggesting that parallels between CSC and plants can be traced [19, 20]. Despite this, it remains unclear whether grapevine CSCs capture selected inducible metabolic features also observed in organ-level systems.

In this work, we harnessed untargeted metabolomics to uncover changes in the metabolome of grapevine CSCs from two grapevine cultivars with contrasting reported disease-susceptibility backgrounds following JA elicitation. Detached leaves were used as a complementary model system to assess the extent and limits of metabolic overlap between CSCs and leaf tissues after JA elicitation. By comparing the two systems under controlled in vitro conditions, we aimed to identify shared JA-associated metabolic features. This comparative approach allows us to establish cell cultures as a controlled, scalable platform for capturing selected JA-inducible metabolic features relevant to grapevine defense.

Materials and methods

Plant material and surface sterilization

Vitis vinifera cv. Cabernet Sauvignon (CS—VIVC 1929) and cv. Chambourcin (CH—VIVC 2436) explants were obtained from the Portuguese ampelographic grapevine collection (CAN, international code PRT051) at the National Institute for Agricultural and Veterinary Research (INIAV), Dois Portos, Portugal. Cultivar selection was based on their contrasting viticultural backgrounds and differences in disease susceptibility/tolerance reported at the Vitis International Variety Catalogue (VIVC; www.vivc.de). For both the cell culture and the leaf elicitation assays, surface sterilization of shoots and leaves was achieved by an initial immersion in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min, followed by 30 min of immersion in a 25% (v/v) solution of commercial sodium hypochlorite containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20. Subsequently, the explants were rinsed three times with sterile distilled water and dried with sterile filter paper.

Callus induction and subculture

Different explants (stem, leaves, petioles, and tendrils) were excised from the sterile shoots of CS and CH. Explants were then sectioned into 1-centimeter (cm) cuts and cultivated on modified Gamborg B5 medium (pH 5.7; [21]), enriched with 0.25 g/L casein hydrolysate, 5.0 g/L PVP, 0.58 g/L MES, 30.0 g/L sucrose, and 7.0 g/L microagar, according to [22]. The culture medium was supplemented with filter-sterilized ascorbic acid (50 mg/L), citric acid (50 mg/L), 6-benzylaminopurine (0.1 mg/L), and 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (0.1 mg/L). Callus induction efficiency was not recorded as a quantitative percentage of responsive explants. Explant selection for CSC establishment was therefore based on qualitative criteria, including callus friability, tendency to oxidize, growth consistency, and ability to sustain liquid culture.

Establishment and maintenance of grapevine cell suspension cultures

Cell suspension cultures were established by transferring 1 cm2 fragments of homogeneous and friable callus into 20 mL of liquid-modified Gamborg B5 medium. The liquid-modified Gamborg B5 medium was supplemented with filter-sterilized ascorbic acid (100 mg/L), citric acid (100 mg/L), L-cysteine (100 mg/L), 6-benzylaminopurine (0.1 mg/L), and 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (0.1 mg/L). Cell suspensions were maintained on a shaker at 110 rpm, in the dark at 25 ± 1 °C. Cell suspensions were subcultured weekly into fresh medium at a 1/5 (v/v) ratio (Fig. 1).

Cell culture elicitation

For grapevine CSCs, elicitation experiments were performed in quadruplicate using 5-day-old cell cultures. Five-day-old CSCs were selected for JA elicitation based on previous grapevine cell culture elicitation studies and preliminary growth observations, which indicated that this time point provided actively growing cultures and sufficient biomass for downstream metabolomic analysis. Filter-sterilized jasmonic acid (JA) (J2500, Sigma-Aldrich) stock solution, which was prepared by dissolving JA in 0.1% (v/v) ethanol (100 mM), was added to CSCs to a final concentration of 50 µM. Control cell cultures were treated with equal volumes of filter-sterilized 0.1% (v/v) ethanol. Cell cultures were incubated in the dark at 25 ± 1 °C on a shaker at 110 rpm. Samples were collected at 2 and 5 days post-elicitation (dpe). Cells were collected by vacuum filtration through Whatman filter paper, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C until further analysis. To evaluate the effect of JA on cultured cell growth, cells were collected by vacuum filtration at each time point and dried to determine DW, as described above. At each time point, four biological replicates were collected and used for the following assays.

Plant elicitation

Elicitation of grapevine leaves was conducted via spray application of 50 µM JA solution prepared in 0.1% (v/v) ethanol. Control leaves were sprayed with 0.1% (v/v) ethanol. After elicitation, leaves were kept on agar plates at 25 °C ± 1 °C on a 16 h light cycle. Samples were collected at 2 and 5 dpe, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C until further analysis. For each condition, a pooled sample of five leaves was used to represent a biological replicate. Three biological replicates were used.

Statistics

To assess the effect of JA on biomass accumulation at the sampling points used for metabolomic analysis, dry weight (DW) values of JA-treated and control CSCs were compared independently at each sampling time using two-sample Student’s t-tests. These analyses were used as pointwise biomass comparisons and were not intended to model the full growth trajectory. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05. Apparent specific growth rate (μ) and doubling time (Td) were calculated over the post-treatment interval, using μ = ln(Xf/Xi)/Δt and Td = ln2/μ, where Xi and Xf correspond to the initial and final cell biomasses (g/L), Δt is the duration of the sampling period in days, μ is the specific growth rate, and Td is the doubling time [23].

Metabolite extraction

Metabolite extraction from grapevine suspension cells and leaves was adapted from [24] and [25]. Cells and leaves were collected at the indicated time points, frozen at –80 °C, and ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Metabolites were extracted from 100 mg fresh weight of ground material using methanol/water (1:1, v/v) at a ratio of 100 mg sample to 1.0 mL solvent. The mixture was vortexed for 2 min and then maintained on an orbital shaker at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, extracts were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and stored at –80 °C prior to analysis. Samples were analyzed in triplicate.

FT-ICR MS analysis

Samples were diluted in methanol (MeOH; 1:10, v/v; LC–MS grade, Merck) and analyzed by direct infusion on a SolariX 2XR 7T Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometer (FT-ICR MS; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Sodium trifluoroacetate (NaTFA, 0.1 mg/mL in 50:50 [v/v] acetonitrile:water) was used for shimming and gating the ICR cell, as well as for external calibration of the mass spectrometer prior to measurement. The temperature of the autosampler was maintained at 15 °C and 4 °C during the analyses for the vials of leaf extracts and cell culture samples, respectively, to prevent thermal degradation of the samples over time. Samples were directly introduced via flow injection analysis at a rate of 5.0 μL/min using an electrospray ionization source. To enhance the detection of secondary metabolites, mass spectra were acquired in magnitude mode in negative ion mode, covering the m/z range 115–1500. Negative ion mode was selected to favor the detection of acidic and phenolic metabolites commonly associated with grapevine defense responses. Data were collected with a 4-megaword time domain, averaging 200 scans per spectrum for each sample, with an ion accumulation time of 0.005 s per scan. During acquisition, six m/z values (215.032789, 242.051755, 255.232954, 283.264254, 311.040856, 463.327642) were used as lock masses to enable online calibration prior to scan accumulation. Both ion source and instrument parameters were optimized via software FTMS-Control V2.3.0.59 (Bruker Daltonics). FTMS Control (2.3.0.59, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) was used for data acquisition. A quality control (QC) pool for each set of samples (leaves and cell cultures) was performed to assess repeatability of the analysis over time. The same dilution (1:10) was done for each QC analyzed.

Data processing

FT-ICR-MS spectra were processed using Compass Data Analysis 5.0 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). For each assay (cell cultures and leaf extracts), the mass spectra were further internally calibrated using a list of known chemical formulas detected as [M—H]− and [M + Cl]− ions. For the leaf extracts, a list of 16 known mass peaks was used for internal calibration (from m/z 133.01425 to m/z 789.11560); for the cell culture samples, a list of 9 mass peaks was used for internal calibration (from m/z 149.00916 to m/z 521.17212). For the different mass spectra, a standard deviation of around 0.1 ppm was achieved. Each peak list was exported with a minimum signal-to-noise ratio of 4, an absolute intensity threshold of 106, and a relative intensity threshold of 0.05%. Signals corresponding to the magnetron and satellite peaks were removed using an algorithm developed in [26]. Peak lists (cell extracts and leaf extracts) were aligned within a 1 ppm window, and only m/z values present in two out of the three replicates were conserved. For the cell–leaf comparative analysis, cell and leaf extract peak lists were compared, and only m/z values present in both datasets were retained.

Metabolite annotation and analysis

The m/z values detected by FT-ICR MS in grapevine cell suspension culture samples and commonly present in leaf extracts were statistically analyzed for each sample using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 ([27]; https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Missing values were imputed by replacing them with one-fifth of the minimum intensity observed in each replicate. No data filtering was applied. Prior to the statistical analysis, data were log-transformed and Pareto-scaled [28]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to explore patterns of overall variation. Significant differences in m/z feature abundance between JA-treated and control samples were identified using t-tests in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (p ≤ 0.05; FDR-corrected values). Differentially accumulated m/z values (fold change) between elicited and control samples were retrieved directly from MetaboAnalyst 6.0. The statistically significant m/z values were submitted to an in-house script and, based on the metabolites present in KEGG’s (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/kegg2.html) Vitis vinifera pathway database and KNApSAcK’s (http://kanaya.naist.jp/KNApSAcK/) Vitis database, were putatively annotated considering the adducts [M-H]− and [M + Cl]−, with a 1.0 ppm error tolerance. Due to the presence of multiple compound candidates for each m/z value, only one matching metabolite was selected for analysis, and the corresponding metabolic pathway was associated based on the databases used for metabolic annotation. Metabolite assignments should be considered putative, as they were based on accurate mass matching against databases and were not confirmed by MS/MS fragmentation or authentic standards.

Results

Grapevine CSCs were initiated from homogeneous and friable callus derived from stem, petiole, and tendril explants of both CH and CS and grown in liquid modified Gamborg B5 medium (Figure S1 and Figure S2). Despite medium supplementation with antioxidants, an initial tendency toward oxidation was observed in the cell suspension. To reduce oxidation during suspension establishment, the concentrations of ascorbic acid and citric acid were increased from 50 to 100 mg/L in the liquid medium, and L-cysteine was added at 100 mg/L. Nevertheless, CH and CS cell suspensions derived from petiole and tendril explants could not be maintained due to high oxidation and cell death.

After establishing stem-derived CSCs for both cultivars, 5-day-old cultures were selected for JA elicitation based on previous grapevine cell culture elicitation studies and preliminary growth observations. This timing allowed JA treatment to be applied to actively growing cultures while providing sufficient biomass for metabolomic analysis.

Impact of JA elicitation on cell biomass

CH and CS cell suspension cultures were treated with 50 µM JA at 5 days post-subculture, and biomass accumulation was monitored after treatment. In CS, JA-treated cultures showed significantly lower DW than control cultures at 2 and 5 dpe (Fig. 2E). This was accompanied by a longer apparent doubling time in JA-treated cultures than in controls (Td = 26.7 and 12.4 days, respectively; Fig. 2E). In contrast, no significant differences between JA-treated and control cultures were detected in CH at the analyzed time points, and apparent doubling times were similar between treatments (Td = 18.73 and 17.3 days, respectively; Fig. 2F). These comparisons were used to evaluate treatment-associated differences in biomass at defined sampling points, rather than to model the full growth trajectory.

Metabolic reprogramming of grapevine cell cultures in response to JA

The metabolic reprogramming of CH and CS cell suspension cultures was analyzed at 2 and 5 days post-elicitation with jasmonic acid (JA) using an untargeted metabolic approach. A total of 1104 m/z values were detected through FT-ICR MS analysis in negative mode across cultivars and time points.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess the main sources of variation in the CSCs metabolomic dataset. In the complete dataset, sample separation was mainly driven by cultivar along PC1 (49.8%), with treatment-related variation contributing to PC2 (9.8%) (Fig. 3). Time-point-specific PCAs further showed that JA-induced separation was more evident in CH than in CS, particularly at 2 dpe, whereas treatment-associated separation increased for both cultivars at 5 dpe (Figure S4). To extend this analysis, the dataset was further examined by putatively annotating significantly accumulated m/z values (considering both [M-H]- and [M + Cl]- adducts). This was followed by their tentative association with Vitis vinifera metabolic pathways as described in the KEGG and KNApSAcK databases. Thus, of the 340 significantly accumulated m/z values, 54 unique metabolite candidates were putatively annotated and mapped to 63 metabolic pathways. Metabolic pathways were grouped into broad functional categories, which, when coupled with the individual accumulation patterns and biological function of their respective metabolites, provide a more complete analysis (Table S1). Furthermore, these categories provide a structural metabolic overview and do not replace biological interpretation focused on individual metabolite behavior.

At the metabolite level, many functional categories are commonly modulated across genotypes, except for amino acid metabolism and redox homeostasis-related metabolites, which are depleted in CH and accumulated in CS. Overall, JA elicitation triggered contrasting metabolic responses between cultivars as previously observed (Fig. 3). CS displayed a broad metabolic activation characterized by a general moderate (log2FC ~ 0.5–1.5) to strong metabolic induction (log2FC 1.5–3) of metabolites associated with primary and secondary metabolic processes maintained across both time points (Fig. 4). This response was marked by the strong modulation of defense-related processes, associated with increased abundance of m/z features putatively assigned to phenylpropanoid-related compounds, including phenolic acids (e.g., gallic acid), stilbenes (e.g., resveratrol), and flavonoids (e.g., epicatechin). Moreover, this accumulation aligns with the detected strong accumulation of m/z features putatively assigned to shikimate and the slight depletion of features putatively assigned to phenylalanine, consistent with their involvement in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. In parallel, most features putatively assigned to amino acid-related metabolism and central metabolism showed moderate to strong accumulation, including the m/z features putatively assigned to alpha-ketoglutaric acid and to citrate, key intermediates of the TCA cycle. An oxidative defense-associated feature putatively assigned to dehydroascorbate also showed a moderate upward accumulation trend in CS cells. This accumulation trend pattern was maintained at 5 dpe, with continued accumulation of defense-related metabolites and the additional detection of m/z features putatively assigned to terpenoids. Amino acid metabolism, oxidative defense and redox homeostasis, and central metabolism categories continued to exhibit upward accumulation trends, particularly for m/z features putatively assigned to TCA cycle-associated organic acids and glutamate, the precursor of glutathione. By contrast, CH exhibited broad metabolic reprogramming in response to JA elicitation. At 2 dpe, this cultivar exhibited a strong modulation (log2FC > 2.5) of the lipid-derived signaling-associated metabolites, here represented by m/z features putatively assigned to oxylipins 13(S)-HODE, 9(S)-HPODE, key compounds in JA response signaling (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, defense-related compounds, including m/z features putatively assigned to stilbenes and flavonoids, were also strongly accumulated. Within the detected metabolite set, this pattern resulted in a higher category-level response despite lower metabolic diversity than in CS. In parallel, metabolites associated with amino acid metabolism and oxidative defense/redox homeostasis-related processes exhibited an overall depletion trend, particularly pronounced for phenylalanine, ascorbate, and glutathione (Fig. 4A). Moreover, central metabolism, nucleotide, and structural complex carbon-related pathways showed positive modulation, mainly driven by the accumulation of m/z features putatively assigned to D-Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and D-Ribose 5-phosphate, key precursors in multiple pathways. Despite highly modulated stress signaling at a category-level, stress signaling was solely represented by m/z features putatively assigned to D-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, whose accumulation was strongly induced by JA elicitation. By 5 dpe, CH exhibited a marked reduction in the overall number of detected metabolites across functional categories, while maintaining accumulation trends observed at 2 dpe (Fig. 4A). Lipid metabolism was the only exception. In addition to an upward accumulation trend, this category showed increased metabolic diversity, including the detection of m/z features putatively assigned to 16-hydroxypalmitate (log2FC = 2.4) and docosanoic acid (log2FC = 2.42). In parallel, defense-related metabolites continued to display positive accumulation trends. Furthermore, stress signaling, amino acid metabolism, and oxidative stress/redox homeostasis maintained their previously observed accumulation trends, although each category was represented by a single putatively annotated metabolite candidate: myo-inositol, tryptophan, and ascorbate, respectively. Conversely, no metabolites associated with structural complex carbon and nucleotide metabolism categories were detected.

Shared metabolic responses between grapevine cell cultures and leaves

The metabolic profiling of CH and CS detached leaves elicited with JA was also analyzed following the same metabolic workflow and sampling time points previously described. This analysis allowed the detection of 1090 m/z values in leaf samples among both cultivars and time points. Of these, 234 m/z values overlapped with those detected in cell suspension samples (considering a 1 ppm tolerance) (Figure S3). Hereafter, all subsequent comparative metabolic analyses will be conducted solely using this shared dataset, detailed in Tables S2 and S3.

PCA of the shared cell–leaf m/z dataset showed that tissue type (PC1: 38.1%) was the main source of variation, followed by cultivar (PC2: 25.0%), indicating that CSCs and leaves retain distinct global metabolic profiles (Fig. 5). When leaves and CSCs were analyzed separately, cultivar and treatment effects were still observed, but their relative contribution differed between systems (Figure S5). Thus, while the shared dataset captured only the m/z features common to CSCs and leaves, it retained broadly comparable separation patterns to those observed in the complete CSC dataset, suggesting that the reduced feature set still captured part of the main metabolic variation. Nevertheless, the overall metabolic structure remained strongly tissue-dependent. Of the 234 shared m/z features, 165 met the previously mentioned p-value and FDR criteria, in at least one JA-treated versus control comparison within the shared dataset (Table S2). To further investigate shared metabolic responses, the shared dataset was examined in greater depth by putatively annotating significantly abundant m/z features and assessing their associations with metabolic pathways, as previously mentioned. Thirty-nine unique metabolite candidates were putatively annotated across all experimental groups from both cultivars. The presence of these metabolite candidates also varied across the experimental groups, with fewer detected at the later time point (5 days post-elicitation) in CH and the opposite trend in CS (Table S3). These metabolites were mapped to 28 unique metabolic pathways commonly shared across tissues and subsequently grouped into functional categories, as previously described. For this dataset, seven functional categories were considered: oxidative defense/redox homeostasis; secondary metabolism (defense and signaling); stress signaling; structural complex carbohydrate synthesis; transport; amino acid metabolism; and central metabolism, encompassing carbon and energy management. Although their overall metabolic profiles were distinct, both systems (CSCs and leaves) showed partial functional convergence in selected metabolic categories, as evidenced by the modulation of overlapping functional categories, each characterized by changes in different putatively annotated metabolite candidates (Table S3).

Cultivar-dependent metabolic response patterns

At 2 dpe, CH showed partial agreement between leaves and cell suspension cultures in functional categories related to cell structure, signaling, secondary metabolism, and oxidative stress response (Fig. 6). However, candidates associated with transport and amino acid metabolism, including metabolites putatively assigned to glutathione and aspartic acid, displayed divergent accumulation patterns between the two systems (Table S3). At the individual metabolite level, four putatively annotated candidates were shared between cells and leaves, including ascorbic acid, which was consistently depleted in both tissues (Fig. 7). By 5 dpe, CH displayed down-accumulation trends for putatively assigned metabolites associated with amino acid metabolism and oxidative stress response, whereas stress signaling and secondary metabolism showed contrasting patterns between systems (Fig. 6). At 2 dpe, leaves and cell cultures displayed marked divergence across several functional categories, including signaling, secondary metabolism, oxidative stress response, central metabolism, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 6). This divergence was associated with opposite accumulation trends in shared candidates such as the putatively assigned alpha-ketoglutaric acid and shikimate, which were depleted in leaves but accumulated in cell cultures (Fig. 7). By 5 dpe, a clearer alignment between systems was observed, with similar upward trends in the number of metabolite candidates associated with signaling, secondary metabolism, oxidative stress response, and central metabolism (Figs. 6 and 7). This pattern was supported by 10 shared putatively annotated candidates that showed consistent accumulation across tissues, including compounds assigned to stilbene-, hydroxycinnamic acid-, flavonoid-, and hydrolyzable tannin-related metabolism (Fig. 7). Divergent responses were limited to fewer candidates, including sucrose, which decreased in cells, and glutathione and 3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonate, which accumulated in leaves (Table S3).

Overall, the comparison revealed that metabolic agreement between cell suspension cultures and detached leaves was partial and strongly dependent on cultivar and time point. The clearest alignment was observed in CH at 2 dpe and in CS at 5 dpe, mainly within secondary metabolism, signaling, and oxidative stress-related processes. In CH, convergence was observed in 60% of cell–leaf functional category comparisons and in four out of five shared candidates (Figs. 6 and 7). In CS, similarity was weaker at 2 dpe but became more evident at 5 dpe, when more shared candidates followed the same accumulation trend in both systems (Figs. 6 and 7).

Discussion

Establishment of grapevine CSCs and cultivar-dependent growth responses

Studies on plant defense mechanisms are predominantly conducted in planta. However, these approaches often face limitations related to seasonality, experimental logistics, biological variability, and the time required to obtain sufficient material. In vitro cell cultures offer a useful complementary platform, providing controlled, scalable systems for studying specific cellular and metabolic responses [29]. In addition, plant cell cultures have considerable biotechnological potential for producing economically relevant compounds [30, 31]. Nevertheless, the extent to which grapevine cell suspension cultures (CSCs) capture inducible metabolic features observed in plant organs remains insufficiently explored. To address this question, we compared the metabolic responses of grapevine CSCs from two cultivars with contrasting reported disease susceptibility backgrounds, Cabernet Sauvignon (CS) and Chambourcin (CH), after jasmonic acid (JA) elicitation, and assessed the extent to which these responses overlapped with those observed in detached leaves.

The establishment of callus and CSCs is a critical step in plant cell culture systems and is strongly influenced by explant type, genotype, culture medium, and oxidation susceptibility. In this study, four explant types — leaves, petioles, stems, and tendrils — were tested to establish viable grapevine CSCs. Stable CSCs from both CH and CS were obtained exclusively from friable callus originating from stem tissue. This outcome may be related to the greater abundance of meristematic or developmentally competent tissues in stems than in more differentiated organs such as leaves [32, 33]. However, because callus induction efficiency was not quantitatively recorded, explant performance was interpreted qualitatively, based on callus friability, oxidation tendency, growth consistency, and the ability to sustain liquid culture. Accordingly, explant selection was based on qualitative performance rather than quantitative induction efficiency, which represents a limitation of the CSC establishment phase.

JA affected biomass accumulation in a cultivar-dependent manner. In CS, JA-treated cultures had lower DW than control cultures at the sampling points used for metabolomic analysis, whereas CH cultures did not show significant differences in biomass between JA and control conditions (Fig. 2E, F). This suggests that CS experienced a stronger treatment-associated growth penalty under JA elicitation, with apparent doubling time increasing from 12.4 days in control cultures to 26.7 days in JA-treated cultures. In contrast, CH maintained biomass accumulation under the same experimental conditions (Td (control) = 17.3 days vs Td (JA) = 18.7 days). This response in CS was accompanied by extensive metabolic changes involving putatively annotated metabolites associated with defense, stress signaling, redox homeostasis, amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism, and structural metabolism. This pattern is consistent with a growth–defense trade-off, in which JA-induced metabolic reprogramming may redirect resources away from biomass accumulation. Similar growth–defense trade-offs have been reported in grapevine cell suspension cultures, where jasmonate elicitation reduced biomass production while promoting stilbene and flavonoid accumulation [17, 34, 35]. Methyl jasmonate elicitation has also been associated with broad metabolic activation, including increased amino acid content and enhanced accumulation of secondary metabolites, supporting the view that jasmonate-driven defense responses can be metabolically demanding [36, 37].

JA elicits cultivar-dependent metabolic reprogramming in CSCs

The metabolic response to JA differed markedly between the two CSC lines. In CS, JA elicitation was associated with a broad accumulation of putatively annotated metabolite candidates across several functional categories and with lower biomass accumulation at the sampled points. By contrast, CH showed a more restricted but apparently coordinated response, with changes particularly associated with lipid-derived signaling, secondary metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and redox-related processes, while maintaining biomass accumulation under JA treatment. The reduced number of modulated metabolite candidates observed in CH at 5 dpe may further indicate a transition towards a later or post-response stage, whereas CS maintained broader metabolic activation. These cultivar-dependent responses reinforce the importance of genotype selection in grapevine CSC elicitation studies, as different cultivars may differ substantially in their capacity to sustain elicitor-induced metabolic changes while maintaining growth [38]. It is also important to note that the present dataset represents the negative-ion detectable metabolome. As this study was focused on two cultivars with contrasting reported disease-susceptibility backgrounds, the negative ionization mode was selected to ensure the detection of phenolic compounds, strongly associated with plant defense. In previous studies, it was shown that this analysis provides greater sensitivity for these compounds [24, 39]. Nevertheless, future analyses in positive ionization mode could be performed to explore other metabolites and metabolic classes that are better ionized in this mode.

CSCs share selected JA-associated metabolic features with detached leaves

After characterizing JA-induced metabolic responses in CSCs, we compared these patterns with those observed in JA-elicited detached leaves. PCA revealed that tissue type was a major source of variation across the shared cell–leaf dataset, indicating that CSCs and leaves retain distinct global metabolic profiles (Fig. 5). CSCs do not globally reproduce leaf metabolism and should not be interpreted as equivalent to differentiated tissues. Nevertheless, when analysis was focused on the subset of m/z features shared between CSCs and leaves, selected functional similarities became apparent, particularly in categories associated with secondary metabolism, stress signaling, oxidative stress response, and redox homeostasis.

A total of 234 shared m/z features were identified between CSCs and detached leaves, representing a limited yet informative subset of the detected metabolic space. Rather than indicating global equivalence between systems, this shared set highlights metabolic features that may be commonly responsive to JA at both cellular and organ levels. Within this subset, partial functional convergence was observed in a cultivar- and time-dependent manner. CH showed stronger alignment between CSCs and leaves at 2 dpe, whereas CS showed clearer alignment at 5 dpe (Fig. 6). This temporal difference is consistent with previous observations that grapevine metabolic responses and response timing can vary substantially among cultivars [40–43]. The strongest similarities between CSCs and detached leaves were associated with secondary metabolism, stress signaling, oxidative stress response, and redox-related processes. These categories are broadly consistent with known JA-associated responses. The accumulation of secondary metabolism-related candidates, particularly in CS samples, is consistent with previous reports of jasmonate-induced modulation of phenylpropanoid metabolism. Stilbene-, phenolic acid-, flavonoid-, and tannin-related compounds have been reported after jasmonate elicitation in Vitis vinifera cell cultures [16, 34, 44, 45], as well as in other plant systems such as Givotia moluccana [46], Pelargonium graveolens [47], Hypericum perforatum [48], and Jatropha curcas [49]. These defense-related compounds often have antioxidant properties and are frequently associated with enhanced oxidative stress responses following JA elicitation [50, 51]. Nevertheless, the redox-related response observed here appears complex. At the most convergent time points, 2 dpe for CH and 5 dpe for CS, both cultivars showed patterns suggesting possible limitations in antioxidant recycling despite the accumulation of phenolic antioxidant-related candidates. In CH, the depletion of glutathione and ascorbate-related candidates may suggest perturbation of the glutathione–ascorbate cycle, particularly at the recycling level [52]. Similar observations have been reported by Laureano et al., who observed a JA-induced decrease in glutathione S-transferase expression [53]. In CS at 5 dpe, the presence of a candidate putatively assigned to L-ascorbic acid 3-glucoside may indicate a shift towards a more stable or storage-associated form of ascorbate [54]. This could be consistent with reduced detectable levels of free ascorbic acid.

Despite these shared functional tendencies, divergent responses were also observed. In CH, partial functional alignment at 2 dpe was followed by greater divergence at 5 dpe, with CSCs and leaves showing contrasting trends in stress signaling and secondary metabolism. This may indicate that CSCs maintained an active JA-induced response for longer, whereas detached leaves may have progressed towards a recovery or reprogramming phase. In CS, the strongest divergence occurred at 2 dpe, when CSCs and leaves showed opposite trends across several functional categories, including candidates associated with central metabolism and phenylpropanoid-related pathways. By 5 dpe, these differences had decreased, and both systems exhibited more similar accumulation patterns (Fig. 6). These results suggest that metabolic alignment between CSCs and detached leaves is not fixed but depends on cultivar, time point, and the functional category considered.

The observed differences are likely linked to the biological nature of the two systems. CSCs are homogeneous populations of undifferentiated cells directly exposed to JA in liquid medium, whereas leaves are differentiated, multicellular organs with tissue-specific regulation, cuticular barriers, and more complex hormonal crosstalk. Therefore, differences in response timing, uptake kinetics, and regulatory complexity are expected. The relevant conclusion is not that CSCs reproduce leaf metabolism globally, but that they can capture selected JA-inducible metabolic features that are also detectable in detached leaves.

Conclusion

This study compared JA-induced metabolic responses in grapevine CSCs and detached leaves of two cultivars with contrasting reported disease-susceptibility backgrounds. JA elicitation induced cultivar-dependent effects on CSC biomass accumulation and metabolism, with CS showing reduced biomass at the sampled points and both cultivars exhibiting broad metabolic reprogramming. Comparison with detached leaves revealed that CSCs do not recapitulate leaf metabolism globally, as evidenced by tissue-driven separation and limited m/z overlap. However, selected functional categories, particularly secondary metabolism, stress signaling, and redox-associated responses, showed partial convergence in a cultivar- and time-dependent manner, suggesting moderate functional overlap in response to jasmonic acid.

These findings support grapevine CSCs as complementary, controlled platforms to study selected inducible metabolic responses, particularly in screening contexts where reproducibility, scalability, and experimental control are important. At the same time, the CSC–leaf comparison should be interpreted within the scope of the experimental design: detached leaves provide an organ-level reference but not a substitute for intact plants, JA delivery differed between systems, and the metabolomic workflow captured the negative-ion-detectable metabolome with putative metabolite annotation. Future work including additional cultivars, elicitors, positive and negative ionization modes, targeted metabolite confirmation, and intact-plant validation will help define the broader applicability of this platform.
