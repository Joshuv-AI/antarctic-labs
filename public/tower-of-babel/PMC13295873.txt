Working years lost in people with epilepsy according to underlying aetiology: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Julie Werenberg Dreier, Betina B Trabjerg, Kasper Lolk, Oleguer Plana-Ripoll, Jakob Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe quantify the loss of working years for people with epilepsy according to underlying aetiology and compare to the general population.MethodsThis population-based cohort study included all individuals aged 18–65 years living in Denmark from 1995 to 2018. Using nationwide registers from 1977 onward, we identified people with epilepsy and grouped them by presumed aetiology (secondary: infection, perinatal insult, congenital malformation, brain tumour, traumatic brain injury, stroke or unknown) and obtained annual data on primary income source during 1995–2020. The main outcome was working years lost comparing people with epilepsy to the general population, reflecting losses of working life from permanent (death, disability pension, early retirement) and temporary (unemployment, sick leave) factors.ResultsThe study included 5 466 144 individuals, with 48 223 (0.9%) having epilepsy of unknown aetiology and 26 754 (0.5%) with secondary epilepsy. Compared with the general population, post-traumatic epilepsy was associated with the greatest average loss of working life (10.2 years, 95% CI 9.9 to 10.4), whereas brain tumour-related epilepsy was associated with a reduction of 9.1 years (95% CI 8.8 to 9.4) and post-stroke epilepsy with a reduction of 6.6 years (95% CI 6.4 to 6.8). Epilepsy of unknown aetiology was associated with a 5.4-year loss (95% CI 5.2 to 5.5). Across all epilepsy types, most reductions in working life resulted from higher rates of disability pension and premature death.ConclusionsEpilepsy, especially when secondary to an underlying brain insult, was associated with significant loss of working life from both disability and premature death. More support is needed to help people with epilepsy secure and retain employment.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a condition with considerable heterogeneity in both aetiology and clinical course, and taking this variability into consideration is important when assessing the consequences of epilepsy. In a recent population-based study from Denmark, we showed that working life is on average 6.6 years shorter in people with epilepsy than in the general population,1 but also found substantial variation, e.g. between sexes, across age of epilepsy onset and for certain epilepsy subgroups. Notably, we observed that loss of working life was more pronounced among those with epilepsy secondary to a presumed underlying aetiology (‘secondary epilepsy’) than among those with unknown aetiology (9.0 vs 5.4 years). Given the diverse nature of secondary epilepsies, we now extend these findings by quantifying working years lost for people with epilepsy according to specific underlying aetiologies (infection, perinatal insult, congenital malformation, brain tumour, traumatic brain injury, stroke) compared with the general population.

Methods

This population-based cohort study was based on the Danish nation-wide administrative social and health registers,24 and included all individuals aged 18–65 years living in Denmark between 1995 and 2018 and who had not yet permanently left the labour market. The methodology and procedures of the present study follow those of the original paper1 where they are described in detail. Here, we briefly outline the methods.

Epilepsy by underlying aetiology

People with epilepsy were identified using diagnostic information from all in-patient (since 1977) and emergency department and outpatient visits (since 1995) to Danish hospitals.3 We considered a person to have secondary epilepsy if the first epilepsy diagnosis was preceded by a potential cause of epilepsy5 (see list of relevant International Classification of Diseases, 8th revision (ICD-8) and 10th revision (ICD-10) codes in online supplemental table 1), and identified six major types of secondary epilepsies: postinfection epilepsy, epilepsy following a perinatal insult, epilepsy with underlying congenital malformation, brain tumour-related epilepsy, post-traumatic epilepsy and poststroke epilepsy. When there was no preceding epileptogenic condition, we considered the epilepsy as being of unknown aetiology.6

Labour market affiliation

Annual data on primary income source or employment during 1995–2020 were obtained from the Employment Classification Module from Statistics Denmark,4 and for each year, every person was classified into one of the following categories: active (actively working, under education), temporary absence (leave, unemployment) and permanent premature exit (voluntary early retirement, disability pension, death). Additional details on classification of labour market affiliation are available in online supplemental table 2.

Statistical analyses

The main outcome of interest was excess working years lost in people with epilepsy, which was estimated for the six major types of secondary epilepsy and for epilepsy of unknown aetiology, capturing years lost from both premature permanent exit and temporary absence from labour market.7 This metric represents the average excess years individuals with epilepsy are neither employed nor in education compared with the general population of same sex and age. Excess working years lost were derived by first estimating remaining working life expectancy at epilepsy onset (and at the same age in the same-sex general population),8 using methods analogous to life-expectancy estimation, and decomposing losses into specific types of workforce disruption with a competing-risks model.9 We then divided expected remaining working life into active and temporarily inactive periods. Finally, we calculated excess losses by subtracting working years lost in people with epilepsy from those in the general population and estimated 95% CIs using non-parametric bootstrapping with 1000 iterations.1 7

Results

The study comprised 5 466 144 individuals, including 48 223 (0.9%) persons with epilepsy of unknown aetiology and 26 754 (0.5%) with secondary epilepsy. The most common underlying potential cause for epilepsy was traumatic brain injury (n=7577), followed by congenital malformation (n=5139), perinatal insult (n=5042), stroke (n=4611), brain tumour (n=3098) and infection (n=1287). The age at epilepsy onset, and hence the residual years of working life potentially lost, varied according to epilepsy aetiology. For instance, the median age at onset (IQR) was lowest for epilepsy following a perinatal insult (8.4 years, IQR: 3.1–15.8 years) and epilepsy with an underlying congenital malformation (15.5 years, IQR: 7.2–26.6 years) and highest for brain tumour-related epilepsy (49.4 years, IQR: 38.6–56.9 years) and poststroke epilepsy (52.5 years, IQR: 43.6–58.5 years). The median (IQR) time between the most recent underlying cause and secondary epilepsy was 1.1 years (0.0–7.5 years).

Active workforce participation and working years lost

The average expected years of active workforce participation after onset of secondary epilepsy was 9.3 years (95% CI 9.2 to 9.5), which on average was 9.0 years (95% CI 8.9 to 9.2) shorter than in people in the general population of same age and sex (figure 1). Years of active workforce participation was most reduced following post-traumatic epilepsy (10.2 years, 95% CI 9.9 to 10.5) and for secondary epilepsies with typical onset at younger ages, when there are also more potential years of working life to lose; that is, epilepsy following a perinatal insult (9.7 years, 95% CI 6.2 to 12.8) and with underlying congenital malformation (9.7 years, 95% CI 9.1 to 10.2). However, due to small numbers at risk in older age groups for these subsets, estimates should be interpreted with caution. For both brain tumour-related epilepsy and poststroke epilepsy, where the median age at epilepsy onset was around 50 years, the average expected remaining years of active workforce participation was only 4.0 (95% CI 3.8 to 4.2) and 4.4 (95% CI 4.2 to 4.6) years, respectively, which corresponded to a reduction of 9.1 (95% CI 8.8 to 9.4) years for brain-tumour related epilepsy and 6.6 (95% CI 6.4 to 6.8) years for poststroke epilepsy, when compared with the general population (figure 1). The average time working or studying following onset of epilepsy of unknown aetiology was 5.4 years (95% CI 5.2 to 5.5) shorter than in the general population of same age and sex.

Causes of working years lost

The average excess loss of working years lost due to temporary absence and permanent premature labour market exit are shown for people with the six types of secondary epilepsy and the general population in online supplemental table 3. As for people with epilepsy in general, the major causes of excess loss of working years in people with secondary epilepsy were disability pension (6.6 years, 95% CI 6.5 to 6.8) and premature death (2.6 years, 95% CI 2.5 to 2.8); whereas only a smaller proportion was lost in excess due to unemployment (0.4 years, 95% CI 0.3 to 0.4) and leave (0.1 years, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.1) (online supplemental table 3). While this pattern was generally observed across all groups of secondary epilepsies, there was also some variation. For instance, for brain tumour-related epilepsy, the most significant cause for reduction in working life was premature death (6.9 years, 95% CI 6.6 to 7.3) and for post-traumatic epilepsy, the average excess loss of working life due to unemployment (1.4 years, 95% CI 1.3 to 1.5) was greater than for any other type of secondary epilepsy.

Discussion

This study extends previous work,1 by quantifying reductions in working life associated with six major types of secondary epilepsy, and suggests that epilepsy, particularly when secondary to an underlying brain insult, is associated with substantial loss of working years from both disability and premature death.

Our findings show that secondary epilepsy overall was associated with an average reduction of 9 years of working life compared with the general population, covering variation across specific types of secondary epilepsy, from 6.6 years for poststroke epilepsy to 10.2 years for post-traumatic epilepsy. However, direct comparison of these estimates warrants caution. First, the potential years of working life to lose depend directly on age at onset, which varies widely across the six secondary epilepsy groups. For example, although poststroke epilepsy was associated with the smallest absolute loss (6.6 years), this represents a 60% relative reduction of residual years of active working life (4.4 years vs 11.0 years in the general population) after epilepsy onset, exceeding that of most other groups. Second, secondary epilepsies arise from underlying conditions that themselves may reduce working life to differing degrees. Our data could not disentangle these contributions. While one could estimate excess working years lost, for eg, traumatic brain injury or stroke alone, comparing those figures to post-traumatic or poststroke epilepsy is problematic. Individuals who develop epilepsy after stroke or injury likely differ from those who do not, for instance in terms of insult severity and survival, and people with post-traumatic epilepsy and post-stroke epilepsy consequently represent selected subsets of persons who survived the initial event but sustained longer-term sequelae, limiting direct comparability. Another limitation of the present study was the inability to confirm the aetiology of individual epilepsy cases. Instead, we inferred aetiology based on prior diagnoses of known epilepsy causes, which may have led to misclassification by attributing epilepsy to an earlier insult that could be unrelated to the development of epilepsy.

In our cohort, traumatic brain injury was the most common identified cause of secondary epilepsy, which likely reflects characteristics of both the study population and methods. First, the distribution of epilepsy aetiologies varies by age,6 with post-traumatic epilepsy comprising a large proportion of secondary cases in mid-life; thus, restricting the cohort to working-age individuals likely increased its relative prominence. Second, our definition of traumatic brain injury included mild cases (ie, concussion), which despite their lower individual risk of epilepsy than severe injuries, contributes to a substantial absolute number of post-traumatic epilepsy cases due to the higher frequency. Finally, we did not restrict the interval between the injury and epilepsy onset, since evidence suggests that epilepsy risk remains elevated for more than a decade after the trauma.10 11 Together, these factors likely contributed to the relatively high proportion of post-traumatic epilepsy observed in our study.

While loss of working years associated with specific types of secondary epilepsy has not previously been reported, some,12 13 but not all,14 previous studies have reported higher unemployment rates in secondary epilepsies than in other epilepsy types, but the evidence remains sparse. There is, however, robust data showing that certain underlying causes of epilepsy, such as traumatic brain injury and stroke, are associated with substantial cognitive and psychiatric sequelae,15 16 which likely contribute to the loss of working life observed in post-traumatic and poststroke epilepsy.

Conclusions

This study highlights that epilepsy, especially when secondary to an underlying brain insult, is associated with substantial reductions in working life expectancy. Our findings indicate an urgent need to reduce mortality and disability in people with epilepsy and to provide stronger support for securing and maintaining employment.
