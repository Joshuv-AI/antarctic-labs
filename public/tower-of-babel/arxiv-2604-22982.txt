Abstract
. Triple differences (DDD) is a workhorse quasi-experimental design in applied economics. But, under staggered adoption, its conventional three-way fixed-effects (3WFE) implementation inherits the forbidden-comparison and interpretation issues now well understood in the difference-in-differences literature. To resolve these issues, I introduce stacked DDD . I extend the stacked difference-in-differences approach to the DDD setting by creating self-contained stacks, each consisting of four cells over an event window: treated and clean comparison cohorts, each with treatment-eligible and treatment-ineligible units. Appending these stacks yields a unified dataset for estimating treatment effects without making forbidden comparisons. I prove that, at each post-treatment event-time, a linear regression with fully saturated fixed-effects applied to the stacked dataset identifies a strictly positive, cell-size-weighted average of stack-level conditional average treatment effects, with stack weights proportional to stack-level cell sizes. Building on this characterization, I outline alternative weighting schemes that recover distinct, transparent causal estimands with clear interpretations. Stacked DDD complements recent GMM and imputation-based frameworks by trading efficiency for regression-based transparency, pairwise (rather than global) parallel trends, and direct control over aggregation weights. I provide two empirical illustrations where stacked DDD yields substantially different quantitative conclusions compared to existing procedures.



1.
Motivation and Introduction

Triple differences (DDD) is among the most widely used quasi-experimental research designs in applied economics. In settings where treatment requires satisfying two criteria---belonging to a group whose members become exposed to a policy and being eligible within that group---DDD permits both group-specific and eligibility-specific departures from parallel trends, and is therefore a more defensible identification strategy than standard difference-in-differences (DiD) whenever either margin alone is implausibly parallel. This flexibility explains its prominence in public, labor, health, and environmental economics.

This paper develops the
stacked triple-differences (stacked DDD)

Second, create a dataset for each treated cohort and its clean comparison group; this is called a stack. Finally, concatenate all stacks into a unified dataset, which I call the stacked dataset. By construction, in the DDD setting, there are both treatment-eligible and treatment-ineligible units within both treatment and control groups in each stack.

Estimating treatment effects on the stacked dataset then exploits three sources of identifying variation simultaneously. First, within each treated group, eligible units are subject to the policy while ineligible units are not, so differencing them removes any confounding trend common to the group regardless of eligibility status. Second, across the treatment and clean comparison groups, differencing outcomes removes eligibility-specific shocks that would be shared by eligible units in both cohorts. Third, difference post- and pre-treatment outcomes then isolates the treatment effects within each group-by-eligibility cell. Restricting all three comparisons to uncontaminated units, the stacked construction delivers a
2\times 2\times 2
sub-experiment for every cohort.

2

2

I will use “stack” and “cohort” interchangeably throughout, which are the nomenclature of the stacked DiD and staggered DiD literature, respectively.

A distinctive feature of DDD is that units apparently lacking direct identifying power nonetheless play an essential role for identification. The ineligible units in the comparison cohort—neither treated nor eligible—anchor the baseline of the clean comparison group, and it is this anchor that permits the third difference to separate the treatment effect from an eligibility-specific trend. Without them, a researcher cannot distinguish a genuine treatment effect from a group-specific shock to eligible units in the treated cohort. The identifying content in the stacked DDD framework is, therefore, present by design.

A central question in the stacked DDD framework is identification of causal estimands.
In practice, it is of interest to characterize the estimand that an event-study OLS regression targets when applied on the stacked dataset.
As a starting point, I first characterize the estimand targeted by the standard DDD event-study OLS regression with unit, group-by-time, and eligibility-by-time fixed effects, and where the treatment timings are staggered. I show that its event-study coefficient at event-time
e
is a linear combination of cohort- and event-time-specific conditional average treatment effects on the treated: the own-event-time cohort weights sum to one but are not guaranteed to be non-negative for individual treatment cohorts, and the weights on other event-times
e^{\prime}\neq e
are generically nonzero. Only under homogeneous treatment effects across cohorts does this estimand collapse to the conventional ATT; outside this case, the coefficient admits negative-weight contamination of the kind documented for DiD by
12
.

In the stacked DDD framework, the answer parallels
25

(
25
)
analysis of DiD: at each post-treatment event-time
e
, the fully saturated OLS regression on the stacked panel targets a strictly positive, cell-size-weighted convex combination of cohort-specific conditional average treatment effects on the treated.
The implied weights from fully saturated OLS regression are a function of cell sizes within each stack, therefore this estimator assigns large weights to stacks whose stack-level treatment effect estimates are likely more precise.

However, it is not clear that the weights implied by fully saturated OLS regression estimated on the stacked dataset lead to a causally interesting aggregation of cohort-level treatment effects. Building on this characterization, I describe alternative aggregation schemes that can be applied to stacks to target estimands with more explicit population causal interpretations: cohort-size weights, which deliver a per-unit average treatment effect on the treated; and equal weights, which deliver a simple average of cohort-level average treatment effects on the treated. The stacked DDD framework, by design, addresses a concern raised by
8
and
9

The stacked design also weakens the identifying assumption: parallel trends need hold only pairwise within each stack, between the treated group and its clean comparison group, and not globally across all pairs of treated and control groups. This is a weaker restriction than the global DDD parallel trends assumed by pooled estimators.
In the stacked DDD framework, estimation of the causal parameters of interest follow from (weighted) fully saturated OLS regressions, and the asymptotic theory is large-
n
regime with both the numbers of time and stacks held fixed. I further show that because the stacked estimator is exactly recovered by a fully saturated OLS regression, applying the cluster-robust standard errors
clustered at the level of treatment

Two empirical applications illustrate the practical consequences from applying stacked DDD. First, applied to
14

23

In both applications, the stacked decomposition discloses which stack-level conditional average treatment effect on the treated drive the aggregated effect.

2.2.
Identification Assumptions

I impose four assumptions in the ensuing identification analyses. The first three assumptions are standard in the difference-in-differences literature. The fourth is specific to the DDD setting.

Assumption 2.1
(Random Sampling)
.

The observed data
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent and identically distributed:

(2.5)

\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}}}{{\sim}}F_{W},

where
W_{i}=(\{Y_{i,t}\}_{t=1}^{T},S_{i},Q_{i},X_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\mathcal{S}\times\{0,1\}\times\mathcal{X}
and
F_{W}
is the population distribution with
\mathbb{E}\|W_{i}\|^{2}<\infty
.

2.1
requires that the observed data consist of an i.i.d. draw from the joint distribution of outcomes across all time periods, the treatment-enabling group indicator
S_{i}
, the eligibility indicator
Q_{i}
, and pre-treatment covariates
X_{i}
. This assumption is standard in the panel data literature and is automatically satisfied when units are sampled randomly from the population of interest. It rules out spatial or network dependence across units but can be relaxed to allow for cluster-level dependence when inference is conducted at the cluster level.

Assumption 2.2
(Overlap)
.

Every cell in the group-by-eligibility partition has positive probability:

(2.6)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathcal{S},\\
q\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q)>0~.

2.2
ensures that every cell in the
S\times Q
partition of units is populated. This is the DDD analogue of the standard overlap (or positivity) condition in the treatment effects literature, stated unconditionally. It requires that no enabling group or eligibility status has zero probability in the population. When covariates are incorporated (Appendix
D
), the overlap condition is strengthened to a conditional version requiring that the generalized propensity scores
\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q\mid X_{i}=x)
are bounded away from zero uniformly over the covariate support.

Assumption 2.3
(No Anticipation)
.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
and
t<g
:

(2.7)

Y_{i,t}(g)=Y_{i,t}(\infty)\quad\text{almost surely.}

2.3
requires that in periods before treatment is enabled for cohort
g
, the potential outcomes under treatment and under no treatment coincide. That is, units do not alter their behavior in anticipation of future treatment. This assumption is standard in the difference-in-differences literature
(
4
)
and is plausible in settings where the exact timing of treatment enablement is not known in advance or where institutional constraints prevent anticipatory behavior.

Assumption 2.4
(DDD Parallel Changes-in-Trends (DDD-PCT))
.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
, all valid comparison groups
g_{c}>g
, and all
t\in\{2,\ldots,T\}
with
t\leq g_{c}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,t-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,t-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,t-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,t-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=0]~.

Assumption 2.5
(No Spillover)
.

For every
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
and every unit
i
with
S_{i}=g
and
Q_{i}=0
, and for all
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
,

(2.9)

Y_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}(\infty)\quad\text{almost surely,}

regardless of the treatment status of eligible units in the same enabling group.

Assumption 2.6
(Admissibility of Comparison Cohorts)
.

For every treated cohort
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
, the set of admissible comparison cohorts

(2.10)

\mathcal{C}(g)\equiv\bigl\{g_{c}\in\mathcal{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g_{c}>g+K~\text{ and }~\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q,G_{i}=g_{c})>0~\text{ for all }~(s,q)\in\{0,1\}^{2}\bigr\}

is non-empty.

2.4
is the core identifying assumption of the stacked DDD framework. It requires that the difference in untreated outcome trends between eligible (
Q_{i}=1
) and ineligible (
Q_{i}=0
) units is the same across the treated cohort
g
and its comparison cohort
g_{c}
. This is a weaker condition than the parallel trends assumptions required in standard DiD settings.
In particular,
2.4
permits group-specific trends
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,t-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g]
to differ freely across enabling groups
g
, which is precisely the type of heterogeneity that invalidates parallel trends in DiD settings. It also permits eligibility-specific trends to differ between eligible and ineligible units within the same treatment-enabling group. What is required is that this within-group differential trend be the same across groups.

2.4
as stated is already pairwise: it is indexed by a single treated cohort
g
and a single comparison cohort
g_{c}
, and the identifying restriction is imposed one pair at a time. A pooled DDD regression compares eligible units against a single fixed comparison pool common to every treated cohort, which amounts to requiring (
2.8
) to hold simultaneously across every
(g,g_{c})
pair the pooled estimator mixes; that is the joint restriction the stacked design relaxes. The stacked estimator, by contrast, invokes (
2.8
) only for the
(g,g_{c}(g))
pairs actually used to build each stack, and different stacks may use different
g_{c}
.

2.5
is the SUTVA-type restriction that makes the within-group comparison of eligible and ineligible units a clean third difference. It fails under within-group spillovers (e.g. general equilibrium effects) that would alter the outcomes of ineligibles once eligibles are treated. When this assumption is suspect, the stacked DDD design must either be reframed around a comparison group that is unambiguously outside the spillover’s reach, or supplemented with a spillover-robust correction such as partial-population designs.

Finally,
2.6
states that for every treated cohort
g
, there is at least one cohort
g_{c}
that (i) remains untreated throughout the event window
[g-L,g+K]
, and (ii) has positive mass in every
(S,Q)
-cell, and the combination of the two conditions guarantees that DDD within stack
g
is well-defined. When there are never-treated groups, condition (i) holds automatically.

When pre-treatment covariates
X_{i}

\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i,g+e}(g)-Y_{i,g+e}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1\right]~.

The CATT parameterization indexes treatment effects by (cohort, exposure duration) rather than (cohort, time), which is natural for studying how effects evolve with time since treatment onset, and the HW event-study specification (

Proposition 3.1
(Weights from auxiliary regression)

The coefficients

Remark 3.1

(Variance-ratio representation of the weights)
.

The matrix-form weight (

3.4

\omega_{g,\ell}^{j,\star}

for

\omega_{g,\ell}^{j,\star}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{R}_{j}(i,t)R_{g,\ell}(i,t)\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{R}_{j}(i,t)^{2}\right]}~.

{\omega_{g,\ell}^{j,\star}=\frac{p_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{j;g,1}^{(g+\ell)}}{\sigma_{j}^{2,\star}}}

Remark 3.2

(Why the partial residual can be negative)
.

The partial residual
\widetilde{R}_{j}(i,t)
is obtained from the aggregate indicator
R_{j}(i,t)=\mathbb{1}\{t-S_{i}=j\}Q_{i}
by two successive projections. First, the three-way demeaning (

) removes unit effects, group-by-time effects, and eligibility-by-time effects, yielding the three-way-demeaned indicator
\ddot{R}_{j}(i,t)

\{\ddot{R}_{e^{\prime}}\}_{e^{\prime}\neq j,-1}
is subtracted, yielding
\widetilde{R}_{j}(i,t)=\ddot{R}_{j}(i,t)-\sum_{e^{\prime}\neq j,-1}\widehat{\gamma}_{j,e^{\prime}}\ddot{R}_{e^{\prime}}(i,t)

\widehat{\gamma}_{j,e^{\prime}}
is the population regression coefficient from the projection.

Consider a treated-eligible unit in cohort
g
at time

with
\ell\neq j
. At this time, the unit is at event-time
\ell
relative to its own treatment, not at event-time
j

is zero for this unit (since

can be nonzero because the demeaning subtracts group-time and eligibility-time means that are contaminated by other cohorts. After further partialling out other event-time indicators,
\widetilde{R}_{j}(i,g+\ell)
can take either sign, depending on the correlation structure between
\ddot{R}_{j}
and
\ddot{R}_{e^{\prime}}
at that time.

The sign of
\widetilde{r}_{j;g,1}^{(g+\ell)}
is driven by the calendar-time composition of the design. Under staggered adoption, at any given time

, different cohorts occupy different positions on the event-time axis. The indicator
R_{j}(i,t)
lights up for the cohort

satisfying

(if such a cohort exists). After demeaning and partialling out, the residual at this time reflects the deviation of cohort
g_{t}

Proposition 3.2

3.4.
The Aggregated ATT

Applied researchers often summarize the event study by computing an aggregated ATT, averaging the post-treatment coefficients with researcher-chosen weights. Define

(3.18)

\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{\mathrm{agg}}=\sum_{j=0}^{K}w_{j}\widehat{\alpha}_{j}~,

where

are non-negative weights with
\sum_{j=0}^{K}w_{j}=1
. Common choices are equal weights (

) or exposure-duration-specific weights. The following proposition characterizes the probability limit of this estimand.

Proposition 3.5
(Aggregated ATT estimand)
.

Under
2.1
–
2.4
, the aggregated ATT (
3.18
) satisfies

(3.19)

\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{\mathrm{agg}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\Omega_{g,\ell}\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)~,

where the aggregated weights are

(3.20)

\Omega_{g,\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{K}w_{j}\omega_{g,\ell}^{j,\star}~.

The aggregated weights

need not be non-negative, but they satisfy the normalization

(3.21)

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\Omega_{g,\ell}=1~.

4.
Estimands, and What Does Stacked OLS Identify?

Before developing the formal identification and estimation theory, I analyze the regression specifications that applied researchers commonly use for triple differences. This section characterizes exactly what each specification targets, under what conditions it recovers an interpretable causal parameter, and where the standard approach breaks down. I begin by deriving the two regression specifications that correctly target the causal estimands of interest, providing rigorous proofs for each claim. I then analyze commonly used specifications that fail to recover these estimands, diagnosing the precise source of each failure. Throughout this section I draw explicit parallels with the interaction-weighted framework of
25
for difference-in-differences and the decomposition results of
8
. The analysis proceeds without covariates; the covariate-adjusted framework is developed in Appendix
D
.

4.1.
Stacked sub-experiments

The central construction of the stacked DDD approach is that of the stacked sub-experiment. The following definition makes this precise.

Definition 4.1

(Stacked Sub-Experiment)
.

For each treatment cohort
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
and comparison group
g_{c}\in\mathcal{S}
with
g_{c}>g
, the
g
-specific stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}=\mathbb{S}(g,g_{c},L,K)
consists of:

(1)

Event window

\{g-L,\ldots,g-1,g,\ldots,g+K\}
, where
L\geq 1
pre-treatment and
K\geq 0
post-treatment periods.

(2)

Treated units
Units with
S_{i}=g
and
Q_{i}=1
(the cohort receiving treatment);

(3)

Within-group controls
Units with
S_{i}=g
and
Q_{i}=0
(same enabling group, ineligible);

(4)

Clean comparison group, eligible
Units with
S_{i}=g_{c}
and
Q_{i}=1
;

(5)

Clean comparison group, ineligible
Units with
S_{i}=g_{c}
and
Q_{i}=0
;

The clean comparison group satisfies
g_{c}>g+K
, so that no unit in the comparison group is treated during the time window of the stack.

Each stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
is a self-contained
2\times 2\times 2
-like experiment. The four cells are defined by the interaction of two binary dimensions:

(4.1)

\underbracket{(S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1)}_{\text{treated}},\quad\underbracket{(S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{within-group}\\
\text{control}\end{subarray}},\quad\underbracket{(S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{comparison}\\
\text{eligible}\end{subarray}},\quad\underbracket{(S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=0)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{comparison}\\
\text{ineligible}\end{subarray}}.

4.2.
Estimands of Interest

I now define the target parameters for the stacked DDD framework, building from cohort-specific treatment effects to aggregated event-study parameters.

Group-time average treatment effect.

The fundamental building block is the group-time average treatment effect on the treated. For each treatment cohort
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
and post-treatment period
t\geq g
, define

(4.2)

\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i,t}(g)-Y_{i,t}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1\right].

This is the average causal effect of treatment for units in cohort
g
who are eligible (
Q_{i}=1
), measured at time
t
. The parameter
\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)
is indexed by both the treatment cohort and time, and thus accommodates heterogeneity in treatment effects across cohorts and over time. This quantity is the DDD analogue of the group-time ATT defined by
4
in the standard DiD setting. Finally, note that
\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)=\mathrm{ATT}(g,g+e)
under the identity
t=g+e
, so the two parameters refer to the same causal contrast.

Remark 4.1

(Heterogeneous treatment effects)
.

The parameter
\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)
averages over the joint distribution of individual treatment effects for treated-eligible units in cohort
g
. No assumption of constant treatment effects within a cohort-time cell is imposed. Individual effects
Y_{i,t}(g)-Y_{i,t}(\infty)
may vary arbitrarily across units sharing the same
(g,t,X_{i})
: the framework permits unrestricted within-cohort heterogeneity. This stands in contrast to the three-way fixed effects regression (
E.1
) in
Appendix

E
, which imposes a single coefficient
\theta
and thereby requires treatment effect homogeneity across all
(g,t)
pairs.

\diamondsuit

Within a given stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}=\mathbb{S}(g,g_{c},L,K)
, I define the
stack-specific treatment effect

(4.3)

\mathrm{ATT}_{\mathbb{S}_{g},g_{c}}(g,t)~,

which is the DDD estimand formed from the four cells in (
4.1
) within the stack’s time window. This is the difference-in-difference-in-differences contrast:

(4.4)

\mathrm{ATT}_{\mathbb{S}_{g},g_{c}}(g,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0\right]\\
-\Big\{\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=1\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=0\right]\Big\},

where
\Delta Y_{i,t}=Y_{i,t}-Y_{i,g-1}
is the outcome change relative to the last pre-treatment period.

Applied researchers typically summarize treatment effects along the event-time axis. For event-time
e=t-g
(periods relative to treatment onset), define the population event-study parameter

(4.5)

\mathrm{ES}(e)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\
g+e\in\{1,\ldots,T\}\end{subarray}}\omega_{g}(e)\mathrm{ATT}(g,g+e),\qquad e\geq 0,

where
\omega_{g}(e)\geq 0
are aggregation weights satisfying
\sum_{g}\omega_{g}(e)=1
for each
e
. Different weight choices yield different summary parameters.

The stacked event-study estimand aggregates stack-level treatment effect estimates across treated cohorts, which is defined as

(4.6)

\mathrm{ES}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\
g+e\in\{g-L,\ldots,g+K\}\end{subarray}}\omega_{g}(e)\mathrm{ATT}_{\mathbb{S}_{g},g_{c}(g)}(g,g+e),

where
g_{c}(g)
denotes the comparison group used for cohort
g
and the summation ranges over cohorts for which event-time
e
falls within the stack’s window. The constraint
-L\leq e\leq K
restricts attention to event-times within the symmetric window shared by all stacks.

Remark 4.2

(Regression interpretation)
.

The DDD estimand (
4.4
) has an exact regression counterpart. Within each stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
, the fully saturated OLS regression of
\Delta Y_{i,t}
on the four cell indicators—intercept, group indicator
\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g\}
, eligibility indicator
\mathbb{1}\{Q_{i}=1\}
, and their interaction
\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1\}
—recovers the sample triple difference as the coefficient on the interaction term. This equivalence means that applied researchers can implement the stacked DDD estimator by running a separate OLS regression within each stack and aggregating the resulting coefficients. The key requirement is that each stack-level regression must be fully saturated in the cell indicators; imposing common slopes across cells or pooling across stacks with a single treatment coefficient alters the target estimand, as I explore in Appendix
D
.

\diamondsuit

4.3.
The Saturated Within-Stack Regression

Consider a single stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
with four cells defined by
(S_{i},Q_{i})\in\{(g,1),(g,0),(g_{c},1),(g_{c},0)\}
. Write
n_{s,q}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q\}
for the number of units in cell
(s,q)
, and
n_{g}=\sum_{s,q}n_{s,q}
for the total number of units in the stack. For a fixed post-treatment period
t\geq g
, the fully saturated regression on long differences
\Delta Y_{i,t}
is

(4.7)

\Delta Y_{i,t}=\mu_{g,t}+\lambda_{g,t}\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g\}+\eta_{g,t}\mathbb{1}\{Q_{i}=1\}+\tau_{g,t}^{\mathrm{sat}}\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1\}+\varepsilon_{i,t}~.

The coefficients
(\mu_{g,t},\lambda_{g,t},\eta_{g,t},\tau_{g,t}^{\mathrm{sat}})
are subscripted by
(g,t)
to emphasize that they are population parameters specific to the stack built around cohort
g

(Saturated regression recovers the triple difference)
.

In regression (

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{g,t}

The saturated regression (

It is purely design-based; the four-cell structure of the stack, combined with the long-differencing that removes time-invariant heterogeneity, delivers the triple difference mechanically. 4.5.
Population estimands under different aggregation schemes

The stack DDD event-study estimator

\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widehat{\omega}_{g}(e)\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(g,g+e)

depends on the aggregation weights
\widehat{\omega}_{g}(e)\geq 0
,
\sum_{g}\widehat{\omega}_{g}(e)=1
. Different weight choices target different population parameters, and the stacked DDD framework makes these targets explicit. I now formally characterize what each weighting scheme identifies when the within-stack estimators are consistent for
\mathrm{ATT}(g,g+e)=\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)
(
Theorem

5.1
).

Cohort-size weights.

Set
\widehat{\omega}_{g}^{\mathrm{cohort}}(e)=n_{g,1}/\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}n_{g^{\prime},1}
, where
n_{g,1}
is the number of treated-eligible units in stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)=\{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}\mid g+e\leq T\}
is the set of cohorts observed at event-time
e
. The population analog is
\omega_{g}^{\mathrm{cohort}}(e)=\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=g\mid S_{i}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e),Q_{i}=1)
, the share of cohort
g
among treated-eligible units observed at event-time
e
. The resulting estimand is the per-capita average treatment effect

(4.16)

\mathrm{ES}^{\mathrm{cohort}}(e)\equiv\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\omega_{g}^{\mathrm{cohort}}(e)\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{ATT}(G_{i},G_{i}+e)\mid G_{i}+e\leq T,Q_{i}=1\right]~,

which averages the cohort-specific effects weighted by the number of treated-eligible individuals in each cohort. This parameter has a direct welfare interpretation as the average effect experienced by a randomly drawn treated-eligible unit at event-time
e
, and coincides with the average effect of switching defined and discussed in
Section

4.6
. It is the natural estimand when the policy question concerns aggregate impact—how much did the treated population benefit, on average? As
9
emphasize, this per-capita parameter is the most policy-relevant estimand in many applications, since it reflects the actual distribution of treatment effects across the affected population.

Equal weights.

Set
\omega_{g}^{\mathrm{eq}}(e)=1/|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)|
, assigning each cohort equal influence regardless of its sample size. The target is the simple average of cohort-specific effects

(4.17)

\mathrm{ES}^{\mathrm{eq}}(e)\equiv\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)~,

which treats each policy adoption event—each cohort’s experience of treatment—as an equally informative observation about the causal mechanism. This estimand answers the question “what is the average treatment effect across the distinct adoption episodes observed at event-time
e
?”, weighting each cohort’s evidence equally. It is appropriate when the research interest lies in the policy mechanism rather than the aggregate impact, or when one cohort is much larger than the others and the researcher does not want a single large cohort to dominate the estimate. Equal weights do not target a population-weighted parameter and should therefore be reported alongside cohort-size weights rather than as a primary specification.

Precision weights.

Set
\omega_{g}^{\mathrm{prec}}(e)=({n_{g}/\sigma_{g}^{2}(e)})/({\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}n_{g^{\prime}}/\sigma_{g^{\prime}}^{2}(e)})
, where
\widehat{\sigma}_{g}^{2}(e)
is a consistent estimator of the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(g,g+e)
. This inverse-variance weighting minimizes the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)
when the within-stack estimators are asymptotically independent (an approximation when stacks share comparison units; see Section
6
for the exact variance formula that accounts for shared controls). The population target of precision weights is

(4.18)

\mathrm{ES}^{\mathrm{prec}}(e)\equiv\frac{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\sigma_{g}^{-2}(e)\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)}{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\sigma_{g}^{-2}(e)}~,

where
\sigma_{g}^{2}(e)
is the asymptotic variance of the within-stack estimator. Unlike cohort-size and equal weights, the precision-weighted estimand depends on the data generating process through the variance terms
\sigma_{g}^{2}(e)
and therefore does not have a fixed causal interpretation that is invariant to the sampling design. In particular, two different samples from the same population will in general target different weighted averages of
\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)
. Precision weights are the statistically optimal choice for minimizing estimation error, but the resulting estimand is harder to interpret substantively. Researchers using precision weights should report the realized weight shares
\widehat{\omega}_{g}^{\mathrm{prec}}(e)
alongside point estimates.

General welfare weights.

The three schemes above are special cases of a general welfare-weighted aggregation. Suppose the researcher assigns welfare weights
v_{g}>0
reflecting the relative importance of cohort
g
’s treatment effect. The welfare-weighted event-study parameter is

(4.19)

\mathrm{ES}^{v}(e)=\frac{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}v_{g}\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)}{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}v_{g}}~.

The stacked DDD framework accommodates any choice of
v_{g}
through the aggregation step, a flexibility that enhances interpretability in the case of stacked DDD. Making the welfare weights explicit is a key advantage of the stacked approach, as it separates the statistical problem (consistent estimation of
\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)
) from the aggregation problem (which cohorts matter more for the policy question at hand). This transparency echoes the recommendation of

9

Remark 5.1

(Pairwise versus global parallel trends)
.

A key advantage of the stacked DDD framework is that

\diamondsuit

Remark 5.2

(Role of each cell in the stack)
.

Each of the four cells in the stack

1. Cohort-size weights.

so that each cohort’s contribution is proportional to the number of treated-eligible units it contains. This weighting targets the population event-study parameter
\mathrm{ES}(e)=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ATT}(G_{i},G_{i}+e)\mid G_{i}+e\in\{G_{i}-L,\ldots,G_{i}+K\}]
, which weights cohorts by their population shares. It is the natural choice when the goal is to estimate an average effect across the treated population.

2. Equal weights.

Set

(6.5)

\widehat{\omega}_{g}(e)=\frac{1}{\big|\{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}g^{\prime}+e\in\{g^{\prime}-L,\ldots,g^{\prime}+K\}\}\big|},

which assigns each cohort equal influence regardless of its sample size or estimation precision. Equal weighting is the most transparent aggregation scheme and places equal value on detecting treatment effects in small and large cohorts alike. However, it has no clear population interpretation and may be inefficient when cohort sizes vary substantially.

Remark 6.1
.

The choice among these two weights involves a familiar trade-off. Cohort-size weights have the most transparent causal interpretation, targeting the “per unit” (of treatment) ATT. Equal weights are the most transparent and are robust to concerns about one or two large cohorts dominating the aggregation, but they may sacrifice both efficiency and interpretability when cohort sizes are heterogeneous. In practice, I recommend reporting results under cohort-size weights as the primary specification, with equal weights as a robustness check. I note that precision weights are statistically efficient but may assign opaque weights that change across event-times, making causal interpretation difficult.

Remark 6.2

(Magnitude of cross-stack correlation)

The cross-stack covariance in (

In applications where the never-treated pool is large relative to the treated cohorts, these units receive small inverse-probability weights, and the cross-stack correlation is modest. Conversely, when the never-treated pool is small and each comparison unit receives substantial weight, the cross-stack dependence is non-negligible and ignoring it leads to confidence intervals that are too narrow.

Remark 6.3

(Multiplier bootstrap)
.

For uniform confidence bands across all event-times, we can implement the multiplier bootstrap proceeds as follows.
For each bootstrap replication

(i)

Draw i.i.d. multiplier weights
\{\xi_{i}^{(b)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from a distribution satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}]=0

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}]=1
, and
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{4}]<\infty

\xi_{i}\sim N(0,1)

\xi_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}

(ii)

Define

.

for each unit

Proof of

.

The proof follows the structure of
E.2
, with the three-way demeaning operator (
3.3
) replacing the two-way operator of the standard 3WFE.

Step 1. Population OLS formula via FWL. Collect the event-time indicators in the column vector
\mathbf{R}_{i,t}=(R_{e}(i,t))_{e\neq-1}^{\intercal}
and their coefficients in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{e})_{e\neq-1}^{\intercal}
. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem applied to (
3.2
), the population regression coefficient vector is

(A.1)

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{\intercal}\right]\right)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}\ddot{Y}_{i,t}\right]~.

The
j
-th component is
\alpha_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. The three-way demeaning operates via the inclusion-exclusion formula (
3.3
). For the event-time indicator
R_{e}(i,t)=\mathbb{1}\{t-S_{i}=e\}Q_{i}
, the demeaned version is

(A.2)

\ddot{R}_{e}(i,t)=R_{e}(i,t)-\overline{R}_{e,i,\cdot}-\overline{R}_{e,S_{i},t}-\overline{R}_{e,Q_{i},t}+\overline{R}_{e,S_{i},\cdot}+\overline{R}_{e,Q_{i},\cdot}+\overline{R}_{e,\cdot,t}-\overline{R}_{e,\cdot\cdot}~,

where
\overline{R}_{e,i,\cdot}=T^{-1}\sum_{t^{\prime}}R_{e}(i,t^{\prime})
is the unit-level time mean,
\overline{R}_{e,S_{i},t}=n_{S_{i}}^{-1}\sum_{j\colon S_{j}=S_{i}}R_{e}(j,t)
is the group-time mean,
\overline{R}_{e,Q_{i},t}=n_{Q_{i}}^{-1}\sum_{j\colon Q_{j}=Q_{i}}R_{e}(j,t)
is the eligibility-time mean, and the remaining barred quantities are the corresponding total means. This demeaning removes three channels of variation from
R_{e}
that the standard 3WFE demeaning (which omits the
\overline{R}_{e,Q_{i},t}
terms) leaves in.

I now compute the key group means explicitly. Fix a unit
i
with
S_{i}=g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
and
Q_{i}=1
(treated-eligible). The unit-level time mean is
\overline{R}_{e,i,\cdot}=T^{-1}\mathbb{1}\{g+e\in\{1,\ldots,T\}\}
, since
R_{e}(i,t^{\prime})=\mathbb{1}\{t^{\prime}=g+e\}Q_{i}
is nonzero at exactly one time period. The group-time mean is

(A.3)

\overline{R}_{e,g,t}=\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g,\cdot}}\mathbb{1}\{t=g+e\}~,

where
n_{g,1}
is the number of eligible units in group
g
and
n_{g,\cdot}=n_{g,1}+n_{g,0}
is the total number of units in group
g
. This is because
R_{e}(j,t)=\mathbb{1}\{S_{j}=g\}Q_{j}\mathbb{1}\{t=g+e\}
for
j
in group
g
, so summing over
j
in group
g
yields
n_{g,1}\mathbb{1}\{t=g+e\}
, divided by
n_{g,\cdot}
. The eligibility-time mean is

(A.4)

\overline{R}_{e,Q_{i}=1,t}=\frac{1}{n_{\cdot,1}}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}n_{g^{\prime},1}\mathbb{1}\{t=g^{\prime}+e\}~,

the fraction of eligible units whose event-time
e
falls at time
t
. For ineligible units (
Q_{i}=0
),
R_{e}(i,t)=0
identically, so all group means involving ineligible units are zero.

Step 2. Potential outcomes substitution and weight identification. The observed outcome decomposes as

selects treated-eligible units of cohort
g
at time
g+\ell

A.1
), by linearity of the demeaning operator

\displaystyle\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}\ddot{Y}_{i,t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}\ddot{Y}_{i,t}(\infty)\right]

(A.5)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\sum_{\ell}\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}R_{g,\ell}(i,t)(Y_{i,t}(g)-Y_{i,t}(\infty))\right]~.

For the second term, at time

selects only treated-eligible units in cohort

is the individual treatment effect. Taking the conditional expectation yields

(g,\ell)

(A.6)

\omega_{g,\ell}^{j,\star}=\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\intercal}\left(\sum_{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,t}^{\intercal}\right]\right)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\ddot{\mathbf{R}}_{i,g+\ell}R_{g,\ell}(i,g+\ell)\right]~.

This is identical in form to (

Property (iii). For

partition the data into independent blocks. The projection onto the space spanned by

operates block-diagonally, so that residualizing observation
(i,t,g)
against these fixed effects uses only data from stack
g
. Formally, for any variable
Z_{i,t,g}
, the residual after projecting out
\{\alpha_{g},\mu_{s,q,g}\}
is

\widetilde{Z}_{i,t,g}=Z_{i,t,g}-\frac{1}{n_{s(i),q(i)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\\
S_{j}=S_{i},Q_{j}=Q_{i}\end{subarray}}\!\!\overline{Z}_{j,\cdot,g}~,

where
\overline{Z}_{j,\cdot,g}=(L+K+1)^{-1}\sum_{t^{\prime}=g-L}^{g+K}Z_{j,t^{\prime},g}
and
s(i),q(i)
denote unit
i
’s cell membership. In particular, the residualization of observation
(i,t,g)
depends only on data within stack
g
and cell
(s(i),q(i))
.

Step 2. Residualize the treatment indicators. Consider the indicator
R_{g^{\prime},e}(i,t,g)=\mathbb{1}\{g=g^{\prime},S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=1,t=g^{\prime}+e\}
. This indicator is nonzero only for observations in stack
g^{\prime}
with
S_{i}=g^{\prime}
and
Q_{i}=1
at time
t=g^{\prime}+e
. By the block-diagonal structure, its residual
\widetilde{R}_{g^{\prime},e}(i,t,g)
is nonzero only within stack
g^{\prime}
. Moreover, within stack
g^{\prime}
,
R_{g^{\prime},e}
is nonzero only in cell
(g^{\prime},1)
at a single time period
t=g^{\prime}+e
, so the residualized indicator is orthogonal to all other residualized indicators
\widetilde{R}_{g^{\prime\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}
for
(g^{\prime\prime},e^{\prime\prime})\neq(g^{\prime},e)
.

Step 3. By the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem, the OLS coefficient
\widehat{\tau}_{g^{\prime},e}
from the pooled regression (
4.12
) equals the coefficient from the bivariate regression of
\widetilde{\Delta Y}_{i,t,g}
on
\widetilde{R}_{g^{\prime},e}(i,t,g)
. Since
\widetilde{R}_{g^{\prime},e}
is nonzero only within stack
g^{\prime}
and the residualized indicators are mutually orthogonal across
(g^{\prime},e)
pairs, this is equivalent to running a separate regression within stack
g^{\prime}
at time
t=g^{\prime}+e
. The residualized regression within stack
g^{\prime}
at time
t
reduces to the saturated regression (
4.7
)—the stack-by-cell fixed effects absorb the intercept, group indicator, and eligibility indicator, leaving only the interaction term. The resulting coefficient is exactly the within-stack triple difference
\widehat{\tau}_{g^{\prime},g^{\prime}+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}
.
∎

Proof of
4.3
.

The regression under analysis is the fully-saturated stacked event-study specification (
4.14
), restated here for reference:

\Delta Y_{i,t,g}=\lambda_{s,t,g}+\eta_{q,t,g}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e=-L\\
e\neq-1\end{subarray}}^{K}\tau_{e}\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1,t=g+e\}+\varepsilon_{i,t,g}~,

where
\lambda_{s,t,g}
is a fixed effect for group
s\in\{g,g_{c}\}
at time
t
in stack
g
, and
\eta_{q,t,g}
is a fixed effect for eligibility type
q\in\{0,1\}
at time
t
in stack
g
. Each observation
(i,t,g)
belongs to stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
, and the same unit
i
may appear in multiple stacks. The treatment indicators
\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1,t=g+e\}
are active only for treated-eligible units in the corresponding stack at the corresponding time. I derive the OLS coefficients
\widehat{\tau}_{e}
and their probability limits in three steps.

Step 1. Block-diagonal projection and strict orthogonality.
Let the full design matrix of fixed effects be
\mathbf{F}
, which contains indicators for every
(s,t,g)
and
(q,t,g)
combination. Because these indicators are fully interacted with stack
g
and time
t
, the projection matrix
\mathbf{M_{F}}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\mathbf{F})^{-}\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
operates block-diagonally by
(g,t)
. Let
\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,t,g)
denote the FWL residual of the event-time indicator
R_{e}(i,t,g)=\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1,t=g+e\}
. Since
R_{e}(i,t,g)
is non-zero only for observations in stack
g
at exactly time
t=g+e
, its residual
\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,t,g)
is strictly zero for all
t\neq g+e
and for all stacks other than
g
.

Consequently, for any two distinct event-times
e\neq e^{\prime}
, the residuals are orthogonal, i.e.

(B.11)

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\sum_{t}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,t,g)\widetilde{R}_{e^{\prime}}(i,t,g)=0~,

because they are non-zero in mutually exclusive time for any given stack. By the FWL theorem, this orthogonality implies that the multiple regression (
4.14
) separates into independent bivariate regressions of
\widetilde{\Delta Y}_{i,t,g}
on
\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,t,g)
for each event-time
e
.

Step 2. Computing the FWL residual within each stack.
By Step 1, the OLS estimator for
\tau_{e}
reduces to a bivariate FWL regression

(B.12)

\widehat{\tau}_{e}=\frac{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,g+e,g)\widetilde{\Delta Y}_{i,g+e,g}}{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,g+e,g)^{2}}~,

where the time summation collapses entirely to
t=g+e
because
\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,t,g)=0
for
t\neq g+e
. I now derive the FWL residual
\widetilde{R}_{e}
and the resulting weight formula in complete detail.

Step 2a. Structure of the residual. Fix a single stack
g
and time
t=g+e
. Within this block, there are
n_{g}
units partitioned into four cells
(s,q)\in\{(g,1),(g,0),(g_{c},1),(g_{c},0)\}
with cell sizes
n_{g,1},n_{g,0},n_{g_{c},1},n_{g_{c},0}
. The treatment indicator restricted to this block is
R_{e}(i)=\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1\}
, which takes the value
1
only for treated-eligible units and
0
otherwise. The fixed effects
\lambda_{s,t,g}
and
\eta_{q,t,g}
, restricted to this block, span three linearly independent regressors

\underbracket{1}_{\text{constant}}~,\qquad\underbracket{\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g\}}_{\text{group indicator}}~,\qquad\underbracket{\mathbb{1}\{Q_{i}=1\}}_{\text{eligibility indicator}}~.

Since
R_{e}(i)=\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g\}\mathbb{1}\{Q_{i}=1\}
and all three regressors are constant within each cell, the FWL residual
\widetilde{R}_{e}
is also constant within each cell. Write
\widetilde{r}_{s,q}
for the value of
\widetilde{R}_{e}
in cell
(s,q)
. The three orthogonality conditions defining the projection are

(B.13)

(O1)

\displaystyle\quad\textstyle\sum_{(s,q)}n_{s,q}\widetilde{r}_{s,q}=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{($\widetilde{R}_{e}\perp 1$)}~,

(B.14)

(O2)

\displaystyle\quad n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}+n_{g,0}\widetilde{r}_{g,0}=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{($\widetilde{R}_{e}\perp\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g\}$)}~,

(B.15)

(O3)

\displaystyle\quad n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}+n_{g_{c},1}\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},1}=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{($\widetilde{R}_{e}\perp\mathbb{1}\{Q_{i}=1\}$)}~.

These are three linear equations in four unknowns
(\widetilde{r}_{g,1},\widetilde{r}_{g,0},\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},1},\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},0})
, so the solution is determined up to a scalar multiple—precisely the one degree of freedom that the treatment indicator spans beyond the main effects.

Step 2b. Solving the orthogonality conditions. From (
B.14
), solving for
\widetilde{r}_{g,0}

(B.16)

\widetilde{r}_{g,0}=-\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g,0}}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}~.

From (
B.15
), solving for
\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},1}

(B.17)

\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},1}=-\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g_{c},1}}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}~.

Substituting both into (
B.13
) to solve for
\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},0}

\displaystyle n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}+n_{g,0}\left(-\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g,0}}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}\right)+n_{g_{c},1}\left(-\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g_{c},1}}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}\right)+n_{g_{c},0}\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},0}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}-n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}-n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}+n_{g_{c},0}\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},0}

\displaystyle=0~,

which gives

(B.18)

\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},0}=\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g_{c},0}}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}~.

Writing
c\equiv\widetilde{r}_{g,1}
for the as-yet-undetermined residual value in the treated-eligible cell, the four residual values are

(B.19)

\widetilde{r}_{g,1}=c~,\qquad\widetilde{r}_{g,0}=-\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g,0}}c~,\qquad\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},1}=-\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g_{c},1}}c~,\qquad\widetilde{r}_{g_{c},0}=\frac{n_{g,1}}{n_{g_{c},0}}c~.

The sign pattern
(+,-,-,+)
mirrors the triple-difference contrast—treated-eligible and comparison-ineligible receive positive residuals, while treated-ineligible and comparison-eligible receive negative residuals. The magnitude of each residual is inversely proportional to the cell size, reflecting the standard inverse-variance scaling of OLS. This parallels the DiD case analyzed by
25
, where the FWL residual of the treatment indicator in a two-cell setup has the sign pattern
(+,-)
; in the DDD setting, the additional eligibility dimension doubles the number of cells and produces the characteristic
(+,-,-,+)
pattern.

Step 2c. Residual sum of squares. The residual sum of squares of
\widetilde{R}_{e}
within stack
g
at time
g+e
is

\displaystyle V_{g,e}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,g+e,g)^{2}=\sum_{(s,q)}n_{s,q}\widetilde{r}_{s,q}^{2}

\displaystyle=n_{g,1}c^{2}+n_{g,0}\frac{n_{g,1}^{2}}{n_{g,0}^{2}}c^{2}+n_{g_{c},1}\frac{n_{g,1}^{2}}{n_{g_{c},1}^{2}}c^{2}+n_{g_{c},0}\frac{n_{g,1}^{2}}{n_{g_{c},0}^{2}}c^{2}

(B.20)

\displaystyle=c^{2}n_{g,1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n_{g,1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g,0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},0}}\right)~.

Step 2d. Pinning down
c
via the FWL orthogonality identity.
Let
M
denote the orthogonal projection onto the complement of the span of the fixed-effects design within stack
g
. By construction,
\widetilde{R}_{e}=MR_{e}
. Because
M
is symmetric and idempotent,

(B.21)

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)R_{e}(i)=R_{e}^{\top}MR_{e}=R_{e}^{\top}M^{\top}MR_{e}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)^{2}=V_{g,e}~.

Equation (
B.21
) is a generic property of orthogonal projection: it reflects the definition of
\widetilde{R}_{e}
as the FWL residual and does not invoke the value of any downstream regression coefficient. Because
R_{e}(i,t,g)=\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1,t=g+e\}
vanishes outside the treated-eligible cell at time
g+e
, the left-hand side of (
B.21
) evaluates directly by (
B.19
):

(B.22)

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)R_{e}(i)=n_{g,1}\widetilde{r}_{g,1}=n_{g,1}c~.

Combining (
B.21
)–(
B.22
) yields the linear identity

(B.23)

V_{g,e}=n_{g,1}c~.

Substituting the quadratic expression (
B.20
) for
V_{g,e}
into (
B.23
) and using
c\neq 0
(guaranteed under
2.2
, since all four cell sizes are strictly positive),

c^{2}n_{g,1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n_{g,1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g,0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},0}}\right)=n_{g,1}c\implies 1=n_{g,1}c\left(\frac{1}{n_{g,1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g,0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},0}}\right)~,

which pins down

(B.24)

c=\left[n_{g,1}\left(\frac{1}{n_{g,1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g,0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},0}}\right)\right]^{-1}~.

Substituting (
B.24
) into (
B.23
) delivers the closed-form for the residual variance within stack
g
:

(B.25)

V_{g,e}=n_{g,1}c=\left(\frac{1}{n_{g,1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g,0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},0}}\right)^{-1}~.

Because
\widetilde{R}_{e}
is orthogonal to the fixed effects,
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)\Delta Y_{i,g+e}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)\widetilde{\Delta Y}_{i,g+e}
. Expanding using the cell-constant structure (
B.19
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)\Delta Y_{i,g+e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(s,q)}n_{s,q}\widetilde{r}_{s,q}\overline{\Delta Y}_{s,q,g+e}

\displaystyle=cn_{g,1}\overline{\Delta Y}_{g,1,g+e}-cn_{g,1}\overline{\Delta Y}_{g,0,g+e}

\displaystyle\qquad-cn_{g,1}\overline{\Delta Y}_{g_{c},1,g+e}+cn_{g,1}\overline{\Delta Y}_{g_{c},0,g+e}

\displaystyle=cn_{g,1}\!\left(\overline{\Delta Y}_{g,1,g+e}-\overline{\Delta Y}_{g,0,g+e}-\overline{\Delta Y}_{g_{c},1,g+e}+\overline{\Delta Y}_{g_{c},0,g+e}\right)

(B.26)

\displaystyle=cn_{g,1}\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}~,

where the last equality invokes the triple-difference formula (
4.11
) from
4.1
. It is now a
consequence
of (
B.26
) and (
B.23
) that the within-stack bivariate regression coefficient recovers the saturated triple-difference estimator:

\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)\Delta Y_{i,g+e}}{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\widetilde{R}_{e}(i)^{2}}=\frac{cn_{g,1}\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}}{V_{g,e}}=\frac{cn_{g,1}\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}}{cn_{g,1}}=\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}~,

where the second equality uses (
B.23
).

Step 2e. Assembling the weighted average. The overall FWL estimator (
B.12
) pools across stacks. Because
\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,g+e,g)
has support only in stack
g
, the numerator and denominator decompose as sums of within-stack contributions. By (
B.26
), the within-stack numerator is
V_{g,e}\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}
(using
V_{g,e}=cn_{g,1}
). Therefore

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}V_{g,e}\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}}{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}V_{g,e}}=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}w_{g}^{\mathrm{FWL}}(e)\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}~,

where the FWL weights are

(B.27)

w_{g}^{\mathrm{FWL}}(e)=\frac{V_{g,e}}{\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}V_{g^{\prime},e}}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{n_{g,1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g,0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c},0}}\right)^{-1}}{\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\left(\frac{1}{n_{g^{\prime},1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g^{\prime},0}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c}(g^{\prime}),1}}+\frac{1}{n_{g_{c}(g^{\prime}),0}}\right)^{-1}}~.

Under
2.2
, all four cell sizes are strictly positive, implying
V_{g,e}>0
. Thus,
w_{g}^{\mathrm{FWL}}(e)\in(0,1)
and
\sum_{g}w_{g}^{\mathrm{FWL}}(e)=1
, which establishes property (i). Property (ii) follows directly, as
\widehat{\tau}_{e}
is isolated from any
\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{sat}}
for
e^{\prime}\neq e
.

Step 3.
To derive the probability limit, scale the numerator and denominator of the weights by the total sample size
N
. Let the scaled residual variance be
\widehat{v}_{g,e}=V_{g,e}/N
. Under
2.1
, sample cell proportions converge to their strictly positive population counterparts by WLLN, meaning
\widehat{v}_{g,e}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}v_{g,e}^{\ast}>0
.
By
4.1
, under
2.1
–
2.6
, each within-stack estimator is consistent for its causal estimand, i.e.
\widehat{\tau}_{g,g+e}^{\mathrm{sat}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\mathrm{ATT}(g,g+e)\equiv\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)
. Applying Slutsky’s theorem and the continuous mapping theorem to the ratio of consistent estimators yields

(B.28)

\widehat{\tau}_{e}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\left(\frac{v_{g,e}^{\ast}}{\sum_{g^{\prime}}v_{g^{\prime},e}^{\ast}}\right)\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)\equiv\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}w_{g}^{\ast}(e)\mathrm{CATT}(g,e)~.

This explicitly proves property (ii) in the population limit.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

The target estimand is
\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(g)-Y_{i,t}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]
. Because
Y_{i,t}(g)
is observed for units with
S_{i}=g
and
Q_{i}=1
when
t\geq g
, the identification problem reduces to recovering the counterfactual mean
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]
.

Write the counterfactual outcome change as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]~.

Under no anticipation (
2.3
),
Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)=Y_{i,g-1}(g)=Y_{i,g-1}
, so the baseline outcome is observed. The DDD PCT assumption (
2.4
) then implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=0]~.

Rearranging gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g-1}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=0]\right)~.

The first term on the right-hand side gives the baseline level for the treated-eligible cell. The second term captures the within-group trend for ineligible units in cohort
g
. The bracket term adjusts for any differential trend between eligible and ineligible units using the comparison cohort
g_{c}
. All three terms are expressed in terms of observed outcome changes for untreated cells.

Substituting into
\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(g)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]
and using
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}(g)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]
(observed treated outcomes) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=g_{c},Q_{i}=0]\right)~,

which is (
5.1
) .
∎

Appendix C
Proofs of Asymptotics Results

Proof of
4.4
.

I prove each result by writing the population limit of the DDD estimator (
4.23
) in terms of potential outcomes and applying the identifying assumptions. By WLLN, each cell mean
\overline{\Delta Y}_{s,q,g+\ell}
converges in probability to its population analog
\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,g+\ell}\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q]
. I now express each cell’s population mean in terms of untreated potential outcomes and treatment effects. For the treated-eligible cell
(g,1)
at time
g+\ell\geq g
, consistency gives
Y_{i,g+\ell}=Y_{i,g+\ell}(g)
and no anticipation gives
Y_{i,g-1}=Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)
, so

\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,g+\ell}\mid g,1]=\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)+\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g+\ell}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)\mid g,1]\equiv\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)+\mu^{g,1}~.

For the three comparison cells
(g,0)
,
(g_{c},1)
, and
(g_{c},0)
, all units are untreated at both times
g+\ell
and
g-1
(ineligible units because
Q_{i}=0
, and eligible comparison units because
g_{c}>g+K\geq g+\ell
). Hence
\Delta Y_{i,g+\ell}=Y_{i,g+\ell}(\infty)-Y_{i,g-1}(\infty)
for each of these cells, and the population means are the untreated trend functions
\mu^{g,0}
,
\mu^{g_{c},1}
, and
\mu^{g_{c},0}
respectively. The population triple difference is therefore

(\mu^{g,1}+\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)-\mu^{g,0})-(\mu^{g_{c},1}-\mu^{g_{c},0})=\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)+(\mu^{g,1}-\mu^{g,0})-(\mu^{g_{c},1}-\mu^{g_{c},0})~.

By DDD-PCT (
2.4
), the eligible–ineligible differential trend
\mu^{g,1}-\mu^{g,0}
is the same for the treated group
g
and the comparison group
g_{c}
, so the second and third terms cancel. An application of Slutsky’s Theorem yields
\widehat{\delta}_{g,\ell}^{\mathrm{DDD}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.3
.

Fix event-time
e\in\{0,\ldots,K\}
and deterministic weights
\omega_{g}(e)\geq 0
with
\sum_{g}\omega_{g}(e)=1
, where the sum is over
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
with
g+e\leq T
.

Step 1: Linearization.
The aggregated estimator is

\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}(e)\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{\mathbb{S}_{g},g_{c}(g)}(g,g+e)~.

By
Theorem

6.2
, each within-stack estimator admits the influence function representation

(C.1)

\widehat{\mathrm{ATT}}_{\mathbb{S}_{g},g_{c}(g)}(g,g+e)-\mathrm{ATT}(g,g+e)=\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(n_{g}^{-1/2})~,

where I abbreviate
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i};g,g+e,g_{c}(g))
. Therefore

(C.2)

\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)-\mathrm{ES}(e)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}(e)\left[\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\right]+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})~.

The remainder is
\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
because
\sum_{g}\omega_{g}(e)\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(n_{g}^{-1/2})=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})
when
n_{g}/n\to\lambda_{g}>0
for each
g
(the proportion of units in each stack converges to a positive constant, since
T
and
|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|
are fixed).

Step 2: Rewrite as a single sum over all units.
Define the indicator
\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)=\mathbb{1}\{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\}
. Since each unit may belong to multiple stacks (if it is a shared comparison unit), I have

(C.3)

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}(e)\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(e)~,

where

(C.4)

\phi_{i}(e)=n\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}(e)\frac{\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)}{n_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=\sum_{gi\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{n\omega_{g}(e)}{n_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})~.

As
n\to\infty
with
n_{g}/n\to\lambda_{g}
, I have
n/n_{g}\to 1/\lambda_{g}
, and

\phi_{i}(e)\to\sum_{gi\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)}{\lambda_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})~.

This is a deterministic linear combination of the within-stack influence functions, summed over all stacks containing unit
i
.

Step 3: Verify CLT conditions.
By construction,
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)]=0
since
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})]=0
for each
g
. The random variables
\{\phi_{i}(e)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. (under
2.1
), since
\phi_{i}(e)
is a measurable function of
W_{i}
and stack membership (which is determined by
S_{i}
).

I verify finite second moments. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{gi\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)}{\lambda_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\bigg)^{\!2}\bigg]\leq|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)^{2}}{\lambda_{g}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)]<\infty~,

where (1) follows from
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}\mid i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}]=\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}<\infty
, and
|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|<\infty
since
T
is fixed.
Since
\{\phi_{i}(e)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. with
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]=V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)<\infty
, the Lindeberg condition is automatically satisfied by
Lemma

G.1
(applied with
\psi_{i}=\phi_{i}(e)
and
\sigma^{2}=V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)
). The Lindeberg–Lévy CLT therefore gives

(C.5)

\sqrt{n}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)-\mathrm{ES}(e)\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(e)+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}\big(0,V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)\big)~,

where
V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]
. Expanding the square yields the covariance structure in (
6.10
).
∎

Proof of
6.4
.

The asymptotic variance from the proof of
Theorem

6.3
is

(C.6)

V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{gi\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)}{\lambda_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\bigg)^{\!2}\bigg]~.

Expanding the square given by

(C.7)

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{=}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)\omega_{g^{\prime}}(e)}{\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g^{\prime}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})\right].

Case 1 Shared comparison units.

When stacks share comparison units (e.g.,
g_{c}(g)=g_{c}(g^{\prime})=\infty
), the set
\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}
is non-empty. A unit
i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}
contributes non-zero influence functions to both stacks. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})\mid i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}]\mathbb{P}(i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}})~.

For units
i\notin\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}
, at least one of
\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)
or
\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)
is zero, so the product vanishes. In particular, for the diagonal terms (
g=g^{\prime}
)

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)^{2}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}\mid i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}]\mathbb{P}(i\in\mathbb{S}_{g})=\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}\lambda_{g}~.

For the off-diagonal terms (
g\neq g^{\prime}
), the cross-covariance is generally non-zero because the same observation
W_{i}
enters both
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}
and
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}
. This is the source of the cross-stack covariance in (
6.10
).

Substituting back into (
C.7
), separating diagonal and off-diagonal terms

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)^{2}}{\lambda_{g}^{2}}\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}\lambda_{g}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}\\
g\neq g^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)\omega_{g^{\prime}}(e)}{\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g^{\prime}}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})]

(C.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)^{2}}{\lambda_{g}}\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\neq g^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)\omega_{g^{\prime}}(e)}{\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g^{\prime}}}\mathbb{P}(i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}})\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}\mid i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}].

Now note that
\mathbb{P}(i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}})
is the population probability that a randomly drawn unit belongs to both stacks. This equals the proportion of units in the overlap specifically, if both stacks share the same comparison group
g_{c}=\infty
, then
\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}=\{iS_{i}=\infty\}
and
\mathbb{P}(i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}})=\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=\infty)=\lambda_{\infty}
. For the general case, define the cross-stack covariance

\displaystyle C_{g,g^{\prime}}(e)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}\big(\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i}),\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})\big)~,

where the equality follows from
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})]=\lambda_{g}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\mid i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}]=0
. The overall variance can then be written compactly as

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)\omega_{g^{\prime}}(e)}{\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g^{\prime}}}c_{g,g^{\prime}}(e)=\sum_{g,g^{\prime}}\omega_{g}(e)\omega_{g^{\prime}}(e)\mathrm{Cov}\big(\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i}),\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}}(W_{i})\big)~,

where the covariance in the last expression is taken over the joint distribution of
W_{i}
(including the event that
i
belongs to the relevant stacks), and I use the convention that
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=0
when
i\notin\mathbb{S}_{g}
. This is (
6.10
).

Case 2 Distinct comparison groups with no shared units.

When
g_{c}(g)\neq g_{c}(g^{\prime})
and
\mathbb{S}_{g}\cap\mathbb{S}_{g^{\prime}}=\varnothing
for
g\neq g^{\prime}
, each unit belongs to at most one stack. Therefore
\mathbb{1}_{g}(i)\mathbb{1}_{g^{\prime}}(i)=0
for all
i
when
g\neq g^{\prime}
, and the off-diagonal terms in (
C.7
) vanish. The variance simplifies to

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\frac{\omega_{g}(e)^{2}}{\lambda_{g}^{2}}\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}\lambda_{g}=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}(e)^{2}\frac{\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}}{\lambda_{g}}~.

Since
n_{g}/n\to\lambda_{g}
, this is asymptotically equivalent to
\sum_{g}\omega_{g}(e)^{2}\Sigma_{g,g+e,g_{c}(g)}/n_{g}
, yielding (
6.11
).
∎

Proof of
6.5
.

Fix an event-time
e\in\{0,\ldots,K\}
. I suppress
e
from the notation where unambiguous, writing
\omega_{g}
for
\omega_{g}(e)
,
\widehat{\omega}_{g}
for
\widehat{\omega}_{g}(e)
, and
t=g+e
for the time corresponding to cohort
g
at event-time
e
. The proof proceeds in five steps. Throughout, I write
\mu_{s,q,t}=\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q]
for the cell-specific population mean of long differences.

Step 1: Rewrite as a sample average of squared terms.
Recall the aggregated influence function
\phi_{i}(e)
defined in (
C.4
)

\phi_{i}(e)=\sum_{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{n\omega_{g}}{n_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})~,

where
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i};g,g+e,g_{c}(g))
is the within-stack influence function from (
6.8
). By the argument in the proof of
Theorem

6.3
(Step 2, equation (
C.3
)), the aggregated estimator satisfies
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)-\mathrm{ES}(e))=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(e)+\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, and the asymptotic variance is
V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]
.

Define the estimated analogue by replacing population quantities with their sample counterparts

(C.9)

\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)=\sum_{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{n\widehat{\omega}_{g}}{n_{g}}\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})~,

where
\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})
is the estimated influence function from (
6.8
) evaluated at sample cell proportions
\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}=n_{s,q}/n_{g}
and sample cell means
\overline{\Delta Y}_{s,q,t}
, with
\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=0
for
i\notin\mathbb{S}_{g}
. The variance estimator (
6.12
) then takes the form

(C.10)

\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)^{2}~.

I must show
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)^{2}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]=V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)
.

Step 2: Consistency of cell-level quantities.
For each stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
and each cell
(s,q)\in\{(g,1),(g,0),(g_{c},1),(g_{c},0)\}
, I establish consistency of the estimated cell shares and cell means.

Cell shares.
The sample cell proportion is
\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}=n_{s,q}/n_{g}
, where
n_{s,q}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q\}
. By
2.1
, the indicators
\{\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q\}\}_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli
(\pi_{s,q})
. By the WLLN,
\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\pi_{s,q}
. Since
\pi_{s,q}>0
by
6.1
, the continuous mapping theorem gives
1/\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}1/\pi_{s,q}
.

Cell means.
The sample cell mean is
\overline{\Delta Y}_{s,q,t}=n_{s,q}^{-1}\sum_{i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q}\Delta Y_{i,t}
. By the WLLN under
6.2
,
\overline{\Delta Y}_{s,q,t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\mu_{s,q,t}
.

Since the number of stacks
|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|
and the number of cells per stack are finite (both are bounded by a function of the fixed
T
), convergence holds jointly over all cells and stacks.

Step 3: Pointwise consistency of estimated influence functions.
Fix a stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
and a unit
i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}
. The population influence function (
6.8
) evaluated at unit
i
activates exactly one cell, namely
(S_{i},Q_{i})

\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=\frac{c_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}{\pi_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}\big(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}\big)~,

where
c_{s,q}\in\{+1,-1\}
are the triple-difference signs. The estimated version is

\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})=\frac{c_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}{\widehat{\pi}_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}\big(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\overline{\Delta Y}_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}\big)~.

I decompose the estimation error as

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})-\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})

\displaystyle=c_{S_{i},Q_{i}}\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}\big(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\overline{\Delta Y}_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}\big)-\frac{1}{\pi_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}\big(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}\big)\right]

(C.11)

\displaystyle=c_{S_{i},Q_{i}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}-\frac{1}{\pi_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}\right)\big(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}\big)\right]

(C.12)

\displaystyle\quad+c_{S_{i},Q_{i}}\left[\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{S_{i},Q_{i}}}\big(\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}-\overline{\Delta Y}_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}\big)\right]~.

Define the data-level (non-unit-specific) error terms

(C.13)

\delta_{\pi,g}\coloneqq\max_{(s,q)}\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}}-\frac{1}{\pi_{s,q}}\right|~,\qquad\delta_{\mu,g}\coloneqq\max_{(s,q)}\left|\overline{\Delta Y}_{s,q,t}-\mu_{s,q,t}\right|~,\qquad\delta_{\widehat{\pi},g}\coloneqq\max_{(s,q)}\frac{1}{\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}}~.

By Step 2,
\delta_{\pi,g}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
,
\delta_{\mu,g}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, and
\delta_{\widehat{\pi},g}=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
(bounded in probability since
1/\widehat{\pi}_{s,q}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}1/\pi_{s,q}<\infty
). Combining (
C.11
) and (
C.12
)

(C.14)

\big|\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})-\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\big|\leq\delta_{\pi,g}|\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t}|+\delta_{\widehat{\pi},g}\delta_{\mu,g}~.

The first term is
\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
times a unit-specific random variable with finite second moment (by
6.2
); the second term is
\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and does not depend on the unit index.

Step 4:
L^{2}
convergence of the estimation error.
I now show that
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)-\phi_{i}(e))^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. I consider deterministic weights first (
\widehat{\omega}_{g}=\omega_{g}
); the extension to estimated weights follows at the end.

With deterministic weights, the estimation error decomposes as

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)-\phi_{i}(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{n\omega_{g}}{n_{g}}\left[\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})-\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\right]+\sum_{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\omega_{g}\left(\frac{n}{n_{g}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{g}}\right)\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})

(C.15)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon A_{i}+B_{i}~.

By the inequality
(A_{i}+B_{i})^{2}\leq 2A_{i}^{2}+2B_{i}^{2}
, it suffices to show
n^{-1}\sum_{i}A_{i}^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and
n^{-1}\sum_{i}B_{i}^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
separately.

Term
B_{i}
.
Define
\delta_{\lambda,g}\coloneqq|n/n_{g}-1/\lambda_{g}|
. By
6.1
,
n_{g}/n\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\lambda_{g}>0
, so by the CMT,
\delta_{\lambda,g}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the sum over at most
|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|
stacks, we get

B_{i}^{2}\leq|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|\sum_{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\omega_{g}^{2}\delta_{\lambda,g}^{2}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}~.

Averaging over
i
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}^{2}\delta_{\lambda,g}^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}

(C.16)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}^{2}\delta_{\lambda,g}^{2}\frac{n_{g}}{n}\underbracket{\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}}_{\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\Sigma_{g,t,g_{c}}<\infty}~.

The sample average of
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}
converges by the WLLN to
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})^{2}\mid i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}]=\Sigma_{g,t,g_{c}}<\infty
, which holds under
6.2
since
\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})
is a linear combination of cell-specific centered outcomes weighted by inverse cell shares. Since
\delta_{\lambda,g}^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
for each of the finitely many
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
, and
n_{g}/n=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
and
\Sigma_{g,t,g_{c}}<\infty
, the entire expression in (
C.16
) is
\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

Term
A_{i}
.
Using (
C.14
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

A_{i}^{2}\leq|\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}|\sum_{g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}\frac{n^{2}\omega_{g}^{2}}{n_{g}^{2}}\left[\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})-\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\right]^{2}~.

By (
C.14
), for each
g

(C.17)

\left[\widehat{\psi}_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})-\psi_{\mathbb{S}_{g}}(W_{i})\right]^{2}\leq 2\delta_{\pi,g}^{2}(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t})^{2}+2\delta_{\widehat{\pi},g}^{2}\delta_{\mu,g}^{2}~.

Averaging over
i
and using
n^{2}/n_{g}^{2}=(n/n_{g})^{2}=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
by
6.1

(C.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\precsim\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\omega_{g}^{2}\frac{n}{n_{g}}\left[2\delta_{\pi,g}^{2}\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t})^{2}+2\delta_{\widehat{\pi},g}^{2}\delta_{\mu,g}^{2}\right]~.

The sample average of
(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t})^{2}
within stack
\mathbb{S}_{g}
equals
\sum_{(s,q)}(n_{s,q}/n_{g})\left(n_{s,q}^{-1}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=s,Q_{j}=q}(\Delta Y_{j,t}-\mu_{s,q,t})^{2}\right)
. By the WLLN, each cell-specific sample second moment satisfies

(C.19)

\frac{1}{n_{s,q}}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=s,Q_{j}=q}(\Delta Y_{j,t}-\mu_{s,q,t})^{2}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\mathrm{Var}(\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q)<\infty~,

where the WLLN for
(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{s,q,t})^{2}
requires
\mathbb{E}[(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{s,q,t})^{4}\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q]<\infty
, which is ensured by
6.3
. It follows that

\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}_{g}}(\Delta Y_{i,t}-\mu_{S_{i},Q_{i},t})^{2}=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)~.

Since
\delta_{\pi,g}^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
,
\delta_{\widehat{\pi},g}^{2}=\mathrm{O}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, and
\delta_{\mu,g}^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, each summand in (
C.18
) is
\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. The sum over the finitely many
g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}
preserves this rate, giving
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

Combining the bounds on
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
via (
C.15
)

(C.20)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)-\phi_{i}(e)\big)^{2}=\mathrm{o}_{\mathbb{P}}(1)~.

Step 5: Conclude.
Decompose the variance estimator as

(C.21)

\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)=\underbracket{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(e)^{2}}_{T_{1}}+\underbracket{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\widehat{\phi}_{i}(e)^{2}-\phi_{i}(e)^{2}\right]}_{T_{2}}~.

Term
T_{1}
.
Since
\{\phi_{i}(e)\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. (each
\phi_{i}(e)
is a measurable function of
W_{i}
, and
\{W_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are i.i.d. by
2.1
) with finite mean
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(e)^{2}]=V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)<\infty
(established in the proof of
Theorem

6.3
, (
C.5
)), the WLLN gives

(C.22)

T_{1}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}V_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)~.

Term
T_{2}
.
By the identity
a^{2}-b^{2}=(a-b)(a+b)

(3WFE decomposition)

\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)\neq\mathrm{ATT}(g^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(

\alpha_{i}

\gamma_{t}

\delta_{S_{i},t}

Step 1. FWL residualization of the treatment indicator. By the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem,

The fixed effects in (

Remark E.1

(Effect homogeneity in 3WFE versus stacked DDD)
.

The 3WFE specification (
E.1
) imposes a single treatment coefficient
\theta
, implicitly assuming that the treatment effect is homogeneous across all
(g,t)
cells. This rules out dynamic treatment effects, cohort-specific effects, and covariate-mediated heterogeneity. The stacked DDD framework targets
(g,t)
-specific parameters
\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)
without any homogeneity restriction, and the aggregation to event-study parameters
\widehat{\mathrm{ES}}_{\mathrm{stack}}(e)
uses researcher-chosen non-negative weights. Under homogeneous treatment effects, no staggering, and a single treatment cohort, 3WFE is consistent and numerically equivalent to the stacked estimator with a single stack. In practice, however, staggered adoption and heterogeneous effects are the norm, making the stacked alternative preferable as a default.

E.1.
The Missing Fixed Effects

Beyond the biases in
E.1
, the 3WFE specification (
E.1
) has a more fundamental structural deficiency. It omits eligibility-by-time fixed effects
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
. A properly specified DDD regression must include all three sets of two-way interactions—unit (absorbing group-by-eligibility), group-by-time, and eligibility-by-time—to isolate the triple-difference variation. The specification (
E.1
) includes only the first two.

The correct DDD regression on levels is

(E.4)

Y_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\delta_{S_{i},t}+\eta_{Q_{i},t}+\tau D_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t}~,

where
\alpha_{i}
absorbs all time-invariant unit heterogeneity (including group, eligibility, and group-by-eligibility main effects),
\delta_{S_{i},t}
are group-by-time fixed effects, and
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
are eligibility-by-time fixed effects. The time fixed effects
\gamma_{t}
from the standard 3WFE are redundant since they are spanned by both
\delta_{S_{i},t}
and
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
.

I now derive the FWL residualized treatment indicator for both specifications, showing precisely what variation each exploits.

FWL residualization of the correct specification. The three sets of fixed effects in (
E.4
) are unit effects
\alpha_{i}
, group-by-time effects
\delta_{S_{i},t}
, and eligibility-by-time effects
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
. To project
D_{i,t}
onto the space spanned by these three sets, I apply the multi-way demeaning formula. Define the following group means of the treatment indicator

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{i,\cdot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}D_{i,t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\text{(unit-level time mean)}~,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{S_{i},t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{S_{i}}}\sum_{j\colon S_{j}=S_{i}}D_{j,t}

\displaystyle\text{(group-time mean)}~,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{Q_{i},t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{Q_{i}}}\sum_{j\colon Q_{j}=Q_{i}}D_{j,t}

\displaystyle\text{(eligibility-time mean)}~,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{S_{i},\cdot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\overline{D}_{S_{i},t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\text{(group mean over time)}~,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{Q_{i},\cdot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\overline{D}_{Q_{i},t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\text{(eligibility mean over time)}~,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{\cdot,t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{j,t}

\displaystyle\text{(cross-sectional mean at time $t$)}~,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{\cdot\cdot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nT}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}D_{j,t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\text{(total mean)}~.

The residualized treatment indicator after projecting out all three sets of fixed effects is

(E.5)

\widetilde{D}_{i,t}=D_{i,t}-\overline{D}_{i,\cdot}-\overline{D}_{S_{i},t}-\overline{D}_{Q_{i},t}+\overline{D}_{S_{i},\cdot}+\overline{D}_{Q_{i},\cdot}+\overline{D}_{\cdot,t}-\overline{D}_{\cdot\cdot}~.

This is the standard inclusion-exclusion formula for three-way demeaning. The residual
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
isolates the variation in
D_{i,t}
that is orthogonal to all three sets of two-way interactions—precisely the triple-difference variation.

Contrast with the 3WFE residual. When the specification omits
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
(the standard 3WFE), the FWL residual reduces to

\widetilde{D}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{3WFE}}=D_{i,t}-\overline{D}_{i,\cdot}-\overline{D}_{S_{i},t}+\overline{D}_{S_{i},\cdot}~,

which is the residual from projecting out only unit and group-by-time effects. Comparing with (
E.5
), the 3WFE residual fails to subtract
\overline{D}_{Q_{i},t}-\overline{D}_{Q_{i},\cdot}-\overline{D}_{\cdot,t}+\overline{D}_{\cdot\cdot}
, which is precisely the demeaned eligibility-by-time component. As a consequence,
\widehat{\theta}^{\mathrm{3WFE}}
is identified from variation that includes the eligibility-by-time channel—the very nuisance that DDD is designed to difference out.

The inclusion of
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
is essential for the triple-differencing logic. The DDD PCT assumption (
2.4
) states that the eligible–ineligible gap in outcome trends is the same across treatment and comparison groups. Without
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
, the regression requires the stronger assumption that level trends are parallel across groups—essentially a difference-in-differences assumption rather than a triple difference. The correct residual (
E.5
), by removing the eligibility-by-time variation, ensures that identification comes only from the triple-difference variation—the extent to which the eligible–ineligible gap changes differentially across treatment and comparison groups.

Why the omission is inconsequential in the single-cohort case. In a single-cohort, two-period setting with treatment group
g
and comparison group
g_{c}
, there are
|\mathcal{S}|=2
groups,
T=2
periods, and
|Q|=2
eligibility types. The group-by-time fixed effects span four cells (
2\times 2
), and the eligibility-by-time fixed effects span four cells (
2\times 2
). The time effects
\gamma_{t}
are spanned by both. With only two groups, the group-by-time effects already absorb one dimension of time variation for each group, and the unit effects absorb the group-by-eligibility cross-section. In this minimal setting,
\overline{D}_{Q_{i},t}-\overline{D}_{Q_{i},\cdot}-\overline{D}_{\cdot,t}+\overline{D}_{\cdot\cdot}
is a linear combination of the group-by-time demeaning terms, so the two residuals
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
and
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{3WFE}}
coincide. The discrepancy arises in the staggered adoption setting with multiple cohorts and many periods, where the eligibility-by-time interaction varies independently of the group-by-time interaction.

For the stacked DDD estimator, this issue does not arise. Each stack operates on a
2\times 2
design (treated group vs. comparison group) within which the saturated regression (
4.7
) automatically controls for all cell-specific effects. The four-cell structure of each stack is the regression analogue of including all three sets of two-way interactions.

25
to the DDD setting.

Under DDD-PCT, the saturated DDD regression has cohort-specific event-time indicators
R_{g,\ell}(i,t)=\mathbb{1}\{S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1,t=g+\ell\}
with coefficients
\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)
. The 3WFE event-study specification (
E.6
) pools these cohort-specific indicators into aggregate event-time indicators
R_{e}(i,t)=\mathbb{1}\{t-S_{i}=e\}Q_{i}
. This pooling omits the cohort-specific detail. The OVB formula decomposes the pooled coefficient as
\mu_{e}=\sum_{g,\ell^{\prime}}\omega_{g,\ell^{\prime}}^{e}\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell^{\prime})
, where the weight
\omega_{g,\ell^{\prime}}^{e}
is the auxiliary regression coefficient from (
E.7
). The contamination arises because the auxiliary regression has
\omega_{g,\ell^{\prime}}^{e}\neq 0
for
\ell^{\prime}\neq e
—the aggregate indicator
R_{e}
is correlated with cohort-specific indicators at other event-times, even after partialling out the fixed effects.

Why are these residual correlations nonzero? In an event study with staggered adoption, the panel is never balanced in both time and time relative to treatment onset. If cohort
g=2013
adopts treatment in 2013, then in calendar year 2015 this cohort is at event-time
\ell=2
, while cohort
g=2015
is at event-time
\ell=0
. The event-time indicator
R_{0}(i,2015)
lights up for cohort 2015 but not for cohort 2013; conversely,
R_{2}(i,2015)
lights up for cohort 2013 but not 2015. After removing unit and time fixed effects, these indicators retain residual correlation because the relative-time composition of the sample changes across time. The group-by-time fixed effects
\delta_{S_{i},t}
absorb some of this variation but do not eliminate it, since the within-group composition between eligible and ineligible units is not collinear with the group-by-time effects.

To illustrate concretely, consider a balanced panel with
T=3
periods, two treatment cohorts
S_{i}\in\{1,2\}
, and no never-treated units (
Q_{i}\in\{0,1\}
). The fully dynamic specification includes
e\in\{-2,0\}
and excludes
e=-1
(the normalization period). Under DDD-PCT and no anticipation, the pre-period coefficient decomposes as

(E.17)

\mu_{-2}=\underbracket{\mathrm{CATT}(2,-2)}_{\text{own period}}+\underbracket{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{CATT}(1,0)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{CATT}(2,0)}_{\text{included, }\ell=0}+\underbracket{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{CATT}(1,1)}_{\text{excluded, }\ell=1}~,

where I have already set
\mathrm{CATT}(g,\ell)=0
for
\ell<0
(no anticipation). Even though the pre-treatment effect
\mathrm{CATT}(2,-2)=0
, the coefficient
\mu_{-2}
equals
\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{CATT}(1,0)-\mathrm{CATT}(2,0))+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{CATT}(1,1)
, which is nonzero whenever effects are heterogeneous across cohorts (
\mathrm{CATT}(1,0)\neq\mathrm{CATT}(2,0)
) or there are lagged effects (
\mathrm{CATT}(1,1)\neq 0
).

In the DDD setting, a third source of contamination is present beyond the two identified by
25
. The 3WFE specification (
E.6
) omits the eligibility-by-time fixed effects
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
that are structurally necessary for triple-difference identification (Section
E.1
). This means the coefficient
\mu_{e}
is identified from variation that includes the eligibility-by-time channel, compounding the cross-period and cross-cohort contamination analyzed in Propositions
E.3
–
E.5
with a bias from failing to partial out eligibility-specific time trends.

Proof of
E.1
.

I establish each source of bias in the three-way fixed effects regression

(E.18)

Y_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{t}+\delta_{S_{i},t}+\tau D_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t},

where
D_{i,t}=\mathbb{1}\{t\geq S_{i}\}Q_{i,t}
(with
Q_{i,t}=Q_{i}
under time-invariant eligibility).

Part (i): Negative weights.

By the FWL theorem, the OLS estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\text{3WFE}}
equals the coefficient from the regression of
Y_{i,t}
on
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
, where
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
is the residual from projecting
D_{i,t}
onto the space spanned by unit fixed effects
\{\alpha_{i}\}
, time fixed effects
\{\gamma_{t}\}
, and group-by-time fixed effects
\{\delta_{S_{i},t}\}
.

Before deriving the FWL residual, I note two structural issues with the 3WFE specification (
E.18
).

First, the group-by-time fixed effects
\{\delta_{S_{i},t}\}
are indexed over all groups
S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
and all time periods
t
. When the group dimension spans all values in
\mathcal{S}
(including the never-treated group), the column space of the group-by-time dummies strictly contains the column space of the global time dummies
\{\gamma_{t}\}
: one can recover
\gamma_{t}
as any weighted average
\sum_{g}(n_{g}/n)\delta_{g,t}
across groups. Hence the global time fixed effects are redundant once group-by-time fixed effects are included. In practice, the software absorbs
\gamma_{t}
into
\delta_{S_{i},t}
, so the residualized treatment depends only on the unit and group-by-time projections.

Second, and more substantively, the 3WFE specification lacks eligibility-by-time fixed effects
\{\eta_{Q_{i},t}\}
. A proper triple-difference design requires absorbing the common eligibility-specific trend that operates across all groups—precisely the trend that the third difference is designed to remove. Without eligibility-by-time fixed effects, the model implicitly assumes that the eligible–ineligible outcome differential is time-invariant unconditionally, rather than allowing it to evolve freely and relying on cross-group differencing to remove it. Consequently, the 3WFE specification does not properly implement the triple-difference design. The “forbidden comparisons” that arise are entirely within the group-by-time dimension; no additional forbidden comparisons are generated across the eligibility dimension because the model fails to exploit that dimension at all.

To see this formally, note that the fully saturated DDD regression should include unit FEs
\alpha_{i}
, group-by-time FEs
\delta_{S_{i},t}
, and eligibility-by-time FEs
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
:

Y_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\delta_{S_{i},t}+\eta_{Q_{i},t}+\tau D_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t}.

The standard 3WFE (
E.18
) omits
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
, so the treatment coefficient
\widehat{\tau}^{\text{3WFE}}
conflates the causal effect with any time-varying eligibility premium that differs from the group-by-time trend structure.

With these caveats, I proceed with the FWL derivation of the standard 3WFE as written. Since group-by-time FEs absorb the global time FEs, the effective projection is onto unit FEs and group-by-time FEs only. The FWL residual of
D_{i,t}
after projecting out
\{\alpha_{i}\}
and
\{\delta_{S_{i},t}\}
is

(E.19)

\widetilde{D}_{i,t}=D_{i,t}-\overline{D}_{i,\cdot}-\overline{D}_{S_{i},t}+\overline{D}_{S_{i},\cdot},

where the averaging operators are:

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{i,\cdot}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{i,t}

\displaystyle\text{(unit mean)},

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{S_{i},t}=\frac{1}{n_{S_{i}}}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=S_{i}}D_{j,t}

\displaystyle\text{(group-by-time mean)},

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{S_{i},\cdot}=\frac{1}{n_{S_{i}}T}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=S_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{j,t}

\displaystyle\text{(group mean)},

and
n_{S_{i}}=|\{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=S_{i}\}|
is the number of units in group
S_{i}
. The global time mean
\overline{D}_{\cdot,t}
and total mean
\overline{D}_{\cdot\cdot}
do not appear separately because they are absorbed by the group-by-time fixed effects.

I now evaluate each term explicitly for a treated unit with
S_{i}=g
and
Q_{i}=1
.
Since
D_{i,t}=\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}Q_{i}=\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}

\displaystyle D_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\},

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{i,\cdot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}=\frac{T-g+1}{T}.

The group-by-time mean for group

is

\overline{D}_{S_{i}=g,t}=\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=g}D_{j,t}=p_{Q=1|S=g}\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\},

where
p_{Q=1|S=g}=n_{g}^{-1}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=g}Q_{j}
is the proportion of eligible units in group
g
, since all eligible units in group
g
have
D_{j,t}=\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}
and all ineligible units have
D_{j,t}=0
.
The group mean for group
g
is

\overline{D}_{S_{i}=g,\cdot}=\frac{1}{n_{g}T}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{j}=g}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{j,t}=p_{Q=1|S=g}\frac{T-g+1}{T}.

Substituting into (
E.19
) for a unit with
S_{i}=g
,
Q_{i}=1

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}-\frac{T-g+1}{T}-p_{Q=1|S=g}\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}+p_{Q=1|S=g}\frac{T-g+1}{T}

(E.20)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{Q=1|S=g})\left(\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}-\frac{T-g+1}{T}\right).

The factor
(1-p_{Q=1|S=g})
is the proportion of ineligible units in group
g

\mathbb{1}\{t\geq g\}-(T-g+1)/T
, is the within-unit deviation of

from its time average: positive for post-treatment periods (

).

Crucially, because the group-by-time FEs absorb the global time FEs, the residualized treatment
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
depends only on the within-group variation of
D_{i,t}
across units (eligible vs. ineligible) and across time (pre vs. post). There is no cross-group component in
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
. The “forbidden comparison” bias arises because different groups

contribute different within-group residuals, and the OLS estimand implicitly forms cross-group comparisons of these residuals.

Remark E.3

(Missing eligibility-by-time FEs and its consequences)
.

The absence of eligibility-by-time FEs
\eta_{Q_{i},t}
in the standard 3WFE specification means that any common time-varying eligibility premium—the trend component that DDD identification is explicitly designed to difference out—is not absorbed by the fixed effects. Instead, it is captured by the treatment coefficient
\widehat{\tau}^{\text{3WFE}}
to the extent that it correlates with the treatment indicator
D_{i,t}
. This conflation is a fundamental deficiency: the 3WFE regression performs a two-way comparison (unit and group-by-time) rather than the three-way comparison required by DDD identification. Including eligibility-by-time FEs would correct this but introduces its own complications under staggered adoption,

\diamondsuit

In the staggered setting with multiple treatment cohorts, the residualized indicator
\widetilde{D}_{i,t}
takes negative values for some
(i,t)
pairs—specifically, for early-treated units in later time periods where the group-by-time demeaning produces a large subtraction. The OLS estimand is

(E.21)

\widehat{\tau}^{\text{3WFE}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\frac{\sum_{i,t}\widetilde{D}_{i,t}\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}]}{\sum_{i,t}\widetilde{D}_{i,t}^{2}}=\sum_{g,t\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}t\geq g}w_{g,t}\mathrm{ATT}(g,t),

where the implicit weights
w_{g,t}=\sum_{i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1}\widetilde{D}_{i,t}/\sum_{i^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\widetilde{D}_{i^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{2}
can be negative. This extends the decomposition of
12
from DiD to the DDD setting, as in
19
. Negative weights arise when some
2\times 2\times 2
sub-comparisons use earlier-treated cohorts as implicit controls.

Part (ii): Covariate bias.

Consider the augmented 3WFE regression:

(E.22)

Y_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{t}+\delta_{S_{i},t}+\tau D_{i,t}+X_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\epsilon_{i,t}.

The coefficient
\beta
is identified from the within-variation of
X_{i}
after absorbing fixed effects. However, since
X_{i}
is time-invariant,
X_{i}^{\prime}\beta
is collinear with
\alpha_{i}
after within-unit demeaning (the FWL residual of
X_{i}
on unit FEs is zero). In practice,
X_{i}^{\prime}\beta
is identified from across-unit variation within group-time cells, and
\widehat{\beta}
reflects a weighted average of the covariate effects across different comparison types.

Following
16
and
20
, the problem arises because the 3WFE regression imposes a
common
covariate coefficient
\beta
across all DDD cells, rather than allowing the covariate adjustment to vary by cell. The DDD identification formula (
Theorem

5.1
) requires integrating the triple difference of conditional expectations over the
treated
covariate distribution. The 3WFE regression, by contrast, uses the
pooled
covariate distribution across all cells and comparison types. When the covariate effect on outcome trends differs across
(S,Q)
cells—as is typical in DDD applications where the treatment eligibility dimension interacts with covariates—the pooled adjustment introduces a bias:

(E.23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{covariate}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}}\sum_{t\geq g}w_{g,t}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}^{\prime}\left(\beta_{\mathrm{pooled}}-\beta_{g,1,t}\right)\left(D_{i,t}-p_{g,t}\right)\mid S_{i}\in\{g,g_{c}\},t\in[g-L,g+K]\right],

where
\beta_{g,1,t}
is the “correct” cell-specific covariate coefficient for the treated population
(S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1)
at time
t
,
\beta_{\mathrm{pooled}}
is the common covariate coefficient estimated by the 3WFE regression,
p_{g,t}=\mathbb{P}(D_{i,t}=1\mid S_{i}=g)
is the within-group treatment probability at time
t
, and the conditioning is over the implicit pairwise sub-experiments
(g,g_{c},t,t^{\prime})
embedded in the 3WFE regression via the FWL projection. The weights
w_{g,t}
arise from the FWL decomposition and sum to one.

The bias arises because the 3WFE regression constrains all cells to share a common
\beta_{\mathrm{pooled}}
. In the DDD identification formula (
Theorem

5.1
), the covariate adjustment is cell-specific: each conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t}\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q,X_{i}=x]
may have a different dependence on
X_{i}
. When the effect of
X_{i}
on outcome trends differs across
(S,Q)
cells—as is typical in DDD applications where the eligibility dimension interacts with covariates—the pooled coefficient
\beta_{\mathrm{pooled}}
is a misspecified average, and the residual
\beta_{\mathrm{pooled}}-\beta_{g,1,t}
is generally non-zero.

The stacked DR estimator eliminates this bias by using flexible, cell-specific nuisance models
m_{t}^{s,q}(x)
and propensity score weights
p_{s,q}(x)
within each stack. The covariate adjustment is nonparametric within each cell and integrates over the treated covariate distribution, rather than imposing a common linear specification.

Part (iii): Forbidden comparisons.

By the Strezhnev decomposition
(
24

, the 3WFE estimand can be written as a weighted sum over all pairwise

sub-experiments formed by choosing two groups

with

(E.24)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{DDD}_{g,g^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]\right]

(E.25)

\displaystyle\qquad-\left[\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=0]\right],

where
\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}=Y_{i,t^{\prime}}-Y_{i,t}

and the post-period
t^{\prime}

For the treated group (
S_{i}=g
,
Q_{i}=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ATT}(g,t^{\prime})+\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g,Q_{i}=0]\right),

where the second term is the untreated eligibility trend difference within group
g

For the comparison group (

Q_{i}=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}\mid S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=0]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ATT}(g^{\prime},t^{\prime})+\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid S_{i}=g^{\prime},Q_{i}=0]\right).

E.25

\displaystyle\mathrm{DDD}_{g,g^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ATT}(g,t^{\prime})-\mathrm{ATT}(g^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(E.26)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbracket{\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid g,1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid g,0]\right)-\left(\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid g^{\prime},1]-\mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t^{\prime}}(\infty)\mid g^{\prime},0]\right)}_{\text{DDD PCT difference}}~,

Combining the three sources, the 3WFE probability limit is

\widehat{\tau}^{\text{3WFE}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\sum_{g,t}w_{g,t}\mathrm{ATT}(g,t)+\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{covariate}}+\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{forbidden}},

where some

,
\mathrm{Bias}_{\mathrm{covariate}}\neq 0
when covariate effects are cell-specific, and

when treatment effects are heterogeneous across cohorts. The stacked DR estimator avoids all three: it uses researcher-chosen non-negative weights, flexible cell-specific covariate adjustment, and clean comparison groups by construction.

Appendix F
Proof of Validity of CRVE in Stacked OLS Regressions

I provide a justification of the use of cluster-robust standard errors (CRVE), clustered at the original unit level, in the stacked event-study OLS regression. I establish that the CRVE is a consistent estimator of the asymptotic variance of the stacked estimator under the sampling and moment conditions established in the main text. Specifically, I maintain
2.1
,
6.1
, and
6.3
throughout this section. As before, the number of units in each stack
g
is denoted
n_{g}
, and the number of units in each
(s,q)
cell within that stack is denoted
n_{s,q,g}
, and
n_{g}/n\to\lambda_{g}>0
and
n_{s,q,g}/n_{g}\to\pi_{s,q,g}>0
under
6.1
.

The stacked event-study coefficient
\widehat{\tau}_{e}
is obtained by estimating a fully saturated ordinary least squares regression on the pooled stacked dataset. By Frisch-Waugh-Lovell, the multivariate regression can be analytically reduced to a bivariate regression of the outcome on the residualized treatment indicator. Let
\widetilde{R}_{i,g}=\widetilde{R}_{e}(i,g+e,g)
denote the FWL residual of the treatment indicator for unit
i
in stack
g
at event-time
e
, obtained after projecting out the stack-by-group-by-time and stack-by-eligibility-by-time fixed effects. Because these fixed effects are mutually orthogonal across stacks and times, the residual
\widetilde{R}_{i,g}
is strictly zero for all periods other than
t=g+e
and for all stacks other than
g
. The estimated coefficient
\widehat{\tau}_{e}
can therefore be written exactly as

(F.1)

\widehat{\tau}_{e}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}Y_{i,g+e}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}^{2}}~.

The population analogue of this coefficient,
\tau_{e}
, is defined by replacing the sample sums with their population expectations. The estimation error of the stacked estimator can be isolated by substituting the outcome
Y_{i,g+e}
with the sum of its conditional expectation and the regression error. The OLS residual for unit
i
in stack
g
is defined as
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i,g+e,g}=Y_{i,g+e}-\widehat{Y}_{i,g+e,g}
, where
\widehat{Y}_{i,g+e,g}
is the OLS fitted value. Let
\widehat{s}_{i}(e)
denote the unit-level estimated score, which aggregates the product of the residualized treatment indicator and the regression error across all stacks in which unit
i
appears:

(F.2)

\widehat{s}_{i}(e)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i,g+e,g}~.

Multiplying by
\sqrt{n}
to study the asymptotic distribution, the scaled estimation error takes the form

(F.3)

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\tau}_{e}-\tau_{e})=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}^{2}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}_{i}(e)~.

This representation mirrors the standard generalized method of moments (GMM) framework, where the first term represents the inverse of the sample Hessian matrix (the “bread”) and the second term is the scaled sample average of the score vector (the “meat”).

The CRVE for the variance of
\widehat{\tau}_{e}
, clustered at the level of the original unit
i
, is constructed using the empirical sandwich formula

(F.4)

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{\tau}_{e})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}^{2}\right)^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}_{i}(e)^{2}~.

To prove that this estimator provides valid inference, I must establish that
n\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{\tau}_{e})
converges in probability to the true asymptotic variance of the scaled estimation error in (
F.3
). The convergence of the bread is immediate. By the WLLN, the scaled denominator
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}^{2}
converges in probability to its population expectation
V=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{g}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}^{2}]
. The critical step is to demonstrate that the scaled meat of the sandwich, given by
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}_{i}(e)^{2}
, converges to the population variance of the score, defined as
\Omega=\mathbb{E}[(s_{i}^{*}(e))^{2}]
. The population score for unit
i
is
s_{i}^{*}(e)=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}\varepsilon_{i,g+e,g}
, where
\varepsilon_{i,g+e,g}=Y_{i,g+e}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g+e}\mid S_{i},Q_{i}]
is the true conditional error term.

I first characterize the rate of convergence of the estimated regression residuals to the population errors. Because the stacked event-study regression is fully saturated by the inclusion of stack-by-group-by-time and stack-by-eligibility-by-time fixed effects, the OLS fitted value for any observation is exactly the sample mean of the outcome within that specific stack-cell-time partition. Specifically, for a unit
i
belonging to cell
(s,q)
within stack
g
, the fitted value is
\widehat{Y}_{i,g+e,g}=\overline{Y}_{s,q,g+e,g}
. The OLS residual can therefore be algebraically decomposed as
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{i,g+e,g}=\varepsilon_{i,g+e,g}-\delta_{s,q,g}
, where
\delta_{s,q,g}=\overline{Y}_{s,q,g+e,g}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,g+e}\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q]
captures the estimation error of the cell mean. By the CLT and
6.1
, the sample mean of i.i.d. outcomes converges to its population expectation at a
\sqrt{n}
rate, implying that
\delta_{s,q,g}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. The unit-level estimated score can then be rewritten as the population score minus an estimation error term

(F.5)

\widehat{s}_{i}(e)=s_{i}^{*}(e)-\Delta_{i},

where
\Delta_{i}\equiv\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}\delta_{s(i),q(i),g}
. Since
\widetilde{R}_{i,g}
is bounded and
\delta_{s,q,g}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, it follows that the aggregate unit-level discrepancy is also
\Delta_{i}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

I next analyze the convergence of the empirical score variance by expanding the scaled meat of the sandwich estimator. Expanding the square of the estimated score yields three distinct components:

(F.6)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}_{i}(e)^{2}=\underbracket{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(s_{i}^{*}(e))^{2}}_{(i)}-\underbracket{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{*}(e)\Delta_{i}}_{(ii)}+\underbracket{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{2}}_{(iii)}~.

I evaluate each of these three terms in turn. The first term (i) is the sample average of the squared population score. Under the finite fourth moments condition stated in
6.3
, the squared score has a finite variance, allowing the application of the WLLN to conclude that
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(s_{i}^{*}(e))^{2}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\mathbb{E}[(s_{i}^{*}(e))^{2}]=\Omega
.

The third term (iii) represents the average of the squared discrepancies. Because
\Delta_{i}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
for all units, its square is
\Delta_{i}^{2}=O_{p}(n^{-1})
. The sample average of these squared terms,
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{i}^{2}
, is bounded by the maximum over the finite number of cells of
O_{p}(n^{-1})
. Consequently, this term vanishes asymptotically, satisfying
O_{p}(n^{-1})=o_{p}(1)
.

The second term (ii) is the cross-product between the population score and the estimation error. Substituting the definition of
\Delta_{i}
, this term can be rearranged by exchanging the summations over units and stacks

(F.7)

\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{*}(e)\Delta_{i}=2\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\sum_{(s,q)}\delta_{s,q,g}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\text{cell }(s,q,g)}s_{i}^{*}(e)\widetilde{R}_{i,g}\right)~.

Within each cell partition
(s,q,g)
, the expression in the parentheses is the sample average of the product
s_{i}^{*}(e)\widetilde{R}_{i,g}
. The population expectation of this product conditional on the cell is exactly zero, because
\mathbb{E}[s_{i}^{*}(e)\mid S_{i}=s,Q_{i}=q]=0
by the definition of the true error term
\varepsilon_{i,g+e,g}
, and
\widetilde{R}_{i,g}
is a constant within the cell. By the CLT, the sample average of a zero-mean random variable scaled by
n
is bounded in probability by
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. The cross-term is therefore the product of a finite number of components, each of which multiplies
\delta_{s,q,g}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
by an average that is
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. The resulting product is
O_{p}(n^{-1})
, which implies that the entire cross-term converges in probability to zero, or
o_{p}(1)
.

Combining these three limits establishes that the empirical variance of the estimated score converges to the true variance of the population score,
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}_{i}(e)^{2}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\Omega
. Finally, applying Slutsky’s theorem to the full CRVE sandwich estimator demonstrates that the scaled variance converges to the exact asymptotic variance of the OLS estimator

(F.8)

n\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat{\tau}_{e})=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{trg}}(e)}\widetilde{R}_{i,g}^{2}\right)^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{s}_{i}(e)^{2}\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}V^{-2}\Omega~.

This rigorously justifies the use of cluster-robust standard errors. By calculating the score at the level of the original unit
i
and implicitly summing the score components across all stacks in which the unit appears before squaring, the CRVE automatically and correctly accounts for the cross-stack dependence induced by the reuse of comparison units.

Appendix G
Auxiliary Results

Lemma G.1
(Lindeberg Condition for Bounded Influence Functions)
.

If
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. with
\psi_{i}=\psi(W_{i})
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}]=0
and
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{i}^{2}]\in(0,\infty)
, then the Lindeberg condition holds:

(G.1)

\forall\varepsilon>0,\qquad\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{i}^{2}\mathbb{1}\{|\psi_{i}|>\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\}\right]\to 0.

Proof.

Since
\{W_{i}\}
are i.i.d., every summand is identical, so

\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{i}^{2}\mathbb{1}\{|\psi_{i}|>\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\}\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{1}^{2}\mathbb{1}\{|\psi_{1}|>\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\}\right]~.

As
n\to\infty
,
\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\to\infty
, so
\mathbb{1}\{|\psi_{1}|>\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\}\to 0
almost surely (since
|\psi_{1}|<\infty
a.s.). The integrand
\psi_{1}^{2}\mathbb{1}\{|\psi_{1}|>\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\}\leq\psi_{1}^{2}
is dominated by the integrable function
\psi_{1}^{2}
(since
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}<\infty
). By the dominated convergence theorem,
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{1}^{2}\mathbb{1}\{|\psi_{1}|>\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}\}]\to 0
.
∎
