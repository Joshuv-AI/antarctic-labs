Airway Decision‐Making in Acute Epiglottitis: A Case Report of Planned Tracheostomy in a Can Ventilate but Cannot Intubate (CVCI) Scenario
Authors: Xueting Wu, Yuquan Deng, Yajing Kan, Jinfeng Meng, Yan Zhang, Wangsheng Deng, Wanwen Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute epiglottitis is a life‐threatening emergency characterized by rapid airway obstruction and challenging airway management due to severe supraglottic edema. We report the case of a 22‐year‐old man who presented with acute epiglottitis complicated by extensive neck edema, inspiratory stridor, and progressive respiratory distress. Despite multiple attempts, endotracheal intubation failed because of severe epiglottic swelling obscuring the laryngeal inlet. However, effective bag‐mask ventilation was maintained, resulting in a “can ventilate but cannot intubate” (CVCI) scenario rather than a “cannot intubate, cannot ventilate” (CICV) emergency. In accordance with difficult airway guidelines, further traumatic intubation attempts were avoided, and a planned bedside tracheostomy was successfully performed by an experienced otolaryngology surgeon with multidisciplinary support from emergency and anesthesiology teams. Oxygenation rapidly improved, and the patient recovered without complications. This case highlights the importance of accurate airway scenario recognition, preservation of oxygenation, avoidance of repeated intubation attempts, and early multidisciplinary collaboration in the management of difficult airways associated with acute epiglottitis.Keywords: acute epiglottitis, airway management, can ventilate but cannot intubate, cricothyrotomy, tracheostomy

Introduction

Acute epiglottitis is a life‐threatening condition characterized by rapid onset of supraglottic inflammation that can progress to complete airway obstruction within hours [1, 2]. Although the incidence of pediatric epiglottitis has declined markedly following widespread Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccination, adult epiglottitis remains a significant clinical challenge, with reported mortality rates of 1%–7% when airway compromise occurs [3, 4].

Securing the airway is the cornerstone of management, yet it is often technically difficult because severe edema obscures normal laryngeal landmarks [5]. Endotracheal intubation is commonly attempted as the initial airway strategy but may fail in a substantial proportion of cases [6]. Following failed intubation, two distinct clinical scenarios must be differentiated: “can ventilate but cannot intubate” (CVCI), in which oxygenation can be maintained with bag‐mask ventilation, and “cannot intubate, cannot ventilate” (CICV), a true airway emergency requiring immediate front‐of‐neck access [7].

The ASA 2022 Practice Guidelines and the DAS 2015 guidelines emphasize that these two scenarios require fundamentally different management strategies [2, 6, 8]. While emergency cricothyrotomy is mandatory in CICV situations, CVCI scenarios allow time to suspend further intubation attempts, preserve oxygenation, and consider alternative approaches, including planned surgical airway [9, 10]. In acute epiglottitis, severe cervical soft‐tissue edema may obscure anterior neck landmarks and increase the technical difficulty and complication risk of emergency cricothyrotomy [11]. This report illustrates guideline‐based decision‐making in a CVCI scenario complicated by extensive neck edema.

Case Presentation

Emergency Interventions and Airway Management

The patient arrived at the emergency department at 19:12 with signs of impending airway obstruction. Initial oxygen saturation was 89% on supplemental oxygen. At 19:14, pharmacological therapy was initiated, including intravenous methylprednisolone (80 mg), intramuscular epinephrine (0.5 mg), and intravenous dexamethasone (10 mg), in an attempt to reduce airway edema.

Cautious sedation with intravenous midazolam (10 mg) to maintain spontaneous ventilation contributed to effective bag‐mask ventilation and preservation of oxygenation. This strategy may minimize airway trauma and support safe decision‐making in difficult airway scenarios. Previous studies have also shown that midazolam premedication can facilitate mask ventilation during anesthesia induction [12]. This dose provided adequate anxiolysis and patient cooperation without suppressing respiratory drive, ensuring that effective bag‐mask ventilation with an oropharyngeal airway could be maintained throughout the three unsuccessful endotracheal intubation attempts, thereby preventing progression to a “cannot intubate, cannot ventilate” (CICV) crisis in this “can ventilate but cannot intubate” (CVCI) scenario, even as oxygen saturation transiently declined from 89% to 83%.

At 19:17, endotracheal intubation was attempted. Direct laryngoscopy revealed severe, cherry‐red epiglottic swelling completely obscuring the laryngeal inlet. Three intubation attempts by experienced emergency physicians were unsuccessful.

Immediately after recognizing the CVCI scenario, the emergency physician contacted the anesthesiology and otolaryngology teams for urgent consultation. A discussion ensued regarding the optimal airway management strategy. Given the patient's extensive anterior neck edema, which obscured anatomical landmarks, and the fact that oxygenation was maintained, further intubation attempts were suspended in accordance with ASA and DAS guidelines [2, 6]. The team decided that a planned bedside tracheostomy under local anesthesia was safer and more feasible than emergency cricothyrotomy. Cricothyrotomy was prepared as a backup emergency airway in case of sudden deterioration. Clear verbal communication, predefined roles, and stepwise coordination ensured the procedure proceeded efficiently, minimized risk, and maintained patient safety. Surgical Airway and Outcome

At 20:07, a bedside tracheostomy was successfully performed under local anesthesia by the otolaryngology surgeon, establishing a definitive airway. Immediately after tracheostomy placement, airway suctioning was performed and mechanical ventilation was initiated, with optimization of ventilator parameters. Oxygen saturation rapidly improved to greater than 98%.

To maintain adequate sedation and patient comfort following airway stabilization, continuous propofol infusion was initiated at 20:12. The patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout. At 20:34, he was escorted by medical staff to the inpatient unit for further management and subsequently admitted to the emergency intensive care unit (EICU) after completion of chest and neck computed tomography imaging.

Clinical Course and Follow‐Up

The patient's subsequent clinical course in the EICU was uneventful. He received mechanical ventilation with continuous sedation, broad‐spectrum antibiotics (ceftriaxone and metronidazole), and systemic corticosteroids. Follow‐up flexible laryngoscopy on day 7 demonstrated marked resolution of epiglottic edema, and the patient was transferred to the otolaryngology ward. The tracheostomy tube was successfully decannulated on day 11. At 1‐month follow‐up, the tracheostomy site had healed completely, with normal voice and no evidence of tracheal stenosis. No additional airway interventions were required. This case illustrates the importance of monitoring airway recovery and functional outcomes following planned tracheostomy in CVCI scenarios.

Discussion

Distinguishing CVCI from CICV is critical in the management of acute epiglottitis. Although both scenarios involve failed intubation, their management pathways differ fundamentally. In CICV situations, immediate front‐of‐neck access—most commonly cricothyrotomy—is mandatory to prevent hypoxic injury and death [6, 13]. In contrast, CVCI scenarios permit a more measured, guideline‐based approach that prioritizes oxygenation, avoidance of repeated traumatic intubation attempts, and consideration of planned surgical airway when appropriate [2, 6].

In the present case, effective bag‐mask ventilation prevented progression to CICV despite multiple failed intubation attempts. This distinction allowed time for multidisciplinary consultation and careful selection of the surgical airway strategy. Although cricothyrotomy is the fastest method of front‐of‐neck access, several factors favored tracheostomy in this CVCI scenario. Extensive anterior neck edema distorted cricothyroid landmarks, increasing the risk of complications such as false passage, subcutaneous emphysema, and posterior tracheal wall injury during cricothyrotomy [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. In addition, tracheostomy provides access below the level of supraglottic inflammation, allows placement of a larger‐diameter tube to facilitate ventilation and secretion management, and is better suited for prolonged airway support when ongoing ventilation is anticipated [19, 20, 21, 22]. Recent studies on supraglottic airway compromise have also emphasized the importance of individualized tracheostomy decision‐making and perioperative airway planning in patients with complex upper airway obstruction [23].

Importantly, this approach was feasible only because oxygenation was maintained and an experienced otolaryngology surgeon was immediately available. In true CICV emergencies or settings without surgical expertise, cricothyrotomy remains the only appropriate lifesaving intervention.

Comparative studies show that cricothyrotomy and tracheostomy have differing complication profiles, emphasizing the need for individualized airway strategy selection based on patient and clinical context. In this case, tracheostomy was considered safer because extensive anterior neck edema obscured the cricothyroid landmarks [13, 24]. Multidisciplinary coordination, including predefined roles and clear communication, was essential for rapid and safe execution.

Clinical Implications

This case underscores the importance of accurate scenario recognition in difficult airway management. While CICV mandates immediate cricothyrotomy, CVCI scenarios with maintained oxygenation allow individualized decision‐making. In selected patients with acute epiglottitis, extensive neck edema, and available expertise, planned tracheostomy may represent a safer alternative to emergency cricothyrotomy.

Limitations

Limitations of this report include its single‐case design, reliance on immediate availability of an experienced otolaryngology surgeon, lack of long‐term airway outcome assessment, and limited generalizability to other settings. Further studies are needed to confirm the safety and efficacy of planned tracheostomy in similar difficult airway scenarios.

Conclusion

In acute epiglottitis presenting as a CVCI scenario with extensive neck edema, planned tracheostomy performed by experienced personnel may be safer than cricothyrotomy. Accurate differentiation between CVCI and CICV, adherence to established difficult airway guidelines, and early multidisciplinary collaboration are essential to achieving optimal patient outcomes.
