Boundary 0 / π 0/\pi logical subspace and bulk dynamical probes in flux-controlled anomalous Floquet quantum walks
WeiCheng Ning, YanSheng Liu, XiaoXue Zhang, XiZheng Zhang

Abstract
We formulate a one-dimensional flux-controlled anomalous Floquet quantum walk and show that it admits a direct microscopic realization in a driven bipartite lattice. The walk consists of a coin-dependent drift step and a momentum-dependent coin mixing step, so the same evolution operator governs quasienergy bands, boundary modes, and bulk dynamics in real space. Because the walk is chiral, the quasienergy gaps at 0 0 and π / T \pi/T carry independent topological information, which organizes trivial, 0 0 -only, π \pi -only, and coexistence sectors in the ( M , ϕ ) (M,\phi) plane. In the coexistence sector, a 0 0 mode and a π \pi mode reside on the same edge and span a natural boundary logical subspace. One Floquet period acts there as a relative phase operation and produces a clear 2 ​ T 2T response in local boundary observables. In the bulk, the same anomalous Floquet structure is probed dynamically in two complementary ways. Frame-resolved mean chiral displacements approach the two winding numbers in the clean pre-reflection window of the symmetric time frames, while selected benchmark cuts at a representative 0 0 gap closing and a representative π \pi gap closing exhibit distinct local stroboscopic responses, with the π \pi gap benchmark showing a much stronger odd-even alternation. The boundary logical subspace and the bulk dynamical probes are therefore organized within one flux-controlled anomalous Floquet quantum walk, suggesting a symmetry-protected route to quantum-walk information primitives in driven microstructured lattices.

II
Flux-controlled Floquet quantum walk and microscopic realization

We consider a one-dimensional walk whose Hilbert space factorizes as

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{p}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{A},\ket{B}\},

(1)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{p}}
labels the lattice position and the two-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
plays the role of the coin. One Floquet period is built from two substeps,

\hat{U}_{T}=\hat{W}_{2}\hat{W}_{1},\qquad\hat{W}_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{T}{2}\hat{H}_{1}},\qquad\hat{W}_{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{T}{2}\hat{H}_{2}}.

(2)

In momentum space the corresponding walk operator will take the form

U(k)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{2}(k)\sigma_{x}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{1}(k)\sigma_{z}}.

(3)

The factor generated by
\sigma_{z}
is diagonal in the coin basis and produces opposite phases for the two coin components, whereas the factor generated by
\sigma_{x}
mixes the two components. Equation (
3
) is already the defining structure of a Floquet quantum walk, with the drift substep producing the coin-dependent phase motion and the mixing substep rotating the coin.

To expose the real space update rule, let

\ket{\Psi(m)}=\sum_{n}\quantity[a_n^{(m)}\ket{n,A}+b_n^{(m)}\ket{n,B}],

(4)

with Bloch spinor

\psi_{k}^{(m)}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(m)}\\
b_{k}^{(m)}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

After the drift substep,

\psi_{k}^{(m+\frac{1}{2})}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{1}(k)\sigma_{z}}\psi_{k}^{(m)},

(6)

so that

a_{k}^{(m+\frac{1}{2})}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{1}(k)}a_{k}^{(m)},\qquad b_{k}^{(m+\frac{1}{2})}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{1}(k)}b_{k}^{(m)}.

(7)

The two coin components therefore acquire opposite momentum-dependent phases. After the mixing substep,

\psi_{k}^{(m+1)}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{2}(k)\sigma_{x}}\psi_{k}^{(m+\frac{1}{2})},

(8)

or explicitly

\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(m+1)}\\
b_{k}^{(m+1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_{2}(k)&-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta_{2}(k)\\
-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta_{2}(k)&\cos\theta_{2}(k)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}^{(m+\frac{1}{2})}\\
b_{k}^{(m+\frac{1}{2})}\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

This is a quantum walk step with a momentum-dependent coin rotation. In particular, the phase
\phi
enters through the drift sector and directly controls how the walk biases the two coin components in momentum space.

We now show that this Floquet walk is realized exactly by a driven bipartite lattice with sublattice operators
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
. The two real space Hamiltonians are chosen as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}={}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}t_{1}\sum_{n}\quantity(\e^{-\ii\phi}a_{n+1}^\dagger a_n-\e^{\ii\phi}a_n^\dagger a_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}t_{1}\sum_{n}\quantity(\e^{-\ii\phi}b_{n+1}^\dagger b_n-\e^{\ii\phi}b_n^\dagger b_{n+1}),

(10)

and

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{2}={}

\displaystyle M\sum_{n}\quantity(a_n^\dagger b_n+b_n^\dagger a_n)

\displaystyle+t_{2}\sum_{n}\quantity(a_{n+1}^\dagger b_n+a_n^\dagger b_{n+1}+\mathrm{H.c.}).

(11)

Under periodic boundary conditions we introduce the Bloch basis

\ket{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kn}\ket{n},\qquad k\in[-\pi,\pi),

(12)

which reduces the two substep Hamiltonians to

\begin{split}&H_{1}(k)=2t_{1}\sin(k-\phi)\,\sigma_{z},\\
&H_{2}(k)=\quantity(M+2t_2\cos k)\sigma_{x}.\end{split}

(13)

It is convenient to define

\begin{split}&\theta_{1}(k)=Tt_{1}\sin(k-\phi),\\
&\theta_{2}(k)=\frac{T}{2}\quantity(M+2t_2\cos k),\end{split}

(14)

which reproduces Eq. (
3
). The explicit Fourier-transform reduction from Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) to Eq. (
13
) is given in Appendix
A
. The lattice model therefore does not merely resemble a quantum walk. It realizes the walk exactly, with a direct microscopic interpretation of the two substeps. The parameter
\phi
is a flux phase in the lattice language and a drift control parameter in the quantum walk language.

III
Anomalous bulk topology

Because
U(k)
is a
2\times 2
unitary matrix, it may always be written as

U(k)=d_{0}(k)\mathbb{I}-\mathrm{i}\,\bm{d}(k)\cdot\bm{\sigma},\qquad d_{0}(k)^{2}+\absolutevalue{\bm d(k)}^{2}=1.

(15)

Using Eq. (
3
), one finds

\displaystyle U(k)={}

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{2}\cos\theta_{1}\,\mathbb{I}-\mathrm{i}\Bigl[\sin\theta_{2}\cos\theta_{1}\,\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle-\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1}\,\sigma_{y}+\cos\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1}\,\sigma_{z}\Bigr].

(16)

The quasienergy eigenvalue equation,

U(k)\ket{u_\pm(k)}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{\pm}(k)T}\ket{u_\pm(k)},

(17)

gives

\cos\quantity[\varepsilon(k)T]=\cos\theta_{1}(k)\cos\theta_{2}(k),

(18)

and hence

\varepsilon_{\pm}(k)=\pm\frac{1}{T}\arccos\quantity[\cos\theta_1(k)\cos\theta_2(k)].

(19)

The gap closes only when

\theta_{1}(k_{*})=m\pi,\qquad\theta_{2}(k_{*})=n\pi,\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z},

(20)

which yields the explicit conditions

Tt_{1}\sin(k_* - \phi)=m\pi,\quad\frac{T}{2}\quantity(M+2t_2\cos k_*)=n\pi.

(21)

If
m+n
is even the closure occurs at quasienergy
0
. If
m+n
is odd the closure occurs at quasienergy
\pi/T
. The trace derivation of the quasienergy bands and the algebra leading to the gap-closing conditions are collected in Appendix
B
.

The walk is chiral because

\sigma_{y}U(k)\sigma_{y}=U^{-1}(k),

(22)

so the chiral operator is

\Gamma=\sigma_{y}.

(23)

For one-dimensional chiral Floquet walks, one must work in two symmetric time frames
Asbóth and Obuse 2013
,

\displaystyle U_{1}(k)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta_{1}(k)}{2}\sigma_{z}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{2}(k)\sigma_{x}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta_{1}(k)}{2}\sigma_{z}},

(24)

\displaystyle U_{2}(k)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta_{2}(k)}{2}\sigma_{x}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta_{1}(k)\sigma_{z}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta_{2}(k)}{2}\sigma_{x}}.

(25)

In these frames,

U_{\ell}(k)=n_{0,\ell}(k)\mathbb{I}-\mathrm{i}\quantity[n_{x,\ell}(k)\sigma_x+n_{z,\ell}(k)\sigma_z],

(26)

where
\ell=1,2
, with

\displaystyle n_{x,1}(k)

\displaystyle=\sin\theta_{2}(k),

(27)

\displaystyle n_{z,1}(k)

\displaystyle=\cos\theta_{2}(k)\sin\theta_{1}(k),

(28)

\displaystyle n_{x,2}(k)

\displaystyle=\cos\theta_{1}(k)\sin\theta_{2}(k),

(29)

\displaystyle n_{z,2}(k)

\displaystyle=\sin\theta_{1}(k).

(30)

The winding numbers are then

W_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dk\,\partial_{k}\arg\quantity[n_{x,\ell}(k)+\ii n_{z,\ell}(k)],

(31)

where
\ell=1,2
and the gap indices are

\nu_{0}=\frac{W_{1}+W_{2}}{2},\qquad\nu_{\pi}=\frac{W_{1}-W_{2}}{2}.

(32)

Appendix
C
gives the explicit multiplication in the two symmetric time frames and shows how the vectors
(n_{x,\ell},n_{z,\ell})
define the winding numbers.

IV
Open-boundary edge sector and the
0/\pi
logical subspace

Under open boundary conditions, the coexistence sector supports boundary states pinned near quasienergies
0
and
\pi/T
. On the left edge we denote these two anomalous modes by
\ket{L,0}
and
\ket{L,\pi}
,
with Floquet eigenvalue equations

\hat{U}_{T}\ket{L,0}=+\ket{L,0},\qquad\hat{U}_{T}\ket{L,\pi}=-\ket{L,\pi},

(36)

up to exponentially small finite size corrections.

For the later numerical panels we define two explicit boundary observables. The site-one projector is

\Pi_{1}=\ket{1,A}\!\bra{1,A}+\ket{1,B}\!\bra{1,B},

(37)

and the boundary window projector is

\Pi_{\mathrm{e}}=\sum_{n=1}^{n_{\mathrm{e}}}\quantity(\ket{n,A}\!\bra{n,A}+\ket{n,B}\!\bra{n,B}),

(38)

where
n_{\mathrm{e}}
is the number of unit cells retained in the edge window. For any stroboscopic state
\ket{\Psi(m)}=\hat{U}_{T}^{m}\ket{\Psi(0)}
we then define

\begin{split}&P_{1}(m)=\matrixelement{\Psi(m)}{\Pi_1}{\Psi(m)},\\
&P_{\mathrm{e}}(m)=\matrixelement{\Psi(m)}{\Pi_{\mathrm e}}{\Psi(m)}.\end{split}

(39)

In panels (b) and (c) of Fig.
2
the initial state is chosen as the left boundary cell state
\ket{\Psi(0)}=\ket{1,A}
.

The two anomalous edge modes span a natural boundary logical subspace,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{(L)}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{L,0},\ket{L,\pi}\},

(40)

with the corresponding logical basis

\ket{0}_{\mathrm{L}}=\ket{L,0},\qquad\ket{1}_{\mathrm{L}}=\ket{L,\pi}.

(41)

This boundary subspace is not an accidental two-level sector. Its existence is tied to the chiral symmetry of the Floquet walk, with
\Gamma=\sigma_{y}
in Eq. (
23
), and to the simultaneous opening of the quasienergy gaps at
0
and
\pi/T
. Local perturbations that preserve the chiral symmetry and do not close either gap cannot remove the two edge modes individually; they can only deform their spatial profiles and modify exponentially small finite-size splittings. In this restricted but physically important sense,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{(L)}
is a symmetry-protected boundary logical sector. The term edge qubit used below refers to this protected Floquet subspace, rather than to a claim of universal fault-tolerant topological quantum computation.

In this basis,

\hat{U}_{T}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{(L)}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\equiv\tau_{z}.

(42)

After
m
periods,

\hat{U}_{T}^{m}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{edge}}^{(L)}}=\tau_{z}^{m}.

(43)

Once the Floquet action has been reduced to Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
), it is natural to regard this two-level logical subspace as an edge qubit operated stroboscopically by the anomalous
0/\pi
phase structure. Therefore a coherent superposition,

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{edge}}(0)}=\alpha\ket{L,0}+\beta\ket{L,\pi},

(44)

evolves into

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{edge}}(m)}=\alpha\ket{L,0}+(-1)^{m}\beta\ket{L,\pi}.

(45)

The relative sign alternates from one period to the next. This is the microscopic origin of the doubled-period response.

For a local boundary observable
\hat{O}_{\mathrm{edge}}
,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}={}

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}\matrixelement{L,0}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,0}+|\beta|^{2}\matrixelement{L,\pi}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,\pi}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m}\alpha^{*}\beta\matrixelement{L,0}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,\pi}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m}\beta^{*}\alpha\matrixelement{L,\pi}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,0}.

(46)

Grouping the constant and alternating pieces gives

\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}=O_{\mathrm{dc}}+(-1)^{m}O_{\mathrm{ac}}.

(47)

Equation (
47
) already shows that a robust
2T
signal requires simultaneous overlap with the
0
and
\pi
sectors. This point becomes sharper if one decomposes a generic boundary localized initial state as

\ket{\Psi(0)}=c_{0}\ket{L,0}+c_{\pi}\ket{L,\pi}+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}\ket{\mu},

(48)

where
\ket{\mu}
denotes every remaining Floquet eigenstate with

\hat{U}_{T}\ket{\mu}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{\mu}T}\ket{\mu}.

(49)

For any boundary local observable
\hat{O}_{\mathrm{edge}}
one then has

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}=

\displaystyle|c_{0}|^{2}O_{00}+|c_{\pi}|^{2}O_{\pi\pi}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m}\quantity(c_0^*c_\pi O_{0\pi}+\mathrm{c.c.})+R(m),

(50)

where
R(m)
collects all terms involving at least one nonanomalous mode. Once the fast oscillatory part
R(m)
dephases, the long-time boundary response falls into four distinct classes,

\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}\longrightarrow\begin{cases}0\ \text{or a small background},&\text{trivial sector},\\[3.0pt]
|c_{0}|^{2}O_{00},&0\text{-only sector},\\[3.0pt]
|c_{\pi}|^{2}O_{\pi\pi},&\pi\text{-only sector},\\[3.0pt]
O_{\mathrm{dc}}+(-1)^{m}O_{\mathrm{ac}},&0+\pi\text{ sector}.\end{cases}

(51)

The crucial point is that a single
0
edge mode or a single
\pi
edge mode produces a stroboscopically constant local expectation value, because the overall Floquet phase cancels in
\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}
. A genuine
2T
signal appears only when both anomalous edge sectors are coherently occupied on the same boundary. Appendix
D
gives the detailed derivation.

V
Bulk dynamical probes of anomalous Floquet topology

The bulk part of the walk should be read in the same language as the edge part. The main question is not whether one can draw another propagation plot, but whether the anomalous Floquet topology leaves direct dynamical fingerprints in bulk observables. In the present model this information appears in two complementary observables: a frame-resolved topological probe and a benchmark comparison of local stroboscopic dynamics at representative
0
gap and
\pi
gap critical points.

V.1
Frame-resolved mean chiral displacement

Because the walk is chiral in two symmetric time frames, a natural bulk probe is the frame-resolved mean chiral displacement. For the numerical data in Fig.
3
we work on a long periodic chain of odd length
L=2L_{0}+1
, choose the central launch cell

n_{0}=\frac{L+1}{2},

(53)

and take the initial bulk localized state

\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{n_0,A}.

(54)

The position operator is measured relative to this preparation center,

\hat{x}=\sum_{n=1}^{L}(n-n_{0})\quantity(\ket{n,A}\!\bra{n,A}+\ket{n,B}\!\bra{n,B}),

(55)

and
\Gamma=\sigma_{y}
acts in the coin subspace. Let
U_{\ell}
denote the Floquet operator in symmetric time frame
\ell=1,2
. We define

C_{\ell}(m)=\matrixelement{\psi_0}{(U_\ell^\dagger)^m\,\hat x\,\Gamma\,U_\ell^m}{\psi_0}\qquad\ell=1,2.

(56)

To suppress finite time oscillations, we use the running average

\overline{C}_{\ell}(m)=\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{r=0}^{m}C_{\ell}(r).

(57)

In our convention the long-time limits satisfy

-2\overline{C}_{1}(m)\to W_{1},\quad-2\overline{C}_{2}(m)\to W_{2},\quad m\to\infty.

(58)

This relation is the dynamical bulk counterpart of Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
). A step-by-step momentum space derivation of the time-averaged part is summarized in Appendix
E
.

58
) is the thermodynamic target, while Fig.

-only point

Selected benchmark critical dynamics

The benchmark bulk probe addresses a different question. Even though both
0

\pi

0

\pi

For the benchmark calculation we again use a long periodic chain and the bulk localized launch state

\ket{\Psi(0)}=\ket{n_0,A},

(59)

with the same central cell
n_{0}
as in Eq. (

). The local return projector is

\Pi_{n_{0}}=\ket{n_0,A}\!\bra{n_0,A}+\ket{n_0,B}\!\bra{n_0,B},

(60)

and the return probability used in Fig.

\begin{split}&P_{\mathrm{ret}}(m)=\matrixelement{\Psi(m)}{\Pi_{n_0}}{\Psi(m)},\\
&\ket{\Psi(m)}=\hat{U}_{T}^{m}\ket{\Psi(0)}.\end{split}

(61)

For the
0

C_{0}=(0,\pi/2)
, the gap closes at

. Writing

with small

(62)

(63)

The critical contribution is therefore organized around
+\mathbb{I}
. No intrinsic factor
(-1)^{m}
is generated by the stroboscopic evolution.

For the
\pi

C_{\pi}=(\pi-2,0)

k_{*}=0
. Expanding around

gives

\theta_{1}\simeq 2q,\qquad\theta_{2}\simeq\pi-q^{2},

(64)

which leads to

U(q)\simeq-\mathbb{I}+\mathrm{i}\,2q\sigma_{z}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}).

(65)

The key difference is the overall factor
-\mathbb{I}
. It implies that the critical contribution naturally carries a stroboscopic factor
(-1)^{m}
, so odd and even periods are expected to separate much more clearly in local observables. The Taylor expansions used in Eqs. (
63

Taken together, the two bulk probes play different roles. The frame-resolved mean chiral displacement provides a bulk dynamical route toward the two winding numbers, while the benchmark critical dynamics shows that selected

\pi

VI
Conclusion

We have formulated a flux-controlled anomalous Floquet quantum walk and connected it to a concrete driven bipartite lattice, so that the same step operator controls the dual gap topology, the boundary
0/\pi
sector, and the bulk dynamical probes. In the coexistence region, the
0
and
\pi
edge modes on the same boundary span a symmetry-protected logical subspace whose relative Floquet phase produces the doubled-period local response. In the bulk, frame-resolved mean chiral displacements approach the winding targets in the two symmetric time frames over the clean pre-reflection window, while selected
0
gap and
\pi
gap benchmarks exhibit distinct local stroboscopic fingerprints with a stronger odd-even alternation at the
\pi
gap benchmark. The required ingredients are experimentally concrete: one-dimensional driven bipartite lattices with controllable hopping phases can be approached in periodically driven optical lattices, photonic quantum-walk architectures, and coupled waveguide or resonator arrays, where site-resolved density, boundary return probability, and mean chiral displacement are natural observables. The model therefore goes beyond a generic demonstration of boundary modes or packet propagation: it gives a minimal microscopic anomalous Floquet quantum walk in which boundary logical structure and bulk dynamical readout are tied to the same flux-controlled protocol.

Detailed derivation of the Floquet quantum walk mapping

Start from the real space Hamiltonians in Eqs. (

(66)

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n}a_{n+1}^{\dagger}a_{n}={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n,k,q}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(n+1)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kn}a_{q}^{\dagger}a_{k}=\sum_{k}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}a_{k}^{\dagger}a_{k},

(67)

(68)

Therefore the

(69)

The
b

-2t_{1}\sum_{k}\sin(k-\phi)b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}.

(70)

H_{1}(k)=2t_{1}\sin(k-\phi)\sigma_{z}.

(71)

\displaystyle H_{2}(k)

Appendix D
Detailed derivation of the boundary phase operation and the sector dependent local periods

The boundary discussion in the main text contains two logically distinct parts. One part shows why a coherent
0/\pi
superposition generates a doubled-period signal. The other part shows why the four topological sectors produce different long-time boundary responses in local observables.

Because

\hat{U}_{T}\ket{L,0}=+\ket{L,0},\qquad\hat{U}_{T}\ket{L,\pi}=-\ket{L,\pi},

(84)

a superposition of the two anomalous edge modes,

\ket{\psi(0)}=\alpha\ket{L,0}+\beta\ket{L,\pi},

(85)

evolves into

\ket{\psi(m)}=\alpha\ket{L,0}+(-1)^{m}\beta\ket{L,\pi}.

(86)

For a local boundary observable
\hat{O}_{\mathrm{edge}}
one then obtains

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}={}

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\psi(m)}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{\psi(m)}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}O_{00}+|\beta|^{2}O_{\pi\pi}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m}\alpha^{*}\beta O_{0\pi}+(-1)^{m}\beta^{*}\alpha O_{\pi 0},

(87)

where

\begin{split}&O_{00}=\matrixelement{L,0}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,0},\\
&O_{\pi\pi}=\matrixelement{L,\pi}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,\pi},\\
&O_{0\pi}=\matrixelement{L,0}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{L,\pi}.\end{split}

(88)

Therefore

\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}=O_{\mathrm{dc}}+(-1)^{m}O_{\mathrm{ac}},

(89)

which is the origin of the doubled-period response.

To classify the long-time local responses of the different topological sectors, we must now include all other Floquet eigenstates. Let

\ket{\Psi(0)}=c_{0}\ket{L,0}+c_{\pi}\ket{L,\pi}+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}\ket{\mu},

(90)

where the remaining eigenstates satisfy

\hat{U}_{T}\ket{\mu}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{\mu}T}\ket{\mu}.

(91)

The evolved state is

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(m)}=

\displaystyle c_{0}\ket{L,0}+(-1)^{m}c_{\pi}\ket{L,\pi}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\varepsilon_{\mu}T}\ket{\mu}.

(92)

Insert Eq. (
92
) into
\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}
. The terms containing only the two anomalous edge modes reproduce Eq. (
89
). Every remaining contribution contains at least one nonanomalous mode and can be grouped into a remainder

\displaystyle R(m)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}\Bigl[c_{0}^{*}c_{\mu}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\varepsilon_{\mu}T}O_{0\mu}+c_{\pi}^{*}c_{\mu}(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m\varepsilon_{\mu}T}O_{\pi\mu}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{c.c.}\Bigr]+\sum_{\mu,\nu}c_{\mu}^{*}c_{\nu}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m(\varepsilon_{\mu}-\varepsilon_{\nu})T}O_{\mu\nu},

(93)

with the obvious notation
O_{ab}=\matrixelement{a}{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}{b}
. Hence

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat O_{\mathrm{edge}}}_{m}=

\displaystyle|c_{0}|^{2}O_{00}+|c_{\pi}|^{2}O_{\pi\pi}

\displaystyle+(-1)^{m}\quantity(c_0^*c_\pi O_{0\pi}+\mathrm{c.c.})+R(m).

(94)

Equation (
94
) immediately explains the sector dependence of local boundary observables. In the trivial sector there is no anomalous edge mode, so the boundary localized initial state overlaps only nonanomalous states and the long-time local edge signal is lost once the packet leaks into the bulk. In the
0
-only sector one has
c_{\pi}=0
, and the only persistent edge contribution is
|c_{0}|^{2}O_{00}
, which is stroboscopically constant. In the
\pi
-only sector one has
c_{0}=0
, and the only persistent edge contribution is
|c_{\pi}|^{2}O_{\pi\pi}
, which is also stroboscopically constant because the single Floquet phase
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi m}
cancels out in the expectation value. Only in the coexistence sector can both
c_{0}
and
c_{\pi}
be nonzero on the same edge, and only then does the alternating term survive. The long-time classification quoted in Eq. (
51
) follows directly.

The optimized state used in Fig.
2
(d) follows from the same formula. For
\hat{O}_{\mathrm{edge}}=\Pi_{1}
, the alternating amplitude is

O_{\mathrm{ac}}=\alpha^{*}\beta\matrixelement{L,0}{\Pi_1}{L,\pi}+\mathrm{c.c.}

(95)

under the constraint
|\alpha|=|\beta|=1/\sqrt{2}
. This amplitude is maximal when

\beta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\arg\matrixelement{L,0}{\Pi_1}{L,\pi}},\qquad\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

(96)

which reproduces Eq. (
52
).

For the frame-resolved bulk probe, translation invariance allows a momentum space derivation of the time-averaged part. For a centered local initial state, the momentum amplitude is uniform, and the position operator acts as

3
