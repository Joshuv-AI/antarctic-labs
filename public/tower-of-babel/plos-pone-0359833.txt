Computational identification of natural CXCR4-binding candidates relevant to endometriosis and ovarian cancer progression

Sinan Karageçili, Deniz Inan, Nouman Ali

ABSTRACT
Endometriosis and ovarian cancer share invasive, inflammatory, and pro-survival mechanisms in which CXCR4 signaling may play an important role, yet phytochemical modulators of this receptor remain insufficiently characterized. This study addressed that gap by integrating receptor reconstruction, ligand-guided docking, pharmacokinetic and toxicity prediction, pharmacophore mapping, density functional theory, replicated membrane-embedded molecular dynamics, and MM/GBSA analysis. A native human CXCR4 model was generated using SWISS-MODEL with 98.2% template coverage and 100% sequence identity, while the co-crystallized IT1t ligand defined the docking cavity. Eighteen phytochemicals were screened, and naringenin achieved the most favorable predicted docking score (−6.361 kcal/mol), exceeding apigenin (−5.927 kcal/mol) and IT1t (−5.155 kcal/mol). Naringenin formed predicted interactions with Tyr116, Arg188, and Tyr255, showed acceptable drug-likeness, solubility, and gastrointestinal absorption, and displayed a wider HOMO–LUMO gap than apigenin. Two independent 100 ns simulations of the CXCR4 complexes with naringenin, apigenin, and IT1t indicated comparatively consistent structural behavior for naringenin, supported by RMSD, SASA, radius of gyration, hydrogen-bond, PCA, FEL, and DCCM analyses. MM/GBSA estimates were also more favorable for naringenin than apigenin across both replicas. Its predicted LD50 was 2000 mg/kg (toxicity class 4), emphasizing that favorable receptor interactions do not establish safety and require experimental toxicological assessment during further preclinical development. Collectively, naringenin emerged as the most coherent in silico prioritized CXCR4-binding candidate. However, the absence of receptor-binding assays, functional validation, and in vivo confirmation remains a major limitation.

Introduction Endometriosis is a chronic, estrogen-dependent inflammatory disorder characterized by the presence of endometrium-like tissue outside the uterine cavity and is frequently associated with pelvic pain, infertility, and impaired quality of life. A population-based investigation using magnetic resonance imaging estimated that approximately 11% of women may have previously undiagnosed endometriosis, although prevalence estimates vary substantially with the population and diagnostic method used [ 1 ]. Endometriosis is biologically distinct from ovarian cancer and does not generally undergo malignant transformation. Nevertheless, epidemiological evidence indicates that women with ovarian endometrioma have an increased subsequent risk of ovarian malignancy, with the association being particularly relevant to endometrioid and clear-cell histological subtypes [ 2 ]. This association, together with the persistence and recurrence of both conditions, supports investigation of molecular pathways that may contribute to cell survival, invasion, inflammatory signaling, and tissue dissemination. The C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) is a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor activated principally by C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12). In endometriosis, CXCR4 expression has been detected in human endometrial and endometriotic tissues, with variation among lesion types. Experimental stimulation of endometriotic epithelial cells with CXCL12 increased AKT phosphorylation and influenced cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas pharmacological CXCR4 blockade produced context-dependent effects depending on the presence of CXCL12 [ 3 ]. In ovarian cancer models, activation of the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis promoted cellular proliferation, migration, invasion, and peritoneal metastatic dissemination [ 4 ]. These findings support CXCR4 as a pharmacologically relevant candidate target in both disease settings, although its biological effects remain dependent on cellular and ligand context. The pharmacological tractability of CXCR4 is supported by the development of chemically distinct antagonists. Plerixafor, also known as AMD3100, has demonstrated clinical utility in combination with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor for hematopoietic stem-cell mobilization, but it is not an established treatment for either endometriosis or ovarian cancer [ 5 ]. Structural studies have also resolved human CXCR4 in complex with the small-molecule antagonist IT1t and the cyclic peptide antagonist CVX15, defining experimentally observed antagonist-binding modes within the receptor cavity [ 6 ]. The co-crystallized IT1t ligand therefore provides an appropriate structural benchmark for validating docking within the CXCR4 orthosteric pocket. The phytochemical library investigated in this study comprised 18 structurally diverse natural compounds selected on the basis of experimentally reported activities relevant to the underlying biology of endometriosis or ovarian cancer, including modulation of inflammatory signaling, cellular proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, angiogenesis, and oxidative-stress responses. Apigenin was included because experimental treatment reduced proliferation and induced apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and endoplasmic-reticulum stress in human endometriosis cell models [ 7 ]. Naringenin similarly suppressed proliferation and induced mitochondria-mediated apoptosis and endoplasmic-reticulum stress in human endometriosis cells [ 8 ]. Its biological relevance was further supported by a rat endometriosis model in which naringenin treatment reduced lesion burden and inhibited invasion-associated molecular markers [ 9 ]. These experimental observations justify the inclusion of apigenin and naringenin in the screening panel but do not establish that either compound directly antagonizes CXCR4. Structure-based computational screening provides a means of generating testable hypotheses regarding ligand orientation, receptor contacts, and relative compound prioritization. Molecular-docking algorithms such as AutoDock Vina can efficiently sample and rank predicted ligand poses, but their scoring functions provide approximate estimates rather than experimentally measured binding affinities [ 10 , 11 ]. Consequently, docking scores should be interpreted together with binding-site interactions, pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions, electronic properties, and receptor dynamics. For a transmembrane receptor such as CXCR4, dynamic evaluation must also preserve the membrane-associated environment of the receptor and assess reproducibility across independent trajectories. Accordingly, the present study screened 18 phytochemicals against a reconstructed native human CXCR4 model using an IT1t-defined binding pocket and a redocking-validated protocol. Candidate prioritization was based on comparative docking, residue-level interaction patterns, predicted ADMET properties, and toxicity-related liabilities rather than docking score alone. Apigenin and naringenin were subsequently subjected to comparative pharmacophore characterization, density functional theory calculations, independent membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations, and binding free-energy analysis. The objective was to determine whether these compounds could be computationally prioritized as candidate CXCR4-binding ligands for subsequent biochemical and experimental evaluation. The analyses were not intended to establish direct CXCR4 antagonism, therapeutic efficacy, or clinical safety. Methodology Reconstruction and structural validation of native human C-X-C chemokine receptor (CXCR4) The X-ray crystallographic structure of human C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) bound to the small-molecule antagonist IT1t was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank ( https://www.rcsb.org/ ) in PDB format using accession ID 3OE6 [ 6 , 12 ]. The 3OE6 structure contains an engineered T4 lysozyme fusion inserted in place of the native third intracellular loop of CXCR4. Therefore, the crystallographic construct was not used directly as the receptor model for molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. The coordinates of the co-crystallized IT1t ligand were retained separately for subsequent binding-site identification and redocking validation. A native human CXCR4 model was generated using the template-based modeling option of the SWISS-MODEL server ( https://swissmodel.expasy.org/ ) [ 13 ]. Before modeling, the T4 lysozyme sequence was excluded from the crystallographic construct, and the native human CXCR4 amino-acid sequence, including intracellular loop 3, was used as the target sequence. The CXCR4 component of PDB ID 3OE6 was selected as the structural template because it provides experimentally resolved coordinates for the receptor transmembrane domain. Template–target alignment was inspected to verify the correspondence of the seven transmembrane helices and to ensure that non-CXCR4 residues from the crystallographic fusion construct were not incorporated into the reconstructed receptor. The generated models were evaluated using the structural quality estimates provided by SWISS-MODEL, including the Global Model Quality Estimate and QMEANDisCo score. The model selected for downstream analysis was downloaded in PDB format and inspected to confirm the continuity of the protein backbone, reconstruction of the native intracellular loop, and absence of T4 lysozyme residues. The selected model was subsequently superimposed on the experimentally resolved CXCR4 portion of the 3OE6 structure to examine preservation of the receptor transmembrane architecture. Stereochemical validation of the reconstructed CXCR4 model was performed using the SAVES server ( https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/ ). Backbone dihedral-angle distributions were evaluated through Ramachandran plot analysis using PROCHECK, while overall non-bonded atomic interactions were assessed using ERRAT [ 14 , 15 ]. The reconstructed and structurally evaluated native CXCR4 model was then used for receptor preparation, molecular docking, and membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations. Co-crystallized ligand-guided definition of the CXCR4 binding site The ligand-binding site of CXCR4 was defined using the experimentally resolved position of the co-crystallized small-molecule antagonist IT1t in PDB structure 3OE6. The original 3OE6–IT1t complex was opened in BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer V25.1.0.24284, and the co-crystallized ligand was selected to identify its spatial location within the receptor-binding cavity [ 16 ]. The geometric center of IT1t was determined from its Cartesian coordinates and used as the reference point for positioning the molecular-docking grid. The resulting grid center was defined at X = 17.6 Å, Y = 28.8 Å, and Z = +73.4 Å. Particular attention was given to retaining the positive sign of the Z coordinate. The position of the grid box was visually inspected in Discovery Studio Visualizer to confirm that it encompassed the complete co-crystallized IT1t ligand and the surrounding CXCR4 binding-site residues. The same binding-site location was subsequently used for redocking IT1t and for docking all screened phytochemicals. Selection and retrieval of phytochemical structures A panel of 18 phytochemicals was compiled based on peer-reviewed experimental reports describing biological activities relevant to endometriosis or ovarian cancer, including modulation of inflammatory signaling, cellular proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and oxidative-stress-associated pathways. The compounds, their reported natural sources, PubChem Compound Identifiers, and the experimental literature supporting their inclusion are summarized in S1 Table . Compound eligibility was assessed against predefined criteria applied before any structural or docking analysis was performed. A compound was considered eligible when peer-reviewed in vitro or in vivo evidence described an effect on at least one of the biological processes listed above in endometriosis, ovarian cancer, or a mechanistically related gynecological or oncological model; when it originated from a dietary or medicinal plant source and was therefore obtainable for subsequent experimental follow-up; when an unambiguous PubChem Compound Identifier and a complete three-dimensional structure were available for retrieval; and when its physicochemical properties fell within the chemical space typically associated with orally administered small molecules, so that the downstream pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions would remain interpretable. The eligible compounds were then grouped by chemical class, and representatives of flavones, flavanones, flavonols, stilbenes, phenolic acids, terpenoids, and alkaloids were retained so that the final panel sampled structurally distinct scaffolds rather than close analogues of a single class. Compounds were excluded when the supporting evidence originated from unrelated disease contexts, when the reported activity could not be traced to a primary experimental study, when the deposited structure was incomplete or stereochemically ambiguous, or when the compound belonged to a chemical class associated with non-specific assay interference. Because the panel was defined solely by literature-based and structural criteria, and because none of the selected compounds had an established CXCR4-directed mechanism of action, the screen was designed to be hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The three-dimensional structures of the selected phytochemicals were retrieved from the PubChem database ( https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ ) in Structure Data File format using their respective PubChem Compound Identifiers [ 17 , 18 ]. Each downloaded structure was inspected for chemical identity, atom connectivity, bond-order assignment, and valence consistency. The corresponding Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System strings were recorded for subsequent pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions. The ligand structures were converted into Protein Data Bank format for molecular docking, while ligand energy minimization and docking-specific preparation were performed as described in the molecular docking subsection. Preparation of the reconstructed CXCR4 receptor The reconstructed and structurally evaluated native human CXCR4 model was used for receptor preparation. The model was processed using PDBFixer V1.12 to inspect the structure for missing heavy atoms, incomplete side chains, non-standard residues, and connectivity-related inconsistencies [ 19 ]. Hydrogen atoms were added using PDBFixer, and the protonation states of ionizable residues were assigned to represent a physiological pH of 7.4. Any structural issues identified during this inspection were corrected before energy minimization. The processed receptor was subsequently imported into Swiss-PdbViewer V4.10 and subjected to energy minimization to reduce local steric clashes and geometrical strain [ 13 ]. The minimized receptor structure was saved in Protein Data Bank format and used for subsequent molecular docking and membrane-system preparation. Molecular docking and interaction studies of phytochemicals with CXCR4 Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding affinity and interaction patterns of the selected phytochemicals with CXCR4 using AutoDock Vina as implemented in SAMSON Connect [ 20 , 21 ]. Before docking, ligand geometries were energy minimized using the Universal Force Field to obtain stable conformations and to reduce strain that could bias docking poses. The prepared receptor and ligand files were converted into the required input format, and all atom types and bond assignments were verified to ensure compatibility with the docking engine. The molecular-docking grid was defined using the experimentally resolved position of IT1t in the 3OE6 structure. The final reconstructed, protonated, and energy-minimized CXCR4 model was structurally aligned with the CXCR4 component of the original 3OE6–IT1t complex in BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer V25.1.0.24284. Following receptor alignment, the crystallographic IT1t coordinates were retained as the spatial reference for defining the ligand-binding region. The grid center was positioned at X = 17.6 Å, Y = 28.8 Å, and Z = 73.4 Å, corresponding to the geometric center of the aligned IT1t ligand. The grid dimensions were set to 20 × 20 × 20.1 Å to encompass the complete crystallographic ligand and the surrounding binding-site residues. The grid placement was visually inspected before docking, and identical grid parameters were applied to IT1t redocking and phytochemical screening. The AutoDock Vina exhaustiveness value was set to 8. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the docking protocol, a validation step was performed using the co crystal ligand present in the CXCR4 structure. The co crystal ligand, Isothiourea-1t, was first extracted from the protein structure and then redocked into the defined binding pocket using the same docking parameters applied to all phytochemicals. Following redocking, the predicted IT1t pose was superimposed on its crystallographic conformation, and the heavy-atom RMSD was calculated using DockRMSD ( https://zhanggroup.org/DockRMSD/ ) [ 22 ]. The crystallographic and redocked poses were also visually compared to evaluate reproduction of the ligand orientation and binding-site contacts. Protein ligand interaction profiling was then carried out using Ligplot+ and Discovery Studio Visualizer to generate two dimensional and three-dimensional interaction maps [ 16 , 23 – 26 ]. These tools were used to identify hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and aromatic interactions between CXCR4 and each ligand, which helps explain docking affinity at the residue level. Interaction analysis is important because docking scores alone do not describe binding mechanisms, and visual inspection of residue level contacts improves confidence in selecting lead compounds for further evaluation. In-silico ADMET analysis on lead In silico pharmacokinetic and toxicity profiling of the lead compound was performed to evaluate its drug likeness, absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and potential toxicity risks using established web-based tools. ADME properties were predicted using the SwissADME server ( http://www.swissadme.ch/ ), where the SMILES format of the lead compound was used as input [ 27 , 28 ]. The analysis included evaluation of physicochemical parameters, lipophilicity, water solubility, gastrointestinal absorption, blood brain barrier permeability, P glycoprotein interaction, and compliance with drug likeness rules such as Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge filters. SwissADME was used because it provides a comprehensive and reproducible platform for early-stage drug screening by integrating multiple predictive models into a single workflow. Toxicity assessment was conducted using the ProTox 3.0 server ( https://tox.charite.de/protox3/ ) where the SMILES structure of the compound was submitted to predict acute toxicity, LD50 values, toxicity class, organ specific toxicity, and various molecular initiating events [ 29 ]. The platform also provided predictions related to carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, immunotoxicity, and interaction with nuclear receptor signaling pathways. Toxicity profiling is essential in computational drug discovery because it allows early identification of potential safety concerns, thereby reducing the likelihood of failure in later experimental or clinical stages. Pharmacophore characterization and Density functional theory (DFT) of lead drug candidate Pharmacophore characterization of the lead compound was performed using the Pharmit server ( https://pharmit.csb.pitt.edu/ ) to identify the key spatial and chemical features required for interaction with the CXCR4 binding pocket [ 30 , 31 ]. The optimized lead ligand structure was uploaded to the server, and pharmacophore features such as aromatic centers, hydrogen bond donors, hydrogen bond acceptors, and hydrophobic regions were mapped based on the docked conformation. This analysis was included to define the essential interaction pattern of the lead molecule in a reproducible way and to support future virtual screening or lead optimization studies using feature-based filtering. Quantum chemical analysis of the lead compound was then carried out using Density Functional Theory in Gaussian 09W software to evaluate its electronic properties and chemical reactivity [ 32 ]. The molecular geometry was optimized at the B3LYP level of theory with the 6 31G d p basis set to obtain a minimum energy conformation suitable for electronic structure calculations. Frontier molecular orbitals, including the highest occupied molecular orbital and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital, were calculated from the optimized structure, and the HOMO LUMO energy gap was determined as the difference between the orbital energies. Molecular electrostatic potential mapping was also performed to visualize electron density distribution and identify regions susceptible to electrophilic or nucleophilic attack. DFT analysis was used because it provides mechanistic insight into the intrinsic reactivity and stability of the lead compound, which complements docking and simulation by explaining how electronic properties may influence binding behavior. Comparative membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations and post-simulation analyses Comparative all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed for three receptor–ligand systems: CXCR4 complexed with naringenin, CXCR4 complexed with apigenin, and CXCR4 complexed with the crystallographic reference antagonist IT1t. Naringenin was evaluated as the computationally prioritized lead compound, apigenin was included as a comparative phytochemical, and IT1t was used as the reference ligand. The selected docked pose of each ligand was combined with the reconstructed native CXCR4 model and used as the starting structure for membrane-system construction. Each CXCR4–ligand complex was oriented relative to the membrane normal using the Positioning of Proteins in Membranes server (PPM) and subsequently submitted to the CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder for system construction [ 33 , 34 ]. The receptor–ligand complexes were embedded in a symmetric, pre-equilibrated 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl- sn -glycero-3-phosphocholine bilayer containing 204 POPC molecules. The systems were hydrated using the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model, with a minimum water thickness of 22.5 Å on both sides of the membrane. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the net system charge and obtain an ionic concentration of 0.15 M. The resulting systems were placed in rectangular periodic boxes of approximately 90 × 90 × 113 Å and contained approximately 85,300 atoms, with minor differences arising from ligand identity and the number of counterions. The CHARMM36m force field was applied to CXCR4, while CHARMM36 lipid parameters were used for the POPC bilayer [ 35 , 36 ]. Ligand topology and parameter files for naringenin, apigenin, and IT1t were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field, and the assigned atom types, net charges, bonded parameters, and molecular connectivity were inspected before system assembly [ 36 ]. The CHARMM-compatible TIP3P model was used for water to maintain consistency with the selected force-field framework. Each assembled system was subjected to energy minimization using the input parameters generated by CHARMM-GUI. The minimized systems were then equilibrated using the standard six-step CHARMM-GUI membrane-equilibration protocol. Positional restraints applied to the receptor, ligand, and lipid headgroups were progressively reduced during consecutive constant-volume and constant-pressure equilibration stages. Temperature and pressure were gradually adjusted to the production conditions while maintaining the receptor orientation within the membrane. The complete equilibration procedure was conducted separately for each receptor–ligand system before production simulation. Production simulations were performed using GROMACS version 2026.1 [ 37 ]. Each of the three receptor–ligand systems was simulated for 100 ns in two independent replicas, resulting in six production trajectories and an aggregate production-simulation time of 600 ns. The replicas were initiated using different random velocity seeds to provide independent sampling of the starting systems. Production simulations were conducted under the NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1 bar. Temperature was controlled using the velocity-rescale thermostat, with the protein–ligand complex, membrane, and solvent–ion groups coupled separately [ 38 ]. Pressure was controlled using the C-rescale barostat with semi-isotropic coupling, allowing independent adjustment of the membrane-plane and membrane-normal dimensions [ 39 ]. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three dimensions. A 2 fs integration time step was used, and covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm [ 40 ]. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle-mesh Ewald method with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm. Lennard–Jones interactions were calculated using a force-switching function between 1.0 and 1.2 nm, consistent with the CHARMM36 simulation settings. Coordinates were recorded every 100 ps for subsequent trajectory analysis. Binding free-energy calculations were performed independently for both replicas of each receptor–ligand complex using gmx_MMPBSA (Version v1.6.5) following completion of the production simulations [ 41 ]. Trajectory frames were extracted from the production trajectories using the same sampling interval for all complexes. A single-trajectory molecular mechanics–generalized Born surface area (MM/GBSA) protocol was employed to estimate the relative binding free energies of naringenin, apigenin, and IT1t. The calculations were performed using the Generalized Born (GB-OBC1) implicit solvent model (igb = 2) with the default internal dielectric constant of 1.0, external dielectric constant of 80.0, and an ionic strength of 0.15 M. The non-polar solvation energy was estimated from the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) using the default LCPO surface-area model implemented in gmx_MMPBSA. Entropic contributions were not included, as the objective of the analysis was to compare the relative binding affinities of the receptor–ligand complexes under identical computational conditions. Per-residue free-energy decomposition was performed using the same trajectory frames to identify the major CXCR4 residues contributing to ligand binding. Representative receptor–ligand structures presented in the manuscript were extracted from the final simulation frame (100 ns) of each replica for structural visualization and interaction analysis. All structural, conformational, and energetic analyses were first performed separately for Replica 1 and Replica 2. Replica-specific profiles were retained to evaluate whether the principal trends were reproduced across independent trajectories. Where combined summary values were reported, the mean and standard deviation were calculated from the two replica-level estimates rather than treating individual trajectory frames as statistically independent observations. Because only two replicas were available for each system, the comparisons were interpreted descriptively and were not used to claim definitive statistical convergence. Results Reconstruction and structural validation of the human CXCR4 model The X-ray crystal structure of CXCR4 deposited under PDB ID 3OE6 was used as the structural template for receptor reconstruction. The template was classified in the Protein Data Bank as a signaling protein–hydrolase complex and was resolved by X-ray diffraction at a resolution of 3.20 Å. The structure was expressed in Spodoptera frugiperda cells and was deposited as a mutated membrane-protein construct containing components derived from Homo sapiens and Tequatrovirus T4. The reported crystallographic refinement statistics included depositor and diffraction-component-correction R-free values of 0.306 and 0.318, respectively, and corresponding R-work values of 0.232 and 0.254. The observed depositor R-value was 0.235. Template-based modeling in SWISS-MODEL produced a reconstructed human CXCR4 structure with 98.2% template coverage and 100% sequence identity across the aligned region. The resulting model contained 306 CXCR4 residues, without the T4 lysozyme fusion partner or the co-crystallized IT1t ligand, as shown in Fig 1A . The model achieved a Global Model Quality Estimate of 0.92 and a QMEANDisCo global score of 0.89 ± 0.05. In silico ADME screening and comparative pharmacokinetic profiling The initial ADME screening showed that all 18 phytochemicals complied with Lipinski’s rule of five and were predicted to exhibit high gastrointestinal absorption. Consensus lipophilicity values ranged from 1.00 for sulforaphane to 4.63 for parthenolide, indicating variation in hydrophobic character across the screened library. Discussion Endometriosis and ovarian cancer progression remain major clinical concerns due to their multifactorial nature, shared molecular pathways, and limited effectiveness of current therapeutic strategies [ 42 ]. Endometriosis is characterized by ectopic implantation of endometrial tissue, leading to chronic inflammation, pain, and infertility, while ovarian cancer is associated with high mortality due to late diagnosis and metastasis [ 43 , 44 ]. Increasing evidence supports a molecular link between these two conditions, particularly through dysregulated chemokine signaling pathways [ 42 ]. Among these, the CXCR4–CXCL12 axis plays a central role in promoting cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis in both diseases, making it a promising therapeutic target [ 43 , 45 ]. The present computational pipeline integrated receptor reconstruction, ligand-guided binding-site definition, molecular docking, pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions, pharmacophore mapping, electronic-structure calculations, replicated membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations, conformational analyses, and MM/GBSA calculations to prioritize a phytochemical candidate against CXCR4. The reconstructed native human CXCR4 model showed high sequence identity and near-complete template coverage, together with favorable stereochemical and non-bonded interaction-quality indicators. These validation results supported its use for the subsequent structure-based analyses. The experimentally resolved position of IT1t in the 3OE6 complex was then transferred to the reconstructed receptor through structural alignment, allowing the docking grid to be defined directly from the crystallographic ligand-binding region rather than from an independently predicted pocket. Docking of the phytochemical library identified naringenin as the highest-ranking compound, with a predicted docking score of −6.361 kcal/mol. Apigenin showed a moderately favorable score of −5.927 kcal/mol, whereas the co-crystallized reference antagonist IT1t produced a score of −5.155 kcal/mol under the same docking conditions. Naringenin was therefore selected as the principal computational lead, while apigenin was retained as a structurally related moderate comparator and IT1t served as the crystallographic reference ligand. This three-system design enabled comparison of the top-ranked phytochemical with both a chemically related flavonoid and an experimentally resolved CXCR4 antagonist. The residue-level docking profiles further differentiated the two flavonoids. Naringenin formed predicted conventional hydrogen bonds with Tyr116, Arg188, and Tyr255, together with aromatic interactions involving Tyr116 and Tyr255. Apigenin retained interactions with Arg188 and Tyr255 but formed a less extensive predicted interaction network. The pharmacophore maps were consistent with these docking patterns. Naringenin contained five hydrogen-bond acceptor features and supported a broader predicted polar-contact network, whereas apigenin contained more aromatic and hydrophobic features. These complementary results suggested that the prioritization of naringenin was not based solely on its docking score but also on the distribution of interaction features capable of supporting polar and aromatic recognition within the CXCR4 cavity. The predicted ADME profiles showed that both naringenin and apigenin satisfied the evaluated rule-based drug-likeness filters, were classified as soluble, and were predicted to exhibit high gastrointestinal absorption without blood–brain barrier permeation. Their physicochemical properties were also closely comparable, making apigenin a suitable baseline compound for assessing the dynamic consequences of the structural differences between the two flavonoids. A predicted distinction was observed in P-glycoprotein behavior, as naringenin was classified as a substrate whereas apigenin was not. This result identifies possible transporter-mediated efflux as a pharmacokinetic consideration for naringenin, although it does not negate its prioritization from the receptor-binding analyses. The predicted CYP-related outcomes should be interpreted cautiously because SwissADME and ProTox apply different algorithms, training datasets, endpoint definitions, and confidence frameworks. Apparent disagreement between platforms therefore reflects model-dependent uncertainty rather than experimental confirmation of enzyme inhibition. Dedicated microsomal and recombinant-enzyme assays will be required to determine whether naringenin or apigenin meaningfully inhibits the predicted CYP isoforms. The predicted acute oral toxicity results introduced an additional distinction between the compounds. Apigenin had a predicted LD 50 of 2500 mg/kg and was assigned to toxicity class 5, whereas naringenin had a predicted LD 50 of 2000 mg/kg and was assigned to toxicity class 4. Thus, apigenin showed the comparatively more favorable predicted acute-toxicity profile. However, these results did not parallel the docking ranking, demonstrating that predicted receptor interaction and predicted systemic toxicity represent separate computational dimensions. Naringenin was consequently prioritized for its predicted CXCR4 interaction profile rather than because it was predicted to be less toxic than apigenin. Both apigenin and naringenin were predicted to interact with estrogen receptor-related endpoints. Because endometriosis and several ovarian tumor subtypes are hormonally responsive, these predictions represent potential off-target liabilities rather than evidence of therapeutic benefit. The direction and biological consequence of receptor modulation cannot be inferred from the binary activity predictions and require receptor-binding, transcriptional, and estrogen-responsive proliferation assays. The DFT analysis provided further differentiation of their electronic behavior. Naringenin displayed a wider HOMO–LUMO gap of 0.18103 a.u. compared with 0.16312 a.u. for apigenin, indicating comparatively greater predicted electronic stability under the applied calculation conditions. Apigenin showed a slightly higher dipole moment, suggesting somewhat greater overall molecular polarity. The molecular electrostatic potential maps of both compounds also showed spatially separated electron-rich and electron-deficient regions capable of supporting polar recognition. These electronic descriptors complemented the docking and pharmacophore findings but were not used independently to infer receptor-binding strength. The replicated membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations provided the most detailed comparison of the three receptor–ligand systems. The CXCR4–naringenin complex showed relatively consistent protein-backbone RMSD profiles across the two 100 ns trajectories, with both replicas approaching similar final deviation ranges. Its SASA and radius-of-gyration profiles also remained within comparatively restricted intervals, indicating broadly maintained receptor surface exposure and compactness. The naringenin-bound receptor displayed localized residue flexibility, particularly around the highly mobile region near residues 220–230, but did not show widespread structural destabilization. Hydrogen bonds were dynamic in both replicas, although Replica 2 retained approximately one to two interactions throughout much of the trajectory and showed stronger late-stage persistence than Replica 1. The CXCR4–apigenin system also maintained broadly stable SASA and radius-of-gyration profiles, but its protein-backbone RMSD values differed more clearly between the two replicas. Replica 1 sampled a higher-deviation state, whereas Replica 2 remained within a lower RMSD range. The hydrogen-bond profiles similarly showed replica-dependent persistence. These findings indicate that apigenin remained associated with a structurally maintained receptor but induced or accompanied more variable conformational sampling than naringenin across the independent trajectories. The IT1t-bound reference complex showed the greatest variation between the early and later portions of the simulations. Both replicas underwent initial conformational adjustments and subsequently converged toward similar backbone RMSD ranges, although these values remained higher than those observed for the naringenin complex. Its hydrogen bonds were comparatively transient, particularly during the later stages of Replica 1. Nevertheless, the absence of persistent hydrogen bonding should not be interpreted as loss of binding because IT1t may also be retained through van der Waals, electrostatic, and hydrophobic interactions. The PCA, FEL, and DCCM analyses showed that none of the systems remained confined to a single rigid state. Instead, all three complexes sampled multiple conformational regions and metastable energy basins. The apigenin replicas differed in the breadth and compartmentalization of their sampled conformational spaces, consistent with the replica-dependent RMSD profiles. The naringenin replicas also sampled multiple states, but their global structural parameters remained comparatively consistent, suggesting that transitions within the principal-component space occurred without progressive loss of receptor compactness. The IT1t simulations showed substantial collective motions concentrated within the first two principal components and transitions between separated conformational ensembles. DCCM analysis further demonstrated that each complex retained networks of both correlated and anti-correlated residue motions, with variations in the strength and distribution of these motions across replicas. The MM/GBSA results provided an energetic comparison consistent with the broader prioritization pattern. Naringenin produced estimated total binding free energies of approximately −20 and −25 kcal/mol in Replicas 1 and 2, respectively. These values were more favorable than those obtained for apigenin, approximately −17.3 and −20.5 kcal/mol, in the corresponding simulations. IT1t showed the greatest energetic variation, with estimated values of approximately −7 and −23 kcal/mol across its two replicas. The favorable binding estimates for all systems were driven primarily by gas-phase interactions, particularly van der Waals contributions, and were partially opposed by polar-solvation penalties. Per-residue decomposition identified recurring receptor regions that contributed to the calculated interaction energies. His203, Leu120, Trp252, and Ile259 appeared repeatedly among the favorable contributors for naringenin, while Tyr116 was prominent in its first replica and Tyr255 appeared in the second. Apigenin showed recurring favorable contributions from Tyr116, His113, Thr117, His203, and Ile259. The IT1t complex displayed a more replica-dependent energetic network, with Arg188 contributing favorably in both trajectories and Gln200, His203, Phe199, and Ile259 becoming particularly important in Replica 2. These profiles indicate that the three ligands occupied the same general CXCR4 cavity but relied on partially different dynamic residue networks. A consolidated comparison of the replicated molecular dynamics and MM/GBSA results for the three CXCR4–ligand complexes is presented in S2 Table . The integrated results prioritized naringenin because it combined the strongest predicted docking score, a broader predicted polar and aromatic interaction network, acceptable predicted ADME and drug-likeness properties, comparatively consistent replicated structural behavior, and the most favorable MM/GBSA estimates across both trajectories. Apigenin remained a useful comparator because of its related scaffold and similar physicochemical characteristics, but it showed a less favorable docking score, less extensive initial interaction network, and greater replica-dependent structural variation. IT1t provided an experimentally grounded reference for binding-site definition and validation but displayed more variable dynamic and energetic behavior under the reconstructed-receptor simulation conditions. Accordingly, naringenin should be described as a computationally prioritized CXCR4-binding candidate, rather than as a confirmed antagonist or experimentally validated inhibitor. The computational prioritization of naringenin is supported by disease-relevant experimental evidence reported in cellular and animal models of endometriosis. Park et al. demonstrated that naringenin reduced the proliferation of human endometriosis-derived cells and promoted mitochondria-mediated apoptosis through loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, increased reactive oxygen species production, induction of endoplasmic-reticulum stress, activation of MAPK signaling, and suppression of the PI3K/AKT pathway [ 8 ]. These experimentally observed effects complement the present structure-based findings, in which naringenin showed the most favorable predicted docking score, a broader polar and aromatic interaction network within CXCR4, and favorable binding-energy estimates across both molecular dynamics replicas. Although the current calculations do not establish that the experimentally reported cellular effects were mediated through CXCR4, they provide a plausible target-level hypothesis through which naringenin may influence migration-, survival-, and proliferation-associated signaling. Further support was provided by Kapoor et al., who evaluated naringenin in an experimental rat model of endometriosis and in primary endometrial cell cultures. Naringenin treatment reduced endometriotic lesion volume and weight, serum TNF-α concentrations, and histopathological scores. It also altered the expression of TAK1, PAK1, VEGF, and PCNA, modulated the Nrf2/Keap1/HO-1 axis in the endometriotic lesions, promoted apoptosis, and reduced cellular invasion through downregulation of MMP-2 and MMP-9 [ 9 ]. The reduction of VEGF and matrix metalloproteinases is particularly relevant because angiogenesis, extracellular-matrix remodeling, and invasive migration contribute to the establishment and persistence of ectopic endometriotic lesions. These experimental observations are consistent with the present pharmacophore and docking results, which indicated that naringenin possesses a combination of hydrogen-bond donor, hydrogen-bond acceptor, aromatic, and hydrophobic features capable of supporting recognition within a signaling receptor associated with cell migration. Nevertheless, the agreement should be interpreted as biological convergence rather than direct proof that naringenin inhibited CXCR4 in the reported animal model. The relevance of naringenin also extends to ovarian cancer. Lin et al. reported that naringenin suppressed the proliferation and migration of A2780 and ES-2 ovarian cancer cells and reduced PI3K expression in vitro. In an ES-2 xenograft model, naringenin decreased tumor volume and weight, while oral administration produced stronger effects than intraperitoneal administration under the conditions examined [ 46 ]. This experimental oral activity is broadly compatible with the present SwissADME prediction of high gastrointestinal absorption. However, the predicted P-glycoprotein substrate status of naringenin indicates that transporter-mediated efflux may influence its exposure and should be examined experimentally. Therefore, the predicted ADME findings support continued investigation of oral delivery but do not establish human oral bioavailability or an effective therapeutic dose. The functional relationship between these pharmacological effects and the selected molecular target is supported by previous studies of the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis in ovarian cancer. Guo et al. showed that CXCL12 stimulation increased the proliferation, migration, invasion, and peritoneal metastatic behavior of CXCR4-expressing ovarian cancer cells, whereas CXCR4-directed antibody treatment or the antagonist AMD3100 reduced these responses [ 4 ]. In addition, analysis of human epithelial ovarian tumors identified CXCR4 expression as a factor associated with tumor progression [ 47 ]. These findings establish CXCR4 as a biologically relevant target in ovarian cancer and provide a rationale for evaluating compounds capable of occupying its ligand-binding cavity. Within this context, the present docking and simulation results suggest a possible molecular basis for investigating naringenin as a CXCR4-directed candidate. Naringenin formed predicted contacts with Tyr116, Arg188, and Tyr255 in the selected docking pose. During the replicated simulations, the CXCR4–naringenin system maintained broadly consistent backbone RMSD, SASA, and radius-of-gyration profiles, while His203, Leu120, Trp252, and Ile259 repeatedly contributed favorably to the calculated MM/GBSA energies. The estimated binding energies were also more favorable for naringenin than for apigenin in both corresponding replicas. These observations suggest that the initial docking contacts reorganized into a broader dynamic residue network rather than remaining limited to the original static pose. The electronic and pharmacokinetic findings provide additional, although secondary, support for this prioritization. Naringenin had a wider predicted HOMO–LUMO gap than apigenin, indicating comparatively greater electronic stability under the applied DFT conditions, while retaining suitable polar regions for receptor recognition in the molecular electrostatic potential analysis. It also complied with the evaluated drug-likeness filters, was predicted to be soluble, and showed high gastrointestinal absorption. Its predicted LD 50 of 2000 mg/kg and toxicity class 4 were less favorable than the corresponding apigenin estimates, demonstrating that naringenin was not selected because of predicted toxicological superiority. Instead, it was prioritized because its stronger docking performance, replicated dynamic behavior, favorable MM/GBSA estimates, and existing experimental activity in endometriosis and ovarian cancer collectively provided the most coherent evidence among the screened phytochemicals. Importantly, the available experimental studies demonstrate the anti-proliferative, pro-apoptotic, anti-invasive, and tumor-suppressive activities of naringenin, but they do not directly demonstrate binding to or antagonism of CXCR4. The present work therefore extends the existing pharmacological evidence by proposing CXCR4 as a candidate molecular target. Confirmation will require receptor-specific binding assays, CXCL12-induced signaling experiments, CXCR4-dependent migration assays, and comparisons with established antagonists such as IT1t or plerixafor. The present study provides a computational prioritization of naringenin as a candidate CXCR4-binding phytochemical; however, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analyses were performed using a reconstructed CXCR4 model rather than a complete experimentally resolved native receptor structure. Although the model showed high sequence identity, near-complete template coverage, and favorable structural-validation parameters, uncertainties may remain in reconstructed loop regions, side-chain orientations, and local binding-site geometry. In addition, the receptor was assigned fixed protonation states representing pH 7.4; therefore, possible changes in residue protonation during ligand binding were not evaluated. The docking results represent predicted binding poses and scoring estimates rather than experimentally measured binding affinities. AutoDock Vina scores cannot directly establish CXCR4 antagonism, and the stronger score obtained for naringenin relative to apigenin and IT1t should therefore be interpreted as a prioritization criterion only. Similarly, the pharmacophore and DFT analyses describe ligand interaction features and electronic properties but do not independently demonstrate receptor binding or biological activity. The predicted ADME and acute-toxicity outcomes were derived from computational models and require experimental confirmation. In particular, the predicted P-glycoprotein substrate status of naringenin may influence its intracellular exposure, while its predicted LD 50 and toxicity classification cannot establish safety in humans. The membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations improved the biological relevance of the study compared with simulations conducted in aqueous solvent alone. Nevertheless, each complex was evaluated using only two independent 100 ns replicas. This sampling duration and replica number may not fully capture slower receptor conformational transitions, ligand rebinding events, or rare activation-associated states. The membrane contained POPC but did not reproduce the full compositional complexity of mammalian membranes, particularly cholesterol and other lipid species known to influence GPCR organization and dynamics. Replica-dependent differences in RMSD, hydrogen bonding, conformational landscapes, and MM/GBSA energies further indicate that additional sampling is necessary before drawing firm conclusions regarding convergence. The MM/GBSA calculations were also subject to methodological limitations. These end-state estimates depend on implicit-solvent approximations, selected dielectric constants, trajectory sampling, and ligand force-field parameters. Entropic contributions were not included, and the calculated energies should therefore not be interpreted as absolute experimental binding free energies. The residue-decomposition results identify predicted energetic contributors but do not prove that individual residues are essential for ligand binding. Moreover, the analysis focused on CXCR4 and did not assess selectivity against other chemokine receptors or unrelated molecular targets. Future computational work should use a complete experimentally resolved native CXCR4 structure when available and examine alternative receptor conformations, protonation states, and ligand orientations. Longer simulations of at least 200–500 ns with a minimum of three independent replicas per system would provide improved conformational sampling. CXCR4 should also be simulated in more physiologically representative membranes containing cholesterol and additional phospholipid species. Enhanced-sampling methods, ligand-pocket distance analyses, ligand heavy-atom RMSD, interaction-occupancy calculations, and replicate-level statistical comparisons would further clarify ligand retention and receptor-state transitions. More rigorous free-energy methods, including alchemical calculations, could be applied to compare naringenin, apigenin, IT1t, and established CXCR4 antagonists. Experimental validation is required to determine whether naringenin directly binds to and functionally modulates CXCR4. Receptor-binding assays, such as radioligand displacement, surface plasmon resonance, or isothermal titration calorimetry, should be used to measure binding affinity. Functional assays should evaluate CXCL12-induced calcium signaling, β-arrestin recruitment, receptor internalization, downstream AKT and ERK signaling, and CXCR4-dependent cellular migration. Site-directed mutagenesis of predicted contributors such as Tyr116, Arg188, His203, Tyr255, and Ile259 could test the residue-level interaction hypotheses generated by docking and MM/GBSA decomposition. Subsequent biological studies should examine concentration-dependent effects of naringenin in endometriosis-derived cells and CXCR4-expressing ovarian cancer models, together with normal-cell controls. CXCR4 knockdown, knockout, or antagonist-rescue experiments would be necessary to determine whether any observed anti-proliferative, anti-invasive, or pro-apoptotic effects are specifically mediated through this receptor. Experimental pharmacokinetic studies should assess intestinal permeability, P-glycoprotein efflux, metabolic stability, cytochrome P450 interactions, plasma protein binding, and systemic exposure. Finally, efficacy and tolerability should be evaluated in relevant animal models of endometriosis and ovarian cancer before naringenin can be considered for translational development. The present findings establish naringenin as an in silico prioritized candidate for experimental CXCR4 investigation, rather than a confirmed CXCR4 antagonist. The proposed computational and experimental studies would help determine whether the predicted interaction profile translates into reproducible receptor binding, pathway modulation, disease-relevant activity, and an acceptable pharmacological profile. Conclusion This study applied an integrated computational workflow to prioritize phytochemicals targeting CXCR4 in the context of endometriosis and ovarian cancer. Among the screened compounds, naringenin showed the most favorable predicted docking score, an extensive CXCR4 interaction network, acceptable predicted ADME and drug-likeness properties, and comparatively consistent behavior across two independent 100 ns membrane-embedded molecular dynamics simulations. Its MM/GBSA estimates were also more favorable than those of apigenin across both replicas, while IT1t provided a crystallographic reference for binding-site definition and comparative evaluation. Pharmacophore and electronic-structure analyses further supported the molecular characteristics of naringenin relevant to receptor recognition. Collectively, these findings identify naringenin as an in silico prioritized CXCR4-binding candidate rather than a confirmed antagonist. Experimental binding, functional signaling, pharmacokinetic, toxicity, and disease-model studies are required to validate its predicted CXCR4-directed activity and therapeutic relevance.
