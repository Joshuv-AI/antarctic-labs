1. Executive Summary
This report documents the Budget Period 2 (BP2) technical progress, analyses, and results for the
U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Vehicle Technologies Office project titled Simultaneous
Greenhouse Gas and Criteria Pollutants Emissions Reduction for Off-Road Powertrains. The
overarching objective of the project is to develop, optimize, and validate integrated engine and
aftertreatment system strategies capable of achieving at least 90% reduction in nitrogen oxides
(NOx) and at least 10% reduction in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions relative to a baseline Final
Tier 4 (FT4) diesel engine and aftertreatment system. These targets are evaluated across a range of
regulatory and application-representative off-road duty cycles to ensure technical robustness and
relevance to real-world operating conditions.

During BP2, Eaton focused on system-level exploration and optimization of a future off-road
diesel powertrain. Engine development activities included optimization of cylinder deactivation
(CDA) strategies, implementation of advanced exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) control, and
refinement of variable geometry turbocharging (VGT) to improve fuel efficiency, reduce
engine-out (EO) NOx emissions, and increase exhaust temperatures to support downstream
aftertreatment performance. Once the engine calibration was set, multiple aftertreatment system
architectures were integrated and evaluated to assess trade-offs between NOx conversion
efficiency, GHG impact, and system complexity.
Comprehensive testing was conducted using the Non-Road Transient Cycle (NRTC), Ramp
Modal Cycle (RMC), and composite performance metrics, with all evaluations performed on
hydrothermally aged catalysts to ensure durability-relevant results. Test results demonstrated that
the first aftertreatment configuration exceeded the Budget Period 3 (BP3) Go/No-Go performance
criteria, achieving the required combination of NOx and GHG reductions.

2. Background
Future off‑road emissions regulations are expected to require simultaneous and substantial
reductions in both criteria pollutants and greenhouse gas emissions, creating significant technical
challenges for conventional diesel powertrains. This DOE‑funded project addresses these
challenges by integrating advanced combustion strategies with modular, scalable aftertreatment
systems that are adaptable across a wide range of off‑highway applications and duty cycles. The
approach emphasizes not only emissions reduction performance, but also system robustness,
durability, and applicability to real‑world operating conditions.

Budget Period 1 (BP1) established the foundational capabilities necessary to support this effort,
including definition of representative duty cycles, development of test infrastructure, construction
and commissioning of baseline and future engine configurations, and validation of modular
aftertreatment hardware. Building on this foundation, BP2 focused on system‑level exploration,
optimization, and architecture down‑selection, with the objective of identifying an integrated
engine and aftertreatment configuration capable of meeting aggressive NOx and GHG reduction
targets while maintaining technical feasibility for final demonstration in Budget Period 3.

3. Project Objectives
The primary objectives of Budget Period 2 (BP2) were to demonstrate, evaluate, and down‑select
integrated engine and aftertreatment system strategies capable of meeting aggressive emissions
reduction targets for off‑road powertrains. Specifically, BP2 aimed to demonstrate the following:

 • ≥90% reduction in NOx from the Tier 4 off-road standard of 0.4 g/kWhr
 • ≥10% reduction in GHG emissions relative to a FT4 baseline engine when evaluated over
 regulatory and application‑representative off‑road duty cycles.

To support these targets, BP2 focused on optimization of future engine operation, including the
development and calibration of CDA strategies, advanced EGR control, and variable geometry
turbocharging (VGT) to improve fuel efficiency, reduce engine‑out NOx, and increase exhaust
temperatures to enhance aftertreatment effectiveness. In parallel, multiple aftertreatment system
architectures were designed, integrated, and evaluated across cold and hot NRTC, RMC, and
composite performance metrics to quantify trade‑offs between NOx conversion efficiency, GHG
impact, system complexity, and operational robustness.

A key objective of BP2 was to identify and down‑select an optimal engine and aftertreatment
configuration that satisfies the BP3 Go/No‑Go performance criteria, demonstrating technical
readiness for final system demonstration. The outcomes of BP2 were intended to establish a clear
and defensible path forward into BP3, enabling focused validation of the selected architecture
under durability‑relevant and application‑representative operating conditions.

4. Program Scope and Metrics
Budget Period 2 (BP2) activities focused on system‑level development, evaluation, and
optimization of integrated engine and aftertreatment technologies for off‑road powertrains. Key
activities included the following:

 • Future engine steady‑state and transient optimization, with emphasis on calibrating
 operating strategies to improve fuel efficiency, reduce engine‑out emissions, and increase
 exhaust temperatures to support downstream aftertreatment performance.

BP2 also included the integration and evaluation of multiple aftertreatment system architectures
to assess trade‑offs between NOx conversion efficiency, greenhouse gas impact, system
complexity, and operational robustness. The configurations planned for evaluation during BP2
included

 • Light-Off selective catalytic reduction (LO‑SCR) combined with a main SCR
 • Two types of electric‑heater (E-Heater) assisted dual‑SCR systems to enhance cold‑start
 performance
 o 6” heater upstream of the light-off-SCR (LO-SCR)
 o 13” heater upstream of the main SCR
 • Fuel‑burner‑based aftertreatment architectures intended to provide supplemental thermal
 energy for emissions control.

The 13” heater configuration and burner configuration were part of the original plan for BP2
testing. A decision was made to focus more time on the 6” heater configuration during BP2 and
test the final configurations in BP3.

Comprehensive emissions testing was conducted over regulatory and application‑representative
duty cycles, including cold and hot NRTC testing, the RMC, and composite performance metrics.
All testing was performed using hydrothermally aged catalysts to ensure results were
representative of durability‑relevant operation and reflective of real‑world system performance.

 • Completion of future engine optimization activities, encompassing steady-state and
 transient calibration development
 • Successful completion of testing for the first integrated aftertreatment configuration.
 These activities enabled a comprehensive assessment of system-level performance across

6. Project Results and Discussion
Engine-Only Calibration Optimization Results

Optimization of CDA, EGR, and VGT strategies resulted in substantial improvements in
engine-out emissions, fuel efficiency, and exhaust thermal management. Engine calibration
development demonstrated up to a 33% reduction in engine-out NOx emissions, primarily
through improved combustion control and increased effective EGR utilization under low-load
operating conditions.

In addition to emissions benefits, CDA-enabled operation produced significant fuel consumption
reductions at low loads, improving overall greenhouse gas performance relative to the baseline
configuration. Importantly, CDA and air-system optimization also yielded exhaust temperature
increases exceeding 100 °C at multiple operating points, improving aftertreatment light-off
behavior and expanding the fraction of operation within the optimal temperature window for
downstream emissions control devices.

Figure 4 presents steady-state engine optimization results illustrating the combined influence of
variable geometry turbocharger (VGT) position, CDA operating mode, and EGR pump speed on
brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC), engine-out NOx emissions, and exhaust temperature.
The results highlight the transition from conventional six-cylinder operation to CDA operation
across the evaluated speed and load range, demonstrating the impact of coordinated air-system
and fueling strategies on efficiency, emissions, and exhaust thermal management.

 Figure 4. Future Engine Optimization Map (Steady State Operation)
Fuel savings achieved through CDA operation were measured across a range of engine speeds and
loads. These results were compared against published 13L diesel engine data. CDA fuel
consumption results at 2100rpm are shown in Figure 5, as a sample of the data trends seen at
different speeds.

Results published by Kakani et al (2024) showed that a 13L off-road diesel engine indicate that
operation with CDA provided measurable fuel consumption reductions across a load range
extending up to 9 bar brake mean effective pressure (BMEP). The greatest benefit was observed
under low-load conditions. Specifically, a 33.7% reduction in fuel consumption was reported at
0.4 bar BMEP, decreasing to 1.4% at 9 bar BMEP.

Engine-only testing for this project demonstrated CDA fuel consumption benefits up to 5 bar
BMEP. Measured reductions included 26% at 0.4 bar BMEP and 1.5% at 5 bar BMEP.
The engine CDA, EGR Pump speed, and VGT control calibration were optimized to achieve
elevated exhaust gas temperatures, supporting stable CDA operation at higher load conditions.
With further optimization, published results suggest CDA operation has potential for fuel
consumption savings extending to approximately 9 bar BMEP, with diminishing benefit as engine
load increases.

Project data from the engine calibration optimization showed a substantially stronger exhaust
temperature response to CDA operation. In this evaluation, CDA produced exhaust gas

CDA and EGR pump operation are complementary with respect to fuel economy and NOx
reduction:

 • CDA increases the effective EGR pressure ratio, resulting in active EGR pump operation.

 • Despite the reduced EGR flow, engine-out NOx emissions decrease.
 • This NOx reduction is maintained even with less EGR, implying that CDA inherently
 reduces engine-out NOx.

The performance of ATS Config 1 showed that cold-start and early transient operations produced

Hot Non-Road Transient Cycle (NRTC) Results Summary

Figure 10 compares tailpipe NOx emissions over the NRTC for the baseline and future engine
configurations, with key performance metrics summarized in Table 2. While cycle work was
comparable between configurations (54.6 vs. 55.0 kWhr), the future configuration demonstrated a
substantial reduction in engine-out NOx emissions, decreasing from 9.30 to 6.30 g/kWhr.

Despite this improvement at the engine level, tailpipe NOx emissions increased for the future
configuration, rising from 0.061 to 0.078 g/kWhr. Cumulative tailpipe NOx trends in Figure 10
indicate increased overall NOx emissions relative to the baseline, consistent with the reported
27% decrease in NOx conversion relative to baseline. The aftertreatment system operated within
an effective temperature window throughout the cycle, as shown by sustained SCR inlet
temperatures in the optimal range.

Total diesel exhaust fluid (DEF) consumption for the future configuration was reduced to 756.1 g,
representing approximately a 25% reduction relative to the baseline. DEF dosing was split
between the LO-SCR (453.6 g) and the main SCR (302.5 g). This reduced dosing strategy
contributed to significantly lower ammonia slip, with NH₃ emissions decreasing from 0.0518 to
0.0018 g/kWhr. In addition, GHG emissions were reduced by 15.8%, from 788.6 to 663.8 g/kWhr,
indicating an overall efficiency benefit for the future configuration.

To address the increase in tailpipe NOx emissions, further optimization of urea dosing distribution
between the LO-SCR and the main SCR was needed. In addition, the low ammonia slip through

to the tailpipe implies that more urea can be dosed during the cycle without negatively impacting
emissions. This dosing optimization and increase was evaluated with the second aftertreatment
configuration (ATS Config 2).

Cold Non-Road Transient Cycle (NRTC) Results Summary

Cold NRTC emissions performance for the baseline and future engine configurations without
additional thermal management is summarized in Figure 11 and the accompanying results in
Table 3. Although the future engine demonstrates lower engine-out NOx (5.90 g/kWhr versus
9.10 g/kWhr for the baseline), tailpipe NOx emissions increase under cold cycle conditions, rising
from 0.355 g/kWhr for the baseline to 0.566 g/kWhr for the future configuration.

As shown in Figure 11, cumulative tailpipe NOx emissions for the future engine increase rapidly
during the early portion of the cycle, indicating reduced NOx conversion efficiency prior to
aftertreatment light-off. Measured SCR inlet temperatures confirm insufficient thermal energy at
the beginning of the cycle, limiting catalyst activity and resulting in elevated cold-start NOx
emissions. These trends demonstrate that improved engine-out NOx alone is insufficient to
achieve cold-cycle tailpipe NOx compliance without additional thermal management.

The results further indicate reduced urea utilization for the future engine. Total DEF consumption
is approximately 681 g, representing a 29% reduction relative to the baseline configuration.
Despite this reduction, measured ammonia slip remains at zero, indicating available margin for
increased urea dosing. DEF distribution data show that dosing to the LO-SCR and main SCR can
be further optimized to improve early-cycle NOx conversion.

Based on these findings, additional thermal energy input is required to accelerate catalyst light-off
and improve cold-start NOx control. This makes the actively heated aftertreatment configurations
more promising. The urea utilization on ATS Config 1 shows an opportunity for optimization on
the other aftertreatment configurations.

Cold and Hot NRTC Emissions Performance with Electrically Heated Aftertreatment –
Initial Results Summary
Cold and hot NRTC emissions results were evaluated to compare unheated aftertreatment
operation (ATS Config 1) with electrically heated dual-SCR configurations (ATS Config 2). The
results demonstrate that the addition of electric heating significantly accelerates catalyst warm-up,

improves early-cycle SCR activity, and substantially reduces cold-cycle NOx emissions while
maintaining GHG performance.

Initial electric heater testing introduced new control levers to influence NOx conversion by
directly managing exhaust thermal conditions upstream of the SCR system. A two-stage e-heating
strategy was implemented, informed by prior Eaton on-highway development experience. The
initial strategy consisted of:

 • A “get-hot” phase, targeting rapid catalyst warm-up using elevated heater power and
 temperature setpoints (230 °C at 5 kW), followed by

 • A “stay-hot” phase, using reduced power to maintain catalyst temperatures within an
 optimal operating window (225 °C at 2.4 kW).

These temperature and power setpoints were adjusted as testing progressed to better meet the
needs of the aftertreatment system.

The overall program goal of 0.04 g/kWhr NOx on the Composite NRTC was broken down into
separate goals for the Hot and Cold NRTC. These goals were flexible targets, as surpassing one
would create margin on the other, due to the calculations of composite cycle emissions. As
summarized in Table 5, ATS Config 1 (no E-Heater) exceeded the Cold NRTC NOx target,
producing 0.5664 g/kW-hr, more than double the flexible goal of 0.275 g/kW-hr. This elevated
cold-cycle NOx is consistent with delayed catalyst light-off and limited ammonia storage during
early operation. In contrast, electrically heated configurations demonstrated substantial cold-cycle
NOx improvement. ATS Config 2 with the initial heating strategy reduced Cold NRTC NOx to
0.4485 g/kW-hr, representing approximately a 21% reduction relative to ATS Config 1. Increasing
heater power and temperature setpoints further improved performance: the second heating
strategy (255 °C, 7 kW) achieved 0.4100 g/kW-hr, corresponding to an approximate 28%
reduction versus ATS Config 1.

The E-Heater was not used for most of the Hot NRTC. The results indicate that electric heating
does not adversely impact NOx performance. All configurations remained above the Hot NRTC
target of 0.0276 g/kW-hr, with measured values ranging from 0.0613 to 0.0905 g/kW-hr. This
behavior reflects the reduced need for supplemental heating once the system is fully warm and
confirms that the E-Heater primarily benefits cold-start and early transient operation.
Composite emissions trends reflect the behavior of the hot and cold cycle emissions, with hot
cycle results affecting the composite more due to the heavier weighting in composite calculations.
GHG emissions remained well below program targets across all configurations. Although electric
heating introduces additional energy demand, total GHG output for heated configurations
remained comparable to or lower than ATS Config 1 due to improved overall system efficiency
and reduced engine-out penalties associated with cold operation.

Overall, the results in Table 5 confirm that electrically assisted aftertreatment operation provides a
robust and tunable approach for mitigating cold-start NOx emissions. The two-stage e-heating
strategy enables rapid catalyst activation without compromising hot-cycle emissions or GHG
performance, establishing electric heating as a key enabler for meeting future off-road emissions
requirements under cold and transient conditions.

Dosing Controls Summary

All data up to this point was reported in the 2025 Annual Merit Review (AMR). Between the
2025 AMR and the close of BP2, refinement of ATS Config 2 controls highlighted that urea
dosing optimization is a complex, highly iterative process with many interacting control levers. A
comprehensive suite of sensors located across the aftertreatment system—including in transition
cones, inlet pipes, and measurement rings—provided detailed system visibility and controls
feedback data. FTIR measurements were used at multiple locations, as shown in Figure 14,
(engine-out, LO-SCR outlet, and tailpipe) to measure emissions and confirm control sensor
measurements.

During this period, sensor availability and reliability emerged as a key challenge. One FTIR
sensor became unavailable, requiring increased reliance on engine-based sensors for engine-out
emissions characterization. In addition, several instances of sensor breakdown or malfunction
delayed testing and complicated data interpretation.
Using the data from previous ATS Config 2 runs, iterative urea dosing and E-Heater control
strategies were made and evaluated. Eight iterations were made between May 13 and June 30,
2025. The work underscored the sensitivity of NOx performance to multiple dosing and thermal
management variables, including temperature thresholds for dosing, split dosing between the
light-off SCR and main SCR, ammonia storage dynamics, and the impact of SCR inlet, mid-bed,

and outlet temperatures. Despite these challenges, engine-based sensors (identified as “green
flags” in Figure 14) proved sufficient data to determine dosing control and overall system
performance, enabling continued progress with the controls iterations.

Overall, the results emphasize both the technical complexity of electrically assisted SCR
optimization and the importance of robust sensor strategies to support iterative calibration
development.

Figure 18 and Figure 19 illustrate the progressive reduction in NOx emissions and the concurrent
maintenance of GHG benefits achieved through iterative refinement of urea dosing calibration
and E-Heater control strategies. Across Budget Period 2 testing, a total of nine unique
combinations of dosing strategies and E-Heater operating conditions were evaluated on the NRTC
and RMC. A representative subset of these results is shown to highlight the key performance
trends and the impact of successive calibration improvements.

The program NOx objective was defined as achieving a balanced split of Cold and Hot NRTC
performance sufficient to reach a composite NRTC target of 0.04 g/kW-hr. In parallel, the GHG
objective required a minimum 10% reduction relative to the baseline Final Tier 4 engine. ATS
Configuration 1 (no electric heating) is included as a reference point to quantify the incremental
benefits provided by electrically assisted aftertreatment operation and dosing optimization.

The ATS Config 2 initial test used a 230 °C, 5 kW E-Heater strategy and demonstrated the
immediate benefit of supplemental thermal management during cold-start operation. As shown in
Figure 18, Cold NRTC NOx was reduced substantially relative to ATS Config 1, confirming that
rapid catalyst warmup and earlier SCR activation are critical enablers for cold-cycle NOx control.
Hot NRTC and composite NOx performance remained largely unchanged, indicating that heater
assistance primarily influences early-cycle operation and does not adversely impact fully warm
conditions.

The 4th iteration of dosing controls on ATS Config 2 ran with an increased heater temperature and
power level (235 °C, 7 kW) focused on refining urea dosing thresholds and distribution between
the LO-SCR and the main SCR. These changes resulted in additional reductions in Cold NRTC
NOx and measurable improvements in composite NOx performance, demonstrating that dosing
calibration—not heater energy alone—is a dominant lever for maximizing SCR utilization once
sufficient thermal conditions are established.

Further refinements between the 4th iteration and 7th iteration maintained the 235 °C, 7 kW heater
strategy while implementing updated dosing logic informed by combined NRTC and RMC
analysis. This iteration delivered the lowest Cold NRTC NOx of the heated configurations so far
and produced a pronounced reduction in RMC NOx, indicating improved ammonia availability
and more effective utilization of downstream SCR capacity under ramped load conditions. These
results highlight the importance of balancing ammonia storage and release dynamics across the
SCR system rather than relying on increased heater energy.

To isolate the impact of dosing optimization, a set of data was collected on June 17 with the E-
Heater disabled. Despite the absence of active heating, NOx performance remained significantly
improved relative to ATS Config 1 across Composite NRTC and RMC metrics. This outcome
confirms that the calibration improvements developed during heated operation translated directly
to unheated conditions, while also reinforcing the continued benefit of E-Heater assistance for
Cold NRTC operation where thermal limitations dominate early-cycle performance.
Installation of additional mixing hardware took place between June 17 and June 25. This allowed
the final NRTC runs to be done on June 30. This set of runs achieved the program goal of <0.04
g/kWhr NOx and >10% GHG reduction on the Composite NRTC, demonstrating the benefit of
aftertreatment thermal management, optimized dosing control, and improved mixing on NOx
conversion.

Figure 19 summarizes the corresponding GHG emissions results for each configuration. Across
all test iterations, GHG emissions remained below the project target, with reductions exceeding
10% relative to the baseline engine on Cold NRTC, Hot NRTC, Composite, and RMC cycles.

Collectively, the results shown in Figure 18 and Figure 19 demonstrate that iterative urea dosing
calibration, supported by targeted electric heating during cold-start conditions, enables substantial

confirm that dosing strategy optimization drives balanced NOx performance across NRTC and
RMC cycles, with the E-Heater serving as an effective enabler for cold-cycle control rather than a
continuous energy demand. These findings establish a clear and technically robust pathway
toward reducing NOx emissions while maintaining compliance with GHG reduction objectives.

7. Significant Accomplishments
The first key accomplishment achieved during Budget Period 2 (BP2) include the successful
demonstration of the BP3 Go/No‑Go performance criteria, confirming that the integrated engine
and aftertreatment system architecture (ATS Config 1) met the required combination of NOx and
GHG emissions reduction targets. Achieving this milestone early in BP2 established a technically
defensible basis for progression toward the overall project goal in the remainder of BP2.
The second key BP2 accomplishment was achieving the Composite NRTC program goal on ATS
Config 2. This was accomplished thanks to the rigorous team effort of calibration optimization,
and the decision to install additional mixing hardware in the aftertreatment system. The RMC was
unfortunately not confirmed to have met the project goal, but previous results through the
calibration iterations show promising results.

BP2 activities also resulted in the publication of a peer‑reviewed SAE technical paper examining
differences between off‑road and on‑road low‑load operating cycles, contributing to improved
understanding of off‑highway duty‑cycle characteristics and their implications for emissions
control strategies. This work supports broader DOE objectives related to representative test cycle
development and technology transfer.

A second peer-reviewed SAE technical paper was written about the results gathered during BP2,
which was accepted in the autumn of 2025 and is set to be published in April 2026.

In parallel, Eaton developed an incremental cost model for advanced engine and aftertreatment
system technologies, providing insight into the cost impacts associated with various emissions
control strategies and supporting assessment of potential pathways toward commercialization.

8. Conclusions
This report documented the successful completion of Budget Period 2 (BP2) activities for the
DOE-funded project Simultaneous Greenhouse Gas and Criteria Pollutants Emissions Reduction
for Off-Road Powertrains. The primary objective of BP2 was to identify, evaluate, and
down-select an integrated engine and aftertreatment system strategy capable of achieving
aggressive reductions in NOx and GHG emissions relative to a Final Tier 4 baseline engine across
representative off-road duty cycles. The results presented herein demonstrate that this objective
was met.

System-level optimization of the future off-road diesel engine, incorporating CDA, advanced
EGR control, and VGT control, delivered substantial reductions in engine-out NOx emissions
while simultaneously improving fuel efficiency. CDA operation provided pronounced benefits at
low-load conditions typical of off-road applications, including significant reductions in fuel
consumption and increases in exhaust temperature exceeding 100 °C at multiple operating points.

These exhaust thermal benefits proved critical in expanding aftertreatment operating windows and
improving downstream emissions control effectiveness.

Evaluation of multiple aftertreatment architectures demonstrated that a light-off SCR combined
with a main SCR system provided a technically viable configuration capable of exceeding the
BP3 Go/No-Go performance criteria. Cold-start and early transient operation remained a key
challenge, with reduced SCR activity and limited ammonia storage constraining cold-cycle NOx
performance. To address this limitation, electrically heated aftertreatment operation was
introduced in ATS Configuration 2 and systematically evaluated.

Results from iterative testing confirmed that targeted electric heater use significantly accelerates
catalyst warm-up and improves cold-cycle NOx control without imposing a greenhouse gas
penalty. Across nine unique combinations of urea dosing strategies and heater operating
conditions, progressive reductions in NOx emissions were achieved on Cold NRTC, Hot NRTC,
and RMC cycles. Data suggested that improved mixing was necessary to achieve the project goal,
and additional mixing hardware was installed upstream of the main SCR. Importantly, the data
demonstrate that urea dosing calibration—particularly dosing thresholds, split dosing between the
LO-SCR and main SCR, and management of ammonia storage and release—emerged as the
dominant lever for NOx improvement once sufficient thermal conditions were established.
Testing with the optimized dosing controls and additional mixing hardware achieved the program
goal of 90% NOx reduction relative to FT4 and 10% GHG reduction relative to the baseline
engine on the Composite NRTC. This finding reinforces the combined roles of CDA, EGR, high
efficiency VGT, the electric heater, and dual dosing.

Collectively, BP2 results demonstrate that an integrated approach combining advanced engine
operation, optimized urea dosing calibration, and targeted electric aftertreatment heating can
deliver simultaneous and substantial reductions in NOx and GHG emissions for off-road
powertrains. The selected system architecture successfully met the BP3 project goal on the
Composite NRTC and demonstrated a promising pathway towards the same on the RMC.
Although Eaton elected not to pursue Budget Period 3, the outcomes of this work provide a strong
technical foundation for future off-road emissions control strategies and contribute valuable
insight into the interactions between combustion control, aftertreatment thermal management, and
SCR calibration under real-world duty cycles.
