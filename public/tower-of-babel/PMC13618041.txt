Simulating Photodissociation
in Strong Field Enabled
by the Random Phase Thermal Wavefunction Approach

Bar Ezra, Shimshon Kallush, Ronnie Kosloff

Abstract

Simulating photodissociation
becomes challenging when the number
of states involved grows large. The computational complexity is usually
reduced by restrictions that limit the model’s scope. We present
a first-principles quantum model for strong-field photodissociation
that treats rotational dynamics explicitly. The random-phase thermal
wavefunction method reduces the large computational cost. The simulation
yields experimental observables, such as the momentum angular distribution
of the photofragments. We study how these observables converge at
two field intensities, with each set at a different temperature. The
method is designed to reach conditions where exact numerical methods
become intractable; here, we validate it against exact results in
the regime where both are available. Beyond the conventional dependence
on system size and number of realizations, we find that convergence
depends on the observable’s sensitivity to coherence.

Introduction

Time-domain spectroscopy can be directly
modeled by solving the
time-dependent Schrödinger equation. Direct modeling for a
strong-field photodissociation process has almost no alternative,
since analytical methods are not applicable and perturbation expansions
do not converge. This work presents a first-principle simulation method
applicable to the photodissociation process of a diatomic molecule.
The simulation is guided by strong-field pulsed experiments. The goal
is that the simulation outcome could be directly comparable to experimental
observables, specifically to the velocity distribution of the photofragments.
The simulation is first-principles in structure: given the Hamiltonian,
initial temperature, charge, and mass, it propagates the dynamics
with no fitted dynamical parameters.

The knowledge of the total
Hamiltonian and initial state is required
to solve the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. The Born–Oppenheimer
decomposition allows us to separate the electronic and the nuclear
terms. Ab initio electronic structure
methods are able to accurately calculate the electronic potential
surfaces and the transition dipole moments associated with the photodissociation.
The present study concentrates on solving the nuclear dynamics, specifically
the dynamics of the vibrational and rotational degrees of freedom
of a diatomic molecule on multiple electronic surfaces. The strong
coupling between the molecular degrees of freedom opens multiple channels
to unbound electronic states where the molecule dissociates.

Within the common experimental framework,
,
 various
physical measures of the photofragments are observed far from the
dissociation point. The simulation should be able to calculate the
same observable and specifically the angular distribution of the outgoing
momentum. Such a calculation is set in a large phase-space volume,
which translates to a very large computational Hilbert space. In the
weak-field regime, one can use perturbation theory, and the calculation
is simplified significantly since selection rules limit the accessible
Hilbert space. In strong-field photodissociation processes, many interference
channels become open. As a result, the calculation must include all
of the relevant quantum states that participate in the dynamical process.

Solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for a large
Hilbert space is a difficult task. The computation effort scales with
the size of the Hilbert space, the product of the energy range, and
time period. The Hilbert space size is determined by the phase-space
volume containing the problem. The phase-space is restricted by the
maximum energy the molecule can carry and by the asymptotic distance
estimated by the effective dissociation point. The number of angular-momentum
states required for the calculation is determined by the initial temperature
of the molecule and the strength of the radiation field. Each participating
photon changes the rotational angular momentum by one unit of ħ.
Therefore, when the molecule is subjected to a strong radiation field,
the size of the manifold of the accessible states increases significantly.
For a photodissociation study, the Hilbert space is vast, of the order
106 states for the representation of the whole ro-vibronic
manifold (where, the vibration is represented on a grid of size (∼103), rotational states (∼ 102) and spin-orbital
states (∼ 101)). In principle, the initial state
is a thermal mixed state formally requiring a density-operator description.
The dimension of the density operator is the square of the Hilbert
space; this means that such a calculation is prohibitively expensive.

Several studies have addressed the computational complexity of
similar systems. In the study of I
2
–, one of the most studied
diatomic molecules, the complexity is reduced by neglecting the rotational
degree of freedom due to the heavy molecule.

 For the H
2

temperatures, resulting in only the ground ro-vibrational state.
−

 A commonly employed approximation assumes that the initial orientation

Hilbert space (practically to M = 0 only) with the price that the simulation scope is
limited.

The random-phase wavefunction (RPW) method is employed
in this

a density-operator calculations by a finite sample of random-phase

Discussion
and Conclusions

In this work, a computational method for
the first-principle simulation
of strong-field photodissociation and other time domain spectroscopy
was developed. Simulations based on an exact solution of the time-dependent
Schrödinger equation are expensive. Typical computational complexity
scales as N
S
3, where N
S is the
number of states involved in the process. Simulations of diatomic
photodissociation processes at typical experimental conditions require
a vast Hilbert space, of the order 106.

To overcome
this challenge, the RPW method has been employed. The
phases of the initial states are randomly chosen and thermal averaged
observables are obtained. The observables are converged to the directly
calculated outcome after K realizations, where K ≪ N
S. Previous studies
on RPW
,,
 showed that
the observable convergence scales as 
[jp6c05555_m030.webp]∼1K·1NS
. Here, we show the required K is not universal: it depends strongly on the observable’s
sensitivity to coherence.

As the RPW method relies on randomness
of the relative phase between
the states, it is unlikely that processes which are driven by high-order
coherent interactions, e.g., multiphoton transitions, would be well
approximated by the method. These types of processes are highly coherent
and employ throughout the dynamics nondiagonal elements of the density
matrix, i.e., coherences.

The dissociation probability 
[jp6c05555_m031.webp]P
 is an example of an observable
in which
the only contribution comes from diagonal elements of the density
matrix in the energy representation. Hence, coherent interferences
have low impact on such observables. Another case is the average kinetic
energy, ΔE

k

rel, an observable that is less
affected by the different interference pathways in the dynamics. At
both intensities, it converges to the exact solution up to the experimental
error of about 0.02 eV after only ∼ 200 realizations, which
means acceleration of the calculation by a factor of ∼20 compared
to the direct method, which propagates every initially occupied state
separately. An explanation can be attributed to the property of the
operators that correspond to these observables that are diagonal in
the energy representation. Thus, they are less dependent on coherences
and interferences.

Another observable that was explored is the
momentum-space distribution
of the dissociated fragments, which provides a visual approach to
study the convergence. Our main result concerning this quantity is
that its dominant features, such as the overall shape and peak position,
are accurately recovered by the method, while fine details such as
the peak height might be difficult to retrieveat high intensity,
the momentum-magnitude peak reaches only about a third of the exact
value, and barely improves between 100 and 2000 realizations. This
is the most coherence-sensitive observable we studied, and its slow
convergence is the clearest illustration of our central finding. Unlike
the dissociation probability and kinetic energy, this distribution
carries substantial coherence between angular-momentum states, so
it converges far more slowly. In addition, the convergence is better
for the angular variable θ than for the magnitude of the momentum,
due to the very different width of the distribution in these two directions.
Within a single calculation, every observable shares the same N
S and the same K, yet at high
intensity, the diagonal observables converge within 200 realizations
while the momentum distribution does not converge after 2000. This
within-run difference cannot be attributed to temperature, and therefore
reflects coherence alone. Consistently, the split is pronounced in
the high-intensity run, where coherence is largest, and negligible
at low intensity, where both observable types converge fast. We remark
that the common use of this tool in the experimental context is usually
more quantitativeits main use is to assign the different fragments
to their electronic channels. Therefore, our finding that the exact
distribution is difficult to recover affects the applicability of
the method only marginally.

The observed error leads us to conclude
that the different observables
converge differently. Each observable is sensitive to different properties
of the photofragments state. The dynamics of the dissociation process
include interferences. Adding random phases changes the interference
pattern, leading to different outcomes. At high intensity, the process
contains multiphoton transitions that induce rare events and interferences
that lead to special states. Thus, adding a random phase leads to
different events and interferences and to different final states.

The observable convergence is subject to the coherence and interference
during the process. We note here that the choice of the wavefunction
basis might be another factor that affects the observable convergence.
However, upon changing the basis from the Wigner D matrices to a direct angular grid representation basis, we did not
observe any significant shift in the convergence rate.

In conclusion,
we constructed a first-principles model for time-domain
spectroscopy for calculating experimental properties. The dynamical
processes are computationally complex due to the large Hilbert space;
thus, we used the RPW method to reduce the complexity. We studied
the convergence properties of the product observables at two different
laser intensities. The same method would apply to the dynamical hole
left in the reactants. We conclude that
the convergence depends on the sensitivity of the observable to the
dynamic interferences that take place in the process. Thus, coherence-sensitive
observables converge more slowly, and a high laser intensity worsens
this by building stronger interference into the final states.
