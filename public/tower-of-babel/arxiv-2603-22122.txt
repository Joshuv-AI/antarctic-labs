Post-selective attack with multi-mode projection onto Fock subspace Preprint: APS/123-QED
Andrei Gaidash, George Miroshnichenko, Anton Kozubov

Abstract
In this work we present a comprehensive analysis of a post-selective attack on quantum key distribution protocols employing phase-encoded linearly independent coherent states (or similar alternatives). The attack relies on multimode projection onto a Fock subspace and enables probabilistic extraction of information by an eavesdropper. We derive analytical expressions for the information accessible to the adversary and show that it depends only on three protocol parameters: the mean photon number of the signal states, the phase separation in the information basis, and the expected optical loss of the quantum channel. Several optical realizations of phase-encoded quantum key distribution protocols are analyzed to illustrate the applicability of the results. Possible countermeasures against the proposed attack are also discussed.

Introduction

Quantum key distribution (QKD) protocols derive their security from the fundamental laws of quantum physics
30
. Over the past decades, substantial progress has been made in the theoretical analysis of QKD security. While initial security analyses were based on the assumption of ideal components and operations, subsequent research has shifted toward more practical models. In recent years, the focus has moved toward understanding how imperfections in real-world devices impact the secrecy and reliability of QKD systems

State preparation plays a central role in QKD security, as it defines the information potentially available to an eavesdropper and constrains optimal attack strategies. While the original BB84 protocol was conceived for single-photon sources, most modern QKD systems rely on attenuated laser radiation. The resulting Poissonian photon-number distribution exposes the system to photon-number-splitting (PNS) attacks
9
;
21
;
20
;
1
. A widely adopted and highly effective countermeasure against this class of attacks is the decoy-state technique
18
;
22
;
35
, including phase randomization and single-photon fraction extraction, also known as the Gottesman-Lo-Lutkenhaus-Preskill (GLLP) framework
7
, at the processing step of a protocol.

However, specifically for phase-coded QKD protocols that will be considered in the article, a generic PNS attack is generally considered less critical than other eavesdropping strategies, as the encoded phase is fragile with respect to photon-number measurements, i.e., the direct projection of a single-mode coherent state on Fock subspace erases information about the phase; see Section 3.2 in
3
. At this point, consideration of collective attacks, e.g., the Holevo bound, for phase-coded states was assumed to be optimal (implying decoy states and single-photon fraction extraction at the processing step of a protocol are not implemented); for instance, see
31
. Then, a thorough analysis of unambiguous state discrimination (that can be implemented for a set of linearly independent states) attacks shows that a post-selection (by blocking undesired outcomes) can, in principle, surpass collective attacks’ estimations, and the only way to observe an eavesdropper’s intervention is to monitor the expected detection (and/or bit error) rate
5
;
6
. As a result, the paradigm of quantum control attack has emerged
12
, as well as effective countermeasures. Preventing, or at least limiting, the ability of an eavesdropper to block undesired outcomes results in the Holevo boundary being re-established as the maximal amount of information retrieved (accompanied by detection and/or bit error rate monitoring conditions), e.g.,
31
.

Later, the formalism of post-selective measurements was developed
15
;
13
;
10
;
11
;
2
;
16
;
14
;
17
;
36
. The central idea is that, for a given ensemble of initial pure quantum states, it is sufficient to establish the existence of a transformation that produces a desired set of output states with a non-zero probability, without requiring explicit construction of the transformation itself. In the paper
13
necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of such a transformation were derived as
G_{in}-p_{s}G_{out}\geq 0
, where
G_{in}
and
G_{out}
are Gram matrices of input and output states and
p_{s}
is the probability of the successful transformation. This formulation allows one to characterize the feasibility of a given post-selective transformation solely through algebraic constraints on the corresponding Gram matrices. A key advantage of this approach is that it circumvents the need to specify the physical realization or operational details of the transformation. Instead, it suffices to demonstrate that a transformation with the required properties exists in principle. This feature has proven particularly valuable in the security analysis of quantum key distribution protocols, where one typically assumes an adversary with unrestricted technological capabilities. However, a general optimization over all possible output states seems to be unachievable (at least for now); recent application cases, for instance
17
, still consider special cases of outcome states. Despite the latter, the attack again surpasses the Holevo bound.

Ideas of post-selection continue to develop in the article with an unexpected combination of PNS-inspired techniques. In contrast to recent papers

II
Key idea

II.1
Multi-mode projection onto the Fock subspace

Consider a coherent state
|\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle
, where phase
\phi_{j}
can be chosen from a set of phases that are equally distributed in a phase plane. Assume that an eavesdropper concatenates a coherent state
|\alpha\rangle
to obtain a state as follows:

\displaystyle|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle=|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle.

(1)

Note, an eavesdropper can add a phase-matched coherent state reliably since there is always a reference beam in every interferometric scheme; see further Section
IV
. The latter state can be projected onto the Fock subspace with the following operator:

\displaystyle Q^{(n)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|n-k\rangle\langle n-k|\otimes|k\rangle\langle k|,

(2)

resulting in the following reduced state:

\displaystyle|\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\langle n-k|\alpha\rangle\cdot|n-k\rangle\otimes\langle k|\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle\cdot|k\rangle}{\sqrt{P(2|\alpha|^{2},n)}}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{e^{i(\phi_{j}k+\theta n)}}{2^{n/2}}\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}}|n-k\rangle\otimes|k\rangle,

(3)

\displaystyle P(2|\alpha|^{2},n)=\text{Tr}\big(Q^{(n)}|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle\langle\psi(\alpha,\phi_{j})|\big),

(4)

\displaystyle P(x,n)=\frac{e^{-x}x^{n}}{n!},

(5)

where
\theta
is a phase of a complex-valued coherent amplitude
\alpha=|\alpha|e^{i\theta}
, and
P(x,n)
is the Poisson distribution. The scalar product of reduced states is given by

\displaystyle\langle\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{i})|\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle=\bigg(\frac{1+e^{-i(\phi_{i}-\phi_{j})}}{2}\bigg)^{n},

(6)

the absolute value of their scalar product as follows:

\displaystyle|\langle\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{i})|\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle|=\bigg(\cos\Big(\frac{\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}}{2}\Big)\bigg)^{n}.

(7)

It is well known that with the help of the latter quantity, the maximal amount of information an eavesdropper can extract is determined, i.e., the Holevo bound. In particular, the scalar product should be considered, implying two states that form an informational basis, i.e., with some phases
\phi_{(0)}
and
\phi_{(1)}
, that correspond to bit values of
0
and
1
respectively in that basis. A note should be made that it is so only if an eavesdropper performs collective measurement after legitimate users reveal information about base choices.
In this case the Holevo bound is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{(n)}(\Delta)=h\Big(\frac{1-|\langle\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{(0)})|\xi^{(n)}(\alpha,\phi_{(1)})\rangle|}{2}\Big)=

\displaystyle=h\Big(\frac{1-\big(\cos(\Delta)\big)^{n}}{2}\Big),

(8)

where

\displaystyle h(x)=-x\log_{2}(x)-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)

(9)

is the binary entropy function and
\Delta=\frac{\phi_{(0)}-\phi_{(1)}}{2}
. The Holevo bound
\chi^{(n)}(\Delta)
determines the total information retrieved from reduced states for a given photon-number outcome of the projection (
2
), that occurs with the probability (
4
). This quantity is essential for the attack scenario described further.

as in Eq. (

:

1.

An eavesdropper can use a beamsplitter (or a unitary operation
U_{1}
in general) to split the signal in a specific way given by:

\displaystyle|\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle\xrightarrow{U_{1}}|\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle\otimes|\sqrt{\eta_{2}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle,

(10)

\displaystyle|\eta_{1}|+|\eta_{2}|=1;

(11)

2.

The state
|\sqrt{\eta_{2}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle
is sent to a receiver while the state
|\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle
is in possession of an eavesdropper;

3.

An eavesdropper proceeds with a transformation (if necessary; some of the considered further examples do not require it; for details, see Section
IV
) to obtain a state of the form (
1
):

\displaystyle|\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle\rightarrow|\psi(\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle;

(12)

4.

Then an eavesdropper applies projection (
2
) to the state as follows:

\displaystyle|\psi(\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle\xrightarrow{Q^{(n)}}|\xi^{(n)}(\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle;

(13)

5.

Reduced states in case of
n=0
are indistinguishable; they are basically vacuum states:
|\xi^{(0)}(\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle=|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle
, and they do not provide any information in regard to the sent state’s phase
\phi_{j}
. So, at this point, an eavesdropper may apply post-selection (for instance, implemented as in
12
):

•

If
n=0
, then an eavesdropper blocks the state
|\sqrt{\eta_{2}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle
sent to a receiver, and the reduced state
|\xi^{(0)}(\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle
is neglected;

•

If
n\geq 1
, then the state
|\sqrt{\eta_{2}}\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle
proceeds to a receiver’s side, and the reduced state
|\xi^{(n\geq 1)}(\sqrt{\eta_{1}}\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle
is stored in a quantum memory.

6.

After the reconciliation step, when informational bases are revealed, each stored in a quantum memory state results in information, quantified by
\chi^{(n)}(\Delta)
, obtained by an eavesdropper. Then, the total information about a key retrieved from reduced states, excluding vacuum states, is given by

\displaystyle I=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},n)\cdot\chi^{(n)}(\Delta)}{1-P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},0)},

(14)

where we utilize the following property of von Neumann entropy
H
:

\displaystyle H\Big(\bigotimes_{j}\rho_{j}\Big)=\sum_{j}H(\rho_{j}),

(15)

and normalize the quantity with respect to blocked events (
n=0
).

III
Parameters adjustment and efficiency estimations

III.1
Splitting coefficients

Note that splitting coefficients
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
(more precisely, their absolute values since a phase does not contribute) should be adjusted in a specific way. In particular, the detection rate (for the sake of simplicity, we consider the Mandel approximation) at a receiver’s side with post-selection should be unchanged compared to the case without the attack; the latter results in the following equation:

\displaystyle\big(1-P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},0)\big)|\eta_{2}|=\eta_{L},

(16)

where real-valued
\eta_{L}
is the expected attenuation of a signal in a quantum channel with length
L
. Considering
P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},0)=e^{-2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2}}\approx 1-2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2}
and
|\eta_{2}|=1-|\eta_{1}|
, the latter equation results in:

\displaystyle 2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2}(1-|\eta_{1}|)=\eta_{L},

(17)

then

\displaystyle|\eta_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{2\eta_{L}}{|\alpha|^{2}}}\Big),\quad|\eta_{2}|=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1\mp\sqrt{1-\frac{2\eta_{L}}{|\alpha|^{2}}}\Big).

(18)

The latter equations for
|\eta_{1}|
and
|\eta_{2}|
show that the attack can be performed only if
\eta_{L}\leq\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}
(the root term should be positive), i.e., in a low transmission or, equivalently, high loss region; otherwise, an eavesdropper cannot maintain the detection rate. In order to perform an attack in the region
\eta_{L}\geq\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}
, the easiest way is to apply the attack only to a fraction of optical signals; the other part of optical signals is to be transferred to a receiver without any losses. Then condition (
16
) can be replaced by

\displaystyle z\big(1-P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},0)\big)|\eta_{2}|+(1-z)=\eta_{L},

(19)

where
z\leq 1
denotes the fraction of attacked signals. Applying the same approximations results in:

\displaystyle|\eta_{1}|=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{2(\eta_{L}-(1-z))}{z|\alpha|^{2}}}\Big),

(20)

\displaystyle|\eta_{2}|=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1\mp\sqrt{1-\frac{2(\eta_{L}-(1-z))}{z|\alpha|^{2}}}\Big).

(21)

The maximal value of
z
in the region
\eta_{L}\geq\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}
is as follows:

\displaystyle z=\frac{1-\eta_{L}}{1-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}.

(22)

Then, for any value of
\eta_{L}
, fraction
z
can be generalized as

\displaystyle z=\min\Big(1,\frac{1-\eta_{L}}{1-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\Big).

(23)

We summarize as follows:

•

If
\eta_{L}\geq\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}
, then
z=\frac{1-\eta_{L}}{1-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}
, and
2|\eta_{1}|=1
;

•

If
\eta_{L}\leq\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}
, then
z=1
, and
|\eta_{1}|
should be defined as in Eq. (
20
).

III.2
Attack efficiency

At this point, total information obtained by an eavesdropper can be estimated by

\displaystyle I=z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},n)\cdot\chi^{(n)}(\Delta)}{1-P(2|\eta_{1}||\alpha|^{2},0)},

(24)

where
z
is defined in Eq. (
23
) and
|\eta_{1}|
is defined in Eq. (
20
). Analysis of the equation shows that
|\eta_{1}|
with a plus sign results in slightly more information to be extracted. Hence, hereinafter we assume
|\eta_{1}|
in Eq. (
20
) to be with a plus sign.

IV
Application cases

In this section, several application cases are considered. We inspect utilized quantum states to be similar to the one in Eq. (
1

IV.1
Mach-Zehnder interferometer-based

In this section, we will consider a QKD protocol based on the optical scheme with two Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZI)
8
;
32
; the first one is for the state preparation, and the second one is for the interference. As we have stated earlier, we are mostly interested in the similarity of utilized states to the one in Eq. (
1
). In the MZI, the coherent state is split into two paths: the short one and the long one (with a time delay). In one of them, a phase modulator is placed. After the modulation, two paths are combined. The time-delay is long enough to consider two coherent states as separate states that are identical to the one in Eq. (
1
):

\displaystyle|\psi_{MZI}(\alpha,\phi_{j})\rangle=|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\alpha e^{i\phi_{j}}\rangle.

(26)

At this point, the proposed attack scenario and all the estimations can be directly implemented in regard to a QKD protocol based on the MZI optical scheme.

Note that no additional states should be considered (decoy states) with different values of
|\alpha|^{2}
; we will catch on to that in Section
V
. However, the phase randomization is not an issue for the proposed attack: there is always a reference beam that matches a global phase with a signal beam.

IV.4
Subcarrier wave

Subcarrier wave (SCW) QKD protocols
25
utilize phase-modulated states, prepared by a modulation with a harmonic electric signal
m\sin(\Omega t+\phi_{j})
applied to a coherent state
|\alpha_{0}\rangle_{\omega}
at frequency
\omega
, of the following form:

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{p=-S}^{S}|\alpha_{0}d^{S}_{0p}(\beta)e^{i\phi_{j}p}\rangle_{\omega+\Omega p},

(37)

where
d^{S}_{ij}(\beta)
is the Wigner (small) d-matrix,
\beta
is proportional to the modulation index
m
,
2S+1
is the number of interacting frequency modes. Usually,
S\gg 1
, and the Wigner d-matrix can be replaced with the Bessel function of the first kind:

\displaystyle\lim_{S\rightarrow\infty}d^{S}_{ij}(\beta)=J_{i-j}(m).

(38)

All sidebands contribute to the signal; its optical power we may define as follows:

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}=|\alpha_{0}|^{2}(1-J^{2}_{0}(m)),

(39)

and
|\alpha_{0}|^{2}J^{2}_{0}(m)
can be considered as the optical power of the central frequency mode, which may be viewed as the reference beam. Usually,
m<1
, and the power of the reference is higher than the total power of the sidebands:

\displaystyle J^{2}_{0}(m)>1-J^{2}_{0}(m).

(40)

Then an eavesdropper may utilize the central frequency mode in order to augment each sideband with the part of the reference (by modulating it with some constant phase
\theta_{e}
) as follows:

Appendix A
Holevo bound

For any non-orthogonal states
|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(0)})\rangle
and
|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(1)})\rangle
, an orthonormal basis
|u_{0}\rangle
and
|u_{1}\rangle
can be obtained by the Gram-Schmidt process:

\displaystyle|u_{0}\rangle=|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(0)})\rangle,

(43)

\displaystyle|u_{1}\rangle=\frac{|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(1)})\rangle-c|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(0)})\rangle}{\sqrt{1-|c|^{2}}},

(44)

where
c=\langle\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(1)})|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(0)})\rangle
, and, in turn, these states can be defined in terms of the found basis:

\displaystyle|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(0)})\rangle=|u_{0}\rangle,

(45)

\displaystyle|\psi(\alpha,\phi_{(1)})\rangle=c|u_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{1-|c|^{2}}|u_{1}\rangle.

(46)

An ensemble density matrix is then given by

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{2}\Big(|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|+|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1+|c|^{2}&c^{*}\sqrt{1-|c|^{2}}\\
c\sqrt{1-|c|^{2}}&1-|c|^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(47)

and its eigenvalues are as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{1\pm|c|}{2}.

(48)

The Holevo bound can be expressed as the von Neumann entropy of a given ensemble density matrix or, equivalently, the Shannon entropy of its eigenvalues:

\displaystyle\chi=-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\log_{2}(\lambda_{i})=h\Big(\frac{1-|c|}{2}\Big),

(49)

and

\displaystyle|c|=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}(1-\cos(2\Delta))}.

(50)
