 ABSTRACT
Under the Rapid Qualification technical area of the Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies
program, simulation-based approaches are being developed to better understand variability during
laser powder bed fusion processing caused by interactions among process parameters, scan paths, part
geometries, and material properties. Previous modeling and simulation work under the program focused
on developing simulation pipelines using a representative volume element approach, which showed the
process-structure-property relationships at specific locations of interest. However, these tools needed
two key features to determine microstructure uniformity across different component geometries, process
conditions, and feedstock compositions: (1) automatic selection the areas of interest via mapping of
the heterogeneity in thermal conditions of a part and (2) assessment of solidification microstructure
composition-sensitivity within the stainless steel 316H specification.
In this research, heterogeneity in a build was assessed using melt pool geometry as a surrogate parameter
for solidification conditions, with supervoxel summary statistics of the melt pool geometry serving as the
inputs to a machine-learning clustering method and connected component analysis to identify regions
of interest. This method was applied to a Challenge Problem build in which process conditions were
intentionally varied at different locations during the build. Results of the clustering analysis showed
the clusters agreed with the intuitive result that clusters are centered on the regions of varying process
parameters. Introducing more complexity in the assumptions of the thermal simulations, namely layer-wise
heat accumulation, resulted in more overlap in melt pool conditions between the regions of varying
processing parameters, which was also captured by the clustering analysis. This validation indicates that
such analysis will enable identification of regions of heterogeneity in future parts in which heterogeneity is
unknown.
Composition-dependent solidification pathways and their effects on microstructure morphology and texture
were analyzed with thermodynamic calculations of interface response functions and cellular automata
predictions of grain growth. Analysis showed that for the three powder compositions that have been used
in the program at the Manufacturing Demonstration Facility, an austenite-dominant solidification mode
is present, which agrees with experimental observations elsewhere in the program. When considering
the composition most susceptible to a mixed ferrite-austenite solidification mode within the ASME
Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section III stainless steel 316H specification, the results showed that
mixed-phase solidification can occur and result in significantly different grain morphology and texture.
Although uncertainty exists in the input parameters to the underlying material models, the result suggests
that experimental validation in the high Creq /Nieq end of the composition specification would be valuable.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program has an overarching goal
to accelerate the development and deployment of materials and manufacturing technologies to the
nuclear energy sector [1]. Under the Rapid Qualification technical area, an area of focus has been on the
investigations of simulation-based approaches toward accelerating material development and qualification
efforts. Manufacturing with laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) results in a linkage between the part
geometry, processing parameters, and material properties, and many of the relevant physical processes
that govern these process-structure-property relationships occur at time- and length-scales that are difficult
to interrogate with in situ sensing. Previous work in that area within the AMMT program focused on
using melt pool and solidification simulation to deepen understanding of the sources of process- and
material-induced variability and quantify the resulting performance variability for LPBF–manufactured
stainless steel (SS) 316 alloy, with a focus on the SS 316H high-carbon specification [2, 3]. This previous
work focused on higher-fidelity modeling to reproduce the melt pool conditions and the subsequent
solidification microstructure morphology and texture at a scale of approximately 1 mm3 . This research
validated the phenomenological models represented in the numerical codes, providing confidence in the
ability to reproduce observed experimental trends in the microstructure grain characteristics.
Interactions of the scan path and part geometry during LPBF have been observed to result in heterogeneous
porosity [4], microstructure [5, 6, 7], and mechanical properties [8]. Consequently, conducting targeted
studies that resolve grain structure at a relatively small length-scale—compared with that of an entire
component or build—required a priori knowledge of where to sample for obtaining and understanding
potential heterogeneity. In the case of the multiparameter “challenge problem” part studied in previous
work [3], the answer for where to sample for testing heterogeneity is intuitive—in the center of each region
of different process parameters. However, in parts built using constant processing parameters, more subtle
changes in melt pool conditions may not be intuited as easily.
To determine the regions in a part where melt pool conditions vary, the underlying problem is that
of identifying similarity between points. Clustering analysis is a fundamental data analytics tool,
generally leveraging unsupervised machine learning to group data points into separate populations.
This is particularly useful when strong prior knowledge is lacking regarding the number of clusters (i.e.,
heterogeneous regions of melt pool conditions). Previous work by Donegan et al. [9] and Knapp et al. [10]
used clustering to identify regions of unique melt pool conditions, which were shown to correlate with
variations in microstructure features [10]. However, those approaches notably focused only on individual
layers in the part, with computational limitations and workflow maturity likely restricting the ability to
scale the analysis to whole parts.
Additionally, the SS 316 steel composition has been observed to play a role in microstructure development.
Researchers have observed in work within the AMMT program that SS 316L [11] shows a different
process parameters sensitivity to microstructure than that of SS 316H [12]. The SS 316L microstructure
tends to comprise truncated columnar grains or equiaxed grains regardless of process parameter, whereas
the SS 316H microstructure tends to have increasingly large columnar grains as energy density increases.
Observations of the relatively process-insensitive equiaxed microstructure morphology have been
attributed to a two-phase solidification pathway that occurs in some compositions of SS 316L [13] under
LPBF conditions; however, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, that two-phase solidification pathway has
not been observed experimentally in SS 316H. The heterogeneity of process parameter sensitivity within
composition space due to the two-phase solidification pathway is understood to be a step-change caused by
activation of the pathway rather than a continuous, gradual change. Understanding where that step-change

 2

occurs for SS 316, and whether it is expected to occur within SS 316H composition space, is important for
ensuring consistency of material properties to any reference material datasets that are developed.
This report addresses both the melt pool- and composition-induced variability for LPBF SS 316H.
Section 2 details the clustering-based approach for determining variability in melt pool conditions within
a part. The algorithmic approach to detecting regions of interest is validated against a challenge problem
build in which intended regions of variability are known a priori. Section 3 describes the computational
study that interrogated the effects of composition within the American Society of Mechanical Engineers
(ASME) SS 316H specification on the resulting microstructure features. Finally, conclusions and future
directions are discussed briefly in Section 4.

 2. MELT POOL-INDUCED VARIABILITY
This section reports the methods and results for an algorithm to predict regions of variability in a part,
including validation that the regions identified through the proposed clustering method correspond with
physical understanding of the regions of the part made with different process parameters. In other words,
each region of different processing parameters in the part should contain at least one identified cluster.
The heat transfer simulations used as a signal for variability in the part and the clustering algorithm are
discussed first, followed by results from the application of the method and discussion of the validity of the
approach.
The process-structure-property relationships derive from the melt pool behavior, such as how the material
melts and solidifies and how the melt pool changes as a function of location in the part. As such, finding a
melt pool–related parameter that can be computed in a reasonable time frame is a necessity for identifying
regions of melt pool variability in the part. To that end, 3DThesis [14] was used to calculate the melt pool
geometry (i.e., width, length, depth) at all locations in a part. 3DThesis is a semianalytical solver for the
heat equation with a moving Gaussian heat source; the tool is notably fast and can be parallelized because
of the analytical nature of the solution and the implementation of the time integration. Although the
analytical solution necessitates modeling a simplified representation of the physics of the LPBF process,
any trends resulting from the solution are expected to correlate with the response of the physical process.
Consequently, this is a fundamentally different analysis from that of the higher-fidelity approach previously
taken in the AMMT program toward modeling the melt pool and resulting microstructure.
To investigate the effects of potential heat accumulation in the part throughout the build, two methods for
thermal simulation were implemented—(1) thermally independent layer simulations and (2) simulations
with heat accumulation between layers. Both cases use the same underlying 3DThesis solver, but each
makes a different assumption about the initial condition for each layer. In the thermally independent case,
each layer is treated as thermally independent, so the initial and far-field temperatures (𝑇0 ) are the same
as the build preheat temperature (298 K). In the case of thermal accumulation, the first layer starts with
𝑇0 at the preheat temperature. After the scan path ends, the temperature field is allowed to diffuse for
100 s, accounting for melting of other parts and recoating of the powder layer. The average temperature
of the domain is then used as the value for 𝑇0 in the subsequent layer. Because of the analytical nature
of 3DThesis, the boundary conditions of the build chamber and any differences between the thermal
diffusivity of the solidified part and powder bed are not considered. For that reason, these two conditions
present bounding cases for testing the impact on the clustering algorithm, not necessarily a realistic
prediction in terms of the amount of heat accumulation that occurs.
These two methods were implemented in Myna [15], a previously developed open-source tool to connect
LPBF build data with simulation workflows that can be found in the repository (https://github.com/
ORNL-MDF/Myna) commit 258fd2a and later within the thesis/melt_pool_geometry_part application. Table
1 shows the parameters for simulating the parts, using the default material parameter set for SS 316H

within Myna. The output of the simulations is a map of the maximum melt pool length, width, and depth
that each point experiences during the raster scanning of the layer. This is shown in Figure 1(a) for the
top-surface XY-plane, where the interaction of the scan path stripes with the part geometry can be seen to
create some localized heat accumulation in certain areas of the part. Before clustering, summary statistics
of mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values were calculated for cubic supervoxels with
fixed edge lengths. Edge lengths of 1, 1.5, 2, and 3 mm were calculated to assess sensitivity to edge
length on the clustering analysis. The results in Figure 1(b) show the mean melt pool length for 1.5 mm
supervoxel edge length, with the edges of each supervoxel outlined in black. Any supervoxels along
the edge of the part that had only partially filled data (<95% filled) were removed to keep the summary
statistics comparable between supervoxels.

The raw melt pool data plotted in Figure 2 show expected levels of variability corresponding to the part
regions (P3, P7, P8, P9). Figure 2(a) shows the layer-wise mean ± one standard deviation of the length and
width. The mean simulated melt pool length has a significant shift as the process parameters change in
both the thermally independent layers and heat accumulation cases. Figure 2(c) shows that considering
heat accumulation between layers leads to gradually growing melt pool sizes, with the rate of increase
proportional to that of the total energy input per layer. Region P9, with the largest power and slowest scan
speed, had a rapid heat accumulation.
The effect of heat accumulation in the distribution of melt pool behavior within each region of the part
can be seen in Figure 2(b) and 2(d). These plots show the mean and variation in melt pool behavior within
each of the regions. In the thermally independent case, little overlap exists between the distributions, which
are represented as ellipses fit to the 90% and 50% confidence intervals of the 2D normal distribution fit of
the data. When considering heat accumulation, the P7 melt pool conditions have more overlap with the P3
and P8 conditions. This overlap qualitatively indicates that whereas heat accumulation increases variability
within these different process parameter regions, more overlap exists in the melt pool behavior across the
entire component.
Once the supervoxels were calculated, the k-means++ method [16] clustering algorithm was used to
identify heterogeneous regions in the part. This method requires the number of clusters to be entered as
an input parameter, and the appropriate number of clusters is determined through metrics that assess the
separation of clusters, specifically the silhouette metric, Calinski-Harabasz score, and Davies-Bouldin
score. From this analysis, five clusters (𝑘 = 5) were found to create a reasonable degree of separation

 4

between clusters, whereas adding more clusters than that created overlap between cluster characteristics.
The number of clusters may be specific to a given component and should be reevaluated or set to a fixed
value, depending on the application. The volumetric result of this clustering analysis is shown in Figure 3,
in which each supervoxel is colored by cluster ID. The ordering of cluster IDs is not fixed in assignment
order; for example, cluster ID 1 in Figure 3(a) is not directly comparable to Figure 3(b).
To identify the locations of regions of interest based on those clusters, connected component analysis
was used to find the largest region of connected supervoxels that had the same cluster ID in the part. The
centroid of those connected components is visualized in Figure 3 as black boxes. (Note that the scale of
boxes is for visualization and does not correspond to the size of the connected region.) The shown results
are for a supervoxel side length of 1.5 mm, based on a qualitative assessment of the cluster side-length
versus region location for the thermally independent layer case. Depending on the application and
length-scale that is important for assessing uniformity, smaller or larger supervoxels may be appropriate.
These results confirm the earlier observation that considering heat accumulation in the thermal simulation
causes more variation in the melt pool behavior, and therefore the supervoxel classification is less
contiguous. Consequently, the thermally independent layers case provides the intuitive answer that regions
of variability and the corresponding representative volume elements for that region correspond to nearby
centroids of each process parameter region. This confirms that the general approach is clustering the data
in a reasonable way. Consideration of the more complex (and likely closer to reality) case in which heat
accumulates through layers shows that the most representative regions are not necessarily where one might
intuitively expect them, and significantly variability is present throughout the part. However, validation of
the quantitative values of heat accumulation is needed before deeper physical conclusions can be drawn.
Although this particular part contains larger magnitudes of differences in terms of heterogeneity than those
of most single-parameter parts, the data clustering method mainly acts on relative rather than absolute
differences in data and therefore would be able to detect variability in different parts. The ultimate goal is
to select these regions of interest for high-fidelity simulation and characterization, which will supplement
these data with quantitative differences for each region in terms of microstructure and properties.

 3. COMPOSITION-INDUCED VARIABILITY
To investigate the impact of SS 316H composition within the guidelines of the ASME codes and standards
committee handbook, researchers performed analyses using thermodynamics, kinetics, heat transfer, and
solidification simulations for a series of powder compositions. Three of the four material compositions
were based on the measured powder compositions from SS 316H builds performed at the Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) Manufacturing Demonstration Facility (MDF) as part of the AMMT
project. Based on the chromium to nickel equivalent (Creq /Nieq ) ratio of these powders, austenite is
expected to be the dominant solidification phase. The fourth material was selected to have the largest
possible Creq /Nieq ratio while still falling within ASME guidelines [17], rendering it the most likely to
produce a microstructure with a non-negligible fraction of ferrite during solidification. The three powder
compositions used in MDF builds were approximated as Fe-Creq -Nieq -Mo-Mn, with empirical equivalents
from DeLong [18] used to combine the nonmetallic elements into Creq and Nieq via the expressions

and

Simulations were performed for each of the four materials using the time-temperature history data
produced by AdditiveFOAM for the first 10 layers of P22 and the two-phase ExaCA model, both described
in a previous report [3]. The simulated microstructures are shown in Figure 5;as-solidified ferrite is
colored black and as-solidified austenite is colored using the inverse pole figure-Z map, and (100)
pole figures from XY slices of the microstructures taken from just below the final deposited layer. The
materials are sorted by calculated Creq /Nieq using the full DeLong equivalents. As expected, the three

 8

SS 316H materials produced almost entirely as-solidified austenite, with microstructures dominated by
epitaxial grains elongated in the build (Z) direction. Some as-solidified ferrite is present at the top surfaces
along melt pool centerlines, though it would be expected to remelt upon deposition of additional layers.
Although 10 layers and 67 degree rotation of the scan pattern between layers are generally not conducive
to texture development, similar textures are observed in the XY cross sections. The maximum intensity
multiples of uniform distribution observed in the (100) pole figures for these materials were generally
between 2.0 and 2.5, whereas the minima were around 0.3 to 0.6. The theoretical SS 316H composition
with the max Creq /Nieq was solidified with a mixture of ferrite and austenite phases, with epitaxial growth
of grains from previously deposited layers interrupted by nucleation and growth in successive layers. This
resulted in a significantly reduced grain size compared with that of the SS 316H materials printed at the
MDF as well as a significantly more uniform (100) pole figure, with multiples of uniform distribution
values generally ranging from 0.5 to 1.5. Although a large amount of uncertainty is present in these
predictions because of inherent uncertainty in the underlying models and thermodynamic data for complex
multicomponent system, these results suggest that some subset of the SS 316H composition space is prone
to multiple solidification modes. This in turn may lead to a significant composition-dependence of the
printed microstructure, particularly at the largest Creq /Nieq within the SS 316H specification, and could
warrant further experiments with powder compositions in this range.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This report details computational pipelines for evaluating two key sources of variability that can affect
as-built part performance resulting from the LPBF of SS 316H components: part-scale heterogeneity
resulting from the LPBF processing and feedstock composition variability affecting microstructure
features.
Part-scale variability was investigated using thermal simulation and clustering analysis. Based on the
simulated melt pool conditions within a component that considered interactions of part geometry,
scan path, and process parameters, this work validated an algorithmic method to select regions of

 9

variability within a component based on melt pool characteristics. Results showed that considering
the heat accumulation between layers will change the regions of variability identified by the algorithm,
especially for regions with high energy input (e.g., high laser power and low scan speed). Future research
directions should consider further development and validation of the layer-wise heat accumulation model,
likely requiring use of a higher-fidelity model to resolve effects of heat conduction in the powder bed.
Thermodynamic calculations combined with coupled melt pool and solidification models showed the
impact of varying austenite- and ferrite-stabilizing elements in SS 316H composition ranges specified in
ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section III. This analysis provided agreement with experimental
observations within the AMMT program that powder feedstock compositions of SS 316H used thus far
should result in the characteristic epitaxial columnar grain growth observed in experiments. In the extreme
corner of composition space most sensitive to the two-phase solidification pathway, thus far only observed
in SS 316L feedstock powders, simulation results indicate the possibility that the two-phase solidification
pathway occurs. This has potential implications on the microstructure morphology and texture that results
from the LPBF process if using powder feedstock near the that corner of allowable composition. Future
work should consider additional computational investigations in that corner of composition space to better
hypothesize where the boundary between single- and two-phase solidification occurs, and, importantly, to
validate those hypotheses with experimental observation.
