Improved Overall Survival Associated With Vaginal Cuff Brachytherapy Boost for Stage II Endometrioid Endometrial Cancer
Authors: Mariam Atobiloye, Jackson Howell, Jessica Cruttenden, Christopher Weil, Neil Taunk, Gita Suneja, David Gaffney, Lindsay Burt, Cristina DeCesaris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTreatment of International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO, 2009) stage II endometrial cancers (ECs) typically involves total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy followed by adjuvant radiation and/or chemotherapy. Existing guidelines recommend whole pelvis radiation therapy (WPRT); however, vaginal cuff brachytherapy (VCB) has conditional support as a boost. We sought to evaluate if there is improved overall survival (OS) associated with WPRT + VCB over WPRT alone for patients with FIGO (2009) stage II endometrioid EC and assess patterns of care. We hypothesize that WPRT + VCB will be associated with improved survival.Methods and MaterialsThe 2023 National Cancer Database was queried for patients with endometrioid ECs who underwent hysterectomy with complete surgical staging, consistent with T2N0M0, and were treated adjuvantly with WPRT + VCB or WPRT alone. Patients were excluded if risk factor information (eg, lymphovascular invasion, peritoneal cytology, myometrial invasion, and surgical margins) was incomplete. Logistic regression analysis was used to determine the propensity for each treatment method. Kaplan-Meier and landmark analyses for OS and univariate and multivariable (MVA) Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate (1) the entire cohort and (2) a propensity score–matched subgroup.ResultsOf 1782 patients included in this analysis, 670 received WPRT alone, and 1112 received WPRT + VCB. Median follow-up was 6.5 years. Logistic regression analysis revealed that few demographic or pathologic features were predictive of the adjuvant regimen received. WPRT + VCB was associated with an OS advantage (MVA: hazard ratio 0.68 [95% CI, 0.55-0.83]; P < .001). After propensity score matching, the OS benefit associated with WPRT + VCB was maintained (MVA: hazard ratio 0.61 [95% CI, 0.47-0.79]; P < .001).ConclusionsThis National Cancer Database analysis suggests that adjuvant treatment with WPRT + VCB is associated with improved OS compared to WPRT alone in patients with FIGO 2009 stage II endometrioid EC. Given the limitations of such database analyses, further exploration in molecularly defined cohorts is warranted.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy in the United States, with a clinical burden that has grown significantly over the past decade.1 Annual projections have climbed from approximately 60,000 cases and 10,000 deaths in 2016 to more than 68,000 cases and 14,000 deaths in 2026.1,2 As defined by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) in 2009, stage II EC involves the cervical stroma without extrauterine extension.3 The staging system expanded in 2023 to include adverse histologies and substantial lympho-vascular space invasion (LVSI).4 Treatment for stage II EC per the FIGO 2009 system involves total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy followed by adjuvant therapies guided by the presence or absence of pathologic risk factors.5

Adjuvant therapies for stage II EC may include any combination of whole pelvic radiation therapy (WPRT), vaginal cuff brachytherapy (VCB), and systemic therapy.5 Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated a reduction in locoregional recurrence with WPRT in patients with early-stage EC with high-intermediate risk factors for recurrence.6, 7, 8, 9 Subsequently, given the propensity for recurrence at the vaginal cuff in early-stage disease, studies comparing adjuvant VCB to WPRT have demonstrated equivalent rates of locoregional recurrence and survival between the 2 modalities in select populations.10,11

Consensus guidelines by the American Society for Radiation Oncology and the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology, the European Society for Radiotherapy & Oncology, and the European Society of Pathology recommend pelvic external beam radiation for most cases of stage II EC.5,12 A VCB boost may be added in the presence of adverse pathologic risk factors, such as deeply invasive disease, positive surgical margins, or substantial LVSI, to enhance local control.5 Conversely, select stage II patients with microscopic cervical stromal invasion, low-grade disease, absence of LVSI, and ≤50% myometrial invasion may be considered for adjuvant VCB alone; however, these recommendations are based on retrospective data.5 Bingham et al13 identified that VCB + WPRT improved survival in stage III patients; however, there are no convincing data demonstrating survival improvement with the addition of a VCB boost in stage II endometrial cancer.

This study uses the most up-to-date National Cancer Database (NCDB) to evaluate whether vaginal cuff brachytherapy combined with whole pelvic radiation therapy (WPRT + VCB) is associated with an overall survival (OS) benefit compared to WPRT alone for patients diagnosed with FIGO (2009) stage II endometrioid EC from 2010 to 2022, to identify covariates associated with VCB utilization, and to assess patterns of care.

Methods and Materials

Data source

The NCDB is a joint project of the Commission on Cancer of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society that consolidates deidentified information from multiple hospital cancer registries throughout the United States. Analysis of this publicly available, deidentified database met our institution’s criteria for nonhuman subject research, and our team was granted a formal oversight exemption by our institutional review board for this analysis.

Patient selection

The 2023 uterine corpus participant user file of the NCDB was queried for patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2022 with stage II endometrioid EC, as defined by the 2009 FIGO staging system, which includes: cervical stromal involvement (T2) along with pN0 or pNX (acceptable only if cN0), and cM0. Baseline demographic information, including race, insurance status, facility type where care was received, and the Charlson-Deyo Comorbidity Score (CDCS), was collected. Pathologic features, including histologic grade, LVSI, peritoneal cytology, and surgical margin status, were also collected.

Patients with missing information in any of these pathologic feature categories were excluded from analysis. Excluding patients with missing pathologic feature information was a deliberate decision made to enhance the characterization of the cohort, maximizing the use of the data variables provided in the NCDB, and aid propensity score matching; however, it came at the cost of reducing the cohort size and statistical power. In particular, LVSI was not tabulated in the NCDB before 2010; thus, our cohort was limited to patients diagnosed from 2010 through 2022.

The adjuvant treatments were categorized by the types of radiation therapy administered (eg, WPRT or VCB). Only patients who received WPRT alone or WPRT + VCB were included in this analysis. Patients were excluded from the analysis if they had positive margins, if they received chemotherapy, if adjuvant radiation commenced more than 12 weeks following hysterectomy, if radiation treatment took longer than 90 days to complete, if treatment was discontinued before completion, if WPRT was delivered in fewer than 16 or more than 33 fractions, or if VCB was delivered in more than 6 fractions.

Vital status is recorded during follow-up within the NCDB, but information on progression-free survival and locoregional/distant recurrence is not. Patients with missing vital status were excluded.

Statistical procedures

All data analysis was performed using STATA 16 (StataCorp) statistical software. Cox modeling was two-tailed with a statistical significance threshold of P < .05. A CONSORT flow diagram demonstrating the filtering of the NCDB to reach our patient cohort is presented in Fig. 1. Kaplan-Meier OS analysis was performed. Subsequently, a propensity score–matched Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed using a stabilized nonreplacement nearest-neighbor algorithm that matched patients based on age, race, CDCS, LVSI, pathologic grade, and peritoneal cytology. A standardized difference table and a propensity score plot for the propensity score–matched comparison are available in Table E1 and Fig. E1, respectively. As an additional method to reduce immortal time bias, a conditional landmark OS analysis using univariable Cox regression and a landmark time of 3 years postdiagnosis was employed for both the unmatched and matched comparisons.

Univariate (UVA) and multivariate (MVA) Cox proportional hazards analyses were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for OS between groups in each comparison. Covariate stratification was employed when necessary to adjust for covariates that failed the proportional hazards assumption, as determined by Schoenfeld residuals testing. Table E2 display the Schoenfeld residual testing results for each Cox model comparison following stratification to demonstrate that the proportional hazards assumption is valid for all remaining covariates and the treatment stratification.

Discussion

Summary of main results

This study used a nationwide hospital-based database to investigate the survival outcomes of WPRT with or without a VCB boost as an adjuvant therapy for patients with FIGO (2009) stage II endometrioid EC. Our analysis demonstrated that the addition of VCB boost to WPRT was associated with significant improvement in OS, even after a 3-year conditional landmark analysis to reduce immortal time bias and early mortality because of comorbidity and treatment-related factors.

When analyzing patterns of care, few novel patterns in demographic or pathologic characteristics emerged to predict treatment selection. Despite this, WPRT + VCB utilization increased over the study period and became the most common treatment modality in this cohort (66.3% of the patients diagnosed in 2022).

Results in the context of published literature

Adjuvant WPRT is the preferred recommendation for patients with FIGO (2009) stage II EC based on American Society for Radiation Oncology and European Society of Gynaecological Oncology, European Society for Radiotherapy & Oncology, and European Society of Pathology guidelines, and a VCB boost may be delivered for those with higher-risk features.5,12 However, the specific question of whether or not WPRT + VCB provides a survival advantage over WPRT alone in this population has not been studied prospectively, and inferences from prospective trials have been conflicting.

The Post-Operative Radiation Therapy in Endometrial Carcinoma (PORTEC)-2 trial compared adjuvant WPRT to VCB in patients with early-stage EC with high-intermediate risk factors, showing no difference in recurrence or survival; however, stage II patients (composing approximately 10% of each arm) were limited to those with endocervical glandular involvement.10 Those who received VCB alone had significantly higher rates of pelvic recurrences than those who received WPRT (6.3% vs 0.9%; P = .004), particularly among those with p53-mutant expression and substantial LVSI, though vaginal cuff recurrences were similar (3.4% vs 2.4%; P = .55) between groups.10 It is notable that patients in PORTEC-2 largely did not receive surgical nodal assessment. The Gynecologic Oncology Group (GOG) 249 trial compared adjuvant WPRT alone to VCB with chemotherapy and included patients with FIGO (2009) stage II.14 Patients in the VCB and chemotherapy arm had greater toxicity and higher rates of pelvic recurrence without a benefit in recurrence-free survival or OS. However, the trial included high-risk histologies, and only approximately 25% of patients in each arm had stage II disease.

Several authors have studied this question in the retrospective setting. For instance, a prior NCDB analysis by Bingham et al13 demonstrated the benefit of VCB boost in addition to WPRT for women with stage III EC with cervical involvement (HR, 0.8; P = .01). However, in this study, women without cervical involvement received no benefit from the addition of VCB boost (P = .84). Wojcieszynski et al15 performed a wider analysis of stage II EC patients from the NCDB, comparing observation, WPRT alone, VCB alone, and WPRT+VCB as adjuvant strategies. In their propensity score–matched comparison of WPRT versus WPRT + VCB, there was found to be no OS advantage to the addition of VCB (P = .56); however, these authors included a wider patient cohort, including those with serous and clear cell histologies and those who received chemotherapy. Additionally, approximately 70% of patients in each arm had unknown LVSI status, which limited the reliability of matching. Similarly, Narasimhulu et al’s16 systematic review and meta-analysis of stage II EC patients found no difference in OS between WPRT with or without VCB and VCB alone; however, this finding was limited to the pooled data from only 5 of the 15 included studies. Furthermore, some of the studies were conducted using the FIGO 1988 staging criteria, which included patients with “stage IIA” disease (defined as endocervical glandular involvement), a group that is no longer classified as stage II in the 2009 FIGO system. However, there was a significant reduction in locoregional recurrence with WPRT ± VCB compared with VCB alone (OR, 0.33 [95% CI, 0.16-0.68]) seen in a pooled analysis of 14 of the 15 included studies.

This current study differs methodologically from prior studies in 2 notable ways. First, patients with incomplete pathologic data were excluded. Compared with previous studies, this methodological choice reduced the statistical power of our analysis; however, it improved the clarity of group comparisons and increased our confidence in the propensity score matching process. Second, the patient population was limited to those with endometrioid adenocarcinomas to reduce the potential number of patients with p53abn mutations, who are at higher risk of distant failure and for whom PORTEC-3 indicates a benefit from the addition of systemic therapy.17 This focus on patients whose dominant mode of failure is local magnified the role of VCB boost in providing local control and potentially a survival advantage.

This study is among the first to show a statistically significant improvement in OS associated with the addition of VCB boost over WPRT alone in patients with FIGO (2009) stage II EC. Our hypothesis for the mechanism underlying this survival association is that VCB improves local control and can support better survival outcomes for patients whose first recurrence is local. Specifically, we do not suggest that VCB improves outcomes for those patients who fail distantly. Thus, excluding patients with nonendometrioid histologies, whose cancers tend to harbor higher rates of p53 mutations and are at increased risk of distant relapse, may explain why a survival benefit was associated with VCB in our study but not in prior studies on this topic. If this were true, it would suggest that local intensification with VCB may be most beneficial for patients with p53-wt cancers. Unfortunately, this hypothesis cannot be directly verified by this data set, as the NCDB does not include data on progression-free survival or patterns of failure. Further study in molecularly defined cohorts is necessary to validate this hypothesis, and inclusion of patient-level quality-of-life data would be critical for understanding the full clinical impact of these treatments.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its large sample size and representation of multiple geographic locations across the United States. The groups in this study were also well-balanced across multiple factors, including: race, age, CDCS, LVSI, pathologic grade, peritoneal cytology, and primary payor status. This study also accounts for multiple insurance and facility types across the country, including academic and community hospital centers, serving urban and rural patients.

However, this study has limitations typical of retrospective database analyses, including immortal time and selection biases. We sought to reduce these biases by including the conditional landmark analysis. A primary limitation is the absence of certain variables that could otherwise inform adjuvant treatment decision-making. For example, the extents of cervical invasion and LVSI are not codified in the NCDB; they are only listed as present or absent. Additionally, retrospective analyses of PORTEC 1, 2, and 3 have also demonstrated the value of utilizing molecular classification to predict adjuvant therapy response in EC.17,18 Though this testing is not yet ubiquitous, it is becoming the backbone of modern cooperative group clinical trials in endometrial cancer (eg, PORTEC 4a, Refining Adjuvant Treatment IN Endometrial Cancer Based On Molecular Features [RAINBO]).19,20 The inclusion of molecular profiling would be invaluable to future studies.

Finally, the lack of recurrence data in the NCDB limits this investigation to evaluating OS and precludes the direct assessment of progression-free survival or locoregional recurrence. Additionally, although the propensity score model we used adjusted for multiple variables recorded in the database, there may still be residual unrecorded confounders. Consequently, as a result of these limitations, we cannot determine whether the associated OS benefit reflects improved locoregional control or is a byproduct of potential residual confounding.

Conclusions

This retrospective analysis of a nationwide hospital-based database suggests that there has been heterogeneity in adjuvant radiation treatments for patients with FIGO (2009) endometrioid EC, though VCB boost following WPRT is gaining national popularity. An OS benefit was associated with WPRT + VCB compared with WPRT alone in both unmatched and propensity score–matched cohorts. Because of the retrospective nature of the NCDB, these results are primarily hypothesis-generating, and further evaluation in prospective studies with recurrence data and molecularly defined cohorts is warranted.
