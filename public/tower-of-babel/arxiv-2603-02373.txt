. 1.
Introduction

The
partitions
of a given
n\in\mathbb{N}
are the integer tuples
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
such that
x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=n
and
x_{1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{k}>0
, where
k\geq 1
is not restricted. For a given
f:\mathbb{N}\to\{0,\pm 1\}
, we define the
f
-
signed partition numbers

\mathfrak{p}(n,f)
to be the “weighted” partition sums

\mathfrak{p}(n,f):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=n\\[1.5pt]
x_{1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{k}>0\\[1.5pt]
k\geq 1\end{subarray}}f(x_{1})f(x_{2})\cdots f(x_{k}).

In other words, for fixed
n
, to any partition
\pi=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})
of
n
we assign the
sign

f(\pi):=f(x_{1})\cdots f(x_{k})
, and
\mathfrak{p}(n,f)
is simply the sum
\sum_{\pi}f(\pi)
with
\pi
running over all partitions of
n
.
Equivalently, one may define
\mathfrak{p}(n,f)
via the series expansion

\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-f(n)x^{n})^{-1}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathfrak{p}(n,f)x^{n}\qquad(\text{for complex $|x|<1$}).

For prime
p>2
, the
Legendre-signed partition numbers
are the quantities
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm(\tfrac{\cdot}{p}))
, where
(\tfrac{\cdot}{p})
is the Legendre symbol modulo
p
: that is, if
p\hskip-1.49994pt\nmid\hskip-1.49994pta
then
(\tfrac{a}{p})
is
1
(respectively
-1
) when
a
is (respectively is not) congruent to a square modulo
p
, and
(\tfrac{a}{p})=0
when
p\hskip-1.99997pt\mid\hskip-1.99997pta
. Throughout this paper
\chi
indicates a Legendre symbol
(\tfrac{\cdot}{p})
modulo some
p>2
.

The Legendre-signed partition numbers
\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)
were introduced in
[
7
]
, wherein the behaviours of different
\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)
as
n\to\infty
were examined. For instance, Corollaries 1.4 and 1.6 in
[
7
]
establish that:
For
p\neq 5
such that
p\not\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,8)
, as
n\to\infty
one has

\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)\asymp n^{c_{p}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa_{p}\sqrt{n}}}\right)

for some constant
c_{p}
, where
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
means that
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
and
b_{n}=O(a_{n})
, and

\kappa_{p}:=\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}(1-\tfrac{1}{p})}.

In particular, for
p\neq 5
with
p\not\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,8)
, one has

(1.1)

\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)\to\infty\qquad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

For comparison, we recall that the “ordinary” partition numbers
\mathfrak{p}(n,1)
satisfy

\mathfrak{p}(n,1)\sim\big(4\sqrt{3}n\big)^{-1}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\kappa\sqrt{n}}}\right)\qquad\text{with $\kappa=\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}$},

as shown by Hardy and Ramanujan
[
11
]
, where
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
indicates
\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

The cases of
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm(\tfrac{\cdot}{5}))
provide a surprising contrast to (
1.1
). Namely, as shown in
[
6
]
, one has

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n,(\tfrac{\cdot}{5})}}\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all $n\equiv 2\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,10)$},

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n,-(\tfrac{\cdot}{5})}}\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all $n\equiv 6\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,10)$}.

In light of (
1.1
)–(
1.3
), it is natural to consider what primes (if any) with
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,8)
also have periodic vanishings in the quantities
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm\chi)
. In computing
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm\chi)
for
n\leq 10000
and primes
p\leq 2000
, one finds only the single “candidate”
p=17
; this paper rigorously establishes these empirical vanishings in
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm(\tfrac{\cdot}{17}))
, as stated in the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

One has

(1.4)

\mathfrak{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n,(\tfrac{\cdot}{17})}}\right)=0\qquad\text{for all $n\equiv 17,19,25,27\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,34)$.}

Equivalently, one has
\mathfrak{p}(n,(\tfrac{\cdot}{17}))=0
whenever
n
is odd and
1-24n
is congruent to a quartic residue modulo 17.

Theorem 1.2
.

One has

(1.5)

\mathfrak{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n,-(\tfrac{\cdot}{17})}}\right)=0\qquad\text{for all $n\equiv 11,15,29,33\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,34)$.}

Equivalently, one has
\mathfrak{p}(n,-(\tfrac{\cdot}{17}))=0
whenever
n
is odd and
1-24n
is congruent to a quadratic-nonquartic residue modulo 17.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are proved by first computing Rademacher-style series formulae for
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm(\tfrac{\cdot}{17}))
, and then showing that the Kloosterman-type sums appearing in the series coefficients vanish on the specified residue classes modulo 34. In the course of our proofs, we find strong evidence for the following conjecture, which is supported by numerical experiments:

The only primes
p>2
for which
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm(\tfrac{\cdot}{p}))
vanishes on some arithmetic progressions modulo
2p
(in the sense of (
1.2
)–(
1.5
)) are
5
and
17
.

The structure of this paper.

The first half of this paper, sections
3
–
7
, derives exact series formulae for
\mathfrak{p}(n,(\tfrac{\cdot}{p}))
by following the classical techniques of Rademacher
[
20
]
and Lehner
[
13
]
. The majority of the second half, sections
8
–
14
, examines some properties of the coefficients of the series for
\mathfrak{p}(n,(\tfrac{\cdot}{p}))
, ultimately leading to a proof of Theorem
1.1
. These latter sections discuss a large number of basic properties of Dedekind sums and their “
\chi
-twisted” analogues.

Section
15
gives an abbreviated proof of Theorem
1.2
, and lastly section
16
collects some elementary lemmata (used elsewhere in the paper) about a combinatorial quantity determined by the residue of
p
modulo 8.

The scope of this paper.

The derivations of our series formulae for
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm\chi)
use classical methods, and the extensions to general
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,4)
are apparent in our proofs. On the other hand, even in our simple cases these formulae suffer from significant “sprawl” in notation and scope—the general formulae are worse still. Thus, in the hopes of a compromise between generality and simplicity, and out of our particular interest in
\mathfrak{p}(n,\pm(\tfrac{\cdot}{17}))
, throughout this paper we consider only

p\quad\text{such that}\quad p<24\quad\text{and}\quad p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,4).

As the reader knows (or, can imagine), the Taylor series of generating functions (à la Rademacher

Throughout this paper

The monikers “

(2.4)

Bold letters

denote subsets of

and have

3.
Functional Equations

Fixing
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,4)
, for complex
|x|<1
we define
\Phi(x)
as the generating function for
\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)
, namely

(3.1)

\Phi(x):=\prod_{a=1}^{p-1}(\chi_{a}x^{a};x^{p})^{-1}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)x^{n}.

Our first step in deriving a series formula for
\mathfrak{p}(n,\chi)
is the collection of several “functional equations” for
\Phi(x)

x
in different parts of the unit disk
|x|<1

Definition 3.1
.

For
\mathbf{a}\subset\{1,\ldots,\frac{p-1}{2}\}

The Dedekind sums

We now recall some basic properties of Dedekind sums—for further exposition see, e.g.,
[

,
17

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mkern-3.0mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\mkern-3.0mu}}\right)

and, using this and (

(8.5)

where again

and

We now define and examine “

and

, noting that

is again reserved for the Legendre symbol

Definition 8.1
.

and, in analogy with (

Remark 8.2

In
[

]
*p. 314 ff., Berndt defines

Akin to the derivation of equation (

From (

Definition 8.4

Lemma 8.5

Suppose that

Proof.

Because

is

Remark 8.7
.

In the case

, one has

Proof.

We first show that

is an integer by showing that
\sum_{\mu\,(p)}\mu^{2}\chi_{\mu}\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,p)

6

We thank Trevor Wooley for showing us an elementary proof of this congruence.
.
Let
g
be a primitive root modulo
p
. Since
\chi_{\mu}\equiv\mu^{(p-1)/2}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,p)
, we have

pB_{2}(\chi)=\sum_{\mu=1}^{p-1}\mu^{2}\chi_{\mu}\equiv\sum_{\mu=1}^{p-1}\mu^{2}\mu^{(p-1)/2}\equiv\sum_{\mu=1}^{p-1}g^{2\mu+\mu(p-1)/2}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,p),

and, separating

by parity, this is

\equiv\sum_{\nu=1}^{(p-1)/2}g^{4\nu}+\sum_{\nu=0}^{(p-3)/2}g^{4\nu+2+(p-1)/2}\equiv(g^{4}-g^{2})\sum_{\nu=0}^{(p-3)/2}g^{4\nu}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,p).

Turning now to the congruences modulo 8, since

[-x]=-1-[x]+\delta(x)
, we have

S(1-y)=\sum_{\mu,\nu\,(p)}(\mu+1-p)\chi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+c+\nu+1}{p}}}\right]-\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{a\mu+c+\nu+1}{p}}}\right)}\!}}\right),

and, using the observation that

\Big[\frac{n+1}{p}\Big]=\Big[\frac{n}{p}\Big]+\delta\Big(\frac{n+1}{p}\Big)\qquad\text{for integer $n$},

it follows that

S(1-y)=\sum_{\mu,\nu\,(p)}(\mu+1-p)\chi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]=S(y)+(1-p)\sum_{\mu,\nu\,(p)}\chi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+c+\nu}{p}}}\right].

From this point, using arguments similar to those for item
(1)
, one can easily show that the latter sum here is 0, and the assertion that
S(1-y)=S(y)
follows.
∎

Remark 9.4
.

If
y\in\tfrac{1}{a}\mathbb{Z}
, say
y=\frac{c}{a}
, it is similarly easy to derive that

S(1-y)=S(y)-\sum_{\mu\,(p)}\mu\chi_{a\mu+c}.

As discussed leading up to (
9.2
), if
(a,b)=1
and
(a,p)=1
, then
\tilde{s}_{\chi}(a,b)
is equivalent to either
S(0)
or
S(0)+S(\frac{1}{2})
modulo
2
. The next lemma shows that in fact
\tilde{s}_{\chi}(a,b)\equiv S(0)\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)
, regardless of the parity of
b
.

Lemma 9.5
.

Fix
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,4)
, and let
a
be odd with
(a,p)=1
. Then

S(\tfrac{1}{2})\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

Proof.

Let
a=2c+1
; since
S(y)
depends only on
a\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,p)
, we may assume that
0\leq c\leq\frac{p-3}{2}
. Reducing modulo 2, we have

\displaystyle S\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\sum_{\mu,\nu=0}^{p-1}\mu\chi_{\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a(2j-1)+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]

(9.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\equiv\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]-\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

For the first sum in (
9.3
), a straightforward computation (using arguments similar to those of the previous lemma) yields that

\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]\equiv c\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

For the second sum in (
9.3
), we similarly derive that

\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+c+\nu}{p}}}\right]\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+c}{p}}}\right]\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

Thus, to conclude that
S(\tfrac{1}{2})\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)
, it suffices to show that

(9.4)

\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+c}{p}}}\right]\equiv c\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)\qquad(a=2c+1).

Let
A_{j}
denote the
j
-th summand in (
9.4
).
First, if
a=1
and
c=0
, then
A_{j}=[\frac{2j}{p}]=0
for
1\leq j\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
, and (
9.4
) holds.
Thus, suppose that
a>1
, so that
c>0
. Writing

\sum_{1\mspace{1.5mu}\leq\mspace{1.5mu}j\mspace{1.5mu}\leq\mspace{1.5mu}\frac{p-1}{2}}\!\!A_{j}=A_{\frac{p-1}{4}}+A_{\frac{p-1}{2}}+\sum_{1\mspace{1.5mu}\leq\mspace{1.5mu}j\mspace{1.5mu}<\mspace{1.5mu}\frac{p-1}{4}}\!\!\big(A_{j}+A_{\frac{p-1}{2}-j}\big),

the remainder of our proof amounts to showing that

A_{\frac{p-1}{4}}\equiv c\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2),\qquad\qquad A_{\frac{p-1}{2}}\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2),

and

A_{j}\equiv A_{\frac{p-1}{2}-j}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)\qquad(1\leq j<\tfrac{p-1}{4}).

It is easy to verify the former two congruences, so we only demonstrate the latter.

Fixing
1\leq j<\frac{p-1}{4}
, we find that

A_{\frac{p-1}{2}-j}=\Big[\frac{2a(\frac{p-1}{2}-j)+c}{p}\Big]=a+\Big[\frac{-2aj-a+c}{p}\Big]\equiv 1+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{-2aj-c-1}{p}}}\right]\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

Since
[-x]=-1-[x]+\delta(x)
, this implies that

A_{\frac{p-1}{2}-j}\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+c+1}{p}}}\right]+\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2aj+c+1}{p}}}\right)\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

Using the fact that
[\frac{n+1}{p}]-\delta(\frac{n+1}{p})=[\frac{n}{p}]
with
n=2aj+c
, it follows that

A_{j}+A_{\frac{p-1}{2}-j}\equiv\Big[\frac{2aj+c}{p}\Big]+\Big[\frac{2aj+c}{p}\Big]\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2),

as claimed, and the assertion of the lemma follows.
∎

Having now established that
\tilde{s}_{\chi}(a,b)\equiv S(0)\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)
, regardless of the parity of
b
, it remains only to consider
S(0)
.

Lemma 9.6
.

Fix
p\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,4)
and
(a,p)=1
, and let
S(y)=S(a,p;y)
. Then

S(0)\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}(\chi_{a}-1)\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

Proof.

Since
\chi_{\nu}\equiv 1\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)
for
0<\nu<p
, we see right away that

(9.5)

S(0)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a(2j-1)+\nu}{p}}}\right]\equiv\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+\nu}{p}}}\right]-\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+\nu}{p}}}\right]\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

Letting
\{x\}=x-[x]
denote the
fractional part
of
x
for the moment, it follows easily from the coprimality of
a
and
p
that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{a\mu+\nu}{p}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(a+1)(p-1)^{2}-\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{a\mu+\nu}{p}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\tfrac{1}{2}(a+1)(p-1)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}(p-1)(p-2)\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2),

so it remains to consider the latter sum in (
9.5
). This time, for
1\leq j\leq\frac{p-1}{2}
we write

2aj=q_{j}p+r_{j},\qquad\text{with $1\leq r_{j}<p$},

noting that
r_{j}=p
is not possible, whereby

S(0)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj+\nu}{p}}}\right]\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{\nu+r_{j}}{p}}}\right]\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\sum_{\nu=p-r_{j}}^{p-1}1\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}r_{j}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

On the other hand we have
r_{j}=2aj-p[\frac{2aj}{p}]\equiv[\frac{2aj}{p}]\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)
, and we deduce that

S(0)\equiv\sum_{j=1}^{(p-1)/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj}{p}}}\right]\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2).

The result then follows from the well-known (see, e.g.,
[
17
]
*p. 32 ff.) congruence

\sum_{j=1}^{(b-1)/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{2aj}{b}}}\right]\equiv\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{a}{b}}}\right)-1}}\right)\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,\,2)\qquad(\text{for $b$ odd and $(a,b)=1$}),

where in this equality
(\frac{a}{b})
is the Jacobi symbol of elementary number theory.
∎

At last, with Lemmata

–

Lemma 10.3

We begin with
