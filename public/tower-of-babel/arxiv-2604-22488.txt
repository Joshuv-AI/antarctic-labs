Remarks on infimum and maximal lower bounds of a set of bounded self-adjoint operators
Matthias Günther 1 ∗ and Lutz Klotz 2

Abstract
. The notions of infimum and maximal lower bounds of a set 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} of bounded self-adjoint operators were mainly studied for a set 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} of two elements. The present paper deals with more general sets 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} , where it is required that 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} is nonempty and bounded from below. Kadison’s theorem on the existence of the infimum of a two-element set is proved for a countable and weak-operator compact set 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} . Stott’s recent results on the structure of the set of maximal lower bounds of a finite set of Hermitian matrices are discussed and partially generalized. We are also concerned with the greatest lower bound and maximal lower bounds under certain restrictions. It is shown that the set of all lower bounds of 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} commuting with all elements of 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} possesses the greatest element if 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} is a set of pairwise commuting operators. The theorem of Moreland and Gudder on the existence of the greatest positive lower bound of a set of two positive matrices is extended to an arbitrary finite set of positive matrices.

Elementary properties of greatest lower bound
and maximal lower bounds

Let
\mathfrak{L}
be a set, which is partially ordered with respect to an
order relation denoted by
\leq
. An element

of
\mathfrak{L}

\mathfrak{L}

greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}
will be denoted by

and the
set of maximal elements by
\Max\mathfrak{L}
.

Let

be a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathfrak{S}
be
the space of bounded self-adjoint linear operators on

,
endowed with Löwner’s partial order.

Let us state an important result,
cf.

[
25
, Thm. 3.2]

matrix-valued measure
[
25
]
and in multivariate

[
19
]

Theorem 2.1
.

Let

be a nonempty subset of

such that

for
some

there exists

with

and

, the greatest
element

of
\mathfrak{M}
exists and

(2.1)

\bigl(G(\mathfrak{M})u,u\bigr)=\sup\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\},\;u\in H.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a nonempty subset of
\mathfrak{S}
. An operator

is called a

of
\mathfrak{M}
if

is

Throughout the rest of the paper,

Theorem 2.2
.

For arbitrary

, there exists

with

. In particular, the set
\Max\mathfrak{L}
is not empty. It is a

\inf\mathfrak{M}
exists. In this case,
\Max\mathfrak{L}=\{\inf\mathfrak{M}\}
.

Proof.

For
L\in\mathfrak{L}
, set
\widetilde{\mathfrak{L}}:=\{S\in\mathfrak{L}:S\geq L\}

Applying Theorem
2.1
to a totally ordered subset of

satisfies
the conditions of Zorn’s lemma and the existence of

is a
maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}
as well, which proves the first assertion.
The second assertion is obvious. Finally, let
\Max\mathfrak{L}=\{M\}
be a
singleton. If
L\in\mathfrak{L}
, according to the first assertion just
proved there exists
\widetilde{M}\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
with

. Since
M
is the only maximal element of

\mathfrak{L}
, it follows
M=\widetilde{M}\geq L

, which
yields

.

\mathfrak{M}/H_{1}:=\bigl\{S/S_{1}:S\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\}.

3.
Generalizations of Kadison’s theorem

The aim of the present section is to apply Kadison’s elegant proof

[
17
]
,
cf.

[
18
, Ex. 2.8.18]
, to a
set
\mathfrak{M}
of more than two elements.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. If there exists a dense subset

D
of the unit sphere of
H
such that

(3.1)

\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\}=0,\quad u\in D,

the infimum of
\mathfrak{M}
exists and is equal to
0
. In addition, let

\mathfrak{M}
be a bounded set. If there exists
u\in H
such that

(3.2)

\alpha:=\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\}>0

the zero operator is not the infimum of
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

The first claim is an obvious consequence of the continuity of the
operators of
\mathfrak{S}
. Assume that (
3.2
) is satisfied. Let

H_{1}
be the space spanned by
u
,
H_{2}
be its
orthogonal complement, and

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&A_{12}\\
A_{12}^{\ast}&A_{2}\end{pmatrix}

a partition of
A\in\mathfrak{M}
according to the decomposition
H=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
. Choose
l_{1}\in(0,\alpha)
. If we can find an operator
L\in\mathfrak{L}
of the form

L=\begin{pmatrix}l_{1}&0\\
0&l_{2}I\end{pmatrix},

this will mean that
0
is not the infimum of
\mathfrak{M}
. By
Theorem
2.3
the inequality
L\leq A
is true if

(3.3)

A_{2}-l_{2}I\geq(a_{1}-l_{1})^{-1}A_{12}^{\ast}A_{12}.

If
\mathfrak{M}
is a bounded set, (
3.3
) is satisfied for all

A\in\mathfrak{M}
and a number
l_{2}\in(-\infty,0)
, where
|l_{2}|
is
large enough.
∎

Using a standard continuity argument, it can be concluded that for a
bounded set
\mathfrak{M}
, relation (
3.1
) is satisfied if and only
if

(3.4)

\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\}=0\quad\text{for all $u\in H$.}

We omit the details and give a simple example showing that for an
unbounded set
\mathfrak{M}
, such an equivalence is not true in general.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\{u_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be an orthonormal basis of a Hilbert
space and
P_{n}
denote the projection onto the space spanned by

u_{n}
. For the unbounded set
\mathfrak{M}:=\{n^{2}P_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
one has

\inf\bigl\{(n^{2}P_{n}u,u):n\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}=0

if
u
is a finite linear combination of the basis vectors, therefore,
(
3.1
) is satisfied, and
\inf\mathfrak{M}=0
. Let

(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive numbers with

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{2}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}na_{n}=\infty.

Then, for
u:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}u_{n}
one has

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(n^{2}P_{n}u,u)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}n^{2}a_{n}^{2}=\infty,

and (
3.4
) is not satisfied.

Our proof of the second assertion of Lemma
3.1
is based on
Kadison’s idea. It does not work for an unbounded set
\mathfrak{M}
and the
following question remains open. Does there exist an unbounded set

\mathfrak{M}
with
\inf\mathfrak{M}=0
although for all dense subsets
D
of the unit sphere, relation (
3.1
) fails?

The following theorem, whose first assertion was stated by
Šmul
′
jan
[
25
, Thm. 1.2]
in a similar form,
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.1
and (
2.3
).

Theorem 3.3
.

If there exist
G\in\mathfrak{S}
and a dense subset
D
of the
unit sphere of
H
such that for
u\in D
,

(3.5)

(Gu,u)=\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\},

the infimum of
\mathfrak{M}
exists and equals
G
. Let, additionally,

\mathfrak{M}
be bounded. If
\inf\mathfrak{M}
exists, condition (
3.5
) is
satisfied for
G:=\inf\mathfrak{M}
and all
u\in H
.

As an application of the preceding theorem we can generalize
Kadison’s result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be countable and weak-operator compact. The infimum of

\mathfrak{M}
exists if and only if there exists
G\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

G\leq A
for all
A\in\mathfrak{M}
. In this case,
G=\inf\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

The ‘if’-part is obvious and is true for an arbitrary set
\mathfrak{M}
.
Conversely, assume that
G:=\inf\mathfrak{M}
exists. Choose
v\in H
. Since
\mathfrak{M}
is weak-operator compact, it is weak-operator
bounded, hence, bounded by uniform boundedness principle. Boundedness
of
\mathfrak{M}
and Theorem
3.3
yield the existence of

A_{n}\in\mathfrak{M}
with

(Gv,v)\leq(A_{n}v,v)\leq(Gv,v)+\frac{1}{n},\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

By weak-operator compactness one can find a subsequence

\bigl(A_{n_{k}}\bigr)
and an operator
A\in\mathfrak{M}
satisfying

(Gv,v)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(A_{n_{k}}v,v)=(Av,v).

Since
A-G\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
, it follows
v\in N(A-G)
, and since

v\in H
was arbitrary, we obtain

(3.6)

H=\bigcup_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}N(A-G).

If
A-G\neq 0
, the set
N(A-G)
is a nowhere dense subset of

H
. Therefore, (
3.6
) and Baire’s category theorem
imply that there exists
A\in\mathfrak{M}
with
G=A
. The equality

G=\inf\mathfrak{M}
is trivial.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If
\mathfrak{M}
is a finite set, the topological proof of the preceding
corollary on the basis of Baire’s category theorem can be replaced by
a purely algebraic proof of the fact, that no vector space over the
field
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
is a finite union of proper subspaces.

We show by examples that the ‘only if’-part of corollary
3.4

is not true if one of the conditions on
\mathfrak{M}
is omitted.

Example 3.6
.

Each of the following three sets
\mathfrak{M}
satisfies
\inf\mathfrak{M}=0
and

0\notin\mathfrak{M}
.

(i)

Let
\{u_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a countable dense subset of the
unit sphere of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
P_{n}
denote the projection onto
the space spanned by
u_{n}
. The set
\mathfrak{M}:=\{P_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is countable and bounded but not closed.

(ii)

Let
\mathfrak{M}=\{P_{u}:u\in\mathbb{C}^{2},\|u\|=1\}
, where
P_{u}

denote the projection onto the space spanned by
u
. The set

\mathfrak{M}
is compact but not countable.

(iii)

The set
\mathfrak{M}
of Example
3.2
is countable and
weak-operator closed but not bounded.

4.
Maximal lower bounds

The present section deals with the structure of the set of maximal
lower bounds of a set
\mathfrak{M}
. We start with an auxiliary result,
which is also of interest in the theory of quantum effects, cf. Section
6
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. If

(4.1)

\bigcap_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}{R}(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})=\{0\}

the zero operator is the only positive lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. Let,
additionally,
\mathfrak{M}
be a finite set. If (
4.1
) is not true,
there exists a positive lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
different from
0
.

Proof.

If
S,T\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
and
S\leq T
, then

R(S^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\subseteq R(T^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
by
Douglas’ theorem
[
11
]
. Thus, (
4.1
) yields
L=0
if

L\in\mathfrak{L}\cap\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. The proof of the second assertion uses
properties of the parallel sum of positive operators, cf.
[
3
,
12
,
23
]
for
definition and basic properties of parallel sum. Let
\mathfrak{M}
be
finite and
S
denote the parallel sum of the operators of
\mathfrak{M}
.
Since
S
is a positive lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
satisfying

R(S^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})=\bigcap_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}),

cf.

[
12
, Thm. 4.2]
or

[
3
, Thms. 10, 11]
, the second assertion follows.
∎

Since there exist
A,B\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
with

N(A)=N(B)=\{0\}
and

R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\cap R(B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})=\{0\}
,
the second assertion of the preceding lemma shows that Theorem 2 of

[
6
]
is not correct.

Note that
M\in\mathfrak{L}
is a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
if and
only if the zero operator is the only positive lower bound of the set

\mathfrak{M}-M
. Therefore, from Lemma
4.1
a characterization of

\Max\mathfrak{L}
can be derived immediately.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
. If
M\in\mathfrak{L}
and

(4.2)

\bigcap_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}R\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)=\{0\},

the operator
M
is a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. Let,
additionally,
\mathfrak{M}
be a finite set. If (
4.2
) is not true,
the operator
M
is not a maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}
.

\square

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
H=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
be a nontrivial
orthogonal decomposition of
H
. Let
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
be a subset
of
\mathfrak{S}(H_{1})
bounded from below and

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}:A_{1}\in\mathfrak{M}_{1}\right\}

a corresponding subset of
\mathfrak{S}(H)
. If
M_{1}
is a
maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
, then

\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

is a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. Let, additionally,

\mathfrak{M}_{1}
be a finite set. Any maximal lower bound
M
of
\mathfrak{M}

has the form

\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

for some maximal lower bound
M_{1}
of
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
.

Proof.

The first assertion is obvious. Let
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
be finite and

M=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&M_{12}\\
M_{12}^{\ast}&M_{2}\end{pmatrix}

be a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. Since

R(S)\subseteq R(S^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
for

S\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}(H)
, from Theorem
4.2
follows

\bigcap_{A_{1}\in\mathfrak{M}_{1}}R\left(\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}-M\right)=\{0\}.

This condition would not be satisfied if

\begin{pmatrix}M_{12}\\
M_{2}\end{pmatrix}\neq 0
. Thus

M=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

and, clearly,
M_{1}
is a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
. If
M\in\mathfrak{L}
and

(4.3)

\overline{\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}N(A-M)}=H,

the operator
M
is a maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}
. If, additionally,

\mathfrak{M}
is a finite set and the ranges of all operators
A-M
,

A\in\mathfrak{M}
, are closed, then (
4.3
) is also necessary for the
maximality of
M
.

Proof.

For a subset
F
of
H
set
F^{\perp}:=\{v\in H:(u,v)=0\text{ for all }u\in F\}
. Recall that
an arbitrary family
\{F_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
of
subsets of
H
satisfies the relation

\Bigl(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}F_{\lambda}\Bigr)^{\perp}=\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda}F_{\lambda}^{\perp}.

Writing this equality down for the family

\{N(A-\mathfrak{M}):A\in\mathfrak{M}\}
, we obtain

(4.4)

\Bigr(\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}N(A-M)\Bigr)^{\perp}=\bigcap_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}\bigl(N(A-M)\bigr)^{\perp}.

Noting that

\Bigl(N(A-M)\Bigr)^{\perp}=\Bigl(N\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)\Bigr)^{\perp}=\overline{R\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)},

and taking the orthogonal complements on both sides of (
4.4
),
we obtain that relation (
4.3
) and the equality

(4.5)

\bigcap_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}\overline{R\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)}=\{0\}

are equivalent. It follows that (
4.3
) yields (
4.2
) and
the first assertion of the corollary is a consequence of the first
assertion of Theorem
4.2
. If for all
A\in\mathfrak{M}
the range
of
A-M
is closed, we have

R\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)=\overline{R\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)}

since

R(A-M)\subseteq R\bigl((A-M)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)\subseteq\overline{R(A-M)}\quad\text{and}\quad R(A-M)=\overline{R(A-M)}.

Thus, (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) are equivalent in this case, and
the second assertion of the corollary follows from the second
assertion of Theorem
4.2

∎

Remark 4.5
.

In general (
4.3
) does not follow from (
4.2
) if the
ranges of
A-M
,
A\in\mathfrak{M}
, are not closed, cf. remarks following
Lemma
4.1
. It is also interesting to compare the condition
(
4.3
) with the characterization (
3.6
) of the greatest
element.

We give an example, which indicates that there is only little chance to
weaken the finiteness condition of the second assertion of
Theorem
4.2
.

Example 4.6
.

The set

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}&\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}

is a countable and compact subset of the set of
2\times 2
matrices.
If
L\in\mathfrak{L}\cap\mathfrak{S}^{+}
, it has the form

L=\begin{pmatrix}l&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for some $l\in[0,1]$}.

The determinant

\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\\[3.0pt]
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}&\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix}-L\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{l}{n}

is negative for
n
large enough if
l>0
. Thus,
0
is the only
positive lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
and
M=0
is a maximal lower bound of

\mathfrak{M}
. If
M=0
, the left-hand side of (
4.3
) is

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0\\
z\end{pmatrix}:z\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
,
which is different from
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

The preceding example shows that the formulation of
Theorem 1.15 of
[
27
]
is not quit correct, see, however,
its corrected version
[
13
, Thm. 1]
.

Recall that, by definition,
L\in\mathfrak{L}
is an
extreme point
of
the convex set
\mathfrak{L}
if equality
L=\frac{1}{2}(L_{1}+L_{2})
for
some
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathfrak{L}
implies that
L_{1}=L_{2}
. As an
application of Theorem
4.2
and relation (
4.3
) we
establish a relation between the set of extreme points and the set of
maximal elements of
\mathfrak{L}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

For a set
\mathfrak{M}
, the set of extreme points of
\mathfrak{L}
is a subset
of the set
\Max\mathfrak{L}
. If
M
is a maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}

satisfying (
4.3
), it is an extreme point of
\mathfrak{L}
.

Proof.

If
L\in\mathfrak{L}
is not maximal and
M
is a maximal element with

L\leq M
, one can write

L=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(2L-M)+M\bigr],

where

2L-M=L+(L-M)
and
M
are different elements of
\mathfrak{L}
. Thus
L

can’t be an extreme point.

To prove the second assertion, note first that if
M
is maximal and

M=\frac{1}{2}(M_{1}+M_{2})
with
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathfrak{L}
, then
necessarily
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
are maximal elements. Recall that

N(S+T)=N(S)\cap N(T)
for

S,T\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. If
M
satisfies (
4.3
), it follows

\displaystyle\overline{\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}N(A-M)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}N\bigl(A-\tfrac{1}{2}(M_{1}+M_{2})\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\sum_{A\in\mathfrak{M}}\bigl(N(A-M_{1})\cap N(A-M_{2)})\bigr)}=H.

Since
M_{1}u=M_{2}u
, if
u\in N(A-M_{1})\cap N(A-M_{2})
, we obtain
M_{1}=M_{2}
.
∎

The following discussion is motivated by

[
26
, Sect. 4]
. Let
u
be a unit vector of
H
. Set

\alpha:=\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\},

and

\mathfrak{M}_{u}:=\bigl\{A\in\mathfrak{M}:(Au,u)=\alpha\bigr\},\quad\mathfrak{L}_{u}:=\bigl\{L\in\mathfrak{L}:(Lu,u)=\alpha\bigr\}.

Let
H_{1}
be the space spanned by
u
and
H_{2}

be its orthogonal complement. Denote by

S=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&S_{12}\\
S_{12}^{\ast}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}

a block operator representation of
S\in\mathfrak{S}
according to the
orthogonal decomposition
H=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{u}
be a nonempty set. If
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
is not empty, then

(4.6)

Au=Bu,\;A,B\in\mathfrak{M}_{u}.

Proof.

For

A=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&A_{12}\\
A_{12}^{\ast}&A_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{M}_{u}\quad\text{and}\quad L=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&L_{12}\\
L_{12}^{\ast}&L_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{L}_{u},

inequality
L\leq A
yields
A_{12}^{\ast}=L_{12}^{\ast}
by
Theorem
2.3
(ii). It follows
Au=Lu
for all
A\in\mathfrak{M}_{u}
.
∎

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{u}
be not empty and (
4.6
) be satisfied. For

B=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&B_{12}\\
B_{12}^{\ast}&B_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{M}_{u}

define

(4.7)

\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}:=\{A_{2}-(a_{1}-\alpha)^{\#}(A_{12}-B_{12})^{\ast}(A_{12}-B_{12}):A\in\mathfrak{M}\}.

By (
4.6
) the definition of
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
does not depend on the
choice of
B
. From Theorems
4.7
and
2.3
one can
immediately derive the following description of the set
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
,
which generalizes a result of
[
26
, Prop. 4.6]
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{u}
be not empty and (
4.6
) be satisfied. An
operator
L
is an element of
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
if and only if its block
operator representation is of the form

(4.8)

L=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&B_{12}\\
B_{12}^{\ast}&L_{2}\end{pmatrix}

for some
L_{2}\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{M}^{\prime})
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
is not
bounded from below, the set
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
is empty.

As the following example reveals, it is possible that

\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{M}^{\prime})
is empty even if (
4.6
) is satisfied.

Example 4.10
.

The set

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n^{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}&\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}

is a countable and compact subset of the set of
2\times 2

matrices. Choosing
u:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
, we have
\alpha=1
,

\mathfrak{M}_{u}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\},

and (
4.6
) is satisfied. The set

\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}=\left\{\frac{1}{n}-\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\{0\}=\left\{\frac{1}{n}-n:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\{0\}

is not bounded from below and according to Theorem
4.8
, the
set
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
must be empty. This can also be seen directly. If

L=\begin{pmatrix}l_{1}&l_{12}\\
l_{12}^{\ast}&l_{2}\end{pmatrix},

would be a lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
satisfying
(Lu,u)=1
, we must have

l_{1}=1
and the determinant

\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n^{2}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}&\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix}-L\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{l_{2}}{n^{2}}-\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-l_{12}\right|^{2}

must be nonnegative for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a contradiction.

If
\mathfrak{M}_{u}
is empty, condition (
4.6
) is trivially
satisfied. Also in this case, there are examples, where
\mathfrak{L}_{u}

is not empty, as well as examples, where
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
is empty.

Example 4.11
.

(i) If

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n}&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad u:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

the set
\mathfrak{M}_{u}
is empty. The set
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
is not empty since

\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{L}_{u}.

(ii) The set

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n}&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{n}&2\\
2&4\end{pmatrix}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and its closure

\overline{\mathfrak{M}}=\mathfrak{M}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
2&4\end{pmatrix}\right\}

have the same lower bounds. If
u:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

one obtains

\mathfrak{M}_{u}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
2&4\end{pmatrix}\right\}

and
\mathfrak{L}_{u}(\mathfrak{M})=\mathfrak{L}_{u}(\overline{\mathfrak{M}})
is empty by
Theorem
4.8
.

The following description of the maximal elements of
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
is
an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.8
.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
M
be a lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. Under the conditions of

the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

the operator

is a maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}_{u}
,

(ii)

the operator
M
is a maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}
and satisfies

,

(iii)

the operator
M
has a block operator representation

M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&B_{12}\\
B_{12}^{\ast}&M_{2}\end{pmatrix},\text{ where }\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&B_{12}\\
B_{12}^{\ast}&B_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{M}_{u}

and

is a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}^{\prime}
.

Remark 4.13
.

Let
H
be separable and
\mathfrak{M}
be a bounded subset of

\mathfrak{S}
. The operator norm closure
\overline{\mathfrak{M}}
of
\mathfrak{M}
is

above, we conclude that for the weak-operator

and an arbitrary unit vector

by
(2.d)

We conclude the present section by proving the existence of a positive
maximal lower bound for an arbitrary set of positive
n\times n

matrices without referring to Zorn’s lemma explicitly. The proof is
by induction over the order
n
of the matrices and could serve as a
basis of an algorithm for computing a positive maximal lower bound.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathfrak{M}
be a set of positive
n\times n

matrices. There exists a positive maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Induction over
n
. If
n=1
a positive maximal lower bound exists and
is even an infimum. Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a set of positive

(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrices and let

\gamma:=\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M},u\in H,\|u\|=1\bigr\}.

Replacing
\mathfrak{M}
by
\mathfrak{M}-\gamma I
, we can assume that
\gamma=0
.
Let
(A_{k})
be a sequence of matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
and
(u_{k})
be a
sequence of unit vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
satisfying

(A_{k}u_{k},u_{k)})<\frac{1}{k}.

Choosing a subsequence if necessary, we can also assume that a unit
vector
u:=\lim_{k\to\infty}u_{k}
exists. If
L
is a positive lower
bound of
\mathfrak{M}
, it follows

0\leq(Lu,u)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(Lu_{k},u_{k})\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}(A_{k}u_{k},u_{k})=0,

hence,
u\in N(L)
. Let
H_{1}
be the space spanned
by
u
and
H_{2}
its orthogonal complement. Write

A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&A_{12}\\
A_{12}^{\ast}&A_{2}\end{pmatrix}

according to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
. From Theorem
2.3
(iii) it follows
that the set

\mathfrak{M}/H_{1}:=\bigl\{A/a_{1}=A_{2}-a_{1}^{\#}A_{12}^{\ast}A_{12}:A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\}

is a set of positive
n\times n
matrices. By the induction assumption,
there exists a positive maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}/H_{1}
. Denote it by
M_{2}
. It follows that
A/a_{1}-M_{2}\geq 0
,

A\in\mathfrak{M}
, and again by Theorem
2.3
that

M:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&M_{2}\end{pmatrix}

is a positive lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. To show that
M
is maximal, let

L:=\begin{pmatrix}l_{1}&L_{12}\\
L_{12}^{\ast}&L_{2}\end{pmatrix}

be a lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
with
M\leq L
. Since
L
is positive, it
has the form

L:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&L_{2}\end{pmatrix}

where
A/a_{1}-L_{2}\geq 0
,
A\in\mathfrak{M}
. Thus,
L_{2}
is a lower
bound of
\mathfrak{M}/H_{1}
and
M_{2}\leq L_{2}
. Since
M_{2}

is a maximal lower bound, it follows that
L_{2}=M_{2}
, which yields

M=L
.
∎

5.
Maximal lower bounds of a two-element set

Let
H
be an arbitrary Hilbert space over
\mathbb{C}
. If

\mathfrak{M}:=\{A,B\}
is a set of two operators on
H
, some
maximal lower bounds can be expressed in terms of
A
and
B

explicitly. For a bounded operator
T
on
H
set
|T|:=(T^{\ast}T)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any bounded and boundedly invertible linear operator
T
on

H
, the operator

(5.1)

M_{T}:=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(A+B-T^{\ast}\bigl|(T^{\ast})^{-1}(A-B)T^{-1}\bigr|T\Bigr)

is a maximal lower bound of the set
\mathfrak{M}=\{A,B\}
.

Proof.

Let
T=I
and

A-B=\int_{a}^{b}t\,E(dt)

be the spectral representation of the self-adjoint operator
A-B
. It
is easy to see that

N(A-M_{I})=R\bigl(E([a,0])\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad N(B-M_{I})=R\bigl(E([0,b])\bigr),

which yields
M_{I}\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
by (
4.3
). The general result
is a consequence of (
2.4
).
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

For a set
\mathfrak{M}=\{A,B\}
of two operators, there exists a maximal
lower bound
M
satisfying

N(A-M)+N(B-M)=H
.

Note that for a unitary operator
U
on
H
the equality

U^{\ast}\bigl|U(A-B)U^{\ast}\bigr|U=|A-B|

is true. If
T
is bounded and boundedly invertible, it has a polar
decomposition
T=U|T|
, where
U
is unitary. It follows that

M_{T}=M_{|T|}
, and the sets

\bigl\{M_{T}:\text{T is bounded and boundedly invertible}\}

and

(5.2)

\bigl\{M_{S}:\text{$S\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}$, $S$ is boundedly invertible}\bigr\}

are equal.

Stott
[
27
, Thm. 2.3]
, cf.
[
13
, Thm. 1]
, has shown by solving a linear maximum
problem that the set described by (
5.2
) coincides with the set

\Max\mathfrak{L}
if
\dim H<\infty
. Note, however, that in
general the correspondence
S\mapsto M_{S}
is not one-to-one. For if

S
leaves invariant the spaces
R\bigl(E[a,0)\bigr)
,

R\bigl(E(\{0\})\bigr)
, and
R\bigl(E(0,b]\bigr)
,
the operator
M_{S}
is equal to
M_{I}
. If
\dim H=\infty
,
the set (
5.2
) is, in general, a proper subset of

\Max\mathfrak{L}
. To see this, choose
A,B\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
such that

N(A)=N(B)=\{0\}
and

R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\cap R(B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})=\{0\}
.
By Lemma
4.1
the operator
0
is the only positive lower
bound of the set
\mathfrak{M}=\{A,B\}
, hence
0\in\Max\mathfrak{L}

and
N(A-0)+N(B-0)=\{0\}
. Since for every

of (
5.1
), the relation

\overline{N(A-M_{T})+N(B-M_{T})}=H

is satisfied, the operator
0
differs from all
operators
M_{T}
of the form (
5.1
). Note that in the just
mentioned example all operators
M_{T}
are not positive. To explain
this, recall that the function
T\mapsto|T|
satisfies the triangle
inequality only in a weakened form,

cf.

[
8
, Thm. III.5.6]
.

Setting
T=I
, formula (
5.1
) generalizes the well known
formula for the minimum of two real numbers, and the following
continuity assertion can be derived immediately. The statement of a
similar measurability result is omitted.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
(T,\tau)
be a topological space with topology
\tau
and

\tau_{1}
be a vector topology on
\mathfrak{S}
such that the map

S\mapsto|S|
,

, is continuous. For any

(\tau,\tau_{1})
-continuous functions
\Phi:T\to\mathfrak{S}
and

\Psi:T\to\mathfrak{S}
, there exists a
(\tau,\tau_{1})
-continuous

with

M(t)\in\Max\mathfrak{L}\bigl(\{\Phi(t),\Psi(t)\}\bigr),\;t\in T.

Problem
.

Extend Corollaries
5.2
and
5.3
from a set of two
elements to an arbitrary finite set.

Taking into account (
2.3
), we can suppose that a set
\mathfrak{M}

of two operators has the form
\mathfrak{M}=\{A,0\}
for some

.
Moreover, by Corollary
4.3
it can be presumed without loss of
generality that
R(A)
is a dense subset of

or,
what is the same, that

(5.3)

N(A)=\{0\}.

The following auxiliary result was used by Stott in the matrix case to
prove Theorem 4.3 of
[
26
]
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
K
be a subspace of
H
.

(i)

If there exists
M\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
satisfying
Mu=Au
,
u\in K
, then
(Au,u)<0
for all
u\in K\setminus\{0\}
.

(ii)

If there exists
M\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
such that
K\subseteq N(M)
, then
(Au,u)>0
for all
u\in K\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

(i) Since
M\leq 0
, we have
(Au,u)\leq 0
,
u\in K
. If

u\in K
and
(Au,u)=0
, then it follows
(Mu,u)=0
, hence

u\in N(-M)=N(M)
since
-M\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. Thus

Au=Mu=0
and

by (
5.3
).

(ii) is proved in a similar way by changing the roles of
A
and
B
.
∎

Stott
[
27
]
,
cf.

[
13
]
,

[
26
]
, has thoroughly studied the set
\mathfrak{L}
if

\mathfrak{M}
is a set of two matrices. Part of his results depend on the
indefinite metric generated by the difference of these two
matrices. We give an alternative proof of one of Stott’s main results,
which, as it seems to us, points out this dependence in a more lucid
way. We refer to
[
7
]
or
[
9
]
for
information on the geometry of spaces with an indefinite metric.

Let
H=\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an
n
-dimensional Hilbert space and

\mathfrak{M}:=\{A,0\}
, where
A
is an invertible
n\times n

matrix. Clearly,
\inf\mathfrak{M}=0
or
\inf\mathfrak{M}=A
if
A
or
-A
,
resp., are positive. It remains to discuss the case that
A
has
p
,

p\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, positive and
q:=n-p
negative eigenvalues. By
(
2.4
) it is enough to study the case that
A
has a
2\times 2

block matrix representation

A=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}&0\\
0&-I_{q}\end{pmatrix}=:J

according to the orthogonal decomposition
H=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
, where
H_{1}=\mathbb{C}^{p}
,

H_{2}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
and
I_{p}
and
I_{q}
denote the

p\times p
and
q\times q
, resp., unit matrices.

Denote by
\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
the linear space of all
p\times q

matrices with complex entries. For
X\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
define
an
n\times n
matrix
S(X)
by

(5.4)

S(X):=\begin{pmatrix}I_{p}+XX^{\ast}&(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X\\
X^{\ast}(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&X^{\ast}X\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 5.5
.

The map

X\mapsto M(X):=J-S(X),\;X\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}

establishes a one-to-one correspondence between

\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
and
\Max\mathfrak{L}
.

Proof.

Step 1: For
X\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
, the matrix
M(X)
is a
maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
. Since
S(X)\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
by
Theorem
2.3
,
M(X)\leq J
. Since

M(X)=-\begin{pmatrix}XX^{\ast}&(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X\\
X^{\ast}(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&I_{q}+X^{\ast}X\end{pmatrix}

and

(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X=X(I_{q}+X^{\ast}X)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

another application of Theorem
2.3
yields
M(X)\leq 0
. To
prove that
M(X)
is a maximal element of
\mathfrak{L}
note first that

N\bigl(S(X)\bigr)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}-(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}Xx_{2}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}:x_{2}\in H_{2}\right\},

which yields

\dim N\bigl(J-M(X)\bigr)=\dim N\bigl(S(X)\bigr)\geq q.

Similarly,

N\bigl(M(X)\bigr)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
-(I_{q}+X^{\ast}X)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}X^{\ast}x_{1}\end{pmatrix}:x_{1}\in H_{1}\right\},

hence,
\dim N\bigl(M(X)\bigr)\geq p
.
Since
M(X)+S(X)=J
yields

N\bigl(M(X)\bigr)\cap N\bigl(S(X)\bigr)=\{0\},

we obtain

N\bigl(0-M(X)\bigr)+N\bigl(J-M(X)\bigr)=\mathbb{C}^{n},

and
M(X)\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
by (
4.3
).

Step 2: The correspondence
X\mapsto M(X)
is one-to-one. Let

X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
and
M(X)=M(Y)
. Comparing the left
upper corners of
M(X)
and
M(Y)
we get

XX^{\ast}=YY^{\ast}
. Since
I_{p}+XX^{\ast}
is invertible, it
follows
X=Y
from the equality of the right upper corners of
M(X)

and
M(Y)
.

Step 3: For arbitrary
M\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
, there exists
X\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
satisfying
M=M(X)
. By Lemma
5.4
the space

N(J-M)
is a negative definite subspace and the space

N(0-M)
is a positive definite subspace with respect to the
indefinite metric generated by
J
. Since

(5.5)

N\bigl(0-M\bigr)+N\bigl(J-M\bigr)=H

by (
4.3
), the space
N(0-M)
is a maximal positive
definite subspace. Let
K
be its angular operator. Recall that

N(0-M)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
Kx_{1}\end{pmatrix}:x_{1}\in H_{1}\right\}

and
K
is a contraction from
H_{1}
into
H_{2}

with the additional property that
I_{p}-K^{\ast}K
is invertible.
Setting

X:=-(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K^{\ast},

we obtain

\displaystyle(I_{p}+XX^{\ast})

\displaystyle=I_{p}+(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K^{\ast}K(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=I_{p}+(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}K^{\ast}K

\displaystyle=(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K+K^{\ast}K)=(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}

and

X^{\ast}X=K(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}K^{\ast},

hence

S(X)=\begin{pmatrix}(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}&-(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}K^{\ast}\\
-K(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}&K(I_{p}-K^{\ast}K)^{-1}K^{\ast}\end{pmatrix}.

Now it is not hard to see that for

u=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
Kx_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in N(0-M),\quad M(X)u=\bigl(J-S(X)\bigr)\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
Kx_{1}\end{pmatrix}=0,

thus

(5.6)

Mu=M(X)u,u\in N(0-M).

If
u\in N(J-M)
and
v\in N(M)
, we have

(Ju,v)=(Mu,v)=(u,Mv)=0
. It follows that
N(J-M)
is a
subspace of the
J
-orthogonal complement of
N(M)
. Since
the
J
-orthogonal complement of a maximal positive definite subspace

N(M)
is a negative definite subspace and since
N(J-M)
is a maximal negative definite subspace, we can conclude
that
N(J-M)
is the
J
-orthogonal complement of

N(M)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
Kx_{1}\end{pmatrix}:x_{1}\in H_{1}\right\},

which yields

N(J-M)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}K^{\ast}x_{2}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}:x_{2}\in H_{2}\right\}.

It is easy to see that

M(X)\begin{pmatrix}K^{\ast}x_{2}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\bigl(J-S(X)\bigr)\begin{pmatrix}K^{\ast}x_{2}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}K^{\ast}x_{2}\\
-x_{2}\end{pmatrix}

or, equivalently,
M(X)v=Jv
,
v\in N(J-M)
. It follows

(5.7)

Mv=M(X)v,\;v\in(J-M).

Finally, equality
M=M(X)
is a consequence of (
5.5
),
(
5.6
), and (
5.7
).
∎

6.
Infimum and maximal lower bounds under restrictions

The present section is mainly devoted to a generalization of
Theorem
1.2
and to an extension of Theorem
1.3
if

\dim H<\infty
. For a set
\mathfrak{M}
, define

\mathfrak{L}^{com}=\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}):=\bigl\{L\in\mathfrak{L}:AL=LA,A\in\mathfrak{M}\bigr\}.

If the greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{com})
of the set
\mathfrak{L}^{com}

exists and if
M
is a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
commuting with
all operators of
\mathfrak{M}
, obviously,
G(\mathfrak{L}^{com})=M
.
Thus, we can state the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be such that
G(\mathfrak{L}^{com})
exists. There exists at
most one maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
commuting with all operators
of
\mathfrak{M}
.
\square

Example 6.2
.

Let

\mathfrak{M}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

It is easy to see that only scalar matrices commute with both
matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
. It follows
G(\mathfrak{L}^{com})=0
. Since the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
is a
lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
, a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
commuting
with both matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
does not exist.

Deriving a generalization of Theorem
1.2
we first prove that
the greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{com})
exists if
\mathfrak{M}
is a finite
set. At the same time, our proof delivers an algorithm for computing

G(\mathfrak{L}^{com})
inductively.

Let
\mathfrak{M}:=\{A_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a set of pairwise commuting
operators. We set

(6.1)

M_{1}:=A_{1},\;M_{n+1}:=\bigl(M_{n}+A_{n+1}-|M_{n}-A_{n+1}|\bigr),\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
denote the subset
\mathfrak{M}_{n}:=\{A_{k}:k=1,\ldots,n\}
of
\mathfrak{M}
and

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n+1}:=\{M_{n},A_{n+1}\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
M_{n}
is the greatest element of

\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}_{n})
.

Proof.

From (
6.1
) it can be concluded that
M_{n}
is a function of

A_{k}
,

k=1,\ldots,n
. Since the operators of
\mathfrak{M}
commute pairwise, it
follows that
M_{n}
commutes with all operators of
\mathfrak{M}
and

M_{n+1}
commutes with
M_{n}
. Moreover, if
S\in\mathfrak{S}
commutes
with all operators of
\mathfrak{M}
, it commutes with
M_{n}
. Let us prove
the first assertion by induction over
n
. The case
n=1
is trivial,
and the case
n=2
is covered by Theorem
1.2
. It follows that

(6.2)

M_{n+1}=G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n+1})\bigr)

particularly,

(6.3)

M_{n+1}\leq M_{n}.

Since
M_{n}=G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}_{n})\bigr)
by induction assumption,
we have

M_{n+1}\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}_{n+1}).

If
L\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}_{n+1})
, then

L\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}_{n})
and
L
commutes with
A_{n+1}
. It
follows
L\leq M_{n}
, hence

L\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n+1})
and
L\leq M_{n+1}
by
(
6.2
), which yields

M_{n+1}=G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M}_{n+1})\bigr).

Theorem 6.4
.

If
\mathfrak{M}
is a set of pairwise commuting operators, the greatest
element of
\mathfrak{L}^{com}
exists.

Proof.

We can presuppose that
\mathfrak{M}
is a subset of
\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. Let

\Lambda
denote the set of finite subsets of
\mathfrak{M}
partially
ordered by the inclusion relation. For
\lambda\in\Lambda
, let

M_{\lambda}
be the greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\lambda)
,
existing according to Theorem
6.3
. The set
\mathfrak{N}:=\{-M_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}
satisfies the
conditions of Theorem
2.2
. Denote the greatest element of

\mathfrak{N}
by
-M
. From (
2.1
) and the definition of the
weak-operator topology easily follows that
-M
is an element of the
weak-operator closure of
\mathfrak{N}
. Therefore,
-M
commutes with all
operators of
\mathfrak{M}
, and we obtain
M\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M})
.
On the other hand, if
L\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M})
, then

L\in\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\lambda)
, hence
L\leq M_{\lambda}
for all

\lambda\in\Lambda
, which implies
L\leq M
. It follows that
M
is
the greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}^{com}
.
∎

From Theorems
6.1
and
6.4
the question arises,
whether for an arbitrary set
\mathfrak{M}
of pairwise commuting
operators there exists a maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
commuting
with all operators of
\mathfrak{M}
. We only have two partial answers.
Our first result generalizes Theorem 6 of
[
6
]
from a
set
\mathfrak{M}
of two commuting operators to an arbitrary finite set of
pairwise commuting operators. Our second result gives an affirmative
answer to our question if
\dim H<\infty
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}:=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}
be a finite set of pairwise
commuting operators. There exists a maximal lower bound
M
of

\mathfrak{M}
commuting with all operators of
\mathfrak{M}
. Moreover, for

j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the orthogonal complements of
N(A_{j}-M)\cap N(A_{k}-M)
in the subspaces
N(A_{j}-M)

and
N(A_{k}-M)
, resp., are orthogonal to one another.

Proof.

By a theorem of von Neuman,
cf.

[
24
, Nr. 130]
, there
exists an operator
S\in\mathfrak{S}
such that all operators of
\mathfrak{M}

are functions of
S
. If

S=\int_{a}^{b}t\,E(dt)

is a spectral representation of
S
, we can write

A_{k}=\int_{a}^{b}f_{k}(t)\,E(dt),

where
f_{k}
is a measurable real function on
[a,b]
,

k=1,\ldots,n
. Set

f(t):=\min\bigl\{f_{k}(t):k=1,\ldots,n\bigr\},\;t\in[a,b],

and

M:=\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,E(dt).

The operator
M
is a lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
and commutes with all
operators of
\mathfrak{M}
. If

\Delta_{k}:=\bigl\{t\in[a,b]:f(t)=f_{k}(t)\bigr\}

we obtain

(6.4)

N(A_{k}-M)=E(\Delta_{k})H,\;k=1,\ldots,n

which implies

\sum_{k=1}^{n}N(A_{k}-M)=H

and
M\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
by (
4.3
). The second assertion of the
theorem immediately follows from (
6.4
).
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

If
\mathfrak{M}
is a set of pairwise commuting matrices, there exists a
unique maximal lower bound of
\mathfrak{M}
commuting with all matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
.

Proof.

Since the matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
can be diagonalized simultaneously by a
unitary matrix, see
[
16
, Thm. 2.5.5]
,
according to (
2.4
) it is enough to prove the
result for a set
\mathfrak{M}
of diagonal
n\times n
matrices. Let
M
be
a diagonal matrix, whose
k
-th element on the principal diagonal is
equal to the infimum of the
k
-th elements on the principal diagonal
of the matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
. Clearly,
M\in\mathfrak{L}
and

M
commutes with all matrices of
\mathfrak{M}
. If
L\in\mathfrak{L}
and
M\leq L
, the
k
-th elements on the principal diagonals of
M
and
L

coincide. The relation
L-M\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
implies that
L
is a diagonal
matrix, hence
L=M
and
M\in\Max\mathfrak{L}
. The uniqueness property is a
consequence of Theorem
6.4
and Theorem
6.1
.
∎

We conclude the first part of the present section with a remark that
essential assertions on
\mathfrak{L}^{com}(\mathfrak{M})
seem to be not known if
the operators of
\mathfrak{M}
do not commute pairwise.

Let
\mathfrak{L}^{+}:=\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M})
denote the set

\mathfrak{L}^{+}=\mathfrak{L}\cap\mathfrak{S}^{+}
of all positive lower bounds of

\mathfrak{M}
. Since
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
is not empty if and only if
\mathfrak{M}
is a
set of positive operators, to the end of our paper we shall require that

\mathfrak{M}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. It is easy to see that
\mathfrak{L}^{+}

has the greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
if and only if there exists a
unique positive maximal element
M
of
\mathfrak{L}
and then
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})=M
.

Theorem 6.7
.

If

(6.5)

\gamma:=\inf\bigl\{(Au,u):A\in\mathfrak{M},u\in H,\|u\|=1\bigr\}>0,

the greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L})
of
\mathfrak{L}
exists if and only if the
greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
exists. In this case,
G(\mathfrak{L})=G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
.

Proof.

If
G(\mathfrak{L})
exists, it is positive, and, hence, also the greatest
element of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
. Conversely, let
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
exist. Since

\gamma I\in\mathfrak{L}^{+}
by (
6.5
),

(6.6)

G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})\geq\gamma I.

If
G(\mathfrak{L})
would not exist, we could find
L\in\mathfrak{L}
and

v\in H
with

\bigl(G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})v,v\bigr)<(Lv,v),

hence, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

(6.7)

\bigl(\bigl((1-\varepsilon)G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})+\varepsilon L\bigr)v,v\bigr)>(1-\varepsilon)\bigl(G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})v,v\bigr)+\varepsilon\bigl(G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})v,v\bigr)=\bigl(G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})v,v\bigr).

Since from (
6.6
) follows that
(1-\varepsilon)G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})+\varepsilon L\in\mathfrak{L}^{+}
for
\varepsilon

small enough, relation (
6.7
) contradicts the assumption that

G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
is the greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
.
∎

Let

Theorem 6.8
.

The greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
exists if and only
if the greatest element
G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})\bigr)

of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})
exists. In this case,

has the form

G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\\
0&G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})\bigr)\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

If
L\in\mathfrak{L}^{+}
,

[

]

. Conversely, if

it belongs to
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
. Thus, the assertion of the theorem follows immediately.
∎

Corollary 6.9
.

If
\dim K
is at most
1
, the greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}

exists.

Proof.

If
K=\{0\}
, from the first assertion of Lemma
4.1

follows
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})=0
. If
\dim K=1
, choose

H_{2}:=K
. Since the elements of

\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})
are nonnegative real numbers,

G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})\bigr)
exists, and the
result follows from Theorem
6.8
.
∎

Corollary 6.10
.

Let
A
be a positive contraction and
P
be a projection. The
greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}\bigl(\{A,P\}\bigr)
exists and is equal to

A/A_{1}
, where
H_{1}
is the orthogonal complement of

H_{2}=R(P)
.

Proof.

The assertion follows from Theorem
6.8
and the fact that

A/A_{1}
is a positive contraction if

∎

Corollary 6.11
.

If the infimum of the set
\mathfrak{M}/K^{\perp}
exists, then the
set
\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M})
has the greatest element.

Proof.

If the set
\mathfrak{M}/K^{\perp}
of positive operators has the
infimum or, equivalently, the set
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{M}/K^{\perp})

has the greatest element, it is positive and clearly the greatest
element of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/K^{\perp})
. Now
Theorem
6.8
can be applied.
∎

Theorem 6.12
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a finite subset of
\mathfrak{S}^{+}
. Let
H_{1}

be a subspace of
H
and
H_{2}
be its orthogonal
complement. If
G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})\bigr)

exists and
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
is equal to

(6.10)

G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&G\bigl(\mathfrak{L}^{+}(\mathfrak{M}/H_{1})\bigr)\end{pmatrix},

the inclusion
K\subseteq H_{2}
is satisfied.

Proof.

Let
S
be the parallel sum of the operators of
\mathfrak{M}
. Since

S\in\mathfrak{L}^{+}
and

\overline{R(S^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})}=K
, it
follows
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})u\neq 0
,
u\in K\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore, if
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
has the form
(
6.10
) according to the orthogonal decomposition
H=H_{1}\oplus H_{2}
, the space
H_{1}
is a
subspace of the orthogonal complement of
K

.
∎

Finally, let us discuss generalizations of Ando’s theorem,
cf.
Theorem
1.3
above. For
A,B\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
, denote by
A:B

its parallel sum. Following Ando
[
4
]
, we set

[A]B:=\lim_{m\to\infty}(mA):B.

Lemma 6.13
.

If
A,B\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}
, the following assertions are true:

(i)

[A]B=B
if

R(B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\subseteq R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
,

(ii)

[A:B]B=[A]B
.

Proof.

By a theorem of Kosaki
[
20
]
and
Pekarev
[
22
]
, the operator
[A]B
is equal to

[A]B=B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}P_{A,B}B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, where
P_{A,B}
is
the projection onto the closure of
\bigl\{u\in H:B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}u\in R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\bigr\}
. If

R(B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\subseteq R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
, the projection
P_{A,B}
is equal to
I

and (i) follows. Since

R\bigl((A:B)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)=R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\cap R(B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}),

it follows

\bigl\{u\in H:B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}u\in R(A^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\bigr\}=\bigl\{u\in H:B^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}u\in R\bigl((A:B)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)\bigr\},

hence
P_{A,B}=P_{A:B,B}
, which yields (ii).
∎

Theorem 6.14
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}:=\bigl\{A^{(1)},\ldots,A^{(n)}\bigr\}
be a finite subset
of
\mathfrak{S}^{+}
and
S
denote the parallel sum of the operators of

\mathfrak{M}
. If there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that

(6.11)

[S]A^{(k)}\leq[S]A^{(j)},\quad j=1,\ldots,n,

the greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
exists and is equal
to
[S]A^{(k)}
.

Proof.

The operator
[S]A^{(k)}
is positive and
S\leq A^{(j)}
,

j=1,\ldots,n
. It follows

[S]A^{(k)}\leq[S]A^{(j)}\leq[A^{(j)}]A^{(j)}=A^{(j)}

by (
6.11
), which yields
[S]A^{(k)}\in\mathfrak{L}^{+}
. If

L\in\mathfrak{L}^{+}
, we have

R(L^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\subseteq\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}R\bigl(\bigl(A^{(j)}\bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr)=R(S^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})

hence,
L=[S]L\leq[S]A^{(k)}
by Lemma
6.13
. It follows that

[S]A^{(k)}
is the greatest element of
\mathfrak{L}^{+}
.
∎

Recall that by Corollary
3.4
condition (
6.11
) is
equivalent to the fact that the set

(6.12)

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}:=\bigl\{[S]A^{(j)}\colon j=1,\ldots,n\bigr\}

has the greatest lower bound.

Taking into account Lemma
6.13
(ii), we obtain that the
preceding theorem is a generalization of the easy half of
Theorem
1.3
from a set

of two operators to an

6.14
the greatest element
G(\mathfrak{L}^{+})
of

\mathfrak{L}^{+}
exists if and only if
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}
has the greatest

.

Recall that Ando’s theorem is equivalent to the theorem of Moreland
and Gudder
[

]
if

. This

]

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
\dim H<\infty
and

\mathfrak{M}:=\bigl\{A^{(1)},\ldots,A^{(n)}\bigr\}

The ‘if’-part follows from Theorem

. Conversely, let

. If

, we have

, hence,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}=\{0\}
and

\bigl(A^{(j)}\bigr)\allowbreak=H

]

[S]A^{((j)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

according to
