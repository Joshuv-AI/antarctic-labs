Characterizing the profile transformation of Helicobacter pylori: increased secondary and multidrug resistance following failed eradication in Ningxia, China
Authors: Xiaoming Su, Yanxia Liu, Zhaorun Li, Yanhong Deng, Wenfu Dong, Lulu Li, Xilong Zhang, Shengjuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal antimicrobial resistance to Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) has escalated significantly.This study aimed to investigate the primary and secondary resistance characteristics of clinical isolates of H. pylori in Ningxia region, and to assess the impact of the number of previous eradication treatment failures on the antibiotic resistance pattern.ResultsThe primary and secondary resistance rates of H. pylori to the six antibiotics were as follows: MET (94.3% vs. 97.5%), CLA (39.8% vs. 74.6%), LEV (40.5% vs. 68.2%), AMO (0.4% vs. 2.5%), FUR (0.0% vs. 0.0%), and TET (0.2% vs. 0.0%). With increasing numbers of treatment failures, the secondary resistance rates of MET, CLA, and LEV all rose significantly, exhibiting a linear correlation (all P < 0.001). In susceptibility testing, the inhibition zone diameter (IZD) of MET consistently fell within the resistant range, while those of CLA and LEV decreased markedly from the susceptible to the resistant range (all P < 0.001). The resistance rates of AMO, TET, and FUR remained consistently low and within the susceptible range; among them, AMO showed a slight reduction in IZD previously treated patients (P < 0.001), though the change was limited. Regarding resistance patterns, Primary drug-resistant strains were predominantly characterized by single (39.9%) and double resistance (35.8%), with the most common combination being MET + CLA/LEV. As treatment failures accumulated, the rate of double drug resistance peaked after the first failure (49.6%), while the rate of multidrug resistance continued to rise—from 21.1% in the treatment-naïve group to 75.0% in the group with ≥ 3 treatment failures (P < 0.001), primarily driven by the MET + CLA + LEV combination.ConclusionsThis study reveals the significant changes pattern of drug resistance of H. pylori in Ningxia region under the background of treatment failure: the resistance rates to MET, CLA and LEV are high, and the secondary resistance increases with the number of treatment failures. The drug resistance pattern shifts from single/double resistance to multiple resistance mainly involving MET + CLA + LEV. In contrast, the resistance rates to AMO, TET and FUR are low and remain stable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05240-z.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a Gram-negative, spiral-shaped, microaerophilic pathogen in the stomach. It is closely associated with the onset and progression of chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, and gastric cancer. In 1994, the World Health Organization classified it as a class-I carcinogen, making it the only prokaryotic pathogen currently recognized as a carcinogenic agent. Eradication of H. pylori effectively treats active gastritis and peptic ulcers, delays the progression of atrophy, and reduces the risk of gastric cancer development [1–3].

However, the eradication of H. pylori remains a clinical challenge. Current guidelines recommend only a limited set of antibiotics for this purpose: metronidazole (MET), clarithromycin (CLA), levofloxacin (LEV), amoxicillin (AMO), furazolidone (FUR), and tetracycline (TET) [3]. In recent years, the diversification of eradication regimens and the extensive use of these limited antibiotics have contributed to a global increase in H. pylori antibiotic resistance, particularly against MET, CLA, and LEV. This rising resistance is regarded as the leading cause of treatment failure, driving down the efficacy of traditional standard triple therapy. Studies report that the eradication rate for standard triple therapy containing MET or CLA has fallen below 70% [4–6]. Escalating resistance poses multiple clinical challenges, including constrained therapeutic options, higher rates of treatment failure, diagnostic complexities, and significant patient distress [7, 8]. Large-sample, multi-center studies in China reveal a severe resistance profile: MET resistance ranges from 49.7% to 93.4%, CLA resistance from 22.1% to 68.0%, and LEV resistance from 19.2% to 56.0%. In contrast, resistance to AMO, FUR, and TET remains relatively low, at 0.0%–9.0%, 0.0%–1.6%, and 0.0%–15.0%, respectively. Furthermore, double resistance is a significant concern, with MET + CLA/LEV double resistance exceeding 38.4% and CLA + LEV double resistance reaching 26.1% [9–12]. Evidence indicates that antibiotic resistance in H. pylori increases following treatment failure, especially for MET, CLA, and LEV, with resistance rates rising in correlation with the number of failed treatments. Among patients with multiple treatment failures, the prevalence of double and multidrug resistance (concurrent resistance to three or more antibiotics) increases significantly [13], severely complicating eradication efforts. Given the marked regional variations in H. pylori infection rates and resistance patterns, alongside increasingly complex resistance profiles—particularly in patients with prior treatment failure—regional resistance surveillance is crucial. Understanding the local epidemiology of primary and secondary resistant strains is essential to inform evidence-based, precise treatment strategies and improve eradication rates.

The H. pylori infection rate in Ningxia is as high as 60.3%, making it a high-incidence region for chronic gastritis and gastric cancer [14]. However, systematic data on antibiotic resistance rates, mean inhibition zone distributions, and multidrug resistance patterns among populations with varying treatment histories in this region are scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to detect and analyze primary and secondary resistance, average inhibition zone diameter(IZD), and resistance patterns in clinical H. pylori isolates from Ningxia, to provide a reference for optimizing local eradication strategies.

Materials and methods

Patients and H. pylori strains

Participants who presented with upper gastrointestinal discomfort symptoms and underwent endoscopy at nine research centers, including the People’s Hospital of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, from June 2021 to June 2023 were selected. These patients were confirmed to have H. pylori infection through the 14 C-urea breath test. Gastric mucosal specimens were collected under endoscopy for in vitro H. pylori culture. Exclusion criteria included: age under 18 years; recent use (within 4 weeks) of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), bismuth compounds, antibiotics, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs); ongoing radiation or chemotherapy; severe hepatic or renal insufficiency; pregnancy or lactation; and participation in other clinical trials. All participants signed informed consent forms prior to endoscopy. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region People’s Hospital (2021SLCJ2486).

In vitro culture of H. pylori and drug sensitivity test

Clinical specimens stored in Amies transport medium (Sigma Transwab®, MWE, UK) were first inoculated onto Columbia blood agar plates (containing 5% sheep blood, Sigma-Aldrich). To inhibit contaminating bacteria, the blood agar was supplemented with vancomycin, polymyxin B, and amphotericin B (at concentrations of 1 mg/mL, 0.5 mg/mL, and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively). Inoculated plates were incubated in a microaerobic conditions (gas composition: 5% O₂, 10% CO₂, and 85% N₂) at 37 °C and 90% humidity for 4 to 5 days; incubation could be extended to 7 days if no growth was observed. Suspect H. pylori colonies were identified based on typical colony morphology, Gram staining microscopy (negative, curved or S-shaped), and positive reactions for catalase and urease.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the agar diffusion method (Kirby-Bauer). A pure culture colony was picked and prepared into a bacterial suspension at a concentration of 0.5 McFarland units using sterile saline. This suspension was evenly spread onto Columbia blood agar plates without antibiotics. Then, the discs containing the following drugs on the surface of Columbia blood agar plates inoculated with bacteria (one disc per plate): LEV (5 µg), CLA (15 µg), MET (5 µg), AMO (10 µg), TET (30 µg), and FUR (100 µg). After incubation for 48 h under the same microaeroblic conditions, the diameter of the inhibition zone around each disc was measured to determine the strain’s susceptibility to antimicrobial agents.Resistance criteria: (1) Resistant: CLA ≤ 13 mm, LEV ≤ 13 mm, AMO ≤ 14 mm, FUR ≤ 14 mm, TET ≤ 14 mm, MET ≤ 16 mm; (2) Susceptibility is defined as CLA ≥ 18 mm, LEV ≥ 17 mm, AMO ≥ 17 mm, FUR ≥ 17 mm, TET ≥ 19 mm, MET ≥ 21 mm [12]. In vitro culture and antibiotic susceptibility test of H. pylori were performed at the China Center for Hp Molecular Medicine (CCHpMM, Shanghai Xinchao Medical Laboratory). Quality control was carried out using the reference strain H. pylori ATCC 43,504, which has been certified by the CLSI M45 standard and can be used as a quality control indicator for evaluating the antibiotics sensitivity of H. pylori. The results of the simultaneous detection of this quality control strain and each batch of clinical isolates are considered valid.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the collected data were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp). Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences in the overall mean diameter of the antibacterial zone distribution across six antibiotics were compared according to the number of previous treatment failures using t-tests or ANOVA. Qualitative data were expressed as case numbers (n) and percentages (%). Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied. Linear correlation analysis assessed the linear trend between drug resistance rates and the number of prior treatment failures. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical factors of participants

A total of 3336 participants who presented to our department between June 2021 and June 2023 underwent endoscopic examination and subsequent in vitro culture for H. pylori. Of these, 1962 participants (58.8%) had positive H. pylori cultures from gastric mucosal specimens and proceeded to antibiotic susceptibility testing against six agents. Based on treatment history, participants were categorized into two groups: 1,490 participants (75.9%) with no history of prior eradication therapy (0 course) were classified as the primary resistance group; 472 participants (24.1%) who had experienced one or more prior eradication failures were classified as the secondary resistance group. This retrospective study lacked detailed antibiotic exposure data for most participants. No statistically significant differences were observed between the secondary and primary resistance groups in terms of age, endoscopic diagnosis and types of antibiotic used (P > 0.05). However, the secondary resistance group had a significantly higher proportion of female participants and greater availability of antibiotic use information, with both differences being statistically significant (P < 0.001). Primary and secondary resistance of H. pylori to different antibiotics

We compared primary and secondary resistance of H. pylori to six antibiotics. Overall, the secondary resistance rates for all other antibiotics were significantly higher than the primary resistance rates, except for FUR and TET. Primary and secondary resistance rates for H. pylori against MET, CLA, LEV, AMO, FUR, and TET were 94.3% vs. 97.5%, 39.8% vs. 74.6%, 40.5% vs. 68.2%, 0.4% vs. 2.5%, 0.0% vs. 0.0%, 0.2% vs. 0.0%, respectively. Notably, MET resistance remained extremely high in both groups (94.3% vs. 97.5%, P = 0.006). We observed a substantial increase in secondary resistance rates for CLA (from 39.8% to 74.6%, P < 0.001) and LEV (from 40.5% to 68.2%, P < 0.001). Although the absolute resistance rate for AMO was low, the secondary resistance (2.5%) was significantly higher than the primary rate (0.4%, P < 0.001). In contrast, there was no significant difference in TET resistance (0.2% vs. 0.0%, P = 1.000), and no FUR resistance was detected in any isolate. Regarding resistance patterns, while the rate of double resistance increased from 35.8% to 41.4%, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.548). However, the rates of triple resistance (20.7% vs. 53.6%) and multidrug resistance (21.1% vs. 54.4%) were markedly higher in the secondary resistance group, with both increases being highly significant (P < 0.001 for both). These findings indicate that a history of prior treatment failure is significantly associated with increased resistance rates to CLA, LEV, MET, and AMO. Table 2.

Association between antibiotic resistance patterns and number of previous treatment failures

Based on the results from Table 2, we further analyzed the relationship between resistance to various antibiotics (and resistance patterns) and the number of prior H. pylori treatment failures. Overall, resistance rates for MET, CLA, LEV, and AMO showed an increasing trend with a greater number of treatment failures, while no such trend was observed for TET or FUR. Resistance to MET was consistently high across all groups (94.3%–100.0%) and exhibited a significant increasing trend with more treatment failures (Pa = 0.005; overall P = 0.022). Both CLA and LEV resistance rates increased significantly with the number of failures (P < 0.001 for both), reaching 87.5% and 75.0%, respectively, in the group with three failures. Although the absolute AMO resistance rate was low (0.4%–5.2%), its increasing trend remained statistically significant (Pa < 0.001). No resistance to FUR was detected in any group. Resistance to TET was minimal and did not differ significantly among groups (P = 1.000). Regarding resistance patterns, the rate of single resistance to MET varied significantly across groups (overall range: 7.3%–39.9%; P < 0.001). However, this variation did not follow a linear trend (Pa = 0.636). The highest rate of MET single resistance was observed in the 0 course group (39.9%), while rates were considerably lower in groups with 1, 2, or ≥ 3 previous failures (8.8%, 12.2%, and 7.3%, respectively). The overall rate of double resistance ranged from 17.6% to 49.6%, showing significant but non-linear variation across groups (P < 0.001; Pa = 0.001). For specific combinations, the MET + CLA pattern was most prevalent in the 1 course group (32.5%), whereas the MET + LEV pattern was distributed more variably. Notably, the rate of multidrug resistance increased markedly and linearly with the number of treatment failures (P < 0.001), rising sharply from 21.1% in the 0 course group to 75.0% in the group with ≥ 3 courses. The MET + CLA+LEV pattern was the predominant multidrug resistant profile, and its trend mirrored the overall increase in multidrug resistance. These results indicate that a history of prior treatment failure, particularly multiple failures, is significantly associated with higher and more complex resistance patterns. Table 3. To further assess whether antibiotic resistance and resistance patterns in different treatment failure subgroups are influenced by previous antibiotic exposure history or treatment regimens, we conducted subgroup analyses for patients who could provide detailed antibiotic exposure history or treatment regimens (Supplementary Table 1). Among the primary resistance group (0 course), 415 patients provided antibiotic information. In the secondary resistance group, the treatment regimens were stratified based on previous treatment failures: 56 with 1 course, 39 with 2 course, and 38 with ≥ 3 course group. Regarding the antibiotic resistance rate, the trend observed in the supplementary subgroups was highly consistent with the entire population (Table 3). The CLA resistance rate showed a significant upward trend with the increase in failure times: from 44.0% in the 0 course group to 87.5% in the ≥ 3 course group (P < 0.001, Pa < 0.001); the LEV resistance rate also increased from 46.4% to 78.9% (P = 0.030, Pa = 0.003); the MET resistance rate remained at a high level (91.3%–100%) across all groups, with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.548). Notably, although AMO showed an increased resistance rate in different treatment failure groups, the differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.524). FUR and TET were not found to be resistant. Regarding the resistance patterns, in different treatment failure subgroups with clear antibiotic exposure history or treatment regimens, the single resistance rate decreased significantly with the increase in failure times (from 36.9% to 13.2%, P < 0.001), the double resistance rate first increased and then decreased (from 38.3% to 42.9% and then to 15.8%, P = 0.037), while the triple and multidrug resistance rates increased sharply with the increase in failure times: 17.6% in the 0 course group, 41.1% in the 1 course group, 53.8% in the 2 course group, and 71.7% in the ≥ 3 course group (both P < 0.001, Pa < 0.001). The above antibiotic resistance rates and resistance patterns trends were highly consistent with the data of the entire population in Table 3, further indicating that under previous antibiotic exposure, as the number of treatment failures increased, the resistance pattern changed from single/double resistance to multidrug resistance dominated by MET + CLA + LEV. The distribution of single, double and multiple drug resistance patterns classified by the number of previous treatment failures

Based on the results of Table 3, we analyzed the dynamic change process of drug resistance patterns as the number of previous treatment failures increased. As the cumulative number of treatment failures increased, the drug resistance spectrum of H. pylori showed a clear trend of systematic transformation from single and double resistance to multiple resistance patterns. In the single resistance pattern, the resistance rate of MET varied significantly among different treatment failure groups (P < 0.001), but its change did not follow a linear trend (Pa = 0.636). Table 3. Specifically, in participants without treatment (0 course group), the single resistance rate of MET was the highest (39.9%), while in participants who had at least one treatment failure, this resistance rate significantly decreased and remained at a lower level (7.3%-12.2%). In contrast, the incidence of double resistance patterns (mainly combinations of MET with CLA or LEV) reached its peak after the first treatment failure (49.6%), and then decreased significantly with the increase in the number of treatments (Pa < 0.001). In sharp contrast, the incidence of triple and multiple resistance showed a continuous and rapid linear increase (Pa < 0.001), rising from 20.7% to 21.1% in the group with 0 course group to 75.0% in the group with ≥ 3 courses group. The MET + CLA + LEV combination is the main pattern of triple resistance. Figure 1. The above data collectively indicate that with the increasing number of treatment failures, the H. pylori resistance patterns shifted from simpler single and double drug resistance to more complex, multidrug resistance.

Comparison of IZD of six antibiotics against H. pylori in treatment-naïve and previously treated patients

We compared the changes in the IZDs of six antibiotics against H. pylori isolates from treatment-naïve and previously treated patients. Overall, the eradication treatment significantly altered the in vitro sensitivity of the strains to most of the tested antibiotics. The IZDs of H. pylori for MET, CLA, LEV, AMO, FUR, and TET in treatment-naïve and previously treated patients were 2.07 mm vs. 1.49 mm, 21.35 mm vs. 9.96 mm, 17.76 mm vs. 9.77 mm, 35.02 mm vs. 33.26 mm, 39.02 mm vs. 38.65 mm, and 37.50 mm vs. 37.63 mm, respectively. The IZDs of CLA and LEV for the treatment-naïve patients were within the sensitive range, but significantly decreased to the resistant range for the patients who had treated previously, with all differences being highly statistically significant (all P < 0.001). The IZD of MET was within the resistant range in both treatment-naïve and previously treated patients, and further significantly decreased after treatment (P = 0.044). The average IZD of AMO was within the sensitive in both treatment-naïve and previously treated patients, but significantly decreased after treatment (P < 0.001). In contrast, the IZDs of FUR and TET remained within the sensitive in both treatment-naïve and previously treated patients, and the changes were not statistically significant (P = 0.376; P = 0.720). These results indicate that with increasing treatment failures, isolates with reduced susceptibility to CLA and LEV were more frequently observed, and these isolates also showed decreased susceptibility to MET and AMO, while susceptibility to FUR and TET remained stable. Comparison of the IZD of different antibiotics and the number of previous treatment failures

According to the results in Table 4, we further analyzed the trend of the distribution of the IZDs of six antibiotics with respect to the number of previous treatment failures. Overall, there are significant differences in the response patterns of the IZDs of different antibiotics to the history of treatment failure. The average IZD of LEV showed a clear downward trend with the increase in the number of treatment failures, significantly decreasing from 17.76 mm (sensitive range) in the group without previous treatment to 4.30 mm (resistant range) in the group with ≥ 3 treatment failures, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). The average IZD of CLA also decreased significantly with the increase in the number of treatments (from 21.35 mm to 9.13 mm, P < 0.001), but no strict linear decline trend was observed. In contrast, the IZD of MET remained at an extremely low level (1.17–2.08 mm, resistant range) in all groups, and there was no statistically significant difference between the groups (P = 0.203). The IZDs of AMO, FUR, and TET remained within the sensitive range in all groups. Although there were statistical differences in the comparisons between groups of AMO and FUR (P = 0.002; P = 0.003), the absolute change amplitudes were very small. The IZD of TET remained stable among all groups (P = 0.373). These results indicate that the history of treatment failure is mainly associated with a significant decrease in the in vitro susceptibility of LEV and CLA, with LEV showing a progressive decline in susceptibility as the number of treatment failures increased. Discussion

H. pylori has been identified as a definite carcinogen for gastric cancer, with a global infection rate exceeding 50% [14]. Currently, the treatment of H. pylori still mainly relies on empirical bismuth quadruple therapy, which is widely used in the first-line treatment. However, with the diversification of eradication regimens and the non-standard use of antibiotics, the resistance of H. pylori to antibiotics is increasingly severe, especially to commonly used drugs such as MET, CLA, and LEV. Antibiotic resistance is considered the primary cause of failure in eradicating H. pylori. The Ningxia region in China is a high-incidence area for chronic gastric diseases and gastric cancer, with an H. pylori infection rate of up to 60.3% [13]. If clinicians in this region can fully understand the antibiotic susceptibility characteristics and resistance patterns of H. pylori in different infection states, it will help to formulate more reasonable individualized eradication plans for participants, including those with initial treatment failure and refractory H. pylori infections. Currently, there is a lack of relevant resistance data in the Ningxia region, especially regarding the resistance information of H. pylori infections with different numbers of treatment failures. In this background, this study aims to systematically evaluate the primary and secondary resistance of H. pylori to MET, CLA, LEV, AMO, FUR, and TET, analyze the distribution of average IZDs of different antibiotics and the relationship with the number of previous treatment failures and resistance patterns, and explore the impact of the number of treatment failures on the resistance rates and resistance patterns of each antibiotic. The research results will provide a basis for formulating treatment strategies that are in line with the current infection status and resistance characteristics of the region, and also provide a reference for the re-treatment of participants with different treatment histories.

Our findings indicate that resistance rates for MET, CLA, and LEV are generally elevated, exhibiting a significant increasing trend with the number of treatment failures (P < 0.05), accompanied by a gradual reduction in average IZDs. Regarding resistance patterns, the proportions of single and double resistance decrease progressively with accumulated treatment failures, while the proportion of multidrug resistance patterns, predominantly MET + CLA+LEV, increases significantly. In contrast, AMO, TET, and FUR maintained consistently low resistance rates, demonstrating favorable stability. The high MET resistance rate may relate to the drug’s accessibility in this region and its widespread use in non-standardized eradication regimens. As a nitroimidazole antimicrobial, MET is frequently used in non-standardized H. pylori eradication therapy, particularly for periodontal disease, gynecological infections, and surgical prophylaxis. Furthermore, deficiencies persist in primary care clinicians’ selection of H. pylori treatment regimens, with traditional standard triple therapy still being employed. Particularly when antibiotic resistance information is unclear, and with MET resistance rates exceeding 90%, the use of MET-containing eradication regimens may increase the risk of treatment failure. The findings of this study are broadly consistent with multiple domestic and international studies [11, 15–19], indicating that MET resistance poses a significant challenge both regionally and nationwide. Of note, some studies have suggested that increasing the MET dose to 1600 mg/day during initial eradication therapy may partially overcome resistance and achieve favorable outcomes; however, MET should be avoided in subsequent eradication regimens [20]. The resistance situation for CLA and LEV is equally concerning. In this study, both primary and secondary resistance rates for these agents exceeded 30%, showing an upward trend with increasing episodes of treatment failure (P < 0.001) and progressively decreasing in average IZDs (P < 0.001). After just one treatment failure, the average IZDs for both CLA and LEV had already fallen within the resistant range, indicating that bacterial susceptibility had declined to clinically ineffective levels. This pattern of increasing resistance with additional treatment failures suggests that strains may exhibit higher level resistance phenotypes after multiple failed attempts. Continued use of drugs from the same class may contribute to the spread of resistant strains, further complicating subsequent treatment. This phenomenon aligns with findings from studies in Beijing, Nanjing, Ecuador, and Hungary, where secondary resistance rates to CLA and LEV increased significantly following treatment failure [11, 17, 21, 22]. Our previous findings indicate that prior use of macrolides and quinolones in populations not receiving eradication therapy is significantly associated with increased resistance rates to CLA and LEV [23]. Furthermore, multiple earlier studies have demonstrated a significant association between prior macrolide and quinolone use and resistance rates to CLA and LEV [24–26]. We further analyzed the various antibiotic resistance rates in different treatment failure subgroups of patients who could provide information on their previous antibiotic exposure history or treatment regimens. The results showed that as the number of treatment failures increased, the resistance rates of CLA and LEV significantly rose and showed a linear correlation. Our research results and those of the above studies indicate that past antibiotic exposure is mainly associated with a significant decrease in the in vitro sensitivity of LEV and CLA. In this region, CLA and LEV are frequently used to treat infections outside the gastrointestinal tract, such as respiratory and urinary tract infections, often with dosing frequencies, doses, and treatment durations that do not meet the requirements of standardized H. pylori eradication therapy. This coexistence of non-standardized treatment with high CLA and LEV resistance rates in our region suggests that prolonged exposure of gastric H. pylori to subinhibitory concentrations may contribute to resistance development, though the precise mechanisms warrant further investigation. Therefore, standardizing antibiotic use and avoiding non-standardized eradication therapy are of critical importance in controlling the spread of H. pylori resistance.

Our findings indicate a significant difference in AMO resistance rates between treatment-naïve and previously treated patients (P < 0.001), with resistance increasing with the number of treatment failures (P < 0.001). However, the IZD of AMO remained within the susceptibility range for both primary and secondary resistance, maintaining above the susceptibility threshold even after multiple treatment failures. This indicates that in the strain population of this study, completely resistant (i.e., with a high and stable resistance mechanism) strains have not yet become the absolute dominant. It is possible that the transmission efficiency of AMO resistant strains is lower than that of CLA or LEV resistant strains. Our results are broadly consistent with multiple studies [17, 19, 27]. A Beijing-based study demonstrated that even after ≥ 3 treatment failures, the secondary resistance rate to AMO rose to only 9.5%, with MIC distributions largely remaining within the susceptible range [11]. These studies indicate that AMO retains considerable stability in current clinical practice. Notably, while AMO resistance rates in this study increased with the number of treatment failures, the average IZD remained within the susceptible range. This observation carries two key clinical implications. First, given its extremely low resistance rate and high availability, AMO remains a cornerstone agent suitable for repeated use in H. pylori eradication therapy. Second, the gradually increased trend in resistance rates in itself serves as a warning signal, suggesting that repeated treatment failures may continuously drive the accumulation of AMO resistance. The underlying mechanism may involve co-selection in multidrug resistant environments. after the failure of the regimen containing AMO (such as triple or bismuth quadruple), the surviving strains are primarily resistant to CLA, LEV, or MET, but may harbor subpopulations with reduced AMO susceptibility. In the context of repeated treatment failures, the proportion of such strains may increase, contributing to a gradual decline in overall AMO susceptibility.This study observed that after the first eradication failure, resistance rates for MET, CLA, and LEV rose to 97.4%, 76.3%, and 57.9%, respectively. and the average IZDs of all three were significantly reduced and fell within the resistant range. at the same time, the IZD of AMO also showed a trend of reduction. This concurrent observation suggests that while the accumulation of CLA and LEV resistant strains is far more pronounced than that of AMO resistant strains, AMO susceptibility also shows some decline as multidrug resistance rates increase. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this decline warrant further investigation in future studies.

Our findings show that both primary and secondary resistance rates to TET were extremely low (< 5%), and no strains resistant to FUR were found. In both treatment-naïve and previously treated patients, the average IZDs of TET and FUR remained within the susceptible range, showing no statistically significant differences and no apparent correlation with the number of treatment failures. These observations are consistent with data reported in the literature. A study from Nanjing found that primary and secondary resistance rates of H. pylori to FUR and TET are both lower than 5% [17]. Similarly, research from Jiangxi reported primary and secondary resistance rates to TET of 0.35% and 1.05%, respectively, while resistance to FUR was 0.0% in both groups [27]. These data suggest that TET and FUR retain high antimicrobial activity against H. pylori in the study population, which presents favorable conditions for eradication therapy in this region. Supporting this, relevant studies have demonstrated that quadruple therapy containing AMO and FUR achieves a first-line eradication rate of 95.3% [28]. A meta-analysis further showed that FUR-containing regimens yielded superior eradication rates compared to other antibiotic regimens under identical treatment durations, with ITT analysis at 81.1% versus 75.4% and PP analysis at 86.6% versus 80.1% [29]. In addition, ITT analysis of bismuth-based quadruple therapy containing TET and AMO reported eradication rates of 82% [31]. However, it is important to note that adverse reactions associated with these regimens warrant careful consideration. One study reported a PP eradication rate of 94.7% for a 14-day bismuth-based quadruple regimen containing FUR, but the incidence of adverse reactions reached 24.8% [30]. Our previous research similarly found that quadruple therapy combining AMO with either TET or FUR, while achieving high eradication rates, was associated with a higher incidence of adverse reactions compared to regimens containing CLA or LEV [23]. FUR-related adverse events appear to correlate with dosage and treatment duration [24], whereas TET may cause hepatic and renal impairment [31]. Therefore, despite the low resistance rates and proven efficacy of FUR and TET in H. pylori eradication, the higher incidence of adverse reactions associated with these agents calls for further evaluation. Prospective clinical studies are needed to assess the risk-benefit profile of these eradication regimens in our region and to guide optimal treatment selection.

Our findings demonstrate that primary resistance rates to MET, CLA, and LEV are high, with primary resistance patterns primarily featuring MET single resistance and double resistance. Among double resistance patterns, MET + CLA and MET + LEV are the most prevalent, accounting for an overall resistance rate of 35.8%. Multidrug resistance was observed in 21.1% of cases, predominantly involving the MET + CLA+LEV combination. As the number of treatment failures accumulated, the composition of resistance patterns underwent significant changes. Following the first treatment failure, the double resistance rate rose to 49.6%, and the multidrug resistance rate increased to 41.7%, with double and multidrug resistance combined reaching 91.3%. This indicates that for most patients experiencing initial treatment failure, the range of empirical treatment options is substantially narrowed. After three treatment failures, the double resistance rate decreased to 17.6%, while the multidrug resistance rate increased to 75.0%, with the combined rate reaching 92.6%. The MET + CLA+LEV pattern alone accounted for a substantial 75% of these cases, significantly increasing the risk of subsequent rescue treatment failure. Notably, the decline in MET single resistance does not signal a reduction in the overall MET resistance problem. Rather, it reflects the integration of MET resistance into more complex multidrug resistance patterns, and this pattern change is also observed in patients who provided history of previous antibiotic exposure or treatment regimens. This observation aligns with the long-standing widespread use of MET in this region and its role as a foundational component of multidrug resistance. These findings indicate that as treatment failures accumulate, resistance patterns shift from single and double resistance toward MET + CLA+LEV-dominated multidrug resistance, a trend consistent with studies from Beijing, Nanjing, and other regions [11, 17]. In light of these resistance characteristics, the high resistance rates of MET, CLA, and LEV constrain their use in empirical therapy, particularly in the absence of susceptibility testing. In recent years, high-dose dual therapy (HDDT) regimens have garnered increasing attention due to their superior efficacy, simplified administration, and reduced adverse effects [32]. Studies have demonstrated that a 14-day HDDT regimen combining a proton pump inhibitor with AMO achieves ITT and PP eradication rates of 70.5–88.0% and 93.0–93.9%, respectively, in first-line treatment, with a low incidence of adverse reactions of 8.5% [30, 33]. AMO-based dual regimens containing vonoprazan have also shown favorable efficacy [34]. Even in patients with multiple treatment failures, eradication rates exceed 90%, making this a viable and simplified salvage therapy option [35]. Furthermore, individualized therapy based on drug susceptibility testing remains a crucial strategy. Our team’s prior research has shown that patients sensitive to CLA and LEV can still be safely and effectively treated with these agents, with low treatment failure rates [23]. Collectively, these therapeutic approaches offer multiple potential strategies for addressing treatment challenges in this region amidst a landscape of high antibiotic resistance.

Our findings reveal that refractory H. pylori infections (≥ 2 treatment failures) demonstrate high resistance rates to MET, CLA and LEV, alongside complex dual and multidrug resistance patterns. This indicates a significantly reduced pool of available antibiotics for refractory H. pylori infections, with AMO, TET, and FUR—which exhibit low resistance rates—emerging as the few remaining core therapeutic options. However, the adverse events associated with TET and FUR warrant careful consideration. Within this challenging context, rescue therapy following multiple treatment failures faces dual constraints: drug resistance and adverse events. Therefore, treatment for refractory H. pylori infections should avoid blindly repeating previously unsuccessful regimens, particularly in patients who have previously used CLA or LEV [36]. In regions with limited access to resistance testing, empirical therapy remains a viable option, though its limitation lies in the inability to provide individualised precision treatment [37]. The study reports multiple salvage regimens for refractory infections, including bismuth-based quadruple therapy with TET and FUR, probiotic adjunctive therapy, high-dose dual therapy with furoxadine, and individualised treatment based on susceptibility testing [23, 35, 38–44]. However, as a cross-sectional survey of regional resistance patterns, this study cannot directly evaluate the actual efficacy of these regimens in the local population with refractory H. pylori infection. Future prospective clinical studies incorporating local resistance characteristics are required for such assessment.

Although this study mainly relied on clinical resistance phenotype detection, resistance of H. pylori to different antibiotics is often closely associated with mutations at specific gene loci. Resistance to CLA is mainly associated with point mutations in the V region of the 23 S rRNA gene, particularly the A2143G mutation. A multicenter study based on 4,242 clinical isolates from five regions in China showed that the CLA resistance genotype was predominantly the mutation at the 2143rd base of the 23 S rRNA gene, with a Kappa consistency coefficient of 0.810 between this mutation and the resistance phenotype [12]. A study in Zhejiang Province also indicated that point mutations in the 23 S rRNA gene were sufficient to predict CLA resistance [45]. Previous studies in this region showed that the Kappa consistency coefficient between the A2143G mutation and the CLA resistance phenotype was 0.809, and the mutation rate of A2143G in secondary resistant strains was 100% [46]. In this study, the secondary resistance rate to CLA (74.2%) was significantly higher than the primary resistance rate (39.8%), and it increased with the number of treatment failures, which might be related to the increased frequency of the A2143G mutation after multiple eradication treatments. Resistance to LEV is mainly associated with amino acid substitutions in the quinolone resistance-determining region of the gyrA gene (N87K, N87I, D91G, D91N, etc.). A multicenter study reported a Kappa consistency coefficient of 0.782 between the levofloxacin resistance genotype and phenotype [12]. A study in Ningxia also demonstrated good consistency between gyrA-related mutations and the LEV resistance phenotype, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.830 [46]. Another study in Shandong reported a correlation between gyrA mutations (N87K/N87I/D91G/D91Y/D91N) and LEV resistance, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.526 [47]. In this study, the secondary resistance rate to LEV (64.1%) was significantly higher than the primary resistance rate (40.5%), which might be related to the increased frequency of gyrA gene mutations after multiple treatment failures. The mechanism of MET resistance is relatively complex. It is closely linked to loss of function of the rdxA gene, which encodes an oxygen-insensitive NADPH nitroreductase responsible for converting the prodrug MET into its active form, point mutations in rdxA accounted for 83.6% of MET resistance, with premature termination of rdxA translation being the predominant inactivating form (51.8%) [48]. A study found that most MET-resistant strains carried truncations in the rdxA and frxA genes, and the Kappa coefficient between rdxA truncation and the resistant phenotype was 0.72 [47]. A whole‑genome sequencing study by our research team revealed that rdxA truncation mutations and the R16H/C substitution had a specificity of 100% for the MET-resistant phenotype but a relatively low sensitivity (approximately 31.5%), suggesting that in that study cohort, a single genotype did not fully correspond to the phenotype, and other mechanisms were involved [49]. Furthermore, a whole-genome sequencing study reported a low resistance rate to AMO but noted that mutations in the pbp1A gene had a limited correlation with AMO resistance, TET resistance is mainly associated with mutations at the AGA926-928 site of the 16 S rRNA gene [12]. In the Shandong study, only a few strains were resistant to TET, and no 16 S rRNA-related mutations were detected [47]. The resistance mechanism of FUR remains unclear, and the known mutation sites in the porD and oorD genes show low consistency with the resistant phenotype [49], in addition, the A1378G mutation in the HP0399 gene that may be associated with TET resistance, and an A149G mutation in the FabH gene that may be associated with multidrug resistance (resistance to three or more antibiotic classes) [49]. A recent study in Ningxia also suggested a link between biofilm-forming ability and multidrug resistance [50], providing new clues for further elucidation of resistance mechanisms. The predominance of the MET + CLA+LEV multidrug resistance pattern in this study is consistent with the high prevalence of the corresponding resistance-conferring mutations (A2143G for CLA, gyrA mutations for LEV, and rdxA inactivation for MET) in the local population, as reported previously [46, 49].

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the determination of H. pylori resistance relied on the disk diffusion method, without verification using the standard agar dilution method, and the reliability of its results requires further confirmation. Secondly, the region currently lacks a comprehensive antibiotic usage monitoring and traceability system. Past medication history primarily relies on patient self-reporting, with incomplete medication information present for some patients (particularly those with multiple treatment failures), introducing recall bias. Concurrently, the small sample size in the multiple treatment failure subgroup may impact the stability of the results. Thirdly, this study did not evaluate the actual eradication efficacy or safety of salvage treatment regimens for resistant (particularly multidrug resistant) strains, nor did it explore underlying resistance mechanisms or strain transmission characteristics in depth. This study mainly described the phenotypic characteristics of H. pylori drug resistance, but did not conduct in-depth analysis of the potential molecular mechanisms of drug resistance. Further research in this area will be carried out in the future. Furthermore, as a cross-sectional study, it cannot directly demonstrate temporal changes in resistance rates with increasing treatment failures; the association between antimicrobial susceptibility results and clinical treatment outcomes also requires validation in subsequent studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals the significant changes pattern of drug resistance of H. pylori in Ningxia region under the background of treatment failure. Resistance to MET, CLA, and LEV was notably high, with secondary resistance rates increasing significantly with the number of treatment failures. As failures accumulated, resistance profiles evolved from simple single or double resistance toward complex multidrug resistance, predominantly the MET + CLA+LEV pattern. Resistance to CLA and LEV rose sharply, while MET resistance remained consistently high throughout. In contrast, AMO, TET, and FUR maintained low and stable resistance rates. Further research and continuous optimisation of precision treatment strategies for H. pylori infection in this region remain essential.
