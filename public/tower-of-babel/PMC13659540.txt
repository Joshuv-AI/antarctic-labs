Photon-counting CT-guided precise subtotal resection of pancreatic head benign tumor with complete protection of pancreaticoduodenal arcades
Authors: Jiashu Han, Mengwei Wu, Jingyi Liu, Liang Zhu, Weibin Wang, Chen Lin
Published: 2026

The pancreatic head and duodenum form a complex anatomical unit with shared vascular supply, presenting unique challenges for surgical intervention in benign diseases. Traditional pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) remains the gold standard for malignant tumors but entails extensive resection and digestive tract reconstruction, often leading to long-term functional impairments in benign conditions. For patients with benign pancreatic head tumors [e.g., solid pseudopapillary tumors (SPTs), neuroendocrine tumors] and chronic pancreatitis, organ-preserving resection techniques—such as subtotal pancreatic head resection—have emerged to balance oncological adequacy with preservation of gastrointestinal and pancreatic function.

Organ-preserving resection for pancreatic head masses has evolved since the introduction of pylorus-preserving pancreatoduodenectomy (PPPD) in 1970. The general principle involves resecting 80–90% of the pancreatic head and pancreatic tissue around the common bile duct, while preserving a 5-mm-thin layer of pancreatic tissue on the dorsal side of the pancreatic head adjacent to the duodenum and part of the medial uncinate process.

The critical challenge in such surgeries is maintaining the pancreaticoduodenal arterial arcade. Disruption of this arcade risks duodenal ischemia, necrosis, or fistula formation, underscoring the need for precise vascular preservation (1).

The vascular anatomy of the pancreatic head-duodenal region is complex. The gastroduodenal artery (GDA) gives rise to the superior pancreaticoduodenal artery (SPDA), while the inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery (IPDA) originates from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). Their anterior and posterior branches (A/PSPDA-A/PIPDA) anastomose to form the anterior and posterior vascular arcades in the descending and horizontal segments of the duodenum, supplying blood to the entire duodenum, papilla, bile duct, as well as branches to the pancreatic head and uncinate process.

Beger’s approach preserves the anterior superior pancreaticoduodenal artery (ASPDA) (2), whereas the modified duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection (DPPHR) (3) transects the ASPDA and gastroepiploic artery, instead relying on the blood supply provided by the posterior vascular arcade formed by the posterior superior pancreaticoduodenal artery (PSPDA) and posterior inferior pancreaticoduodenal artery (PIPDA) (4).

Advances in imaging, such as photon-counting computed tomography (PCCT), now enable three-dimensional visualization of vascular anatomies and variant patterns, facilitating personalized preoperative planning. Here, we report a case of a 33-year-old male with a pancreatic head SPT who underwent PCCT-guided subtotal pancreatic head resection with complete preservation of the pancreaticoduodenal arcade. This case highlights the combined utility of endoscopic stenting, advanced imaging, and surgical techniques in optimizing outcomes for benign pancreatic head lesions.

A 33-year-old male was found to have a pancreatic head lesion during a routine screening physical examination via abdominal ultrasound, with no symptoms of abdominal pain, distension, or jaundice. Tumor markers and laboratory test results were normal, and there was no significant past medical history.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound and abdominal contrast-enhanced CT were completed. Imaging revealed an irregular 4.1 cm lesion in the pancreatic head, with relatively clear boundaries but an irregular shape. It showed hypoenhancement in the arterial phase, multiple cystic non-enhanced areas, and multi-septal delayed enhancement in the delayed phase. The superior mesenteric vein was slightly narrowed due to compression. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration was performed, and pathology confirmed a SPT.

SPT is a borderline neoplasm with low malignant potential, carrying risks of local invasion and distant metastasis (e.g., liver, peritoneum). Surgical resection is the primary treatment. Options for pancreatic head SPT include localized tumor enucleation, DPPHR, and pancreaticoduodenectomy. Enucleation is feasible when the tumor is encapsulated, well-demarcated, superficial, or exophytic without vascular/visceral involvement. In this case, the tumor was located within the pancreatic parenchyma, large, and adjacent to major vessels. Given the tumor’s biological behavior, favorable prognosis, and the patient’s QoL expectations, aggressive resection was avoided, but conservative management was also deemed inappropriate. A DPPHR was chosen to balance oncological safety with organ function preservation.

To optimize preservation of the duodenal, papillary, and biliary blood supply, the patient underwent preoperative endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST), accessory papilla cannulation, placement of a 3-cm single pigtail pancreatic stent, and an 8.5-Fr×7-cm biliary “Christmas tree” stent. Concurrently, high-resolution photon-counting CT with vascular reconstruction was performed to delineate the anterior and posterior pancreaticoduodenal arcades, precisely map the anatomy and course of the SPDA, and identify the origin of the anterior/posterior branches of the IPDA from the SMA (Figure 1, Videos 1-3). Using the superior border of the horizontal duodenum as a fixed anatomical reference, distances from the IPDA origin were measured to guide surgical planning.

Intraoperative techniques prioritized vascular preservation and precision, the CHA was dissected free, and after fully exposing the GDA, its root was temporarily clamped with a vascular bulldog clamp to reduce bleeding during pancreatic head resection and distend the arterial arcade. During resection along the tumor’s posterior border, the biliary stent served as a key reference, combined with intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) angiography to mobilize the CBD while preserving the ASPDA, PSPDA, and small vessels supplying the CBD. Regarding the pancreatic duct, it was transected during the present procedure, followed by a conventional pancreaticojejunostomy. The right peritoneal layer of the SMA was incised for exposure, and distances measured by preoperative PCCT guided protection of the AIPDA, PIPDA, and JV1.

Despite inflammatory exudation, friable pancreatic tissue prone to bleeding, and proximity between the tumor and CBD/SMV. Both anterior/posterior pancreaticoduodenal arterial arcades were preserved intact during en bloc removal of the pancreatic head, uncinate process, and tumor. Post-resection, both arcades demonstrated restored blood flow, satisfactory duodenal perfusion, normal peristalsis, and no signs of ischemia. Time of surgery was 3 hours and estimated blood loss was approximately 100 mL.

The patient had an uncomplicated postoperative course. Guided by enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, enteral nutrition was initiated on postoperative day (POD) 1, and the abdominal drainage tube was removed POD 5 following negative amylase levels. The patient demonstrated normal duodenal motility on imaging, with no evidence of ischemia or anastomotic leakage, and no complaints suggesting digestive and GI motility abnormality. He was discharged POD 7 with stable vital signs, adequate oral intake, and no complications. Pathological analysis confirmed complete resection with negative margins, consistent with SPT.

Organ-preserving pancreatectomies are extremely challenging. In terms of technical operation, Kocher’s maneuver may increase the risk of damaging blood supply from the posterior peritoneum. The PSPDA and its branches run between the common bile duct and duodenum, making them prone to injury during dissection of the pancreatic head-bile duct.

Anatomical variations of the vascular arcade also exist among different patients (6,7). The SPDA may directly arise from the common hepatic artery or proper hepatic artery instead of the GDA (8); the IPDA may originate from variant branches of the SMA (such as the accessory right colic artery), share a common trunk with the first jejunal artery, or even arise directly from the abdominal aorta. The anastomotic points of the vascular arcade may shift upward to the upper part of the descending duodenum or downward to the horizontal part. Even with careful surgery and adequate preservation, there are differences in the branches emitted by the vascular arcade and the richness of anastomoses in the formed vascular network. The degree of vascular sparsity directly affects ischemic sensitivity; in 10–15% of patients, the anterior or posterior vascular arcade may lack anastomotic branches and be supplied by a single arterial branch alone. Whether the preserved vessels and network can provide sufficient blood supply needs to be individually evaluated for each patient.

PCCT has revolutionized pancreatic surgical planning through its submillimeter spatial resolution and quantum-level spectral analysis. Traditional CT struggles to visualize small arterial branches (e.g., tertiary and distal abdominal arterial divisions) due to spatial resolution limitations, while its contrast resolution constraints hinder venous system assessment (9). By contrast, PCCT using ultra-high resolution (UHR) mode (0.2 mm slice thickness) with multi-material decomposition algorithms enables highly consistent inter-observer agreement in evaluating anatomical variations of abdominal vascular trunks and secondary branches. It can even identify <1 mm pancreatic microvasculature—unnamed branches of unclear pathological significance—previously invisible on conventional imaging (10).

Preoperative PCCT plays a critical role in individualizing surgical planning for organ‑preserving pancreatectomy, rather than relying on a standardized surgical protocol regardless of anatomical variations. It enables precise preoperative quantification of key anatomical metrics, such as the distance from the duodenal upper border to the IPDA branching point from the SMA, which closely matches intraoperative anatomical findings and improves the predictability of dissection planes. Furthermore, intra‑operative real‑time correlation of PCCT images with robotic surgical visualization functions as an intuitive anatomical roadmap, helping surgeons identify critical vascular landmarks and avoid iatrogenic vascular injury. Such high‑resolution anatomical mapping is particularly valuable given the high prevalence of vascular variations in the pancreaticoduodenal arcade, and effectively facilitates precise, safe dissection under robotic guidance.

Organ-preserving pancreatic resection for benign pancreatic head diseases and pancreatitis requires precise balance between lesion eradication and functional preservation, challenged by complex anatomy, interindividual vascular variations, and high risks of ischemic complications from vascular injury. This case exemplifies the technical demands of DPPHR, where severe local adhesions and close tumor-CBD association posed unique challenges. By integrating PCCT-guided vascular mapping, preoperative stent placement, and intraoperative vascular preservation strategies, we achieved intact preservation of both anterior and posterior pancreaticoduodenal arcades. The technological advancement of PCCT, enabling ultra-high-resolution anatomical visualization and detailed vascular imaging, was pivotal in formulating personalized surgical plans. This approach underscores the principle of "less is more"—minimizing unnecessary resection while ensuring oncological safety—through synergistic integration of advanced imaging, preoperative planning, and refined surgical technique.
