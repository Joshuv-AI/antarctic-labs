Shared urinary metabolomic signatures as potential biomarkers for early intestinal Schistosoma infection across multiple species
Authors: Peerut Chienwichai, Phornpimon Tipthara, Joel Tarning, Yanin Limpanont, Kanthi Tanasanprasert, Nuttapohn Kiangkoo, Tipparat Thiangtrongjit, Poom Adisakwattana, Onrapak Reamtong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchistosomiasis is a parasitic disease with a high global burden. Early diagnosis of intestinal schistosomiasis remains challenging because the current diagnostic methods do not detect early-stage infection. The application of metabolomics is a promising strategy for identifying biomarkers of host-parasite interactions. In this study, we applied untargeted metabolomic profiling to investigate potential urinary biomarkers of early infection with three intestinal species of Schistosoma using a mouse model. Urine samples were collected at multiple time-points post-infection and analyzed using mass spectrometry-based metabolomic methods. More than 23,000 metabolic features were detected. Differential metabolite screening was performed using statistical and machine-learning approaches, including fold-change, adjusted P-value, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant, Random Forest, and receiver operating characteristic analyses. Through this integrated workflow, 71 significant features were identified. Two putative compounds (2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01) demonstrated strong discriminatory performance during the early stages of infection. The level of 2-hydroxyglutaric acid showed a declining trend from one week post-infection and was significantly reduced from two weeks onward. This pattern aligned with linear mixed-effects modeling, which accounted for inter-individual variability. In contrast, NCGC00380820-01 exhibited an increasing trend following infection and was significantly different in the infected mice compared to the control mice from four weeks post-infection. Our findings indicate that 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01 are promising biomarkers of early-stage intestinal schistosomiasis and provide a foundation for the development of non-invasive diagnostic tools for the early detection of this neglected disease.Keywords: Intestinal schistosomiasis, Schistosoma spp., Metabolomics, Urinary biomarker, Early detection

Introduction

Schistosomiasis is a parasitic disease that creates a substantial global health burden. It is caused by Schistosoma spp. blood flukes and occurs in two major clinical forms: intestinal and urogenital schistosomiasis. The intestinal form is primarily caused by S. mansoni and S. japonicum, as well as S. mekongi, S. intercalatum, and S. guineensis. In contrast, urogenital schistosomiasis is caused exclusively by S. haematobium (McManus et al., 2018). An estimated 800 million people, particularly children, are at risk of schistosomiasis, with approximately 250 million cases worldwide, primarily in Africa, Central and South America, and Asia (Anisuzzaman and Tsuji, 2020). Despite ongoing control efforts, the disease remains a major public health burden, accounting for approximately 1.43 million disability-adjusted life years and 280,000 deaths annually in sub-Saharan Africa alone (McManus et al., 2018).

In intestinal schistosomiasis, adult flukes reside in the mesenteric blood vessels and release eggs that are excreted in feces. However, many eggs become trapped in host tissues, particularly the liver, where they induce inflammatory responses and cause symptoms such as right upper quadrant abdominal pain, diarrhea, fatigue, and malaise (McManus et al., 2018; Wilson, 2020). In contrast, S. haematobium adult flukes inhabit the venous plexus of the urinary bladder, and their eggs are excreted in urine. Eggs can become trapped in the bladder wall, leading to pelvic pain, dysuria, hematuria, and an increased risk of bladder cancer (McManus et al., 2018; Santos et al., 2021). Detecting Schistosoma eggs in feces or urine is the gold-standard diagnostic method for intestinal or urogenital schistosomiasis, respectively; however, its sensitivity is often below 50%, particularly in low-prevalence and low-intensity settings (McManus et al., 2018; Rotejanaprasert et al., 2023). In addition, pathological changes may occur before eggs become detectable in clinical samples. Therefore, more sensitive methods capable of detecting early-stage infections are urgently needed.

Parasite-derived proteins have been explored as biomarkers of schistosomiasis. Point-of-care diagnostic assays detecting circulating cathodic antigen and circulating anodic antigen, two glycoproteins secreted by Schistosoma species into the bloodstream that can be detected as early as two weeks post-infection (w.p.i.) (Van ‘t Wout et al., 1995). However, false-positive results and relatively high costs limit their widespread implementation, particularly in resource-limited settings (van Dam et al., 2013; Ogongo et al., 2018). Circulating parasite DNA has also shown promise, with S. japonicum DNA detectable in serum as early as three days post-infection using nested PCR and at one w.p.i. using mitochondrial DNA targets, even in light infections (Xu et al., 2017; Ullah et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the clinical adoption of circulating cell-free DNA-based diagnostics faces several challenges, including high costs, lack of standardization, and the requirement for specialized technical expertise (Osei-Poku et al., 2024). Given these limitations, there are ongoing research efforts to identify novel, sensitive, and cost-effective biomarkers of early-stage schistosomiasis.

In addition to proteins and genetic material, metabolites are another promising source of biomarkers. Globisch et al. (2013) profiled metabolites to screen for potential biomarkers of Onchocerca volvulus infection in human patients and identified N-acetyltyramine-O-glucuronide, a neurotransmitter-derived metabolite secreted by the parasite into the host circulation, as a specific biomarker for onchocerciasis. This discovery was subsequently translated into a point-of-care lateral flow assay with 85% sensitivity, which was capable of distinguishing active infections from past infections and outperformed conventional immunological methods (Shirey et al., 2018). This work highlights the potential of metabolomics for biomarker discovery and the downstream development of clinically applicable diagnostic tools. In the context of schistosomiasis, multiple studies have demonstrated that host biofluid metabolomes are valuable sources of potential biomarkers, with many metabolic alterations occurring prior to classical parasitological detection. Hu et al. (2020) conducted metabolomic analyses of blood and urine samples from S. japonicum-infected mice and reported that metabolic profiles were altered before the onset of parasite egg production. They proposed several candidate biomarkers, including the blood metabolites phosphatidylcholine and colfosceril, as well as the urinary metabolites xanthurenic acid and naphthalenesulfonic acid. Similarly, Huang et al. (2020) identified 17 serum metabolites in S. japonicum-infected mice at 1 w.p.i. and demonstrated correlations between disease progression and metabolite levels (e.g. diphenol glucuronide and glycerol tribenzoate levels). Furthermore, in a previous study, we reported the serum metabolomic profiles of S. mekongi-infected mice and identified early biomarkers of Mekong schistosomiasis, such as heptadecanoyl ethanolamide, picrotin, and theophylline, which were detectable at 2 w.p.i (Chienwichai et al., 2022). Although these studies successfully identified promising metabolite biomarkers, each focused on infection with a single species of Schistosoma. Given that intestinal schistosomiasis is caused by five different Schistosoma spp., broader comparative studies that encompass multiple species are needed to identify robust, cross-species metabolite biomarkers with wide diagnostic applicability.

To date, only one study has incorporated more than one species of Schistosoma into the biomarker discovery process (Chienwichai et al., 2024). In that study, we performed fecal metabolomic profiling to identify biomarkers of intestinal schistosomiasis by screening for commonly disrupted metabolites shared among mice infected with S. mansoni, S. japonicum, and S. mekongi. We identified alterations in several fecal metabolites, including 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 and 1α-hydroxy-2β-(3-hydroxypropoxy) vitamin D3, as early as 1 w.p.i., highlighting the feasibility of identifying metabolite biomarkers applicable to multiple Schistosoma species.

Urine is a biofluid rich in metabolites that has traditionally been used for the diagnosis of urogenital schistosomiasis (Adebayo et al., 2018; Midzi et al., 2024), and several studies have demonstrated that urine samples are also useful for the detection of biomarkers of intestinal schistosomiasis (Garcia-Perez et al., 2010; Balog et al., 2011).

Building on this evidence, we aimed to identify urinary metabolite biomarkers of intestinal schistosomiasis that are present prior to classical parasitological detection using a metabolomics-based approach. To achieve this aim, we first divided nine mice into three groups destined for S. mansoni, S. japonicum, and S. mekongi infection. Urine samples were collected from each mouse before infection and at 1, 2, 4, and 8 w.p.i. to monitor temporal changes in their urinary metabolite profiles. Metabolites were extracted and analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). All data were subsequently integrated, and a biomarker discovery pipeline was applied to identify shared metabolic signatures. The screened markers were verified for inter-individual variability using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM). Our findings have implications for the development of robust multi-species schistosomiasis biomarkers, which will enhance early diagnosis and reduce the overall health burden associated with schistosomiasis.

Materials and methods

Animal husbandry, infection, and sample collection

Eight-week-old female ICR mice were obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center, Mahidol University, and maintained in controlled environmental conditions at the Animal Care Unit, Faculty of Tropical Medicine, Mahidol University, for the duration of the study. The mice were divided into three groups of three animals. The mice in each group were infected with 25–30 cercariae of S. mansoni, S. japonicum, or S. mekongi, respectively, via abdominal skin exposure. Infection was verified using modified Kato-Katz techniques. Urine samples were collected directly after voiding. Contamination with feces, bedding, or other materials was avoided during urine collection. Samples were collected longitudinally from the same mice before infection, which served as pre-infection control, and subsequently at 1, 2, 4, and 8 w.p.i. All urine samples were immediately stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Metabolite extraction

All urine samples were processed in a single batch, following the protocol described by Lu et al. (2014). In brief, 20 μl of urine was mixed with 80 μl of cold methanol and vortexed vigorously for 1 min. The mixture was then incubated at 4 °C for 20 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and dried using a speed vacuum concentrator (Tomy Digital Biology, Tokyo, Japan). The dried metabolite extracts were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Mass spectrometry

The metabolomic analysis was performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography instrument (Agilent 1260 Quaternary Pump, Agilent 1260 High-Performance Autosampler, and Agilent 1290 Thermostatted Column Compartment SL, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) coupled to an electrospray ionization (ESI) quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Q-TOF-MS; TripleTOF 5600+, SCIEX, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a C18 reversed-phase column (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters, MA, USA) fitted with a guard column (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3, 2.1 × 5 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters). The mobile phases consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B).

The dried metabolite extracts were reconstituted in 200 μl of a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of mobile phases A and B and transferred to LC vials, which were maintained at 6 °C in the autosampler. A 5 μl aliquot was injected at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min, with the column temperature set at 40 °C. The gradient elution program was as follows: 5% B for 2.0 min (0.0–2.0 min), 5–60% B for 0.5 min (2.0–2.5 min), 60–80% B for 1.5 min (2.5–4.0 min), 80–100% B for 8.0 min (4.0–12.0 min), 100% B for 5.0 min (12.0–17.0 min), 100–5% B for 0.1 min (17.0–17.1 min), and re-equilibration at 5% B for 2.9 min (17.1–20.0 min).

The Q-TOF-MS system was operated in both positive and negative ESI modes. Ion source gas 1, gas 2, and curtain gas were set to 45, 40, and 30 psi, respectively, with the source temperature maintained at 450 °C. The ion spray voltage was set at 5500 V in positive mode and at −4500 V in negative mode. Data-dependent acquisition was employed, consisting of a TOF-MS survey scan followed by 10 dependent product ion scans in high-sensitivity mode with dynamic background subtraction. The collision energy and collision energy spread were set to 30 V and 15 V, respectively. The MS1 scan range was m/z 100–1000, and the MS2 scan range was m/z 50–1000. Data acquisition and processing were performed using Analyst software version 1.7 (SCIEX). QC samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots of all metabolite extracts and were injected before, during, and after the analytical run to monitor system stability and performance.

Metabolite annotation and identification

The LC-MS/MS raw data files (.wiff and.wiff.scan) were converted to Analysis Base File (.abf) format using the Reifycs Abf Converter (Reifycs, Tokyo, Japan). The metabolites were annotated using the MS-DIAL platform developed by Tsugawa et al. (2015). MS-DIAL version 5.5 was used, with the parameter settings optimized for soft ionization and chromatographic separation. Data were acquired using a data-dependent metabolomics approach, enabling the collection of both MS1 and MS/MS spectra. The mass accuracy tolerances were set to 0.01 Da for MS1 and 0.025 Da for MS2. Peak detection was carried out with a minimum intensity threshold of 1000 and a mass slice width > 0.1 Da. The sigma window for spectral deconvolution was set to 0.5, with no cut-off applied for MS/MS abundance.

Metabolites were identified by searching comprehensive public MS/MS metabolite databases that comprised 326,575 records in positive ion mode and 53,337 records in negative ion mode. The retention time tolerance was set to 100 min, while the accurate mass tolerances were maintained at 0.01 Da for MS1 and 0.025 Da for MS2. Common adduct ions were included in the search criteria. For peak alignment, the retention time and MS1 mass tolerances were set to 0.1 min and 0.015 Da, respectively.

Data preprocessing and exploratory analysis

To identify biomarkers of infection with different Schistosoma species, data from S. mansoni, S. japonicum, and S. mekongi were integrated according to the duration of infection to generate a unified dataset representing Schistosoma spp. infection. The annotated metabolomic data were subsequently analyzed using the MetaboAnalyst online platform (version 6.0) (Pang et al., 2024). Preprocessing and analyses were conducted using the Statistical Analysis [One Factor] module. The metabolomic features were first filtered based on the interquartile range using the median peak intensity values. The data were then normalized, transformed, and scaled using quartile normalization, cube-root transformation, and range scaling, respectively. To assess the robustness of the metabolite annotation, all samples (including the QC samples) were subjected to PCA. Dataset quality was confirmed by the clustering of the QC samples at the center of the PCA score plot (Godzien et al., 2015). Only datasets that met this criterion were included in subsequent analyses.

For the exploratory analysis, PLS-DA was performed to evaluate the separation between the urinary metabolomic profiles of the control and infected mice. The data were normalized, transformed, and scaled as mentioned earlier and fed to the algorithm. Leave-one-out cross-validation was conducted to evaluate the performance of the model. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated to visualize metabolic alterations across disease stages using Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage method. To identify metabolomic features exhibiting substantial changes following infection, differentially altered putative metabolites were identified based on a fold change of ≥ ±2 relative to the control mice and an adjusted P-value of < 0.05. These metabolites were visualized using volcano plots, while the duration-specific metabolite distributions were further illustrated using UpSet plots (Khan and Mathelier, 2017). The metabolites were classified by querying the metabolite classes in the PubChem database (Kim et al., 2025). The metabolites that were significantly altered at each post-infection time-point were identified, and their class distributions were visualized using pie charts.

Biomarker selection

To identify the metabolites with potential biomarker value, oPLS-DA and Random Forests (RF) modeling were performed to calculate VIP scores and MDA values, respectively. The oPLS-DA technique is widely used in metabolomics to screen potential biomarkers of infectious diseases (Zhou et al., 2015; Cui et al., 2016; Osakunor et al., 2020). Candidate biomarkers were identified based on their VIP scores (> 1) and top-right and bottom-left positions in the S-plot, which represent variables with high covariance and correlation contributing to class separation (Wiklund et al., 2008). RF modeling is frequently used to identify metabolomic biomarkers (Nguyen et al., 2015; Depke et al., 2020; Anh et al., 2024). For the RF model, the number of trees to grow (ntree) was set to 500, and the number of predictors to try (mtry) was set to seven with randomness on. To increase the robustness of metabolite selection, the results from multiple analytical approaches were integrated. Metabolites were considered candidate biomarkers if they satisfied all of the following criteria: (i) significant differential abundance (fold change ≥ ±2 and adjusted P < 0.05), (ii) VIP score > 1 from oPLS-DA, and (iii) a positive MDA value from RF. The candidates that passed these criteria were then evaluated for their discriminatory performance between the control samples and each infection time-point using the Biomarker Analysis module in MetaboAnalyst. Metabolites with an AUROC value > 0.8 were deemed common biomarkers of Schistosoma infection.

Biomarker verification

To account for the longitudinal study design and repeated measurements collected from the same mice, the abundance of candidate biomarkers was further evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM). The biomarkers’ preprocessed abundances were analyzed with time-points included as a fixed effect and mouse identity included as a random intercept to account for within-mouse correlations and inter-individual variability. The models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood. Inter-mouse variability was quantified using the ICC. Results were considered significant when P was < 0.05. All analyses were performed in Python using the statsmodels package.

Results

Following parasite exposure, the mice were monitored regularly, and fecal samples were examined for the presence of parasite eggs to determine the period of early infection. Parasite eggs were first detected at 8 w.p.i. Hence, the period from 1 to 4 w.p.i. was defined as the early stage of infection. No mice developed clinical symptoms throughout the experiment.

The quality of the metabolomic data was evaluated to ensure the reliability of the MS system. The predefined criterion for acceptable system performance was tight clustering of the quality control (QC) samples in the principal component analysis (PCA) plots. All QC samples (yellow dots) clustered closely together in both the positive and negative ionization modes (Supplementary Fig. S1A and C). In addition, the relative standard deviation (RSD) values in both ionization modes were < 20% (Supplementary Fig. S1B and D), indicating low analytical variability. Collectively, these results indicated good instrumental stability and reliable metabolite detection, thereby supporting the robustness of the subsequent analyses.

Our multivariate analysis, which was performed using partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), revealed that the samples taken at different time-points were separated (Fig. 1A). The control samples (red dots) were positioned on the left side of the score plot, whereas the post-infection samples progressively shifted to the right. The samples collected 1 w.p.i. (green dots) and 2 w.p.i. (purple dots) showed partial overlap with the control samples, while the samples collected 4 w.p.i. (blue dots) and 8 w.p.i. (pink dots) were clearly separated from the control samples. This pattern indicates a gradual disruption of the urinary metabolite profile over the course of infection. The PLS-DA model demonstrated good performance (R2 = 0.9869, Q2 = 0.7506), supporting the reliability of the observed group discrimination. Furthermore, we examined the top 100 metabolic features using hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis (Fig. 1B). The heatmap revealed distinct temporal changes in metabolite abundance across the infection stages, with the most pronounced differences observed between the control samples and those collected 8 w.p.i., the same time-point at which parasite eggs were first detected in fecal samples. This suggests that metabolomic alterations may reflect the course of infection and the host’s response during the early stages of infection.

MS-based metabolomic profiling detected 23,285 features in both positive and negative ionization mode (Supplementary Table S1). After applying the cut-off criteria for significant differences (fold change ≥ ±2 and adjusted P-value < 0.05), the number of significantly altered features fluctuated over the course of infection (Supplementary Fig. S2). As expected, the samples collected 8 w.p.i. exhibited the highest number of significantly altered features (n = 357; 139 positive and 218 negative charged features), followed by those collected 2 w.p.i. (n = 157; 83 positive and 74 negative charged features). The samples collected 1 and 4 w.p.i. showed fewer significantly altered features: 15 (6 positive and 9 negative charged features) and 39 (15 positive and 24 negative charged features) features, respectively.

Of the 15 significantly altered features detected 1 w.p.i., six were annotated as putative metabolites. Among these, CI-1044 and 5-benzofuranpropanoic acid were the most increased metabolites, whereas cholesterol and N-α-acetyl-L-asparagine were the most decreased metabolites (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S2). Classification of the annotated metabolites revealed that amino acids/peptide derivatives constituted the largest metabolite group (33.3%) (Fig. 2B).

Among the 157 significantly altered features detected 2 w.p.i., 39 were annotated as putative metabolites. The most increased putative metabolites were Z-tetrachlorvinphos and acetazolamide, while fluorescein and perfluoromethane sulfonamido propyl dimethyl amine were the most decreased putative metabolites (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S3). Metabolites classified as “other” were the most abundant (47.5%), followed by flavonoids (12.5%) (Fig. 2D).

Of the 39 significantly altered features detected 4 w.p.i., 28 were annotated as putative metabolites. Pyrrolnitrin and FT-thioether were the most increased metabolites, whereas cholesterol and 2-hydroxyglutaric acid were the most decreased metabolites (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table S4). The metabolites in the “other” category accounted for the largest proportion (69%), followed by fatty acid/lipid derivatives (13.8%) (Fig. 2F).

Among the 357 significantly altered features detected 8 w.p.i., 245 were annotated as putative metabolites. The most increased metabolites were 2-[(1S,2S,4aR,8aS)-1-hydroxy-4a-methyl-8-methylidene-1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8a-octahydronaphthalen-2-yl]prop-2-enoic acid and FT-thioether, while fluorescein and flavone base + 4O,O-malonylhex were the most decreased metabolites (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Table S5). Again, the “other” category was the most predominant group (51.6%), and flavonoids were the second largest group (12.3%) (Fig. 2H).

The majority of the significantly altered features were found to be significantly different at only a single time-point. Only one feature, 2-hydroxyglutaric acid, was consistently significantly altered from 2 w.p.i. to 8 w.p.i. In addition, 13 and 12 features overlapped between 8 w.p.i. and 4 w.p.i., and between 8 w.p.i. and 2 w.p.i., respectively (Fig. 3). These overlapping features are potential biomarkers of early-stage schistosomiasis.

An Upset plot showing the metabolomic features that were significantly altered at multiple post-infection time-points.

We then applied the criteria for biomarker screening. First, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (oPLS-DA) was performed to calculate variable importance in projection (VIP) scores. Metabolites with a VIP score > 1 were considered potential biomarkers. At all time-points, the control and infection samples were clearly separated, and the model performance was satisfactory (Fig. 4A–C, E, G). The R2Y values ranged from 0.818 to 0.897. The predictive performance (Q2) increased progressively from the early to late infection stages (0.397–0.589), indicating stronger metabolic separation at the later stages of infection. These results showed that there were distinct differences in the urinary metabolic profiles between the control mice and those infected with Schistosoma parasites.

At 1 w.p.i., the metabolites with the highest VIP scores were cholesterol and N-α-acetyl-L-asparagine, consistent with the results of the univariate analysis (Supplementary Table S6). At 2 w.p.i., perfluorobutanol and PFCA-unsaturated; C6HF7O2, a medium-chain fatty acid, showed the highest VIP scores (Supplementary Table S7). At 4 w.p.i., 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01 (1H-2-benzoxacyclotetradecin-1,7(8H)-dione, 3,4,5,6,9,10-hexahydro-8,9,16-trihydroxy-14-methoxy-3-methyl-, (3S,8S,9R,11E)-) were identified as the top discriminating metabolites (Supplementary Table S8). At 8 w.p.i., the metabolites with the highest VIP scores were flavone base + 4O, O-malonylhex, and N4-acetylsulfamethazine (Supplementary Table S9). When visualized using oPLS-DA S-plots, these metabolites were located in the extreme regions of the plots, indicating high covariance and correlation values and identifying them as key contributors to the observed metabolic differences between the control and infected groups (Fig. 4B–D, F, H).

In addition, we used a RF model to identify potential biomarkers of early schistosomiasis. The 500 trees (ntree = 500) were generated for classification, and seven variables were randomly sampled at each split (mtry = 7) for testing model performance. The out-of-bag error rate ranged from 0.111 to 0.167, corresponding to prediction accuracies of 83–89%. These results indicate robust discrimination between the infected and control groups. The features with a positive mean decrease in accuracy (MDA) value were considered informative and retained for further analysis. Notably, the putative metabolites ranked highly by the RF model differed substantially from those identified in the univariate analysis and oPLS-DA. At 1 w.p.i., the metabolites with the highest MDA values were dimefuron, N-acetylcarnosine, and NCGC00180624-03 (Supplementary Table S10). At 2 w.p.i., 5-methoxypsoralen, cis-4-hydroxy-D-proline, and NCGC00169318-02 showed the highest MDA values (Supplementary Table S11). At 4 w.p.i., piperine, the oligopeptide Glu-Val-Phe, and D-glucuronolactone were the top-ranked features (Supplementary Table S12). At 8 w.p.i., zoalene, NCGC00381187, and 2-hydroxyglutaric acid exhibited the highest MDA values (Supplementary Table S13). To identify robust biomarker candidates, the univariate analysis, oPLS-DA, and RF model results were integrated.

As shown in Table 1, when the criteria of a VIP score > 1 and a positive MDA value were applied, 2, 8, 9, and 52 putative metabolites were identified in the samples obtained at 1, 2, 4, and 8 w.p.i., respectively. These putative metabolites were subsequently evaluated for their diagnostic performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Notably, they all exhibited AUROC values > 0.8 and thus exceeded the cut-off for robust biomarkers of common Schistosoma infection (Supplementary Table S14). We then focused on the metabolites that met the selection criteria at more than one time-point, particularly during early infection, as such molecules are more diagnostically relevant than those that exhibit more transient, time-specific changes. Two putative metabolites fulfilled these requirements: 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01. The abundance of 2-hydroxyglutaric acid was significantly lower in the infected mice from 2 w.p.i. onward and consistently demonstrated AUROC values > 0.8 across all post-infection time-points (Fig. 5). This metabolite exhibited VIP scores > 1 at all time-points, although positive MDA values were observed only at 2 and 8 w.p.i. (Table 1). Interestingly, the level of 2-hydroxyglutaric acid exhibited a consistent trend across the mice infected with the different Schistosoma species (Fig. 5A, dotted lines), suggesting the potential use of this metabolite as a cross-species biomarker. Similarly, the abundance of NCGC00380820-01 was significantly higher from 4 w.p.i. onward and showed AUROC values > 0.8 throughout all post-infection time-points (Supplementary Fig. S3). While the VIP scores for this metabolite were > 1 at all time-points, positive MDA values were detected only at 4 and 8 w.p.i. (Table 1). The level of this molecule also exhibited a consistent trend, similar to that observed for 2-hydroxyglutaric acid (Supplementary Fig. S3A, dotted lines). These candidate biomarkers were subsequently evaluated for their longitudinal association with Schistosoma infection using an LMM.

The LMM revealed that the level of 2-hydroxyglutaric acid was significantly lower after infection. It was lower at all post-infection time-points compared with the pre-infection baseline (all P < 0.01), with the magnitude of reduction increasing from 1 to 4 w.p.i. and remaining suppressed at 8 w.p.i. (Fig. 5). In contrast, the level of NCGC00380820-01 was significantly higher after infection, with early elevation detected at 1 w.p.i. and sustained increases observed at later time-points (all P < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. S3). These findings indicate that Schistosoma infection substantially altered the levels of both metabolites over the course of infection, even after accounting for repeated measurements within individual mice. In addition, the models revealed distinct patterns of baseline heterogeneity between the two metabolites. The inter-mouse variability was substantial for 2-hydroxyglutaric acid (random-effect variance = 0.014; intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC = 0.36), indicating pronounced baseline differences among the infected mice, whereas NCGC00380820-01 exhibited low inter-mouse variability (random-effect variance = 0.005; ICC = 0.09), suggesting greater homogeneity at baseline (Supplementary Table S15). Nevertheless, infection consistently altered the levels of both metabolites in a time-dependent manner.

Together, these results show that 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01 exhibited consistent discriminative performances, that their levels were altered early in infection, and that the changes were sustained across multiple post-infection time-points. Therefore, 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01 have potential as urinary biomarkers of early-stage intestinal schistosomiasis.

Discussion

Diagnosing early-stage schistosomiasis is challenging, particularly the intestinal form, which is caused by multiple species. While potential diagnostic biomarkers have been proposed, there are concerns about their applicability across different parasite species because they were identified in studies conducted with single species of Schistosoma. Detection of parasite eggs in fecal samples by microscopy is considered a gold standard method for diagnosis of intestinal schistosomiasis; however, other biofluids have also shown promise as sources of biomarkers. Therefore, we adopted an untargeted metabolite profiling approach to identify potential urinary metabolite biomarkers of intestinal schistosomiasis caused by different species. As a result, we identified two metabolites with strong potential as shared biomarkers of early-stage infection.

The number of significantly altered features detected at each post-infection time-point closely aligns with parasite development in the host. Following skin penetration, the cercariae rapidly transform into schistosomula, which migrate to the lungs via the blood. The time at which the parasite load peaks in the lungs varies among species, but it is generally within the first week of infection (Nation et al., 2020). Patients typically exhibit few or no clinical symptoms at this point, which is consistent with our observation of the lowest number of significantly altered features being detected at 1 w.p.i. Our findings align with those of Hu et al. (2020), who examined urinary metabolomic changes during early S. japonicum infection in mice. Their multivariate analyses similarly demonstrated that S. japonicum infection influenced the host’s metabolic profile from 1 w.p.i., although specific differential metabolites were not reported. Metabolomic studies of other biological matrices, including serum and feces, have also identified alterations in the early stages of infection (Hu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2020; Chienwichai et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that metabolites found in the host’s biofluids are impacted by Schistosoma infection and have potential utility as sources of diagnostic biomarkers.

Following migration to the lungs, schistosomula systemically travel to, accumulate in, and further develop in the liver (Nation et al., 2020). During this migratory phase, Katayama fever is often present, which consists of systemic hypersensitivity reactions that typically manifest as non-specific symptoms, such as fever, fatigue, myalgia, and urticaria around 2 w.p.i. (Ross et al., 2007). Consistent with this immunologically active phase, we observed a marked increase in the number of significantly altered features at 2 w.p.i. Notably, several of the urinary metabolites identified in this study corresponded to serum metabolites reported in previous studies. For instance, uridine levels were lower at 2 w.p.i. in our dataset (Supplementary Table S3), consistent with the findings of Huang et al. (2020), who reported altered serum uridine levels with high VIP scores in S. japonicum-infected mice from 1 to 5 w.p.i. Similarly, theophylline – a compound involved in caffeine metabolism and known for its bronchodilatory effects – was significantly lower in our study (Supplementary Table S3), and it was previously proposed as a serum biomarker of S. mekongi infection (Chienwichai et al., 2022).

After developing in the liver, the parasites migrate to the mesenteric blood vessels (Nation et al., 2020). In this study, no eggs were detected in the fecal samples at 4 w.p.i., indicating that eggs were not being produced at that time. At 4 w.p.i., the number of significantly altered metabolites was lower than at 2 w.p.i., which indicated a phase of partial immune adaptation to the parasites, during which the dominant T helper 1 response has declined while the T helper 2 response induced by parasite egg production has not yet been fully established (Pearce and MacDonald, 2002). However, once egg production began (eggs were observed in fecal samples at 8 w.p.i.), the number of significantly altered metabolites sharply increased. Notably, cytidine-5′-monophosphate was significantly lower at 8 w.p.i. (Supplementary Table S5), consistent with a previous study that identified this compound as a serum biomarker of early S. japonicum infection (Huang et al., 2020). These concordant findings derived from different studies and the use of different biological matrices further strengthen the reliability and biological relevance of the metabolic features identified in the present study.

To better understand the observed urinary metabolomic alterations, the significantly altered metabolites were categorized according to their chemical class. The “other” category was the largest group at nearly all time-points. This was followed by flavonoids at 2 and 8 w.p.i., and fatty acid/lipid derivatives at 4 w.p.i. Flavonoids are plant-derived polyphenolic compounds with well-established antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Panche et al., 2016). Previous studies have reported associations between urinary flavonoid metabolites and alterations in liver enzyme activity and systemic inflammatory markers (Bullón-Vela et al., 2023). Therefore, the post-infection increases in the proportion of flavonoid-related metabolites in the urine may have been due to parasite-induced pathology, particularly during the migratory and egg-deposition stages, which involve the liver and are accompanied by immune-mediated systemic inflammation. Regarding alteration of lipid metabolism molecules, it has been consistently reported in schistosomiasis. For example, Li et al. (2011) identified significantly altered lipid metabolites in the urine, plasma, and fecal water of S. mansoni-infected mice at approximately 7 w.p.i. In addition, we previously found that glycerophospholipid species were significantly altered in the serum of S. mekongi-infected mice (Chienwichai et al., 2022). Glycerophospholipids were also found to be significantly altered in fecal samples from mice with intestinal schistosomiasis (Chienwichai et al., 2024). Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that gut microbiota mediate host metabolic changes during schistosomal infection. Hu et al. (2020) demonstrated that S. japonicum infection alters the gut’s microbial composition alongside the host’s metabolite profile. Specifically, the levels of certain lipids in the colon, such as LysoPC (20:1), positively correlated with bacterial taxa, including Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 and Roseburia. These findings suggest that alterations in lipid metabolites may partly originate from shifts in intestinal microbial populations. Therefore, the metabolite changes observed in other non-fecal biological matrices, including urine, may reflect the systemic absorption and redistribution of microbiota-associated metabolites.

Following the identification of significantly altered putative metabolites, oPLS-DA models were constructed using urinary metabolite profiles at each stage of infection. oPLS-DA is widely applied in metabolomics for biomarker screening in infectious diseases (Zhou et al., 2015; Cui et al., 2016; Osakunor et al., 2020). Candidate metabolites were prioritized based on VIP scores (> 1) from the oPLS-DA and their positions in the S-plot (top-right and bottom-left regions), which represent variables with high covariance and correlation contributing to class separation (Wiklund et al., 2008). In parallel, the RF algorithm was applied for biomarker selection. Similar to oPLS-DA-based approaches, RF is frequently used in metabolomic biomarker discovery studies (Nguyen et al., 2015; Depke et al., 2020; Anh et al., 2024). RF identified a subset of discriminative features at each time-point based on positive MDA values. To enhance robustness, features were further screened using combined criteria: (i) significant change (fold change ≥ ±2 and adjusted P-value < 0.05); (ii) VIP score > 1; and (iii) positive MDA value. Across all time-points, 71 features met these criteria. ROC analysis demonstrated that all selected features achieved AUROC values greater than 0.8, indicating good discriminatory performance between infected and control groups. Finally, to identify consistent biomarker candidates, we focused on putative metabolites detected in at least two time-points. Among these, 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01 emerged as the strongest candidates for further validation.

2-hydroxyglutaric acid is a host-derived metabolite generated from α-ketoglutarate, an intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Two enantiomers exist, L-2-hydroxyglutarate and D-2-hydroxyglutarate, each associated with distinct biological functions. D-2-hydroxyglutarate is widely recognized as an oncometabolite that is produced through mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase and accumulates in several cancers, particularly gliomas (Chou et al., 2021; Hao et al., 2024). In contrast, mutations in L-2-hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase lead to the accumulation of L-2-hydroxyglutarate and the rare neurometabolic disorder L-2-hydroxyglutaric aciduria, which is characterized by various psychomotor abnormalities (Van Schaftingen et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2020). In addition, both enantiomers have been recognized as immunometabolites capable of modulating inflammatory responses (Foskolou et al., 2023). Altered levels of 2-hydroxyglutaric acid have also been reported in infectious diseases. For example, urinary levels of this metabolite were significantly altered in patients with tuberculous meningitis during treatment, suggesting its involvement in host metabolic responses to infection (Isaiah et al., 2024). In a helminth-related study involving a Caenorhabditis elegans model, the presence of mutant isocitrate dehydrogenase led to the accumulation of D-2-hydroxyglutarate and increased susceptibility to vitamin B12 deficiency (Ponomarova et al., 2024). Moreover, knocking down the parasite’s D-2-hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase resulted in embryonic lethality, mitochondrial defects, and the upregulation of ketone body metabolism genes, highlighting the importance of this metabolic pathway for parasite development (Ponomarova et al., 2023). Other recent studies provide further evidence that 2-hydroxyglutarate plays an immunomodulatory role during helminth infection. Human macrophages exposed to helminth-derived glutamate dehydrogenase had more 2-hydroxyglutarate than unexposed macrophages, which was associated with the suppression of leukotriene production and proposed to promote parasite chronicity (Bohnacker et al., 2024). Similarly, ex vivo culturing of S. mansoni in mouse serum significantly increased the level of 2-hydroxyglutarate, suggesting that this metabolite may be produced or induced by the parasite to support its survival within the host (Skelly and Da'dara, 2025). In contrast to these observations, we detected a decreasing trend in the level of urinary 2-hydroxyglutaric acid following infection, which began 1 w.p.i. and was statistically significant from 2 w.p.i. onward. The LMM results confirmed this reduction while accounting for inter-individual variation among the mice. Considering the reported roles of 2-hydroxyglutarate in helminth-associated immunometabolic regulation, we hypothesize that Schistosoma infection affects the host’s metabolic or immune pathways in such a way that the urinary excretion of 2-hydroxyglutarate is reduced. Notably, the strong predictive performance of 2-hydroxyglutarate across our multiple analytical approaches highlights its potential as a biomarker of early-stage intestinal schistosomiasis.

The compound NCGC00380820-01 is related to zearalenone. Zearalenone is a secondary metabolite produced by fungi of the genera Fusarium and Gibberella and is widely recognized as a mycotoxin with diverse biological effects on multiple organs and immune cells (Bulgaru et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). Zearalenone-related compounds have been detected in urine in previous metabolomic and biomonitoring studies, and their presence has been attributed to dietary exposure to mycotoxin-contaminated food (Ali and Degen, 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). In addition, in our study, the detected NCGC00380820-01 may have been produced by fungal members of the gut microbiome (the mycobiome), which is increasingly recognized as contributing to the host’s metabolic signature and disease state (El-Jurdi and Ghannoum, 2017; Mohammadi et al., 2025). However, the biological relevance of this compound to intestinal schistosomiasis remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

A key strength of our study is that we screened for urinary biomarkers that have utility across multiple species of Schistosoma that cause intestinal disease. This is the first study to identify multi-species urinary biomarkers, and it follows our previous study, in which we identified multi-species fecal biomarkers of intestinal schistosomiasis (Chienwichai et al., 2024). Identifying biomarkers that can be used to detect infection with different species helps overcome the limitations associated with species-specific biomarkers, such as the applicability of biomarkers to regions where certain parasite species are prevalent. Another strength of this study is that we employed comprehensive statistical and machine learning-assisted analyses to systematically screen for potential biomarkers with strong predictive performance. This allowed us to identify two biomarker candidates from more than 23,000 detected features.

Our study also has several limitations. First, we used a mouse model to identify urinary biomarkers. Physiological differences between humans and mice can limit the translation of findings in animal models to tools and techniques that are applicable in humans. However, it is less challenging to replicate the early stages of infection in controlled animal studies than in human studies. Moreover, 2-hydroxyglutaric acid is present in both humans and mice, which may facilitate its translation. Secondly, the sample size was relatively small (n = 9 mice). We attempted to mitigate this limitation by applying an LMM to evaluate metabolite changes while accounting for background variation among the mice, an approach that allowed us to identify consistent trends associated with infection. Future studies should focus on validating the biomarker potential of the identified metabolites in experiments with larger sample sizes. In addition, our study used pre-infection samples as the control instead of time-matched controls at each sampling point. While environmental conditions were strictly controlled throughout the experiment, the potential influence of unmeasured exogenous factors on the urinary metabolome over the course of the study cannot be completely excluded. Finally, while the untargeted metabolomic approach applied in this study supports the discovery of potential biomarkers, it tends to yield only putative metabolite identification and relative quantification. Therefore, targeted metabolomic approaches should be applied to validate and accurately quantify these candidate biomarkers.

Conclusions

In summary, we used a mouse model to investigate urinary metabolomic alterations following infection with three species of Schistosoma that cause intestinal disease. Using statistical and machine learning-assisted analyses, we identified two putative metabolites with strong potential as biomarkers of early-stage infection, namely 2-hydroxyglutaric acid and NCGC00380820-01, which showed significant alterations from 2 to 4 w.p.i., respectively. Given that 2-hydroxyglutaric acid has strong associations with helminth-related immune and metabolic responses, it has potential as a urinary biomarker for intestinal schistosomiasis. Our findings provide a foundation for future validation studies and implications for the development of diagnostic tools for early-stage intestinal schistosomiasis, which will help reduce the global burden of this disease.
