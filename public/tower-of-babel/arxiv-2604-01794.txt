Abstract
. We develop a novel framework for sparse multiscale kernel approximation of large scattered data problems based on a samplet representation. Samplets form a multiresolution analysis of localized discrete signed measures and enable quasi-sparse representations of kernel matrices associated to asymptotically smooth kernels as well as smoothness detection of scattered data. Building on the latter, we introduce an adaptive data site selection strategy based on the localization of the native reproducing kernel Hilbert space norm in the samplet expansion coefficients. The selection results in a small set of representative data sites, significantly reducing the effective problem size. On the corresponding reduced kernel subspace, we solve an ℓ 1 \ell^{1} -regularized least-squares problem using a trust-region semismooth Newton method in a normal-map formulation, stabilized by an online low-rank SVD on the active set to handle the notorious ill-conditioning of kernel matrices. Numerical experiments in two and three dimensions, including multi-kernel models with varying lengthscales, demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves accurate reconstructions with considerably sparser representations and good computational efficiency.

1.
Introduction

Kernel-based methods are a powerful tool for the approximation of
functions from scattered data
[
38
,
13
]
, with
applications ranging from uncertainty quantification

[
18
,
22
]
and PDE-constrained optimization

[
33
]

to financial econometrics and machine learning

[
34
,
40
]
.
Their feasibility for large-scale problems is, however, limited by the dense
and ill-conditioned nature of kernel matrices, which leads to prohibitive
computational cost and memory requirements.

The computational cost as well as memory requirements can be mitigated by
compretion techniques for kernel matrices. Existing approaches comprise
low-rank techniques
[
39
,
32
,
14
]
as well as
block low-rank techniques such as the fast multipole method

[
16
]
or
\mathcal{H}/\mathcal{H}^{2}
-matrices

[
17
,
6
]
. For asymptotically smooth kernels,
samplets, which form a multiresolution analysis of discrete signed measures,
see
[
19
]
, yield quasi-sparse representations of the corresponding
kernel matrices that can be compressed to essentially linearly many remaining
entries in terms of the number of data sites.
In contrast to classical wavelet or Fourier constructions, see, for example,

[
24
]
, samplets are specifically tailored
to scattered data, making them particularly effective for
kernel-based approximation.

A second, complementary mechanism exploits the multiscale organization of the
data to reduce the number of degrees of freedom.
Within the samplet structure, which are organized in terms of a cluster tree,
the information content of the data is
naturally localized in the clusters at the different scales.
We leverage this structure to introduce a tree-adaptive subsampling strategy
that selects only a small set of representative data sites prior to solving
the kernel regression problem.
The relevance of each cluster can be quantified through its contribution to the
to the kernel’s native reproducing kernel Hilbert space norm.
This yields a geometrically meaningful, kernel-dependent measure of importance,
which accounts for correlation and smoothness effects.
Following the adaptive tree selection strategy of
[
4
]
,
cluster contributions are aggregated bottom-up and propagated top-down,
leading to the identification of a high-energy subtree. Selecting representative
data sites from each of the remaining clusters, the resulting reduced set
reflects the intrinsic multiscale complexity of the data, rather than its raw
size, and enables a substantial reduction of the effective problem dimension.

On the coressponding adaptively reduced subspace of kernel translates
corresponding to the selected data sites, we consider

\ell^{1}
-regularized least-squares formulations to promote sparse
representations of the solution.
Such formulations can be interpreted in the spirit of basis pursuit and
sparse approximation in kernel dictionaries

[
28
,
35
,
36
]
,
and have recently been studied in the samplet setting

[
3
]
.
Beyond sparsity, the
\ell^{1}
-penalty provides a mechanism to automatically
select relevant kernel contributions, which is particularly effective in
multi-kernel settings combining kernels with varying lengthscales.
The resulting optimization problem is solved by a trust-region semismooth
Newton method
[
30
,
21
,
37
]
based on a normal-map
formulation
[
26
,
27
]
,
combined with an online low-rank SVD update of the active kernel block,
see
[
7
]
. This approach allows to exploit
second-order information while increasing robustness with respect to
ill-conditioning, without forming dense factorizations.

The main objective of this work is to develop a multiscale framework for
sparse kernel approximation that combines samplet-based compression with
kernel-driven adaptive subsampling. By first reducing the set of data sites
and then promoting sparsity in the
coefficients, the proposed approach achieves accurate multiscale
reconstructions with significantly reduced computational cost.
The combination of samplet compression, adaptive data site selection
and sparse regression enables efficient treatment of large-scale,
heterogeneous data sets.

Section

\ell^{1}
-regularized formulation together with the proposed solution algorithm.
Section

2.
Kernel-based approximation framework

In this section, we introduce the kernel-based approximation framework
underlying the proposed approach. We briefly recall the main concepts of
reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, describe the samplet-based
compression of kernel matrices, and present the tree-adaptive
subsampling strategy used to construct reduced approximation subspaces.

2.1.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

Let
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
be a Hilbert space of
continuous, real-valued functions
h\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
defined on a bounded
Lipschitz region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
is a

reproducing kernel Hilbert space
and exhibits as such a

reproducing kernel

\mathcal{K}\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

with
\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}

for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega
and
h({\boldsymbol{x}})=\langle\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}},\cdot),h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
The reproducing kernel is symmetric and positive definite in the sense
that the associated

kernel matrix

(1)

{\boldsymbol{K}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

is symmetric and positive definite for any set of mutually distinct points

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\in\Omega
and any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}
and a
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega
, we denote the point evaluation
functional by

\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}h({\boldsymbol{x}}),\ {\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega

and notice that there holds
\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
for any

{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega
. By the reproducing property, the
kernel
\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}},\cdot)
is then nothing but the Riesz representer
of
\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
i.e.,

\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(h)=\langle\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}},\cdot),h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\ \text{for every }h\in\mathcal{H}.

Given the
data sites

X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}\}\subset\Omega,

we define the finite-dimensional subspace

(2)

\mathcal{H}_{X}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\spn\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{H},

of
kernel translates

\phi_{i}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\cdot)
,

i=1,\ldots,N
, associated to
X
. By the Riesz isometry,
the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{X}
is isometrically
isomorphic to the subspace

\mathcal{H}_{X}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\spn\{\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}},\ldots,\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}}\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\prime}.

More precisely, for any coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{i}]_{i=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
we identify

h^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}}\in\mathcal{H}_{X}^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad h=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{X}.

Especially, there holds

\langle\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}},\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}=\langle\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\cdot),\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}),

such that the Gramian of either basis becomes the kernel matrix
(
1
).
The duality between
\mathcal{H}_{X}

and
\mathcal{H}_{X}^{\prime}
implies that
the
\mathcal{H}
-orthogonal projection
of a function
h\in\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{X}
is given by the kernel interpolant

s_{h}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\cdot),

which satisfies the interpolation conditions

s_{h}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=h({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})

for
i=1,\ldots,N
.
Equivalently,
s_{h}
is characterized by the variational formulation

(3)

\langle s_{h},v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle h,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{H}_{X}.

This is seen by choosing the basis of kernel translates
as ansatz and test functions in (
3
).
The expansion coefficients

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[\alpha_{i}]_{i=1}^{N}

can be retrieved by solving the linear system

(4)

{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}={\boldsymbol{h}},\quad{\boldsymbol{h}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[h({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})]_{i=1}^{N}.

For a growing number of data sites, assembly and solution of (
4
)
easily become prohibitive. To speed up computations, we transform the
system using a multiresolution basis resulting in a quasi-sparse
kernel matrix.

2.2.
Samplets and kernel matrix compression

Samplets are discrete, localized signed measures that exhibit vanishing
moments. We briefly recall their underlying concepts as introduced in

[
19
]
. To this end, we shall consider the space

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\mathcal{H}_{X}^{\prime}
equipped with a different inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
such
that
\langle\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}},\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}=\delta_{i,j}
, see
[
2
]
for details.
Then, there holds

\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}v_{i},\quad u^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}},\ v^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}},

and we retrieve the Euclidean geometry for the coefficient vectors.
This is in contrast to the restriction of the Hilbert space inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
to
\mathcal{H}_{X}
, which is rather expressed in
terms of the kernel matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
from (
1
) than by the
identity matrix. Specifically, for
u,v\in\mathcal{H}_{X}
represented in the
basis of kernel translates with coefficient vectors

{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[u_{i}]_{i=1}^{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[v_{i}]_{i=1}^{N}
, we have

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}={\boldsymbol{u}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{v}}.

In this regard, the spaces
(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}})

and
(\mathcal{H}_{X}^{\prime},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}})
are equivalent, where
the norm equivalence constants are the smallest
and the largest eigenvalue of the kernel matrix, respectively.

The construction of samplets is based on a multiresolution analysis of the space

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, i.e., a nested sequence of subspaces

\mathcal{X}_{0}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{X}_{1}^{\prime}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{X}_{J}^{\prime}=\mathcal{X}^{\prime},

where
J\sim\log N
. This multiresolution analysis is obtained by
hierarchically clustering the point evaluation functionals spanning
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}

with respect to their supports and applying a suitable change of
basis along the branches of the resulting hierarchical cluster tree
\mathcal{T}
.
This change of basis can be constructed such that we obtain an

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-orthogonal splitting
\mathcal{X}_{j+1}^{\prime}=\mathcal{X}_{j}^{\prime}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{j+1}^{\prime}

in terms of the
detail spaces

\mathcal{S}_{j+1}^{\prime}\perp\mathcal{X}_{j}^{\prime}
. Conseqently, there holds

\mathcal{X}_{J}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{J}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\mathcal{X}_{0}^{\prime}
.
The union of the bases
\{\sigma_{j,k}\}_{k}
of the spaces
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\prime}
for

j=0,\ldots,J
is called a
samplet basis
for
\mathcal{X}_{J}^{\prime}
.

The following theorem collects the main properties of the samplet basis,
see
[
19
,
1
]
for details.

Theorem 2.1
.

The samplet basis
\bigcup_{j=0}^{J}\{\sigma_{j,k}\}_{k}

forms an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, satisfying
the following properties:

(i)

There holds
\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{j}\sim 2^{dj}
.

(ii)

The samplet basis exhibits
vanishing moments
of order
q+1
, i.e.,

(5)

\sigma_{j,k}(p)=0\quad\text{for all}\ p\in\mathcal{P}_{q},\ j\geq 1,

where
\mathcal{P}_{q}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\operatorname{span}\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}\leq q\}

is the space of polynomials of degree at most
q
.

(iii)

The coefficient vector

{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{j,k}=\big[\omega_{j,k,i}\big]_{i}

of the samplet
\sigma_{j,k}
satisfies

\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{j,k}\|_{1}\lesssim 2^{(J-j)d/2}
.

(iv)

Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
f\in C^{\alpha}(x_{0})
for some
\alpha\geq 0

with
q\geq\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
. Then, for every cluster

\tau_{j,k}\in\mathcal{T}
containing
x_{0}
, there holds

|\sigma_{j,k}(f)|\lesssim\operatorname{diam}(\tau_{j,k})^{\alpha}\sqrt{\#\tau_{j,k}},

where
C^{\alpha}(x_{0})
denotes the Jaffard regularity at
x_{0}
.

Due to the orthonormality of the samplet basis, the samplet transform

[\sigma_{j,k}]_{j,k}={\boldsymbol{T}}[\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}}]_{i=1}^{N}

has the property
{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{T}}={\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\intercal}={\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. If the samplet transform

{\boldsymbol{T}}
is computed recursively with respect to the hierarchy induced
by
\mathcal{T}
, its cost is of order
\mathcal{O}(N)
.

Applying the Riesz isometry to
\{\sigma_{j,k}\}_{j,k}
gives rise to
a basis in
\mathcal{H}_{X}
defined as

\psi_{j,k}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{j,k,i}\,\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{X}.

The Gramian of this transformed samplet basis is obviously given by

(6)

\big[\langle\psi_{j,k},\psi_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big]_{j,k,j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}={\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\intercal}\mathrel{=\mathrel{\mathop{:}}}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}.

Therefore, solving the linear system
{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}={\boldsymbol{h}}
arising from
(
3
), is equivalent to solving

(7)

{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}={\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{h}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{\beta}}={\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},

Furthermore, the basis
\{\psi_{j,k}\}_{j,k}
inherits the vanishing
moment property. Specifically, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that

h|_{\supp\sigma_{j,k}}=p|_{\supp\sigma_{j,k}}
for some
p\in\mathcal{P}_{q}
,
the vanishing moment property (
5
) implies

\langle\psi_{j,k},h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0.

The condition
h|_{\supp\sigma_{j,k}}=p|_{\supp\sigma_{j,k}}
can be
translated into the requirement that there exists a polynomial of
total degree
q
interpolating all points in the support of
\sigma_{j,k}
.
Thus, if the coefficient vector of
h
in the basis of kernel translates
of
\mathcal{H}_{X}
can locally be interpolated by a polynomial of degree at
most
q
, the corresponding samplet coefficients are zero. For such
functions
h
, we obtain sparse representations in the samplet basis.

In what follows, we assume that
\mathcal{K}
is
asymptotically smooth
, i.e.,
there exist constants
C,r>0
such that

\bigg|\frac{\partial^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|+|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\partial{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\mathcal{K}({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\bigg|\leq C\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|+|\boldsymbol{\beta}|)!}{r^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|+|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}^{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|+|\boldsymbol{\beta}|}}

for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq{\boldsymbol{y}}
,
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Omega

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
This means that
\mathcal{K}
is an analytic function whenever

\|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}\geq\rho
for a given
\rho>0
.
Then, as a consequence of the vanishing moment property,

{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}
becomes quasi-sparse and can efficiently be computed.
In detail, there holds the following result, see
[
20
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be quasi-uniform with
\#X=N
and
set all coefficients of
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}
from
(
6
) to zero which satisfy the admissibility condition

\dist(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\geq\rho\max\{\diam(\tau),\diam(\tau^{\prime})\}
,
\rho>0
,
where
\tau
is the cluster supporting
\sigma_{j,k}
and
\tau^{\prime}
is the
cluster supporting
\sigma_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
, respectively.
Then, there exists
a constant
C>0
, such that the resulting compressed matrix

{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma,\rho}
satisfies

{\big\|{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}-{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma,\rho}\big\|_{F}}\leq C\bigg(\frac{r\rho}{d}\bigg)^{-2(q+1)}{\big\|{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}\big\|_{F}}.

The compressed matrix
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma,\rho}
has
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
nonzero
entries.

Theorem
2.2
establishes that, for asymptotically smooth kernels,
the kernel matrix admits a compressed, sparse representation in the samplet basis
with controllable accuracy.
In contrast, the matrix
\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}
exactly represents the kernel matrix
in the samplet basis.
A visualization of the sparsity pattern of the compressed matrix

\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma,\rho}
is shown in Figure (
1
).

Figure 1
.

\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}
and
\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma,\rho}

(left and right), constructed using the Matérn-
3/2
kernel on
N=10\,000
points
uniformly distributed in the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
. Both matrices are displayed on

blocks colored by mean absolute value.
The transition from
\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}
to
\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma,\rho}
is obtained by
thresholding small coefficients to zero, reducing the number of nonzero entries
to
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
.

2.3.
Tree-adaptive sub-sampling

The formulations (
4
) and (
7
) employ all

N
data sites for the representation of the interpolant. As a consequence,
the solution of the resulting system is no longer computationally feasible in
the regime of very large
N
, without employing additional compression
techniques.
In this section, we develop an adaptive selection strategy to identify
a subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{X}
that approximates the orthogonal
projection of a given function
h\in\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{X}
,
cf. (
2
), up to a prescribed consistency error.

To this end, we employ the
adaptive tree approximation

from
[
4
]
, where the initial tree is obtained from the cluster
tree
\mathcal{T}
underlying the samplet basis.
Then, given a relevant subtree, we select a representative data site from
each remaining cluster to construct the desired subspace.

We start from the samplet transformed data values

{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}={\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{h}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{h}}=[h({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})]_{i=1}^{N}

and apply tree-coarsening from
[
4
]
to this setting.
We define the
energy
contained in a cluster
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
as the
sum of energies of its children and add the squared Euclidean norm of the
samplet coefficients belonging to
\tau
. This yields

(8)

e(\tau)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{child}(\tau)}e(\tau^{\prime})+\sum_{\sigma\in\tau}\big(\sigma(h)\big)^{2},

where we mean by
\sigma\in\tau
the samplets that are supported on
\tau
.
Especially we make the convention that the root of
\mathcal{T}
also contains
the scaling distributions spanning
\mathcal{X}_{0}^{\prime}
.
Then, the quantity
e(\tau)
is the contribution
of the subtree rooted in
\tau
to the squared Euclidean norm

\big\|{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}\big\|_{2}^{2}
. Especially, there holds

e(X)=\big\|{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}\big\|_{2}^{2}
. Due to the orthogonality of the
samplet basis, the energy
e(X)
is localized in the clusters

\tau\in\mathcal{T}
.

Based on the energies (
8
), we next define

\tilde{e}(\tau^{\prime})\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}q(\tau)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\frac{\sum_{\mu\in\operatorname{child}(\tau)}e(\mu)}{e(\tau)+\tilde{e}(\tau)}\tilde{e}(\tau)\quad\text{for all}\ \tau^{\prime}\in\operatorname{child}(\tau)

with
\tilde{e}(X)\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}e(X)
. Employing the modified energies

\tilde{e}(\tau)
,
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, we apply the thresholding version
of the second algorithm from
[
4
]
with threshold

t=\varepsilon^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}\|_{2}^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|_{2}^{2}
.
The result of this procedure is a subtree
\mathcal{T}_{t}
that approximates

{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}
up to a relative error of
\varepsilon^{2}
in the Euclidean
norm.

In view of the discussion in the previous subsection, the Euclidean norm
of the coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}
corresponds to the

\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-norm of the functional

h_{X}^{\prime}=\sum_{j,k}h_{j,k}^{\Sigma}\sigma_{j,k}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime},

while its
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm is given by
\|h_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}=\|Rh_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}={\boldsymbol{h}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{h}}=({\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma})^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma},

where
R
denotes the Riesz isometry.
Different from the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-norm, the
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm is
not localized by the samplet coefficients in the nodes of the cluster tree.
In particular, the entries

k^{\Sigma}_{(j,k),(j^{\prime},k^{\prime})}=\langle\psi_{j,k},\psi_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

of the kernel matrix in samplet coordinates
with
(j,k)\neq(j^{\prime},k^{\prime})
capture cross-scale and spatial interactions.
This also does not change when replacing
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}
by its compressed
counterpart
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma,\rho}
.

To achieve localization of the
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm in the clusters
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
,
similarly to
the Euclidean norm, we define the interaction vector

\boldsymbol{z}^{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{h}^{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Then,
z_{i}^{\Sigma}
quantifies the contribution of
h_{i}^{\Sigma}
modulated by its
correlations with all other coefficients.
By introducing the directionally lumped Gramian

\boldsymbol{D}^{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\Sigma}\oslash\boldsymbol{h}^{\Sigma})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

via elementwise division and the convention
d_{i,i}=0
whenever
h_{i}^{\Sigma}=0
,
we obtain a cluster-localized representation in the sense that the
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm
can be expressed as a sum of cluster contributions, i.e.,

\|Rh_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=({\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{D}^{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{h}}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}.

Remark 2.3
.

With respect to the directionally lumped Gramian
\boldsymbol{D}^{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
,
the norms
\|{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}\|_{2}
and
\|h_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}=\|Rh_{X}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

become comparable whenever
\boldsymbol{D}^{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
is close to the identity matrix.
This happens, for example, when the kernel is sufficiently localized such that
the Gramian
{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}
is close to the identity and, consequently,

\boldsymbol{z}^{\Sigma}
is close to
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\Sigma}
.

The identity matrix corresponds to the
\ell^{2}
-kernel,
which does not introduce correlations between data sites,
whereas less localized kernels model correlations between them.
Therefore, the choice of the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}

the most conservative one, while the choice of the
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

a less localized kernel yields a sparser subsample. We refer to

•

In a

descendants, resulting in a functional
e(\tau)
that measures the energy of
the subtree rooted at
\tau
.

•

In a subsequent
top-down

energy along the tree to obtain a propagated functional
\tilde{e}(\tau)
,
which reflects the relative importance of each cluster within the entire
hierarchy.

The clusters are then sorted in decreasing order of
\tilde{e}(\tau)
and the
adaptive subtree
{\mathcal{T}}_{t}\subset\mathcal{T}
is constructed by adding clusters
until the remaining energy falls below the prescribed threshold.

we introduce the set representative data sites

{X}_{t}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}:i\in I\},
which reflects the energy distribution of the data.

3.
Multiscale kernel lasso

In this section, we introduce an
\ell^{1}
-regularized formulation of the
kernel approximation problem, which promotes sparse representations in
the multiscale setting, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
12
,
10
]
.
We first describe the corresponding a kernel basis pursuit formulation,
and then present the semismooth Newton method used to solve the resulting
optimization problem, including its efficient realization based on an
online SVD and a trust-region strategy.

3.1.
Kernel basis pursuit

We consider the sets of data sites

X_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\big\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{(\ell)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}\big\}
,

\ell=1,\ldots,L
, and
a corresponding dictionary of kernels

\mathcal{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{L}
and seek a (sparse) representation of the form

(9)

s_{h}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\alpha_{\ell,i}\mathcal{K}_{\ell}\big(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{(\ell)}\big).

This representation particularly comprises the multiscale scattered data
interpolation framework, see for example
[
9
,
25
,
36
]
,
if a sequence of kernels with decreasing lengthscale
parameters is chosen associated to a sequence of nested point sets.

Setting
M\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}N_{1}+\ldots+N_{L}
as well as

{\boldsymbol{K}}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[{\boldsymbol{K}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{K}}_{L}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},\quad{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\big[\mathcal{K}_{\ell}\big({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{(\ell)}\big)\big]_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,N\\
j=1,\ldots,N_{\ell}\end{subarray}},

and

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\intercal}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{L}^{\intercal}]\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\quad{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}},

it is well known that a sparse solution to (
9
) is obtained by
solving the convex optimization problem

(10)

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\;\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}|\alpha_{i}|,

for a given weight vector
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. The entries of

{\boldsymbol{w}}=[w_{i}]_{i=1}^{M}
act as
component-wise regularization parameters, thereby controlling the level of
sparsity in each coefficient individually.
We refer to, e.g.,
[
10
,
23
,
31
]

for a detailed analysis of the regularizing properties
and strategies for appropriate parameter selection.

In contrast to the single scale formulation (
10
), it has been
observed in
[
3
]
that a richer class of sparse
representations can be obtained by employing a samplet basis. This yields the
optimization problem

(11)

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|^{2}_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}|\beta_{i}|

with

{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[{\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{T}}{\boldsymbol{K}}_{L}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{L}^{\intercal}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}

and

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\intercal}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}[{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}^{\intercal},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{L}^{\intercal}]\in\mathbb{R}^{M},\quad{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\ell}},

where
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\ell}
denotes the samplet transform on

X_{\ell}
.
Due to the orthonormality of the samplet basis, there particularly holds

\|{\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}.

Hence, the formulations (
10
) and (
11
) only differ
in the choice of the regularization term.

3.2.
Semismooth Newton method

To numerically solve (
10
) and (
11
), we apply the
trust-region semismooth Newton approach proposed in
[
27
]
.
To compute the Newton update in a numerically stable way, we employ the online
SVD approach from
[
7
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we
recall the key algorithms and adapt them to our framework. To this end,
we start from the minimization problem

(12)

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\;\Phi({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}),\quad\text{where }\Phi({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})+g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})

with

f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\tfrac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}^{2},\quad g({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}|\alpha_{i}|.

The functional
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable
and reflects the data-fidelity, while
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to[0,\infty)

is a convex, lower semicontinuous and proper mapping that enforces sparsity.
The vector
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a critical point of
\Phi
if and only
if

F^{\lambda}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}\big({{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\lambda\nabla f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\big)}=\boldsymbol{0}.

Herein, the
soft-shrinkage operator
is given by

[\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]_{i}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\operatorname{sign}(\alpha_{i})\,\max(|\alpha_{i}|-\lambda w_{i},0),\quad i=1,\ldots,M.

Following the normal-map reformulation from
[
27
]
,
we introduce the associated normal-map operator

\displaystyle F^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{nor}}({\boldsymbol{z}})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\nabla f\big(\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}({{\boldsymbol{z}}})\big)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big({\boldsymbol{z}}-\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}({{\boldsymbol{z}}})\big)

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{K}}^{\intercal}\big({\boldsymbol{K}}\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda{\boldsymbol{w}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})-{\boldsymbol{h}}\big)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big({\boldsymbol{z}}-\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda{\boldsymbol{w}}}({\boldsymbol{z}})\big),

If
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
satisfies
F^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{nor}}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\mathbf{0}
, then
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}({{\boldsymbol{z}}})
is a critical point of
\Phi
.
Conversely, if
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is a critical point of
\Phi
,
then
{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\lambda\nabla f({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
satisfies

F^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{nor}}({\boldsymbol{z}})=\mathbf{0}
.

To compute the roots of the normal map, we employ the semismooth Newton method

\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}^{-1}F^{\lambda}_{\mathrm{nor}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}),

where

{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}.

Herein, we define the active and inactive sets as

(13)

\mathcal{A}_{k}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\{i\in\{1,\dots,M\}:|z_{k,i}|>\lambda w_{i}\},\quad\mathcal{I}_{k}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\{i\in\{1,\dots,M\}:|z_{k,i}|\leq\lambda w_{i}\}

and
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
,

{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}

are partial identity matrices corresponding to the active- and inactive indices,
respectively.
For the active component of the Newton update
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we need to solve the linear system

(14)

{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\,\boldsymbol{s}_{k}=-{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{k}).

Online SVD

A numerically stable solver for (
14
) is obtained from
the online SVD of
{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}
, which keeps track of the columns
of
{\boldsymbol{K}}
that have been active up to iteration
k
. A block version of
the corresponding procedure from
[
7
]
is shown in
Algorithm

.

Trust-region step management

To globalize the semismooth Newton iteration and ensure robustness in the
presence of active-set changes, we employ the trust-region strategy proposed
in
[
27
]
and adapt it to our framework.
At iteration
k
, a reduced trust-region model is constructed on the active
index set
\mathcal{A}_{k}
. The corresponding quadratic model reads

(15)

m_{k}(\boldsymbol{q})=\langle{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{k}),\boldsymbol{q}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle.

Let
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}
denote an approximate minimizer of (
15
)
within the trust-region of radius
\Delta_{k}
. We compute
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}
by the
previously described online SVD solver.
The candidate Newton step is subsequently obtained through the lifting procedure

\bar{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{q}_{k}-\lambda\big(F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})+{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}\big),\qquad\boldsymbol{s}_{k}=\min\bigg\{1,\frac{\Delta_{k}}{\|\bar{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k}\|_{2}}\bigg\}\bar{\boldsymbol{s}}_{k},

which yields an approximate semismooth Newton direction satisfying the
trust-region constraint, see
[
27
]
.
Due to the active-inactive decomposition, the lifted step admits a simple
structure. For indices
i\in\mathcal{A}_{k}
one has
\bar{s}_{k,i}=q_{k,i}
, whereas
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
the update is given by

\bar{s}_{k,i}=-\lambda\big(\big[F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\big]_{i}+\left[{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{\intercal}{\boldsymbol{K}}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\mathcal{A}_{k}}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}\right]_{i}\big).

Hence, the Newton update is effectively determined by the active subspace.

Now, step acceptance is based on the standard ratio between actual and predicted
reduction,

\tilde{\rho}_{k}=\frac{\mathrm{ared}_{k}}{\mathrm{pred}_{k}}.

Following
[
27
]
, the actual reduction is computed through the
merit function

(16)

H_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z})=\Phi(\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{z}))+\frac{\tau\lambda}{2}\|F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z})\|_{2}^{2},\quad\mathrm{ared}_{k}=H_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})-H_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}+\boldsymbol{s}_{k}),

with
\tau\in(0,1)
. The predicted reduction is defined as

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{pred}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau\|F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\|_{2}}{2}\min\{\lambda,\Delta_{k},\lambda\|F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\|_{2}\}

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu_{k}\|F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\|_{2}}{\min\{\Delta_{k},\lambda\|F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\|_{2}\}}\|\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}+\boldsymbol{s}_{k})-\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\|_{2}^{2},

with
\nu_{k}\in[0,1)
.

The trust-region radius
\Delta_{k}
is updated in the standard way based
on the reduction ratio
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
, also enforcing a lower and upper bound
for the updated radius.

Finally, since the trust-region iteration is formulated in terms of the
normal-map variable
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}
, we set

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}=\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}).

The trust-region semismooth Newton procedure is summarized
in Algorithm
2
.

Numerical results

In this section, we assess the performance of the proposed framework for
large-scale kernel approximation. In Section
4.1
,
we first isolate and validate the tree-adaptive subsampling strategy.
In Section
4.2
, we consider a second set of experiments
with the full pipeline, combining tree-adaptive subsampling,
and
\ell^{1}
-regularized regression solved by the TR-SSN method.
All computations have been performed at the Centro Svizzero di Calcolo
Scientifico (CSCS) on a single node of the Alps cluster
1
1

1

https://www.cscs.ch/computers/alps

with two AMD EPYC 7742
@2.25GHz CPUs with 470GB of main memory. For the computations, we have
used up to 12 cores. The implementation is publicly available at

https://github.com/muchip/fmca
.
All experiments share the same algorithmic framework and compression strategy,
as well as certain parameter choices that are common across tests,
which we describe next.

Samplet compression

Throughout all experiments, we use the samplet-compressed representation of the
kernel matrix as outlined in Section
2.2
. The accuracy is
controlled by the admissibility parameter, which we set to
\rho=d

and the number of vanishing moments, which we set
q+1=4
.
Furthermore, we apply an a-posteriori thresholding of small matrix entries with
a relative threshold of
\kappa=10^{-7}

with respect to the Frobenius norm of the matrix.

Choice of the
\ell^{1}
-weights

The weights
\boldsymbol{w}
in the
\ell^{1}
penalty are scaled with respect to the
number of selected data sites in order to ensure a consistent balance between
the data-fidelity term and the sparsity regularization.
Let
M
denote the total number of columns of the kernel matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
.
Then, letting
{X}_{t}
be the set of centers obtained by adaptive subsampling,
we set

w_{i}=1/\sqrt{|{X}_{t}|},\ i=1,\dots,M.

Choice of the parameter
\lambda

The parameter
\lambda
defines the normal map
F_{\mathrm{nor}}^{\lambda}
and
plays a role analogous to a stepsize in proximal-gradient methods.
Several works based on the same fixed-point mapping suggest choosing
\lambda

sufficiently small and inversely proportional to the Lipschitz constant of

\nabla f
, see e.g.
[
41
,
11
,
42
]
. In particular,
if
f
is twice continuously differentiable, the fixed-point residual is
monotone provided that

0<\lambda\leq\frac{2}{\mathcal{L}},

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f
.
Following this guideline, we set

\lambda=\frac{2}{\mathcal{L}},

where
\mathcal{L}
is approximated in practice via a power iteration applied to
the compressed kernel matrix.

Trust-region radius update

We adopt the TR-SSN algorithm described in
Algorithm
2
.
Within this scheme, the trust-region radius
\Delta_{k}
is initialized
with
\Delta_{0}=1
and updated based on the ratio
\tilde{\rho}_{k}

between actual and predicted reduction.
We employ acceptance thresholds
\eta_{1}=10^{-3}
and
\eta_{2}=10^{-1}

and enforce the bounds

\Delta_{\min}=10^{-5}
and
\Delta_{\max}=10^{3}
.

Parameters in the reduction ratio
\tilde{\rho}_{k}

The reduction ratio
\tilde{\rho}_{k}
defined in
(
16
)-(
17
)
depends on the parameters
\tau
and
\nu_{k}
.
These parameters are chosen in accordance with the global and local
convergence conditions established in
[
27
]

for trust-region semismooth Newton methods applied to the normal-map formulation.
In particular, global convergence requires the parameters
\tau
and
\nu_{k}

to satisfy conditions
involving the Lipschitz constant
\mathcal{L}
of
\nabla f
.
We therefore set

\tau=\frac{2c_{\tau}}{\mathcal{L}^{2}\lambda^{2}+2}=\frac{c_{\tau}}{3},\qquad\nu=\frac{1}{2}\min\!\left\{\tau,\;c_{\nu}\left[1-\frac{\tau}{2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}+1\right)\right]\right\}=\frac{\tau}{2},

with
c_{\tau}=c_{\nu}=0.05
.
The safeguard parameter
\nu_{k}
appearing in the predicted reduction
is updated adaptively in order to control the inexactness of the Newton steps.
We define

(18)

\nu_{k}\mathrel{\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=}\min\Bigl\{\nu,\,\Bigl(n_{\mathcal{S}}(k)\log^{2}\big(n_{\mathcal{S}}(k)\big)\|\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}+\boldsymbol{s}_{k})-\operatorname{SS}_{\lambda\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\|_{2}\Bigr)^{2\tilde{p}}\Bigr\},

with
\tilde{p}=0.1
and where
n_{\mathcal{S}}(k)
denotes the number of
successful trust-region steps up to iteration
k
.
With this choice, the global convergence conditions remain valid,
while the inexactness tolerance is progressively tightened,
which yields fast local convergence of the TR-SSN method.
In particular, the convergence results of

[
27
, Theorem 4.8 and Theorem 6.2]

ensure global convergence of the normal-map residual and
eventually superlinear (quadratic in our quadratic-
\ell^{1}
-setting)
local convergence.

Continuation strategy

To enhance robustness and support sparsity identification,
we adopt a continuation strategy in which the TR-SSN solver
is applied within an outer loop that progressively reduces the

\ell^{1}
-weight.
Starting from an initial ramp parameter
r_{0}
, and a given

\gamma\in(0,1)
, we define the sequence

(19)

r_{j}=r_{0}\,\gamma^{j},\qquad j=0,1,2,\dots,\quad

and the process is terminated when

r_{j}<r_{\min},

for a prescribed threshold
r_{\min}>0
.
At each continuation level
j
, we solve the weighted
\ell^{1}
-regularized
least-squares problem

(20)

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\;\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{h}-\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}+r_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}|\alpha_{i}|,

using TR-SSN with the solution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j-1}
from the previous level as
initial guess.
Depending on the specific test case, this problem can be formulated in the
samplet domain, within a multi-kernel setting, or in a combined framework
involving both, with the corresponding compressed operators.
This continuation progressively relaxes the sparsity penalty, stabilizes the
early iterations, and improves both robustness and convergence speed of the
TR-SSN method. This procedure has been investigated earlier in

[
5
]
. We remark that this approach of successively increasing the
number of active coefficients is necessary to make the use of the
block online SVD from Algorithm
1
computationally
feasible. Otherwise, one would have to resort to an iterative solver for
the computation of the Newton update, whose convergence might be critical
due to the ill-conditioned kernel matrices involved.

4.1.
Validation of tree-adaptive subsampling

We validate the tree-adaptive subsampling strategy of

in isolation.
The goal is to assess its ability to select a reduced set of representative
centers that capture the essential structure of the data, independently of
any sparsity-promoting regularization.
We compare the two energy measures based on the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-norm and the

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm, which guide the adaptive selection and lead to different
subsampling patterns.

Test 1: Heterogeneous multiscale function

We consider a very heterogeneous composite test function

h\colon[0,6]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

and localized irregularities, shown in Figure

The function is sampled on a large set of

data sites

\boldsymbol{h}=[h(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})]_{i=1}^{N}

To exploit the multiscale structure of the data, we construct the
samplet tree associated with
X
and compute the samplet coefficients

\boldsymbol{h}^{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{h}
.
An adaptive tree search is then performed based on a threshold parameter

\varepsilon^{2}
, applied either in the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-norm or in the
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm,
see Section
2.3
. This procedure retains the most
significant coefficients of
\boldsymbol{h}^{\Sigma}
and yields a reduced set of
representative centers
X_{t}\subset X

Given
{X_{t}}

exponential kernel

(21)

\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\exp\bigg(-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}}{\ell}\bigg),

with lengthscale
\ell

h_{X_{t}}

\ell=10h_{X_{t}}

increase adaptivity but may worsen conditioning, while larger values improve
stability at the cost of resolution.
This leads to the kernel matrix

\boldsymbol{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
with
M=|X_{t}|
and entries

K_{ij}=\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})
,

\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in X
,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\in X_{t}
.
The resulting least-squares problem is formulated in samplet coordinates,

\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\|\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{h}^{\Sigma}\|_{2},

where
\boldsymbol{K}^{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{K}
denotes the compressed kernel matrix,
and is solved by a QR decomposition. This setting allows us to isolate
the effect of the adaptive subsampling strategy, without introducing
any sparsity-promoting regularization.
The experiment is repeated for several values of the threshold

\varepsilon^{2}
and for both subsampling criteria, enabling a direct
comparison between the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-norm and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm selections.

N_{\mathrm{eval}}=10^{6}
uniformly distributed test points, and the
relative
\ell^{2}
-error is used to quantify the approximation quality.
Consistent with the discussion in Section
2.3
, the

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
-norm criterion produces significantly sparser subsets,
as it concentrates points in regions of high kernel
energy, whereas the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
-norm yields a more conservative selection.

5.
Conclusions

We have presented a multiresolution framework for sparse

\ell^{1}
-regularized kernel approximation that combines samplet-based
compression, native space driven adaptive subsampling and a stabilized
trust-region semismooth Newton solver.
The proposed approach achieves accurate reconstructions while
drastically reducing the number of active coefficients in the solution. The
adaptive selection concentrates data sites in regions of high variability,
leading to reduced problem sizes that reflect the intrinsic multiscale
structure of the data. Furtheremore, the samplet compression of the
kernel matrix enables efficient matrix operations, while the TR-SSN method
with the online SVD as interior solver ensures robust convergence,
even in the presence of ill-conditioned kernel matrices.
The method yields significantly sparser representations and improved
computational efficiency compared to single scale formulations and enables
scalability to problem sizes beyond what is feasible with
non-adaptive approaches. In multi-kernel settings, the

\ell^{1}
-regularization acts as an effective mechanism for automatic
kernel selection across different lengthscales.
