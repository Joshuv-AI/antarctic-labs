Analysis of the OPTIMUM (Optimising care of Patients via Telemedicine In Monitoring and aUgmenting their control of diabetes Mellitus) tele-monitoring system in primary care as perceived by healthcare professionals using the CFIR framework
Authors: Cia Sin Lee, Pei Pei Gong, Gerald Choon Huat Koh, Ngiap Chuan Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTelemedicine has been increasingly adopted post-COVID-19 to optimise chronic disease management, improving patient outcomes. The multi-component OPTIMUM (Optimising care of Patients via Telemedicine In Monitoring and aUgmenting their control of diabetes Mellitus) tele-monitoring (TM) system yielded favourable health outcomes and was introduced in primary care nationwide in Singapore. This study aims to understand the factors associated with its implementation during the randomised controlled trial (RCT).DesignA qualitative study was conducted using individual semi-structured interviews. Data were transcribed, audited, coded and analysed using thematic analysis. The CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research) framework, consisting of five domains (Intervention characteristic, Outer setting, Inner setting, Characteristics of individuals and Process), was adopted as the underpinning framework.SettingPrimary care setting in Singapore.ParticipantHealthcare professionals involved in the care provided to patients enrolled in the OPTIMUM RCT.ResultsTM facilitated behavioural change in patients, and its robust treatment algorithm allowed continuous monitoring and personalised advice. The associated external factors were manpower, costing and data security. Measures to mitigate internal risks included managing additional healthcare professionals’ burden, reducing potential adverse events and addressing medicolegal risks related to TM. While TM enhanced provider satisfaction and the patient-healthcare professional relationship, strategies to enhance user experience and streamline the TM processes are required to improve its acceptance and adoption.ConclusionThe CFIR served as a useful framework to identify factors associated with successful implementation of an intervention in a real-world setting. Understanding these factors enables healthcare leaders to address the challenges before scaling up the intervention in routine clinical services.

Introduction

The adoption and implementation of telemedicine have accelerated since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic.1 It encompasses the use of technologies and communication systems to administer healthcare to patients who are geographically separated from providers for diagnosis and treatment.2 It has been well received by patients and healthcare providers and can be equivalent or more clinically effective than in-person care for certain acute and chronic conditions.3 4 It has been widely adopted across the majority of disciplines, including cardiovascular, respiratory, neurology, nephrology, dermatology, endocrinology, obstetrics, psychiatry and multidisciplinary care.4 The modalities are varied, ranging from email, phone calls, text messaging and video conferencing to other online communications (such as smartphone apps or web-based portals).4 Furthermore, the use of telemedicine in primary care allows better access to quality care and increased provision for needy patients, such as those with mobility issues.5

Particularly in countries with ageing populations and an increasing prevalence of chronic diseases,6 there is a need to strengthen primary care through adoption of telemedicine to provide more operationally efficient, sustainable, scalable and cost-effective healthcare services.7 In Singapore, the telemedicine programme was implemented as a mainstream service in public primary healthcare services since 2020, targeting adults with essential hypertension and subsequently diabetes mellitus.8 9 For example, the OPTIMUM study (Optimising care of Patients via Telemedicine In Monitoring and aUgmenting their control of diabetes Mellitus) is a randomised controlled trial (RCT) conducted in primary care setting in Singapore, has shown significant favourable outcomes.10 Participants in the intervention group who completed 6 months of home tele-monitoring (TM) showed better diabetic control and improvements in blood pressure and cholesterol levels compared with those receiving usual care.10 This study also showed that adopting a theoretical framework, such as the health belief model, in telemedicine intervention can effectively drive health behaviour change within patients and sustain it over a longer period.11–13

While telemedicine has been shown to improve patient outcomes, it can also pose a greater burden to healthcare professionals, such as increasing their workload.14 A scoping review identified various challenges faced by physicians in telemedicine services.15 The common challenges include issues related to communication, inadequate technology or support, the need for training and knowledge to use the technology and data security.15 On the other hand, some of the opportunities identified were related to improved access to services, benefits related to the sharing of information and experience and training in using technology.15 Another systematic review has also shown that physicians across different specialties, geographic locations, practice locations and care situations appear to have a moderate to high level of satisfaction when engaging in telemedicine.16

The implementation of the OPTIMUM TM system on a larger scale requires an understanding of the factors that may influence its success. This paper presents the results of the qualitative component of the OPTIMUM study using the CFIR framework (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research) to understand the factors that can influence the implementation of the OPTIMUM TM system and to guide the development of strategies by leveraging the system’s positive aspects and effectively addressing its challenges.

Methods

Background of OPTIMUM study

The OPTIMUM study17 was conducted at a public primary care clinic (polyclinic) located in Singapore. The polyclinic provides a range of services, ranging from preventive health and managing acute diseases to chronic disease management, with an estimated 60% of patient attendances related to the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension and dyslipidaemia. Participants recruited into the OPTIMUM study consisted of adult patients with T2DM, with or without hypertension and dyslipidaemia. A total of 165 adults with poorly controlled T2DM (HbA1c ≥7.5% and ≤10%) were randomised to the intervention arm in which the participants received TM, tele-support and tele-education as defined by Singapore’s National Telemedicine Guidelines.18 The clinical parameters such as blood pressure and capillary glucose levels were relayed remotely to a trained nurse according to the trial protocol.17 The summary of the treatment algorithm is summarised in table 1 where different parameters’ thresholds trigger different alert/risk levels. The parameters were grouped in a matrix to stratify the level of intervention based on defined range of readings: normal—motivational messages to encourage continuity of TM; low risk—automated messages sent to participants as push notification to remind medication adherence and recheck the parameter; moderate to high risk—phone call by TM nurse to assess high risk status and adherence to treatment; high risk—participants to complete additional in-app survey prior to phone call by TM nurse. The TM nurse would call up subjects with (1) paucity of or (2) abnormal blood pressure or glucose readings.

Additionally, within the tele-education component, patients received nine mandatory and nine optional educational videos through the customised OPTIMUM mobile app (online supplemental material 1). The videos addressed a range of topics related to T2DM, such as foundational diabetes information, guidance on healthy eating, techniques for self-monitoring, demonstrations of physical activities and strategies for managing cardiovascular risks. TM was provided for 6 months, after which patients returned to standard care and continued to track their clinical metrics independently, without oversight or support from the TM nurse for the following 18 months. In contrast, the control group received usual care, which consisted of regularly scheduled appointments—typically three to four visits over 12 months—with their primary care physician or nurse, without the use of TM for the entire 24-month duration of the study.

Study design and data collection

A qualitative study was conducted with the aim of evaluating the factors associated with the implementation of OPTIMUM TM system as perceived by healthcare professionals. Participants consisted of healthcare professionals who were involved in providing care to the patients enrolled in the OPTIMUM study. In‐depth interviews were conducted by the principal investigator or trained clinical research coordinator (CRC) in a quiet room or via the ZOOM video conference platform after obtaining their written consent from the CRC.

Using a semi-structured interview guide (online supplemental material 2) with open-ended questions, the interviewers sought information on the experiences of using the OPTIMUM TM system in managing the patients in the intervention arm. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed into scripts by a professional transcriber and subsequently audited for accuracy by the CRC.

Data analysis

Using thematic content analysis, the investigators first familiarised themselves with the transcripts. To ensure data objectivity and analytical rigour, two investigators independently coded a subset of the transcripts using NVivo V.13 software to develop an initial codebook. This codebook incorporated both deductive codes based on the CFIR constructs and inductive codes from emerging themes. Following the independent coding of all remaining transcripts, the investigators held regular consensus meetings to compare their coding matrices. Any discrepancies in code assignment or theme generation were resolved through negotiated consensus, with a third senior investigator available to adjudicate unresolved differences. Through this interactive process, a reliable final coding framework was established, and the finalised codes were mapped onto the domains of the CFIR framework. Briefly, CFIR provides a comprehensive understanding of the determinants of implementation-effectiveness of health service innovations, and it contains the five domains, namely, Intervention characteristics, Outer setting, Inner setting, Characteristics of individuals and Process.19 The descriptions of these domains, alongside our mapped findings, are illustrated in table 2 in the Results section.

Results

A total of nine healthcare professionals who were involved in the care provided to the patients in the OPTIMUM study were interviewed. They comprised two telehealth nurses and seven family physicians. The findings of this study (table 2) are classified according to the domains in the CFIR framework.

Intervention characteristics

The strengths of OPTIMUM TM system include the robustness of the OPTIMUM treatment algorithm, and its ability to drive behavioural change. For example, OPTIMUM algorithm allows patients to continuously self-monitor their glucose levels, supported by an automated reminder system and timely intervention by the TM nurse to provide personalised advice.

When the sugar is too high, it triggers the alert system when the nurse calls them. At least they know that how to change their behaviour quite immediately and identify what is the issue, for example I saw one, she just aware that her sugar went up after she eat her sushi, but she just loves sushi. What have we really had to discuss about how to go about to still be able to eat sushi, but still need to control the sugar level. (Participant 06)

Behavioural change was driven by visual cues that trigger reflection, supplemented by nudges, or motivational interviewing from the TM nurse, leading to patient empowerment and better self-care.

When they check, they have ownership of their own control, and then if it ties in with their own dietary intake and their activity level. They can correlate the findings with their behaviour. So, they will be more motivated in that sense. (Participant 08)

We just need to encourage them, we need to listen. We just listen and, we don’t condemn them. Either they agree or we tell them that what they should be doing. We asked them: “What do you think is the best idea?”. We asked them for the solution. Rather than we give them a solution. After all, they are adults, so they will tell us: “Yes, I think I should have not done that. I should be listening. I should be doing this way I should have actually cut down on my dinner, probably my lunch I eat little bit. I can eat my rice and then probably my dinner I just eat soup even so”. So probably there’ll be some changes. (Participant 09)

Additionally, two healthcare professionals suggested that AI (artificial intelligence) could further strengthen the OPTIMUM TM system, such as by supplementing the nurse’s role and providing AI-driven data analytics features.

For now, I think, we probably should still need a nurse to check on the patients. But in future, with further technological advancements, maybe the humans may be replaced. (Participant 02)

This technology or this ability to help us do … to interpret things without a human analysing it. So, for me, I think AI is still being developed. (Participant 04)

Outer setting

Factors related to external influence include manpower, cost to the patients and managing data security. Regarding manpower needs, suggestions vary on the number of patients a nurse can manage, which is determined by the workload rather than simply the patient count.

For example, if I’m only alerted if the results are abnormal right, then easily I could have like … 50-80 patients under me? Because … it depends on how poorly controlled they are. So, this is just a ball-park figure. Because I don't know how frequent they have to make abnormal calls, I think this one can be fed back from the current study. (Participant 07)

The cost to the patients should be considered, as the price patients are willing to pay, as perceived by healthcare professionals, varies, ranging from a few dollars to 30 Singapore dollars a month.

There is a need to be a fee for such services. Hopefully on a larger scale, it can reduce the cost. But I would think that generally patients will aspect these costs to be as low as possible. (Participant 02)

Lastly, data security should be considered to ensure that data is kept secure.

I think the risk of the patient’s confidentiality. Once I use my phone to access all these patients’ information, it is very risky. I would prefer a better, secure port, and system where I can monitor the patient. (Participant 06)

Inner setting

Some of the internal risks that require mitigation include managing potential adverse events or medicolegal risks related to TM, as well as the added burden to healthcare professionals.

I think the problem is for something like a tele-monitoring system, we wonder if let’s say the patient has a hypertensive event, or hypoglycaemia, something that is catastrophic. Will we be held liable? (Participant 04)

A small number of healthcare professionals expressed concerns about the added burden of interpreting more data and the extra time required for patient consultations.

The physician probably needs more time when the patient comes. They actually need to spend more time with the patient because number one: first of all, the patient will have more things to tell the doctor because of their experience, you know, they will share things. And number two: the doctor also got more things to refer to. So, you know, you can see the charts, you can analyse the points of concern, and would this translate to better care? (Participant 04)

Characteristics of individuals

Patients play an important role in the adoption of novel TM system. The most common factors that may influence patient acceptance include patients who are not IT savvy, especially older individuals or those with lower education levels, and non-English speakers who may have difficulty understanding a TM system that operates only in English. Other factors include the patients’ commitment or motivation and the inconvenience of using the TM system.

I say boils down to those patients who are inherently already quite motivated. It is a lot of self-motivation. There are patients who kind of play down the conditions. Patients who don’t seem to put too much emphasis on the on managing their diabetes, don’t see the importance of controlling their targets, then I would think that they are less likely to want to enrol into any topic tele-monitoring or may need more push to join the tele-monitoring. (Participant 08)

With that, there is a need to consider some strategies to improve acceptance, such as through patient engagement. Healthcare professionals shared some ideas to improve patient acceptance of the OPTIMUM system, which include publicity and leveraging pre-existing services to promote the programme.

If you do road shows, you need to tell them (patients) the benefits. Usually you can promote it (through) the nurse counselling room when the nurse can talk more about it, and we can enrol the patients from there. (Participant 09)

In addition, OPTIMUM TM system was noted to enhance the patient-healthcare professional relationship and contribute to overall staff satisfaction.

Through this study also I get to get in touch with patients more closely; I develop rapport with them, I get to know them much closer, and I get them to really trust me as in to work together with me hand in hand in their control of diabetes. (Participant 01)

And they (referring to patients) remember our names. So, it is very good. It is very delighting to know that when the patient calls me. So, any of the nurses would definitely be very happy when someone calls you by your name. So, the rapport, you will know you have done something well. (Participant 09)

Process

The process of implementing OPTIMUM TM system can be improved with further enhancement of user experiences for both the patients and healthcare professionals. Some of the feedback that may improve the users’ experience includes providing training on the use of the application, modifying how the data is displayed, and streamlining the navigation between the OPTIMUM TM system and the electronic medical record.

Some of them (referring to patients) say it’s not quite clear the data points that give them a graphical trend, they think it is quite messy, and they would prefer it to … some of the patients say something like a Fitbit or something like an Apple Watch where you can display it nicely in a graphical form for them. (Participant 05)

Maybe we could come up with induction slides or some form of a video that can teach the doctors how to use the application. (Participant 05)

We have to access through another laptop which is very tedious. We have to look at the EMR (electronic medical record) at the same time we have to access another laptop to look at their (patients’) vitals (parameters). (Participant 06)

Some participants foresee how the OPTIMUM TM system can be further enhanced to supplement usual care or be integrated into the current care delivery. The suggestions include additional in-app data collection to guide management plans, supplementing other telehealth services such as teleconsultations, and exploring future opportunities for better resource planning, such as centralised telehealth services and further strengthening of team-based care.

I’m just thinking if we want to consider, self-reported questionnaires or tools to assess mood, the only thing I can think of probably like using the PHQ tool which only has two questions, so that it will be easy for the patient. (Participant 02)

I think tele-monitoring will be helpful, and it can work well with video consultation, especially since we are moving forward to have more digital health. (Participant 02)

The suggestion that if there’s a central, so-called monitoring centre of maybe a group of nurses looking at the charts, in the sense that they can pick up something abnormal, and then subsequently transmit anything abnormal to the team. It might be a better division of labour, in a sense. (Participant 04)

I can see that we can work in terms of triaging the patients and, and better manage the patients within the team-based care. (Participant 06)

Discussion

Summary of the principal findings

Interviews with healthcare professionals involved in the care of patients via the OPTIMUM TM system revealed several perceived strengths of the interventions. Notably, the system was recognised for its effectiveness in influencing patient behaviour through a robust treatment algorithm. External factors to be considered during large-scale implementation include optimal manpower support, costs incurred by patients and management of data security. Internal factors requiring mitigation include the additional burden on healthcare professionals and potential medicolegal risks due to adverse events related to TM. While the OPTIMUM TM system improved patient-healthcare professional relationships and staff satisfaction, there is a need to include strategies to increase its adoption. The process can be further strengthened through the refinement of user experience for healthcare professionals and by integrating the OPTIMUM TM system to supplement current care delivery.

Comparison to other studies

The findings of this study are consistent with those of a meta-synthesis where the use of TM was found to improve care, such as self-care and the ability to provide individualised treatment plans, and to enhance communication between healthcare providers and patients, as well as among staff.20 While TM was perceived as convenient and acceptable, there were mixed opinions regarding its utility, as participants faced difficulties with using the devices, the functionality of the devices, and the need for cultural adoption of the TM system.20 These mixed opinions can be better understood through the lens of established technology acceptance frameworks, such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT).21 Research using a modified UTAUT2 model indicates that healthcare professionals will accept and use a telemedicine system primarily if they perceive that it will positively affect their clinical performance.22 Furthermore, studies examining the predictive factors of telemedicine acceptance confirm that perceived ease of use directly impacts both perceived usefulness and the behavioural intention to use the system.23 In the context of our findings, the initial learning curve—manifesting as difficulties with device functionality—likely lowered practitioners’ self-efficacy and perceived ease of use, which in turn diminished their perception of the system’s overall utility. Therefore, the mixed opinions observed are less about inherent practitioner heterogeneity or a lack of digital literacy, and more about the initial cognitive friction and workflow disruptions. To overcome this initial barrier, standard training is insufficient; instead, interventions must focus on enhancing user self-efficacy, ensuring seamless system integration to improve record accessibility, and providing clear incentives that demonstrate the system’s positive impact on clinical performance.23 24

Implications to clinical settings

The RCT findings of the OPTIMUM TM system, published elsewhere, demonstrated improvements in patient outcomes, including diabetes control, medication compliance and self-care, with effects sustained for up to 18 months postintervention.10–12 Additionally, the OPTIMUM TM system was shown to be cost-effective.25 While the OPTIMUM TM system is proven to be clinically and cost-effective, the findings of this qualitative study further contribute to an overview of factors to be considered for the implementation of the OPTIMUM TM system on a larger scale. A systematic review suggested the following domains to be considered for the implementation planning of a TM programme: (1) fidelity and adaptability, (2) setting and workforce, (3) sustainability, (4) reach and acceptability and (5) infrastructure.26

To bridge our qualitative analysis with practical scale-up strategies, we developed a conceptual flowchart (figure 1). This flowchart demonstrates how the specific findings mapped onto the CFIR framework are subsequently translated into actionable measures across these five implementation domains. Based on this mapping, several targeted strategies can be put in place. To ensure fidelity and adaptability, user design must be refined to accommodate diverse patient demographics and simplify the technology to help older patients overcome the learning curve. In terms of setting and workforce, optimising nursing manpower through the establishment of a centralised monitoring centre is essential to manage resources efficiently. To promote sustainability, focusing on workload management and refining care processes will reduce the burden on healthcare professionals. For broad reach and acceptability, implementation strategies must engage eligible patients through targeted publicity emphasising the benefits of TM. Simultaneously, securing buy-in from healthcare professionals is critical. As scaling requires accounting for practitioner heterogeneity, targeted change management, comprehensive training and phased rollouts are necessary to overcome technology anxiety and workflow reluctance among late adopters. Finally, robust infrastructure modifications are required to integrate the TM system seamlessly into existing IT frameworks. The reported tediousness of using separate laptops alongside the EMR was a temporary technical hurdle specific to the standalone platform used during the RCT. Nevertheless, this feedback underscores that full interoperability with existing EMRs is a fundamental system architecture requirement to eliminate workflow duplication. Effectively navigating these inherent tensions between technological innovation and operational stability is crucial for the successful integration of digital health solutions into routine primary care.27

Implications to future research

With an ageing population and an increasing chronic disease burden globally, the use of technology-based solutions has developed rapidly in the past decade.28 The OPTIMUM TM system was introduced at an opportune moment to enhance healthcare delivery efficiency, as Singapore is projected to become a ‘super-aged’ society by 2026, with one in five residents aged 65 or older.29 Using a rule-based algorithm, the OPTIMUM TM system allows nurses to monitor more patients and intervene when necessary, as prompted by the system according to different levels of urgency. However, as highlighted in our findings, the continuous influx of self-monitored clinical parameters significantly increases the cognitive workload and data interpretation burden on healthcare professionals. Therefore, future enhancements to the OPTIMUM TM system must integrate AI not merely as a technical add-on, but as an essential solution to mitigate this cognitive strain. As recent literature highlights, AI-driven analytics are pivotal in shaping the future of healthcare by synthesising large volumes of incoming data into actionable insights and improving operational efficiency.30 Virtual health assistants, such as chatbots, can handle routine patient coaching and education. By automating these processes and providing concise data summaries, AI will allow healthcare professionals to focus their cognitive resources on complex clinical decision-making, ultimately ensuring the sustainability of the TM system without compromising staff well-being.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its use of qualitative methods, which provide both breadth and depth of data useful for understanding the factors related to the implementation of the OPTIMUM TM system. It also allows for the generation of ideas from users to further enhance the OPTIMUM TM system.

Although this study interviewed most of the healthcare professionals who participated in providing care for patients enrolled in the OPTIMUM study, the findings may not be generalisable to other healthcare professionals. The participants may be early adopters who were willing to learn about TM and are motivated to adopt the new technology in their daily practice. In addition, we did not interview the patients recruited into the OPTIMUM study using the CFIR framework. Nonetheless, previous results, where patients recruited into the OPTIMUM study were interviewed using the health belief model to understand how the OPTIMUM TM system modifies health behaviour, complement this study.13

Conclusion

The OPTIMUM TM system has demonstrated its strength in changing health-seeking behaviour, its treatment algorithm and in improving healthcare professionals’ satisfaction and the therapeutic relationship with patients. For large-scale implementation, factors to be considered include enhancing user experience, refining care processes, manpower planning, costing and strategies to increase adoption. The potential of AI to include data-analytic functions and virtual health assistants can be further integrated into the OPTIMUM TM system in the future.
