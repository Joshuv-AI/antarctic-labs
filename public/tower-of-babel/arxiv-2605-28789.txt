1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Setup and background

We consider the
focusing Calogero–Sutherland derivative NLS
which can be written as

\boxed{i\partial_{t}u=-\partial_{x}^{2}u-2D\Pi(|u|^{2})u\quad\mbox{with}\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}}

(CS)

Here
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
denotes the one-dimensional torus and we denote
D=-i\partial_{x}
. The operator
\Pi
stands for the Cauchy–Szegő projector, i.e.,

\Pi\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{u}(n)\mathrm{e}^{inx}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\widehat{u}(n)\mathrm{e}^{inx}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widehat{u}(n)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}u(x)\mathrm{e}^{-inx}\,dx,

which is the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
onto the Hardy space

L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\mid\mbox{$\widehat{u}(n)=0$ for $n<0$}\right\}.

We recall the essential fact that elements in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
can be canonically identified via boundary values with the space of holomorphic functions
f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
with
\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|^{2}<\infty
. In our analysis, we will strongly make use of this classical fact; see
[
GMR16
]
for a general background on Hardy spaces.

Let us mention that (
CS
) formally arises from considering the continuum limit of discrete Calogero–Moser systems, which form an intriguing class of classically completely integrable systems; see
[
ABW09
]
for a formal derivation of (
CS
) in the physics literature as well as
[
Mat23
]
for a formal analysis of so-called multiphase solutions. With regard to physical applications, let us also mention that the defocusing variant of (
CS
), where the sign ‘
-
’ in front of nonlinearity is changed to ‘
+
’, previously derived in
[
PG95
]
under the name ‘intermediate nonlinear Schrödinger equation’ as an effective model in fluid dynamics.

For the mathematical study of (
CS
), the first rigorous result in the space-periodic setting were obtained by Badreddine in
[
Bad24
,
Bad25
]
. More
precisely, she established the following results.

•

Lax pair structure
on the Hardy space
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
with infinitely many conserved quantities.

•

Global well-posedness
in
H^{s}_{+}(\mathbb{T}):=L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\cap H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
for any
s\geq 0
below the critical
L^{2}
-mass threshold

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}<1.

(1.1)

•

Derivation of an
explicit formula
for solutions of (
CS
).

•

Classification of traveling solitary waves and
finite-gap potentials
.

The role of condition (
1.1
), with the explicit formula, is pivotal in
[
Bad24
]
. In the present paper, we will use the explicit formula as the main tool used to construct smooth finite-time blow-up solutions of (
CS
) with initial data for which the smallness condition (
1.1
) fails. We also mention the further recent work
[
ABCD26
]
, where the explicit formula for (
CS
) is used to design a new numerical scheme.

Comparison to the real line problem

When
\mathbb{T}
is replaced by
\mathbb{R}
, we remark that (
CS
) is usually referred to as the focusing

Calogero–Moser derivative NLS
given by

i\partial_{t}u=-\partial_{x}^{2}u-2D\Pi_{\mathbb{R}}(|u|^{2})u\quad\mbox{with}\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},

(CM)

with the corresponding Cauchy–Szegő projector on the real line, i.e., in Fourier space we have

\widehat{(\Pi_{\mathbb{R}}{f})}(\xi)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi\geq 0\}}\widehat{f}(\xi)\quad\mbox{for $f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})$}.

Not surprisingly, it turns out that (
CM
) also features a Lax pair structure on the corresponding Hardy space

L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mid\mathrm{supp}\,\widehat{f}\subset[0,\infty)\}.

In fact, the body of mathematical results on (
CM
) is much more advanced when compared to (
CS
). For the reader’s convenience, we give a brief summary as follows. The rigorous mathematical analysis of (
CM
) was initiated in
[
GL24
]
, where global well-posedness in
H^{s}_{+}(\mathbb{R})=L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})\cap H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
with
s\geq 1
was shown when
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\pi
, together with the explicit construction of weakly turbulent
N
-solitons blowing up at infinite time. Later on, global well-posedness in the scaling-critical space
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<2\pi
was obtained in
[
KLV25
]
by means of an explicit formula. More recently, smooth finite-time blow-up solutions for (
CM
) were first constructed in
[
KKK24
]
by a delicate perturbative analysis for smooth initial data in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
with slightly supercritical
L^{2}
-mass; see also
[
HK24
]
for another blow-up result. Also, we mention the result in
[
KK24
]
on soliton resolution for (
CM
). Further blow-up results for so-called non-chiral solutions, i.e., when the Hardy space assumption on the initial data is dropped, were obtained in
[
JK24
,
KKK24
]
. More recently, Frank and Read proposed an inverse scattering transform for (
CM
)
[
FR25
]
.

Strategy of proofs

However, the existing arguments for proving finite-time blow-up for (
CM
) do not seem to offer any insight into a possible construction of blow-up solutions for (
CS
). To overcome this, we develop an alternative approach which strongly rests on the explicit formula found for (
CS
). In this regard, let us mention that the use of explicit formulae for integrable PDEs with Lax pairs on Hardy spaces (e.g. the Benjamin–Ono equation, the cubic Szegő equation, CS, CM, and the half-wave maps equation) have turned out to be extremely powerful, yielding results on scaling-critical global well-posedness, weak turbulence, zero-dispersion limits, classification of solitary waves, and soliton resolution; see
[
Bad24
,
Bad25
,
Che25
,
Gas23
,
GGM26
,
Ger24
,
GG15
,
GL24
,
GL25
,
GL26
,
GP23
,
GP24
,
HK24
,
KLV25
,
Mae26
]
.

In the approach taken below, we construct a family of smooth finite-time blow-up solutions within a subclass of finite-gap
potentials. More precisely, we identify a resonant algebraic condition on the coefficients, and this resonance produces finite-time blow-up solutions. Furthermore, the explicit formula allows us to compute the complete dynamics of the finite-time blow-up. In the complementary non-resonant case, we prove global existence and uniform-in-time Sobolev bounds within the same finite-gap family. Thus the finite-dimensional class considered here exhibits a sharp resonance/non-resonance dichotomy. To the best of our knowledge, this gives the first construction of finite-time blow-up solutions for the focusing Calogero–Sutherland DNLS equation. A distinctive feature of the construction is that the blow-up mechanism is obtained directly from the explicit formula. In our companion work
[
CL26
]
, we implement this strategy of constructing finite-time blow-up via an explicit formula for (
CM
) posed on the non-compact domain
\mathbb{R}
.

1.2.
Class of initial data

We consider the following class of initial data given by

\boxed{u_{0}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}z^{m}\beta_{p}(z)\left(a+\frac{c}{1-\bar{p}z}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\beta_{p}(z)=\frac{z-p}{1-\bar{p}z}}

(1.2)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
p\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
are given and the parameters
a,c\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy the nonlinear constraint

a\bar{c}+\frac{|c|^{2}}{1-|p|^{2}}=2.

(1.3)

Clearly, any initial datum
u_{0}
is a rational function with no poles inside the closed unit disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\{|z|\leq 1\}
. Hence we infer that
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})=L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\cap H^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
holds.

In fact, the initial data
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
of the form above belong to the larger set of so-called
finite-gap potentials
, which have been introduced and studied in
[
Bad25
]
. However, for our purposes here, it will be convenient to restrict our attention to the special subclass of finite-gap potentials as given by (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) above.

1

1

a,c,p
differs from
[
Bad25
]
by complex conjugation.
The reason why such data
u_{0}
are referred to as finite-gap potentials stems from the fact that the corresponding self-adjoint Lax operator

L_{u_{0}}=D-T_{u_{0}}T_{\bar{u}_{0}}

(1.4)

on the Hardy space
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
exhibits special spectral properties involving finite-dimensional subspaces invariant under
L_{u_{0}}
; see Section
3
below for details. In fact, these features of
L_{u_{0}}
will enable us to completely analyze the dynamics of the corresponding solution
u(t)
via the explicit formula for (
CS
) found in
[
Bad24
]
.

1.3.
Finite-time blow-up

We are now ready to formulate the first main result of this paper, which provides an explicit family of smooth finite-time blow-up solutions for (
CS
). To this end, we consider initial data
u_{0}
of the form (
1.2
), where in addition to (
1.3
) we also impose the resonant condition

2a+c=0\,.

(1.5)

By an elementary calculation using (
1.3
) and (
1.5
), we find that

a=\mathrm{e}^{i\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1-|p|^{2}}{1+|p|^{2}}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad c=-2\mathrm{e}^{i\phi}\sqrt{\frac{1-|p|^{2}}{1+|p|^{2}}}

(1.6)

for some
\phi\in\mathbb{T}
. By absorbing the constant phase
\mathrm{e}^{i\phi}
into the factor
\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}
, we are led to initial data of the following form

u_{0}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\sqrt{\frac{1-|p|^{2}}{1+|p|^{2}}}z^{m}\beta_{p}(z)\left(1-\frac{2}{1-\bar{p}z}\right)

(1.7)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and
p\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
can be chosen arbitrarily.

We have the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
be the finite-gap potential given by
(
1.7
) with
\theta\in\mathbb{T},m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},
and
p\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
. Set

r:=|p|^{2},\qquad\rho:=|p|,\qquad\gamma:=\sqrt{\frac{1-r}{1+r}},\qquad a:=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\gamma,

and

T:=\frac{\pi(1-r)}{4\rho}.

(1.8)

Then the solution
u(t)
of (
CS
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
belongs to
C([0,T);H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
and blows up at
t=T
such that

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\sim\sqrt{\Gamma(2s+1)}\left(\frac{(1+r)^{3}}{4r(1-r)}\right)^{s}\frac{1}{(T-t)^{2s}}\quad\mbox{as $t\nearrow T$}.

(1.9)

for every
s>0
. Furthermore, the following hold.

(i)

Blow-up dynamics.

We have that

u(t,z)-\frac{\beta_{m}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{m}(t)z}\longrightarrow u_{*,m}(z)\quad\mbox{in $H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$ as $t\nearrow T$},

(1.10)

where the functions
\alpha_{m}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{D}
and
\beta_{m}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{C}
satisfy

|\alpha_{m}(t)|^{2}=1-c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

(1.11)

|\beta_{m}(t)|^{2}=c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

(1.12)

with the positive constant

c_{0}:=\frac{4r(1-r)}{(1+r)^{3}}.

(1.13)

(ii)

Blow-up remainder and weak limit.

The limit profile in (
1.10
) is explicitly given by

u_{*,m}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}z^{m}\left[ap+ia\rho(1+r)\frac{z\mathrm{e}^{-i(\Theta_{*}+2mT)}}{1-i\rho\bar{p}\,z\mathrm{e}^{-i(\Theta_{*}+2mT)}}\right],

(1.14)

with

\Theta_{*}:=\frac{(1+r)\pi}{4\rho}.

(1.15)

In addition,

u(t)\rightharpoonup u_{*,m}\quad\mbox{weakly in $L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$ as $t\nearrow T$}.

(1.16)

The profile
u_{*,m}
is a traveling solitary wave profile for (
CS
).

(iii)

Quantization of blow-up mass.

We have that

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|u_{*,m}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1.

(1.17)

i.e., the blow-up part in (i) exactly concentrates an
L^{2}
-mass equal to 1.

Remarks
.

1) For the
L^{2}
-mass of the blow-up initial data
u_{0}
in (
1.7
), a direct calculation shows that

1<\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1+3|p|^{2}}{1+|p|^{2}}<2

as
|p|
ranges over
(0,1)
, this covers the entire interval
(1,2)
. This underlines the fact that our approach is non-perturbative. In particular, we construct finite-time blow-up solutions for (
CS
) that are not necessarily close to the critical threshold
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1
, which is provided by the global well-posedness result in
[
Bad24
]
. In particular, our blow-up result above shows that the threshold
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1
is almost optimal.

2) The proof of Theorem
1.1
will be first derived for the case
m=0
. The cases
m\geq 1
then easily follow by applying a discrete Galilean-type symmetry of (
CS
); see below.

We note that the blow-up time in Theorem
1.1
satisfies
T\to+\infty
as
p\to 0
. Correspondingly, we find that the initial data converge as

u_{0}(z)\to\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}z^{m+1}\quad\mbox{in $H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$}\quad\mbox{as}\quad p\to 0\,,

which are profiles of traveling solitary waves of plane-wave type for (
CS
) with
L^{2}
-mass equal to 1; see
[
Bad25
]
. As a direct consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 1.2
.

For any integer
N\geq 1
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, the traveling solitary waves of plane-wave type

u(t,z)=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\mathrm{e}^{-iN^{2}t}z^{N}

solving (
CS
) are unstable in the
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
-topology under perturbations leading to finite-time blow-up.

1.4.
On global existence and instability of blow-up

We now discuss initial data
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
given by (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) but subject to the non-resonant condition

2a+c\neq 0\,.

(1.18)

We complement the blow-up result in Theorem
1.1
by showing the following result on global existence along with a priori bounds on all Sobolev norms for finite-gap potentials as initial data as above.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
is a finite-gap potential of the form (
1.2
), where (
1.3
) and (
1.18
) hold. Then the corresponding solution
u\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
of (
CS
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
exists globally in time and satisfies the a priori bounds

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim_{u_{0},s}1

for every
s>0
.

Remark
.

A calculation shows that the
L^{2}
-mass of the initial data in Theorem
1.3
covers the entire range
(1,\infty)
, i.e., for every
\ell\in(1,\infty)
we can find
a,c\in\mathbb{C}
and
p\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\ell
with
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
as in (
1.2
) such that (

) and (

) hold. Hence we obtain global existence for a class of initial data with arbitrarily large

-mass. A further analysis (which we omit to shorten the paper) yields that

is quasi-periodic in time and, for special choice of

, we obtain exact periodicity in

.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem

, we deduce that the blow-up solutions in Theorem

.

) and (

u_{0,k}\to u_{0}

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Local well-posdeness and explicit formula

We first recall some results from
[
Bad24
]
concerning the local well-posedness of (
CS
) in
H^{s}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
with
s>\frac{3}{2}
, which in turn is based on a Kato-type iteration scheme in
[
GL24
]
for the corresponding result posed on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., the Calogero–Moser derivative (
CM
) above. For smooth initial data, we obtain the following local well-posedness result together with an infinite set of conversed quantities.

Lemma 2.1

(LWP and conserved quantities)
.

The initial-value problem for (
CS
) is locally well-posed in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. That is, for every
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, there exists a unique solution
u\in C(I;H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
with
u(0)=u_{0}
, where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
denotes corresponding the maximal time interval of existence. Moreover, we have the conserved quantities

I_{k}[u]=\langle u,L_{u}^{k}u\rangle\quad\mbox{for $k=0,1,2,3,\ldots$}

where
L_{u}=D-T_{u}T_{\bar{u}}
is the self-adjoint Lax operator defined in (
2.2
) below.

Finally, if the forward maximal time of existence
T_{\max}(u_{0})=\sup I<+\infty
is finite, then

\lim_{t\nearrow T_{\max}}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1/2}}=+\infty.

Likewise, if
T_{\min}(u_{0})=\inf I>-\infty
then
\lim_{t\searrow T_{\min}}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1/2}}=+\infty
.

Remark
.

Note that
I_{0}[u]=\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
corresponds to the
L^{2}
-mass,
I_{1}[u]=\langle u,Du\rangle-\|T_{\bar{u}}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
is the gauged momentum for (
CS
), and

I_{2}[u]=\langle u,L^{2}_{u}u\rangle=\langle L_{u}u,L_{u}u\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|Du-u\Pi(|u|^{2})|^{2}\,dx

is (up to trivial constant factor) equal to the energy for (
CS
).

Proof.

By
[
Bad24
]
(based on the adaptation of the iterative arguments in
[
GL24
]
), we obtain local well-posed in
H^{s}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
for every
s>\frac{3}{2}
. By persistence of regularity, we obtain the claimed result in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
. The conservation of the quantities
I_{k}[u]
follow from the Lax equation

\frac{d}{dt}L_{u(t)}=[B_{u(t)},L_{u(t)}]=[\tilde{B}_{u(t)},L_{u(t)}]

with
B_{u}=T_{u}T_{\partial_{x}\bar{u}}-T_{\partial_{x}u}T_{\bar{u}}+i(T_{u}T_{\bar{u}})^{2}
.

It remains to justify the
H^{1/2}
-blow-up alternative. We prove the forward-time
statement; the backward-time statement is identical. Let

T_{\max}:=\sup I<+\infty.

Suppose, by contradiction, that

\lim_{t\nearrow T_{\max}}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{T})}\neq+\infty.

Then there exist
t_{n}\nearrow T_{\max}
and
M>0
such that

\|u(t_{n})\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq M.

Using the conserved quantities
I_{k}[u]
and arguing exactly as in the proof of

[
GL24
, Corollary 2.1]
, the
H^{1/2}
-bound at the times
t_{n}
, together with the previously obtained lower Sobolev bounds, controls the lower order terms in the expansion

I_{k}[u]=\|u\|_{\dot{H}^{k/2}}^{2}+\mbox{lower order terms}.

Consequently, for every integer
N\geq 1
,

\sup_{n}\|u(t_{n})\|_{H^{N}(\mathbb{T})}<+\infty.

In particular,

\sup_{n}\|u(t_{n})\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T})}<+\infty.

The local well-posedness theory in
H^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
gives a lifespan depending only on the

H^{2}
-norm of the initial datum. Hence, for
n
large enough, the solution with initial
datum
u(t_{n})
can be continued beyond
T_{\max}
, contradicting the maximality of

I
. Therefore

\lim_{t\nearrow T_{\max}}\|u(t)\|_{H^{1/2}(\mathbb{T})}=+\infty.

The proof is complete.
∎

Here,

denotes the constant function on
\mathbb{T}
with value 1. Furthermore, the operator
S^{*}:L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
denotes the
backward shift
whose action on
f=\sum_{n\geq 0}\widehat{f}_{n}z^{n}\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
is given by

S^{*}f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\widehat{f}_{n+1}z^{n},

which equivalently can be written as

S^{*}f(z)=\frac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}.

Its adjoint
S=(S^{*})^{*}
is the
forward shift
with
Sf(z)=zf(z)
and we readily find the classical identities

S^{*}S=\mathrm{Id},\quad SS^{*}=\mathrm{Id}-\langle\cdot,1\rangle.

In (
2.1
), we recall that
L_{u_{0}}
denotes the Lax operator formally defined as

L_{u_{0}}=D-T_{u_{0}}T_{\bar{u}_{0}},

(2.2)

where
D=-i\partial_{x}
and
T_{\phi}:L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
stands for the Toeplitz operator with symbol
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
given by

T_{\phi}f=\Pi(\phi f)\quad\mbox{for $f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$},

where
\Pi
is the Cauchy–Szegő projection onto the Hardy space
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Since
T_{u_{0}}T_{\bar{u}_{0}}
is bounded and self-adjoint, it directly follows from the Kato–Rellich theorem that the Lax operator
L_{u_{0}}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
with the operator domain
\mathrm{Dom}(L_{u_{0}})=H^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. As a consequence of this, the operator
\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}
is a unitary map on
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.2.
Stability principle and blow-up criterion

In order to gain insight into possible blow-up vs. global existence of solutions for (
CS
), we will study the mapping properties of the right-hand side in (
2.1
) by following the approach taken in
[
GL26
]
developed for general explicit formulae on
L^{2}
-based Hardy spaces. Thus, for
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
given, we introduce the family of operators indexed by
t\in\mathbb{R}
given by

\Sigma_{t}^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*}:L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T}),

and we rewrite the explicit formula (
2.1
) as

u(t,z)=\mathcal{M}\left((\mathrm{Id}-z\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{-1}u_{0}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad(t,z)\in I\times\mathbb{D}.

(2.3)

Here

\mathcal{M}f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f\,=\langle f,1\rangle=f(0)

denotes the mean of
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
and
\mathcal{M}:L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathbb{C}
corresponds to the orthogonal projection in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
onto the subspace
\mathbb{C}\subset L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
of constant functions on
\mathbb{T}
. Since
\Sigma_{t}^{*}=\Omega_{t}S^{*}
with the unitary operator
\Omega_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}
, we can readily check that

\Sigma_{t}^{*}\Sigma_{t}=\mathrm{Id},\quad\Sigma_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{*}=\mathrm{Id}-\mathcal{M}.

In particular, we see that
\Sigma_{t}=S\Omega_{t}^{*}
is an isometry, i.e., we have
\|\Sigma_{t}f\|_{L^{2}}=\|f\|_{L^{2}}
for all
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. We also note that
\mathrm{Ker}(\Sigma_{t}^{*})=\mathrm{Ran}(\mathcal{M})=\mathbb{C}
. Following the notation in
[
GL26
]
, we define the family of maps
\mathscr{U}(t):L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
and defined as

(\mathscr{U}(t)f)(z)=\mathcal{M}\left((\mathrm{Id}-z\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{-1}f\right)\quad\mbox{for $f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$ and $z\in\mathbb{D}$}.

(2.4)

From
[
GL26
, Section 8]
we infer the general property

\mathscr{U}(t_{n})f\rightharpoonup\mathscr{U}(t)f
weakly in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
for all
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
as
t_{n}\to t

(2.5)

together with the following result.

Lemma 2.2

(Stability principle)
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, we have

\|\mathscr{U}(t)f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{n}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Moreover, the map
\mathscr{U}(t)
is
unitary
on
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if the discrete semigroup
\{(\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
is
strongly stable
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{n}f\|_{L^{2}}=0\quad\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$ and $t\in\mathbb{R}$}.

In this case, the map
f\mapsto\mathscr{U}(t)f
belongs to
C(\mathbb{R};L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
for any
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
.

Remark
.

Since
\Sigma_{t}^{*}
is a contraction, the non-negative sequence
\|(\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{n}f\|_{L^{2}}
is monotone decreasing, whence
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(\Sigma_{t}^{*})^{n}f\|_{L^{2}}
exists for any
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
.

With the aid of Lemma
2.2
, we deduce the following sufficient and necessary criterion for global-in-time existence for smooth solutions of (
CS
), which we refer to as the blow-up criterion via the explicit formula.

Lemma 2.3

(Blow-up criterion via stability principle)
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
and denote by
u\in C(I;H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
its corresponding maximal solution of (
CS
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

I=\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*})^{n}f\|_{L^{2}}=0
for all
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*})^{n}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remarks
.

1) We could generalize the criterion to initial data
u_{0}\in H^{s}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
with
s>\frac{3}{2}
, which guarantees a strong local well-posedness theory for (
CS
). But since we consider smooth initial data, we omit this here.

2) It is a remarkable fact that the strong stability of the discrete semigroup
\{(\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*})^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
on the initial datum
u_{0}
, i.e., statement (iii) also implies (ii). Its proof exploits the set of infinitely many conserved quantities of smooth solutions of (
CS
); see below.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
. Suppose
u\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
is a global smooth solution of (
CS
) with
u(0)=u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. We consider the operator-valued initial-value problem

\frac{d}{dt}U(t)=B_{u(t)}U(t)\quad\mbox{for $t\in\mathbb{R}$},\quad U(0)=\mathrm{Id},

(2.6)

with the bounded skew-adjoint operator
B_{u}=T_{u}T_{\partial_{x}\bar{u}}-T_{\partial_{x}u}T_{\bar{u}}+i(T_{u}T_{\bar{u}})^{2}
on
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, which comes from the Lax pair structure for (
CS
) used in
[
Bad24
]
. Note that
U(t)
is a unitary map on
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. From
[
Bad24
, Proof of Proposition 2.6]
, which based on the commutation relations between
S^{*}
and
B_{u_{0}}
, we infer, for any
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
,

(U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}f)(z)=\left\langle(\mathrm{Id}-z\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*})^{-1}f,1\right\rangle\quad\mbox{for $(t,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{D}$.}

Thus the unitary map
U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}
identifies to the map
\mathscr{U}(t):L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
introduced above. By Lemma
2.2
with
\Sigma_{t}^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*}
, we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*})^{n}f\|_{L^{2}}=0\quad\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$ and $t\in\mathbb{R}$},

which is clearly equivalent to statement (ii).

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
. This is obviously true.

(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
. Let
u\in C(I;H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
be the maximal solution of (
CS
) with
u(0)=u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. From the explicit formula, we know that

u(t,z)=(\mathscr{U}(t)u_{0})(z)\quad\quad\mbox{for $(t,z)\in I\times\mathbb{D}$}.

Assume now that (iii) holds, but we have
I\neq\mathbb{R}
. Suppose now that
T=\sup I<+\infty
is finite and let
t_{n}\in I
be a sequence with
t_{n}\to T
. By Lemma
2.1
, this implies that

\|u(t_{n})\|_{H^{1/2}}\to+\infty.

(2.7)

On the other hand, from (
2.5
) we see that
u(t_{n})=\mathscr{U}(t_{n})u_{0}\rightharpoonup\mathscr{U}(T)u_{0}
weakly in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. By assumption (iii) together with
L^{2}
-conservation and the identity in Lemma
2.2
, we conclude that equality
\|\mathscr{U}(T)u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}
holds. Hence the sequence
u(t_{n})
converges strongly in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Thus we can exploit the following tightness property using Fourier series. That is, for every
\eta>0
there exists some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

u_{\leq N}(t_{n}):=\sum_{0\leq k\leq N}\widehat{u}_{k}(t_{n})\mathrm{e}^{ikx},\quad u_{>N}(t_{n}):=\sum_{k>N}\widehat{u}_{k}(t_{n})\mathrm{e}^{ikx}

satisfy

\|u_{\leq N}(t_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\lesssim N\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\|u_{>N}(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\eta\quad\mbox{for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$}.

Next, from
[
Bad24
]
, we recall the estimate

\|T_{\bar{g}}h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left(\langle Dh,h\rangle+\|h\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

for
g\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
and
h\in H^{1/2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. This yields

\displaystyle\|T_{\bar{u}(t_{n})}u(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|T_{\bar{u}_{\leq N}(t_{n})}u(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|T_{\bar{u}_{>N}(t_{n})}u(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim N\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\eta\langle Du(t_{n}),u(t_{n})\rangle.

Taking
\eta>0
sufficiently small, the conservation of
I_{1}
implies that

I_{1}[u_{0}]=I_{1}[u(t_{n})]=\langle Du(t_{n}),u(t_{n})\rangle-\|T_{\bar{u}(t_{n})}u(t_{n})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\langle Du(t_{n}),u(t_{n})\rangle-C\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

with some constant
C>0
independent of
n
. This shows that the sequence
\|u(t_{n})\|_{H^{1/2}}
is bounded, which contradicts (
2.7
). Therefore, we conclude that
\sup I=+\infty
. The proof that
\inf I=-\infty
holds is analogous.
∎

Lemma
2.3
immediately yields a useful finite-time blow-up criterion.
Indeed, the failure of strong stability is automatic if, for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, the contraction

A_{\tau}:=\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{u_{0}}}S^{*}

has a
unimodular
eigenvalue, i.e., an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
of
A_{\tau}
with
|\lambda|=1
. This is the form of the criterion that will be used
below, because the finite-gap analysis reduces the problem to finding unimodular
eigenvalues of
A_{\tau}
on a finite-dimensional subspace.

Lemma 2.4

(Unimodular eigenvalue blow-up criterion)
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, and let

u\in C(I;H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))

be the corresponding maximal smooth solution of (
CS
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
.
Assume that there exists a time
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
such that

A_{\tau}:=\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{u_{0}}}S^{*}

(2.8)

has a unimodular eigenvalue on
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Then the solution cannot be global, i.e.,

I\neq\mathbb{R}.

In particular, the initial datum
u_{0}
generates a finite-time blow-up solution of (
CS
).

Proof.

Suppose, by contradiction, that the solution is global so that

I=\mathbb{R}.

By Lemma
2.3
, global existence implies the strong stability
condition

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\left(\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*}\right)^{n}f\right\|_{L^{2}}=0\qquad\mbox{for all }f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\mbox{ and all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Now assume that
A_{\tau}
has a unimodular eigenvalue. Then there exists

\phi\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\setminus\{0\},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\qquad|\lambda|=1,

such that

A_{\tau}\phi=\lambda\phi.

Hence, for every
n\geq 0
,

A_{\tau}^{n}\phi=\lambda^{n}\phi,

and therefore

\|A_{\tau}^{n}\phi\|_{L^{2}}=|\lambda|^{n}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}\neq 0.

This contradicts the strong stability condition with
t=\tau
and
f=\phi
. Thus the maximal solution cannot be global, and the initial datum
u_{0}
leads to a
finite-time blow-up solution of (
CS
).
∎

Remark
.

The criterion above should be understood only as a sufficient condition for finite-time
blow-up solutions. At present, we do not know whether it is also necessary; in particular, we cannot prove that every initial datum leading to finite-time blow-up solution must produce a unimodular eigenvalue of

A_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*}

for some time
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In the resonant finite-gap family studied below, however,
this criterion is effective: we will explicitly find such a unimodular eigenvalue and then
use the explicit formula (
2.3
) to study the blow-up dynamics.

In Section
4.1
and Section
5
of the paper we apply Lemma
2.4
and
Lemma
2.3
to the finite-gap potentials introduced in
(
1.2
) with
m=0
. In the resonant case
2a+c=0
, we shall prove that
A_{\tau}
has a
unimodular eigenvalue at an explicit time
\tau
. Then from Lemma
2.4
, we shall identify finite-time
blow-up initial data. In the non-resonant case
2a+c\neq 0
, we shall instead prove strong stability of
A_{t}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, which yields global existence of solutions.

2.3.
Galilean-type transformation

Lemma
2.4
detects finite-time blow-up from the spectral behavior of

\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{u_{0}}}S^{*}
. We shall also use a simple covariance of (
CS
), which
allows us to reduce the shifted data
z^{m}v_{0}
to the core case
m=0
. Thus, in the
rest of the paper, the spectral and dynamical computations will be carried out for the
two-dimensional core block, and the cases
m\geq 1
will be obtained by the following
Galilean-type transformation.

Lemma 2.5

(Galilean-type covariance)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval with
0\in I
and suppose that

v\in C(I;H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))

be a smooth solution of (
CS
) with initial datum
v(0)=v_{0}\in H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. For every
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, define

u^{(m)}(t,z):=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}t}z^{m}v(t,\mathrm{e}^{-2imt}z),\qquad t\in I,\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(2.9)

Then

u^{(m)}\in C(I;H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))

is also a smooth solution of (
CS
) with initial datum
u^{(m)}(0,z)=z^{m}v_{0}(z)
.

Moreover, for every
s\geq 0
and
t\in I
, it holds

c_{m,s}\|v(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|u^{(m)}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{m,s}\|v(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}

(2.10)

with some positive constants
C_{m,s}\geq c_{m,s}>0
depending only on
m
and
s
. For
s=0
, the transformation is an isometry:

\|u^{(m)}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}=\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

(2.11)

Proof.

We first prove the covariance. In the
x
-variable, (
2.9
)
reads

u^{(m)}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{imx-im^{2}t}v(t,x-2mt).

Set

y:=x-2mt,\qquad\Phi(t,x):=\mathrm{e}^{imx-im^{2}t}.

Then

u^{(m)}(t,x)=\Phi(t,x)v(t,y).

A direct computation gives

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u^{(m)}

\displaystyle=m^{2}\Phi v+i\Phi\partial_{t}v-2im\Phi\partial_{y}v,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}u^{(m)}

\displaystyle=-m^{2}\Phi v+2im\Phi\partial_{y}v+\Phi\partial_{y}^{2}v.

Therefore

i\partial_{t}u^{(m)}+\partial_{x}^{2}u^{(m)}=\Phi(t,x)\left(i\partial_{t}v+\partial_{y}^{2}v\right)(t,y).

(2.12)

It remains to check the nonlocal term. Let
\tau_{a}
denote translation by
a
,

(\tau_{a}f)(x):=f(x-a).

Then

|u^{(m)}(t,x)|^{2}=|v(t,x-2mt)|^{2}=(\tau_{2mt}|v(t,\cdot)|^{2})(x).

Since both
\Pi
and
D=-i\partial_{x}
commute with translations on
\mathbb{T}
, we have

D\Pi(|u^{(m)}(t,\cdot)|^{2})(x)=D\Pi(|v(t,\cdot)|^{2})(x-2mt)=D\Pi(|v(t,\cdot)|^{2})(y).

Thus

D\Pi(|u^{(m)}|^{2})u^{(m)}=\Phi(t,x)\left[D\Pi(|v|^{2})v\right](t,y).

(2.13)

Combining (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), we get

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u^{(m)}+\partial_{x}^{2}u^{(m)}+2D\Pi(|u^{(m)}|^{2})u^{(m)}

\displaystyle=\Phi(t,x)\left[i\partial_{t}v+\partial_{y}^{2}v+2D\Pi(|v|^{2})v\right](t,y)=0,

since
v
solves (
CS
). Hence
u^{(m)}
also solves (
CS
).

In analytic variables,
z=\mathrm{e}^{ix}
and

\mathrm{e}^{iy}=\mathrm{e}^{i(x-2mt)}=\mathrm{e}^{-2imt}z.

Therefore the above transformation is exactly

u^{(m)}(t,z)=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}t}z^{m}v(t,\mathrm{e}^{-2imt}z).

and we have

u^{(m)}(0,z)=z^{m}v(0,z)=z^{m}v_{0}(z).

Thus
u^{(m)}
is the smooth solution of (
CS
) on
I
with initial datum
z^{m}v_{0}
.

We now prove the comparison estimate for the
H^{s}
-norms. Write

v(t,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}v_{n}(t)z^{n}.

Then

u^{(m)}(t,z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}t}\mathrm{e}^{-2imnt}v_{n}(t)z^{n+m}.

Hence

\|u^{(m)}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(1+(n+m)^{2}\right)^{s}|v_{n}(t)|^{2}.

For fixed
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
s\geq 0
, the weights

1+(n+m)^{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad 1+n^{2}

are uniformly comparable for
n\geq 0
. Thus there exist constants

0<c_{m,s}\leq C_{m,s}<+\infty
such that

c_{m,s}^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}(1+n^{2})^{s}|v_{n}(t)|^{2}\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}(1+(n+m)^{2})^{s}|v_{n}(t)|^{2}\leq C_{m,s}^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}(1+n^{2})^{s}|v_{n}(t)|^{2}.

This proves (
2.10
). In the case
s=0
, the weights are all
equal to one, and therefore

\|u^{(m)}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 0}|v_{n}(t)|^{2}=\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}.

This proves (
2.11
).
∎

Remark
.

Lemma
2.5
is the mechanism by which the shifted data

z^{m}v_{0}
will be treated below. We shall first analyze the core case
m=0
. The
cases
m\geq 1
are then obtained by applying (
2.9
).
In particular, finite-time blow-up, global existence, and uniform Sobolev bounds are
transferred by this transformation. The
L^{2}
-norm is preserved exactly, while the

H^{s}
-norms for
s>0
are equivalent up to constants depending only on
m
and
s
.

This completes the abstract part of the argument. We now turn to the finite-dimensional
family of rational finite-gap potentials. The spectral analysis will be carried out only for
the core case
m=0
. In the resonant case
2a+c=0
, we find a unimodular eigenvalue
and then use the explicit formula to compute the corresponding blow-up dynamics. In the
non-resonant case
2a+c\neq 0
, we prove strong stability for the core datum, which yields
global existence and uniform Sobolev bounds. The general shifted data
z^{m}v_{0}
,

m\geq 1
, are then obtained from the core case by Lemma

2.5
.

3.
Spectral properties of
L_{u}

In view of the Galilean covariance established above, it is enough to analyze the core
case
m=0
. Thus, in this section, we consider initial data of the form

u_{0}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\beta_{p}(z)\left(a+\frac{c}{1-\bar{p}z}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad\beta_{p}(z)=\frac{z-p}{1-\bar{p}z},

(3.1)

where

,

satisfy the nonlinear constraint (

). For notational convenience, we shall often write

instead of

henceforth. Following the analysis of finite-gap potentials in

]
, we define

\psi(z)=\beta_{p}(z)^{2}=\left(\frac{z-p}{1-\bar{p}z}\right)^{2},

, i.e., we have
\psi\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})

for a.e.

. We denote by

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=(\psi L^{2}_{+})^{\perp}=\{f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T}):\mbox{$\langle f,\psi g\rangle=0$ for all $g\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$}\}.

the model space generated by
\psi
. Since
\psi
is a finite Blaschke product (of degree 2), it is a classical fact that
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
can be explicitly determined. In fact, we find

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=\mathrm{span}\,\{e_{0},e_{1}\}\quad\mbox{with}\quad e_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{p}z},\quad e_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-\bar{p}z)^{2}},

Let

as above. We have the following result.

Proposition 3.1

(The core two-dimensional block)
.

The two-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is invariant under
L_{u}
and
S^{*}
, i.e., it holds that
L_{u}(\mathcal{K}_{\psi})\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
and
S^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{\psi})\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
. With respect to the basis
(e_{0},e_{1})
, the corresponding matrices are given by

[L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\alpha\\
\bar{p}&\beta\end{array}\right],\quad[S^{*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{p}&1\\
0&\bar{p}\end{array}\right],

where

\alpha=\frac{2p(a+c)}{c(1-r)},\quad\beta=\frac{2r(a+c)-2a-c(1-r)}{c(1-r)},\quad r=|p|^{2}.

Remarks
.

1) Note that he matrix for
L_{u}
on
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is not Hermitian due to the fact that
(e_{0},e_{1})
is not orthonormal. However, this choice of basis will be beneficial for the analysis below.

2) If we additionally impose that
2a+c=0
, the expressions for
\alpha
and
\beta
simplify to

\alpha=\frac{p}{1-|p|^{2}},\quad\beta=\frac{2|p|^{2}}{1-|p|^{2}}.

Proof.

The proof follows from direct calculations by adapting the analysis in
[
Bad24
]
. In particular, the invariance of
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
under
L_{u}
rests on the constraint (
1.3
). For the reader’s convenience, we sketch the main steps here.

Step 1.
We observe that
e_{0}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\bar{p}^{n}z^{n}
, which gives us

S^{*}e_{0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\bar{p}^{n+1}z^{n}=\bar{p}e_{0}.

Likewise, we use that
e_{1}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}n\bar{p}^{n-1}z^{n}
to conclude that

S^{*}e_{1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)\bar{p}^{n}z^{n}=e_{0}+\bar{p}e_{1},

which directly shows that
S^{*}(\mathcal{K}_{\psi})\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
with the matrix
[S^{*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}
as claimed.

Step 2.
To calculate the action of
L_{u}
on
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, we need a more elaborate calculation as follows. Without loss of generality we can assume that
\theta=0
holds in (
3.1
), since
L_{u}
is not affected by a constant phase in
u
.

Since
e_{0}(z)
is the reproducing kernel at
p
, we have
f(p)=\langle f,e_{0}\rangle
for all
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Because of
u(p)=0
, this implies
\langle f,T_{\bar{u}}e_{0}\rangle=\langle uf,e_{0}\rangle=u(p)f(p)=0
for any
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. This shows that

T_{\bar{u}}e_{0}=0.

Thus we find

L_{u}e_{0}=De_{0}=z\partial_{z}\left(\frac{1}{1-\bar{p}z}\right)=\bar{p}e_{1}.

Next we compute
T_{\bar{u}}e_{1}
. Here we use that
f^{\prime}(p)=\langle f,e_{1}\rangle
for all
f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, since
e_{1}(z)
is derivative reproducing kernel at
p
. Using that
u(p)=0
and
f(p)=\langle f,e_{0}\rangle
, we infer

\langle f,T_{\bar{u}}e_{1}\rangle=\langle uf,e_{1}\rangle=(uf)^{\prime}(p)=u^{\prime}(p)f(p)=u^{\prime}(p)\langle f,e_{0}\rangle\quad\mbox{for all $f\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})$}.

Hence
T_{\bar{u}}e_{1}
is parallel to
e_{0}
with
T_{\bar{u}}e_{1}=\bar{u}^{\prime}(p)e_{0}
. We calculate

\bar{u}^{\prime}(p)=\frac{\bar{\xi}}{1-r}\quad\mbox{with $\displaystyle\xi=a+\frac{c}{1-r}$ and $r=|p|^{2}$}.

Noting that the constraint (
1.3
) is equivalent to
\bar{\xi}c=2
, we conclude

T_{u}T_{\bar{u}}e_{1}=\frac{2}{c(1-r)}ue_{0}.

Next, we observe that

De_{1}=z\partial_{z}\left(\frac{z}{s^{2}}\right)=\frac{z}{s^{2}}+\frac{2\bar{p}z^{2}}{s^{3}}\quad\mbox{with}\quad s=1-\bar{p}z.

Some straightforward manipulations yield

L_{u}e_{1}=\alpha e_{0}+\beta e_{1}+\frac{B}{s^{3}}=\alpha e_{0}+\beta e_{1},

where the vanishing of
B=0
follows from using the constraint (
1.3
) and we have

\alpha=\frac{2p(a+c)}{c(1-r)},\quad\beta=\frac{2r(a+c)-2a-c(1-r)}{c(1-r)}.

This shows
L_{u}(\mathcal{K}_{\psi})\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
and that the matrix
[L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}
is given as claimed.

This completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
∎

The preceding proposition isolates the only spectral block that will be needed in the
rest of the paper. The shifted initial data
z^{m}u
,
m\geq 1
, will no longer be treated
by a separate block-matrix analysis. Instead, all results for
m\geq 1
will be obtained
from the core case
m=0
by the Galilean covariance in Lemma

2.5
. Thus the resonance condition, the blow-up dynamics, and
the global/non-resonant behavior are first proved for the two-dimensional block

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, and then transferred to the shifted data.

4.
Finite-time blow-up

We now turn to the proof of finite-time blow-up solutions. The starting point is the abstract criterion obtained in Lemma
2.4
: if, for some
time
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the contraction

A_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*}

has a unimodular eigenvalue, then the corresponding smooth solution must blow up in finite-time.

The purpose of this section is to verify this criterion for some finite-gap potentials initial data. More generally, we consider the family (
1.2
) under the
constraint (
1.3
) and show that the existence of a unimodular eigenvalue is
equivalent to the resonance condition

2a+c=0.

This gives the spectral mechanism responsible for blow-up. After establishing this
mechanism, we return to the explicit formula (
2.3
) and compute the corresponding blow-up dynamics.

In the subsection on unimodular eigenvalues, the variable
t
denotes the Lax time in

A_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}S^{*}
. In the subsection on blow-up dynamics,
t
denotes the physical
time of the PDE. The conversion between the two is always

t_{\rm Lax}=2t_{\rm phys}.

4.1.
Existence of unimodular eigenvalues

We now identify the spectral resonance responsible for blow-up in the core case
m=0
. Thus
u=u_{0}
is given by
(
3.1
), and we keep the notation

\psi=\beta_{p}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=(\psi L^{2}_{+})^{\perp}=\mathrm{span}\,\{e_{0},e_{1}\},

where

e_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{p}z},\qquad e_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-\bar{p}z)^{2}}.

In this subsection, the parameter
t
in

A_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}S^{*}

denotes the Lax time. Thus, in the explicit formula (
2.3
), the corresponding
physical time is one half of this Lax time, since the operator in (
2.3
) contains

\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{u_{0}}}S^{*}
.

Proposition 4.1

(Unimodular eigenvalues for the core block)
.

Let
u
be the core potential (
3.1
), and assume that the constraint (
1.3
) holds. Then

A_{t}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi},\qquad A_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}S^{*},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover,

A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\quad\mbox{has a unimodular eigenvalue for some }t\in\mathbb{R}

if and only if

2a+c=0.

More precisely, if
2a+c=0
, then

e_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-\bar{p}z)^{2}}

is a unimodular eigenvector of
A_{t_{\ell}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
for every

t_{\ell}=\frac{(2\ell+1)\pi(1-|p|^{2})}{2|p|},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

(4.1)

Consequently, at the physical times

T_{\ell}:=\frac{t_{\ell}}{2}=\frac{(2\ell+1)\pi(1-|p|^{2})}{4|p|},

the operator

\Sigma_{T_{\ell}}^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-iT_{\ell}}\mathrm{e}^{-2iT_{\ell}L_{u}}S^{*}

also has a unimodular eigenvalue. On the other hand, if
2a+c\neq 0
, then

A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
has no unimodular eigenvalue for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The constant phase
\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}
in (
3.1
) does not affect the Lax
operator. Indeed, if
u=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}v
, then

T_{u}T_{\bar{u}}=T_{\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}v}T_{\mathrm{e}^{-i\theta}\bar{v}}=T_{v}T_{\bar{v}}.

Hence
L_{u}=L_{v}
. We may therefore assume
\theta=0
throughout the proof.

Set

r:=|p|^{2}.

By Proposition
3.1
, the model space
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is invariant under both

L_{u}
and
S^{*}
. Since
L_{u}
is self-adjoint and
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is finite dimensional,

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is reducing for
L_{u}
. Therefore

\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Combining this with
S^{*}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, we obtain

A_{t}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}.

With respect to the basis
(e_{0},e_{1})
, Proposition
3.1
gives

[L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha\\
\bar{p}&\beta\end{bmatrix},\qquad[S^{*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{p}&1\\
0&\bar{p}\end{bmatrix},

where

\alpha=\frac{2p(a+c)}{c(1-r)},\qquad\beta=\frac{2r(a+c)-2a-c(1-r)}{c(1-r)}.

We also set

\kappa:=\alpha\bar{p}=\frac{2r(a+c)}{c(1-r)}.

The constraint (
1.3
) implies

a\bar{c}=2-\frac{|c|^{2}}{1-r}\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
c\neq 0
, it follows that

\frac{a}{c}=\frac{a\bar{c}}{|c|^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}.

Consequently,

\kappa\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\beta\in\mathbb{R}.

We first show that every unimodular eigenvector of
A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
must be
proportional to
e_{1}
. Suppose that

A_{t}g=\lambda g,\qquad g\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\setminus\{0\},\qquad|\lambda|=1.

Since
\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}
is unitary,

\|g\|_{L^{2}}=\|\lambda g\|_{L^{2}}=\|A_{t}g\|_{L^{2}}=\|\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}S^{*}g\|_{L^{2}}=\|S^{*}g\|_{L^{2}}.

But

\|S^{*}g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-|g(0)|^{2}.

Thus
g(0)=0
. Since

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=\mathrm{span}\,\{e_{0},e_{1}\},\qquad e_{0}(0)=1,\qquad e_{1}(0)=0,

the subspace of vectors in
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
vanishing at the origin is exactly
\mathbb{C}e_{1}
.
Therefore every unimodular eigenvector of
A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
is proportional to
e_{1}
.

It follows that
A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
has a unimodular eigenvalue if and only if

A_{t}e_{1}\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}

for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, if
A_{t}e_{1}=\mu e_{1}
, then
\mu
is automatically
unimodular. Since
e_{1}(0)=0
,

\|S^{*}e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}=\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}.

Using the unitarity of
\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}
, we obtain

\|A_{t}e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}=\|\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}S^{*}e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}=\|S^{*}e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}=\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}.

Thus
A_{t}e_{1}=\mu e_{1}
implies
|\mu|=1
.

We are therefore reduced to deciding when
A_{t}e_{1}\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}
. Since

S^{*}e_{1}=e_{0}+\bar{p}\,e_{1},

define

v(t):=\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u}}(e_{0}+\bar{p}\,e_{1}).

Then

v(t)=A_{t}e_{1}.

Write

v(t)=x(t)e_{0}+y(t)e_{1}.

The condition
A_{t}e_{1}\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}
is equivalent to

x(t)=0.

From

\partial_{t}v(t)=-iL_{u}v(t),

and from the matrix representation of
L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
, we obtain

x^{\prime}=-i\alpha y,\qquad y^{\prime}=-i(\bar{p}x+\beta y).

Therefore

x^{\prime\prime}=-i\alpha y^{\prime}=-\alpha\bar{p}\,x-i\beta x^{\prime},

or equivalently,

x^{\prime\prime}+i\beta x^{\prime}+\kappa x=0.

(4.2)

The initial conditions are

x(0)=1,\qquad x^{\prime}(0)=-i\kappa.

(4.3)

The restriction
L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
is self-adjoint with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner
product inherited by
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
. Hence its eigenvalues are real. Moreover, they cannot
coincide. Indeed, if
L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
had only one eigenvalue
\lambda
, then, by
self-adjointness, it would have to be a scalar
operator, which implies

L_{u}e_{0}=\lambda e_{0}.

But from the computation above,

L_{u}e_{0}=\bar{p}\,e_{1}.

Since
p\neq 0
and
e_{0},e_{1}
are linearly independent, this is impossible. We may
therefore denote the two eigenvalues of
L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
by

\lambda_{+}>\lambda_{-}.

The characteristic polynomial of
L_{u}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
, equivalently the characteristic
polynomial associated with the scalar equation (
4.2
), is

\lambda^{2}-\beta\lambda-\kappa=0.

Thus

\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-}=\beta,\qquad\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}=-\kappa.

The solution of (
4.2
) is

x(t)=A\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda_{+}t}+B\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda_{-}t},

where the initial conditions (
4.3
) give

A=\frac{\kappa-\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}},\qquad B=\frac{\lambda_{+}-\kappa}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}.

Since
\lambda_{\pm}
and
\kappa
are real,

A,B\in\mathbb{R},\qquad A+B=1.

We now determine when
x(t)
can vanish. If
x(t)=0
for some real
t
, then

A\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda_{+}t}=-B\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda_{-}t}.

Taking absolute values gives

|A|=|B|.

Since
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
and
A+B=1
, this is equivalent to

A=B=\frac{1}{2}.

Conversely, if
A=B=1/2
, then choosing
t
such that

\mathrm{e}^{-i(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})t}=-1

gives
x(t)=0
. Hence

x(t)=0\mbox{ for some }t\in\mathbb{R}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A=B=\frac{1}{2}.

Using the formula for
A
, this is equivalent to

\kappa=\frac{\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-}}{2}=\frac{\beta}{2}.

Substituting the expressions for
\kappa
and
\beta
, we find

\frac{2r(a+c)}{c(1-r)}=\frac{2r(a+c)-2a-c(1-r)}{2c(1-r)}.

Equivalently,

4r(a+c)=2r(a+c)-2a-c(1-r),

and hence

(1+r)(2a+c)=0.

Since
1+r>0
, this is equivalent to

2a+c=0.

This proves the necessity of the resonance condition.

Conversely, assume that

2a+c=0.

Then

a+c=\frac{c}{2}.

Therefore

\kappa=\frac{r}{1-r},\qquad\beta=\frac{2r}{1-r}.

Moreover,

\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}=\sqrt{\beta^{2}+4\kappa}=\frac{2|p|}{1-r}.

Since
A=B=1/2
, we get

x(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\beta t/2}\cos\left(\frac{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}{2}t\right).

Thus
x(t)=0
precisely when

\frac{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}{2}t=\frac{(2\ell+1)\pi}{2},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}.

Equivalently,

t=t_{\ell}=\frac{(2\ell+1)\pi(1-r)}{2|p|}.

For every such
t_{\ell}
, we have

A_{t_{\ell}}e_{1}\in\mathbb{C}e_{1}.

Thus there exists
\mu_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}
,
|\mu_{\ell}|=1
, such that

A_{t_{\ell}}e_{1}=\mu_{\ell}e_{1}.

Finally, passing from the Lax time
t_{\ell}
to the physical time

T_{\ell}:=\frac{t_{\ell}}{2},

we have

\Sigma_{T_{\ell}}^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-iT_{\ell}}\mathrm{e}^{-2iT_{\ell}L_{u}}S^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-iT_{\ell}}A_{t_{\ell}}.

Hence

\Sigma_{T_{\ell}}^{*}e_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{-iT_{\ell}}\mu_{\ell}e_{1}.

Since

|\mathrm{e}^{-iT_{\ell}}\mu_{\ell}|=1,

the physical-time operator
\Sigma_{T_{\ell}}^{*}
also has a unimodular eigenvalue.

If
2a+c\neq 0
, then the equivalence proved above gives
x(t)\neq 0
for every

t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since every unimodular eigenvector of
A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
must be proportional
to
e_{1}
, it follows that
A_{t}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
has no unimodular eigenvalue for any

t\in\mathbb{R}
.

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark
.

For the blow-up datum (

) with

4.2.
Blow-up dynamics

We now compute the blow-up dynamics. In this subsection,
t
denotes the physical time
of the PDE. The main point is that only the core case
m=0
has to be analyzed directly.
The shifted cases
m\geq 1
are then obtained by Galilean covariance, Lemma

2.5
.

Proposition 4.2

(Blow-up dynamics for the core datum)
.

Let

w_{0}(z)=a\,\beta_{p}(z)\left(1-\frac{2}{1-\bar{p}z}\right),\qquad a:=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\sqrt{\frac{1-|p|^{2}}{1+|p|^{2}}},\qquad 0<|p|<1,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{T}.

Set

r:=|p|^{2},\qquad\rho:=|p|,\qquad T:=\frac{\pi(1-r)}{4\rho}.

Let
v(t)
be the solution of (
CS
) with initial datum
w_{0}
. Then

v\in C([0,T);H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})).

Moreover, there exist functions

\alpha_{0}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{D},\qquad\beta_{0}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{C},

such that

v(t,z)-\frac{\beta_{0}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{0}(t)z}\longrightarrow v_{*}(z)\qquad\mbox{in }H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T,

(4.4)

where

v_{*}(z)=ap+ia\rho(1+r)\frac{ze^{-i\Theta_{*}}}{1-i\rho\bar{p}\,ze^{-i\Theta_{*}}},\qquad\Theta_{*}:=\frac{(1+r)\pi}{4\rho}.

(4.5)

Furthermore,

|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{2}=1-c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),\qquad|\beta_{0}(t)|^{2}=c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

(4.6)

where

c_{0}:=\frac{4r(1-r)}{(1+r)^{3}}>0.

Consequently, for every
s>0
,

\|v(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\sim\sqrt{\Gamma(2s+1)}\left(\frac{(1+r)^{3}}{4r(1-r)}\right)^{s}(T-t)^{-2s},\qquad t\nearrow T.

(4.7)

In addition,

v(t)\rightharpoonup v_{*}\qquad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T,

and

\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|v_{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1.

(4.8)

Finally,
v_{*}
is a rational one-pole traveling solitary wave profile.

Proof.

We write

w_{0}(z)=\beta_{p}(z)\left(a+\frac{c}{1-\bar{p}z}\right),\qquad c=-2a.

Since

|a|^{2}=\frac{1-r}{1+r},

we have

2a+c=0,\qquad a\bar{c}+\frac{|c|^{2}}{1-r}=2.

Thus
w_{0}
is the resonant core datum from (
3.1
).

Step 1: use of the explicit formula and the rational formula.

For the datum
w_{0}
, set

A_{\tau}^{(0)}:=\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{w_{0}}}S^{*},\qquad\tau=2t.

By the explicit formula (
2.3
),

v(t,z)=\mathcal{M}\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-z\mathrm{e}^{-it}A_{2t}^{(0)}\right)^{-1}w_{0}\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(4.9)

Here
t
is the physical time and
2t
is the Lax time.

Let

\psi=\beta_{p}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=(\psi L^{2}_{+})^{\perp}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{0},e_{1}\},

where

e_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\bar{p}z},\qquad e_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-\bar{p}z)^{2}}.

Since

w_{0}(0)=ap,

and since
S^{*}w_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, Proposition
3.1
gives

A_{\tau}^{(0)}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi},\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

Expanding the resolvent in (
4.9
) as a Neumann series for

|z|<1
, we obtain

v(t,z)=ap+z\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathcal{M}\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-z\mathrm{e}^{-it}A_{2t}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)^{-1}A_{2t}^{(0)}w_{0}\right).

(4.10)

Thus the computation of
v(t,z)
reduces to the two-dimensional space
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
.

With respect to the basis
(e_{0},e_{1})
, and using
2a+c=0
, Proposition

3.1
gives

[L_{w_{0}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=L_{0}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\dfrac{p}{1-r}\\[3.99994pt]
\bar{p}&\dfrac{2r}{1-r}\end{bmatrix},\qquad[S^{*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=B:=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{p}&1\\
0&\bar{p}\end{bmatrix}.

Set

\mu:=\frac{r}{1-r},\qquad\omega_{0}:=\frac{\rho}{1-r}.

Then

L_{0}=\mu I+M,\qquad M:=\begin{bmatrix}-\mu&\dfrac{p}{1-r}\\[3.99994pt]
\bar{p}&\mu\end{bmatrix},\qquad M^{2}=\omega_{0}^{2}I.

Hence, with

\Omega(t):=\frac{2\rho t}{1-r},\qquad C:=\cos\Omega(t),\qquad S:=\sin\Omega(t),

we have

\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{0}}=\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}\left(CI-i\frac{S}{\omega_{0}}M\right).

Consequently,

[A_{2t}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{0}}B=\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{p}(C+i\rho S)&C\\[3.00003pt]
-i\dfrac{\bar{p}^{2}(1-r)}{\rho}S&\bar{p}\left(C-i\dfrac{1}{\rho}S\right)\end{bmatrix}.

In particular,

\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{2t}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}\bar{p}\left(2C-i\frac{1-r}{\rho}S\right),

and

\det\left(A_{2t}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)=\mathrm{e}^{-4i\mu t}\bar{p}^{2}.

Next,

[S^{*}w_{0}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=a\begin{bmatrix}3r-1\\[1.99997pt]
-2\bar{p}(1-r)\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore

[A_{2t}^{(0)}w_{0}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})}=a\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}\begin{bmatrix}N(t)\\[1.99997pt]
-\bar{p}(1-r)\left(2C-i\frac{1-r}{\rho}S\right)\end{bmatrix},

where

N(t):=(3r-1)C+i\rho(1+r)S.

Let

w:=z\mathrm{e}^{-it},\qquad\zeta:=w\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}=z\exp\left(-i\frac{1+r}{1-r}t\right).

Equivalently,

\zeta=z\mathrm{e}^{-i\Theta(t)},\qquad\Theta(t):=\frac{1+r}{1-r}t.

For a
2\times 2
matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix},

we use the identity

(1,0)(I-w\mathcal{A})^{-1}y=\frac{(1-wa_{22})y_{1}+wa_{12}y_{2}}{\det(I-w\mathcal{A})}.

Applying this identity to

\mathcal{A}=[A_{2t}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})},\qquad y=[A_{2t}^{(0)}w_{0}]_{(e_{0},e_{1})},

and using the trace and determinant above, we find

\det(I-w\mathcal{A})=1-\bar{p}\left(2C-i\frac{1-r}{\rho}S\right)\zeta+\bar{p}^{2}\zeta^{2}.

Moreover, the numerator simplifies to

(1-wa_{22})y_{1}+wa_{12}y_{2}=a\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}\left[N(t)-(1+r)\bar{p}\,\zeta\right].

Since
w\mathrm{e}^{-2i\mu t}=\zeta
, (
4.10
) gives

v(t,z)=ap+a\zeta\frac{N(t)-(1+r)\bar{p}\,\zeta}{1-\bar{p}\left(2C-i\frac{1-r}{\rho}S\right)\zeta+\bar{p}^{2}\zeta^{2}}.

Thus

v(t,z)=ap+a\zeta\frac{N(t)-(1+r)\bar{p}\,\zeta}{\mathcal{D}_{t}(\zeta)},

(4.11)

where

N(t):=(3r-1)\cos\Omega(t)+i\rho(1+r)\sin\Omega(t),

and

\mathcal{D}_{t}(\zeta):=1-\bar{p}\left(2\cos\Omega(t)-i\frac{1-r}{\rho}\sin\Omega(t)\right)\zeta+\bar{p}^{2}\zeta^{2}.

At
t=0
, this formula reduces to the initial datum
w_{0}
.

Step 2: factorization of the denominator.

Let

q(t):=2\cos\Omega(t)-i\frac{1-r}{\rho}\sin\Omega(t).

Then

\mathcal{D}_{t}(\zeta)=1-\bar{p}q(t)\zeta+\bar{p}^{2}\zeta^{2}.

Indeed, if
C=\cos\Omega(t)
,
S=\sin\Omega(t)
, and

\sigma=(1-r)/\rho
, then

q(t)^{2}-4=(2C-i\sigma S)^{2}-4=-(4+\sigma^{2})S^{2}-i4\sigma CS.

For
0<t<T
, we have
C>0
and
S>0
, so the imaginary part is nonzero; at

t=T
, the expression equals
-(4+\sigma^{2})\neq 0
. Consequently, for
0<t\leq T
, we may choose the continuous branch

\Delta(t):=\sqrt{q(t)^{2}-4}

such that

\Delta(T)=i\frac{1+r}{\rho}.

Define

\alpha_{1}(t):=\frac{\bar{p}}{2}\left(q(t)-\Delta(t)\right),\qquad\alpha_{2}(t):=\frac{\bar{p}}{2}\left(q(t)+\Delta(t)\right).

Then

\mathcal{D}_{t}(\zeta)=(1-\alpha_{1}(t)\zeta)(1-\alpha_{2}(t)\zeta).

At the time
T
, we have

\Omega(T)=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Thus

q(T)=-i\frac{1-r}{\rho},

and hence

\alpha_{1}(T)=-i\frac{\bar{p}}{\rho},\qquad|\alpha_{1}(T)|=1,

whereas

\alpha_{2}(T)=i\rho\bar{p},\qquad|\alpha_{2}(T)|=r<1.

Thus only the pole parameter associated with
\alpha_{1}(t)
reaches the unit circle.

We also record that both pole parameters remain strictly inside the unit disk for

0<t<T
. Write

C:=\cos\Omega(t),\qquad S:=\sin\Omega(t),

and define

\lambda_{j}(t):=\frac{\rho}{2}\left(q(t)\mp\Delta(t)\right),\qquad j=1,2.

If

p=\rho\mathrm{e}^{i\vartheta},

then

\alpha_{j}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\vartheta}\lambda_{j}(t),\qquad|\alpha_{j}(t)|=|\lambda_{j}(t)|.

The numbers
\lambda_{1}(t),\lambda_{2}(t)
are the roots of

\lambda^{2}-\left(2\rho C-i(1-r)S\right)\lambda+r=0.

Let
\lambda=R\mathrm{e}^{i\varphi}
be one of these roots. Dividing the equation by

\lambda
, we get

\lambda+\frac{r}{\lambda}=2\rho C-i(1-r)S.

Therefore

\left(R+\frac{r}{R}\right)\cos\varphi=2\rho C,\qquad\left(R-\frac{r}{R}\right)\sin\varphi=-(1-r)S.

If
R\geq 1
, then

R+\frac{r}{R}\geq 1+r,\qquad R-\frac{r}{R}\geq 1-r.

Hence

\cos^{2}\varphi\leq\frac{4rC^{2}}{(1+r)^{2}},\qquad\sin^{2}\varphi\leq S^{2}.

Since
0<t<T
implies
C>0
, and since

\frac{4r}{(1+r)^{2}}<1,

we obtain

1=\cos^{2}\varphi+\sin^{2}\varphi<C^{2}+S^{2}=1,

a contradiction. Thus
R<1
. Consequently,

|\alpha_{1}(t)|<1,\qquad|\alpha_{2}(t)|<1,\qquad 0<t<T.

At
t=0
, the denominator is

\mathcal{D}_{0}(\zeta)=1-2\bar{p}\zeta+\bar{p}^{2}\zeta^{2}=(1-\bar{p}\zeta)^{2},

so the pole parameter is
\bar{p}
, whose modulus is strictly smaller than one. Hence

v\in C([0,T);H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})).

Step 3: singular pole decomposition.

For
0<t<T
, write

a\zeta\frac{N(t)-(1+r)\bar{p}\,\zeta}{(1-\alpha_{1}(t)\zeta)(1-\alpha_{2}(t)\zeta)}=\frac{B_{1}(t)\zeta}{1-\alpha_{1}(t)\zeta}+\frac{B_{2}(t)\zeta}{1-\alpha_{2}(t)\zeta}.

Comparison of coefficients gives

B_{1}(t)=a\frac{N(t)\alpha_{1}(t)-(1+r)\bar{p}}{\alpha_{1}(t)-\alpha_{2}(t)},

and

B_{2}(t)=a\frac{(1+r)\bar{p}-N(t)\alpha_{2}(t)}{\alpha_{1}(t)-\alpha_{2}(t)}.

Thus

v(t,z)=ap+\frac{B_{1}(t)ze^{-i\Theta(t)}}{1-\alpha_{1}(t)ze^{-i\Theta(t)}}+\frac{B_{2}(t)ze^{-i\Theta(t)}}{1-\alpha_{2}(t)ze^{-i\Theta(t)}}.

(4.12)

At
t=T
, we have

N(T)=i\rho(1+r).

Therefore

N(T)\alpha_{1}(T)=(1+r)\bar{p},

and hence

B_{1}(T)=0.

Moreover,

N(T)\alpha_{2}(T)=-r(1+r)\bar{p},

and

\alpha_{1}(T)-\alpha_{2}(T)=-i\frac{1+r}{\rho}\bar{p}.

It follows that

B_{2}(T)=ia\rho(1+r).

Since
|\alpha_{2}(T)|=r<1
, the second pole remains uniformly away from the unit circle
as
t\nearrow T
. Thus

v(t,z)-\frac{B_{1}(t)ze^{-i\Theta(t)}}{1-\alpha_{1}(t)ze^{-i\Theta(t)}}\longrightarrow v_{*}(z)

in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
as
t\nearrow T
, where
v_{*}
is given by (
4.5
). Defining

\alpha_{0}(t):=\alpha_{1}(t)e^{-i\Theta(t)},\qquad\beta_{0}(t):=B_{1}(t)e^{-i\Theta(t)},

we obtain (
4.4
).

Step 4: asymptotics of the dangerous pole.

Set

\eta(t):=\Omega(t)-\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{2\rho}{1-r}(t-T).

Thus
t\nearrow T
is equivalent to
\eta\to 0^{-}
. Write

p=\rho e^{i\vartheta},\qquad\alpha_{1}(t)=e^{-i\vartheta}\lambda_{1}(t),

where
\lambda_{1}(t)
is the branch of the root of

\lambda^{2}-\rho q(t)\lambda+r=0

satisfying

\lambda_{1}(T)=-i.

Since

\Omega(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}+\eta,

we have

\cos\Omega(t)=-\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}),\qquad\sin\Omega(t)=1-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{4}).

Hence

\rho q(t)=-i(1-r)-2\rho\eta+\frac{i}{2}(1-r)\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}).

Writing

\lambda_{1}(t)=-i+d_{1}\eta+d_{2}\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}),

and inserting this expansion into the quadratic equation, we obtain

d_{1}=-\frac{2\rho}{1+r},

and

d_{2}=-\frac{i}{1+r}\left(d_{1}^{2}+2\rho d_{1}-\frac{1-r}{2}\right).

In particular,

d_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad d_{2}\in i\mathbb{R}.

Therefore

|\lambda_{1}(t)|^{2}=1+\left(d_{1}^{2}+id_{2}-i\bar{d}_{2}\right)\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(|\eta|^{3}).

Using the values of
d_{1},d_{2}
, one gets

d_{1}^{2}+id_{2}-i\bar{d}_{2}=-\frac{(1-r)^{3}}{(1+r)^{3}}.

Since
|\alpha_{1}(t)|=|\lambda_{1}(t)|
, this yields

1-|\alpha_{1}(t)|^{2}=\frac{(1-r)^{3}}{(1+r)^{3}}\eta(t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(|\eta(t)|^{3}).

(4.13)

We now expand the residue
B_{1}(t)
. Since

\alpha_{1}(t)=e^{-i\vartheta}\lambda_{1}(t),\qquad\bar{p}=\rho e^{-i\vartheta},

we may cancel the common phase and write

B_{1}(t)=a\frac{N(t)\lambda_{1}(t)-(1+r)\rho}{\lambda_{1}(t)-\lambda_{2}(t)}.

At
t=T
,

N(T)=i\rho(1+r),\qquad\lambda_{1}(T)=-i,\qquad\lambda_{2}(T)=ir.

Thus the numerator vanishes at
t=T
. Moreover,

N(t)=i\rho(1+r)-(3r-1)\eta-\frac{i}{2}\rho(1+r)\eta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}),

and

\lambda_{1}(t)=-i-\frac{2\rho}{1+r}\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

Hence

N(t)\lambda_{1}(t)-(1+r)\rho=-i(1-r)\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

Since

\lambda_{1}(T)-\lambda_{2}(T)=-i(1+r),

we find

B_{1}(t)=a\frac{1-r}{1+r}\eta+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{2}).

Therefore

|B_{1}(t)|^{2}=|a|^{2}\frac{(1-r)^{2}}{(1+r)^{2}}\eta(t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(|\eta(t)|^{3}).

(4.14)

Since

|a|^{2}=\frac{1-r}{1+r},

we get

|B_{1}(t)|^{2}=\frac{(1-r)^{3}}{(1+r)^{3}}\eta(t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(|\eta(t)|^{3}).

(4.15)

Finally,

\eta(t)^{2}=\frac{4r}{(1-r)^{2}}(T-t)^{2}.

Combining (
4.13
) and
(
4.15
), and using

|\alpha_{0}(t)|=|\alpha_{1}(t)|,\qquad|\beta_{0}(t)|=|B_{1}(t)|,

we obtain (
4.6
).

Step 5: Sobolev blow-up rate.

Let

P_{0}(t,z):=\frac{\beta_{0}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{0}(t)z}.

Then

\widehat{P_{0}(t)}(0)=0,\qquad\widehat{P_{0}(t)}(n)=\beta_{0}(t)\alpha_{0}(t)^{n-1},\qquad n\geq 1.

Set

q_{t}:=|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{2}.

With the convention

\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(1+n^{2})^{s}|\widehat{f}(n)|^{2},

we get

\|P_{0}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}=|\beta_{0}(t)|^{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}(1+n^{2})^{s}q_{t}^{\,n-1}.

As
q_{t}\to 1^{-}
, the standard Abelian asymptotic gives

\sum_{n\geq 1}(1+n^{2})^{s}q_{t}^{\,n-1}\sim\frac{\Gamma(2s+1)}{(1-q_{t})^{2s+1}}.

Therefore

\|P_{0}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}\sim|\beta_{0}(t)|^{2}\frac{\Gamma(2s+1)}{(1-|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{2})^{2s+1}}\text{ as }t\nearrow T.

Using (
4.6
), we find

\|P_{0}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\sim\sqrt{\Gamma(2s+1)}\left(\frac{(1+r)^{3}}{4r(1-r)}\right)^{s}(T-t)^{-2s}.

Since
v(t)-P_{0}(t)
remains bounded in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
as
t\nearrow T
, the same
asymptotic holds for
v(t)
. This proves (
4.7
).

Step 6: weak limit and mass quantization.

First, the singular pole
P_{0}(t)
converges weakly to
0
in
L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Indeed,
each fixed Fourier coefficient tends to zero because
|\beta_{0}(t)|\to 0
, while

\|P_{0}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{|\beta_{0}(t)|^{2}}{1-|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{2}}

remains bounded by (
4.6
). Together with
(
4.4
), this gives

v(t)\rightharpoonup v_{*}\qquad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T}).

It remains to compute the lost mass. A direct calculation gives

\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1+3r}{1+r}.

From (
4.5
), write

v_{*}(z)=ap+B_{*}\frac{z}{1-q_{*}z},

where

B_{*}:=ia\rho(1+r)e^{-i\Theta_{*}},\qquad q_{*}:=i\rho\bar{p}\,e^{-i\Theta_{*}}.

Then

|q_{*}|^{2}=r^{2},\qquad|B_{*}|^{2}=|a|^{2}r(1+r)^{2}.

Since the first term is constant and the second term has no constant Fourier mode,

\|v_{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=|a|^{2}r+\frac{|B_{*}|^{2}}{1-|q_{*}|^{2}}.

Using

|a|^{2}=\frac{1-r}{1+r},

we obtain

\|v_{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{r(1-r)}{1+r}+\frac{\frac{1-r}{1+r}r(1+r)^{2}}{1-r^{2}}=\frac{2r}{1+r}.

Therefore

\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|v_{*}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1+3r}{1+r}-\frac{2r}{1+r}=1.

This proves (
4.8
).

Finally, since
|q_{*}|<1
, the function
v_{*}
is a rational one-pole profile:

v_{*}(z)=A_{*}+\frac{C_{*}}{1-q_{*}z}

for suitable constants
A_{*},C_{*}\in\mathbb{C}
. By the classification of one-pole traveling wave
profiles for (
CS
), equivalently by a direct
substitution into the traveling-wave ansatz,
v_{*}
is a traveling solitary wave profile.

The proof is complete.
∎

Proposition
4.2
contains the only direct singular-pole analysis needed in the
proof. We now pass from the core datum to the shifted data in (
1.7
).
This step uses no additional spectral computation: by the Galilean covariance in
Lemma
2.5
, the solution with initial datum
z^{m}w_{0}
is obtained
from the core solution
v(t)
by a phase factor, a rotation of the variable
z
, and a
fixed shift of the Fourier modes. These operations preserve the
L^{2}
-mass, preserve the
blow-up time, and do not change the leading Sobolev blow-up rate.

Proposition 4.3

(Blow-up dynamics for all shifted data)
.

Let
u_{0}
be given by (
1.7
), with

m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\qquad 0<|p|<1,\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{T}.

Set

r:=|p|^{2},\qquad\rho:=|p|,\qquad\gamma:=\sqrt{\frac{1-r}{1+r}},\qquad a:=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\gamma,

and

T:=\frac{\pi(1-r)}{4\rho}.

Then the corresponding solution
u(t)
of (
CS
) satisfies

u\in C([0,T);H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})).

Moreover, there exist functions

\alpha_{m}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{D},\qquad\beta_{m}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{C},

such that

u(t,z)-\frac{\beta_{m}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{m}(t)z}\longrightarrow u_{*,m}(z)\qquad\mbox{in }H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T.

(4.16)

The limit profile is given explicitly by

u_{*,m}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}z^{m}\left[ap+ia\rho(1+r)\frac{z\mathrm{e}^{-i(\Theta_{*}+2mT)}}{1-i\rho\bar{p}\,z\mathrm{e}^{-i(\Theta_{*}+2mT)}}\right],\qquad\Theta_{*}:=\frac{(1+r)\pi}{4\rho}.

(4.17)

Furthermore,

|\alpha_{m}(t)|^{2}=1-c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),\qquad|\beta_{m}(t)|^{2}=c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

(4.18)

where

c_{0}:=\frac{4r(1-r)}{(1+r)^{3}}>0.

Consequently, for every
s>0
,

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\sim\sqrt{\Gamma(2s+1)}\left(\frac{(1+r)^{3}}{4r(1-r)}\right)^{s}(T-t)^{-2s},\qquad t\nearrow T.

(4.19)

In addition,

u(t)\rightharpoonup u_{*,m}\qquad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T,

and

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|u_{*,m}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1.

(4.20)

Finally,
u_{*,m}
is a traveling solitary wave profile for (
CS
).

Proof.

Let

w_{0}(z)=a\,\beta_{p}(z)\left(1-\frac{2}{1-\bar{p}z}\right).

Then

u_{0}(z)=z^{m}w_{0}(z).

Let
v(t)
be the solution with initial datum
w_{0}
. By Lemma

2.5
, the solution with initial datum
u_{0}=z^{m}w_{0}
is

u(t,z)=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}t}z^{m}v(t,\mathrm{e}^{-2imt}z).

(4.21)

Since
v\in C([0,T);H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}))
, the same holds for
u
.

From Proposition
4.2
,

v(t,z)-\frac{\beta_{0}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{0}(t)z}\longrightarrow v_{*}(z)\qquad\mbox{in }H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T.

Substituting
z\mapsto e^{-2imt}z
and multiplying by

\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}t}z^{m}
, we get

u(t,z)-\frac{\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)z^{m+1}}{1-\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)z}\longrightarrow u_{*,m}(z)\qquad\mbox{in }H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T}),

(4.22)

where

\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t):=\mathrm{e}^{-2imt}\alpha_{0}(t),\qquad\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t):=\mathrm{e}^{-i(m^{2}+2m)t}\beta_{0}(t),

and

u_{*,m}(z):=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}z^{m}v_{*}(\mathrm{e}^{-2imT}z).

Using (
4.5
), this gives exactly (
4.17
).

The singular term in (
4.22
) naturally has numerator

z^{m+1}
. To put it in the form used in Theorem
1.1
, note that

|\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)|=|\alpha_{0}(t)|\to 1\qquad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T.

For
t
sufficiently close to
T
, define

\alpha_{m}(t):=\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t),\qquad\beta_{m}(t):=\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)\bigl(\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)\bigr)^{-m}.

This is well-defined near
T
, because

|\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)|=|\alpha_{0}(t)|\to 1\mbox{ as }t\nearrow T.

Then

\frac{\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)z^{m+1}}{1-\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)z}=\frac{\beta_{m}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{m}(t)z}-\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)\bigr)^{j-1-m}z^{j},

with the convention that the sum is empty when
m=0
. Since

|\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)|=|\beta_{0}(t)|\to 0\mbox{ as }t\nearrow T,

the polynomial correction tends to zero in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Thus
(
4.22
) implies (
4.16
). We extend

\alpha_{m}
and
\beta_{m}
arbitrarily to the remaining part of
[0,T)
, with

\alpha_{m}(t)\in\mathbb{D}
, since only their asymptotic behavior as
t\nearrow T
is used.

The pole asymptotics follow immediately from Proposition
4.2
. Indeed,

|\alpha_{m}(t)|=|\alpha_{0}(t)|,

and

|\beta_{m}(t)|^{2}=|\beta_{0}(t)|^{2}|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{-2m}.

Since

|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{2}=1-c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

we have

|\alpha_{0}(t)|^{-2m}=1+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{2}).

Together with

|\beta_{0}(t)|^{2}=c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

this proves (
4.18
).

It remains to check the Sobolev blow-up rate. It suffices to compute the norm of

\widetilde{P}_{m}(t,z):=\frac{\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)z^{m+1}}{1-\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)z}.

Indeed, the difference between
\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)
and

\frac{\beta_{m}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{m}(t)z}

is a polynomial tending to zero in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, and the remainder in
(
4.22
) remains bounded in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
.

Let

q_{t}:=|\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)|^{2}.

The nonzero Fourier coefficients of
\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)
are

\widehat{\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)}(m+1+k)=\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)\bigl(\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)\bigr)^{k},\qquad k\geq 0.

Hence

\|\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}=|\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)|^{2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(1+(m+1+k)^{2}\right)^{s}q_{t}^{k}.

As
q_{t}\to 1^{-}
,

\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(1+(m+1+k)^{2}\right)^{s}q_{t}^{k}\sim\frac{\Gamma(2s+1)}{(1-q_{t})^{2s+1}}.

Using the pole asymptotics above, we obtain

\|\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\sim\sqrt{\Gamma(2s+1)}\left(\frac{(1+r)^{3}}{4r(1-r)}\right)^{s}(T-t)^{-2s}\mbox{ as }t\nearrow T.

Since
u(t)-\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)
remains bounded in
H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, this proves
(
4.19
).

The weak convergence also follows from the shifted decomposition. For each fixed Fourier
mode, the Fourier coefficient of
\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)
tends to zero because

|\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)|=|\beta_{0}(t)|\to 0\mbox{ as }t\nearrow T.

Moreover,

\|\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{|\widetilde{\beta}_{m}(t)|^{2}}{1-|\widetilde{\alpha}_{m}(t)|^{2}}

remains bounded as
t\nearrow T
. Hence

\widetilde{P}_{m}(t)\rightharpoonup 0\qquad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\mbox{ as }t\nearrow T.

Together with (
4.22
), this gives

u(t)\rightharpoonup u_{*,m}\qquad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\mbox{ as }t\nearrow T.

Finally, multiplication by
z^{m}
, rotation
z\mapsto e^{-2imT}z
, and multiplication
by the scalar phase
e^{-im^{2}T}
preserve the
L^{2}
-norm. Therefore

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}},\qquad\|u_{*,m}\|_{L^{2}}=\|v_{*}\|_{L^{2}}.

Using (
4.8
), we get

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|u_{*,m}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1.

This proves (
4.20
).

Since
v_{*}
is a traveling solitary wave profile, applying the Galilean transformation
to the corresponding traveling wave solution shows that
u_{*,m}
is again a traveling
solitary wave profile. Equivalently, (
4.17
) can be written in the form

u_{*,m}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}T}z^{m}\left(A_{m}+\frac{C_{m}}{1-q_{m}z}\right),

where

q_{m}:=i\rho\bar{p}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i(\Theta_{*}+2mT)}\in\mathbb{D}

and
A_{m},C_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
are suitable constants. This completes the proof.
∎

Combine Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.3
, we can now complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
u_{0}
be the datum (
1.7
). With

r:=|p|^{2},\qquad\rho:=|p|,\qquad\gamma:=\sqrt{\frac{1-r}{1+r}},\qquad a:=\mathrm{e}^{i\theta}\gamma,

we can write

u_{0}(z)=z^{m}w_{0}(z),\qquad w_{0}(z)=a\,\beta_{p}(z)\left(1-\frac{2}{1-\bar{p}z}\right).

Equivalently, the core datum is of the form

w_{0}(z)=\beta_{p}(z)\left(a+\frac{c}{1-\bar{p}z}\right),\qquad c:=-2a.

The precise dynamics are given by Proposition
4.2
in the
core case, and by Proposition
4.3
for all shifted data

z^{m}w_{0}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. In particular,

u\in C([0,T);H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})),

and there exist functions

\alpha_{m}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{D},\qquad\beta_{m}:[0,T)\to\mathbb{C},

such that

u(t,z)-\frac{\beta_{m}(t)z}{1-\alpha_{m}(t)z}\longrightarrow u_{*,m}(z)\qquad\mbox{in }H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T.

This is exactly (
1.10
). The limiting profile is the one
given in (
1.14
), which agrees with (
4.17
). Moreover,
Proposition
4.3
gives

|\alpha_{m}(t)|^{2}=1-c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),\qquad|\beta_{m}(t)|^{2}=c_{0}(T-t)^{2}+\mathcal{O}((T-t)^{3}),

where

c_{0}=\frac{4r(1-r)}{(1+r)^{3}}.

This proves (
1.11
).

The same proposition gives the weak convergence

u(t)\rightharpoonup u_{*,m}\qquad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})\quad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T,

and the mass quantization identity

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|u_{*,m}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=1.

Thus (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) hold.

Finally, the Sobolev blow-up rate follows from (
4.19
):

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\sim\sqrt{\Gamma(2s+1)}\left(\frac{(1+r)^{3}}{4r(1-r)}\right)^{s}(T-t)^{-2s},\qquad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T.

This proves (
1.9
). In particular,

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\to+\infty\qquad\mbox{as }t\nearrow T

for every
s>0
, and therefore
T
is a finite blow-up time. The proof of
Theorem
1.1
is complete.
∎

5.
On global existence

We now prove the global existence result, Theorem
1.3
. In view of
Lemma
2.5
, it is enough to prove the result for the core datum

m=0
. More precisely, we first prove strong stability and a uniform spectral gap for
the two-dimensional core block. Then the shifted data
z^{m}w_{0}
,
m\geq 1
, are obtained
from the core case by Galilean covariance.

Throughout this section, the core potential is

w_{0}(z)=\beta_{p}(z)\left(a+\frac{c}{1-\bar{p}z}\right),\qquad 0<|p|<1,

with

a\bar{c}+\frac{|c|^{2}}{1-|p|^{2}}=2,\qquad 2a+c\neq 0.

As before, we write

r:=|p|^{2},\qquad\psi:=\beta_{p}^{2}.

Proposition 5.1

(Strong stability and a uniform spectral gap for the core datum)
.

Let

A_{\tau}^{(0)}:=\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{w_{0}}}S^{*},\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}\right)^{n}w_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}=0.

(5.1)

Moreover, if

\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}:=\mathrm{e}^{-it}\mathrm{e}^{-2itL_{w_{0}}}S^{*},

then there exists
q_{0}\in(0,1)
such that, for every physical time
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

r_{\rm sp}\left(\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}\right)\leq q_{0}.

(5.2)

Proof.

We first prove the strong decay (
5.1
).

Step 1: reduction to the finite-dimensional model space.

We claim that

w_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}.

Indeed, write

s(z):=1-\bar{p}z,\qquad e_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{s(z)},\qquad e_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{s(z)^{2}}.

Then

\beta_{p}(z)=\frac{z-p}{s(z)}=-p+(1-r)ze_{0}(z),

and

\frac{\beta_{p}(z)}{s(z)}=\frac{z-p}{s(z)^{2}}=-pe_{0}(z)+(1-r)e_{1}(z).

Moreover,

e_{0}=1+\bar{p}\,ze_{0},\qquad e_{1}=ze_{0}+\bar{p}\,ze_{1}.

Therefore both
\beta_{p}
and
\beta_{p}/s
belong to

\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}=\mathcal{K}_{z}\oplus z\mathcal{K}_{\psi},

and hence

w_{0}=\beta_{p}\left(a+\frac{c}{s}\right)\in\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}.

Next, for every
F\in\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}
and every
h\in L^{2}_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, we have

\langle S^{*}F,\psi h\rangle=\langle F,S\psi h\rangle=\langle F,z\psi h\rangle=0.

Thus

S^{*}\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}.

(5.3)

By Proposition
3.1
, the space
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is invariant under
L_{w_{0}}
.
Since
L_{w_{0}}
is self-adjoint,
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is reducing for
L_{w_{0}}
. Hence

\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{w_{0}}}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi},\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

Combining this with (
5.3
), we get

A_{\tau}^{(0)}\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}.

In particular,

A_{\tau}^{(0)}w_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi}.

Since
A_{\tau}^{(0)}\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, it follows that, for every
n\geq 1
,

\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}\right)^{n}w_{0}=\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)^{n-1}A_{\tau}^{(0)}w_{0}.

(5.4)

Step 2: pointwise strong stability.

By Proposition
4.1
, the non-resonance condition

2a+c\neq 0

implies that

A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}

has no unimodular eigenvalue for any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
A_{\tau}^{(0)}
is a contraction,
the finite-dimensional operator
A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
is also a contraction. Hence
all its eigenvalues have modulus at most one. Since there is no eigenvalue on the unit
circle, every eigenvalue has modulus strictly smaller than one, i.e.

r_{\rm sp}\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)<1.

Because
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is finite dimensional, this implies

\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)^{n}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\mbox{in operator norm as }n\to\infty.

Using (
5.4
), we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}\right)^{n}w_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}=0.

This proves (
5.1
).

Step 3: uniform spectral gap for the core block.

We now prove the uniform spectral gap. As in the proof of Proposition

4.1
, write

A_{\tau}^{(0)}e_{1}=x(\tau)e_{0}+y(\tau)e_{1}.

There we found

x(\tau)=b_{+}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda_{+}\tau}+b_{-}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda_{-}\tau},

where

b_{+},b_{-}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad b_{+}+b_{-}=1.

Moreover,

x(\tau)=0\quad\mbox{for some }\tau\in\mathbb{R}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2a+c=0.

Since
2a+c\neq 0
, we have
b_{+}\neq b_{-}
. Because
b_{+},b_{-}\in\mathbb{R}
and

b_{+}+b_{-}=1
, this implies

|b_{+}|\neq|b_{-}|.

Consequently,

|x(\tau)|\geq\bigl||b_{+}|-|b_{-}|\bigr|=:\delta>0\qquad\mbox{for every }\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.5)

We claim that

\sup_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}r_{\rm sp}\left(A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}\right)<1.

(5.6)

Suppose, by contradiction, that (
5.6
) fails. Then there exist

\tau_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, eigenvalues
\mu_{n}
of
A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
, and normalized
eigenvectors
g_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, such that

A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}g_{n}=\mu_{n}g_{n},\qquad\|g_{n}\|_{L^{2}}=1,\qquad|\mu_{n}|\to 1.

Since
\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau_{n}L_{w_{0}}}
is unitary,

|\mu_{n}|^{2}=\|A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}g_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|S^{*}g_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|g_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-|g_{n}(0)|^{2}.

Thus

g_{n}(0)\to 0.

Since

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{0},e_{1}\},\qquad e_{0}(0)=1,\qquad e_{1}(0)=0,

the unit vectors
g_{n}
approach the one-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{C}e_{1}
. After passing
to a subsequence and multiplying
g_{n}
by harmless complex phases, we may assume that

g_{n}\to\frac{e_{1}}{\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}}\qquad\mbox{in }\mathcal{K}_{\psi}.

We now show that this forces
A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}e_{1}
to approach
\mathbb{C}e_{1}
. Since

A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}
is a contraction,

\displaystyle\left\|A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}e_{1}-\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}g_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|e_{1}-\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}g_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}}\longrightarrow 0.

On the other hand,

A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}g_{n}=\mu_{n}g_{n}.

Hence

\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}g_{n}=\|e_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\mu_{n}g_{n}\in\mathbb{C}g_{n}.

Since the lines
\mathbb{C}g_{n}
converge to the line
\mathbb{C}e_{1}
, we obtain

\operatorname{dist}\left(A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}e_{1},\mathbb{C}e_{1}\right)\longrightarrow 0.

Let
P_{e_{0}}
denote the coordinate projection onto the
e_{0}
-component in the basis

(e_{0},e_{1})
. This is a continuous linear functional on the finite-dimensional space

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, and it vanishes on
\mathbb{C}e_{1}
. Therefore

P_{e_{0}}\left(A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}e_{1}\right)\to 0.

But, by definition of
x(\tau)
,

P_{e_{0}}\left(A_{\tau_{n}}^{(0)}e_{1}\right)=x(\tau_{n}).

Thus

x(\tau_{n})\to 0,

which contradicts the uniform lower bound (
5.5
). This
proves (
5.6
).

Finally, we pass from
A_{\tau}^{(0)}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}
to

\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}
. Since

\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}=\mathrm{e}^{-it}A_{2t}^{(0)},

the scalar phase
\mathrm{e}^{-it}
does not affect the spectral radius. Moreover, by
(
5.3
), with respect to the decomposition

\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}=\mathcal{K}_{\psi}\oplus\mathbb{C}\psi,

the operator
\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}
has the block form

\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{\psi}}&*\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

The additional one-dimensional block contributes only the eigenvalue
0
. Therefore
(
5.6
) implies the existence of
q_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

r_{\rm sp}\left(\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}\right)\leq q_{0}\qquad\mbox{for every }t\in\mathbb{R}.

This proves (
5.2
).
∎

We now pass from the core stability statement to global existence for the whole shifted
finite-gap family (
1.2
). The only input beyond Proposition

5.1
is the Galilean covariance from Lemma

2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
u_{0}
be of the form (
1.2
), satisfying (
1.3
) and

2a+c\neq 0.

After absorbing the harmless constant phase into
a
and
c
, we may write

u_{0}(z)=z^{m}w_{0}(z),\qquad w_{0}(z)=\beta_{p}(z)\left(a+\frac{c}{1-\bar{p}z}\right).

Indeed, multiplying both
a
and
c
by the same unimodular constant leaves

a\bar{c}+\frac{|c|^{2}}{1-|p|^{2}}

unchanged, and it multiplies
2a+c
by the same unimodular constant. Thus both
(
1.3
) and the non-resonance condition are preserved.

We first consider the core case
m=0
. Let
v
be the maximal smooth solution with
initial datum
w_{0}
. By Proposition
5.1
, for every

\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\left(\mathrm{e}^{-i\tau L_{w_{0}}}S^{*}\right)^{n}w_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}}=0.

Therefore the stability principle, Lemma
2.3
, implies that the
core solution is global:

v\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})).

It remains to prove the uniform Sobolev bounds in the core case. We use the explicit
formula. Since

w_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}

and

\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}\subset\mathcal{K}_{z\psi},

the solution can be computed on this finite-dimensional space:

v(t,z)=\mathcal{M}\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-z\,\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}\right)^{-1}w_{0}\right).

(5.7)

By (
5.2
), there exists
q_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

r_{\rm sp}\left(\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}\right)\leq q_{0}\qquad\mbox{for every }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the closure of the family

\left\{\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}|_{\mathcal{K}_{z\psi}}:\ t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

in the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{z\psi})
. Since the operators

\Sigma_{t}^{(0),*}
are contractions,
\mathcal{M}
is compact. Since the spectral radius
is continuous in finite dimension,

r_{\rm sp}(M)\leq q_{0}\qquad\mbox{for every }M\in\mathcal{M}.

Choose
q\in(q_{0},1)
, and then choose
R>1
such that

Rq<1.

For every
M\in\mathcal{M}
and every
|z|\leq R
, we have

r_{\rm sp}(zM)\leq Rq_{0}<Rq<1.

Hence
\mathrm{Id}-zM
is invertible. By compactness, the inverse is uniformly bounded:

C_{R}:=\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sup_{|z|\leq R}\left\|\left(\mathrm{Id}-zM\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}_{z\psi})}<+\infty.

It follows from (
5.7
) that
v(t,z)
extends
holomorphically to the disk
\{|z|<R\}
, with a uniform bound

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{|z|\leq R}|v(t,z)|\leq C_{R,w_{0}}.

By Cauchy’s estimate, the Fourier coefficients of
v(t)
satisfy

|\widehat{v}(t,n)|\leq C_{R,w_{0}}R^{-n},\qquad n\geq 0,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Consequently, for every
s>0
,

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|v(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}\leq C_{R,w_{0}}\sum_{n\geq 0}(1+n^{2})^{s}R^{-2n}<+\infty.

Thus

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|v(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{w_{0},s}1,\qquad s>0.

(5.8)

We now treat the shifted cases
m\geq 1
. By Lemma
2.5
, the
solution with initial datum

u_{0}(z)=z^{m}w_{0}(z)

is given by

u(t,z)=\mathrm{e}^{-im^{2}t}z^{m}v(t,\mathrm{e}^{-2imt}z).

Since
v
is global, this formula defines a global smooth solution
u
. By uniqueness
of smooth solution, this is the maximal solution with initial datum
u_{0}
.
Therefore

u\in C(\mathbb{R};H^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{T})).

Moreover, by the Sobolev comparison in Lemma
2.5
, for every

s>0
,

\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{m,s}\|v(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}.

Together with (
5.8
), this gives

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim_{u_{0},s}1.

This proves Theorem
1.3
.
∎
