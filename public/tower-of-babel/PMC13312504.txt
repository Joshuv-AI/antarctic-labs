Frameworks for adverse childhood experiences in rural India
Authors: Sowmyashree Mayur Kaku
Published: 2026

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) are known predictors of lifelong mental and physical health problems,1, 2, 3 with research demonstrating that there is dose–response association between ACEs and later depression, suicidality, substance use disorders, and chronic diseases.4 The ACE framework that captures ten categories of abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, has been used increasingly in global mental health research, including for designing prevention measures. However, this inadequately captures forms of adversity prevalent in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) contexts.5

Gaps in conventional ACE frameworks have been identified across LMIC regions including African, South Asian and Latin America.1,6,7 These studies highlight adversities such as child labour, forced migration, refugee crisis, limited access to education and malnutrition. Even though the nature of these adversities varies across countries and contexts, they share common underpinnings rooted in poverty, social exclusion, and inequitable systems. In this paper, rural India is used as an illustrative context to explore these global limitations.

In rural India, childhood adversity arises from structural and gender inequities that are not directly under the traditional ACEs framework. In India, 65% children still live in rural areas, and have exposure to poverty, food insecurity, parent-child separation, caste-based discrimination, and limited access to health and educational services. The National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5) data revealed that 23% of women aged 20–24 years were married before the age of 18, with a higher prevalence in several rural districts. Even with legal prohibitions, adolescent pregnancy remains common and domestic violence is reported by over 30% of ever-married women, indicating relational trauma within family systems.8 These factors are usually present alongside existing issues, such as a limited nurturing environment, a lack of secure attachments, and significant school drop-out rates.9

The limitations of conventional ACE measures are particularly evident when considering youth mental health outcomes. India records one of the highest numbers of youth suicides globally. The National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) documented over 11,000 suicides among children and adolescents in 2023, demonstrating a 20–25% increase across the last decade.10 Most frequently, causes recorded in such surveys are family problems, marriage-related and relationship issues, substance abuse, and failure in examination, which provide minimal insight into underlying psychosocial determinants.11 Qualitative and hospital-based studies, scoping and systematic reviews indicate that early marriage, interpersonal violence, dowry-related coercion, caste discrimination, chronic poverty and other stressors that are rooted in family structures contribute substantially to self-harm in rural settings.9,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Unfortunately, these experiences are not well documented in ACE frameworks used in research or clinical screening (Box 1).Box 1Illustrative caseA 16-year-old girl from rural India, who had completed Grade 10 with good academic performance, discontinued her education following an early marriage. Illustrative case

A 16-year-old girl from rural India, who had completed Grade 10 with good academic performance, discontinued her education following an early marriage. Within a short period, she experienced multiple spontaneous abortions, likely in the context of poor nutrition and limited access to healthcare. She was subjected to ongoing domestic violence and dowry-related harassment by her husband and in-laws. Her parents declined to provide support after the marriage, resulting in social and emotional isolation. She subsequently attempted suicide by ingesting pesticide and required emergency medical care. This case illustrates forms of adversity including early marriage, reproductive loss, gender-based violence, and abandonment that are not explicitly captured in conventional ACE frameworks but are critical determinants of distress in rural Indian adolescents.

One of the key challenges in adapting ACE frameworks to LMIC contexts is the need to distinguish between adversities that are individually directed and those that are socio-culturally perceived as normative. Theoretically, we can frame them as (1) Individually directed adversities, such as abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, (2) Relational and community-level adversities, including family violence, school exclusion, and peer dynamics, and (3) Structural and socio-cultural adversities, such as gender discrimination, child labour, child marriage, caste-based exclusion, and chronic poverty. Structural and socio-cultural adversities may not necessary be perceived as adverse, despite clear evidence of impact on developmental pathways and mental health. This therefore, highlights the gap in conventional ACE tools that primarily pick up events that the individual experiences.

Existing tools such as the WHO Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) could be modified to incorporate these context-specific barriers that capture structural and socio-cultural (e.g., assessment of child marriage, child labour, gender-based role restriction, social exclusion, access to health care and education) to bolster cross-cultural comparison and contextual sensitivity.

It is of utmost importance to measure these adversities to understand risk pathways better and improve the already existing interventions. There is a pressing need for LMIC-adapted and validated ACE instruments that incorporate structural, gender-based, and cultural stressors with a focus on rural settings. Although illustrated through rural India, these considerations are relevant to several LMIC contexts where adversity is embedded within structural and socio-cultural systems. Community-based early identification of adversities through primary health care systems, schools, and frontline workers can also bolster early intervention and support. If we have to reduce adversities for children in LMIC contexts such as rural India, there is a need to strongly enforce child marriage laws, increase access to secondary education, address domestic violence and expand social protection schemes for vulnerable families. Cross cultural research is also essential to bolster understanding of experiences across different settings and exploring practices that are socially normative.

Childhood adversity in many LMIC settings is deeply entrenched in the existing social systems. A context-sensitive understanding of adversity is therefore necessary if ACE frameworks are to meaningfully inform prevention strategies and child and adolescent mental-health policy in these regions.
