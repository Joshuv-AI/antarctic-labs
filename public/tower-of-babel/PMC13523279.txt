The modified balloon-anchored technique for iliac branch device deployment via ipsilateral femoral access
Authors: Chen Xu, Guo-xiong Xu, Xue-feng Gu, Lei Chen, Yi-qi Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFor iliac branch device (IBD) procedures, contralateral femoral access is generally regarded as the standard route, whereas upper-extremity access is usually reserved for selected anatomically challenging cases. However, both approaches may be difficult or undesirable in patients with a short common iliac artery (CIA), an unfavourable aortic bifurcation, prior endograft reconstruction, or access-related concerns. This study aimed to describe a modified balloon-anchored technique for IBD deployment via ipsilateral femoral access and to evaluate its technical feasibility and early outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective, two-centre study included consecutive patients who underwent IBD deployment via ipsilateral femoral access between May 2023 and February 2024. Eligibility was limited to patients in whom contralateral femoral access was considered unsuitable because of predefined hostile anatomical or procedural features, including CIA length < 50 mm, an unfavourable aortic bifurcation or iliac tortuosity that limited coaxial sheath advancement, prior EVAR or aorto-iliac stent-graft configuration, or relevant access limitations. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) from the aortic arch to the femoral arteries was reviewed, and the modified balloon-anchored technique was applied according to a standardised algorithm. Technical success was defined as successful IBD deployment with preserved IIA branch patency and no type I or III endoleak on final angiography and CTA within 30 days.ResultsTwenty-three patients (91.3% male; median age, 75 years) were included. Indications were aorto-iliac aneurysm with reduced CIA working length (n = 13, 56.5%) and type Ib endoleak after previous aortic stent-graft placement (n = 10, 43.5%). Technical success was achieved in all cases (100%). Over a median follow-up of 16 months (interquartile range [IQR], 14–18 months), the 1-year patency rates for the IBD main body and bridging stent were 100% and 95.7%, respectively. No aortic- or IBD-related re-interventions were required. Two complications occurred (8.7%): one asymptomatic bridging-stent occlusion and one conservatively managed type II endoleak. No major adverse events or clinically evident pelvic ischaemic complications were observed.ConclusionThe ipsilateral balloon-anchored technique appears feasible for IBD deployment and was associated with encouraging early outcomes in selected patients unsuitable for conventional access strategies.

Introduction

Endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) is an established treatment for abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) in suitable anatomy, offering lower early mortality and faster recovery than open surgery [1]. However, approximately one-third of patients with AAA present with a concomitant common iliac artery (CIA) aneurysm [2]. In these patients, achieving adequate distal fixation and seal may require extension of the endograft beyond the orifice of the internal iliac artery (IIA) [3–4]. Bilateral coverage or embolisation of the IIA is associated with pelvic ischaemic complications, including buttock claudication, erectile dysfunction, and, rarely, colonic or perineal necrosis [5–6]. Current clinical guidelines therefore recommend preservation of at least one IIA whenever feasible [7].

IBD deployment is increasingly used to preserve IIA perfusion, with favourable mid-term outcomes in selected anatomy [2, 8–9]. For most IBD procedures, the contralateral femoral approach represents the standard access route, while upper-extremity access remains an alternative for selected situations, including previous EVAR, a short CIA, a narrow or steep aortic bifurcation, or failed transfemoral catheterisation [10]. Nevertheless, a standard contralateral route may be technically challenging when the CIA provides limited working length, when the aortic bifurcation is unfavourable, or when a prior stent-graft neobifurcation increases the risk of device stress during up-and-over manipulation [3, 11]. Although a CIA length of 40 mm may meet the minimum instructions for use for several IBD platforms, a length below 50 mm can still reduce the margin for stable gate orientation and sheath control, particularly when other adverse features coexist.

To address these constraints, this study describes a modified balloon-anchored technique for ipsilateral femoral delivery of the IBD main body and bridging stent, thereby avoiding upper-extremity access and limiting manipulation across the aortic bifurcation. The report follows the STROCSS guidance for observational surgical studies [12].

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective study analysed all consecutive patients who underwent IBD deployment via a modified balloon-anchored ipsilateral femoral approach at two medical centres between May 2023 and February 2024. The decision to use the ipsilateral technique was made during preoperative planning when standard contralateral femoral access was considered unsuitable or potentially unstable. Hostile anatomy was operationally defined as one or more of the following: (1) CIA length < 50 mm, particularly when the IIA ostium lay close to the aortic bifurcation; (2) prior EVAR or an aorto-iliac stent-graft configuration in which up-and-over access was expected to stress the neobifurcation; (3) an unfavourable aortic bifurcation or iliac tortuosity that limited coaxial sheath advancement; or (4) access-related limitations that made upper-extremity or contralateral femoral access undesirable. A CIA length < 50 mm was not considered an absolute contraindication to standard IBD use; rather, it was used as a pragmatic marker of reduced working length. Patients with incomplete perioperative CTA images were excluded. The study design is depicted in Fig. 1. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (J-2025-022-H01), and requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Imaging assessment and IBD management

Preoperative arterial-phase CTA was performed from the aortic arch to the common femoral arteries and was analysed using three-dimensional workstation software (iNuition Aquarius; TeraRecon, San Mateo, CA, USA) by a surgeon experienced in EVAR procedures. The assessment included aneurysm morphology, prior endograft configuration, aortic bifurcation anatomy, IIA ostial height and orientation, iliac tortuosity, and femoral access quality. Flow centrelines were generated for the aorto-iliac and IIA segments to measure vessel diameters and lengths. The iliac tortuosity index was calculated as the ratio of total iliac length along the reconstructed centreline to the straight-line distance from the iliofemoral junction to the aortic bifurcation after three-dimensional volume rendering [10]. Vessel calcification at the femoral access site was classified as mild (0–25% circumferential involvement), moderate (26–75%), or severe (> 75%) [13].

All aortic stent-grafts used were from the Ankura system (Lifetech Scientific, Shenzhen, China), and all IBD main bodies were from the G-iliac system (Lifetech Scientific). Bridging stent-grafts included the SilverFlow (Lifetech Scientific) and Viabahn VBX (W.L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ, USA) systems. Device selection followed the instructions for use and was individualised according to landing-zone diameter, branch length, tortuosity, and the need for radial support. Given the small sample size, outcomes were not stratified by bridging-stent platform.

Procedural details

To maintain consistency and reduce potential learning-curve effects, all procedures followed a standardised protocol and were performed by the same multidisciplinary team, consisting of two vascular surgeons and one interventional radiologist; the primary surgeon travelled between the two centres. Procedures were performed percutaneously in a hybrid suite under spinal or local anaesthesia. Patients received intravenous heparin (100 U/kg) to maintain an activated clotting time of 250–300 s. Under ultrasound guidance, puncture was performed in a vessel segment free of severe calcification to allow guidewire insertion. A small skin incision was made, followed by blunt subcutaneous dissection to facilitate device delivery.

For patients with bilateral aorto-iliac aneurysms and reduced CIA working length, the treatment strategy consisted of embolisation and coverage of one IIA while reconstructing the contralateral IIA. The IIA selected for reconstruction had to provide a distal landing zone > 20 mm in length and > 5 mm in diameter. Contralateral IIA embolisation or coverage was performed only when preservation of at least one IIA was achievable and the collateral circulation was considered acceptable on CTA. Bilateral femoral access was obtained using a preclose technique with two ProGlide devices (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA), followed by insertion of two 10 F sheaths (Terumo, Somerset, NJ, USA). After aorto-iliac arteriography, the aortic stent-graft was advanced over a 0.035-inch super-stiff guidewire (Lunderquist; Cook, Bloomington, IN, USA). The contralateral iliac endoprosthesis was deployed over the same wire, extending into the CIA or external iliac artery (EIA). A suitable distal landing zone was defined as a non-diseased arterial segment > 20 mm in length and < 18 mm in diameter. A second super-stiff guidewire was then introduced to facilitate deployment of the ipsilateral iliac endoprosthesis into the CIA. No additional bridging stent-graft was routinely placed between the bifurcated aortic graft and the IBD main body when adequate overlap and seal were obtained according to CTA-derived measurements and device-specific instructions for use.

The modified balloon-anchored technique is summarised in Fig. 2. For reproducibility, the procedural workflow comprised six steps: (1) ipsilateral introduction of an 18 F sheath and advancement of the IBD main body; (2) CIA angiography and orientation of the side-arm marker; (3) IIA cannulation through a 10 F steerable sheath; (4) temporary use of a 4-mm balloon over a V18 guidewire to facilitate entry of the steerable sheath into the side branch; (5) inflation of a 14-mm stabilising balloon within the IBD main body to anchor the steerable sheath while the bridging stent was advanced; and (6) balloon deflation before bridging-stent deployment and completion angiography.

After CIA angiography, the IBD main body was deployed with the side-arm marker approximately 2 cm proximal to the IIA ostium. Instead of the typical 1-cm reference, a larger offset was chosen to provide sufficient working length for ipsilateral sheath manipulation and balloon-sheath overlap in short CIAs. However, the final distance should be individualized based on CTA-defined IIA anatomy and the planned overlap. A 10 F steerable sheath (Fustar; Lifetech Scientific) was advanced over the IBD bifurcation to cannulate the IIA using a 4 F catheter and a 0.035-inch guidewire (both Terumo). After exchange for a V18 guidewire (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) and catheter removal, a 4-mm balloon (Jiahang; Polyrey Medical, Suzhou, China) was introduced and inflated briefly to guide the sheath into the side branch. The balloon was then deflated, and angiography was used to confirm that the sheath tip was fully within the side branch. A 14-mm balloon (Armada; Abbott Vascular, Beringen, Switzerland) was advanced over the super-stiff wire within the IBD main body and inflated to nominal pressure (typically 10 atm). The balloon diameter was selected to approximate the internal diameter of the IBD main body while accounting for the space occupied by the 10 F steerable sheath, thereby creating controlled balloon-wall apposition rather than oversizing. The balloon was maintained only for the interval required for bridging-stent advancement and fluoroscopic positional confirmation, usually less than 2 min. A single inflation was sufficient in most cases; if alignment was suboptimal, brief repeated inflation could be used after reassessment. This configuration was intended to prevent steerable-sheath kickback, migration, or tilting during bridging-stent advancement. The stabilising balloon was deflated before bridging-stent deployment to minimise the risks of graft distortion, temporary flow compromise, or device injury. Completion angiography and early CTA were reviewed for persistent graft deformation, branch stenosis, type I or III endoleak, and IIA branch patency.

For patients undergoing revision for type Ib endoleak after prior stent-graft placement, IBD deployment was performed. After insertion of an 18 F sheath through the ipsilateral femoral access, CIA angiography was used to identify the IIA ostium and the distal seal zone. The IBD was overlapped with the prior iliac limb according to the planned landing-zone measurements; no dedicated intermediate bridging component was used when overlap and seal were satisfactory. The subsequent balloon-anchored steps followed the same protocol.

Definitions and follow-up

The primary outcome was technical success, defined as successful IBD deployment with maintained patency of the IIA branch and no type I or III endoleak on final angiography and CTA within 30 days. Secondary outcomes were stratified into in-hospital and follow-up outcomes. In-hospital outcomes included procedural time, fluoroscopy duration, IIA reconstruction time, and length of hospital stay. Follow-up outcomes included 30-day mortality, patency of the IBD main body and bridging stent, post-procedural complications, pelvic ischaemic symptoms, and re-intervention rates. Stent patency was defined as a luminal diameter reduction of < 50% on follow-up CTA [2]. Re-interventions were categorised as minimally invasive or surgical procedures performed to address aortic- or IBD-related events.

Patients were prescribed oral dual antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel 75 mg daily and aspirin 100 mg daily) for three months after the procedure, followed by lifelong single antiplatelet therapy and management of comorbidities. Follow-up was scheduled at 1, 3, and 6 months and annually thereafter to evaluate stent patency, endoleak, pelvic ischaemic symptoms, and other complications. Data were collected from digital clinical records and operative documentation by the same operator. CTA results were reviewed by a radiologist with more than five years of experience.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were summarised as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%).

Given the single-arm descriptive design, descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics, procedural details, and clinical outcomes. No formal comparative analysis with contralateral femoral, upper-extremity, or other ipsilateral techniques was performed. Time-to-event data were analysed using Kaplan-Meier analysis, with results presented as cumulative patency rates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals where applicable. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 19.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). All included cases had complete perioperative and follow-up records.

Results

Patients and procedural details

The characteristics of the patient cohort and preoperative anatomy are detailed in Table 1. A total of 23 patients were included (median age, 75.0 years; IQR, 71.0–79.0 years), of whom 21 (91.3%) were male. Indications included aorto-iliac aneurysm with reduced CIA working length (< 50 mm) and other hostile anatomical features (n = 13, 56.5%) and type Ib endoleak after previous aortic stent-graft placement (n = 10, 43.5%). Eight patients (34.8%) underwent contralateral IIA embolisation as part of bilateral aorto-iliac aneurysm treatment. The median diameter of the aortic bifurcation was 39.6 mm (IQR, 31.5–45.8 mm), and the median CIA length was 46.4 mm (IQR, 34.5–52.2 mm). Procedural characteristics are presented in Table 2. A total of 24 bridging stents were deployed in the IIA branches. One patient required an additional stent because of insufficient support from the initial stent in a highly tortuous IIA.

Technical success and clinical outcomes

Technical success was achieved in all cases (100%; Fig. 3). No type I or III endoleak was observed within 30 days. Clinical outcomes are provided in Table 3. No intraoperative complications, including intimal injury, IIA dissection, access-vessel injury, or sheath advancement failure, occurred. The median procedural time was 109.0 min (IQR, 87.0–124.0 min), the median fluoroscopy time was 59.0 min (IQR, 53.0–69.0 min), and the median IIA reconstruction time was 43.0 min (IQR, 34.0–50.0 min). The median length of hospital stay was 4.0 days (IQR, 3.0–5.0 days).

Over a median follow-up of 16.0 months (IQR, 14.0–18.0 months), the 1-year patency rates for the IBD main body and bridging stent were 100% and 95.7%, respectively. The 30-day mortality rate was 0%. No clinically evident pelvic ischaemic symptoms were recorded, including buttock claudication, erectile dysfunction, or colonic ischaemia, among the eight patients who underwent contralateral IIA embolisation. Two post-procedural complications occurred (8.7%). One SilverFlow bridging stent occluded at its distal overlapping segment 12 months after the procedure, probably related to highly tortuous IIA anatomy requiring additional Viabahn stenting during the index procedure; the patient remained asymptomatic during subsequent follow-up. Another patient developed a type II endoleak from retrograde flow via the inferior mesenteric and lumbar arteries; this was managed conservatively under close surveillance because no aneurysm enlargement was observed. No aortic- or IBD-related re-intervention was required, and no major adverse event was reported.

Discussion

This retrospective two-centre study evaluated the feasibility and early outcomes of a modified balloon-anchored technique for IBD deployment via ipsilateral femoral access. The technique achieved technical success in all 23 selected cases, with no pelvic ischaemic complications and no re-interventions during early follow-up. The overall post-procedural complication rate was 8.7%, comprising one asymptomatic bridging-stent occlusion and one type II endoleak. These findings suggest that the approach is technically feasible and may be considered as an alternative strategy for carefully selected patients in whom conventional access is unsuitable. However, the results should be interpreted as preliminary because of the retrospective, single-arm design and small sample size.

Current IBD studies have shown favourable outcomes, including high technical success, durable branch patency, and relatively low rates of complications and re-interventions in appropriately selected patients [2, 8–9, 14]. In contemporary practice, contralateral femoral access is commonly used as the standard approach because it avoids some of the access-related risks associated with upper-extremity routes and provides a familiar pathway for branch cannulation [10, 15–16]. Nevertheless, it does not eliminate all technical limitations. A short CIA, an unfavourable bifurcation angle, severe iliac tortuosity, or a prior aortic neobifurcation may reduce sheath stability, increase device manipulation, prolong fluoroscopy, and potentially increase stress on the existing endograft [11, 17–19]. This study did not include a comparator group, it cannot determine whether the modified ipsilateral approach reduces procedure time, radiation exposure, or clinical events compared with contralateral femoral, brachial, axillary, or other ipsilateral techniques.

Several alternative access strategies have been proposed for anatomically challenging aorto-iliac interventions. Upper-extremity routes may facilitate antegrade manipulation, and open axillary access has been described as an alternative when femoral access is unsuitable for complex iliac stenting [20]. In the IBD setting, Stern et al. introduced a looped-wire, pre-cannulated gate technique for ipsilateral IBE deployment [21]. This method can be useful but requires maintenance of wire tension across both ends of the crossover wire and may be influenced by device availability. Riess et al. reported a balloon-abutment method in which a semi-compliant balloon supported advancement of a sheath into the IIA [3]. The present modification differs by using a 14-mm balloon within the IBD main body to stabilise the steerable sheath in both vertical and horizontal planes during bridging-stent advancement. This design aims to reduce sheath kickback and tilting at the IBD bifurcation without crossing the aortic bifurcation or requiring upper-extremity access.

The observed complication rate of 8.7% is within the broad range reported for IBD procedures using conventional access strategies [2, 8–9, 22–23]. The single bridging-stent occlusion occurred at the distal overlap of a branch requiring adjunctive stenting in a tortuous IIA, suggesting that branch geometry and overlap configuration may be more relevant to late patency than access route alone. The conservatively managed type II endoleak was aortic-related rather than IBD-related. These observations reinforce the importance of detailed CTA planning, branch landing-zone assessment, careful stent selection, and selective embolisation of relevant collateral arteries when clinically indicated.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, non-comparative design and small cohort limit the strength of the conclusions and preclude claims of superiority or equivalence relative to contralateral femoral, brachial, axillary, or other ipsilateral techniques. Second, the cohort included both primary aorto-iliac aneurysm cases and revision cases for type Ib endoleak, introducing anatomical and procedural heterogeneity that may influence outcome interpretation. Third, hostile anatomy was defined pragmatically rather than by fixed thresholds for bifurcation angle or tortuosity index, it reduces reproducibility and should be addressed in future studies. Finally, outcome assessment relied mainly on technical success, CTA patency, and clinical events; advanced quantitative evaluation of IIA flow restoration, detailed branch deformation, or anastomotic stenosis was not performed. The technique should be regarded as a proof-of-concept approach requiring validation in larger prospective comparative studies with longer follow-up.

Conclusion

The modified ipsilateral balloon-anchored technique appears feasible for IBD deployment and was associated with encouraging early outcomes in selected patients unsuitable for conventional access strategies. Larger comparative studies are required regarding safety, efficacy, durability, and procedural advantages.
