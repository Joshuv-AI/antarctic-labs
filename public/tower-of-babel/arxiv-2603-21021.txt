Abstract
. We prove determinantal-Pfaffian formulae that simultaneously generalise the Pfaffian minor summation formula of Ishikawa and Wakayama and Byun’s recent minor summation formula. These formulae are based on factorisation formulae for the determinant of the sum of a skew-symmetric matrix and a rank-1 matrix. Applications include a Cauchy-type identity for skew Schur functions.

1.
Introduction and statement of main results

Non-intersecting paths
are instrumental in many areas of enumerative
combinatorics, in particular in the enumeration of plane partitions
and tableaux, see e.g.
[
7
, Sec. 10.13]
and
[
5
, Sec. 4]
.
Moreover, they also appear in the guise of
vicious walkers

as objects of their own interest in statistical physics,
cf.
[
3
]
.

The fundamental result on the enumeration of non-intersecting paths
is Lindström’s determinantal formula
[
8
, Theorem 1]

for the number of non-intersecting paths in an acyclic directed graph which
have fixed starting and ending points. This theorem was rediscovered
several times — and also anticipated in a more restricted form,
see
[
6
, Footnote 5]
.

In several situations — most prominently in the enumeration of
plane partitions with symmetry (cf.
[
5
, Sec. 6]
),
it is necessary to count families of non-intersecting lattice
paths where either starting or ending points are not fixed.
In view of Lindström’s determinantal formula, this leads us to
the problem of computing the sum of all maximal minors of a
rectangular matrix. Okada’s
minor summation formula

[
10
, Theorem 3]
provides a Pfaffian for this sum
(cf. also
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers, where
m
is even.
Furthermore, let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix. Then we have

(1.1)

\underset{|I|=m}{\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}}\det A^{I}=\operatorname{Pf}\bigl(AU_{n}A^{t}-AU_{n}^{t}A^{t}\bigr),

where, by definition,
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, where

A^{I}
denotes the submatrix of
A
consisting of the columns
indexed by
I
,
and
U_{n}
is the upper triangular matrix with all entries
above the diagonal equal to
1
and all other entries equal to
0
.

This theorem can also be used in the case where
m
is odd, that is,
for the evaluation of the sum of odd-size minors: augment the

m\times n
matrix
A
(with
m
odd) to the
(m+1)\times(n+1)

matrix
\hat{A}
be defining

\hat{A}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}A_{i,j},&\text{for $1\leq i\leq m$ and $1\leq j\leq n$},\\
1,&\text{for $i=m+1$ and $j=n+1$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and apply Theorem
1
to the augmented matrix
\hat{A}
.

Ishikawa and Wakayama
[
4
]
have generalised (
1.1
)
to the
Pfaffian minor summation formula
, see
Theorem
13
in Subsection
5.2
.

Recently, Byun
[
2
, Theorem 1.3]
came up with a new
formula for the sum of all maximal minors of a rectangular matrix.

Theorem 2
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers.
Furthermore, let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix. Then we have

(1.2)

\left(\underset{|I|=m}{\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}}\det A^{I}\right)^{2}=\det\bigl(A(2U_{n}+\operatorname{Id}_{n})A^{t}\bigr),

with the same conventions as in Theorem
1
.
Here, and in the sequel,
\operatorname{Id}_{n}
stands for the
n\times n
identity
matrix.

We point out that a particularly intriguing feature of this
striking formula is the fact that it holds for even
and

odd
m
alike, as opposed to Okada’s formula in
Theorem
1
.

For us it was clear that the formula (
1.2
) could not just
be an isolated coincidence; something more general had to be lurking
behind. Consequently, we set us the goal to find the hidden, more
general identities that were waiting to be discovered.

The purpose of this article is to report our corresponding findings.
They let us discover much more general identities, special cases of
which also cover the Pfaffian minor summation formula of Ishikawa
and Wakayama. Moreover, as by-products, we also came across
Pfaffian and determinantal identities that, as we believe, are
interesting in their own right.

Since we also think that the path of discovery is interesting and
instructive,
we next describe how we went along and approached our goal, which in
the end let us arrive at our first main result, Theorem
3
.

Our first thought was that, with
A
and
A^{t}
appearing
in (
1.2
), there should actually be
two
rectangular
matrices,
A
and
B
, in the sought-for generalisation.
It did not make sense to make
A^{t}
into
B^{t}
since this does
not give anything “nice” (whatever that means) already in the
case where
m=1
. However, in (
1.2
) there is this term

2U_{n}
; why not split it into
U_{n}+U_{n}
? Then an “
A
–
B
”
generalisation suggests itself: what if, instead of the right-hand
side of (
1.2
), we consider

(1.3)

\det\bigl(AU_{n}B^{t}+BU_{n}A^{t}+AB^{t}\bigr)\ ?

We went to the computer, and the computer told us that this
determinant decomposed into the product of two factors.
Not a square anymore, it was also not the product of one factor
depending on
A
and the other depending on
B
; rather both
factors depended on both
A

and

B
. In any case, we
managed to work out guesses for the factors, see Theorem
17
in
Subsection
5.3

for the complete factorisation.

Nevertheless, the asymmetry in (
1.3
) kept us thinking.
If we interchanged
A
and
B
in (
1.3
), then, according to
our previous (experimental) findings, also

(1.4)

\det\bigl(AU_{n}B^{t}+BU_{n}A^{t}+BA^{t}\bigr)

factorised into two factors, as predicted in Theorem
17
.
So, maybe we should interpolate between (
1.3
)
and (
1.4
)? The most straightforward way to do this
was to connect
AB^{t}
and
BA^{t}
by a convex combination
of the two; that is, to consider

(1.5)

\det\bigl(AU_{n}B^{t}+BU_{n}A^{t}+\lambda AB^{t}+(1-\lambda)BA^{t}\bigr),

where
\lambda
is some additional variable. We went again to the
computer, which told us that this determinant decomposed into
two factors.

However, this is still asymmetric. If we transpose the matrix inside
the determinant in (
1.5
), then our computer calculations
show that, with
L_{n}
denoting the transpose of
U_{n}
, also

\det\bigl(AL_{n}B^{t}+BL_{n}A^{t}+\lambda BA^{t}+(1-\lambda)AB^{t}\bigr)

decomposes into a product of two factors. So then, what about
the convex combination

(1.6)

\det\Bigl(\mu\bigl(AU_{n}B^{t}+BU_{n}A^{t}\bigr)+(1-\mu)\bigl(AL_{n}B^{t}+BL_{n}A^{t}\bigr)+\lambda AB^{t}+(1-\lambda)BA^{t}\Bigr)\ ?

The computer told us that this determinant decomposes into a
product of two factors.

It did not take us very long to realise that this was still not
“general enough”. Let us replace
\mu U_{n}+(1-\mu)L_{n}+\lambda\operatorname{Id}_{n}

in (
1.6
)
by an
arbitrary

n\times n
matrix
X
. The computer still
said that this would factor. Leaving the path to the
identification of
the factors aside, this leads us to our first main result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
A
and
B
be
m\times n
matrices,

X
an
n\times n
matrix, and

\mathcal{J}=(1)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
. If
m
is even, we have

(1.7)

\det\bigl(AXB^{t}+B(\mathcal{J}-X^{t})A^{t}\bigr)=f_{A,B}(X)f_{B,A}(\mathcal{J}-X^{t})

where

f_{A,B}(X)=\underset{|I|=|J|=m/2}{\sum_{I,J\subseteq[n]}}\det X_{I,J}\det(A^{I}B^{J}),

with
X_{I,J}
denoting the submatrix of
X
consisting of the entries
in the
rows indexed by
I
and columns indexed by
J
,
A^{I}
denoting
the submatrix of
A
consisting of the columns indexed by
I
,
with an analogous meaning of
B^{J}
, and with
A^{I}B^{J}
denoting
the concatenation of the matrices
A^{I}
and
B^{J}
.

If
m
is odd, we have

(1.8)

\det\bigl(AXB^{t}+B(\mathcal{J}-X^{t})A^{t}\bigr)=g_{A,B}(X)g_{B,A}(\mathcal{J}-X^{t})=(-1)^{(m-1)/2}g_{A,B}(X)g_{B,A}(X^{t}),

where

g_{A,B}(X)=\underset{|I|=(m+1)/2,\ |J|=(m-1)/2}{\sum_{I,J\subseteq[n]}}\det\bigl(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J}\bigr)\det(A^{I}B^{J}),

where
\mathbf{1}
denotes a column of length
(m+1)/2
consisting
entirely of
1
’s.

As we are going to show, these factorisation formulae follow from
factorisations of the determinant of the sum of a skew-symmetric
matrix and a rank-1 matrix, together with the following Pfaffian
expansion formula, which is our second main result.

Theorem 4
.

With the notation of Theorem
3
, for even
m
we have

(1.9)

\operatorname{Pf}(AXB^{t}-BX^{t}A^{t})=(-1)^{\binom{m/2}{2}}f_{A,B}(X).

Remark 5
.

The specialisation
X=\operatorname{Id}_{n}
, that is,

\operatorname{Pf}(AB^{t}-BA^{t})=(-1)^{\binom{m/2}{2}}f_{A,B}(\operatorname{Id}_{n})=(-1)^{\binom{m/2}{2}}\underset{|I|=m/2}{\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}}\det(A^{I}B^{I}),

can be considered as a Pfaffian analogue of the Cauchy–Binet
theorem. Okada has also given Pfaffian analogues of the Cauchy–Binet theorem,
see
[
11
, Sec. 3]
,
but they are completely unrelated.

Remark 6
.

By observing that

AXB^{t}-BX^{t}A^{t}=(A\,B)\begin{pmatrix}O&X\\
-X^{t}&O\end{pmatrix}(A\,B)^{t},

where
(A\,B)
denotes the concatenation of the matrices
A
and
B
,
one could prove Theorem
4
using the Pfaffian minor
summation formula of Ishikawa and Wakayama
in Theorem
13
. However, we will
provide an independent proof in Section
3
since
we will argue in Subsection
5.2
that the special case
of Theorem
4
in which
A=B

implies
the Pfaffian minor summation formula.
In that sense, although equivalent,
Theorem
4
may be considered as a
non-symmetric extension of that formula.

Our paper is organised as follows.
In the next section, we prove factorisation formulae for the
determinant of the sum of a skew-symmetric matrix and a rank-1 matrix,
see Propositions
8
and
9
. In
Section
3
, we provide the proof of
Theorem
4
, and we prove a related auxiliary result
that we need later for the proof of the odd case of
Theorem
3
. By combining the results from
Sections
2
and
3
, we are then in the position
to provide the proof of Theorem
3
, which we do in
Section
4
. Several applications of our formulae are given
in the final section, Section
5
.
Subsection
5.1
contains the explicit statements of some
interesting special cases of the factorisations proved in
Section
2
. In Subsection
5.2
we show that
Theorem
4
implies the Pfaffian minor summation formula
of Ishikawa and Wakayama. Subsection
5.3
presents an asymmetric
extension of Byun’s minor summation formula in
Theorem
2
and of Okada’s minor summation formula
in Theorem
1
.
Finally, in Subsection

we

For the sake of completeness, we provide a proof of the fact that the

the proof of Theorem
3
.) This may well be hidden
somewhere in the literature, but we were not able to find an explicit
place where a proof is given.

2.
The determinant of a skew-symmetric plus a rank-
1
matrix

In this section, we prove two factorisations of the determinant of
the sum of a skew-symmetric matrix and a rank-1 matrix, see
Propositions
8
and
9
below. They will
be instrumental in the proof of Theorem
3
given in
Section
4
. Moreover, as we believe, they are interesting
in their own right.

For the proofs of Propositions
8
and
9
,
we heavily rely on Stembridge’s combinatorial proof

[
13
, Proof of Prop. 2.2]
of the classical fact that
the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix is equal to the
square of the Pfaffian of the matrix. For the convenience of
the reader, we recall that proof here.

To begin with, given a skew-symmetric matrix
Y
of size
m
, where

m
is even, its Pfaffian may be defined by

\operatorname{Pf}(Y)=\sum_{\mu\text{ a perfect matching of }[m]}\operatorname{sgn}\mu\cdot\underset{\{i,j\}\in\mu}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}}Y_{i,j},

where the sign
\operatorname{sgn}\mu
of the perfect matching is defined
as
(-1)^{\text{cr}(\mu)}
, with
\text{cr}(\mu)
the number
of crossings in the pictorial representation of
\mu
; alternatively,

\text{cr}(\mu)
is the number of quadruples
(i,j,k,l)
with

i<j<k<l
such that
\{i,k\}
and
\{j,l\}
are pairs in the
matching
\mu
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
Y
be a skew-symmetric matrix of size
m
, where
m
is even.
Then

(2.1)

\det(Y)=\operatorname{Pf}^{2}(Y).

Proof.

By definition, the determinant
\det(Y)
equals

(2.2)

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i,\sigma(i)},

where
S_{m}
denotes the set of permutations of
[m]
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation that contains an odd-length cycle
in its disjoint cycle decomposition.
Among the odd-length cycles of
\sigma
,
let
c=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
be the one that contains the smallest
element. Then form another permutation
\sigma^{\prime}
by reverting the
direction of the cycle
c
, that is, by starting with
\sigma

and subsequently replacing
c
by
(a_{k},a_{k-1},\dots,a_{1})
.
Clearly, we have
\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=\operatorname{sgn}\sigma^{\prime}
. On the other hand, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i,\sigma(i)}=-\prod_{i=1}^{m}Y_{i,\sigma^{\prime}(i)}

since
Y
is skew-symmetric, and hence the reversion of the
cycle
c
causes a sign of
(-1)^{k}=-1
. As a consequence, the
contributions of
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
cancel in the
sum (
2.2
). Thus, only permutations
\sigma
in which all
cycles in their disjoint cycle decompositions are even contribute
to (
2.2
).

Given such a permutation
\sigma
with all cycles of even length,
we may now constructa pair
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
of perfect matchings
of
[m]
in the following way: for each cycle
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})

with
k
even and
a_{1}
the smallest element in the cycle, let

\mu_{1}
contain the pairings
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
,
\{a_{3},a_{4}\}
, …,

\{a_{k-1},a_{k}\}
, and let

\mu_{2}
contain the pairings
\{a_{2},a_{3}\}
,
\{a_{4},a_{5}\}
, …,

\{a_{k},a_{1}\}
.
This will immediately explain the equality in (
2.1
)
once we show that
\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{1})\cdot(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{2})
.
This is done in the proof of
[
13
, Prop. 2.2]
, and it is
essentially afforded by
[
13
, Lemma 2.1]
.
∎

Proposition 8
.

Let
Y
be a skew-symmetric matrix of size
m
and

M=(a_{i}b_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
a rank-
1
matrix of the same size.
If
m
is even then

(2.3)

\det(Y+M)=\operatorname{Pf}(Y)\Bigg(\operatorname{Pf}(Y)+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(-1)^{i+j-1}(a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i})\operatorname{Pf}(Y(i,j))\Bigg),

where
Y(i,j)
arises from
Y
by deleting the
i
-th and
j
-th
rows and columns.

Proof.

We use multilinearity of the determinant in the rows to expand

\det(Y+M)
. Since
M
is a rank-1 matrix, the result is

\det(Y+M)=\det(Y)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\det(Y\leftarrow M_{i}),

where
Y\leftarrow M_{i}
symbolises the matrix that results from
Y
by
replacing
the
i
-th row by the
i
-th row of
M
. For example, we have

(Y\leftarrow M_{2})=\det\begin{pmatrix}0&Y_{1,2}&Y_{1,3}&\dots&Y_{1,m}\\
a_{2}b_{1}&a_{2}b_{2}&a_{2}b_{3}&\dots&a_{2}b_{m}\\
-Y_{1,3}&-Y_{2,3}&0&\dots&Y_{3,m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-Y_{1,m}&-Y_{2,m}&-Y_{3,m}&\dots&0\end{pmatrix}.

Now we expand each of the determinants
\det(Y\leftarrow M_{i})

along the special row, that is, the
i
-th row.
Since, in this expansion, the term
a_{i}b_{i}
gets multiplied
by the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of odd size,
the corresponding term vanishes. Otherwise, we adapt the preceding proof
of Proposition
7
for our purposes. Let us say
that we want to determine the cofactor of
a_{i}b_{j}
with
i\neq j
.
The arguments from above can be used almost verbatim.
The only difference is that here we must consider permutations
which map
i
to
j
. The involution from the previous proof cancels all
contributions from permutations that contain an odd-length cycle not
containing
i
and
j
. Since
m
is even,
if
i
and
j
are contained in an odd-length cycle,
then there must be another odd-length cycle. Consequently,
contributions from such permutations are also cancelled by the
involution. In summary, only permutations survive
which map
i
to
j
, and in which all cycles have even length.

Let
\sigma
be such a permutation. Let

(i,j,a_{3},\dots,a_{k})
, with
k
even, be the cycle in the disjoint
cycle decomposition of
\sigma
that maps
i
to
j
.
From the previous proof, we know that the permutation
\sigma

can be mapped to a pair
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
of perfect matchings
with the property that

(2.4)

\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{1})\cdot(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{2})

by — essentially — “cutting” each cycle of
\sigma
into
two matchings. One of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
will contain the
pairings
\{i,j\}
,
\{a_{3},a_{4}\}
, …,
\{a_{k-1},a_{k}\}
,
while the other contains the pairings
\{a_{k},i\}
,
\{j,a_{3}\}
, ….
Without loss of generality, let
\mu_{2}
contain
\{i,j\}
.
Then
\mu_{1}
contributes to
\operatorname{Pf}(Y)
on the right-hand side
of (
2.3
), while
\mu_{2}\backslash\{\{i,j\}\}
contributes
to
\operatorname{Pf}(Y(i,j))
. Since we know that (
2.4
) holds, it remains
to determine the effect of removing
\{i,j\}
from
\mu_{2}
.
Indeed, the number of crossings of
\mu_{2}
in which
\{i,j\}

is involved is congruent to
i-j-1
modulo 2. Thus, if
i<j

there is a sign of
(-1)^{i+j-1}
which has to be recorded,
while if
i>j
it is
-(-1)^{i+j-1}
(due to lack of skew-symmetry
of
a_{i}b_{j}
versus
a_{j}b_{i}
).

This explains the claimed result.
∎

Proposition 9
.

Let
Y
be a skew-symmetric matrix of size
m
and

M=(a_{i}b_{j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
a rank-
1
matrix of the same size.
If
m
is odd then

(2.5)

\det(Y+M)=\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{i-1}a_{i}\operatorname{Pf}(Y(i))\Bigg)\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j-1}b_{j}\operatorname{Pf}(Y(j))\Bigg),

where
Y(i)
arises from
Y
by deleting the
i
-th
row and column.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of Proposition
8
. That is,
we start by using multilinearity of the determinant in the rows to expand

\det(Y+M)
. Here we obtain

\det(Y+M)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\det(Y\leftarrow M_{i}),

where
Y\leftarrow M_{i}
has the same meaning as before.
Now we expand each of the determinants
\det(Y\leftarrow M_{i})

along the special row, that is, the
i
-th row.
Let us say
that we want to determine the cofactor of
a_{i}b_{j}
.
Again, we must consider permutations
which map
i
to
j
. The involution from the proof of
Proposition
7
cancels all
contributions from permutations that contain an odd-length cycle not
containing
i
and
j
. With
m
being odd, we see that
only contributions from permutations survive
which map
i
to
j
, in which
i
and
j
are contained in an
odd-length cycle, and in which all other cycles have even length.

Let
\sigma
be such a permutation. Let

(j,a_{3},\dots,a_{k},i)
, with
k
odd, be the cycle in the disjoint
cycle decomposition of
\sigma
that maps
i
to
j
.
Inspired by the proof of Proposition
7
, we map
\sigma

to the pair of matchings
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
, where for each even-length
cycle we do the decomposition as in that proof, and where we
stipulate that the pairings
\{j,a_{3}\}
,
\{a_{4},a_{5}\}
, …,

\{a_{k-1},a_{k}\}
are put into
\mu_{1}
, and
the pairings
\{a_{3},a_{4}\}
, …,
\{a_{k-2},a_{k-1}\}
,

\{a_{k},i\}
are put into
\mu_{2}
. Clearly, the matching
\mu_{1}

is a perfect matching on
[m]\backslash\{i\}
, while
\mu_{2}

is a perfect matching on
[m]\backslash\{j\}
.

Instead of
(\sigma,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
, let us for the moment consider

(\sigma^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}_{1},\mu_{2})
, where
\sigma^{\prime}
arises from
\sigma
by
replacing the cycle
(j,a_{3},\dots,a_{k},i)
by
(a_{3},\dots,a_{k},i)
,
and where
\mu_{1}^{\prime}
arises from
\mu_{1}
by replacing the element
j

by
i
. Then the matchings
\mu_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mu_{2}
arise from the
permutation
\sigma^{\prime}
by a decomposition process as
in the proof of Proposition
7
, respectively by
a slight modification
thereof (when decomposing the special cycle
(a_{3},\dots,a_{k},i)
we followed
a different rule). In any case,
the permutation
\sigma^{\prime}
— which is a permutation on

[m]\backslash\{j\}
— and the pair of matchings
(\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2})

— the latter being perfect matchings on
[m]\backslash\{j\}
,
still satisfy the sign relation (
2.4
), that is,

\operatorname{sgn}\sigma^{\prime}=(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{1}^{\prime})\cdot(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{2}).

As is easy to see, we have
\operatorname{sgn}\sigma^{\prime}=-\operatorname{sgn}\sigma
and

\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{1}^{\prime}=(-1)^{i-j-1}\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{1}
. Thus, the above sign relation
implies

\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=(-1)^{i+j}(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{1})\cdot(\operatorname{sgn}\mu_{2}).

Clearly, the matching
\mu_{1}
contributes to

Lemma 14

.

Proof.

We prove the claimed identity by induction on
\ell
, also using
the result from Lemma
15
.

The claim is trivially true for
\ell=0
(if we interpret the empty
product as 1)
and for
\ell=1
. Therefore, from now on, we assume that
\ell\geq 2
.

For the induction step, we distinguish several cases.

•

If
i_{\ell+1}=j_{\ell}
, then the last row of
X_{I,J}

contains only one non-zero element, namely

X_{i_{\ell+1},j_{\ell}}=X_{i_{\ell+1},i_{\ell+1}}

as the last entry in the row. By expansion along the last row,
we get

(5.5)

\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})=(-1)^{\ell}\det(X_{I\backslash\{i_{\ell+1}\},J})+X_{i_{\ell+1},i_{\ell+1}}\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I\backslash\{i_{\ell+1}\},J\backslash\{j_{\ell}\}}).

Since
i_{\ell}<i_{\ell+1}=j_{\ell}
, by Lemma
15
and
by induction hypothesis, we obtain

\displaystyle\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}X_{i_{k},i_{k}}^{\chi(i_{k}=j_{k})}(1-X_{i_{k},i_{k}})^{\chi(j_{k-1}=i_{k})}

\displaystyle\kern 28.45274pt+X_{i_{\ell+1},i_{\ell+1}}(-1)^{\ell-1}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell-1}X_{i_{k},i_{k}}^{\chi(i_{k}=j_{k})}(1-X_{i_{k+1},i_{k+1}})^{\chi(j_{k}=i_{k+1})}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}(1-X_{i_{\ell+1},i_{\ell+1}})\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}X_{i_{k},i_{k}}^{\chi(i_{k}=j_{k})}(1-X_{i_{k},i_{k}})^{\chi(j_{k-1}=i_{k})}

for the case where
i_{1}\leq j_{1}\leq j_{2}\leq\dots\leq j_{\ell}
.
If that condition is violated,
we get zero for both summands on the right-hand side of (
5.5
),
again due to Lemma
15
and the induction hypothesis.
This is in accordance with (
5.4
).

•

If
i_{\ell+1}<j_{\ell}
, then the last
column of
X_{I,J}
is a column of 1’s. Consequently, the determinant

\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})
vanishes, as
desired.

•

If
i_{\ell+1}>j_{\ell}
then the last row of
X_{I,J}
is a row of 0’s.
We may therefore expand the determinant
\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})

along the last row and obtain

\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})=(-1)^{\ell}\det(X_{I\backslash\{i_{\ell+1}\},J}).

If
i_{1}\leq j_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\dots\leq j_{\ell}
, then, by
Lemma
15
, we infer

\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})=(-1)^{\ell}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}X_{i_{k},i_{k}}^{\chi(i_{k}=j_{k})}(1-X_{i_{k},i_{k}})^{\chi(j_{k-1}=i_{k})},

which is in accordance with (
5.4
) since
j_{\ell}<i_{\ell+1}
.
If the above inequality chain does not hold then, again by
Lemma
15
, the determinant

\det(X_{I\backslash\{i_{\ell+1}\},J})
vanishes, and hence also

\det(\mathbf{1}\,X_{I,J})
.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are now in the position to state and prove our asymmetric extension
of Byun’s minor summation formula.

Theorem 17
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers, and
let
A
and
B
be
m\times n
matrices.
Then we have

(5.6)

\det\bigl(AU_{n}B^{t}+BU_{n}A^{t}+AB^{t}\bigr)\\
=\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq j_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{2}<\cdots\leq n}\det(A^{i_{1}}B^{j_{1}}A^{i_{2}}B^{j_{2}}\cdots)\right)\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<j_{1}\leq i_{2}<j_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n}\det(B^{i_{1}}A^{j_{1}}B^{i_{2}}A^{j_{2}}\cdots)\right),

where
A^{i}
is short for
A^{\{i\}}
, that is, for the
i
-th column
of
A
, and where
A^{i_{1}}B^{j_{1}}A^{i_{2}}B^{j_{2}}\cdots
denotes
concatenation of
m
columns, so that the last column in the
concatenation is
B^{j_{m/2}}
or
A^{i_{(m+1)/2}}
, depending on the
parity of
m
. Analogous conventions apply to the second factor
on the right-hand side of (
5.6
).

Remark 18
.

By setting
A=B
in the above theorem, we retrieve Byun’s minor
summation formula in Theorem
2
since equalities of
indices in the sums on the right-hand side of (
5.6
) produce
vanishing determinants.

Proof of Theorem
17
.

We choose

Let first

By definition, we have

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle f_{A,B}(U_{n}+\operatorname{Id}_{n})

\displaystyle={\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq j_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{2}<\cdots\leq j_{m/2}\leq n}}\det(A^{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m/2}\}}B^{\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m/2}\}})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\binom{m/2}{2}}{\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq j_{1}<i_{2}\leq j_{2}<\cdots\leq j_{m/2}\leq n}}\det(A^{i_{1}}B^{j_{1}}A^{i_{2}}B^{j_{2}}\cdots B^{j_{m/2}}).

Here we use Theorem

from the previous section to derive
a Cauchy-type identity for skew Schur functions.

Before we can state
the identity, we need to recall a few basics from the theory of

9
]
and
[
12

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)

\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)

The
n
-th

is defined by

h_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\dots\leq i_{n}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}.

A

Given two partitions

Theorem 20
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers, where
m
is even.
Then we have

(5.10)

\underset{|R|=|S|=m/2}{\sum_{R\cup S=[m]}}(-1)^{\sum_{r\in R}(r-1)}\underset{\lambda(J)/\lambda(I)\text{ hor.\ strip}}{\sum_{I,J\subseteq[n],\,|I|=|J|=m/2}}s_{\lambda(I)/\lambda(R)}(\mathbf{x})\cdot s_{\lambda(J)/\lambda(S)}(\mathbf{y})\\

where
\lambda\bigl((i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m/2})\bigr):=(i_{m/2}-\frac{m}{2},\dots,i_{2}-2,i_{1}-1)

We choose

Appendix: The Pfaffian is an irreducible polynomial

Lemma
A
.

The Pfaffian of an even size skew-symmetric matrix
Y
is an irreducible
polynomial in the entries of
Y
.

Proof.

The proof is modelled after
[
1
, proof of Theorem 1,
pp. 176/177]
.

The assertion is obvious if
Y
has size 2. We may therefore assume
that
Y
has size at least 4.
Suppose that

\operatorname{Pf}(Y)=g(Y)\cdot h(Y),

where
g(Y)
and
h(Y)
are polynomials in the entries of
Y
.
The polynomial
\operatorname{Pf}(Y)
is linear in each of the entries
Y_{i,j}

of
Y
. In particular, it is linear in
Y_{1,2}
, and therefore
one of
g(Y)
and
h(Y)
contains
Y_{1,2}
, but not the
other. Without loss of generality let
g(Y)
contain
Y_{1,2}
.
Then
h(Y)
must not contain any of the entries
Y_{1,j}

and
Y_{2,j}
with
j\geq 3
, due to the defining expansion of the
Pfaffian in terms of non-crossing matchings. If
h(Y)
is not constant,
then it contains some
Y_{i,j}
with
i,j\notin\{1,2\}
. In its turn,
this implies that
g(Y)
must not contain
Y_{1,i}
. However, we
already inferred that
h(Y)
does not contain
Y_{1,i}
. We have
reached a contradiction. Consequently, the polynomial
h(Y)
is
actually constant, and hence
\operatorname{Pf}(Y)
is irreducible.
∎
