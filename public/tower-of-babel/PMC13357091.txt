Accuracy of machine learning in detecting lymph node metastasis of esophageal cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Chen, Jiren Weng, Xuefeng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, the detection of early lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer during preoperative assessments is still challenging. Machine learning has been used in the detection of early lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. However, its detection accuracy remains controversial. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to explore the accuracy of machine learning in the detection of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Web of Science databases were searched for related studies published before May 7, 2026. Studies were excluded based on the following criteria: meta-analyses, reviews, guidelines, and expert opinions; studies solely conducting risk factor analysis without constructing complete machine learning models; studies failing to report essential model accuracy evaluation metrics; and studies merely validating mature scales without machine learning model development. The PROBAST tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies. Subgroup analyses were performed according to various datasets and modeling variables, including explainable clinical features, genomics, radiomics, and the combination of radiomics with clinical features.ResultsIn total, 49 original studies were included, of which 20 used radiomics, including 19,755 medical records. The meta-analysis revealed that in the validation dataset, the C-index of machine learning was 0.79 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.76–0.83, I2=43.9%, P=0.058] in detecting lymph node metastasis of esophageal cancer. Subgroup analysis revealed that the C-index was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.80–0.84, I2=50.1%, P=0.01) for machine learning models based on both radiomics and clinical features.ConclusionsGiven clinical applicability, cost, and detection accuracy, machine learning models based on both radiomics and clinical features, with a higher C-index, appear to be a more favorable technique for detecting the early lymph node metastasis status of esophageal cancer compared with machine learning models based on clinical features alone. However, given its inherent heterogeneity, the results should be interpreted with caution and need to be validated in future research.Keywords: Machine learning, esophageal cancer, lymph node metastasis, radiomics, meta-analysis

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Machine learning models exhibit promising predictive performance for the preoperative identification of lymph node metastasis in patients with esophageal cancer.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality. In 2020, there were approximately 604,000 new cases and 544,000 deaths from esophageal cancer, with an incidence rate of 6.3 and a mortality rate of 5.6 cases per 100,000 individuals (1). The main treatment methods for esophageal cancer include surgical treatment, endoscopic resection, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and immunotherapy. Due to the frequent widespread metastasis of lymph nodes in the neck, chest, and abdomen in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), lymph node dissection is often performed as much as possible to reduce the risk of recurrence. However, the extent of lymph node dissection is still inconclusive (2). Some studies suggest that ESCC is a systemic disease, and radical lymph node dissection may actually increase the incidence of postoperative complications in patients. Moreover, the scope of lymph node dissection for esophageal cancer can be expanded, possibly causing injury to vital organs such as the trachea, aorta, pulmonary vessels, and recurrent laryngeal nerves (3,4). A multi-center retrospective study by McCulloch et al. (5) has found that unregulated three-field lymph node dissection may increase mortality rates. If no lymph node metastasis is observed in patients, any form of radical lymph node resection is unnecessary (6). Therefore, the timely detection of lymph node metastasis is imperative for the clinical management of esophageal cancer.

The status of lymph node metastasis is currently evaluated using imaging techniques. For example, Puli et al. (7) have examined the accuracy of endoscopic ultrasound in detecting distant and retroperitoneal lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. Jimenez-Lillo et al. (8) have analyzed the accuracy of indocyanine green (ICG)-guided lymphatic mapping for detecting lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. Furthermore, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron-emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) is also utilized to assess lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer (9). The accuracy of these detection methods still needs to be improved, and some of the techniques are relatively expensive, which may cause delays in the preoperative detection of the lymph node metastasis status of esophageal cancer in some underdeveloped or resource-limited areas.

Machine learning has been increasingly used in the diagnosis of various tumors, such as esophageal cancer. For example, machine learning methods have been utilized to predict the outcomes after concurrent chemoradiotherapy for esophageal cancer (10) and to detect esophageal cancer early (11). Although some studies have investigated the accuracy of machine learning in detecting the status of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer, the detection accuracy of machine learning models remains controversial. To date, there is no evidence to compare the performance of early lymph node metastasis prediction models for esophageal cancer using different modeling variables and different modeling approaches. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the accuracy of machine learning in the early identification of lymph node metastasis of esophageal cancer, offering an evidence-based foundation for promoting artificial intelligence in this domain. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0353/rc).

Data sources and search strategy

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Web of Science databases were comprehensively searched. To minimize the risk of omitting newly published studies, we updated our database searches on May 7, 2026. The search strategy was designed by combining subject headings and free-text terms. No restrictions were imposed on regions. The detailed search strategy is shown in Appendix 1.

Study selection and data extraction

The searched articles were imported into EndNote. Duplicates were excluded. The titles and abstracts were screened to exclude irrelevant studies. Full-text articles were then downloaded and thoroughly assessed to identify eligible studies. Before data extraction, we developed a structured data extraction form. The collected data comprised the article title, DOI/PMID, lead author, year of publication, country of the authors, study design, patient population source, type of cancer investigated, tumor stage, total number of cases with lymph node metastasis, total number of study participants, number of cases with lymph node metastasis in the training dataset, total number of participants in the training dataset, method for creating the validation dataset, strategies employed to prevent overfitting, number of cases with lymph node metastasis in the validation dataset, total number of participants in the validation dataset, approach to handling missing data, method of variable selection, types of machine learning models utilized, and the categories of variables included in the modeling process.

Two researchers independently screened studies and extracted data. Then, they cross-checked their results. If there were any disputes, a third researcher was consulted to make a final decision.

Risk of bias in studies

The risk of bias in the eligible studies was assessed using PROBAST (12). The tool comprised a series of questions across four domains: study participants, predictors, outcomes, and statistical analysis. These questions were answered by “yes/probably”, “no/probably not”, or “no information”. If at least one question in a domain was answered by “no” or “probably no”, the risk of bias in the domain would be considered high. On the contrary, the risk of bias in a domain was deemed low, only if all questions within each domain were answered with “yes” or “probably”. If all domains were evaluated as low risk, the overall risk of bias in the study was classified as low. However, if a high risk was identified in any domain, the overall bias risk was deemed high. The risk of bias assessment was performed by two independent researchers, who then cross-checked their findings. In the event of discrepancies, a third researcher was consulted to make a final decision.

Outcomes

This systematic review compared the performance of different machine learning models for binary classification prediction of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. The C-index was the core outcome measure. The included studies focused on binary diagnostic tasks. However, studies on survival analysis and multi-class prediction tasks were excluded. In binary modeling, both the C-index and area under the ROC curve (AUC) were the same outcome measure used to determine the discriminative power of models. Furthermore, this study also investigated the sensitivity and specificity of each model.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis of the C-index was performed to determine the precision of the machine learning models using Stata 15.0. For dichotomous logistic regression models, the C-index can be simplified as the probability that the predicted prevalence of a disease in patients is greater than that of controls. It has been shown that the C-index for dichotomous models is equivalent to the AUC. AUC mainly reflects the predictive performance of dichotomous logistic regression models, whereas C-index can be used to evaluate the accuracy of prediction results of various models. Therefore, we used C-index as an important outcome index to evaluate the accuracy of models. When the original studies did not provide the 95% confidence interval (CI) and the standard error of the C-index, we would compute the standard error utilizing the approach suggested by Debray et al. (13). Given the discrepancies in variables and parameters among different machine learning models, a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis of the C-index. Furthermore, a bivariate mixed-effects model was utilized for the meta-analysis of sensitivity and specificity. Since most of the original studies did not provide a diagnostic four-fold table, we calculated it using two methods: (I) integrating the case count with sensitivity, specificity, and precision; (II) obtaining sensitivity and specificity from the Youden’s index of maximum likelihood and incorporating them with the total number of cases. A P value below 0.05 denotes statistical significance.

Study characteristics

All 49 original studies were case-control studies. Among these studies, there were 19,755 patients, of whom 5,419 had lymph node metastasis. Twelve studies (23,27,30,39,41,42,46,47,57,59,61,62) enrolled participants from multiple centers. One study (29) collected patient data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Other studies enrolled patients from a single center. The validation set was generated using random sampling in 10 studies (19,20,28,33-35,39,40,50,52) and multi-center data in seven studies (22,23,41,57,59,61,62), while seven studies (14,15,17,21,31,32,49) were prospective. A total of 24 studies used clinical characteristics as modeling variables; 5 studies used genomics; 3 studies used radiomics, and 17 studies used radiomics combined with clinical features. Clinical features included age, sex, smoking history, depth of invasion, tumor differentiation, tumor size, and tumor location. The details can be found in Table 1.

Risk of bias in studies

In total, the 49 original studies included 101 models. Among them, three models came from the TCGA database, and their participants and predictors were assessed as low risk of bias. The remaining 98 models were all from case-control studies, and their participants were assessed as high risk of bias. However, of these 98 models, 35 models used radiomics as the modeling variables in case-control studies. In these studies, we believed that radiomics would not affect the assessment of predictors, and the risk bias of the predictors for these 35 models was assessed as low. All models used pathological methods to confirm the lymph node metastasis status, and thus, the risk of bias in the outcomes was low. In the assessment of statistical analysis, 35 models demonstrated an events per variable (EPV) ratio of less than 10, with no independent validation set, or an insufficient size of less than 100 in the validation set, and thus were assessed as high risk of bias. Six models were difficult to judge their weight coefficients and were assessed as unclear risk. The remaining models were assessed as low risk. Detailed information is provided in Figure 2.

Clinical characteristics

A total of 30 machine learning models were developed based on interpretable clinical features to assess the lymph node metastasis status in esophageal cancer. In the training set, the pooled C-index was 0.81 (95% CI: 0.78–0.84), as illustrated in Figure S1. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.75 (95% CI: 0.68–0.80) and 0.77 (95% CI: 0.73–0.81), respectively, as shown in Figure S2. The funnel plot of the C-index revealed significant publication bias among the models, and Egger’s test showed P<0.05 (Figure S3).

In the validation set, the pooled C-index was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.72–0.78), as depicted in Figure S4. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.65 (95% CI: 0.55–0.73) and 0.76 (95% CI: 0.69–0.82), respectively, as illustrated in Figure S5. The funnel plot of the C-index showed significant publication bias among the models, and Egger’s test showed P<0.05 (Figure S6). In the sensitivity analysis, after excluding each study, the pooled results were stable (Figures S7-S12)

Genomics

Five machine learning models were constructed based on genomics. In the training set, the C-index was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.79–0.92), as illustrated in Figure S13. The bivariate mixed-effects model showed that the sensitivity and specificity were 0.82 (95% CI: 0.73–0.89) and 0.80 (95% CI: 0.70–0.87), respectively, as illustrated in Figure S14.

In the validation set, the C-index was 0.81 (95% CI: 0.76–0.87), as shown in Figure S15. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.72 (95% CI: 0.66–0.78) and 0.87 (95% CI: 0.78–0.93), respectively, as shown in Figure S16.

Radiomic features

Twenty models were developed using radiomics or the combination of radiomics with interpretable clinical features to discern the lymph node metastasis status in esophageal cancer. Only one model was based on PET/CT; the rest were based on CT. In the training set, the C-index of radiomics-based models was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.79–0.87), as depicted in Figure S17. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.80 (95% CI: 0.69–0.87) and 0.82 (95% CI: 0.72–0.89), respectively, as illustrated in Figure S18. The C-index of models based on radiomics combined with interpretable clinical features was 0.84 (95% CI: 0.82–0.87) (Figure S17). The sensitivity and specificity were 0.78 (95% CI: 0.74–0.81) and 0.86 (95% CI: 0.80–0.90), respectively (Figure S19). The funnel plot of the C-index showed significant publication bias among the models, and Egger’s test showed P=0.005 (Figure S20).

In the validation set, the pooled C-index of radiomics-based models was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.76–0.83) (Figure S21). The sensitivity and specificity of radiomics-based models were 0.71 (95% CI: 0.65–0.76) and 0.78 (95% CI: 0.71–0.85), respectively (Figure S22). The pooled C-index of models based on radiomics combined with interpretable clinical features was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.80–0.84) (Figure S21). The sensitivity and specificity of models based on radiomics combined with interpretable clinical features were 0.73 (95% CI: 0.68–0.78) and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.79–0.88), respectively (Figure S23). The funnel plot of the C-index showed significant publication bias among the models, and Egger’s test showed P<0.05 (Figure S24).

Discussion

Key findings

Our systematic review demonstrates that machine learning is relatively accurate in the detection of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. Specifically, the sensitivity and specificity of models based on radiomics combined with clinical features in detecting lymph node metastasis of esophageal cancer within the validation set were 0.73 (95% CI: 0.68–0.78) and 0.84 (95% CI: 0.79–0.88), respectively. Compared with models based on only clinical features, models based on radiomics appear to show higher sensitivity and accuracy in the early prediction of lymph node metastasis of esophageal cancer.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to provide an evidence-based reference for the application of machine learning methods in diagnosing lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. Nonetheless, there are several limitations in this study. First, only a very small number of genomics-based studies accurately reflect their effectiveness in predicting lymph node metastasis. Second, most of the included studies used random sampling to generate the validation set without multi-center data, which may lead to data bias. Particularly in radiomics-based studies, a lack of multi-center validation data may further limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, currently, the algorithm used for model construction is mainly logistic regression, despite the promising advantages of deep learning, especially in radiomics. The variety and number of models need to be further improved. Therefore, it is not possible to evaluate the superiority of deep learning over traditional machine learning based on radiomics for tumor diagnosis. Further research is needed to validate this finding. In addition, genomics also shows promising performance in this study, but more research is needed to further validate its performance. As the studies included in our analysis did not distinguish between pathologic types of esophageal cancer (squamous and adenocarcinoma), we cannot further discuss pathologic types.

Comparison with similar research

Previous studies mainly focus on imaging methods for evaluating lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. Puli et al. (7) have investigated the accuracy of endoscopic ultrasonography in assessing distant and abdominal axial lymph node metastasis in patients with esophageal cancer. Their study reveals that endoscopic ultrasonography exhibits a sensitivity of 67.2% (95% CI: 62.6–71.6%) and a specificity of 97.9% (95% CI: 97.1–98.6%) for detecting distant metastasis, and a sensitivity of 66.6% (95% CI: 61.9–71.1%) and a specificity of 98.1% (95% CI: 97.3–98.7%) for identifying abdominal axial metastasis. Jimenez-Lillo et al. (8) have assessed the accuracy of lymphatic mapping using ICG in detecting lymph node metastasis in patients with esophageal cancer, with sensitivities and specificities of 84% (95% CI: 64–94%) and 15% (95% CI: 3–45%), respectively. Hu et al. (9) have utilized 18F-FDG PET/CT to evaluate the presence of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer cases. For patients who do not receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the sensitivity and specificity of 18F-FDG PET/CT are 0.57 (0.45–0.69) and 0.91 (0.85–0.95), respectively. In contrast, among patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the corresponding sensitivity and specificity are 0.53 (0.35–0.70) and 0.96 (0.86–0.99). In summary, when a single imaging method is used to evaluate lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer, its sensitivity is often relatively low. Although the sensitivity of the ICG method is high, its specificity is low (15%). Therefore, its accuracy may be low when used as a single, independent imaging evaluation indicator.

Explanations of findings

In this study, models based on radiomics combined with clinical features achieved a good balance between sensitivity and specificity (0.73 and 0.84, respectively). Especially, with particularly ideal sensitivity, these models are more accurate in the detection of lymph node metastasis of esophageal cancer. Medical imaging is initially expected to assess tumor heterogeneity and lymph node metastasis status. However, imaging features are usually visually assessed and qualitatively described by radiologists or nuclear medicine physicians. These visual assessments may be greatly influenced by differences in prior knowledge between observers (63). Compared to pure imaging, the regions of interest and volumes of interest from medical images of radiomics are automatically segmented or manually delineated by experienced radiologists or radiation oncologists. During this process, quantitative features, known as radiomics features, are extracted to provide an objective and numerical description of tumor phenotypes. These features can reveal tumor characteristics that are not apparent to the unaided eye (64). Therefore, radiomics appears to have more advantages in tumor diagnosis.

Implications and actions needed

Deep learning methods appear to improve sensitivity without reducing specificity under similar conditions, thus achieving better diagnostic accuracy. Traditional machine learning, also known as feature-engineered radiomics methods, manually extracts radiomics features from images, which is time-consuming and labor-intensive. Moreover, manual segmentation of regions of interest by observers may be subjective, and the robustness and accuracy of automatic segmentation algorithms cannot be guaranteed. In practical applications, if manual segmentation is adopted, researchers should prioritize the reproducibility and consistency of image observers and between different observers. On the other hand, deep learning, also known as non-feature-engineered radiomics methods, is highly dependent on data. The larger the data, the better its performance. It can directly extract radiomics features from images and has strong non-linear modeling capabilities, making it more suitable for complex tasks. However, overfitting may occur if the training samples are limited, or bias in gender and race may occur when the training data are imbalanced (65). The volume of radiological data associated with each patient is growing significantly, and issues such as data collection, multicollinearity, and high computational costs are becoming increasingly challenging. In addition, data security, patient privacy, and data storage are also becoming increasingly important and expensive in scientific research.

This study demonstrated the efficacy of genomics in determining the lymph node metastasis status of esophageal cancer. The sensitivity and specificity of the genomics-based models in the validation set were 0.72 and 0.87, respectively. Nevertheless, given the small sample size of the included studies, the results still need to be further validated. Nonetheless, genomics has disadvantages. For instance, high-throughput sequencing is high-cost, and more samples are needed to exclude heterogeneity at the genetic level. In summary, there are various diagnostic techniques for assessing lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer, which may introduce heterogeneity into clinical workflows.

Conclusions

Machine learning methods have shown relatively favorable accuracy for the early detection of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer. Considering clinical applicability, cost, and accuracy, machine learning models based on radiomics appear to be a more favorable technique for detecting early-stage lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer compared to machine learning models based solely on clinical features. However, this finding is primarily based on internal validation. Furthermore, the included studies exhibit some methodological deficiencies, and there is heterogeneity among the included studies. Therefore, future research should include images from different times and geographic locations, fully consider the influence of image protocols and segmentation methods, and construct high-performing machine learning models to facilitate AI-assisted diagnosis of lymph node metastasis in esophageal cancer.
