Effects of TNF-α in Human Precision-Cut Liver Slices and its Implications for Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis Progression
Authors: Ke Luo, Mei Li, Alan R. Gorter, Dorenda Oosterhuis, Vincent E. de Meijer, Peter Olinga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) represents a severe form of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), largely due to metabolic dysregulation and sustained liver inflammation. TNF-α plays a pivotal role in MASH pathogenesis by inducing cell death, inflammation, and decreased insulin sensitivity. In this study we investigated the effects of TNF-α in human precision-cut liver slices (PCLS) under healthy or steatotic conditions, to provide insights into MASH pathogenesis. PCLS were prepared from human liver tissue and cultured in control (WEGG) and hyper-nutritive (GFIPO) mediums with or without TNF-α (50 ng/mL) for 96 h. Viability was assessed via ATP content, lipid accumulation by triglyceride (TG) assay, and transcriptomic changes through Next-Generation Sequencing. The protein levels of cytokines, chemokines, and fibrotic mediators released from PCLS were quantified using Luminex assay and ELISA. TNF-α significantly altered the transcriptional profiles in PCLS, inducing pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signaling, and downregulating lipid metabolic processes in both WEGG and GFIPO media. TNF-α showed a trend in elevating intracellular TG in both conditions, albeit not statistically significant. On protein levels, TNF-α supplementation to WEGG medium induced the expression of IL8, CCL2, CCL19, PDGF-AB/BB, TGF-α, and MMP9. GFIPO medium alone induced inflammatory and fibrotic responses indicated by elevated levels of IL8, CCL2, and Pro-collagen 1A1. GFIPO medium with TNF-α supplementation further exacerbated the inflammatory and fibrotic responses, characterized by increased release of cytokines and growth factors. This human PCLS model effectively demonstrated the co-occurrence of key features of MASH, such as steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, highlighting the impact of metabolic stress and inflammatory cytokine TNF-α on these disease characteristics, and the potential of the PCLS model in exploring mechanism of MASH progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10753-025-02384-4.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is characterized by fat infiltration of the liver in the absence of a readily identified alternative cause of steatosis [1]. MASLD has become the most common cause of chronic liver disease affecting around one-third of the general population worldwide [2]. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is a more severe form of MASLD, which features not only the presence of steatosis, but also lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and often fibrosis [3]. MASH pathogenesis is multifactorial and complex, often described by the “multiple-hit hypothesis,” which supersedes the older “two-hit hypothesis“ [4]. Among the numerous hits that regulate MASH development, uncontrollable and sustained liver inflammation plays a central role for the triggering and progression of MASH [5]. Mechanistically, multiple parallel, extrahepatic hits (such as gut-derived endotoxin, also known as lipopolysaccharide, and cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 released by adipose tissue), and intrahepatic hits (such as lipotoxicity) may promote liver inflammation [6, 7].

Among the multiple regulators for mediating inflammation, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) is a well-established principle regulator, which induces cell death, inflammation, and decreases insulin sensitivity [8]. Studies have shown elavated circulating TNF-α levels in MASH patients compared to healthy controls, and TNF-α levels increased in accordance with the disease severity [9–11]. Experimental interventions, such as TNF-α-receptor inhibitors or knock-out, demonstrated amelioration of MASH characterized by reduced liver steatosis, hepatocellular injury and fibrosis [12, 13], highlighting the critical role of TNF-α in pathogenesis of MASH.

MASH poses a significant challenge in hepatology due to its intricate pathogenesis and the risk of progression to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. In vitro models, including primary human hepatocytes, immortalized cell lines, and hepatocyte-like cells derived from stem cells [14], are extensively used for MASH research but often fail to capture the liver’s cellular complexity. Preclinical murine models, established through dietary, genetic, or chemical interventions [15], have yielded valuable insights into the progression of the disease and identification of potential therapeutic targets. However, the intrinsic metabolic differences between mice and humans restrict the direct applicability of these findings. This gap and the lack of cellular complexity in current cell models underscore the potential of using PCLS model derived from human liver tissue.

In our previous study, hepatic steatosis was successfully induced in human PCLS by mimicking metabolic disorder ex vivo. We showed that the medium consisting of different dietary components can drive de novo lipogenesis and reduce lipid catabolism, culminating in the accumulation of triglycerides paired with hallmark features of MASLD in PCLS cultured up to 96 h [16, 17]. Based on these promising results, here we tested how TNF-α, a clinically relevant inflammatory mediator in MASH, reprograms lipid metabolism, inflammatory, and fibrogenic signaling in human PCLS under healthy and steatotic conditions. We studied whether metabolic stress and TNF-α act additively to reproduce a MASH-like phenotype in PCLS and to delineate pathway-level mechanisms that may drive progression.

Methods

Ethical Statement

The use of human material was in accordance with Dutch legislation and the Code of Conduct for dealing responsibly with human tissue in the context of health research issued by the Committee on Regulation of Health Research, the COREON Foundation, refraining the need of written consent for ‘further use’ of coded-anonymous human tissue [18]. Human liver tissues were sourced from leftover non-tumorous margin after partial hepatectomy (resection specimen) or from donor livers declined for transplantation and were characterized by the provider as clinically healthy (n = 7). The available donor characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Preparation of Precision-Cut Liver Slices (PCLS)

Biopsies with a diameter of 6 mm were made and subsequently sliced using a Krumdieck tissue slicer (Alabama Research and Development, Munford, USA) filled with ice-cold Krebs-Henselheit buffer according to the protocol described by de Graaf et al[19]. PCLS had an estimated thickness of 250 µm and wet weight of 4-5 mg, were stored at 4 °C in University of Wisconsin preservation solution (UW) (Cat. BUWC, Bridge to Life Ltd., London, UK). The number of PCLS and analyses conducted were constrained by the size of tissue available from donations. For each analytical procedure, tissues from a minimum of five distinct donor livers were utilized. For each donor, ≥ 3 slices per condition were analyzed for each readout. Livers that failed to exhibit adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production after 1 h of culture were considered non-viable and consequently excluded from the study.

Culture Condition

Williams medium E (Cat. 32551020, Invitrogen, Bleiswijk, the Netherlands) supplemented with Glucose (25 mM, Cat. 1.08342.1000, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and Gentamycin (50 µg/mL, Cat. 15750-037, Invitrogen) was used as control medium (WEGG). To model metabolic syndrome, a culture medium with a supraphysiological addition of Glucose (36 mM), Fructose (5 mM; Cat. F3510-100G, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA), human Insulin (1 nM; Cat. I9278, Sigma-Aldrich), Palmitic acid (240 µM; Cat. P0500-10G, Sigma-Aldrich) and Oleic acid (480 µM; Cat. 75096-1L, Sigma-Aldrich) was used (GFIPO). PCLS from 7 independent donors (n = 7) were assigned to four 96-h conditions: WEGG (basal), WEGG + TNF-α (50 ng/mL; Cat. PHC3016, Gibco), GFIPO, and GFIPO + TNF-α (50 ng/mL). Medium was refreshed every 24 h. The plates were incubated in a shaking incubator (90 cycles/min) with continuous 5% CO2 and 20% O2 supply[17].

ATP Determination

After incubation, slices were collected individually in safe-lock Eppendorf tubes with minibeads and 1 ml sonification solution, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Viability was assessed by determining the ATP content of the slices using a bioluminescence kit (Cat. 11699695001, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) as previously described [19]. Values for each group were corrected for total protein content.

Histology

PCLS were fixed in 4% buffered formalin overnight and stored at 4 °C in 70% ethanol. Fixed PCLS were embedded in paraffin and sectioned 4 μm thick. Tissue morphology were assessed through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Hematoxyline: Cat. 4085.9002, Klinipath, Duiven, The Netherlands; Eosin: Cat. HT110232, Sigma-Aldrich). Stained tissue sections were scanned using a Nanozoomer Digital Pathology Scanner (NDP Scan U10074-01, Hamamatsu Photonics K.K., Shizuoka, Japan).

Intracellular Triglyceride Quantitation

To determine the amount of steatosis, the level of triglycerides was measured using a Trig/GB kit (Cat. 20767107322V5, Roche Diagnostics), according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Three PCLS for one condition were pooled, snap-frozen, and stored at -80 °C. PCLS were homogenized in Tris-buffered saline. Fat was isolated with the Bligh & Dyer method [20]. Values were calculated using the absorption at 540 nm after one hour, corrected for total protein content, and are displayed as absolute TG/protein (mg/g). Triglyceride data were analyzed as paired observations (each donor under four conditions). Four pre-specified paired t-tests were performed with Holm–Šídák correction.

Total Protein Measurement

The total protein content in PCLS was quantified using the BioRad DC Protein Assay (Cat. 5000113, Cat. 5000114; Hercules, CA, USA), with a BSA calibration curve [21]. The quantified protein levels were subsequently used to normalize the measurements of ATP and TG to ensure precise and standardized results.

RNA Isolation and Purification

To measure mRNA levels, nine PCLS were pooled per condition with pre-added Qiazol buffer (Cat. No. 79306, Qiagen, Venlo, the Netherlands), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C. RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Cat. No. 74106, Qiagen), and was further purified using RNase-Free DNase Set (Cat. No. 79254, Qiagen). The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were assessed using the RNA Screen Tape assay on TapeStation 4200 System (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Next Generation Sequencing

RNA sequencing was conducted on the collected RNA samples by GenomeScan (Leiden, The Netherlands) utilizing Illumina’s next-generation sequencing technology. For each sample, the trimmed reads were mapped to the human GRCh38.p13 reference provided in the References directory using the STAR RNA-Seq alignment tool (STAR2 v2.7.10). Data analysis began with principal component analysis (PCA) using fast.prcomp function in R. The first two principal components were visualized in a scatter plot. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), the matrix of read counts and the design files were imported to R to compares gene expression between two conditions using DESeq2 (v. 1.38.3) package, employing shrinkage techniques to enhance the stability and interpretability of count data for differential analysis estimates. Fold changes (FC) were log2 normalized and p-values were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR) by applying Benjamini-Hochberg correction. A threshold of p.adj < 0.05 and absolute log2FoldChange > 1 were used as a cut-off for defining differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To visualize the DEGs, a volcano plot was generated using the ggplot2 package in R. This plot displayed the negative logarithm of the adjusted p-value (y-axis) against the log2 fold change (x-axis), enabling the identification of genes that were significantly upregulated or downregulated. Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2FoldChange > 1 were highlighted in the plot. Gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were conducted to categorize the DEGs using clusterProfiler package in R. Only terms with a adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Top 10 upregulated and downregulated terms were visualized in the plots. Heatmaps were created to visualize the expression patterns of the interested DEGs related to lipid metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis using pheatmap package in R. The data were normalized and hierarchical clustering was applied to group genes with similar expression profiles.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Gene expression was determined using RT-qPCR. Reverse transcription was performed using the Reverse Transcription System (Promega, Leiden, the Netherlands). RT-qPCR analyses were conducted on a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). This utilized SYBR Green primers and SYBR Green reagent (Cat. 04913914001, Roche Diagnostics). We assessed the gene expression with the double delta Ct analysis (2 −∆∆Ct), using 18S as a reference gene.

Measurement of Protein Release

Culture medium, collected every 24 h during the 96 h incubation was pooled together per condition used for quantification of protein release. The concentrations of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors in the culture medium were quantified using the Luminex® Human XL Cytokine Premised Kit (Cat. FCSTM18B; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) levels were measured using the Luminex® Human MMP Premixed Kit (Cat. FCSTM07; R&D Systems). Medium samples were diluted 2 times (XL cytokine kit) and 5 times (MMPs) respectively. They were then processed according to the manufacturer’s protocols and analyzed using a Luminex FLEXMAP 3D and xPONENT software (version 4.2). The multiplex data were analyzed using Milliplex Analyst (version 5.1, Vigene Tech Inc.) to generate standard curves using a five parameter logistic (5-PL) curve-fit to determine the concentration of each analyte in the sample.

As a key biomarker of fibrosis, release of Pro-Collagen 1A1 into culture medium was determined. Medium was diluted at a ratio of 1:20. Levels of excreted protein were measured using the human Pro-Collagen I alpha 1 kit (ab210966, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to instructions from the manufacturer.

Statistics

Significant differences were sought by comparing conditions to their respective control. Each experiment was repeated in at least five individual livers. For each individual liver, at least three PCLS were used per condition. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, USA). Significance was determined using a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test (triglycerides, ATP, cytokines, and qPCR) unless specified otherwise (next-generation sequencing data). Results were considered statistically significant when the calculated p-value was smaller than 0.05.

Results

Furthermore, the addition of TNF-α to both the WEGG and GFIPO medium did not significantly reduce ATP, suggesting that 50 ng/mL TNF-α didn’t affect the viability of PCLS during long-term incubation (Fig. 1 A). Intracellular triglyceride (TG) content was measured to assess lipid accumulation, a characteristic feature of MASH (Fig. 1B). PCLS cultured in GFIPO medium exhibited a marked increase in TG content compared to those cultured in WEGG medium. Upon stimulation with TNF-α, there was an increasing trend in TG content of PCLS in WEGG medium (p = 0.0608), but not in slices that were cultured in GFIPO medium.

Transcriptional Profiles of PCLS Incubated with GFIPO and TNF-α

Initially, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was utilized to examine the Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) data (Fig. 1 C), with the goal of visualizing variations among livers and conditions, as well as elucidating the distinct influences of GFIPO and TNF-α on the transcriptional profiles of PCLS. The results showed a distinct clustering pattern between PCLS in GFIPO treated with and without TNF-α (GFIPO + TNF-α vs. GFIPO). Similarly, PCLS treated with TNF-α were clearly separated from those in WEGG (WEGG + TNF-α vs. WEGG), while a notable overlapped area was observed between GFIPO and WEGG. These findings indicate a substantial influence of TNF-α on the transcriptional profiles in both media conditions.

Identiﬁcation of Differentially Expressed Genes and Related Pathways

Volcano plots were utilized to depict the differential gene expression induced by TNF-α treatment in PCLS in both WEGG and GFIPO conditions (Figure 2A). Upon comparing 'WEGG+TNF-α' with 'WEGG', we identified a total of 1,124 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising of 391 upregulated and 733 downregulated genes. In GO enrichment analysis, the upregulated DEGs were notably enriched in biological processes related to immune and inflammatory responses, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, including pathways such as 'cytokine-mediated signaling pathway', 'response to lipopolysaccharide', 'leukocyte migration', and 'extracellular matrix organization'. These pathways underscore the pro-inflammatory milieu stimulated by TNF-α. Notably, the downregulated genes mainly enriched in metabolic and catabolic processes including lipid catabolic and fatty acid metabolic processes (Figure 2B). KEGG analysis further confirmed the enrichment of immune and inflammatory pathways among upregulated genes such as the 'cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction', 'TNF signaling pathway', 'NF-kappa B signaling pathway', and 'IL17 signaling pathway' (Figure 2C). Additionally, KEGG analysis emphasized lipid metabolism among downregulated genes. In the GFIPO medium, TNF-α treatment resulted in a total of 1,012 DEGs, with 427 genes upregulated and 585 downregulated. Similar to WEGG medium, both GO and KEGG analyses indicated a consistent upregulation of inflammatory pathways and downregulation of metabolic pathways including lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and detoxification. In addition, top 10 upregulated DEGs by TNF-α in both medium shared common genes, including FDCSP, STAR, MGAT4C, ENPP6, SLC30A2, and SPIB (Fig. 2D), indicating a strong association between them and TNF-α stimulation. Among them, FDCSP was implicated as a regulator of B-cell responses [22]. STAR is a key regulator of steroid hormone synthesis. It was increased 7- and 15-fold in steatosis and MASH patients, respectively, compared to healthy controls [23]. MGAT4C is involved in glycosylation processes, and its expression was also found be upregulated in MASH patients [24]. In addition, CYP3A4 was the only common genes among top downregulated DEGs in both conditions, which might play a significant role in the metabolic disruptions induced by TNF-α treatment. CYP3A4 is a key enzyme in the cytochrome P450 family involved in the metabolism of a wide range of endogenous and exogenous substances, including drugs, lipids, and toxins [25]. Significantly lower CYP3A4 protein expression and activity was found in MASH patients [26].

The lipid metabolism-related heatmap (Fig. 3 A) shows that ACLY, a key gene in de novo lipogenesis, was increased after 96 h of incubation in GFIPO medium compared to WEGG medium. ACLY encodes the critical substrate for fatty acid synthesis, converting citrate to acetyl-CoA [27]. Meanwhile, genes associated with lipid efflux (APOC3), and fatty acid oxidation (ACOX1, ACOX2, ACADM) exhibited a decrease in expression due to GFIPO, aligning with what we described previously [17]. After TNF-α treatment, most of the lipid metabolism-related DEGs in both WEGG and GFIPO media were decreased. Adding TNF-α significantly downregulated the key transcription factor SREBF1 (encoding SREBP1c) and MLXIPL (encoding ChREBP) which are both crucial for de novo lipogenesis, along with their downstream genes involved in fatty acid synthesis (FASN, SCD, ACACA, ELOVL6) and TG assembly (DGAT). Furthermore, genes associated with lipid efflux (HNF4A, APOB, MTTP, APOC3, TM6SF2), and different steps of fatty acid oxidation — including activation of fatty acids (ASCL1), transport into the mitochondria (CPT2, SLC25A20), and β-oxidation spiral (ACADL, ACADM, ACADS, HADH, ACOX1, ETFDH) — were also reduced by TNF-α in both WEGG and GFIPO. In addition, ELOVL5, which is involved in the elongation of fatty acids, was upregulated upon TNF-α treatment in both media.

The heatmap of inflammation-related DEGs revealed a proinflammatory cascade initiated by TNF-α (Fig. 3B), as indicated by the upregulated gene expression of proinflammatory cytokines (ILs), and chemokines (CCLs, CXCLs) in both media. While GFIPO alone did not modify the expression of these genes, its combination with TNF-α exhibited an additive effect promoting inflammation in PCLS, as evidenced by the intense red clusters of inflammatory markers in the heatmap. Notably, IL27, and CCL16 transcripts were downregulated significantly by TNF-α treatment in both media, while CRP was only inhibited by TNF-α in GFIPO medium, in contrast to the rest of inflammation-related DEGs. IL27, a cytokine with both pro- and anti-inflammatory properties, has been recently reported to alleviate hepatic steatosis in hepatocytes [28]. CCL16, a chemokine predominantly found in normal liver tissue, might inhibit the development of liver cirrhosis through inactivating hepatic stellate cells [29] and was observed to decrease following LPS treatment in hepatocytes [29]. Downregulated IL27 and CCL16 might contribute to the progression of MASH. Gene expression of CRP, an acute phase inflammatory protein, was unexpectedly inhibited by TNF-α supplementation to GFIPO medium. This is in line with another study where TNF-α inhibited the gene expression of CRP in IL1- and IL6-stimulated hepatocytes [30].

Similarly, in the heatmap of fibrosis-related DEGs, GFIPO alone did not significantly affect the expression of these genes, while TNF-α treatment alone led to an increase in genes encoding ECM components (e.g., COL5A2, COL6A1), metalloproteinases (MMPs, ADAMTSs), growth factors (PDGFB, TGFB1I1), integrins (ITGAV, ITGA3, ITGB8), and other pro-fibrogenic genes (SERPINE1, SERPINH1) in both media. Conversely, insulin-like growth factors (IGF1, IGF2) were inhibited by TNF-α treatment in both media. Their gene expression has been negatively correlated with the progression of fibrosis in patients with MASLD [31, 32], and IGF1, in particular, has been documented to mitigate fibrogenesis and facilitate liver regeneration [33].

Pro-Inflammatory and Fibrotic Response On Protein Levels

Subsequently, we investigated whether the notable effects of GFIPO and TNF-α on gene expression related to inflammation and fibrosis were also evident on protein levels. The assessment was conducted by measuring the release of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines (Fig. 4), and fibrosis-related proteins (Fig. 5) from PCLS into the medium. This was done using multi-panel Luminex assay and ELISA. GFIPO alone contributed to an inflammatory phenotype, which was not reflected by the gene expression at the 96-hour timepoint, as indicated by significant increased release of IL8 and CCL2. TNF-α alone only significantly increased levels of IL8, CCL2, and CCL19. However, in GFIPO medium, TNF-α significantly increased most measured inflammatory cytokines, with the exception of CCL19 and CCL20, where the increases were not significant. Discussion

The PCLS model has emerged as a promising tool that replicates the multicellular architecture of the human liver, while preserving species-specific metabolic processes. Notably, increased TNF-α levels have been observed in MASH patients, correlating with disease severity [9–11]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of TNF-α on PCLS in a healthy condition and in a steatotic condition induced by GFIPO medium. By incorporating the inflammation inducer, TNF-α, into the hyper-nutritive medium, GFIPO, we have developed and characterized an ex vivo human model to simulate a MASH phenotype in PCLS.

Lipid Accumulation and Metabolic Dysregulation

Disrupted hepatic lipid homeostasis leading to hepatic triglyceride accumulation is a defining feature of MASLD. The increased TG accumulation in PCLS might result from the imbalance between the four major pathways of hepatic lipid homeostasis: uptake of circulating lipids, de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid efflux in very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL).

With upregulated gene associated with de novo lipogenesis and suppressed genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and lipid efflux, GFIPO consequently exacerbated TG accumulation, which is in line with previous studies [16, 17]. On the other hand, genes related to de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid efflux all showed a downregulated expression pattern in response to TNF-α in both media, except for ELOVL5—gene encoding a FA elongase playing a key role in monounsaturated FA and polyunsaturated FA synthesis —which notably also exhibits increased levels in patients with MASH [34]. De novo lipogenesis is the metabolic pathway through which acetyl-CoA is converted into fatty acids, which are then esterified into triglycerides [35]. The suppression of de novo lipogenesis by TNF-α could be mediated through the inhibition of two key transcription factors, SREBP-1c and ChREBP, which are essential for the expression of lipogenic enzymes such as FASN, ACACA, and SCD [36]. Interestingly, MASH patients also exhibited lower SREBP-1c and ChREBP expression at gene and protein levels respectively, compared to simple steatotic counterparts [37, 38]. Our study suggests that the enhanced de novo lipogenesis might be impaired in an inflammatory environment induced by TNF-α.

The decrease of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation suggested that TNF-α impair the liver’s ability to oxidize fatty acids in both healthy condition and steatotic condition induced by GFIPO. A recent study revealed that increasing MASLD severity corresponded with a loss in hepatic fatty acid oxidation and an impairment in mitochondrial biogenesis, with a dramatic attenuation of PGC1α (encoded by PPARGC1A) gene expression in MASH [39]. The suppression of fatty acid oxidation by TNF-α could be a result of alterations in impairment in mitochondrial biogenesis (reflected by decreased PPARGC1A) or the suppressed expression of PPARA, which can directly upregulate genes encoding enzymes involved in fatty acid oxidation or function as a fatty acid sensor, and ineffective PPAR-α sensing can lead to reduced energy burning resulting in hepatic steatosis and steatohepatitis [40].

In addition to fatty acid oxidation, the export of TG through VLDL is the only way to reduce hepatic lipid content [41]. Patients with genetic abnormalities in the APOB or MTTP gene are more likely to develop hepatic steatosis because of impaired TG export [42, 43]. Hepatic gene expression of APOB and MTTP were also lower in patients with MASH compared to patients with steatosis [44], indicating defected TG efflux in more severe disease stage. Downregulation of APOB, MTTP, TM6SF2, and APOC3 by TNF-α in both media can lead to impaired assembly and secretion of VLDL and more hepatic TG accumulation. The effects here might be related to TNF-α’s inhibition of the following three transcription factors regulating these lipid efflux-related genes. HNF4A can directly regulate expression of MTTP, APOB and TM6SF2 [45, 46]. ChREBP, controls not only the transcription above mentioned enzymes involved in de novo lipogenesis, but also the expression of MTTP and TM6SF2 [47, 48]. Besides, given the upregulating effects of PPARα on MTTP and APOB [49], inhibition of PPARA by TNF-α might also lead to lower APOB and MTTP levels.

The exacerbating effects of TNF-α on metabolic dysregulation in both media suggest the direct impact of inflammation on lipid metabolism in the liver, which remains incompletely understood [50]. TNF-α treatment downregulated key genes involved in de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid efflux, and the disrupted lipid metabolism resulted in a trend of increased hepatic TG accumulation in both healthy and steatotic PCLS, although these effects on TG accumulation were not significant.

Inflammatory Response Induced By TNF-α in Healthy and Steatotic Conditions

Hepatic inflammatory response has a fundamental role during MASH development [5]. TNF-α is a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine that can activate NF-κB signaling pathways, leading to transcriptional activation of a wide array of inflammatory genes, including cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, and enzymes involved in the inflammatory response [51]. The pro-inflammatory effects of TNF-α have been effectively demonstrated in both healthy PCLS and steatotic PCLS induced by GFIPO medium on gene expression levels, as evidenced by the NGS data. Many of these upregulated inflammation-related DEGs induced by TNF-α treatment (marked by red stars in Fig. 4B and supported by Table S5) were also found to be elevated in MASH patients compared to healthy individuals. The further aggravated inflammation in steatotic PCLS suggests that TNF-α treatment may model the inflammatory milieu of MASH in human PCLS. Additionally, TNF-α might lead to a stronger inflammatory response, revealed by the cytokine release, in steatotic PCLS induced by GFIPO medium compare to healthy PCLS.

Effects On Fibrogenesis and ECM Remodeling

The extent of fibrosis is a critical determinant of disease severity, prognosis, and mortality in MASH [52]. On transcriptomic levels, TNF-α induced a pro-fibrotic signature in both healthy and steatotic PCLS by activating key signaling pathways that lead to the expression of growth factors, cytokines, structural ECM components, ECM-remodeling enzymes, and cell adhesion molecules. The increased gene expression levels of MMPs and ADAMTS enzymes suggests an active remodeling of the ECM, which is an essential event during fibrosis. In MASH patients, higher levels of MMP2, MMP9, MMP10, and MMP19 suggest MMPs as potential biomarkers for the disease [53–55]. These upregulation of MMPs by TNF-α treatment can occur directly through trans-activation of MMP promoters via NF-κB and AP-1, or indirectly via other TNF-α-induced cytokines or growth factors that trigger cell signaling in trans-activation of promoters via AP-1 and PEA3 [56]. Furthermore, TNF-α’s effects on regulating PDGF-AB/BB and TGF-α on protein levels in both media reflected a shift towards a pro-fibrotic environment. However, these effects did not result in a significant increase of either COL1A1 gene expression or pro-collagen 1A1 release, which are key markers for liver fibrosis. This likely reflects kinetics whereby transcriptional activation and matrix-remodeling cues precede measurable collagen deposition in PCLS during the culture time. Our 96 h window is optimized for viability and multi-analyte readouts but is likely too short to resolve incremental matrix deposition. Future studies using extended culture and collagen-specific assays (e.g., Sirius Red staining or hydroxyproline quantification) will be better suited to demonstrate net matrix accumulation.

The profibrotic effects of GFIPO has been previously described [17]. At 96 h time point GFIPO failed to regulate the fibrosis-related genes as TNF-α did, but it promoted the COL1A1 gene expression and pro-collagen 1A1 release significantly.

In summary, TNF-α downregulated de novo lipogenesis, FAO, and VLDL-secretion programs (including SREBF1/MLXIPL targets, PPAR-α axis, APOB/MTTP/TM6SF2), while upregulating immune and ECM-remodeling signatures in human PCLS. These transcriptional changes were paralleled by increased cytokine, chemokine, growth factor, and MMP release. Under GFIPO, TNF-α further intensified inflammatory/pro-fibrotic protein readouts, indicating that metabolic and inflammatory ‘hits’ cooperate to advance a MASH-like state. PCLS therefore represent a valuable ex vivo human model to elucidate the key pathways and mediators involved in MASH pathogenesis, with potential implications for therapeutic discovery.
