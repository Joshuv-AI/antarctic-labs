Human Papillomavirus Vaccine Communication Skills for Pediatric Primary Care Clinicians: Evaluation of a Randomized Clinical Trial of the C-LEAR Training on Clinicians’ Behavior
Authors: Carma L Bylund, Lindsay A Thompson, Marta D Hansen, Kennan DeGruccio, Gabriela Sanchez, Tuo Lin, Pengchen Wang, Stephanie AS Staras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCancer prevention can be improved through increasing the uptake of the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine resulting from enhanced pediatric primary care clinician recommendations. Although clinician recommendation has been shown repeatedly to be associated with increased vaccination, quality recommendations are not always given.Study designThirty pediatric clinics and their 58 respective clinicians were randomized using a cluster design to 3 groups regarding trainings for recommending the HPV vaccine to parents of 11- to 12-year-olds: (1) the Counsel-Listen-Empathize-Answer-Recommend (C-LEAR) approach with bundled counseling (all 3 vaccines together) (n = 20), (2) the C-LEAR approach with benefits counseling (focused on HPV vaccine benefits) (n = 17), and training deferred to after the trial (n = 21). Training consisted of a 1-hour virtual session, including small group role play. As a secondary outcome, we evaluated clinicians’ adherence through simulated and real patient visits and self-reported surveys.ResultsOf the 37 clinicians, 36 completed the training. Adherence to the C-LEAR approach averaged 77% during simulated patient visits (n = 35), 68% in real patient visits (n = 22), and 82% on monthly surveys (n = 31). During the simulated patient visits, the clinicians trained in the bundled group had significantly greater adherence than the benefits group to the C-LEAR approach. During real patient visits, some, but not all, of the counseling statement components were more likely to be used by clinicians trained to use them, compared with untrained clinicians. Comparing the self-reported counseling statements of HPV vaccine recommendation before and 1 year after the training, the frequency of using 3 or more of the trained components remained similar in the deferred and bundled groups and increased within the benefits group.ConclusionFollowing a brief, virtual, communication skills workshop using the C-LEAR approach for vaccine communication, trained clinicians were more likely than untrained clinicians to use some of the counseling strategies consistent with recommended approaches.Keywords: HPV vaccine, provider–patient communication, communication skills training

Cancer prevention can be improved through the increased uptake of the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine. The HPV vaccine prevents up to 6 types of cancer, including cervical and oropharyngeal cancers, and is recommended by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices for universal coverage among 11- to 12-year-olds.1, 2, 3, 4 Nationwide, among 13- to 17-year-olds in 2024, only 78% have initiated the vaccine (receipt of 1 dose or more) and 63% have completed the series (adolescents who start the series before 15 years of age need 2 doses and those older than 15 years require 3 doses).5 A similar percentage of 13- to 17-year-olds living in Florida in 2024 have initiated the HPV vaccine (84%) and are up to date (59%),5 although Florida in 2022 had the eighth highest rate of HPV-related cancers by state (14.4 per 100 000).6

One strategy that can be targeted for improvement in vaccination rates is clinician recommendations. Although a clinician's recommendation has been shown repeatedly to be associated with positive vaccine outcomes for boys and girls at series initiation and completion,7,8 recommendations are not always given, and clinician's recommendation approaches can unintentionally undermine receipt of the vaccine.9 The frequency of recommendations vary, with as low as 24% and up to 88% of parents reporting receiving a clinician recommendation for the HPV vaccine.10 National health agencies such as the American Academy of Pediatrics, the Centers for Disease Control, and the President's Cancer Panel have disseminated suggestions for how clinicians should introduce the HPV vaccine to parents, recommend the vaccine, and answer questions.1,2,4,11,12 More guidance is needed because clinicians continue to report concerns about parental vaccine hesitancy as a barrier to HPV vaccine recommendation.13 Knowing how to use patient-centered communication to effectively respond to parents' questions and concerns, often indicating vaccine hesitancy,14 is expected to improve HPV vaccine uptake. Further, although empathy has been shown to impact the clinician–patient relationship and important outcomes,15 the current recommendations do not include empathy as central to this crucial conversation.

Building on current recommendations and research evidence on clinician–patient communication, we developed the Counsel-Listen-Empathize-Answer-Recommend (C-LEAR) approach for clinicians to recommend HPV vaccinations. This approach begins with a statement of counsel followed by listening to questions or concerns, using empathy, answering the question or concern, and ending with a strong recommendation. We previously pilot tested this approach with a group of academic faculty and resident pediatricians who endorsed satisfaction with the method.16 As part of a randomized clinical trial aiming to increase HPV vaccination, we trained clinicians to use the C-LEAR approach. The training was offered in 2 versions: one that was focused on HPV vaccination benefits (benefits) and the other that included all 3 routinely recommended vaccines for 11- to 12-year-olds, including the HPV vaccine; the tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis; and the meningococcal vaccine (bundled).17 We randomized at the clinic level to avoid contamination. Clinics, and the clinicians within them, were randomized to participate in the benefits, bundled, or no training groups. The primary aim was to increase HPV vaccination rates, and the goal of the current study was to evaluate the impact of the C-LEAR approach training on the communication skills behavior of practicing pediatric primary care clinicians at multiple sites. The study had 2 aims:

•Evaluate the impact of the training on participants' use of the C-LEAR approach; and

Evaluate the impact of the training on participants' use of the C-LEAR approach; and

•Examine the impact of the training on participants' use of the components of their assigned counsel statement. The 2 assignments were bundled (presenting the HPV vaccine as 1 of 3 recommended vaccines for 11- to 12-year-olds) or benefits (highlighting the HPV vaccine benefits).

Examine the impact of the training on participants' use of the components of their assigned counsel statement. The 2 assignments were bundled (presenting the HPV vaccine as 1 of 3 recommended vaccines for 11- to 12-year-olds) or benefits (highlighting the HPV vaccine benefits).

Methods

Study Design

The study design was an evaluation of an intervention embedded within a larger randomized clinical trial (NCT05006833). To evaluate the training, we used a version of Kirkpatrick's assessment model modified for communication skills education.18, 19, 20 The model describes 4 levels of assessment, with progressively complex behavior change. Level 1 assesses participants' reaction to the program, and we have previously published these results.16 Level 2 assesses learning measured through activities such as standardized role plays. Level 3 assesses change in behavior in clinical settings. Level 4 assesses the results of the program. In this article, we focus on level 2 (standardized patient educator [SPE] visits) and level 3 (real patient visits and clinician surveys) to examine the impact of the training on communication skills behavior.

Participants

Participants were pediatric primary care clinicians (pediatricians and advance practice providers within pediatric offices) from 30 clinics that enrolled in a randomized clinical trial designed to test the effects of 2 different HPV recommendation strategies. Clinics were recruited from December 2020 to March 2022. Clinics were eligible to participate if they (1) were located in Florida, (2) had HPV vaccine first dose rates under the state median (68%),21 (3) administered the HPV vaccine in clinic, (4) had 70 or more active 11-to-12-year-old patients, (5) were able to send text message reminders or have the study team send them on their behalf, and (6) were willing to be randomized. Clinics completed contracts with the university before randomization. Randomization occurred at the clinic level. Each clinic, along with its respective clinicians, was randomly assigned to 1 of 3 arms in a 1:1:1 parallel fashion: 2 training groups or to a deferred training group. Both training groups were taught to use the 5-step C-LEAR approach.16 Stratified randomization by baseline vaccination rates was performed by the statistical analysis team using SAS programs (SAS Institute). With these programs, clinics were assigned 1, 2, or 3. Study implementors assigned the numbers to the groups and informed the clinics of their assignment. The statistical analysis team was blinded to study arm assignment. After randomization, all clinicians who treated patients aged 11 and 12 years were invited to participate by the study's main contact person with the clinic. Between November 2021 and September 2022, study staff held group and one-on-one virtual sessions to explain the consent. Clinicians signed consent forms before the training. The study sample size of 30 clinics (10 clinics per arm; 2 clinicians per clinic) was powered to detect differences in HPV vaccine initiation and was not evaluated for the exploratory outcomes presented here. One training group was instructed to use a benefits counsel statement, which focuses on the President's Cancer Panel recommendations, focusing on safety, cancer prevention, and vaccination at ages 11-12 years. The other group was instructed to use the bundled counsel statement, which is consistent with national health agency recommendations for the Announcement Approach (Figure 1).4,11,22,23 The University of Florida Institutional Review Board approved this study (#202001633).

Training Procedures

Between February and September 2022, we invited participants in the 2 training groups to attend a 1-hour, synchronous, communication skills training session over Zoom. The goals of the sessions were to educate pediatric primary care clinicians about the importance of the HPV vaccine, explain the critical role of clinician communication in improving HPV vaccine rates, introduce the rationale for and steps of the C-LEAR approach, and give the participants an opportunity to practice using the C-LEAR approach in a facilitated small group role play setting. The training materials were developed by 3 of the authors and included PowerPoint slides that were narrated live, a recorded demonstration video, a live demonstration role play, and handouts for participants with the C-LEAR approach and FAQs about the HPV vaccine with example answers.

The authors conducted and facilitated the small groups. The 3 role play scenarios were written based on clinical experiences increasing in communication difficulty. Participants rotated playing the role of the pediatrician, the parent, and an observer. Sessions were recorded for fidelity evaluation.

We developed a set of guidelines for the small group facilitators to strengthen intervention fidelity based on previous work.24 Following the sessions, 2 coders independently coded video recordings of the training sessions for facilitator adherence to these guidelines. After independently coding, the coders met to reconcile differences, discussing with another author when questions arose.

Evaluation Procedures

Standardized Patient Education Visit (Training Groups)

Within an average of 4.5 weeks after completing the training session, clinicians in the training groups participated in a virtual visit with a SPE playing the role of a mother to evaluate their use of the C-LEAR approach over Zoom. The clinician was given instructions to use the C-LEAR approach to introduce the vaccine(s) to the mother. The SPE followed a decision tree to guide how to answer, depending on how well the clinician followed the C-LEAR approach (Figure 2). This decision tree was designed to give additional opportunity to the C-LEAR approach if they missed any steps the first time. After each role play, the SPE provided the clinician with feedback on their use of the C-LEAR strategy.

Decision tree for standardized patient educator to use in OSCE, after the recommendation: SPE: “Does he really need that one/the HPV? He's only 11.”Real reason: (only given following empathy), “Riley's only 11 and not having sex yet, so why is it needed?”

All recordings of the SPE visits were transcribed and deidentified. We developed a scoring sheet for rating the transcriptions of the SPE visit, worth up to 5 points, with 1 point for each C-LEAR component, including partial credit for partial use of any component. One author and the SPE independently rated transcripts of the first 11 SPE visits and reconciled differences. Thereafter, the SPE scored all transcripts and an author scored every fifth visit's transcript. Inter-rater reliability was good, with a single measures intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.78. Although collected as discrete values, to enable visual comparison with the survey data results, scores were converted to percentages with discrete options (approximately continuous).

Audio Recordings of Clinical Visits (All Groups)

Ten to 12 months after the training between September 2022 and April 2023, we collected audio recordings of real patient visits—these were clinical visits between clinicians and parents of an 11- or 12-year-old child who had not yet received any adolescent vaccines. Parents met with study staff in one-on-one virtual sessions to review and sign the consent. With parents’ permission, adolescents also met virtually with study staff and completed assent to participate. All but one of the audio recordings were collected remotely, with the parent recording the visit on their phone through a Zoom call with a research coordinator.25 Recordings were collected for clinicians in the training groups as well as clinicians in the deferred training group.

All clinical audio recordings were transcribed and deidentified. An author and the SPE met and coded together the first 8 transcripts based on the C-LEAR approach, adjusting the coding system as needed. Thereafter, they each independently coded the next 4 transcripts and had 100% agreement. The SPE coded the rest of the audio recordings, with an author coding every fifth recording. Overall, for all double-coded audio recordings, inter-rater reliability was moderate with single measures intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.58. An author and the SPE reconciled all differences.

Using similar processes of double coding, the SPE and an author coded the transcripts for the presence of explicitly stated benefits and bundled components. We evaluated any statement said by the clinician about vaccines throughout the entire conversation. We compared the presence of the statement components between those who were trained vs not. For example, only the bundled group was trained to say “given today”; therefore, we compared the frequency of stating “given today” in the bundled group with the 2 other groups (benefits and deferred).

Self-Report of Communication Skills (Training Groups + All Groups)

We assessed clinicians’ adoption of the C-LEAR approach and counsel statement with 2 types of surveys administered via a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies: Research Electronic Data Capture, hosted at the University of Florida.26,27 First, to assess the use of the C-LEAR approach, we sent trained clinicians up to 3 surveys each month for a year (between May 2022 and July 2023 based on clinic study start dates) for their first 3 well visits with 11- to 12-year-olds. If the clinician saw the child (question 1) and recommended the HPV vaccine to the parent (question 2), they were asked a third question of how closely they followed the C-LEAR approach to recommend the HPV vaccine to the child. Response options were a 5-item Likert-type scale ranging from not at all to very closely. Based on scheduled patient visits, clinicians were asked to complete between 0 and 36 surveys with an average response rate of 76%. Among the 33 clinicians who completed at least 1 survey, we averaged their responses across the completed surveys to create an adherence estimate from 0 to 100.

Second, to assess change in the use of the counseling statement, we invited clinicians to complete behavioral surveys at 3 time periods: approximately 2 weeks before the training in January and September 2022 (pretraining), following the training for trained clinicians only in February and November 2022 (immediate post training), and approximately 1 year after the training in September 2022 and October 2023 (post trial). One question asked clinicians to self-report their counsel statement with the following directions: “What do you typically say when you introduce the HPV vaccine to parents of 11- to 12-year-olds? Please write it out as if you were actually speaking.” Among all respondents to the specific question, we coded these self-reported counsel statements for the presence of any of the benefits and bundled components explicitly stated. We evaluated the presence of the statement components by training arm: bundled, benefits, and deferred.

Analysis

For training adherence, data were summarized using the mean and SD within each training group, and group differences were assessed using generalized estimating equations (GEEs). To compare the counsel statements between training groups, we used GEEs adjusted for clinic-level clustering of the clinician. For within-group pretraining/post-training comparisons, we applied GEEs to incorporate repeated measures within clinicians over time nested within clinics, using sandwich variance estimators to account for both within-clinic and within-clinician correlations. When comparisons involved a group with zero counts, the GEE working correlation became noninvertible; in these cases, the mid-P exact test was used for independent groups and the McNemar exact test was used for within-group comparisons. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.1.2; R Core Team 2021). One clinician initiated the bundled training and then was transferred to a benefit clinic after completing the training and the post-trial survey. The clinician was included in the overall population and the post-trial survey, and was omitted from other analyses.

Results

We conducted 14 training sessions for the 2 training groups (bundled and benefits) during the study, with a total of 36 of the 37 invited clinicians (97%) attending a session. Sessions ranged from 1 to 8 participants, with each small group role play session having no more than 3 participants. Facilitator fidelity to the guidelines was 80%, which is considered acceptable for communication skills training sessions.20

During the SPE visits, average adherence to the C-LEAR approach was between 72% and 82%. Clinicians assigned to the bundled group had significantly higher scores than those in the benefits group (P < .05). During real patient visits, the average adherence scores ranged from 63% to 75%, with no significant differences between the trained groups and the deferred group. Finally, self-reported adherence on the monthly surveys was highest and had no differences between the training arms.

Impact of Training on Adherence to Assigned Version of Counsel Statement

To understand differences in clinicians’ adherence to the version of the counsel statement they were assigned (ie, bundled vs benefits), we coded the counsel statements used in the real patient visits and compared them with the components of their assigned version. Results were similar when considering the introductory statement by the clinician or the entire conversation; therefore, for simplicity and conservativeness, presented findings refer to the entire conversation. Training differences were observed via recordings during the real patient visits. Participants assigned to the bundled group were more likely to state the HPV vaccine in the middle of the other vaccines (50%) compared with the groups not trained in this component (OR, 10.4; 95% CI, 1.76-61.6; P = .01) (Table 3). Participants assigned to the benefits group were more likely to say the vaccine was safe (42%) and instruct parents to bring the child back in 6-12 months (92%) compared with groups not trained in these components (safe P = .006 and come back P = .003). The frequency of clinicians using all trained components was higher among the bundled (40%) and benefits (50%) trained clinicians compared with the deferred group (0%; P = .022).

Over the course of the study, clinicians changed how they reported that they typically introduce the HPV vaccine to parents (Table 4). Of the 57 participating clinicians (37 clinicians who completed the training and 20 deferred), the pretraining survey was completed by 57 (100%), with 54 (95%) completing the self-reported counsel statement question. The post-trial survey was completed by 52 (91%), with 40 (74%) completing the self-reported counsel statement question. The immediate post survey was offered to the 37 trained providers. All 37 (100%) completed the survey and 33 (89%) completed the self-reported counsel statement question. The clinician who switched arms was not included in the analysis, making the total participants in Table 4 for the pretraining 53 of 57 (93%), post-training 32 of 37 (86%), and post-trial 39 of 57 (68%) surveys. We observed stronger training effects on the immediate post-training survey than the post-trial survey. Among the bundled-trained group, we found significantly increased reporting between the pretraining to the immediate post-training survey on 3 of the 4 individual components of the strategy and at least 3 trained components. Among the benefits-trained group, increases from pretraining to the immediate post-training period were reported for saying the word “recommend” and mentioning coming back in 6-12 months to get the second dose. At the 1-year follow up, increases persisted for stating the word “recommend.” From before to 1 year after the training, the benefits training group participants increased the frequency of saying the vaccine was safe and included at least 3 of the components of the approach.

Discussion

Improving clinicians' communication with parents about the HPV vaccine is an important strategy to implement for cancer prevention. In this study, we describe a virtual communication skills training program that uses the C-LEAR model for HPV vaccine recommendation. The C-LEAR model is a novel concept, integrating both the doctor's strong recommendation, which is critical to improving adherence to vaccine recommendations,7,8,14 with patient-centered communication, such as empathic listening and response, which has been shown to be important to patient and clinician outcomes.28

After the training, we confirmed that participants were able to demonstrate many of the skills taught in the course. A strength of our study is that we used multiple methods of assessment: SPE visits, audio recordings of real visits, and self-report. The Kirkpatrick Assessment model describes SPE visits being at level 2, assessing learning; audio recordings of real patient visits and self-report assessments are at level 3, assessing behavior. The model notes that behavior change is more difficult the farther you go up the model, as the complexity of behavior change increases.29 Although the research literature has had mixed findings about whether communication skills are transferred from training sessions to behavior in clinical practice, our results showing that there was no significant difference (decline or increase) between the SPE visits and the real patient visits adds to the evidence that skills learned in brief trainings can transfer to behavior.30

When we examined the SPE visits, we found that the group that was taught the bundled introduction had greater adherence to the C-LEAR approach than those taught the benefits introduction. Clinicians may be more skilled at the bundled approach after our brief training for 2 reasons. First, the bundled approach may be familiar to the clinicians because it has been recommended nationally.2,12 Second, because 3 vaccines are recommended for 11-to 12-year-olds,31 discussing the vaccines together as recommended in the bundled approach may be more consistent with existing practice.

When we examined the audio recordings of real patient visits, we found that the those clinicians who were in training groups were more likely than those in the deferred group to use the counseling components. This provides confirmation that the clinicians were able to take what they learned and put it into practice with real patients. Finally, when we examined the self-report surveys, we found that trained clinicians were more likely to use some counseling components they were taught at post training, although at 1 year of follow-up, the benefits group was the only one with sustained change.

Our study adds to existing published clinician trainings for HPV vaccine recommendations by demonstrating training effects on key words or phrases in clinician recommendation content. To our knowledge, this is the first clinician training study to show qualitive content differences in recommendations pre training vs post training and between training group differences from patient recordings. Two clinician training studies showed evidence of clinicians’ response to trainings with self-reported quantitative measures: one showed 86% reporting using the “same-day same-way strategy” post training and another showed quality metrics increased pre training to post training (importance, getting it that day, and routine care).32,33 The C-LEAR training may present a reasonable approach to enhance the quality of clinician HPV vaccination recommendations for the 39% of health care providers nationwide who provide lower-quality recommendations.34

This study also adds to the research literature on communication skills training because it shows that participants were able to demonstrate high adherence (70%) of trained communication skills following a brief 1-hour virtual training on a specific framework for giving vaccine recommendations. In addition, there was no significant drop-off in skills between the learning (level 2) and behavior (level 3). Moreover, specific key words and phrases were integrated into clinicians’ counsel statements after the training.

Importantly, two of our evaluation strategies—SPEs and real patient visits—were also used as a means by which to give feedback. Participants received verbal feedback directly from the SPE after the virtual visit and received written feedback from the team after the clinical audio recording. As such, these evaluations also acted as a booster to the intervention. This was particularly true for the SPE visit, which happened within 1 month on average after the training. Because the larger randomized clinical trial includes evaluating the impact of these communication skills interventions on vaccine rates, it was important to take the opportunities to provide feedback.

Limitations of this study included that we were unable to have all the clinicians complete the clinical audio recordings, resulting in a smaller sample size. Furthermore, because we did not observe clinicians’ recommendations before the training, our ability to evaluate causality with SPE and real patients is limited. Finally, we focused on the age group of 11- to 12-year-olds recommended for universal vaccine coverage; however, professional organizations including the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Cancer Society suggest starting routinely vaccinating at age 9 years.35,36 Although the benefits strategy would remain unchanged for this age group, the bundled strategy requires adjustment because only the HPV vaccine is available for 9- and 10-year-olds.37

The strengths of the study are that we had a diversity of clinician participants by sex, race/ethnicity, clinical practice type, and years of primary care experience. In addition, we collected a wide range of qualitative measures to evaluate the clinicians’ recommendation practices following the training.

Our analysis suggests that, during the randomized clinical trial, clinicians were adherent to approximately 60%-70% of the trained components and increased the inclusion of key words or phrases in their counsel statements. This study adds improvement in clinician recommendation content in near-real and real settings to 2 prior studies that evaluated self-reported quantitative measures.33 Although clinician trainings can increase HPV vaccination rates,32,38 further research should consider the modifying effect of the training adherence on HPV vaccination initiation and up to date rates among clinicians to enhance trainings. At a time when vaccine safety is an increasing concern for parents,39 arming clinicians with evidence-based key phrases may improve their efficacy and vaccination confidence in parents and caregivers.
