or retained for the purpose of utilizing the emitted radiation. The sealed radioactive source consists of a
known or estimated quantity of radioactive material contained within a sealed capsule, sealed between
layer(s) of non-radioactive material, or firmly fixed to a non-radioactive surface by electroplating or other
means intended to prevent leakage or escape of the radioactive material.“ (Ref. 4).

CCP-AK-LANL-008. The description and radionuclide distributions for Cm-244, Cf-252 and Np-237
sources are provided in this memo.

TRU waste must contain alpha-emitting radionuclides with atomic numbers greater than uranium (92) and
with half-lives greater than 20 years, at concentrations greater than 100 nanocuries per gram of waste.

252 (2.5 years). However, the decay of Cm-244 produces Pu-240 as its first daughter, which meets the
definition of a WIPP eligible transuranic radionuclide for WIPP disposal when present in concentrations
over 100 nCi/g. Original source material for Cm-244 contains small amounts of transuranic impurities

 RADIOLOGICAL DISTRIBUTIONS

The radionuclide distributions provided below are the basis for characterization of sources using the
WIPP approved characterization software. The derivation of radionuclide distributions and quantification
of uncertainties are based on analytical data sheets for different production batches of source materials,
source manufacturers’ certifications on individual sources that also include the isotopic content of the
batch of source material, AK information on material types from WIPP approved LANL AK documents,
and other data (References D8009, D8020, M8021, M8026, M8028, M8040, M8050, M8051, M8421,
M8422 and M8472) . The representative distributions were developed based on available analytical
information for each sealed source primary isotope. The data, calculations, and results of the calculations
to derive the representative radionuclide distributions and uncertainties are described in the LANL
Technical Report “Radiological Characterization of Actinide Sealed Source Waste for Disposal at WIPP”
and supporting calculation documents (Attachment 1 References D8007, D8013, D8016, D8017, D8018,
D8019, D8021, M8008, M8009, M8010, M8011, M8133, M8421, M8422 and M8472) and in references
5 thru 15, and 19 thru 21 of this Memo.

Summaries of the representative radionuclide distributions taken from the above technical reports for both
mass and activity fractions for Pu-238, Pu-239 and Am-241 are provided in Table 1-1, Summary of

The decay and in-growth of the radioactive material in a sealed source begins at the time of manufacture
and produces a variety of daughter radionuclides, including U-233. Each type of source (i.e., Am-241,
Pu-238, Pu-239) decays to common and unique radionuclides as a function of the Bateman equations
embedded in RadDecay. To account for radioactive decay of the materials in the sealed sources from the
time of manufacture to the time of shipment, the OSRP uses a commercially developed software product
(Reference D8007). This software requires entry of an initial radionuclide quantity and a beginning and
end date for the decay period. The output includes those radionuclides produced by decay processes and
the activity present after the specified period of decay. The contribution of radionuclides from fission and
activation were found to be very small relative to uncertainties in material impurity concentrations
(Attachment 1 References M8008, M8009, M8010, M8011, M8050, and M8133). Also, the half-lives of
the TRU isotopes prevent significant decreases in their quantities during decay correction.

Cm-244 Sources

Curium-244 has been used for the manufacture of sealed sources. The originating material from which
these sources were produced was made at both the Savannah River Site (SRS) and Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) reactors. It was subsequently purified in chemical processing facilities at ORNL.
This material was offered for sale to commercial entities or other DOE laboratories where the sealed
sources were fabricated (Ref. 5). Cm-244 sealed sources were designed for use as electro-plated alpha
sources, as low energy gamma and X-ray sources and as heat generation sources. The isotopes and
impurities present in source production material were evaluated to allow characterization of sources using
AK in a manner similar to that previously used for plutonium and americium sources (Ref. 5, 6, 7, 8, and
9).

The sizes and configuration of the Cm-244 sources vary with the application and intended use for the
source. Electroplated alpha sources were deposited (as a zero-valence metal) on inert platinum (Pt) discs
and were offered by commercial suppliers in sizes ranging from 1 μCi, up to 1 mCi. Low energy X-ray
sources were manufactured by Amersham Corporation in sizes up to 1.25 curies (46.3 GBq) of Cm-244.
The source material was incorporated as an integral component of a curium oxide ceramic matrix which
was bonded and housed into an insert of stainless steel. The outer capsule is a nickel – copper alloy
(Monel®) with a beryllium window. The inner capsule is then placed into an annular stainless-steel insert
with a tungsten backing. This design is similar to the multi layered encapsulation used for Am-241 X-ray
fluorescence capsules. The capsule seams were sealed by argon arc welding prior to leak testing and
special form certification. Capsule diameters as large as 36 mm (1.42 in.) were manufactured. The X-ray
fluorescence sources had an annular beryllium window 1 mm (0.04 in.) thick. A very limited number of
Cm-244 sources have been made as heat sources for specific applications. Depending on the application
and heating requirement, the Cm-244 content for heat sources could be as large as one curie or more. Heat
source packaging is necessarily robust. For example, the NASA SNAP–III RTG design calls for double
encapsulated 304 stainless steel.

Cf-252 Sources

Californium-252 is produced at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory in a series of irradiation campaigns
starting with the irradiation of plutonium oxide. After irradiation, californium is chemically separated
from the other heavy elements produced and further purified and formed into californium oxide (Cf2O3).
Cf-252 decays with an effective half-life of 2.646 years. It decays by both alpha emission (96.9 %, T1/2 =
2.731 yr.) and by spontaneous fission (3.1 %, T1/2 = 85.5 yr.). It is commonly distributed for source
manufacture as californium oxide or a californium-palladium alloy and is usually doubly encapsulated in
stainless steel. The californium-palladium alloy is Cf2O3, in a palladium matrix and shaped into wires.
Source manufacturers purchase the Cf-252 in bulk and manufacture sources of desired activity by
controlling the length of the wire placed into a source or working with the ceramic material directly.
Typically, the source material is cut to a length corresponding to the desired activity and doubly

Np-237 Sources

The Np-237 material in the recovered sealed sources is a byproduct of defense weapons grade Pu
production in Savannah River Plant (SRP) reactors. The primary product, Pu-239 was produced in
irradiated enriched uranium fuels and in depleted uranium and natural uranium targets
through neutron capture reactions in uranium atoms (Ref. 16).

Like the Pu isotopes, the Np-237 was also separated from the irradiated U fuel/target material in the
PUREX process, primarily for use as target material in Pu-238 production (Ref. 17). The intent to
produce Pu-238 was to manufacture heat source material for both defense and non-defense applications.

Over the period when Np-237 was being produced, small amounts were transferred to ORNL from SRS
for distribution by the ORNL Isotope Sales Program. Monsanto Research Corporation (MRC) bought
Np-237 from ORNL. The Np-237 sources are largely manufactured by Monsanto Research Corporation
(MRC).

Neptunium-237 sealed sources were developed as threshold detectors model MRC-TD-Cu-W-Np for fast
neutrons and were used to map neutron emission fields from generators and reactors. The radioisotope
Np-237 in the form of metal oxide was encapsulated in electrolytically refined copper with single or
double encapsulation.

The Np-237 source material is approximately 99.99 wt/% Np-237, with the remainder of the material
containing trace impurities of other radionuclides resulting from the separation and purification processes.

The threshold detector sources are fabricated from 2 thin discs of copper sheet and a washer shaped ring
of copper sized in thickness to give the desired internal volume. The thin discs are attached to the washer
by inert gas welding. Double encapsulation of these sources was available.

4. CHARACTERIZATION METHODS AND LIMITATIONS

In December of 2003 a Peer Review Committee met to Review the feasibility of Characterizing sealed
sources using AK data exclusively. This peer review panel was convened to review the adequacy of the
available data to reasonably support the determination of the radionuclide content for compliance with the
WIPP Contact-Handled Transuranic (TRU) Waste Acceptance Criteria (WAC). These records include
original manufacturing records, shipping data sheets, and source control information such as the Nuclear
Materials Management and Safeguards System (NMMSS). In addition, other corroborating sources of
information, such as sealed source engraved markings, Nuclear Regulatory Commission
(NRC)/Agreement State regulatory approval data and US Department of Transportation (DOT) records
have been collected to support the assignment of radiological properties.

From these considerations, the Peer Review Panel concluded that the various data records collected
provide either uniquely, or as the sum of several individual records, adequate documentation for
determining the radionuclide type, the radionuclide content/activity and either the date of manufacture or
other more conservative date for purposes of decay correction (Ref. 21). The Peer Review Panel
concluded (and the EPA concurred) that these data are adequate for assigning, with a high degree of
certainty, the radiological information required for the disposal of this material at WIPP.

Radioactive sealed sources have always been fabricated under highly controlled production requirements.
That has resulted in a large number of detailed historical records. LANL uses manufacturing records and
other available documents as a basis for characterizing the radiological content of sources at the time of
their fabrication. The characterization software then applies the principles of radioactive decay and
ingrowth to calculate the change in radioactive content of the sources to the time of submittal for disposal.

LANL uses this fabrication data to derive all of the necessary radionuclide activities for disposal at WIPP.
The ten radionuclides required to be tracked upon disposal at WIPP are U-233, U-234, U-238, Pu-238,
Pu-239, Pu-240, Am-241, Pu-242, Cs-137, and Sr-90. Prior to shipment to WIPP, the contents in the
drums are characterized with respect to isotopic content and total activity; as well as Fissile Gram
Equivalent (FGE), Plutonium Equivalent Curies (PE-Ci.), Wattage, and TRU Alpha Concentration.

The radiological characterization method described above relies on the use of AK records to determine
the software input information to be used in the characterization process. The required information is:

 PREVALENT RADIONUCLIDES

The total gram value for each individual radionuclide was divided by the total mass of all radioactive
constituents in the waste stream and converted to a percentage. To determine the radionuclide weight
percent range for individual containers, the radiological mass in each container in the waste stream was
summed. The mass of each individual radionuclide in a container was divided by the total radiological

data for each container. For containers that reported only either mass or curie values, the specific activity
of the nuclide was used to calculate the other parameter.
