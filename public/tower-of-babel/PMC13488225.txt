European occupational therapists’ attitudes toward the dark side occupations: an in-depth E-survey in 32 countries
Authors: Zuzana Rodová, Petra Petrová, Vladimír Rogalewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe concept of “dark side occupations” remains ethically and culturally sensitive within occupational therapy practice. While the topic has been explored conceptually, less is known about how therapists across European regions address such occupations in clinical dialogue. This study examined regional differences in the perception of ethically sensitive or socially contested occupations and explored how professionals see the term itself.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among occupational therapy professionals across four European regions: Central and Eastern, Southern, Western, and Northern Europe. The questionnaire combined closed-ended items assessing the frequency with which specific topics (e.g., sexuality, addictions, politically or socially sensitive activities) are addressed in practice, with open-ended questions exploring avoided topics and perceptions of the term “dark side occupations.” Quantitative data are presented descriptively with comparison across regions. Qualitative responses were subjected to thematic content analysis.ResultsMarked regional differences were identified. Topics such as sexuality, addictions, and other socially sensitive activities were addressed significantly less frequently in Central and Eastern Europe and, to a lesser extent, Southern Europe compared to Western/Northern Europe. In several regions, a pattern of pre-emptive topic avoidance was evident rather than explicit therapeutic refusal. Open-text responses suggested that contextual factors, including cultural norms, institutional climate, and perceived professional boundaries, shape clinical decision-making. Perceptions of the term “dark side occupations” were varied, with some respondents considering it a useful analytical framework and others viewing it as potentially normatively loaded or stigmatizing.ConclusionsThe perception of ethically sensitive or socially contested occupations and topics in occupational therapy practice appears to be context-dependent and regionally patterned. Avoidance of certain topics may have implications for client autonomy and therapeutic dialogue. These findings highlight the need for culturally informed ethical reflection and clearer professional guidance in addressing complex socially sensitive or socially contested occupations within European occupational therapy practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01532-1.

Background

Occupational therapy, which can be considered part of the rehabilitation profession, helps people develop or maintain the skills needed for daily living, working, and leisure. Its goal is making individuals with physical, cognitive, or mental health challenges able to live more independently by adapting tasks or environments. It traditionally views the concept of “occupation” (intentional human activity) as something positive, health-promoting, and socially valuable. However, in recent years, this view has faced growing criticism from authors who point to the existence of activities that are subjectively meaningful but at the same time stigmatized, risky, or even harmful. In this paper we will collectively refer to these as dark side occupations. We chose this term based on numerous discussions with participants of European and World occupational therapy congresses hosted by Occupational Therapy Europe (OT Europe) and the World Federation of Occupational Therapists (WFOT). The concept of the “dark side of occupation” [1] extends the traditional framework of occupational therapy and encourages a re-evaluation of the assumption that meaningful activities are always health-promoting, ethically acceptable, and therapeutically useful [2, 3].

Dark side occupations encompass a whole range of activities that are meaningful and purposeful for individuals but are considered unhealthy, unsanctioned, or morally questionable from the perspective of the dominant discourse [4]. These include, for example, excessive work engagement, repetitive gaming, substance abuse, or certain forms of intimate behaviour [2, 5]. These activities challenge traditional therapeutic norms and raise an ethical dilemma: Should occupational therapy support clients in activities that are meaningful to them, even if they are viewed as problematic from a health or social norms perspective? Does the therapist have the right to reject these activities or even control them based on moral judgments?

The concept has not developed in a fully uniform way. Twinley’s earlier work [2] also highlighted occupations involving harm to others, including violence, thereby foregrounding moral responsibility and professional boundaries. Later contributors broadened the discussion toward occupations that are meaningful to individuals but socially unsanctioned, stigmatized, unhealthy, or otherwise normatively contested. In this study, we primarily work with this broader practice-oriented understanding.

For example, activities such as playing computer games or gambling can be a source of cognitive stimulation, social connection or stress relief, but at the same time they pose a risk to daily functioning or mental health [4]. Similarly, gambling can function as a coping strategy, social ritual or form of resistance, but it is also associated with addiction and social problems. Using the example of beauty contests, Sy, Martinez, and Twinley [6] show that even seemingly innocent performance activities can involve hidden labour, psychological risk or submission to norms, thus blurring the lines between “good” and “bad” occupations.

Similar debates about client autonomy, risky or unsanctioned behaviour, and therapeutic boundaries also arise in other health professions, such as psychology or physical therapy. Goddard [7] reports a high level of moral distress among physical therapists and occupational therapists who feel constrained by institutional regulations in their efforts to provide truly client-centred care. Similarly, Bushby et al. [8] point to a number of ethical tensions in occupational therapy practice, particularly as a result of systemic constraints, lack of resources, or conflicting values.

Although theoretical recognition of these topics in occupational therapy is growing, empirical knowledge remains fragmented. Kiepek et al. [4, 9] point to the continuing silence and lack of open discourse on activities that do not conform to Western norms of health and normality. They emphasize [10] that this silence is not neutral, but rather reflects hegemonic approaches that favour Western, middle-class, and ableist conceptions of “good” occupation. Greber [3] points to the ethical tension faced by therapists in situations where the client wishes to continue activities that are problematic from a rehabilitation perspective (e.g., smoking, drinking alcohol). Do therapists have the right to decide which activities are appropriate? Or is it their job to respect the meaning that the client attaches to these activities?

In the European context, these issues are proving to be very topical. For example, a survey of Czech occupational therapists showed that 66.3% of them sometimes deal with their clients’ dark side occupations, but only 15.1% say they are open to all such topics [11]. Similarly the authors of a similar 2024 survey among Danish occupational therapists conclude that “there is no clear consensus when it comes to addressing unilluminated [= dark side] occupations in interventions“ [12]. Topics as sexuality, addictions, unsanctioned or risky leisure activities often remain unaddressed in therapy, due to ethical uncertainty, lack of support, or fear of judgment. These findings point to the need for global research to clarify how therapists in different cultures and health systems perceive and deal with the dark side occupations.

Despite growing interest, however, there is still a lack of systematic research examining how occupational therapists in different countries perceive, evaluate, and therapeutically address activities referred to as “dark side occupations”. This gap limits our understanding of the cultural, institutional, and ethical factors that influence clinical practice and prevents the development of recommendations for an ethically grounded and contextually sensitive approach.

Understanding the dark side occupations does not mean normalizing unsanctioned behaviour. It means broadening the therapeutic understanding of human experience with respect for individuality, meaning, and social context, even if it means stepping outside the boundaries of traditional health discourse. As Kramer [13] notes, ignoring these occupations means limiting the significance of occupational therapy in today’s world full of uncertainty, marginalization, and value pluralism.

The aim of this study is therefore to map the attitudes of European occupational therapists towards the “dark side occupations”, i.e., activities that are controversial, unsanctioned, or stigmatized, but may nevertheless be perceived as meaningful by clients. The research combines a quantitative survey with qualitative analysis to capture the nuances of perception, ethical dilemmas, and clinical decision-making frameworks across culturally diverse settings.

Our article does not aim to resolve all the ethical dilemmas in occupational therapy. By surveying the views of occupational therapists working in Europe, we hope to gain a clearer understanding of an area that seems rather vague and ambiguous. The data obtained will contribute to a deeper understanding of how European occupational therapists currently balance client goals, professional ethics, and social norms when they are faced with controversial activities. At the same time, it will enable reflection on how occupational therapy education, research, and policy can be adapted to the reality of everyday practice. In this regard, the article builds on the appeal of Kiepek and Magalhães [9] and Twinley [1, 2, 14], who call for a broader and more inclusive conceptualization of occupation that would embrace its paradoxes and ethical tensions.

Methods

The survey is based on our original questionnaire mapping experiences and opinions of the respondents. The questionnaire was drafted in Czech language and piloted in the Czech occupational therapy environment, where 86 respondents showed interesting results [11]. Then it was translated into English and sent to four occupational therapists (from Malta, New Zealand, Colombia and the Philippines) in February 2025. Several questions were reformulated for better clarity following their feedback. The final questionnaire is provided in Supplemental Materials.

The questionnaire consists of 18 questions. First eight (closed) questions specify the respondent´s profile. Further closed questions (Q9, Q10, Q12, Q14 and Q15) ask respondents which common and/or dark topics they open up about/do not open up about during therapy. Open text questions (Q11 and Q13) allowed respondents to give more details. These core questions are supplemented by three open items. Question Q16 asks about respondent´s own dark side occupations (the responses to this question will be published separately). Question Q17 asks for an opinion on the term “dark side occupations”. The last item Q18 is open for any additional comments that respondents would like to share with the research team. Thus, the questionnaire addressed not only occupations in a narrow sense, but also socially sensitive topics and occupation-related goals as they emerge in therapeutic dialogue.

Data were collected online through Google Forms. The link to the questionnaire was sent by emails to all delegates (and their alternates) of the World Federation of Occupational Therapists asking them for a distribution in their country, and it was also propagated through social media and the authors´ international contacts. Generally, the survey was distributed in a decentralized manner, using non-probabilistic convenience sampling. Data were collected from February 11 to March 31, 2025.

The study gained ethical approval from the Czech Association of Occupational Therapists on 10 February 2025 and adhered to the principles of informed consent. No sensitive or personal data were collected. Before participating in the study, the occupational therapists were fully informed about the research and their rights, and they provided their informed consent before filling out the questionnaire.

We collected 389 questionnaires, excluded one respondent who was not an occupational therapist, and analysed 388 questionnaires using descriptive statistics. This paper focuses on 308 European responses, with brief mention of the remaining 80 from non-European regions. Europe was divided into four regions (West, North, South, Central and Eastern) for analysis of opinion differences. The answers to closed questions were analysed using descriptive and basic inductive statistics, and the results are shown in a tabular and graphic way. Open-ended responses were analysed using a manual qualitative content analysis approach. Initial familiarisation with the data was followed by inductive coding, where recurring themes and patterns were identified without the use of specialised software. Codes were grouped into categories based on conceptual similarity, adhering to established qualitative research practices [15–17]. This analytical process allowed for a systematic interpretation of respondents’ insights while maintaining contextual nuance.

The core of the survey consisted of questions Q9 and Q10, which concerned how often respondents bring up selected topics (both common and dark) with their clients during the initial examination (Q9) and during therapy (Q10). Importantly, these items addressed not only occupations as such, but also socially sensitive topics emerging within therapeutic dialogue. The option scale was never (coded as 0), sometimes (1), often (2), and always (3). The results showed that respondents mostly answered both questions in the same way, so separate analysis of both questions does not make much sense. Therefore, we combined the answers to Q9 and Q10 (for each topic) and used the higher of the two answers as the result (topics 15–23 were only included in Q10, so this answer was used). Figure 1 clearly shows that occupational therapists largely avoid topics that could be classified as dark side occupations. There is an obvious boundary separating common and dark side occupations. Although the situation is generally poor, it is not the same across the entire continent. Pearson’s chi-square test shows that the regions differ in their approach to individual topics (see Table 3). Overall, we can say with some reservation that dark side occupations are discussed significantly less than average in Central and Eastern Europe, and more than average in Northern Europe. In Southern Europe therapists ask questions about everyday life more often during therapy than elsewhere in Europe.

We asked respondents explicitly which activities they directly or indirectly avoid with their patients/clients, or which activities they do not want to talk about with them. First, we gave them a list to choose from (Q12). A relatively large proportion of respondents ticked the “none of the above” box, i.e., they do not avoid anything. However, responses to this question vary across different parts of Europe (p = 0.0208). While this was the majority response in the Nordic countries (54%) and close to half of respondents in Western and Southern Europe (48% each), only 30% of respondents in Central and Eastern Europe countries were open in this way.

The most problematic topic (which therapists avoid the most) is sex, but attitudes toward it vary across Europe (p = 0.112). While only about one-third of therapists (32–34%) avoid it in Northern and Southern Europe, the figure is 40% in Western Europe and a high 57% in Central and Eastern European countries. The answer to the question about sex significantly influenced the answer that the therapists do not avoid anything (“none of the above”). Therefore, we also calculated the proportion of therapists who “either do not avoid anything or only avoid the area of sex.” The answer defined in this way then significantly increased the frequency. Nevertheless, the differences between regions remain statistically significant (p = 0.0483), with relative frequencies of North 70%, West 61%, South 52%, Central and East 49%; it is obvious that respondents from Central, Eastern and Southern European countries are significantly more reluctant to discuss certain topics. Therapists from Central, Eastern and Southern countries almost universally respond differently (more cautiously) than therapists from the West and North when it comes to dark side occupations.

As concerns alcohol, occupational therapists are quite open, and the geographical differences are statistically insignificant. The topic of smoking is not raised by 19% of occupational therapists in Central and Eastern Europe, compared to only 5–9% in the rest of Europe (South, North and West). The differences are statistically significant (p = 0.0135). The worst situation in terms of substance abuse and prescription drug abuse (least willingness to talk about it) is in Eastern Europe and, in the case of substances (not prescription drugs), also in Southern Europe. It is interesting to compare these two categories: in the first, the differences are statistically significant (p = 0.0488), while in the second they are not (p = 0.5311), with Southern Europe being the main difference. Raising the issue of poor hygiene does not cause problems for most therapists across Europe, with results ranging between 9% and 12% of those who have reservations.

The answer to the open question Q13, i.e. free-text comments to the avoided topics, show that a significant proportion of respondents did not consider providing their additional comments (220 respondents, i.e. nearly 75% of them, left this item blank). Out of those who provided their views, large number did not identify any specific topic that they would avoid in practice, or provided only general, unspecific responses (General unspecific reflection, n = 27; No avoided topics, n = 20). These responses suggest either the absence of clearly defined taboos, the low relevance of the question to the respondents’ everyday practice, or limited understanding of dark side occupations. In addition, there are comments emphasizing that the openness of sensitive topics depends on the context, specific situation, or nature of the client (Context-dependent, n = 7). Smaller but clearly identifiable groups of respondents mentioned specific topics that they avoid in their work. They most frequently mentioned politics (n = 11), followed by sexuality and intimacy (n = 7), and violence or traumatic experiences (n = 6). Less frequently, respondents mentioned religious topics (n = 4). Overall, the results suggest that while some respondents identify specific sensitive areas, most emphasize openness or contextual conditioning in working with these topics rather than the existence of fixed areas that they would systematically avoid.

To further explore this topic, we asked respondents a direct question (Q14): “Imagine that during the process of setting goals in occupational therapy with a patient/client, you discover that his/her goal is focused on a dark side occupation (e.g. lighting a cigarette with a lighter, drinking a pint of beer, driving a car despite a temporary driving ban, learning how to bet online, etc.). Do you choose that dark side occupation as a goal for therapy?” Unlike the preceding items, Q14 focused more explicitly on occupation-related goals within therapy. The differences in the answer were statistically insignificant across Europe (p = 0.2616), when most respondents chose “never” (27%) or “sometimes” (58%).

Finally, respondents were asked with whom and how often they consult about the dark side of their clients’ occupations (Q15). The professions were selected to represent the most common options. The possible answers were again never (0), sometimes (1), often (2), or always (3), and the results (Table 4) showed that they hardly consult with anybody.

Discussion

The attitude to dark side occupations varies systematically across European regions, indicating that ethical practice in occupational therapy is context-dependent. Our data show that topics such as sexuality, addictions, and other “dark” activities are addressed significantly less frequently in certain regions, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe and, to a lesser extent, Southern Europe. This pattern does not concern only the rejection of specific therapeutic goals, but the systematic absence of prior discussion. Thus, our findings do not concern only the acceptance or rejection of specific dark side occupations as therapeutic goals. They also suggest differences in therapeutic openness toward occupations associated with sexuality, addiction, risk, illegality, or social disapproval, which is consistent with occupational science literature on the silencing and marginalisation of occupations framed as unhealthy, illegal, or deviant [10].

From an ethical perspective, this is directly related to the principle of autonomy. Autonomy does not imply endorsement of unsanctioned behaviour; rather, it requires that clients are given space to articulate and examine such goals. When therapists pre-emptively avoid certain topics, clients lose the opportunity to reflect on them within the context of a safe professional dialogue. This pattern was already evident in the Czech pilot study [11], and the present European data confirm that this is not an isolated national phenomenon. Twinley’s original conceptualisation [1, 14] is relevant here because the ethical challenge of the dark side is not limited to self-regarding risk or socially disapproved behaviour; it may also involve occupations that harm other people, including violent acts. Our survey did not explicitly focus on violence-centred occupations, so this dimension cannot be examined in depth from the present data. Nevertheless, this perspective remains important because it extends the ethical discussion beyond stigma and health risk to include interpersonal harm and professional boundaries.

Regional differences should be interpreted within the broader cultural diversity of Europe, including but not limited to occupational therapy traditions. A recent scoping review highlights the heterogeneity of European occupational therapy traditions and value frameworks [20]. Lower openness in Central and Eastern Europe and Southern Europe likely reflects wider socio-cultural norms, generational expectations, and assumptions about age-appropriate or socially legitimate occupations rather than simply the reluctance of individual therapists. These findings point to influences that go beyond individual moral attitudes.

At the same time, the absence of certain dark side topics from therapy may reflect not only professional or personal reluctance, but also broader societal expectations regarding what is considered relevant, appropriate, or imaginable for a particular client. Age, life stage, gendered expectations, and the social visibility of specific occupations may shape both what clients disclose and what therapists ask about [10, 21]. For example, some occupations may be perceived as less relevant or less likely in older age and therefore may go unaddressed not because they are irrelevant to professional ethics, but because they fall outside dominant assumptions about who engages in them. In this sense, therapeutic silence may also mirror wider social norms about normality, deviance, and age-appropriate occupation.

The ethical question, then, is not whether dark side activities should be supported, but whether their systematic neglect limits meaningful client participation in the therapy. In addition, the low frequency with which dark side occupations are adopted as explicit therapeutic goals suggests that avoidance may extend beyond conversational boundaries and interfere with the setting of treatment priorities. Avoidance of unsanctioned topics may be motivated by the principle of non-maleficence—an attempt not to legitimise harmful or illegal behaviour. However, this protective approach creates tension between safeguarding clients and respecting their autonomy.

Durocher and Kinsella [22] describe this situation as one of “competing allegiances”, i.e. conflicts between loyalty to the client, professional duty, institutional expectations, and safety requirements. Mønsted and colleagues [12] found that the Danish occupational therapists saw a potential ethical dilemma between encouraging safe, health-promoting occupational engagement and a client-centred approach, if a client preferred dark side occupations. Our findings empirically reflect this approach. Therapists may be committed to providing client-centred care, but at the same time they may be constrained by legal or organisational obligations, community expectations, or their own values, when ethical guidelines or their own scope of practice prevent them from addressing dark side occupations.

The notably low rate of consultations with psychiatrists, psychologists, or addiction specialists is a significant finding. When therapists encounter goals involving addictive or high-risk activities, the absence of interdisciplinary collaboration may represent an ethical vulnerability. This issue does not concern a dilemma at the level of autonomy, but rather professional responsibility. Our data suggest that this may also be the case here; issues related to addiction or high-risk behaviours are predominantly addressed within occupational therapists themselves, while consultations with other professionals remain relatively rare.

Another question concerns the threshold beyond which it is no longer appropriate to treat a case independently, and referring it becomes a professional obligation. Where substantial health or social risks are present, interdisciplinary collaboration may be not only desirable but also ethically necessary. The low rate of consultation may therefore indicate a gap between formal competencies and the structural realities of practice.

Generally, the contrast between Northern/Western Europe and Central and Eastern/Southern Europe is consistent. Therapists in Northern and Western Europe demonstrate greater openness toward sensitive topics. These differences are better interpreted as structural rather than purely individual.

European occupational science develops within diverse political and cultural traditions [20]. If clients in certain regions are systematically exposed to lower levels of openness toward discussing sensitive occupations, this reflects not only cultural differences, but also potential inequality in access to participatory care. The ethical implications therefore extend beyond individual decision-making to the systemic conditions under which practice takes place. Inequality lies in the varying availability of opportunities for participation in different regions.

The preliminary analysis of the 80 non-European responses (East Asia and Pacific 39, Middle East and North Africa 15, Latin America and Caribbean 9, Sub-Saharan Africa 8, North America 7, South Asia 2) that the authors received in their survey reveals even greater variability in attitudes, particularly concerning sexuality and religiously sensitive topics. In some contexts, reluctance was greater than in Central and Eastern European countries, while in others, openness was comparable to Northern Europe. These findings reinforce the conclusion that attitudes toward dark side occupations are culturally conditioned rather than universal professional constants.

Responses to Q17 reveal a contradictory attitude towards the term itself. While some respondents consider dark side occupations a useful conceptual framework, others perceive it as normatively loaded. Occupational science literature has shown that activities framed as unhealthy, illegal or deviant are often silenced or marginalised through dominant normative discourses [10]. The classification of an occupation as dark is therefore not inherent to the activity itself but reflects socially constructed evaluative patterns. Labelling an activity as dark inevitably introduces normative framing. Moralising language may reinforce stigma; on the other hand, insisting on strict neutrality can lead to clinically relevant harms not being adequately addressed. Ethical engaging in dark side occupations therefore depends not only on individual judgement, but also on institutional and socio-cultural frameworks in which the practice takes place.

In purely statistical terms, out of 308 European respondents, 135 (44%) supported the term “dark side occupations”. Ninety-three (30%) expressed criticism of it; their arguments typically concerned the term’s value-charged nature, its potentially stigmatizing character, or its difficulty of application in practice; however, only three of them recommended an alternative term. Finally, 76 (25%) respondents were unable to take a clear position or made general conceptual remarks relating to a broader theoretical framework or the nature of human activities in general, without explicitly expressing agreement or disagreement. Only four respondents did not provide an answer to this question.

The study relies on convenience sampling and self-reported data, limiting its generalisability. The reported openness may have been influenced by social desirability bias. Regional categorization simplifies heterogeneity both within and between countries. The inclusion of 32 European countries provides a robust basis for identifying consistent regional patterns. Non-European data are just exploratory, as we received only a very limited number of responses that do not represent most regions. Next to that, the questionnaire was oriented more toward socially sensitive, stigmatized, or normatively contested occupations and topics than toward occupations involving explicit violence or direct harm to others in the narrower sense, as emphasized by Twinley and Addidle in their original formulation [2].

Conclusion

The ethical implications of dark side occupations do not lie in their inherent moral status, but in how they are addressed or avoided within clinical practice. When sensitive or socially contested activities remain outside professional dialogue, the space for client autonomy may be narrowed, even in an environment that otherwise supports client-centred care.

The findings of this study highlight a persistent tension between respect for autonomy, concerns about harm, and professional responsibility. This tension does not reflect individual failure but rather the conditions under which practice takes place. Regional differences suggest that approaches to open and participatory dialogue are shaped by broader socio-cultural and institutional environments, and by professional limitations established through historical categorisation of occupations and prejudice against people with disabilities.

Ethical engagement in dark side occupations therefore requires more than individual discretion. It calls for reflective practice, clearer professional guidance, and institutional contexts that support interdisciplinary collaboration and critical dialogue. Recognising this complexity is essential if occupational therapy is to maintain truly participatory and ethically responsive care.
