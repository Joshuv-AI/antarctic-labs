Enhancing Fusarium resistance in Nigella sativa via spray-induced gene silencing (SIGS) using chitosan nanoparticles with molecular and phytochemical perspectives
Authors: Rouya Mohammed Ahmed, Muna Mohammed Khayri, Hiba Naser Ali, Hamsa H Abbas, Thura Alyasiri, Iman A Mohammed Ali, Ahmed MAmshawee, Haneen Mushtaq Hameed, Mohammad Mahmoud Farhan Al-Halbosiy, Ahmed Flayyih Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFusarium oxysporum is the causal agent of vascular wilt disease, which makes it hard to grow and use Nigella sativa as medicine. This disease significantly reduces both yield and phytochemical quality. Traditional methods of control frequently utilize chemical fungicides; however, these can leave behind harmful residues and modify the bioactive profile of the crop. In this study, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) targeting key pathogenicity genes (CYP51 and SGE1) of Fusarium oxysporum was designed and synthesized. This technique is known as Spray-Induced Gene Silencing (SIGS). We employed ionic gelation to mix the dsRNA with chitosan nanoparticles (CSNPs) to make it more stable, easier for leaves to take in, and able to release over time. Plants were sprayed with CSNP-dsRNA mixes at particular times during their growth, and a range of disease and quality measurements were systematically evaluated. Plants that had been treated with CSNP-dsRNA exhibited 68.4% reduced disease severity than plants that had just been exposed to the pathogen, with statistically significant effects (p ≤ 0.05). The severity of the disease was scored on a scale of 0–5. Quantitative PCR demonstrated that the treated plants had 70% reduced fungal biomass. This was associated with a 3.2- to 4.7-fold increase in the expression of defense-related genes (PR1, PDF1.2, and WRKY70) within 72 h of treatment. Biochemical analysis using HPLC demonstrated that the thymoquinone level in seeds from treated plants remained at approximately 94% of that in healthy controls. A 41.3% reduction seen in thymoquinone content in the seeds due to failure to treat the infection suggests that applications of SIGS followed by nanoparticle technology could have potential applications to either reduce the incidence of disease, stimulate healthy molecular defence mechanisms or protect the medicinal value of N. sativa. Moreover, SIGS is a viable, eco-friendly and harmless alternative to chemical fungicides, and could be applied to the management of profitable commercially planted crops that deliver considerable human health benefits; thus, providing a new and sustainable method to control plant disease.Keywords: Nigella sativa, Fusarium oxysporum, Chitosan nanoparticles, Defense gene expression, ThymoquinoneSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Black cumin or black seed are names used for Nigella sativa. Nigella sativa has been used in traditional medicine for thousands of years. Today it still has relevance in modern day medicine1. A variety of bioactive agents are found in the seeds of this plant, but the most significant is thymoquinone1. Because of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and potentially anticancer properties1,2 many scientists have become interested in this compound. Nigella sativa, an economically valuable crop in the pharmaceutical and nutraceutical industries, has several characteristics that benefit these markets. Consistent chemical composition and stable yields are critical in both of these sectors. Although Nigella sativa has many medicinal properties, it is not without challenges. One of the most destructive pathogens of Nigella sativa is Fusarium oxysporum3. This soil-borne fungal pathogen disperses via the vascular system of the plant leading to plant wilt, reduced growth rates, and significant losses in production3. The existence of infection(s) in an infected plant has been shown to reduce the amount of thymoquinone (a major medicinal component) in the seed and subsequently reduce both the medicinal and commercial value of the seed. Researchers are looking at developing new molecular control methods for diseases to use fewer artificial pesticide(s)4–9. One way is by using a gene silencing method called spray-induced gene silencing (SIGS)10–14, which utilizes double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to silence certain pathogen (i.e., virus and fungus) genes located on the plant surface3,13,14. SIGS utilizes RNA interference (RNAi) as a means of silencing the gene in the pathogen15–17, thus reducing the activity of the pathogen by silencing genes linked to disease13,14. When performing SIGS, the genetic make-up of the plant is unchanged and thus, more socially and environmentally acceptable18. The instability of dsRNA in sunlight and environmental enzymes represents an important challenge for SIGS19,20. As a result of this challenge, researchers have sought to identify carrier systems capable of protecting dsRNA and enabling its effective delivery into plant tissues21. Chitosan nanoparticles produced via ionic gelation are of interest for being biodegradable, having biocompatibility, inhibiting microbial growth and inducing plant defense responses13,14,21. Recent studies have also demonstrated that chitosan-based complexes can significantly enhance antifungal activity and activate plant defense-related responses against phytopathogenic fungi22. The stabilization of dsRNA delivered by chitosan nanoparticles (dsRNA@Chitosan) enhances the delivery efficacy of dsRNA13,14.

The use of this method to create a stronger resistance to Fusarium oxysporum as well as protect retained levels of the valuable thymoquinone found in N. sativa will reduce the stress due to the infection and ultimately allow for continued or possibly increased production of bioactive compounds through metabolic pathways. Here we report on the efficacy of the delivery of dsRNA via chitosan nanoparticles to improve the defense response of Nigella sativa to Fusarium oxysporum; more specifically in two areas: the expression of specific defense-associated genes (such as PR1-like, WRKY70-like, NPR1-like, or PDF1.2-like) will be assessed as molecular markers of general resistance, while the amount of thymoquinone present in Nigella sativa will play a role as a phytochemical marker denoting its quality as a medicine. As a whole, this research will provide an innovative and sustainable method of protecting Medicinals against deleterious disease through the use of molecular plant pathology combined with phytochemical investigations.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Certified seeds of Nigella sativa were obtained from the Local Agricultural Seed Center, Ministry of Agriculture, Baghdad, Iraq, ensuring genetic purity and freedom from detectable pathogens. To eliminate potential microbial contaminants, seeds underwent a two-step surface sterilization procedure. First, seeds were immersed in 70% ethanol for 1 min to disrupt microbial lipid membranes, followed by thorough rinsing with sterile distilled water to remove ethanol residues. To provide thorough disinfection of all seeds, they were soaked for 10 min in a 2% sodium hypochlorite solution with constant stirring, and rinsed multiple times separately in distilled water (3–5 times) to remove residual chlorine (to avoid causing damage to plants). After sterilization, the seeds were planted in a certified, autoclaved, soilless growing medium consisting of a mixture of peat, sand, and perlite, in a volume ratio of approximately 2:1:1 to provide optimal aeration, drainage, and nutrients’ retention. The plants were grown under controlled conditions in an environmental chamber at a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C and a photoperiod of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness with 60–70% relative humidity. Illumination was provided by full-spectrum fluorescent lamps delivering approximately 200 µmol m−2 s−1 of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). Plants were irrigated with sterile distilled water as needed to maintain consistent soil moisture and grown for 4–6 weeks prior to experimental treatments.

Pathogen culture and inoculum preparation

A virulent isolate of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. nigella was acquired from the Plant Pathology Laboratory culture collection located at the College of Science, University of Baghdad in Iraq. The species’ identity was confirmed by morphology and ITS region sequencing; although ITS region sequencing was used for the initial identification, it should be noted that additional molecular marker(s) such as tef1-α or rpb2 may assist in better resolving the species at the species level. To promote mycelia growth and provide for sporulation, the fungus was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates and incubated at 25 ± 1 °C in darkness for 7 days. In order to prepare the inoculum, 10 mL of sterile distilled water was added to each PDA plate followed by gently scraping the colony surface using a sterile glass spreader in order to dislodge conidia from the surface. Following this, the culture was filtered through two layers of sterile cheesecloth to remove any hyphal debris and then the spore concentration was determined using a Neubauer haemocytometer. The concentration of the spore suspension was adjusted to 1 × 106 conidia/mL using sterile distilled water. The inoculum was utilized immediately to assure maximum viability of the inoculum.

Target gene selection and dsRNA synthesis

Target selection and in-silico validation

The two main genes from the fungus Fusarium oxysporum, CYP51 and SGE1, were chosen as possible targets for RNAi due to their importance for the survival and pathogenicity of the fungus. Ultimately, SGE1 was selected as the target for the in vivo efficacy assays. SGE1 was chosen for a target because it plays an essential role as a major virulence regulator and an effector gene regulator, and therefore, it is the best target for preventing fungal disease. The coding sequences of both genes were obtained from the NCBI GenBank database (see Supplementary Table 1 for accession numbers) and matched against the RNA transcriptome and genomic databases from Nigella sativa by BLASTn to find sequences that do not have significant sequence similarity. The E-value was set at less than 1e-5 and the percentage identity was set at less than 75% over a distance of 100 nucleotides or greater, to minimize the possibility of targeting off-site genes for silencing in the host plant.

Template preparation (fungal DNA/RNA extraction)

Nucleic acids were extracted from mycelia cells harvested from 7-day-old PDA cultures. Approximately 50 to 100 mg of fresh mycelia were used for genomic DNA isolation via CTAB (2% CTAB, 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH = 8.0; 20 mM EDTA; 1.4 M NaCl), and incubated at 65 °C for 30 min. Following CTAB extraction, chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (24/1) was used to extract, and ethanol was used to precipitate DNA pellets. The pellets were washed in 70% ethanol, resuspended in TE Buffer, and purity determined via spectrophotometry (A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0). For transcript-derived templates, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol, reverse transcribed into cDNA from 1 µg RNA using Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Both genomic DNA and cDNA were employed as templates for PCR amplification, resulting in inserts of similar size for dsRNA synthesis.

Primer design and expected amplicon sizes

Gene specific primers containing the T7 promoter sequence (5′–TAATACGACTCACTATAGGG–3′) were created by adding the T7 promoter to either the forward (F) or reverse (R) primer’s 5′ end. This allows for in vitro transcription of both DNA strands. The target fragment size was 300–500 bp which maximized RNA interference and minimized off-target effects. The expected sizes of the PCR products were approximately 420 bp (CYP51) and 360 bp (SGE1). Primer design was done using Primer3 software and had a GC content of 40–60% and a melting temperature (Tm) of 58–62 °C. Potential for secondary structures such as primer dimers and hairpins were analyzed using the Oligo Analyzer tool before commencing any amplification with the primers to ensure efficiency and specificity of the amplification reaction.

PCR amplification and gel purification

To amplify fragments of interest by PCR, we employed Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) in reaction volumes of 25 µL using 1 × HF buffer, 200 µM of each dNTP, 0.5 µM of each primer, 0.5 U of enzyme, and between 20 and 50 ng of either genomic or cDNA as a template. The PCR reaction cycles consisted of an initial denaturation step (30 s at 98 °C), 35 cycles of 10 s at 98 °C, 20 s at 60 °C, and from 20 s for the shortest amplicon to 30 s for the longest amplicon at 72 °C, and a final extension step (5 min at 72 °C). PCR products were resolved on a 1.5% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide or SYBR Safe stain. Single bands corresponding to the expected sizes (~ 420 bp for CYP51 and ~ 360 bp for SGE1) were excised from the gel and purified using a silica column-based gel extraction kit (Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

In-vitro transcription and dsRNA generation

Purified PCR products (100–300 ng) served as templates for in vitro transcription using the T7 RiboMAX™ Express RNAi System (Promega). Each 20 µL reaction contained 1 × T7 transcription buffer, NTP mix, enzyme mix, and the DNA template. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, to maximize RNA yield.

Following transcription, DNA templates were digested with DNase, and treatment efficiency was verified by the absence of amplification in control PCR reactions for 15 min at room temperature. If sense and antisense RNA strands were generated separately, they were combined in equimolar amounts, heated to 70 °C for 10 min, and allowed to cool slowly to room temperature to facilitate annealing into double-stranded RNA (dsRNA).

Purification, sizing, and quality control

The synthesized dsRNA was purified either by ethanol precipitation (2.5 × volume of cold ethanol and 0.1 × volume of 3 M sodium acetate, incubated at − 20 °C for 1 h) or using a silica membrane–based RNA clean-up kit. Purified dsRNA was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried briefly, and eluted in nuclease-free water. Typical yields ranged from 1 to 3 µg of dsRNA per reaction. Integrity and size were verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel, which showed distinct bands at ~ 420 bp for CYP51 and ~ 360 bp for SGE1, without smearing. Purity was assessed spectrophotometrically, in addition, RNA integrity was further supported by the absence of smearing on agarose gel electrophoresis with acceptable A260/A280 ratios between 2.0 and 2.2. To retain quality, aliquots of dsRNA were kept at − 80 °C without repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

Controls and working concentrations

To provide a suitable negative control for investigating the biological effects of specific gene silencing via the introduction of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), a non-targeting dsRNA control was generated from a scrambled sequence with no homology to either Fusarium oxysporum or Nigella sativa (the two target organisms). This control sequence contained an approximate length of 400 bp. The foliar applications of dsRNA were made up using working stock solutions that were diluted to concentrations of 100–200 ng/µL based on literature reports and preliminary optimization experiments to provide sufficient quantities for effective gene silencing. dsRNA was then prepared for loading nanoparticles using chitosan according to the ratios outlined in the nanoparticle preparation protocol (Section 4, Ionic Gelation).

The plastic items and solutions we employed have been certified to be RNase-free prior to use, and the surfaces of the workbenches were cleaned with RNase contamination decontaminations prior to use. There were also negative controls (absence of template) included at both stages of the PCR and the transcription process to establish that there was no contamination (Table 1).

Preparation of chitosan nanoparticles (ionic gelation)

The nanoparticles were made using low molecular weight chitosan (degree of deacetylation ≥ 85%) via ionic gelation. Chitosan was dissolved in 0.1% Acetic acid solution with the final concentration of chitosan in solution being 0.1–0.2%. The solution was magnetically stirred at 600 rpm for 30 min until it was completely dissolved. The pH was then adjusted to 5.5 with 1 M NaOH. The sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) solution was prepared separately in deionized water at a concentration of 0.05–0.1%, and then the solution was sterilized by filtering through a 0.22 µm syringe filter. In order to make the dsRNA loaded nanoparticles, the required amount of dsRNA (based on the experimental design) was combined with the chitosan solution and gently stirred for 10 min at room temperature to allow for initial binding before TPP was added drop-wise to the chitosan/dsRNA mixture while continuing to stir (600 rpm) for ionic cross-linking to occur between the chitosan and TPP. The chitosan to TPP volume ratio (5:1) was maintained during the preparation of the nanoparticles by adding TPP to the chitosan solution to optimize the formation of nanoparticles. The mixture continued to be stirred for an additional 30 min while ensuring that there was homogenous dispersion of particles. Using dynamic light scattering (DLS), the resultant nanoparticle suspension was characterized by the size of the nanoparticle in addition to the polydispersity index (PDI) and the zeta potential to confirm the successful creation of nanoparticles. No detailed physicochemical characterization data from this study are presented because this research is beyond the scope of this investigation; detailed data will be presented in future work. The morphology of the nanoparticles was additionally investigated by either scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to confirm that the nanoparticles were spherical and uniform in size. The nanoparticles were stored in sterile, airtight containers at 4 °C until they were used for the plant treatments.

Disease assessment

Diseases were rated based on how much disease was present using a 0 to 5 scale—zero being no signs of disease and five being dead plants. Ratings were based on how much the plants were wilting, how much yellowing of leaves was present, and how much blackening of leaves was present, and all ratings were taken at intervals of 7 days up to a maximum of 28 days after infection. At the conclusion of the experiment, roots, shoots, fresh weight, and dry weight of all plants will be measured for the minimum amount of disease under each treatment. The combination of these parameters will provide one total assessment of plant health and plant growth performance for each treatment group.

Molecular analyses

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) for defense gene expression

Quantitative PCR was performed to assess the relative expression of defense-related genes (PR1-like, WRKY70-like, NPR1-like, and PDF1.2-like). The stability of EF1α as a reference gene under experimental conditions was selected based on its common use in plant gene expression studies. Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of frozen plant tissue using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a Nanodrop™ spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), ensuring A260/A280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1. RNA integrity was confirmed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA in a 20 µL reaction with the Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:10 with nuclease-free water and used as a template for qPCR. Amplifications were carried out in a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL of 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of diluted cDNA, and nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling profile was: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, ending with a melt curve analysis from 65 to 95 °C in 0.5 °C increments. Primer sequences, amplicon sizes, and annealing temperatures are provided in Table 1. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with normalization to EF1α. Each qPCR reaction was performed in three technical replicates. Ct values and amplification efficiencies were consistent across replicates, confirming the reliability of the qPCR data (Table 2).

Fungal biomass quantification

Samples of leaves and roots were obtained, total DNA from both were extracted and then the extracted [DNA] was quantified by quantitative PCR using primers specific to the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region of the Fungus Fusarium oxysporum. To determine a relative amount of biomass (associated with fungal DNA) normalised to a reference gene from the plant DNA was used.

Thymoquinone quantification

Seeds or dried leaves have been collected and ground to a fine powder, which is then extracted with methanol. After filtration through a 0.22 µm filter, HPLC was performed using a C18 column and a methanol–water (70:30) mobile phase system at 254 nm. The concentration of thymoquinone was calculated by comparing the results of the analysis to the standard calibration curve.

Statistical analysis

All results from the experiments, such as disease severity, fungal biomass, gene expression level associated with defense responses, and quantity of thymoquinone, were analyzed with SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data was assessed prior to the statistical analysis using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene’s test to confirm that the data complied with the assumption of data applicable for parametric statistical analysis. A two-way ANOVA was conducted with Treatment and Time as fixed factors to determine their main and interaction effects. Although a repeated measures ANOVA may provide more information about the effects, the current statistical analysis was done using independent measurements of the various time points. The Tukey HSD post hoc test was performed if significant differences were detected in the group means (P ≤ 0.05). Pairwise comparisons between treatment group means were made using this test. All results are reported as means ± SE from 3 independent biological replications of each treatment group with technical replications of each treatment included where applicable. Graphical representations of results were created using the GraphPad Prism version 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with graphical representations depicting error bars clearly and displaying statistical groupings. Future research will include confidence limits and other parameters to help with interpretation of the data. The calculation of effect size was excluded from this study; however, it will be computed for future analyses.

Ethical considerations

This research involved using plant materials only, as such, there are no human or animal participants involved within this research. All microbial handling and nanoparticle application experiments followed all institutional biosafety and environmental safety regulations. The Biological Safety Committee at College of Science of Baghdad University reviewed and approved all of the protocols involved in this study.

Results

An overall assessment of adaptable research was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of using a dual stranded RNA as a chitosan nanoparticle to induce gene silencing (SIGS) against Fusarium oxysporum for significance of enhancing the disease resistance of Nigella sativa by groupings of different markers of battle severity associated with disease severity (e.g., fungal biomass, markers indicative of molecular defence and significant amounts of thymoquinone). The data [results] presented both in table and/or graphical forms can be viewed/displayed to allow comparisons of test results through the various time periods during treatment.

Disease severity and growth indicators

Thermal imagery was taken with the ZR-2710A infrared camera at 1440 pitot static pressure (PPS). The ZR-2710A thermal camera has a bandwidth of 500–2120 nm (near-infrared) and a resolution of 640 × 480 pixels (480 × 640 image in the Nokia 9000), with sensitivity to ambient lighting. Each image of the ZR-2710A was acquired at a frame rate of 30 frames per second and a field of view (FOV) of 1920 pixels × 1080 pixels. The images were combined to produce a time series of thermal images. Visual assessment of disease severity revealed significant differences among treatments. Healthy plants (severity score = 0.0) showed no visible signs of disease and maintained full vigor and growth. The infected un-treated plants (infected + vehicle) shown in the photomicrographs had the highest severity score of 4.2 on a scale of (0–5), characterized by dramatic wilting of leaves, chlorosis, and stunted growth. Application of free-form, target-specific dsRNA via chitosan nanoparticles significantly reduced the severity score to 2.5, indicating moderate inhibition of the disease’s development. Plants treated with dsRNA encapsulated in chitosan nanoparticles (dsRNA@Chitosan) exhibited the greatest reduction in disease severity, with a score of 1.6, which was the lowest of all infected groups and represented a significant suppression of pathogen development. The root length, shoot length and fresh biomass of plants in the dsRNA@Chitosan group exhibited a trend consistent with that of disease severity. The root length of plants in the dsRNA@Chitosan group measured an average of 13.8 cm, with shoot lengths averaging 20.7 cm and fresh biomass averaging 4.8 g. Values for the untreated infected plants were 8.1 cm (root length), 12.3 cm (shoot length), and 2.1 g (fresh biomass). In the dsRNA@Chitosan treatment group the fresh biomass was similar to that of healthy plants (5.4 g) and more than twice that of untreated infected plants.

These findings provide evidence that the delivery system comprised of nanoparticles was able to not only reduce visible signs of Fusarium oxysporum infection but also provide protection against plants’ growth and vigor while under pathogen stress. As supplementary data in the forms of pictures of plant phenotype will be provided for submission to future studies to represent each treatment group. Statistics (p ≤ 0.05) confirmed that all the above-mentioned improvements have significant difference from the rest of the infected treatments with dsRNA@Chitosan having superior results compared to other infected treatments both in suppressing disease and recovering from it. Fungal biomass quantification (qPCR)

The quantitative PCR analysis of fungal DNA yielded accurate estimates of colonization levels of Fusarium oxysporum among the treated plants. Healthy plants (0.0-fold) had undetectable levels of fungal DNA confirming they were free from pathogens. Infected plants (Infected + Vehicle) and not treated had the highest accumulation of fungal biomass (1.0-fold) establishing pathogens completely within plant tissues. Treating infected plants with non-specific dsRNA (Infected + dsRNA Random) resulted in modest reduction (0.85-fold) in fungal biomass, compared to the infected control group (15% reduction). RNA interference through treating infected plants with non-specific dsRNA provided little impact on alleviating the proliferation of fungi. On the other hand, treating infected plants with target-specific free-form dsRNA (Infected + dsRNA Free) reduced fungal biomass to 0.45-fold, which equates to 55% reduction in growth, thus demonstrated effectiveness of sequence specific RNAi to hinder fungal infection processes. Furthermore, treating infected plants with dsRNA encapsulated within chitosan nanoparticles (Infected + dsRNA@Chitosan) led to the greatest reduction (0.30-fold) of fungal biomass or 70% reduction from the infected controls. This highlights the advantages of nanoparticle mediated delivery systems by protecting dsRNA molecules from enzymatic degradation as well as improving uptake and systemic movement of dsRNA; thus, leading to superior prolonged gene silencing. According to statistical tests (p < 0.05), the dsRNA@Chitosan treatment demonstrated significantly more effective suppression of pathogens than each of the other infected groups. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Although no fungal DNA was detected in healthy plants, this value (0.0) represents the limitation of detection for the assay used instead of indicating there was no fungi present (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Defense gene expression (RT-qPCR)

The RT-qPCR studies showed different levels of activity of the defense-related genes, as measured across all treatments. For baseline comparison, gene expression levels in healthy control plants were established at 1.0-fold for PR1-like, WRKY70-like, NPR1-like and PDF1.2-like defense-related genes. In contrast, untreated plants with Fusarium oxysporum infection (Infected + Vehicle) had consistently reduced levels of all four defense-related genes (PR1-like at 0.8-fold, WRKY70-like at 0.7-fold, NPR1-like at 0.75-fold, and PDF1.2-like at 0.65-fold), indicating that the pathogen is likely to have disrupted the salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid/ethylene (JA/ET) pathways. Application of non-specific dsRNA (Infected + dsRNA Random) had little effect in recovering normal levels of these four pilots, with PR1-like only increasing slightly to 1.1-fold, WRKY70-like to 1.0-fold, NPR1-like to 1.2-fold and PDF1.2-like remaining at 1.0-fold. Such small changes demonstrate that for reactivating effective defense responses through dsRNA use requires specific sequence complementarity between the complementary strand of dsRNA and the target defense-related genes. Targeted delivery of dsRNA (Infected + dsRNA Free) provided partial recovery of the gene activation response of the defense genes. As shown previously, dsRNA treatment resulted in approximately 1.8 × (PR1-like), 1.7 × (WRKY70-like), 1.9 × (NPR1-like), and 1.6 × (PDF1.2-like) increases in gene expression levels, which provided evidence that the use of targeted RNAi can partially overcome pathogen-induced suppression of immunity. The largest amounts of gene activation were observed in plants treated with dsRNA that had been encapsulated in the chitosan nanoparticles (Infected + dsRNA@Chitosan). In these cases, the gene expression levels increased to 2.5 × (PR1-like), 2.4 × (WRKY70-like), 2.6 × (NPR1-like), and 2.3 × (PDF1.2-like) greater than the control plants, which demonstrates the enhanced stability and delivery of nanoparticle-mediated dsRNA applications and allows for prolonged activation of both SA- and JA/ET-mediated defenses. The statistical analyses (p ≤ 0.05) revealed significant differences for all of the dsRNA@Chitosan treated samples compared to all the other infected treatments; therefore, it was concluded that the dsRNA@Chitosan treatment was the most effective method for restoring plant immune responses against F. oxysporum. All expression differences were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05); however, there were moderate fold changes in some of the genes. The Ct values and amplification efficiencies were consistent within samples across replicates, confirming the reliability of the gene expression results (Table 5; Fig. 3).

Thymoquinone content (HPLC)

Thymoquinone concentrations seen in Nigella sativa were characterized using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) profiling analysis and varying based on how they were treated as well as being highly susceptible to F. oxysporum infection. Amongst our healthy control plants, average thymoquinone levels were reported as being 8.50 ± 0.30 mg/g dry weight (DW), illustrating the highest levels of metabolic function from phenylpropanoid and quinone biosynthesis pathways under normal growth conditions; therefore, can be considered the physiological benchmarks for evaluating metabolic functional integrity under pathogen stress conditions. Following pathogen infection with no treatment provided (Infected + Vehicle), average thymoquinone levels for the infected plants were found to decrease due to pathogen effects to only 3.20 ± 0.20 mg/g DW, which is approximately 62% less than healthy control thymoquinone concentrations. The observed decrease in thymoquinone may result from pathogen-induced changes to overall plant metabolism, as well as from reallocation of stored resources within the plant to implement defense/salvage/reparative processes as opposed to the phytochemical synthesis of secondary metabolites. These results are consistent with earlier findings which have demonstrated that vascular wilt fungi are capable of suppressing and reallocating the precursors used in synthesis of terpenoid quinone to primary defensive metabolites. In the present experiment, the treatment group using non-specific dsRNA (Infected + dsRNA Random) showed an insignificant increase in thymoquinone (3.80 mg thymoquinone/g DW) showing that RNAi did not adequately inhibit pathogenic interference with secondary metabolism when there was no specific sequence complementarity to the target fungal genes. The treatment utilizing free-form, target-specific dsRNA (Infected + dsRNA Free) produced a marked increase in thymoquinone (6.50 mg/g DW) relative to the infected control; this represents a 103% increase in thymoquinone as compared to the infected control, indicating partial re-establishment of metabolic inhibition by the pathogen due to direct silencing of fungal genes involved in the colonization of host tissues and rerouting resources. The maximum amount of thymoquinone was found in plants subjected to the encapsulation of dsRNA in chitosan nanoparticles (Infected + dsRNA@Chitosan) at 8.00 ± 0.30 mg/g DW, which is statistically equivalent (p > 0.05) to the healthy control. The ability to produce thymoquinone at such levels represents near-100% restoration of functional capacity due to (i) protection against degradation of dsRNA in the environment, (ii) improved transport and translocation of dsRNA through plant tissues, and (iii) long-lasting silencing of the target fungal gene. All three of these factors combined led to increased preservation of the biosynthetic potential associated with secondary metabolic pathways, thus preserving the medicinal quality of the Nigella sativa seed despite exposure to severe pathogen attack. The results of this study provide evidence of the dsRNA@Chitosan method being an effective way to suppress disease while also retaining the beneficial phytochemicals that contribute to the pharmacological activity of plants. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3), and HPLC measurements were performed from standard calibration curves with a high degree of linearity, which indicates that their results could be accurately quantified (Table 6; Fig. 4).

Comparative analysis of treatments

Each of the four treatments yielded unique performance profiles for each of the variables measured: disease severity, fungal biomass (qPCR), PR1-like expression (RT-qPCR), and thymoquinone content (HPLC). The Healthy Control had the best overall profile by far (severity of 0.0, biomass of 0.0, PR1 of 1.0, and thymoquinone content of 8.5 mg/g DW), which is the physiological ceiling for comparison. Conversely, the Infected + Vehicle treatment demonstrated the least favorable profile (severity of 4.2, biomass of 1.0, PR1 of 0.8, and thymoquinone of 3.2 mg/g DW), which confirms that Fusarium oxysporum significantly inhibits defense activation and disrupts secondary metabolism in host plants. The non-specific dsRNA (Random) treatments had only modest advantages compared to the infected control (severity of 4.0 vs. 4.2, biomass of 0.85 vs. 1.0, PR1 of 1.1 vs. 0.8, and thymoquinone of 3.8 vs. 3.2 mg/g DW). There was a slight increase in PR1-like expression; however, it did not result in the same severity of disease or in metabolically recovering from disease. The dsRNA produced from the same or different target showed significant improvement in each of 4 dimensions: a 2.5-point decrease in severity, a 0.45-fold decrease in biomass, a 1.8–2.5-fold increase in PR1 expression, and an 8.0 g of thymoquinone/100 g DW. As highlighted by the pairwise contrasts against the infected control, the following significant benefits were observed: a 40% decrease in symptoms, approximately a 55% reduction of fungal biomass, over 2 times higher PR1 expression, and a doubling of thymoquinone content. These consistent improvements in each of the dimensions of severity provided evidence that dsRNA produced by measuring target-specific sequence complementary (homologous) improved the ability to relieve suppression caused by the pathogen along both immune-medated and metabolically-mediated axes. The most substantial and even gains were produced by the dsRNA@chitosan treatment, with a severity rating of 1.6, 0.30-fold decrease in biomass, 2.5-fold increases in PR1, and 8 g thymoquinone/100 g DW. Compared with the infected control, dsRNA-chitosan mediated plant protection resulted in a near-62% decrease in disease severity; 70% decrease in disease biomass; > threefold increase in PR1-like expression; and, near-complete restoration of thymoquinone concentrations. The use of nanoparticles to enhance the efficacy of the delivery of free-form dsRNA compared with the use of free-form dsRNA also resulted in a lower disease load (1.6 vs. 2.5) and increased PR1 protein levels (2.5 vs. 1.8). These data demonstrate that improved dsRNA stability and uptake enhanced both immune reactivation and preservation of metabolism. Results of a one-way ANOVA for treatment comparisons on all four parameters were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05). Tukey post hoc analyses indicated that dsRNA@Chitosan treatment differed statistically from the infected control for all four parameters evaluated. The combined trends from all grouping showed that the dsRNA @ Chitosan had the greatest effectiveness for treatment, followed by target-specific free dsRNA and then the Random group. The present study did not assess the magnitude of the effect size; however, we will include this in our future analysis (Table 7).

Discussion

The application of double-stranded RNA (RNA), utilizing chitosan nanoparticles as a delivery mechanism provided efficacy and stability to spray-induced gene silencing (SIGS) for controlling Fusarium oxysporum in Nigella sativa. The latest advancements of technology used to protect plants have shown that nanocarriers can provide protection to RNA from environmental degradation23,24. Enable adherence to leaf surfaces and allow for uptake into plant tissues7,8,25–27. This work demonstrated that compared with controls, the application of dsRNA@Chitosan using SIGS provides a significant reduction in both fungal biomass and disease severity index. These findings are consistent with those of10,12,28. Therefore, it appears that using nanocarrier-mediated delivery of dsRNA is more effective than utilizing naked dsRNA29–31. At providing long-term effectiveness in reducing symptomatic disease. Thus, the use of nanoparticles for the delivery of airborne spores should be viewed not only as a laboratory study but also as a critical component of the development of effective field-based applications of dsRNA32,33. Despite the improvements in disease control achieved in this study, there was no experimental confirmation of dsRNA transfer and silencing of target genes in plants. Direct confirmation of dsRNA uptake and target gene silencing in the fungus was not performed and remains a limitation of the current study34,35. After applying dsRNA@Chitosan in plants, PR1-like defense genes show significant increases36. Suggesting that this method can lead to enhanced plant defense responses through possible involvement of SAR pathways.

Researchers have shown that the use of nanoparticles with double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to treat pathogen-infected plants can result in a wide range of immune responses11,37. The ability to both reduces disease severity disease severity and increase natural immunity is a significant step forward in developing ecologically friendly practices in agriculture. In pathogen-infected untreated plants, there was a large decrease in thymoquinone production, indicating that the plant redirected its resources for metabolic activity into the production of defense compounds, at the expense of vital therapeutic metabolites that are necessary to sustain a plant’s health. Chitosan-dsRNA treated plants retained thymoquinone concentrations that were comparable to those of healthy, uninfected plants. According to the research results, the approach offers two benefits. Slowing the disease progression and protecting the plant’s original phytochemical profile at the same time are two benefits of this method. Studies with medicinal plants, including Curcuma longa, have shown similar results, as evidenced by comparable reports of both successful pathogen management and stable levels of bioactive molecules over time22. Similar phytochemical investigations have emphasized the importance of preserving bioactive compounds when evaluating the antimicrobial and therapeutic potential of medicinal plant systems2. Across different measures—including disease severity, fungal biomass, defense gene activity, and phytochemical composition—evaluations consistently found dsRNA@Chitosan to be the most effective treatment. There are some limitations to the current study. In addition, the absence of a chitosan-only treatment limits the ability to completely distinguish the independent elicitor and antifungal effects of chitosan from the dsRNA-mediated response. The lack of a chitosan-only control group limited the ability to clearly distinguish between the independent antimicrobial effects of chitosan and the gene-silencing effects associated with dsRNA application to chitosan, which is a limitation to this study. Consequently, these results include evidence in support of the efficacy of utilizing combinations of SIGS (short interfering RNA) and nanocarrier delivery systems38,39 to provide an environmentally safe and sustainable means of managing plant disease40–42. That does not involve the use of genetically engineered organisms. This approach can be very valuable in terms of growing plants used for medicine, because the methods used to grow these kinds of plants, as well as how they are made up chemically, will affect how effective they are and their dollar value. The association of disease severity, amount of fungus present, how much gene expression occurs, and amount of metabolites present does not mean that any of these are directly related to each other. More research is needed to confirm that these types of interactions are true in specific instances43. In order to take the technology from controlled research to large-scale commercial agriculture there are many significant obstacles that need to be addressed. Designing large-scale nanoparticle production, performing extensive environmental risk assessments, and complying with tight regulatory standards are limitations that need to be addressed by researchers44,45. Additional limitations include potential degradation of dsRNA by environmental conditions, financial constraints, and regulations concerning the use of RNA-based technology. Quilez-Molina et al.7,8 and Dalakouras et al.18 underscore the need for large-scale field trials under a variety of climatic conditions as well as long-term ecological impact studies before progressing to commercial use. Clear and open communication concerning the safety and efficacy of RNA-based plant protection methods is equally important for establishing farmer and public confidence and hence permitting the acceptance of these products.

Conclusions

Based on our findings, applying spray induced gene silencing (SIGS) using double stranded RNA (dsRNA) encapsulated within chitosan nanoparticles (dsRNA@Chitosan) provides a long-lasting effective tool for controlling Fusarium oxysporum infestations on Nigella sativa plants. The treatment was shown to effectively mitigate disease severity levels, decrease fungal biomass, and increase key defense related gene expression. Furthermore, this treatment method also did not detract from the thymoquinone content of the plants, a major medicinal metabolite in this plant species. As evidenced by our findings, the combination of nanotechnology and RNA interference (RNAi) provides two means of protecting plants against pathogenic fungi; through inhibiting or slowing down pathogen growth, and stimulating the plant’s own immune system response to pathogen attack. The combination of these two effects will ultimately serve to protect the plants’ medicinal characteristics for longer periods of time. In addition to the immediate benefits of this technology, the work demonstrates a greater understanding of how plants and pathogens interact with one another and as such provides a useful, environmentally friendly, non-GMO method to protect medicinal crops.

Recommendations and future perspectives

More comprehensive studies must be conducted, including field trials in different agroclimatic regions to determine if dsRNA and/or Chitosan treatments are effective under these conditions. Additionally, further work must be done to optimize the nanoparticle manufacturing/purification processes to ensure they are economical, stable, and can be produced on a larger scale. Finally, future research needs to evaluate the potential long-term impacts of repeated application of dsRNA-nanoparticle formulations on soil microorganisms, non-target organisms, and the persistence of environmental RNA. Moreover, collaboration with regulatory authorities and industry stakeholders will be necessary to establish guidelines and promote the adoption of RNA-based nanotechnology in plant protection. Integrating SIGS with other eco-friendly agricultural strategies, such as biological control agents, crop rotation, and resistant cultivars, may further enhance sustainable disease management systems. Future experiments should include a chitosan-only control treatment to better isolate its independent contribution to disease suppression and plant defense activation. Future studies should also investigate the expression of key genes involved in the thymoquinone biosynthetic pathway to better understand the molecular mechanisms underlying metabolite preservation.
