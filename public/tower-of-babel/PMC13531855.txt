Ballistic fragment embolism to superior mesenteric artery after the shrapnel injury to the chest: a case report of casualty from Russia-Ukraine war
Authors: Igor Lurin, Eduard Khoroshun, Serhii Panasenko, Volodymyr Nehoduiko, Serhii Shypilov, Maksym Gorobeiko, Andrii Maliuga, Andrii Dinets
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Russia–Ukraine war has produced widespread lethal ballistic trauma with unusual injury patterns, including rare cases of projectile migration and arterial embolism. Prompt differential management near frontlines is crucial to improve outcomes and reduce evacuation risk. The aim of the study was to present clinical features, diagnostic workup, and management of a combat patient with a penetrating chest wound, cardiac involvement, and subsequent migration of a ballistic fragment into the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), and to contextualize this case within existing literature on ballistic emboli.Case presentationA 41-year-old serviceman sustained a chest injury from FPV-drone munitions. Initial delayed evacuation and staged imaging (X-ray, FAST, whole-body CT) identified a ballistic channel from chest to abdomen and a metallic intraluminal fragment in the SMA. Clinical decision-making weighed endovascular versus open approaches amid tactical constraints and patient consent dynamics. Contrast-enhanced CT suggested transcardiac migration of the fragment into the SMA. With absent critical mesenteric ischemia and delayed consent, laparotomy on day 14 revealed an intraluminal metallic fragment that was removed via arteriotomy with primary repair. The patient recovered and was discharged after 21 days.ConclusionsThe arterial ballistic emboli must be suspected in patients with isolated chest- or thoracoabdominal injuries. The whole-body contrast-enhanced CT scan is an important diagnostic tool for patients with gunshot injuries. In the absence of evidence of bowel necrosis, surgery can be postponed in patients with SMA emboli. An important role in visualization is played by contrast-enhanced MSCT. We recommend removing projectile fragments from the major blood vessels.Keywords: Gunshot injury, Ballistic injury, Heart injury, Bullet migration, Injury to superior mesenteric artery

Introduction

The Russia-Ukraine war is associated with a rapid development of lethal weapons, resulting in severe trauma in those who survived in both the hybrid period of 2014–2022, as well as in the full-scale invasion period since February 2022 [1–4]. Previously published studies showed various kinds of severe injuries to extremities, thoracoabdominal area, pelvis, as well as vascular injuries, etc [4]. Out of all kinds of ballistic injuries, the bullet migration and arterial embolism are rare [5–7]. The studies suggest a differential approach to ballistic injuries, given cases with unusual presentations that require rapid decisions in complex tactical situations and limited medical resources. Although, Ukrainian war surgeons follow the common guidelines for the combat trauma managements such as damage control resuscitation and damage control surgery, 3D printing etc., it was suggested to act appropriately in cases of rapid tactical changes, to provide the best management for the severe ballistic trauma from various kinds of weapons, which is converted into the application of the modern medical and surgical tools nearby (20–40 km) from frontline (e.g. CT scan, hemodialysis, laparoscopy and thoracoscopy etc.) [8]. This approach is associated with better treatment outcomes, as well as decreasing the risk for the patients and evacuation medical teams during the transportation from the attacks for FPV-drones to sanitary transport by russian army. The injury patterns are also unexpected in some cases, with various clinical signs or unusual complications due to the ballistic properties of the wounded shelling, as shown in clinical and experimental studies. Previously published reports demonstrate an experience of unusual presentations of the wounded projectiles in anatomical locations different from inlet holes, as well as artery embolism by wounded shell [9–12]. Such a hidden trauma is a high risk for the patient and a challenge for the surgeon because of the unexpected course of the projectile. However, one should be aware that the absence of the wound canal, despite anamnestic and clinical signs of its presence, might indicate its migration, which is usually limited to the borders of the cavity, with the length and diameter of the natural or pathological pathway of the wounded projectile. In cases of ballistic injury, the projectile might be expected in any part, as suggested by our clinical experience from various Roles of deployed field hospitals, as well as basic studies of wound ballistics [13–15]. It is worth noting that migration of shrapnel or fragments of wounded projectiles is rare. It might also be a different migration pathway, either in one direction to the parts of the respiratory tract, gastrointestinal tract or to the vascular bed (major veins and arteries, heart), genitourinary tract, in cavities (pleural, abdominal, pericardium, joints), in soft tissues in case of suppuration, when the source is a foreign body that moves with the flow of pus. It might also be migrated in several directions. The migration of the ballistic projectiles to the mesentery arteries might also be considered in gunshot trauma in civil or combat settings. One may expect signs of acute mesenteric ischemia due to decreased intestinal perfusion in cases of mesenteric occlusion from a projectile; however, little is known about the clinical course of mesenteric occlusion caused by a migrated bullet fragment or shrapnel. The analyses of the published series suggest the migration of the ballistic fragment to the mesentery as a rare event in both civil and war surgery.

The aim of this study was to demonstrate the clinical features of diagnosis and management of isolated penetrating ballistic wounds to the chest with injury to the heart, damage, and migration of a ballistic fragment into the superior mesenteric artery in combat patients injured during the invasion period of the Russo-Ukrainian War.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old male serviceman of the Armed Forces of Ukraine received a ballistic injury to the chest in the ballfield area in East Ukraine due to munition dropped from the russian FPV drone. The patient applied first aid to himself and received first aid from his teammates. Because of the complicated tactical situation, the patient was admitted to the medical stabilization point only 5 days after the injury. At the stabilization point, the patient received antibiotics, rabies immunoglobulin, and wound dressing. Two hours after admission to the medical stabilization point (5th day after the injury), the patient was evacuated to Forward Surgical Team facilities and received a chest and abdominal X-ray, ultrasonography with FAST protocol. The Forward Surgical Team detected a 4th right rib fracture, pulmonitis of the upper part of the right lung, and right-sided pneumothorax. A thoracocentesis was performed to the right, followed by pleural drainage by Bulaü. It is worth mentioning that not all cardiac injuries are accompanied by hemopericardium. In this case, heart surgery was not indicated. The metal fragment migrated along the arterial channel. The hemopneumothorax suggests performing a drainage of the pleural cavity. The total amount of blood received was 300 ml by drainage. At the time of arrival at the Role 3 Hospital, there was no air or blood discharge through the drainage tube. Given the patient’s injuries and possible hidden wounds, the patient was evacuated to a higher level of medical care at a Role 3 deployed field hospital in Kharkiv (on the 5th day after the injury). Upon admission to Role 3 hospital, the patient underwent a physical examination, which revealed a small inlet hole measuring 5 × 5 mm with an irregular border and no signs of bleeding at the right anterior middle clavicular line. Also, the patient underwent blood and urine testing, a coagulation test, and ultrasonography. The patient was hemodynamically stable. An esophagogastroduodenoscopy showed no pathological changes in microcirculation in the mucous membranes. According to the abdominal ultrasound, there was no evidence of free air or free fluid. There were no clinical data indicating necrosis of the small intestinal wall. A multispiral computed tomography (CT) scan with a slice thickness of 0.5 mm was performed using previously described equipment and protocols on 6th day after the injury [16]. The whole body CT scan revealed a ballistic canal from the chest to the abdomen as well as the presence of a metal density foreign body in the abdominal cavity (Fig. 1). The decision was made to perform a contrast-enhanced CT scan, and its analyses to suggest the path of the shrapnel from the soft tissues in the right part of the chest through the 4th right rib in direction to the right heart ventricle and ventricular septum, followed by migration to the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) by blood flow (Fig. 2).

The patient refused the operation for the removal of the migrated ballistic fragment; however, his consent was received on the 14th day after the injury. The contrast-enhanced CT scan did not show any specific changes prior to surgery. The endovascular approach was not used because no large emboli were identified in the SMA. At laparotomy, the SMA was identified, and revision showed the presence of the metal fragment inside the artery and no additional injuries to abdominal organs. The arteriotomy was performed, and a metal ballistic fragment was removed (Fig. 3), followed by artery suturing. According to the analyses of the patient injury patterns and the results of the CT scan, there is no evidence of calcified pancreatitis. A metal fragment of a foreign body was removed during operative treatment, and tracing the cause-and-effect relationship indicates that the foreign body migrated through the arterial channel. The patient was discharged in 7 days after the operation. The length of a hospital stay was 21 days. The 6-month follow-up showed full recovery for the patient, who continued his service in the Armed Forces of Ukraine after rehabilitation. The medical data were not available for the follow-up period.

Discussion

In this case report, we showed an unusual case of management of combat patients after the ballistic injury to the chest with a projectile path to the heart, followed by its migration to the SMA. This hypothesis is based on the presence of a foreign body of gunshot origin in the lumen of the SMA in the presence of a chest wound with heart damage in the absence of a hemopericardium, which is confirmed by a causal relationship and the absence of other injuries that could lead to the migration of a foreign body. We also hypothesize that the absence of clinical manifestations of mesenteric ischemia in the setting of a foreign body in the SMA lumen may be explained by collateral blood flow. Endovascular operation was not considered due to the absence of endovascular facilities and limited experience with such interventions, which is a limitation on the one hand. On the other hand, it is a challenge to establish an endovascular surgery under the consideration of full-scale war and frequent artillery and missile strikes on medical facilities by the russian army.

This case report is another piece of evidence for the various ballistic injury trajectories that surgeons must be aware of in both war and civilian settings. The literature analyses suggest that most reports of “foreign bodies” in the heart describe various objects (e.g., needles, catheters, their fragments, or retained bullets) that embolize to the right heart/pulmonary circulation. It is worth mentioning that true migration of an inanimate object from the heart into the systemic mesenteric arteries requires a left-heart or aortic source (e.g., left atrial/ventricular mass, left-sided device, or iatrogenic entry) and appears to be far less commonly reported than tumor/thrombus/vegetation emboli [10, 17–19].

To our opinion, the possible mechanism of the migration of the projectile in our case might be explained by the entry of a foreign body with arterial blood flow into the SMA due to the anatomy the SMA course from the aorta at an appropriate angle, the larger diameter of the artery than the foreign body, and the presence of vortices during the movement of the foreign body, which eventually directed the foreign body into the lumen of the SMA. In contrast, embolic thrombosis results from the migration of emboli originating proximally and is associated with segmental circulatory disorders of the affected artery. The presence of a foreign body in the SMA lumen is an indication for emergency laparotomy. However, in this case, the metal projectile in SMA was not associated with critical ischemia due to good collateral blood flow; an acute mesenteric thrombosis did not occur, which is rare, as SMA embolism is the most common cause of acute mesenteric ischemia. Considering the absence of mesenteric thrombosis and due to the patient’s lack of consent for the operation, a laparotomy was performed 14 days after the injury. Still, it is important to take into account the high risk of mesenteric thrombosis and plan laparotomy or endovascular surgery [20].

The CT scan is an important tool to detect the metal fragment after the gunshot injuries; it is not always informative [5]. Lombard et al. presented a case of bullet migration to the popliteal artery in a patient with thoracoabdominal gunshot injury, who also underwent exploratory laparotomy, but the bullet embolization was diagnosed only after the development of critical ischemia of the right lower limb [5]. Although the arterial embolism by the bullet is rare, the migration of the wounded shrapnel might be seen in various anatomic locations, including the proximal superficial femoral artery, as reported by Pereira et al. and Tanenbaum et al. [6, 7].

The vascular ballistic injuries were and remain a clinical challenge due to the high risk of mortality and frequent unexpected course [21, 22]. The CT scan is a useful tool for detecting hidden wounded projectiles [6]. A whole-body CT scan is recommended for all combat and civilian patients to enable earlier, better diagnosis of injuries across all Roles of military medical provision, which is sometimes unavailable due to limited medical resources and is a matter of strategic planning even under wartime conditions [23]. Another possible direction for fast and accurate diagnosis might be the application of artificial intelligence, which is currently considered a promising and additional tool in both civil and military medicine [24, 25].

Nicol AJ and Navsaria PH suggested considering the J-wave as an ECG sign for evaluating cardiac injury [26]. In our case, multiple ECGs were performed, and hemodynamic stability was confirmed. Considering the absence of hemopericardium, we did not consider the surgical intervention on the heart. The possible damage to the pulmonary vessels was considered, but it was not confirmed by CT scan data, showing the absence of extravasation, which is seen in case of vessel wall defects.

In contrast to other studies from war surgery research or clinical guidelines, Ukrainian war surgeons proved the possibility to apply minimally invasive surgery (i.e., video-assisted thoracotomy (VATS) and laparoscopy) at Role 2 and higher medical echelons in combat settings. However, that kind of clinical approach must be considered in a patient-centered approach, and in this case, the advantage was to perform a laparotomy. We also frequently apply magnetic surgical tools to remove the bullets or other ballistic fragments with ferromagnetic properties; in this case, an arteriotomy was sufficient for the extraction.

Conclusions

The arterial ballistic emboli must be suspected in patients with isolated chest- or thoracoabdominal injuries. The whole-body contrast-enhanced CT scan is an important diagnostic tool for patients with gunshot injuries. In the absence of evidence of bowel necrosis, surgery can be postponed in patients with SMA emboli. An important role in visualization is played by contrast-enhanced MSCT. We recommend removing projectile fragments from the major blood vessels.
