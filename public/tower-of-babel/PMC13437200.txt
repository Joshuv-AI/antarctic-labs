Response to “Automated Breast Ultrasound in Dense-Breast Screening: Beyond Additional Cancer Detection”
Authors: Su Min Ha, Inyoung Youn, Jung Min Chang
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Bolaños Bermúdez and colleagues for their interest in our work [1]. Dr. Bolaños Bermúdez and colleagues provided thoughtful comments and emphasized the broader role of automated breast ultrasound (ABUS) as a supplemental tool in dense breast screening [2]. As the authors noted, our study was designed not simply to compare the cancer detection performance of ABUS [3], but rather to investigate whether ABUS could be incorporated into a more efficient screening workflow when combined with digital mammography and ABUS artificial intelligence–computer-aided diagnosis (AI-CAD).

This concept aligns with a growing trend in breast imaging, where AI is increasingly used to triage examinations. In mammography, AI systems are being applied to identify cases that warrant radiologist review while allowing radiologists to omit the review of examinations assessed as negative by AI [4]. In addition, as the authors noted, ABUS may offer greater standardization and reproducibility than handheld breast US [5]. In this context, AI-CAD may also function as an effective second reader when ABUS is interpreted by a single radiologist or a non-expert physician, potentially improving reliability.

More recently, breast US has increasingly been used in more selected populations, particularly among women with dense breasts and higher-risk profiles [6]. However, even in these populations, the widespread use of breast ultrasound remains constrained by limited resources. In many countries, the increasing demand for breast cancer screening continues to exceed the availability of radiologists with dedicated expertise in breast imaging. In this context, having radiologists interpret all ABUS examinations is becoming increasingly difficult to sustain, particularly because most screening examinations are normal or benign. Accordingly, the integration of ABUS with AI-CAD may help reduce barriers to the broader implementation of ABUS as a supplemental screening tool, thereby improving accessibility and enabling more women to benefit from supplemental imaging.

Thus, we simulated an AI-CAD-guided selective review strategy in which women with negative digital mammography findings and negative AI-guided ABUS findings would not undergo additional human review. This approach allows radiologists to focus on examinations requiring further assessment on mammography or those with AI-flagged abnormalities on ABUS, potentially improving workflow efficiency. However, for such a selective reading strategy to be clinically acceptable, it is important to maintain high sensitivity of the AI system while minimizing false-positive findings and unnecessary recalls.

Although our results suggested improved specificity and reduced abnormal interpretation rates, the number of cancers in our study was limited. Therefore, further studies with larger screening populations are needed. Future investigations comparing a “radiologist-reads-all-ABUS” approach with an “AI-CAD-guided selective ABUS review” approach would be valuable. In addition, mammography in our study was interpreted without AI assistance. It would be of interest to investigate a more integrated and fully automated screening pathway in which both mammography and ABUS are supported by AI systems.

We hope that our study represents an initial step toward the development of AI-supported ABUS screening pathways that are clinically effective and operationally feasible, particularly in resource-limited settings. This may allow more women to benefit from supplemental imaging. We thank the authors for their insightful comments and for the opportunity to further elaborate on these important considerations.
