Mechanistic insights into modulation of productive substrate accessibility for efficient PET depolymerization
Authors: Dongwoo Ki, Jiyoung Park, Hwaseok Hong, Hogyun Seo, Beomsu Kim, Hyeonwoo Hwang, Kyung-Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) hydrolases have been extensively studied for their potential applications in plastic degradation. However, the structural and mechanistic factors that limit their catalytic efficiency are not yet fully understood. Here, we identify the protruding, surface-exposed C-terminal loop (SEC-loop) in Cryptosporangium aurantiacum PETase (CaPETase) that negatively impacts enzymatic activity by restricting productive access of enzyme to PET. Loop replacement experiments show the non-protruding SEC-loop enhances PET depolymerization rates, despite being ~25 Å from the active site. Kinetic and adsorption studies indicate the non-protruding SEC-loop promotes productive PET access to the enzyme without affecting binding affinity. To further assess the broader applicability of this strategy across diverse PETases, SEC-loop replaced variants of representative PETases are characterized through kinetic and adsorption analyses. We show an engineering strategy focused on modulating enzyme accessibility rather than simply modifying the catalytic site, in rational enzyme design aimed at improving PET degradation efficiency.

Introduction

Biocatalysts have been investigated for the depolymerization of plastics, particularly polyethylene terephthalate (PET)1–4, and are being developed for industrial recycling applications. Efficient PET hydrolases, such as Ideonella sakaiensis PETase (IsPETase)2, leaf compost cutinase (LCC)5, metagenome-derived thermophilic hydrolase (PHL7/PES-H1)6, bacterium HR24 PETase (BhrPETase)7, Cryptosporangium aurantiacum PETase (CaPETase)8, Micromonospora pattaloongensis and Kutzneria buriramensis PETases (Mipa-P and Kubu-P)4, belong to the polyesterase-lipase-cutinase family. They have been discovered through extensive database searches4,8,9 as well as microbial isolation and metagenomic analysis of environmental samples2,5,6. Although the natural enzymes acting on PET were identified, their wild-type forms showed limited PET-degrading performance insufficient for industrial applications4, necessitating enzyme engineering to enhance their activity and stability. Thus, numerous artificial enzymes have been engineered from the natural enzyme sequences using various engineering technologies3,4,8,10–22.

To guide enzyme engineering, crystallographic evidence has been reported regarding the atomic structures of the PETases and their binding interactions with the terephthalic acid moiety23–26. Mutational studies and structural analyses have further shown the ester bond cleavage mechanism mediated by the catalytic triad and oxyanion hole10,23,27,28. Due to the difficulty in observing nanoscale behavior within polymer-enzyme systems, extensive in silico molecular dynamics (MD) and docking analyses10,27,29–37 have been employed to predict the conformations of the proteins or their interactions with single PET chains. For example, ref. 10. proposed the existence of four subsites in IsPETase for PET chain binding, based on the covalent docking calculation of 2-hydroxyethyl-(monohydroxyethyl terephthalate)4 and the enhanced activity of IsPETaseR280A; However, this single substrate-binding mode and the ethylene glycol trans/gauche conformation ratio of the docked molecule has been refuted by other researchers29. Additionally, MD and/or quantum and molecular mechanics studies31–36 have helped to calculate the activation energies and to understand the conformational preferences of PETases with Re-face and Si-face prochiral selectivity. Reference37 also attempted to elucidate the binding mechanism using a ternary enzyme-PET oligomer-trehalose glassy system and proposed two subsites in TfCut2 and a processive mechanism of the enzyme.

These computational approaches were based on single-chain PET models, whereas actual solid-state PET exists as multiple intertwined chains with restricted conformations at interfaces. Glass transition temperature of PET and differences in conformational flexibility of surface polymer chains significantly influence enzyme activity, which is manifested by dramatic increases in activity with elevated temperature4. Thus, studies on enzyme interactions with free single-chain PET are inevitably limited in understanding depolymerization mechanism, and strong evidence is needed for how enzymes access bulk PET. Recent studies have investigated accessibility changes through surface charge modification38,39 and bulk PET model simulations40, though the underlying mechanism remains ambiguous.

When investigating the effects of the relatively protruded CaPETaseM9 loop on enzyme activity in comparison to other PETases, we identified several engineering points on this loop corresponding to IsPETase R280A and other PETases, which contributed to activity variation. To enhance the properties of CaPETaseM9, which has a different structure from other PETases, detailed studies are needed to understand how the distant loop from the active site influences enzyme activity.

In this study, we provide the first experimental evidence for a non-catalytic site of PET hydrolase that impedes enzyme accesses to PET. A protruding C-terminal loop of CaPETase influences the depolymerization rate of PET, despite being located approximately 25 Å away from the catalytic site. The various loop-chimeric variants created in this study and their crystal structures demonstrated that the protrusion of the loop reduces the initial depolymerization rate, which is similar to the rationale proposed for the IsPETaseR280A variant10. Additional kinetic studies and adsorption experiments provided evidence that removal of the protruding region increases productive binding, thereby enhancing activity. This study will offer a reliable rationale for understanding the molecular mechanism of PET depolymerases.

Results

Effect of protruding loop alteration on the initial depolymerization rate

While comparing the structure of CaPETase with those of well-studied PETases, we observed that the surface-exposed C-terminal loop (SEC-loop) exhibits structural diversity across these enzymes, with variations in amino acid length (6–12) (Fig. 1a). Notably, the SEC-loop in CaPETase (C279–V290) is distinctly more protruded compared to those in other PETases (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, although the loop region is located approximately 25 Å away from the catalytic serine residue, the SEC-loop corresponds to the hotspot of mutation points3,4,8,10,13–15,38,41,42 that affect the activity in various PET-degrading enzymes (Fig. S1). For example, the R280 residue, located at the corresponding region, was reported to block the proposed subsite IIc in IsPETase10 (Fig. S2). Moreover, CaPETaseM9 contains a S291C variation on the region, forming a disulfide bond with R242C8. To investigate the influence of number of amino acids in the SEC-loop on PET-degrading activity, we replaced the loop region of CaPETaseM9 with polyalanine cassettes of varying lengths, ranging from 6 to 12 alanine residues (Fig. 1b). All polyalanine cassette replacements showed a slight decrease in Tm values compared to CaPETaseM9, which is believed to be due to the weakened interaction between the replaced polyalanine cassette and the protein core (Figs. 1c and S4). Unexpectedly, all variants showed similar or increased activities compared to CaPETaseM9, and there was a tendency for activity to increase as the polyalanine cassette length decreased (Fig. 1c). In particular, the 7Ala variant showed 1.76 times higher activity compared to CaPETaseM9 (Fig. 1c). Moreover, the 10Ala variant, which has the same SEC-loop length as CaPETaseM9, also showed enhanced activity compared to CaPETaseM9 (Fig. 1c). These results indicate that the amino acid length of the SEC-loop significantly affects the enzyme activity of CaPETaseM9 and suggest that the structural conformation of the region plays a crucial role in PET polymerization activity.

The diversity of the SEC-loop and its effect on initial PET depolymerization rate

To identify the diversity and distribution of SEC-loop in various PET hydrolases, the set of 2064 sequences utilized in the previous literature4, including CaPETase, were analyzed. The SEC-loop was defined in 1915 sequences that possess a conserved disulfide bond at the position corresponding to the Cys279-Cys291 in CaPETase (Fig. S5)43. These 1915 sequences contained SEC-loops composed of varying numbers of amino acids, ranging from 5 to 13 (Fig. 2a). The majority of SEC-loops, accounting for 77%, were composed of 7 or 8 amino acids, with a considerable number consisting of 9 or 10 amino acids (Fig. 2a). Interestingly, well-characterized PETases generally possess short SEC-loops, with those of LCC, Mipa-P, and Kubu-P consisting of 6 amino acids, and that of IsPETase consisting of 7 amino acids (Table S1). Notably, the amino acid compositions are significantly diverse even among SEC-loops composed of the same number of amino acids (Fig. S6). To evaluate the effects of length and amino acid composition of SEC-loop on PET depolymerization activity, we designed chimeric loop (CL) variants by replacing the SEC-loop of CaPETaseM9 with various SEC-loop sequences found in other enzymes. We developed a total of 10CL variants by excluding the rarely observed 5- and 11-unit SEC-loops and selecting two and three sequences from the 6- and 7-unit SEC-loops, respectively, considering the frequency observed in benchmark enzymes (Table S1). When thermal stability of the 10 CL variants was measured, 4 variants, such as 6CLb, 9CL, 10CL, and 12CL, showed higher Tm values compared to CaPETaseM9, while the other 6 variants showed lower Tm values (Figs. 2b and S7). To our surprise, regardless of loop length and amino acid compositions, the initial PET depolymerization rate was substantially enhanced in all 10 variants, with increases ranging from approximately 34 to 129% (Fig. 2b). Interestingly, the three variants with the same number of amino acids, such as 6CLa, 6CLb, and 6CLc, showed highly variable Tm values and initial PET depolymerization rate. Combined with the results from the polyalanine cassette replacements, these findings suggest that both the length and amino acid composition of the SEC-loop simultaneously influence its structural conformation, thereby affecting the thermal stability and PET depolymerization activity of enzyme.

Kinetics and adsorption properties of the loop-chimeric variants

Although the SEC-loop is approximately 25 Å away from the catalytic site, the CaPETaseM9 chimeric variants exhibited enhanced activity. We speculate that this increased activity is more likely due to improved enzyme adsorption onto the PET polymer rather than direct involvement of the loop in enzyme catalysis. Thus, we measured the enzyme adsorption of CaPETaseM9 on PET polymer using the bicinchoninic acid assay and Langmuir isotherm analysis44 and compared it with those of the top three variants showing high initial PET depolymerization rate: 6CLb, 9CL, and 12CL. However, contrary to expectations, the maximum adsorption capacities (Γmax) of the three variants were quite similar to that of CaPETaseM9, and the 9CL variant was even lower than CaPETaseM9 (Fig. 3a). We then perform detailed kinetic analysis using inverse Michaelis–Menten model45 to determine how enzyme concentration affects the initial PET depolymerization rate. As observed in enzyme adsorption analysis above, the invKm values of CaPETaseM9 and all three variants were similar to each other and did not show significant relationship with PET depolymerization activity (Fig. 3b and Table S2). However, the maximum velocities of the 6CLb, 9CL, and 12CL variants were much higher than CaPETaseM9, with 2.8-, 2.7-, and 2.3-fold increase, respectively (Fig. 3b). These results clearly indicate that these chimeric loop variants are kinetically faster than CaPETaseM9 and catalytic ability of the variants has increased. Alterations in residues typically enhance specific catalytic activity by affecting the structure of the catalytic site. However, structural analysis of the SEC-loop in both CaPETaseM9 and the chimeric loop variants revealed minimal influence through the intrinsic force network, suggesting that the observed increase in catalytic ability is not due to direct modifications at the catalytic site. The important point here is that the maximum adsorption capacities of the variants are similar to or even lower than that of CaPETaseM9 (Fig. 3a). Taken together, we suggest that other structural features, beyond simple substrate adsorption, likely influenced the catalytic ability of the chimeric loop variants.

Structural insight into activity reduction due to the SEC-loop protrusion

To identify the structural features responsible for the higher catalytic ability of the three chimeric loop variants compared to CaPETaseM9, we determined the crystal structures of the variants (Table S3). As expected, the structures of the SEC-loop regions in the variants adopted markedly different conformations compared to CaPETaseM9, whereas the structures of other regions remained largely similar (Figs. 4a and S8). By assuming a plane perpendicular to the direction in which the substrate approaches the catalytic site, we identified a common structural feature that may account for the higher activity of the variants compared to CaPETaseM9 (Fig. S9). In all three variants, we found that the SEC-loop had significantly less protrusion from the plane compared to CaPETaseM9 (Fig. 4a). The non-protruded SEC-loop structure of three chimeric variants showed many interacts with core structure (Figs. S10 and S11). The side chain of 6CLb N280 and D283 on SEC-loop formed salt bridge and hydrogen bonds with core residues (Fig. S10a). The 9CL variant also showed many hydrogen bonds in between N282, S285, D286, S287, and Q288 with internal residues (Fig. S10b). In particular, although the 12CL variant has a longer SEC-loop of 12 amino acids compared to CaPETaseM9, it forms a compact helical structure in the loop region, which fixed by weak bonds of G290, H283, and D286, resulting in less protrusion from the plane (Figs. 4a and S10c). The trajectories from 100-ns scale molecular dynamic simulations showed that the SEC-loop structures of these variants maintain a non-protruding state even in dynamic conformations (Fig. S12). Additionally, when comparing the backbone fluctuation of variants, there is not distinctive differences (Fig. S13). To further demonstrate that the protrusion of the SEC-loop affects PET depolymerization activity, we substituted the bulky residues Leu286 and Phe287, located at the outermost point of the protruding region in the SEC-loop of CaPETaseM9, with the smaller residue alanine. Interestingly, both the L286A and F287A variants exhibited enhanced activity, with 1.18- and 1.33-times higher activity, respectively, compared to CaPETaseM9 (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, when both residues were replaced with alanine, the L286A/F287A variant showed 1.62-times higher activity (Fig. 4b). These results provide strong evidence that the protruding conformation of the SEC-loop can reduce PET depolymerization activity in CaPETase.

Importance of productive access of enzyme to the substrate in PET depolymerization

As mentioned above, the 10CL variant possesses a less protruding SEC-loop and exhibits higher activity compared to CaPETaseM9, despite its SEC-loop (PGPRDGLFGE) having the same length as that of CaPETaseM9 (PGPSTGLFAP) (Fig. S14). Furthermore, the SEC-loop of 10CL has more fluctuation than that of CaPETaseM9 but, it does not extend over the plane (Fig. S15). This observation also supports that the protruding SEC-loop structure has a greater influence on enzyme activity rather than loop flexibility. The amino acid compositions of the SEC-loops in these two proteins differ significantly, and structural comparison revealed substantial differences in interactions between the SEC-loop and the protein core. In the 10CL variant, the Glu289 residue of the SEC-loop forms a hydrogen bond network with the Asn153 and Thr255 residues of the protein core, resulting in the Leu286 and Phe287 residues, which protrude in CaPETaseM9, to be oriented toward the protein core (Fig. S16). In contrast, in CaPETaseM9, proline is positioned at the Glu289 site, preventing the formation of a hydrogen bond network with the protein core and providing dihedral restraints (Fig. S17). Interestingly, the P289E mutation of CaPETaseM9 showed 1.53-times enhanced activity with a SEC-loop conformation similar to that of the 10CL variant, whereas the E289P mutation of the 10CL variant led to decreased to 0.68-times activity with the conformation of the SEC-loop returning to that of CaPETaseM9 (Figs. 5a–c and S18). To investigate whether the effects of the two SEC-loop substitutions extend to other enzymes, we substituted short (6-residues) SEC-loop of LCC and Kubu-P, with the CaPETaseM9 (M9CL) and 10CL SEC-loops, generating Kubu-PM9CL, Kubu-P10CL, LCCM9CL, and LCC10CL. As expected, the adsorption patterns of these loop-swapping variants to PET substrate showed no significant difference from the wild-type (Fig. 5d). However, it is noteworthy that the SEC-loop from CaPETaseM9, which adopts a protruding conformation, caused a sharp decrease in activity, whereas the 10CL variant SEC-loop, which adopts a non-protruding conformation, exhibited catalytic efficiency similar to the wild-type (Figs. 5e and S19 and Table S4). These results demonstrate that the effect of loop protrusion structure extends beyond the CaPETaseM9 template, emphasizing the importance of the SEC-loop region in PET-degrading enzymes.

The formation of an optimal catalytic site and the adsorption of the enzyme to the PET polymer have been known as crucial factors in determining the efficiency of PET depolymerization. The kinetic and structural observations in this study suggest that substrate accessibility for productive activity should be considered another crucial factor in the efficient depolymerization of PET. The surface of the PET polymer is believed to vary in shape and degree of protrusion depending on the region. Therefore, the enzyme needs to approach the PET surface from various directions for catalytic activity, and depending on the direction of approach, it may either form a productive access leading to activity or a non-productive access that prevents activity (Fig. 5f). The conformation of the SEC-loop appears to play a crucial role in determining the productive accessibility to the PET polymer for catalytic activity. The high PET depolymerization activity observed in the variants can be interpreted as a result of the increased productive access to the substrate, which is facilitated by the release of the protruding region of the SEC-loop. Finally, we propose that, in addition to optimizing a catalytic site and enhancing enzyme adsorption to the PET polymer, improving the productive accessibility of the enzyme also plays a crucial role in enhancing PET depolymerization, offering an approach in the enzyme engineering of PET-degrading enzymes (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

In this study, we identified that the non-catalytic structural region, the SEC-loop, significantly affects the activity of PETases, as confirmed through various experiments using multiple chimeric loop variants. Through adsorption and kinetic analyses, along with the structural analysis, we propose that the observed enhancement in catalytic activity is due to increased productive access. This molecular mechanism has been similarly proposed for interfacial enzymes such as cellulases46,47 and lipases48 however, it is of great importance that this mechanism also exists in PET-degrading enzymes, and that we were able to identify for the first time the specific structural region that significantly influences this process. In previous literature, it was suggested that the increased activity of the IsPETaseR280A variant was due to facilitated binding of continuous PET chains. Interestingly, the protruding R280 residue in IsPETase is located at a region corresponding to the SEC-loop; therefore, our findings imply that the enhanced activity of the IsPETaseR280A variant may have arisen through a mechanism similar to that of the chimeric loop variants with improved activity. This leads to the conclusion that subsite regions should be evaluated from the perspective of binding to bulk and multichain substrates, rather than relying solely on the single PET chain binding mode previously proposed for IsPETase.

The dramatic structural changes in the loop induced by partial sequence variations, or even by single amino acid substitutions, highlight a hidden challenge in accurately predicting dynamic loop conformations. The SEC-loops of the crystal structures used in this study adopted stable conformations even in the absence of crystal contacts or other external stabilizing factors. However, the loop conformations predicted by AlphaFold3 substantially differed from those of the experimentally determined crystal structures, despite relatively high pLDDT scores (Figs. S20 and S21). Therefore, in enzyme engineering that relies on the analysis of subtle structural differences, we suggest that experimentally derived structural information should be prioritized over blind reliance on predicted structures.

Understanding the molecular mechanism underlying productive substrate access crucial for the development of enzymes that act on specialized substrates, such as polymers. This appeared to be also relevant to the long-standing debate regarding thermophilic and mesophilic PET hydrolases. Although the flexibility of interfacial PET chains, and consequently enzyme accessibility, increases with temperature, enzymes have traditionally been categorized based on apparent activity without considering substrate dynamics. Thus, further theoretical advancements in the reaction kinetics of polymer-degrading enzymes, along with the development of experimental tools and methodological approaches for measuring enzyme adsorption, are required to achieve a more detailed understanding of interfacial enzymes. A refined adsorption model would enable the elucidation of how enzymes approach PET at the molecular level and could be utilized to design more effective enzymes for various polymer substrates with semi-crystalline suprastructures. In this regard, the suggestion from this study that the SEC-loop conformation plays a critical role in productive access to PET substrate is believed to offer a clue in understanding the mechanism of PET hydrolases and provides a foundation for rational design aimed at enhancing their functionality.

Methods

Plasmid construction and site-directed mutagenesis

Polyalanine variants of CaPETaseM9 were codon optimized for E. coli expression and synthesized without signal peptide sequences and NdeI and XhoI restriction sites were added to the 5’ and 3’ ends of the target gene sequences, respectively (Twist Bioscience). The synthesized sequences of polyalanine variants are provided in Supplementary Dataset 1. The CaPETase, LCC, Kubu-P, and all their variants were subcloned into the pET21b expression vector (NovoPro, #V011022) using NdeI and XhoI restriction enzymes (New England Biolabs, #R011L and #R0146L). Site-directed mutagenesis for other variants was performed by PCR with oligonucleotides and all variants were verified by Sanger sequencing (BIONICS, Republic of Korea). All oligonucleotide sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Dataset 2.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

The target genes, including CaPETase, Kubu-P, LCC and all their variants were cloned into plasmid and transformed into E. coli Rosetta gami-B competent cells. The cells were cultured in lysogeny broth (LB) medium containing 100 mg L−1 of Ampicillin at 37 °C until the optical density reached 0.6 at 600 nm. To induce protein overexpression, isopropyl-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to a final concentration of 500 μM and incubated for 18 h at 18 °C. Cultured cells were harvested using centrifugation at 4000 × g for 15 min and obtained cell pellet was resuspended in buffer A (40 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). Cell disruption was carried out using ultrasonication, and the cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 30 min. The cell-free crude supernatant containing enzymes were purified using Ni-NTA agarose column (Quiagen). After washing with buffer A containing 30 mM imidazole, the target enzymes were eluted using buffer A containing 300 mM imidazole. All enzyme purification experiments were performed at 4 °C. The purities of the enzymes were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and Coomassie blue staining. The purified enzymes quantified using a BioTekTM Epoch microplate spectrophotometer by measuring absorbance at 280 nm and Gen5TM microplate data analysis software. Concentration of the enzymes was performed using an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal filter (molecular weight cutoff value of 10,000, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA).

Melting temperature (Tm) measurements

Thermal stability of CaPETaseM9 and variants were evaluated by melting curve with a protein thermal dye (Applied Biosystems #4461146) using a StepOnePlus Real-time PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The reaction mixture contains 5 μg of proteins, 10 mM Na2HPO4-HCl (pH 7.0), and protein thermal shift dye in a final volume of 20 μl. Signal changes were monitored by increasing the temperature from 25 to 99 °C at a rate 0.1 °C·s−1. Melting temperatures were evaluated by the first derivative curve and all RFU curves are presented in Supplementary information (Figs. S4 and S7).

PET sample preparation

In this study, post-consumer transparent PET powder (PC-PETTransparent) was produced by crushing PET flakes purchased from a recycling company (Yoochang R&C, Republic of Korea). The PET flakes were washed with water and dried at a 160 °C for 6 h. Subsequently, dried PET flakes were extruded at 275 °C and the resulting PET filament was rapidly cooled by immersion in water. The PET filament was broken down into 3 mm lengths and micronized using a cryogenic Micro Ball Mill. The obtained PET powder was sieved through a 200 μm mesh for use in the experiments.

Initial velocity measurement in pH-stat bioreactor

A customized bioreactor (MARADO-PDA, BIOCNS Co. Ltd, Republic of Korea) was operated with bath circulator for heating and refrigeration (RW3-1025, JEIO TECH, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The pH variance during reaction was detected using a pH electrode (pH Sensor INPro3030/120, METTLER TOLEDO, Switzerland) and titrated by 0.3575 M NaOH (40%; extra pure grade, Duksan, Republic of Korea) solution using PID controller to maintain pH 8.0. For the reaction, 3.75 × g of PC-PETTransparent and CaPETase variants were added to a final concentration of 2.5 μM in 150 ml of 10 mM Na2HPO4-HCl (pH 8.0) in the reactor4. All the bioreactor operations were started at 55 °C for 1 h. NaOH consumption was measured using a micro-precision scale (EK-410i; AND, Seoul, Republic of Korea), and the PET depolymerization rate was calculated based on the number of hydrolyzed ester bonds, as reflected by the amount of NaOH consumed (in moles).

Adsorption capacity measurement

The adsorption capacity was determined with 200 μm sieved PC-PETTransparent concentration of 50 mg ml−1 and enzyme concentrations ranging from 0 to 35 μg ml−1 in 1 ml8. The PET powder and enzymes mixtures containing 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) were incubated in an incubator at 30 °C for 1 h using 1.5 ml MaxyClear Snaplock Microcentrifuge Tube (Axygen). After incubation, each vial was centrifugated at 13,500 rpm for 1 min to separate supernatant using temperature-controlled centrifuge at 30 °C. The enzyme concentration in supernatant was measured using a Micro BCATM Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific #23235). And supernatant 200 μl was mixed with 200 μl of freshly prepared BCA working solution. This mixture incubated at 30 °C for 1 h. Afterward, absorbance of each sample was measured at 562 nm using an immunoplate (SPL Life Sciences #32296, Maxi binding plate) with plate reader to quantify free enzyme concentration. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Kinetics analysis

To measure initial hydrolysis rate of CaPETaseM9 chimeric variants, inverse Michaelis–Menten approach was applied. For each reaction, 10% (w/v) of 200 μm sieved PC-PETTransparent was loaded with 1 ml of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0). And the purified 6CLb, 9CL, and 12CL were loaded to a final concentration in ranging from 0 to 370 μg ml−1 and the reaction mixtures were incubated at 30 °C for 3 h. Afterward, the supernatant of reaction mixtures was filtrated using Nylon syringe filter (0.22 μm) for eliminating enzyme and substrate. The main products terephthalate acid, mono(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate acid, and bis(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).

HPLC analysis

Reaction samples were analyzed using a CBM-20A system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) connected to a UV/Vis detector (SPD-20A) and a C18 column (Shimadzu Shim-pack GIST C18, 3 μm, 4.6 × 5 mm) protected by a guard cartridge (YMC ODS-A, 3 μm). Mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid) and B (100% methanol) were used with an isocratic flow of 33% concentration of Mobile phase B at a 1 ml min−1 and a column was incubated in oven at 40 °C. The products (TPA, MHET, and BHET) were detected at 260 nm and their peak areas were integrated. The amount of the products was calculated using standard curves of peak area.

Crystallization and structure determination of CaPETase chimeric variants

Purified 6CLb, 9CL, 10CL, 12CL, CaPETaseM9 + P289E, and 10CL + E289P were further purified by fast-protein liquid chromatography using a HiPrep 26/60 Sephacryl S-100

HR column (320 mL, GE Healthcare Life Sciences) equilibrated with the buffer A for crystallization. Eluted proteins were concentrated using same Amicon Ultra Centrifugal filter (molecular weight cutoff value of 10,000, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Crystallization of CaPETase variants was initially performed using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method at 20 °C with spares-matrix screening buffer PEG/Ion, Index (Hampton Research), Wizard I and II (Rigaku), and Wizard CRYO I and II (Rigaku). Each enzyme solution 1.0 μl (6CLb: 21.0 mg ml−1, 9CL: 23.8 mg ml−1, 10CL: 22.3 mg ml−1, 10CL + E289P: 22.1 mg ml−1, 12CL: 19.2 mg ml−1 and CaPETaseM9 + P289E: 13.3 mg ml−1) was mixed with 1.0 μl of the screening buffer and equilibrated with 50.0 μl of reservoir solution. The best-quality crystals of the 6CLb, 9CL, 10CL, 12CL, CaPETaseM9 + P289E, and 10CL + E289P appear in Tube 3 of Wizard Classic 2, Tube 43 of PEG/Ion ScreenTM, Tube 92 of IndexTM, Tube 9 of PEG/Ion 2 ScreenTM, Tube 39 of PEG/ Ion 2 ScreenTM, and Tube 9 of PEG/Ion 2 ScreenTM, respectively. The highest quality crystals were soaked in reservoir solution with 20% (v/v) glycerol for cryoprotection and fished out using loop. The crystals were flash-frozen in a nitrogen gas flow under 100 K and all data were collected at the 7 A beamline of the Pohang Accelerator (Pohang, Korea) using a ADSC Q270 CCD or Eiger2 S 9 M detector49. All diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using the HKL-200050 and XDS software51. The Molecular replacement of all data of variants were performed using the MOLREP of CCP4i software suite52. The variants models were built using the WinCoot program and structure refinement was conducted using REFMAC5 of CCP4i software suite51,53. All data statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. The final refined models of 6CLb, 9CL, 10CL, 10CL + E289P, 12CL, and CaPETaseM9 + P289E were deposited in the Protein Data Bank with PDB codes of 9UM3, 9UM4, 9UM5, 9UM6, 9UM7, and 9UM8, respectively.

MD simulations

The deposited structures of 7YM9, 7YME, 9UM3, 9UM4, 9UM5 and 9UM7 were used for CaPETaseWT, CaPETaseM9, 6CLb, 9CL, 10CL and 12CL residues and inner cavity water coordinates. All protein models were prepared with protonation states at neutral. The models were simulated in GROMACS 202454 using CHARMM36-jul202255 force field force field. The systems were prepared with spc216 parameters for explicit water and solvated in a dodecahedron box of 1 Å length. The systems were minimized using GROMACS 2024 with conjugate gradient algorithm, and NVT minimize was performed at 300 K for initial temperature, for 100 ps with a force constraint of 10 kJ mol−1 nm−1. The simulations were produced for 100 ns at 300 K. All simulations were performed triplicate using GROMACS 2024 version package.
