Measurement of the | V c ​ b | |V_{cb}| element of the CKM matrix in t ​ t ¯ t\bar{t} decays with the ATLAS detector
The ATLAS Collaboration

Abstract
The first measurement of the magnitude of the CKM quark mixing matrix element | V c ​ b | |V_{cb}| using on-shell W W -boson decays is presented. The measurement is made with t ​ t ¯ t\bar{t} events from p ​ p pp collision data corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 140 140 fb -1 at a centre-of-mass energy of 13 TeV collected by the ATLAS experiment at the Large Hadron Collider. A value of: —V_cb— = (50 +11 -14 )×10^-3 is measured, where the uncertainties have roughly equal contributions from the limited size of the data sample and from systematic effects. This is consistent with existing measurements made at much lower energy scales in B B hadron decays. While the sensitivity does not yet rival that of previous determinations, this novel approach probes a very different physical situation, namely the rare hadronic t → b ​ b ¯ ​ c t\rightarrow b\bar{b}c decay at high momentum-transfer, rather than semileptonic B B decays at low momentum-transfer.

Introduction

The Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa (CKM) matrix
[
1
,
2
]

is a
3\times 3
unitary matrix which parameterises the couplings of the
W
gauge bosons to the quarks. Its
elements are among the fundamental parameters of the Standard Model (SM), which can be determined only by experiment. Its
|V_{cb}|
element was first determined in 1983, following the initial measurement of the typical
B
hadron lifetime
[
3
,
4
]
, which was found to be surprisingly long at
{\cal O}(1
ps). This lifetime, in turn, implied that
|V_{cb}|\sim 0.05\ll|V_{us}|

[
5
]
, heralding for the first time the realisation that the CKM matrix was strongly hierarchical
[
6
]
.
In the subsequent years, the determination of
|V_{cb}|
has improved dramatically, both via a sequence of measurements of increasing precision on semileptonic decays of
B
hadrons, and from improved theoretical understanding
[
7
]
of the extraction of
|V_{cb}|
from the measured quantities.
The current PDG average is
|V_{cb}|=(41.1\pm 1.2)\times 10^{-3}

[
8
]
, but this masks an unresolved discrepancy of around three standard deviations between the determinations made in inclusive and in exclusive semileptonic
B
decays. It is thus timely to introduce the novel approach to the measurement of
|V_{cb}|
reported here, which is based on the method proposed in Ref.
[
9
]
and uses
W
bosons produced in the decays of top-quark pairs (
t\bar{t}
).

During LHC Run 2 (2015-2018),
{\cal O}(10^{8})

t\bar{t}
pairs were produced and decayed in the ATLAS experiment. In such events, assuming the SM, both top quarks decay via an on-shell
W
boson, and in this sense, the LHC operates effectively as a
W
boson factory. While single
W
events are even more numerous, single-lepton
t\bar{t}
events have a big advantage for studying hadronic
W
decays in ATLAS. Such events allow relatively easy triggering on the charged lepton, which also helps control hadronic backgrounds in offline analysis.
The method used here uses these events, in which one top quark decays into
\bar{b}\ell^{-}\bar{\nu}

(\ell\equiv e
,
\mu
or leptonically decaying
\tau
-lepton), while the other top quark decays hadronically via the
t\rightarrow bW^{+}\rightarrow b\bar{b}c
decay.
1
1

1

Charge-conjugate decays are assumed throughout this paper.
The physical context of the measurement is thus very different from those of traditional approaches using
B
decays, and the experimental uncertainties obtained are of significantly different origins.

Traditional measurements of
|V_{cb}|

[
10
]
exploit the decays of
B
mesons, and are thus made at an effective momentum-transfer squared
(q^{2})
in the region of
\left(\frac{m_{b}}{2}\right)^{2}
, where
m_{b}
is the mass of the
b
-quark. They use a variety of theoretical techniques in low energy hadronic physics to relate the measured quantities to
|V_{cb}|
, including lattice gauge theory for the exclusive semileptonic
B
decays and the heavy quark expansion for the inclusive
B
decays. These methods each require the introduction of distinct theoretical uncertainties into the respective measurements.
Here, the first measurement of the CKM matrix element
|V_{cb}|
is presented, made using top quark decays via an on-shell
W

(q^{2}\simeq M_{W}^{2})
. The top quark
decays before it hadronises, and the couplings are probed more directly at the parton level.
Thus, the sources of systematic uncertainty of this measurement differ significantly from those using
B
hadron decays.
Like other couplings,
|V_{cb}|
is expected to run above the weak scale, albeit quite slowly
[
11
,
12
]
, although
it is expected that such evolution ceases below the weak scale
[
8
]
.
Therefore, if the value of
|V_{cb}|
extracted in this measurement were to differ significantly from that determined in
B
decays, the explanation could be either due to new physics in the
t\rightarrow b\bar{b}c
decay or a poorly understood aspect of the low-
q^{2}

B
decay phenomenology.

The measurement determines
|V_{cb}|
via the ratio of on-shell
W^{+}\rightarrow c\bar{b}
decays to
W^{+}\rightarrow c\bar{q}
, where
q
is any down-type quark. The leading-order Feynman diagram for the
t\rightarrow bW^{+}\rightarrow b\bar{b}c
and leptonic
\bar{t}
decay signal process is shown in
Figure

1
.

t\bar{t}\rightarrow bW^{+}\,\bar{b}W^{-}\rightarrow b\bar{b}c\,\bar{b}\ell^{-}\bar{\nu}
decay. Vertices are labelled with the appropriate CKM matrix elements.

Taking the ratio of the branching fraction of these decays to that of all similar decays in which the
W
daughter quark partnering the
c
-quark has any flavour, determines
|V_{cb}|
via the following relation, valid in the three-generation SM:

The two top-quarks decay independently. Negligible differences in phase space between the three final states in the denominator are ignored. These are calculable from standard formulae (see e.g. Ref.
[
13
, Sec. 4.5]
) and are found to be at the level of
\lesssim 0.3\%
.

|V_{cb}|^{2}=\frac{|V_{cb}|^{2}}{\sum_{q}|V_{cq}|^{2}}=\frac{{\cal B}(W^{+}\rightarrow c\bar{b})}{{\cal B}(W^{+}\rightarrow c\bar{q})}=\frac{{\cal B}(t\rightarrow b\bar{b}c)}{{\cal B}(t\rightarrow b\bar{q}c)}=\frac{{\cal N}(t\overline{t}\rightarrow b\bar{b}c\,\,\bar{b}\ell^{-}\bar{\nu})}{{\cal N}(t\overline{t}\rightarrow b\bar{q}c\,\,\bar{b}\ell^{-}\bar{\nu})},

(1)

where
q=d,s,b
, and
{\cal N}
represents the event yield of the decay mode in question.
This is a challenging measurement due to the small ratio of event yields,
|V_{cb}|^{2}\simeq 1.7\times 10^{-3}

[
8
]
.
Backgrounds and imperfect detector response complicate the extraction of
|V_{cb}|
significantly compared with the simple picture implied by Eq. (
1
).
The phenomenological study of the sensitivity of this measurement reported in Ref.
[
9
]
did not take account of all experimental backgrounds. The analysis presented here applies this method for the first time, using data collected from the ATLAS detector, with a complete treatment of backgrounds and systematic uncertainties.

2
ATLAS detector

The ATLAS detector
[
14
]
at the LHC covers nearly the entire solid angle around the collision point.
3
3

3

ATLAS uses a right-handed coordinate system with its origin at the nominal interaction point (IP)
in the centre of the detector and the
z
-axis along the beam pipe.
The
x
-axis points from the IP to the centre of the LHC ring,
and the
y
-axis points upwards.
Polar coordinates
(r,\phi)
are used in the transverse plane,

\phi
being the azimuthal angle around the
z
-axis.
The pseudorapidity is defined in terms of the polar angle
\theta
as
\eta=-\ln\tan(\theta/2)
and is equal to the rapidity

y=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{E+p_{z}c}{E-p_{z}c}\right)
in the relativistic limit.
Angular distance is measured in units of
\Delta R\equiv\sqrt{(\Delta y)^{2}+(\Delta\phi)^{2}}
.

It consists of an inner tracking detector surrounded by a thin superconducting solenoid, electromagnetic and hadronic calorimeters,
and a muon spectrometer incorporating three large superconducting air-core toroidal magnets.

The inner-detector system (ID) is immersed in a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
axial magnetic field
and provides charged-particle tracking in the range
|\eta|<2.5
.
The high-granularity silicon pixel detector covers the vertex region and typically provides four measurements per track,
the first hit generally being in the insertable B-layer (IBL) installed before Run 2
[
15
,
16
]
.
It is followed by the SemiConductor Tracker (SCT), which usually provides eight measurements per track.
These silicon detectors are complemented by the transition radiation tracker (TRT),
which enables radially extended track reconstruction up to
|\eta|=2.0
.
The TRT also provides electron identification information
based on the fraction of hits (typically 30 in total) above a higher energy-deposit threshold corresponding to transition radiation.

The calorimeter system covers the pseudorapidity range
|\eta|<4.9
. Within the region
|\eta|<3.2
, electromagnetic calorimetry is provided by barrel and
endcap high-granularity lead/liquid-argon (LAr) calorimeters,
with an additional thin LAr presampler covering
|\eta|<1.8

to correct for energy loss in material upstream of the calorimeters.
Hadronic calorimetry is provided by the steel/scintillator-tile calorimeter,
segmented into three barrel structures within
|\eta|<1.7
, and two copper/LAr hadronic endcap calorimeters.
The solid angle coverage is completed with forward copper/LAr and tungsten/LAr calorimeter modules
optimised for electromagnetic and hadronic energy measurements respectively.

The muon spectrometer (MS) comprises separate trigger and
high-precision tracking chambers measuring the deflection of muons in a magnetic field generated by the superconducting air-core toroidal magnets.
The field integral of the toroids ranges between
2.0
and
6.0\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}

across most of the detector.
Three layers of precision chambers, each consisting of layers of monitored drift tubes, cover the region
|\eta|<2.7
,
complemented by cathode-strip chambers in the forward region, where the background is highest.
The muon trigger system covers the range
|\eta|<2.4
with resistive-plate chambers in the barrel, and thin-gap chambers in the endcap regions.

The luminosity is measured mainly by the LUCID–2
[
17
]
detector that records Cherenkov light produced in the quartz windows of photomultipliers located close to the beampipe.

Events are selected by the first-level trigger system implemented in custom hardware,
followed by selections made by algorithms implemented in software in the high-level trigger
[
18
]
.
The first-level trigger accepts events from the
40\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
bunch crossings at a rate below
100\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
,
which the high-level trigger further reduces in order to record complete events to disk at about
1.25\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
.

A software suite
[
19
]
is used in data simulation, in the reconstruction
and analysis of real and simulated data, in detector operations, and in the trigger and data acquisition
systems of the experiment.

4
Event reconstruction and selection

Events are required to have at least one reconstructed
pp
interaction vertex with a minimum of two associated tracks with transverse momenta
p_{\text{T}}>0.5\penalty\ \text{GeV}
.
The primary vertex is defined as the vertex with the highest sum of squared transverse momenta of associated tracks
[
65
]
.

Electron candidates are reconstructed from energy deposits in the EM calorimeter matched to a track in the ID
[
66
]
.
The track is required to be matched to the primary vertex, where the track longitudinal impact parameter
z_{0}
, must satisfy
\left|z_{0}\sin{\theta}\right|<0.5
mm and the ratio of the transverse impact parameter
d_{0}
to its uncertainty
\sigma(d_{0})
must satisfy
\left|d_{0}/\sigma(d_{0})\right|<5
.
Electron candidates are required to be within
\left|\eta\right|<2.47
, excluding the transition region between the barrel and the endcap calorimeters,
1.37<\left|\eta\right|<1.52
.
They must satisfy
p_{\text{T}}>25\penalty\ \text{GeV}
and the
Tight
likelihood identification criteria and
Tight
isolation criteria
[
66
]
.

Muon candidates are reconstructed by associating tracks in the ID with tracks or track segments in the MS
[
67
]
, refined through a global fit that uses hits from both sub-detectors
[
68
]
.
The track longitudinal and transverse impact parameters must satisfy the requirements
\left|z_{0}\sin{\theta}\right|<0.5
mm and
\left|d_{0}/\sigma(d_{0})\right|<3
, respectively.
Muon candidates must satisfy
p_{\text{T}}>25\penalty\ \text{GeV}
,
\left|\eta\right|<2.5
and the
Medium
identification and
Tight
isolation criteria
[
67
]
.

Jets are reconstructed using the particle flow algorithm, which combines topological clusters of calorimeter cells with tracking information
[
69
,
70
]
.
These objects are then clustered by the anti-
k_{t}
algorithm
[
71
,
72
]
with a jet radius parameter
R=0.4
.
The jet energy scale (JES) is calibrated using both data and simulation, as detailed in Ref.
[
70
]
. Procedures are adopted to match the jet energy resolution (JER) in simulation to that observed in data
[
70
]
.
Jets are required to satisfy
p_{\text{T}}>25\penalty\ \text{GeV}
and
\left|\eta\right|<2.5
.
To suppress jets originating from pile-up interactions, the jet vertex tagger (JVT) multivariate likelihood
[
73
]
discriminant, using track-based variables, is applied to jets satisfying
p_{\text{T}}<60\penalty\ \text{GeV}
and

Jets originating from beauty and charm hadrons (
b
- and
c
-jets) are identified using the DL1r deep neural network algorithm
[
74

5
Overview of the analysis

As noted above, only events with exactly four reconstructed jets satisfying the kinematic selection are used in the analysis. Jets are tagged uniquely as either
b
-,
c
- or light-flavoured (for all other jets), and the events are divided into tagging categories (TCs), denoted “
mbnc
”, based on the numbers
m
of
b
-tagged and
n
of
c
-tagged jets they contain.
Similarly, production flavours (PFs) are defined using the same
mbnc
notation, based on the truth-level flavour of jets in each event. The truth-level flavour of a jet is assigned by matching the jet to truth-level hadrons within an angular distance of
\Delta R<0.3
and following the priority order
b>c>\text{light}

[
74
]
.
TC event yields are different from the corresponding PF event yields, due to jet-flavour mistagging which causes significant numbers of events to migrate between categories.

As suggested by Eq. (
1
), sensitivity to
|V_{cb}|
comes primarily from the ratio of the number of events in the
3b1c
“signal” TC to that in the
2b1c
TC:
|V_{cb}|^{2}\simeq N(3b1c)/N(2b1c)
.
However, a number of effects complicate this simple picture. First, mistagging of jet flavour means that the signal TC contains additional events which need to be accounted for, according to a suitable calculation involving the numbers of events in other PFs and the various jet mistag probabilities which determine the migration between categories. Second, significant numbers of background events enter the signal TC, as a result of physics processes other than pure single lepton weak decays of
t\bar{t}
pairs.
In particular, the most significant backgrounds arise from
t\bar{t}+\mathrm{HF}
events, in which QCD processes generate an extra pair of heavy quarks (
c\bar{c}
or
b\bar{b}
) in addition to the usual weak decay products of the
t\bar{t}
decays, while simultaneously not increasing the number of detected jets. This can happen when, e.g. quark pairs do not form resolved jets, or when one or more jets fall outside the acceptance, leaving just four jets of uncontrolled flavour.
The rates of these
t\bar{t}+\mathrm{HF}
processes are often underestimated in simulation, and the approach taken in this analysis is to determine these rates, simultaneously with the measurement of
|V_{cb}|
.

A final complication of this analysis is that although central ATLAS calibrations of the jet-flavour (mis)tag probabilities (technically, measured weights parameterising data/MC differences) exist for the jet flavours considered, those for true
c
-jets are calibrated using the same channel as the analysis, namely single lepton
t\bar{t}
decays
[
76
]
, with the
|V_{cb}|
value fixed. Thus, for this analysis the provided
c
-jet calibration cannot be used directly, and must instead be determined simultaneously with the measurement of
|V_{cb}|
.
The sensitivity of this channel to the
c
-jet tagging efficiency,
\epsilon_{c}
, comes directly from the fact that the flavour composition of the vast majority of single lepton
t\bar{t}
decays is very well known from the unitarity of the CKM matrix: hadronic
W
boson decays to two light-flavoured quarks occur with the same rate as those with one light and one c-quark, up to the small contribution given by
|V_{cb}|^{2}\penalty\ \sim 0.2\%
. Thus, the ratio of the yields of events with these flavours after tagging is directly sensitive to the charm tagging efficiency:
N(2b1c)/N(2b0c)\simeq\epsilon_{c}/(2-\epsilon_{c})
, where the dependence on
|V_{cb}|
makes only a negligible correction. Furthermore, among genuine single lepton
t\bar{t}
decays, the
3b0c
PF has negligible rate in the SM as it is suppressed by
|V_{ub}|^{2}
, and the yield of events seen in the corresponding TC is directly proportional to the
c
-to-
b
mistagging probability:
N(3b0c)\propto f_{cb}
, allowing the latter to be determined.

From the above discussion, it is clear that the analysis needs to include the numbers of events in several TCs, in addition to the signal TC,
3b1c
. In practice, eight TCs are used, requiring at least two heavy flavour tags, including at least one
b
-tag:

1b1c,\penalty\ 1b2c,\penalty\ 1b3c,\penalty\ 2b0c,\penalty\ 2b1c,\penalty\ 2b2c,\penalty\ 3b0c,\penalty\ 3b1c.

The
4b0c
TC is not used as it does not provide a statistically significant contribution to the analysis.

The
t\bar{t}+\mathrm{HF}
background events which pass the selection criteria are expected to be incomplete (they have at least six primary partons, with only four jets reconstructed), so that they generally differ kinematically from the primary signal events.
Thus, in order to help separate them from the signal, a neural network (NN), trained to distinguish signal events from such incomplete background events, is deployed. The remaining contribution of the
t\bar{t}+\mathrm{HF}
background to the signal-enriched regions is constrained using background-enriched regions.
The NN score,
\sigma
, is defined on a scale between 0 and 1, and the events are separated into three distinct regions
(\text{NNR})
denoted low, med and high.
The boundaries of the
NNR
are obtained by optimising the expected sensitivity to
|V_{cb}|
, resulting in
\sigma_{\text{low}}\leq 0.643
,
0.643<\sigma_{\text{med}}<0.736
and
0.736\leq\sigma_{\text{high}}
.
The same
NNR
boundaries are used across all eight TCs to give
3\times 8=24
“fit-regions”.
Eventually, the event yields in all 24 regions are fitted using a binned profile-likelihood fit
[
81
]
,
while floating
|V_{cb}|^{2}
, the total
t\bar{t}
rate, the rates of
t\bar{t}+c
and
t\bar{t}+b

\epsilon_{c}
and
f_{cb}
separately in each of the three
NNR

6
Details of the analysis

6.1
Neural network configuration

A classifier is designed to discriminate between correctly reconstructed
t\bar{t}
events and background events, defined in the following paragraph. The classification is based on the output of a NN, which is trained on simulated events and deployed on data and MC. The NN takes a set of reconstructed kinematic inputs from each event, and outputs an activation (score), bound between zero and one, where
background events are assigned a low activation, and signal events a high activation. To avoid biasing the extraction of the
c
-jet tagging probabilities, no flavour-tagging information is used as an input to the NN.

For the NN training, signal events are defined as single lepton
t\bar{t}
events in simulation, in which the reconstructed lepton and jets can be matched to the corresponding generator-level particle and parton, respectively, within a given angular acceptance. Such events are identified by finding the jet assignment that minimises
\sqrt{\sum_{i}\Delta R_{i}^{2}}
, where
\Delta R_{i}
is the angular separation between the
i
’th reconstructed jet and its paired generator-level parton. In accordance with the jet radius used in the jet reconstruction algorithm, a threshold of
\Delta R<0.4
is applied to define matched events. In addition, the reconstructed and generator-level leptons are required to be matched within
\Delta R<0.3
.
Single lepton
t\bar{t}
events that fail the matching criteria, as well as simulated events from other processes (including dilepton
t\bar{t}
and non-
t\bar{t}
), are labelled as background in the training set and weighted according to their respective cross sections.

A set of reconstructed kinematic features is chosen as input, and is split into two distinct input structures. The first is a vector, containing nine features that are independent of the jet assignment (permutation) to parent
W
and
t
-quarks.
The other nine features in the second input structure do depend on the selected jet permutation, e.g. the angular distances between the two top candidates, the hadronic
W
candidate and the lepton.
Additionally, the reconstructed masses of the leptonically and hadronically decaying top-quarks (
m(t_{\text{lep}})
and
m(t_{\text{had}})
), and the hadronic
W
boson (
m(W_{\text{had}})
) are included.
There are 12 unique
4
4

4

The reconstructed quantities used are not sensitive to swapping the assigned
W
daughter jets.
assignments between four jets and the four partons from a single lepton
t\bar{t}
decay, each of which yields a unique set of values for the
permutation-dependent features. The second NN input structure is therefore a
12\times 9
matrix.
The shapes of the input distributions are standardised
to ensure that all inputs have the same absolute scale and width. This is done by applying a Yeo-Johnson power transformation
[
82
]
, and then normalising each distribution to zero mean and unit standard deviation.

The vector input is processed by a branch of fully connected layers, and the matrix input is processed by a branch of convolutional layers, the latter exploiting the information encoded in the transition between one jet permutation and the next. The outputs of the two branches are processed further by a set of fully connected layers.
In addition to background discrimination, the NN is trained to predict the most likely jet permutation as an auxiliary task. This is included as a second output from the NN architecture, and is used for validation plots, but not for the analysis itself.

Two datasets are produced by sorting the full set of simulated events that satisfy the selection criteria into even and odd event numbers. Two NNs with identical architecture are trained, one on each dataset, and are deployed on simulated and observed events in the opposite dataset.
For each dataset,
20\%
is reserved for validation during the training routine.
Each NN is trained for a maximum of 50 epochs, the internal weights of each model being saved after each epoch. The final trained model is selected as the one with the minimum loss on the validation set. The trained models select signal events with an efficiency of
81\%
while the corresponding background efficiency is
18\%
, and assigns the jet permutation with an efficiency of
63\%
, when deployed on the validation set.

To verify the importance of the input variables while preserving physical correlations, a local sensitivity analysis
[
83
]
is employed based on the partial derivative of the discrimination output
\sigma
with respect to each input variable
x_{i}
.
The importance is quantified by the magnitude of the averaged sensitivity,
|\langle\partial\sigma/\partial x_{i}\rangle|
, which captures the response to infinitesimal perturbations.
5
5

5

The perturbation magnitude is set to 1% of the standard deviation for each input variable.

As shown in
Figure

W_{\text{had}}

6.2
In situ
charm tagging calibration

The
c
-jet tagging efficiencies are calibrated
in situ
using a data-driven method based on the relative event yields in the different TCs. Jet flavour tagging is described by a matrix of tagging rates that encodes both tagging efficiencies and mistag probabilities between light-, charm-, and bottom-flavoured jets,

T=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{l}&f_{lc}&f_{lb}\\
f_{cl}&\epsilon_{c}&f_{cb}\\
f_{bl}&f_{bc}&\epsilon_{b}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where
\epsilon_{x}
denotes the tagging efficiency for jets of true flavour
x
and
f_{xy}
the probability for a jet of true flavour
x
to be tagged as flavour
y
. As each row of
T
must be normalised to unity, the matrix has six independent parameters, which are chosen to be those in the second and third columns. These tagging rates are defined as kinematically averaged probabilities within the three mutually exclusive
NNR
s defined in Sec.
5
.

Since the standard ATLAS
c
-jet calibration assumes the SM value of
|V_{cb}|
and relies on the same data sample used in this analysis, the
c
-jet tagging efficiency
\epsilon_{c}
and the
c
-to-
b
mistag rate
f_{cb}
are instead calibrated directly in the fit. On the other hand,
for
b
- and light-flavoured jets, the tagging rates are corrected by applying the simulation-to-data scale factors and associated uncertainties derived in dedicated calibrations
[
75
,
77
]
, to account for the differences between data and simulation.

In a first step, the six independent tagging rates chosen from the
T
matrix are determined in each
NNR
from simulation.
The simulated events include all events from the fifteen possible PFs without any tagging preselection.
They are partitioned into a signal process (
k=S
, including only
|V_{cb}|=1
) and a background process (
k=B
, including all the other
t\bar{t}
and single top-quark processes) to reduce correlations with
|V_{cb}|
and to improve the sensitivity to the tagging rates.
The determination of the tagging rates is mainly driven by the
|V_{cb}|=0
component of the background.
Events are classified into one of the PFs according to their truth-level jet flavour composition, and the probability,
\mathcal{P}_{ij}(T)
, for an event in the
i
-th PF to be reconstructed in the
j
-th TC is computed from combinatorial expressions involving only the tagging rates encoded in
T
. In this calculation, the tagging probabilities for each jet are treated as independent of those for the other jets in the event, an assumption validated by a subsequent closure test.
The probability for an event of process
k
to be tagged into TC
j
is then given by

p^{k}_{j}(T)=\sum_{i}^{\text{PFs}}t^{k}_{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{ij}(T),

(3)

where
t^{k}_{i}
is the fraction of events of process
k
in the
i
-th PF.

A single, shared set of tagging rates is extracted by minimising a total
\chi^{2}
constructed from the separate contributions of the signal and background processes:

\chi^{2}=\sum_{j,k}\left(\frac{N^{k}_{j}-N^{k}\cdot p^{k}_{j}(T)}{\Delta N^{k}_{j}}\right)^{2},

(4)

where
N^{k}_{j}
is the simulated event yield for process
k
in TC
j
,
N^{k}
is the total yield,
p^{k}_{j}(T)
is the tagging probability given
T
. The uncertainty
\Delta N^{k}_{j}
is defined as
\sqrt{N^{k}_{j}}
, which models the Poisson fluctuation expected in the real data.
The subscript
j
runs over the eight considered TCs, and
k
runs over the signal and background processes, giving sixteen terms in total.
The fit is performed separately in each
NNR
to reduce the impact of any kinematic differences between the regions.
Closure tests are performed by applying the extracted tagging rates to
Equation

3
to evaluate the predicted event fractions, and then comparing these predictions with the simulated fractions in each TC. Good agreement is observed between the two within the expected statistical fluctuation of the data, as represented by
\Delta N^{k}_{j}
.

The second step is performed as part of the final profile-likelihood fit for
|V_{cb}|
, in which the
c
-jet tagging efficiency
\epsilon_{c}
and
c
-to-
b
mistag rate
f_{cb}
are incorporated via floated normalisation factors that correct the event yields in each TC to fit the data.
In this step, all elements of
T
except
\epsilon_{c}
and
f_{cb}
are fixed to their calibrated values, and distinct tagging rates are used for the low, medium, and high
NNR
s. The tagging rates,
\epsilon^{\text{NNR}}_{c}
and
f^{\text{NNR}}_{cb}
(
\text{NNR}=
low, med, high), together with their associated uncertainties, are fitted simultaneously with
|V_{cb}|
.

The
in situ
calibration is applied to all
t\bar{t}
and single top-quark events simulated with
Powheg
and
Pythia
. For the other minor processes, a normalisation uncertainty of 30% per true
c
-jet is assigned.

7
Systematic uncertainties

The measurement of
|V_{cb}|
is affected by systematic uncertainties arising from experimental sources, the modelling of physics processes, and the finite size of MC samples.
For two-sided uncertainties, where both upward and downward variations are available, the symmetrised uncertainty is calculated as the signed average of the original variations.
In cases of one-sided uncertainties, where only a single alternative scenario is compared against the nominal one, an opposite variation of equal magnitude is assumed.

7.1
Experimental Uncertainties

Experimental uncertainties related to the reconstruction of physics objects, pile-up modelling and integrated luminosity are considered.
These uncertainties are derived from dedicated measurements using Run 2 data and are applied to all simulated processes.
This analysis is primarily sensitive to the experimental uncertainties originating from jet energy and light-jet tagging calibrations.

The uncertainty in the combined 2015-2018 integrated luminosity is 0.83%
[
21
]
.
Additionally, the distribution of the average number of interactions per bunch crossing in simulation is rescaled to match that in data.
Uncertainty in this rescaling process is propagated by varying the corresponding reweighting factors.

Uncertainties associated with electrons and muons arise from the trigger, reconstruction, identification, and isolation efficiencies, as well as their momentum scale and resolution.
These are derived from measurements primarily using
Z\rightarrow\ell\ell
(
\ell=e
or
\mu

[
66
,
80
,
67
,
79
,
84

85
]
.

Uncertainties in the JES and JER are evaluated using a series of simulation-based techniques and
in situ
measurements
[
70

86
]

These include uncertainty components related to the jet flavour composition,
\eta
-intercalibration, punch-through, single-particle response, calorimeter response to different jet flavours and pile-up effects.
They consist of 30 uncorrelated JES components and 13 uncorrelated JER components.
An uncertainty associated with the JVT efficiency is also included
[
73
]

Uncertainties related to the calibration of the DL1r flavour tagging algorithm for

and

These uncertainties are decomposed into a set of uncorrelated eigenvectors, including 45 components for

Additionally, there are four uncertainty components to account for the extrapolation to high-

Uncertainties from Ref.

Finally, uncertainties associated with the energy scale and resolution of leptons and jets are propagated to

Additional uncertainties in

Modelling uncertainties for

The dominant systematic uncertainties in this analysis are related to

Uncertainties related to the QCD scale variations in the ME are estimated by varying

Ultimately, the best-fit
|V_{cb}|
and its uncertainty are not found to depend significantly on these treatments.

For the dominant background process
t\bar{t}_{|V_{cb}|=0}+\text{light}
, the uncertainties related to the PS and hadronisation, FSR, and colour reconnection are decomposed into TC and NN components.
The TC component captures event migration between TCs, while the NN component captures event migration between NN score bins within each TC.
This treatment prevents the fit from propagating constraints from a sensitive component to an insensitive one, which would otherwise underestimate the total uncertainty.
To further reduce the constraint on the NN component of PS and hadronisation uncertainty in
t\bar{t}_{|V_{cb}|=0}+\text{light}
, the original variation is 50% decorrelated across the NN score bins by splitting it into one correlated and three uncorrelated components, each scaled by
1/\sqrt{2}
.
This treatment significantly relaxes the constraints associated with this source of the uncertainty while having a minor impact on the
|V_{cb}|
sensitivity.

An extra uncertainty in the calculation of the interference between
tW
and
t\bar{t}
processes is considered for single top-quark modelling. This uncertainty is estimated by comparing the baseline
tW
sample simulated using diagram-removal, to an alternative sample using diagram-subtraction
[
57
,
58
]
with otherwise identical settings.

The
t\bar{t}
normalisation is determined in the fit to data.
A cross-section uncertainty of 5.4% is applied to single top-quark processes
[
96
]
.
For the remaining processes, including
W\text{+\,jets}
,
Z\text{\,+\,jets}
, diboson and
t\bar{t}+X
, an overall uncertainty of 45-50% is applied
[
97
,
98
]
. An overall 100% uncertainty is applied to the small multijet background.
These uncertainties have a negligible effect on the final results.

Statistical analysis and results

8.1
Fit setup

The value of
|V_{cb}|
is extracted using a binned profile-likelihood fit
[
99
]
implemented within the
TRExFitter
framework
[
100
]
.
A normalisation factor,
\mu_{|V_{cb}|}
, defined as the ratio of the post-fit value to the pre-fit one, is used throughout this section. The pre-fit value of
|V_{cb}|
is set to a nominal value of
0.04173
, used by the event generators. In addition to
\mu_{|V_{cb}|}
, the overall normalisations of the
t\bar{t}
,
t\bar{t}
+
c
, and
t\bar{t}
+
b
components are treated as free parameters. The
c
-jet tagging efficiencies and mistag rates are also treated as free parameters and are independent in the three NN score regions.
Systematic uncertainties are incorporated as nuisance parameters that affect the predicted event yields through bin-by-bin variations. Most nuisance parameters are constrained by Gaussian probability density functions. The nuisance parameters related to the statistical uncertainty of the simulated samples are constrained by gamma distributions following the Beeston-Barlow technique
[
101
]
.
The fit is performed simultaneously across 24 bins, comprising eight TCs for each of the three NN score regions.

The likelihood function is the product of Poisson probability terms over all analysis bins, multiplied by constraint terms for the nuisance parameters, and is defined as

\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{data}\mid\mu_{|V_{cb}|},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{k\in\mathrm{bins}}\mathrm{Pois}\left(n_{k}\mid\nu_{k}(\mu_{|V_{cb}|},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\;\times\;\prod_{i}\mathrm{Gauss}\left(\theta_{i}\mid\theta_{i,0},1\right),

(5)

where
n_{k}
denotes the observed number of events in bin
k
, and
\nu_{k}
is the corresponding expected yield, which depends on the signal strength parameter
\mu_{|V_{cb}|}
, the set of normalisation factors
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and the nuisance parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
All nuisance parameters, except those accounting for the limited size of the simulated samples, are constrained by Gaussian terms.

Systematic uncertainties with an impact of less than 0.5% on the yield of a given sample in a given bin are omitted for that sample and bin to improve numerical stability and computational performance.
It was verified that variations of this threshold have a negligible impact on the fit results.

Table
1
summarises the impact of the fit parameters on the different simulated samples.
A common normalisation factor is applied to all
t\bar{t}
samples, while additional scaling parameters control the relative contributions of the heavy-flavour components.
With this parametrisation, the value of
|V_{cb}|
is directly extracted from the fit through its effect on the
t\bar{t}_{|V_{cb}|=1}
normalisation.

The model is fit to pseudo-data to validate its robustness.
The pseudo-data is constructed from the total prediction, where the contribution of the nominal
t\bar{t}
sample is replaced by the
Sherpa

t\bar{t}
prediction.
The results indicate that the fit to the pseudo-data can recover the inserted values of
\mu_{|V_{cb}|}
and the other free parameters within the considered modelling uncertainty.

9
Conclusion

The first measurement of the CKM matrix element
|V_{cb}|
at the weak scale is presented. The measurement uses single lepton
t\bar{t}
events with exactly four jets, taken from
140
fb
-1
of
pp
collision data at
\sqrt{s}=13

TeV
collected with the ATLAS detector at the LHC. The measurement determines
|V_{cb}|
, via the ratio of on-shell
W^{+}\rightarrow c\bar{b}
decays to
W^{+}\rightarrow c\bar{q}
, where
q
is any down-type quark. The normalisations of
t\bar{t}
processes with additional heavy quarks, together with the charm-jet tagging efficiency and mistag rates are determined simultaneously.

The measured value

|V_{cb}|=\left(50\begin{subarray}{c}+11\\
-14\end{subarray}\right)\times 10^{-3}

(9)

is found to be consistent, within one standard deviation, with earlier measurements made at lower energy scales in
B
decays. Within the Standard Model, this value can be reinterpreted as the branching fraction

{\cal B}(t\rightarrow b\bar{b}c)=(8\pm 4)\times 10^{-4}.

(10)

This first measurement of
|V_{cb}|
using top quark decays demonstrates the potential to measure
|V_{cb}|
at hadron colliders, and sets the stage for more precise measurements at the LHC in the future. Such measurements will profit from significant progress in flavour tagging algorithms, larger data samples and extended analysis categories.
