 Niowave Leak Testing Analysis

Background
Niowave leak testing was proposed in order to identify an associated leak rate with 6 vessels. The 6 welded
aluminum vessels being leak tested consisted of 4 short vessels and 2 longer vessels, each size is depicted in
Figure 1.. Two types of gaskets were proposed in order to identify the leak rate for the vessel dependent on the
specific gaskets utilized.

 Figure 1 Long and Short Vessel

The gasket types proposed were silver-pl plat
 ated and stainless steel unplated variants. Each vessel was sealed
using Swagelok ~1” VCR fittings. The vessels with and without these fittings are depicted in Figures 2 and 3,
the fittings are designed to create extremely leak tight, metal to metal seals. Theyy are crucial for high integrity
applications, particularly in ultra-high purity systems where contamination free gas and fluid delivery is
essential. A complete VCR assembly includes a male nut, a female nut, two glands, and a metal gasket.

 Figure 2 Unfitted Vessel Figure 3 Fitted Vessel
The sealing mechanism of a VCR fitting involves the compression of a metal gasket between two precision-
machined beads located on the faces of the glands. When the nuts are threaded together and tightened, the
glands are drawn towards each other, compressing the metal gasket. This action creates a secure, leak tight,
metal to metal seal that performs effectively across a wide range of pressures, from high vacuum to high
pressure which is why they were recommended for this application. Proper installation often involves hand
tightening the nut followed by an additional 1/8th of a turn with a wrench.

 The metal gasket is a vital component for achieving a leak tight seal in VCR fittings. It deforms during
 tightening to fill any microscopic imperfections on the sealing surfaces, thus creating a robust seal. While the
 VCR fitting itself is reusable, the metal gasket must be replaced each time the connection is remade to
 ensure seal integrity.

 The testing was to be performed using traditional helium leak detection equipment. The vessels were filled
 with helium in a makeshift bag tent purged with helium to create a helium environment before being sealed
 with associated gaskets and VCR fittings. After sealing, the parts were transferred to a bell jar seen in
 Figure 4 hooked up to a helium detection device that pulled vacuum to identify leak rates over a set period
 of time.

 Figure 4 Bell Jar

Initial Investigation
Initial testing involved a set of trial runs of differing gaskets at varying tightening degrees in an effort to optimize
testing parameters. All tightening was performed in the previously discussed bag tent purged with helium. All 6
vessels were fitted with both gasket variants and tightened to hand-t -tight followed by tightening with 2 wrenches
to ¼ turn in one set of experiments followed by tightening to 1/3 turn in a second set of experiments. After
various failed runs at both tightened levels, it was decided to ensure manufacturing requirements for the turns
were being met. After a discussion with the local Swagelok vendor, it was determined that 1/8 turn was the
proper specification for both gasket variants.

Further testing confirmed this guidance to be ideal. This testing led to 5 out of 6 un
 unplated gasket successes
down to a set leak rate goal of at least 5E-09 (atm*cc/s) within a reasonable period of time after initiating the
leak test. However, during this it was discovered that the silver-plated gaskets were not capable of reaching
this goal and all 6 vessels failed with the silver-plated gaskets.

This testing led to the conclusion that the silver-plated gaskets would not be reasonable to prevent material
loss in a leak and they were to be omitted for the official testing. Instead, only the stainless steel unplated
gaskets would be tested for all vessels to determine a fair leak rate for the 6 vessels. To get informative data
the official testing required confirmation that a preset 5E-09 (atm*cc/s) leak rate was possible within a 3030-
minute period of leak rate testing. This would be conducted 2 times for the short vessels and 3 times for the
long vessels.

Considerations

It should be noted that not all runs on vessel 3 were successful. The reason for failure was revealed to
be an issue with the VCR fittings not sealing appropriately on vessel 3. The fitting was determined to be
at fault, it failed to achieve a passable leak rate in sequential runs. There was no evidence from visual
inspection that the fitting would fail. It is suspected that the fitting may have been damaged by an
incident of over tightening. To determine it was not the vessel, the fitting was swapped with a VCR
fitting from one of the other vessels, it passed as normal. This fail data was omitted since it is not
inherently linked to the vessel or gaskets being tested. For future work, it would be wise to ensure that
all VCR fittings be tested prior to application.

Conclusion

From the data tabulated it is seen that all vessels are capable of sealing with a VCR compression setup
with non-plated stainless steel gaskets. The silver-plated gaskets do not adequately seal when
manufacturer-recommended guidance for sealing is applied. When vessels reach the target leak rate of
5E-09 (atm*cc/s), they proceed to hit safe and low leak rates on average for both vessel types all within
the range of 2.3E-09 and 9.4E-10 atm*cc/s. Following this data, and based on the results of equipment
tested, the recommended approach would involve use of stainless steel unplated gaskets, tightened to
the manufacturing recommendation of a 1/8 turn past hand-tight for fresh and re-used fittings.
