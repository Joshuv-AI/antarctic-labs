Activity-dependent antioxidant gene therapy protects against epileptogenesis
Authors: Prince Kumar Singh, Gabriele Lignani, Matthew C Walker, Andreas Lieb, Tawfeeq Shekh-Ahmad
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Epilepsy affects >50 million people worldwide, and nearly one-third are drug resistant. Current antiseizure medications primarily provide symptomatic seizure control but do not effectively modify the underlying disease process or halt the progression of epilepsy1. Oxidative stress can be both a cause and a consequence of seizures, yet constitutive antioxidant therapy interferes with physiological redox signaling2. Here, we developed an activity-dependent gene therapy that couples Nrf2, a transcription factor and upstream modulator of antioxidant responses3, to cfos, an immediate-early gene promoter selectively induced in hyperactive neurons4.

We first confirmed the specificity of cfos activity. Minimal expression of AAV9-cfos-EGFP was detected in sham animals, whereas kainic acid-induced status epilepticus (SE) triggered robust expression in NeuN+ neurons in CA1 and CA3 (Fig. 1a). Temporal analysis revealed induction within 4 h, peak expression at 12–24 h, and a decrease by 48 h (Fig. 1a), which is consistent with the changes in cfos dynamics. In cfos-Nrf2 animals, the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream target NQO1 significantly increased at 24 h (supplementary figure deposited at Figshare; 10.6084/m9.figshare.31343032), confirming the functional engagement of antioxidant pathways. No colocalization was observed with GFAP+ astrocytes (Fig. 1a) or Iba1+ microglia (data not shown), confirming neuronal restriction. Further characterization with GAD1 staining confirmed that cfos-driven EGFP expression was largely confined to excitatory neurons (~80% in CA1/CA3), with only a minority of inhibitory neurons positive (15–20%). These findings confirm that the cfos-Nrf2 vector reliably drives the transient, activity-dependent engagement of antioxidant pathways in excitatory hyperactive neurons.

We have previously shown that Nrf2 is transiently upregulated after seizures and that pharmacological activation exerts neuroprotective effects by reducing neuronal death and enhancing antioxidant capacity3,5. However, the activity-dependent, cell-type-specific induction of Nrf2 has not been tested. To address this, we used a sequential pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) challenge paradigm4 to determine whether activity-dependent engagement of antioxidant pathways could confer temporally restricted seizure protection. Animals received bilateral hippocampal injections of cfos-Nrf2 or cfos-EGFP vectors, followed by a two-week recovery period, after which PTZ was administered at 0 h, 24 h, and 2 weeks. Seizure severity was comparable between the groups at 0 h, which was consistent with the minimal baseline cfos activity (Fig. 1b). At 24 h, when cfos activity was maximal, compared with control animals, Nrf2-treated animals had significantly lower seizure scores (Fig. 1b). By 2 weeks, when cfos activity had returned to baseline, seizure severity had converged (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, compared with cfos-EGFP animals, cfos-Nrf2 animals demonstrated markedly improved survival across the repeated PTZ challenges (75% vs. 25%, respectively). These findings demonstrate that cfos-driven Nrf2 expression provides temporally restricted, activity-dependent protection, leading to attenuated seizure severity and enhanced survival outcomes.

We next tested whether repeated activity-dependent bursts of Nrf2 could alter epilepsy development in the kainic acid-SE model. The animals received bilateral hippocampal injections of cfos-Nrf2 or cfos-EGFP. Two weeks later, SE was induced with kainic acid and terminated after 2 h with diazepam. We confirmed that cfos-driven Nrf2 basal expression does not affect the latency to SE onset (cfos-EGFP: 103 ± 18 min; cfos-Nrf2: 119 ± 11 min; p = 0.466; unpaired Student’s t test). Continuous video-electrocorticography (vECoG) monitoring over 12 weeks revealed that compared with the control treatment, cfos-Nrf2 expression markedly reduced the spontaneous recurrent seizure frequency and cumulative seizure burden, while significantly increasing the number of seizure-free days (Fig. 1c, supplementary figure deposited at Figshare; 10.6084/m9.figshare.31343032). The therapeutic effect was also evident by the persistent decrease in interictal spike activity over the 12 weeks of monitoring, indicating the stabilization of epileptic networks (supplementary figure deposited at Figshare; 10.6084/m9.figshare.31343032). Importantly, the comparable latencies to SE onset between groups confirm that cfos-Nrf2 expression did not alter the severity or timing of the initial insult but instead acted downstream to interfere with the recurrence of spontaneous seizures. Histopathological analysis revealed no differences in neuronal density between the cfos-EGFP and cfos-Nrf2 groups in CA1 or CA3 after SE (data not shown), indicating that cfos-Nrf2 does not reduce acute SE-induced neuronal injury. Furthermore, immunofluorescence at week 13 after vECoG recording confirmed that cfos promoter activity is also responsive to spontaneous seizures and is not restricted to SE (Fig. 1c, right).

Epilepsy is frequently accompanied by behavioral and cognitive impairments, and we evaluated comorbidities at week 6 following SE. The cfos-EGFP animals showed reduced locomotion, heightened anxiety-like behavior, and impaired working memory, as assessed by behavioral tests (supplementary figure deposited at Figshare; 10.6084/m9.figshare.31343032). In contrast, cfos-Nrf2- overexpressing animals traveled greater distances, spent more time in the central zone and open arms of behavioral mazes (open field and elevated plus mazes, respectively), and performed significantly better in spontaneous alternation tasks, which is consistent with preserved hippocampal function (supplementary figure deposited at Figshare; 10.6084/m9.figshare.31343032). These findings show that activity-dependent Nrf2 induction not only reduces seizures but also prevents the development of seizure-associated comorbidities, a major unmet need in epilepsy therapy.

Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of cfos-Nrf2 in animals with established chronic epilepsy. In cohort II, the animals underwent kainic acid-induced SE and were allowed to develop chronic epilepsy for 10–12 weeks.

After three weeks of stable spontaneous seizure activity confirmed by vECoG monitoring, the animals were randomly assigned to receive intrahippocampal injections of cfos-EGFP or cfos-Nrf2. Although cfos-Nrf2 did not fully abolish seizures in this chronic setting (Fig. 1d), it significantly reduced the cumulative seizure burden compared with control animals (Fig. 1d, inset). Importantly, the cfos promoter remained responsive to spontaneous seizure activity in the chronic phase, driving activity-dependent Nrf2 expression (supplementary figure deposited at Figshare; 10.6084/m9.figshare.31343032). These findings indicate that while early intervention yields more pronounced therapeutic effects, delayed administration shows efficacy, highlighting the translational potential of temporally restricted, activity-dependent gene therapy for drug-resistant epilepsy.

Genetic and pharmacodynamic evidence robustly positions Nrf2 as a viable therapeutic axis3; although agents such as dimethyl fumarate confer neuroprotection and seizure attenuation, their systemic administration leads to widespread Nrf2 activation, which can disrupt physiological redox signaling and produce off-target effects, revealing the inherent constraints of systemic pharmacotherapy5. In contrast, cfos-Nrf2 gene therapy enables transient, seizure-triggered antioxidant defenses restricted to excitatory neurons, thereby circumventing off-target effects and preserving physiological redox signaling. Together, these findings demonstrate that activity-dependent Nrf2 induction activates protective pathways, mitigates seizures, and sustains cognitive integrity. While clinical translation will require further refinement and rigorous safety profiling, this paradigm addresses key limitations of conventional approaches and supports the development of adaptive, disease-modifying interventions for epileptogenesis.
