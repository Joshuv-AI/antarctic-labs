Single-cell transcriptomics unveils pyroptosis-related immune microenvironment dynamics and prognostic modeling in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Hui Zou, Qinglin Ren, Jun Wu, Jiansheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal cancer remains a leading cause of global cancer mortality with limited therapeutic efficacy. This study aims to characterize pyroptosis-related immune mechanisms in esophageal cancer and develop a clinical prediction model to improve prognostic evaluation.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing data from seven esophageal cancer tissues and one normal control were integrated to construct a cellular atlas. Immune subpopulations were isolated, and pyroptosis-associated differentially expressed genes were identified. We intersected these genes with pyroptosis-related genes(PRG), identifying 13 key pyroptosis genes. Computational analyses included pyroptosis scoring, subcluster re-analysis, pathway enrichment, and intercellular communication mapping. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was applied to high-pyroptosis immune subsets. A clinical prognostic model incorporating PRG was validated using bulk sequencing data, followed by immune infiltration quantification and chemotherapy response assessment.ResultsBy integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic datasets, we found that pyroptosis-related genes were enriched in immune-inflammatory pathways in ESCC immune cells. B cells showed extensive interactions within the ESCC immune microenvironment. The identified pyroptosis-related genes appeared to be involved in immune-cell state transitions and intercellular communication. A two-gene pyroptosis-related prognostic model based on GSDMB and CYCS showed moderate predictive performance in the TCGA training cohort. However, its performance in the external GSE53625 validation cohort was limited, indicating that the model should be interpreted as an exploratory prognostic signature rather than a clinically established prediction tool. A nomogram integrating the risk score with pathological stage was further constructed to explore its potential clinical applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04474-2.Keywords: Pyroptosis-related genes, Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, Single-cell transcriptomics, Prognosis

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) ranks as the 11th most common cancer globally and is the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. The two primary histological subtypes are esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) [2]. The most common histological subtype of EC in China is ESCC, and China accounts for 53% of global esophageal cancer cases [3]. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, most ESCC patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a poor 5-year overall survival rate of less than 30% [4]. Identifying novel prognostic biomarkers or developing an integrated prediction model is crucial for improving clinical outcomes in ESCC, as current tools for routine practice remain inadequate [5–7].

Pyroptosis, an inflammasome-mediated form of programmed cell death, involves rapid cell expansion leading to membrane rupture and release of cellular contents, which triggers a strong inflammatory response [8, 9]. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of pyroptosis in cancer prognosis, influencing tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. ESCC is increasingly recognized for its complex tumor microenvironment (TME), which contains diverse, highly specialized immune cells with dynamic transcriptional profiles [10]. These immune cells, with heterogeneous phenotypes and functions, play key roles in tumor development and are associated with patient prognosis. The Pyroptosis-Related Genes (PRGs) signature has recently been identified as a prognostic marker in ovarian [11], gastric [12], and lung adenocarcinoma [13]. A newly constructed model based on PRGs was found to be associated with the prognosis and immune microenvironment of ESCC, providing new insights for constructing prognostic models [14]. Another pioneering prognostic signature for ESCC, developed using four carefully selected PRGs, accurately predicts clinical outcomes and aids in the tailored selection of therapeutic strategies [15].

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing (scRNA-Seq) provides a detailed view of gene expression at the single-cell level, allowing for high-resolution analysis of cellular heterogeneity, development, and differentiation across various systems, offer a powerful means to explore the molecular landscape of individual cells [16]. This technology is particularly valuable for identifying distinct cell subtypes and revealing the complexity of tumor subpopulations, revealing new therapeutic targets for future therapies [17].

This study constructs a clinical prediction model for ESCC based on PRGs, which is associated with prognosis and the immune microenvironment in ESCC. The model may provide guidance for clinical treatment and offers a new perspective on predicting ESCC prognosis.

Materials and methods

Data download

ESCC scRNA-seq data GSE188900 was downloaded from the GEO database [18]. This dataset comprises 8 samples, including 7 tumor samples from ESCC patients and 1 normal control. Importantly, all patients were treatment-naïve, meaning they had not received any prior chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy before sample collection [18]. Clinical and gene expression data for 130 ESCC patients and 34 normal controls were sourced from TCGA database. Additionally, bulk RNA sequencing data for ESCC (GSE53625), comprising 179 ESCC patients and 179 normal controls, were downloaded from the GEO database [19].

Quality control and clustering dimension reduction

The Seurat R package [20] (version 4.2.0) was utilized to merge and normalize the scRNA-seq data. Cells exhibiting over 20% mitochondrial gene expression were filtered out. Additionally, cells with fewer than 200 counts were excluded to eliminate low-quality cells and empty droplets, while those with over 2,000 genes—indicative of doublet cells—were also removed for subsequent analyses. We normalized the sequencing depth using the “Normalize Data” function, applying the “Log Normalize” method. Variable features were identified using the ‘vest’ method within the ‘FindVariableFeatures’ function, yielding 4,000 features. Data scaling was performed with “ScaleData” to mitigate the effects of sequencing depth. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to identify significant principal components, from which we selected 20 PCs for t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) for dimensionality reduction. The “FindNeighbors” function was executed with default parameters, using the 20 PCs to construct a K-nearest neighbor graph based on Euclidean distance in PCA space. Subsequently, the “FindClusters” function clustered the cells into 26 distinct groups. Dimensionality reduction was finalized using the RunTSNE function. Cell clusters were annotated via the singleR package (version 3.8.1) [21], resulting in the identification of seven cell types: T cells, B cells, epithelial cells, monocytes, iPS cells, smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (adjusted P-value < 0.05), and the most significant differentially expressed genes were visualized in a heatmap generated by the DoHeatmap function.

Identification of differentially expressed genes of PRGs

A total of 66 candidate PRGs were initially collated, including 6 genes from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) with a relevance score > 7 for the keyword ‘pyroptosis’, 27 genes from the Molecular Signature Database (MsigDB, version 7.4) for Reactome pyroptosis, and 33 genes from previous literature reports. After removing 15 duplicate genes found across these datasets, a final set of 51 unique PRGs was included in this study. We intersected the differentially expressed genes from TCGA-ESCA data with the marker genes of immune cell subpopulations exhibiting high AUCell scores in the single-cell dataset.

AUCell analysis

We used the AUCell package (version 1.5.5) in R to analyze the enrichment of pyroptosis-related genes at the single-cell level. We classified cells into high and low Area Under the Curve (AUC) score groups based on an activation threshold (AUC Score = 0.18) calculated using the AUCell_exploreThresholds function. We then used the “FindMarkers” function to identify differentially expressed genes between the high and low pyroptosis-score immune cells, with the filtering criteria set to P < 0.05 and |avg_log2FC|>0.5.

Sub-cluster reclustering

Based on AUCell scores, we extracted the high-scoring cell subsets and performed reclustering using the same method. We selected 20 principal components for analysis, resulting in 14 sub-clusters. Similarly, we used the “FindAllMarkers” function to identify differentially expressed genes between cell subtypes, with the filtering criteria set to P < 0.05. We visualized the expression of marker genes in high-scoring sub-clusters using violin plots.

Cell–cell communication analysis

R package ‘CellChat’ (version 1.1.3) was used to perform cell-cell communication analysis. We visualized the number and strength of interactions using circular plots, and the key ligand-receptor pairs involved in signaling between endothelial cells and immune cells were visualized using bubble plots.

Pseudotime analysis

Pseudotime cell developmental trajectory analysis was analyzed by Monocle 2 package to explore the developmental sequence of the cell clusters from a dynamic perspective. Based on the pseudo-time trajectory, explore the branch points in the development trajectory. The “plot_genes_branched_pseudotime” function was used to the differences in expression trends of PRGs at branch points.

TCGA-ESCA differential expression analysis

The DESeq2 R package was used to perform analysis of differential expression, with an adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05. Genes with a logFC > 0.5 were considered up-regulated and down-regulated if the logFC were < 0.5. The results of the differential expression analysis were visualized with a heatmap using the R package Pheatmap. The differentially expresssed genes were intersected with differentially expressed genes in TCGA ESCA, and the marker genes of high AUCell-score immune cell subpopulations in TCGA and single-cell data databases were obtained.

Analysis of functional enrichment

Using the clusterProfiler R package, we performed Gene Ontology(GO) annotation (see Tables 1 and 2) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes(KEGG)pathway (see Table 2) enrichment analysis on The clusterProfiler package of R software was utilized to perform GO function and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between the high/low AUCell score groups, as well as on the intersecting differentially expressed genes between the TCGA-ESCA data and the marker genes of highAUCell score group, an FDR p-value 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction of prognostic model

To investigate the role of differentially expressed PRGs in esophageal cancer, we conducted univariate Cox regression on 13 PRGs and identified two genes significantly associated with survival (P < 0.05). Using the TCGA-ESCA dataset as a training set and the GSE53625 dataset as a validation set, we applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to construct a risk model based on gene expression levels. The risk score, calculated using LASSO-Cox regression coefficients, was used to divide patients into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were performed to compare overall survival (OS) between the groups, while time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the model’s predictive accuracy, with the AUC calculated for further validation. The model’s performance was confirmed using the validation dataset, incorporating clinical data to ensure robust survival prediction.

Construction of nomogram model

To determine whether the risk score’s predictive power for OS is independent of other clinical characteristics (such as age, sex, and pathological stage), Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of clinical variables were performed using 129 esophageal cancer samples with available clinical data from the TCGA database(see Table 1). Subsequently, the nomogram was constructed, and the clinical pathological staging was incorporated into the nomogram for risk prediction. The Harrell concordance index (C-index) was measured to quantify the discrimination performance of the nomograms. Additionally, A calibration curve was generated by comparing the predicted value from the nomogram with the actual survival to assess the performance of the nomogram.

Immune infiltration analysis of the prognostic model

The TCGA-ESCA gene expression matrix (TPM, Transcripts Per Kilobase Million) was uploaded to the CIBERSORT by employing the LM22 signature.

Filtered out samples with P < 0.05, and developed the immune cell infiltration matrix. The ggplot2 package was used to draw bar graphs to show the distribution of 22 immune cell infiltrations in each sample.

Drug sensitivity analysis of the prognostic model

The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (www.cancerrxgene.org) can be used to search for tumor drug response data and genome sensitive markers. Using the pRRophetic algorithm, A ridge regression model based on the CCLE cell line expression profile and the TCGA-ESCA gene expression profile was constructed to predict the sensitivity of high- and low-risk groups to common anticancer drugs using IC50 values.

Correlation between prognostic pyroptosis-related gene expression and immune checkpoints

Using the TCGA esophageal cancer samples, we analyzed the correlation between the expression of prognostic pyroptosis-related genes and common immune checkpoints (CD27, TIGHT, LAIR1, CD96, BTLA, CTLA4, PDCD1, ICOS, CD44, CD276, CD274, TNFSF18, LGALS9, TNFSF15, HHLA2, TNFRSF14, etc.). Scatter plots were generated for the most significant correlations. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was estimated using the unpaired Student t-tests and Wilcoxon tests for normally distributed variables and non-normally distributed variables, respectively. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variable comparisons. For multiple group comparisons, Statistical significance was analyzed Kruskal–Wallis test.

Scoring of single-cell pyroptosis

We employed the AUCell algorithm to score cells based on the expression of key PRGs in a single-cell dataset, using AUCell explore Thresholds to calculate the activation threshold (AUC = 0.18). Cells were classified into high and low scoring groups according to this threshold (Fig. 4A). First, we visualized the AUC scores of various immune cells using a t-SNE plot (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, we identified differentially expressed genes between the high and low scoring cell groups using the “FindMarkers” function, with selection criteria of P < 0.05 and abs(avg_log2FC) > 0.5. We intersected the differentially expressed genes with 51 pyroptosis-related genes, identifying 24 genes associated with pyroptosis. GO analysis of these 24 genes revealed significant enrichment in biological processes (BP) such as leukocyte chemotaxis, T cell migration, granulocyte chemotaxis, neutrophil migration, and antimicrobial humoral response. Cellular components (CC) were primarily enriched in cytosolic part, specific granule lumen, vesicle lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, and inflammasome complex, while molecular functions (MF) were predominantly enriched in peptidase activator activity, growth factor receptor binding, receptor ligand activity, cytokine activity, and chemokine activity (Fig. 4C; Tables 3 and 4). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted pathways related to immune inflammation, including Legionellosis, Necroptosis, Pertussis, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 4D; Table 5).

Analysis of cell -cell communication in pyroptosis-related subpopulations

We used CellChat to infer and quantify the communication between immune cells (Monocytes, B cells, T cells) with high and low pyroptosis scores. The number (Fig. 6A) and strength (Fig. 6B) of cellular communications were visualized through circular plots. It can be observed that immune cells with both high and low pyroptosis scores exhibit a greater number of interactions, with higher interaction strength, among which B cells show the most diverse and complex interactions with other cells. We also compared all significant receptor-ligand pairs during signal emission/reception in high- and low-scoring immune cell groups, using chord diagrams (Fig. 6C) and dotplots (Fig. 6D). The results demonstrated that the MIF-(CD74 + CD44) signaling pathway plays a critical role in the interactions between high-scoring cell groups and immune cells. In addition, we analyzed the secretory cell communication patterns of endothelial and immune cells with high and low pyroptosis scores. Through a heatmap (Fig. 6E), we visualized how endothelial and immune cells with high and low pyroptosis scores coordinate with each other, as well as the specific signaling pathways driving these communications. The results revealed that for high-scoring B cells, the outgoing pathway is MIF, and the incoming pathways are MIF, GALECTIN, and BAFF. For high-scoring monocytes, the outgoing pathways are GALECTIN, BAFF, and CXCL, while the incoming pathways are MIF and GALECTIN. Analysis of immune cell pseudotime based on high pyroptosis scoring

Single-cell sequencing technology provides a powerful tool for further understanding the mechanisms of cancer due to its high resolution; however, cancer development is a dynamic process. In the pseudotime analysis, high-pyroptosis immune cells were further annotated based on canonical immune-cell markers. Cluster 0 was annotated as cytotoxic T cells, cluster 1 as CD4 + cytotoxic T cells, clusters 2, 11, 12, and 13 as B cells, clusters 3, 5, and 14 as regulatory T cells, cluster 7 as CD8 + T cells, cluster 8 as monocytes, and cluster 10 as mast cells. Cluster 9 could not be confidently assigned to a known immune-cell subtype based on the marker genes available in the current analysis and was therefore labeled as “Unknown.” This cluster was retained in the pseudotime analysis to avoid arbitrary exclusion of potentially informative immune cells. Nevertheless, its biological identity remains uncertain, and further annotation using additional reference atlases or experimental validation will be required. The t-SNE plot displays the annotation results for each cell population (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, to analyze the developmental processes of the cell clusters from a dynamic perspective, we performed pseudotime analysis on these cells using the R package Monocle. Our results indicate that the developmental processes between cell clusters do not follow a single pathway; instead, they exhibit a relatively complex landscape, with two branching points dividing the clusters into a near-dumbbell development pattern (Fig. 7B) and allowing for the distinction of five different developmental stages (Fig. 7C). We further illustrated the changes in each cell type during the developmental process (Fig. 7D). Finally, we conducted further analysis on the genes that drive the processes during these five developmental stages (Fig. 7E), revealing that the expression of 42 pyroptosis-related genes changed, thereby extending the cell developmental trajectory into different stages. This further validates the significant impact and importance of pyroptosis on the development of immune cells in esophageal cancer from a dynamic perspective. Analysis of esophageal cancer patients and pyrotosis

We divided the TCGA-ESCA samples into two groups (ESCA and Normal) and initially used the DESeq2 package for differential analysis of sequencing data to identify differentially expressed genes. Genes with logFC > 0.5 and P value < 0.05 were classified as up-regulated (up regulated genes), while genes with logFC < -0.5 and P value < 0.05 were classified as down-regulated (down regulated genes), resulting in 2898 up-regulated genes and 2399 down-regulated genes (Table S1). The heatmap illustrates the expression of 13 key PRGs identified from single-cell data in TCGA tumor samples compared to normal samples (Fig. 8A), while the volcano plot displays the gene expression differences between the two groups (Fig. 8B). Subsequently, we performed differential analysis on the expression of the 13 key pyroptosis-related genes between tumor and normal samples (Fig. 8C), revealing that these genes were significantly overexpressed in tumor samples (AIM2: P = 0.01; GSDMD: P < 0.001; IL1B: P = 0.002; GZMB: P < 0.001; PYCARD: P < 0.001; IRF1: P < 0.001; CASP8: P = 0.001; CHMP4B: P < 0.001; GPX4: P < 0.001; CYCS: P < 0.001; CHMP3: P = 0.033), suggesting that pyroptosis plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of esophageal cancer. Additionally, we intersected the differentially expressed genes obtained from TCGA data with marker genes selected from single-cell data based on pyroptosis scoring, resulting in 551 differentially expressed genes associated with pyroptosis (Fig. 8D). GO analysis of these 551 genes revealed that they are primarily enriched in biological processes (BP) such as neutrophil-mediated immunity, T cell activation, positive regulation of cytokine production, regulation of lymphocyte activation, regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway, and leukocyte migration. In terms of MF, the genes are mainly enriched in chemokine receptor binding, integrin binding, RAGE receptor binding, chemokine activity, and cytokine activity. For CC, they are predominantly associated with cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, focal adhesion, tertiary granules, coated vesicles, and endocytic vesicles (Fig. 8F; Table 2). KEGG analysis results indicate that these genes are significantly enriched in pathways such as Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Apoptosis, Chemokine signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, Necroptosis, and Natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Fig. 8F; Table 3). These pathways and functions are all related to immunity and cell death.

Construction and validation of a pyroptosis gene scoring model

We established a pyroptosis-related gene scoring model using TCGA-ESCA as the training cohort and GSE53625 as the external validation cohort. Univariate Cox regression identified two survival-associated genes, GSDMB and CYCS, which were subsequently incorporated into a LASSO-Cox prognostic model. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. In the TCGA training cohort, the time-dependent ROC analysis yielded AUC values of 0.701, 0.607, and 0.645 for 2-, 3-, and 4-year overall survival, respectively, suggesting moderate predictive performance. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients in the low-risk group had significantly better overall survival than those in the high-risk group (P = 0.019). In the external GSE53625 validation cohort, the corresponding AUC values were 0.560, 0.561, and 0.545 for 2-, 3-, and 4-year overall survival, respectively. Although the Kaplan–Meier curve showed a trend toward better survival in the low-risk group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.076). Therefore, the external validation results suggest that the prognostic model has limited generalizability across independent datasets. These findings indicate that the two-gene model may capture pyroptosis-related prognostic signals in ESCC, but its clinical predictive utility requires further validation in larger, harmonized, and prospectively collected cohorts.

Construction of pyroptosis associated clinical prognostic model

We integrated pyroptosis related risk scores with different clinical pathological features based on the TCGA-ECSA dataset, starting with univariate and multivariate Cox analyses. The results of the univariate forest plot indicated that both necroptosis risk scores and tumor pathological stage are risk factors (Figs. 9 and 10A). We then constructed a predictive nomogram to estimate OS for esophageal cancer patients (Fig. 11B). We utilized the C-Index to calculate the discriminatory ability of the nomogram, which demonstrated a high concordance (concordant = 0.686). Calibration curves indicated a good agreement between the nomogram’s estimated OS values for 1, 2, and 3 years and the actual observed values of the patients (Fig. 9C). The results of the decision curve analysis (DCA) showed that this prognostic model outperformed the extreme line within a certain range, and that the prognostic model incorporating necroptosis risk scores had better efficacy than one using only pathological staging (Fig. 10D). The risk score was constructed based on the LASSO coefficients. Specifically, CYCS functions as a risk factor, where its upregulation contributes to a higher risk score and is associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment. In contrast, GSDMB functions as a protective factor, where its upregulation is associated with increased immune infiltration and a lower risk score.

Analysis of immune infiltration levels in pyroptosis-related clinical prognostic models

To analyze the differences in immune infiltration levels associated with the pyroptosis scoring model in esophageal cancer, we utilized the CIBERSORTx algorithm to calculate the infiltration levels of 22 types of immune cells in the TCGA-ESCA dataset (Fig. 11A). We then analyzed the correlation between two genes and immune scores, finding that GSDMB was significantly positively correlated with the Immune Score (P = 0.005, R = 0.220) (Fig. 11C), while CYCS showed significant negative correlations with the Immune Score (P < 0.001, R=-0.276) (Fig. 11D), Stromal Score (P < 0.001, R=-0.302) (Fig. 11E), and ESTIMATE Score (P < 0.001, R=-0.304) (Fig. 11F). We also analyzed the correlation between two prognosis-related pyroptosis genes and immune cell infiltration in esophageal cancer. We found that CYCS was significantly negatively correlated with the infiltration levels of NK cells (P < 0.001, R=-0.464), Mast cells (P < 0.001, R=-0.375), iDC (P < 0.001, R=-0.347), Cytotoxic cells (P < 0.001, R=-0.327), TFH (P < 0.001, R=-0.313), DC (P < 0.001, R=-0.306), Tcm (P < 0.001, R=-0.296), NK CD56dim cells (P < 0.001, R=-0.296), TReg (P = 0.001, R=-0.254), T cells (P = 0.001, R=-0.253), Macrophages (P = 0.003, R=-0.229), B cells (P = 0.005, R=-0.221), CD8 T cells (P = 0.020, R=-0.183), and Th1 cells (P = 0.034, R=-0.167) (Fig. 11G), suggesting that high expression of CYCS may be associated with the suppression of various immune cells. The expression of GSDMB was positively correlated with the infiltration levels of Th17 cells (P < 0.001, R = 0.581), Eosinophils (P < 0.001, R = 0.553), T helper cells (P < 0.001, R = 0.320), NK CD56dim cells (P < 0.001, R = 0.314), Tem (P < 0.001, R = 0.313), CD8 T cells (P < 0.001, R = 0.303), pDC (P < 0.001, R = 0.298), T cells (P < 0.001, R = 0.284), B cells (P < 0.001, R = 0.265), and Mast cells (P = 0.026, R = 0.175), while it was negatively correlated with Tcm (P < 0.001, R=-0.327), NK cells (P < 0.001, R=-0.306), Tgd (P = 0.009, R=-0.204), NK CD56dim cells (P = 0.016, R=-0.190), and iDC (P = 0.042, R = 0.160) (Fig. 11H). Discussion

Many patients with ESCC are diagnosed at an advanced or metastatic stage, making prognosis challenging. For locally advanced cases, surgery alone has not produced satisfactory outcomes [22]., Traditional histopathological prognostic factors, such as tumor size, stage, and grade, often fail to fully address diagnostic and prognostic needs in ESCC [23], To improve prognostic stratification in ESCC, we developed a pyroptosis-related risk score based on GSDMB and CYCS. In the TCGA training cohort, this model showed moderate predictive performance and was associated with overall survival. However, the external validation performance in GSE53625 was limited, with relatively low AUC values for 2-, 3-, and 4-year survival and a Kaplan–Meier log-rank P-value that did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, the prognostic model should not be interpreted as a fully validated clinical tool at this stage. Instead, it should be regarded as an exploratory pyroptosis-related signature that requires further optimization and validation.

Several factors may explain the reduced performance in the external cohort, including differences in sequencing platforms, sample composition, clinical annotation, treatment background, batch effects, and cohort-specific biological heterogeneity. In addition, the use of only two genes may restrict model complexity and reduce its ability to capture the full prognostic heterogeneity of ESCC. Future studies should validate this signature in additional ESCC-specific cohorts and may consider integrating clinical variables, immune features, and additional pyroptosis-related genes to improve model robustness and generalizability.When integrated with pathological staging into a nomogram, the combined model demonstrated strong clinical efficacy, aligning with findings from previous studies [14, 24]. Models with high pyroptosis-related risk scores are strongly associated with poor prognosis in esophageal cancer. This is supported by single-cell immune cell analyses, which reveal elevated expression of 13 key PRGs in tumor cells. The significance of this elevated expression lies in its role in promoting an inflammatory tumor microenvironment. Pyroptosis, as a form of proinflammatory cell death, fosters conditions favorable to tumor growth, thereby exacerbating disease progression and leading to a worse prognosis. This explanation is consistent with findings from previous studies, which have confirmed the tumor-promoting effects of pyroptosis [25, 26].

In our study, AUCell scores based on 13 pyroptosis-related genes were used to classify immune cells into high and low expression groups. Immune cells with higher pyroptosis gene expression exhibited more frequent and stronger interactions. The MIF-(CD74 + CD44) signaling pathway played a crucial role in communication between high-scoring immune cells, with B cells showing the most complex interactions. Tumor-infiltrating B lymphocytes (TIL-Bs) were significantly elevated in responders to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy for ESCC [27]and were associated with improved outcomes in patients receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 treatment [28]. Additionally, cancer-derived HMGB1 promoted angiogenesis by driving proliferating B cells toward proangiogenic signals, further contributing to disease progression [29].

We found that patients with high pyroptosis-related gene risk scores exhibited greater sensitivity to drugs such as CCT007093, LFM-A13, Lenalidomide, JNJ-26,854,165, VX-702, AZD6482, KIN001-135, AP-24,534, and IPA-3 compared to the low-score group. This discovery offers new insights for the development of chemotherapy drugs for esophageal cancer. Future experiments based on these findings could potentially lead to breakthroughs in esophageal cancer treatment.

Emerging evidence suggests that pyroptosis, a newly recognized form of programmed cell death, plays a crucial role in both tumor development and response to cancer therapies [30, 31]. Our findings align with these observations, showing that PRGs in ESCC immune cells are significantly enriched in key immune and inflammatory pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, apoptosis, chemokine signaling, TNF signaling, B cell receptor signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, NOD-like receptor signaling, IL-17 signaling, necroptosis, and natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity. This indicates that PRGs are closely intertwined with the immune microenvironment in ESCC.

Our findings provide potential guidance for clinical immunotherapy strategies in ESCC. Specifically, the correlation analysis revealed that GSDMB (a protective factor associated with the low-risk group) was significantly positively correlated with critical immune checkpoints, including PD-L1 (CD274), CTLA4, and LGALS9. High expression of PD-L1 is a widely accepted biomarker for response to Immune Checkpoint Blockade (ICB). Therefore, patients in the low-risk group, characterized by high GSDMB expression and a ‘hot’ immune microenvironment enriched with CD8 + T cells, may derive greater clinical benefit from immunotherapy. Conversely, the high-risk group (associated with high CYCS) exhibited a negative correlation with immune checkpoints and lower immune infiltration, indicative of an immunosuppressive ‘cold’ tumor phenotype. For these high-risk patients, immunotherapy alone might be insufficient, and the specific chemotherapeutic agents identified in our drug sensitivity analysis (e.g., inhibitors targeting specific pathways shown in Fig. 12) might offer a more effective therapeutic alternative. In addition, cluster 9 in the pseudotime analysis remained unannotated. Although alternative marker-based annotation was attempted using canonical immune-cell markers, this cluster did not show a sufficiently specific marker profile for confident assignment to a recognized immune-cell subtype. This may reflect a transitional immune-cell state, low-quality or mixed transcriptional features, or limitations of reference-based annotation in the ESCC immune microenvironment. Because cluster 9 participated in the inferred pseudotime trajectory, we retained it in the analysis but interpreted it cautiously. Future studies using larger single-cell datasets, multi-reference annotation strategies, and protein-level validation will be needed to clarify the identity and functional relevance of this cluster.

This study has several limitations. First, the conclusions are primarily based on bioinformatic analyses and require validation through in vivo and in vitro experiments. Another limitation of this study is the imbalance between tumor and normal samples in the single-cell RNA-seq dataset. The scRNA-seq analysis included seven ESCC tumor samples but only one normal control sample. Although this dataset allowed us to characterize tumor-associated immune heterogeneity and pyroptosis-related immune-cell states at single-cell resolution, the limited number of normal controls may introduce bias when comparing tumor and non-tumor cell populations. Inter-individual heterogeneity in the single normal sample may also influence the identification of differentially expressed genes and marker genes. Therefore, the single-cell findings should be interpreted with caution, and validation using larger scRNA-seq datasets with balanced tumor and normal samples is needed. Third, although the pyroptosis-related prognostic model showed moderate performance in the TCGA training cohort, its performance in the external GSE53625 validation cohort was limited. Therefore, the model should be considered exploratory and requires further validation in larger, independent, and clinically harmonized ESCC cohorts. Finally, one immune cluster in the pseudotime analysis could not be confidently annotated, and its biological identity requires further investigation.
