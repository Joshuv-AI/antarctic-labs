En face OCT in branch retinal artery occlusion: quantitative assessment of epiretinal macrophage-like cells and optical intensity ratio
Authors: Yunkao Zeng, Xiangting Chen, Yue Zhou, Wanni Chen, Xuelin Chen, Yan Zhu, Feng Wen, Ruiming Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIn branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO), the regional distribution of epiretinal macrophage-like cells (eMLC) and the objective assessment of ischemic tissue alterations using en face optical coherence tomography (OCT) have not been characterized. This study aimed to quantify eMLC and the optical intensity ratio (OIR) on en face OCT in acute BRAO.MethodsThis retrospective study included 17 patients with unilateral acute BRAO. En face OCT was acquired using a 3 × 3 mm scan pattern centered on the fovea. Structural en face images from a 3-µm slab above the inner limiting membrane were used to identify and quantify eMLC, while slabs at the retinal nerve fiber layer, superficial capillary plexus (SCP), and deep capillary plexus (DCP) were used to measure optical intensity and calculate OIR. eMLC density and OIR were compared between affected and unaffected regions.ResultseMLC density was significantly higher in BRAO eyes than in contralateral eyes across all regions, including the global macula, the affected region and the unaffected region (all p ≤ 0.001). Further analysis showed that eMLC density was higher in the affected region than in the unaffected region (p = 0.015). Global SCP OIR in BRAO eyes was comparable to contralateral eyes (p = 0.113), but global DCP OIR was lower (p = 0.001). OIR in the affected region was higher than in the unaffected region for both SCP and DCP (both p < 0.001). Compared with contralateral eyes, DCP OIR was higher in the affected region (p = 0.013) but lower in the unaffected region (p < 0.001).ConclusionsEn face OCT enables simultaneous assessment of cellular and ischemic changes in acute BRAO, revealing presumed eMLC aggregation in the affected region and a paradoxical OIR pattern. This technique offers a noninvasive platform for evaluating ischemic neuroinflammation, though the OIR findings warrant further investigation.

Introduction

Retinal artery occlusion is an ophthalmic emergency that can cause severe ischemia of the inner retina [1, 2]. Depending on the location of the occlusion, it can be classified into central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO), branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO), and cilioretinal artery occlusion [3]. BRAO is an acute obstruction of a branch of the central retinal artery, resulting in segmental inner retinal ischemia [4]. It accounts for approximately one-third of all acute retinal artery occlusions [5]. Although reperfusion occurs in most cases, infarction of the inner retina may still lead to permanent vision loss, primarily due to secondary reperfusion injury following the restoration of blood supply [6, 7]. The structural and cellular changes induced by this injury are the pathological basis of vision loss.

Inner retinal ischemia induces significant structural and cellular changes. As the retina is an extension of the central nervous system, retinal artery occlusion represents a form of focal cerebral stroke, with secondary neuroinflammation as a key driver of inner retinal injury [8, 9]. Thus, as a visible neural tissue, the retina may offer insights into local neuroimmune responses following acute ischemia. En face OCT is able to detect ischemic changes in different layers of the retina. Previous studies have reported that ischemia causes hyperreflective changes in the inner retina, especially in the inner nuclear layer [10–13]. The extent of these hyperreflective changes appears as a whitening patch on en face OCT images and may be quantified as optical intensity ratios (OIR) to reflect the severity of ischemia [13–15]. Additionally, en face OCT can visualize structures interpreted as macrophage-like cells (MLCs) above the inner limiting membrane in retinal vascular diseases, including diabetic retinopathy, central retinal artery occlusion and retinal vein occlusion [16–20]. Therefore, en face OCT offers a noninvasive platform to simultaneously assess regional tissue alterations and neuroinflammatory cell dynamics in retinal vascular disorders.

Using en face OCT, we found a generalized increase in macular epiretinal MLC (eMLC) density in acute CRAO [16]. In retinal vein occlusion, we found that eMLCs are lower in the occlusive-affected region than in the unaffected region, indicating that eMLCs are mainly recruited in regions with better perfusion [17]. However, en face OCT studies in BRAO remain limited, with most being case reports [10–12]. Specifically, no study has characterized the regional distribution of eMLC between affected and unaffected regions, nor has the severity of ischemia been quantitatively assessed in the en face OCT images of BRAO. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the regional and quantitative changes in eMLC and OIR on en face OCT in patients with acute BRAO.

Materials and methods

Participants

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study involving patients with acute nonarteritic unilateral reperfused BRAO at their first visit between October 2021 and October 2022 to Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, China. In the present study, reperfused BRAO was defined as either (1) fluorescein angiographic evidence of complete arterial filling in the affected branch, or (2) the complete absence of any non‑perfused area on standardized OCT angiography in the affected vascular territory. This definition aligns with the international consensus that OCTA parameters, particularly the presence or absence of non‑perfusion areas, are critical for characterizing ischemic retinal conditions [21, 22]. The time from symptom onset to OCT examination was recorded; only patients imaged within 7 days after onset were included. The unaffected fellow eyes served as controls. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (2022KYPJ096). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All patients underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, including intraocular pressure measurement, autorefractometry, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), slit-lamp fundus examination, OCT angiography, and en face OCT. Exclusion criteria were: (1) refractive error > 3 diopters; (2) any other inflammatory retinal or choroidal disease (e.g., uveitis, white dot syndrome); (3) history of other ocular vascular disease; (4) history of ocular trauma, surgery, or steroid treatment; (5) poor-quality OCT images, defined as either (1) device‑generated Scan Quality Index < 6/10 on the AngioVue system, or (2) presence of obvious artifacts (e.g. motion artifacts causing stripes or discontinuities, projection artifacts from overlying retinal vessels, segmentation failures, media opacities, or blurriness of vascular features) [23]. All quality assessments were performed by a single grader (Y.K.Z.) masked to clinical data.

OCT angiography (OCTA) and en face OCT imaging

Macular parameters were obtained using the AngioVue OCT Angiography system (RTVue-XR Avanti; Optovue, Fremont, CA, USA). The high-density 3 × 3 mm macular scan protocol centered on the fovea was used to measure vessel density (VD) in the superficial (SCP) and deep capillary plexus (DCP). The SCP was defined as the slab extending from the inner limiting membrane (ILM) to 10 μm above the inner plexiform layer (IPL) [24, 25]. The DCP was defined as the slab extending from 10 μm above the IPL to 10 μm below the outer plexiform layer (OPL) [24, 25]. For patients with poor visual acuity, an external fixation light was provided when conducting OCTA examination to ensure image quality. After acquisition, en face OCT and OCTA images were automatically generated. VD in the macular region was automatically calculated by the AngioVue software. Retinal segmentation was manually checked and corrected when necessary.

eMLC isolation and quantification

To visualize eMLCs, a 3-µm slab above the inner limiting membrane (ILM) was manually adjusted [25]. The retinal nerve fiber layer was also segmented for reference as previously described [26]. Using these two slabs, eMLCs were isolated and quantified via a semiautomated binarization process in Fiji (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) following previously described methods [16, 26, 27]. Briefly, the binarization procedure included noise reduction to remove background irregularities and vessel artifacts, signal enhancement to improve cell identification, and thresholding to delineate cell profiles [16]. The “Analyze Particles” function in Fiji was then applied to count eMLCs. All quantifications were performed by a single masked grader blinded to clinical and imaging data.

Regional definitions and eMLC density calculation

The affected and unaffected regions were manually delineated using Fiji. The en face OCT slab of the deep capillary plexus (DCP) was used for delineation because this layer is highly sensitive to ischemic changes and shows characteristic hyperreflective whitening [10–13]. The hyperreflective area was defined as the affected region, while the remaining area within the 3 × 3 mm field served as the unaffected region (Fig. 1A-B). The outline of the affected region was first drawn on the DCP slab and then superimposed onto the SCP slab and the eMLC slab. The areas of the affected and unaffected regions were calculated. For each region, eMLC density was calculated as the number of eMLCs divided by the corresponding regional area (Fig. 1C-D).

Optical intensity and OIR measurement

Optical intensity is defined as the average gray level of selected regions on a scale from 0 (pure black) to 255 (pure white) [28–30]. This value represents the local reflectivity of the tissue and is automatically calculated by Fiji software. Optical intensity can be affected not only by tissue reflectivity but also by other confounding factors, such as signal strength and OCT laser power [30]. Therefore, an optical intensity ratio (OIR) was used to normalize the effect of these confounders [29]. It is a descriptive metric to indicate the severity of ischemia [29]. Using Fiji, mean optical intensity was measured on en face OCT slabs of the global 3 × 3 mm RNFL, SCP, and DCP. It was also measured on the affected and unaffected regions within the SCP and DCP slabs. The RNFL slab served as a reference to normalize for confounding factors. Thus, for each slab and each region, the OIR was calculated as the mean intensity of the region divided by the mean intensity of the RNFL slab. For contralateral healthy eyes, which were not subdivided into affected/unaffected regions, the global OIR of the entire 3 × 3 mm slab was used as the reference for comparison with both the affected and unaffected regional OIRs of BRAO eyes. Of note, the grayscale values were derived from vendor‑processed en face OCT images. Thus, the calculated OIR may be device‑dependent and should be considered an exploratory metric.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed variables as median with interquartile range. For comparisons between BRAO eyes and contralateral healthy eyes, the paired t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed variables. For comparisons between affected and unaffected regions within the same eye, the same paired tests were applied accordingly. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the limited sample size, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Thus, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and ocular structural characteristics

A total of 17 patients with unilateral acute BRAO were enrolled in this study, including 11 males and 6 females. The mean age was 56.06 ± 10.66 years. The mean time from symptom onset to OCT examination was 3.41 ± 1.70 days (range: 1–7 days). In the BRAO eyes, the logMAR BCVA were: 0.359 ± 0.249, range (0.097–1.000). In the contralateral healthy eyes, the logMAR BCVA were 0.029 ± 0.065, range (-0.079 to 0.155). The mean Scan Quality Index was 7.47 ± 0.43 (range 6–9) for BRAO eyes and 7.71 ± 0.85 (range 6–9) for fellow eyes, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.392).

Macular vessel density in both the SCP and deep capillary plexus DCP was significantly lower in BRAO eyes than in contralateral healthy eyes (p = 0.001 and p = 0.011, respectively). The median area of the affected region was 3.18 (2.55, 4.54) mm², and that of the unaffected region was 5.82 (4.47, 6.45) mm². eMLC characteristics in the BRAO eyes and fellow eyes

Global macular eMLC density was significantly higher in BRAO eyes than in fellow eyes (p < 0.001). Further regional analysis revealed that eMLC density in both the affected region and the unaffected region of BRAO eyes was significantly higher than the global eMLC density of fellow eyes (both p < 0.001, Fig. 2). Within BRAO eyes, eMLC density in the affected region was higher than both the global macular eMLC density (p = 0.017) and the density in the non‑affected region (p = 0.015). The eMLC density in the non‑affected region was lower than the global macular eMLC density in BRAO eyes (p = 0.022) (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Optical intensity and OIR characteristics

Of note, the most unexpected finding was that global DCP OIR was decreased in BRAO eyes despite a higher regional OIR in the affected area, whereas the unaffected region showed lower OIR compared with contralateral eyes. Regarding optical intensity, there was no significant difference between BRAO eyes and fellow eyes in the RNFL slab (p = 0.943) or the global SCP slab (p = 0.227). However, the global DCP slab showed significantly lower optical intensity in BRAO eyes than in fellow eyes (p = 0.001). Within BRAO eyes, the optical intensity in the affected region was significantly higher than that in the unaffected region for both the SCP and DCP slabs (both p < 0.001).

The global SCP OIR did not differ significantly between BRAO eyes and fellow eyes (p = 0.113). However, the global DCP OIR was significantly lower in BRAO eyes than in fellow eyes (p = 0.001). When comparing regional OIRs in BRAO eyes with fellow eyes, the global OIR of the fellow eye was used as the reference for both the affected and unaffected regional OIRs of BRAO eyes. Regional analysis within BRAO eyes revealed that OIR in the affected region was significantly higher than in the unaffected region for both the SCP and DCP slabs (both p < 0.001). OIR in the affected region was also significantly higher than in the global macular region for both the SCP and DCP slabs (both p < 0.001). When compared with contralateral eyes, the OIR in the SCP affected region was significantly higher (p < 0.001), whereas the OIR in the SCP unaffected region was significantly lower (p = 0.006). The OIR in the DCP affected region was significantly higher (p = 0.013), whereas the OIR in the DCP unaffected region was significantly lower (p < 0.001). (Table 2; Fig. 3). Discussion

In this study, we used en face OCT to quantify eMLC density and optical intensity ratio (OIR) in acute unilateral BRAO. eMLC density was higher in BRAO eyes than in contralateral eyes, especially in the affected regions. Global DCP OIR was lower in BRAO eyes, whereas global SCP OIR did not differ significantly. OIR in both SCP and DCP was higher in the affected region than in the unaffected region. Compared with contralateral eyes, SCP OIR and DCP OIR were higher in the affected region but lower in the unaffected region. These findings demonstrate that en face OCT captures what may represent immune cell aggregation and distinct optical intensity patterns after acute retinal ischemia, offering a noninvasive view of the heterogeneous tissue response in BRAO.

In line with our previous observations in CRAO and RVO [16, 17], the present study found a significant increase in eMLC density in acute BRAO, with a predilection for the affected region. In CRAO, we reported a generalized increase in macular eMLC density that correlated with the severity of ischemia [16]. In BRVO, we observed higher eMLC density in unaffected region than in affected region, suggesting that better perfusion facilitates immune cell recruitment [17]. This apparent discrepancy between BRAO and BRVO can be explained by differences in hemodynamics and immune cell delivery, as discussed below. First, it is important to understand the potential cellular origin of eMLCs characterized by en face OCT. Under normal conditions, the majority of eMLCs visualized on en face OCT are thought to be retinal microglia, with minor contributions from vitreous hyalocytes and perivascular macrophages [31, 32]. Under inflammatory conditions, however, their major cellular composition may shift to monocytes and monocyte‑derived macrophages [31].

Circulating monocytes/macrophages may be recruited to the inflamed tissue via diapedesis. As reviewed by Muller, the inflammatory response recruits leukocytes through post‑capillary venules, where the sequential steps of rolling, adhesion, and transendothelial migration take place [33, 34]. When venous occlusion occurs, blood stasis develops and the endothelium becomes compromised, which in turn may restrict the delivery of circulating monocytes to the ischemic territory. This situation worsens with the formation of non‑perfused region, when most endothelial cell dropout occurs [17]. Alternatively, macrophages/microglia are less likely to aggregate in ischemic regions because their activation may depend critically on endothelial cell glycolysis [35]. In contrast to BRVO, BRAO is characterized by acute arterial occlusion followed by spontaneous or therapeutic reperfusion in most cases [7, 36]. Therefore, the venous and capillary beds may remain relatively more intact than RVO. In our previous study on retinal vein occlusion, most patients exhibited capillary non‑perfusion [17], but none of the 17 patients in the present study showed any non‑perfused regions on OCTA. Consequently, circulating immune cells could be delivered to the affected region via the venous system in our cohort. Moreover, the acute ischemic insult upregulates chemotactic signals that may actively recruit monocytes/macrophages and activate resident microglia in the affected region [37, 38]. This may explain why eMLC density in the BRAO affected region was higher than in the unaffected region. Together, these findings highlight that the pattern of eMLC aggregation differs fundamentally between arterial and venous occlusive diseases, reflecting their distinct hemodynamic and inflammatory profiles.

Beyond the topographic differences in eMLC aggregation, the OIR findings in BRAO eyes presented an apparent paradox that warrants careful interpretation. In the present study, global DCP OIR was significantly decreased in BRAO eyes compared with contralateral eyes. Regional analysis revealed that OIR within the affected region was actually higher than in the unaffected region. Compared with contralateral eyes, DCP optical intensity and OIR were higher in the affected region but lower in the unaffected region. The unaffected region is structurally normal, so its reflectivity should be comparable to that of the corresponding area in contralateral eyes. Even if we assume that this region also suffers from ischemic stress leading to structural changes, one would expect mildly increased reflectivity (and thus a slightly higher OIR), not a decrease. Therefore, the observed lower OIR in the non‑affected region might not be explained by biological alterations alone, and while alternative explanations such as mild subclinical structural changes cannot be entirely excluded, we propose a technical explanation related to en face OCT image generation.

En face OCT images are reconstructed from volumetric scans by projecting OCT signal intensities between segmentation boundaries to generate two‑dimensional grayscale images (0-255) [39]. When an affected region with markedly increased reflectivity is present, it may become the brightest signal within the entire 3 × 3 mm field. In digital image processing, it is common practice to perform global contrast stretching, mapping the brightest signal to white and adjusting the entire grayscale range accordingly [40, 41]. As a result, signals from the normal-appearing unaffected region are compressed into lower grayscale values, appearing darker than in contralateral eyes where no such high-reflectivity lesion exists [41]. Because the unaffected region occupies the majority of the 3 × 3 mm macular field (Table 1), its reduced OIR dominates the global average and may contribute to the global DCP OIR reduction despite the focal increase in the affected region. This averaging effect, combined with device induced contrast compression, explains why global and regional OIRs point in opposite directions. We speculate that global contrast stretching or automatic gain adjustment during en face OCT reconstruction may contribute to this paradoxical pattern. However, direct evidence is lacking, and alternative explanations remain possible. Given these uncertainties, our OIR findings should be considered hypothesis‑generating and exploratory. Validation using raw intensity data in future prospective studies is required before OIR can be used as a reliable quantitative biomarker. We acknowledge that the affected region was delineated based on DCP hyperreflectivity, which introduces partial circularity when comparing DCP OIR between regions. Thus, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, this phenomenon does not diminish the clinical value of OIR as a potential biomarker, but rather highlights the need for careful interpretation when comparing global and regional metrics in eyes with focal hyperreflective lesions.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size, and the single‑center, retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Second, the lack of longitudinal follow‑up precludes assessment of the temporal evolution of eMLC and OIR or their association with visual prognosis. Larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm our findings. Third, the use of contralateral healthy eyes as controls may not completely eliminate the influence of systemic vascular factors, as patients with BRAO may have subclinical bilateral vascular abnormalities. Nevertheless, in our cohort, all fellow eyes were confirmed to have normal OCT angiography and no clinically detectable retinal pathology. Fourth, due to the limited sample size, we were unable to perform correlation analyses between eMLC/OIR metrics and visual acuity. Larger cohorts are needed to explore these associations. Fifth, OIR measurements were performed on exported en face images, which may introduce non‑linear grayscale mapping. Therefore, our OIR findings should be viewed as exploratory and device‑dependent. This result require validation using raw intensity data in future studies. Last, requiring high‑quality 3 × 3 mm scans may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusion

En face OCT is a noninvasive tool that simultaneously captures cellular and tissue changes from the same scan. In the current study, we used en face OCT to investigate potential inflammatory changes in acute BRAO. We found aggregation of presumed eMLCs in BRAO, especially in the affected region. The paradoxical decrease in global DCP OIR despite an elevated regional ratio in the affected region may reflect an image processing artifact rather than structural abnormality in the unaffected region. Given that the retina is a visible extension of the CNS, en face OCT‑based assessment of eMLC and OIR in BRAO may serve as exploratory metrics for evaluating ischemic burden and neuroinflammation, offering a potential ocular model for studying local neuroimmune responses after acute retinal ischemia.
