Pulmonary function trajectories in children with symptom-controlled asthma —a 10-year retrospective cohort study in China
Authors: Xiaoling Wei, Min Xue, Bingbing Fan, Yanqin Liu, Jing Wang, Miao Liu, Yun Zhang, Xiang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-term changes in lung function in children with symptom control asthma are rare. To assess the trajectory of pulmonary function in children and analyze the influence factors.MethodsThe effects of gender, age, Body Mass Index (BMI), Immunoglobulin E (IgE), eosinophil count, Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide (FeNO), and so on on pulmonary function pattern were analyzed.ResultsAmong the 1931 children, three predicted FEV1 trajectory clusters were identified: downward trend followed by an upward trend (34.96%), downward trend but close to average (32.88%) and continuous downward trend (32.16%). Among the three different trajectories, the first, second, and third clusters had 432 (7.22%), 210 (3.96%), and 327 (3.12%) detections were below the cut point, respectively. And they differed according to the level of FeNO, the count of lymphocyte, interval between diagnosis of asthma and first onset and onset age at enrollment. For FEV1/FVC% pred, 1633 /1931(84.57%) children showed a saddle-shaped form: a gradual rise at first, then a large fluctuation, and finally returned to normal. Other 298 (15.43%) children showed a gradual continuous upward trend. Among the 1858 patients completed of MEF25% pred, 377 (20.29%) had a higher MEF25% pred, 419 (22.55%) children had a trajectory that near the average level, and 1062 (57.16%) children had a trajectory below the average and then catch up at last. Multiple logistic analysis showed that BMI, lymphocyte count, FeNO, time interval of delayed diagnosis and onset age were associated with the change of lung function.ConclusionSome children with symptom controlled asthma showed a continuous decline in lung function. The decrease of lung function was closely related to BMI, lymphocyte count, FeNO, time interval of delayed diagnosis and onset age.

Introduction

Asthma is one of the most common chronic diseases, affecting an estimated 358 million people worldwide in 2015 [1] and about 14% of children globally [2]. In the latter part of the last century, asthma prevalence has rapidly increased worldwide. About 1 in 12 school-age children has asthma in the United States [3]. In China, the prevalence of asthma in children has increased from 1.09% in 1990 to 2.04% in 2000 to 3.02% in 2010, indicating that it will double almost every 10 years [4]. As a chronic disease, asthma is associated with the incidence of respiratory, cardiovascular, and metabolic abnormalities and all-cause mortality [5, 6]. A previous study reported that the adjusted hazard rate for all-cause mortality in asthma cases versus non-asthma cases was 1.46 (95% CI 1.33 to 1.62) and that the highest rate of increase appeared in children aged 5–15 years [7]. In a word, asthma has become an important disease affecting children’s physical and mental health.

Lung function is used to monitor the incidence rate and therapeutic effect of treatment for asthma. Recent studies have highlighted that early life impairment of lung function growth can lead to the development of asthma in adulthood [8]. A significant number of longitudinal studies have suggested that recurrent respiratory symptoms are associated with poor lung function throughout life [9, 10]. Similarly, evidence shows that impaired lung function at birth and early life persists into adulthood [10, 11] and that respiratory senescence is closely related to early life respiratory morbidity and lung function impairment [9, 11–15]. Therefore, long-term treatment to control asthma symptoms and avoid risk factors for poor outcomes (including lung function) is very important. However, the effects of long term standardized treatment on asthma control and lung function development in children, especially the changes of lung function development trajectory, are rarely reported. In the present study, we prospectively collected clinical data of children diagnosed with asthma in our hospital since 2013 and analyzed the lung function trajectory of 1931 asthmatic children with long-term treatment in 2023. Our objective is to understand the outcomes of asthma and lung function in children over 5 years of age with long-term treatment, and to analyze the major risk factors that affect pulmonary function.

Methods

Definitions and descriptions

Childhood asthma was diagnosed according to the criteria in China [16,17] and treated according to the the guidelines in China [16, 17] and Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines (GINA) [18]. The diagnostic criteria of childhood asthma: (1)Repeated episodes of wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath, and chest tightness are mostly related to exposure to mutants, cold air, physical and chemical stimuli, respiratory tract infections, and exercise, often occurring or exacerbated at night and waking. (2) Doctor hear wheezing during the acute attack. (3)The above symptoms and signs may disappear with anti-asthma treatment or resolve spontaneously. (4) Excluding other diseases that cause these symptoms. (5)Those with atypical clinical manifestations should have at least one of the following items: (a) Positive bronchial provocation test or exercise provocation test. (b) Reversible airflow limitation is confirmed. Those who meet 1–4, or 4 and 5 can be diagnosed).

Definition of symptom control asthma [18]: In the past 4 weeks, none of the following has been shown:1)daytime asthma symptoms more than twice/week, 2) any night waking due to asthma, 3)Short-acting and long-acting β2-agonists (SABA) reliever for symptoms more than twice/week, 4) any activity limitation due to asthma. In this study, all children met the criteria for symptom control at each follow-up visit.

Definition of asthma exacerbation [18]: Excerbations of asthma are episodes characterized by a progressive increase in symptomes of shortness of breath, cough, wheezing or chest tightness and progressive decreases in lung function i.e. they represent a change from the patient’s usual status that is sufficient to require a change in treatment. It include①Mild exacerbation: An exacerbation characterized by a progressive increase in asthma symptoms (e.g., shortness of breath, cough, wheezing, or chest tightness) that requires a temporary change in the patient’s usual treatment (e.g., increased use of reliever medication) but does not require systemic corticosteroids or an unscheduled healthcare visit. ②Moderate exacerbation: An exacerbation that requires the use of systemic corticosteroids (oral or injectable) for at least three days, or an increase from the patient’s usual stable maintenance dose of systemic corticosteroids for at least three days.③Severe exacerbation: An exacerbation that requires hospitalization or an emergency department visit that leads to hospitalization, or requires intensive care unit admission for asthma management. In the present study, children were excluded if they experienced ≥ 1 episodes of moderate-to-severe asthma exacerbation, or ≥ 2 episodes of mild exacerbations.

The cut points of pulmonary function test: We used the cut point in the Chinese guidelines for children’s lung function to identify abnormalities [19]. FEV1/FVCpred% ratio ≥ 92%, FEV1%pred ≥ 80%, and MEF25%pred ≥ 65% were defined as normal. That is to say that the ratio of FEV1/FVCpred% < 92%, FEV1% pred < 80% and MEF25% pred < 65% were below normal.

Study design and population

The clinical data and treatment process of children with asthma over 5 years old treated in Children’s Hospital affiliated to Shandong University (Jinan Children’s Hospital, JCH) since Jan 2013 to June 2023 were retrospectively analyzed. It includes the patient’s basic information (name, gender, age, age at diagnosis of asthma, age at first attack of asthma, etc.), lung function, Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide (FeNO), Immunology E (IgE), and blood cell analysis (include white blood cells, lymphocyte cells and eosinophils, etc.), symptoms and signs of asthma and the level of asthma control. All patients were prescribed anti-inflammatory therapy by a respiratory specialist, including at least ICS and/or other anti-inflammatory drugs, and were followed up regularly. The data was collected every 2–4 months during the follow-up. The process of patient inclusion and exclusion is shown in Fig. 1.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committees in JCH (No: IRB-RP-2022034).

Lung function measurement

We measured pre-bronchodilator lung function every 2–4 months during regular follow-up visits. Children were symptom-free at the time of lung function assessment. SABA and LABA were withheld for at least 4 and 24 h before testing, respectively. All pulmonary function tests were performed by professional technicians in the asthma center in JCH, and the results were confirmed by a pulmonologist. All participants underwent lung function testing with the JAEGER Master Screen (Hoechberg, Germany). At least three acceptable maneuvers were obtained for each participant, and the highest FEV₁ and FVC values from acceptable curves were selected for analysis. The pulmonary function index includes forced vital capacity in percent predicted values (FVC%pred), FEV1%pred, FEV1/FVC%pred ratio, peak expiratory flow in percent predicted values (PEF%pred), peak expiratory flow in percent predicted values of 50% (FEF50%pred), and peak expiratory flow in percent predicted values of 75% (FEF75%pred, MEF25%pred). Given the limited space of the article, FEV1%pred, FEV1%/FVC%pred ratio, and MEF25%pred were used in trajectory analysis.

Blood tests

Venous blood samples were collected from all participants at enrollment or within ± 3 days of the first spirometry examination. For participants who had more than one blood test, the measurement taken closest to the date of the first spirometry was selected for analysis, in order to best reflect the baseline allergic status in relation to subsequent lung function trajectories. Total IgE and allergen-specific IgE were quantitatively measured using the UniCAP fluoroenzymeimmunoassay (FEIA) system (Pharmacia, now Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden). A specific IgE level ≥ 0.35 kU/L was defined as positive. Peripheral blood samples were obtained for complete blood count analysis using a standard automated hematology analyzer as part of routine clinical care.

Statistical analysis

The primary analyses were limited to children who had more than three lung function tests during follow-up. Prior to trajectory analysis, the %predicted values of FEV₁, FEV₁/FVC, and MEF₂₅ were standardized by age (i.e., converted to age-specific within-sample standardised values within the sample) to remove the effect of age and to place the indices on a comparable scale. Model-based methods were used to identify different trajectory patterns of the three indexes. Repeated trajectory analyses were performed to identify the latent classes, and the optimal number of groups was finally determined to be three. The shapes and optimal number of groups were determined by the following criteria: (1) combined with clinical practice, the proportion of each subgroup was not less than 20%; (2) the Bayesian information criterion decreased by at least 20. Model-based methods were performed with EMCluster (version 0.2.13) package in R (version 4.0.3). With lung function as the outcome, multiple logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between gender, BMI, rhinitis and eczema history, allergens, and lung function indexes and so on. The statistical significance level was set to α = 0.05 for all association analyses. Continuous variables conform to normal distribution and are described by mean±standard deviation (SD). Otherwise, they are described by median± interquartile range. Categorical variables are described by frequency (percentage). All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.0.3.

Results

Lung-function trajectory patterns in symptom control asthma children

In the present study, a total of 1931 children with asthma were included. These participants had a total of 16,065 lung function tests. The within-sample standardised values of lung function indexes FEV1%pred, FEV1/FVC%pred, and MEF25%pred were calculated, and a three-trajectory-group solution was found to be the most parsimonious model for every index (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

The trajectory of FEV1%pred consists of three performances. Green line (Group 1, n = 675, 34.96%) represents a saddle-shaped cluster, while both red line (Group 2, n = 635, 32.88%) and blue line (Group 3, n = 621, 32.16%) represent a downward trend, in which red line declines relatively slowly and early, while the blue line declines relatively rapidly and lately (Fig. 2).

For FEV1/FVC%pred, the children were also divided into three trajectory groups. In most children (n = 1633, 84.57%), the change of FEV1/FVC%pred showed a gradual rise at first, then a large fluctuation, and finally returned to normal (green line and red line). Among them, the FEV1/FVC%pred of the first group was close to the average most of the time (green line, group 1), and the index of the second group was lower than the average most of the time (red line, group 2). A total of 298 (15.43%) children showed a gradual continuous downward trend (blue line, Group 3) (Fig. 3).

A total of 1858 patients completed 14,702 measurements of MEF25 (FEF75)%pred (the data of MEF25 were incompletely recorded). The trajectory of MEF25 was also divided into three groups. The result shows that 377 children (20.29%, Group 2, red line) had a consistently higher MEF25%pred than other 2 groups. Meanwhile, 419 (22.55%) children had a trajectory that near the average level (Group 1, blue line), and 1062 (57.16%) children had a trajectory below the average and last catch up (Group 3, green line) (Fig. 4).

Lung function level in children with symptom control asthma

If we observe these indicators using the currently recognized normal or abnormal standards, we will find a more realistic picture. In the case of FEV1%pred, we typically use 80% as the cutoff point. In the first group of FEV1%pred trajectory, 432 tests (432/5978, 7.22%) of 210 patients (210/675, 31.11%) were lower than normal, 327 (327/5194, 3.96%) pulmonary function tests of 150 (150/635, 23.62%) patients in the second group were lower than normal, and 132 tests (132/4225, 3.12%) of 79 patients (79/621, 12.72%) in the third group were abnormal.

All 1931 patients completed 15,406 measurements of FEV1/FVC%pred, and of these 15,406 tests, 2289 (14.86%) in 801 (41.48%) patients were below normal (92%) 18. In Group one, 331 children (331/1055, 31.37%) had a total of 622 (622/7830, 7.94%) FEV1/FVC%pred lower than 92%. Of the 578 children in the second group, 376 (65.05%) children had a total of 1457 (1457/5681, 25.65%) tests lower than normal. In the third group, 94 (31.54%) children had 366 (170/1894, 8.98%) lung function indexes below the cut-off point.

If we use ≥ 65% 18 as the normal range for MEF25%pred, this will mean that among the 419 children in the first group, 1497 examinations (1497/2751, 54.42%) of 358 (358/419, 85.44%) children were lower than the normal value. Of the 377 children in the second group, 227 patients (73.47%) had 1046 tests (1046/3105, 33.69%) lower than normal. Of the 1062 patients in the third group, 987 patients (92.94%) had 5256 examinations (5256/8469, 62.06%) below the normal value.

Characteristics of different FEV1%pred trajectory groups

In the present study, we found that 90.98% of asthmatic children had rhinitis history, and 57.33% had eczema in the first year of life. No significant difference was observed in the prevalence of rhinitis and eczema among the three different FEV1%pred trajectory groups. Table 1 summarizes the distribution of participant characteristics by FEV1%pred trajectory group. The three lung-function groups differed according to onset age, interval between diagnosis of asthma and first onset (P all < 0.001), count of lymphocyte (P = 0.033), and FeNO (P = 0.03). On the other hand, all participants were similar with respect to sex, BMI, rhinitis and eczema history, count of eosinophils, hemameba and neutrophil, IgE and allergens (all P > 0.05).

Subsequently, we used the random forest method to fill in the missing data. Variables with p ≤ 0.3 in Table 1 were included in the logistic regression model, and stepwise regression was used. Taking the third group (blue line) with a continuous downward trend as the reference group, we found that after multinomial logistic regression, various factors such as BMI, onset age were identified as influencing factors for different patterns of FEV1%pred (Table 2). When compared with group 3, each 3 kg/m2 increase in BMI increased the likelihood of a sustained decline in pulmonary function by 24.6% and 23.7% in groups 1 and 2, respectively. For every 5 ppb increase in FeNO, the likelihood of a sustained decline in lung function in group 1 was increased by 4.8%, as compared with the group 3. Meanwhile, the onset age and interval of delayed diagnosis were significant factors influencing lung function, too. If the onset age of childhood asthma was between 1 and 3 years old, the likelihood of persistent lung function decline in the first and second groups of asthmatic children is reduced by 83.5% and 70%, respectively, when compared to the reference group. If the onset age > 3 years old, this likelihood is reduced by 50% and 78.8%, respectively. However, the level of dust mite allergy was not associated with the lung function trajectory.

Discussion

In the present study, we conducted a trajectory analysis and grouping by observing the changes of lung function of asthma children with continuous treatment and got good symptom control. In this cohort study, these trajectories were identified by using model-based methods, and all children were clearly divided into three clusters.

Among the three trajectory clusters, trajectory 2 and 3 were associated with marked reductions in baseline FEV1%pred, accounting for 65.04% of all patients. For FEV1/FVC%pred, there was 15.43% children showed a gradual continuous downward trend and 29.93% children’s lung function were always below average. Our findings were consistent with the results of Robab Kohansal’s group, which showed that asthma led to a decline in FEV1 [20]. Similarly, the ALSPAC birth cohort found that all wheezing phenotypes are associated with impaired lung function by school age [21]. The Manchester Asthma and Allergy Study birth cohort reported that reduced lung function is associated with a history of transient and persistent wheezing in children [22, 23]. The Melbourne asthma cohort tested spirometry at 7-year intervals from 7 to 42 years old. At the time of the final review, 267 spirometry tests were measured (55% of follow-up) and the FEV1/FVC ratios of those children initially classified with asthma was consistently lower than that of the control group [24]. The Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study assessed the lung function and history of asthma-like symptoms in 613 subjects (59% follow-up) aged 9–26 years. Participates who reported persistent or recurrent wheezing during the observation period had lower FEV1/FVC ratios from 9 years of age than children without wheezing [9]. In the Childhood Asthma Management Program (CAMP) cohort, about a quarter of children with mild to moderate asthma had a predicted decline in bronchodilator FEV1 of 1% or more per year, although this was not accompanied by increased symptom severity [25]. All these studies point to one thing: asthma or wheezing can lead to decreased lung function in children, which was consistent with our research. Our study confirmed that children with good symptom control and standard-managed asthma continue to show a decline in lung function, which was lacking in previous studies. However, it is well recognized that lung function in children is subject to natural variability during growth and development, and published reproducibility limits for longitudinal pediatric spirometry should be considered when interpreting such trends. In our study, all measurements were obtained from children with symptom-controlled asthma at the time of each visit, and we explicitly excluded tests performed during or after acute exacerbations or respiratory tract infections. This design ensures that our data reflect stable-state lung function with minimal transient variability. Using the trajectory predictions from Fig. 2, the rapid continuous decline trajectory (group 3) showed a sustained downward trend with a net change of − 0.24 standard deviations over approximately 140 months, which exceeds the expected 5–8% coefficient of variation [26, 27]. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to interpret pediatric asthma lung function trajectories by integrating conventional %predicted cut-offs, published spirometry reproducibility limits [28] and a study design that excludes acute event-related fluctuations.

Glucocorticoid inhalation is recognized as a cornerstone treatment for asthma; however, the effects of glucocorticoid therapy on lung function are less than satisfactory. The Inhaled Steroid Treatment as Regular Therapy study is a 3-year, randomized, double-blind study to evaluate whether early intervention with low-dose ICS will prevent severe asthma-related events and accelerated lung function decline. 7241 patients with mild persistent asthma less than 2 years were included in the study. The results showed that regardless if patients received budesonide or placebo, the predictive value of FEV1% after bronchodilator decreased in 3 years. Although the lung function decline was less in the ICS group compared with the placebo group, the difference in the 3-year change of FEV1 after bronchodilator between the budesonide group and the placebo group was narrower in the 5- to 10-year-old age group [29]. After 2 years, the same study got the result that ICS reduced the average decline in the FEV1/FVC ratio in adults during the treatment period. The treatment effect of children under 11 years of age was not as significant as that of adults, and it was not significant in adolescents [30]. Moreover, in another recent clinical trial, 2 years of daily ICS in preschoolers was insufficient to prevent the recurrence of symptoms [31]. Combined with the results of the above studies and our observations, the findings suggest that ICS treatment may not stop the decline of lung function in children with asthma. The potential reason may be the presence of corticosteroid-independent mechanisms, such as neurogenic inflammation [32], or structural changes in the airways, such as subepithelial fibrosis and increased elastin deposition [33]. It may be argued that therapeutic adherence has a greater effect on lung function, but the results of the CAMP study suggested that ICS and placebo adherence is similar, so low compliance may not have a significant effect on lung function [34]. In the present study, a follow-up study of children with very high adherence to ICS led to a similar conclusion that ICS did not significantly prevent progressive decline in lung function in children with asthma. Fortunately, in our previous 3-year follow-up of 198 children with standardized treatment, we found that most children’s lung function remained at normal levels after 3 years of treatment. However, the lung function of children with uncontrolled asthma was still lower than that of children with well-controlled asthma [35]. We obtained similar results in the present large-scale follow-up study. Although the overall trend showed a decline, at the end of the study, the FEV1%pred of most patients (86.48%) could still be maintained at or above the normal level. Up to 37.65% of the children in the first cluster showed a decrease in FEV1%pred; however, the frequency only accounted for 9.11% of the total frequency, and most of them were concentrated in the initial stage of treatment and gradually stabilized at a normal level. Therefore, what kind of treatment is needed for asthma to achieve symptom control and recovery of lung function also needs to be further studied.

In the present study, lymphocyte, FeNO, time interval of delayed diagnosis and onset age, showed statistical differences among different FEV1 trajectory groups. We found that BMI, FeNO, onset age, time interval of delayed diagnosis, and were closely related to FEV1 decline by multiple logistic regression. In 2008, Taylor B et al. [36] reported that obese asthmatics have more severe and frequent respiratory symptoms, a greater rate of exacerbation, and a reduced quality of life associated with asthma compared with lean asthmatics. In 2011, a cross-sectional analysis of the Severe Asthma Research Program study showed that patients with early-onset asthma (< 12 years old) associated with obesity were more likely to have greater airway obstruction (lower FEV1/FVC%), higher bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and higher responses to bronchodilator (higher FEV1 improvement) [37]. In the same year, Lang et al. found that obese boys who developed asthma early (6–11 years) had significant reduction of FEV1/FVC ratio (P < 0.01) [38]. Established obesity in early years of life (10 years or younger) is an independent risk factor for incident asthma [39]. Several studies also indicate that obese children have a higher overall risk of developing asthma [40–42], which was consistent with ours found. On the other hand, in view of the adverse effects of obesity on children’s lives, cardiovascular system, and asthma, we should pay attention to the weight management of asthma children so as to achieve better control of asthma.

Epidemiological evidence shows that early childhood allergies and subsequent asthma are associated with subsequent impairment of lung function [43–46]. In one cohort of 1314 children, sensitization to perennial allergens in the first 3 years of life was associated with subsequent decreased lung function at school age. This effect was augmented when exposure levels to allergens were high [47]. Interventional studies examining the effects of allergen avoidance showed a significantly better specific airway resistance in 3-year-old children, but were unable to find any protective long-term effect on lung function [48]. Although the ALSPAC study [10] reported that allergies in early life were associated with reduced lung function, no association between allergic sensitization and lung function was observed during adolescence. In the present study, we also found no association between allergens and changes in lung function in symptom-controlled children with asthma. This may be related to asthma subtypes, which we did not analyze in this study. It may also be age-related, but the children in our study were all over the age of 5 in order to obtain lung function results, which may also be the cause of this result.Therefore, our future research could potentially focus on different subtypes of asthma, examining the trajectory changes in lung function among children, and conducting a more comprehensive analysis of their allergic status. Furthermore, we found that the age of onset was closely related to lung function in children with asthma, which was consistent with the findings of many studies [10, 49–51].

Our study has several limitations. First, this is a long-term retrospective observation without a control group, and the historical data are somewhat imperfect (e.g., MEF25%pred, allergen and FeNO). Second, asthma is a chronic disease, and prolonged observation and follow-up may be more conducive to observing changes in lung function. Third, additional risk factors, including genetic factors, childhood respiratory infections, and environmental exposures, were not available for analysis in the present study but would be interesting to assess in future studies. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single center, and although our hospital is the largest children’s hospital in Shandong Province serving a population of over 100 million, the lack of external validation remains a limitation. Meanwhile, although we found that some lung function parameters in a subset of children fell below the current threshold, this may not be associated with clinical worsening of asthma. Given the complex pathological mechanisms and heterogeneous phenotypes of asthma, a comprehensive multidimensional assessment is required for evaluating disease status. In any case, this is the largest real-world population cohort study of children with asthma, and the implications for our overall understanding of childhood asthma control are enormous.

In summary, asthma is the most common chronic disease. Even after long-term anti-inflammation treatment in strict accordance with the guidelines, the pulmonary function of some children shows a downward trend, especially the small airway indicators such as MEF25%pred are more obvious. Pulmonologists and asthma specialists need to be vigilant, and a longer and larger-scale survey of childhood asthma trajectories is necessary.
