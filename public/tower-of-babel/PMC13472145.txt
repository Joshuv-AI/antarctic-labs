Prenatal Internal Exposure to Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons, Maternal Genetic Susceptibility, and Child Growth Trajectories: Evidence from a Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jinlang Lyu, Na Han, Cuilin Zhang, Yuelong Ji, Jue Liu, Qin Li, Hui Wang, Rong Zhang, Yunxia Pu, Hai-Jun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prenatal exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) is a widespread environmental health concern, yet its impact on early childhood growth trajectories remains poorly understood. In a prospective birth cohort in China (PKUBC-T), we identified distinct growth trajectories from birth to age 3 years using K-means clustering for longitudinal data (N = 1467) and measured 16 plasma PAHs in first-trimester maternal samples (N = 333). Twenty maternal single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to PAHs metabolism were genotyped. Modified Poisson and weighted quantile sum regression showed that higher prenatal PAHs exposure was associated with increased risk of rapid higher growth trajectory, with phenanthrene, pyrene, and fluoranthene contributing most. This trajectory is characterized by a marginally higher BMI Z-score at birth, followed by rapid growth during infancy and sustained high levels thereafter. Furthermore, the AHR rs2066853 variant significantly modified the association, with the positive association being observed only among mothers carrying GA/AA genotypes (RR, 2.44; 95% CI, 1.51–3.97) but not among those with GG homozygotes (P for interaction is 0.049). We provide evidence that prenatal PAHs exposure may alter early childhood growth patterns, with effects modified by maternal genetic susceptibility. If confirmed, these findings highlight the mechanistic importance of AHR signaling in environmental developmental toxicity and underscore the need for exposure reduction and targeted maternal monitoring.

Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are organic compounds comprising two or more fused aromatic rings, mainly produced by incomplete combustion of fuels or biomass. Humans can be exposed to PAHs through inhalation of cigarette smoke, vehicle exhaust fumes, and ambient air pollution or through ingestion of grilled meats and contaminated water. Due to their environmental persistence, widespread distribution, and significant toxicological effects, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) has designated 16 PAH compounds as priority pollutants. Asia is the largest contributor to global PAH emissions, accounting for over 50% of the overall release. Given the substantial emissions and the associated ecological and health risks, PAH exposure has emerged as a growing public concern, underscoring the need for comprehensive risk assessment and targeted prevention.

Notably, maternal-fetal exposure to PAHs has attracted increasing attention due to their wide detection in maternal blood, urine, placenta, and breast milk. In addition to causing endocrine disruption and oxidative stress in pregnant women, PAHs can also transfer across the placental barrier, directly posing threats to fetuses during their most vulnerable and sensitive stages. Toxicological studies showed that offspring of PAH-exposed dams exhibited increased weight and fat mass from postnatal days 21 to 60. In population-based studies, Rundle et al. reported that prenatal PAH concentrations assessed by personal air monitoring during the third trimester were associated with an increased risk of overweight and obesity at ages 5 and 7. Importantly, in their subsequent analysis of BMI Z-score trajectories from ages 5 to 14 years, the BMI growth curves of children with low versus high prenatal PAH exposure became progressively less distinct and converged around age 11 years. A cohort study in Bangladesh also found no significant associations between maternal urinary PAH metabolites and child anthropometric indicators at 10 years of age. These findings suggest that the effects of prenatal PAHs exposure on offspring growth may emerge during early childhood. Given that infancy represents a period with rapid somatic growth and heightened metabolic plasticity, this stage may be a more sensitive window for detecting exposure-related growth alterations, underscoring the importance of focusing on early dynamic growth trajectories.

The first three years of life cover the first growth spurt period after birth and are a critical stage for early growth and development. Importantly, childhood growth dynamics during this stage are more predictive of later health than cross-sectional assessment. Children who undergo accelerated growth during early life are more likely to develop chronic diseases later in adulthood, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes. , However, on the basis of our literature review, no study has investigated the association of prenatal exposure to PAHs with growth trajectories during early childhood. Although one longitudinal study investigated the association between PAH exposure and child growth indicators from ages 1 to 4 years, the use of linear mixed-effects models limited the analysis to estimating an average effect coefficient and, in the absence of trajectory analysis, may fail to capture potential effects of PAHs on abnormal patterns. Modeling these changes as distinct growth trajectories will help to precisely characterize developmental variability. More importantly, understanding modifiable factors that lead to adverse growth patterns is also essential for early interventions to reduce the future burden of chronic diseases.

After entering the maternal body, PAHs first activate aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signaling and then undergo biotransformation primarily through phase I and phase II metabolic pathways. In phase I, enzymes such as cytochrome P450 convert PAHs into reactive intermediates capable of inducing both DNA damage and metabolic dysregulation. These intermediates are then detoxified by phase II enzymes into more water-soluble and excretable forms. Significant interactions between maternal PAHs exposure and metabolic enzyme gene variants have been observed to influence benzo­[a]­pyrene-DNA adducts levels in cord blood, the risk of allergic diseases and mental development in offspring. , These findings suggest that genetic polymorphisms may affect maternal–fetal susceptibility to PAH-induced adverse effects and ultimately modify the association between prenatal PAHs exposure and child health outcomes. However, to date, no studies have accounted for these genetic factors in analyses focusing on childhood growth and development, underscoring the need for further research to identify genetically susceptible populations and implement targeted early-life interventions.

To address the aforementioned knowledge gaps, this study aimed to explore the associations between maternal PAHs exposure during pregnancy and child growth trajectories through the first three years of life and further investigate the interaction effects between prenatal PAHs exposure and SNPs involved in PAHs metabolism on early childhood growth.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

The Peking University Birth Cohort in Tongzhou (PKUBC-T) was a prospective birth cohort designed to investigate the impact of prepregnant and prenatal exposure factors on maternal health, birth outcomes, and childhood growth and development in the first six years of life. This cohort was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03814395), and more detailed information has been described elsewhere. This study was embedded in PKUBC-T (see flowchart in Figure S1). Pregnant women (N = 1803) were included according to the following criteria: gestational age at enrollment was <14 weeks; maternal age ranged from 18 to 49 years; planned to have prenatal care and delivery in Tongzhou Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital of Beijing; with singleton live birth and consent to follow up of children. The mother–child pairs were further excluded according to the following criteria: diagnosed as diabetes, hypertension, chronic hepatitis, heart diseases or kidney diseases before pregnancy; lack of repeated anthropometric measurements. Finally, a total of 1467 mother–child pairs were included in the trajectory analysis. The distributions of baseline characteristics were comparable between participants with sufficient repeated follow-up anthropometric measurements and those with insufficient repeated measurements (Table S1). From these, a subgroup of 333 pairs with available first-trimester maternal blood was selected for PAH measurements. The distributions of general baseline characteristics were similar among participants included in the trajectory analysis, those with PAHs detection, and those who were excluded (Table S2). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Peking University (IRB00001052-21093). All participants signed consent forms for themselves and their offspring.

Anthropometric Measurements of Offspring

At ages of 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 9 months, 1 year, 1.5 years, 2 years, 2.5 years, and 3 years, anthropometric measurements of offspring were performed by trained health professionals using standardized procedures and calibrated instruments. Age- and sex-specific body mass index (BMI) Z-score was calculated according to the World Health Organization (WHO) growth reference to characterize nutritional status of children.

For children who underwent at least four anthropometric measurements during specified time intervals from birth to three years (having newborn measurements; having at least one measurement at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months; having at least one measurement at 9 months, 12 months, and 18 months; having at least one measurement at 2 years, 2.5 years, and 3 years), we identified their longitudinal growth trajectories (N = 1467). To ensure the robustness of trajectory identification, two commonly used approaches for longitudinal trajectory modeling were applied. First, growth mixture modeling (GMM), a parametric latent class approach, was implemented using the lcmm package in R. Model selection for GMM was guided by multiple criteria, including minimization of the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), maximization of entropy, an average posterior class membership probability greater than 70% for each trajectory group, and a minimum class size accounting for more than 5% of the study population. Second, the K-means for longitudinal data (KML) method, a nonparametric clustering approach, was applied using the kml R package to identify BMI Z-score trajectories. This nonparametric method assigns objects into clusters based on similarity by the distance between the centroid and each object and has been widely used to characterize the growth trajectories of fetuses and children. − The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the maximization of the Calinski–Harabasz (CH) index and the clinical applications of the trajectories. Both trajectory modeling methods were based on observed longitudinal data within predefined follow-up time windows, without additional imputation of missing measurements. , Trajectory identification using repeated measurements and the subsequent analyses were interpreted under the assumption that missing anthropometric data were approximately missing at random.

The trajectory groups and shapes identified by GMM and KML are presented in Figures S2 and S3, respectively. To further explore the associations between trajectory group membership and subsequent health outcomes, we examined the associations between trajectory groups and the BMI Z-score at age 4 years (Table S3). We also compared the predictive ability of trajectory-based classifications derived from different methods for the risk of being overweight at age 4 years. Predictive performance was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation. The mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.655 for the GMM-derived trajectory model incorporating covariates and 0.815 for the KML-derived trajectory model incorporating covariates (Figure S4). Formal comparison indicated that the KML-derived trajectories demonstrated significantly better predictive performance than the GMM-derived trajectories (P value of <0.05).

Given the stronger associations with BMI Z-score at age 4 years and the higher predictive performance for risk of overweight, the three trajectory groups identified by the KML method were selected in this study. The trajectories were named according to their shapes and temporal patterns: the rapid higher trajectory (a marginally higher BMI Z-score at birth, followed by rapid growth during infancy and maintaining a stable high level afterward), the stable medium trajectory (a moderate BMI Z-score at birth with stable maintenance within the normal range), and the stable lower trajectory (a lower BMI Z-score at birth with stable maintenance within the lower normal range). The proportion of participants with the three trajectories was 24.74% (rapid higher), 45.13% (stable medium) and 30.13% (stable lower), respectively. Children’s specific anthropometric measures at different ages for each trajectory group were presented in Table S4. Our prior study also demonstrated that children exhibiting rapid higher trajectories between birth and 2 years were at significantly increased risk of overweight or obesity by preschool age. Given that the stable medium trajectory and the stable lower trajectory were similar in shape and the average BMI Z-scores in the both groups were almost within the normal range, the two groups were combined and labeled as the stable normal trajectory, which served as the reference group in subsequent association analyses. The distributions of trajectory groups were similar among participants included in the trajectory analysis, those with PAHs detection, and those who were excluded (see Table S5).

PAHs Measurements

Venous blood samples were collected at the participants’ first prenatal visit after fasting overnight. After centrifugation at 2500 relative centrifugal force for 10 min, the plasma and red blood cell (RBC) fractions were separated. Plasma was extracted and tested for biochemical indicators including triglyceride, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein with standard biochemical assay methods. The remaining fasting plasma was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C refrigerator until assayed.

The 16 PAHs including naphthalene (NAP), acenaphthylene (ANY), acenaphthene (ACE), fluorene (FLU), phenanthrene (PHE), anthracene (ANT), fluoranthene (FLO), pyrene (PYR), benz­(a)­anthracene (BAA), chrysene (CHR), benzo­(b)­fluoranthene (BBF), benzo­(k)­fluoranthene (BKF), benzo­(a)­pyrene (BAP), indeno (1,2,3-cd)­pyrene (I1P), dibenz­(a,h) anthracene (DahA), and benzo­(g,h,i)­perylene (BghiP) were measured, using gas chromatography coupled with a triple-quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer instrument (GCMS-TQ8050 NX; SHIMADZU) equipped with a HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). These compounds are prioritized for control on the list of USEPA.

The plasma samples were extracted and cleaned up using the method developed by Jia et al. with some modifications. Briefly, one milliliter of methanol was added to each tube containing 0.5 mL of the sample, and the tubes were vortexed for 2 min to precipitate proteins. Organic components in the plasma were extracted three times with a mixture of n-hexane and methyl tert-butyl ether (1:1, v/v). The organic supernatants were gathered into another glass tube and blown to near dryness with nitrogen gas and then transferred into a preconditioned AlO/SiG column. After elution of impurities with n-hexane, 12 mL of a mixture of n-hexane and methyl tert-butyl ether (1:4, v/v) was used to wash out the target PAHs. The eluate was concentrated to a final volume of 0.1 mL for analysis and was spiked with the internal standard. The limit of detection (LOD) for PAHs ranged from 0.01 to 0.07 ng/mL, and concentrations below the LOD were replaced by the LOD divided by the square root of 2. Details of the instrumental parameters, quality control procedures, and data processing methods are provided in Text S1.

Single-Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) Selection and Genotyping

PAHs acquire toxicity after they have been activated by xenobiotic-metabolizing enzymes to various epoxides. Candidate SNPs were selected according to the following criteria: (1) SNPs that have been previously reported or hypothesized to have potential functional relevance in PAHs metabolism or detoxification and (2) a minor allele frequency of >5% in Han Chinese. Genomic DNA of pregnant women was extracted from the blood leukocytes using the standard high-salt method. Genotyping was conducted on a MassARRAY system (Agena Bioscience Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), and the resulting spectra were processed using a MassARRAY Typer (Agena Bioscience Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For quality assurance, 5% of the samples were randomly selected for duplicate genotyping, yielding a concordance rate of 100%.

Only samples with an overall genotyping call rate of at least 90% were retained for subsequent analyses. At the SNP level, variants with call rates below 95% or deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P value of <0.05) were excluded. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was subsequently performed, and when two SNPs showed strong LD (r 2 > 0.8), one representative SNP was retained from each LD block. Finally, a total of 20 SNPs across 10 PAH metabolism-related genes and 320 samples were included in the analysis. Detailed genetic and genotyping information is presented in Table S6.

Covariates

We selected the covariates referring to previous studies. ,− Maternal characteristics were obtained from questionnaire or medical records including educational levels, age, parity, and lifestyles (dietary energy intake, physical activity, passive smoking, and alcohol drinking). Prepregnancy BMI was calculated based on self-reported weight and measured height. Birth-related outcomes (i.e., sex, gestational weeks, birth weight) were obtained from medical records. Preterm birth (PTB) was defined as gestational age of <37 completed weeks. Low birth weight (LBW) is defined as a birth weight of <2500 g. Infant characteristics were collected during the follow-up visits by questionnaires including breastfeeding status at one month, duration of physical activity, and dietary intake. Dietary quality indicators were calculated according to the Chinese diet balance index for preschool children (DBI_C).

Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages (%), while continuous variables as mean (SD). Due to skewed distributions of PAHs levels, they were converted to a natural log scale before further analyses. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between PAH levels. In the single exposure analysis, modified Poisson regression was used to examine the association between prenatal PAHs exposure (modeled as a continuous variable or categorized into tertiles) and child growth trajectories, with the stable normal trajectory serving as the reference group. Sandwich error estimation was applied to generate robust standard errors. Trend tests were conducted using the median values within each tertile of PAHs as continuous variables. In the mixed-exposure analysis, the weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression model was implemented to evaluate the joint effect exposure to eight PAHs on child growth trajectories. Concentrations of the individual detected PAHs were divided into tertiles before creating a WQS index, where each PAH was assigned a weight contribution (between 0 and 1 and summing up to 1). In the WQS regression framework, models were fitted using the gWQS package with a random training-validation split (40% and 60%) and 100 bootstrap iterations. Separate WQS models were fitted under positive and negative directional constraints, and all models were adjusted for the same covariates as those included in the single-exposure analyses.

To assess potential gene–environment interactions, product terms between SNPs and PAHs exposure were included in the regression models to investigate their interaction effects on child growth trajectories. Genetic polymorphisms were analyzed assuming a dominant genetic model, where carrying one or two copies of the minor allele was considered as having the risk genotype. , To account for multiple comparisons in the gene-environment interaction analyses, FDR correction was primarily applied within each SNP. This SNP-level approach was used as the primary exploratory correction, because the analysis focused on exposure-specific effect modification within each candidate SNP. In addition, FDR correction across all SNP-exposure interaction tests was performed as a supplementary analysis, as this global correction may be more conservative. SNP-level FDR-adjusted P-values of <0.05 were considered nominally significant. For SNPs demonstrating statistically significant interaction effects, the main effects of prenatal PAHs exposure on child growth were further estimated within genotype-defined strata.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions with three knots were applied to further explore potential nonlinear associations between prenatal PAHs exposure and child growth trajectories. (2) Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the potential modifying effect of fetal sex, given previous evidence suggesting a sex-specific impact of prenatal PAH exposure on child growth. (3) The characteristics of offspring during infancy (i.e., physical activity duration and dietary quality) were additionally adjusted as exploratory analyses, aiming to control for their potential effects on child growth. (4) The association analysis was repeated after excluding the five participants (1.4%), whose abnormal PAH measurements were replaced with compound-specific median values in the main analysis. (5) Association analysis was repeated after excluding participants with PTB or LBW. (6) To address potential selection bias related to the inclusion criteria, inverse probability weighting (IPW) was applied. We developed weights based on factors assessed for all women in the cohort at the enrollment that may affect the likelihood of participation or sample availability, including prepregnancy BMI, maternal educational level, maternal age, and parity. Regression models assessing the association between prenatal PAHs exposure and growth trajectory groups were weighted accordingly, and weighted estimates are reported. (7) To assess whether collapsing the three trajectory groups into a binary outcome influenced the observed associations, we additionally performed multinomial modified Poisson regression, modeling the three KML-derived BMI trajectories as a nominal outcome. The stable median trajectory, representing stable growth within the normal range, was used as a reference group.

The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05 for two-tailed tests. Statistical analyses were performed by using R (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Associations of Prenatal Exposures to PAHs and Child Growth Trajectories in Single Exposure Analyses

When PAHs were treated as a continuous variable, higher log-transformed concentrations of PYR at the first trimester were significantly associated with an increased risk of rapid higher trajectory in offspring with relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 1.14 (1.03–1.27). When PAHs treated as tertile categories, compared to the lowest tertile group, the risks of rapid higher trajectory significantly increased in the highest tertile groups of FLU (RR = 1.58, 95% CI = 1.05–2.37, P trend = 0.025), PHE (RR = 1.72, 95% CI = 1.12–2.63, P trend = 0.013), FLO (RR = 1.60, 95% CI = 1.04–2.46, P trend = 0.033), and PYR (RR = 1.72, 95% CI = 1.12–2.64, P trend = 0.012) (see Figure ). The third tertiles of ∑PAHs (RR = 1.58, 95% CI = 1.05–2.38, P trend = 0.031), ∑H-PAHs (RR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.02–2.31, P trend = 0.039), and ∑L-PAHs (RR = 1.63, 95% CI = 1.08–2.46, P trend = 0.021) were also found to be positively associated with the risk of rapid higher trajectory (see Figure ).

In sensitivity analyses using restricted cubic splines, prenatal PAHs exposure did not exhibit statistically significant nonlinear relationships with child growth trajectories, with all P-values for nonlinearity >0.05 (Figure S6). A potential effect modification by fetal sex was observed in the association between maternal ACY concentrations and child growth trajectories, with marginal statistical significance (P-value for interaction = 0.044). Higher ACY exposure was associated with increased risks of rapid higher growth trajectory among females (RR = 1.63, 95% CI = 1.04–2.54, P-value = 0.032) but not in males (RR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.73–1.26, P-value = 0.736). No modification by fetal sex was found for other compounds. Additionally, after further adjusting for characteristics during childhood (physical activity duration and dietary quality), the overall direction of the associations remained consistent with the results of the main analyses (see Table S7). The associations between prenatal PAHs exposure and child growth trajectories remained similar after excluding the five participants with imputed PAHs exposure values, or after excluding 16 participants with preterm birth or low birth weight (see Tables S8 and S9). In addition, the results from IPW analyses to account for potential selection bias were consistent with the primary findings (see Table S10). In multinomial regression models, using the stable median trajectory as the reference group, results were generally consistent with the main findings (see Table S11). Specifically, prenatal exposures to FLU, PHE, FLO, PYR, ∑H-PAHs, and ∑L-PAHs were associated with elevated risks of the rapid higher growth trajectory. In contrast, ACY and ACE exposure was positively associated with the stable lower trajectory, whereas associations between other PAHs and the stable lower trajectory were not statistically significant.

In mixed exposure analysis, a one unit increase in the WQS index of combined maternal PAHs exposure was associated with a 1.73-fold increased risk of rapid higher trajectory (95% CI = 1.20–2.52, P-value = 0.033). The WQS index weights for each PAHs chemical under a positive directional constraint were shown in Figure . PHE (WQS weight = 0.257), PYR (WQS weight = 0.218), FLO (WQS weight = 0.185) had weights exceeding the cut-point of 0.125, indicating major contributions of these PAHs to the overall effect of the mixture. No statistically significant inverse association was found under the negative-direction WQS model (RR = 1.24, 95% CI = 0.89–1.73, P-value = 0.380).

Gene-Environment Interactions on Child Growth Trajectories

None of the selected SNPs showed a significant association with child rapid higher growth trajectories (see Table S12). Among all the SNPs, only rs2066853 variant genotype was observed to have a significant interaction effect with PAHs on child growth trajectory after SNP-level FDR correction (see Table and Table S13). In mothers with the GA or AA genotypes (variant-type), higher exposure to PYR during the first trimester was significantly associated with higher risk of rapid higher trajectory among children (RR = 2.44; 95% CI = 1.51–3.97; P-value of <0.001). Among mothers with the GG genotype (wild-type), the association was insignificant (RR = 0.86; 95% CI = 0.51–1.46; P-value = 0.580). The P-value for interaction was 0.049 after FDR-correction. Similarly, the association between prenatal ∑H-PAHs exposure and child growth trajectory was more pronounced in offspring of mothers carrying the GA or AA genotypes of rs2066853 (RR = 2.58; 95% CI = 1.57–4.24; P-value of <0.001), compared to those with the GG genotype (RR = 0.81; 95% CI = 0.48–1.38; P-value = 0.435). The P-value for interaction was 0.033 after FDR-correction. These results suggested that AHR polymorphism rs2066853 influenced children’s susceptibility for growing with a rapid higher pattern with higher prenatal exposure to PAHs. No multiplicative-scale interactions were observed between other PAHs or the other SNPs and the risk of rapid higher trajectory.

Discussion

On the basis of a prospective birth cohort, the present study investigated the associations between prenatal PAHs exposure and child growth trajectories within the first three years of life. We found that prenatal exposure to higher levels of PAHs (FLU, PHE, FLO, and PYR) was associated with an increased risk of rapid higher growth trajectory in single-exposure analysis. This association was also observed when considering the whole mixture of PAHs, with PHE, PYR, and FLO showing greater contributions to the mixture effect. Importantly, we further identified maternal AHR rs2066853 genotype as a modifier of this association, uncovering novel gene-environment interactions in early-life growth regulation.

Although previous studies have suggested the association between prenatal PAH exposure and excessive postnatal growth in offspring, most have focused on later childhood or adolescence. , Overall, these studies suggest a positive association between PAH exposure and childhood growth indicators, whereas epidemiological data during infancy remain limited. A study from the US reported that PAHs exposure was associated with higher weight and length Z-scores in children from 1 to 4 years of age but not with BMI. However, this study relied on population-average analyses using mixed-effects models, which may obscure heterogeneity across distinct growth patterns and potentially attenuate the overall effect estimates. Given the substantial interindividual variability in early growth, researchers have advocated for refined models to classify individuals into clinically meaningful trajectories, capture key features (baseline, velocity, and growth direction), and reduce reliance on single time-point data. , K-means longitudinal clustering offers a flexible framework for identifying nonlinear growth dynamics. , Trajectories derived using this method in our cohort and others have consistently demonstrated robust predictive ability for later overweight and obesity. , Extending previous literature, our findings indicate that prenatal PAH exposure may influence child growth as early as infancy, particularly during the critical first 1000 days. Importantly, this effect does not simply manifest as shifts in population-average growth indicators but more likely operates by altering growth trajectory differentiation, increasing the proportion of children entering high-risk growth trajectories and thereby reshaping the distribution of early-life obesity risk.

Although children in the rapid higher trajectory exhibited normal birth size, their postnatal growth progressively accelerated and reached WHO-defined thresholds for overweight and obesity risk. Notably, the associations between prenatal PAH exposure and early childhood growth trajectories remained robust after excluding preterm births and low birth weight infants, suggesting that the observed trajectory divergence was not merely driven by differences in birth size but more likely reflects sustained postnatal growth dysregulation following intrauterine exposure. This finding was supported by previous epidemiological studies indicating that associations between prenatal PAH exposure and child weight and height strengthen with advancing age during infancy. Animal models also provide similar evidence, showing that mice prenatally exposed to PAHs exhibited greater weight gain and fat accumulation from postnatal days 21 to 60, despite comparable birth weights with control groups. Within the DOHaD framework, exposure to adverse environments during critical developmental windows may induce compensatory adaptations, including metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic remodeling, to support short-term growth and survival. However, these adaptations may also increase vulnerability to subsequent nutritional or environmental challenges, manifesting as rapid early growth, excess adiposity, and metabolic dysregulation later in life. Without intervention, such high-risk growth patterns during childhood were associated with earlier adiposity rebound and an elevated risk for obesity and metabolic or cardiovascular disorders later in life. By identifying modifiable early-life environmental risk factors, our study findings underscored the importance of monitoring maternal PAH exposure and reducing elevated exposure levels during pregnancy to promote healthy growth. This is particularly critical in the context of the ongoing epidemiological transition, whereby noncommunicable conditions are contributing an increasing disease burden among children and adolescents, with evidence of progressively earlier onset. Early pregnancy therefore represents a critical window for preventive intervention, with the potential to yield sustained health benefits across the life course.

In this study, we quantified PAHs in maternal plasma to accurately assess individual exposure levels from all of the potential sources. Single-exposure analysis revealed significant associations between five PAHs (FLU, PHE, FLO, and PYR) and growth trajectories. Considering that combined exposure may produce complex interactions, we also employed mixed-exposure models and identified the dominant drivers (PHE, PYR, and FLO) contributing to adverse effects. Similar to our results, a recent study from the US demonstrated positive associations between urinary concentrations of PHE and PYR metabolites and weight Z-scores at 3 and 4 years of age. A cohort study in rural Bangladesh revealed that maternal urinary metabolites of PYR were positively associated with birth weight. This positive trend also persisted in relation to the WAZ and HAZ from birth to 10 years. Beyond the well-recognized carcinogenicity, the disruptive effects of PAHs on growth and development also warrant greater attention. The identification of PHE, PYR, and FLO as significant hazardous compounds underscores the need for targeted regulations at their major sources (vehicular exhausts, coal combustion, and biomass burning). , In addition, it is also essential to increase awareness among pregnant women about reducing daily exposure through cleaner cooking practice and limiting intake of grilled or smoked foods.

Furthermore, we identified sex-specific differences in the effects of PAH exposure. In our study, female offspring appeared to be more vulnerable to the ACY exposure, as no significant result was found in males. Few studies have investigated the sex-specific effects in infant growth associated with PAHs, and conclusions were still inconsistent. Similar to our findings, a study from China revealed that the harmful impact of prenatal PAHs exposure during the first trimester was observed exclusively in female offsprings. Another research from the US reported that the impact of prenatal PAHs exposure on BMI Z-score did not differ by sex. Although the overall mechanism remains unclear, it has been proposed that the higher expression of cytochrome P450 genes in females compared to males might contribute to their increased vulnerability. , Additionally, certain PAHs possess estrogenic properties and may exert sex-specific effects by disrupting the normal function of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. To inform targeted risk prevention strategies for vulnerable populations, whether female offsprings are more sensitive to the obesogenic effects of prenatal PAHs exposure requires further confirmation.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), encoded by a ∼50 kb gene on chromosome 7, is a canonical ligand-activated transcription factor that plays a central role in mediating PAHs toxicity. Upon activation by PAHs, AHR can regulate the transcription of key xenobiotic-metabolizing enzymes, such as CYP1A1 and CYP1A2, thereby influencing downstream biological responses. The rs2066853 polymorphism, a well-characterized nonsynonymous variant in the AHR coding region, results in an arginine-to-lysine substitution at codon 554. Experimental evidence indicates that this substitution reduces AHR transcriptional activity and attenuates the induction of downstream target genes, potentially compromising detoxification capacity and leading to prolonged internal exposure to bioactive PAHs metabolites. , We observed that the association between PYR exposure and the rapid higher growth trajectory was stronger among maternal A-allele carriers, which may be attributable to impaired detoxification that permits more bioactive metabolites to induce DNA damage and metabolic dysregulation. Similarly, the Japanese birth cohort reported higher maternal dioxin concentrations and toxicity equivalence quantities among the A-allele carriers. Previous studies in adults also indicated that A-allele carriers may be more susceptible to environment-induced perturbations. , Collectively, these findings support a potential role of maternal rs2066853 in shaping early-life susceptibility to environmental exposures. As this interaction analysis was based on a relatively modest sample size, the statistical power might be limited. The observed interaction signals should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, particularly because they did not remain statistically significant after global FDR correction. Nevertheless, the identified SNP is located in gene involved in PAH metabolism and xenobiotic-response pathways, providing some biological plausibility for the results. Further studies with larger sample sizes, independent birth cohorts, and functional experiments are warranted to validate and clarify the modifying role of maternal AHR rs2066853. Our study results underscore the importance of precision public health strategies, including identifying genetically susceptible subgroup of populations and implementing targeted early-pregnancy interventions.

The biological mechanisms linking PAHs exposure to altered growth trajectories remain incompletely understood, but emerging evidence points to several plausible pathways. One key mechanism is the disruption of energy homeostasis and lipid metabolism through the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ), a critical regulator of adipocyte differentiation. Experimental studies demonstrated that gestational PAHs exposure upregulated PPAR-γ gene expression in offspring, leading to increased adiposity, elevated fat mass accumulation, and adipocyte hypertrophy. Epigenetic reprogramming represents another critical pathway as PAHs were found to be associated with DNA methylation states of genomic DNA and growth-related genes (IGF1 and IGF2) in the umbilical cord blood. PAHs could also disrupt endocrine function by disrupting maternal thyroid hormone transport and altering glucocorticoid receptor signaling through programming of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. These disruptions may lead to metabolic imbalances in the offspring.

The strength of our study lies in its prospective design. The use of medical records, structured questionnaires, and quality control protocols ensured high-quality outcome and covariate indicators. Second, by measuring internal exposure in blood samples as biomarkers rather than relying on air monitoring or questionnaires, we were able to more accurately reflect individual long-term exposure levels and capture all exposure routes. Additionally, we characterized postnatal growth during the first three years using trajectory analysis methods, which provided more details on offspring’s dynamic growth processes. The limitations of the current study need to be mentioned. First, we focused only on the three years after birth. Continued follow-up is essential to elucidate the long-term effects of PAHs exposure and to determine the impact of rapid higher trajectories on subsequent diseases. Despite adjustment for a broad array of potentially influential covariates during both pregnancy and the postnatal period, our findings remain susceptible to residual confounding from unaccounted variables.

In conclusion, we found that prenatal exposure to PAHs was associated with elevated risks for rapid higher growth trajectory during the first three years of life. Furthermore, maternal genetic variations may modify the impacts of PAH exposure on child growth. Given the established link between early life growth trajectories and later-life health outcomes, proactive monitoring and reduction of PAH exposure among susceptible pregnant women is imperative. Further studies are needed to validate our findings and track the long-term health consequences among children with different growth trajectories and translate these insights into healthcare prevention strategies to reduce downstream disease burden in children and adolescents.
