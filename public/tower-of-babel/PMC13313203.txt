Twf1 regulates adipocyte proliferation and differentiation through Yap nucleus accumulation
Authors: Chen Kai, Haiyan Xu, Suzhen Lv, Xin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity involves white adipose tissue (WAT) expansion via hyperplasia and hypertrophy. Impaired adipocyte proliferation leads to pathological hypertrophy, inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Aberrant F‑actin turnover disrupts proliferation/differentiation. Twinfilin‑1 (Twf1) regulates actin dynamics and cell proliferation/differentiation, but its role in adipocytes is unknown. Obese mice were induced by high‑fat diet (HFD) and weight loss by calorie restriction. Inguinal, epididymal and scapular adipose tissues were collected. Proteomics used pressure cycling, DDA library building and DIA quantification. Twf1 expression was validated by RT‑qPCR and Western blot. In C3H10T1/2 preadipocytes, Twf1 knockdown and overexpression were established. Differentiation was induced with dexamethasone, rosiglitazone, insulin and IBMX. Adipogenesis and proliferation were assessed by RT‑qPCR, Western blot, Oil Red O and CCK8. Nuclear‑cytoplasmic fractionation evaluated Yap localization and HIPPO activity. HFD mice showed weight gain, dyslipidemia and adipocyte hypertrophy, reversed by weight loss. Proteomics identified 43 proteins intersecting obesity, weight loss, and actin‑related datasets; Twf1 was upregulated in obese epididymal/inguinal fat and downregulated after weight loss. In C3H10T1/2 cells, Twf1 knockdown enhanced white adipocyte differentiation (upregulated Pparg2, Fsp27, Fabp4) and proliferation (2.28‑fold vs. control), promoted Yap nuclear accumulation, and inhibited HIPPO signalling. Twf1 overexpression reduced differentiation, lipid droplets, and proliferation (to 44.7% of control). Twf1 is upregulated in white/beige adipose tissue of obese mice and downregulated after weight loss. Twf1 knockdown promotes Yap nuclear accumulation, inhibits HIPPO and enhances adipocyte proliferation and white adipocyte maturation, indicating a key role in obesity development.KEYWORDS: Obesity, Twf1, white adipose tissue differentiation, proliferation, actin

Introduction

Obesity, defined as excessive fat accumulation, is a major public health issue [1]. Over the past 50 years, its prevalence has steadily risen worldwide. Based on data from 222 million individuals across 200 countries (1990–2022), the global age‑standardized obesity prevalence in 2022 reached 18.5% in women (374 million) and 14.0% in men (504 million). Among school‑aged children/adolescents, prevalence was 6.9% in girls (65.1 million) and 9.3% in boys (94.2 million) [2].

Overweight and obesity are also growing concerns in China. Over 40 years, dietary changes, physical activity and environment have driven rapid increases [3]. Using Chinese criteria (overweight: BMI 24–<28; obesity: BMI ≥28) [4], a 2019 real‑world study of 15.8 million adults found overweight in 34.8% and obesity in 14.1% of the population; complications were significantly more common in these groups than in normal‑weight individuals [5]. Another study estimated that in 2012, China had 453 million overweight/obese adults, projected to reach 790 million in 2023 (65.3% of adults), with obesity alone reaching 274 million; substantial increases among youth were also projected [6].

Obesity raises the risk of many metabolic diseases: type 2 diabetes, fatty liver, cardiovascular diseases (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, myocardial infarction, stroke), osteoarthritis, Alzheimer’s, depression and certain cancers (breast, ovarian, prostate, liver, kidney, colon) [7–13]. Our group previously identified high BMI as the leading risk factor for global diabetes burden [14]. In 2017, high BMI contributed to 4.7 million deaths and 147.7 million DALYs, increasing risks of diabetes, ischaemic heart disease, stroke and cancer [15]. The World Obesity Federation and medical associations in the United States and Canada have declared obesity a chronic, progressive disease that reduces quality of life and life expectancy [16]. In 2022, WHO launched the ‘Accelerating Obesity Control Plan’, emphasizing that curbing obesity is critical to meeting the 2030 target of reducing premature NCD mortality by one‑third.

Adipose tissue regulates systemic energy expenditure and is central to obesity. Three types exist: white (WAT), brown and beige [17–19]. WAT stores lipids. In obesity, it expands via hyperplasia (healthier) and hypertrophy (linked to early hypoxia, dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances) [20]. Healthy expansion requires adequate angiogenesis and ECM remodelling; pathological hypertrophy involves reduced angiogenesis, abnormal ECM deposition, M1 macrophage infiltration, insulin resistance and fibrosis [21]. Impaired hyperplasia exacerbates obesity and metabolic dysfunction [22–25]. In humans/mice, impaired WAT hyperplasia limits lipid storage, causing ectopic fat in liver, pancreas or muscle and thus insulin resistance [12,26]. Hypertrophy stores lipids but reduces surface‑area‑to‑volume ratio, impairs nutrient transport and disrupts signalling, promoting dysfunction [27].

Despite global weight-management efforts (clinics, anti‑obesity drugs), obesity prevalence continues to rise [17]. Even effective treatments like GLP‑1 agonists often lead to weight regain after stopping [28]. Thus, many will remain overweight/obese. Promoting adipocyte hyperplasia while alleviating pathological hypertrophy – thereby preserving metabolic health – is a promising strategy.

Adipogenesis (precursor differentiation into mature adipocytes) underlies hyperplasia [29] and depends on actin cytoskeleton remodelling [30,31]. Actin exists as G‑actin (globular) and F‑actin (filamentous); the cytoskeleton is mainly F‑actin, maintaining cell shape [32]. F‑actin turnover (polymerization/depolymerization) is dynamic [33]. In metabolically unhealthy obesity, impaired F‑actin turnover causes mitochondrial/metabolic dysfunction, inhibiting adipocyte proliferation and differentiation [34,35]. Before differentiation, precursors show accelerated F‑actin turnover; G‑actin accumulation facilitates cytoskeletal remodelling for lipid storage [36]. Conversely, excessive F‑actin accumulation promotes hypertrophy and dysfunction [37]. During hypertrophy, increased lipid droplets reduce F‑actin, lowering the F‑actin/G‑actin ratio and impairing insulin‑dependent Glut4 transport, contributing to insulin resistance [38].

Twf (Twinfilin) is an ADF‑H domain family member [39], encoded on human chromosome 12, critical for actin remodelling [40]. Mammals have Twf1 (non‑muscle tissues) and Twf2 (cardiac/skeletal muscle) [41]. Twf1 is highly expressed in cytoplasm, regulates cytoskeletal remodelling by sequestering actin monomers and capping barbed ends. It is often overexpressed in cancer, linked to poor prognosis [42,43] and upregulated in aged/diseased cholangiocytes [44]. Mouse/In-vitro studies show Twf1 overexpression reduces actin polymerization and inhibits F‑actin turnover [45,46]. In myoblasts, Twf1 deficiency increases F‑actin, activates Yap1 and its targets, modulates HIPPO and stimulates proliferation.

The HIPPO pathway controls organ size and homoeostasis. Yap is a key downstream effector; with TEAD, it inhibits HIPPO and promotes proliferation [47]. Core HIPPO: Stk3/Mst2‑Stk4/Mst1 with Sav1 phosphorylate and activate Lats1/2 with Mob1; Lats1/2 then phosphorylate/inactivate Yap/Taz [48]. Beyond proliferation, Yap/Taz are metabolically regulated and involved in obesity, diabetes, NAFLD, cardiovascular disease and cancer, with Yap activation mediated by AMPK, PFK1 and hexosamine pathway [49–52]. Yap promotes tissue homoeostasis and enhances glycolysis, lipogenesis and glutaminolysis [53]. Recent evidence also shows Yap regulates the cortical actomyosin network and inhibits F‑actin polymerization [54].

In this study, we established an HFD‑induced obese mouse model and mimicked weight loss via caloric restriction. Adipose tissue was collected at multiple time points (4, 8, 17, 21, 24 weeks) and during 10–15% and 20–25% weight loss. H&E staining and proteomics were performed. Integrating proteomic data from HFD and weight‑loss phases with GO actin‑related datasets, we identified differentially expressed proteins across three adipose tissues. Twf1 was selected. We generated Twf1 knockdown and overexpression models in C3H10T1/2 preadipocytes using lentiviral vectors, induced white adipocyte differentiation, assessed proliferation and explored the HIPPO pathway mechanism via nuclear/cytoplasmic protein analysis.

Materials and methods

Experimental cells

C3H/10T1/2, Clone 8, and HEK 293T cells were purchased from ATCC.

Mouse models

Male SPF C57BL/6J mice (Nanjing Jicui) were housed in Zhejiang University’s SPF facility (12 h light/dark cycle). Eighty 10-week-old mice with similar body weights were randomly divided into control (n = 30, chow diet: 22.8% protein, 13.8% fat, 63.4% carb) and HFD (n = 50, 20% protein, 60% fat, 20% carb) groups. Tissues were collected at baseline (10 weeks) and after 4, 8, 17, 21, 24 weeks of feeding. For caloric restriction, mice fed HFD for 17 weeks were baseline, then restricted with CD or HFD. Tissues were collected when weight loss reached 10–15% and 20–25% of baseline: CD 10–15% WL at 20 weeks; CD 20–25% WL at 21 weeks; HFD 10–15% WL at 21 weeks; HFD 20–25% WL at 23 weeks. Mice were anesthetized (i.p.) and cardiac-perfused. Bilateral epididymal, inguinal and interscapular brown adipose tissues were collected. One side was processed for pathology (H&E staining), the other snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for proteomics.

Sample size

Sample sizes were determined a priori with G*Power 3.1.9.7. Pilot data on adipocyte size (control vs. Twf1‑deficient) gave Cohen’s d = 1.2; with α = 0.05, power = 0.8, required n = 6/group. To account for variability, we used n = 8/group. For secondary outcomes (effect size 0.9), n = 8/group. All experiments were repeated three times independently. Total animal numbers are in figure legends.

Proteomics and PPI network

Proteomic samples (three biological replicates/group) were processed using pressure‑cycling technology. Peptides were fractionated by alkaline reversed‑phase HPLC. DDA established a spectral library (Spectronaut Pulsar X). DIA raw data were quantified with DIA‑NN. PPI networks were built using STRING (Mus musculus).

Cell culture

DMEM high‑glucose with 10% FBS, plus penicillin–streptomycin. Cryopreserved C3H/10T1/2 and HEK 293T cells were thawed rapidly at 37°C, transferred to medium, centrifuged (1200 rpm, 3 min), resuspended and cultured in 10 cm dishes at 37°C, 5% CO2. Medium changed every 2 days. Cells were passaged at ~60% confluence: washed with PBS, trypsinized, stopped with medium and reseeded. For cryopreservation at ~70% confluence, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, resuspended in freezing medium and stored at −80°C.

CCK8 assay

Exponential C3H/10T1/2 cells were trypsinized, resuspended to 4 × 104 cells/mL, seeded into 96‑well plates (100 μL/well), cultured for 24 h, then incubated with 10 μL CCK‑8 for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

White adipocyte differentiation

Exponential C3H/10T1/2 cells were seeded into 6‑well (2 mL/well), 12‑well (1 mL/well) or 6 cm dishes (4 mL/dish) and cultured to 100% confluence. After 24 h, differentiation was induced with dexamethasone, rosiglitazone, insulin, IBMX. After 48 h, medium was replaced with insulin‑containing medium changed every 2 days. Cells were observed at 96 h.

Oil Red O staining

Stock: 0.5 g Oil Red O in 100 mL isopropanol (dark storage). Cells were fixed with 10% formaldehyde (≥15 min), washed, then stained with working solution (6 mL stock +4 mL ddH2O) for 1 h at RT, washed three times and imaged.

RT‑qPCR

Reverse transcription: 200 ng RNA, reverse‑transcript enzyme, RNase‑free water to 10 μL. Programme: 37°C 15 min, 85°C 5 s, 4°C hold. qPCR: 10 μL 2× SYBR® Green Premix, 0.4 μL each primer (10 μM), 6 μL cDNA, 3.2 μL RNase‑free water. Programme: 95°C 30 s; 95°C 5 s, 60°C 30 s, 40 cycles; melt curve. Primers are listed in Table 1. Expression = 2−ΔΔCt.

Western blot

Protein lysates (20–40 µg) were resolved by 10% SDS‑PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked (Blocking One‑P, 1 h), probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, washed, incubated with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h), and visualized with ECL Prime. Results are fold changes normalized to control.

Twf1 stable cell lines

Gene construction: Primers with XbaI and SalI sites were designed. Twf1 coding sequence was amplified from mouse adipocyte cDNA, cloned into a vector, confirmed by colony PCR and digestion, then subcloned into pCMV‑GFP. The construct was sequenced and endotoxin‑free plasmid prepared.

Lentivirus packaging: HEK293T cells at 70–80% confluence were co‑transfected (PEI) with PLKO.1.CTR, PLKO.1.shTwf1, CMV‑GFP, CMV‑Twf1 and packaging plasmids. Supernatant was collected at 48 h and 72 h, combined, centrifuged (1000 rpm, 5 min) and stored at 4°C or −80°C.

Transduction: Cells seeded at ~50% confluence; virus + polybrene (10 μg/mL) added. After 4 h, medium added; after 14 h, medium replaced. At 72 h, puromycin (5 μg/mL) selected stable lines. Knockdown and overexpression were confirmed by RT‑qPCR and Western blot. Five lines were generated: normal control, empty GFP, Twf1 OE, empty CTR, Twf1 KD. Stable lines were maintained with low puromycin.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism 9 and R were used. Unpaired t‑test for two groups; one‑way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis for >2 groups. Data are mean ± SEM. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.01 (sic; likely ***p < 0.001).

Study approval

Animal procedures were approved by Zhejiang University’s IACUC (No. 2021‑129). All surgeries and tissue collections were performed under general anaesthesia (ketamine 80–100 mg/kg + xylazine 10–15 mg/kg, i.p.) to minimize suffering. Depth was monitored by pedal and palpebral reflexes. Euthanasia was by cervical dislocation under deep anaesthesia, following AVMA guidelines.

Result

High‑fat feeding increased body weight and adipocyte area in mice

To model obesity, mice were fed a high‑fat diet (HFD); controls received a standard diet (CD). White (epididymal), beige (inguinal) and brown (interscapular) adipose tissues were collected at 0, 4, 8, 17, 21 and 24 weeks. At 17 and 21 weeks, HFD mice had significantly higher body weight than CD mice (17 weeks: 45.83 g ± 1.29 vs. 28.31 g ± 1.38, p < 0.001; 21 weeks: 45.87 ± 1.40 vs. 29.64 ± 1.21, p < 0.001). Total cholesterol was also higher in HFD mice (17 weeks: 4.06 ± 0.25 vs. 2.05 ± 0.04 mmol/L, p < 0.05; 21 weeks: 4.51 ± 0.27 vs. 2.28 ± 0.28 mmol/L, p < 0.01).

At 17 weeks, H&E staining showed that adipocyte cross‑sectional area in inguinal and epididymal fat was increased vs. baseline (0 weeks) in both diets, but HFD induced a larger area than CD (p < 0.001). Epididymal adipocyte area in HFD was 2.00‑fold that of CD; inguinal area was 2.92‑fold. Interscapular brown adipocytes also showed increased area at 17 weeks (HFD vs. CD: 1.60‑fold, p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S1).

Using HFD 17-week body weight as baseline, four calorie‑restricted groups were studied: CD with 10–15% weight loss (WL), CD 20–25% WL, HFD 10–15% WL, and HFD 20–25% WL. H&E staining revealed reduced adipocyte area and lipid accumulation in all three fat depots vs. age‑matched HFD mice (21 weeks). In epididymal fat, area decreased by 34.52% (CD 10–15% WL, p < 0.01), 33.28% (CD 20–25% WL, p < 0.01), 29.79% (HFD 10–15% WL, p < 0.05) and 50.79% (HFD 20–25% WL, p < 0.001). Thus, weight loss alleviates lipid accumulation (Figure 1).

H&E staining of mouse adipose tissue during weight loss and quantification of adipocyte area.

Proteomics: common differentially expressed proteins

Proteomics detected 10,426 proteins across the three fat types. ANOVA (adj. p < 0.05) during HFD feeding identified 1080 (inguinal), 3423 (epididymal) and 2405 (interscapular) differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Venn analysis showed 1597 DEPs common to at least two fat types (Figure 2(A)). For weight‑loss models (baseline HFD 17 weeks, threshold fold change > 1.2, p < 0.05), we found 1586 (inguinal), 2850 (epididymal) and 1972 (interscapular) DEPs; total 5031 across all three types (Figure 2(B)). Comparison of HFD‑induced and weight‑loss DEPs revealed 1103 overlapping proteins (Figure 2(C)), including those involved in lipid storage, actin regulation and mitochondrial respiration.

Screening of differentially expressed proteins in mouse adipose tissue during obesity development and weight loss.

Actin cytoskeleton‑related common DEPs

From the GO database (GO:0032970, 460 actin regulatory genes), intersection with the 1103 overlapping DEPs gave 43 proteins (Figure 3(A,C)). Heatmap analysis of these 43 in CD vs. HFD at 17 weeks: in epididymal fat, most upregulated were Flii, Capg, Tmod; downregulated Hras, Mfn2, Myo1c (Supplementary Figure S2). In inguinal fat, upregulated Pam, Sptan1, Gmfb; downregulated Add3, Add1, Hdac2 (Supplementary Figure S3). In interscapular fat, upregulated Pam, Sptan1, Gmfb; downregulated Arap1, Pik3ca, Ptk2b (Supplementary Figure S4). Proteins with consistent changes in white/beige but opposite in brown fat included Twf1, Tmod3, Pam, Ptk2b, Gmfb (up in epididymal/inguinal) and Apoa1, Kank2 (down). Apoa1 is known to be down in obesity; Tmod3 linked to insulin‑induced actin remodelling; Gmfb involved in thermogenesis. PPI network (STRING) placed Twf1 among top five (Figure 3(B)). Twf1 was upregulated in obesity in inguinal/epididymal fat and decreased after weight loss.

Identification of actin‑related genes among differentially expressed proteins in adipose tissue during weight loss and obesity.

Twf1 is upregulated in white/beige adipocytes of HFD mice

RT‑qPCR and Western blot confirmed that Twf1 RNA and protein were significantly elevated in epididymal and inguinal fat of HFD vs. CD at 17 weeks, but not in brown fat (Figure 4(A,B)). In epididymal fat, HFD also increased adipogenic factors (Fabp4, Adipoq) and inflammatory factors (Ccl2, Ccl5, IL‑1β, TNF‑α) (Figure 4(C)).

Upregulation of Twf1 in epididymal and inguinal adipose tissues of high‑fat diet‑induced obese mice, along with enhanced expression of adipogenic and inflammatory factors in epididymal fat.

Obesity activates Yap/Taz and their targets

In inguinal and epididymal fat of HFD mice at 17 weeks, elevated Twf1 accompanied upregulation of Yap/Taz target genes (Supplementary Figure S5A,B). In epididymal fat, Yap increased 4.13‑fold (p < 0.01) and Taz 5.28‑fold (p < 0.01). Target genes Ctgf, Cyr61, Lats2, AmotL2 were also significantly increased.

Twf1 knockdown promotes preadipocyte proliferation

Lentiviral transduction generated stable Twf1‑knockdown C3H/10T1/2 preadipocytes (puromycin selection, Supplementary Figure S6). Successful knockdown was confirmed at RNA/protein levels during white adipogenic differentiation (days 0, 2, 4, 6; Figure 5). Knockdown cells retained normal proliferation. CCK‑8 assay (2 h incubation) showed that Twf1‑knockdown preadipocytes proliferated 2.28‑fold faster than controls (p < 0.01; Supplementary Figure S7).

RNA and protein quantification of Twf1‑knockdown cells on days 0, 2, 4 and 6 of adipogenic differentiation.

Twf1 knockdown enhances Yap expression and nuclear accumulation

RNA quantification revealed that Yap and Taz were upregulated in Twf1‑knockdown preadipocytes (Yap 2.19‑fold, p < 0.05; Taz 2.04‑fold, p < 0.05; Supplementary Figure S8(A)). During differentiation, Yap remained elevated (Supplementary Figure S8(B–D)). Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation and Western blot showed that in knockdown cells, cytoplasmic Yap increased 1.67‑fold (p < 0.001), cytoplasmic p‑Yap 1.39‑fold (p < 0.01), and nuclear Yap 1.52‑fold (p < 0.001), indicating a negative correlation between Twf1 and Yap (Supplementary Figure S9).

Twf1 knockdown promotes white adipogenic differentiation

Under white adipogenic induction, Twf1‑knockdown preadipocytes showed earlier and more abundant lipid droplets. On day 6, Oil Red O staining was markedly deeper in knockdown than control, indicating enhanced differentiation (Figure 6). Adipogenic genes (Pparg2, Fsp27, Fabp4) were significantly upregulated, especially from day 2 to 4. Thermogenic genes (Ucp1, Cidea, Cox8b, Ppargc1α) showed no notable changes except Elovl3. Ccl2 expression was reduced (Supplementary Figure S10).

Knockdown of Twf1 facilitates white adipocyte maturation of preadipocytes.

Twf1 overexpression suppresses preadipocyte proliferation

Lentiviral overexpression of Twf1 in C3H/10T1/2 preadipocytes was confirmed (puromycin selection, Supplementary Figures S11 and S12). CCK‑8 assay showed that Twf1‑overexpressing cells proliferated at only 44.7% of control rate (p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S13).

Twf1 overexpression suppresses Yap/Taz and their targets

RNA levels of Yap and Taz were decreased (Yap to 67.09% of control, p < 0.05; Taz to 59.91%, p < 0.05; Supplementary Figure S14(A)). During differentiation, Yap remained lower; Taz was lower except a transient increase on day 2 (Figure S14(B–D)). Target genes Ctgf, Cyr61, AmotL2, Lats2 were also downregulated.

Twf1 overexpression suppresses white adipogenic differentiation

After induction, few Twf1‑overexpressing cells formed large lipid droplets. Oil Red O staining was much lighter than control, indicating reduced differentiation efficiency (Supplementary Figure S15). Adipogenic factors (Pparg1, Fsp27, Fabp4) were downregulated throughout differentiation. Thermogenic gene Ppargc1α was upregulated, but Ucp1 showed no change. IL‑6 transcription increased; Ccl2 was unaffected (Supplementary Figure S16).

Discussion

In this study, HFD‑induced obese mice showed increased adipocyte area, which decreased after caloric restriction. Proteomic analysis combined with GO actin‑related data identified Twf1 as a differentially expressed protein: upregulated in obesity and downregulated after weight loss. Twf1 knockdown promoted preadipocyte proliferation by inhibiting HIPPO via Yap nuclear accumulation, while overexpression had opposite effects.

Obesity arises from energy imbalance, causing metabolic disturbances in liver, muscle, and fat [55]. WAT stores excess triglycerides, and its progressive lipid accumulation drives cardiometabolic risk [56–58]. Adipose expansion occurs through hypertrophy and hyperplasia [25,59,60]. Unhealthy hypertrophy involves reduced vascularization, fibrosis, immune cell recruitment, and inflammation; healthier expansion is protective [61–63]. Thus, targeting adipocyte maturation/proliferation may aid obesity management.

Morphological changes during adipocyte expansion require actin remodelling. High‑fat feeding shifts towards enhanced actin polymerization and suppressed F‑actin turnover [42]. We established HFD and CD mouse models with weight‑loss groups. Proteomics of three fat depots at multiple time points identified 1,103 common differentially expressed proteins (HFD vs. weight loss), including those regulating lipid storage, cell morphology, and mitochondrial respiration. Intersection with GO actin‑regulatory proteins gave 43 overlapping proteins. Changes in Twf1 and other cytoskeletal proteins indicate plastic morphological alterations during weight gain and loss.

Twf1 is a cytoplasmic actin‑binding protein that binds ADP‑actin monomers, blocks nucleotide exchange, and prevents filament assembly, thereby regulating cytoskeletal remodelling [37,38]. Preadipocytes undergo F‑actin disruption via RhoA/ROCK downregulation to allow lipid accumulation [34,59]. F‑actin increases with cell size and is further elevated by HFD. Actin remodelling (polymerization/depolymerization balance) is essential for adipocyte differentiation, insulin signalling, and glucose transport [42].

In epididymal WAT, HFD promoted expression of adiponectin, Fabp4, and inflammatory factors (Ccl2, IL‑1β), indicating lipid accumulation and metabolic dysregulation. Twf1 was significantly upregulated in epididymal and inguinal fat of HFD mice compared to brown fat, suggesting an important role in WAT during obesity.

Existing reports show Twf1 is upregulated in injured cholangiocytes [44], overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma (poor prognosis, correlates with immune infiltration) [64], and suppressed by miR‑103‑3p in obesity‑induced muscle atrophy, leading to increased F‑actin, Yap nuclear translocation, and cell cycle progression [65]. Twf1 deficiency reduces MyoD/MyoG and impairs myoblast fusion [45]. In cardiomyocytes, phenylephrine decreases miR‑1, upregulates Twf1, and induces hypertrophy [66].

We constructed Twf1‑knockdown C3H/10T1/2 preadipocytes using lentivirus. CCK‑8 showed proliferation rate 2.28‑fold that of controls. Under white adipogenic induction (dexamethasone, rosiglitazone, insulin, IBMX), knockdown cells had higher differentiation efficiency and more lipid droplets, indicating that Twf1 knockdown promotes both proliferation and differentiation. Conversely, Twf1 overexpression reduced proliferation to 44.7% of controls, suppressed white adipocyte maturation, and downregulated adipogenic factors.

Excessive lipid production in energy surplus impairs cell function, disrupts metabolic homoeostasis, and causes fatty acid spillover into non‑adipose tissues, leading to steatosis and insulin resistance [67]. Worsening obesity reduces hyperplastic expansion, causing hypoxia and inflammation. Hyperplasia‑favouring expansion mitigates negative effects [50,68]. We therefore examined HIPPO pathway components Yap/Taz. Twf1 knockdown increased Yap expression in both cytoplasm and nucleus; overexpression downregulated Yap and Taz, indicating a negative correlation. HIPPO signalling is a key negative growth regulator [50]. Yap/Taz are downstream effectors; HIPPO kinases activate Warts, which negatively regulates CycE and Diap1 [69]. Yap nuclear accumulation is critical for gene transcription. Twf1 knockdown increased nuclear Yap and enhanced proliferation. Thus, Twf1 negatively regulates Yap to suppress HIPPO and promote preadipocyte proliferation.

This study provides proteomic profiles of adipocytes from HFD‑induced obese mice and identifies Twf1 as a potential target. It is the first to explore Twf1’s role in preadipocyte proliferation/differentiation. Limitations include lack of flow cytometry to quantify total adipocyte number in HFD mice; no dynamic observation of Twf1‑mediated Yap translocation during proliferation/differentiation; limited time for in‑depth proteomic enrichment analysis; and no in-vivo adipocyte‑specific Twf1 knockout to validate Yap regulation. These will be addressed in future work.

In summary, Twf1 is markedly upregulated in WAT of HFD‑induced obese mice and downregulated after weight loss. Twf1 knockdown promotes adipocyte proliferation and white adipose hyperplasia, alleviating metabolic dysfunction. These findings identify a novel target for regulating WAT in obesity and offer a potential clinical strategy to combat metabolically unhealthy conditions.

Conclusions

In HFD‑induced obese mice, adipocyte area was significantly enlarged in epididymal, inguinal, and interscapular fat, with upregulation of adipogenic factors. Caloric restriction‑induced weight loss reduced adipocyte area in all three depots. Twf1 expression was elevated in epididymal and inguinal fat of obese mice. Twf1 knockdown promoted white adipogenic differentiation of preadipocytes, whereas overexpression suppressed white adipocyte maturation. Additionally, Twf1 knockdown enhanced Yap transcription and its target genes, promoted nuclear Yap accumulation, inhibited the HIPPO pathway, and thus facilitated preadipocyte proliferation. These results indicate that Twf1 plays a critical role in obesity development and progression.
