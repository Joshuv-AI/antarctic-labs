Error bounds for splitting methods in unitary problems
F. Casas, A. Murua, [2ex] Departament de Matemàtiques and IMAC, Universitat Jaume I12071-Castellón, Spain Email: Fernando.Casas@mat.uji.es, [1ex] Konputazio Zientziak eta A.A. Saila, Informatika Fakultatea, EHU/UPVDonostia/San Sebastián, Spain Email: Ander.Murua@ehu.es [1ex]

Abstract
Splitting methods constitute a widely used class of numerical integrators for ordinary and partial differential equations, particularly well suited to problems that can be decomposed into simpler subproblems. High-order splitting schemes are available that achieve high accuracy while preserving key qualitative properties of the underlying dynamical system, and are successfully used across a broad range of fields. In this work, we present a systematic analysis of both local and global errors arising from arbitrary splitting methods applied to unitary problems. Two complementary types of error estimates are derived. The first is expressed in terms of operator norms, while the second is formulated using norms of commutators and can, under suitable assumptions, be extended to certain classes of unbounded operators. Special attention is devoted to the case where only two operators are involved. The theoretical results are illustrated by deriving explicit error bounds for some representative schemes.



Keywords:
Splitting methods, error bounds, commutator-scaling property.

1
Introduction

Splitting methods constitute a natural option for getting approximations to the solution of differential equations when they
can be subdivided into different sub-problems easier to solve than the original system
McLachlan & Quispel 2002
.
Their range of applications covers a wide spectrum:
numerical analysis of differential equations, sampling of probability distributions in statistics and molecular dynamics
with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo methods, Vlasov–Poisson equations in plasma physics and quantum Monte Carlo methods,
to name just a few
Blanes et al. 2024
. They are also popular in the simulation of quantum systems in digital quantum computers, where they are called

product formulas

Childs & Su 2019
;
Chen et al. 2022
, and constitute a paradigmatic example of geometric numerical integrators
Hairer et al. 2006
, since they preserve by construction structural
properties of the exact solution, such as symplecticity (in classical Hamiltonian dynamics) and unitarity (in quantum evolution problems)
Blanes & Casas 2025
.

To establish the framework of our discussion, we start by considering the linear equation

\frac{du}{dt}=Au=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}\,u,\qquad u(0)=u_{0},

(1)

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}
are bounded linear operators acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
u(t)\in\mathcal{H}
for all
t
and

\|A_{j}\|=a_{j}<\infty
for
j=1,2,\ldots,N
.

The exact solution of (
1
),
u(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}u_{0}
, is supposed to be well defined for all
t\geq 0
. Associated
with equation (
1
), we have

\frac{d}{dt}U(t)=A\,U(t),\qquad U(0)=I,

(2)

in the sense that
u(t)=U(t)u_{0}
and

U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}

satisfies (
2
). It often happens that evaluating the action of the evolution operator
U(t)
on
u_{0}
is a difficult or computationally expensive task,
whereas computing each
\mathrm{e}^{tA_{j}}
separately is much more feasible. In that case
u(t)
can be approximated by

\mathrm{e}^{tA_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{tA_{N}}u_{0}
. In practice, however, this approximation performs poorly unless
t>0

is sufficiently small. Therefore, when the goal is to approximate the solution of (
1
) at a not-so-small final time
t=t_{f}

the standard approach is to divide the interval
[0,t_{f}]
into
K
subintervals of length
h
,
with
Kh=t_{f}
, and then compute the sequence

u_{k+1}=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\,u_{k},\qquad k\geq 0,

(3)

(or any other permutation of the operators
A_{j}
)
so that
u_{k+1}\approx u(t_{k+1})
with
t_{k+1}=(k+1)h
. This corresponds to the Lie–Trotter scheme, and a direct calculation shows that

\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}=\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\qquad\mbox{ as }\qquad h\rightarrow 0,

so that it is of first order of accuracy. A higher-order approximation can be achieved with a symmetrized version of (
3
),

S_{2}(h)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{2}}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\,\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{1}}.

(4)

This corresponds to the Strang splitting, and verifies

S_{2}(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}=\mathcal{O}(h^{3})\qquad\mbox{ as }\qquad h\rightarrow 0.

The more general composition

\Psi(h)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{h\,\alpha_{N}^{(j)}A_{N}}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{h\,\alpha_{2}^{(j)}A_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{h\alpha_{1}^{(j)}A_{1}},

(5)

can achieve a given order
p

if the number
s
and the coefficients
\alpha_{i}^{(j)}
are appropriately chosen so that

\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}=\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1})\qquad\mbox{ as }\qquad h\rightarrow 0.

(6)

In particular, a necessary and sufficient condition for order
p=1
is

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}^{(j)}=1,\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

(7)

For
p>1
, the parameters
\alpha_{i}^{(j)}
have to satisfy an additional set of algebraic equations (the so-called order conditions) whose
number and complexity growths exponentially with
p

Blanes et al. 2024
. For this reason, very often
compositions of the basic Lie–Trotter scheme (
3
) or the Strang splitting (
4
) are considered instead of the direct approach (
5
), since
then the number of order conditions is significantly reduced. More specifically:

•

Compositions of the basic Lie–Trotter scheme:

Let
\chi(h)
denote the basic Lie–Trotter scheme:

\chi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}},

(8)

and
\chi^{*}(h)
its adjoint:

\chi^{*}(h)=\chi(-h)^{-1}=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}},

(9)

both of which provide first-order approximations. One can then construct higher-order methods by taking compositions of the form

\Psi(h)=\chi(\alpha_{2s}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{2s-1}h)\cdots\chi(\alpha_{2}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{1}h),

(10)

where the coefficients
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2s}\in\mathbb{R}
are appropriately chosen so as to satisfy the required order conditions.

•

Compositions of the Strang splitting:

Define

\displaystyle\Psi(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi\left(\frac{\gamma_{s}}{2}h\right)\chi^{*}\left(\frac{\gamma_{s}}{2}h\right)\cdots\chi\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}h\right)\chi^{*}\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}h\right)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{2}(\gamma_{s}h)\cdots S_{2}(\gamma_{1}h),

with
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
.
This is a particular case of composition (
10
), with coefficients
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2s}
given by

(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{2s-1},\alpha_{2s})=\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2},\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2},\ldots,\frac{\gamma_{s}}{2},\frac{\gamma_{s}}{2}\right).

The well-known triple-jump
Yoshida 1990
and quintuple-jump
Suzuki 1991
methods of arbitrary order
p
are specific instances of (
11
). However, for orders
p\geq 6
, alternative choices of coefficients lead to more efficient schemes
Blanes et al. 2024
. These are typically obtained
by minimizing in a certain sense the main term in the local truncation error, that is, the term multiplying
h^{p+1}
in (
6
). In the analysis,
terms multiplying
h^{q}
with
q>p+1
are generally ignored based on the implicit assumption that, for sufficiently small
h
, their contribution to the error can be neglected
in comparison with the leading error term. Nevertheless, there are relevant physical examples where, for a fixed
h
, this is no longer the case. These
include near-neighbor lattice Hamiltonians
Childs & Su 2019
and some quantum chemistry systems
Wecker et al. 2014
. It is then much more helpful
to have rigorous (and ideally sharp) bounds for the error committed both when approximating
\mathrm{e}^{hA}
with
\Psi(h)
, that is, for

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}\equiv\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\,(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}\|

and for the global error after
k
iterations of the map,

\mathcal{E}_{g}=\|\Psi(h)^{k}-\mathrm{e}^{kh\,(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}\|

for an arbitrary positive integer
k
. Having explicit bounds for
\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
and
\mathcal{E}_{g}
is indeed of interest in several contexts.
In particular, in the
numerical analysis of differential equations, they can be used either to analyze the relative efficiency of different integrators by comparing
the respective bounds obtained or to construct new schemes by minimizing the bounds. Also, in the
Hamiltonian simulation in quantum computers by product formulas, minimizing the bound may lead to a reduction in complexity of the required
quantum circuit implementing the simulation
Childs et al. 2021
.

Previous analyses of error bounds for splitting methods go back to the work of Suzuki
Suzuki 1985
, where optimal bounds were obtained for the
Lie–Trotter (
3
) and Strang splitting (
4
) in terms of the norm of commutators of the
A_{j}
operators. The analysis for the
Strang splitting was later extended to unbounded operators in
Jahnke & Lubich 2000
and to splitting methods
of arbitrary order
p
in
Thalhammer 2008
;
Descombes & Thalhammer 2010
. An elementary approach for low-order schemes can be found in
Iserles & Kropielnicka 2024
, whereas in
Childs et al. 2021
a general theory is presented leading to estimates of the form

\mathcal{E}_{\ell}=\mathcal{O}\left(\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathrm{comm}}\,t^{p+1}\,\mathrm{e}^{4ts\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}}\right),

for a splitting method (
5
) of order
p
and
s
stages. Here

\tilde{\alpha}_{\mathrm{comm}}
are bounds on the norm of nested commutators involving
p+1
operators
A_{j}
.

In this work we carry out a systematic treatment of the error committed by splitting methods of arbitrary order
p
, providing two types of estimates.
The first type is formulated in terms of the norm
a_{j}
of the operators
A_{j}
involved.
In the second, we get explicit bounds depending on the norm of commutators
of the operators, generalizing the approach of
Iserles & Kropielnicka 2024
and sharpening the analysis of
Childs et al. 2021
. These results are
particularly relevant when the operators nearly commute and can in principle be generalized, under certain assumptions, to unbounded operators.

For simplicity, in the rest of the paper we will assume that the operators
A_{j}
in (
1
) are skew-adjoint, that is,
A_{j}^{\dagger}\equiv A_{j}^{*}=-A_{j}
, so that

U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tA}
is a unitary operator for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
U^{*}(t)U(t)=I
, and
\|U(t)\|=1
. This encompasses the important case
of quantum Hamiltonian problems.

2
Error bounds depending on the norm of the operators

Our approach for obtaining error bounds in terms of the norm of the operators
A_{j}
consists in identifying a suitable representation for both the exact solution of eq. (
1
)
and the numerical approximation rendered by the splitting method,
and then estimating the difference between them. We first establish a general result and then apply it to several
types of compositions.

2.1
General estimates of the local error

The most general splitting scheme (
5
) can be written in the form

\Psi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}=I+\sum_{n\geq 1}h^{n}\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=n}\frac{C_{r}^{j_{r}}\cdots C_{1}^{j_{1}}}{j_{r}!\cdots j_{1}!},

(12)

with
r=sN
, and

C_{i+N(j-1)}=\alpha_{i}^{(j)}\,A_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,\qquad 1\leq j\leq s.

If the consistency condition (
7
) holds, then

C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r}=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N}
, and the local error
\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}
of the scheme can be written simply as

\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{r}+\cdots+C_{1})}.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
are bounded skew-adjoint operators, then, for all
q\geq 1
,

\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}=I+\sum_{n=1}^{q}h^{n}\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=n}\frac{C_{r}^{j_{r}}\cdots C_{1}^{j_{1}}}{j_{r}!\cdots j_{1}!}+h^{q+1}\mathcal{R}_{q+1}(h),

(13)

where

\|\mathcal{R}_{q+1}(h)\|\leq\frac{\big(\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|\big)^{q+1}}{(q+1)!}.

Proof.

Consider the initial value problem

\frac{d}{d\tau}Y(\tau)=hA(\tau)Y(\tau),\qquad Y(0)=I,

(14)

where

A(\tau)=C_{j}\quad\text{for }\quad\tau\in[j-1,j],\qquad j=1,\ldots,r.

The solution of (
14
) at
\tau=r
is

Y(r)=\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}.

(15)

Since
A^{\dagger}(\tau)=-A(\tau)
, the solution operator
Y(\tau)
is unitary for all
\tau
and can be expanded as a power series in
h
(cf.
Blanes et al. 2024
),

Y(\tau)=I+\sum_{n=1}^{q}h^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}A(\tau_{1})\cdots A(\tau_{n})\,d\tau_{n}\cdots d\tau_{1}\,+h^{q+1}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{q+1}(\tau,h),

where for all
n
,

\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\tau,h)=\int_{0}^{\tau}\cdots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}A(\tau_{1})\cdots A(\tau_{n})Y(\tau_{n})\,d\tau_{n}\cdots d\tau_{1}.

Thus, by expanding the exponentials in (
15
), we get (
13
), with

\mathcal{R}_{q+1}(h)=\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{q+1}(r,h).

Let us determine next a bound for
\mathcal{R}_{n}(h)
. Since
Y(\tau)
is unitary, then

\|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(\tau,h)\|\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}\cdots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}\|A(\tau_{1})\|\cdots\|A(\tau_{n})\|\,d\tau_{n}\cdots d\tau_{1}.

Define now
a(\tau)=\|A(\tau)\|
, so that

\|\mathcal{R}_{n}(h)\|=\|\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{n}(r,h)\|\leq\int_{0}^{r}\cdots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}a(\tau_{1})\cdots a(\tau_{n})\,d\tau_{n}\cdots d\tau_{1}.

Next, consider the scalar initial value problem

\frac{d}{d\tau}y(\tau)=ha(\tau)y(\tau),\qquad y(0)=1,

whose solution admits the expansion

y(\tau)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}h^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\cdots\int_{0}^{\tau_{n-1}}a(\tau_{1})\cdots a(\tau_{n})\,d\tau_{n}\cdots d\tau_{1}.

(16)

Since

a(\tau)=\|C_{j}\|\quad\text{ for }\;\;\tau\in[j-1,j],\qquad j=1,\ldots,r,

we have

y(r)=\mathrm{e}^{h(\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|)}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}h^{n}\frac{(\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|)^{n}}{n!}.

(17)

Comparing (
17
) with expression (
16
) evaluated at
\tau=r
, we obtain the bound

\|\mathcal{R}_{n}(h)\|\leq\frac{(\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|)^{n}}{n!},

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2.1
, for all
q\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}\|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{q}h^{n}\left\|\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=n}\frac{C_{r}^{j_{r}}\cdots C_{1}^{j_{1}}}{j_{r}!\cdots j_{1}!}-\frac{(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})^{n}}{n!}\right\|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{h^{q+1}}{(q+1)!}\left((\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|)^{q+1}+\|C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r}\|^{q+1}\right).

Application of Corollary
2.2
to the general splitting scheme (
5
) with
r=s\,N
and

C_{i+N(j-1)}=\alpha_{i}^{(j)}\,A_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,\qquad 1\leq j\leq s,

(18)

leads directly to the following result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that the product formula

\Psi(h)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathrm{e}^{h\,\alpha_{N}^{(j)}A_{N}}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{h\,\alpha_{2}^{(j)}A_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{h\alpha_{1}^{(j)}A_{1}},

with
\alpha_{i}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}
, provides an approximation of order
p
to
\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}
,
with
A_{j}
skew-adjoint operators verifying that
\|A_{j}\|=a_{j}<\infty
for
j=1,\ldots,N
. Then, the following estimate holds:

\left\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}}\right\|\leq\frac{h^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}\left(\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\right]^{p+1}+\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\,\left(|\alpha_{i}^{(1)}|+\cdots+|\alpha_{i}^{(s)}|\right)\right]^{p+1}\right)

Proof.

Taking into account (
18
), the consistency condition (
7
), and the fact that
\Psi(h)
is of order
p
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}\left((\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|)^{p+1}+\|C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r}\|^{p+1}\right),

but
\|C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r}\|=\|A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N}\|\leq a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}
and

\|C_{1}\|+\cdots+\|C_{r}\|=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_{i}^{(1)}|\,a_{i}+\cdots+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_{i}^{(s)}|\,a_{i}.

∎

2.2
Estimates for compositions of the Lie–Trotter and Strang schemes

The previous analysis can be particularized when the product formula is a composition of Lie–Trotter and Strang schemes.
Specifically, composition (
10
) can be expressed as a product (
12
) of
r=2Ns

bounded operators
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
,

\Psi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}},

with

C_{(2j-2)N+k}=\alpha_{2j-1}A_{k},\qquad C_{(2j-1)N+k}=\alpha_{2j}A_{N-k+1},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N,\quad j=1,\ldots,s.

In consequence, the norms
\|C_{j}\|
are given by

\begin{array}[]{l}\|C_{(2j-2)N+k}\|=|\alpha_{2j-1}|\,a_{k},\\
\|C_{(2j-1)N+k}\|=|\alpha_{2j}|\,a_{N-k+1},\end{array}\qquad j=1,\ldots,s;\;k=1,\ldots,N,

so that

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\|C_{j}\|=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big(|\alpha_{2j-1}|\,a_{k}+|\alpha_{2j}|\,a_{N-k+1}\Big)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2s}|\alpha_{j}|\right)\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k}.

Therefore, in virtue of Corollary
2.2
, we have the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that the
2s
-stage composition

\Psi(h)=\chi(\alpha_{2s}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{2s-1}h)\cdots\chi(\alpha_{2}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{1}h),

with
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2s})\in\mathbb{R}^{2s}
, and

\chi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}},\qquad\chi^{*}(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}},

provides an approximation of order
p
to
\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}
,
with
A_{j}
skew-adjoint operators verifying that
\|A_{j}\|=a_{j}<\infty
for
j=1,\ldots,N
. Then, the following estimate holds:

\left\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\left[1+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2s}|\alpha_{i}|\right)^{p+1}\right]\,\left(h\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\right)^{p+1}.

(19)

Taking into account that the composition (
11
) of the Strang splitting constitutes a particular example of (
10
), we get an analogous
error estimate in this case.

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose that the
s
-stage composition

\Psi(h)=S_{2}(\gamma_{s}h)\,\cdots\,S_{2}(\gamma_{2}h)\,S_{2}(\gamma_{1}h)

with
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, and
S_{2}(h)=\chi(h/2)\chi^{*}(h/2)
with

\chi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}},\qquad\chi^{*}(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}},

provides an approximation of order
p
to
\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}
,
and
A_{j}
skew-adjoint operators verifying that
\|A_{j}\|=a_{j}<\infty
for
j=1,\ldots,N
. Then, the following estimate holds:

\left\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{(p+1)!}\left[1+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}|\gamma_{i}|\right)^{p+1}\right]\,\left(h\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\right)^{p+1}.

(20)

It is worth remarking that bounds (
19
) and (
20
) depend solely on the 1-norm of the coefficient vectors
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2s})
and

(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s})
, respectively. This observation provides some theoretical ground for the common practice when designing of numerical schemes, namely minimizing this 1-norm for getting more efficient integrators
Blanes et al. 2024
.

2.3
Estimates of the local error for symmetric schemes

Composition schemes (
10
) and (
11
) with a palindromic sequence of coefficients, that is,

\Psi(h)=\chi(\alpha_{2s}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{2s-1}h)\cdots\chi(\alpha_{2}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{1}h),\quad\mbox{ with }\quad\alpha_{2s+1-j}=\alpha_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq 2s

(21)

and

\Psi(h)=S_{2}(\gamma_{s}h)\,\cdots\,S_{2}(\gamma_{2}h)\,S_{2}(\gamma_{1}h)\qquad\mbox{ with }\qquad\gamma_{s+1-i}=\gamma_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq s,

(22)

respectively, are a common choice for high-order methods, as they preserve time symmetry and typically offer a favorable trade-off between the number of maps in the composition and the resulting accuracy.
It turns out that the previous error estimates can also be improved for these cases, as we next show.

The mapping (
21
) admits a symmetric factorization,

\Psi(h)=\Phi^{*}(h)\,\Phi(h),

(23)

where

\Phi^{*}(h)=\Phi(-h)^{-1}
, and

\Phi(h)=\chi^{*}(\alpha_{s}h)\,\chi(\alpha_{s-1}h)\,\cdots\,\chi(\alpha_{2}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{1}h)\qquad\mbox{ for $s$ odd},

(24)

and

\Phi(h)=\chi(\alpha_{s}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{s-1}h)\,\cdots\,\chi(\alpha_{2}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{1}h)\qquad\mbox{ for $s$ even}.

(25)

We will make use of the following auxiliary result for arbitrary approximations
\Psi(h)
of
\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n})}
that admit the symmetric factorization (
23
):

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Phi(h)
be a one-parameter family of unitary operators, and consider the symmetric factorization
\Psi(h)=\Phi^{*}(h)\,\Phi(h)
.
Then

\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\,(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}\|\leq\|\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\|,

where

\Theta(h)=\Phi(h)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{h}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}.

(26)

Proof.

The result follows from the identity

\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\,(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}=\Phi^{*}(h)\,\big(\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})},

and the unitarity of both
\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}
and
\Phi(h)
.
∎

Clearly, the scheme is of order of accuracy
p
if and only if

\mathcal{E}(h):=\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)=\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1})\quad\mbox{as}\quad h\to 0.

(27)

Observe that,
\mathcal{E}(-h)=-\mathcal{E}(h)
, which shows that symmetric schemes necessarily attain even orders of accuracy.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose that the time-symmetric
2s
-stage composition (
21
)

\Psi(h)=\chi(\alpha_{1}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{2}h)\cdots\chi(\alpha_{2}h)\,\chi^{*}(\alpha_{1}h),

with

2.4
Global error

Up to this point, we have examined the error produced after a single integration step. However, for practical applications it is also important
to understand how this error accumulates over multiple steps, say after
k

The resulting global error can be estimated as follows. We have

\|u_{k}-u(t_{k})\|=\|\Psi^{k}(h)u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{khA}u_{0}\|,

but we can write

\Psi^{k}(h)u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{khA}u_{0}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Psi^{k-1-j}(h)\,\big(\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{hA}\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{jhA}u_{0}.

Since both
\Psi(h)
and
\mathrm{e}^{hA}
are unitary (so that
\|\Psi^{m}(h)v\|=\|v\|
for any vector

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Psi^{k}(h)u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{khA}u_{0}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\big(\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{hA}\big)\,\mathrm{e}^{jhA}u_{0}\|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{hA}\|\,\|\mathrm{e}^{jhA}u_{0}\|

\displaystyle=k\,\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{hA}\|\|u_{0}\|,

so that, in the unitary case, the global error after
k

k
times the local error.

\|A_{j}\|\leq a_{j}
. Then, for
q\geq p
, the local error satisfies

\left\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}}\right\|\leq\sum_{n=p+1}^{q}d_{n}^{\mathrm{sym}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s})\,\lambda^{n}+\frac{\lambda^{q+1}}{(q+1)!\,2^{q}}\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{s}|\gamma_{i}|\right)^{q+1},

where
\lambda=h(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N})
and

d_{n}^{\mathrm{sym}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s})=2\sum_{m\geq 1}\sum_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{m}=n}\frac{|v_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s})|}{i_{1}!\cdots i_{m}!}

(48)

if
n
is odd and
d_{n}^{\mathrm{sym}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{s})=0
if
n
is even.

4
Error bounds in terms of commutators

While the previous method relies on computing power series expansions of both the exact solution and the numerical map, and then bounding the norm of their difference, the approach we now introduce is more indirect. Instead, we first derive the linear differential equation satisfied by the numerical flow. The error is then estimated by bounding the norm of the difference between the coefficient matrix of this equation and the operator
A=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N}
.
In other words, rather than working with expansions in the unitary group, we consider expansions in the associated Lie algebra of skew-adjoint operators. This allows the resulting bounds to be expressed directly in terms of commutators of the operators involved. We now summarize the main steps of this approach.

4.1
Estimates based on the modified equation satisfied by the splitting method

As in Proposition
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
, we begin by considering the approximation of
\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}
, where
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
are arbitrary skew-adjoint matrices, by the product of exponentials

\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}.

Our goal is to estimate the norm of the difference

\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}
. We define

M(t)=\left(\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{e}^{tC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{tC_{1}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-tC_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{-tC_{r}},

(50)

so that
\Psi(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{tC_{1}}
is the solution operator of the initial value problem

\frac{d}{dt}\Psi(t)=M(t)\,\Psi(t),\qquad\Psi(0)=I.

(51)

Lemma 4.1
.

Given
r>1
and arbitrary skew-adjoint matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
, then

\|\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}\|\leq\int_{0}^{h}\|M(t)-(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})\|\,dt,

where
M(t)
is given by (
50
).

Proof.

The operator
W(t)=\Psi(t)-\mathrm{e}^{t(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}
clearly verifies

\frac{d}{dt}W(t)=(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})W(t)-R(t)\Psi(t),\qquad W(0)=0,

where
R(t)=M(t)-(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})
. Integrating this equation leads to

W(h)=-\int_{0}^{h}\mathrm{e}^{(h-t)(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}R(t)\Psi(t)dt,

so that

\|W(h)\|\leq\int_{0}^{h}\|\mathrm{e}^{(h-t)(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}\|\,\|R(t)\|\,\|\Psi(t)\|dt=\int_{0}^{h}\|R(t)\|\,dt,

since both
\mathrm{e}^{t(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}
and
\Psi(t)
are unitary operators.
∎

An explicit expression of (
50
) can be obtained as

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=C_{r}+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{r}}}C_{r-1}+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{r}}}\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{r-1}}}C_{r-2}+\cdots

(52)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{r}}}\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{3}}}C_{2}+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{r}}}\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{2}}}C_{1},

where
\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y=[X,Y]=XY-YX
, and

\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y=\mathrm{e}^{t\,X}\,Y\,\mathrm{e}^{-t\,X}.

Observe that, if
X
is a skew-adjoint operator, then

\left\|\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y\right\|\leq\|Y\|.

It is straightforward to check that
S(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}}
actually satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}S(t)Y=S(t)\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}Y,\qquad S(0)=Y,

which implies the well-known identity

\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{k}Y=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\underbrace{[X,[X,\ldots,[X}_{k},Y]]].

(53)

The expression of
M(t)
given by (
52
) can be obtained in a more compact way if we introduce
the operators
Z_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,r
, defined recursively as

\displaystyle Z_{1}=C_{1}

(54)

\displaystyle Z_{k}(t)=C_{k}+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}Z_{k-1}(t),\qquad\qquad k=2,\ldots,r.

Then, clearly,
M(t)=Z_{r}(t)
.

For each
k\geq 2
,
Z_{k}(t)
can be expanded as a series in powers of
t
as

Z_{k}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}Z_{k,n},

(55)

where

\displaystyle Z_{k,0}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+\cdots+C_{k},

(56)

\displaystyle Z_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{j!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{j}Z_{k-1,n-j},\quad n\geq 1,

or explicitly,

\displaystyle Z_{1,n}=0,\qquad n\geq 1

(57)

\displaystyle Z_{k,n}=\sum_{j=2}^{k}\,\sum_{i_{j}+\cdots+i_{k}=n}\frac{\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{i_{k}}\cdots\mathrm{ad}_{C_{j}}^{i_{j}}}{i_{k}!\cdots i_{j}!}C_{j-1},\quad k\geq 2,\quad n\geq 0.

We next aim at expressing
M(t)
as the
n
th order truncation of that series plus a remainder.
To this end, we will apply Lemma
4.2
below, which is a simple consequence of the Taylor expansion of the exponential function
with remainder in integral form.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X,Y
be two linear operators and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for any
n\geq 1
,

\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}}Y=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{k}Y+t^{n}G_{n}\left(t,X,Y\right),

where

G_{n}(t,X,Y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{e}^{t\,(1-\sigma)\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}}\,\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{n}Y\,d\sigma.

(58)

We can express (
55
) for any given
q\geq 1
as

Z_{k}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}t^{n}Z_{k,n}+t^{q}R_{k,q}(t),\qquad k\geq 2,

(59)

where the expression of
R_{k,q}(t)
can be obtained from (
54
) as follows:

\displaystyle Z_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=C_{k}+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}Z_{k-1}(t)=C_{k}+\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}t^{n}\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}Z_{k-1,n}+t^{q}\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}R_{k-1,q}(t)

\displaystyle=C_{k}+\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}t^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{q-1-n}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{j}Z_{k-1,n}+t^{q}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}G_{q-n}\left(t,C_{k},Z_{k-1,n}\right)+t^{q}\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}R_{k-1,q}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}t^{n}Z_{k,n}+t^{q}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}G_{q-n}\left(t,C_{k},Z_{k-1,n}\right)+t^{q}\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}R_{k-1,q}(t),\

with
R_{1,q}(t)=0
. We thus have, for
k\geq 2
,

R_{k,q}(t)=\hat{R}_{k,q}(t)+\mathrm{e}^{t\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}R_{k-1,q}(t),

(60)

where

\hat{R}_{k,q}(t)\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{q}G_{n}\left(t,C_{k},Z_{k-1,q-n}\right).

In consequence,

M(t)=Z_{r}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}C_{j}+\sum_{n=1}^{q-1}t^{n}Z_{r,n}+t^{q}\,R_{r,q}(t),\qquad q\geq 1,

(61)

where

\|R_{r,q}(t)\|\leq\|\hat{R}_{2,q}(t)\|+\cdots+\|\hat{R}_{r,q}(t)\|.

Taking into account the definition of
\hat{R}_{k,q}(t)
and (
58
), we can write
for
k\geq 2

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{k,q}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{t\,(1-\sigma)\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}}\,\left(\sum_{n=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,q-n}\right)d\sigma,

and hence,

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{R}_{k,q}(t)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,q-n}\right\|d\sigma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider (
52
) and (
56
) for arbitrary skew-adjoint matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
. Then

\left\|M(t)-\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}t^{n}\,Z_{r,n}\right\|\leq t^{q}\sum_{k=2}^{r}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,q-n}\right\|d\sigma.

This, together with Lemma
4.1
leads to the following result:

Proposition 4.4
.

Given arbitrary skew-adjoint matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{r}
,

\|\mathrm{e}^{hC_{r}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hC_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(C_{1}+\cdots+C_{r})}\|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{q-1}\frac{h^{n+1}}{n+1}\|Z_{r,n}\|+h^{q+1}\,\mathcal{R}_{q}(h),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{q}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q+1}\sum_{k=2}^{r}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,q-n}\right\|d\sigma,

(62)

and the skew-adjoint operators
Z_{k,n}
are given by the recursion (
56
).

At this point it is illustrative to specify these bounds for the most elementary splitting methods.

(a) Lie–Trotter.

We consider
\Psi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}
for skew-adjoint operators
A_{1},\ldots,A_{N}
. Application of Proposition
4.4
with
r=N
,
C_{j}=A_{j}
,
q=1
, directly gives

\|\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}\|\leq\frac{h^{2}}{2}\,\sum_{k=2}^{N}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[A_{k},A_{j}]\,\right\|.

(b) Strang splitting with two operators.

We now consider the Strang splitting with
N=2
, that is
\Psi(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}A_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{tA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}A_{1}}
.
Application of Proposition
4.4
with
q=2
,
r=3
,
C_{1}=C_{3}=\frac{1}{2}A_{1}
,
C_{2}=A_{2}
,
Z_{2,2}=0
(since it is of second order) gives

\|\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+A_{2})}-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\|\leq h^{3}\mathcal{R}_{2}(h),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma\,\|\mathrm{ad}_{C_{2}}^{2}C_{1}\|\,d\sigma+\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\mathrm{ad}_{C_{3}}\mathrm{ad}_{C_{2}}C_{1}+\sigma\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{3}}^{2}(C_{1}+C_{2})\right\|d\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\,\|\mathrm{ad}_{C_{2}}^{2}C_{1}\|+\frac{1}{12}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|(1-\sigma)\,[A_{1},[A_{2},A_{1}]]\right\|d\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{12}\,\|[A_{2},[A_{2},A_{1}]\|+\frac{1}{24}\left\|[A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]]\right\|.

We thus have

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+A_{2})}-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\|\leq\frac{h^{3}}{12}\|[A_{2},[A_{1},A_{2}]]\|+\frac{h^{3}}{24}\|[A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]]\|,

(63)

i.e, we get the optimal bound, reproducing previous results
Suzuki 1985
;
Childs et al. 2021
;
Iserles & Kropielnicka 2024
. The bound (
63
) is
optimal in the sense that, in virtue of the symmetric Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula
Blanes & Casas 2025
, one has

\lim_{h\to 0}h^{-2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+A_{2})}-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\right)=\frac{1}{12}[A_{2},[A_{1},A_{2}]]+\frac{1}{24}[A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]],

so that the coefficient
\frac{1}{12}
or
\frac{1}{24}
cannot be replaced by smaller ones in (
63
). Indeed, this would lead to a contradiction for operators
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
such that
[A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]]=0
or
[A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]]=0
respectively.

Remark 4.5
.

The estimate for the remainder of the
(q-1)
th order truncated expansion of
M(t)
given in Lemma
4.3
above can be simplified by applying the triangular inequality to the integrand in the estimate as follows:

\left\|M(t)-\sum_{n=0}^{q-1}t^{n}\,Z_{r,n}\right\|\leq t^{q}\sum_{k=2}^{r}\sum_{n=1}^{q}\left\|\frac{1}{n!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,q-n}\right\|.

However, using that simplified upper bound of the remainder of
M(t)
would lead to the following suboptimal estimate of the local error of Strang splitting

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+A_{2})}-\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}\|\leq\frac{h^{3}}{12}\|[A_{2},[A_{1},A_{2}]]\|+\frac{h^{3}}{8}\|[A_{1},[A_{1},A_{2}]]\|,

(64)

instead of the optimal one (
63
).

(c) Strang splitting with an arbitrary number of operators.

Although Proposition
4.4
provides the optimal bound for the local error committed by the Strang splitting with two operators, this is not generally the
case when
N>2
. A better estimate is obtained by following the same approach as in subsection
2.3
, i.e., by considering the symmetric
factorization (
23
),
with
\Phi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{hA_{1}}
(or any other permutation of the factors) and
\Phi^{*}(h)=\Phi(-h)^{-1}
. To proceed, we need the following
preliminary result.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\Theta(t)
be a one-parameter family of unitary operators and consider the skew-adjoint operator

M(t)=\left(\frac{d}{dt}\Theta(t)\right)\Theta(t)^{-1},

(65)

so that
\Theta(t)
is the solution operator of

\frac{d}{dt}\Theta(t)=M(t)\Theta(t),\qquad\Theta(0)=I.

Then, for all
h\in\mathbb{R}
,

\|\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\|\leq\int_{0}^{h}\|M(t)+M(-t)\|\,dt.

Proof.

The proof is very similar indeed to the one of Lemma
4.1
. In this case we take
W(t)=\Theta(t)-\Theta(-t)
, so that

\frac{d}{dt}W(t)=M(t)W(t)+B(t),\qquad W(0)=0,

with
B(t)\equiv(M(t)+M(-t))\,\Theta(-t)
. In consequence
Coddington & Levinson 1955
,

W(t)=\Xi(t)\int_{0}^{t}\Xi^{-1}(s)B(s)\,ds,

where
\Xi(t)
is the fundamental matrix of the system
\frac{d}{dt}W(t)=M(t)W(t)
. Since
\|\Xi(t)\|=1
, then

\|W(t)\|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|B(s)\|ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|M(s)+M(-s)\|\,ds,

and the result follows.
∎

Equipped with this lemma, we can now deduce a refined error estimate for the 2nd-order scheme

S_{2}(h)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{2}}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{hA_{N}}\,\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}hA_{1}}.

Notice that
S_{2}(h)
admits the factorization (
23
),
S_{2}(h)=\Phi^{*}(h)\Phi(h)
, with

\Phi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{N}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}A_{1}}
. Let us introduce the operator

\Theta(t)=\Phi(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}A_{N}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}A_{1}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}.

(66)

A straightforward calculations shows that

S_{2}(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots A_{N})}=\Phi^{*}(h)\big(\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\big)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots A_{N})}

and application of Lemma
4.6
leads to

\|S_{2}(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}\|\leq\|\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\|\leq\int_{0}^{h}\|M(t)+M(-t)\|\,dt,

(67)

where
M(t)
is given by (
65
) and
\Theta(t)
by (
66
). In consequence,
by taking
r=N+1
,
q=2
,
C_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})
,
C_{j+1}=\frac{1}{2}A_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,N
in
expression (
61
), we have

M(t)=tZ_{r,1}+t^{2}R_{r,2}(t),

and Lemma
4.6
gives

\|M(t)+M(-t)\|\leq 2\,t^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{N+1}\int_{0}^{1}\|\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}Z_{k-1,1}+\sigma\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{2}Z_{k-1,0}\|\,d\sigma.

Since
Z_{k-1,0}=-\frac{1}{2}(A_{k-1}+\cdots+A_{N})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{2}Z_{k-1,0}=-\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{ad}_{A_{k-1}}^{2}(A_{k}+\cdots+A_{N}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}Z_{k-1,1}=\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k-2}\mathrm{ad}_{C_{j+1}}Z_{j,0}=-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^{k-2}\mathrm{ad}_{A_{k-1}}\mathrm{ad}_{A_{j}}(A_{j+1}+\cdots+A_{N})

and (
67
) leads finally to

\displaystyle\|S_{2}(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{N})}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{h^{3}}{12}\sum_{k=2}^{N+1}\Big(\big\|[A_{k-1},\sum_{j=1}^{k-2}[A_{j},A_{j+1}+\cdots+A_{N}]]\big\|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\big\|[A_{k-1},[A_{k-1},A_{k}+\cdots+A_{N}]]\big\|\Big).

This expression reproduces the previous estimate (
63
) when
N=2
.

4.2
Error bounds for splitting methods with two operators

4.2.1
Generic estimates

The case when the problem (
1
) can be separated into only two parts, i.e.,

\frac{du}{dt}=(A+B)u,\qquad u(0)=u_{0},

(68)

is very common in applications. Here and in the sequel the two skew-adjoint operators involved are denoted as
A
and
B
for simplicity, and the goal
is to get specific error estimates for time-symmetric splitting methods of the form

\Psi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{ha_{1}A}\,\mathrm{e}^{hb_{1}B}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{ha_{s}A}\,\mathrm{e}^{hb_{s}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{ha_{s}A}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{hb_{1}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{ha_{1}A},

(69)

in terms of a basis of the free Lie algebra generated by the two operators
A
and
B
. Notice that since it is possible to set
b_{s}=0
, this format includes
schemes where the central exponential involves the operator
A
instead of
B
. Likewise, integrators whose first (and last) term corresponds to the exponential of
B
can be
reproduced by (
69
) by setting to zero the corresponding coefficients.

Proceeding in an analogous was as for the Strang splitting, we introduce the operators

\Phi(h)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}b_{s}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{ha_{s}A}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{hb_{1}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{ha_{1}A},

so that
\Psi(h)=\Phi^{*}(h)\Phi(h)
, and

\Theta(t)=\Phi(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{2}(A+B)}.

(70)

As before,

\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}=\Phi^{*}(h)\big(\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\big)\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}(A+B)},

and therefore

\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}\|\leq\|\Theta(h)-\Theta(-h)\|\leq\int_{0}^{h}\|M(t)+M(-t)\|\,dt.

(71)

The expression of
M(t)
can be obtained from (
65
) with

\Theta(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{t}{2}b_{s}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{ta_{s}A}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{e}^{tb_{1}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{ta_{1}A}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{2}(A+B)}.

(72)

In other words,
M(t)
is given by (
61
) with
r=2s+1
,

\displaystyle C_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}(A+B),\qquad C_{2s}=a_{s}A,\qquad C_{2s+1}=\frac{1}{2}b_{s}B

(73)

\displaystyle C_{2j}=a_{j}A,\qquad C_{2j+1}=b_{j}B,\qquad j=1,\ldots,s-1

Let us assume now that the symmetric scheme (
69
) is of (even) order
2p
. Taking into account (
27
) and (
70
), then

M(t)+M(-t)=\mathcal{O}(t^{2p})
. In consequence, application of Lemma
4.3
leads to

\displaystyle\|M(t)+M(-t)\|

\displaystyle\leq t^{2p}\,\|R_{2s+1,2p}(t)+R_{2s+1,2p}(-t)\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\,t^{2p}\,\sum_{k=2}^{2s+1}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{2p}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,2p-n}\right\|d\sigma,

and we have the following error estimate:

Proposition 4.7
.

Given the time-symmetric splitting method (
69
) of order
2p
, with
A
,
B
skew-adjoint matrices, then

\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}\|\leq\frac{2}{2p+1}h^{2p+1}\,\sum_{k=2}^{2s+1}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{2p}\frac{\sigma^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,\mathrm{ad}_{C_{k}}^{n}Z_{k-1,2p-n}\right\|d\sigma,

where the skew-adjoint operators
Z_{k,n}
and
C_{k}
are given by (
56
) and (
73
), respectively.

Remark 4.8
.

Whereas this result has been obtained from Lemma
4.3
with
q=2p
, the order of the method, it is clear that one can get
more refined estimates by taking larger values of
q
, bounding appropriately the quantities
Z_{2s+1,n}
, with

n=2p+1,2p+3,\ldots,q
and finally bounding the corresponding remainder.

: it yields significantly sharper estimates in

in many relevant applications one encounters the identity

[[[A,B],B],B]=0.

(78)

This is the case, for instance, of classical Hamiltonian systems whose kinetic energy is quadratic in momenta and the potential
only depends on coordinates, and also the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, to be discussed below.
In such cases, all basis elements

involving this

corresponding contributions
\theta_{k,n,i}
and

substantially and become considerably sharper.

From an algebraic point of view,
the sequences of indices

and

used in Table

generated by

and

.

constitute the

\leq 7
, of the basis for the free Lie algebra
proposed in

.
This basis is a particular instance of a Hall–Viennot–Sirsov basis

Viennot 1978
;
Reutenauer 1993
, constructed so as to enjoy a triangular recursive
structure compatible with the adjoint actions (
75
).
An additional useful feature of the basis introduced in
McLachlan & Murua 2019
is that it yields a natural direct
sum decomposition of the free Lie algebra

\mathfrak{L}(A,B)\;=\;I\;\oplus\;\mathfrak{L}(A,B)/I,

where

is the Lie ideal generated by the element
[[[A,B],B],B]
.
In other words, the basis elements split into

•

those that lie in
I
(and hence vanish for operators satisfying

[[[A,B],B],B]=0
), and

•

those that project to nonzero elements in the quotient

\mathfrak{L}(A,B)/I
.

The indices typeset in
boldface
in Table

identify
precisely the elements of the basis of the quotient
\mathfrak{L}(A,B)/I
,
i.e., the Lie monomials that remain nonzero whenever
[[[A,B],B],B]=0
.
The indices in normal (nonbold) type correspond to elements of the ideal

,
and hence to commutators that vanish automatically in any setting where

.

4.2.3
Numerical illustration

The estimate (
76
) for the local error bound is illustrated next on some standard schemes applied to a system
split into two parts such that condition (
78
) holds.

As a first example
we take again the 6th-order time-symmetric composition (
49
) of order 6 and 13 stages
already considered in section
3.4
with

S_{2}(\gamma_{j}h)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}\gamma_{j}A}\,\mathrm{e}^{h\gamma_{j}B}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}\gamma_{j}A}.

By carrying out the necessary calculations we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}\|\leq 10^{-5}\cdot h^{7}\Big(0.0865\,\|E_{24}\|+0.6488\,\|E_{25}\|+0.5591\,\|E_{26}\|

\displaystyle+\underline{1.4199\,\|E_{27}\|}+\underline{0.2866\,\|E_{28}\|}+0.4072\,\|E_{29}\|+\underline{1.9947\,\|E_{30}\|}

\displaystyle+\underline{1.1001\,\|E_{31}\|}+0.8357\,\|E_{32}\|+0.1288\,\|E_{33}\|+0.6438\,\|E_{34}\|

\displaystyle+\underline{2.002\,\|E_{35}\|}+1.6287\,\|E_{36}\|+\underline{1.1208\,\|E_{37}\|}+2.6227\,\|E_{38}\|

\displaystyle+\underline{2.6531\,\|E_{39}\|}+\underline{1.7839\,\|E_{40}\|}+0.7644\,\|E_{41}\|\Big).

Underlined
terms disappear from the error bound when the identity
[[[A,B],B],B]=0
is satisfied.

Next we take the two well known Runge–Kutta–Nyström splitting methods of order 4 and 6 presented in
(
Blanes & Moan 2002
, Table 3)

and recommended in
Blanes et al. 2024
for their high efficiency. The error bounds are given, respectively, by

\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}\|\leq 10^{-3}\cdot h^{5}\Big(10.15\,\|E_{9}\|+1.04\,\|E_{11}\|+0.022\,\|E_{12}\|+3.56\,\|E_{14}\|\Big)

and

\displaystyle\|\Psi(h)-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}\|\leq 10^{-7}\cdot h^{7}\Big(0.13146\|E_{24}\|+0.46785\|E_{25}\|+0.63853\|E_{26}\|

\displaystyle+0.69042\|E_{29}\|+1.59218\|E_{32}\|+0.05566\|E_{33}\|+0.924511\|E_{34}\|

\displaystyle+3.85208\|E_{36}\|+3.81\|E_{38}\|+0.1649\|E_{41}\|\Big).

Observe that the individual terms in the error bound for this specially designed scheme attain significantly smaller numerical values than those corresponding to the composition (
22
), in agreement with the behavior observed in practical integrations.

4.3
Estimates for unbounded skew-adjoint operators

The error bound provided by Proposition
4.7
(or alternatively by eq. (
76
))
has been obtained with the requirement that
A
and
B
are bounded. It turns out, however, that it can be extended (under suitable
general assumptions) to the case
when
A
and possibly
B
are unbounded
skew-adjoint operators acting on
a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Thus, suppose we have a splitting method (
69
) which is of order
2p
in the classical, bounded setting (i.e., it satisfies eq. (
77
)) and that we want to bound the local error

\|\Psi(h)u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}u_{0}\|
for some
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.
We make
the following assumptions on
u_{0}
and the operators
A
and
B
:

•

Assumption 1
.
A
,
B
, and
A+B
are skew-adjoint operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and
u_{0}
belongs
to the domain of
A
,
B
and
A+B
.

•

Assumption 2
.
G
is a group of unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
containing
\mathrm{e}^{sA}
,
\mathrm{e}^{sB}
,
and
\mathrm{e}^{s(A+B)}
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Assumption 3
. There exists a
G
-invariant dense subspace
\mathcal{D}(G)
in
\mathcal{H}
such that for
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and for
all
g\in G
, then
g\,u_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(G)
and the Lie elements
E_{i}
with
\deg(i)=2p+1

are well-defined on
\mathcal{D}(G)
.

•

Assumption 4
. There exist constants
d_{i}\geq 0
such that
\sup_{g\in G}\|E_{i}\,g\,u_{0}\|\leq d_{i}
, with

\deg(i)=2p+1
.

From the proof of Lemma (
4.6
) we have

\|\Psi(h)u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}u_{0}\|\leq\int_{0}^{h}\|(M(t)+M(-t))\,\gamma u_{0}\|dt,

with

\gamma=\Theta(-t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{h}{2}(A+B)}\in G\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad M(t)+M(-t)=t^{2p}\big(R_{2s+1,2p}(t)+R_{2s+1,2p}(-t)\big).

On the other hand,

\|R_{2s+1,2p}(t)\gamma u_{0}\|\leq\sum_{k=2}^{2s+1}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{2p}G_{n}(t,C_{k},Z_{k-1,2p-n})\gamma u_{0}\right\|\leq\sum_{k=2}^{2s+1}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\mathcal{I}_{k,2p}(\sigma)\,\gamma\,u_{0}\right\|d\sigma,

so that

\left\|\Psi(h)u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{h(A+B)}u_{0}\right\|\;\leq\;h^{2p+1}\,\sum_{\deg(i)=2p+1}\left(\frac{2}{2p+1}\,\sum_{k=2}^{2s+1}\int_{0}^{1}|\omega_{k,i}(\sigma)|\,d\sigma\right)d_{i}.

(79)

Time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation

i\psi(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\psi(x,t)+V(x)\psi(x,t),\qquad\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}

can be interpreted as an initial value problem of the form (
68
) by setting
u(t)=\psi(\cdot,t)
and

(Aw)(x)=\frac{i}{2}\Delta w(x),\qquad(Bw)(x)=-iV(x)w(x)

for a a sufficiently regular function
w:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
. In that case,

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
,
A
is clearly an unbounded linear operator, the previous Assumptions 1 and 2
are satisfied and the Lie bracket

[[[A,B],B],B]w(x)=0
for all
w(x)
. If in addition the initial condition is such that Assumptions 3 and 4 hold, then the previous
bound (
79
) applies, with elements
E_{i}
whose subindex
i
is typeset in boldface in Table
2
, thus
leading to improved constants in the local error estimate.

5
Concluding remarks

In this work we have carried out a detailed error analysis of splitting and
composition methods applied to finite-dimensional unitary problems.
Explicit bounds have been derived both in terms of the norms of the
operators involved and in terms of the norms of their nested commutators.
We have considered splittings involving an arbitrary number of operators, and then
we specialized the discussion to the case
of two operators and, in particular, to time-symmetric schemes. In this
setting, sharper bounds were obtained by exploiting a basis of the free
Lie algebra generated by the two operators, leading to tighter constants
and a more refined description of the structure of the local error.

Although we have assumed that
the operators are bounded, the formulation of the error bounds in terms
of commutators allows for extensions to situations where one of the
operators is unbounded, provided suitable domain and regularity
assumptions are satisfied. This framework is especially relevant for the
time-dependent Schrödinger equation, where the intrinsic algebraic and
unitary structure leads to improved constants in the corresponding error
estimates.

The results obtained here could be useful in several contexts. From the
perspective of numerical analysis of differential equations, they provide
a systematic tool for assessing the relative efficiency of different
splitting and composition methods. This feature has been illustrated here for a specific class of composition methods.
The explicit characterization of the
error constants also offers guidance in the construction of new schemes,
allowing for the selection of coefficients that minimize dominant error
terms. In the context of quantum simulation, the bounds derived in this
work supply quantitative information and explicit values for the error
constants, which is essential for reliability estimates and resource
assessment in practical implementations.

Several directions for future research naturally emerge from this study.
A detailed treatment of problems involving genuinely unbounded operators
would require a careful analysis of domain invariance and regularity
conditions, and would extend the applicability of the present results to
a broader class of evolution equations. Another promising direction is
the investigation of splitting methods with complex coefficients in the
unitary and parabolic settings, where stability and structural properties
play a crucial role. Extensions to schemes involving commutators
explicitly, as well as to nonlinear problems, also constitute natural
continuations of this work. In the latter case, the Lie formalism for
ordinary differential equations provides a formal linear framework that
may serve as a starting point for a rigorous analysis. Finally, in the
area of quantum simulation, it would be of interest to apply the present
theoretical bounds to concrete models arising in practice, thereby
obtaining problem-dependent performance estimates and further insight
into the efficiency of splitting-based algorithms. All these issues require further study and will be addressed in a subsequent work.
