Prdm15 Deficiency Results in Impaired Hippocampal Neurogenesis and Autism-Like Behaviors
Authors: Qiwen Dong, Weiwei Xiao, Zheng Sun, Zhongsheng Sun, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex, heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorder, with epidemiological studies consistently reporting a rising prevalence worldwide. Our previous human genetic sequencing analysis identified PRDM15 (PRDI-BF1 (Positive regulatory domain I-binding factor 1) and RIZ1 (Retinoblastoma protein-interacting zinc finger 1) homologous domain-containing protein 15), a transcriptional regulator and a member of the PRDM family, as a novel candidate risk gene for ASD. However, its precise role in brain development and subsequent behavioral outcomes remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that Prdm15 deficiency in mice recapitulates core autism-like behaviors, including increased repetitive grooming, deficits in social interaction, and impaired social recognition memory. Structural MRI (Magnetic resonance imaging) and histological analyses revealed abnormal hippocampal architecture, characterized by reduced neuronal density in the CA1 (Cornu Ammonis 1) subregion. Through single-cell RNA sequencing of the developing hippocampus in mice, we found that Prdm15 deficiency impaired neurogenic lineage differentiation, leading to an accumulation of neural stem cells and a concomitant reduction in mature neurons, which were associated with transcriptional alterations in these cells. Crucially, conditional ablation of Prdm15 specifically in neural stem/progenitor cells achieved by crossing Prdm15-floxed mice (Prdm15fl/fl) with the Nestin-Cre strain, was sufficient to partially recapitulate the cellular and behavioral phenotypes observed in constitutive Prdm15-deficient mice. Collectively, our findings identify PRDM15 as a critical regulator of hippocampal neurogenesis and provide evidence that its dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of ASD-like behaviors in mice.Graphical Abstract Prdm15 loss links neurogenic deficits to autism-like behaviors in mice. Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction as well as restricted interests or repetitive behaviors, as defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Current epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 1 in 36 children aged 8 years in the United States meets diagnostic criteria for ASD, with prevalence rates continuing to rise (Maenner et al. 2023). ASD has long been considered sex-biased, with higher diagnostic prevalence in males (Loomes et al. 2017). This diagnostic gap may partly reflect that autistic females present differently: less overt restricted/repetitive behaviors, greater use of social camouflaging, and more internalizing symptoms, which diagnostic frameworks from male-dominated cohorts may miss (Lai et al. 2015; Cruz et al. 2025; Dolfi and Tudose 2025). Genetically, females diagnosed with ASD carry a higher burden of deleterious variants than affected males, supporting a “female protective effect” and sex-dependent neurodevelopmental thresholds (Jacquemont et al. 2014). Therefore, sex-stratified analyses in animal models are essential to distinguish sex-independent primary genetic effects from sex-dependent behavioral expression.

Recent advances in next-generation sequencing technologies, such as whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing, have substantially advanced the genetic dissection of ASD (Zhou et al. 2022; Fu et al. 2022). These efforts aim to identify disease-associated genetic variations, including de novo copy number variations, de novo single nucleotide variants, and small insertions or deletions, thereby elucidating the genetic basis of ASD. Although more than 1,500 ASD-associated genes have been included in the AutDB database (Pereanu et al. 2018), functional validation of these genes in animal models remains limited to a small number of cases, such as CHD8 (Chromodomain helicase DNA binding protein 8) (Bernier et al. 2014; Katayama et al. 2016), SHANK3 (Src homology 3 and multiple ankyrin repeat domains 3) (Wang et al. 2016; Peca et al. 2011), MECP2 (Methyl-CpG binding protein 2) (Liu et al. 2016), CNTNAP2 (Contactin associated protein 2) (Penagarikano et al. 2011), PAK2 (p21 activated kinase 2) (Wang et al. 2018), CSMD3 (CUB (named after complement C1r/C1s, Uegf–Urchin epidermal growth factor, Bmp1–Bone morphogenetic protein 1) and Sushi multiple domains 3) (Song et al. 2022), and NPAS3 (Neuronal PAS (Per-Arnt-Sim) domain protein 3) (Li et al. 2022).

Notably, several chromatin-remodeling enzymes and regulatory factors have been implicated in the regulation of early neural development and synaptic plasticity (Chang et al. 2015), and their dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of ASD (Ronan et al. 2013). For example, CHD8, an ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler, positively regulates the WNT signaling pathway through modulation of Polycomb group (PcG) protein complexes; Chd8 deficiency has been causally linked to cortical maldevelopment and ASD pathogenesis (Durak et al. 2016). Similarly, deficiency in the ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler ARID1B (AT-rich interaction domain 1B) is associated with reduced H3K9 (Histone H3 lysine 9) acetylation, transcriptional dysregulation, and consequent emergence of autism-like behavioral phenotypes and intellectual disability in both human patients and genetically engineered mouse models (Jung et al. 2017). Additionally, SETD5 (SET (Suppressor of variegation, Enhancer of zeste, Trithorax)-domain containing 5), a histone methyltransferase belonging to the SET-domain containing gene family, regulates transcriptional activation of neurodevelopmental genes through interaction with the HDAC3-PAF1 (Histone deacetylase 3-Polymerase-associated factor 1) complex (Deliu et al. 2018). De novo loss-of-function mutations in SETD5, a gene located within the critical region of 3p25.3 microdeletion syndrome, cause intellectual disability, which represents the core phenotype of this syndrome (Grozeva et al. 2014; Kuechler et al. 2015). Furthermore, Pinto et al. identified SETD5 as a candidate susceptibility gene for ASD based on de novo copy-number variants (CNVs) detected in a cohort of 2,446 individuals with ASD (Pinto et al. 2014). Therefore, identifying additional chromatin remodelers and elucidating their functional roles in neurodevelopment is essential to understanding mammalian brain development and its vulnerability to neurodevelopmental disorders.

Through whole-genome sequencing of 32 simplex ASD families, comprising 32 individuals with sporadic autism and their unaffected parents, our previous study identified a de novo mutation in the PRDM15 (PRDI-BF1 (Positive regulatory domain I-binding factor 1) and RIZ1 (Retinoblastoma protein-interacting zinc finger 1) homologous domain-containing protein 15) gene in one affected individual (Wu et al. 2018), suggesting its candidacy as an ASD risk gene. PRDM15 is a member of the PRDM family that is characterized by an N-terminal PR/SET domain and multiple C-terminal zinc-finger domains and functions as a histone methyltransferase or transcriptional regulator (Mzoughi et al. 2016). Accumulating evidence indicates that PRDM family members play critical roles in key neurodevelopmental processes. For instance, PRDM16 is selectively expressed in neural stem and progenitor cells, where it regulates cortical development during embryogenesis by modulating oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species levels (Chuikov et al. 2010; Shimada et al. 2017; Inoue et al. 2017). PRDM13 acts as a transcriptional regulator recruited by various bHLH (basic helix-loop-helix) family members to chromatin, where it suppresses ventral-specific genes to ensure proper dorsal neural tube patterning (Mona et al. 2017). Notably, PRDM15 mutations have been found to impair NOTCH and WNT/Planar Cell Polarity (PCP) signaling, causing holoprosencephaly, a severe congenital forebrain malformation (Mzoughi et al. 2020a). However, whether PRDM15 dysfunction contributes to ASD pathogenesis and what the underlying molecular mechanisms are remain incompletely understood.

In this study, utilizing both constitutive and conditional Prdm15 knockout mouse models, we systematically investigated whether Prdm15 deficiency leads to ASD-relevant morphological and behavioral phenotypes. Furthermore, we characterized the neuroanatomical and cellular alterations induced by Prdm15 deficiency using structural MRI (Magnetic resonance imaging) and immunostaining analyses. To dissect the underlying mechanisms, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing to characterize the transcriptional and cellular perturbations resulting from Prdm15 deficiency during early hippocampal development. Finally, we generated a conditional Prdm15 knockout mouse model to delineate the specific contribution of Prdm15 in neural stem and progenitor cells to the observed phenotypes. Collectively, these multifaceted lines of evidence establish a critical role for PRDM15 in neural and brain development as well as in complex behaviors.

Materials and Methods

Mice

All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility and maintained in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines. The housing environment was kept at a constant temperature (20–24 °C) and humidity (30–70%), with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IOZ-IACUC-2024-348). Postnatal day 0 (P0) and embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5) mice were euthanized by hypothermia-induced anesthesia followed by decapitation; all other mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, following institutionally approved protocols. All mice used in this study were maintained on a C57BL/6 background.

A 26-base-pair deletion in exon 3 of the Prdm15 gene was introduced using CRISPR (Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats)/Cas9-mediated genome editing. Prdm15 knockout alleles were genotyped by PCR amplification of the target locus followed by Sanger sequencing. The primer sequences used for genotyping are listed below.

Forward primer: 5’-CAGGGTGTGAAGACTGCAGCC-3’;

Reverse primer: 5’-CTTGCCCTGCTCAGCACAAAG-3’.

Following a previously published strategy (Mzoughi et al. 2020a), a conditional Prdm15 floxed allele (Prdm15fl/fl) was generated by inserting loxP (Locus of X-over P1) sites flanking exon 4 of the Prdm15 gene. To achieve neural stem/progenitor cell-specific knockout, Prdm15fl/fl mice were crossed with the Nestin-Cre strain (Jackson Laboratory) to generate conditional knockout mice (Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice).

Behavioral Tests

Behavioral assessments were performed on mice from independent breeding cohorts to control for litter effects. For WT vs. Prdm15+/– mice: Behavioral tests were conducted on mice derived from 5 independent litters, with each litter containing both genotypes and both sexes. For Prdm15fl/fl vs. Prdm15Nestin−Cre and Prdm15fl/fl vs. Nestin-Cre mice: males were obtained from six litters, and females were from an additional six litters. Testing began at 8 weeks of age and concluded at 12 weeks of age. All behavioral experiments consisted of 5–10 mice per sex and per genotype.

Self-grooming Test (SGT)

SGT was conducted as previously described (Li et al. 2022). Individual male and female mice were first habituated for 10 min in a clean, empty cage before video recording began. Grooming episodes, including paw licking, fur stroking from head to tail, and scratching, were scored over a 10‑minute observation period.

Three-Chamber Social Interaction Test (TCST)

TCST was conducted as previously described (Li et al. 2022). Social behavior was examined in a rectangular three-chamber polypropylene arena. After 10 min of habituation to the empty arena, a novel stimulus mouse (S1; age-matched male C57BL/6J, previously habituated to confinement) was placed under a wire cup (10 cm diameter) in one side chamber, while an identical empty cup (Ob) served as a control in the opposite chamber. Interaction time (snout within 2 cm of the cup) was recorded over a 10-minute period to compute the sociability index: (S1 – Ob)/(S1 + Ob). In the social novelty phase, a second novel mouse (S2) was introduced, and the interaction time was similarly recorded to derive the novelty index: (S2 – S1)/(S2 + S1).

Novel Object Recognition Test (NOR)

NOR was conducted as previously described (Song et al. 2022). This test was performed in an open-field arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm). During habituation, mice were allowed to freely explore an empty arena for 10 min. During the familiarization phase, two identical novel objects (Ob1 and Ob2; plastic cuboids, 5 cm × 5 cm × 8 cm) were positioned in opposite corners of the open-field arena, and exploratory behavior was recorded for 10 min. After a 1-hour interval, during which mice were returned to their home cages in the experimental facility to ensure identical housing conditions, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object (Novel; glass cylinder of similar volume). Object exploration, defined as snout contact within a 2 cm radius of the object, was measured for 10 min, and the discrimination index was calculated as (Novel – Ob1)/(Novel + Ob1).

Morris Water Maze (MWM)

A circular Morris water maze pool (150 cm in diameter) filled with opaque water maintained at 23 ± 2 °C was used. The maze was conceptually divided into four equal quadrants. A hidden platform (8 cm in diameter) was placed 2 cm below the water surface at the center of one quadrant (the target quadrant), and distal visual cues were affixed to the walls surrounding the pool. Mice were habituated to the behavioral testing room 1 h prior to the first acquisition trial. Spatial acquisition training was conducted over five consecutive days, with two trials per day. During each training trial, mice were released from one of four pseudorandomly assigned starting locations and allowed 60 s to locate the hidden platform. Upon finding the platform, mice were permitted to remain on it for 10 s. If a mouse failed to find the platform within 60 s, it was gently guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 10 s; in this case, the escape latency was recorded as 60 s. Escape latency and swimming distance to the platform were automatically tracked using ANY‑maze software (Stoelting Co.). For the probe test on day 6, the platform was removed, and mice were allowed to swim freely for 60 s; the time spent in each quadrant and the number of platform‑location crossings were recorded.

Open Field Test (OFT)

OFT was conducted as previously described (Song et al. 2022). Mice were allowed to explore a square open-field chamber (40 cm × 40 cm × 40.5 cm) under dim illumination (50 lx) for 30 min, starting from the center zone. Locomotor activity was tracked via an overhead camera and analyzed using ANY-maze software (Stoelting Co.). Total distance traveled was used to assess locomotor activity over the entire 30‑minute session, whereas anxiety-like behavior was evaluated based on time spent in the center zone during the final 10 min.

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM)

EPM was conducted as previously described (Li et al. 2022). The elevated plus maze consisted of two open arms (30 cm × 7 cm × 0.5 cm) and two enclosed arms (30 cm × 7 cm × 16 cm), all radiating from a central square platform (7 cm × 7 cm), and was elevated 100 cm above the floor. Each mouse was placed on the central platform facing an open arm and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Behavior was video-recorded using an overhead camera and analyzed with ANY-maze software (Stoelting Co.) to quantify time spent in and distance traveled within the open arms.

Buried Food Test (BFT)

BFT was conducted as previously described (Aubry et al. 2025). Before testing, mice were subjected to a 24‑hour fasting period. A single 2‑g regular chow pellet was buried 8 cm beneath the surface of fresh bedding in one corner of the test cage. Each mouse was placed into the corner opposite the buried food, and the latency to locate the pellet was recorded.

Mouse Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

Following induction of anesthesia with isoflurane, in vivo MRI of the mouse brain was performed using a Bruker PharmaScan 70/16 US magnetic resonance system. Parameters for detecting brain volumetric changes were as follows: a T2‑weighted 3D fast spin‑echo sequence with columnar k‑space sampling was employed, using the following parameters: repetition time (TR) 4500 ms, echo time (TE) 35 ms per echo (6 echoes in total), field of view 20 mm × 20 mm, and matrix size 256 × 256. This acquisition protocol yielded voxel dimensions of 0.08 mm × 0.08 mm × 0.35 mm. Ten biological replicates per group were used. The mice for this experiment were derived from three litters of 8-week-old males.

Structural MRI Data Analysis

Preprocessing and statistical analysis of T2‑weighted mouse brain images were carried out using the spmmouseIHEP toolbox (Nie et al. 2019) implemented in SPM12 software (Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK). All structural images (WT, n = 10; Prdm15+/−, n = 10) were processed according to the following pipeline: First, each structural image was reoriented with the origin set at the third ventricle using the IHEP mouse template. The image was then segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid probability maps based on template tissue priors, and normalized to the Paxinos and Franklin atlas space via a diffeomorphic anatomical registration algorithm. Gray matter probability maps were subsequently smoothed using a 4‑mm full‑width at half‑maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Voxel‑based morphometry (VBM) analysis was performed on the smoothed, modulated images. A generalized linear model was employed, and group comparisons were conducted using a two‑sample t‑test with an uncorrected voxel‑level threshold of p < 0.005 and a cluster‑size threshold of 5 voxels. Brain regions exhibiting significant volume differences are displayed using a color scale representing t‑values from the two‑sample t‑test, with cool colors indicating volume reduction and warm colors indicating volume increase in Prdm15+/− mice relative to WT controls.

Immunofluorescence

Mouse brains were immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4 °C and subsequently paraffin-embedded. Tissue sections were incubated for an hour at room temperature in a blocking solution consisting of 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma, A1933) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Solarbio), followed by washing with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NeuN (Abclonal, A19086), WFS1 (Proteintech, 26995-1-AP), cleaved Caspase-3 (CST, 9661), Ki67 (Abcam, ab279653), and HOPX (Proteintech, 11419-AP). After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies for an hour at room temperature. Finally, slides were washed and coverslipped using an anti-fade mounting medium (Abcam, AB104139). 4–6 biological replicates per group were used. The mice for this experiment were derived from three litters of 8-week-old males, one litter of P0 males, and one litter of E16.5 animals.

Immunofluorescence Image Analysis

Images were obtained using an Olympus SpinSR super-resolution system, with imaging parameters kept constant across samples. All quantitative analyses were conducted in ImageJ (NIH). Fluorescence images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and a consistent threshold was applied to eliminate background signal. The integrated density was measured within defined regions of interest (ROIs), and cell numbers were determined using the Analyze Particles function.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from whole-hippocampal tissues of WT and Prdm15+/– at P0. High-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing was performed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. Raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger software (v9.0) with alignment to the mm10 mouse reference genome, yielding a filtered gene expression matrix. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses were conducted in RStudio (v4.0.3) using the Seurat package (v4.0.5). 2 biological replicates per group were used, with one WT-Prdm15+/– pair derived from each of two independent heterozygous breeding pairs.

Quality Control and Data Preprocessing

Quality control filtering was applied to retain cells with 200–7,500 unique genes detected and mitochondrial gene content below 5%. This yielded a total of 103,648 high-quality cells. Gene expression data were normalized using the LogNormalize method, scaled via the ScaleData function, and highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures. Batch effects across samples were corrected using the Harmony method, followed by principal component analysis (RunPCA). The first 20 principal components were selected for all downstream analyses.

Cell Clustering and Annotation

Unsupervised clustering was performed by constructing a shared nearest-neighbor graph (FindNeighbors, dims = 1:20) and applying the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.2 (FindClusters). The resulting clusters were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). A total of 11 distinct cell populations were annotated based on established marker genes (Yao et al. 2021; Artegiani et al. 2017).

Differential Gene Expression and Functional Enrichment Analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function, with significance thresholds set at |log2Fold Change| > 0.1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Bonferroni correction). Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using the Metascape platform (https://metascape.org/).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using HiScript III reverse transcriptase (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was then performed with SYBR Green chemistry on a Stratagene Mx3000P system. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 60 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were quantified using the ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the RT-qPCR primers used were listed in Supplementary Table 1. 4–5 biological replicates per group were used, with technical replicates of 3.

Analysis of ASD-Related Gene Network in the Hippocampus of Prdm15+/– Mice

ASD genes were downloaded from SFARI (Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative Database) Gene database (https://gene.sfari.org/database/human-gene/). A Venn diagram (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/) was used to detect overlapping genes among gene sets. The STRING database (Szklarczyk et al. 2021) was used to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for differentially expressed ASD genes in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice.

Statistical Analysis

Sample sizes for this study were determined based on preliminary experiments with 3–5 mice per group to estimate effect sizes and variability. The minimum sample size was calculated with a power of 80% following the method as previously described (Kadam and Bhalerao 2010) and effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were reported for all formal experiments to justify the sample size. The number of independent experimental replicates was provided in the respective figure legends. Statistical data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v. 7) or R (v. 4.3). Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Bartlett’s test for parametric comparisons. If assumptions of normality or equal variance were violated, appropriate non-parametric tests were employed. Detailed numerical data are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Significance was determined using two-way ANOVA with Brown-Forsythe corrections, Games-Howell’s multiple comparison test, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test, or one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using two-tailed unpaired t-tests, two-tailed unpaired Welch’s t-test, and Mann-Whitney test. Investigators were blinded to genotype and treatment group assignment during data collection, behavioral analysis, and histological assessment, wherever feasible. All statistical analyses were performed by an investigator who remained blinded to experimental group allocation (Michel et al. 2020; Weissgerber et al. 2015).

Results

Rare PRDM15 Mutations Identified in ASD

Through whole-genome sequencing of 32 Chinese trios affected by ASD, we previously identified a deleterious nonsynonymous mutation (p. A821V) in PRDM15 in one of the 32 individuals (Wu et al. 2018). Moreover, three rare, predicted-damaging PRDM15 variants, comprising two de novo and one inherited, were identified in unrelated individuals diagnosed with ASD (Table 1) (Satterstrom et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2021; Iossifov et al. 2014). The amino acid residues affected by these variants, including p. A821V, which maps to the zinc-finger domain (Fig. 1A), exhibit strong evolutionary conservation across vertebrate species (Fig. 1B). To directly evaluate the nature of these variants, we assessed their conservation based on physicochemical properties (charge, polarity, hydrophobicity) and BLOSUM62 substitution scores. Of the four missense changes, G520S, A821V, and N1075S were classified as conservative, whereas E336K was classified as non‑conservative, driven primarily by the reversal of charge (Table 2). Structural modeling using AlphaFold3 predicted that each of the four missense variants induces distinct conformational changes in the PRDM15 protein (Supplementary Fig. 1). All four variants are computationally predicted to exert deleterious effects on protein structure and function (Table 1).

Residual variation intolerance score (RVIS) is a population-genomic metric that quantifies a gene’s tolerance to functional genetic variation in humans; a lower (more negative) RVIS score indicates stronger evolutionary constraint and intolerance to functional disruptive variants (Petrovski et al. 2013; Stawinski and Ploski 2024). Notably, PRDM15 exhibits a RVIS of − 2.00 (1.73%), which is substantially lower than the mean RVIS of − 0.56 (19.54%) for developmental disorder risk genes, indicating strong evolutionary constraint against functional genetic variation.

Collectively, these genetic observations implicate rare, predicted-damaging PRDM15 mutations in ASD susceptibility.

Prdm15 Deficiency in Mice Leads to Autism-Like Behaviors and Cognitive Deficits

To investigate the neurobiological functions of PRDM15, we generated a constitutive Prdm15 knockout mouse model (Fig. 2A and B), and genotyping confirmed the disruption of the Prdm15 allele (Fig. 2C). Given the lethality of Prdm15 homozygous knockout mice, consistent with prior findings (Mzoughi et al. 2017), adult male and female Prdm15 heterozygous (Prdm15+/–) mice were subjected to a series of behavioral assays, in which the levels of Prdm15 mRNA were reduced (Fig. 2D and E).

Compared to wild-type (WT) littermates, both male and female Prdm15+/– mice exhibited significantly increased grooming duration and bout frequency in the self-grooming test (SGT), as quantified over a 10-minute observation period (Fig. 2F, G, and Supplementary Table 2), indicative of stereotyped behavior. In the three-chamber social test (TCST), both male and female Prdm15+/– mice showed significantly reduced time spent interacting with a novel conspecific mouse (S1) relative to an object (measured as interaction duration and sociability index), and significantly decreased exploration of a novel (S2) mouse (measured as interaction duration and social novelty index) during the social novelty phase (Fig. 2H, I, Supplementary Fig. 2 A, and B), suggesting impaired social interaction.

We next evaluated cognitive abilities using the novel object recognition (NOR) and Morris water maze (MWM) tests. During the familiarization phase of the NOR test with two identical objects, both WT and Prdm15+/– mice showed similar exploration (Supplementary Fig. 2 C). However, in the test phase with one familiar and one novel object, both male and female WT mice displayed a strong preference for the novel object, whereas Prdm15+/– mice of both sexes showed no significant preference (Fig. 2J, K, and Supplementary Fig. 2D), indicating a recognition memory deficit that was comparable between sexes.

During the MWM acquisition phase, both male and female Prdm15+/– mice exhibited a slightly longer escape latency to locate the hidden platform compared to WT controls (Fig. 2L), revealing an initial spatial learning impairment. However, following five days of training trials, all four groups exhibited comparable performance in the final probe test, with no significant differences in either the latency to reach the former platform location or the number of platform crossings (Fig. 2M, N and Supplementary Fig. 2E). This suggests that repeated training could compensate for the initial learning deficit in Prdm15+/– mice, irrespective of sex. To determine whether the spatial memory deficit in Prdm15+/– mice arises from non-neural factors, particularly motor impairment, we assessed swimming speed in MWM and locomotor activity in an open-field test (OFT, Supplementary Fig. 2 F and G). No statistically significant differences were detected between WT and Prdm15+/– mice, suggesting that the impaired performance in the MWM is unlikely to be attributable to motor dysfunction. Additionally, assessments of anxiety-like behavior conducted using the OFT and elevated plus maze (EPM) showed no significant differences between Prdm15+/– and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 2H–K). These results suggest that Prdm15 deficiency is associated with behavioral impairments relevant to ASD.

Prdm15 Deficiency Reduced Neuronal Density in the Hippocampus

To investigate whether structural alterations are present in the brains of Prdm15+/– mice, structural MRI was performed on a cohort of adult Prdm15+/– mice and WT littermate controls. Quantitative volumetric analysis revealed a significant reduction in the gray matter volume of the hippocampal CA1 subregion in Prdm15+/– mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 3A), suggesting reduced neuronal density in this region. This finding aligns with reports of reduced hippocampal volume in a subset of individuals with ASD (Raymond et al. 1996; Aylward et al. 1999).

To further validate the impact of Prdm15 deficiency on hippocampal morphology, we performed immunofluorescence staining for NeuN, a marker of mature neurons, on adult brain sections. Consistent with the MRI data, both NeuN signal intensity and neuronal density were significantly reduced in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice compared to WT littermates, particularly in the CA1 and dentate gyrus (DG) subregions (Fig. 3B–E). Immunostaining for WFS1, a marker enriched in CA1 pyramidal neurons, further confirmed a significant reduction in neuronal density in the CA1 region (Fig. 3F). These results indicate that Prdm15 deficiency leads to decreased neuronal density in the hippocampus. To determine whether Prdm15 deficiency exerts effects during earlier development, we analyzed hippocampal neurons at P0. Immunofluorescence staining showed a significant decrease in NeuN immunoreactivity in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice compared to WT controls at this stage (Fig. 3G).

Notably, at embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5), cleaved Caspase‑3 immunoreactivity showed no significant difference in apoptotic cell signal intensity between genotypes, whereas Ki67 immunostaining demonstrated a significant reduction in the proliferative cells in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– embryos relative to WT littermates (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). These findings indicate that Prdm15 deficiency does not induce overt apoptosis during this early developmental window but compromises hippocampal progenitor proliferation.

Collectively, these observations demonstrate that Prdm15 deficiency leads to a selective reduction in hippocampal neuronal density, which is detectable as early as postnatal day 0 and appears to arise from a developmental impairment in the proliferative capacity of embryonic progenitors rather than from enhanced apoptosis.

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals Altered Cellular Composition and Impaired Differentiation in the Hippocampus of Prdm15-Deficient Mice

To investigate the molecular changes in hippocampal cell populations upon Prdm15 deficiency during early hippocampal development, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on dissociated hippocampus from P0 mice. After quality filtering, 103,648 cells from two WT and two Prdm15+/– littermate mice were retained for downstream analysis. Unsupervised clustering identified 21 distinct clusters, which were annotated into 11 major cell types based on established marker genes (Yao et al. 2021; Artegiani et al. 2017): neural stem cells (NSCs), neural progenitor cells (NPCs), pyramidal neurons, mature granule cells, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), myelin-forming oligodendrocytes (MFOLs), microglia, interneurons, astrocytes, pericytes, and endothelial cells (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 4 A). Comparative analysis showed modest alterations in the hippocampal cellular composition of Prdm15+/– mice relative to WT littermates, with a subtle increase in the proportion of NSCs and NPCs, and a corresponding decrease in pyramidal neurons and mature granule cells (Fig. 4B).

To decipher the underlying molecular alterations, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across all cell types. Cell types exhibiting the most extensive transcriptional changes included pyramidal neurons (289 upregulated, 375 downregulated DEGs), NSCs (145 upregulated, 319 downregulated DEGs), mature granule cells (124 upregulated, 172 downregulated DEGs), and NPCs (82 upregulated, 49 downregulated DEGs) (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table 3). In NSCs, functional enrichment analysis of DEGs indicated that upregulated genes were enriched in pathways related to negative regulation of neuronal differentiation and neurodegenerative processes. NPCs showed upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. 4B, and Supplementary Table 4). These findings suggest that the observed accumulation of neural stem/progenitor cells in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice arises from impaired differentiation. In contrast, downregulated genes in pyramidal neurons and mature granule cells were associated with synaptic function, implying potential functional deficits (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 4B). Furthermore, upregulated genes in pyramidal neurons were enriched for negative regulation of cell proliferation, aligning with their reduced abundance (Fig. 4D).

The impaired differentiation capacity of NSCs and NPCs likely accounts for their accumulation and the concomitant reduction in mature neurons. Analysis of differentiation-related DEGs confirmed this hypothesis: NSCs harbored numerous differentiation-related DEGs (45 upregulated, 47 downregulated), while NPCs predominantly exhibited downregulation of such genes (8 upregulated, 17 downregulated) (Fig. 4E). Notably, HOPX, a key marker and maintainer of quiescent NSCs (Berg et al. 2019), was significantly upregulated in both NSCs and NPCs from Prdm15+/– mice (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. 4 C). This increase in HOPX+ cells was further validated by immunofluorescence staining, which showed a significant expansion in both the CA region and DG of Prdm15+/– hippocampi at P0 (Fig. 4G–I). Additionally, the expression of multiple genes that maintain neural stem cell quiescence, such as Hes1 (Maeda et al. 2023), Hes5 (Jones et al. 2015), and Cdkn1b (Andreu et al. 2015), was significantly upregulated in Prdm15+/– hippocampi (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). In mature hippocampal neurons of Prdm15+/– mice, synaptic-associated genes that are essential for synaptic function (Bornschein et al. 2025; Korkut et al. 2013), including synaptotagmins Syt1 and Syt4, are also downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 4D and E).

Collectively, these findings position PRDM15 as a critical regulator that maintains the quiescence-differentiation equilibrium of hippocampal neural stem and progenitor cells.

Prdm15 Deficiency Disrupts an ASD-Related Gene Network in the Hippocampus

To assess the functional convergence between Prdm15 deficiency and ASD pathogenesis, we analyzed the overlap between DEGs identified by scRNA-seq across four hippocampal cell types (NSCs, NPCs, mature granule cells, and pyramidal neurons) in Prdm15+/– mice and a set of 1,267 ASD-associated genes from the SFARI database. As shown in Fig. 5A, we identified 15, 7, 24, and 75 ASD-related genes overlapping with cell type-specific DEGs in NSCs, NPCs, mature granule cells, and pyramidal neurons, respectively. After removing duplicates, a total of 94 ASD-related genes were differentially expressed in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice (Supplementary Table 5). Among these, Gnb2 (Guanine nucleotide binding protein, beta 2) was consistently downregulated across all four cell types (Fig. 5B and C). Notably, variants in GNB2 have been established as a genetic cause of developmental delay (Fukuda et al. 2020).

To identify potential direct targets of PRDM15, we integrated published PRDM15 chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) datasets from mouse embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and whole embryos harvested at embryonic day 6.5 (Mzoughi et al. 2020a). Among the 94 ASD-related genes, Adgrl1 (Adhesion G protein-coupled receptor L1), Cux1 (Cut-like homeobox 1), and Jarid2 (Jumonji and AT-rich interaction domain containing 2) were identified as PRDM15-associated loci (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 5 A). Consistent with this, Adgrl1 expression was significantly reduced in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice, whereas Cux1 and Jarid2 showed no significant differential expression (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 5B–C). Haploinsufficiency of ADGRL1 has been implicated in multiple neurodevelopmental disorders and has been shown in mouse models to alter synaptic activity and behavior (Vitobello et al. 2022).

Further protein-protein interaction network analysis revealed that these 94 overlapping and differentially expressed ASD-related genes analyzed in Fig. 5A form a functional module enriched in pathways associated with neuronal differentiation, cell adhesion, synaptic organization and transmission (Fig. 5E). Collectively, these findings suggest that PRDM15 is a critical transcriptional regulator of hippocampal development and function, and that it regulates an ASD-associated gene network critical for neuronal differentiation and synaptic transmission.

Prdm15 Deficiency in Neural Stem Cells Impairs Neuronal Differentiation and Increases the Number of Quiescent Neural Stem Cells

To investigate the role of Prdm15 in NSCs, a conditional mouse model was generated by flanking exon 4 of the Prdm15 gene with loxP sites (Prdm15fl/fl) (Mzoughi et al. 2017). To ablate Prdm15 specifically in NSCs, Prdm15fl/fl mice were crossed with Nestin-Cre mice, yielding Prdm15fl/fl;Nestin-Cre mice (Prdm15Nestin−Cre) and control Prdm15fl/fl littermates (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in Prdm15 mRNA levels in both the hippocampus and cortex of Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice compared to controls (Fig. 6B and C). As expected, immunofluorescence staining revealed decreased NeuN levels and numbers in the hippocampus of Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice at P30 (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, immunostaining for the stem cell marker HOPX indicated an increased number of NSCs in the dentate gyrus region of the hippocampus in Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice (Fig. 6E). qPCR analysis of the hippocampus from Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice revealed significant upregulation of stem cell quiescence-associated genes Hopx (Homeodomain only protein homeobox), Hes1 (Hairy and enhancer of split family basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor 1), Hes5 (Hairy and enhancer of split family basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor 5), and Cdkn1b (Cyclin dependent kinase inhibitor 1B), consistent with the observations in Prdm15+/– mice (Fig. 6F). Concomitantly, expression of the autism-associated genes Gnb2 and Adgrl1 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 6F).

These findings demonstrate that Prdm15 ablation in neural stem cells impairs neuronal differentiation, as evidenced by decreased neuron density. Notably, the concomitant increase in HOPX+ neural stem cells in the dentate gyrus suggests that PRDM15 plays a critical role in regulating the balance between stem cell maintenance and neuronal commitment in the adult hippocampus.

Prdm15 Deficiency in Neural Stem Cells Leads to Autism-Like Behaviors and Cognitive Impairments

To determine whether conditional deletion of Prdm15 in NSCs affects mouse behaviors, we performed a series of behavioral tests in both male and female mice and found that adult Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice exhibited behavioral alterations similar to those observed in Prdm15+/– mice. In the SGT, both male and female Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice displayed a significantly higher number of grooming episodes within the same timeframe compared to Prdm15fl/fl controls, indicating increased stereotyped behavior (Fig. 7A and B).

In the TCST, both male and female Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice showed markedly reduced social preference compared to control mice (Fig. 7C, D, and Supplementary Fig. 6 A), as well as diminished interest in a novel social partner (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Fig. 6B). In the NOR test, neither Prdm15fl/fl nor Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice showed significant preference between the two identical objects during the familiarization phase (Supplementary Fig. 6 C). However, in the test phase, both male and female Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice exhibited a significantly attenuated preference for the novel object compared to controls (Fig. 7F and Supplementary Fig. 6 C), suggesting impaired novel object recognition memory that was comparable between sexes.

The MWM test was used to assess spatial learning and memory. In both sexes, Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice showed no significant differences in learning or memory performance relative to their Prdm15fl/fl littermates (Fig. 7G–K). Additionally, no performance differences were observed in locomotor activity or olfactory function between genotypes or between sexes (Supplementary Fig. 6E–G). To exclude the potential influence of Cre recombinase, we also compared the behavior of Prdm15fl/fl and Nestin-Cre mice and found no significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 6H–L). Collectively, these results indicate that Prdm15 loss in neural stem cells is a key factor underlying the behavioral alterations in Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice.

Discussion

Our study establishes PRDM15 as a regulator of hippocampal neurogenesis and its deficiency is associated with abnormal hippocampal development and ASD-relevant behaviors. In mice, PRDM15 deficiency recapitulates core autistic features, including repetitive behaviors, social deficits, and cognitive impairments. Interestingly, several behavioral measures showed greater inter-individual variability in Prdm15+/− mice than in WT controls. This phenotypic heterogeneity resembles the clinical heterogeneity observed in ASD patients with identical high-penetrance mutations (Berg and Geschwind 2012), suggesting that lower Prdm15 dosage renders behavioral phenotypes more sensitive to minor genetic background differences, environmental factors, and developmental noise, thereby amplifying individual variation. These behavioral manifestations are underpinned by specific developmental and cellular disruptions within the hippocampus, a brain region critically implicated in ASD (Banker et al. 2021). Mechanistically, the accumulation of HOPX+ stem cells, coupled with a reduction in mature neurons, particularly within the hippocampal CA1 and DG subregions, suggests an impairment of neuronal differentiation during hippocampal development.

Notably, conditional deletion of Prdm15 specifically in Nestin-expressing neural stem and progenitor cells recapitulates key behavioral and cellular phenotypes observed in Prdm15+/– mice, including stereotyped grooming, social interaction deficits, impaired novel object recognition, and expansion of the hippocampal stem cell pool. These findings demonstrate that PRDM15 plays a critical function in the neural stem cell lineage, particularly during early stages of neurogenesis. Notably, the spatial learning deficit present in Prdm15+/– mice was absent in the conditional knockout model. This phenotypic discrepancy likely arises from three non-mutually exclusive mechanisms. First, Prdm15+/– mice carry haploinsufficiency in all tissues, including peripheral organs such as muscle and liver, which may indirectly affect cognitive performance through systemic metabolic or inflammatory signals (Mzoughi et al. 2020b). Second, despite the reduction in neuron density in the hippocampus, it is possible that compensatory mechanisms during development in Prdm15Nestin−Cre mice preserve certain circuit functions required for spatial navigation (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.152112399Kjelstrup et al. 2002). Third, the water maze test relies on multiple neural circuits beyond the hippocampus. Global heterozygosity might affect additional brain regions or neuroendocrine signaling. These findings highlight the functional heterogeneity of PRDM15 across distinct cellular contexts and developmental time windows.

Similar to established ASD-risk genes CHD8 and ARID1B, PRDM15 functions as a critical regulator of transcriptional programs essential for synaptic function. For example, the differentially expressed, ASD-related genes in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice are enriched for synaptic organization and transmission. Specifically, the levels of synaptic-associated genes, including synaptotagmins Syt1 and Syt4, are downregulated in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice. De novo missense mutations in the SYT1 gene identified in individuals with ASD were reported to reduce dendritic length and synapse number (van Boven et al. 2024). Moreover, the level of the growth-associated protein GAP43, a key presynaptic cytoskeletal component critical for neuroplasticity (Neve et al. 1998), was also downregulated in the hippocampus of Prdm15+/– mice. Consistent with its functional importance, Gap43-deficient mice display diverse brain abnormalities and autism-like behaviors (Zaccaria et al. 2010). These findings suggest that even after neuronal differentiation is initiated, Prdm15 deficiency may impair neural circuit function. However, the precise molecular mechanisms—whether through its transcriptional co-regulation or modulation of key signaling pathways like WNT or NOTCH (Mzoughi et al. 2020a)—remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies should identify the direct transcriptional targets of PRDM15 in hippocampal NSCs and neurons to define the regulatory cascade underlying its role in neurodevelopment.

Through curation of the available genomic literature, we identified four rare, predicted deleterious PRDM15 variants in ASD cohorts; none overlap with the pathogenic mutations previously linked to holoprosencephaly (HPE) and Galloway–Mowat syndrome (GAMOS) (Mzoughi et al. 2020a; Mann et al. 2021). Although the mutational locations differ, it is noteworthy that considerable phenotypic and mechanistic overlap exists across these disorders. Both ASD and GAMOS/HPE are characterized by central nervous system abnormalities, ranging from the subtle neurodevelopmental alterations typical of ASD to the severe brain malformations seen in GAMOS/HPE, suggesting that PRDM15 plays a critical role across this spectrum of neurodevelopmental severity. Mechanistically, PRDM15 has been shown to act upstream of WNT signaling during forebrain development (Mzoughi et al. 2020a), a pathway well established in both neurodevelopment and ASD pathogenesis (Durak et al. 2016; El Khouri et al. 2021). Thus, despite differences at the variant level, distinct PRDM15 mutations may converge on common downstream pathways. A limitation of this study is that our Prdm15+/− mouse model represents a heterozygous loss-of-function scenario that may not fully recapitulate the consequences of the missense mutations observed in individuals with ASD. PRDM15 contains an N-terminal PR/SET domain involved in protein–protein interactions and multiple C-terminal zinc fingers that mediate DNA binding. All currently reported ASD-associated PRDM15 missense variants localize to the zinc finger domains, suggesting they may perturb sequence-specific DNA binding affinity. In contrast, HPE/GAMOS-associated variants include both zinc finger mutations (e.g., C844Y) that compromise protein stability and transcriptional activity, and PR domain mutations that affect protein stability alone. These location-dependent functional consequences support a model of allelic heterogeneity, in which mutation type and location dictate the phenotypic manifestation, from ASD-like behaviors to severe forebrain malformations. Thus, the observation that different PRDM15 mutations lead to divergent clinical outcomes may result from differential effects on DNA binding, protein stability, or protein interactions.

ASD exhibits sexual dimorphism: it has historically been diagnosed more frequently in males, yet accumulating evidence indicates that females are systematically underdiagnosed, largely attributable to sex-specific differences in behavioral phenotypes and clinical presentation (Lai et al. 2015; Cruz et al. 2025; Dolfi and Tudose 2025). In our study, we observed no overt behavioral differences between male and female Prdm15+/− mice across multiple assays. This finding suggests that PRDM15 haploinsufficiency similarly disrupts neurodevelopment in both sexes and that the sexually dimorphic presentation of ASD in humans may arise from downstream modifiers or sex-specific factors rather than from the initial genetic defect. We cannot rule out the existence of subtle sex-specific effects, which may have remained below the detection threshold of our current behavioral paradigms. These observations highlight the importance of including both sexes in preclinical ASD models; future studies employing more sensitive or sex-specific behavioral assays will be necessary to fully delineate such potential differences.

This study has several limitations. Due to limited clinical data for reported PRDM15 mutation carriers, a detailed cross-case comparison of cognitive outcomes is currently not feasible, and our study therefore focuses on the in vivo functional consequences of PRDM15 haploinsufficiency. While the mouse models strongly support a functional role in neurodevelopment, further validation in human cellular models, such as cortical or hippocampal organoids derived from individuals with PRDM15 variants, would strengthen the clinical relevance. Furthermore, the exact contribution of the hippocampal abnormalities to each specific behavioral domain requires more precise circuit-level interrogation, such as chemogenetic or optogenetic manipulation of the affected cell populations. We also acknowledge that comprehensive molecular and histological characterization in female mice remains limited, precluding robust inference of sex‑dependent molecular mechanisms downstream of PRDM15. Future studies with larger female cohorts and multi‑omics comparisons will be essential to evaluate conserved versus sexually dimorphic features of PRDM15‑associated neurodevelopmental pathology. We used publicly available PRDM15 ChIP-seq datasets from ESCs and E6.5 embryos (Mzoughi et al. 2020a) to identify the potential genomic binding loci. Among the genes associated with these loci, only Adgrl1 exhibited differential expression in the postnatal hippocampus, whereas Cux1 and Jarid2 showed no detectable expression changes. This likely reflects the differences in cellular contexts, as chromatin binding in early embryogenesis does not always confer transcriptional regulation in postnatal hippocampal lineages. Transcriptional regulation of Cux1 and Jarid2 may therefore depend on additional co-factors or developmental signals. Definitive identification of direct PRDM15 targets will require cell-type-specific ChIP-seq profiling in hippocampal cells at postnatal developmental stages. While our findings establish a correlation among PRDM15 deficiency, impaired neurogenesis, and ASD-like behaviors, definitive validation of the causal relationship will require further investigation, including targeted rescue experiments and complementary gain- and loss-of-function studies.

In conclusion, our integrative analysis spanning behavioral phenotyping to single-cell transcriptomics demonstrates that PRDM15 serves as a critical regulator of neural stem cell differentiation during hippocampal development. Prdm15 deficiency is associated with altered hippocampal cellular architecture and ASD-like behavioral deficits. These findings not only expand the genetic landscape of ASD but also offer a new perspective on how dysregulation of neurogenic transcriptional programs contributes to complex behavioral phenotypes, providing a foundation for future mechanistic investigations.
