On amplitudes in Chiral Higher Spin Gravity


Abstract
We extract cubic interactions from the covariant equations of motion of Chiral Higher Spin Gravity and compute the corresponding amplitudes. These amplitudes are found to agree with earlier results obtained in the light-cone gauge. We also classify all possible cubic amplitudes that can be constructed from chiral fields arising in twistor theory. In addition, we derive propagators for arbitrary spin in the Feynman/Lorenz gauges. These propagators are then used to solve Berends–Giele recursion relations for a truncation of Chiral Higher Spin Gravity, which is a higher-spin extension of self-dual Yang–Mills theory. We confirm that all tree-level amplitudes vanish.

3.4
Three-point amplitudes from the equations of motion

When computing amplitudes, it is useful to keep track of the helicities. In this purpose, some extra parameters can be inserted into the free fields. As such,

\displaystyle\omega(x|y,{{\bar{y}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{({q}\ e\ {\bar{k}})}{\langle{qk}\rangle+\sigma yq}e^{\sigma{{\bar{y}}}{{\bar{k}}}}\,,

\displaystyle C(x|y,{{\bar{y}}})=\tfrac{1}{2}\exp(\sigma yk+\sigma^{-1}{{\bar{y}}}\bar{k})\,,

\sigma

S=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\int\Psi^{A(2s)}H_{AA}\wedge d\omega_{A(2s-2)}\,,

4
A word about quartic and higher contact terms

The infinite set of multilinear maps on the right-hand side of equations (
3.27a
) and (
3.27b
), expressing the theory as an FDA, is required for Lorentz covariance of the theory. However, it is possible to show that all tree-level quartic amplitudes constructed from
\mathcal{V}(\omega,\omega,C)
and
\mathcal{U}(\omega,C,C)
vanish. Using the plane-wave solutions as well as very schematic expressions for the vertex operators leads straightforwardly to the result. Let us start with the expressions

\mathcal{V}(\omega,\omega,C)=p_{23}\int_{\Delta_{2}}\sum_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}(y,u,v)\ \omega(y_{2},\bar{y}_{2})\ \omega(y_{3},\bar{y}_{3})\ C(y_{4},\bar{y}_{4})

(4.1)

and

\mathcal{U}(\omega,C,C)=p_{02}\int_{\Delta_{2}}\sum_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}(y,u,v)\ \omega(y_{2},\bar{y}_{2})\ C(y_{3},\bar{y}_{3})\ C(y_{4},\bar{y}_{4})\,,

(4.2)

where the way has been paved to compute the amplitude by introducing a physical field
\omega(y_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
or
C(y_{1},\bar{y}_{1})
.
\mathcal{O}_{i}(y,u,v)
are operators depending on the variables
u
and
v
as well as the twistor variables
y^{A}
. One directly sees that the former expression is zero if the fields are replaced by the plane-wave solutions. Indeed, the operator
p_{23}
contracts two auxiliary momentum spinors
q_{A}
together. This, of course, requires to choose one and the same reference spinor for all positive helicity plane-waves. The situation is slightly less trivial for the latter expression, but the same conclusion can be drawn by examining the corresponding amplitude.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{4}

\displaystyle=e^{\partial\cdot\partial_{1}}\int_{M}\omega(y_{1},\bar{y}_{1})({y_{1}}\ H\ {y_{1}})\ p_{02}\int_{\Delta_{2}}\sum_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}(y,u,v)\ \omega(y_{2},\bar{y}_{2})\ \omega(y_{3},\bar{y}_{3})\ C(y_{4},\bar{y}_{4})

\displaystyle=-p_{12}\int_{\Delta_{2}}\sum_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}(-\partial_{1},u,v)\int_{M}({q}\ e\ {\bar{k}_{1}})({q}\ e\ {\bar{k}_{2}})({y_{1}}\ H\ {y_{1}})\ f_{1}(y_{1}q)f_{2}(y_{2}q)\ C(y_{3},\bar{y}_{3})\ C(y_{4},\bar{y}_{4})\,.

For convenience,
\omega
has been expressed as
({q}\ e\ {\bar{k}})f
, where
f
encodes the dependence on both twistor variables and space-time coordinates. Using the identities presented in Appendix

\varepsilon_{A}{}^{B}=-\varepsilon^{B}{}_{A}

\delta^{A}_{B}
. The epsilon symbol defines an antisymmetric inner product,
\langle{pq}\rangle=p^{A}q_{A}=-p_{A}q^{A}
. Similarly,
[pq]=p^{A^{\prime}}q_{A^{\prime}}
.

The spinor notation is very handy for the following reason. As the space of (left or right) spinors is 2-dimensional, one can make the following decomposition of an arbitrary spin-tensor
T_{AB}
.

T_{AB}=T_{(AB)}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{AB}\ T_{C}{}^{C}\,.

(A.7)

T_{C}{}^{C}
is the trace of
T_{AB}
. The spin-tensor has simply been decomposed into its symmetric (
T_{(AB)}
) and antisymmetric (
T_{[AB]}
) components. The key point is that any antisymmetric tensor is proportional to the epsilon symbol. Then, only the symmetric part has a non-trivial structure. This rule will be the key to all the identities involving differential forms that will be shown in the next section.

A very useful tool for manipulating two-dimensional spinors is the Schouten identity (sometimes called Fierz identity),

\phi_{AB}{}^{B}+\phi^{B}{}_{AB}{}+\phi_{B}{}^{B}{}_{A}=0\,.

(A.8)

This is another way of writing the statement
\phi_{[ABC]}=0
. Of course, antisymmetrizing more than two indices gives a trivial result. In this paper, the identity is mostly used for momentum spinors and is therefore written as follows.

\langle{ij}\rangle\langle{kl}\rangle+\langle{ik}\rangle\langle{lj}\rangle+\langle{il}\rangle\langle{jk}\rangle=0\,.

(A.9)

Another way to derive this result is to write one of the spinors as a linear combination of the others. Indeed, three two-dimensional spinors cannot all be linearly independent. For example,

j\rangle=\frac{\langle{jl}\rangle}{\langle{kl}\rangle}k\rangle+\frac{\langle{jk}\rangle}{\langle{lk}\rangle}l\rangle\,,

where the coefficients can be easily determined by taking some appropriate spinor products. Contracting this relation with
\langle i
yields (
A.9
). The identity can also be written in terms of
\varepsilon
.

\varepsilon^{AB}\varepsilon^{CD}+\varepsilon^{AC}\varepsilon^{DB}+\varepsilon^{AD}\varepsilon^{BC}=0\,.

(A.10)

Of course, the identity also holds for the other bracket product (
[{ij}][{kl}]+[{ik}][{lj}]+[{il}][{jk}]=0
).

The goal is to write the non-linear equations of motion for the gauge field in momentum space and solve them for each order in the coupling constant. The last line is obtained by relating
J(1)J(2,3)
and
J(1,2)J(3)
to
J(1,2,3)
. It is straightforward to write the generic formula for
1<k<n-1
,

J(1,\ldots,k)J(k+1,\ldots,n)=J(1,\ldots,n)\ \frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{kq}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}\,.

(E.14)

The last step is to show that the terms in brackets are equal to
p_{123}^{2}
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle{q2}\rangle}\left[\langle{12}\rangle(\langle{q2}\rangle[{12}]+\langle{q3}\rangle[{13}])+\langle{23}\rangle(\langle{q1}\rangle[{13}]+\langle{q2}\rangle[{23}])\right]

\displaystyle=\langle{12}\rangle[{12}]+\langle{23}\rangle[{23}]+\frac{[{13}]}{\langle{q2}\rangle}(\langle{q3}\rangle\langle{12}\rangle+\langle{q1}\rangle\langle{23}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\langle{12}\rangle[{12}]+\langle{23}\rangle[{23}]+\langle{13}\rangle[{13}]

\displaystyle=p_{123}^{2}

The final result has the expected structure;

J^{(2)}_{123}(y)^{B^{\prime}}=(yq)\ q_{B}(\pi_{123})^{BB^{\prime}}J(1,2,3)\ p_{123}^{2}\,.

(E.15)

The higher the order of the current, the more cumbersome the previous manipulations become. The next section presents the general proof for the expression of Berends–Giele currents in SDYM.

Appendix F
All-order Construction of Berends–Giele currents in SDYM

Let us give an inductive proof of the general expression (
5.16
), starting from the expression

J^{(n-1)}_{12\ldots n}(y)^{B^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{N+1}\partial_{B}(\pi_{12\ldots n})^{BB^{\prime}}\ \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}J^{(k-1)}_{12\ldots k}(y)_{C^{\prime}}J^{(n-k-1)}_{(k+1)\ldots n}(y)^{C^{\prime}}\,,

(F.1)

and assuming that for
k<n
, the currents follow the expected pattern

J^{(k-1)}_{12\ldots k}(y)^{B^{\prime}}=(yq)\ q_{B}(\pi_{12\ldots k})^{BB^{\prime}}J(1,2,\ldots,k)\ p_{12\ldots k}^{2}\,,

(F.2)

where

\displaystyle J(1,2,\ldots,k)=\frac{1}{\langle{q1}\rangle\langle{12}\rangle\langle{23}\rangle\ldots\langle{(k-1)k}\rangle\langle{kq}\rangle}\,,

(F.3)

\displaystyle p_{12\ldots k}=p_{1}+p_{2}+\ldots+p_{k}\,,

(F.4)

\displaystyle\pi_{12\ldots k}=p_{12\ldots k}/p_{12\ldots k}^{2}\,.

(F.5)

First, using the simple relation

J(1,\ldots,k)J(k+1,\ldots,n)=J(1,\ldots,n)\ \frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{kq}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}

allows to write the n-point current as

\phi^{(n-1)}_{12\ldots n}(y)^{B^{\prime}}=(yq)\ q_{B}(\pi_{12\ldots n})^{BB^{\prime}}\ J(1,\ldots,n)\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qk}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{qj}\rangle[{ij}]\,.

The main part of the proof consists of proving that the sum following
J(1,\ldots,n)
is equal to
p_{12\ldots n}^{2}
, or more explicitly,

\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\langle{ij}\rangle[{ij}]\,.

(F.6)

From the calculation of
J_{123}^{(2)}(y)^{B^{\prime}}
, one can guess that the general procedure is to factor out each
[ij]
and use the Schouten identity (
A.9
) (as many times as necessary) with the angle-bracket products, until forming
\langle{ij}\rangle
. This can be achieved by massaging summation indices, without using any property of the spinor brackets. As a result, the following equality holds

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qk}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{qj}\rangle[{ij}]=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}[{ij}]\left(\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{qj}\rangle\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qk}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}\right)\,.

(F.7)

The last step of the proof consists of establishing that

\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{qj}\rangle\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qk}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}=\langle{ij}\rangle\,.

(F.8)

This can be demonstrated by induction. Of course, when
j=i+1
, the equality is trivially satisfied. Let us assume that the equality is satisfied for some integer
j=l>2
and illustrate that it holds for
j=l+1
. Equation (
F.8
) is thus our induction hypothesis. It is assumed to hold for some
j
and the objective is to show its validity for
j+1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=i}^{j}\frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qk}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\frac{\langle{k(k+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qk}\rangle\langle{q(k+1)}\rangle}+\frac{\langle{j(j+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qj}\rangle\langle{q(j+1)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{ij}\rangle}{\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{qj}\rangle}+\frac{\langle{j(j+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qj}\rangle\langle{q(j+1)}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle{qj}\rangle}\left(\frac{\langle{q(j+1)}\rangle\langle{ij}\rangle+\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{j(j+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{q(j+1)}\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\langle{qj}\rangle}\left(\frac{\langle{qj}\rangle\langle{i(j+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{q(j+1)}\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{i(j+1)}\rangle}{\langle{qi}\rangle\langle{q(j+1)}\rangle}\,.

The second line follows from the induction hypothesis. The fourth one is the consequence of the Schouten identity. This completes the proof of Formula (
5.16
).
