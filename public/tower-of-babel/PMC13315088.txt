Investigating the neuroprotective potentials of Aspilia africana and chrysin in phenanthrene exposed rats: in silico and biochemical studies
Authors: O.V. Akinleye, P.T. Akindoye, D.I. Aderonmu, E.T. Adediran, O.S. Osuntokun, K.O. Ajeigbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurotoxicity induced by environmental pollutants such as phenanthrene, a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH), has been linked to oxidative stress, inflammatory responses and cholinergic crisis contributing hugely to neurodegenerative conditions. In this light, this study explored neuroprotective potentials of Aspilia africana (AA) against phenanthrene-induced neurotoxicity in rats. Seven bioactive phytochemicals in AA were evaluated by elucidating their binding dynamics P2X7 and predict their pharmacokinetic properties using molecular docking and ADMET prediction. Neurotoxicity was induced by oral administration of phenanthrene for 26 days, followed by oral treatment with aqueous extract of AA (500 mg/kg or 750 mg/kg) or Chrysin (25 mg/kg) for 18 days. Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-10 (IL-10), Inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS), Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF-κB) Malondialdehyde (MDA), Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT) Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2–Related Factor 2 (Nrf2), Acetylcholinesterase AChE levels were measured using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits. Molecular docking and ADMET prediction were done using CB-Dock, mcule and SwissADME servers. Phenanthrene administration significantly elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and, (p < 0.05), and reduced IL-10. Conversely, antioxidant enzymes activity was markedly reduced, along with decreased AChE activity. AA significantly downregulated the pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulated anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) and restored antioxidant levels in a dose-dependent manner. AChE activity was also normalized, and Nrf2 expression was significantly upregulated. The seven identified most bioactive compounds of AA including chrysin docked favourably with P2X7 receptor with binding affinities between −9.9 and −5.6 kcal/mol. The ADMET profiles showed drug ability, GI absorptions, and moderate or no toxicity for all. AA confers neuroprotection against phenanthrene-induced toxicity by attenuating inflammation, enhancing antioxidant responses via Nrf2 pathway activation, and modulating cholinergic imbalance.

Introduction

The brain is a complex organ that plays an important role in coordination, cognitive functions, motor control and autonomic processes. Neurological disorders with environmental toxins have gained significant attention. These neurological disorders are characterized by disruptions in neurotransmitter balance, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and cholinergic crisis, all of which can lead to neurodegenerative diseases. One potential cause of these disorders is exposure to an environmental pollutant called phenanthrene.

Phenanthrene is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH), composed of three fused benzene rings. PAHs have two or more benzene rings, they are semi volatile, are mobile over long distance and bio-concentrate (Lu et al., 2015). Phenanthrene is a compound that is crystalline in nature and has some light reflecting properties. It has the molecular formula C14H10. It can be found in cigarette smoke, industrial waste, incomplete combustion of variety of organic compounds such as wood, fossil fuels. (Ge et al., 2017).

Phenanthrene induces neurotoxicity by generating oxidative stress that disrupts neuronal integrity, activating neuroinflammatory pathways such as microglia activation, NF-κB and NOD-like Receptor Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, and inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, which leads to acetylcholine accumulation and cholinergic crisis (Zhang et al., 2023; He and Liu, 2023).

Inflammation is initiated when phenanthrene binds to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) in microglia, leading to microglial activation and increased oxidative stress (He and Liu, 2023). This promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, which contribute to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (Joshua et al., 2012). In addition, oxidative stress and cytokine signaling activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB pathway, further amplifying the inflammatory response (Yu et al., 2023).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) are key antioxidant enzymes widely used as biomarkers to assess oxidative stress and cellular redox status. Phenanthrene (PHE) exposure can modulate antioxidant activity, either enhancing or suppressing cellular defense systems. Elevated stress responses increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radical (OH), and superoxide anion (O2−), which causes lipid peroxidation and contributes to tissue injury (Emel and Saadet, 2004). In addition, chronic stress and elevated glucocorticoids have been shown to disrupt ROS homeostasis and increase basal ROS levels (Emel and Saadet, 2004) .

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) is a cholinergic enzyme primarily found at postsynaptic neuromuscular junctions, where it hydrolyzes acetylcholine, a key neurotransmitter. Exposure to phenanthrene can inhibit AChE through reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated damage, which may alter the enzyme's structure or active site and impair its catalytic function (Aguilar et al., 2023). This inhibition leads to the accumulation of acetylcholine at synapses, resulting in excessive cholinergic stimulation and potential neurotoxicity (He and Liu, 2023).

Chrysin (5,7-dihydroxyflavone) is a hydroxylated flavonoid found in bee propolis, honey, and a variety of plants and fungi, including Passiflora species, Pelargonium crispum, Oroxylum indicum, and certain mushrooms (Sabry et al., 2023). It exhibits pharmacological activity characterized by antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which contribute to its protective role against neuronal injury. Specifically, chrysin reduces oxidative stress and suppresses pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby limiting neuronal damage and functional impairment (Ramajayan, 2019).

Aspilia africana (Pers.) C. D. Adams, commonly called wild sunflower, is a medicinal plant of the Asteraceae family that is widespread across tropical Africa (Okello et al., 2021). Phytochemical studies of A. africana have identified multiple bioactive constituents including monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes (e.g., α-pinene, caryophyllene and germacrene D), diterpenes such as phytol, and flavonoids like quercetin and apigenin that have, as reported widely anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities (Okoli et al., 2007). These properties are directly relevant to neuroprotection, as oxidative stress and inflammation are key drivers of neuronal damage. By scavenging reactive oxygen species and modulating pro-inflammatory pathways, these compounds may help preserve neuronal integrity and function, thereby mitigating neurotoxicity within the context of this study.

In this study, chrysin is used as a comparative compound to compare its neuroprotective effect to that of Aspilia africana. Chrysin has been established to be a neuroprotective agent as its mitigate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and increasing antioxidant enzymes in the brain. However, Aspilia africana has not been established to mitigate neurotoxicity, its role in alleviating neuroinflammation, oxidative stress and cholinergic crisis has not been well studied.

Aim of study: The aim of this study is to explore the neuroprotective potential of Aspilia africana and chrysin on phenanthrene induced neurotoxicity. The study aims to establish the neuroprotective role of Aspilia africana; mitigating neuroinflammation, oxidative stress and cholinergic crisis.

Methods

Plant extraction

The plant used in this study, Aspilia africana (commonly known as wild sunflower), was collected fresh, and authenticated at the herbarium of the Department of Plant Science and Biotechnology, Federal University, Oye-Ekiti where a voucher specimen is deposited. The leaves (single batch) were air-dried at room temperature (25–28°C) in a shaded area for 7–10 days after then pulverized into a fine powder using an electric blender and stored in an airtight container until use.

About 60 g of the plant powder were weighed using Mettler weighing balance and homogenized in a clean electric blender containing 80 ml sterile distilled water as previously reported (Ajeigbe et al., 2013). These gave 750% concentration (using 7.5 g/10 ml). The mixture was allowed to cool slightly and then filtered first through a muslin cloth and subsequently through Whatman No.1 filter paper to obtain a clear aqueous extract.

The resulting filtrate was then subjected to freeze drying (lyophilization) to preserve its phytochemical integrity and increase shelf life. The freeze-dried extract was collected as a fine powder and stored in airtight containers at 4°C until further use. The crude extract gotten eventually was approximately 10% of the original fine powdered leaf. Upon phytochemical analysis using standard methods (AOAC, 2000), tannins, alkaloids, saponin, flavonoids, phenols, glycoside are found in substantial quantity.

Animal grouping and experimental design

Fifty – six (56) healthy male albino rats of the Wistar strain, weighing 150–220 g, were used for this study. The animals were housed in transparent plastic cages with wire-mesh covers, bedded with wood shavings, and maintained under standard laboratory conditions with free access to commercial pellet diet and water. They were acclimatized to the laboratory environment for 7 days prior to the commencement of the experiment.

Phenanthrene exposure was maintained for 26 days in all groups. Aspilia africana aqueous extract was administered for 18 days, while chrysin was administered for 11 days concurrently.

All substances were administered via oral route Aspilia africana extract was administered using distilled water as the solvent, while phenanthrene was administered using olive oil as the vehicle.

The selected doses of Aspilia africana (500 and 750 mg/kg) were based on previous studies demonstrating significant biological activity and safety in experimental rats. A standalone treatment group at 500 mg/kg was not included, as the study was designed to prioritize evaluation of the combined effects with phenanthrene and the response at a higher dose, thereby providing clearer insight into dose-dependent and interaction effects. Chrysin (25 mg/kg) was chosen as a moderate pharmacological dose within the established non-toxic range reported in rodent studies, while phenanthrene (10 mg/kg) was selected in line with commonly used low-dose polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon exposure models in toxicological research (Ajeigbe et al., 2013; Yao et al., 2021).

Studies on animal experimentation were done following the current animal care regulations and standards approved by (ILAR, 1996) and protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the College of Medicine, Federal University, Oye-Ekiti, Nigeria (ID: FUOYE/AECCM/PHS/2025/020).

Biochemical assay

Biochemical evaluation of pro-inflammatory markers, anti-inflammatory markers, antioxidant enzymes, and acetylcholinesterase activity was carried out using homogenized brain tissue samples. The biomarkers assessed include IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, AChE, iNOS, NF-κB, MDA, GPx, SOD, CAT, and Nrf2. These biomarkers were selected based on their established roles in inflammation, oxidative stress, and cholinergic dysfunction associated with phenanthrene-induced neurotoxicity.

At the end of the experimental period, the animals were sacrificed by cervical dislocation The brains were rapidly excised, rinsed in ice-cold normal saline, and dissected on an ice-cold surface. Tissue samples were collected immediately and processed for biochemical analysis.

Brain tissues were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) using a glass-Teflon homogenizer to obtain a 10% (w/v) homogenate. The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for abut 10 min and the supernatants were collected and stored at −20°C until analysis.

Determination of the biomarkers was performed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits obtained from Elabscience biotechnology Co. All biochemical assays were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Standard ELISA procedures were followed, including sample incubation with specific antibodies, washing steps, substrate reaction, and absorbance measurement at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Biomarker concentrations were calculated from standard calibration curves generated for each assay.

Molecular docking and ADMET analysis

Target protein selection

The three-dimensional crystal structure of the P2X7 receptor (PDB ID: 6U9W) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. P2X7 receptor was selected as the target protein due to its central role in ATP- mediated inflammatory signaling and its ability to activate NLRP3 inflammasome and promote pro-inflammatory cytokine release (Liu et al., 2024). Phenanthrene induces oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to extracellular ATP release, which in turn activates P2X7 receptor and triggers inflammasome-driven cytokine production. Since phenanthrene-induced neurotoxicity is strongly associated with oxidative stress, glial activation and inflammation, P2X7 serves as a relevant molecular target for docking studies.

Ligand selection

Nine ligands were selected for the study, comprising seven bioactive compounds reported in Aspilia africana (α-pinene, betulin, apigenin, caryophyllene, germacrene D, phytol, and quercetin), along with chrysin. The ligand structures were obtained in SDF format from PubChem.

Ligand and protein preparation

Protein preparation was carried out by removing water molecules, ions, and co-crystallized ligands. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and appropriate partial charges were assigned using AutoDock. The prepared protein structure was saved in PDBQT format for docking.

Ligand preparation involved energy minimization and geometry optimization using CB dock. Charges were assigned, and the ligands were converted to the required format (PDBQT) for docking. These steps ensured that both receptor and ligands were in energetically stable and biologically relevant conformation.

Docking procedure

Molecular docking was performed using the CB-Dock 2.0. A blind docking approach was employed, in which the entire protein surface was scanned for potential binding cavities without predefining the active site. CB-Dock integrates cavity detection with docking calculations using the AutoDock Vina scoring function.

Binding affinities were reported in kcal/mol, with more negative values indicating stronger binding interactions. The best docking poses were selected based on binding energy and interaction profiles. Visualization and analysis of receptor–ligand interactions were performed using Discovery Studio , including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking.

ADMET evaluation

Pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness properties of the ligands were evaluated using SwissADME, while toxicity predictions were conducted using Mcule.

The following ADMET parameters were assessed:•Absorption: gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate status•Distribution: blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability•Metabolism: inhibition of cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP3A4)•Drug-likeness: Lipinski's rule of five and bioavailability score•Toxicity: predicted toxicity endpoints using Mcule platform

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as Mean ± SEM and subjected to one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey test using GraphPad prism version 10 for windows from GraphPad software, as they are normally distributed. P values < 0.05 were regarded significant when comparing the means across the groups.

Discussion

The results of this study described the effect of aqueous extract of Aspilla Africana and chrysin on inflammation and cholinergic crisis associated with phenanthrene induced neurotoxicity in rats.

Phenanthrene exposure increased IL-1β and TNF-α while reducing IL-10 in a rat model of neuroinflammation. It also inhibited acetylcholinesterase (AChE), the enzyme responsible for acetylcholine breakdown at cholinergic synapses. This inhibition reduced AChE activity and caused accumulation of acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft, disrupting cholinergic transmission. Such impairment may contribute to cholinergic dysfunction, including muscle weakness and neurotoxicity.

Inflammation is mediated by different mechanisms including the activation of microglia, oxidative stress, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and NF-kB signaling. Phenanthrene binds to Aryl Hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) thereby activating the microglia leading to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (He and Liu, 2023). This may lead to an increase in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Joshua et al., 2012), followed by different inflammatory cascades.

In the results, rats treated with phenanthrene only exhibited increased levels of IL-1β when compared with rats in the control group. Also, there is an increase in the level of TNF-α when compared with rats in the control group. However, there is a significant reduction in the level of IL-10 in rats treated with phenanthrene only when compared with rats in the control group.

Cholinergic crisis is characterized by the accumulation of acetylcholine in the neuromuscular junction caused by the inhibition of acetylcholinesterase; an enzyme that hydrolyzes acetylcholine (Lacomis, 2005). Phenanthrene induces the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which have been shown to modify acetylcholinesterase, thereby inhibiting its ability to hydrolyze acetylcholine in the neuromuscular junction (Schallreuter et al., 2004).

In the results, rats treated with phenanthrene only showed a significant decrease in the level of AChE when compared with rats in the control group.

The results of this study show that Aspilla africana may exert a neuroprotective effect on neuroinflammation and cholinergic crisis induced by phenanthrene. Aspilla Africana contains some phytochemicals such as saponins, flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, tannins and phenolic compounds. These phytochemicals have been studied to exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects (Okoli et al., 2007). These compounds may scavenge reactive oxygen species and enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses, thereby limiting oxidative damage to neuronal structures and enzymes such as acetylcholinesterase (AChE). Also, they may suppress pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including cytokine release and microglial activation, which contribute to neuronal injury.

In the results, rats treated with Phenanthrene and Aspilla africana showed reduced level of IL-1β and TNF-α when compared with rats treated with phenanthrene only.

Aspilla africana has shown an increase in the level of IL-10, in the rats treated with phenanthrene and Aspilla africana there is an increase in the level of IL-10 when compared to rats treated with phenanthrene only.

When comparing the rats treated with both Aspilla africana (500 mg and 750 mg) and phenanthrene, the level of AChE increases than in rats treated with phenanthrene only.

Chrysin is a synthetic compound which is under the class of flavonoid. It has been reported to exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. In this study chrysin has been shown to mitigate inflammation and cholinergic crisis induced by phenanthrene (Filho et al., 2016). It scavenges reactive oxygen species and enhances endogenous antioxidant defenses, thereby preventing oxidative damage to neuronal components, including acetylcholinesterase (AChE). At the same time, chrysin inhibits pathways such as NF-κB, leading to reduced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

In the results, rats treated with Phenanthrene and chrysin showed reduced level of IL-1β and TNF-α when compared with rats treated with phenanthrene only.

In the rats treated with phenanthrene and chrysin there is an increase in the level of IL-10 when compared to rats treated with phenanthrene only. Comparing the rats treated with phenanthrene and chrysin, there was a significant increase in the level of AChE than in rats treated with phenanthrene only.

Chrysin has been shown to exhibit more anti-inflammatory effect by greatly reducing the level of IL-1β and TNF-α and increasing the level of IL-10 in rats when compared to the anti-inflammatory effect of Aspilla Africana.

Molecular docking and mechanistic implication

The P2X7 receptor is a recognized mediator of neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. Docking analysis indicates that several bioactive compounds from Aspilia africana exhibit favorable binding to this target. Quercetin (−9.0 kcal/mol), betulin (−9.9 kcal/mol), apigenin (−8.0 kcal/mol), and chrysin (−7.5 kcal/mol) show stronger binding affinities than caryophyllene (−7.3 kcal/mol), germacrene D (−6.7 kcal/mol), α-pinene (−6.0 kcal/mol), and phytol (−5.6 kcal/mol). These interactions involve hydrogen bonding with key residues (Tyr298, Thr90, Lys64, Ala296, Arg294), along with π-interactions (Lys297, Tyr295, Leu96, Tyr90) and hydrophobic contacts (Leu56, Pro96, Leu95, Lys297, Tyr295). The higher binding affinity of flavonoids, particularly quercetin, apigenin, and chrysin, suggests a greater potential to inhibit P2X7 receptor activation.

Interpretation

The docking results indicate that flavonoids are the primary contributors to P2X7 inhibition, which aligns with their established antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Terpenoids such as phytol and caryophyllene, despite moderate binding affinity, may contribute through complementary mechanisms, supporting a combined or synergistic effect. The convergence of receptor-level inhibition and acceptable pharmacokinetic properties strengthens the case for Aspilia africana as a source of compounds with therapeutic relevance in neuroinflammatory conditions.

Conclusion

This study shows that Aspilia africana extract and chrysin exert neuroprotective effects against phenanthrene-induced neurotoxicity. Phenanthrene disrupted oxidative balance, increased inflammatory markers, and altered acetylcholinesterase activity. Treatment with both agents reversed these changes by improving antioxidant status, reducing inflammation, and normalizing cholinergic function.

In silico results supported strong binding of the selected phytochemicals to neuroinflammatory targets, aligning with the biochemical findings and suggesting possible mechanistic action.

Overall, the study indicates that Aspilia africana and chrysin may help counteract environmentally induced neuronal damage. Further work is on-going in our laboratory focusing on detailed pathway studies, dose optimization, and long-term behavioral evaluation.
