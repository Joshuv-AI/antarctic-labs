Resilience paths: maternal experiences in coping with syphilis exposure or congenital infection and the role of support networks
Authors: Talia de Souza Fernandes Poggianella, Giovanna Cristina Machado-Kayzuka, Ana Lúcia de Lima Guedes, Natália Maria Vieira Pereira Caldeira, Lise Maria Carvalho Mendes, Laís Celeste Medeiros Mendes da Fonseca, Fernanda Maria Vieira Pereira Ávila, Lucila Castanheira Nascimento, Flávia Azevedo Gomes-Sponholz, Nayara Gonçalves Barbosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital syphilis is a significant public health problem and can lead to several unfavorable perinatal outcomes and severe sequelae in children. In Brazil, the number of cases of congenital and gestational syphilis continues to increase, highlighting the need for timely diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. A lack of adequate follow-up during prenatal care may result in unwanted outcomes for the neonate, such as prolonged hospitalization and impacts on neurodevelopment. Although many studies have focused on the clinical aspects of congenital syphilis, there is a gap in understanding maternal experiences, especially regarding affective and psychological elements and the importance of support networks.ObjectivesTo explore the experiences of mothers of children exposed to syphilis during pregnancy or with congenital infection regarding the diagnosis and hospitalization of the child and the influence of their support networks.MethodsThis is a descriptive study with a qualitative approach based on the concept of social support networks. After ethical approval, individual and semi-structured interviews were conducted and submitted for content analysis. The study enrolled 11 mothers of children with congenital infection and two mothers of children exposed to gestational syphilis at an outpatient clinic in Juiz de Fora, Minas Gerais, Brazil.ResultsThe guilt of the mother and her responsibility for the vertical transmission were identified. The women experienced feelings of sadness, doubts about the child, mistaken conception of the disease, and fear of the child’s death. The experience was permeated by stigma and prejudice. The hospitalization of the child and the consequent need for invasive procedures was a moment of shock for the mother. The perspective of the child’s well-being and the recognition of the benefits of the treatment, as well as faith and the presence of a support network, were fundamental in the process.ConclusionThe presence of a support network in the process of coping with congenital syphilis or exposure to infection can modulate maternal health. This highlights the need for health education and more inclusive actions in the context of maternal and child health from the time of prenatal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09441-2.

Introduction

Syphilis is an infection caused by Treponema pallidum that can be transmitted sexually, hematogenously, or vertically via the transplacental route or, less frequently, from contact with maternal genital lesions in the birth canal [1]. Maternal syphilis is a public health problem, and vertical transmission can occur at any time during pregnancy or at any stage of maternal infection, with most being asymptomatic, and, therefore, must be managed as having late syphilis, with an assumed risk of fetal involvement [2]. Congenital syphilis can cause unfavorable perinatal outcomes, such as spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, neonatal and post-neonatal mortality, prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, neurological sequelae, and late clinical manifestations [1, 3].

Although congenital syphilis can be prevented through screening and treatment of pregnant women, social, economic, and cultural barriers persist in preventing access to prenatal care. These are most related to poverty, low education, race, gender inequities, violence, precarious housing, and other social determinants of health [1, 2, 4, 5]. Studies have also shown that younger maternal age and fewer previous pregnancies are associated with increased vulnerability, possibly reflecting reduced access to information, limited experience with prenatal care, and challenges in navigating health services [6]. In Brazil, syphilis indicators have increased substantially over the past decade. In 2022, the detection rate of syphilis in pregnancy reached 32.4 cases per 1,000 live births, while congenital syphilis occurred at a rate of 10.3 cases per 1,000 live births. In the same year, 200 deaths from congenital syphilis were recorded, corresponding to a mortality rate of 7.8 per 100,000 live births. All indicators showed a rising trend following the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting significant gaps in timely diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up during prenatal care [7].

Considering the increase in cases of congenital syphilis, timely diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of vertical transmission remain a priority for public health [8]. In Brazil, every pregnant woman should be screened for syphilis at the first prenatal visit. The test should be repeated in the third trimester and at the time of birth. Seropositive women and their sexual partners should undergo treatment [9]. However, less than 50–60% of syphilis during pregnancy complete the recommended regimen, and treatment of sexual partners is even less frequent [10, 11]. However, to eradicate congenital syphilis, the Pan American Health Organization and World Health Organization recommend achieving a 95% or greater rate of sufficient maternal treatment [12]. Furthermore, the goal is to stop the occurrence of congenital syphilis in the Americas, aiming for an incidence of 0.5 cases per 1000 live births [12, 13].

Congenital syphilis can cause prolonged hospitalization in neonatal intensive care units and is associated with significant morbidity and long-term consequences for children when diagnosis or treatment is delayed [1, 14–16]. Hospitalization during this stage of life can have various consequences for both the baby’s neurodevelopment and the establishment of parental bonds and roles [17, 18]. In addition to the comorbidities observed due to syphilis, mothers and babies are still exposed to an environment of care and stimuli that do not match the ideal environment during pregnancy [17, 18]. For mothers, emotional and relational repercussions due to the impossibility of fulfilling their family role and the fragile vision of the baby imply damage to the mental health of the mother, who begins to find challenges in supporting her hospitalized baby [17, 18]. The feeling of loneliness is also a reality, either due to the impossibility of being close to the family support network or the lack of support from the unit’s health team [18].

While numerous studies have focused on understanding the factors linked to the development of congenital syphilis and its implications and complications for both mothers and neonates, there is limited research on maternal experience in this context, including the affective and psychological issues that arise throughout the diagnostic process [14–16]. The literature increasingly seeks answers about the effectiveness and importance of support for different populations, but there is still a gap in understanding congenital infectious diseases, primarily due to the social taboo surrounding them [13, 17, 18]. The contagion and transmission mechanism of syphilis from mother to baby is accompanied by social stigmas, which, in addition to the evident failures of the health system and its care for pregnant women, constitute essential topics for the quality of primary care [11, 16].

Most available studies addressing maternal emotions and coping during neonatal hospitalization focus on mothers of preterm or high-risk infants [19, 20], rather than infants with congenital syphilis, showing limited qualitative research on maternal experience in this context, including the affective and psychological issues that arise throughout the diagnostic process [21–23]. Thus, little is known about how women interpret the diagnosis, navigate stigma, and mobilize their support networks when facing this particular congenital infection. Understanding these psychosocial and emotional dimensions is crucial for complementing existing clinical knowledge [8, 9, 21–23] and enhancing family-centered care. Thus, this study aimed to explore the experiences of mothers of children with congenital syphilis, or those exposed to Treponema pallidum during pregnancy, influenced by their support networks regarding the diagnosis and hospitalization of the child.

Methods

Study design

This is an exploratory study with a qualitative approach based on the support network theory concept. The COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) was used as a guideline to report the essential elements that must be reported in a qualitative research design to improve its rigor, scope, and credibility [24].

Theoretical-methodological concept

The concept of social support network was adopted to guide the analysis and discussion of this study. This is defined as a protective measure against various negative experiences that people encounter [25, 26]. Concerning the specificity of the parental role, Winnicott, when studying maternal influence on child development, employed the concept of a support network to emphasize its crucial role in establishing an adequate maternal role [27]. It is also called protective cover from the support that intervenes around the mother, which is essential, especially in the earliest stage of child development, where bonds are developed and stimuli are initiated. When the mother has such support, often initiated by the father, she is more likely to present ideal conditions to meet the child’s environmental, psychological, or physical needs. External support, formed by family or other relationships built by different groups and social institutions, is also essential for forming this “protective covering” [27].

Place of study, participants, and recruitment

The study was conducted at the Pediatric Infectious Diseases outpatient clinic of a university hospital located in a large urban center in the state of Minas Gerais, Brazil. This outpatient clinic serves as a regional and macro-regional referral center for the investigation, treatment, and follow-up of infectious and parasitic diseases, including congenital and perinatal infections. The clinic provides care to the pediatric population up to 17 years of age and conducts an average of 12 consultations per week (approximately six first-time consultations), all scheduled through the municipal appointment regulation system.

The population included mothers of children with congenital syphilis or exposed to Treponema pallidum who were followed up for at least one month at the site, with an estimated maximum follow-up period of one year, regardless of the age of the child. Including mothers whose children were at different stages of follow-up allowed us to capture both immediate reactions at birth and later reflections after experiencing outpatient care. Adoptive mothers and other caregivers, as well as mothers younger than 18 years, were excluded from the study because they had not received a diagnosis during pregnancy/birth. A total of 13 mothers were included, which represented the point at which thematic saturation was reached, as no new codes emerged [28].

The case of congenital syphilis was defined in three situations: (1) every newborn, stillborn, or miscarriage from a woman with untreated or inadequately treated syphilis; (2) every child under 13 years of age in at least one of the following situations, in which the possibility of acquired syphilis in a situation of sexual violence has been ruled out: (a) clinical manifestation, cerebrospinal fluid, or radiological alteration of congenital syphilis and reactive non-treponemal test (NTT); (b) NTT titles of the newborn higher than the maternal ones and at least 2 dilutions of peripheral blood collected simultaneously at the time of delivery; (c) ascending NTT in at least 2 dilutions in the follow-up of the exposed child; (d) titles still reactive after 6 months of age in children adequately treated in the neonatal period; (e) reactive treponemal tests (TT) after 18 months of age without a previous diagnosis of congenital syphilis; (3) microbiological evidence of infection by Treponema pallidum in a nasal secretion sample or skin lesion, biopsy, or necropsy of a child, miscarriage, or stillborn [29].

A child exposed to maternal syphilis was defined as the offspring of a woman diagnosed with syphilis during pregnancy and adequately treated. A child classified as exposed to syphilis must be asymptomatic and have an NTT that is non-reactive, or reactive with a titer lower than, equal to, or up to one dilution higher than the maternal titer [29].

From an epidemiological perspective, adequate maternal treatment includes the complete regimen for the respective clinical stage of syphilis, using benzathine penicillin, initiated up to 30 days before delivery. Pregnant women who do not meet this criterion are considered inadequately treated. However, while the treatment of sexual partners is not included in the epidemiological criteria for defining congenital syphilis cases, this treatment is known to be essential considering the possibility of reinfection during pregnancy. Therefore, for clinical and care purposes, several factors are considered for the adequate treatment of pregnant women with syphilis, such as: (a) administration of benzathine penicillin; (b) initiation of treatment up to 30 days before delivery; (c) a therapeutic regimen consistent with the clinical stage; (d) adherence to the recommended dose interval; (e) assessment of reinfection risk; and (f) documentation of a decline in the NTT titer by at least two dilutions within three months, or four dilutions within six months after treatment completion [30, 31].

Given the diagnostic gaps in congenital syphilis at birth, systematic clinical and laboratory follow-up of all reported congenital syphilis or infants exposed to syphilis in pregnancy is imperative. This minimizes diagnostic and treatment failures and allows for the early detection of sequelae to ensure appropriate interventions.

The recruitment process involved identifying potential participants through a prior review of electronic medical records of individuals waiting for medical consultations in the outpatient clinic. Data reviewed included objective clinical information relevant to the maternal experience, including adequacy of maternal treatment during pregnancy, syphilis testing of sexual partners, delivery characteristics, neonatal laboratory results, clinical manifestations and follow-up data after discharge. These variables were used to contextualize the narratives and ensure accurate characterization of the sample.

The participants were then approached by the first author, an undergraduate student with a bachelor’s degree in nursing, supervised by the last author, a doctor in nursing with experience in qualitative research. The student had no previous contact with the service and/or participants. The researcher introduced herself to the participants and invited them to participate in the study, explaining its objectives. Fourteen mothers participated in the research and signed the consent form; however, one was excluded due to incomplete medical records. There were no refusals or dropouts throughout the study. After agreeing to participate, each mother was taken to a private room where the interview was conducted without disrupting her previous medical schedule. They also received a sociodemographic questionnaire, which contained self-reported information through open-ended questions (e.g., racial identification, age of the child, etc.).

Data collection and organization

The data were collected from May to November 2022 through semi-structured interviews. The interviews were recorded after obtaining the participants’ authorization and were conducted in a private room, once with each participant. Each interview lasted an average of approximately 57 min, ranging from 40 min to 1 h and 15 min.

The same recruitment researchers conducted the interviews. We used a semi-structured script prepared by the researchers (Supplementary file), comprising two parts: the first regarding the sociodemographic characterization of the participants and the second containing questions related to maternal experiences in the face of the diagnosis and treatment of the child: How was the birth of your child and the discovery of the congenital infection? What feelings did you experience at the time of the child’s diagnosis? How did you cope with these feelings? What had you heard about this disease? How was the treatment while your child was in the hospital? How did you feel during your child’s hospitalization?

Data analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim by the first author using Microsoft Word software and were submitted to the double-checking process by the last author. The analytical process was conducted by the last author and reviewed by the research group to ensure the reduction of analysis bias. No software was used, and the precepts of the inductive thematic analysis were followed, as proposed by Braun and Clarke [32], namely: (1) Familiarization with the data through repetitive readings of the transcriptions; (2) Initial coding by listing the ideas presented by the data and identifying similar characteristics among the data; (3) Classification of the codes into initial themes through the list of different codes and relevant data for each code; (4) Review of the themes through the identification of patterns between the initial codes and themes; (5) Definition of themes through the identification of the history of each theme to answer the research question; and (6) Clear and cohesive description of the results obtained to sustain the research. Ethical aspects

The study strictly followed the ethical precepts established in the Resolution of the National Health Council N. 466 of December 12, 2012, which addresses research with human beings, with the participants signing the informed consent form. The Research Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of the Federal University of Juiz de Fora approved the research under N. 5.832.284 in 2022. Participants were identified by the letter P (participant) and assigned Arabic numbers (from P1 to P14) to ensure anonymity.

The women’s median age was 26 years. The majority of participants self-identified as black/mixed (n = 10; 76.92%) and single (n = 12; 92.86%). Among the main clinical manifestations recorded in the infants’ medical charts were neonatal sepsis (n = 3), neurosyphilis (n = 2), microcephaly (n = 1), and intrauterine growth restriction (n = 1). According to the medical record review, three women were diagnosed during labor and remained untreated for syphilis, while six received incomplete or late treatment during pregnancy, increasing the risk of congenital infection. The majority of sexual partners (n = 9; 69,23%) had not received treatment. In contrast, two mothers who received adequate syphilis treatment delivered an infant without congenital infection.

The analysis revealed two main themes that reflected the maternal experience of their children during the diagnostic process: (1) “Meaning attributed to the diagnosis of in-utero exposure or congenital syphilis: encompassing feelings of guilt, anger, ignorance, powerlessness, and judgment”, and (2) “Between anguish and redefinition of hospitalization: maternal perspectives on the treatment of newborns exposed to syphilis or with congenital infection”.

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of mothers of children exposed to Treponema pallidum during pregnancy or with congenital syphilis influenced by their support networks regarding the diagnosis and hospitalization of the child. Mothers attributed the meaning to the diagnosis and treatment of their children, with feelings of guilt, sadness, anguish, and fear of the child’s sequelae and possible death being common. The participants in our study expressed feelings of disappointment stemming from the failure to prevent syphilis infection, lack of proactivity for treatment from their partners, and insufficient knowledge about the disease. Additionally, they felt burdened by judgment and criticism from family members and their support network, which intensified their guilt about the diagnosis. This led them to seek information about their child’s diagnosis in secret.

The significance attributed to the diagnosis of intrauterine exposure to syphilis or congenital infection for women in our study is related to negative feelings, as seen in the literature related to other congenital diagnoses. The perception of failure in their motherhood roles and the inefficiency of treatment for the disease during prenatal care are some of the experiences that can contribute to the emergence of feelings such as guilt, worry, and fear of the future [33, 34]. The mothers’ insecurity and shame were also highlighted in our findings, mainly by the efforts to omit information about the child’s health and the syphilis diagnosis, fearing the judgment of society, especially in the family context [33–35]. When connecting these experiences to their limited support network, we can see how the absence of a protective role influences the safety and development of the maternal role during the disease process. As described by Winnicott, the lack of support, especially by the partner, makes it difficult to create the conditions considered ideal for the development of the child, which adds to the consequences of the syphilis diagnosis and contributes to the mother’s guilt [27].

Further described by Winnicott and other authors, constituent elements of external support, such as institutions and groups, are also an essential part of an effective support network for mothers [27]. In our study, we found that many women facing the diagnosis and treatment of congenital syphilis have, in health teams, support that they often do not find in their inner circle. During the prenatal period, for example, lack of knowledge was mentioned as a grounding pillar of insecurity in the maternal role. The role of education in the context of health originates from the health team, who can provide the necessary tools for empowering their patients, strengthening their support network, and their consequent sense of security, thereby valuing care that reflects the cultural, ethnic, and family values of women [2]. Similarly, our findings showed that a lack of knowledge about intrauterine exposure to syphilis or congenital infection caused greater suffering for women, with the mistaken conception that it was an incurable disease that could lead to the death of the child.

Although most women recognize syphilis as a sexually transmitted infection (STI), there is still a lack of awareness about its impact during pregnancy among the general population. This contributes to high infection rates in newborns, making it challenging to conduct routine tests for detecting and intervening early in the disease [2, 36]. Women with lower levels of education, in vulnerable situations, and with limited access to information from healthcare teams are often at a greater risk of contracting gestational and congenital syphilis [2, 5]. Ethnic and racial inequities also play an important role in screening women during prenatal care, as evidence shows that black women are less likely to be diagnosed and treated [5, 9, 36]. The predominant profile of black women included in the study reflects ethnic-racial inequities in prenatal care. The lack of reception of these women by society can contribute to their isolation and perpetuation of evasive health behaviors, hindering diagnosis and treatment [5].

The mothers in our study also emphasized the importance of professionals’ assurance about the benefits of treatments and their decision-making for their children’s health. Through comprehensive professional support, mothers of children with congenital syphilis were empowered to face the challenges of hospitalization more effectively while being able to take care of their children in the best possible way. In such situations, health teams can become an integral part of the support network of women during the child’s hospitalization, mainly through the encouragement and training of mothers to provide care to the child [37]. This may involve guidance on basic care, drug administration, and vital sign monitoring, giving the mother the confidence to play an active role in caring for her baby [37, 38]. In addition, health teams can assist in the resignification of hospitalization, as found in our study, helping mothers understand the importance of hospital care for the recovery of their child and offering emotional support to address the challenges associated with being apart from each other [39, 40]. The use of principles of family-centered care, such as the promotion of maternal presence in the neonatal unit, the encouragement of skin-to-skin contact and participation in the daily care of the newborn, encourages and facilitates the strengthening of the mother-child bond, promoting fundamental pillars for the healthy emotional and physical development of the maternal role [39].

During hospitalization, mothers sought a positive perspective regarding the treatment and improvement of their children. Interpersonal connections with health teams and the construction of a trustful relationship are then shown to be tools for supporting the maternal experience [2]. In this sense, the communication established between the mother and the professional mediates relationships, promoting holistic care for the patient and family [39]. Recognizing the multiple needs of the binomial is paramount during delicate periods such as hospitalization [38]. By recognizing and respecting the beliefs and instruments of patients and their families, health professionals can play a vital role as an integral part of the support network [39, 41]. This involves providing adequate medical care and treatment, as well as offering emotional and spiritual support, while considering the patient’s holistic needs [2, 37].

Religious and spiritual influences play a role in some women’s understanding of health and disease [2]. Religion can play an even more essential role, especially for those who lack a support network composed of family members and partners, as highlighted in our results. In these circumstances, religious faith can become a crucial source of emotional and spiritual support, filling the void left by the absence of primary social support, constituting the external support described by Winnicott [27]. Through spiritual practices and religious rituals, these women can find solace, strength, and a sense of belonging to a community that shares their beliefs and values [42, 43]. Religion provides not only a source of hope and comfort but also a framework for dealing with the stress and anxiety associated with a congenital diagnosis, helping mothers find meaning and purpose in their maternal care journey [42].

From this perspective, providing care that respects and responds to the user’s preferences and integral needs is essential. Feeling supported and considered in an environment free of judgment and with a safe space for questioning and discussing their concerns helps women feel safe in understanding and making the best decisions about their child’s health [2]. Health professionals should then consider the cultural aspects that may influence the social experiences of women beyond the moment of the child’s hospitalization, seeking to identify key people for the best development of the baby [13].

Social support proved to be a fundamental aspect of our results, especially in the experience of women during the occurrence of syphilis infection and vertical transmission of the disease. Family and partner support, described by Winnicott as part of the primary maternity support network [27], was important for the women in this study. Such support, when present, could limit negative experiences and increase the feeling of acceptance of these women, especially during the period of diagnosis of congenital syphilis [35]. During the perinatal period, partners are crucial in supporting women as they build new family roles and bonds [9]. This study revealed that some women may choose not to disclose a diagnosis of congenital syphilis due to fear of losing this support.

Syphilis can present the symbolic meaning of promiscuity, causing women to relate the infection to something shameful [34]. In societies marked by the asymmetry of gender relations, mothers may hide the fact that their child has congenital syphilis due to a fear of facing stigmatization and prejudice. This is a way for them to protect themselves from potential judgments and insults [8, 35]. The option of omitting information about the diagnosis of the child from the family and people of the social network may occur by the initiative of the woman or by common agreement with their sexual partners [34], as evidenced in the study. This fact indicates the challenges of having open dialogs, fear of misinterpretation, and the burden of guilt on women for infection [8, 9].

In this sense, women feel uncomfortable talking to their partners, leading to concealments and silences that weaken them [8, 9]. However, the omission of the disease may provide a limited support network at a sensitive moment of motherhood construction [33]. Pregnant women diagnosed with syphilis need counseling, qualified listening, and emotional support to reveal their diagnosis to their partner and family [9, 44]. Our findings align with evidence from previous studies, which show that negotiating partner treatment remains a significant challenge for many women. Gender asymmetries, fear of conflict, financial dependence, and stigma associated with sexually transmitted infections can limit women’s ability to insist on partner testing or treatment [9]. These relational barriers contribute directly to inadequate maternal treatment and increase the risk of vertical transmission, reinforcing the need for strategies that actively involve partners and promote shared responsibility during antenatal care [45].

At the same time, the support network played a key role in helping women cope with congenital syphilis, providing them with emotional, practical, and informative support. When mothers receive internal support, mainly from their partners, or external support networks, which are formed by extended family, friends, and other groups, such as religious and health teams, they can resist the stress, stigma, and anxiety associated with the disease and are provided with important resources for the development of maternity [27, 44, 45]. Even if limited to a few people, as highlighted in our study, network support can strengthen the bond between mother and baby, promoting an environment of care and love essential for the child’s physical and emotional well-being [46, 47]. Ultimately, the presence of a solid support network can address and overcome the challenges of treating congenital syphilis, ensuring a smoother and more positive path for mothers and their children.

The narratives in our study highlight a fundamental public health issue: preventing congenital syphilis depends strongly on consistent antenatal attendance, early diagnosis, correct maternal treatment, and crucially partner testing and treatment. Strengthening health education during prenatal care is essential so that women and their partners understand the risk of vertical transmission, the importance of completing the therapeutic regimen, and the consequences of inadequate treatment [6, 48]. While broader socioeconomic vulnerabilities also influence access and adherence, improving the quality and continuity of antenatal care remains one of the most effective and feasible strategies for preventing congenital syphilis and avoiding neonatal hospitalization [49].

Our study contributes to a greater understanding of the challenges faced by mothers affected by STI, particularly about the importance of support networks. These networks are crucial for delivering comprehensive healthcare that prioritizes the well-being of patients and their families. However, its limitations are related to the difficulty of women talking about syphilis, highlighting that stigma and shame are still associated with these conditions. Information regarding the content and quality of prenatal counseling about vertical transmission of syphilis was not systematically collected. Therefore, it was not possible to examine potential associations between the level of information received during pregnancy, adequacy of maternal treatment, or feelings of guilt. We also acknowledge the possibility of recall bias in mothers of older infants, as we haven’t limited the child’s age at the time of the interview. However, connections created by the researcher, along with conducting interviews in a safe environment, provided elements to overcome these challenges.

Coping with congenital syphilis involves a holistic approach, considering both the information and emotional needs of mothers and families. Our results reinforce the need for health education and the protagonism of health teams in this type of care. Providing clear information and emotional support during prenatal care, actively involving mothers’ support networks, and adopting a family-centered approach from the moment this period begins are fundamental actions to overcome the challenges exposed in this study. In addition, it is crucial to recognize the importance of religious and spiritual influences on women’s lives and to promote a culture of acceptance to combat the stigma associated with the disease. By implementing these strategies, we can improve the experiences of mothers and babies affected by congenital syphilis and promote better long-term health outcomes. Future studies should include larger samples and mixed-methods designs to examine maternal experiences alongside prenatal counseling practices, partner involvement, and their influence on treatment adherence and emotional outcomes.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of congenital syphilis or in-utero exposure to Treponema pallidum is often accompanied by the guilt, stigma, and social isolation experienced by the mother, who may feel ashamed to share her child’s condition with her social circle. The child’s hospitalization is a moment permeated by insecurities, especially due to the lack of information for mothers, who associate the disease with death and an incurable condition. Separation from the newborn causes anguish, and invasive procedures during hospitalization cause emotional suffering. A better understanding of the disease, guidance from health teams, the perspective of a cure, and the support of a social network are fundamental to the process.

Women whose children are diagnosed with syphilis exposure or congenital infection require continuous professional support, which must extend to their sexual partners, families, and broader support networks. Despite the difficulties in addressing syphilis due to a still-present social stigma, this study contributed to filling a scientific gap, highlighting the importance of the support network in the journey of coping with syphilis exposure or congenital infection and pointing to the need for more sensitive and inclusive approaches in the context of maternal and child health.
