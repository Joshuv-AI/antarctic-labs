Ambient Air Pollution, Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons, and Pubertal Development: A Critical Review of Emerging Evidence
Authors: Alicia K. Peterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewPuberty is a sensitive period of endocrine development with lifelong implications for cardiometabolic and reproductive health. This review evaluates evidence linking ambient air pollution and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) to pubertal development.Recent FindingsStudies suggest that associations between air pollution and pubertal timing depend on pollutant composition, exposure timing, and exposure intensity. Component-level analyses of PM2.5 implicate organic matter (OM) and sulfate fractions in associations with earlier menarche and precocious puberty, with OM enriched in combustion-derived PAHs. In contrast, high chronic PM2.5 exposure has also been associated with delayed menarche in heavily polluted settings. Biomarker studies further support biological plausibility, associating PAH exposure to earlier breast development and disruption of hypothalamic kisspeptin–GnRH signaling. Summary Evidence suggests that air pollution may alter pubertal timing through endocrine-disrupting mechanisms, with PAHs representing a plausible mechanistic pathway. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal cohorts, pollutant mixture analyses, and male populations.

Introduction

Puberty is a critical developmental window during which the neuroendocrine system undergoes extensive reorganization, and departures from typical timing can shape health trajectories throughout life [1]. Accumulating evidence suggests that early-life exposures influence pubertal development through neuroendocrine, metabolic, and epigenetic pathways [2]. In girls, earlier pubertal onset has been associated with increased risks of breast and endometrial cancer, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, depression, and earlier initiation of high-risk behaviors [3–7]. Conversely, later pubertal timing has been associated with lower bone mineral density and adverse psychosocial outcomes [8, 9]. Pubertal timing in girls has shifted markedly over recent decades, with the onset of breast development occurring approximately three months earlier per decade since the late 1970s [10], while the average age at menarche has declined by roughly 2–3 years over the past century [11]. Similar trends have been observed in boys [12]. These secular changes have occurred too rapidly to be explained by genetic drift alone, and environmental factors including nutritional, psychosocial, and chemical exposures are increasingly recognized as contributors [13–16].

Among environmental chemical exposures, air pollution has emerged as an important area of investigation. Nearly the entire global population breathes air that exceeds the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for fine particulate matter (PM) [17]. Combustion-related air pollutants, including PM 2.5 micrometers or smaller in diameter (PM2.5), PM 10 micrometers or smaller in diameter (PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), may influence endocrine function through effects on steroid hormone synthesis, receptor signaling, inflammation, and oxidative stress [18, 19]. Epidemiologic studies have increasingly linked early-life air pollution exposure with a range of pediatric endocrine and metabolic outcomes, supporting the biologic plausibility that air pollutants may also influence pubertal development [20].

Because puberty is regulated through tightly coordinated neuroendocrine signaling pathways, environmental exposures may exert different effects depending on the developmental stage at which they occur. One particular sensitive period is mini-puberty, a transient activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis occurring during the first months of life [21]. Supporting this developmental framework [22], a recent multicenter U.S. cohort study reported associations between prenatal and early postnatal exposure to PM2.5 and NO2 and altered androgen-sensitive reproductive markers, including anogenital distance and penile width [23]. Similarly, a Belgian birth cohort demonstrated that prenatal exposure to PM2.5, NO2, and black carbon was associated with altered androgenic steroid precursor concentrations in cord blood at birth [24]. These findings suggest that air pollution may disrupt endocrine signaling at both hormonal and morphologic levels during critical windows of fetal and early postnatal development. Beyond early development, air pollution has been associated with disruption across multiple reproductive health endpoints spanning the female lifespan, including reduced ovarian reserve, menstrual cycle irregularity, and earlier menopause, suggesting that endocrine-disrupting effects of combustion-related pollutants are not limited to any single reproductive transition [25–28]. Pubertal timing represents a particularly sensitive and measurable endpoint within this continuum, with implications for downstream reproductive and cardiometabolic health that make it an important focus of epidemiologic investigation.

Despite growing interest in this topic, epidemiologic findings examining air pollution and pubertal development in adolescence have remained inconsistent. Substantial heterogeneity exists in exposure assessment methods, exposure levels and timing windows, study populations, and pubertal outcomes examined, and most studies to date have been conducted in Asian populations, limiting generalizability across environmental and population contexts. A recent systematic review by Lee et al. [29] identified only six eligible studies published through August 2023 and concluded that the evidence remained inconclusive because of methodological heterogeneity and inconsistent outcome definitions. Since that review, however, several large epidemiologic studies have substantially advanced the field. In addition, prior reviews have not integrated the PAH biomarker literature, limiting mechanistic interpretation of broader ambient air pollution findings. The present review addresses these gaps by (1) incorporating studies published since August 2023, (2) integrating PAH biomarker studies as a mechanistic complement to ambient air pollution data, (3) synthesizing emerging component-level PM2.5 analyses that identify potentially relevant chemical elements, (4) incorporating evidence from South Asia and other underrepresented settings and (5) synthesizing evidence on critical windows of exposure during which air pollution may exert differential effects on pubertal development. Together, these developments provide increasingly consistent epidemiologic and mechanistic evidence linking air pollution exposure to altered pubertal development. This review synthesizes epidemiological studies published through March 2026, identified through searches of PubMed and Web of Science using terms related to air pollution, particulate matter, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, puberty, pubertal development, and menarche, and supplemented by studies included in the systematic review by Lee et al. [29]

Although findings across epidemiologic studies have not always been consistent, several recurring themes have emerged that help explain heterogeneity across the literature. This review examines how the direction and magnitude of reported associations vary according to the developmental window of exposure, pollutant composition, exposure intensity, and biological sex, and discusses the mechanistic pathways that may underlie these differences. Together, these themes provide a useful framework for interpreting the epidemiologic literature and understanding why the direction and magnitude of reported associations differ across studies.

Epidemiological Evidence: Ambient Air Pollution and Pubertal Timing

Foundational Evidence and Unresolved Questions

Lee et al. [29] identified six epidemiological studies examining associations between ambient air pollution and pubertal timing, with inconsistent findings across study populations, exposure metrics, and outcomes [30–35]. The predominant finding, from cohorts in South Korea [31], Poland [33], and China [34] is that greater childhood exposure to PM10 or PM2.5 is associated with earlier menarche or central precocious puberty in girls. However, effect sizes varied substantially across studies, from modest associations in individual-level analyses with address-specific exposure estimates to considerably larger estimates in studies using zone-classified categorical exposure, likely reflecting differences in exposure assessment precision and ambient concentration ranges across settings. In Hong Kong’s ‘Children of 1997’ birth cohort higher prenatal and infant PM10 exposure was associated with delayed rather than earlier pubertal development [30], and in Germany’s GINIplus and LISA cohorts, where mean PM10 was approximately 22 µg/m³ (roughly one-third of levels in the East Asian studies) no associations were detected [35]. These divergent findings suggest that the timing of exposure relative to developmental windows and the ambient concentration range under study are important sources of heterogeneity across the literature. A U.S. cohort using residential traffic proximity as an exposure proxy reported earlier adrenarche (pubic hair development, reflecting adrenal androgen production) but not thelarche (breast development, reflecting gonadal estrogen production) [32], raising the possibility that specific pollutant fractions within the traffic-related mixture act selectively on adrenal rather than gonadal maturation pathways. Collectively, these findings highlight exposure heterogeneity as a key challenge for the field and underscore the need for component-level analyses and attention to critical exposure windows.

New Evidence: Critical Windows, PM2.5 Components, and Geographic Context

Building on the foundational literature, several large studies published since August 2023 have extended prior findings through larger cohorts, component-level analyses of PM composition, and new geographic settings (Table 1). Hood et al. [36] examined PM exposure and menarche timing in 5,201 girls from the Growing Up Today Study 2 (GUTS2), the largest prospective U.S. study to address this question to date. Exposure to PM2.5, PM2.5–10, and PM10 was estimated using nationwide spatiotemporal models linked to maternal residential addresses updated every two years, allowing for time-varying exposure assessment across multiple windows. Higher PM2.5 exposure was associated with earlier menarche across all exposure windows examined, including in utero (Hazard Ratio [HR] 1.03; 95% CI: 1.00–1.06), one year prior to menarche (HR 1.06; 95% CI: 1.02–1.10), and cumulative from birth (HR 1.06; 95% CI: 1.02–1.10), per interquartile range increase of 4 µg/m³ in PM2.5. PM10 associations were similar but attenuated, while the coarse fraction PM2.5–10 was not consistently associated. Short-term fluctuations in PM2.5 above a girl’s cumulative average were not independently associated with menarche timing after adjustment for cumulative exposure, suggesting that chronic rather than acute exposure drives the association. Notably, median cumulative PM2.5 in this cohort was 14.2 µg/m³, substantially lower than in the East Asian studies reporting positive associations, indicating that effects on pubertal timing are detectable even at moderate ambient concentrations. Component-level analyses have begun to identify which PM2.5 constituents are driving these associations. Li et al. [37] conducted the first component-level analysis of PM2.5 and menarche timing, examining 855 girls from the longitudinal China Health and Nutrition Survey using weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression to decompose the pollutant mixture and estimate the relative contribution of each component. Joint exposure to PM2.5 and its components was associated with 11% higher odds of early menarche defined as < 12 years of age (Odds Ratio [OR] 1.11; 95% CI: 1.01–1.20), with sulfate carrying the largest mixture weight (0.495), followed by ammonium (0.307) and nitrate (0.081); organic matter and black carbon were not significant after covariate adjustment. Zhou et al. [38] extended component-level analyses to precocious puberty in the largest sample examined to date, drawing on the PRODY multicenter cross-sectional survey of 34,105 children aged 6 to 9 years across 30 cities in China. Total PM2.5 mass was significantly associated with precocious puberty in girls (OR 2.12; 95% CI: 1.27–3.55 per interquartile range increase) but not boys. In WQS mixture analysis, organic matter carried the dominant weight (0.71), followed by black carbon (0.12) and nitrate (0.10); sulfate and ammonium contributed minimally, a notably different component profile from Li et al. [37], likely reflecting differences in pollution source composition across study regions. The prominence of organic matter, produced primarily by incomplete combustion of solid fuels and enriched in PAHs [39], provides a direct chemical link between ambient combustion-derived air pollution and the endocrine-disrupting mechanisms discussed in the following section. Oh et al. [40] did not look at components but examined over 1.2 million children in a nationwide South Korean retrospective cohort, identifying precocious puberty through a clinically stringent outcome definition requiring both International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic coding and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist treatment. Long-term PM2.5, PM10, sulfur dioxide (SO2), and Ozone (O3) exposure were each positively associated with precocious puberty onset in girls (48-month HR range: 1.006–1.037) but not boys, with findings robust to two-pollutant models and sensitivity analyses. NO2 was not significant across any exposure window or sex.

Perhaps the most striking new contribution comes from India. Chattopadhyay et al. [41] linked the 2019–2021 National Family Health Survey (n = 179,972 women aged 15–24 for menarche analysis) with satellite-derived PM2.5 data at 1 km resolution from 29,065 monitoring sites. Exposure to PM2.5 above the Indian national standard of 40 µg/m³ was associated with higher odds of late menarche defined as ≥ 15 years (OR 1.24; 95% CI: 1.17–1.32), and with earlier menopause defined as ≤ 45 years (HR 1.23; 95% CI: 1.17–1.30), a dual finding that frames air pollution as a compressor of the entire female reproductive lifespan. The delayed menarche finding, seemingly at odds with the acceleration reported in East Asian and North American studies, is internally coherent: India’s northern belt records mean PM2.5 concentrations exceeding 50 µg/m³, the highest sustained ambient levels in any identified study to date. Very high chronic exposures may suppress HPG-axis activation rather than stimulate it, potentially through mechanisms including oxidative damage to the hypothalamus, disruption of kisspeptin tone, or acceleration of ovarian follicular atresia, though direct evidence linking ambient PM2.5 at extreme concentrations to these specific pathways in humans remains limited [42].

Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons: A Mechanistic Bridge

PAH Biomarkers and Pubertal Development Studies

PAHs are generated through the incomplete combustion of organic matter and are ubiquitous environmental pollutants found in ambient air pollution, vehicle exhaust, tobacco smoke, and indoor air in settings that use biomass fuels [43, 44]. Following exposure, PAHs undergo metabolic activation to reactive intermediates that can be quantified indirectly through urinary metabolites, which primarily reflect recent exposure (hours to days), and through protein or DNA adducts, which reflect cumulative biologically effective dose over longer periods. Because these biomarker classes capture distinct exposure windows and different aspects of PAH metabolism and toxicokinetics, distinguishing between them is important when interpreting associations with developmental outcomes such as pubertal development [45, 46].

PAHs are endocrine-active compounds that activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a ligand-activated transcription factor that regulates xenobiotic metabolism but also interacts with steroid hormone signaling pathways, including estrogen receptor α (ERα), through shared co-regulators and downstream transcriptional networks [47]. The relevance of PAHs to the air pollution–puberty association is further supported by their presence within the organic matter fraction of PM₂.₅, a component that has been implicated in studies of pubertal development [38]. Together, these observations provide a biologically plausible pathway through which combustion-derived air pollution may influence neuroendocrine development during sensitive windows of HPG-axis maturation.

The PAH biomarker and pubertal development literature reflects both potential biological sensitivity and substantial methodological heterogeneity (Table 2). Prenatal PAH exposure studies have reported inconsistent associations with pubertal timing. Kehm et al. [48] studied 196 Black and Hispanic girls from a New York City birth cohort and assessed prenatal PAH exposure using third-trimester personal air monitors worn by their mothers during pregnancy, along with benzo[a]pyrene-DNA adducts measured in umbilical cord blood at delivery. Higher prenatal exposure to eight carcinogenic PAHs (Σ8 PAHs) was associated with later pubertal development, including approximately 11-month later pubertal growth spurt onset and 7-month later menarche among girls with lower birthweight. [48] Higher cord blood benzo[a]pyrene-DNA adduct levels were also associated with delayed breast development, suggesting that prenatal PAH exposure may influence pubertal timing through developmental programming effects. [48] This inverse relationship between prenatal PAH exposure and pubertal timing mirrors the pattern observed with prenatal PM10 exposure in the Hong Kong cohort [26] and is biologically consistent with experimental evidence that PAHs can disrupt endocrine and reproductive developmental pathways during critical developmental windows [49]. In contrast, Malin Igra et al. [50], studying 582 mother-daughter pairs from a prospective birth cohort in rural Bangladesh, measured maternal urinary PAH metabolites during early pregnancy and assessed pubertal development in daughters using clinician assessed Tanner staging and age at menarche. Higher maternal concentrations of phenanthrene metabolites, including 1-hydroxyphenanthrene and summed phenanthrene metabolites, were associated with earlier menarche, although no associations were observed for breast or pubic hair development. These discrepant prenatal findings may reflect differences in PAH composition, exposure intensity, population characteristics, and pubertal endpoints assessed.

Studies evaluating childhood and adolescent PAH exposure have likewise produced heterogeneous findings. John et al. (2022) [51] evaluated urinary PAH metabolites and pubertal development in a prospective cohort of 358 girls from the San Francisco Bay Area participating in the California PAH Study nested within the Epidemiology and Genetics of Adult Cancer from Youth (LEGACY) Girls Cohort. In multivariable-adjusted longitudinal analyses, urinary PAH metabolite concentrations measured in the child were not associated with breast development, pubic hair development, menarche timing, or pubertal tempo overall. However, stratified analyses suggested earlier breast development among overweight girls with higher concentrations of selected PAH metabolites, including 2-hydroxynaphthalene (2-NAP), 1-hydroxyphenanthrene (1-PHEN), summed phenanthrene metabolites (∑PHEN), and summed PAH metabolites (∑PAH), raising the possibility of effect modification by adiposity.

John et al. (2025) [52], studying the same California PAH cohort but using serum PAH-albumin adducts rather than urinary metabolites, found that higher adduct levels were associated with earlier breast development. In contrast, Fang et al. [53], in a longitudinal cohort of 734 girls in Chongqing, China, found that higher urinary concentrations of several PAH metabolites, including 2-hydroxynaphthalene, 2-hydroxyfluorene, and 9-hydroxyphenanthrene, were associated with delayed breast, pubic hair, and axillary hair development.

Taken together, these findings indicate that associations between PAH exposure and pubertal development are influenced by exposure timing, biomarker selection, biospecimen matrix, metabolic variability, and population-level differences in exposure mixtures. Urinary metabolites likely reflect recent exposure, whereas protein adducts measured in blood may capture longer-term integrated dose, which could differentially relate to endocrine-sensitive developmental windows.

Mechanistic Evidence

Experimental studies provide biological plausibility for these associations. In human fetal hypothalamic cell models, benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) has been shown to disrupt GnRH and kisspeptin signaling pathways, as well as impair the migration of GnRH-secreting neurons during fetal neurodevelopment [54]. These effects suggest that PAH exposure may influence early hypothalamic organization and the developmental programming of the HPG-axis.

Mechanistically, these observations align with established AhR-mediated signaling pathways, through which PAHs can modulate endocrine and neurodevelopmental processes relevant to reproductive maturation. However, evidence directly linking AhR-mediated disruption to variation in human pubertal timing remains limited, with most support derived from experimental and animal models rather than longitudinal human studies.

Beyond AhR-mediated PAH signaling, several additional mechanisms have been proposed through which ambient air pollution may influence pubertal timing. PM2.5-induced oxidative stress and systemic inflammation can disrupt steroidogenesis and alter HPG axis signaling, consistent with evidence linking particulate exposure to reduced estradiol levels and accelerated hormonal decline during the menopausal transition [42, 55]. Epigenetic modification of puberty-regulating genes represents another plausible pathway, as both PM2.5 and PAH exposure have been proposed to alter DNA methylation patterns at neuroendocrine loci relevant to HPG axis activation [56, 57]. Finally, ambient air pollution exposure has been associated with increased adiposity in children [58], and adiposity is itself a well-established upstream determinant of pubertal timing [59], suggesting an indirect pathway through which air pollution may influence pubertal onset. Taken together, these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and likely operate in combination, with their relative contributions potentially varying by developmental window, exposure level, and pollutant composition.

Critical Appraisal and Synthesis

The epidemiologic evidence reviewed above converges around several recurring themes that help explain the apparent heterogeneity across studies. Associations between air pollution and pubertal timing are not uniform but vary systematically according to the developmental window of exposure, pollutant composition, exposure intensity, and biological sex. Mechanistic evidence further suggests multiple interacting endocrine pathways through which these effects may occur. Rather than reflecting contradictory findings, the current literature increasingly supports a model in which pubertal timing is an exposure-sensitive neuroendocrine outcome whose directionality depends on the stage of HPG-axis maturation at the time of exposure. Postnatal exposure to moderate-to-high levels of PM2.5 and PM10 is more frequently associated with earlier pubertal onset in studies from Asia and Europe, whereas prenatal exposure, very high chronic exposure settings, and PAH-specific prenatal biomarker studies more often report delayed development. This divergence is consistent with stage-dependent vulnerability in neuroendocrine developmental programming rather than a single uniform endocrine-disrupting mechanism.

Within this framework, heterogeneity across studies may also reflect differences in pollutant mixture composition. PM2.5 is a chemically heterogeneous exposure, and component-level analyses increasingly suggest that combustion-derived fractions are most biologically relevant for pubertal outcomes [37, 38]. Organic matter, which is enriched in PAHs, plausibly acts through receptor-mediated endocrine disruption pathways [54, 60], while sulfate (representing secondary inorganic aerosol formation from SO₂ emissions) may function as a marker of broader combustion-related pollution mixtures and associated oxidative stress pathways. Importantly, these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may operate concurrently depending on exposure context and developmental timing.

A second axis of heterogeneity relates to biological sex and outcome specificity. The current evidence base remains heavily weighted toward female pubertal outcomes, limiting inference regarding male HPG-axis maturation. This represents a meaningful gap, as adrenal and gonadal axes may differ in temporal sensitivity and endocrine responsiveness to environmental perturbations, potentially resulting in sex-specific effects that are not yet adequately characterized in epidemiologic studies.

Methodological differences across studies further contribute to observed variability, particularly in exposure assessment and biomarker selection. Divergent findings between urinary PAH metabolites and protein adduct measures likely reflect differences in exposure time scale and biological integration windows, which may not align with critical periods of neuroendocrine susceptibility. More broadly, heterogeneity in exposure modeling, outcome definitions, and timing of assessment complicates direct comparison across cohorts and limits formal synthesis. Covariate adjustment strategies also vary substantially across studies, ranging from minimal demographic adjustment (age, sex, region) to more comprehensive models incorporating socioeconomic status, adiposity, lifestyle factors, and perinatal characteristics.

Evidence from ecological studies provides additional but lower-tier support for geographic variability in associations. An ecological analysis from northern Spain reported higher rates of precocious puberty in areas with greater ambient air pollution [61]. However, this evidence is limited by its ecological design, small case numbers, and absence of individual-level confounder control, precluding causal inference. Accordingly, it was not included in the primary synthesis, though it may still suggest potential spatial heterogeneity in underrepresented European populations rather than provide reliable effect estimates.

Taken together, the current evidence base supports a conceptual shift in how air pollution is understood in relation to pubertal development. Rather than acting as a uniformly directional endocrine disruptor, air pollution appears to function as a context-dependent mixture exposure whose effects are shaped by developmental timing, chemical composition, and underlying neuroendocrine maturation stage. This framework helps reconcile previously inconsistent findings and provides a biologically coherent structure for future epidemiologic and mechanistic research.

Conclusions

The field of air pollution and pubertal development has progressed from an inconclusive evidence base at the time of the 2024 review by Lee et al. [29] toward a more mechanistically informed literature incorporating epidemiological, exposure-mixture, and chemical-speciation evidence.

Collectively, current evidence suggests that associations between air pollution and pubertal timing are context-dependent, varying by developmental window, exposure intensity, and pollutant composition rather than following a uniform directional pattern. Combustion-related PM2.5 components, particularly organic matter and sulfate fractions, appear most consistently associated with altered pubertal outcomes.

Despite these advances, important uncertainties remain. Evidence is limited in males, and heterogeneity in exposure assessment and pubertal outcome definition continues to constrain comparability across studies. In addition, potential non-linear exposure–response relationships at very high ambient concentrations remain insufficiently characterized. Future research should also examine air pollution effects across the full female reproductive lifespan, from fetal steroidogenesis and mini puberty through pubertal onset, menstrual function, ovarian reserve, and menopause, rather than treating these transitions as isolated endpoints. The emerging evidence that high chronic PM2.5 exposure is associated with both delayed menarche and earlier menopause in the same population [41] suggests that combustion-related pollutants may compress the reproductive lifespan as a whole, a hypothesis that warrants dedicated longitudinal investigation.

From a public health perspective, reducing emissions from traffic and coal combustion remains a priority given their contribution to key PM2.5 components implicated in these associations. However, translation into exposure-relevant thresholds will require longitudinal studies with repeated exposure measurement, standardized clinical outcomes, and improved representation of high-exposure populations across diverse geographic settings. Overall, the literature indicates that the key question is not whether air pollution influences pubertal timing, but under what conditions, through which mixtures, and during which developmental windows these effects are most pronounced.
