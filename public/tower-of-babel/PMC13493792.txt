Complete mitochondrial genome sequence of Koelreuteria paniculata (Sapindaceae) and comparative analysis within the family Sapindaceae
Authors: Young-Ho Ha, Jei-Wan Lee, Ji-Young Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKoelreuteria paniculata (Sapindaceae) is an economically and culturally important species with ornamental traits, durable wood, and medicinal use. Plant mitochondrial (MT) genomes exhibit complex structures and variations in size. Thus, they provide valuable insights into species identification and evolution. However, the K. paniculat MT genome remains largely understudied. Herein, we report the first complete MT genomic sequence of K. paniculata. The complete circular genome contains 58 unique genes: 32 protein-coding, four ribosomal RNA, and 22 transfer RNA genes. The transfer of a 2,047-bp chloroplast-derived fragment containing rrn16 and trnV-GAC into the MT genome was verified. Additionally, we identified 197 simple sequence repeats and 352 dispersed repeats; the longest repeats spanned 2,084 bp. Codon usage favored A/U-ending codons, a conserved feature in terrestrial plants. Moreover, 468 C-to-U RNA-editing sites were predicted across protein-coding genes (PCGs); nad4 was the most affected. For 25 shared PCGs among Sapindaceae species, the mean Ka/Ks ratio (0.64) indicated purifying selection. Meanwhile, atp8, matR, and nad2 showed positive selection, implying adaptive evolution. Phylogenetic analysis of 28 conserved PCGs placed K. paniculata within Sapindaceae. Dynamic MT rearrangements were observed within this family, elucidating the genome architecture and evolutionary dynamics of K. paniculata. Introduction

Koelreuteria Laxm., belonging to the family Sapindaceae, is highly valued for its ornamental appeal, particularly its attractive yellow flowers that bloom during the summer. Historically, up to seven species have been recognized within this genus1,2. However, the current taxonomy includes only three species3. Koelreuteria paniculata Laxm. is known for its horticultural importance and hard-textured seeds. Its seeds are traditionally used to craft prayer beads, reflecting their cultural and religious significance in East Asia4,5. The wood of K. paniculata is valuable for furniture and interior finishing, whereas its leaves and flowers are used as sources of dyes6,7. Furthermore, this species has considerable economic and ecological importance, including its application in traditional medicine8. K. paniculata is native to China. It was cultivated worldwide in the mid-18th century and has since spread throughout Europe9. Although considered an introduced species in South Korea, the presence of a large population (over 600 individuals) in Wando (Daemun-ri) suggests a historical distribution on the Korean Peninsula10.

Mitochondria (MT) are essential organelles found in eukaryotic cells. They supply energy and play pivotal roles in intracellular compound production, as well as in cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis11,12. Plant MT genomes show remarkable structural variation and complexity compared to animal and fungal MT genomes13. In angiosperms, MT genome sizes vary from approximately 66 kilobases (kb) to more than 11.3 megabases (Mb). This variation is attributed to the accumulation of repetitive elements, insertion of foreign DNA, and expansion or contraction of noncoding regions14–17. Unlike the relatively simple circular genomes of animals, plant MT genomes display highly dynamic architectures; they often comprise multi-circular, linear, or branched multi-genomic structures with a master circle13,18. Frequent homologous recombination events driven by repetitive sequences contribute to the expansion of genomic diversity19,20. Overall, the abundance of these repeats promotes intra- and inter-molecular recombination, thereby generating subgenomic isomers and multi-chromosomal structures21. Owing to this structural complexity, the sequencing and assembly of plant MT genomes are considerably more demanding than those of animal MT genomes or chloroplast (CP) genomes. Despite the relatively stable number of functional genes across species, intracellular gene transfer (IGT) occurs frequently, wherein CP -derived DNA fragments are incorporated into the MT genome22,23. Therefore, the analysis of MT genomes provides essential insights into evolutionary dynamics, structural diversity, and the mechanisms underlying genome stability and rearrangement in plants.

Despite the economic and cultural importance of K. paniculata, previous studies have primarily focused on its seed germination and dormancy24,25, tolerance to environmental stress, and phytoremediation potential26,27. In the present study, we report the complete MT genomic sequence of the genus Koelreuteria. We sought to evaluate gene composition and organization, codon usage bias, distribution of repetitive motifs, CP-derived sequence [via MT plastid DNA transfer (MTPT)] RNA editing, and gene transfer based on the assembled MT genome. Additionally, we aimed to characterize the structural organization and evolutionary patterns of the genome. Overall, our findings provide an MT genome resource for Koelreuteria, offering valuable insights into its genomic characteristics and establishing a foundation for future studies on genetic diversity and resource conservation.

Results

Characteristics of the K. paniculata MT genome

In this study, the MT genome of K. paniculata was recovered as a single circular configuration using the POLAP-based assembly pipeline, with a total length of 610,364 bp (Fig. 1). This recovered assembly was used as the representative MT genome for subsequent analyses. Considering the structural plasticity of plant MT genomes, this result does not exclude the possibility of alternative conformations or heteroplasmy. Additionally, the mean coverage of the long-read sequencing data for the complete MT genome was 327.7×.

Genes transcribed clockwise and counterclockwise are shown on the outer and inner sides of the circle, respectively. The inner, gray-shaded circle indicates the guanine–cytosine (GC) content.

The MT genome of K. paniculata contains 58 unique genes: 32 protein-coding (PCGs), 22 transfer RNA (tRNA), and four ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (Table 1). Three of the tRNA genes were identified as trnN-GUU, and two copies each were identified as trnC-GCA and trnP-UGG. This finding indicates the presence of several copies of tRNAs sharing the same anticodon, although they do not necessarily have identical sequences. Introns were detected within several genes, including ccmFc, cox2, and rps3, each containing a single intron. All genes of the NADH dehydrogenase complex (NAD), except nad3 and nad9, harbor introns, with numbers varying from three to four. Three of these NAD genes (nad1, nad2, and nad5) are trans-spliced, whereas nad4 and nad7 are cis-spliced.

A marked feature is the presence of a region within the K. paniculata MT genome that is presumed to originate from the CP; this region includes rrn16 and trnV-GAC. The transferred region is 2,047-bp long in the MT genome. A comparison of the MT rrn16 sequence with its homolog from the publicly available K. paniculata CP genome in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database revealed minor insertion/deletion (indel) events (Supplementary Fig. S1). To validate the gene transfer event and eliminate the possibility of assembly errors due to sequence similarity, we investigated the origin of reads spanning the rrn16 - trnV-GAC region. Raw long-read sequencing data were mapped using rrn16 as a reference. A total of 33,953 reads were mapped. Of them, 27,642 reads fully covered the region transferred from rrn16 to trnV-GAC. Among the total reads, 84% (23,364) aligned to the CP genome, whereas 8% (2,230) corresponded to MT sequences. The remaining 8% (2,048 reads) were excluded due to quality issues. The presence of a distinct set of reads mapped directly to the MT context confirmed that this was a true transfer event rather than an artifact.

To further screen for CP-derived sequences in the MT genome, the CP genome with one removed inverted repeat (IR) copy was queried against the MT genome using BLASTn. This analysis revealed two major CP–MT homologous regions of 2,293 and 2,060 bp, with sequence identities of 96.86% and 99.13%, respectively. In addition, a shorter homologous region of 647 bp with 97.53% identity was detected, whereas the remaining hits were shorter or showed lower sequence identity (Supplementary Table S2).

Simple sequence repeat analysis

In total, 197 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified in the K. paniculata MT genome (Table 2). Mononucleotide repeats were the most common, with 59 instances. Importantly, the lengths of these mononucleotide SSRs ranged from 10 to 16 bp (poly-A). The second most abundant type was tetranucleotide repeats, with 45 identified occurrences. Moreover, 38 trinucleotide and 35 dinucleotide repeats were observed, with trinucleotide repeats varying from 3 to 7 bp in length. Furthermore, 15 pentanucleotide and 5 hexanucleotide repeats were identified.

The following dispersed repeats within both CP and MT genomes were examined: forward, complementary, reverse, and palindromic repeats. We identified 325 dispersed repeats consisting exclusively of forward and palindromic repeats (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S1). Importantly, the genome lacks reverse and complementary repeats. In total, 169 forward and 156 palindromic repeats were identified. The longest forward repeat is 2,084-bp long and is located within the MT genome at two positions: 174,764–176,848 bp and 584,237–586,321 bp (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, the longest palindromic repeat detected between the CP and MT genomes was a 1,369-bp shared homologous repeat-like sequence. Repeat sequence connections between the CP and MT genomes are illustrated using a Circos plot in Fig. 2. Furthermore, a concentrated distribution of repetitive sequences was observed within the region containing rrn16 (located between 100 and 110 kb in the CP genome); this was presumed to be a CP-derived sequence (via MTPT). This 10-kb segment contains 24 forward and 6 palindromic repeats, representing the highest density of repeats among all analyzed 10-kb intervals.

Codon usage and RNA-editing analysis

We further examined the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values of all MT-derived PCGs in K. paniculata to assess potential codon usage bias (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S3). Analysis of the 61 sense codons indicated a pronounced preference for codons ending with adenine (A) or uracil (U) (thymine [T] in DNA)—a feature frequently detected in the MT genomes of terrestrial plants. Among the 61 codons, 30 are preferentially used (RSCU > 1). Of these, 28 (93.3%) are terminated with A or U. The exceptions are ACC (Thr) and TTG (Leu). Specifically, the codon GCT (Ala) exhibited the highest RSCU value (1.586), whereas TAC (Tyr) exhibited the lowest value (0.473). Additionally, the initiation codon ATG (Met) and codon TGG (Trp) were used without bias, each having an RSCU value of 1.0. This RSCU value was expected because ATG and TGG are the sole codons for their respective amino acids. Analysis of the amino acid composition showed that phenylalanine (Phe) and isoleucine (Ile) are the most frequently encoded amino acids, whereas cysteine (Cys) is least frequently encoded. Among the three termination codons, TAA and TGA were most frequently used (44.1% each) and TAG was the least used (11.8%).

In the K. paniculata MT genome, 446 putative RNA-editing sites were identified among 34 PCGs (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S3). All identified RNA-editing events involved C-to-U conversion, the typical and most widespread form of RNA editing in plant MT genomes. Importantly, this post-transcriptional modification often restores conserved amino acid residues, thereby maintaining the functionality of the encoded proteins. RNA-editing sites were unevenly distributed across genes. Specifically, nad4 contains 35 editing sites, the highest among all genes. No RNA-editing sites were identified in nad3, rps14, or atp9, indicating that these genes are either less reliant on post-transcriptional editing or have evolved to maintain functionality without it.

Selective pressure analysis

To investigate selective pressure on the MT-derived PCGs of K. paniculata, we performed a comparative analysis using the MT genomes of seven closely related Sapindaceae species (Xanthoceras sorbifolium, Acer truncatum, A. miaotaiense, A. yangbiense, Sapindus mukorossi, Nephelium lappaceum, and Litchi chinensis). Additionally, the ratio of non-synonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates was estimated for 25 PCGs shared among the species (Fig. 5). The ratio of Ka to Ks substitutions is widely used to infer selective forces acting on PCGs. A Ka/Ks value > 1 denotes adaptive evolution, a value of approximately 1 suggests neutral evolution, and that < 1 indicates purifying selection. In K. paniculata, the average Ka/Ks value calculated across the 25 genes was 0.68, indicating that most genes have evolved under strong purification constraints (Supplementary Table S5). Specifically, 21 genes (accounting for 84% of the total) exhibited Ka/Ks ratios < 1, implying that they are functionally conserved and subject to selective constraints that eliminate deleterious mutations. This pattern of conserved evolution is consistent with the observations for most MT genes. In contrast, the remaining three genes—atp8 (Ka/Ks = 3.35), matR (Ka/Ks = 3.31), and nad2 (Ka/Ks = 3.93)—showed extremely high values (Ka/Ks > 1), indicating that these genes likely evolved under a positive selection pressure.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses of sapindaceae species

To clarify the phylogenetic relationship of K. paniculata in the Sapindaceae family, we constructed concatenated sequences of 28 conserved PCGs from 10 species, including 2 outgroup species (Hibiscus cannabinus and Hopea hainanensis) (Fig. 6). Details of the phylogenetic dataset, including the conserved genes recovered for each species and the alignment length of each gene, are provided in Supplementary Table S6. In the phylogenetic tree, all Sapindaceae species formed monophyletic groups (bootstrap support [BS] = 100%). K. paniculata formed a sister group with S. mukorossi, L. chinensis, and N. lappaceum, which constituted a well-supported subclade (BS = 100%). Similarly, the three analyzed Acer species (A. truncatum, A. miaotaiense, and A. yangbiense) clustered to form distinct, strongly supported monophyletic groups (BS = 100%). In contrast, X. sorbifolium was the most basal lineage within Sapindaceae.

The ML tree (left) was constructed based on concatenated MT protein-coding genes, with bootstrap support values shown at each node. The synteny relationships (right) were visualized using ntSynt-viz, with gray bars representing MT genomes and red lines indicating conserved syntenic blocks among species.

To identify structural variations in the MT genome, we performed a synteny analysis comparing the MT genome of K. paniculata with that of nine related species within the family Sapindaceae. The analysis revealed extensive structural rearrangements among the species—a pattern widely observed in plant MT genomes known for their structural fluidity and frequent recombination events. Moreover, a complex intertwined ribbon pattern was observed even among three closely related species within the same genus (A. truncatum, A. miaotaiense, and A. yangbiense). This indicated the frequent occurrence of extensive rearrangement events even between the closest relatives, highlighting the rapid structural evolution of plant MT genomes. In contrast, the three taxa phylogenetically closest to K. paniculata—Litchi, Nephelium, and Sapindus—were linked by relatively thick and long single ribbons, indicating that large syntenic blocks are well conserved among these lineages, with fewer genomic rearrangements than in other lineages. However, the number of homologous collinear blocks in these three genera was similar to that in K. paniculata, which is located in a more ancestral position in their clade. Contrastingly, their lengths were notably shorter in K. paniculata than in these genera. This genomic fragmentation pattern in K. paniculata suggests that the evolutionary conservation of the MT genome structure does not always directly correlate with phylogenetic proximity. Collectively, these results demonstrate the high structural dynamism of MT genomes and reveal that species within the Sapindaceae family have evolved through heterogeneous and diverse rearrangement patterns.

Discussion

Herein, we report the first complete MT genomic sequence of K. paniculata. We characterized the genomic features of this sequence based on a 610,364-bp circular-mapping assembly. Our analyses encompassed PCGs, repetitive sequence patterns, RNA-editing events, codon usage, and CP-derived sequences (MTPTs). These features collectively provide a comprehensive genomic framework for understanding the evolutionary divergence of the genus Koelreuteria within the Sapindaceae family. The structural polymorphism of plant MT genomes—characterized by master circles as well as linear, multi-linear, and branched architectures—presents a significant challenge for assembly and analysis28,29. Despite this complexity, elucidating these structures is essential for understanding plant physiology and evolutionary history. In particular, our findings highlight the contributions of structural variations and sequence integrations to the overall genomic plasticity of this lineage. MT genomic data from diverse species are becoming increasingly available with ongoing advances in sequencing technologies, enabling broader comparative genomic studies19,30. In this context, we examined the phylogenetic position of the genus Koelreuteria. This analysis provides foundational data for investigating the evolutionary dynamics of plant MT genomes. Although our assembly pipeline yielded a single circular configuration, this should be interpreted as a representative assembly model rather than definitive evidence excluding alternative MT conformations.

Structural complexity and evolutionary context of the MT genome of K. paniculata

The MT genome size of K. paniculata (610,364 bp) was within the size range of the species used for comparison in this study (57–81 Mb)31,32. Additionally, the GC content (45.5%) was within the range of 44.4–45.8% observed among the 12 species, including the outgroup species H. hainanensis and H. cannabinus. This stability in genome size and GC content across the Sapindaceae family suggests a relatively conserved core genomic architecture despite lineage-specific variations. Generally, PCGs in MT genomes exhibit a high level of conservation across species, suggesting that gene content remains highly consistent among closely related taxa33,34. However, evolutionary events, such as gene rearrangement, loss, and duplication, can introduce variability, leading to differences in gene numbers or arrangements, even among closely related species. In K. paniculata, these variations may reflect lineage-specific evolutionary divergence in MT genome organization. Additionally, non-coding regions play a major role in structural variations due to the integration of repeated elements and exogenous DNA, particularly in CP-derived sequences35.

In this study, we identified 197 SSRs and 325 dispersed repeats in the K. paniculata MT genome, including a relatively long forward repeat of 2,084 bp. Such repetitive sequences are important substrates for homologous recombination and large-scale genomic rearrangement in plant MT genomes36,37. In particular, the rrn16 region exhibited the highest repeat density (30 repeats per 10-kb interval), suggesting that this region may be associated with localized structural instability around the MTPT site. The extensive structural rearrangements observed among related species within the Sapindaceae family are consistent with this interpretation. Collectively, these findings suggest that the accumulation of repetitive sequences in the K. paniculata MT genome may contribute to its structural fluidity, and to the dynamic genome reorganization associated with plant MT genome evolution38,39. This pattern is consistent with previous studies reporting extensive structural dynamism in angiosperm mitochondrial genomes, further supporting the role of repeat-mediated recombination as a major driver of mitochondrial genome evolution.

Discovery of MTPTs and their evolutionary significance

One of the core findings of this study was the transfer of a CP fragment encompassing rrn16 to the MT genome (Fig. 2). In K. paniculata, the transfer of CP-derived DNA into the MT genome represents a typical case of IGT—a phenomenon widely observed during terrestrial plant evolution40. To validate the occurrence of this event, we identified numerous long reads spanning the entire transfer region, indicating that the detected MTPT is unlikely to be an assembly artifact. Specifically, 2,047 reads (8% of the total reads covering the region) were directly mapped to the MT genome, supporting this integration.

Although transfers of tRNA and PCGs from the CP to the MT genome have been reported in several taxa, including Aria alnifolia and Stemona tuberosa19,20, the transfer of an rRNA gene appears to be relatively uncommon30. This pattern may reflect the strong sequence conservation and functional constraints typically associated with rRNA genes, making the retention of such a sequence in the MT genome particularly noteworthy.

A concentrated distribution of repetitive sequences was observed around rrn16 within the MTPT region (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S1). The co-localization of dense repeats with the integrated rrn16 fragment suggests that this region is associated with localized structural dynamism in the MT genome. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that MTPT-associated regions can facilitate recombination-related structural variation and subsequent sequence divergence41,42. Collectively, these findings further suggest that inter-organellar sequence transfer, including rare rRNA-associated events, contribute to plant MT genome evolution and structural diversity43. These findings suggest that intracellular gene transfer events, including rare rRNA-associated transfers, may contribute to MT genome architecture.

Codon usage bias and gene expression regulation

RSCU is a key parameter for assessing codon usage patterns in organelle genomes and provides insights into species-specific evolutionary characteristics. In the present study, a notable preference for codons ending in A or U was observed in K. paniculata mtDNA, a feature commonly reported in the MT DNA of other plants44,45. Excluding the sole codons for methionine (ATG) and tryptophan (TGG), pronounced differences in codon usage frequencies were observed for individual amino acids. Specifically, the GCT (Ala) codon demonstrated the highest usage frequency, whereas the TAC (Tyr) codon showed the lowest frequency (Supplementary Table S3). Examination of codon usage patterns provides valuable insights into the molecular processes underlying biological adaptation and aids in elucidating evolutionary relationships among species46.

Codon usage bias is primarily shaped by adaptive selection pressures rather than mutation-driven bias, indicating that specific codon usage confers selective advantages related to transcription and translation efficiency47,48. Additionally, the dominance of codons ending in A or T reflects the general AT bias typically observed in the third position of codons within the MT genes of higher plants49. This is an important factor to consider when elucidating gene expression efficiency and designing related genes for enhancing tolerance.

Evidence of adaptive evolution through selection pressure analysis

The Ka/Ks ratio is widely used to evaluate the type and strength of selective pressures acting on PCGs, providing insight into the evolutionary forces shaping genomic divergence50. In this study, 21 of the 25 shared genes exhibited Ka/Ks values < 1, indicating that most of these genes are under purifying selection. This pattern suggests that most MT genes in K. paniculata have been evolutionarily conserved to maintain essential biological functions, such as respiration and energy metabolism, as commonly observed in the MT genomes of land plants51,52.

The Ka/Ks values of three genes—atp8, matR, and nad2—suggested that they may have experienced distinct evolutionary pressures53. Given their functional roles in ATP synthesis, RNA splicing, and respiratory chain complex I, respectively, these genes may be candidates for lineage-specific adaptive divergence or relaxed selective constraint in K. paniculata. Although these patterns do not directly indicate environmental stress, they suggest that components of the MT energy-production and RNA-processing machinery may have followed different evolutionary trajectories within this lineage.

Determination of phylogenetic position and genome structure evolution within Sapindaceae

Phylogenetic relationships reconstructed using 28 conserved MT PCGs placed K. paniculata close to Sapindus, Litchi, and Nephelium, with Xanthoceras occupying a more basal position among the sampled taxa (Fig. 6). This topology is consistent with previously proposed phylogenetic relationships based on nuclear datasets, including Angiosperm35354. The congruence between our mitogenome-based phylogeny and the Angiosperm353 framework suggests that conserved MT PCGs can provide informative phylogenetic signals for resolving relationships within the family Sapindaceae.

Synteny analysis revealed extensive genomic structural rearrangements among the compared taxa. This finding highlights the marked structural fluidity of plant MT genomes and suggests that genome architecture may evolve more dynamically than gene-based phylogenetic relationships. The substantial rearrangements observed even among related species support the theory that the MT genome structure is highly labile during lineage divergence and evolution30. The observed incongruence between genome structure and phylogenetic relationships further indicates that structural evolution in plant mitochondrial genomes may proceed independently of sequence-based evolutionary history. In this context, these results provide a useful framework for understanding both the phylogenetic placement and structural evolution of the K. paniculata MT genome within Sapindaceae.

Conclusion

In the present study, we generated the first complete MT genome of K. paniculata Laxm. based on a representative circular-mapping assembly of 610,364 bp using long-read sequencing data. The assembled genome contained a 2,047-bp CP-derived MTPT region including rrn16 and trnV-GAC, and showed a preference for A/U-ending codons. This is consistent with conserved compositional patterns reported in land-plant mitogenomes. Ka/Ks analysis indicated that most shared genes are under purifying selection to maintain essential functions, whereas atp8, matR, and nad2 showed signatures consistent with distinct evolutionary pressures, including possible positive selection. Phylogenetic analysis placed K. paniculata close to Sapindus, Litchi, and Nephelium within Sapindaceae, whereas synteny analysis revealed extensive structural rearrangements, highlighting the marked structural fluidity of MT genomes in this lineage. Collectively, these findings provide a robust genomic foundation for future comparative and evolutionary studies of MT genome diversity in Sapindaceae.

Methods

Plant materials and DNA extraction

Fresh K. paniculata leaves were collected from the conservation area of the Forest Bioresources Department at the National Institute of Forest Science (Suwon, Republic of Korea). No specific permission was required for the collection of plant material, as K. paniculata is not a protected species. All field sampling was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institute of Forest Science and relevant national legislation in the Republic of Korea. A voucher specimen has been deposited at the Warm-Temperate and Subtropical Forest Research Center, National Institute of Forest Science, Republic of Korea, under voucher number 10,031,762. The specimen was identified by Young-Ho Ha. Genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB protocol55. DNA purity was assessed using an N120 NanoPhotometer (Implen, Munich, Germany), and fragment size profiles were analyzed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Additionally, we generated both short- and long-read sequencing data by constructing the corresponding sequencing libraries. Short-read sequencing data were obtained using the Illumina HiSeq X platform (2 × 150 bp paired-end), and long-read sequencing data were generated from the PacBio HiFi SMRTbell library on the PacBio Sequel II platform.

Assembly and annotation of the organelle genomes

The complete genome of the same K. paniculata individual was previously assembled by Kim et al.56 and used as a reference in subsequent analyses (NC_037176). Illumina short (HiSeq X platform, 2 × 150 bp paired-end) and long reads (PacBio HiFi; Sequel II platform) were obtained for the MT genome. The MT genome was reconstructed de novo using POLAP57—a pipeline that incorporates Flye for long-read assembly and an FMLRC-based polishing step using short-read data. In this workflow, PacBio HiFi long reads were used for the primary assembly, whereas Illumina short reads were used for polishing to improve sequence accuracy. The final assembly was recovered as a single circular-mapping MT genome. Commonly detected regions from GESEQ58 and BLASTn were also annotated. Additionally, the tRNAs of both organelle genomes were identified using tRNAscan (version 1.4)59.

Analysis of codon usage bias and SSRs, and prediction of RNA-editing sites

PCGs were extracted using PhyloSuite v1.1.1660 with default settings. Additionally, the RSCU for all PCGs was calculated using an R pipeline modified from RSCUcaller61. SSRs, tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats were identified using MISA with parameters 1–10, 2–5, 3–4, 4–3, 5–3, and 6–362.

PCGs were uploaded to the PREPACT online platform (http://www.prepact.de/prepact-main.php) to predict RNA-editing sites. The MT genome of Arabidopsis thaliana was used as a reference template for site prediction, with a cutoff value of 0.00163.

Repeat sequence analysis and MTPT identification

The complete CP genome of K. paniculata (GenBank accession no. NC_037176) was retrieved from the NCBI database. For subsequent analyses, the downloaded complete genome and assembled MT genome were concatenated into a single sequence to facilitate the identification of repeats shared between the genomes. Given that the CP genome possesses two IR regions, one copy was excluded before analysis to eliminate redundancy. Four types of repeats (forward, reverse, complementary, and palindromic) were initially detected using the REPuter server with default options (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-biele.org.de/reputer/)64. However, only forward and palindromic repeat types were considered in downstream analyses. Homologous segments between the CP and MT genomes, identified based on repeat similarity, were visualized using Circos v0.69-565. One copy of the CP IR was removed to further screen for CP-derived homologous regions in the MT genome; the resulting non-redundant CP genome was used as a query against the MT genome using BLASTn.

Ka/Ks ratio analysis

Briefly, the MT genomes of seven Sapindaceae species (A. miaotaiense, A. truncatum, A. yangbiense, L. chinensis, N. lappaceum, S. mukorossi, and X. sorbifolium) were retrieved from the NCBI database to determine the Ka/Ks ratio of each gene. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT v7.31066. Finally, the Ka/Ks ratios were calculated using Ka/Ks Calculator v2.052.

Synteny plot

A total of 10 MT genomes, including two outgroup species (H. cannabinus and H. hainanensis), were selected for comparative synteny analysis. Collinearity blocks between species were identified and visualized using the “One-Step MCScanX” function implemented in TBtools version 2.35767. This procedure generated multiple intermediate outputs, including collinear gene pairs, gene linkage tables, gene-type classifications, and GFF annotations. Thereafter, the pairwise comparison results were merged using the “File Merge for MCScanX” function to produce integrated.chr,.gff, and.link files for multispecies comparisons. Finally, the merged datasets were employed to visualize syntenic relationships using the “Multiple Synteny Plot” function.

Phylogenetic analysis

For phylogenetic analysis, 28 conserved PCGs (atp1, atp4, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, rpl5, rps3, rps4, rps12, rps14, sdh3, and sdh4) across 10 MT genomes were retrieved using PhyloSuite v1.1.1656. PCG sequences were individually aligned using MAFFT and concatenated into a supermatrix. Thereafter, this combined alignment was used to construct a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE, with the best-fit substitution model automatically selected68. The MT genome sequences of the compared species were retrieved from NCBI, including A. miaotaiense (MZ636518), A. truncatum (MZ318049), A. yangbiense (NC_059858), L. chinensis (PP932631), N. lappaceum (PP916047), S. mukorossi (NC_050850), X. sorbifolium (MK333231), H. cannabinus (NC_035549), and H. hainanensis (PP861158).
