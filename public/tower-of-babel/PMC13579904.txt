Interpretable machine learning for Parkinson’s disease diagnosis, staging, and biological mechanism exploration: a multicenter analysis
Authors: Lu Hao, Zihao Wang, Yutong Zhu, Xizheng Wang, Minghui Zhu, Kalibunuer Mahemuti, Yangtai Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate an interpretable machine learning model based on multicenter T1-weighted MRI radiomics data for the three-way classification of Parkinson’s disease (PD)—Normal (NM), early-stage, and mid-late-stage—and to clarify the diagnostic value of key cortical nuclear features, with further elucidation of the molecular basis through proteomic association analysis.MethodsA total of 200 patients from multiple centers were included and divided into a training set (n = 76), an internal validation set (n = 33), and an external validation set (n = 91). Six core nuclei, including the caudate nucleus (CN), putamen (PUT), globus pallidus (GP), red nucleus (RN), substantia nigra (SN), and nucleus accumbens (NAc), were segmented using the DKT template. A total of 107 radiomics features were extracted per nucleus. After a three-step dimension reduction (variance thresholding, univariate selection, and multi-class LASSO), key features were identified. Five machine learning models were constructed. Model performance was evaluated using micro/macro-AUC and accuracy (ACC). SHAP analysis was employed to reveal the contribution of specific features to the diagnostic process. Additionally, proteomic analysis was performed on a subset of patients from Center 1 to screen for differentially expressed proteins among groups, and correlation analysis between DEPs and key radiomic features was conducted to explore biological mechanisms.ResultsNine key features were finally selected, predominantly consisting of gray-level non-uniformity within the PUT. The GBT model performed optimally, with macro-AUC/micro-AUC of 0.890/0.902 in the internal validation set and 0.876/0.869 in the external validation set, respectively. SHAP analysis indicated that the NM group primarily relied on texture features of the PUT and NAc; early-stage PD was characterized by structural changes in the GP and PUT; and mid-late-stage PD was correlated with features from the SN and RN. Proteomic analysis identified 514 DEPs between early PD and controls (411 upregulated, 103 downregulated), which were enriched in cytoskeletal and immune pathways (FDR < 0.05), and 123 DEPs between mid-to-late and early PD (26 upregulated, 97 downregulated), which were enriched in proteasome and neurodegeneration pathways (FDR < 0.05). Multiple significant correlations between key radiomic features and DEPs were identified (all FDR-corrected P < 0.01), including original_gldm_DependenceNonUniformity_PUT with HGFAC (r = 0.510), original_ngtdm_Contrast_GP with PLAT (r = 0.453), and original_glrlm_RunEntropy_GP_QSM with both ASPN (r = 0.62) and TFPI (r = − 0.58).ConclusionInterpretable machine learning models based on multicenter T1-weighted nuclear radiomics features can accurately diagnose and stage PD. Integration of radiomics and proteomics enhances model interpretability by linking imaging features to underlying biological mechanisms, providing an objective imaging basis for clinical practice and exhibiting strong generalizability and potential for clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13040-026-00574-w.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, resting tremor, and postural instability, as well as an array of non-motor manifestations [1–2]. With the global intensification of population aging, the prevalence of PD is rising annually, imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden [3–4]. Currently, the clinical diagnosis and staging of PD predominantly rely on the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) criteria and subjective neurological scales [5]. However, these traditional methods are prone to observer bias and lack sensitivity to early pathological changes. Consequently, many patients have already suffered irreversible neuronal loss by the time of diagnosis, missing the optimal window for neuroprotective intervention [6–9]. Thus, identifying objective imaging biomarkers for accurate early diagnosis and longitudinal staging is of paramount clinical importance.

Advancements in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technology have provided a powerful tool for exploring brain structural changes in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). T1-weighted MRI (T1WI), with its high spatial resolution and excellent anatomical contrast, is widely used to assess subcortical nuclear atrophy and cortical thinning. However, subtle pathological changes in the early stages of PD are often difficult to identify with the naked eye [10–13]. As an emerging field, radiomics can extract high-throughput quantitative features from medical images, capturing microstructural changes that are undetectable by conventional imaging examinations [14]. When combined with machine learning (ML) algorithms, radiomics has shown tremendous potential in the automatic classification of neurodegenerative diseases [15–17]. Nevertheless, many “black-box” machine learning models lack transparency, hindering their clinical application and promotion. The introduction of interpretability techniques (such as SHAP analysis) has made it possible to quantify the contribution of features, thereby bridging the gap between complex algorithm outputs and clinical decision-making [18–20]. Additionally, the correlation analysis between radiomics and biological mechanisms has emerged as a research hotspot in recent years. Numerous studies have conducted correlation analyses between key radiomic features and multi-omics data, aiming to decipher the predictive contribution of radiomic features from the perspective of biological mechanisms and further enhance the interpretability of radiomic models [21–25].

Previous studies have demonstrated that radiomic features of the basal ganglia, such as the substantia nigra and putamen, can indirectly reflect microstructural pathology in PD [26]. However, several critical gaps remain in the current literature. First, most studies focus on binary classification (PD vs. healthy controls) or single-stage identification, failing to capture the continuous pathological progression across the normal, early, and mid-late stages of PD. Second, the relative contributions of different subcortical nuclei to disease progression remain inadequately explored. Finally, existing machine learning models generally lack interpretability, and the absence of association pathways between radiomic features and biological mechanisms of traditional omics limits their clinical translation.

To address these challenges, this multicenter study performed in-depth radiomic analysis of six core nuclei—caudate nucleus (CN), putamen (PUT), globus pallidus (GP), red nucleus (RN), substantia nigra (SN), and nucleus accumbens (NAc)—based on 3D-T1WI imaging data, and constructed a three-classification machine learning model. The objectives of this study are as follows: (1) to evaluate the application value of 3D-T1WI radiomics in PD diagnosis and staging; (2) to systematically analyze the differential contributions of features from different nuclei across various PD stages; (3) to reveal the imaging basis of model decisions through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) interpretability analysis; and (4) to enhance model interpretability and provide support for clinicians to understand the prediction basis through multicenter data validation and association analysis between radiomic features and the biological mechanisms of proteomics.

Materials and methods

Study population

This multicenter retrospective study analyzed 200 subjects recruited from two centers between January 2022 and January 2025. The cohort was divided into three groups: normal control group (NM, n = 90), early-stage PD group (Early-stage PD, n = 47), and mid-late-stage PD group (Mid-late-stage PD, n = 63). All PD patients met the clinical diagnostic criteria of the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society (MDS) and were staged according to the Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) scale. Early-stage PD: H&Y stage 1–2; mid-to-late stage PD: H&Y stage 3–5.Inclusion criteria included: (1) aged 40–80 years; (2) no history of other Parkinsonian-plus syndromes (e.g., multiple system atrophy [MSA], progressive supranuclear palsy [PSP]); (3) availability of high-quality 3D-T1WI imaging data. Exclusion criteria: History of stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinsonian syndromes, secondary Parkinson’s disease, meningitis, malignant tumors, liver or kidney dysfunction, coagulation disorders, psychiatric diseases, cognitive impairment caused by other factors, etc.; long-term heavy drinking or use of drugs that may affect the examination results.

The development set (n = 109), consisting of data from Center 1 (the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University), was split at a 7:3 ratio into a training set (n = 76) for model development and an internal validation set (n = 33) for hyperparameter tuning using stratified random sampling to preserve the distribution of disease stages. Consequently, the training set comprised 35 cases of NM, 17 of Early-stage PD, and 24 of Mid-late-stage PD, while the internal validation set contained 15 cases of NM, 8 of Early-stage PD, and 10 of Mid-late-stage PD.Additionally, an independent external validation set (n = 91) was collected from The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Center 2) to rigorously evaluate the model’s generalization performance and robustness across scanners from different centers.This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No.: KY2024052104) and conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All subjects signed informed consent forms.The flow chart of this study’s design is shown in Fig. 1.

Scanning sequence parameters

All patients’ images in this study were automatically segmented using the asegdkt module of FastSurfer (https://github.com/Deep-MI/FastSurfer) [27], which can accurately segment 6 core nuclei related to PD: CN, PUT, GP, RN, SN, and NAc. To ensure standardized anatomical definition while avoiding interference from spatial normalization deformations on texture features, this study conducted radiomic feature extraction in the Conformed Native Space. All raw 3D-T1WI images were first preprocessed via the FastSurfer pipeline, including bias field correction using the N4 algorithm to eliminate intensity inhomogeneities and deep learning-based skull stripping. Subsequently, all images were resampled to an isotropic voxel resolution of 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm; the Cubic B-spline interpolation algorithm was selected for resampling due to its superior ability to preserve high-frequency texture details while suppressing aliasing effects. Anatomical parcellation was performed using the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas-based segmentation automatically generated by FastSurfer; these segmentation masks natively reside in the aforementioned resampled individual space, requiring no additional nonlinear spatial transformation, thereby ensuring both the precision of boundary definitions for basal ganglia and subcortical nuclei (particularly the substantia nigra and striatum relevant to PD pathology) and the authenticity of internal textures. Finally, to eliminate intensity scale discrepancies between subjects and scanning sequences, Z-score normalization was applied to voxel intensities within each ROI.

All ROIs segmented by FastSurfer were subjected to 100% double-blind visual quality control (QC) by a senior neuroradiologist with 10 years of experience. Segmentation results with ambiguous anatomical boundaries or segmentation failures were manually revised and confirmed. To quantitatively validate segmentation accuracy, 30 subjects were randomly selected to calculate the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) between automated segmentation and manual gold-standard segmentation; the mean DSC for all six target nuclei was above 0.90, indicating excellent consistency.The brain region segmentation image is presented in Fig. 2.

Feature extraction and selection

Radiomic features were extracted from the 6 automatically segmented nuclei using the PyRadiomics software package (version 3.0.1; https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/). The extraction was performed in 3D mode using default settings, with intensity discretization based on a Fixed Bin Width of 25. A total of three categories of radiomic features were extracted from each nucleus, covering comprehensive structural and textural characteristics of brain tissue: (I) First-order statistical features (n = 18): These features quantify the distribution of voxel intensities within each region, including metrics such as mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, median, minimum/maximum intensity, and interquartile range, reflecting the overall tissue density and homogeneity; (II) Shape features (n = 14): These features describe the geometric properties of each brain region, such as volume, surface area, sphericity, aspect ratio, and compactness, capturing differences in regional anatomical morphology among the Normal (NM), Early-stage PD, and Mid-late-stage PD groups; (III) Texture features (n = 75): Derived from the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), Gray-Level Run-Length Matrix (GLRLM), Gray-Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), and Gray-Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM), these features include contrast, correlation, energy, entropy, run-length non-uniformity, and zone size non-uniformity, which characterize spatial heterogeneity and tissue microstructural patterns. After extraction, an initial set of 107 radiomic features was obtained per nucleus.

A three-stage sequential feature selection approach was adopted in this study to reduce dimensionality, with the goal of removing redundant or irrelevant features from the training set and retaining the most discriminative ones for developing the three-classification model. This strategy was designed to prevent overfitting and enhance model interpretability. Firstly, a variance threshold of 0.8 was applied, and features with variance below this threshold (i.e., those showing minimal variation across all patients) were excluded. Secondly, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for continuous features, and only those with statistical significance (P < 0.05) were retained to ensure significant differences among the three groups. Finally, multi-class least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to the remaining features; this method imposes a penalty on feature coefficients, shrinking the coefficients of irrelevant features to zero and selecting only those with non-zero coefficients.

Model construction and evaluation

To construct a three-classification prediction model for PD diagnosis and staging, this study employed five algorithms commonly used in classification model development—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), and gradient boosting tree (GBT). Each algorithm took the results of radiomic features extracted from the 6 nuclei after feature selection on the training set as input. During model training, GridSearch was used for hyperparameter tuning, and methods such as class_weight were adopted to mitigate the problem of class imbalance in the training set. The diagnostic performance differences were analyzed by comparing receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves, confusion matrices, and various evaluation metrics [e.g., area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, F1 score] across the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set. Additionally, SHAP values were used to analyze model interpretability, and beeswarm plots, bar plots, and waterfall plots were employed to reveal the contribution direction and intensity of key features to different classifications. All analytical codes involved in this study, including feature extraction, radiomics pipeline construction, and machine learning modeling, have been made publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/jy2137/PD-radiomics).

Biological mechanism correlation analysis

From the main imaging cohort (Center 1), 40 subjects (16 normal controls, 15 early-stage PD, and 9 mid-to-late-stage PD) were selected as an age- and sex-matched subgroup with sufficient serum quality for proteomic analysis. Serum samples underwent low-abundance protein enrichment using C18 magnetic beads, followed by trypsin digestion (enzyme-to-protein ratio 1:50, 37 °C, 16 h). Peptides were separated by nano-UPLC (25 cm × 75 μm, 1.6 μm C18 column) using a 120-min gradient (mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid in water; mobile phase B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) and analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer operating in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode (resolution: 60,000 at m/z 200; AGC target: 200%; maximum injection time: auto; scan range: 350–1400 m/z). Pooled quality control (QC) samples, prepared by mixing equal aliquots from all subjects, were inserted every 10 study samples to monitor system stability, yielding a median coefficient of variation (CV) of 12.7% for protein intensities. As all samples were processed in a single batch, no batch effect correction was required.

Raw DIA data were processed using Spectronaut v.18.0 (Biognosys) against the UniProt human database (version 2024_02). Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while acetylation (protein N-terminus) and oxidation of methionine were set as variable modifications. Trypsin/P was used with a maximum of two missed cleavages allowed. Protein inference was performed at a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at both peptide and protein levels. Protein intensities were normalized by total ion current (TIC). Proteins with missing values in ≥ 50% of samples were excluded; for the remaining missing values, imputation was performed using half of the minimum non-zero intensity for each protein. Differential expression among the three groups was assessed by Welch’s ANOVA, followed by Games-Howell post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined as those with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) and an absolute fold change (|FC|) ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 0.83 in any pairwise comparison.

The core objects of correlation analysis included 9 key radiomic features screened in the previous stage and 30 DEPs obtained from proteomic analysis. First, data standardization was performed on all core analysis objects to eliminate the impact of dimensional differences on correlation results. Subsequently, a “radiomic features-proteomic features” correlation analysis system was constructed to sequentially conduct global correlation analysis, systematically exploring correlated feature pairs. Pearson correlation analysis was used to test the linear correlation between radiomic features and DEPs, with the screening criteria for strongly correlated feature pairs set as: absolute correlation coefficient |r| ≥ 0.4 (adjusted based on actual data distribution) and statistical significance P < 0.01.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and machine learning modeling were implemented in Python 3.7, with key libraries and their versions explicitly specified to ensure computational reproducibility: scikit-learn (v1.6.1) for model construction, hyperparameter tuning, and performance evaluation; shap (v0.47.2) for model interpretability and feature importance quantification; SimpleITK (v2.4.0) for medical image processing and radiomic feature extraction; alongside numpy (v1.26.4) for numerical computation.

Model performance was analyzed via ROC curves. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Macro-AUC (arithmetic mean of AUC values for each class) and micro-AUC (weighted mean considering sample weights) were calculated, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were determined using the Bootstrap method. Confusion matrices were employed to analyze the correct prediction rate, misclassification rate, and missed diagnosis rate of each class, including specific metrics such as sensitivity (SEN), specificity(SPE), precision, F1 score, and accuracy (ACC). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the linear relationships. To address the risk of false positives in high-dimensional searches without applying an over-conservative Bonferroni correction (which may mask true biological signals), we utilized a dual-filtering approach. Only feature pairs meeting both a stringent statistical significance threshold (P < 0.01) and a strong correlation threshold (|r| ≥ 0.4) were retained for biological interpretation. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple testing in correlation analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 200 participants from two multi-center cohorts were enrolled in this study, including 90 cognitively normal (NM) individuals, 47 patients with early-stage PD, and 63 patients with mid-late-stage PD. The development cohort (Center 1, n = 109) was divided into a training set (n = 76) and an internal validation set (n = 33) at a 7:3 ratio, while an independent external validation cohort (Center 2, n = 91) was established for model verification. Demographic data showed no statistically significant differences in gender or age among the NM, early-stage PD, and mid-late-stage PD groups across all datasets (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). Specifically, the mean age of the NM group was 62.03 ± 8.03 years, with 34.2%(13/38) males; the early-stage PD group had a mean age of 66.44 ± 8.91 years, with 39.00%(16/41) males; and the mid-late-stage PD group had a mean age of 65.20 ± 7.12 years, with 33.30%(10/30) males. All participants met the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, and no significant differences in educational background were observed among the groups, ensuring the comparability of the cohorts for subsequent model construction and validation.

The results of feature selection

A three-step sequential feature selection strategy was applied to the initial 642 radiomic features (107 features extracted from each of the 6 core nuclei). Firstly, variance thresholding (threshold = 0.8) eliminated features with minimal variation. Secondly, univariate ANOVA analysis (P < 0.05) excluded non-significant features. Finally, multi-class LASSO regression further refined the feature set, resulting in 9 key discriminative radiomic features for model construction (Table 3). These features were predominantly derived from the PUT nucleus (5 features), followed by the NAc (1 feature), CN (2 features), GP (1 feature), and SN (1 feature), covering three categories: gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM) features (4 features), gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features (1 feature), gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM) features (1 feature), neighboring gray tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features (1 feature), and shape features (2 features). The core features and their coefficients are presented in Table 1, with original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT_T1 showing the largest absolute coefficient (-0.281), indicating its prominent role in classification.

Model performance

Five machine learning models (LR, RF, SVM, KNN, GBT) were constructed for the three-classification task (NM vs. early-stage PD vs. mid-late-stage PD), and their performance was comprehensively evaluated across the training, internal validation, and external validation sets. As shown in Table 4, the GBT model achieved the optimal overall performance, followed by the RF model, while the SVM model exhibited the lowest efficacy in the internal validation set.

SHAP analysis

SHAP analysis was employed to quantify the contribution of each key feature to the three-classification model and visualize the decision-making mechanism (including SHAP feature weight bar plots, beeswarm plots, and waterfall plots). The results revealed that feature importance and action directions exhibited stage specificity: For the NM group classification (Fig. 5A and B), the core contributing features were original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT (mean |SHAP value| = 0.24), original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT (mean |SHAP value| = 0.23), and original_gldm_DependenceNonUniformity_PUT (mean |SHAP value| = 0.21). These features showed a positive contribution to the prediction of the NM class—higher gray-level non-uniformity in the PUT nucleus promoted the prediction of the NM class, reflecting the intact structural homogeneity of these nuclei in normal individuals.For the early-stage PD classification (Fig. 5D and E), the key discriminative features were original_ngtdm_Contrast_GP (mean |SHAP value| = 0.18), original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_NAc (mean |SHAP value| = 0.11), and original_gldm_DependenceNonUniformity_PUT_T1 (mean |SHAP value| = 0.10). These three features exerted a negative contribution to the prediction of early-stage PD. Increased contrast in the GP nucleus (elevated NGTDM contrast values) and altered gray-level dependence in the PUT nucleus were associated with negative SHAP values.For the mid-late-stage PD classification (Fig. 3G and K), the most impactful features were original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT (mean |SHAP value| = 0.17), original_glrlm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT (mean |SHAP value| = 0.16), and original_shape_Sphericity_SN (mean |SHAP value| = 0.15). These three features had a negative contribution to the prediction of mid-late-stage PD. Decreased sphericity of the SN nucleus (reflected by negative SHAP values) strongly predicted mid-late-stage PD, which may correspond to progressive neurodegeneration and structural disruption of these nuclei during the advanced stage of the disease.

Discussion

Based on 3D-T1WI radiomics and interpretable machine learning, this study constructed a three-classification model for the diagnosis and staging of PD, which demonstrated robust performance across two multi-center cohorts. As the optimal model, the gradient boosting tree (GBT) achieved a macro area under the curve (macro-AUC) of 0.979 (95% CI: 0.953–0.999) in the training set, 0.890 (95% CI: 0.766–0.978) in the internal validation set, and 0.876 (95% CI: 0.806–0.932) in the external validation set. This performance outperforms most existing PD diagnostic models: Accioly et al. [17] developed an early-stage PD binary classification model based on T1WI, which achieved an AUC of 0.93 but could not distinguish between early and mid-late stages; Fu et al. [15] built a PD binary classification model using T2WI/FLAIR images, with an external validation AUC of 0.85, lacking both staging capability and interpretability analysis. In contrast, our RF/GBT combined model achieved a macro-AUC of up to 0.87 in the external validation set, effectively addressing the clinical need for precise PD staging. More importantly, by incorporating data from two independent medical centers, our model overcomes the limitations of single-center studies, significantly enhancing its generalizability and clinical applicability.

SHAP analysis revealed the biological logic behind the model’s decisions, which aligns closely with the core pathological mechanisms of PD. For the normal group classification, the core features include original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT (mean |SHAP value| = 0.24) and original_shape_Sphericity_SN (mean |SHAP value| = 0.15). Higher gray-level non-uniformity in the PUT nucleus and intact sphericity in the SN nucleus correspond to positive SHAP values, reflecting intact structural homogeneity of these nuclei. This may indicate that SN structural symmetry is a hallmark of normal aging. For early-stage PD classification, original_ngtdm_Contrast_GP (mean |SHAP value| = 0.18) is the primary discriminative feature. Increased NGTDM contrast in the GP nucleus (indicating enhanced tissue heterogeneity) correlates with negative SHAP values, driving the model to classify cases as early-stage PD. This aligns with the core role of the GP in the basal ganglia motor loop: Bu et al. [23] noted that reduced dopaminergic input from the SN leads to GP hyperactivity and subsequent microstructural disruption, which can be accurately captured by texture features such as GLDM and NGTDM. For mid-late-stage PD classification, regarding the sphericity feature of the SN nucleus (original_shape_Sphericity_SN_T1): the lower the feature value, the more likely the model is to predict mid-late-stage PD. This aligns with pathology: extensive dopaminergic neuron loss in the SN pars compacta causes nuclear shrinkage and reduced sphericity, a typical structural marker of mid-late-stage PD [13].

Our GBT/RF combined model shows great potential in early PD diagnosis, providing an objective tool to address the clinical pain point of “difficult early diagnosis”. Traditional diagnosis relies mainly on subjective clinical symptoms and scale assessments, which lack sufficient sensitivity in the early stages of the disease [8]. The GBT model has significant clinical utility, particularly in solving the “difficult early diagnosis” problem of PD. Current clinical diagnosis relies heavily on subjective motor symptoms such as resting tremor, leading to missed diagnoses in many early-stage patients [24]. The GBT model in this study achieved excellent performance in early-stage PD classification: an AUC of 0.960 in the training set and 0.848 in the external validation set. The calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) presented in Supplementary Figure S1 demonstrate that the model exhibits excellent probability calibration and strong clinical utility across all subgroups. By capturing subvisual microstructural changes in nuclei, our model can provide early warnings when symptoms are not yet typical.

Critically, SHAP analysis gives the model a “transparent” decision logic. Clinicians no longer need to confront a “black box”, further improving clinical acceptance. Supplementary Figure S2 displays SHAP waterfall plots for representative cases with successful predictions (Fig. S2A) and failed predictions (Fig. S2B). Notably, key features contributing to these predictions differ markedly between the two groups. For instance, the feature original_gldm_GrayLevelNonUniformity_PUT shows high values in successfully predicted cases but low values in mispredicted cases, substantially influencing the model’s output. Further analysis of the confusion matrix reveals that errors from the optimal model (GBT in E-val set) are not confined to adjacent stages but include significant cross-stage misclassifications—particularly the under-staging of mid-late PD cases as normal or early (10 out of 29 misclassified), and the over-staging of some normal controls as mid-late (2 out of 40). This pattern suggests that while the model captures the overall trend of disease progression, it still struggles with cases exhibiting atypical feature profiles, likely reflecting challenges posed by biological heterogeneity or center-specific imaging variations.Previously, clinicians often distrusted AI models due to unclear decision logic, but SHAP analysis significantly enhances model interpretability—allowing clear visualization of the specific features (e.g., reduced sphericity of the GP) that drive the model’s diagnostic judgments [25, 26]. This interpretability greatly enhances the model’s credibility and clinical utility, helping clinicians understand, validate, and ultimately adopt the model’s recommendations, thus enabling the transition from empirical medicine to data-driven precision medicine, which aligns with the “AI-clinical assessment integration” workflow proposed by Reddy et al. (2019) [27–29].

Our biological mechanism correlation analysis addresses the interpretability gap of radiomic models by linking imaging phenotypes to molecular pathways. Notably, the putamen-derived original_gldm_DependenceNonUniformity_PUT (reflecting gray-level dependence heterogeneity) showed the strongest positive correlation with HGFAC (r = 0.510, P < 0.001), while the globus pallidus-derived original_ngtdm_Contrast_GP correlated significantly with PLAT (r = 0.453, P < 0.01) and PDGFD (r = 0.448, P < 0.01). These associated proteins are involved in growth factor regulation and inflammatory responses, key pathways in PD pathogenesis. As core basal ganglia components linked to PD motor dysfunction, the correlation between their radiomic alterations and regulatory proteins validates radiomic features as non-invasive surrogates for molecular changes, helping clinicians comprehend the model’s predictive basis.

Despite the achievements of this study, several limitations remain. First, our model is based solely on the T1WI sequence and does not integrate multi-modal information such as quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) (sensitive to iron deposition) or diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) (reflecting white matter integrity) [30–31], which may have led to the omission of important pathological features. Second, the sample size included in this study is relatively small, and deep learning algorithms were not used for higher-dimensional model development [32]. Third, although we adopted strict data analysis strategies including stratified data splitting and independent external validation, nested cross-validation is still needed in future work to further verify the model reliability. Fourth, in the radiomics-proteomics association analysis, although several moderate-to-strong correlations (|r| > 0.4) with significant raw p-values were identified, these associations did not remain significant after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction due to the large number of multiple tests (270 tests in total), suggesting that these findings are exploratory and require validation in larger and independent cohorts. Future research should optimize the model in the following ways: (1) Conduct larger-scale multi-center prospective studies to further validate and enhance the model’s robustness [33]; (2) Integrate multi-modal MRI and even functional imaging data such as PET to construct a more comprehensive biomarker system [34–35]; (3) Combine radiomic features with detailed clinical, genetic, and biochemical indicators and introduce deep learning models to build a true “imaging-clinical” combined model, aiming to achieve ultra-early identification, precise staging, and personalized treatment outcome prediction for PD [36].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this multicenter study demonstrates that T1WI-based radiomic features from six core subcortical nuclei effectively distinguish normal controls from early-stage and mid-late-stage PD patients, with the model showing excellent diagnostic performance and generalizability. Systematic analysis reveals differential nuclear contributions across PD stages, and SHAP analysis clarifies the imaging basis of model decisions. Furthermore, integrating proteomic correlation analysis enhances model interpretability and supports clinicians in understanding the predictive rationale, bridging quantitative imaging phenotypes with PD-related molecular mechanisms. Collectively, our findings demonstrate potential utility as a decision-support tool for the precise diagnosis and staging of Parkinson’s disease.
