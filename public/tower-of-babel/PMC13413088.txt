Augmenting and Assaying Nav1.1 Protein Quantity for Dravet Syndrome Therapy
Authors: Aiswarya Saravanan, Jordan Safran, Anna Loughman, Grace Lin, Shivanshi Vaid, Dana Allababidi, Peter M Tessier, Louis T Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDravet Syndrome (DS) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy predominantly caused by heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants in SCN1A, which encodes Nav1.1. Conserved upstream open reading frames (uORFs) in SCN1A were validated to regulate translation in reporter assays, demonstrating the therapeutic viability of increasing Nav1.1 from a wild‐type allele. A human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line with endogenously HiBiT‐tagged Nav1.1 showed dose‐dependent Nav1.1 downregulation with gapmer antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) and upregulation with splice‐switching ASOs, validating a sensitive quantification platform for endogenous Nav1.1 translation. This iPSC line provides a timely resource for screening translational modulators and validating therapeutic strategies for SCN1A‐related disorders.Keywords: epilepsy, HiBiT, high throughput screen

Introduction

DS is a severe childhood epilepsy characterized by drug‐resistant seizures, developmental delay, and premature death [1]. In ~80% of patients, DS results from heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants in SCN1A, leading to reduced Nav1.1 [2]. Current treatments address symptoms rather than the underlying genetic cause, underscoring the need for gene‐directed therapies that restore Nav1.1 to physiological levels.

Over 50% of human genes are translationally regulated by upstream open reading frames (uORFs) [3]. uORFs compete with primary ORFs (pORFs) for ribosome binding, reducing pORF translation efficiency by 30%–80% [4, 5]. Previous studies demonstrated that antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting uORFs can increase protein expression [6], positioning these regulatory elements as potential therapeutic targets.

Developing such therapies requires quantification of Nav1.1 protein, but conventional techniques are limited. Immunoblotting is low throughput and requires large amounts of material, and Nav1.1 antibodies have limited sensitivity and specificity. Transcript‐level analysis is similarly insufficient, as translational regulation can alter protein abundance independent of mRNA levels.

To enable efficient screening of translational regulators, we developed an induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) reporter line with endogenously tagged Nav1.1 using the HiBiT (Promega, Fitchburg, WI) system [7]. The 11‐amino acid HiBiT peptide tag (VSGWRLFKKLA), when combined with the complementary LgBiT protein, reconstitutes active nanoluciferase. This system provides minimal structural disruption, quantitative readouts suitable for 96‐well formats, and compatibility with live‐cell assays. The reporter line was validated to respond to ASOs designed to downregulate and upregulate SCN1A expression, providing a sensitive, quantitative, medium‐throughput screening platform for endogenous Nav1.1 abundance.

Materials and Methods

Bioinformatic Analysis of SCN1A Transcripts

Human SCN1A transcript sequences were obtained from the RefSeq database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/6323). The 5′ leader sequence shared among the first coding exon of the major human isoforms was used to determine interspecies conservation using the UCSC Genome Browser, PhyloP, and Multiz alignments.

Generation and Testing of uORF Reporter Constructs

The SCN1A 5′ leader reference sequence followed by a start codon and the initial coding sequence of Nav1.1 was cloned upstream of nanoluciferase, with nanoluciferase in‐frame with the Nav1.1 coding sequence (Figure 1D). Upstream start codons (uATG) were systematically changed to AGT through site‐directed mutagenesis (Q5 Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit, NEB). In 96‐well plates, HEK‐293 cells were co‐transfected with nanoluciferase reporters and firefly luciferase using FuGENE HD (Promega). Cells were lysed with GloLysis Buffer 18–24 h post‐transfection, and divided into 2 separate plates for luminescence assays using nanoluciferase substrate and firefly luciferase reagent, and measured with a GloMax luminometer (Promega).

CRISPR/Cas9 Strategy for HiBiT Tagging

Three guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting near the SCN1A stop codon were designed: gRNA‐A (5′‐AAATTGTTTACAGCCTGTGA‐3′), gRNA‐B (5′‐GCAAAGATGAAAAAGCCAAA‐3′), gRNA‐C (5′‐CCGGGTGACAAAGCCAATTG‐3′). These were cloned into PX459 (Addgene #62988), which encodes Cas9 and puromycin resistance [8]. Donor DNA was synthesized (Genewiz) containing 500 bp homology arms flanking the SCN1A stop codon, with the HiBiT coding sequence preceded by valine‐serine linker inserted before the SCN1A stop codon (Figure 2A). Silent mutations in the protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs) were inserted to preserve protein sequence while preventing re‐cutting by Cas9 after homology‐directed repair.

iPSC Transfection and Clone Validation

Control human iPSCs that had been derived from foreskin fibroblasts [9] were transfected with gRNA and donor DNA using Lipofectamine Stem (ThermoFisher Scientific) and subjected to puromycin selection. Colonies were isolated by manual picking and expanded in mTeSR Plus (STEMCELL Technologies). PCR screening of colonies employed primers to detect HiBiT integration, confirm 5′ and 3′ junctions, and exclude chromosomal rearrangements. Candidate clones underwent SNP array analysis (Illumina Infinium CoreExome‐24 v1.4 BeadChip) and Sanger, next‐generation (Illumina MiSeq), and long‐read (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Plasmidsaurus) sequencing to characterize both on‐ and off‐target editing.

HiBiT Functional Validation in iNeurons

iPSCs carrying validated HiBiT (gRNA‐C clone 8) were used to generate doxycycline‐inducible NGN2‐overexpression lines and differentiated into induced excitatory glutamatergic neurons (iNeurons) as previously described [10]. At differentiation day 5, iNeurons were treated with either a proprietary splice‐switching ASO‐SC designed to enhance Nav1.1 expression [11], ASO‐8 (TGCACCCCTTGAAACTGGTC) targeting the SCN1A 3′ UTR for knockdown, or scrambled control ASO‐9 (CTCAGTATCACCCCTGGGTA). ASO‐8 and ASO‐9 are 5–10‐5 gapmers with a central segment of 10‐mer DNA flanked by 5‐mer 2′‐O‐methoxyethyl‐modified RNA bases and fully phosphorothioate‐modified linkages. At differentiation days 17, 24, and 31, Nav1.1‐HiBiT was quantified with a GloMax luminometer using the Nano‐Glo HiBiT Lytic Detection System (Promega).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal‐Wallis tests with Tukey's or Dunn's multiple comparisons, as appropriate (GraphPad Prism 9.0). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Conserved SCN1A uORFs Regulate Translation

Interspecies sequence alignment revealed high conservation of four SCN1A uAUGs in exon 5, located at positions −40, −29, −17, and −8 nucleotides relative to the primary AUG (pAUG), with the three most proximal uORFs terminating after the pAUG (Figure 1). All are present in chimpanzee, monkey, rabbit, pig, dog, ferret, and elephant, and three of the four conserved in mouse, rat, and armadillo, but there is less conservation in Xenopus and zebrafish (Figure 1A), suggesting functional importance in regulating Nav1.1 across mammals. Additionally, the four uAUGs most proximal to the pAUG are present in all major transcript variants (Figure 1B,C).

In transfected HEK‐293 cells, simultaneous mutation of all four uAUGs significantly increased nanoluciferase activity 6.9‐fold over WT (Figure 1D,E). Systematic analysis of individual and combination mutations revealed that proximal uAUGs exerted stronger effects: uAUG4 alone increased expression 1.6‐fold, while combined uAUG3 + 4 mutations yielded over a 4‐fold increase. Mutating individual distal uAUGs (uAUG1 and uAUG2) had no effect. Mutating uAUG2, 3, and 4 together produced ~8‐fold increase in luciferase (Figure 1E). These results confirmed that multiple uORFs cooperatively suppress SCN1A translation, with the three most proximal uORFs having the strongest impact.

Nav1.1‐HiBiT Reporter Development

To evaluate changes in endogenous Nav1.1 in a human context, we developed a tagged Nav1.1 iPSC line. Of the 3 gRNAs designed, only transfections with gRNA‐C, with the cut site positioned −47 bp relative to the SCN1A stop codon, showed successful CRISPR/Cas9 editing and HiBiT integration (Figure 2A). iPSC line gRNA‐C clone 8 exhibited the HiBiT tag integrated in‐frame with intact mutated PAM sequences on one allele, and an 8‐base deletion at the gRNA cut site on the second allele (chr2(GRCh38):g.165991299_165991306del; NM_001165963.4:c.5969_5976delCAAAGCCA; NP_001159435.1:p.(T1990Nfs*6)). iPSC line gRNA‐C clone 12 showed proper HiBiT integration on one allele with a single‐base insertion causing a frameshift on the other (chr2(GRCh38):g.165991297insA; NM_001165963.4:c.5977dup; NP_001159435.1:p.(I1993Nfs*6)). A minor population (< 10%) of sequences from gRNA‐C clone 12 showed discrepancies for an engineered PAM mutation located beyond the stop codon on both alleles, but this is not predicted to interfere with reporter function. Off‐target analysis did not reveal off‐target editing (Table S1), and SNP array did not detect any likely pathogenic or pathogenic chromosomal structural variants, though HiBiT clone 8 and its NGN2 derivatives had a 20q11.21 duplication including BCL2L1, which can confer a survival advantage (Table S2). This heterozygous HiBiT integration with loss‐of‐function mutations on untagged alleles for both clones provides a model of DS haploinsufficiency.

Nav1.1‐HiBiT Sensitively Detects ASO Regulation of Nav1.1

To validate that Nav1.1‐HiBiT accurately reports Nav1.1 levels, iNeurons were generated from gRNA‐C clone 8 and tested for response to ASO‐mediated modulation. Treatment with ASO‐8, designed to degrade SCN1A mRNA, produced dose‐dependent decreases in HiBiT‐luciferase signal, while scrambled control ASO‐9 showed no effect (Figure 2B), demonstrating that the HiBiT system sensitively detects reductions in Nav1.1 protein. Conversely, treatment with ASO‐SC, designed to enhance Nav1.1 through modulation of RNA splicing [11, 12], produced a robust 4‐ to 7‐fold increase in luciferase activity compared to vehicle control at various time points (Figure 2C). These bidirectional responses validate that Nav1.1‐HiBiT accurately quantifies changes in endogenous Nav1.1 in response to therapeutic interventions.

Discussion

This study validates conserved uORFs as translational regulators of Nav1.1, opening new therapeutic avenues. Interestingly, uORFs 2, 3, and 4 all terminate past the pAUG (Figure 1C), preventing translation reinitiation of Nav1.1, making them prime candidates for therapeutic manipulation. While our preliminary experiments with ASOs targeting uAUG2, uAUG3, or uAUG4 failed to increase luciferase activity in transfected cells or in vitro translation systems (data not shown), targeting multiple uORFs simultaneously could provide stronger effects (Figure 1E). As it is also unknown whether disruption of SCN1A uORF peptides has physiological consequences, more experiments are needed to optimize this therapeutic approach.

The Nav1.1‐HiBiT reporter line provides several advantages. It genetically models DS haploinsufficiency, as the allele without the HiBiT tag contains a premature truncating variant. C‐terminal tagging was chosen, as the C‐terminus of Nav1.1 is not involved in voltage sensing or ion conduction, and the small HiBiT tag is designed to minimize functional disruption [13]. Nanoluciferase exhibits > 100‐fold luminescence over firefly or Renilla luciferases, enabling detection of low‐abundance endogenous proteins [14]. This iPSC line can be differentiated into multiple relevant cell types, allowing therapeutic testing across tissues.

Bidirectional modulation of the Nav1.1‐HiBiT signal demonstrates the platform's sensitivity to Nav1.1 regulation. The low baseline luciferase signal in day 17–31 iNeurons likely reflects the developmental timing of Nav1.1 expression, which increases during neuronal maturation and is higher in inhibitory neurons than in excitatory neurons [15]. Future studies will optimize conditions to improve dynamic range. Of note, each HiBiT assay was conducted from a single plating of cells, with the inclusion of biological and technical replicates and a variety of concentrations or timepoints of collection, due to limitations in the ASO reagents. Despite these caveats, we anticipate that Nav1.1‐HiBiT can expedite preclinical screening of existing drug libraries, as well as the development of alternative approaches, including the use of ASOs to modulate uORFs for DS therapy.
