Introduction

Idiopathic ventricular fibrillation (VF) in the absence of structural heart disease or surface electrocardiographic abnormalities accounts for 6.8% of patients who survive an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and is more frequently seen in young adults.1

Recently, the role of premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) triggering the arrhythmia has been reported.2 Case reports have shown that PVCs arising from the right or left distal Purkinje conduction system, the ventricular outflow tract, the moderator band (MB), and, rarely, a left ventricular (LV) false tendon are triggers.3 PVCs arising from the right ventricle (RV) inducing VF are rare, and only case reports have been published.4

Drug therapy with antiarrhythmic agents rarely prevents recurrent VF episodes, and catheter ablation of PVCs triggering VF is potentially lifesaving.5,6

Our case is a rare presentation in a patient without other structural heart disease of PVCs originating from the RV free wall at the site of attachment of a false cord extending from the tricuspid valve annulus. Ablation of substrate at the RV insertion site led to the detachment of the false chord and resulted in the elimination of VF episodes.

Case report

A 68-year-old woman with a history of a cardiac arrest in the setting of VF and an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implant 10 years earlier presented after multiple ICD shocks. She had a complex cardiac history including multiple admissions for VF storm. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging revealed normal LV size and thickness with a normal ejection fraction (60%). Coronary angiography showed mild coronary artery disease. She was initially treated with oral amiodarone and stabilized. 2 years later, she again had VF storm with ICD shocks and was transitioned to sotalol with a good response. Over the course of the next 8 years, she had several admissions for ICD shocks related to sotalol noncompliance and electrolyte disturbance. After 3 years of doing well, she again had more ventricular tachycardia (VT)–/VF-related shocks. Trials of flecainide and mexiletine were only transiently effective.

At the index admission, she presented with VF storm with 15 shocks. Careful review of telemetry showed short-coupled PVCs preceding VF, which correlated with the ICD electrogram analysis (Figure 1). The PVC morphology was left bundle branch block with left superior axis; rapid downstrokes of the QRS in the precordial leads, and late precordial transition (>V4) (Figure 2) suggestive of a PVC origin from the region of the MB of the RV. As inpatient episodes of VF continued and were preceded by short-coupled PVC with identical morphology of PVC on electrocardiogram, the patient was sedated and intubated owing to antiarrhythmic refractory VF storm and associated pain. This resulted in significant suppression of PVCs.

Initial electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with a ventricular rate of 100 beats per minute, normal axis, and frequent premature ventricular contractions.

The patient was brought to the electrophysiology laboratory, and the procedure was performed under general anesthesia. At the start of the procedure, there were spontaneous PVCs after the mapping system patches were placed, which allowed for a CARTO pattern to be obtained (Figure 3). Unfortunately, the PVCs were absent after catheter placement precluding catheter mapping of the ectopy.

12-lead electrocardiogram of PVC on the recording system. Owing to complete suppression of PVCs with general anesthesia, we used a pacing mapping strategy with 98% match to localize the site of origin. PVC = premature ventricular contraction.

Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) showed a normal RV cavity and a modest MB connecting the inferior interventricular septum and the anterior papillary muscle. Endocardial definition of the RV, anterior papillary muscle, and MB was created using the CARTOSOUND module for image integration. Electroanatomic mapping and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) were performed using the Biosense Webster CARTO 3D electroanatomic mapping system (CARTO; Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA).

The target PVCs remained absent at baseline, after an electrophysiological induction protocol of up to 3 extra stimuli, and after isoproterenol infusion. The decision was made to map for PVC origin using catheter-induced PVCs from a contact force–sensing, irrigated ablation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch Surround Flow; Biosense Webster). Initial catheter-induced PVC mapping demonstrated a good pace-map correlation of 95% over the apical free wall of the RV. This pace-map site was tagged, and repeat ICE images showed a tight cord extending from the lateral aspect of the tricuspid valve annulus to this location (Figure 4). RFA with deflectable sheath support was done at this location using a power of 40 W and a prolonged duration of 150 seconds.

Intracardiac echocardiography imaging of the RV false cord attachment to the RV free wall tagged in green and red circles. RV = right ventricle.

An ICE catheter was positioned in the RV inflow tract to provide a live view of the lesion being created while carefully monitoring impedance drops (target of 10%–12% from baseline). No sudden changes in impedance were noted. At 150 seconds, there was a 12 Ω drop (Figure 5).

Simultaneous 3-dimensional anatomic map, with overlay of CARTOSOUND images and intracardiac echocardiography imaging.

After ablation, ICE imaging showed that the cord had detached from the RV free wall endocardium. ICE imaging showed the flail cord to now be balled up and remained attached to the tricuspid valve annulus. Doppler assessment showed no significant tricuspid regurgitation.

The next day, the patient was extubated, and transthoracic echocardiography showed no significant tricuspid regurgitation. The patient was discharged off antiarrhythmic drug therapy, and at ICD follow-up over 12 months, the patient was free of arrhythmia-related symptoms. ICD interrogation showed no recurrent VT/VF and a PVC burden of <1%.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a unique anatomic cord from the tricuspid valve to the tricuspid annulus to the RV free wall as an arrhythmia substrate for VT and VF. We termed the structure a “false cord” because, in detaching its attachment from the RV free wall, we did not alter the overall mechanism of the tricuspid valve.

The mechanism of PVCs originating from ventricular false cords is postulated to be either increased automaticity of substrate within muscle fibers or increased excitability owing to mechanical traction at the false tendon attachment site. In this case, both mechanisms were addressed with prolonged-duration irrigated ablation.

Idiopathic VF is defined as spontaneous VF in the absence of congenital or acquired structural or electrical heart disease. Idiopathic VF can be caused by a short-coupled ventricular premature beat.2 These patients mostly presented with VF recurring despite antiarrhythmic drugs and requiring multiple defibrillating shocks. During periods of arrhythmia, they had isolated or repetitive premature beats with a morphology identical or similar to that triggering VF, allowing mapping of their common origin.7

Current data regarding idiopathic ventricular arrhythmias has focused on substrate with abnormal automaticity or a focal mechanism of triggered activity. However, the role of mechanical triggers is slowly evolving. Mechanical trigger from the LV false tendon has been shown to result in VF.8 The dominant PVC morphologies in patients with mitral valve prolapse are those arising from the papillary muscle region and the outflow tract.9 The ectopy is presumed to be at least in part owing to regional stretch, either as a consequence of direct mechanical forces or triggered activity owing to damaged tissue.

Most studies thus far have focused on the His-Purkinje system because it may generate or maintain arrhythmias by automaticity, reentry, or triggered activity during multiple conditions, such as electrolyte imbalance, catecholamines, or other drug exposure.

Anatomic considerations of the RV

The RV cavity is delineated into 3 primary components: the inlet, apical trabecular, and outlet portions. The inlet segment envelops and upholds the leaflets of the tricuspid valve, with its 3 groups of papillary muscles (medial or conal, anterior, and inferior) at the base. Beyond these distal attachments, the coarsely trabeculated ventricular part occupies the outer curvature of the ventricular cavity. The outlet or pulmonary infundibulum (conus) is a tubular muscular structure that supports the leaflets of the pulmonary valve.10

The most common ventricular arrhythmias arising from the RV originate from the outflow tract and the RV MB.11 VF arrhythmias from the RV outflow tract have also been described.7

Localization of foci origination has been determined by echocardiography. However, traditional imaging planes with transthoracic echo and transesophageal echo may be unsuitable for detection, given that off-axis imaging is often required. ICE imaging overcomes this issue because imaging can be done in any axis.

Our case is unique in describing a new false tendon location in the RV and the successful prolonged-duration endocardial ablation. Prolonged-duration ablation lesions have previously been described for the treatment of ventricular arrhythmias originating from the LV summit.12 Given challenges with stability, we determined that we needed a prolonged-duration lesion to create a larger lesion to affect the surrounding tissue and the false cord attachment to force it to detach from the endocardium. This report has shown that this strategy is safe and effective for targeting RV free wall structures when the lesion formation is closely observed with real-time ICE.

PVC triggering VF storm is a true electrophysiological emergency and prompts careful evaluation. Treatment of PVC-triggered VF in patients without structural heart disease should first be aimed at studying and promptly removing any reversible proarrhythmic cause, such as myocardial ischemia, electrolyte imbalances, QT-prolonging drugs, and inflammation. There is a paucity of data regarding the effectiveness of antiarrhythmic drugs in this population. Quinidine has been shown to be effective in some short-coupled VF and idiopathic VF studies.6 If this ablation was not successful, this was to be our last antiarrhythmic choice.

Post-traumatic stress disorder is a significant issue in this patient population because of the risk of recurrence with the only therapy being ICD shocks. Without definitive treatment despite surviving multiple admissions, she lived in constant fear of recurrent shocks.

Conclusion

RV false cord attachment to the tricuspid valve can be associated with PVCs that can trigger VF storm in an otherwise structurally normal heart. RFA with an ICE-guided electroanatomic approach should be considered to improve the efficacy and safety of this procedure by direct visualization of the dynamic endocavitary structures. Prolonged-duration RFA from adjacent endocardial locations is a safe and effective technique for successfully targeting RV chordal and endocardial structures.
