Recurrence after coil embolization of intracranial aneurysms is generally attributed to coil compaction or aneurysm regrowth.1–5 Conventional follow-up imaging primarily evaluates residual lumen morphology but provides limited information about the spatial relationship between the remnant cavity and the outer contour of the aneurysm dome, which is critical for detecting dome-remnant dissociation.

MR cisternography enables delineation of the aneurysm outer surface independently of intraluminal signal, whereas silent MRA has been shown to improve visualization of slow intra-aneurysmal flow after coil embolization.6–8 Fusion imaging of these modalities therefore allows simultaneous evaluation of dome morphology and residual flow.

In this report, we describe serial imaging findings demonstrating spatial dissociation between the coiled dome and residual cavity after coil embolization of a basilar tip aneurysm and discuss its potential implications for early detection of aneurysm recurrence.

Illustrative Case

A woman in her 70s with an unruptured basilar tip aneurysm underwent coil embolization. The aneurysm measured 14.4 × 15.2 × 15.0 mm with a neck of 11.4 × 6.5 mm. A Target XL-360 coil (Stryker) was used for framing, and 13 coils were deployed, achieving a volume embolic rate of 23.4%. Final angiography demonstrated near-complete occlusion with a minimal residual neck (Fig. 1).

Immediate postembolization angiograms of the basilar tip aneurysm. Anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) projections demonstrating near-complete occlusion after coil embolization. The minimal remnant cavity at the neck is indicated by arrows.

MR cisternography was performed on a 3T MR system (Signa Pioneer, GE HealthCare) using heavily T2-weighted 3D fast spin-echo imaging with TR/TE 1900/100 msec, field of view 180 × 180 mm, matrix 356 × 256, section thickness 1.2 mm, number of excitations 1, bandwidth 31.25 kHz, echo-train length 128, and acquisition time 5 minutes 53 seconds, yielding 60 contiguous axial source images. Silent MRA was then acquired using a Silent Scan sequence with TR/TE 880/0.016 msec, flip angle 5°, field of view 180 × 180 mm, matrix 200 × 200, spokes per segment 320, section thickness 1.0 mm, number of excitations 1.5, bandwidth 31.25 kHz, and acquisition time 12 minutes 48 seconds, yielding 400 contiguous axial source images. All images were obtained without contrast administration.

Dome volume was calculated on the MR cisternography source images by serial masking of the dome contour on each slice. Voxel volume was determined from the in-plane pixel size and slice pitch, and total dome volume was calculated as voxel count multiplied by voxel volume. Each value represents the mean of three independent measurements.

Follow-up imaging with serial MR cisternography and silent MRA was performed at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. MR cisternography demonstrated gradual dome enlargement and contour irregularity, whereas silent MRA showed a small but persistent remnant cavity. Because of declining renal function, retreatment was deferred. The patient remained asymptomatic and died 2 years later of an unrelated condition.

Patient-specific computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed using vascular geometries reconstructed from silent MRA datasets and analyzed with commercial software (Hemoscope version 1.4, EBM and AMIN Corp.), as previously reported.9 Remnant morphology indices, including remnant volume, neck size, depth, surface area, and aspect ratio, were calculated. Hemodynamic analysis included the number of inflow peak sites, the flow rate ratio; mean, minimum, and maximum wall shear stress (WSS) magnitude ratios, and the temporal variability of instantaneous WSS magnitude and direction, as previously described.10–14

Results

Serial MR cisternography demonstrated progressive enlargement of the aneurysmal dome over the 2-year follow-up period (Fig. 2). Subtle contour irregularity was visible at 6 months, followed by localized dome protrusion at 12–18 months and marked enlargement at 24 months. Dome volume increased from 1933.3 mm3 immediately after treatment to 7880.0 mm3 at 24 months (Table 1).

Silent MRA consistently demonstrated a small remnant cavity along the neck plane (Fig. 3). Although the remnant cavity increased modestly, it remained small, representing only 3.4%–8.8% of dome volume (Table 1), and its distribution along the neck plane was uneven, suggesting a component of remnant drift. However, remnant drift alone is insufficient to explain the observed morphological progression.

Multiple inflow peak sites were identified along the neck plane, increasing from 3 at 6 months to 7 at 18 months. CFD demonstrated persistent inflow coupling between the basilar artery and the remnant cavity throughout follow-up. The flow rate ratio increased gradually over time, and the maximum WSS ratio reached 3.6 at 24 months (Table 2). These hemodynamic changes preceded substantial dome enlargement and were consistent with previously reported associations between elevated WSS and aneurysm growth.10–12

Discussion

The present case demonstrates spatial dissociation between the coiled aneurysm dome and the residual intra-aneurysmal cavity following coil embolization. This finding was visualized using fusion imaging of MR cisternography and silent MRA, which enabled simultaneous evaluation of the outer contour of the aneurysm wall and residual flow. Such spatial information cannot be obtained using conventional angiographic techniques alone.

Previous studies have primarily attributed aneurysm recurrence after coil embolization to coil compaction or aneurysm regrowth.1–5 However, the present observations suggest that asymmetric remodeling of the residual cavity may precede overt angiographic recurrence. In this regard, dome-remnant dissociation may represent a distinct morphological pathway of postcoiling progression.

In the present case, fusion imaging of MR cisternography and silent MRA enabled simultaneous assessment of dome morphology and persistent remnant flow, allowing visualization of spatial dissociation between the coiled dome and the residual cavity that could not be detected with conventional angiographic techniques alone.6–9 This capability may be particularly important when the remnant cavity remains small but continues to transmit persistent hemodynamic energy to the aneurysm wall, potentially contributing to asymmetric dome remodeling.

In this case, CFD suggested redistribution of intra-aneurysmal flow following coil embolization, with migration of the inflow impingement region along the aneurysm wall. Similar hemodynamic mechanisms have been associated with aneurysm growth and rupture in previous CFD studies.10–14 Repeated exposure to shifting hemodynamic stress may contribute to localized wall vulnerability and delayed dome remodeling, even when remnant enlargement itself is limited.

In addition, mechanical constraints imposed by adjacent anatomical structures may influence deformation of the aneurysm dome. Perianeurysmal contact with surrounding tissues may restrict expansion at contact sites while permitting deformation in adjacent free regions, resulting in redistribution of vascular wall stress. Such interactions between hemodynamic redistribution and external mechanical constraint have been implicated in aneurysm wall remodeling in recent imaging-based investigations.9,15,16

Although this report describes a single case, these findings suggest that dome-remnant dissociation detected primarily by MR cisternography, in combination with silent MRA fusion imaging, may represent an early imaging marker of asymmetric hemodynamic redistribution preceding aneurysm recurrence. Further studies are needed to determine whether dome-remnant dissociation can serve as a predictive marker for delayed recurrence in larger cohorts.

Observations

Serial MR cisternography demonstrated progressive enlargement and contour remodeling of the aneurysm dome despite a small residual cavity along the neck plane. Silent MRA confirmed persistent remnant flow, and CFD revealed sustained inflow coupling with evolving WSS patterns preceding dome enlargement. These findings indicate that dome remodeling revealed by MR cisternography, rather than remnant cavity expansion alone, contributed to aneurysm progression in this basilar tip case.

Lessons

Postcoiling aneurysm behavior cannot be assessed solely by remnant cavity size as evaluated with silent MRA. MR cisternography can reveal subtle dome remodeling that may precede angiographic recurrence, and even small remnant cavities may transmit sufficient hemodynamic stress to drive delayed dome enlargement. Dome-remnant dissociation represents a recurrence pathway distinct from conventional remnant drift and highlights the importance of evaluating dome morphology in addition to remnant cavity size during follow-up. Recognition of dome-remnant dissociation may therefore help identify aneurysms at risk for delayed recurrence after coil embolization.
