Spermidine in Alzheimer’s Disease: Evidence from Animal Models and Human Studies
Authors: Francesco Angelucci, Jiří Cerman, Jana Amlerova, Katerina Sheardova, Jan Pavlik, Jakub Hort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpermidine is a naturally occurring polyamine involved in multiple cellular processes, including growth regulation, protein translation, and autophagy. Increasing attention has been devoted to its potential neuroprotective effects, particularly in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by β-amyloid and phosphorylated tau accumulation, synaptic dysfunction, and progressive neuronal loss. In this narrative review, we examine potential mechanisms through which spermidine may influence AD pathophysiology and summarize available preclinical and clinical evidence. Preclinical studies indicate that spermidine induces autophagy, a key cellular clearance pathway responsible for removing damaged organelles and aggregated proteins. Because impaired neuronal autophagy contributes to the accumulation of β-amyloid and tau in AD, increasing intracellular spermidine levels may enhance the degradation of these toxic species. In addition, spermidine exhibits anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, attenuates microglial activation, and supports mitochondrial function. In animal models of AD and brain aging, spermidine administration has been associated with improvements in cognitive performance and synaptic function. However, human clinical evidence remains limited and largely inconclusive. Observational studies suggest associations between higher dietary spermidine intake and better cognitive outcomes, but do not establish causality. Randomized clinical trials to date are few, include small and heterogeneous populations, and have not demonstrated consistent effects on primary cognitive endpoints. Overall, spermidine represents a biologically plausible modulator of pathways relevant to neurodegeneration, but translation of preclinical findings into clinical benefit remains uncertain. Current evidence is insufficient to support its use as a therapeutic or preventive intervention in AD, and further well-designed clinical studies are required to clarify its efficacy and mechanisms of action.

Introduction

Spermidine is a natural polyamine, a small organic molecule containing amino groups (–NH2).1

It is ubiquitously present in all living cells and was originally isolated from seminal fluid, from which its name is derived. Spermidine is also obtained through the diet and can be found in a variety of foods, including sprouted grains, legumes, broccoli, cauliflower, mushrooms, and aged cheeses.2

Spermidine exerts multiple biological functions, including stabilization of DNA and RNA structures, regulation of cell growth and proliferation, and participation in cellular stress responses. Notably, spermidine plays a key role in autophagy, a conserved cellular process responsible for the degradation and recycling of damaged organelles and proteins, thereby contributing to cellular homeostasis.3

In recent years, spermidine has attracted increasing scientific interest as a potential modulator of aging-related processes. Evidence from animal models and epidemiological studies in humans suggests an association between spermidine intake and increased longevity, as well as beneficial effects on cardiovascular, neurological, and immune system function.4

This manuscript is a narrative review that summarizes current evidence on the role of spermidine in Alzheimer’s disease, with a focus on mechanistic pathways, preclinical models, and available human data. Relevant literature was identified through searches of PubMed database, with no time limits, with the terms Spermidine and Alzheimer’s disease used alone or in combination with the following terms: human, animal models, in vitro models, diet, clinical trials and assumption.

Several recent reviews have addressed the general role of spermidine in aging, autophagy, and systemic health. However, a focused synthesis integrating experimental findings from Alzheimer’s disease–relevant models with emerging clinical data remains limited. The aim of this review is therefore to provide a structured overview of the potential role of spermidine in AD pathophysiology, with particular emphasis on the relationship between mechanistic insights and translational evidence.

Spermidine in Brain

Accumulating evidence indicates that spermidine plays an important role in brain physiology and neuronal function. Consequently, spermidine has been proposed as a potential modulator in neurological disorders characterized by neurodegeneration, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD).5,6

Summary of Selected Preclinical Studies Evaluating Spermidine in Models Relevant to Neurodegeneration

In neuronal cell systems, spermidine protects against cytotoxic stress by preserving autophagic flux and reducing apoptosis. In PC12 cells exposed to staurosporine, spermidine inhibits caspase-3 activation and prevents Beclin-1 cleavage, thereby maintaining autophagy-dependent cell survival.14 Similarly, in SH-SY5Y cells expressing mutant P301L tau, spermidine improves mitochondrial bioenergetics, increases ATP production and membrane potential, reduces reactive oxygen species, and restores impaired mitophagy.17

In vivo, spermidine exerts comparable effects on cellular homeostasis. In models of cerebral ischemia/reperfusion, spermidine reduces neuronal injury through inhibition of apoptosis and preservation of autophagic pathways.14 In aging models, including D-galactose-induced and naturally aged rats, spermidine reduces oxidative and inflammatory stress, improves mitochondrial function, and upregulates key autophagy-related genes.13

Collectively, these findings indicate that spermidine acts as a regulator of cellular quality control mechanisms, particularly autophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis. However, these models differ substantially in their relevance to Alzheimer’s disease, and most findings remain limited to preclinical systems.

Human Data

Direct evidence linking spermidine to neuronal autophagy in humans remains limited. Most available data are derived from observational studies, which report associations between higher dietary spermidine intake and improved cognitive outcomes. However, these findings do not establish causality, and the underlying biological mechanisms remain largely unverified in the human brain.2

Although ongoing and pilot studies are exploring whether spermidine supplementation can influence autophagy-related pathways, there is currently no robust clinical evidence demonstrating increased neuronal autophagy in humans following spermidine intake. Moreover, most human studies rely on dietary intake estimates or peripheral biomarkers, which may not accurately reflect central nervous system polyamine metabolism or autophagic activity.

Hypothesized Links Between Polyamine Dysregulation, Nucleolar Function, and Alzheimer’s Disease

The nucleolus is a specialized subnuclear compartment responsible for ribosomal RNA (rRNA) transcription and processing, assembly of ribosomal subunits, and coordination of multiple processes related to ribosome biogenesis and cellular stress responses. Beyond ribosome production, the nucleolus plays an important role in chromatin organization and epigenetic regulation.

Polyamines, including putrescine, spermidine, and spermine, are polycationic molecules that interact with negatively charged nucleic acids and chromatin. Through these interactions, they may contribute to the structural stabilization of rRNA, ribonucleoprotein complexes, and chromatin within and around the nucleolus.

Polyamine homeostasis is tightly regulated, and its disruption has been proposed to influence transcriptional activity, RNA structure, and chromatin organization. On this basis, it has been hypothesized that altered polyamine metabolism may contribute to nucleolar stress or dysfunction. Such mechanisms have been discussed in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), although direct experimental evidence remains limited.

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain potential links between polyamine dysregulation and AD-related processes. One model suggests that cellular stress may alter polyamine synthesis or recycling, potentially disrupting the balance between anionic RNAs and cationic polyamines within the nucleolus. This imbalance has been proposed to affect nucleolar dynamics and, indirectly, epigenetic regulation of genes located in nucleolus-associated domains, including APP, MAPT, and APOE.18

These alterations have been proposed to affect epigenetic regulation in peri-nucleolar chromatin, potentially influencing the expression of genes located in nucleolus-associated domains, including presenilin-1, APP, MAPT (tau), and APOE. The hypothesis further suggests indirect links to tau phosphorylation through altered S-adenosylmethionine availability and to cholinergic dysfunction via changes in acetyl-CoA metabolism.

In experimental models of tauopathy, alterations in polyamine metabolism have been described, including changes in the activity of spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase (SAT1). These observations have been interpreted as a “polyamine stress response,” which may, in principle, influence nucleolar homeostasis. However, direct evidence linking spermidine-driven polyamine imbalance to nucleolar dysfunction remains lacking.16

Additional hypotheses involve stress-induced transcription of repetitive elements, such as Alu sequences, which may compete for binding with nucleolar proteins, including nucleolin. This process has been proposed to affect peri-nucleolar chromatin organization and nucleolar integrity, although this mechanism remains speculative and has not been directly demonstrated in vivo.18

Current Limitations and Open Questions

Overall, current evidence linking polyamine dysregulation, nucleolar alterations, and Alzheimer’s disease is largely indirect and hypothesis-driven. Importantly, there is no direct experimental evidence that spermidine supplementation induces nucleolar disruption in vivo.

It also remains unclear under which conditions polyamine-related processes may shift from potentially protective effects, such as structural stabilization and enhanced autophagy, toward detrimental outcomes associated with nucleolar stress. Further experimental studies are required to clarify the relevance of these mechanisms in the context of human neurodegenerative disease.

Spermidine in Models of Alzheimer’s Disease and Brain Aging

Animal Models

The mechanistic effects of spermidine described above (Table 1) have been further explored in experimental models more directly related to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and brain aging.

In transgenic mouse models of amyloid pathology, such as APP/PS1 mice, spermidine administration has been associated with reduced levels of soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42, accompanied by decreased microglial activation and attenuation of inflammatory signaling.15 These findings suggest that spermidine may influence amyloid-related pathology through modulation of neuroinflammatory and autophagy-related pathways.

In models of accelerated brain aging, including SAMP8 mice, spermidine supplementation improves cognitive performance, including object recognition and exploratory behavior.12 These effects occur alongside reductions in oxidative stress and improvements in mitochondrial function, consistent with enhanced cellular quality control mechanisms as outlined above.

In tauopathy models, alterations in polyamine metabolism, including accumulation of acetylated polyamines, have been linked to tau pathology. Experimental modulation of polyamine metabolism, including regulation of spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase (SAT1), influences tau-related processes, although the direction and magnitude of these effects appear to depend on the specific biochemical context and polyamine species.16

Beyond individual experimental systems, multimodal approaches integrating animal models, invertebrate systems, and human observational data provide complementary insights. In such studies, spermidine has been shown to cross the blood–brain barrier, enhance eIF5A hypusination, and improve mitochondrial function and cognitive performance in animal models, while observational human data suggest an association between higher dietary spermidine intake and reduced risk of cognitive decline.2

Overall, while preclinical findings consistently support a neuroprotective profile of spermidine, the heterogeneity of experimental models and limited translational evidence warrant cautious interpretation.

Summary and Translational Considerations

In summary, spermidine exerts reproducible neuroprotective effects in animal models of Alzheimer’s disease, tauopathy, and brain aging, primarily through modulation of autophagy/mitophagy, mitochondrial function, and neuroinflammation. However, these effects appear to depend on the metabolic state of polyamines, as well as on dosage, duration of exposure, and disease context. Despite compelling preclinical evidence, clinical translation remains limited, and larger, well-controlled human studies are required to define the therapeutic relevance of spermidine in AD.

Taken together, preclinical and human evidence differ substantially in both scope and strength. In experimental models, spermidine consistently modulates key pathways relevant to neurodegeneration, including autophagy, mitochondrial function, and inflammatory responses, and in some cases is associated with improvements in cognitive performance. In contrast, human data remain limited and heterogeneous. Observational studies suggest associations between higher dietary spermidine intake and favorable cognitive outcomes, whereas interventional studies have not demonstrated consistent effects on primary cognitive endpoints. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in model systems, dosing, and biological complexity, as well as the absence of biomarker-based stratification in most clinical studies. Consequently, while preclinical findings provide a strong biological rationale, their translation into clinically meaningful outcomes remains uncertain.

Clinical Evidence of Spermidine in Humans

Clinical evidence on spermidine in the context of cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease remains limited and is derived primarily from observational studies and small interventional trials.

Observational data from prospective cohort studies in older adults, including individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD)19 suggest that higher dietary spermidine intake is associated with a lower risk of cognitive decline and better cognitive performance. While these findings are consistent with a potential neuroprotective role of spermidine, they remain associative and do not establish causality.

Interventional evidence is currently limited. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating spermidine supplementation have generally included small sample sizes and heterogeneous populations (Table 2). The largest study to date, the SmartAge trial, enrolled older adults with SCD but without biomarker-based stratification for Alzheimer’s disease. Participants received spermidine supplementation for 12 months; however, the primary endpoint (global memory performance) did not differ significantly between the treatment and placebo groups. Secondary and exploratory analyses suggested potential effects on selected cognitive subdomains and inflammatory markers, but these findings require independent confirmation.20 The absence of biomarker-based enrichment likely increased etiological heterogeneity and may have limited the ability to detect disease-specific effects. Randomized Clinical Trials on Oral Spermidine Administration

Additional pilot and exploratory studies, including ongoing trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, are evaluating different dosing regimens and formulations. However, large, multicenter RCTs with clearly defined clinical endpoints and biomarker integration are still lacking. Available data suggest that spermidine supplementation (eg., approximately 24–40 mg/day) is generally well tolerated, although its impact on systemic levels and, importantly, brain bioavailability remains insufficiently characterized.21

Studies assessing circulating spermidine levels and biomarkers have yielded mixed results. Several investigations report nonlinear or even inverse associations between serum spermidine concentrations and cognitive or neuroimaging outcomes. These findings may reflect compensatory metabolic responses, residual confounding, or the limited ability of peripheral measurements to capture central nervous system polyamine homeostasis.22

Overall, while there is substantial observational evidence and a strong biological rationale supporting a potential role of spermidine in brain health, experimental clinical evidence in humans remains insufficient to support its use as a therapeutic or preventive intervention for Alzheimer’s disease. Large randomized clinical trials with robust, disease-specific clinical endpoints in overt AD are still lacking or currently ongoing.

Overall, while observational data and biological rationale support a potential role of spermidine in brain aging, current clinical evidence is insufficient to support its use as a therapeutic or preventive intervention for Alzheimer’s disease. Further well-designed randomized trials incorporating disease-specific endpoints and biomarker stratification are required to clarify its clinical relevance.

Implications for Preventive or Therapeutic Strategies

If dysregulation of spermidine and related polyamines contributes to nucleolar stress, this process may represent one of several hypothetical components of the pathogenic cascade in neurodegenerative diseases. Proposed downstream effects include alterations in ribosomal RNA synthesis, ribosome biogenesis, and translational homeostasis, which could, in principle, contribute to protein misfolding and neuronal dysfunction.

In addition, altered polyamine homeostasis has been hypothesized to influence epigenetic regulation, particularly within peri-nucleolar chromatin domains. Such effects may affect the expression of genes implicated in Alzheimer’s disease, including those related to tau and amyloid precursor protein (APP) metabolism, although these mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

From a conceptual perspective, regulation of polyamine metabolism has been discussed as a potential factor in maintaining nucleolar and cellular homeostasis. However, these considerations remain theoretical, and there is currently no experimental or clinical evidence supporting targeted modulation of spermidine levels as a preventive or therapeutic strategy in AD.

Within this framework, two non-mutually exclusive strategies have been proposed: Preventive approaches: Optimization of dietary polyamine intake may contribute to maintenance of cognitive health and potentially delay the onset of cognitive decline, particularly in individuals at increased risk (eg., advanced age, genetic susceptibility, or family history of Alzheimer’s disease).Complementary therapeutic approaches: Spermidine may hypothetically serve as an adjunct to established pharmacological therapies by supporting cellular proteostasis, autophagy-related clearance mechanisms, and mitochondrial function.

Dietary Spermidine Intake and Bioavailability

Spermidine is present in foods either in free form or bound to proteins and phospholipids.23 During digestion, particularly in the stomach and small intestine, enzymatic processes release spermidine from these complexes, enabling subsequent intestinal absorption. Absorption occurs predominantly in the small intestine, including the jejunum and ileum, via a combination of passive transport mechanisms and specific polyamine transport systems that also recognize structurally related molecules such as putrescine and spermine.23 Estimates of dietary spermidine bioavailability range from approximately 60% to 90%, depending on food matrix composition and intestinal physiological state.

Following absorption, spermidine enters the portal circulation and undergoes first-pass hepatic metabolism, where it may be utilized by hepatocytes, converted into other polyamines (eg., spermine), or released into the systemic circulation. From there, spermidine is distributed to peripheral tissues, where it participates in cellular processes including autophagy, nucleic acid stabilization, and protein synthesis.

The intestinal microbiota also contributes substantially to systemic spermidine availability. Certain gut bacterial taxa are capable of endogenous spermidine production, and dietary patterns that support a diverse and metabolically active microbiota—such as diets rich in fiber, polyphenols, and fermented foods—may enhance endogenous polyamine levels. Conversely, intestinal dysbiosis may impair spermidine production and absorption, further complicating the relationship between dietary intake, circulating levels, and tissue bioavailability.24

Taken together, current evidence supports spermidine as a context-dependent modulator of cellular homeostasis and brain aging rather than a validated therapeutic intervention for Alzheimer’s disease.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Spermidine represents a biologically relevant modulator of cellular homeostasis, with reproducible effects on autophagy, mitochondrial function, neuroinflammatory pathways, and cellular stress responses across experimental systems. These mechanisms are implicated in neurodegenerative processes relevant to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and provide a coherent biological rationale for further investigation in the context of brain aging and neurodegeneration.

At present, however, evidence supporting a role of spermidine in AD remains predominantly preclinical. While observational studies and early clinical investigations suggest potential associations with cognitive outcomes, causal relationships and disease-modifying effects have not been established. Translation into clinical practice will require adequately powered, well-controlled randomized trials incorporating disease-specific endpoints and biomarker-based stratification, including amyloid and tau imaging, cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, and measures of polyamine metabolism.

Importantly, current data do not support the use of spermidine as a therapeutic or preventive intervention in AD. Any potential role in this context should be considered investigational and requires rigorous clinical validation.

Future research should focus on clarifying the relationship between peripheral and central polyamine metabolism, defining optimal dosing strategies, and evaluating safety across different clinical populations. In addition, further studies are needed to determine whether spermidine-related pathways may serve as biomarkers of brain aging or targets for modulation within broader, multimodal therapeutic strategies.
