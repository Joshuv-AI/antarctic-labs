ABSTRACT
This document serves as an interim report to summarize sample analyses performed by
Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and Los Alamos National Laboratory
(LANL) on a series of five nuclear forensics samples provided by the Institute of Nuclear
Physics (INP) in the Republic of Kazakhstan. The sample set contains four uranium oxide
powders and one low-enriched uranium fuel pellet. The samples were provided as part of a
broader collaboration that involved a set of joint sample analyses conducted by INP and the US
National Laboratories. The joint analyses are being conducted using well-developed analytical
plans. These activities are designed to support the advancement of nuclear forensic science and
capacity building in all three institutes in both countries. This report builds upon an earlier
preliminary summary of the joint interactions [1] and will be augmented by a final report. The
final report will summarize the results and value of all sample analyses performed at the three
institutes (INP, LLNL, and LANL). The final report will also provide an intercomparison of the
results, analytical methods and best practices employed, as well as outline future collaborative
nuclear forensics activities that are being developed in the Kazakhstan region.

1 INTRODUCTION
Over the last few decades, the field of nuclear forensics has become an essential component in
international efforts to combat illicit trafficking of nuclear and radiological materials. Nuclear
forensics relates to the application of scientific methods and techniques to characterize the
physical nature and chemical composition of an unknown nuclear material. This information
may allow for the age, origin, processing history, and suspected use of a sample to be identified,
catalogued in specially-designed libraries [2], and shared with law enforcement and related
national agencies responsible for managing interdicted materials found outside of normal nuclear
regulatory controls [3].
For a given material sample, the range of techniques that can be used in a nuclear forensics
analysis is diverse, in some cases complex and may yield fairly distinctive characteristics
associated with a given material sample. An analytical plan for a sample of unknown
composition and origin may encompass a sequence of non-destructive and destructive
assessments to define its gross physical and chemical features, measure its radiation emissions,
and identify its fundamental elemental and isotopic composition. Analyses may begin, for
example, with simpler assessments such as visual inspection and characterization, optical
microscopy, mass density and weight, and basic dosimetry. More complex analyses may follow,
such as alpha and gamma spectrometry, scanning electron microscopy, energy dispersive X-ray
analysis, and mass spectrometry [3].
Different laboratories may have differing technical capabilities and analytical expertise that may
lead to different analytical approaches for a sample. They may have varying levels of experience
in the area of nuclear forensics, specifically as it relates to defining scientifically and legally
defensible analyses and work-flows, maintaining custody of samples, and effectively interfacing
with police and legal authorities in the overall effort. In an international arena, they may also be
subject to different legal requirements and processes in effect in individual countries.
Below, we report on a series of ongoing nuclear forensics collaborations between the Institute of
Nuclear Physics (INP) in the Republic of Kazakhstan, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
(LLNL), and Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) in the United States. The INP is a
leading nuclear forensics laboratory in the Republic of Kazakhstan responsible for the analysis of
samples found outside of regulatory control in the country. Kazakhstan itself is the world's
largest producer of mined uranium, accounting for almost 50% of annual world production and is
the most economically dominant country in the Central Asia region. The INP has been a long-
standing partner of the U.S. Department of Energy, National Nuclear Security Administration's
Office of Global Material Security (DOE/NNSA/GMS) and is an active member of the
international nuclear forensics community, and the Nuclear Forensics International Technical
Working Group (ITWG) [4]. A portion of the interactions between the U.S. and Kazakhstan
laboratories are coordinated through the International Science and Technology Center (ISTC)
and GMS and involve joint, collaborative analyses of nuclear material samples exchanged
between the labs. Here, we report on ongoing work with a sample set provided to LLNL and
LANL by INP.
1.1 Role of a Joint Sample Analysis
A collaborative, joint sample analysis between different laboratories can serve several different
purposes. It allows the participating laboratories to employ similar, as well as complementary

 4

techniques to learn more about these nuclear forensic materials and the methods and best work-
flow practices used to handle, analyze, interpret, and catalog their characteristics [1, 5]. It
provides opportunities for the participants to learn, practice, and demonstrate competence with
new or existing analytical techniques. It also provides opportunities for a laboratory, or a
network of laboratories, to demonstrate continued competence in performing specific types of
analyses, as through a regular performance evaluation (quality assurance) program (PEP).
1.2 Current Analytical Exercise
The current analytical exercise involves 5 samples provided to LLNL and LANL by INP. The
sample set contains four uranium-bearing powders and one low-enriched uranium fuel pellet, all
of unknown origin and exact composition (Figures 1.1 and 3.1). The four powder samples are
denoted by UL3, UL4, UL6, and UL7 and the pellet sample is denoted by PM7-2. Samples UL3,
UL4, UL6 are comprised of approximately 4 g of a yellow-colored powder, sample UL7
represents approximately 4 g of black-colored powder, and sample PM7-2 is a 4.8 g solid,
cylindrically-shaped pellet. INP retained additional portions of the same powders for their own
analyses and a separate but similar pellet sample (PM7-1). All samples were originally shipped
to LLNL. Subsequently, aliquots of the four powder samples were shared with LANL for their
independent analysis. As described below, it was decided (for now) to not subject the pellet
sample to any destructive types of analysis, which prohibited breaking it up to share with LANL.

The joint analysis of these materials is being conducted by INP and the US labs using well-
developed analytical plans and standard operating procedures at each institution. The analytical

individual samples. These may differ among the labs because of differing or limited capabilities
and may differ among the samples because they require specific or unique approaches for

2 LLNL AND LANL ANALYTICAL PLANS
LLNL and LANL designed and completed a sequence of non-destructive and destructive
analyses on the INP samples, as summarized in their analytical plans shown in Table 2.1. These
are briefly summarized below and may be supplemented by additional analyses in the near future
if funding and time allow. Results and conclusions drawn from these analyses are provided in
subsequent sections of this report below.
2.1 LLNL Analyses
LLNL conducted preliminary sample investigations on all samples that included:
 • Visual Inspection and Photographic Documentation as a means to record and describe
 macroscopic features and color of the samples.
 • Weighing and Mensuration as a means to measure sample masses and/or dimensions.
 • Optical Microscopy and 3D Imaging as a means to describe, visualize, measure, or
 identify surface morphology, grain size, and material heterogeneity of the samples.
In addition, the pellet sample (alone) was subjected to a Gas Pycnometry measurement to
determine its mass density.
The pellet sample (PM7-2) and four powder samples (UL3, UL4, UL6, and UL7) were then
subjected to three non-destructive assessments comprised of:
 • Multi-Group Analysis for Uranium (MGAU) Gamma Spectrometry to identify uranium
 isotopic ratios and fission product abundance in the samples.
 • Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), to identify microscale structure and
 elemental composition of the samples.
 • X-ray Diffraction (XRD) to characterize material crystallography and composition of the
 samples.
Next, the four powder samples (UL3, UL4, UL6, and UL7) were subjected to a sequence of
destructive assessments:
 • Thermal Gravimetric Moisture Analysis was applied to determine the water content
 (wt %) of the powder samples.
 • An Elemental Analyzer, High Vacuum Fluorination System, and Davies- Gray Titration
 technique were used to conduct elemental analyses for carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, oxygen
 and uranium.
 • Isotope-Ratio Mass Spectrometry (IRMS), Multi Collector Inductively Coupled Plasma
 Mass Spectrometry (MC-ICP-MS), and Thermal Ionization Mass Spectrometry (TIMS)
 were used to determine isotope ratios for carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, oxygen, uranium, lead,
 and strontium.
 • High Resolution Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (HR-ICP-MS) was
 used to perform a full quantitative analysis of 58 trace elements in each sample.
It was decided to not subject the pellet sample (PM7-2) to any further destructive analyses at this
time. This decision may be revisited in the future.
Summaries and results of these analyses will be presented separately below.

destructive and destructive investigations (Table 2.1). Some of these were similar to the LLNL

Nondestructive tests conducted by LANL included:

Destructive tests conducted by LANL included:
 • Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS), as used
 for quantitative elemental and isotopic analysis of solid samples.

3.3 Optical Microscopy
Optical microscopy assessments were performed on the pellet sample (PM7-2) only. Physical
and visible morphological features were documented using an optical microscope (Leica M165
optical microscope with direct and oblique LED illumination) with the pellet positioned under
the microscope in an uncovered container.
The pellet has a hole through the center of its longest axis and chamfers on each circular side.
One circular side displays a significant defect while the other does not (Figure 3.3). This defect is
more visible in the light microscope with oblique illumination. A few oblong defects are
apparent on the lateral side however these are shallower than the larger defect (Figure 3.4).
Higher magnification images show smaller defects, mostly on the circular faces and chamfers
(Figure 3.5). Each end of the lateral hole appears relatively uniform (Figure 3.6).

3.6 MGAU Gamma-Spectrometry
The pellet and powder samples were counted with a planar HPGe detector, and a Multi Group
Analysis for Uranium (MGAU) screening assessment was performed to estimate uranium
isotopic composition in all samples using Canberra MGAU software (v4.2). The current results
are derived from fitting a small energy range (0-300 keV) and applying the ratios of certain
peaks to determine enrichment percentage (Please refer to Table 3.3). Samples with lower
234
 U/235U values may require longer counting to get more accurate results. The observed mass
fractions of 234U, 235U, and 238U in the powder samples are somewhat different than their natural
abundance counterparts (< 0.01, 0.7, and 99.3, respectively) but should be regarded as
approximate until additional analyses with longer counting times are available. For comparison
purposes, activity ratios for 235U/238U were calculated from the mean mass fraction results.
3.7 Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)
Microstructural characteristics and elemental composition of the pellet and powder samples were
studied using a Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) analysis system. These analyses will be summarized separately.
3.7.1 Observation of Pellet Sample PM-7
The sample was uncoated and mounted on a stub with conductive copper tape, allowing
secondary electron and backscattered electron images to be acquired. Secondary electron
imaging is sensitive to surface topography while backscattered electron imaging is sensitive to
chemical composition and density of the material being analyzed. Micromorphological features,
defects and impurities, as well as semi-quantitative chemical compositions, were identified at
several surficial locations on the pellet sample.

 Figure 3.12: Electron images of the lateral side of PM7-2; secondary (left) and backscattered (right). Notice
 the flatted scraping marks almost parallel in the images.

Figure 3.13: Electron images of the flat side of PM7-2; secondary (left) and backscattered (right) showing the
 grain structure.

 Figure 3.14: Backscattered electron images of the surface of PM7-2 showing pores throughout; lateral side

The lateral/curved sides of the pellet showed a rougher surface (Figure 3.12) than the flat/circular
surfaces (Figure 3.13), possibly from the differences in machining of the two surfaces. At higher
magnification, both surfaces show pores throughout, approximately 500 nm in diameter and
smaller (Figure 3.14). These pores are most likely evidence of escaping gases from a high
temperature process such as sintering. Examination of the defect areas (Figure 3.15) near the flat
surfaces showed little difference in morphology from the neighboring, intact surfaces.

Secondary electron and backscattered electron images of the samples were acquired. Localized
elemental analysis via an EDS overscan confirmed the major amounts of uranium in all spectra
(> 80 wt%), with minor amounts of oxygen and carbon (Figure 3.16). Trace amounts of C, Na,
Al, Mg, and Si were also identified, possibly resulting from surface contaminants from shipping
and handling. The results in Figure 3.16 have been converted to atomic concentrations for
inclusion in Table 3.4.

 Figure 3.16: Overscan spectrum from the lateral side of PM7-2 showing a typical bulk composition (wt %)

A few particles located on the top surface were slightly higher in sodium than the bulk (minor as
compared to trace in the bulk). Spectra from the lateral defect areas were slightly higher in Al

instrument (e.g., forceps). Using a carbon adhesive tab, a tape lift was applied to all surfaces of
the pellet. Particles from the lift were similar in morphology to the bulk (Figure 3.18), with some
having the pore structure previously identified. Most particles had higher oxygen than the bulk
surface, an indicator or oxidation products, but no additional minor or trace elements.

3.7.2 Powder Sample Introduction
All 4 powder samples were initially mechanically crushed in an agate mortar and pestle to break
the agglomerates and dispersed/mounted onto Aluminum-SEM stubs with carbon adhesive tabs.
They were then analyzed under a SEM without any coating, operating at 20 kV. Optical,
secondary electron, and backscattered electron images showing color and morphology of four
powder materials are shown in Figure 3.19.
As will be discussed below, particle morphology was found to vary between samples. Notably,
sample UL7 (black powder) showed a sintering grain structure of UO2 pellet surface, suggesting
its being derived originally from a fuel pellet. Results of SEM and EDS analyses match the X-
Ray Diffraction results presented in a subsequent section.

3.7.3 Observation of Powder Sample UL3

3.9 Thermal Gravimetric Moisture Analysis
A Thermal Gravimetric Moisture Analysis (TGA) was conducted to determine the water content
(in wt %) of the four powder samples. A TA Discovery thermogravimetric analyzer with an
infrared-heated furnace (TA Instruments) was used in the process. Samples of 10 mg (± 1 mg)
were heated in the TGA instrument at constant rate of 10 °C per minute from 35 °C to 500 °C,
and continuously weighed. Cumulative reductions in mass were measured over two intervals: (1)
between 35 and 110 °C and (2) between 110 and 500 °C. These reductions were attributed to
water loss as vapor and reported for each interval as a wt %. These intervals basically represent
water losses first from (1) adsorbed water on the surface of particles in the 35 and 110 °C
interval, and (2) molecularly bound water in the 110 and 500 °C interval. Sample UL3 had the
greatest water content (11.71 %) while UL7 had the lowest (0.32 %). Results are tabulated in
Table 3.5.

4.4 X-ray Computed Microtomography (XCT)
4.4.1 Technique Overview
X-ray computed microtomography (XCT) is used to produce a three-dimensional computer
model of the sample where contrast in the resulting image is based on how strongly various
sample components absorb and scatter X-rays. The volume of the analyzed material is sampled
in units known as voxels, or three-dimensional pixels. In the resulting image, dark areas
correspond to regions of the material where X-rays are transmitted through the sample (pockets
of air appear black) and bright areas correspond to regions where X-rays are attenuated, or
absorbed, by the material due to higher density and thickness. The samples are analyzed from
multiple angles, and an algorithm is used to reconstruct slices of tomographic images into a 3-D
visualization. Ultimately, this technique is used to determine sample volume and assess material
homogeneity.
4.4.2 Sample Preparation
Samples UL3, UL4, UL6 and UL7 were analyzed by XCT at E-6 (Non-destructive Testing and
Evaluation) in their original glass vials with no additional sample preparation.
4.4.3 Methods
XCT analyses for all samples were performed using a North Star Imaging X5000 system with a
Perkin Elmer 1622 detector. Data were collected using the following instrument parameters: 445
kV, 400 µm focal spot size, focal spot mode of “fine” and 1500 µA current; and the following
detector parameters: 200 x 200 µm pixel pitch, 0.5 pF gain, binning of 2x2, and a 2-fps frame
rate. The data were segmented and the number of pixels of “material” were counted with a
calculated nominal pixel size of 133.4 µm. A 1” calibrated steel ball was used to calculate pixel
size which was determined to be 132.29 ± 0.68 µm (lower than the nominal). The measured pixel
size of 132.29 µm was multiplied by the number of pixels in the segmentation to calculate the
volume. The resulting error using this method is ± 1-3% due to pixel uncertainty.

4.5.1 Technique Overview
Pycnometry measures the true skeletal volume of a material based on the displacement of inert
He gas and the Ideal Gas Laws. When combined with the mass of the sample (obtained
elsewhere using an analytical balance), pycnometry volume measurements can be used to
determine a sample’s density.
4.5.2 Gas Pycnometry Methods
Density analysis was conducted on subsample aliquots of these materials in order to reduce any
potential contamination during pycnometry measurements. The masses of these subsamples were
first measured. A calibrated Sartorius® analytical balance equipped with an antistatic ionizer was
used to determine the weight of the subsamples. A series of calibrated check weights and an
internal reference vial were weighed to verify the operation of the balance. To determine sample
weight, each subsample was transferred into a pre-weighed sample analysis sleeves used for the
pycnometer, which included specialized lids that prevent particle dispersal into the pycnometer.
Subsamples of these materials, still in their as as-received state, were transferred to the
pycnometry analysis sleeves using plastic spatulas cleaned using ethanol wipes. The sleeve and
sample were then weighed again; the sample mass was subsequently determined by difference.
The uncertainty on the sample mass was calculated by addition of two standard deviations (2
SD) of replicate masses of both the empty analysis sleeve and the sleeve plus sample in
quadrature. The subsample weights are shown in Table 4.2. Only one subsample aliquot per
sample was taken.
Pycnometry measurements were then made using a MicroMeritics® AccuPyc II 1345 10 cm3 gas
displacement pycnometry system. Ultra-high purity He gas (99.999% He) was used for gas
displacement within a 1 cm3 sample chamber insert. The pycnometer was calibrated using
volume-certified tungsten carbide spheres. Analysis sleeves containing subsamples of UL3, UL4,
UL6, and UL7 were inserted into the instrument and analyzed at room temperature using ASTM
Test Method D-6226 (1998) and the following parameters: 10 pre-analysis purges, a chamber
pressure of 19.5 psi, a purge pressure of 19.25 psi, and an equilibration rate of 0.0005 psi/second.
Each sample was measured five times for true skeletal volume from which the average value and
standard deviation were computed. Density calculations were performed using the quotient of the
averaged subsample mass and averaged true skeletal volume; uncertainty propagation (2 SD)
followed standard statistical treatment of independent variables. Measured skeletal volume
results of these subsamples are provided in Table 4.2.

4.6 Scanning Electron Microscopy and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy
 (SEM-EDS)
4.6.1 Technique Overview
Electron microscopy is routinely used to evaluate the size and morphology of materials in many
fields of study. Analyses are performed in a vacuum chamber by rastering a tightly focused beam
of high energy electrons onto a sample. Scattered, reflected, and secondary electrons are
detected and reconstructed into an image. Secondary electrons provide the highest fidelity
images, while backscattered electrons reveal ‘Z’ contrast, which shows materials with higher
atomic mass as brighter regions in images. In addition to imaging, when high energy electron
interactions occur with a surface, the relaxation of exited species can result in the emission of x-
rays with characteristic energies. These x-rays can be detected by an energy dispersive
spectrometer (EDS), which reveal the chemical composition of the materials being scanned with
detection limits down to tenths of an atomic percent.
4.6.2 Sample Preparation
For all samples, a small aliquot of each powder was placed into glass scintillation vials in 400
mL of methanol. The vials were ultrasonicated for 15 min to suspend particles in the methanol
and separate any large agglomerates. From each suspension, 200 µL of solution was pipetted
onto doped silicon wafers and dried on a hot plate set at 50 °C. This evenly dispersed and
adhered particles to the wafer surface. No carbon coating or additional sample preparation was
done prior to SEM analysis.

 44

4.6.3 SEM-EDS Methods
All scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses, including imaging and energy dispersive X-
ray spectroscopy (EDS), were done using an FEI Quanta 200F field emission SEM coupled with
an AMETEK EDAX Elite silicon drift EDS detector. All images and EDS measurements were
collected using an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, an FEI spot size setting of 4 (setting or
measuring beam current is not an option on this system), at a working distance of 10 mm and
with magnification varying depending on feature size. Both secondary electron (SE) and
backscatter electron (BSE) images were collected for all samples with contrast and brightness
adjusted to highlight sample morphology (SE) and Z-contrast (BSE). EDS measurements were
taken with an amplifier (amp) time of 3.84 µs for 30 seconds and were processed using
AMETEK EDAX APEX Advanced software. All resulting EDS results were processed using
EDAX APEX default eZAF quant with no external standardization. Particle sizes for all samples
were estimated using the measurement tool in the FEI SEM software to establish particle
dimensions. All EDS element maps were collected with the same SEM settings as spot analyses
but with the following EDS detector/software settings: amp time of 7.68 µS, resolution of 512 x
400, 32 frames, dwell time 200 µS, for a total analysis time of 25 min, 40 sec.

4.7 X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)
4.7.1 Technique Overview
X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) is a non-destructive analytical technique that offers insight into the
crystal structure and physical properties of materials. XRD was used to determine the phase
purity of the uranium powders.

 54

4.7.2 XRD Methods
Scans were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with unconditioned Cu radiation
and a 1-D Si(Li) Lynxeye detector with an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and 40 mA current.
Data was collected over 6 hours in the range of 10 to 80° 2θ, 0.02° step size, and 6 s per step.
The samples were spun at a rate of 15 rpm to mitigate preferential orientation of the crystals. Due
to the hazards of working with powdered uranium materials, the samples were mounted in
airtight domed polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) sample holders. Resulting spectra were
analyzed via MDI JADE pattern processing software.[6]
4.7.3 XRD Results
Sample UL3 was evaluated twice with a 9.5-month interval between analyses, denoted as UL3
t=0 and UL3 t=9.5. The diffraction patterns are shown in Figure 4.14. The initial scan, UL3 t=0,
is consistent with a high purity mixture of uranyl nitrate hexahydrate (UNH), UO2(NO3)2·6H2O,
and uranyl nitrate trihydrate (UNT), UO2(NO3)2·3H2O. The second scan, UL3 t=9.5, is
consistent with only the trihydrate phase. Peak splitting is additionally observed (16° 2θ, 25° 2θ,
etc.), which can occur due to crystalline phase transitions.
The diffraction patterns for UL4, UL6, and UL7 are shown in Figures 4.15, 4.16 and 4.17,
respectively. UL4 consists primarily of uranyl acetate dihydrate, C4H6O6U2·2H2O, with UNH as
a minor component phase. Notably, no crystallographic data beyond ~57° 2θ was available for
C4H6O6U2·2H2O, and thus, peaks beyond this range in the UL4 spectrum cannot be assigned.
Nonetheless, due to the lack of any additional minor peaks in the range of 10 to 57° 2θ, the
presence of any additional crystalline phases in sample UL4 were likely below the detection
limits of XRD.
Conversely, the complexity of the UL6 spectrum suggests a mixture of several crystalline
phases. UNH is likely present, in addition to β-UO2(OH)2, a dehydrated schoepite phase.
Additional significant peaks appear to correlate to ammonium uranyl nitrate,
(NH4)2(UO2)(NO3)4, where peaks from both the monoclinic and orthorhombic polymorphs align
with high intensity low angle peaks (10 to 25° 2θ) in the sample spectrum. Crystallographic data
for the full sample spectrum was similarly not available for β-UO2(OH)2 and the monoclinic
(NH4)2(UO2)(NO3)4 phase. Certainty in the peak assignments, particularly with the ammonium
uranyl nitrate phases, is limited due to the significant peak overlap along with unidentified peaks
at ~19 and 24.5° 2θ. This suggests significant additional minor component phases are likely
present in the UL6 sample.
The UL7 sample spectrum correlates to U3O8 as the primary phase, with minor component α-

4.8 Autoradiography
4.8.1 Technique Overview
Autoradiography is one of the oldest techniques for detecting radioactivity, dating back to Henri
Becquerel. These techniques have undergone extensive development since that time, but the
basic mechanisms remain the same: the radiation deposits energy into an emulsion resulting in a
chemical excitation. Current technology involves the use of Phosphor image plates (IP). The IPs
are made of a photostimulable phosphor crystal (mainly BaFBr:Eu2+), and the image is created
when incident radiation ionizes the Eu2+ to Eu3+. Fluorine atoms trap the liberated electrons into
what are known as F centers, where they will remain trapped until stimulated by laser light and
they recombine with the Eu3+ ions. This scintillating reunion prompts the emission 390 nm light,
which is detected (typically by a photo-multiplier tube or PMT), and the intensity of this light (or
the number of emissions at this wavelength) is proportional to the amount of radiation that was
incident on that location of the IP. [7]
4.8.2 Autoradiography Methods
For this work, the samples were placed on a BAS-MS (multipurpose standard) IP. The samples
were placed inside petri dishes and were placed atop the IP (Figure 4.19). The IPs were exposed
to the samples for 5- and 30-minutes intervals. Following the exposure, the IPs were processed
on a GE Typhoon FLA 7000 scanner in phosphor imaging mode, with a pixel size of 50 µm. A
total of 4 samples were imaged on the same 20x25” image plate. Both sets of IPs were scanned
using the GE Typhoon, with a PMT voltage of 675 V, and a 50 μm resolution. The samples were
all exposed on the same floor, and thus the images were all processed with the exact same
settings.

 58

4.8.3 Autoradiography Results
There was not sufficient activity in the samples for the 5 minutes exposure to provide any
information regarding the physical distribution of radioactive material in the sample. The 30-
minute exposures were, however, successful in providing insight into the physical distribution of
radioactivity in the samples (Figure 4.20). The radioactivity in UL3 and UL4 was uniformly
distributed across the material, while for UL6 and UL7 it was heavily concentrated in several
larger particles. While the distribution of the radioactive material varies among the four samples,
the overall activity measured by the autoradiography techniques was similar across the four
samples. The sample with the highest overall activity measured was UL4, with UL3 and UL6
having 95% and 94% percent less activity, respectively. Sample UL7 had the lowest activity, and
the integrated activity measured in the radiography was 11% lower than that of UL4.

4.9 HPGe Gamma Spectrometry
4.9.1 Technique Overview
Gamma spectrometry is a non-destructive analytical technique that provides qualitative and/or
quantitative analysis of radionuclides present in a sample through the detection of gamma-rays
emitted from the nuclear decays in the sample. This technique relies on the interaction of
gamma-rays in a semi-conductor material such as germanium. High purity germanium (HPGe)
detectors are typically used for nuclear forensics analyses as their high resolution allows the
detection of a wide range of gamma emitting radionuclides. The efficiency of the gamma
detector depends on the measurement geometry; therefore, gamma spectrometric analysis must
be performed in a specific measurement geometry in which the detector efficiency is calculated
to provide quantitative activity concentration measurements. For this analysis, a broad energy
germanium (BEGe) detector was used. Broad energy germanium detectors are capable of
detecting gamma rays in a very wide energy range.
Following the initial gamma spectrometric screening, an aliquot of solution from subsamples

4.10 Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS)
4.10.1 Technique Overview
Laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) is used for
quantitative elemental and isotopic analysis of solid samples. A pulsed laser is focused onto the
sample surface to ablate nano- to micrograms of sample material. Ablated particles and clusters
of sample material are transported by a carrier gas into an ICP-torch to be atomized and ionized
and subsequently undergo mass/charge separation by mass spectrometry. The laser focal spot
size, laser energy and laser repetition rate can be controlled to optimize the signal intensity and
spatial resolution, and to minimize sample damage. The spatially resolved analysis (permitted by
the laser focal spot) allows single-particle measurements and sample mapping.
4.10.2 Sample Preparation
For each sample, a small quantity (<10 mg) of powder was placed into pre-cleaned 25 mL

 61

Savillex™ vial. Using disposable plastic tweezers, a double-sided carbon adhesive disk (12.5
mm diameter, Ted Pella Inc.) was brushed around the inside of each sample vial to collect
particles. The adhesive disk was then adhered to a stainless-steel substrate (1” diameter) (with
the powder facing upwards) and placed inside an air-tight laser ablation cell (about 12” × 12” ×
4”) with internal XYZ stage. After the cell was filled with the four samples and two reference
materials, it was sealed shut and transported to the laser ablation instrument.
4.10.3 LA-ICP-MS Methods
All LA-ICP-MS analyses were conducted using an Applied Spectra Inc. Femto iX femtosecond
laser for ablation, and a Thermo Scientific Element XR single-collector ICP for analysis.
Utilizing a femtosecond laser for ablation minimizes the damage caused to the sample material,
resulting in less cratering and a smaller shock wave. This permits repeated analysis shots on
small particles, allowing for longer ICP integration times and improved precision. The laser
ablation cell is a two-volume sample chamber with a washout time of <50 ms. Carrier gases
helium and argon were supplied at 0.8 l/min and 1.0 l/min, respectively. Before connecting the
laser cell to the ICP, the ICP was tuned in liquid mode (i.e. with a spray chamber and
autosampler) to maximize signal intensity and stability. Correction factors were also calculated
for counting, analogue and faraday collection modes using standards of indium in nitric acid
(1ppm and 150ppm). After this, the laser ablation cell was connected and NIST standards
NIST610 and NIST612 were used to correct for mass offsets. Periodically throughout the
experiments, these NIST standards were re-analyzed to check and correct the mass offsets. A
correction factor was applied to the 238U signals in post-analysis as the faraday correction factor
was found to be unreliable. The need for a correction was identified when comparing our results
to those of thermal ionization mass spectrometry measurements (TIMS; the technique and
resulting data are outside the scope of this work and are excluded from this report). The
234
 U:235U ratio calculated by the LA-ICP-MS technique was very close to the TIMS results,
whereas the ratios of 234U:238U and 235U:238U were both around 9% higher than reported by
TIMS. We concluded that the 238U signal – having been recorded on the faraday detector – was
erroneously low, and a correction factor of 1.0967 was used to increase the value of the 238U
signals. This value was chosen as the average difference between the LA-ICP-MS and TIMS
ratios for 234U:238U and 235U:238U.
The repetition rate of the laser and the acquisition rate of the ICP were synchronized at 10 Hz, so
the transient signal intensity caused by each ablation shot was observed in its entirety. For all
experiments, the laser spot size was maintained at 30 µm, and the stage movement speed at 0.01
mm/s – creating a shot offset of 3.3%. With these settings, a pattern of laser shots was ablated
across each measured particle, with the aim of ablating as much of the particle surface as
possible, to increase the duration of collected signal. As the particle dimensions were non-
uniform, this led to different numbers of laser measurements being performed on each particle.
An example of patterns drawn across different sample particles is shown in Figure 4.21. Most
patterns seen here took between 25 and 40 seconds of analysis (250 to 400 data points per
particle). A subset of thorium and uranium isotopes (232Th, 234U, 235U and 238U) were analyzed
for each measurement. 230Th was also measured in a preliminary analysis but was below the limit
of quantification, and so it was excluded from future analysis.

5 SUMMARY OBSERVATIONS
This report describes a joint forensic study conducted by Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory (LLNL), Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL), and the Institute of Nuclear
Physics (INP) of Kazakhstan aimed to strengthen nuclear forensic capabilities and international
cooperation by analyzing 5 uranium-bearing materials of unknown provenance. The 5 materials
in the sample set included four uranium oxide powders (UL3, UL4, UL6, UL7) and one low-
enriched uranium fuel pellet (PM7-2) provided by the INP to LLNL and LANL for their mutual
and independent characterization. This report includes results provided by LLNL and LANL,
while a future update will include additional results provided by INP.
The overall rationale for the work stems from the need to develop and compare analytical
capabilities, methodologies, and best practices for characterizing unknown radiologic materials
across laboratories, fostering best practices and ensuring consistency in analytical plans and
results. This is vital for national and international nuclear security and law enforcement
activities, particularly in Kazakhstan given its status as the world’s largest uranium producer.
In the work complied here, a comprehensive suite of analytical methods was employed,
including non-destructive techniques such as visual inspection, weighing, optical microscopy, X-
ray computed microtomography (XCT), gas pycnometry, gamma spectrometry, X-ray diffraction
(XRD), scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS),
and autoradiography. Destructive techniques included thermal gravimetric analysis, elemental
analysis for C, N, S, and O, and various mass spectrometry methods for isotope ratios and trace
elements. Both laboratories found that the powder samples were depleted uranium compounds,
with ²³⁵U content well below natural levels and identified their major chemical phases—such as
uranyl nitrate hydrate, uranyl acetate dihydrate, and U₃O₈—using XRD. The four powder
samples exhibited notable differences in physical and chemical properties: UL3 was identified as
a fine, yellow powder predominantly composed of uranyl nitrate hydrate, with fragile
agglomerates and relatively high water content; UL4 consisted of coarser, crystalline yellow
grains, mainly uranyl acetate dihydrate, and displayed variable opacity and some quartz as a
minor phase; UL6 was a yellow powder with diverse particle shapes and phases, including
uranyl nitrate and possible ammonium uranyl nitrate, and showed higher sodium and sulfur
contamination; UL7 stood out as a black or dark brown powder, primarily U₃O₈ with minor
hydrated uranium oxides, and had the highest density and lowest water content, as well as
evidence of sintering grain structure similar to that of fuel pellets. The fuel pellet PM7-2 was
confirmed as low-enriched uranium dioxide, consistent with commercial nuclear fuel.
In general, the powder sample results from LLNL and LANL were broadly consistent, with both
labs observing similar physical and chemical characteristics, particle morphologies, isotope
ratios, and impurity profiles in the powders. Minor differences in analytical approach and sample
preparation were noted, but no significant discrepancies in findings were reported. This part of
the study demonstrated the effectiveness of collaborative, multi-laboratory nuclear forensics in
accurately characterizing diverse uranium materials. It is expected that the same level of
effectiveness and collaboration will be evident in the final part of the study when the INP results
are included.
