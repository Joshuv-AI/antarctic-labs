Sublinear Risk-Limiting Audits from Direct Ballot Selection and Statistical Ballot Manifests
Benjamin Fuller, Abigail Harrison, Alexander Russell

Abstract
Risk-limiting audits (RLAs) are post-election auditing procedures that rigorously guarantee a specified maximum probability that an incorrect electoral outcome will not be detected. Aside from ready access to physical ballots, known RLAs require a software-independent accounting of the sizes of each ballot batch, called a ballot manifest . While typical electoral procedures automatically provide rough estimates for batch sizes, even slight inaccuracies (commensurate with the margin of the contest under audit) completely invalidate conventional RLAs (Lindeman et al., EVT 2012). Thus, establishing a sufficiently accurate manifest often requires handling every ballot and can be the dominant cost of conducting the RLA. With the goal of providing audits with sub-linear efficiency across the full spectrum of election architectures, we propose two new risk-limiting techniques. The first is a statistical mechanism for ensuring that the batch sizes reported by an untrusted tabulation are, in fact, an accurate manifest; this effectively “bootstraps” from a rough manifest to an accurate one with sublinear effort. Secondly, to handle election architectures that do not provide efficient indexing of ballots by identifier (such as is provided by typical voter-facing tabulators), we propose a new class of RLAs called direct ballot selection . This method reverses the traditional comparison procedure and compares uniformly selected ballots against their cast vote records, therefore requiring a new statistical test for identifier duplication. Taken together, these techniques provide a striking reduction in the complexity of RLAs across a wide swath of election configurations and margins. Our two main findings are as follows: (1) The time to create a manifest can be drastically reduced with a modest increase in the number of ballots sampled in the audit. For California (1st in population in the United States) at a 3% margin, there is a reduction in the overall audit time for comparison, polling, and direct selection audits by factors of 400 400 , 20 20 , and 8 8 , respectively. (2) Direct ballot selection provides substantial improvement over state-of-the-art polling for small margins. For Connecticut (29th in population) at a 1% margin, it beats Minerva (USENIX Security 2022)/Providence (USENIX Security 2023) by 55% in ballot sample complexity.

Introduction

Large-scale elections are fundamental civic processes that are unusually challenging to analyze from a security perspective. They are complex hybrid systems, combining software, hardware, paper records, and human procedures at scale. Among the most important advances in election security has been the development of
risk-limiting audits
(RLAs), which provide strong guarantees in a natural security model.

: If the reported outcome of an election is incorrect, an RLA establishes a known, pre-specified maximum chance—called the

—that the audit will fail to detect the error.

A critical, defining property of RLAs is that they are
software independent
; in particular, they make no trust assumptions on ballot tabulators or other electronic equipment. This can be achieved by assuming that auditors have access to physical ballots that reflect the intent of the voters. RLAs are typically designed to further guarantee
transparency
; in other words, a third party observing the audit proceedings can verify the outcome of the audit.

RLAs call for auditors to hand count a collection of randomly selected ballots, with results that are incorporated into an appropriate statistical test. Thus, an important metric for evaluating the efficiency of an RLA is
sample complexity
: the total number of ballots that must be counted by hand in order to guarantee the desired risk limits. This topic has been given extensive attention in the literature and depends on both the audit method and the
margin

\mu
of the contest. (For a conventional two-candidate, first-past-the-post election, the margin is the difference between the vote totals for the candidates as a fraction of the total number of ballots cast.)

The prevailing methods have a further requirement: a
ballot manifest
. In the typical case where the ballots are organized into physical batches, a ballot manifest provides a determination of the size of each batch. Sample-efficient RLA methods are extremely sensitive to ballot manifest inaccuracies: in particular, multiplicative inaccuracies in the declared batch sizes of order
1\pm\delta
where
\delta\approx\mu
are sufficient to completely invalidate the audit (Lindeman et al., EVT 2012; see Appendix
D
).
As the demand for strict software independence rules out the use of electronic devices for manifest creation and high accuracy is mandatory, hand counting is the standard for manifest creation.
The time to create a manifest scales linearly with the total number of ballots in the election. In circumstances where the underlying statistical tests have moderate sample complexity, creating the ballot manifest dominates the time to conduct the audit.

An additional challenge arises for
ballot comparison audits
, which provide the best sample efficiency among known audit methods. These audits require a detailed “ballot-by-ballot” tabulation of the election, consisting of a comprehensive table of “cast vote record” (CVR) entries; an individual CVR declares the votes appearing on a particular ballot. Ballots are sampled by uniformly picking a CVR, identifying the individual cast ballot associated with it, and lastly retrieving and interpreting the ballot. While modern tabulators can imprint identifiers onto ballots as they are cast, voter-facing tabulators—that is, tabulators that interact directly with voters at the moment of ballot casting—intentionally randomize ballot order to preserve voter privacy
[
7
]
. As a result, even in election architectures that provide authoritative accounting for batch sizes, such ballots must be reprocessed after casting to establish an index that can be used to swiftly locate individual ballots. (If batch sizes are known, a costly alternate method is to hand interpret the whole batch, called batch comparison; see below.)

We propose two new techniques for risk-limiting audits that address these challenges. They improve RLA efficiency across a wide spectrum of election architectures and auditing methods.

•

Statistical Accuracy Testing for Ballot Manifests.
Coarse ballot manifests—merely providing accuracy to within, say, 10%—can be obtained by bulk methods with low effort, such as weighing or measuring ballots. On the other hand, electronic tabulation provides an untrusted, but typically highly accurate, manifest. We provide a statistical test that “bootstraps” the trusted, coarse manifest into a statistically guaranteed, high-accuracy manifest: the method either rejects the veracity of a tabulator-supplied manifest or results in a manifest with both high confidence and high accuracy. We integrate the risk of this statistical manifest test with a conventional RLA, significantly reducing the total burden of the audit.

•

Comparison RLAs via Direct Ballot Selection.
In circumstances with high-accuracy manifests that do not support in-order identifiers for ballot comparison audits, we propose a new RLA method. Procedurally, these new audits—which we call
direct ballot selection
audits—reverse the conventional doctrine for ballot comparison audits by randomly selecting a physical ballot from a selected batch and comparing the votes appearing on the ballot against its declared CVR, as provided by the tabulator.
Direct ballot selection audits require the introduction of new statistical tests to address the possibility of
identifier duplication
, discussed in detail below. For this reason, direct ballot selection exhibits a rather different scaling from that of existing audits. These new audits provide striking efficiency improvements over state-of-the-art polling and batch comparison audits, which are the only suitable alternatives when identifiers are not in order.

1.1
The Risk-Limiting Auditing Landscape

Traditional RLAs can be roughly organized into three methods:

•

Ballot polling audits
randomly sample ballots and test whether their empirical statistics support the reported winner. Polling requires
O\left({\log(1/\alpha)}/{\mu^{2}}\right)
sampled ballots, where
\alpha
is the risk limit and
\mu
is the tabulated margin.

•

Batch comparison audits
assume that ballots are organized into batches, each of which has been separately tabulated. The audit selects and hand counts random batches, comparing these subtotals against the corresponding tabulator output. This requires
O\left({B\log(1/\alpha)}/{\mu}\right)
sampled ballots, where
B
is the average batch size.

•

Ballot comparison audits
assume that the tabulation includes a CVR for each tabulated ballot—a CVR is a declaration of how a ballot was interpreted by the voting equipment. The audit randomly selects CVRs and compares them to human interpretations of the corresponding ballots. This requires
O\left({\log(1/\alpha)}/{\mu}\right)
sampled ballots and requires a method to efficiently find a specific ballot within a batch.

Ballot manifests.

An essential component of all these procedures is an authoritative
ballot manifest
—a determination of the total number of ballots in the election and, in the typical circumstances when ballots are organized into batches, the number of ballots in each batch. As mentioned above, accurate, software-independent manifests are critical for correctness; as a result, establishing a ballot manifest requires handling every ballot in the election. There are certain electoral settings where such exhaustive handling of ballots is unavoidable, such as unpacking mailed envelopes and updating the corresponding voter’s record in the pollbook; however, in settings where voters cast ballots directly into a voter-facing tabulator, manifest creation is an additional step that scales linearly with the overall size of the election. In these cases, manifest creation is a dominant factor in the overall effort.

Lindeman et al.
[
17
]
point out the necessity of an accurate ballot manifest and demonstrate that small errors (significantly smaller than margin) can be tolerated in conventional RLAs.

1.2
Statistically Approximate Manifests

We say that a manifest is
\delta
-accurate for a
\delta>0
if every batch size is correct to within a multiplicative factor of
1+\delta
. Obtaining a “coarse” manifest that is accurate to within, say, 10% can be achieved by efficient bulk methods. For example, weighing ballots—even in storage boxes with known tare weights—can reliably produce such estimates; weighing stacks of ballots themselves can produce even better estimates: for example, standard counting scales can simultaneously accommodate 100 lb. capacity while providing accuracy exceeding
1\%
. Similarly, counting the number of issued ballots or voters can provide an
upper bound
on the number of cast ballots. Ballot comparison requires an upper bound on the number of cast ballots to prevent eliding ballots, see Appendix
D
. Polling requires a lower bound as well. (We remark that comparison audits, however, make other demands on the manifest—a means for locating individual identified ballots.)
A typical election setting, then, provides the following:

•

An immediate means to obtain “coarse manifests” which establish batch sizes with accuracy
\Delta
in this 1%-10% range;

•

A tabulation total, providing an untrusted declaration for batch sizes; this is claimed to have very high accuracy
\delta\ll\mu
.

In order to prepare for an RLA, it is necessary to establish a trusted manifest with accuracy
\delta\ll\mu
, the margin of the election. Of course, an audit must support the case when
\mu<\Delta
, so the coarse manifest is not directly useful for a general RLA. We observe that it can, however, provide a sampling distribution that bootstraps a rigorous high-accuracy test: in particular, a simple statistical test can use the coarse
\Delta
-accurate manifest to check the veracity of the declared manifest given by the tabulation. Specifically, after ensuring that the tabulated sizes are sufficiently close (with error
\approx\Delta
) to those appearing in the coarse manifest, batches are repeatedly drawn with probability proportional to sizes given by the coarse manifest and then accurately counted (by hand). These totals are compared against the size declared by the tabulation, permitting a small margin
\approx\delta
of error. Despite the fact that the sampling distribution has low accuracy, we show that if the declared manifest survives sufficiently many iterations of this test, then, except with small failure probability, the true size-proportional distribution has small distance in total variation to the declared distribution. (Looking forward to the formal audit, it will be convenient to slightly adapt both the procedure and the conclusion, but this discussion communicates the basic statistical framework.)
We then establish the soundness of polling, comparison, and direct methods when the ideal uniform sampling distribution is replaced by one whose distortion is controlled through this procedure.

1.3
Direct Ballot Selection

The asymptotic scalings of current RLAs appear to establish ballot comparison audits as the dominant approach, especially since audits must typically be planned and provisioned under a worst-case assumption of small
\mu
(e.g.,
\approx 0.5\%
). However, ballot comparison audits make the strong assumption that auditors can efficiently locate the ballot associated with a specific CVR.

Locating a particular ballot is a noted challenge in settings with
voter-facing tabulators
, which allow voters to cast ballots directly into the tabulation system. This procedure improves both the chain of custody of ballots and the forensic record of the election, as it requires no storage of untabulated ballots. However, such tabulators intentionally randomize ballot order (by dropping them into a large collection bin), which is crucial to protect voter privacy (see, e.g., Crimmins et al.
[
7
]
). For this very same reason, rows in the CVR table generated by the tabulator are intentionally exported in random order (rather than the order in which ballots were cast). Thus there is no natural association between the CVRs in the table and resulting physical order of ballots. Many modern tabulators can imprint identifiers on ballots as they are cast, which provides a ready method for associating a particular ballot with a particular CVR entry. We remark that such identifiers must be suitably random to protect voter privacy and maintain RLA transparency. Therefore, as ballots are randomly reordered during casting, the ordering of imprinted identifiers is unrelated to the resulting physical order. These circumstances lead to two natural approaches to locate ballots associated with particular CVR entries:

•

Linear search.
In settings with imprinted identifiers, linear search can locate a particular ballot. Of course, on average, locating a single ballot requires reading the identifiers of half of the batch.

•

Transitive tabulation:
Ballots can be re-tabulated by a process that preserves order. Physical position itself can then act as a method for locating ballots. If the (re-)tabulator can imprint (or read previously imprinted identifiers), the physical ordering is now consistent with a known ordering of identifiers and can be used to quickly locate ballots according to a requested identifier. This approach handles every ballot.

In these settings, sample complexity is not a comprehensive measure of efficiency, and a full accounting must reflect additional factors such as time to locate ballots, audit preparation, and the availability of appropriate equipment.
As a result, jurisdictions with imprinting often use batch comparison and polling audits, which require significantly more ballot interpretations. For example, in the United States, the average precinct size in the 2020 presidential election was 900.

We propose a new approach to ballot comparison audits in settings with voter-facing tabulators and imprinted ballots:
direct ballot selection
. The approach avoids linear searches through batches or retabulation. Direct ballot selection can be implemented with random ballot selection in the style of a polling audit. The high-level principle reverses the indexing of a standard comparison audit:

(1)

Randomly select a physical ballot (e.g., using
k
-cut
[
25
]
),

(2)

Read its imprinted identifier,

(3)

Compare against the corresponding CVR.

As the basic procedure calls simply for drawing a ballot uniformly, this avoids the operational challenges associated with locating a
particular, distinguished
ballot in a large collection. An immediate concern about this high-level approach is that
identifier duplication
can undermine risk guarantees. Fuller, Harrison, and Russell showed that duplicated identifiers do not impact risk if one samples CVR rows uniformly
[
9
]
. However, if duplicates are common (relative to the margin), it is easy to show that, when sampling ballots, by manipulating CVRs that list identifiers that appear on no ballot—which will never be examined by such an audit—election outcomes can be changed undetectably.

Thus, direct ballot selection must introduce additional statistical testing in order to deliver a rigorous risk limit. We establish that if duplicate (or blank) identifiers are sufficiently rare, this “reverse” indexing yields a RLA; motivated by this, we develop a simple statistical test for duplicate identifier detection.
Ultimately, the audit calls for randomly selecting
k
ballots uniformly at random, and invokes two statistical tests:

•

Comparison.
Ballots are compared against the corresponding CVR to establish consistency;

•

Uniqueness testing.
Ballots identifiers are compared against each other to establish uniqueness (and appearance) of identifiers.

We provide a detailed analysis establishing explicit risk-limits for the procedure (as functions of margin and sample complexity). The total sample complexity of the procedure is asymptotically

O\left(\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\mu}\right)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{N\log(1/\alpha)}{\mu}}\right)\,,

where
N
is the size of the total population of ballots. The two terms here correspond to the two statistical tests (which can be run in parallel with each other, using the same ballot samples). The first term is exactly the asymptotic sample complexity of a conventional comparison audit. The second term—reflecting the sample complexity of ensuring sufficiently few duplicate identifiers—involves the total population size, a factor that does not appear in any of the conventional audit approaches. The favorable dependence on
\sqrt{N}
(for uniqueness testing) arises from the classical “birthday paradox,” which asserts that the probability of observing no collisions among
k
objects sampled with replacement is
\exp(-\theta(k^{2})/N)
, scaling quadratically in
k
. Compared against polling, these asymptotics indicate that direct ballot selection audits perform advantageously for total ballot populations
N=O(\log(1/\alpha)/\mu^{3})
.

While such asymptotic statements are useful for intuition and understanding large-scale behavior, an evaluation suitable for practical RLA demands explicit numerics for concrete parameters. Since polling and direct ballot selection require the same setup and conventions for sampling random ballots from prescribed batches, one can decide which method to use after computing the margin
\mu
for any races to be audited. This is in contrast to ballot comparison, where one needs to place infrastructure ahead of time to find identifiers or distribute transitive tabulators to auditors.

Finally, we remark that by externalizing duplicate (collision) detection, the analysis of direct ballot selection can be framed with the familiar notion of “discrepancy.” In particular, it follows that the extension of such audits to more complex electoral contexts—e.g., contests involving multiple races, multiple candidates, or alternative voting rules—follows directly from existing techniques (see, e.g., Stark
[
31
]
) that suitably (re-)define discrepancy for these settings.

By augmenting the identifiers of a tabulation that does not have this promise (and may have repeated identifiers across batches) with an appended batch identifier, a tabulation can be transformed to one with global distinctness; as batch identifiers can be determined from ballots, this presents no difficulties for associating ballots with identifiers.

Statistical Tests

A standard approach for designing RLAs
is to consider the discrepancy
\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})
of a uniformly selected
row

r
from the CVRs in a tabulation
T

in comparison with some ballot
\mathbf{b}
with the drawn identifier
r_{\iota}
. If the election is invalid, one has that

\Exp_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{$\mathbf{b}$ matching}\\
\textrm{random row}\end{subarray}}[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})]=\frac{\mathsf{D}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}}\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}+\mu^{\mathrm{act}}\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}}\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\,.

(One needs to handle labels
\iota
that don’t appear on any ballot
[
9
]
; this is straightforward by treating the missing ballot as the worst interpretation for the audit.)
Independently carrying out such observations results in
a sequence of discrepancy observations
\mathsf{D}_{1}^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathsf{D}_{2}^{\mathrm{obs}},\ldots
taking values in

\{-2,\ldots,2\}
. An RLA can then be given by a (single-tailed) statistical test
for the null hypothesis that
E
is invalid and hence

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{i}^{\mathrm{obs}}]\geq\frac{\mathsf{D}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}}\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}

(which is guaranteed when the election is invalid); the alternate hypothesis is that the election is indeed correct. In this language, the risk of the audit is the probability that the null hypothesis is rejected when it is in fact true.

Observe that, in contrast, the quantity
\Exp_{\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}}[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})]
arising from the natural “discrepancy of a randomly selected ballot” experiment is not necessarily greater than, say,
\mathsf{D}/\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}
. One goal of the full analysis below is to establish a sufficient connection between these quantities (and hence to
\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}
). Ultimately, as mentioned above, we will be able to rely on standard one-sided statistical tests, which we define and discuss below.

2.2
The Formal Auditor–Adversary Model

We build on the formal auditing model of
[
9
]
, adapted to our setting to account for random ballot selection and approximate manifests. The model introduces an abstract party—the adversary
\mathcal{A}
—responsible for labeling ballots, grouping them into batches, and producing tabulations (and CVRs). The model does not require the adversary to assign unique identifiers to ballots; moreover, it permits the adversary to form batches after observing the ballots. As a result, the adversary may induce highly irregular allocations of votes across batches. Once the adversary has chosen identifiers and formed batches, these assignments are permanent throughout the audit.

Then, a
coarse manifest

is provided to the Auditor. Concretely, this coarse manifest gives an approximate size

\mathsf{Manifest}^{\mathrm{coarse}}=(\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{coarse}}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{coarse}}_{m})

for the batches: it must satisfy, for every batch
\beta
,

\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{coarse}}_{\beta}\in\left[\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}}{1+\Delta_{0}},(1+\Delta_{0})\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}\right].

(3)

This reflects the practical point emphasized in the introduction: rough batch-size information can often be efficiently obtained through bulk methods such as weighing or stack measurement. Within the constraints given by (
3
), the coarse manifest may be chosen adversarially. The quantity
\Delta_{0}
is determined from a parameter of the model
(\Delta)
by the rule
(1+\Delta_{0})^{2}=1+\Delta
; see below.

The model does place one important structural constraint on the tabulation
(CVRs) produced by the adversary: each identifier only occurs once in the CVRs. This reflects the practical fact that CVRs are available to
the auditor and can therefore be checked for duplicate identifiers. (Such duplicates can alternatively be handled with “CVR transformations” that do not increase risk
[
9
]
.)

Beyond this, however, the tabulation produced by the adversary is not assumed
to satisfy any accuracy conditions. Instead, the auditor may compare the batch
sizes induced by the tabulation,

\mathsf{Manifest}^{\mathrm{tab}}=(\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{m}),

against the coarse manifest. In particular, if the auditor verifies that, for
every batch
\beta
,
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}\in[{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{coarse}}_{\beta}}/{(1+\Delta_{0})},(1+\Delta_{0})\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{coarse}}_{\beta}]
,
then, considering that

\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{coarse}}_{\beta}\in[{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}}/{(1+\Delta_{0})},

(1+\Delta_{0})\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}]
, it follows that

\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}\in\left[\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}}{(1+\Delta_{0})^{2}},(1+\Delta_{0})^{2}\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}\right]=\left[\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}}{1+\Delta},(1+\Delta)\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}\right].

Thus a successful comparison against the coarse manifest certifies that the
batch sizes arising from
T
constitute a
\Delta
-accurate manifest.

Our final auditing procedure provides guarantees against
any adversary
satisfying these conditions. Because the adversary represents exactly those aspects of the election that are not assumed trustworthy or fixed in advance, the resulting guarantee applies in any practical setting adequately reflected by the model.

The Auditor–Adversary Game

The

Auditor
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}
, and the
Adversary
, denoted
by
\mathcal{A}
, together interact with a family of ballots reflecting the results of the election to be audited. Figure
1
formalizes the game.
An important feature of the game is the mechanism by which the auditor obtains random ballot samples. As discussed above, statistical audits require sampling accuracy commensurate with the margin of the contest under audit. While the model allows the auditor to sample uniformly within any chosen batch, it does not directly allow sampling batches with probability proportional to their true sizes. Instead, the auditor is given a coarse manifest—which may induce a distribution on batches that is substantially nonuniform—together with a mechanism for determining the true size of any batch on request.

A straightforward solution would be to determine the exact sizes of all batches, and thereby recover the correct size-proportional distribution; ballots could then be sampled accordingly. The practical obstacle is that obtaining the exact size of a batch has cost linear in that batch’s size. Thus a key challenge is to limit the total size of the batches whose true counts are requested while still ensuring that the resulting sampling distribution is sufficiently close to uniform to support the desired risk bound.

The Direct Ballot Selection Auditor

The direct ballot selection RLA is designed to operate for any

(1)

(3)

\rho_{\mathrm{tv}}+\rho_{\mathrm{dup}}<1

The auditor uses the coarse manifest only to certify a global
\Delta

tabulated

\alpha_{\mathrm{tv}}

3.3
Manifest Accuracy –
\Psi

The purpose of
\mathtt{BoundSize}
is to certify that the ballot-sampling rule induced by the tabulated batch sizes is sufficiently accurate for the final ballot-discrepancy test. (As noted above, the accuracy also affects the number of samples drawn for duplicate detection.) The certificate we want is stated in terms of the two batch probability laws that matter downstream:

r^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}:=\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}},\qquad r^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}:=\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}}\,.

is the
tabulation-induced batch probability law. (The coarse manifest is used only in

\Delta
-accuracy condition.)

Define the set of

\mathsf{Bad}:=\left\{\beta\in[m]:\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}\notin\left[\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}}{1+\delta},(1+\delta)\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}_{\beta}\right]\right\},

and define their total
tabulated ballot mass
by

p:=\sum_{\beta\in\mathsf{Bad}}r^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathsf{Bad}}\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}_{\beta}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}}.

Because
\mathtt{BoundSize}
samples batches directly from the tabulated batch probability law

, a sampled batch lands in the bad set
\mathsf{Bad}
with probability
exactly
p
.
Therefore, for a particular target threshold
p^{\star}
, the probability that

\mathtt{BoundSize}
accepts after
k
independent samples when

is at most

(1-p^{\star})^{k}\leq\exp(-kp^{\star}).

When
p\leq p^{\star}

is a
(p^{\star},\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
the tabulated batch probability law
r^{\mathrm{tab}}
. The remaining question is what
p^{\star}
should be selected in order to establish the distributional guarantees necessary for auditor’s later invocations of
\mathsf{Sample}

It is shown in Theorem
6

that if the bad set has tabulated mass at most
p
then

\frac{1}{1+\tau(p)}\leq\frac{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{act}}}{\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}}\leq 1+\varepsilon(p)

of Appendix

Consequently, the retained margin in the final

,
\tau(p)

4

5
), and (

4
Efficiency Improvements Over Existing Methods

Our efficiency numbers focus on evaluating two primary questions:

(1)

Does direct ballot selection avoid the high cost of ballot polling—both Minerva and Providence methods—for small margins?

(2)

Does the use of a statistically accurate manifest yield meaningful savings in the time to conduct a direct ballot selection, ballot comparison, and ballot polling audit?

3

3

We do not take into account batch comparison audits in our analysis, because they require too many ballots. For example, for the 2024 General Election, Georgia conducted a (MACRO) batch comparison audit of the presidential race,

At our presumed 25 seconds per ballot, this would take a total of 36,787 hours before we factor in
manifest creation. We adopt the stance that batch comparison is most effective for small ballot populations in which
you are likely to pivot to a full hand recount, as you have already completed a majority of the work to hand recount.

For all methods, we assume the goal is a

We build on the election simulation of Fuller, Harrison, and Russell

We make a few assumptions when it comes to the practical time to create an accurate ballot manifest.
We imagine a setting where auditors need to set up stacks of ballots for efficient manifest creation. Ballots are often transported in ballot bags; while many of these ballots remain stacked
neatly and can be pulled out in one large stack, some get scattered throughout the bag and will need to be picked up individually.
We assume 90% of the ballots remain stacked and 10% of the ballots become scattered within the bag. We assume an auditor can remove a stack of 50 ballots within 60 seconds, and it takes 6 seconds to retrieve a scattered ballot and
add it to the stack. Saputra et al.
[
24

[

In addition, we run Lindeman et al’s technique for supporting statistically accurate manifests. The technique treats each CVR as having

Appendix B
Ethical Considerations

This work proposes new risk-limiting audits for elections that are usable in various municipalities throughout the world. Some statistical data is used from publicly available sources on various Secretary of State websites throughout the United States. In addition, we remark on the necessity of accurate ballot manifests in currently used risk-limiting audits. Vendors (and previous literature) already stress the need for an accurate manifest, so we do not believe this exposes a vulnerability in current systems.

Appendix C
Polling and Comparison Audits with Statistical Manifests

Statistical manifests yield a “
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated” accounting for the sizes of the batches, which is to say that all batches are within a
(1+\Delta)
factor of their true size, and all but a
p
-fraction of them are in fact within a
(1+\delta)
factor of their true size. Considering the efficiency improvements afforded by permitting this uncertainty, it is of interest to explore how conventional polling and comparison audits fare in this setting.

Polling.

A standard polling audit is defined by introducing the function
A:\mathbf{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
A(\mathbf{b})=W_{\mathbf{b}}-L_{\mathbf{b}}
. Then

\Exp[A(\mathbf{b}_{U})]=\frac{\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}}{|\mathbf{B}|}\,,

where
\mathbf{b}_{U}
is drawn from the uniform distribution
U
on
\mathbf{B}
.
Standard results from probability theory guarantee that if
\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{k}
are drawn uniformly (with replacement, say) from
\mathbf{B}
, then the empirical mean converges to the expected value,

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}A(\mathbf{b}_{i})\approx\Exp_{\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}}[A(\mathbf{b})]\,,

and, furthermore, the error in this approximation satisfies suitable tail bounds; for example,

\Pr\left[\left|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}A(\mathbf{b}_{i})-\Exp_{\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}}[A(\mathbf{b})]\right|\geq\lambda\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}k}{2M^{2}}\right)\,,

where
M=\max\{|A(\mathbf{b})|\}
.

As statistical manifests immediately yield
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated sampling distributions on the underlying ballots, the natural question is what can be said about this expectation when sampling is performed with the distorted distribution. Specifically, observe that if ballots are instead drawn from a distorted distribution
\pi
on
\mathbf{B}
, the same tail bounds apply to the expected value

\Exp[A(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]\,,

where
\mathbf{b}_{\pi}
denotes a ballot drawn from
\pi
(rather than the uniform distribution). In light of Lemma
4
,

\left|\Exp[A(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]-\Exp[A(\mathbf{b}_{U})]\right|\leq 2\cdot M\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U)\,.

Recalling Theorem
4
, this distance in total variation
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U)
is no more than

\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{(1+\delta)p\Delta}{1+p\delta}\,.

This provides an immediate means for adapting a conventional polling audit to a setting with a distorted sampling distribution given by a statistical manifest.

Comparison.

A standard comparison audit draws CVR rows uniformly from the tabulated CVR. A conclusion of Fuller, Harrison, and Russell
[
9
]
is that this process is risk-limiting so long as that the total number of CVR rows is at least the total number of ballots. (In particular, duplicated identifiers cannot compromise risk.) In this setting, the only quantity of direct relevance is the total number of ballots, an upper bound for which is directly provided by Theorem
6
in the context of an approximate manifest. Specifically, for a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting,

|\mathbf{B}|\leq\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}(1+\epsilon(p))=\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}\left(1+[(1-p)\delta+p\Delta]\right)\,.

Lindeman et al.
[
17
]
suggested adding dummy rows and marking them when sampled with the maximum discrepancy. This is a risk-limiting audit with parameters that depend on the gap between the size of the CVR and the upper bound on the size of the ballot population. As discussed in the body, for standard parameterization of Kaplan-Markov, obtaining a comparison audit that terminates requires
\mu\approx\epsilon(p)/5
. Kaplan-Markov is designed for nonzero values to be rare, and so adding items that cause a discrepancy of
2
causes the audit to greatly inflate the number of samples required to complete.

Instead, for a CVR
\mathrm{cvr}
and a row
r
of
\mathrm{cvr}
, consider the value
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)
defined to be the minimum—taken over all ballots
\mathbf{b}
that match the CVR row identifier—of
\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}(\mathbf{b})
; following Harrison, Fuller, and Russell
[
9
]
, in the event that there is no matching ballot (or this is a “dummy” row of the CVR), define the discrepancy as though the row
r
is matched with a ballot holding a vote for the loser, which is to say that
\mathsf{L}_{\mathbf{b}}-\mathsf{W}_{\mathbf{b}}=1
. It is easy to argue
[
9
]
that by this convention

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\,,

for any invalid election. (Here
r
is drawn uniformly from the CVR rows.)

Assuming, for simplicity, that the ballots are organized into a single batch, let
\mathrm{cvr}^{\prime}
be a tabulated CVR expanded by adding
\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}\cdot\epsilon(p)
additional dummy rows. As described above, this ensures that the size of the CVR is, at least, the total number of ballots. Then

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}

\displaystyle\leq\Exp_{r}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]

\displaystyle=\Exp_{r|r\in\text{ Added Rows}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}^{\prime}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]\Pr[r\in\text{ Added Rows}]

\displaystyle+\Exp_{r|r\in\mathrm{cvr}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]\Pr[r\in\mathrm{cvr}]

\displaystyle\geq 1\cdot\frac{\epsilon(p)}{1+\epsilon(p)}+\frac{\Exp_{r|r\in\mathrm{cvr}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]}{1+\epsilon(p)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon(p)+\Exp_{r|r\in\mathrm{cvr}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]}{1+\epsilon(p)}.

So instead of computing on the expanded
\mathrm{cvr}^{\prime}
, we use Kaplan-Markov to test whether

\Exp_{r\in\mathrm{cvr}}[\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{cvr}}^{\mathbf{B}}(r)]\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}(1+\epsilon(p))-\epsilon(p).

This provides satisfactory results as long as
\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}>\epsilon(p)
.
As discussed in the body, our technique can be seen as controlling the margin “sacrificed” by adding these rows so they do not unduly cause the audit to output
Inconclusive
.

Appendix D
Prior Audits are not Risk-Limiting with Highly Inaccurate Manifest

We provide simple examples showing that polling, ballot comparison audits, and direct selection audits are not risk-limiting when the size of each batch is distorted by
\Delta
of comparable size to the margin
\mu
. This discussion applies to methods that uniformly sample ballots to compute the batch size. This is true for most methods in each class, but does not apply to taint tracking methods
[
14
]
. In all discussion below batch sizes are manipulated by less than the
\Delta=.1
considered in our experimental results.

Ballot polling.

Consider an election with
\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}
votes for the true winner and
\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}
for the true loser. Furthermore, the declared margin is
\mu_{\mathrm{tab}}
. In simplified terms, a polling audit bounds the probability that

\Pr\left[(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}})/\mathsf{S}<\mu/c\right]

for some constant
c
. Recall that since
\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}=\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}}\mathsf{W}_{\mathbf{b}}
and
\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}=\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathbf{b}}
one can estimate these quantities by uniformly sampling (without) replacement a
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}
computing
\mathsf{W}_{\mathbf{b}}-\mathsf{L}_{\mathbf{b}}
. With an accurate manifest each
\mathbf{b}
is selected with probability
1/\mathsf{S}
as
\mathsf{S}=|\mathbf{B}|
. Define
\mu^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathbf{b}}:=(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathbf{b}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathbf{b}})/\mathsf{S}
. The goal of the statistical test is to lower-bound the expectation
\Exp_{\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}}\mu^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathbf{b}}\,.

This expectation is highly sensitive to the sampling of
\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}
which can be adjusted by manipulating the size of batches. We use
U
to denote the proper uniform sampling of ballots. Consider the following simplified example where there are two batches of size
49
and
51
denoted as
\mathsf{L}
and
\mathsf{W}
respectively, where all ballots in
\mathsf{L}
have a vote for candidate
\mathsf{L}
and all ballots in
\mathsf{W}
have a vote for
\mathsf{W}
. The correct
\Exp_{\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}_{U}}\mu^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathbf{b}}=-.02
for this election. One can adjust the diluted margin from
.02
for the
\mathsf{W}
to
.02
for the loser by adjusting the sizes to
51
and
49
respectively. This creates a new distribution of sampled ballots
D_{\mathcal{A}}
where
\Exp_{\mathbf{b}\leftarrow\mathbf{B}_{D_{\mathcal{A}}}}\mu^{\mathrm{obs}}_{\mathbf{b}}=.02
.

The above example is very simplified; the quality of the manipulation is proportional to the number of batches that have strong margins for one candidate or another. However, the polarization displayed does mirror current electoral trends in the United States. Often, large cities have overwhelming Democratic preferences while rural areas have overwhelming Republican preferences. Taking the 2024 Presidential Election in Connecticut as an example, 26% of batches had a margin that was 30% different than the overall statewide margin.

Ballot comparison.

The goal of traditional ballot comparison is to determine with statistical confidence whether
\mathsf{D}/|\mathbf{B}|=((\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{tab}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{tab}})-(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}))/|\mathbf{B}|\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}
. However, discrepancy is defined differently. In Fuller, Harrison, and Russell it is defined with respect to the rows of the CVR instead of over ballots (identifiers are again assumed to be unique in the CVR).
In the case of an inaccurate estimate on
|\mathbf{B}|
, the
\mathrm{cvr}
can have too few rows, which can bias sampling. We continue with the same example of two batches from the ballot polling above. By reporting the
\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}
batch as having
47
ballots instead of
51
the diluted margin becomes
2.1\%
for the
\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}
. Since the above measure of discrepancy only considers identifiers in the
\mathrm{cvr}
one can simply create a correct
\mathrm{cvr}
for the
49
ballots from the
\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}
batch, and one can never observe a non-zero discrepancy. Unlike ballot polling, it isn’t clear how to inflate the sizes of desirable batches for the adversary, as creating a
\mathrm{cvr}
with identifiers that don’t exist on physical ballots only increases discrepancy
[
9
]
. However, this example does not depend on the distribution of ballots between batches, the adversary can directly suppress votes for the actual winner in any batch.

Direct ballot selection.

Direct ballot selection without
\mathtt{BoundSize}
is also vulnerable in a similar way to ballot comparison. However, instead of reducing the size of the
\mathrm{cvr}
the adversary increases it. So the adversary increases the size of the loser batch to size
53
by creating
\mathrm{cvr}
rows with identifiers that don’t correspond to any ballot. Because these can never be sampled, they are not identified as having a positive discrepancy. This shows the dual risk of sampling from ballots (which allows creation of CVR rows that don’t correspond to a ballot) and from CVR rows (which allows not listing the CVRs for undesirable ballots).

Appendix F
Simple duplicate detection

We return to the proof of Lemma
1
. We begin with a simpler version adopting a sampling distribution with replacement; the lemma then follows by a simple monotonicity argument.

Theorem 3

(Simple duplicate-detection bound under i.i.d. sampling)
.

Let
A
be a finite set with
|A|=n
, let
f:A\to B
be a function with

|B|=n-\ell
, and let
\Pi
be a probability distribution on
A
such that

\frac{1}{n(1+c)}\leq\Pi(x)\leq\frac{1+c}{n}\qquad\text{for all }x\in A,

for some
c\geq 0
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
be i.i.d. from
\Pi
, and define the no-collision event

E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}:=\Bigl\{\forall i\neq j\leq k,\ f(Y_{i})=f(Y_{j})\implies Y_{i}=Y_{j}\Bigr\}.

Thus
E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}
is the event that no duplicate identifier is
detected among the
k
draws, where repeated draws of the
same

element are not counted as collisions. If
k\leq n(1+c)
then

\displaystyle\Pr(E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\ell\left[1-\exp\left(\frac{-k}{n(1+c)}\right)\right]^{2}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k^{2}\ell}{4n^{2}(1+c)^{2}}\right).

(14)

Proof.

For each
b\in B
, let

F_{b}:=f^{-1}(b),\qquad s_{b}:=|F_{b}|.

Let

B_{+}:=\{b\in B:s_{b}>0\}

be the set of nonempty fibers. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in B_{+}}s_{b}=n,\qquad\sum_{b\in B_{+}}(s_{b}-1)=n-|B_{+}|

\displaystyle=n-|\operatorname{im}(f)|\geq n-|B|=\ell.

Define

\tilde{\ell}:=\sum_{b\in B_{+}}(s_{b}-1),

so that
\tilde{\ell}\geq\ell
. Further define

L:=\frac{1}{n(1+c)}\,.

Step 1: Poissonization.

Let
N\sim\mathrm{Pois}(k)
, independent of everything else, and let

Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}
be i.i.d. from
\Pi
. Let
E^{\mathrm{P}}
denote the
event that no duplicate identifier is detected among these
N
draws.

For each
x\in A
, let

M_{x}:=\#\{i:Z_{i}=x\}.

Then the random variables
(M_{x})_{x\in A}
are independent and satisfy

M_{x}\sim\mathrm{Pois}(k\Pi(x)).

Now define

I_{x}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{M_{x}>0\}}.

Then the
I_{x}
are independent Bernoulli random variables with

\Pr[I_{x}=1]=1-e^{-k\Pi(x)}\geq 1-e^{-kL}=:r.

Fix
b\in B_{+}
. The event that the fiber
F_{b}
causes no detected collision
is

A_{b}:=\left\{\sum_{x\in F_{b}}I_{x}\leq 1\right\}.

This is a coordinatewise decreasing event in the family
(I_{x})_{x\in F_{b}}
.
Since each
I_{x}
stochastically dominates a Bernoulli
(r)
, monotonicity of
product measures implies

\Pr[A_{b}]\leq\Pr\!\bigl[\mathrm{Bin}(s_{b},r)\leq 1\bigr].

Now

\displaystyle\Pr\!\bigl[\mathrm{Bin}(s_{b},r)\leq 1\bigr]

\displaystyle=(1-r)^{s_{b}}+s_{b}r(1-r)^{s_{b}-1}

\displaystyle=(1-r)^{s_{b}-1}\bigl(1+(s_{b}-1)r\bigr).

Using
1+mr\leq(1+r)^{m}
for every integer
m\geq 0
, we obtain

\Pr[A_{b}]\leq(1-r)^{s_{b}-1}(1+r)^{s_{b}-1}=(1-r^{2})^{s_{b}-1}.

Because different fibers involve disjoint collections of independent variables,
the events
(A_{b})_{b\in B_{+}}
are independent. Therefore

\Pr[E^{\mathrm{P}}]=\prod_{b\in B_{+}}\Pr[A_{b}]\leq\prod_{b\in B_{+}}(1-r^{2})^{s_{b}-1}=(1-r^{2})^{\tilde{\ell}}.

Using
1-u\leq e^{-u}
and
\tilde{\ell}\geq\ell
,

\Pr[E^{\mathrm{P}}]\leq e^{-\tilde{\ell}r^{2}}\leq e^{-\ell r^{2}}=\exp\!\bigl(-\ell(1-e^{-kL})^{2}\bigr).

Step 2: De-Poissonization.

For each
m\geq 0
, let

p_{m}:=\Pr[E_{m}^{\mathrm{iid}}],

where
E_{m}^{\mathrm{iid}}
denotes the no-collision event for exactly
m

i.i.d. draws from
\Pi
. The sequence
(p_{m})
is nonincreasing in
m
,
since adding more draws can only create more opportunities for collision.

Because
N\sim\mathrm{Pois}(k)
,

\Pr[E^{\mathrm{P}}]=\sum_{m\geq 0}p_{m}\,\Pr[N=m].

Since
p_{m}\geq p_{k}
for all
m\leq k
, it follows that

\Pr[E^{\mathrm{P}}]\geq p_{k}\,\Pr[N\leq k].

Hence

p_{k}\leq\frac{\Pr[E^{\mathrm{P}}]}{\Pr[N\leq k]}.

Combining this with the bound from Step 1 yields

\Pr[E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}]=p_{k}\leq\frac{\exp\!\bigl(-\ell(1-e^{-kL})^{2}\bigr)}{\Pr[\mathrm{Pois}(k)\leq k]}\,.

Recalling that
\Pr[\mathrm{Pois}(k)\leq k]\geq 1/2
, we conclude that

\Pr[E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}]=p_{k}\leq 2\exp\!\bigl(-\ell(1-e^{-kL})^{2}\bigr)\,.

Finally, if
kL\leq 1
, then

1-e^{-kL}\geq\frac{kL}{2}.

Thus

\Pr[E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\ell\left(\frac{kL}{2}\right)^{2}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k^{2}\ell}{4n^{2}(1+c)^{2}}\right),

which is (
14
).
∎

We now prove Lemma
1
as a corollary of the previous theorem.

Corollary 2

(Reduction to sequential weighted sampling without replacement)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
3
. Let

X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
be sampled without replacement according to the sequential law

X_{1}\sim\Pi,

and for
t\geq 1
,

\Pr[X_{t+1}=x\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{t}]=\frac{\Pi(x)}{1-\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Pi[X_{j}]}

for every
x\notin\{X_{1},\dots,X_{t}\}
.

Define

E_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}:=\{\forall i\neq j,\ f(X_{i})\neq f(X_{j})\}.

Then, for
k\leq n(1+c)
,

\displaystyle\Pr[E_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\ell\left[1-\exp\left(\frac{-k}{n(1+c)}\right)\right]^{2}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k^{2}\ell}{4n^{2}(1+c)^{2}}\right).

(16)

Proof.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
be an infinite i.i.d. sequence with law
\Pi
. Define
stopping times

\tau_{1}:=1,\qquad\tau_{m+1}:=\min\{t>\tau_{m}:\ Y_{t}\notin\{Y_{\tau_{1}},\dots,Y_{\tau_{m}}\}\}.

Since
\Pi(x)>0
for every
x\in A
, these stopping times are almost surely
finite for
m\leq n
. Set

\widetilde{X}_{m}:=Y_{\tau_{m}},\qquad m=1,\dots,k.

Then
(\widetilde{X}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{k})
has exactly the sequential
without-replacement law above: conditional on

\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{t}=x_{t}
, the next accepted value is the
first future i.i.d. draw that lands outside
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
, so for

x\notin\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
,

\displaystyle\Pr

\displaystyle[\widetilde{X}_{t+1}=x\mid\widetilde{X}_{1}=x_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{t}=x_{t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Pi(x_{j})\right)^{m-1}\Pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pi(x)}{1-\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Pi(x_{j})}.

Now, if
E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}
fails for the first
k
i.i.d. draws

Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
, then there exist
i\neq j\leq k
such that
Y_{i}\neq Y_{j}

but
f(Y_{i})=f(Y_{j})
. Those two distinct elements have both appeared by time

k
, so they are among the first
k
distinct values

\widetilde{X}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{k}
. Hence
E_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}
also
fails for
\widetilde{X}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{k}
. Therefore

E_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}(\widetilde{X}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{X}_{k})\subseteq E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}),

which gives

\Pr[E_{k}^{\mathrm{seq}}]\leq\Pr[E_{k}^{\mathrm{iid}}].

The stated bounds now follow from Theorem
3
.
∎

Lemma
1
follows immediately.

Appendix G
Bounding Contaminated Reweighting of Induced Ballot Distribution

In this section, we focus on showing the soundness of the
\mathtt{BoundSize}
procedure.
Recall Definition
12
from the main text:
if
P
and
Q
are probability distributions on a finite set
X
,
then
Q
is a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
P

if there exist a function
w:X\to(0,\infty)
and a set

\mathsf{Bad}\subseteq X
such that

\displaystyle Q(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(x)w(x)}{\sum_{y\in X}P(y)w(y)}

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in X,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\delta}

\displaystyle\leq w(x)\leq 1+\delta

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in X\setminus\mathsf{Bad},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq w(x)\leq 1+\Delta

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in X,

and
P(\mathsf{Bad})\leq p
, where
0\leq\delta\leq\Delta
.

This section records the quantitative consequences of this notion that
are used in the analysis of the auditor and in the manifest-certification
step.

Recall the definition of distance in total variation.

Definition 14

(Total variation distance.)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability distributions on a finite set
X
. Then the distance between
P
and
Q
in total variation, denoted
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)
, is given by the quantity

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in X}|P(x)-Q(x)|\,.

Lemma 2

(General reweighting estimates)
.

Let
P
be a probability distribution on a finite set
X
, let

w:X\to(0,\infty)
, and define

m:=\sum_{x\in X}P(x)w(x),\qquad Q(x):=\frac{P(x)w(x)}{m}.

Then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{x\in X}P(x)\,|w(x)-m|.

Moreover,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\sum_{x\in X}P(x)\,|w(x)-1|.

Proof.

Since
Q(x)=P(x)w(x)/m
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in X}\left|P(x)-Q(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in X}P(x)\left|1-\frac{w(x)}{m}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{x\in X}P(x)\,|w(x)-m|.

This proves the identity.
For the inequality, define the finite measure

M(x):=P(x)w(x)\qquad(x\in X),

so that
\sum_{x}M(x)=m
and
Q=M/m
. By the triangle inequality,

\|P-Q\|_{1}\leq\|P-M\|_{1}+\|M-Q\|_{1}.

Now

\|P-M\|_{1}=\sum_{x\in X}P(x)|1-w(x)|,

and

\displaystyle\|M-Q\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in X}\left|M(x)-\frac{M(x)}{m}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|1-\frac{1}{m}\right|\sum_{x\in X}M(x)=|m-1|.

Also,

|m-1|=\left|\sum_{x\in X}P(x)(w(x)-1)\right|\leq\sum_{x\in X}P(x)|w(x)-1|.

Hence

2\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\|P-Q\|_{1}\leq 2\sum_{x\in X}P(x)|w(x)-1|,

which proves the lemma.
∎

Corollary 3

(Total variation under bounded reweighting)
.

Let
P
be a probability distribution on a finite set
X
, let

w:X\to(0,\infty)
, and define

m:=\sum_{x\in X}P(x)w(x),\qquad Q(x):=\frac{P(x)w(x)}{m}.

Assume that for some
0<\alpha\leq\beta
,

\alpha\leq w(x)\leq\beta\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

Then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\beta}+\sqrt{\alpha}}.

In particular, if

\frac{1}{1+\eta}\leq w(x)\leq 1+\eta\qquad\text{for all }x\in X,

then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{\eta}{2+\eta}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{x\in X}P(x)\,|w(x)-m|.

Also
m\in[\alpha,\beta]
, since
m
is a
P
-average of values in

[\alpha,\beta]
.

Fix
m\in[\alpha,\beta]
. The function
y\mapsto|y-m|
is convex on

[\alpha,\beta]
, so by convexity it lies below its secant line on that
interval:

|y-m|\leq\frac{\beta-y}{\beta-\alpha}(m-\alpha)+\frac{y-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}(\beta-m)\qquad(y\in[\alpha,\beta]).

Averaging with respect to
P
and using
\sum_{x}P(x)w(x)=m
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}P(x)|w(x)-m|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta-m}{\beta-\alpha}(m-\alpha)+\frac{m-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}(\beta-m)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\beta-m)(m-\alpha)}{\beta-\alpha}.

Hence

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{(\beta-m)(m-\alpha)}{m(\beta-\alpha)}.

Consider

f(m):=\frac{(\beta-m)(m-\alpha)}{m(\beta-\alpha)}=\frac{\alpha+\beta-m-\alpha\beta/m}{\beta-\alpha}.

Then

f^{\prime}(m)=\frac{-1+\alpha\beta/m^{2}}{\beta-\alpha},

so
f
is maximized at
m=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
. Evaluating there gives

f(\sqrt{\alpha\beta})=\frac{\sqrt{\beta}-\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\beta}+\sqrt{\alpha}}.

This proves the first claim.

For the special case
\alpha=1/(1+\eta)
and
\beta=1+\eta
, we get

\frac{\sqrt{1+\eta}-1/\sqrt{1+\eta}}{\sqrt{1+\eta}+1/\sqrt{1+\eta}}=\frac{(1+\eta)-1}{(1+\eta)+1}=\frac{\eta}{2+\eta}.\qed

Remark.

The bound in Corollary
3
is optimal. Equality is attained
on a two-point space when
w
takes the two values
\alpha
and
\beta

with
P
-masses proportional to
\sqrt{\beta}
and
\sqrt{\alpha}
, so
that
\sum_{x}P(x)w(x)=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
.

Theorem 4

(Elementary bounds for contaminated reweightings)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability distributions on a finite set
X
.
Suppose that
Q
is a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of

P
, witnessed by a function
w:X\to(0,\infty)
and a set

\mathsf{Bad}\subseteq X
.

Define

\lambda:=\left(\sum_{x\in X}P(x)w(x)\right)^{-1},

so that

Q(x)=\lambda\,P(x)w(x).

Also define

\varepsilon(p):=(1-p)\delta+p\Delta,

and

\tau(p):=\left(\frac{1-p}{1+\delta}+\frac{p}{1+\Delta}\right)^{-1}-1.

Then

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)}\leq\lambda\leq 1+\tau(p)\leq 1+\varepsilon(p).

Moreover,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{(1+\delta)p\Delta}{1+\delta p}.

Proof.

Since
Q
is witnessed by
w
and
\mathsf{Bad}
, we have

\frac{1}{\lambda}=\sum_{x\in X}P(x)w(x).

Using the upper bounds on
w
,

\frac{1}{\lambda}\leq(1-p)(1+\delta)+p(1+\Delta)=1+\varepsilon(p).

Using the lower bounds on
w
,

\frac{1}{\lambda}\geq\frac{1-p}{1+\delta}+\frac{p}{1+\Delta},

hence

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)}\leq\lambda\leq 1+\tau(p).

Since
x\mapsto 1/(1+x)
is convex on
[0,\infty)
,

\frac{1-p}{1+\delta}+\frac{p}{1+\Delta}\geq\frac{1}{1+(1-p)\delta+p\Delta}=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)},

and therefore
1+\tau(p)\leq 1+\varepsilon(p)
.

If
p=1
, then
w(x)\in[1/(1+\Delta),\,1+\Delta]
for all
x\in X
, so
Corollary
3
gives

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{\Delta}{2+\Delta},

which is at most

\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{(1+\delta)p\Delta}{1+\delta p}.

Now assume
0\leq p<1
. Define

\displaystyle P_{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{P(x)}{1-p}\qquad(x\in X\setminus\mathsf{Bad}),

\displaystyle m_{G}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x\notin\mathsf{Bad}}P_{G}(x)w(x),

and

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathsf{Bad}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{Bad}}P(x)\,|w(x)-1|.

Next define

v(x):=\begin{cases}w(x),&x\in X\setminus\mathsf{Bad},\\
1,&x\in\mathsf{Bad},\end{cases}\qquad A:=\sum_{x\in X}P(x)v(x).

Since
v=w
on
X\setminus\mathsf{Bad}
and
v=1
on
\mathsf{Bad}
,

A=(1-p)m_{G}+p.

Also, because
v(x)\in[1/(1+\delta),\,1+\delta]
for every
x\in X
,
Corollary
3
gives

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,V)\leq\frac{\delta}{2+\delta},\qquad V(x):=\frac{P(x)v(x)}{A}.

Define

r(x):=\frac{w(x)}{v(x)}.

Then

r(x)=1\quad(x\in X\setminus\mathsf{Bad}),\qquad r(x)=w(x)\quad(x\in\mathsf{Bad}),

and

Q(x)=\frac{V(x)r(x)}{\sum_{y\in X}V(y)r(y)}.

Applying Lemma
2
with source law
V
and
reweighting function
r
, we obtain

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(V,Q)\leq\sum_{x\in X}V(x)|r(x)-1|=\sum_{x\in\mathsf{Bad}}V(x)|w(x)-1|.

Since
v(x)=1
on
\mathsf{Bad}
, we have
V(x)=P(x)/A
there, and therefore

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(V,Q)\leq\frac{1}{A}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{Bad}}P(x)|w(x)-1|=\frac{\Gamma_{\mathsf{Bad}}}{(1-p)m_{G}+p}.

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,V)+d_{\mathrm{TV}}(V,Q)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{\Gamma_{\mathsf{Bad}}}{(1-p)m_{G}+p}.

Since
|w(x)-1|\leq\Delta
on
\mathsf{Bad}
, we have
\Gamma_{\mathsf{Bad}}\leq p\Delta
, so

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{p\Delta}{(1-p)m_{G}+p}.

Also
m_{G}\geq 1/(1+\delta)
, hence

(1-p)m_{G}+p\geq\frac{1-p}{1+\delta}+p=\frac{1+\delta p}{1+\delta}.

Therefore

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{(1+\delta)p\Delta}{1+\delta p}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
4
. ∎

Uniformity bounds from contaminated reweightings.

For duplicate detection, we will need a pointwise closeness-to-uniform guarantee,
rather than only a total-variation bound.

Lemma 3

(Reverse-direction max-divergence from contaminated reweighting)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability distributions on a finite set
X
, and let

\varepsilon(p):=(1-p)\delta+p\Delta.

Assume that either

(1)

Q
is a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
P
, or

(2)

P
is a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
Q
.

Then for every
x\in X
,

\frac{1}{(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p))}\leq\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}\leq(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p)).

Consequently, if either
P
or
Q
is uniform on
X
, then the other is

\eta_{\mathrm{rev}}
-close-to-uniform, where

\eta_{\mathrm{rev}}(p,\delta,\Delta):=(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p))-1.

In particular, since
\varepsilon(p)\leq\Delta
,

\eta_{\mathrm{rev}}(p,\delta,\Delta)\leq(1+\Delta)^{2}-1.

Proof.

We first consider case (1), where
Q
is a

(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
P
. Then there exist a
function
w:X\to(0,\infty)
and a set
\mathsf{Bad}\subseteq X
such that

Q(x)=\lambda\,P(x)w(x),\qquad\lambda:=\left(\sum_{y\in X}P(y)w(y)\right)^{-1},

with

\frac{1}{1+\Delta}\leq w(x)\leq 1+\Delta\qquad\text{for all }x\in X.

By Theorem
4
,

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)}\leq\lambda\leq 1+\tau(p)\leq 1+\varepsilon(p).

Therefore, for every
x\in X
,

\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}=\lambda w(x)\in\left[\frac{1}{(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p))},\,(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p))\right].

Now consider case (2), where
P
is a

(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
Q
. By the argument above,

\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\in\left[\frac{1}{(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p))},\,(1+\Delta)(1+\varepsilon(p))\right],\forall x\in X.

Taking reciprocals yields exactly the same interval for
Q(x)/P(x)
.

The close-to-uniform conclusion is immediate when one of the two distributions
is uniform on
X
. The final inequality follows from
\varepsilon(p)\leq\Delta
.
∎

Appendix H
Bounding Ballot Discrepancy; the Analysis of the Auditor

This section proves our main theorem (Theorem
7
). The structure proceeds as follows:

(1)

Assuming a uniform sampling distribution law, the average discrepancy can be lower bounded in terms of the total tabulation error, up to additive losses from two sources: duplicate ballots (captured by the duplicate rate
\kappa
) and added CVR rows that don’t correspond to any ballot.

(2)

For an arbitrary sampling distribution, we show the expected discrepancy change is bounded by the total variation distance shown in Theorem
4
. The expectation can decrease by at most
4\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U)
, since discrepancies lie in
[-2,2]
.

(3)

Finally, we apply the above result to a distribution induced by batchwise sampling and bounding the contaminated reweighting of the induced ballot distribution.

Setup and notation.

Fix a tabulation
T=(\mathrm{cvr}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathrm{cvr}^{(m)})
and let
\mathbf{B}=\bigcup_{\beta}\mathbf{B}_{\beta}
be a
set of physical ballots. Write

N:=|\mathbf{B}|,\qquad M:=\mathsf{S}^{\mathrm{tab}}=\sum_{\beta}\mathsf{S}_{\beta}^{\mathrm{tab}},\qquad\lambda:=\frac{M}{N}.

For a ballot
\mathbf{b}
, we let

m(\mathbf{b})\in\{-1,0,1\}

denote its contribution to the winner–loser margin:
m(\mathbf{b})=1
if
\mathbf{b}
is a vote for the reported winner but not the reported loser,
m(\mathbf{b})=-1
if
\mathbf{b}
is a vote for the reported loser but not the reported winner, and
m(\mathbf{b})=0
otherwise. Likewise, for a CVR row
r
, let

m(r)\in\{-1,0,1\}

denote the corresponding quantity of that row. For a dummy row (with no corresponding ballot)
d
, set

m(d):=0
.

Ballot discrepancy.

Recall the definition of discrepancy from Def.
7
: For each physical ballot
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}
, let
r_{\mathbf{b}}
be the CVR row returned by the lookup rule for ballot
\mathbf{b}
, if such a row exists. The discrepancy is defined to be

\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}):=\begin{cases}m(r_{\mathbf{b}})-m(\mathbf{b}),&\text{if lookup returns a row }r_{\mathbf{b}},\\
1-m(\mathbf{b}),&\text{if lookup returns no row.}\end{cases}

Thus

\mathsf{D}_{T}:\mathbf{B}\to[-2,2].

Sampled ballot.

If
\pi
is any probability distribution on
\mathbf{B}
, let

\mathbf{b}_{\pi}\sim\pi

denote a ballot drawn from
\pi
. In particular, let
U
denote the
uniform distribution on
\mathbf{B}
, and write
\mathbf{b}_{U}\sim U
.

Total discrepancy and tabulated margin.

Define the total signed discrepancy

\Delta_{T}:=(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{tab}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{tab}})-(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}).

As elsewhere in the paper, let

\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}:=\frac{\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{tab}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{tab}}}{M}

denote the tabulated diluted margin. If the reported outcome is wrong, then

\Delta_{T}\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}M.

Duplicate rate.

Let
\mathbf{b}_{\operatorname{id}}
denote the label of ballot
b
. Define

\kappa:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\iota\in{L}_{\mathbf{B}}}\left(\#\{b\in\mathbf{B}:\mathbf{b}_{\operatorname{id}}=\iota\}-1\right).

Equivalently,
\kappa N
is the number of ballots discarded after keeping at
most one ballot of each label.

Lemma 4

(Total-variation comparison for bounded functions)
.

Let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
P,Q
be probability distributions on

\Omega
. If
|f(x)|\leq M_{f}
for all
x\in\Omega
, then

\bigl|\Exp[f(X_{P})]-\Exp[f(X_{Q})]\bigr|\leq 2M_{f}\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q),

where
X_{P}\sim P
and
X_{Q}\sim Q
.

Proof.

By definition,

\Exp[f(X_{P})]-\Exp[f(X_{Q})]=\sum_{x\in\Omega}(P(x)-Q(x))f(x).

Therefore

\displaystyle\bigl|\Exp[f(X_{P})]-\Exp[f(X_{Q})]\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in\Omega}|P(x)-Q(x)|\,|f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq M_{f}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|P(x)-Q(x)|.

Since

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|P(x)-Q(x)|,

the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 5

(Uniform-ballot lower bound)
.

Under the uniform ballot law
U
,

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{U})]\geq\frac{\Delta_{T}}{N}-2\kappa-(\lambda-1)_{+}.

Proof.

Choose one representative ballot for each label and call all remaining ballots
with that label
extra duplicates
. There are exactly
\kappa N
such
ballots.

Construct an injective “repaired assignment” of ballots to genuine CVR rows and
(new) dummy rows as follows:

•

if a representative ballot
b
has a matching CVR row under the lookup
rule, assign it to that row;

•

if a representative ballot
b
has no matching CVR row, assign it to an
unused genuine CVR row if one is available, and otherwise to a (new) dummy row;

•

assign each extra duplicate ballot similarly to an unused genuine CVR row
if one is available, and otherwise to a (new) dummy row.

Let

\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})

denote the discrepancy of
\mathbf{b}
under this repaired injective assignment.

Of course, one has
\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})=\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})
for any representative ballot, as the assignment is unchanged. For a ballot
\mathbf{b}
with no matching CVR row, we see that

\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})=1-m(\mathbf{b})\geq m(r)-m(\mathbf{b})=\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})\,,

where
r
is the row assigned to
\mathbf{b}
in the repaired correspondence. Finally, for an extra duplicate ballot
\mathbf{b}
we can only promise
|\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})-\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})|\leq 2
; in particular,

\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})\geq\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})-2\,.

Thus only extra duplicates can increase discrepancy under repair, and we conclude that

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{B}}\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})\geq\sum_{b\in\mathbf{B}}\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})-2\kappa N.

Let
S\subseteq T
be the set of genuine CVR rows used by the repaired
assignment. Then

\sum_{b\in\mathbf{B}}\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})=\sum_{r\in S}m(r)-(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}),

while

\Delta_{T}=\sum_{r\in T}m(r)-(\mathsf{W}^{\mathrm{act}}-\mathsf{L}^{\mathrm{act}}).

Thus

\Delta_{T}-\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}}\widetilde{\mathsf{D}}_{T}(\mathbf{b})=\sum_{r\in T\setminus S}m(r)\leq|T\setminus S|,

where the suggestive notation
T\setminus S
indicates the set of those rows of
T
that are not in correspondence with a ballot. Because the repaired assignment uses as many genuine CVR rows as possible,

|S|=\min\{M,N\},

so

|T\setminus S|=(M-N)_{+}\,.

Therefore

\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}}\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})\geq\Delta_{T}-2\kappa N-(M-N)_{+}.

Dividing by
N
gives

\displaystyle\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{U})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbf{B}}\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Delta_{T}}{N}-2\kappa-\frac{(M-N)_{+}}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{T}}{N}-2\kappa-(\lambda-1)_{+}.\qed

Theorem 5

(Lower bound under an arbitrary ballot law)
.

Let
\pi
be any probability distribution on
\mathbf{B}
. Then

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]\geq\frac{\Delta_{T}}{N}-2\kappa-(\lambda-1)_{+}-4\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U).

If the reported outcome is wrong, then

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\lambda-2\kappa-(\lambda-1)_{+}-4\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U).

Proof.

Apply Lemma
4
with

f=\mathsf{D}_{T},\qquad M_{f}=2,\qquad X_{P}=\mathbf{b}_{\pi},\qquad X_{Q}=\mathbf{b}_{U}.

Since
\mathsf{D}_{T}(b)\in[-2,2]
for all
b
,

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]\geq\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{U})]-4\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U).

Now apply Lemma
5
.

If the reported outcome is wrong, then

\Delta_{T}\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}M,

hence

\frac{\Delta_{T}}{N}\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\frac{M}{N}=\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\lambda.\qed

Corollary 4

(Comparison bound from total variation and size control)
.

Assume that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi,U)\leq\gamma

and

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\leq\lambda\leq 1+\tau

for some
\gamma,\tau,\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then, if the reported outcome is
wrong,

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]\geq\min\!\left\{\frac{\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}}{1+\varepsilon},\,\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}(1+\tau)-\tau\right\}-2\kappa-4\gamma.

Proof.

By Theorem
5
,

\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b}_{\pi})]\geq\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\lambda-(\lambda-1)_{+}-2\kappa-4\gamma.

Define

g(\lambda):=\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\lambda-(\lambda-1)_{+}.

For
\lambda\leq 1
, we have
g(\lambda)=\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\lambda
, so the minimum on

[1/(1+\varepsilon),1]
is attained at
\lambda=1/(1+\varepsilon)
, giving

\frac{\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}}{1+\varepsilon}.

For
\lambda\geq 1
, we have
g(\lambda)=\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\lambda-(\lambda-1)
, which
is decreasing because
\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}\leq 1
. Thus the minimum on

[1,1+\tau]
is attained at
\lambda=1+\tau
, giving

\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}(1+\tau)-\tau.

Taking the smaller endpoint value proves the claim.
∎

Application to sampling from the tabulated batch sizes.

Let
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}>0
be the actual batch sizes and
m_{1},\dots,m_{K}>0
the
tabulated batch sizes. Define the corresponding batch laws

u_{i}:=\frac{n_{i}}{N},\qquad r_{i}:=\frac{m_{i}}{M}.

Let
\pi_{u}
be the ballot law obtained by first choosing batch
i
with
probability
u_{i}
and then choosing a ballot uniformly from that batch; this
is exactly the uniform distribution on physical ballots. Let
\pi_{r}
be the
analogous ballot law obtained from the tabulated batch law
r
.

A direct calculation shows that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{r},\pi_{u})=d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u).

Thus, once we have bounds on
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u)
and on the size ratio

M/N
, Corollary
4
immediately yields the
desired lower bound for the comparison stage. We now establish these bounds in
the batchwise setting relevant to manifest certification.

Batchwise sampling model.

We now apply our results to the specific setting of distorted batch sizes rather than an arbitrary distribution.

Theorem 6

(Batchwise contaminated reweighting)
.

Let
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}>0
be actual batch sizes and
m_{1},\dots,m_{K}>0
be
reported batch sizes. Define

N:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}n_{i},\qquad M:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}m_{i},\qquad u_{i}:=\frac{n_{i}}{N},\qquad r_{i}:=\frac{m_{i}}{M}.

Let
\mathsf{Bad}\subseteq[K]
, and define its
tabulated mass
by

p:=\sum_{i\in\mathsf{Bad}}r_{i}.

Assume
0\leq\delta\leq\Delta
, and that

\frac{1}{1+\delta}\leq\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}}\leq 1+\delta\qquad(i\notin\mathsf{Bad}),

while

\frac{1}{1+\Delta}\leq\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}}\leq 1+\Delta\qquad(1\leq i\leq K).

Then the actual batch law
u
is a

(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of the tabulated batch law
r
.

More explicitly, if we define

w(i):=\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}},

then

u_{i}=\frac{r_{i}\,w(i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}r_{j}w(j)}\qquad(1\leq i\leq K),

the function
w
satisfies the
\delta
- and
\Delta
-bounds above, and

r(\mathsf{Bad})=p.

Consequently, defining

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p)

\displaystyle:=(1-p)\delta+p\Delta,

(17)

\displaystyle\tau(p)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{1-p}{1+\delta}+\frac{p}{1+\Delta}\right)^{-1}-1,

(18)

and

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{(1+\delta)p\Delta}{1+\delta p}.

(19)

we have

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)}\leq\frac{M}{N}\leq 1+\tau(p),

and

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u)\leq\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p).

Proof.

Define
w(i):=n_{i}/m_{i}
. Then

\sum_{i}r_{i}w(i)=\sum_{i}\frac{m_{i}}{M}\cdot\frac{n_{i}}{m_{i}}=\frac{N}{M},

and therefore

\frac{r_{i}w(i)}{\sum_{j}r_{j}w(j)}=\frac{(m_{i}/M)(n_{i}/m_{i})}{N/M}=\frac{n_{i}}{N}=u_{i}.

Thus
u
is a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of
r
,
witnessed by the function
w
and the set
\mathsf{Bad}
.

Applying Theorem
4
to
r
and
u
gives

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)}\leq\frac{M}{N}\leq 1+\tau(p).

The same theorem yields

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u)\leq\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}+\frac{(1+\delta)p\Delta}{1+\delta p}.

This yields

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u)\leq\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p).\qed

Theorem 7

(Ballot-discrepancy lower bound under contaminated reweighting)
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be partitioned into batches
1,\dots,K
, with actual sizes

n_{1},\dots,n_{K}>0
and reported sizes
m_{1},\dots,m_{K}>0
. Let

N:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}n_{i}=|\mathbf{B}|,\qquad M:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}m_{i}=|T|.

Assume the tabulation
T
is uniquely labeled, and let
\mathbf{b}
be the random ballot
obtained by first choosing batch
i
with probability
r_{i}=m_{i}/M
and then
choosing a ballot uniformly from that batch.

Suppose that the reported outcome is wrong and that the actual batch law
u

is a
(p,\delta,\Delta)
-contaminated reweighting of the tabulated batch law

r
. Define
\varepsilon(p)
,
\tau(p)
, and
\Gamma_{\text{tv}}(p)
as in Equations (
17
), (
18
), and (
19
).

Then

\displaystyle\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})]

\displaystyle\geq\min\!\left\{\frac{\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}}{1+\varepsilon(p)},\;\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}(1+\tau(p))-\tau(p)\right\}

\displaystyle-2\kappa-4\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p).

Proof.

Let
\pi_{r}
denote the ballot law induced by the tabulated batch distribution

r
, and let
\pi_{u}
denote the ballot law induced by the actual batch
distribution
u
. As noted above,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{r},\pi_{u})=d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u),

and
\pi_{u}
is exactly the uniform ballot law

By Theorem
6
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(r,u)\leq\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p)

and

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(p)}\leq\frac{M}{N}\leq 1+\tau(p).

Applying Corollary
4
with

\lambda=\frac{M}{N},\qquad\gamma=\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p),\qquad\varepsilon=\varepsilon(p),\qquad\tau=\tau(p),

yields

\displaystyle\Exp[\mathsf{D}_{T}(\mathbf{b})]

\displaystyle\geq\min\!\left\{\frac{\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}}{1+\varepsilon(p)},\;\mu^{\mathrm{tab}}(1+\tau(p))-\tau(p)\right\}

\displaystyle-2\kappa-4\Gamma_{\mathrm{tv}}(p).

∎
