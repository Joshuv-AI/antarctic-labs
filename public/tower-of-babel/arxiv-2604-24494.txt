Zhijie Zhang (张志杰), Xiaoxia Zhang (张小霞), Taotao Fang (方陶陶), Hui Li (李辉), Greg L. Bryan, Federico Marinacci, Paul Torrey, Mark Vogelsberger, Junfeng Wang (王俊峰), Haiguang Xu (徐海光), Qingzheng Yu (余清正), Feng Yuan (袁峰)

Abstract
The origin and structure of the hot ( T ≳ 10 6 T\gtrsim 10^{6} K) gaseous halo around Milky Way (MW)-mass galaxies provide a critical test for galaxy formation models. We perform a comprehensive comparison for a sample of MW analogues from the TNG50 cosmological simulation by generating synthetic soft X-ray emission and O VII/O VIII absorption lines. The simulated halos successfully reproduce the observed global soft X-ray luminosity and key inner-halo properties, including X-ray surface brightness, emission measure, and O VII absorption strength. However, two critical discrepancies are identified: (i) the simulated X-ray surface brightness profiles decline too steeply at large radii compared to extended eROSITA stacking emission, and (ii) the halos systematically underproduce O VIII absorption, indicating a deficit of hotter-phase gas at T ∼ ( 1.6 − 3.2 ) × 10 6 T\sim(1.6-3.2)\times 10^{6} K. These findings indicate that the simulated halos are spatially too compact and lack the hotter gas phase observed in real galaxies. This suggests that the feedback model in TNG50, while generating hot gas, may deposit its energy too centrally and too vigorously to sustain a gently extended, multi-phase corona.

I
Introduction

The presence of an extended, hot gaseous halo is a well-established theoretical prediction for Milky Way (MW)-mass galaxies. In the standard galaxy formation paradigm, gas accreted from the intergalactic medium is shock-heated to temperatures of several million Kelvin, forming a diffuse corona that may constitute a major baryonic component of the halo
(
Spitzer 1956
;
White & Rees 1978
;
White & Frenk 1991
;
Tumlinson et al. 2017
)
. The thermodynamic state and spatial distribution of this corona are governed by the competing effects of gravitational heating, radiative cooling, and energetic feedback from stars and active galactic nuclei (AGN), as well as cosmic rays
(
Hopkins et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Correspondingly, a key question is to what extent the hot halo gas is established by cosmological accretion versus internal feedback.

Observations provide compelling evidence for hot gaseous halos around MW-like galaxies. Within our own Galaxy, detections of both the diffuse soft X-ray background
(
Snowden et al. 1997
;
Yoshino et al. 2009
;
Henley & Shelton 2013
, e.g.,)
and absorption/emission line from highly ionized species like O VII and O VIII
(
Fang et al. 2003
;
Fang et al. 2006
;
Gupta et al. 2012
;
Fang et al. 2015
;
Miller & Bregman 2015
;
Luo et al. 2018
;
Pan et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
confirm the existence of million-degree plasma extending into the halo. Studies of nearby, edge-on galaxies revealing extraplanar X-ray emission morphologically associated to star-forming disks and superbubbles
(
Strickland et al. 2004
;
Tüllmann et al. 2006
;
Li & Wang 2013
, e.g.,)
. On larger scales, stacking analyses of galaxy samples have statistically detected extended, low-surface-brightness X-ray emission around MW-mass galaxies, suggesting the presence of substantial coronae on scales of several tens to hundreds of kiloparsecs
(
Chadayammuri et al. 2022
;
Comparat et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
He & Li 2026
, e.g.,)
.

Numerical simulations have yielded divergent conclusions regarding the origin of the hot halo
(
Vogelsberger et al. 2020
)
. Some models suggest stellar feedback can generate significant X-ray emission, though often more centrally concentrated than observed
(
Henley et al. 2015
;
Peters et al. 2015
;
Li & Tonnesen 2020
, e.g.,)
, while others emphasize the critical role of AGN feedback in regulating the large-scale thermodynamic state of the circumgalactic medium
(
Truong et al. 2020
;
Oppenheimer et al. 2020
, CGM;)
.
Recent X-ray predictions from TNG, EAGLE, and SIMBA have revealed substantial diversity in hot CGM properties
(
Truong et al. 2023
;
Schellenberger et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, underscoring the need for detailed comparisons with new observational data.
Our previous work with the high-resolution SMUGGLE model
(
Marinacci et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
, starting without a pre-existing hot corona;)
showed that stellar feedback alone can reproduce the warm Galactic halo traced by O VI
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, but fails to generate the extended hot corona seen in X-ray observations, with emission confined to the inner regions
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
, hereafter Z25)
. This highlights the necessity for a cosmological framework that self-consistently includes both cosmological gas accretion and AGN feedback to advance our understanding of the hot halo’s origin
(
Bogdán et al. 2013
)
.

As the highest-resolution realization of the IllustrisTNG project, the TNG50 simulation provides the necessary framework for modeling galaxy formation within a full cosmological context
(
Vogelsberger et al. 2013
;
Vogelsberger et al. 2014a
)
. It self-consistently includes the critical processes that set the thermodynamic state and spatial distribution of the hot halo, such as cosmological gas accretion and coupled stellar and AGN feedback
(
Nelson et al. 2019
;
Pillepich et al. 2019
)
. Moreover, its combination of a cosmologically representative volume and high mass resolution makes TNG50 well-suited for studying the detailed properties of the hot gaseous corona around MW-mass galaxies.
In this work, we generate synthetic observations of the hot gaseous halo from a sample of MW-analogues in TNG50, focusing on its soft X-ray emission and O VII and O VIII absorption lines from both internal (Solar) and external viewpoints. Our analysis aims to determine whether this state-of-the-art simulation can reproduce the observed properties of hot halo gas. This comparison provides a critical test for AGN feedback models, as MW-mass galaxies reside in a mass regime where stellar and AGN feedback are expected to compete, making the thermodynamic state of the hot halo particularly sensitive to the implementation of AGN physics. Consequently, a successful match would help illuminate the roles of accretion and feedback, while a significant discrepancy may point to specific limitations in the implementation of AGN feedback.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
II
describes the TNG50 simulation, the selection criteria for our MW-like galaxy sample, and our methodology for generating synthetic X-ray and absorption-line observables. We present our main results in Section
III
, comparing the simulated hot halo properties, such as X-ray luminosities, surface brightness profiles, emission measures, and O VII and O VIII absorption strengths, with a suite of multi-scale observations. The implications of these findings for models of hot halo origin are discussed in Section
IV
. We summarize our conclusions in Section
V
.

II
Methodology

II.1
The TNG50 Simulations

The TNG50 simulation is the highest-resolution run of the IllustrisTNG project
(
Marinacci et al. 2018
;
Naiman et al. 2018
;
Nelson et al. 2018
;
Pillepich et al. 2018a
;
Springel et al. 2018
)
. It is designed to model galaxy formation within a full cosmological context
(
Nelson et al. 2019
;
Pillepich et al. 2019
)
. The simulation builds upon and substantially improves its predecessor, the original Illustris simulation
(
Vogelsberger et al. 2014b
)
, through key advancements including the inclusion of magnetohydrodynamics and a comprehensively revised model for feedback from stars and AGN
(
Weinberger et al. 2017
;
Pillepich et al. 2018b
)
. These improvements, implemented in the moving-mesh code
AREPO

(
Springel 2010
)
, enable TNG50 to produce a galaxy population in better agreement with a range of observations
(
Nelson et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.

The simulation tracks structure formation in a periodic volume with a comoving side length of approximately 51.7 Mpc. The high resolution is reflected by in the mass resolution of
8.5\times 10^{4}\,M_{\odot}
for baryonic particles and
4\times 10^{5}\,M_{\odot}
for dark matter particles. The spatial resolution of the gas is determined by an adaptive mesh that can refine cells down to scales of several tens of parsecs. At
z=0
, the gravitational softening lengths are 0.29 kpc and 0.74 kpc (proper) for stellar and dark matter particles, respectively.

For our study, a crucial aspect is that the TNG model self-consistently includes the cosmological accretion of gas and the coupled effects of stellar and AGN feedback. AGN feedback operates in two distinct modes: a thermally dominated “quasar mode” at high accretion rates, and a kinetically dominated “radio mode” at low accretion rates
(
Weinberger et al. 2017
)
.
The kinetic “radio mode” is active at late times when the black hole accretion rate drops sufficiently. In this mode, AGN feedback operates through collimated jets that inject momentum into the surrounding gas. These jets inflate buoyant bubbles that can displace central hot gas to larger radii, thereby reducing the central gas density and suppressing radiative cooling. This mechanism is intended to help maintain an extended, diffuse corona.

II.2
The MW-like Galaxy Sample

Our sample of MW analogues is constructed from the parent sample of disky central galaxies at
z=0
in the TNG50 simulation
(
Ramesh et al. 2023
;
Pillepich et al. 2024
)
. The parent sample comprises galaxies with stellar masses in the range
10^{10.5}<M_{*}/M_{\odot}<10^{10.9}
, measured within a 30 kpc aperture.
Furthermore, these galaxies reside in halos with masses
M_{\mathrm{200,c}}<10^{13}\,M_{\odot}
(with typical values of
10^{11.7}-10^{12.5}\,M_{\odot}
), and satisfy an isolation criterion to exclude those in rich group environments.

To match the present-day evolutionary state of the MW, we impose an additional constraint on the instantaneous star formation rate (SFR), selecting galaxies with
1<\rm{SFR}/(M_{\odot}\,{\rm yr^{-1}})<3
. This range is consistent with modern estimates for the MW
(
Licquia & Newman 2015
, the red star in Figure
1
;)
and ensures that our sample galaxies have a comparable level of recent stellar feedback. Applying this SFR criterion to the parent sample yields our final set of 32 MW analogues, shown as the blue squares in Figure
1
.

II.3
Synthetic Observables

To compare the simulated hot halo gas with available observations, we generate synthetic data for two primary diagnostics: soft X-ray (0.5–2.0 keV) emission and absorption lines from highly ionized species. The calculation of soft X-ray emission follows the same procedure as Z25 and is summarized below.

II.3.1
X-ray Emission

The emissivity for each gas cell under the assumption of collisional ionization equilibrium (CIE) is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}=n_{e}n_{\mathrm{H}}\,\Lambda(T,Z),

(1)

where
n_{e}
and
n_{\mathrm{H}}
are the electron and hydrogen number densities,
T
and
Z
are the gas temperature and metallicity, and
\Lambda(T,Z)
is the cooling function calculated using the APEC model
(
Smith et al. 2001
, v3.0.9;)
.
Star-forming gas cells are not explicitly removed, since their low temperatures (
T\ll 10^{6}
K) make their contribution to the soft X-ray band negligible, and because they reside predominantly in the disk plane, they have little impact on the high-latitude sightlines (
|b|>20^{\circ}
) used in this work. Similarly, we do not apply any special masking of wind particles; at
z=0
, recently launched winds have cooled and mixed with the ambient halo gas, so their impact on the X-ray emission is minimal.

To generate observables comparable to actual observations, we project quantities along specific lines of sight, defining two primary viewpoints. For the
internal (Solar) viewpoint
, we first determine the galactic disk plane by computing the net angular momentum vector of star particles within a radial shell of (1–2)
R_{*,\mathrm{half}}
to robustly trace the large-scale stellar disk, where
R_{*,\mathrm{half}}
is the three-dimensional stellar half-mass radius. An observer is then placed at 8.2 kpc on this plane
(
Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016
)
.

To obtain robust statistics and mitigate potential bias from a single fixed location, we adopt a multi-observer approach commonly used in synthetic observations
(
Zheng et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
, e.g.,)
. We place four such observers in the disk plane, separated by
90^{\circ}
in azimuth. From each observer, we generate 500 random sightlines. The Galactic longitude
l
is sampled uniformly over
[0^{\circ},360^{\circ})
, and the latitude
b
is sampled over
[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]
with a probability density proportional to
\cos(b)
. When comparing with MW halo observations that typically avoid the disk
(
Henley & Shelton 2013
)
, we restrict our analysis to sightlines with
|b|>30^{\circ}
.
Excluding gas within 3 kpc of the Solar observers changes the median of all high-latitude sightline quantities considered in this work by less than 0.1 dex; we therefore retain all gas in the subsequent analysis.

For the
external viewpoint
, designed for comparison with randomly oriented external galaxies, we define the observation geometry by generating 100 randomly oriented lines of sight for each galaxy
(
Zheng et al. 2020
, following the common practice in synthetic observations, e.g.,)
. These sightlines will be used to construct azimuthally averaged radial surface brightness profiles.

The middle panels reveal the complex and centrally concentrated soft X-ray emissivity associated with stellar feedback and inner halo processes, calculated for each gas cell using Eq. (
1
) and then projected along the line of sight. Finally, the bottom panels show that the hot halo becomes more diffuse but remains structured when viewed on the scale of
R_{\rm 200,c}
, based on projecting the soft X-ray emissivity out to the virial radius (
R_{\rm 200,c}
).

From the emissivity, we derive several key observables using the
yt
analysis toolkit
(
Turk et al. 2011
)
to perform the necessary summations, integrations, and projections.
The X-ray luminosity within a specified volume is obtained by direct summation over gas cells:

\displaystyle L_{\rm X}=\sum\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}\,V_{\mathrm{cell}}.

(2)

For comparisons with external galaxy samples, we compute
L_{\rm X}
within a cylindrical region centered on the galactic center and extending vertically
\pm 5
kpc from the disk midplane. This corresponds to the median extent used for spectral analysis in the observed sample
(
Wang et al. 2016
)
.

The X-ray surface brightness
S_{\rm X}
along a given sightline is computed by integrating the emissivity

\displaystyle S_{\rm X}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\epsilon_{X}\,ds,

(3)

where
s
is path length of the sightline.
The factor
1/(4\pi)
gives
S_{\rm X}
in units of intensity per steradian. For comparison with the Galactic halo observations of Henley et al. (2013), we convert this to
\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}\,deg^{-2}}
when constructing all-sky maps (Figure 5).

For the internal (Solar) viewpoint, the integration is performed from the observer out to a radius of 260 kpc, matching the typical extent of observational constraints for the Galactic halo
(
Henley & Shelton 2013
, e.g.,)
. This yields sightline-specific values of
S_{\rm X}
projected onto a sky defined by Galactic
(l,b)
coordinates.

For the external viewpoint, we project the emission along each random sightline through the entire simulated halo.
To enable a fair comparison with the eROSITA stacking profiles, we convolve the projected two-dimensional images with a Gaussian kernel matching the eROSITA PSF before azimuthal averaging. We adopt a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of
30^{\prime\prime}

(
Merloni et al. 2024
)
, corresponding to
\approx 47
kpc at the median redshift
z\approx 0.08
of the MW-mass CEN sample in
Zhang et al. 2024a
.
We then compute the azimuthally averaged radial surface brightness profile
S^{\rm ext}_{\rm X}(R)
by collecting and averaging the
S_{\rm X}
values from 100 random sightlines for each radial bin relative to the galaxy center.

Beyond the integrated luminosity and projected surface brightness, the thermodynamic state of the hot halo gas provides crucial diagnostics for its origin and heating mechanisms.
The emission measure (EM) for the hot gas phase is calculated by integrating the density squared for gas cells with
T>10^{6}\,\mathrm{K}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{EM}=\int n_{e}n_{\mathrm{H}}\,ds,

(4)

and the emission-weighted temperature (
T_{\mathrm{ew}}
) is derived by weighting the gas temperature with the X-ray emissivity:

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{ew}}=\frac{\int T\,\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}\,ds}{\int\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}\,ds}.

(5)

Because
\epsilon_{\mathrm{X}}
is negligible for gas at temperatures much below
10^{6}\,\mathrm{K}
in the soft X-ray band,
T_{\mathrm{ew}}
effectively represents the characteristic temperature of the X-ray-emitting hot gas. These quantities are computed for the same sets of sightlines as
S_{\rm X}
, enabling direct comparison with observational derivations from X-ray spectral fitting.

II.3.2
Absorption Lines

To compare with absorption-line observations of the MW’s hot halo, we generate synthetic profiles for the O VII and O VIII K
\alpha
transitions, which have characteristic wavelengths of approximately 21.6Å and 19.0 Å, respectively. These ions serve as key diagnostics for the hot gas. Under the assumption of CIE, their fractional abundances peak at characteristic temperatures of
\sim 2\times 10^{6}
K and
\sim 3\times 10^{6}
K, respectively
(
Mazzotta et al. 1998
)
. From the synthetic profiles, we compute the equivalent width (EW) for direct comparison with measurements from the soft X-ray spectra of background AGN.

To model the absorption line, we first compute the number density of the ion in each gas cell. Using O VII as an example:

\displaystyle n(\text{{O~VII}})=n_{\mathrm{O}}\,f_{\rm O\,{\textsc{vii}}}(T),

(6)

where
n_{\mathrm{O}}
is the oxygen number density in the cell, computed from the tracked oxygen mass fraction in the TNG50 simulation.
The ionization fraction
f_{\mathrm{O\,\textsc{vii}}}(T)
is obtained from CIE calculations
(
Mazzotta et al. 1998
)
. An identical calculation is performed for O VIII using its corresponding ionization fraction.

For a given sightline, the optical depth profile
\tau(\lambda)
is determined by the integrated column density of the ion, its oscillator strength, and the line-of-sight velocity structure of the gas. We incorporate line broadening from thermal motions and shifts in the line center arising from both Hubble expansion and the peculiar velocities of the intervening gas
(
Fang et al. 2002
)
.
To isolate absorption features arising from the Galactic hot halo, consistent with observational analyses
(
Luo et al. 2018
)
, we only include gas with a line-of-sight velocity
|v_{\rm LSR}|\lesssim 500\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
relative to the Local Standard of Rest.
This cut excludes high-velocity clouds and satellite features, which contribute negligibly along high-latitude sightlines.
Finally, the equivalent width is computed by integrating the resulting absorption profile over the wavelength:

\displaystyle\mathrm{EW}=\int\left(1-e^{-\tau(\lambda)}\right)d\lambda.

(7)

, probing the halo in various Galactic

directions while excluding
b<20^{\circ}
. This approach directly mimics the observational strategy of using background AGN to study the MW’s hot gaseous halo
(
Fang et al. 2015
)
.

First, we compare the global soft X-ray output of our simulated galaxies with observations. Figure
4
shows the X-ray luminosities of our TNG50 MW analogues overlaid on the empirical
L_{\rm X}
–SFR relation for nearby, highly inclined disk galaxies
(
Wang et al. 2016
)
. While the SFRs of our selected galaxies are narrowly distributed around the MW’s value, their X-ray luminosities span nearly three orders of magnitude. The median luminosity of the TNG50 sample agrees with both the observed value for the MW
(
Snowden et al. 1997
;
Miller & Bregman 2015
)
and the empirical trend defined by external disk galaxies. This indicates that the simulation produces a characteristic X-ray luminosity for MW-like galaxies that is consistent with current observational constraints. The large predicted scatter suggests a greater halo-to-halo diversity than is currently observed.

Then we examine the soft X-ray surface brightness of the simulated hot halos from both the internal and external perspective.
Figure
5
(a) presents the median
S_{\rm X}
from the Solar viewpoint for each TNG50 MW analogue. The spread in median values across the simulated galaxy sample is approximately three times wider than the observed range for the Galactic halo (gray band). Despite this greater halo-to-halo variation, most simulated galaxies still fall within or near the observational band, indicating that the characteristic median surface brightness is consistent with MW measurements.

Figure
5
(b) compares the distribution of
S_{\rm X}
values from all individual Solar sightlines with the observational distribution. While the median values are in agreement, the simulated sightlines exhibit a significantly broader distribution than the observational one, spanning over four orders of magnitude. This large sightline-to-sightline scatter reflects the highly inhomogeneous and centrally concentrated distribution of the hot gas in the simulations (see also Figure
2
).
We note that the broad distribution in panel (b) reflects the full sample; individual halos can reproduce both the median and scatter of the observed sightline-to-sightline distribution. For instance, Halo 569251, which best matches the
Henley & Shelton 2013
data, has a median
S_{\rm X}
of
1.44^{+1.49}_{-0.87}\times 10^{-12}
erg s

-2

-2
, consistent with the observed value of
1.54^{+1.35}_{-0.56}\times 10^{-12}
erg s
-1
cm
-2
deg
-2
.
The narrower observed distribution could suggest that the hot gas in the actual Galactic halo is more smoothly distributed than in TNG50, or it may be influenced by observational limitations. Specifically, the observed distribution is truncated at low surface brightness due to sensitivity limits, whereas the simulation includes arbitrarily faint emission. Accounting for a realistic detection threshold would likely narrow the simulated distribution at the low-brightness end. A detailed investigation of this effect is beyond the scope of this paper but represents an important consideration for future, more direct comparisons.

IV
Discussion

Our analysis presents a detailed comparison between the TNG50 simulated hot halos and observations, revealing both successes and clear limitations. The simulation reproduces key global and inner-halo observables for MW-mass galaxies: the soft X-ray luminosity, inner-region surface brightness, and overall emission measure are consistent with observations
(
Henley & Shelton 2013
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
.
The good match in EM indicates that TNG50 generates approximately the correct density-squared integral (and thus hot gas mass) along inner-halo sightlines. Furthermore, the strength of O VII absorption, a tracer of warm-hot gas at
T\sim 10^{6}
K, is largely consistent with observational data
(
Fang et al. 2015
)
. These agreements suggest that the simulation captures key aspects of the hot gaseous halo, particularly its mass and the characteristic temperature of its X-ray-bright component.

However, our analysis identifies two significant and interconnected discrepancies that point to specific limitations in the current AGN feedback implementation of TNG50. First, the simulated O VIII absorption is systematically weaker than observed (Figure
9
), indicating a pronounced deficit of gas in the temperature range
T\sim(1.6-3.2)\times 10^{6}
K
(
Mazzotta et al. 1998
)
.
Second, and likely related, the simulated X-ray surface brightness profile is fainter and declines more steeply with radius than observational constraints from eROSITA stacking analyses
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
He & Li 2026
)
. This reveals that the hot gas in TNG50 is too centrally concentrated and lacks a sufficiently extended, diffuse component.

Several factors may contribute to these discrepancies. One possibility concerns the relative roles of different AGN feedback modes. The radiative “quasar mode” feedback, which dominates during earlier evolutionary stages and in more actively accreting systems, may be insufficient to establish an extended hot corona. For many MW-mass galaxies, the kinetic “radio mode” may only become active recently or intermittently, leaving limited time for its redistributive effects to operate. Even when active, the kinetic feedback may deposit energy too violently. Rather than gently lifting gas outward through buoyant bubbles, the energy injection could overheat gas in situ, pushing it to temperatures beyond the O VIII-forming window (
T\gtrsim 3\times 10^{6}
K) while failing to redistribute it to larger radii
(
Pillepich et al. 2021
)
. This interpretation is consistent with independent findings that the TNG model produces a hotter-than-observed CGM around MW analogues
(
Oppenheimer et al. 2020
)
and drives high-velocity outflows in these systems
(
Ramesh et al. 2023
)
.

Other factors may also contribute to the observed differences. Limitations in the treatment of stellar feedback could affect the energy balance in the inner halo. Numerical resolution effects might influence the ability to resolve multiphase structure and mixing processes.
Additionally, the simulated and observed samples have slightly different selection criteria: our TNG50 sample is restricted to
M_{200}<10^{13}\,M_{\odot}
, whereas the eROSITA stacking may include a small fraction of more massive halos, which could boost the stacked signal.
The eROSITA stacking analyses also involve assumptions about background subtraction, contamination from unresolved sources, and the conversion from stacked signal to individual halo properties. Deeper observations of individual MW-mass galaxies would provide valuable validation.

In contrast to idealized, isolated galaxy simulations that rely solely on stellar feedback and lack both cosmological gas accretion and a pre-existing hot corona, the TNG50 simulation incorporates a more complete set of physical processes. By self-consistently modeling cosmological gas accretion alongside both stellar and AGN feedback, TNG50 is able to successfully produce hot, gaseous halos around MW-mass galaxies. This represents a significant improvement over stellar-feedback-only models such as SMUGGLE, which fail to generate a large-scale corona altogether (Z25). However,
the specific discrepancies identified here, namely the overly compact X-ray emission and the deficit of O VIII absorption, indicate that the current feedback implementation still does not fully reproduce the observed hot halo properties.

These challenges are not unique to TNG50.
For instance,
Truong et al. 2023
analyzed soft X-ray emission line profiles (O VII, O VIII, Fe XVII) in TNG100, EAGLE, and SIMBA at z=0, finding significantly different radial profiles among the three simulations with TNG100 predicting centrally concentrated emission consistent with our TNG50 broadband results.
Silich et al. 2025
analyzed 28 MW-mass galaxies from seven simulation suites, including 8 TNG50 galaxies, and found that TNG50 produces the shallowest broadband X-ray surface brightness profiles among all simulations studied, yet these remain steeper than eROSITA stacked observations.
Schellenberger et al. 2024
similarly found that the detectability of hot CGM emission with future microcalorimeters depends sensitively on the underlying simulation model, with TNG and EAGLE halos exhibiting substantially different O VII and O VIII surface brightness profiles and temperature structures. Taken together, these studies show that the discrepancy with observed extended X-ray emission is not specific to TNG50.
These comparisons underscore that the X-ray diagnostics studied here provide strong constraints for refining feedback models. Future analysis of simulated gas phase diagrams and their correlation with individual feedback events will be crucial to test these interpretations. Continued progress in both observational capabilities and theoretical modeling will be essential for advancing our understanding of the hot gaseous halos that surround galaxies like our own.

V
Summary

We have presented a systematic comparison between the hot gaseous halos of MW-mass galaxies in the TNG50 cosmological simulation and existing observational constraints. Our analysis, based on synthetic X-ray observables (e.g., luminosity, surface brightness, emission measure) and O VII/O VIII absorption line strengths, leads to the following main conclusions:

(i)

The simulation reproduces key global and inner-halo observables for MW-mass galaxies. The soft X-ray luminosities, inner-region surface brightness, and emission measure and O VII absorption strength all agree with MW measurements, indicating that TNG50 successfully generates the warm-hot (
T\sim 10^{6}
K) gas phase in the central regions.

(ii)

The simulated hot halos are spatially too compact. The azimuthally averaged X-ray surface brightness profile is broadly consistent with eROSITA stacking observations within
\sim 50
kpc, but falls below the observed emission by up to
\sim 1
dex at
r\gtrsim 100
kpc, revealing a lack of diffuse X-ray bright gas at large galactocentric distances.

(iii)

The simulations underproduce gas in a specific hotter temperature phase. The strength of O VIII absorption line, which traces gas at
T\sim(1.6-3.2)\times 10^{6}
K, is systematically weaker than observed toward the Galactic halo, indicating a pronounced deficit of this hotter-phase gas in the TNG50 simulations.

The results demonstrate that the TNG50 simulation captures the basic properties of hot gaseous halos but reveals two key structural shortcomings: an overly compact spatial distribution and a deficit of gas in the hottest observable temperature phase. These interconnected discrepancies suggest that the energy injected by AGN feedback in TNG50 may be deposited too violently and too centrally to gently sustain a large-scale, X-ray-bright atmosphere with the correct multiphase balance. This highlights an important area for future refinement of feedback models in cosmological simulations that can be tested by deeper multi-wavelength observations.
