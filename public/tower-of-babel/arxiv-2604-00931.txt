PsychAgent: An Experience-Driven Lifelong Learning Agent for Self-Evolving Psychological Counselor
Yutao Yang, Junsong Li, Qianjun Pan, Jie Zhou, Kai Chen, Qin Chen, Jingyuan Zhao, Ningning Zhou, Xin Li, Liang He

Abstract
Existing methods for AI psychological counselors predominantly rely on supervised fine-tuning using static dialogue datasets. However, this contrasts with human experts, who continuously refine their proficiency through clinical practice and accumulated experience. To bridge this gap, we propose an Experience-Driven Lifelong Learning Agent ( PsychAgent ) for psychological counseling. First, we establish a Memory-Augmented Planning Engine tailored for longitudinal multi-session interactions, which ensures therapeutic continuity through persistent memory and strategic planning. Second, to support self-evolution, we design a Skill Evolution Engine that extracts new practice-grounded skills from historical counseling trajectories. Finally, we introduce a Reinforced Internalization Engine that integrates the evolved skills into the model via rejection fine-tuning, aiming to improve performance across diverse scenarios. Comparative analysis shows that our approach achieves higher scores than strong general LLMs (e.g., GPT-5.4, Gemini-3) and domain-specific baselines across all reported evaluation dimensions. These results suggest that lifelong learning can improve the consistency and overall quality of multi-session counseling responses.

Introduction

Psychological counseling remains difficult to access in many settings because demand for mental-health support exceeds the supply of qualified professionals. Recent large language models (LLMs), including foundation models such as GPT-4o
(
hurst2024gpt
)
, Llama
(
touvron2023llama
)
, and DeepSeek
(
liu2024deepseek
)
, have shown strong semantic understanding, making them promising for tasks ranging from empathetic support to preliminary psychological assistance.

In response, the research community has increasingly specialized LLMs for mental health. Early efforts, such as ChatCounselor
(
liu2023chatcounselor
)
and SoulChat
(
chen2023soulchat
)
, used domain-specific fine-tuning to improve professional capability and emotional resonance. More recent work has moved toward agent-based systems with stronger clinical structure. For example, AutoCBT
(
xu2025autocbt
)
employs multi-agent collaboration to deliver structured Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, while PsyDT
(
xie2025psydt
)
introduces “Digital Twins” to model the personalized styles of specific therapists. Addressing the temporal dimension, MusPsy
(
wang2025psychological
)
emphasizes the importance of multi-session tracking. However, many of these systems remain largely static after training: whether relying on fixed weights or pre-defined workflows, they provide limited support for updating strategies from ongoing interactions.

This static paradigm stands in stark contrast to the professional growth of human counselors. A human therapist does not become an expert solely by studying textbooks or datasets; rather, they cultivate expertise through an
experience-driven loop
by accumulating “clinical intuition” from years of successful and unsuccessful cases.
This discrepancy suggests three key challenges in current AI counseling: 1) unlike human experts who refine techniques and abstract practical skills from experience, many SFT-based models remain fixed after training and do not update their strategies from interaction history; 2) counseling is inherently longitudinal, and human counselors rely on long-term memory and strategic planning to guide clients over weeks or months, whereas current models often lack persistent context for multi-session coherence; 3) human experts gradually transform accumulated knowledge into more internalized abilities that can transfer across scenarios, while existing models often depend on external retrieval or rigid prompts, with limited evidence that these learned behaviors are reflected more directly in model parameters.

To address this gap, we propose an Experience-Driven Lifelong Learning Agent (
PsychAgent
) for AI psychological counseling. Drawing inspiration from how human counselors improve through practice, our framework combines a Memory-Augmented Planning Engine for longitudinal continuity, a Skill Evolution Engine for extracting practice-grounded therapeutic skills from historical counseling trajectories, and a Reinforced Internalization Engine for integrating these evolved skills into model parameters via rejection fine-tuning. Experimental results show that PsychAgent achieves higher scores than strong general LLMs (e.g., GPT-5.4
(
openai2026gpt54
)
, Gemini-3
(
google2025gemini3
)
and domain-specific baselines, supporting the usefulness of learning from accumulated counseling experience.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
PsychAgent
, which, to our knowledge, is among the first frameworks to formulate AI psychological counseling as an experience-driven lifelong learning problem, loosely inspired by the professional development of human counselors.

•

We design a Memory-Augmented Planning Engine for longitudinal continuity, and a Skill Evolution mechanism combined with Reinforced Internalization to enable the autonomous discovery and assimilation of therapeutic skills. Extensive experiments show that our approach achieves state-of-the-art performance, demonstrating superior generalization and consistency in providing professional counseling services compared to existing methods.

Related Work

2.1
LLMs for Psychological Counseling

Recent work has adapted LLMs to mental-health support and psychological counseling through domain-specific data and supervision. Early efforts such as ChatCounselor
(
liu2023chatcounselor
)
, Psy-LLM
(
lai2023psy
)
, SoulChat
(
chen2023soulchat
)
, and MindChat
(
MindChat
)
mainly improve empathy, emotional support, and counseling-oriented dialogue generation. Subsequent work injects stronger therapeutic structure into the model or system design, for example through cognitive reframing
(
xiao2024healme
)
, CBT-grounded modeling
(
lee2024cactus
;
na2024cbt
)
, multi-agent coordination
(
xu2025autocbt
)
, or therapist-style modeling via digital twins
(
xie2025psydt
)
. In parallel, evaluation has become more systematic, covering counseling competency, therapy-specific ability, and ethical risks
(
nguyen2025large
;
zhang2025cbt
;
iftikhar2025llm
)
, together with broader human-evaluation frameworks for healthcare LLMs
(
tam2024framework
)
.

A parallel line of work emphasizes that counseling is inherently longitudinal rather than single-turn. MusPsy
(
wang2025psychological
)
, PsyDial
(
qiu2025psydial
)
, and PsychEval
(
pan2026psycheval
)
move toward multi-session data and evaluation, while TheraMind
(
hu2026theramindstrategicadaptiveagent
)
incorporates cross-session planning and adaptation into the agent itself. However, these systems are still largely static after training: they may better model history or follow structured workflows, but they do not explicitly extract new therapeutic skills from accumulated interactions and internalize them into model parameters. Our work targets this gap by framing AI psychological counseling as an experience-driven lifelong learning problem.

2.2
Experience-Driven Lifelong Learning

The evolution of Continual Learning (CL) has undergone a paradigm shift, transitioning from merely mitigating catastrophic forgetting in static architectures to enabling dynamic adaptation in LLMs
(
yang2025recent
)
. Traditional approaches largely addressed the stability-plasticity dilemma through regularization techniques like EWC
(
kirkpatrick2017overcoming
)
or experience replay mechanisms
(
rolnick2019experience
;
hadsell2020embracing
)
. While recent frameworks have introduced sophisticated memory management
(
huai2025task
)
and reinforcement learning
(
yang2025reinforced
)
to handle long-term interactions, they predominantly focus on data retention rather than high-level skill acquisition, often struggling with the semantic complexity of open-ended real-world tasks
(
wang2024comprehensive
)
.

Building on this direction, recent work has increasingly explored Self-Evolving Agents that refine capabilities through recursive loops of experience and reflection
(
gao2025survey
;
zheng2025lifelong
)
. Frameworks such as SAGE
(
liang2025sage
)
and Richelieu
(
guan2024richelieu
)
suggest that agents can improve reasoning through self-reflective critique. However, many existing systems still rely heavily on transient in-context learning or external retrieval (RAG) to exhibit “growth.” As formalized by Cai et al.
(
cai2025building
)
, Experience-Driven Lifelong Learning (ELL) ideally requires agents to autonomously acquire, refine, and use skills from dynamic environments. In this work, we apply this perspective to psychological counseling by modeling a cycle of accumulating clinical experience, abstracting therapeutic skills, and internalizing them through training.

3
Our Methods

As illustrated in Figure
1
, we propose
PsychAgent
, an experience-driven framework intended to capture aspects of how counselors improve through practice. The architecture includes three components: the Memory-Augmented Planning Engine, which supports therapeutic continuity across longitudinal sessions through long-term context; the Skill Evolution Engine, which extracts successful intervention strategies into a structured hierarchical skill tree; and the Reinforced Internalization Engine, which incorporates these explicit skills into model parameters via rejection-based reinforcement. By iterating over these stages,
PsychAgent
is designed to reduce reliance on static imitation and heavy external retrieval.

We formulate the AI psychological counseling task as a Lifelong Sequential Decision-Making problem, where an agent
\pi_{\theta}
interacts across a longitudinal timeline
\mathcal{T}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{T}\}
. For each session
S_{t}
, the agent generates a response trajectory conditioned on the evolving client state
\mathcal{M}_{t}
(Memory) and a dynamic repository of therapeutic strategies
\mathcal{K}_{t}
(Skills). The overarching objective is to maximize the cumulative therapeutic effectiveness
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{R}(S_{t})
through an iterative closed-loop process:
intra-session
, the agent utilizes a Memory-Augmented Planning Engine and a Skill Evolution Engine to retrieve explicit knowledge for immediate reasoning;
inter-session
, the agent employs a Reinforced Internalization Engine to lock in the optimal trajectory
S_{t}^{*}
—identified via rejection sampling from parallel rollouts—thereby continuously updating its parameters
\theta
to internalize successful clinical experiences.

3.1
Memory-Augmented Planning Engine

Standard LLMs often suffer from temporal incoherence in long-context interactions, failing to maintain a consistent therapeutic trajectory. To mitigate this, the Memory-Augmented Planning Engine (Figure
1
a) serves as the agent’s cognitive substrate, ensuring longitudinal continuity.
This engine is designed to support longitudinal continuity through three functions:
Dynamic Memory Construction
,
Strategic Planning
, and
Context-Aware Generation
.

Dynamic Memory Construction.

Unlike static context windows, we maintain a structured memory store
\mathcal{M}_{t}
that evolves alongside the therapy. This is composed of two parts:

•

Evolving Client Profile (
\mathcal{P}_{t}
):
Rather than a fixed intake form, the client profile is treated as a dynamic semantic memory. At the conclusion of each session
S_{t-1}
, the agent explicitly extracts new attributes (e.g., changes in family status, new medical diagnoses) to incrementally refine the profile:

\mathcal{P}_{t}\leftarrow\text{Update}(\mathcal{P}_{t-1},\text{Extract}_{\text{attr}}(S_{t-1}))

(1)

This helps the agent maintain an updated understanding of the client’s life circumstances.

•

Episodic Session Summaries (
\mathcal{H}_{t}
):
To compress long-term history, the agent generates a structured summary
h_{t-1}
of the previous interaction, capturing key emotional shifts and intervention outcomes, which is appended to the history:
\mathcal{H}_{t}=\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\cup\{h_{t-1}\}
.

Goal-Oriented Strategic Planning.

Therapy requires foresight. Before initiating session
S_{t}
, the engine performs
Plan Reasoning
to synthesize the updated memory
\mathcal{M}_{t}=(\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t})
into a coherent roadmap. The model predicts a structured plan
G_{t}
, encompassing:
1) Therapeutic Stage: Identifying the global phase (e.g.,
Case Conceptualization
,
Core Intervention
,
Consolidation and Prevention
); 2) Session Objectives: Formulating specific, actionable goals for the current session (e.g., “Reinforce cognitive restructuring techniques”).
This planning step provides a more structured framework for subsequent dialogue generation, rather than leaving the interaction purely reactive.

To endow the agent with these capabilities, we construct high-quality instruction data
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y)\}
for each sub-task and train the model to minimize the negative log-likelihood across all tasks:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{mem}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{plan}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{resp}}}\log P_{\theta}(y|x)

(2)

Specifically, the training corpus is constructed to cover the core stages of the counselor’s cognitive workflow, mapping distinct inputs
x
to targeted outputs
y
across three tasks: for Memory Extraction ((
\mathcal{D}_{\text{mem}}
)), the model learns to distill raw dialogue history into structured profile updates and session summaries; for Plan Reasoning (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{plan}}
), it learns to translate the accumulated memory state
\mathcal{M}_{t}
into strategic goals
G_{t}
; and for Response Generation (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{resp}}
), it conditions on memory, plan, and user input (
(u_{i},\mathcal{M}_{t},G_{t})
) to jointly generate chain-of-thought reasoning
z_{i}
and the final therapeutic response
r_{i}
. Explicit supervision of these steps is intended to improve the model’s structured counseling decision-making.

3.2
Skill Evolution Engine

While the Memory Engine anchors the agent in the client’s past, the Skill Evolution Engine (Figure
1
b) is designed to expand the model’s explicit repository of therapeutic techniques over time. Unlike static systems relying on fixed prompt engineering, this engine maintains a dynamic ontology of therapeutic techniques. We unify both the
utilization
and
evolution
of skills under a generative framework, acquired through rigorous Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on expert-annotated cognitive tasks.

Hierarchical Skill Taxonomy.

We formalize the skill knowledge base as a hierarchical tree structure
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{E})
, where nodes
\mathcal{N}
represent therapeutic skills organized into four granular levels:

•

Root Paradigms (
n_{root}
):
The foundational counseling frameworks that define the theoretical basis (e.g., CBT, Humanistic Therapy).

•

Therapeutic Stages (
n_{stage}
):
The macro-phases of the counseling process, specifically defined as
Case Conceptualization
,
Core Intervention
, and
Consolidation and Prevention
.

•

Meta Skills (
n_{meta}
):
High-level strategic categories nested within each stage (e.g.,
Empathy Building
in the conceptualization phase).

•

Atomic Skills (
n_{atomic}
):
Concrete, executable intervention tactics used in single turns (e.g., “Socratic Questioning,” “Metaphorical Reflection”).

Context-Aware Skill Retrieval.

During inference, the agent must select the optimal intervention strategy
\kappa^{*}
from the skill tree
\mathcal{T}_{t}
to guide the current response. This is modeled as a context-dependent generation task. Given the current dialogue state
s_{t}=(u_{i},\mathcal{M}_{t},G_{t})
, the model computes the probability of selecting specific atomic skills:

\kappa^{*}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{\kappa\in\mathcal{N}_{atomic}}P_{\theta}(\kappa|u_{i},\mathcal{M}_{t},G_{t})

(3)

The retrieved skill
\kappa^{*}
is then explicitly prepended to the generation context (visualized as the
<skill>
token), serving as a high-level directive for the subsequent response
r_{i}
.

Generative Skill Evolution.

A key capability of the Skill Evolution Engine is its ability to update the skill tree after each session through a hierarchical, reflective process. This is enabled by fine-tuning the model on two specialized tasks designed to enforce structural consistency:

•

Targeted Skill Extraction:
rather than generic abstraction, the evolution is anchored in the session’s therapeutic goal. The model first retrieves the most relevant Meta-Skill (
M
) aligned with the session goal. It then analyzes the high-reward trajectory
S^{*}
to abstract a specific Atomic Skill (
\kappa_{atom}
) that instantiates this meta-strategy:

\kappa_{atom}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(S^{*}\mid M,\text{Goal})

(4)

This ensures that new skills are categorized under the correct pedagogical branch.

•

Adaptive Skill Management:
To maintain an efficient knowledge base, the model performs a context-aware update within the identified Meta-Skill branch. It retrieves the existing atomic skill (
\kappa_{ref}
) most semantically similar to
\kappa_{atom}
. The model then executes a decision policy
\pi_{mgmt}
to either
append
the new skill as a distinct node or
merge
it with the existing one to refine the definition:

\mathcal{T}_{t+1}\leftarrow\begin{cases}\mathcal{T}_{t}\cup\{\kappa_{atom}\}&\text{if }\pi_{mgmt}(\kappa_{atom},\kappa_{ref})=\text{Append}\\
\text{Merge}(\mathcal{T}_{t},\kappa_{ref},\kappa_{atom})&\text{if }\pi_{mgmt}(\kappa_{atom},\kappa_{ref})=\text{Merge}\end{cases}

(5)

This mechanism prevents redundancy while allowing the continuous refinement of existing techniques.

Analogous to the optimization strategy employed in the Memory module, we construct specialized SFT data to equip the agent with these evolutionary capabilities. Through this targeted fine-tuning,
PsychAgent
learns to autonomously curate its own professional knowledge base, realizing true open-ended learning.

3.3
Reinforced Internalization Engine

While the Skill Evolution Engine expands the agent’s explicit knowledge base, the Reinforced Internalization Engine (Figure
1
c) focuses on transforming this external knowledge into endogenous intuition. To achieve this, we employ an iterative Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) mechanism
(
yuan2023scaling
)
, which mimics the human process of reflecting on and reinforcing successful counseling experiences while discarding ineffective ones.

Iterative Trajectory Rollout and Selection.

Unlike standard reinforcement learning that explores at the token level, our engine performs exploration at the
session level
to ensure coherent long-term strategies. As illustrated in the bottom panel of Figure
1
, the process unfolds iteratively for a sequence of sessions:

•

Parallel Rollout:
At any session step
t
, given the history of the previous optimal sessions
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{t-1}
, the agent generates
N
parallel candidate sessions
\{S_{t}^{(1)},\dots,S_{t}^{(N)}\}
. Each candidate explores different combinations of retrieved skills and dialogue strategies.

•

Reward Evaluation:
We employ a comprehensive Reward Model to assess the quality of each completed session candidate, denoted as
S_{t}^{(k)}
. To capture the complexity of real-world counseling, the evaluation metric
\mathcal{R}
is designed as a holistic integration of client- and counselor-centric dimensions using LLMs-as-a-Judge
(
gu2024survey
)
, following
(
pan2026psycheval
)
. The final reward score serves as a critical feedback signal, guiding the model toward strategies that are both clinically valid and psychologically supportive.

•

Greedy Selection:
We select the optimal session
S_{t}^{*}
that yields the maximum reward:
S_{t}^{*}=\operatorname{argmax}_{k}\mathcal{R}(S_{t}^{(k)})
. Crucially, the memory state for the subsequent session
t+1
is updated based
only
on this optimal trajectory
S_{t}^{*}
. This ensures that the agent’s lifelong learning path is constructed solely from its most successful interactions (marked with trophies in Figure
1
).

Internalization via Rejection Fine-Tuning.

After constructing the optimal lifelong trajectory
\mathcal{T}^{*}=\{S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*},\dots,S_{T}^{*}\}
, the engine utilizes this data for parameter optimization. We treat the rejected sessions (those with lower rewards, marked with crosses) as negative samples implicitly. The model is fine-tuned to maximize the likelihood of the selected ”golden” trajectory
\mathcal{T}^{*}
.

\mathcal{L}_{RFT}(\theta)=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log P_{\theta}(S_{t}^{*}|\mathcal{H}^{*}_{t-1})

(6)

Through this process, the explicit skills and planning logic used to generate
S_{t}^{*}
are gradually ”distilled” into the model’s weights
\theta
. Over time, this allows
PsychAgent
to produce high-quality responses intuitively, reducing its dependence on heavy external retrieval.

4.1
Experimental Setups

Datasets and Metrics.

We build our experiments on
PsychEval

(
pan2026psycheval
)
. Following its protocol, we first perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the released multi-session counseling corpus, and then reuse its pool of 2,000+ client profiles for both rollout training and evaluation. For each therapeutic school, we sample 140 client profiles, using 120 for training and 20 for evaluation.
We adopt the evaluation protocol of
PsychEval
. Across all therapeutic schools, we report the shared counseling metrics, including
PANAS
,
RRO
,
SRS
,
Custom_Dim
,
HTAIS
,
WAI
, and
Dialogue_Planning
. In addition, each school is evaluated with two school-specific metrics: behavioral therapy with
MITI
and
STAI
, cognitive-behavioral therapy with
CTRS
and
BDI_II
, postmodernist therapy with
EFT_TFS
and
SFBT
, humanistic-existential therapy with
TES
and
CCT
, and psychodynamic therapy with
PSC
and
IPO
. Following
PsychEval
, we report both counselor-side and client-side results, further organized into therapy-shared and therapy-specific dimensions.

Baselines.

We compare
PsychAgent
against two categories of baselines to disentangle the effects of general reasoning ability and domain-specific counseling specialization.
For
General-purpose LLMs
, we include several strong off-the-shelf LLMs: GPT-5.4
(
openai2026gpt54
)
, Gemini-3
(
google2025gemini3
)
, Qwen3-Max
(
yang2025qwen3
)
, and DeepSeek-V3.2
(
liu2025deepseek
)
. These baselines represent state-of-the-art general models and allow us to assess whether strong counseling performance can be achieved from general capability alone.
For
Psychology-Specific LLMs
, we include PsyLLM
(
lai2023psy
)
, PsyDTLLM
(
xie2025psydt
)
, CPsyCounX
(
zhang2024cpsycoun
)
, and TheraMind
(
hu2026theramindstrategicadaptiveagent
)
. PsyLLM incorporates explicit diagnostic and therapeutic reasoning, PsyDTLLM models counselor-specific style via digital twins, CPsyCounX is fine-tuned on reconstructed counseling dialogues, and TheraMind is designed for longitudinal counseling with cross-session planning and adaptation.

Implementation Details.

We finetune
PsychAgent
with Qwen3-32B and set the maximum context length to 32,768 tokens.
All models are trained with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 in
bf16
precision, using a per-device batch size of 1, gradient accumulation of 8, a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
, cosine decay, and 10% warmup for 3 epochs. The rollout number
N=8
.
History masking is enabled in all experiments unless otherwise specified. Training is conducted on two servers, each equipped with 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs.

Conclusions and Further Work

We introduced
PsychAgent
, an experience-driven lifelong learning framework for AI psychological counseling that integrates memory-augmented planning, skill evolution, and reinforced internalization. By moving beyond static fine-tuning,
PsychAgent
enables the counselor agent to improve through accumulated experience and deliver more consistent, professional, and effective multi-session support. Experiments across multiple therapeutic schools show that our framework consistently outperforms both strong general-purpose LLMs and psychology-specific baselines. Future work will focus on evaluating the framework in more realistic counseling settings and strengthening safety and privacy protections for real-world deployment.

B.1
Raters and Annotation Protocol

We conducted human evaluation on multi-session counseling dialogues generated by PsychAgent, Qwen3-Max, and TheraMind. The two human raters were graduate students with psychology-related training, and the third evaluator was LLM Rater (Gemini-3). All three raters evaluated the
counselor’s performance
, rather than the client’s state, on four dimensions:
Ethics
,
Interaction
,
Intervention
, and
Perception
. All scores were assigned on a
1–5
ordinal scale. Because these labels are ordered categorical variables, we report Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) as the primary agreement metric; QWK is more appropriate than unweighted agreement measures because it accounts for the ordinal distance between adjacent scores.

Human Evaluation Rubric and Annotation Principles

Evaluation target

Rate the
counselor’s performance
, rather than the client’s state, on four dimensions:
Ethics
,
Interaction
,
Intervention
, and
Perception
.

General principles

Ratings must rely only on information explicitly stated in the dialogue; unsupported inference is disallowed. A score of 3 serves as the default when evidence is limited, performance is unremarkable, or the relevant ability is not clearly manifested. Scores of 4–5 require clear supporting evidence, and a score of 5 is used sparingly. Obvious risks, boundary violations, misreadings, and unsafe responses lower the score. Opportunity-sensitive abilities are not penalized when the relevant scenario is absent.

Ethics

Whether the counselor responds safely, respects boundaries, avoids misleading diagnosis or labeling, and does not provide clearly unsafe or inappropriate guidance.

Interaction

Whether the counselor maintains a coherent, natural, and responsive conversation, avoids obvious repetition or non sequiturs, and moves the dialogue forward at an appropriate pace and tone.

Intervention

Whether the counselor provides help that is appropriate to the client’s current problem and state, and whether the guidance is clear, feasible, and practically usable.

Perception

Whether the counselor accurately understands and responds to the client’s emotions and psychological state, conveys empathy, and helps the client better understand themselves or see possible resources, hope, or direction.

Scoring rule

1 = clearly poor; 2 = weak; 3 = acceptable, unremarkable, or insufficient evidence; 4 = good with clear strengths and no major problems; 5 = very good, used conservatively and only when multiple clear strengths are supported by explicit evidence.

Evidence recording

For each dimension, raters provide one overall 1–5 score, one key positive evidence span, and, when applicable, one negative evidence span in a structured format.

Why this matters.

This rubric enforces evidence-grounded and conservative scoring, reduces annotation ambiguity, and makes human–LLM comparisons more interpretable and reproducible.

The matched evaluation set contains 522 fully rated dialogues from 25 cases and 188 case-session units. This yields 6,264 dimension-level observations and 2,088 paired ratings for each rater pair. One dialogue was excluded because all three rating files failed for the same item, leaving a nearly complete matched sample. The sample is balanced across counselor models (173 PsychAgent dialogues, 175 Qwen3-Max dialogues, and 174 TheraMind dialogues), which supports comparison at the overall, by-dimension, by-model, and model-by-dimension levels.

C.1
Extracting New Skills from Conversations

To better understand why
PsychAgent
improves counseling performance, we examine whether its extracted skills merely restate the initial skill library or instead capture
new practice-grounded skill units
from real counseling dialogue. Here, we define a practice-grounded skill unit as an extracted skill that introduces
non-trivial practical increments
beyond the closest existing micro-skills, rather than simply rephrasing or elaborating pre-existing entries. Concretely, the comparison baseline is the
v3
library within the same therapeutic orientation and stage, where micro-skills are already fine-grained and typically include
when_to_use
and
trigger
fields. As a result, a skill is counted as novel only when it adds practical structure that is not already captured by the nearest existing micro-skills.

Under this criterion, the answer is clearly positive. Across 715 dialogue records, the extraction pipeline produces 9,701 atomic skills, among which 5,923 qualify as practice-grounded skill units (
B
+
C
). This pattern holds across all five orientations: the proportion of practice-grounded skill units reaches 73.29% in BT, 69.77% in CBT, 56.0% in HET, 51.61% in PDT, and 63.49% in PMT. These results show that the dominant outcome of extraction is not restatement, but the distillation of practically enriched skill units relative to the closest existing micro-skills.

The practical increments are also highly consistent across orientations. The most frequent additions are clearer operational sequencing, minimum viable actions, quantitative thresholds, structured templates, monitoring or recording loops, and pause or rollback rules. In other words, the model does not mainly invent completely new therapy techniques; instead, it absorbs interactional experience from counseling trajectories and re-expresses it as more executable local skill units. At the same time, our stricter global audit finds no sufficiently supported candidates for
entirely novel therapy techniques
relative to the initial library. Even the strongest cases are better understood as stable repackagings of existing techniques with added thresholds, templates, monitoring rules, or minimum-action designs.

Representative cases from all five orientations further illustrate this pattern.

C.2
Operational New Functional Skills

To better understand why internalization improves counseling performance, we compare post-internalization behavior against both the initialized skill library and the SFT baseline. Two observations are central. First, internalization does not simply increase the number of multi-skill turns. Instead, it reorganizes existing micro-skills into more stable and functionally coherent turn-level packages. Second, some of these internalized packages are reused across therapies, suggesting that the model acquires transferable counseling principles rather than only therapy-specific templates. Together, these patterns indicate that internalization pushes the model toward a more general and versatile counselor.

The clearest evidence for internalization is not a mechanical increase in multi-skill turns, but the emergence of recurrent higher-order packages with clearer local intervention functions. In other words, the model does not merely place multiple skill labels in the same turn; rather, it increasingly organizes several existing micro-skills into compact intervention units that behave like
new functional skills
at the operational level.

Importantly, these packages should not be interpreted as newly invented canonical techniques. A more conservative interpretation is that internalization compresses multiple known micro-skills into recurrent, stable, and functionally closed turn-level units. This is why the strongest evidence for compositional generalization comes from the quality and stability of these packages, rather than from raw bundle counts alone.

Across therapies, these cases support the same conclusion: the model increasingly relies on recurrent functional packages built from existing skills. This pattern supports a conservative claim of
skill-level compositional generalization beyond the original inventory
: the model is not primarily inventing entirely new techniques, but reorganizing known micro-skills into higher-order functional units with clearer operational value.

A second important effect of internalization is that some of these functional packages become reusable across therapies. Here, however, the strongest evidence does not come from exact cross-therapy bundles within the same turn, since such strict mixed-therapy combinations remain limited. Instead, the more informative pattern is
cross-therapy reuse at the functional-family level
: similar intervention logics recur across different therapy-specific contexts while preserving related local counseling goals.

What is reused here is the structure of first stabilizing and naming the emotional experience, and only then moving toward deeper cognitive or experiential work.

Several reusable functional families illustrate the same pattern.
Goal-to-Practice Bridge
appears across multiple therapies as a shared logic for translating insight, discussion, or formulation into concrete between-session action.
Progress Consolidation Package
recurs as a stable way of summarizing gains, affirming client competence, and orienting the next phase of work.
Affect Attunement Before Deeper Work
shows another transferable structure, in which the counselor first stabilizes or names the client’s emotional experience before moving into more challenging exploratory work. In each case, what travels across therapies is not merely a skill label, but an intervention principle.

This distinction is important. The evidence does not support the stronger claim that internalization turns the model into a fully therapy-agnostic counselor, nor that it frequently produces large numbers of exact cross-therapy bundles. What it does support is a more careful conclusion: internalization enables some higher-order functional skills to be reused in multiple therapeutic contexts, suggesting that the model learns counseling principles that are portable beyond a single therapy-specific template.

Taken together, these findings indicate that internalization improves both the compositional organization and the portability of counseling skills. The model becomes better at turning existing micro-skills into reusable functional units, and some of these units begin to generalize across therapies. In this sense, internalization moves the model toward a more general and versatile counselor.
