Masked Unfairness: Hiding Causality within Zero ATE
Zou Yang, Sophia Xiao, Bijan Mazaheri

Abstract
Recent work has proposed powerful frameworks, rooted in causal theory, to quantify fairness. Causal inference has primarily emphasized the detection of average treatment effects (ATEs), and subsequent notions of fairness have inherited this focus. In this paper, we build on previous concerns about regulation based on averages. In particular, we formulate the “causal masking problem” as a linear program that optimizes an alternative objective, such as maximizing profit or minimizing crime, while retaining a zero ATE (i.e., the ATE between a protected attribute and a decision). By studying the capabilities and limitations of causal masking, we show that optimization under ATE-based regulation may induce significant unequal treatment. We demonstrate that the divergence between true and causally masked fairness is driven by confounding, underscoring the importance of full conditional-independence testing when assessing fairness. Finally, we discuss statistical and information-theoretic limitations that make causally masked solutions very difficult to detect, allowing them to persist for long periods. These results argue that we must regulate fairness at the model-level, rather than at the decision level.



1
Introduction

While modern methods for AI and machine learning are fantastic optimizers, they exhibit concerning potential for bias and unfair inference
[
12
]
.
1
, for example, showed that many statistical tools for parole are biased against minority groups.
As such, it is essential to develop formal notions of fairness that can be integrated into increasingly automated decision processes.

Numerous works have attempted to formalize fairness over the past decade
[
11
]
. One emerging thread of interest is the use of causal principles to distinguish systematically biased decisions from “confounded” imbalances driven by population and demographic differences. For example, hiring primarily male luggage handlers may still be considered “fair” so long as the decisions are based on the physical strength of job applicants and not a direct bias against women. However, explicitly denying employment to strong women is evidence of direct unfair bias. The framework introduced in
25
states that sex should not have a
causal
effect on manager hiring decisions.

A causal notion of fairness is advantageous because it enables the application of an increasingly rich body of literature on causal inference. Potential outcome
[
18
]
and graphically-based
[
26
]
models have revolutionized our ability to determine the causal effects of an intervention, usually expressed as the
average effect
across a population.

The ladder of causality places an important distinction between population-level effects (e.g, “What will happen, on average, if I take an action?”) and counterfactuals (e.g., “What would have happened if I had done something differently”)
[
26
]
. Counterfactuals exist at the pinnacle of causality because they represent the individual treatment effect (ITE) across multiple worlds, while the second rung only requires recovery of the average treatment effect (ATE) across a population.

Although ITEs are strictly more informative than ATEs, stricter parametric assumptions on the equations governing the system are required for their identification. Meanwhile, ATEs can be computed nonparametrically in many settings, only requiring knowledge of a network of dependencies, called a structural causal model (SCM), and a joint probability distribution. As such, much of the causal literature has focused on the second rung of the causal ladder: computing ATEs and conditional ATEs (CATEs) within more restricted populations.

Building a formal notion of fairness on the literature of causal inference implicitly inherits its gaps and insufficiencies. In the context of this paper, one such insufficiency is the focus on ATEs. In fact, previous work has pointed out that
average
and even parity-based notions of fairness can lead to unequal treatment of subgroups through “subset targeting”
[
11
]
. To address this,
19
proposed a regularization framework to ensure that the regression results are homogeneously fair. Still, enforcing the utilization of such techniques is difficult. Regulators often focus on the
outcomes
of potentially biased models rather than their algorithmic output, making statistical
detectability
critical.

All current causality-based (and many non-causality-based) methods for
detecting
unfair treatment focus on average population-level fairness. This paper studies the task of evading these fairness criteria (intentionally or unintentionally) by optimizing an auxiliary goal. Our findings demonstrate the importance of integrating modern notions of heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs)
[
36
]
, mixtures of treatment effects (MTEs)
[
21
]
, tests of conditional independence
[
38
,
10
,
4
,
32
]
into the study of fairness. We caution that conditional independence testing is an information-theoretically difficult task, and many tests for heterogeneous dependence impose strict assumptions and require large datasets
[
4
,
33
,
14
]
. This may allow causally masked policies to evade statistical detection if regulation is left at the level of decision data. Instead, we suggest building fairness regulations on the
model outputs
and enforcing them through “
in-process mechanisms
”
[
28
]
.

1.1
Summary of Contributions

In this paper, we introduce the “causal masking” problem, which seeks to optimize an auxiliary reward while satisfying a zero ATE between two variables of interest. A motivational example maintains a zero ATE between a protected attribute and admissions decisions, while optimizing for student graduations (or graduation rate). Similar structures may emerge in other settings, such as minimizing recidivism while maintaining a zero ATE for race on parole decisions. We show that
globally
regulated optimization can lead to significant violations of
stratified
fairness, despite appearing fair to average-based fairness metrics. This behavior is empirically demonstrated in
Section

7
for the parole setting using the well-known COMPAS dataset from ProPublica
[
1
]
. Code for both real-data and synthetic data experiments is provided in an
anonymized GitHub link
.

Under some simplifying assumptions, the causal masking problem can be formulated as a linear program (LP), which we outline in
Section

4
. This provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for studying the role of confounding variables in the disparity between “true” and “masked” fairness. We provide theorems in
Section

5
that illustrate how causal masking is inherently driven by the statistical dependence between a public source of information and the protected attribute, as well as by the dependence between that information and the optimized outcome.

Causal masking may be malicious or a naturally occurring consequence of optimization, as shown by genericity arguments in
Section

5
with more details in Appendix
B
. To mitigate the risk of causal masking, we advocate for fairness metrics based on full conditional-independence testing. This approach is inherently data-intensive, and systems that are heavily confounded may be causally masked for extended periods before we collect sufficient data to detect their behavior. This underscores the need for
proactive
and
model-level
regulatory action, rather than a (potentially easier)
reactive
approach based on data from decision outcomes.

1.2
Related Works

Formalizing Fairness.

Many works have formalized
definitions
of fairness as independence properties between the protected attributes and the decision
[
28
]
. Statistical parity holds if the protected attribute is
independent
from the decision
[
11
]
. Conditional statistical parity relaxed this notion to parity across subgroups
[
8
]
.
25
formalizes the problem within causal effects, while
20
discusses a related notion based on counterfactuals (at the ITE level), which uses structural equations. Path-specific fairness has refined these notions to decompose different mechanisms of unfair treatment
[
5
,
29
]
.

Our work studies the
detection
of fairness violations in decision-making.
20
’s use of learned functions in a model is in line with our suggestion to regulate models instead of decisions, where we are not vulnerable to statistical weaknesses of conditional independence testing.

Optimization and Fairness.

11
was the first to formalize fairness as a constraint within optimization, defining an LP that optimizes decisions over individuals.
8
studied this in more detail, quantifying the “cost of fairness” in the objective function. Also within the scope of optimization,
7
studied the geometry of fairness constraints and showed that fairness constraints often yield suboptimal solutions, hurting the groups they intended to help. Our optimization problem differs only slightly, focusing on decision rates across groups rather than decisions on individuals. We study the effect of ATE-based constraints, which were not addressed in these earlier works.
24
presented an alternative formulation of policy optimization for online reinforcement learning with an algorithmic focus, using the path-specific effect constraints of
25
(which are also averages). Our paper is primarily concerned with the choices of constraints and is therefore complementary.

Fairness in Predictions.

A parallel line of work has studied fairness with respect to predictions, e.g., equalized odds
[
2
]
, equal opportunity
[
13
]
, and predictive parity
[
6
]
and its relationship to calibration
[
30
]
. Principal fairness, based on potential outcomes, states that individuals who are affected similarly by a decision should be treated similarly
[
17
]
.

Computing HTEs.

There are many methods for
computing
heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs), which may be useful for detecting causal masking.
36
developed causal forests to determine HTEs within clusters, which pairs closely with a cluster-specific optimization that likely assigns a different CATE to each cluster.
Mixtures of treatment effects (MTEs) have also been studied to learn heterogeneity with significant overlap in a covariate space
[
21
]
. While our work focuses on naturally- and adversarially-occurring heterogeneity that is separated by observed covariates, it is possible that subgroups that are indistinguishable by observed covariates may still receive unequal treatment. MTE-based methods may extend our concepts to counterfactual notions of fairness.

We focus solely on
detecting
unfairness rather than
computing
its distribution, for which hypothesis testing is a sufficient statistical tool. Within this line of work, there are a number of heterogeneity tests that focus on computational and statistical challenges associated with continuous variables and regression
[
9
,
16
]
. We argue that such tests will become critical for assessing fairness in human decisions, and when the underlying target frequencies given by a model are unknown.

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[D^{(1)}-D^{(0)}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\Pr(x)[\mathbb{E}[D\>|\>x,p_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[D\>|\>x,p_{0}]].

Causal Graphs.

Causal masking settings will be described using “structural causal models” with directed edges representing causal relationships between the random variables (the vertices). A critical insight from the work of
26
is that the causal structure informs the statistical independencies of the random variables via graphical d-separation rules. The faithfulness assumption, often utilized when learning causal structures
[
35
]

[
23
]
, but

\varepsilon
, meaning that finite-sample regimes that test for conditional independence with significance thresholds are much more likely to struggle with the consequences of “near” faithfulness violations. This insight will become important when discussing relaxations of the fairness and masking constraints (defined later).

4
Causal Masking

Motivated by the previous section, we formalize the causal masking problem as a linear program (LP). Suppose that we have a decision
D\in\{0,1\}
representing “when to participate.” Examples of participation include granting parole, making a hire, or admitting a student. The goal of these decisions is to maximize a reward
Y
when
D=1
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[Y\>|\>d_{1}]
. An equivalent formulation is to maximize
\mathbb{E}[Y\>|\>d_{1}]\Pr(d_{1})
subject to the participation rate,
\Pr(d_{1})
, being constant.

The features on which we may base our policy are divided into
P
, which is protected, and
X
, which is unprotected. For simplicity, we treat
X\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
P\in\{0,1\}
as scalars. Continuous and multivariate
\mathbf{X}
can be reduced to this setting using clustering algorithms, such as k-means. While this reduces the information capacity of the more complicated
\mathbf{X}
, the results obtained in the coarsened
X
serve as a lower bound on the achievable objective in the more general case, with the parameter
k
providing a natural way to study the problem’s “resolution”. It is also possible to extend to multivariate and continuous
\mathbf{P}
using multivariate and continuous interventions
[
26
]
, but such extensions are beyond the scope of this work. Our choice of binary
Y
is arbitrary, since we only maximize its expectation and conditional expectation.

A decision
policy
is given by a function
\alpha(x,p)
that maps the support of
X,P
onto
[0,1]
, indicating the “rates of participation” for each
x,u
pair. Such a decision policy yields a random variable with probabilities given by these participation rates,
D\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\alpha(\mathbf{x},p))
. Under this specification, we may now assume without loss of generality that we are optimizing a deterministic function of
x,p
, which we call
\gamma(x,p)=\mathbb{E}[Y\>|\>x,p]
. Our goal is to select an “optimal” decision policy that maximizes
\mathbb{E}[Y\>|\>d_{1}]\Pr(d_{1})
subject to a constraint of constant participation rate
\Pr(d_{1})
.

4.1
LP Formulation

When
X,P
are discrete, a policy is represented by a vector of frequencies
\alpha_{x,p}=\Pr(d_{1}\>|\>x,p)
, and a reward function is represented by a vector of expected rewards
\gamma_{x,p}=\mathbb{E}[Y\>|\>x,p]
, each of which is indexed by the values of
X
and
P
. This also allows us to index
\Pr(x,p)=\pi_{x,p}
.

Using the law of total expectation and
D\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Y\>|\>X,P
, the objective is:

\mathcal{W}(D)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[Y\cdot D\mid X,P]]=\sum_{x,p}\gamma_{x,p}\alpha_{x,p}\pi_{x,p}.

The following three optimization problems formalize “exploitation”, “fairness”, and “masking” as linear programs. In order maintain equivalence with maximizing the
rate
of wins, we add a participation constraint
\sum_{x,p}\alpha_{x,p}\pi_{x,p}=\rho
.

Proposition 4.1

(Optimal Exploit Policy)
.

The “exploit” approach optimally uses
X,P
without further constraints:

\displaystyle\max_{\{\alpha_{x,p}\}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(D)=\sum_{x=1}^{k}\gamma_{x_{i},1}\alpha_{x,1}\pi_{x,1}+\gamma_{x,0}\alpha_{x,0}\pi_{x,0}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\alpha_{x,p}\leqslant 1\quad\forall x,p

\displaystyle\sum_{x,p}\alpha_{x,p}\pi_{x,p}=\rho.

The optimal policy participates greedily with nonzero
\alpha_{x,p}
in
(x_{1}^{*},p_{1}^{*})
with the highest possible reward function,
(x_{1}^{*},p_{1}^{*})=\arg\max_{x,p}\gamma(x,p)
, then the second highest
(x_{2}^{*},p_{2}^{*})
, and so on until the participation rate is exhausted.

Proposition 4.2

(Optimal
\varepsilon
-Fair Policy)
.

The optimal
\varepsilon_{\text{fair}}
-fair policy,
D_{\text{fair}}(\varepsilon_{\text{fair}})
, imposes a “within
\varepsilon_{\text{fair}}
”
fairness constraint
of
P\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}D\>|\>X

. The additional constraint is

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle-\varepsilon_{\text{fair}}\leqslant\alpha_{x,1}-\alpha_{x,0}\leqslant\varepsilon_{\text{fair}}\quad\forall x=1,\ldots,k.

The optimal solution for
\varepsilon=0
can be expressed using

w_{x}^{\text{avg}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[Y\mid x]=\gamma_{x,1}\Pr(p_{1}|x)+\gamma_{x,0}\Pr(p_{0}|x).

Let
x^{*}_{1}=\arg\max_{x}w_{x}^{\text{avg}}
, and all following

so that
x^{*}_{1},x^{*}_{2},\ldots,x^{*}_{k}
represent an ordering of the best to worst settings. The optimal fair policy is to always participate in context
x^{*}_{1}
, then

Proposition 4.3

(Optimal
\varepsilon
-Masking Policy)
.

The optimal
\varepsilon_{\text{mask}}
-masking policy,
D_{\text{mask}}(\varepsilon_{\text{mask}})
, is the solution to the LP with a

that bounds the ATE:

\displaystyle\max_{\{\alpha_{x,p}\}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(D)=\sum_{x=1}^{k}\gamma_{x,1}\alpha_{x,1}\pi_{x,1}+\gamma_{x,0}\alpha_{x,0}\pi_{x,0}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle-\varepsilon_{\text{mask}}\leqslant\sum_{x}(\pi_{x,0}+\pi_{x,1})(\alpha_{x,1}-\alpha_{x,0})\leqslant\varepsilon_{\text{mask}}

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\alpha_{x,p}\leqslant 1\quad\forall x,p

\displaystyle\sum_{x,p}\alpha_{x,p}\pi_{x,p}=\rho.

Both

5
Theoretical Results

This section establishes key properties of causal masking. We lower-bound the benefit of causal masking relative to the fair solution and ultimately argue that masking is a
natural outcome
of optimization when subject to a zero ATE constraint, even when the decision-maker is not acting adversarially. All proofs are in Appendix
A
.

5.1
Improvements from Masking

The optimal fair policy,
D_{\text{fair}}
, is a “water-filling” algorithm that selects the best strata by average reward until the participation budget
\rho
is met. If
\rho
is sufficiently small, then this procedure simply chooses the single best stratum
x^{*}=\arg\max_{x}w_{x}^{\text{avg}}
.
Starting from this baseline, a decision-maker could be tempted to make an “unfair” swap to increase utility. For example, they could stop participating with group
p=0
in fair stratum
x_{i}
and compensate by increasing participation with group
p=0
in some other stratum
x_{j}
. Such a change must then scale the participation rate to keep it constant. This swap-and-scale procedure provides a lower bound on the performance gap that is valid in “low participation rate” (small
\rho
) scenarios.

Definition 5.1
.

Define the ”arbitrage rate of return” for a policy that trades participation between states
(i,\overline{p})
and
(j,p)
as:

R_{p}(j)\coloneqq\frac{w_{i,\overline{p}}+w_{j,p}}{\Pr(\overline{p}|x_{i})+\Pr(p|x_{j})}

where
\overline{p}=\lvert 1-p\rvert
and
w_{x,p}=\gamma_{x,p}\Pr(p|x)
.

Theorem 5.2

(The Gap between Masking and Fairness)
.

Assume the participation rate
\rho
is sufficiently small such that the optimal fair policy,
D_{\text{fair}}
, only participates in the best stratum
x^{*}=x_{i}
. The performance gap,
\Delta=\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{mask}})-\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{fair}})
, is bounded by:

\Delta\geqslant\rho\cdot\max\left(0,\max_{j\neq i,p\in\{0,1\}}\left\{R_{p}(j)-w_{i}^{\text{avg}}\right\}\right)

To see this theorem in action, we apply it to the motivating example in
Table

1
. First, we calculate that the best stratum for the fair policy is
x^{*}=x_{0}
, with an average reward of
w_{0}^{\text{avg}}=0.5
. The most profitable arbitrage opportunity involves trading between state
(i,\overline{p})=(0,0)
and
(j,p)=(1,1)
. This yields an arbitrage rate of return
R_{1}(1)=\frac{5}{6}
.
Plugging these values into
Theorem

5.2
, the performance gap is bounded by
\Delta\geqslant\rho\cdot(5/6-0.5)=\rho/3
. The specific masking policy shown in the table has a participation rate of
\rho=0.1
. For this value, the theorem guarantees a minimum gap of
0.1/3=\nicefrac{{1}}{{30}}
. Remarkably, for this example, the bound is tight. The optimal fair policy yields a utility of
\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{fair}})=0.1\times 0.5=\nicefrac{{1}}{{20}}
, while the
D_{\text{mask}}
policy yields a utility of
\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{mask}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{12}}
. The true gap is
\nicefrac{{1}}{{12}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{20}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{30}}
, exactly matching our lower bound.

5.2
The Role of the Observed
X

The performance gap between the masking and fair policies is a direct consequence of data dependencies. A gap can only emerge if the optimizer can exploit either (1) demographic disparities between strata or (2) heterogeneous outcome patterns across those strata. These correspond to the dependencies
P\not\perp\!\!\!\perp X
(confounding) and
X\not\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid P
(heterogeneity), respectively.

From
Theorem

5.2
, a gap can exist if the arbitrage rate of return,
R_{p}(j)
, exceeds the fair policy’s return,
w_{i}^{\text{avg}}
; the existence of such a gap is characterized by two sufficient conditions:

1. Heterogeneity (
X\not\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid P
):

Even if there is no confounding (
P\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X
, so
\Pr(P=p|x)=\Pr(P=p)
), the rewards
\gamma_{x,p}
can still vary across strata
x
. The context-weighted rewards
w_{x,p}=\gamma_{x,p}\Pr(P=p)
will therefore also vary. The arbitrage rate
R_{p}(j)
becomes a mix of different reward terms (e.g., from
\gamma_{i,1-p}
and
\gamma_{j,p}
) that can easily outperform the simple average reward
w_{i}^{\text{avg}}
of the fair policy’s chosen stratum. Therefore, heterogeneity in outcomes is a sufficient driver for a performance gap.

2. Confounding (
P\not\perp\!\!\!\perp X
):

Even if there are no heterogeneous effects (
X\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Y\mid P
, so
\gamma_{x,p}=\gamma_{p}
), the propensity scores
\Pr(p|x)
can vary across strata
x
. In this case, the arbitrage rate of return is

R_{p}(j)=\frac{\gamma_{1-p}\Pr(\overline{p}|x_{i})+\gamma_{p}\Pr(p|x_{j})}{\Pr(\overline{p}|x_{i})+\Pr(p|x_{j})}.

This is a weighted average of the base rewards
\gamma_{p}
and
\gamma_{1-p}
. The fair policy’s return,
w_{i}^{\text{avg}}=\gamma_{0}\Pr(p_{0}|x_{i})+\gamma_{1}\Pr(p_{1}|x_{i})
, is also a weighted average. Because the weights (the propensities) in
R_{p}(j)
are drawn from the different propensities of strata

j

The following theorems formalize that a gap exists if and only if either of these two dependencies holds.

Theorem 5.3

(Necessary Conditions for a Performance Gap)
.

Let
\Delta=\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{mask}})-\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{fair}})

P\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X
AND
X\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Y\mid P

5.3
The Role of Regulation

Testing for non-zero ATEs can be done with a simple z-test whose sample complexity does not depend on

Causally masked solutions require a full heterogeneity test, which partitions the data into

This implies that unfair solutions that are not causally masked are likely to be easier to detect than those that are.
This subsection argues that ATE-based regulation does not necessarily improve fairness and instead pushes solutions into this “hard-to-detect” realm.

In the infinite sample regime, we give basic genericity results in Appendix
B
, summarized in three points:

1.

Without regulation, solutions are unfair and unmasked.

2.

Optimized solutions are also unfair and unmasked.

3.

ATE-based constraints still result in unfair solutions.

This shows that imposing zero ATE regulations almost inevitably leads to causally masked policies, for which fairness is more difficult to detect than in unregulated settings.

We can also quantify the size of the feasible sets. In Appendix

In this section, we simulate a parole policy based on causal masking while optimizing for minimal 2-year recidivism. We show that the resulting outcome evades detection of its ATE while remaining unfair. Due to the statistical difficulties in conditional independence testing, the new masked policy persists for a long time until there is sufficient data to detect its (stratified) unfairness.

8
Discussion

This paper introduces the causal masking problem, formulates it as an LP, and examines its properties. We showed that ATE-based regulation almost always leads optimizers to unfair solutions. Although this unfairness is essentially certain, detecting such unfairness is information-theoretically difficult. As a result, these unfair models can remain undetected for years.
More complex observed covariates (larger
k
) significantly widen the detectability gap between generically unfair and masked unfair policies, while also allowing more tempting masking solutions. If we restrict ourselves to decision statistics, we should accept causal masking as inevitable and essentially impossible to detect or regulate.

These limitations are fundamentally grounded in the detectability of conditional independence and therefore extend beyond causal notions of fairness to any form of conditional parity. This has widespread implications for many fairness applications, including college admissions and criminal justice, as discussed in this paper, as well as affirmative action
[
15
]
, and the gender wage gap
[
27
]
— all of which have been predominantly analyzed using conditional (adjusted) averages. Given these challenges, we argue that fairness regulation should operate at the model level, where the internal decision mechanism is accessible, rather than solely at the level of observed decisions.

There are several natural extensions to our work. The first involves the study of
success rate maximization
with an unfixed participation rate. Examples include maximizing the win rate when counting cards (while avoiding detection) or maximizing graduation rate or average GPA. Such a problem is a linear-fractional program with different properties.

Another important extension will involve generalizing to measures of global dependence beyond ATEs, such as global odds ratios, which can be detected using a CMH test
[
37
]
, or conditional mutual information. These properties impose distinct constraints, thereby opening avenues for significant future work.

Appendix A
Deferred Proofs

A.1
Proof of Theorem
5.2

Proof.

The proof proceeds by comparing the utility of the optimal fair policy to a specially constructed, valid masking policy candidate,
D^{\prime\prime}_{p,j}
.

1. Utility of the Optimal Fair Policy:

Under the small
\rho
assumption, the optimal fair policy is to participate only in stratum
i
with rates
\alpha_{i,1}=\alpha_{i,0}=\rho/\Pr(X=i)
. The utility is:

\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{fair}})=\rho\cdot w_{i}^{\text{avg}}.

2. Construct a Valid Masking Candidate Policy,
D^{\prime\prime}_{p,j}
:

We build a candidate policy from scratch that is inspired by an arbitrage between states
(i,1)
and
(j,0)
(the argument for
(i,0)
and
(j,1)
is symmetric).

Step A: Define the Unscaled Arbitrage Policy (
D^{\prime}
).

Let’s construct an intermediate policy
D^{\prime}
that has non-zero participation only for rates
\alpha^{\prime}_{i,1}
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{j,0}
. To satisfy the zero ATE constraint, these rates must obey the balance equation:
\Pr(X=i)\alpha^{\prime}_{i,1}=\Pr(X=j)\alpha^{\prime}_{j,0}
. To make this policy as extreme as possible while keeping all rates
\leqslant 1
, we set one rate to 1 and solve for the other. This results in the policy:

\alpha^{\prime}_{i,1}=\frac{\min(\Pr(X=i),\Pr(X=j))}{\Pr(X=i)}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha^{\prime}_{j,0}=\frac{\min(\Pr(X=i),\Pr(X=j))}{\Pr(X=j)}.

For example, if
\Pr(X=i)
is larger, then
\alpha^{\prime}_{i,1}<1
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{j,0}=1
.

Step B: Calculate the Rate of Return of
D^{\prime}
.

The utility of this unscaled policy is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(D^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}_{i,1}\gamma_{i,1}\pi_{i,1}+\alpha^{\prime}_{j,0}\gamma_{j,0}\pi_{j,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\min(\dots)}{\Pr(x_{i})}\gamma_{i,1}\Pr(P=1|x_{i})\Pr(x_{i})+\frac{\min(\dots)}{\Pr(x_{j})}\gamma_{j,0}\Pr(P=0|x_{j})\Pr(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\min(\Pr(X=i),\Pr(X=j))\cdot[w_{i,1}+w_{j,0}].

The participation rate of this policy is:

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}_{i,1}\pi_{i,1}+\alpha^{\prime}_{j,0}\pi_{j,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\min(\dots)}{\Pr(X=i)}\Pr(P=1|x_{i})\Pr(x_{i})+\frac{\min(\dots)}{\Pr(X=j)}\Pr(P=0|x_{j})\Pr(x_{j})

\displaystyle=\min(\Pr(X=i),\Pr(x_{j}))\cdot[\Pr(P=1|i)+\Pr(P=0|j)].

The key insight is that the rate of return,
\mathcal{W}(D^{\prime})/\rho^{\prime}
, is symmetric and simplifies because the “min” term cancels out:

\frac{\mathcal{W}(D^{\prime})}{\rho^{\prime}}=\frac{w_{i,1}+w_{j,0}}{\Pr(P=1|i)+\Pr(P=0|j)}=R_{0}(j).

Step C: Scale to Create Valid Candidate
D^{\prime\prime}
.

We now define our final candidate policy
D^{\prime\prime}_{0,j}
by scaling all rates of
D^{\prime}
by a factor of
(\rho/\rho^{\prime})
. This policy is guaranteed to be valid if we assume
\rho
is sufficiently small (specifically,
\rho\leqslant\rho^{\prime}
). By construction, its participation rate is exactly
\rho
, and its utility is:

\mathcal{W}(D^{\prime\prime}_{0,j})=\rho\cdot R_{0}(j).

3. Bounding the Gap:

The optimal masking policy’s utility,
\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{mask}})
, must be at least as high as the utility of the best of these constructed candidates. The performance gap
\Delta
is therefore bounded by:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{mask}})-\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{fair}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\max_{j\neq i,p\in\{0,1\}}\left\{\mathcal{W}(D^{\prime\prime}_{p,j})\right\}-\mathcal{W}(D_{\text{fair}})

\displaystyle=\rho\cdot\max_{j\neq i,p\in\{0,1\}}\left\{R_{p}(j)\right\}-\rho\cdot w_{i}^{\text{avg}}.

Factoring out
\rho
and ensuring the gap is non-negative completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Genericity of Constraints and Solutions

B.1
Genericity of Constraints

A policy is a vector
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{x_{1},0},\alpha_{x_{1},1},\dots,\alpha_{x_{k},0},\alpha_{x_{k},1})
in the policy space
\mathcal{P}=[0,1]^{2k}
. We first show that an arbitrarily chosen policy is almost never fair or masked.

Theorem B.1
.

The set of policies
\vec{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{2k}
that satisfy (a) the fairness constraint or (b) the masking constraint are sets of Lebesgue measure zero in
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

The space of all possible policies
\mathcal{P}=[0,1]^{2k}
is a
2k
-dimensional hypercube with volume 1.

(a) Fairness Constraint:
The fairness constraint is
A\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}U\mid X
, which corresponds to the set of
k
linear equality constraints:

\alpha_{x,1}-\alpha_{x,0}=0\quad\text{for each }x\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}.

These
k
constraints are independent, as each involves a disjoint pair of coordinates of
\vec{\alpha}
. Geometrically, the set of policies satisfying these constraints is the intersection of
k
distinct hyperplanes with the hypercube
\mathcal{P}
. This intersection forms a
k
-dimensional manifold within the
2k
-dimensional space
\mathcal{P}
. For any
k\geqslant 1
, we have
k<2k
. A
k
-dimensional manifold has zero Lebesgue measure in a
2k
-dimensional space. Thus, the set of fair policies has measure zero.

(b) Masking Constraint:
The masking constraint corresponds to a single linear equality:

\sum_{x=1}^{k}\Pr(X=x)(\alpha_{x,1}-\alpha_{x,0})=0,

where
\Pr(X=x)=\pi_{x,0}+\pi_{x,1}
. Assuming not all
\Pr(X=x)
are zero, this is a single non-trivial linear constraint on the coordinates of
\vec{\alpha}
. Geometrically, this equation defines a
(2k-1)
-dimensional hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{2k}
. The set of policies satisfying the constraint is the intersection of this hyperplane with the hypercube
\mathcal{P}
, which is a
(2k-1)
-dimensional manifold. Since
2k-1<2k
, this set has Lebesgue measure zero in
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Theorem B.2
.

The set of world parameters

Proof.

Let
w^{\prime}_{x,u}\coloneqq\gamma_{x,u}\pi_{x,u}
. The objective is
\mathcal{W}(D)=\sum_{x,u}w^{\prime}_{x,u}\alpha_{x,u}
. For a generic choice of parameters, the values of
w^{\prime}_{x,u}
will be unique. In this case, the optimal exploit policy is a pure strategy: set
\alpha_{x^{*},u^{*}}=1
for
(x^{*},u^{*})=\arg\max_{x,u}w^{\prime}_{x,u}
, and all other
\alpha_{x,u}=0
.

(a) Fairness Constraint:
The pure exploit policy sets one coordinate to 1 and the rest to 0. For this to satisfy the fairness constraints
\alpha_{x,1}=\alpha_{x,0}
for all
x
, every coordinate must be 0 (if all
w^{\prime}_{x,u}\leqslant 0
) or 1. A non-trivial pure exploit policy necessarily violates the constraint at stratum
x^{*}
, where it sets
(\alpha_{x^{*},1},\alpha_{x^{*},0})
to either
(1,0)
or
(0,1)
.

A non-pure exploit policy is optimal only if there is a tie for the maximum value of
w^{\prime}_{x,u}
. For an optimal exploit solution to be fair, it must be that a fair policy is also an optimal exploit solution. This requires a specific alignment of parameters. For instance, if the optimal fair policy is
\alpha_{x^{\prime},1}=\alpha_{x^{\prime},0}=1
for some
x^{\prime}
, then for this to also be an optimal exploit solution, we need
w^{\prime}_{x^{\prime},1}+w^{\prime}_{x^{\prime},0}\geqslant w^{\prime}_{x,u}
for all
(x,u)
and for any other fair policy. A necessary condition for such a policy to be expressible as an optimal exploit solution is a tie, such as
w^{\prime}_{x^{\prime},1}=w^{\prime}_{x^{\prime},0}
being the two highest values. This equality,
\gamma_{x^{\prime},1}\pi_{x^{\prime},1}=\gamma_{x^{\prime},0}\pi_{x^{\prime},0}
, is a single non-trivial equality constraint on the continuous parameters
(\pi,\gamma)
and thus defines a set of Lebesgue measure zero in
\Theta
.

(b) Masking Constraint:
Plugging the pure exploit policy
\alpha_{x^{*},u^{*}}=1
into the masking constraint gives
\Pr(X=x^{*})(\delta_{u^{*},1}-\delta_{u^{*},0})=0
, which implies
\Pr(X=x^{*})=0
, a trivial case. Thus, a pure exploit policy cannot satisfy the masking constraint.

The constraint can only be satisfied if the optimal exploit policy is non-pure, which requires a tie, e.g.,
w^{\prime}_{x_{A},u_{A}}=w^{\prime}_{x_{B},u_{B}}=\max_{x,u}w^{\prime}_{x,u}
. This is an equality constraint on
(\pi,\gamma)
, e.g.,
\gamma_{x_{A},u_{A}}\pi_{x_{A},u_{A}}=\gamma_{x_{B},u_{B}}\pi_{x_{B},u_{B}}
, which defines a measure-zero set.
∎

Theorem B.3
.

The set of world parameters
(\pi,\gamma)\in\Theta
for which the optimal masking policy is also a fair policy is a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

Proof.

The fair policies are a strict subset of the feasible set for the masking problem. Therefore,
\mathcal{W}(A_{\text{mask}})\geqslant\mathcal{W}(A_{\text{fair}})
. The solutions are identical if and only if the maximum over the larger set happens to lie in the smaller subset.

The optimal masking policy is a double-threshold strategy:
\alpha_{x,1}=\mathbb{I}(w_{x,1}>\lambda)
and
\alpha_{x,0}=\mathbb{I}(w_{x,0}>-\lambda)
, where
w_{x,u}\coloneqq\gamma_{x,u}\Pr(u|x)
and
\lambda
is a Lagrange multiplier chosen to satisfy the constraint (we ignore measure-zero cases where the decision is fractional).

For this policy to be fair, it must be that
\alpha_{x,1}=\alpha_{x,0}
for all
x
. This means that for the optimal
\lambda^{*}
, every stratum
x
must have a policy of either
(1,1)
or
(0,0)
. No stratum can have an ”unfair” policy of
(1,0)
or
(0,1)
. A policy is
(1,0)
if
w_{x,1}>\lambda^{*}
and
w_{x,0}\leqslant-\lambda^{*}
. A policy is
(0,1)
if
w_{x,1}\leqslant\lambda^{*}
and
w_{x,0}>-\lambda^{*}
.

The masking LP is free to select such ”unfair” policies for individual strata if doing so increases the global objective, provided the choices can be balanced to satisfy the zero ATE constraint. For instance, suppose stratum
x_{A}
has a large positive
w_{x_{A},1}
and stratum
x_{B}
has a large positive
w_{x_{B},0}
. The masking LP can potentially set
\alpha_{x_{A},1}=1
and
\alpha_{x_{B},0}=1
(and others zero), yielding a high objective value. This policy is unfair. It is feasible for the masking problem if
\Pr(X=x_{A})(1-0)+\Pr(X=x_{B})(0-1)=0
, i.e., if
\Pr(X=x_{A})=\Pr(X=x_{B})
.

The optimal masking solution is fair only if, for the given parameters
(\pi,\gamma)
, no such profitable ”unfair” combination of choices exists. The condition where an unfair policy (like the one above) gives an objective value *equal to* the optimal fair policy’s objective value is an equality constraint on the parameters
(\pi,\gamma)
. For instance:

\gamma_{x_{A},1}\pi_{x_{A},1}+\gamma_{x_{B},0}\pi_{x_{B},0}=\max_{x}\left(\gamma_{x,1}\pi_{x,1}+\gamma_{x,0}\pi_{x,0}\right)

Such equations define the boundaries between regions in the parameter space
\Theta
where different types of policies are optimal. These boundaries are lower-dimensional manifolds and thus have Lebesgue measure zero. For a generic choice of parameters, one side of the inequality will hold, and it is overwhelmingly likely that there exists some profitable, unfair trade-off. The masking solution will thus almost never be a fair one.
∎

Appendix C
The Geometry of Fair and Masked Policies

Let the world of interest be defined by a policy vector
\vec{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{2k}
, with constraints carving out a feasible region within this hypercube. The size of this region represents the “freedom” the optimizer has to find a good solution. When constraints are perfectly enforced (
\varepsilon=0
), the feasible sets become lower-dimensional affine subspaces. Their dimension quantifies their size.

Theorem C.1

(Dimensionality of Feasible Sets)
.

Let
V_{\text{fair}}
and
V_{\text{mask}}
be the feasible sets for the perfectly fair and masking problems (
\varepsilon_{\text{fair}}=\varepsilon_{\text{mask}}=0
), respectively.

1.

Containment:

V_{\text{fair}}\subseteq V_{\text{mask}}
.

2.

Dimensionality:
The dimension of
V_{\text{fair}}
is
k-1
, while the dimension of
V_{\text{mask}}
is
2k-2
.

3.

Growth of Freedom:
The masking problem has
k-1
more dimensions of freedom than the fair problem.

Proof.

(1) Any policy satisfying the
k
local fairness constraints,
\alpha_{x,1}-\alpha_{x,0}=0
, also satisfies the global masking constraint,
\sum_{x}\Pr(X=x)(\alpha_{x,1}-\alpha_{x,0})=0
.
(2) The policy space is
2k
-dimensional. The fair problem imposes
k
local fairness constraints and 1 global participation constraint, for a total of
k+1
independent linear equality constraints. The resulting dimension is
2k-(k+1)=k-1
. The masking problem imposes 1 global ATE constraint and 1 global participation constraint, for 2 total constraints. The dimension is
2k-2
.
(3) The difference is
(2k-2)-(k-1)=k-1
.
∎

This result shows that the masking policy’s advantage, in terms of the dimensionality of its search space, grows linearly with the number of strata,
k
. When
\varepsilon>0
, the constraints define “slabs” instead of thin hyperplanes, and the feasible sets become full-dimensional bodies. We can compare their volumes.

Proposition C.2

(Volume of Feasible Sets)
.

Let
\text{Vol}(V(\varepsilon))
be the volume of the feasible set for a given
\varepsilon>0
. For a small
\varepsilon
, the volumes scale as:

1.

\text{Vol}(V_{\text{fair}}(\varepsilon))\propto\varepsilon^{k}

2.

\text{Vol}(V_{\text{mask}}(\varepsilon))\propto\varepsilon^{1}

Proof (Sketch).

The
\varepsilon
-fair problem constrains the policy vector
\vec{\alpha}
to lie within
k
independent slabs, each of width proportional to
\varepsilon
. The volume of their intersection thus scales with
\varepsilon^{k}
. The
\varepsilon
-masking problem imposes only a single slab constraint, so its volume scales linearly with
\varepsilon
.
∎

The ratio of the feasible volumes,
\text{Vol}(V_{\text{mask}})/\text{Vol}(V_{\text{fair}})\propto 1/\varepsilon^{k-1}
, is therefore immense. This disparity in “freedom to operate” provides a clear geometric reason for the masking policy’s ability to find significantly better solutions.

Appendix D
Hypothesis Tests

D.1
Testing for Zero ATE

In the setting where
P
is binary, we employ a Z-test to test the null hypothesis
H_{\text{ATE}}:\text{ATE}=0
.
The test statistic is

\widehat{\text{ATE}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{p}_{x_{i}}\,\hat{\tau}_{x_{i}}

where

\hat{p}_{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}=x\}},\qquad x\in\{0,1\},

and

\hat{\tau}_{x}=\frac{\sum_{i:X_{i}=x,\;P_{i}=1}D_{i}}{\sum_{i:X_{i}=x,\;P_{i}=1}1}-\frac{\sum_{i:X_{i}=x,\;P_{i}=0}D_{i}}{\sum_{i:X_{i}=x,\;P_{i}=0}1}.

The Z-statistic is then
Z=\widehat{\text{ATE}}\big/\widehat{\text{SE}}(\widehat{\text{ATE}})
, which is asymptotically standard normal under the null hypothesis
H_{\text{ATE}}
. We use a significance level of
5\%
.

D.2
Testing for Conditional Independence

A more nuanced way to detect unfairness is to directly test the null hypothesis of conditional independence; i.e.,
H_{\text{CATE}}:D\perp P\ |\ X
.
To evaluate this, first for each stratum,
x\in\{1,...,k-1\}
, we apply Fisher’s exact test
[
22
]
for independence to obtain a corresponding p-value.

To test the joint null hypothesis that conditional independence holds across all strata, we combine the per-stratum p-values using Fisher’s method,

S=-2\sum_{x\in\nu}\log p_{x},

where the
\nu
denotes the set of strata with observations in the
P=1
and
P=0
groups. Under
H_{\text{CATE}}
, the combined statistics satisfy
S\sim\chi^{2}_{2|\nu|}
. We reject the null hypothesis when the combined p-value falls below the
5\%
significance level.
