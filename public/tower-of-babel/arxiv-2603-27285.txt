Surfactant reorientation under shear: dynamic surface tension and droplet deformation Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Alexandra J. Hardy, Abdallah Daddi-Moussa-Ider, Elsen Tjhung

Abstract
We study the deformation of a surfactant-covered droplet under shear flow using a phase-field model that explicitly accounts for both the surfactant concentration and its polarization, representing the average molecular orientation. We first consider a flat interface and show that an imposed tangential shear causes the surfactant polarization to tilt away from the interface normal. This reorientation reduces the ability of surfactants to lower the interfacial free energy, leading to an increase in the effective surface tension and demonstrating that surface tension can be dynamically modified by shear. We then examine droplet deformation under shear in both weakly and strongly confined geometries. In the weak-confinement regime, numerical results recover the linear Taylor scaling at small capillary numbers, while at larger capillary numbers they are accurately described by a modified Maffettone–Minale phenomenological model. The presence of surfactants enhances deformation through a reduction in the effective surface tension. In the strong-confinement regime, wall effects further increase droplet deformation, with results qualitatively captured by including the Shapira–Haber correction. Overall, our findings show that surfactant reorientation under flow provides a microscopic mechanism for shear-dependent surface tension and has significant implications for droplet deformation in confined multiphase flows.



I
Introduction

Surfactants are well known for their ability to lower the surface tension between two immiscible fluids
Mulqueen and Blankschtein 2002
;
Möbius, Miller, and Fainerman 2001
.
Because of this property, they are widely used across many industrial applications, including detergents, emulsification, food processing, pharmaceuticals, and enhanced oil recovery
Tadros 2006
;
Schramm and Schramm 2000
;
Shaban, Kang, and Kim 2020
;
Singh and Cameotra 2004
;
Nitschke and Silva 2018
.
In particular, the addition of surfactants gives rise to a wide range of interfacial phenomena
Scamehorn 1986
;
Rosen and Kunjappu 2012
;
Dave and Joshi 2017
, including enhanced droplet deformation under an imposed shear flow.

Surfactants consist of a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail and they preferentially align perpendicular to the interface.
However under shear flow, this orientation can be modified, leading to a coupling between flow and interfacial properties.
Using a phase-field model that explicitly incorporates both surfactant concentration and orientation
Hardy, Daddi-Moussa-Ider, and Tjhung 2024
;
Hardy et al. 2026
,
we demonstrate that an imposed tangential shear induces a tilt of the surfactant polarization (or average orientation) away from the interface normal.
This reorientation reduces the ability of surfactants to lower the interfacial free energy, resulting in an increase in the effective surface tension.
As a consequence, the surface tension becomes a dynamic and local quantity that depends not only on surfactant concentration but also on the local shear rate.

Shear-dependent surface tension can directly influence droplet deformation. In pure systems, i.e., without surfactants, several theoretical models have been developed to describe droplet behavior under shear.
This is quantified by the capillary number, which measures the ratio of viscous to interfacial forces. For sufficiently small capillary numbers, Taylor’s classical theory predicts a linear scaling between droplet deformation and the capillary number
Taylor 1934
. For larger deformations, phenomenological models such as the Maffettone–Minale (MM) model have been developed
Maffettone and Minale 1998
;
Minale 2010
. Extensions incorporating wall confinement effects have been proposed by Shapira
Shapira and Haber 1990
and Vananroye
Vananroye, Van Puyvelde, and Moldenaers 2006
. Numerical studies have confirmed these behaviors and revealed additional features, including overshoot, oscillatory relaxation, and eventual breakup at high capillary numbers
Sibillo et al. 2006
;
Feigl et al. 2003
;
Cristini et al. 2003
;
Amani et al. 2019
;
Guido 2011
;
Ioannou, Liu, and Zhang 2016
.

In the presence of surfactants, the droplet dynamics become more complex due to surfactant redistribution and the resulting Marangoni stresses
Janssen, Boon, and Agterof 1997
;
Phillips, Graves, and Flumerfelt 1980
.
Previous studies generally report enhanced deformation due to reduced surface tension
Stone and Leal 1990
;
Kruijt-Stegeman, Van de Vosse, and Meijer 2004
;
Feigl et al. 2007
, with additional effects arising from non-uniform surfactant distributions
Milliken and Leal 1994
;
Eggleton and Stebe 1998
.
However, most existing models treat surfactants as scalar concentration fields and neglect their molecular orientation.
As a result, they do not capture the coupling between flow, surfactant orientation, and interfacial mechanics.

To address this limitation, we employ a phase-field model that explicitly incorporates surfactant polarization (average orientation) and its coupling to the interface
Hardy, Daddi-Moussa-Ider, and Tjhung 2024
;
Hardy et al. 2026
. Within this framework, we first show that shear flow dynamically modifies the effective surface tension through surfactant reorientation. We then investigate droplet deformation under shear, both in the absence and presence of surfactants, and in regimes of weak and strong confinement. In the weak-confinement regime, we recover the classical Taylor scaling at small capillary numbers and show that deformation at larger capillary numbers is accurately captured by a modified Maffettone–Minale theory. Surfactants enhance deformation by reducing the effective surface tension, which can be incorporated via an effective capillary number accounting for shear-dependent interfacial properties. In the strong-confinement regime, wall effects further increase deformation, and the combined influence of confinement and surfactants is described by including the Shapira–Haber correction.

Overall, this work establishes a direct link between surfactant orientation, shear-dependent surface tension, and droplet deformation in both unbounded and confined geometries, providing a unified framework for understanding surfactant-covered multiphase flows under shear.

II
Phase Field Model

We consider a two-phase fluid system (e.g., oil and water) containing surfactant molecules.
Surfactants have a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail, causing them to preferentially adsorb at the interface between two fluid phases, typically aligning perpendicular to the interface.
Within a continuum phase-field description, the two fluid phases are described by a single scalar order parameter
\phi(\mathbf{r},t)
, which takes values approximately
+1
in the oil phase and
-1
in the water phase.
The interface between the two fluid phases is diffuse, so that
\phi
varies smoothly between these two values across a finite interfacial region of thickness
\xi
.
The surfactant molecules are represented by two additional fields: the local surfactant concentration
c(\mathbf{r},t)
and the average molecular orientation

\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{r},t)
, referred to as the polarization field.

The thermodynamic behaviour of the system is described by a free-energy functional
\mathcal{F}[\phi,c,\mathbf{p}]
, which captures the coupling between the two fluid phases, the surfactant concentration, and the surfactant orientation
Hardy et al. 2026
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\phi,c,\mathbf{p}]

\displaystyle=\int\bigg\{\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\phi^{2})^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\,|\nabla\phi|^{2}+k_{\mathrm{B}}Tc\ln(\xi^{d}c)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{d}{2}\,k_{\mathrm{B}}Tc|\mathbf{p}|^{2}+\chi\ell c\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla\phi\bigg\}\,d^{d}r\,.

(1)

The first term corresponds to the bulk free-energy density, which has minima at
\phi=\pm 1
, representing the oil and water phases, with
\beta>0
setting the energy scale.
The second term represents the interfacial energy associated with gradients of
\phi
, where
\kappa>0
and
\beta
together determine the surface tension and the thickness of the diffuse interface.
The third term, proportional to the thermal energy scale
k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, represents the entropic contribution associated with the surfactant concentration
c
, arising from the translational Brownian motion of the molecules.
The fourth term, also proportional to
k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, corresponds to the orientational entropy of the surfactants arising from rotational Brownian motion, which favours an isotropic state with
\mathbf{p}\simeq\boldsymbol{0}
.
The final term describes the coupling between the surfactant orientation and the fluid interface:
\chi>0
sets the strength of the interaction between surfactant molecules and the interface, while
\ell>0
is the characteristic molecular length scale of the surfactants.
Finally,
d
denotes the spatial dimension of the system. In this work, we focus primarily on
d=2
, since the results in
d=3
are qualitatively similar.

In the absence of surfactants (
c=0
), the system relaxes toward an equilibrium state that minimizes the free energy stated by Eq. (
1
).
For example, one equilibrium configuration is a flat interface located at
y=0
, for which the order parameter is given by
\phi(\mathbf{r})=\tanh(y/\xi)
, where
\xi=\sqrt{2\kappa/\beta}
defines the interfacial thickness.
Physically,
\xi
is on the order of molecular length scales, although in numerical simulations it is typically chosen several orders of magnitude larger to ensure stability. From the equilibrium
\phi(\mathbf{r})
, the surface tension in the absence of surfactants is given by
\sigma_{0}=\sqrt{8\kappa\beta/9}
. In the presence of surfactants, however, the effective surface tension is modified and can vary locally and dynamically depending on the shear rate (see Section
IV
).

The full system dynamics are then described by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\phi\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=M\nabla^{2}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}\,,

(2a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(c\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left(\frac{c}{\Gamma_{t}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}\right)\,,

(2b)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.5359pt\frac{\partial\mathbf{p}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla})\mathbf{p}

\displaystyle=-\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}\cdot\mathbf{p}+\frac{Bd}{d+2}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{D}}}\cdot\mathbf{p}-\frac{d-1}{d\Gamma_{r}c}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\,,

(2c)

where
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r},t)
is the fluid velocity satisfying the incompressible Stokes equation
Happel and Brenner 2012
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{u}\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}P+\eta\nabla^{2}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{f}\,.

(3b)

In Eqs. (
2
),
M>0
sets the mobility of the binary fluid, while
\Gamma_{t}>0
and
\Gamma_{r}>0
denote the translational and rotational friction coefficients of the surfactant molecules, respectively.
For simplicity, we assume the translational friction
\Gamma_{t}
to be isotropic, although in general it may be a tensor.
The parameter
B\in[0,1]
in Eq. (
2
) is the Bretherton constant, which characterizes the rotation of non-spherical objects in a fluid flow.
If surfactants are approximated as ellipsoids with aspect ratio
\Delta
, the Bretherton constant is given by
B=(\Delta^{2}-1)/(\Delta^{2}+1)
.
Thus,
B=0
corresponds to spherical particles, while
B=1
corresponds to infinitely thin needle-like particles.
We also define the strain rate tensor
\underline{\underline{\mathbf{D}}}
and the vorticity tensor
\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}}
as:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha})\,,

(4a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}-\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha})\,.

(4b)

Finally
\eta
in Eq. (
3
) is the fluid viscosity and
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{g}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}^{\prime}
is the hydrodynamic force density which depends on
\phi
,
c
, and
\mathbf{p}
.
Here,

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\phi\partial_{\alpha}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\phi}-c\partial_{\alpha}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta c}-p_{\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta p_{\beta}}\,,

(5a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{Bd}{2(d+2)}\left(p_{\alpha}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta p_{\beta}}+p_{\beta}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta p_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{\alpha}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta p_{\beta}}-p_{\beta}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta p_{\alpha}}\right)\,.

(5b)

Equations (
2
) and (
3
) are solved numerically in a two-dimensional domain
(x,y)\in[0,L_{x}]\times[0,L_{y}]
. Periodic boundary conditions are applied in the
x
-direction, while no-flux and no-slip conditions are enforced at the walls
y=0
and
y=L_{y}
. The bottom and top walls move with velocities
-\dot{\gamma}L_{y}\hat{\mathbf{x}}/2
and
+\dot{\gamma}L_{y}\hat{\mathbf{x}}/2
, respectively, where
\dot{\gamma}
is the imposed shear rate (see Figs.
1
and
4
).

It is worth noting that, aside from the free energy structure in Eq. (
1
), the dynamics of
\phi
,
c
,
\mathbf{p}
, and
\mathbf{u}
in Eqs. (
2
), (
3
), and (
5
) are largely identical to those of polar liquid crystal emulsions
Bonelli et al. 2019
;
Negro et al. 2019
;
Cates and Tjhung 2018
.

III
Numerical Scheme

For simplicity, we take the interfacial thickness
\xi=\sqrt{2\kappa/\beta}
as the unit of length, the surfactant rotational relaxation time
\Gamma_{r}^{-1}
as the unit of time, and
k_{\mathrm{B}}T
as the unit of energy. In these units,
\kappa=\beta/2
,
\Gamma_{r}=1
, and
k_{\mathrm{B}}T=1
. We also define a small dimensionless parameter
\varepsilon=\chi\ell/(\xi k_{\mathrm{B}}T)
to represent the weak coupling between the fluid and surfactant. In these dimensionless units, the free energy (
1
) becomes
Hardy et al. 2026
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\phi,c,\mathbf{p}]

\displaystyle=\int\bigg\{\frac{\beta}{4}(1-\phi^{2})^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}\,|\nabla\phi|^{2}+c\ln(c)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{d}{2}\,c|\mathbf{p}|^{2}+\varepsilon c\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla\phi\bigg\}\,d^{d}r\,.

(6)

The dynamics remain governed by Eqs. (
2
) with
\Gamma_{r}=1
.

Numerically, the spatial coordinates
\mathbf{r}=(x,y)
are discretized as
x=i\Delta x
and
y=j\Delta x
, where
i=0,1,2,\dots,N_{x}-1
and
j=0,1,2,\dots,N_{y}-1
.
Here,
\Delta x
and
\Delta y
denote the spatial grid spacings along the
x
- and
y
-directions, respectively.
The physical lengths of the domain are then
L_{x}=N_{x}\Delta x
and
L_{y}=N_{y}\Delta y
.
On the other hand, time is discretized into
t=0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,\dots
, where
\Delta t
is the time step.
In all simulations we fix
\Delta x=\Delta y=0.5
and
\Delta t=0.001
.
The fields
\phi
,
c
, and
\mathbf{p}
are evolved using a finite-difference scheme with forward Euler time stepping.
The velocity field
\mathbf{u}
is computed using a spectral method.
All numerical computations are implemented in Python using the NumPy
Harris et al. 2020
and SciPy
Virtanen et al. 2020
libraries.

III.1
Finite Difference Method

Equations (
2
) are solved using a finite-difference scheme with forward Euler time stepping. Spatial derivatives are approximated using a finite-difference stencil
Pooley and Furtado 2008
. For a generic field value
q^{i,j}
, where
q
may represent
\phi
,
c
,
p_{x}
, etc., the first-order spatial derivatives are computed as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{i,j}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{12\Delta x}\left(q^{i+1,j+1}-q^{i-1,j+1}+4q^{i+1,j}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-4q^{i-1,j}+q^{i+1,j-1}-q^{i-1,j-1}\right),

(7a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial q^{i,j}}{\partial y}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{12\Delta y}\left(q^{i+1,j+1}-q^{i+1,j-1}+4q^{i,j+1}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-4q^{i,j-1}+q^{i-1,j+1}-q^{i-1,j-1}\right).

(7b)

It should be noted that
\Delta x=\Delta y
in practice.
The Laplacian is computed as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}q^{i,j}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{6\Delta x\Delta y}\Big(q^{i-1,j+1}+4q^{i,j+1}+q^{i+1,j+1}

\displaystyle\quad+4q^{i-1,j}-20q^{i,j}+4q^{i+1,j}

\displaystyle\quad+q^{i-1,j-1}+4q^{i,j-1}+q^{i+1,j-1}\Big)\,.

(8)

To apply no-flux boundary conditions at
y=0
and
y=L_{y}
, we introduce a set of ghost points at
j=-1
and
j=N_{y}
.
The boundary conditions for
q=\phi
or
c
at these ghost points are then given by:

q^{i,j=-1}=q^{i,j=0}\quad\text{and}\quad q^{i,j=N_{y}}=q^{i,j=N_{y}-1}\,,

(9)

for all
i
.
Since Eqs. (
2a
) and (
2b
) can be written in the form of continuity equations
\partial_{t}\phi+\boldsymbol{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}}_{\phi}=0
and
\partial_{t}c+\boldsymbol{\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}}_{c}=0
,
we also impose the following boundary conditions on the currents.
Specifically, for a generic current component
q=J_{\phi,x}
,
J_{\phi,y}
,
J_{c,x}
or
J_{c,y}
we apply:

q^{i,j=-1}=-q^{i,j=0}\quad\text{and}\quad q^{i,j=N_{y}}=-q^{i,j=N_{y}-1}\,,

(10)

for all
i
.
The no-flux boundary conditions (
9
-
10
) ensure the conservation of
\phi
and
c
.

III.2
Spectral Method

For the fluid velocity
\mathbf{u}
, we employ a spectral method. Specifically, a Fourier transform is applied in the
x
-direction, while a sine transform is used in the
y
-direction to satisfy the required no-slip boundary conditions:

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{k})e^{ik_{x}x}\sin(k_{y}y)\,,

(11)

where the summation is taken over the discrete set of wavevectors
\mathbf{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
.
The allowed wavenumbers are
k_{x}=2\pi n/L_{x}
with integer
n
, and
k_{y}=m\pi/L_{y}
with integer
m
.
With this choice of basis functions, the no-slip boundary conditions are automatically satisfied, yielding
\mathbf{u}(y=0)=\mathbf{u}(y=L_{y})=\mathbf{0}
.
Numerically, we apply a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) in the
x
-direction and a Discrete Sine Transform (DST) in the
y
-direction consecutively to obtain
\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{k})
from
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
.

After transformation, the Stokes equation (
3
) then becomes:

\hat{u}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{\eta k^{2}}\left(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}}{k^{2}}\right)\hat{f}_{\beta}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(12)

for
\mathbf{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.
Here
\hat{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{k})
denotes the mixed Fourier–sine transform of the hydrodynamic force
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{r})
, which depends on
\phi
,
c
, and
\mathbf{p}
[cf. Eq. (
5
)].
Thus, we use (
12
) to update
\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{k})
.
The inverse transforms are then used to recover
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})
from
\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{k})
.

To impose a shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
, we superimpose the simple shear solution onto the velocity field obtained above:

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})\rightarrow\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r})+\dot{\gamma}\left(y-\frac{L_{y}}{2}\right)\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,.

(13)

This is permissible because the simple shear flow
u_{x}\propto y
is itself a solution of the Stokes equation and can therefore be added linearly to the computed velocity field. The resulting velocity field satisfies the no-slip boundary conditions at the walls, with wall velocities given by
\pm\dot{\gamma}L_{y}\hat{\mathbf{x}}/2
as required.

We note that the present approach differs from the more established lattice Boltzmann method
Carenza et al. 2019
.
In lattice Boltzmann simulations, shear flow is typically imposed through moving-wall boundary conditions implemented via bounce-back schemes acting on the distribution functions, which are only first-order accurate in spatial discretisation
Krüger et al. 2017
.
By contrast, in the present method the wall shear velocities are imposed exactly via the sine transformation in Eq. (
11
).

IV
Dynamic Surface Tension

In this section, we examine how the effective surface tension of a surfactant-covered interface is modified in the presence of an imposed tangential shear flow.
It is well known that, under equilibrium conditions, increasing the surfactant concentration reduces the effective surface tension.
However, because surfactant molecules are anisotropic rather than spherical, they can tilt in response to an imposed shear flow; see Fig.
1
.
This tilting modifies their orientation relative to the interface and can counteract the surface-tension-reducing effect observed in the equilibrium case, where the molecules tend to align perpendicular to the interface.

To analyse the dependence of the effective surface tension
\sigma
on both the average surfactant concentration
c_{0}
and the shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
, we consider a flat interface located at
y=L_{y}/2
; see Fig.
1
, with the oil phase (
\phi>0
) occupying the region
y<L_{y}/2
and the water phase (
\phi<0
) occupying
y>L_{y}/2
.
In the absence of fluid flow (
\mathbf{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
), the surfactant concentration
c(\mathbf{r})
and the polarization
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{r})
will relax toward an equilibrium state in which
c(y)
exhibits a peak at
y=L_{y}/2
, indicating adsorption at the interface. At the same time,
\mathbf{p}(y)\propto\hat{\mathbf{y}}
near the interface, showing that the surfactants tend to align perpendicular to the surface.

In the presence of an imposed tangential shear flow
\mathbf{u}=\dot{\gamma}(y-L_{y}/2)\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, the polarization
\mathbf{p}
is no longer perpendicular to the interface but instead becomes tilted by an angle
\theta
relative to the interface normal (see Fig.
1
).
To quantify this tilting angle, we substitute the simple shear flow
\mathbf{u}=\dot{\gamma}(y-L_{y}/2)\hat{\mathbf{x}}
into Eqs. (
2
).
By symmetry and in steady state, the out-of-plane component of the polarization vanishes,
p_{z}=0
.
The remaining fields
\phi
,
c
,
p_{x}
and
p_{y}
only depend on the coordinate

.
In steady state, Eqs. (

) then becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}c}{\partial y^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[c\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{d}{2}\left(p_{x}^{2}+p_{y}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon p_{y}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\right)\right],

As in the equilibrium case, the addition of surfactant lowers the effective surface tension, whereas an imposed tangential shear counteracts this reduction. Parameters used:
\varepsilon=1
and
M=\Gamma_{t}=\beta=B=\eta=1
.

Finally, using the steady–state solution (
15
), the effective surface tension
\sigma(c_{0},\dot{\gamma})
can then be obtained from the excess free energy.
First, we define the grand potential to be:

\Phi[\phi,c,\mathbf{p}]=\mathcal{F}[\phi,c,\mathbf{p}]-\int\mu_{c}\,c(\mathbf{r})\,d^{d}r=\int w(\mathbf{r})\,d^{d}r\,,

(17)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the free energy defined in Eq. (
6
).
Mathematically,
\Phi
is the Legendre transform of
\mathcal{F}
.
The quantity
\mu_{c}
is the chemical potential of the surfactant reservoir, which is fixed as
\mu_{c}=\ln(c_{0})+1
.
This choice ensures that the equilibrium condition
\delta\Phi/\delta c=0
is satisfied.
Additionally, we define
w(\mathbf{r})
to be the grand potential density.
The effective surface tension is then defined to be the excess grand potential across the interface:

\sigma=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[w(\tilde{y})-w(\tilde{y}\rightarrow\infty)]\,d\tilde{y}\,,

(18)

where
\tilde{y}=y-L_{y}/2
.
Note that the interface, assumed to be planar, is located at
y=L_{y}/2
.
Eq. (
18
) has also been shown to be equivalent to Gibbs adsorption isotherm
Hardy et al. 2026
.

Substituting the perturbative steady state solution (
15
) into (
18
),
we then obtain the effective surface tension as a function of average surfactant concentration
c_{0}
and shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
:

\sigma(c_{0},\dot{\gamma})=\sigma_{0}-\frac{2c_{0}\lambda^{2}\left[\lambda^{2}-A_{+}(A_{+}+2A_{-})\right]}{3d(\lambda^{2}-A_{+}A_{-})^{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,,

(19)

where
\sigma_{0}=2\beta/3
is the bare surface tension in the absence of surfactant (
c_{0}=0
).
Note that
\lambda\propto\Gamma_{r}^{-1}
and
A_{\pm}\propto\dot{\gamma}
.
Thus, In the case weak tangential shear flow,
\dot{\gamma}\ll\Gamma_{r}^{-1}
, the effective surface tension (
19
) reduces to

\sigma(c_{0},\dot{\gamma}\ll\Gamma_{r}^{-1})=\sigma_{0}-\frac{2c_{0}}{3d}\,\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

(20)

In both cases, the surface tension decreases linearly with increasing surfactant concentration
c_{0}
.
Note that equations (
19
) and (
20
) are derived for a general
d
-dimensional system, where we have an interface on the
x
-
z
plane and shear flow on the
x
-
y
plane.
However, in numerical simulations below, we restrict ourselves to
d=2
dimensional case.

In Fig.
3
we show the effective surface tension
\sigma
as a function of the average surfactant concentration
c_{0}
for different shear rates
\dot{\gamma}
.
In the absence of shear, the addition of surfactant reduces the surface tension due to adsorption at the interface.
However, as the shear rate increases, this reduction becomes weaker because the imposed shear tilts the surfactant orientation away from the interface normal, thereby reducing its ability to lower the interfacial energy.
Thus, shear can dynamically modify the effective surface tension.

When the droplet is placed midway between the two oppositely moving walls that generate the shear flow, it begins to stretch and deform in the direction of the flow, as shown in Fig.

D=\frac{L_{\parallel}-L_{\perp}}{L_{\parallel}+L_{\perp}}\,,

(21)

where
L_{\parallel}
and
L_{\perp}

Numerically, the deformation parameter

is computed from the inertia tensor:

\underline{\underline{\mathbf{I}}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\sum_{i}{\Delta y_{i}^{2}}&-\sum_{i}{\Delta x_{i}\Delta y_{i}}\\

\end{matrix}\right),

where
\Delta x_{i}=x_{i}-x_{\text{mean}}
and
\Delta y_{i}=y_{i}-y_{\text{mean}}
, and

and

are the spatial coordinates of pixels that are occupied by the droplet phase.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we have investigated the interplay between surfactant orientation, interfacial mechanics, and droplet deformation under shear flow.
Using a phase-field model that explicitly incorporates both surfactant concentration and polarization/average orientation, we have shown that the anisotropic nature of surfactant molecules leads to a fundamentally new effect: a shear-dependent surface tension.

For a flat interface, we demonstrated that an imposed tangential shear induces a tilt of the surfactant polarization relative to the interface normal.
Because the surfactant orientation couples directly to the interfacial gradient in the free energy, this reorientation reduces the ability of surfactants to lower the interfacial energy.
As a result, the effective surface tension increases with shear rate and becomes a dynamic quantity depending on both surfactant concentration and flow.
Our perturbative analysis yields an explicit expression for this shear-dependent surface tension, which shows good agreement with numerical simulations.

We then investigate the effects of surfactants for droplet deformation under shear beyond the linear Taylor regime.
In the weak-confinement regime, droplet deformation is accurately described by a modified MM theory.
In the presence of surfactants, the deformation is enhanced due to the reduction in effective surface tension.
It is important to note, however, that although shear flow can in principle strongly modify the effective surface tension, this effect becomes significant only at very large shear rates,
\dot{\gamma}\gg\Gamma_{r}^{-1}
.
In this regime, the droplet is typically unstable and undergoes breakup into smaller droplets.
A detailed investigation of droplet breakup therefore lies beyond the scope of the present work and will be addressed in future studies.

In the strong-confinement regime, where wall effects become significant, droplet deformation is further enhanced.
We find that the combined effects of confinement and surfactants can be captured qualitatively by incorporating the Shapira–Haber correction into the theoretical framework.
Overall, our numerical results show qualitative agreement with these theoretical predictions.

In related systems, vesicles subjected to shear flow in confined geometries exhibit dynamical modes such as tank-treading and tumbling
Kaoui, Harting, and Misbah 2011
;
Kaoui, Krüger, and Harting 2012
.
The key distinction from our system is that vesicles are enclosed by an elastic membrane, whereas here the interface is fluid.
Consequently, the velocity field remains continuous across the interface; moreover, under the assumption of equal viscosities inside and outside the droplet, such tank-treading and tumbling dynamics are not observed.

The present work highlights the importance of surfactant orientation in determining interfacial properties under flow.
By identifying a microscopic mechanism for shear-dependent surface tension and demonstrating its impact on droplet deformation in both unbounded and confined geometries, this study provides a more complete description of surfactant-covered multiphase flows.
These findings may have implications for the control of emulsions and microfluidic systems, where both confinement and flow-induced effects play a crucial role.
Finally, the modelling framework developed here can be readily extended to study active emulsions
Bonelli et al. 2019
.
