Vector-field guided constraint-following control for path following of uncertain mechanical systems
Hui Yin, Xiang Li, Yifan Liu, Weijia Yao

Abstract
This note proposes a general control approach, called vector-field guided constraint-following control, to solve the dynamics control problem of geometric path-following for a class of uncertain mechanical systems. More specifically, it operates at the dynamics level and can handle both fully-actuated and underactuated mechanical systems, heterogeneous (possibly fast) time-varying uncertainties with unknown bounds, and geometric desired paths that may be self-intersecting. Simulations are conducted to demonstrate the effectiveness of the approach.

I
Introduction

Accurately following a geometric path is a fundamental task for robots, which requires a robot to converge to and move along the desired path with sufficient accuracy. This task does not require any temporal information on the desired path, thereby can alleviate performance
limitations inherent to another important robot task—trajectory tracking
[
1
]
.
Research on path-following algorithms has made remarkable progress in the past two decades
[
15
]
. Among them, those using a guiding vector field (GVF) have shown the ability to handle complex low- and high-dimensional geometric desired paths
[
7
,
18
,
9
]
, and to achieve higher following accuracy using the least control effort compared to some other well-known algorithms
[
15
]
.

However, currently the GVF builds its general theory based on the
kinematic
model rather than the
dynamic
model of a robot.
The kinematic model only describes the relationship between the geometric states (e.g., displacement, velocity) of a robot and its internal actuators, without involving any forces/torques. Therefore, a GVF-based control generally provides velocity/direction (rather than force/torque) guidance signal inputs to the robot, with the assumption that the robot-specific inner-loop dynamics control can accurately realize these inputs to support its effectiveness
[
11
,
15
,
13
]
. In other words, the current general theoretical results of GVF do not guarantee the convergence of robot dynamics to the desired path, which however is crucial for ensuring stable and accurate path-following
. The integration of the dynamic model of a robot into the GVF-based path-following control design in a general theoretical framework remains challenging, especially when the dynamic model incorporates heterogeneous uncertainties, which is the common case in robot applications.

The constraint-following control (CFC) approach
[
4
,
3
]
, which uses the servo constraint
[
2
]
—mathematically a class of first-order ordinary differential equations (ODEs), to describe various control tasks (e.g., stabilization, trajectory-following), is specifically developed for the dynamics control of mechanical systems. More concretely, unlike the GVF, CFC builds its general theory by the
dynamic
model rather than the
kinematic
model of a mechanical system. It provides force/torque inputs to the system, guaranteeing the convergence of system dynamics to the servo constraint. Furthermore, it avoids linearizations, nonlinearity cancellations, and auxiliary variables (e.g., Lagrange multiplier) or pseudo variables (e.g., generalized speed). Without initial condition deviations and uncertainties, it is optimal for costs defined by either the integral or the instantaneous weighted norm of the control
[
16
,
17
]
.
However, regarding following tasks of robots, currently most CFC studies focus on
trajectory-tracking
rather than path-following.
It is unclear yet how to generalize the CFC approach to solve geometric path-following problems. This note will, for the first time, reveal that the challenge arises from the servo constraint design to encode geometric desired paths.

Recognizing the importance of dynamics control in path-following, and noting the inherent connection between GVF and CFC, this note initiates a general approach that solves the dynamics control problem of geometric path-following for uncertain mechanical systems. The main contributions are twofold as follows.

•

A vector-field guided constraint-following control (VFCFC) framework is proposed, which operates at the dynamics level, and can deal with underactuated systems and self-intersecting desired paths.
Specifically, we design a vector-field guided constraint (VFC) to encode desired paths, by discovering the compatibility between the vector-field-guided ODE in GVF and the constraint ODE in CFC. The relationship between VFC-following error and path-following error is rigorously analyzed, which theoretically supports VFC as a bridge connecting GVF and CFC that enables GVF to guide the system dynamics.

•

The adaptive robust extension of the VFCFC framework for path following of mechanical systems is introduced, which accommodates a broad class of time-varying uncertainties as detailed in Remark
1
.
Specifically, we construct an adaptive law to estimate the unknown uncertainty bound, and then use the estimated bound for uncertainty suppression (hence robust control).

The contributions output two classes of VFCFCs, namely the nominal VFCFC guaranteeing vanishing path-following error for the nomnial system, and the adaptive robust VFCFC guaranteeing ultimately bounded path-following error for the uncertain system.

The remainder of this note is organized as follows. Section
II
reviews necessary preliminaries and formulates the problem. Section
III
presents the main theoretical results. Section
IV
provides the simulation results. Section
V
concludes the note.

II-A

Uncertain mechanical systems

The dynamics of a controlled uncertain mechanical system can be generally described as

\begin{split}&M(q(t),\sigma(t),t)\ddot{q}(t)+C(q(t),\dot{q}(t),\sigma(t),t)\dot{q}(t)\\
&+g(q(t),\sigma(t),t)=B(q(t),\sigma(t),t)\tau(t).\end{split}

(1)

Here
q

\dot{q}
,
\ddot{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the configuration, velocity, and acceleration vectors, respectively;
t\geq t_{0}\geq 0

t_{0}
the initial time;
\sigma(t)\in\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the time-varying uncertainty with
\Sigma

\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
(
m\leq n
) is the control
force/torque. The inertia matrix

is assumed to be positive definite and thus invertible, which holds for the vast majority of practical applications
[
3
]
;
C(q,\dot{q},\sigma,t)\dot{q}
,
g(q,\sigma,t)
are the Coriolis/centrifugal and gravitational forces respectively,
B(q,\sigma,t)
is the input matrix. The functions
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
C:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
. The system (

.

Remark 1
.

We do not assume the time derivative of uncertainty
\sigma
to be bounded, hence the uncertainty can be fast time-varying. Furthermore, we do not assume the uncertainty
\sigma
to be fully matched or to only include sufficiently small mismatched uncertainty
[
25
]
. The uncertainty

is also not restricted to a specific part of the system, and may originate from parameters, unmodeled dynamics, or external disturbances.
For instance, parametric uncertainties can be directly described by

∎

II-B

Constraint-following control

Roughly speaking, the constraint-following control (CFC) is a solution to the so-called servo constraint problem of designing control forces that drive the state of a dynamical system to move in or toward a prespecified (servo) constraint manifold, i.e., constraint-following for short. To be precise, we first give a general definition of constraint following for dynamical systems. Then we particularize this definition for mechanical system (
1
),
and subsequently gives the definition of CFC.

Definition 1

(Constraint-following for dynamical systems)
.

Consider a dynamical system
\dot{x}(t)=F(x(t),t)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, with
F
piecewise-continuous in
t
and Lipschitz continuous in
x
. Given the initial condition
x(t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, if there exists a constraint function
\beta:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq t_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{z}
, such that
\|\beta(x(t),t)\|=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq t_{0}}
, then the system trajectory
x(t)
is said to
consistently satisfy
the constraint
\beta(x,t)=0
. If
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\beta(x(t),t)\|=0
, or, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
T\in(0,\infty)
such that
\|\beta(x(t),t)\|\leq\epsilon

for all
t\geq T
, then
x(t)
is said to
asymptotically follow
the constraint
\beta(x,t)=0
. If
\|\beta(x(t),t)\|
is uniformly ultimately bounded
[
12
, Definition 4.6]
, then
x(t)
is said to
approximately follow
the constraint
\beta(x,t)=0
.

By Definition
1
, if one replaces
x(t)
with
(q(t),\dot{q}(t))
of the mechanical system (
1
), and particularizes the constraint function
\beta
to a specific form, then the definition of constraint-following for mechanical systems in this study is given below.

Definition 2

(Constraint-following for mechanical systems)
.

Given the initial condition
(q(t_{0}),\dot{q}(t_{0}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and the control input
\tau:\mathbb{R}_{\geq t_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
for the mechanical system (
1

(q(t),\dot{q}(t))
of (

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{li}(q,t)\dot{q}_{i}-c_{l}(q,t)=0,\qquad l=1,\dots,m

(2)

for all

, where
\dot{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the

-th entry of

, and

are differentiable functions of

(q(t),\dot{q}(t))

the constraint (
2
). Let
\beta_{l}(q,\dot{q},t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{li}(q,t)\dot{q}_{i}-c_{l}(q,t)\,(l=1,\cdots,m)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),t))\|=0

(q(t),\dot{q}(t))
is said to

the constraint (
2

\|\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),t))\|
is uniformly ultimately bounded, then
(q(t),\dot{q}(t))
is said to
approximately follow
the constraint (
2
).

By Definition
2
, the definition of CFC for mechanical systems is given below.

Definition 3

(CFC for mechanical systems)
.

Given the initial condition
(q(t_{0}),\dot{q}(t_{0}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, if the trajectory
(q(t),\dot{q}(t))
of the mechanical system (
1
) under a control input
\tau:\mathbb{R}_{\geq t_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

or

2
), then

1
).

Rearranging (
2

A(q,t)\dot{q}=c(q,t),

(3)

where
A=[a_{li}]_{m\times n}
and

. Taking the time derivative of (

), we can obtain its second-order form as:

A(q,t)\ddot{q}=b(q,\dot{q},t),

where

Assumption 1
.

For each
(q,\dot{q},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}

Remark 2
.

Assumption

II-C

Guiding vector field

This subsection first introduces the simple 2-D guiding vector fields (GVFs) to show the basic concept. Then the more abstract general case with higher dimensions is presented.

II-C
1
GVF in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for non-self-intersecting desired path

Suppose the desired path
\mathcal{P}
is described by

\mathcal{P}=\{(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\psi(q_{1},q_{2})=0\},

where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is twice continuously differentiable. If
\mathcal{P}
is non-self-intersecting, then a valid GVF
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
for
\mathcal{P}
can be designed as
[
11
,
18
]
:

\chi(q_{1},q_{2})=E\nabla\psi(q_{1},q_{2})-k\psi(q_{1},q_{2})\nabla\psi(q_{1},q_{2}),

(6)

where
E=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
,
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a constant,
\nabla\psi(q_{1},q_{2})
is the gradient of
\psi
. On the RHS (right-hand side) of (
6
), the first term
is “tangential” to
\mathcal{P}
, called the
propagation term and steering a system to traverse along
\mathcal{P}
. The second term is perpendicular to the first term, called
the convergence term and steering a system to move closer to
\mathcal{P}
.

Even under the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
is non-self-intersecting, there may be singularity points where
\chi(q_{1},q_{2})=0
in (
6
) resulting in the vanishing of guiding signals. For example, when
\mathcal{P}
is a simple closed circle centered at the origin, the origin is a singular point. Therefore, the following will introduce the singularity-free GVF for the general case, where the desired path can be either simple closed or self-intersecting
[
19
]
.

II-C
2
General singularity-free GVF

Suppose a physical desired path
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
that may be simple closed or self-intersecting is described by the intersection of
(m-1)
hypersurfaces

\mathcal{P}=\{\zeta:=[q_{1},\dots,q_{m}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\psi_{i}(\zeta)=0,i=1,\cdots,m-1\},

(7)

which can be parameterized by

q_{1}=f_{1}(w),\quad\dots,\quad q_{m}=f_{m}(w).

Here,
\zeta
is the physical coordinate,
f_{i}\in C^{2}
,
i=1,\cdots,m,
and
w\in\mathbb{R}
is the parameter; furthermore, there exists a scalar
\epsilon>0
such that
f^{\prime}_{i}(w):=\frac{{\rm d}f_{i}(w)}{{\rm d}w}
satisfies
|f^{\prime}_{i}(w)|\leq\epsilon
.

Let
\phi_{i}(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},w):=q_{i}-f_{i}(w)
(
i=1,\cdots,m
) and
\xi:=[\zeta^{\top},w]^{\top}
with
w
being viewed as an additional virtual coordinate. Then we can obtain a higher dimensional desired path in the
(m+1)
-dimensional
\xi
-space as

\mathcal{P}^{hgh}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}:\phi_{i}(\xi)=0,i=1,\dots m\}.

Remark 3
.

Intuitively, the
(m+1)
-D path
\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
is obtained by “cutting” and “stretching” the
m
-D path
\mathcal{P}
along the virtual
w
-axis. Inversely speaking, the path
\mathcal{P}
is the orthogonal projection of the path
\mathcal{P}^{hgh}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
in the space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the remaining
w
-axis as zero.
We can also obtain that, for a virtual system trajectory
\xi(t)
living in the same
(m+1)
-D space as
\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
, its orthogonal projection in the
m
-D space where
\mathcal{P}
lives, is the physical system trajectory
\zeta(t)
. It has been shown in
[
19
]
that the path-following errors dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
and dist
(\zeta(t),\mathcal{P})
have the same
convergence behaviour. Therefore, we can investigate dist
(\zeta(t),\mathcal{P})
by dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
.
∎

The gradients of
\phi_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,m
) are
\nabla\phi_{i}=[0,\cdots,1,\cdots,-f^{\prime}_{i}(w)]^{\top}
, where “
1
” is the
i
-th element of
\nabla\phi_{i}
.
Then by
[
19
]
, the GVF for
\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
is constructed as

\chi(q_{1},\dots,q_{m},w)=\times(\nabla\phi_{1},\cdots,\nabla\phi_{m})-\Sigma_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\phi_{i}\nabla\phi_{i},

where “
\times
” is the generalized cross product
[
19
, Eq. (5)]
, and
k_{i}>0,i=1,\cdots,m
, are constant gains. By direct substitution, we have

\chi(\xi)=\begin{bmatrix}(-1)^{m}f^{\prime}_{1}(w)-k_{1}\phi_{1}(\xi)\\
\vdots\\
(-1)^{m}f^{\prime}_{m}(w)-k_{m}\phi_{m}(\xi)\\
(-1)^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\phi_{i}(\xi)f^{\prime}_{i}(w)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}.

(8)

The first and second terms on the RHS of (
8
) are the propagation and converging terms, respectively, which are always linearly independent unless they all equal zero. Therefore, the last element of
\chi
in (
8
) is always non-zero, and the GVF
\chi
is singularity-free, i.e.,
\chi(\xi)\neq 0
for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
.
This appealing property of
\chi
is attributed to the introduction of the additional dimension

, and thus the assumption of no singular points that may limit the range of admissible desired paths becomes unnecessary. Therefore, this note will adopt only the singularity-free GVF, and all the results can also be applied to the general GVF in
[
18
]
.

By the GVF
\chi
, the trajectories of the following system:

\dot{\xi}(t)=\chi(\xi(t))

(9)

globally asymptotically
converge to and move along the desired path
without requiring the origin of the system to be stable
[
19
, Theorem 2]
,
[
21
, Corollary 1]
,
[
8
]
.
Therefore, if one can design a control to drive the trajectory of a mechanical system to satisfy (

II-D

Problem formulation

II-D
1
Problem statement

Consider uncertain mechanical systems with dynamic model (
1
), the control problem is to design a state feedback control
\tau
that drives the system trajectories to follow the desired path
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined like (
7
):

\mathcal{P}=\{q^{\rm s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\psi_{i}(q^{\rm s})=0,i=1,\cdots,m-1\},

(10)

where
q^{\rm s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
comprises some or all entries of
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, not necessarily being
[q_{1},\dots,q_{m}]^{\top}
.

The proposed solution is motivated by capability analysis of existing CFCs and GVF-based controls, which will be further explained in the following subsection. Because the GVF
\chi
in (
8
) will be used, the control
\tau
may draw values from the domains of both physical states
(q,\dot{q})
and extended state
w
.

II-D
2
Motivated solution strategy

In the CFC approach, the constraint is specified as (
3
) to describe control tasks.
Using (
3
), conventional methods reformulate desired path (
10
) into the following equivalent form
:
\dot{\psi}(q^{\rm s})+\Lambda\psi(q^{\rm s})=0
with
\Lambda>0
a constant matrix. Such methods pose two issues. First, the resulting matrix
A
includes the derivative of
\psi(q^{\rm s})
with respect to
q^{\rm s}
. This indicates that
A
will be state-dependent once
\psi(q^{\rm s})
is nonlinear in
q^{\rm s}
, which may lead to the violation of Assumption
1
(as illustrated by an example in the simulation section). Second, the resulting CFC can only guarantee system trajectories to follow the above constraint to approach the desired path, but cannot guarantee system trajectories to move along the desired path.

In the GVF-based control approach, given the GVF (
8
), the remaining task is to design a control such that the dynamics of
\xi
associated with a mechanical system satisfies (
9
). However, current GVF-based control design approaches use kinematic models of robotic systems, assuming an effective system-specific inner-loop dynamics control, and cannot be directly applied to the dynamic model (
1
) of mechanical systems.

Comparing ODEs (
3
) and (
9
), we can find that (
9
) can be cast into the form of (
3
), with a constant (state-independent) matrix
A
. This implies a bridge between CFC and GVF. Given the complementary strengths and limitations of CFC and GVF in addressing path-following tasks,
bridging them suggests a promising solution to the stated control problem. The realization of this idea represents the first contribution claimed in the introduction, which is detailed in subsequent sections.

11

\dot{\zeta}(t)=A\dot{q}(t)=\chi^{\rm s}(Aq(t),w(t)).

(13)

Comparing (
3
) and (
13
), one immediately obtains that (
13
) can be viewed as the constraint in the CFC framework. Namely, the CFC can be used to enable the trajectory
q(t)
of uncertain mechanical system (
1
) to satisfy constraint (
13
) and eventually follow the desired path encoded in (
13
). As (
13
) is constructed based on the vector field, we call it the
vector-field guided constraint (VFC)
, and the resulting CFC is called the
vector-field guided constraint-following control (VFCFC)
. The second order form of (
13
) can be cast into (
4
) with
A
being the same as that in (
13
) and

\begin{split}b(q,\dot{q},w)&=\dot{\chi^{\rm s}}(q,w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{wpart}}}{{=}}\frac{\partial\chi^{\rm s}(q,w)}{\partial q}\dot{q}\\
&+\frac{\partial\chi^{\rm s}(q,w)}{\partial w}\left[(-1)^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}k_{i}\phi_{i}(\xi)f^{\prime}_{i}(w)\right].\end{split}

Remark 4
.

Note that the part of
\dot{w}
(
12
) is not included in the VFC, since (
12
) is a designed virtual system which as an equation always holds for each real-time measurement of
q
. Nevertheless, (
12
) will serve as a virtual “adjoint system” governing the evolution of
w
to “shape” the VFC (
13
) dynamically for the control design. Concretely, for any given initial value
w(t_{0})
and each real-time measured
q
, the control algorithm simply needs to substitute
q
into (
12
) and
solve it to obtain

w
. The resulting
w
can then be used in the VFC (
13
) as the desired system motion being the control goal.
In a word, to fulfill the path-following task, we only need to
design a control to drive the motion of system (
1
) to follow (
13
)
with a virtual “adjoint system” (
12
) governing
w
, which is similar to the adaptive law governing adaptive parameters in adaptive controls
[
10
]
.
∎

Remark 5
.

It is worth emphasizing that the matrix
A
of the VFC is constant and of full row rank, which brings essential benefits to the CFC design for path-following. The most salient one is, with such a matrix
A
, the fundamental Assumption
1
to rule out impossible constraint-following tasks is always satisfied. While in conventional CFC design, Assumption
1
has to be cautiously verified. Furthermore, the matrix
A
is generally involved in some other assumptions within the CFC framework, e.g.,
[
3
]
assuming
A
to be of full row rank. A constant
A
of full row rank, instead of a state-dependent
A
, will greatly ease the restrictions arising from these assumptions.
∎

III-B

Error analysis

In this subsection, error analysis will be implemented to assure the effectiveness of VFC for encoding
the path-following task. From the above discussion, we can find that there are three error signals of the actual system trajectory.

The first error signal is the
path-following error
:

{\rm dist}(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})=\inf\{\|\xi(t)-p\|:p\in\mathcal{P}^{hgh}\}.

Rendering dist(
\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
) to converge to
0
or to be (ultimately) bounded as
t\to\infty
is the ultimate control goal. However, dist(
\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
) is generally computationally complicated. The second error signal is the
path-function error
:

\|\phi(\xi)\|=\|[\phi_{1}(\xi),\phi_{2}(\xi),\cdots,\phi_{m}(\xi)]^{\top}\|,

which is more straightforward for computation. It is introduced to “represent” the path-following error dist(
\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
), in the sense that
\|\phi(\xi)\|
converging to zero implies dist(
\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
) converging to zero along a trajectory
\xi(t)
, and the boundedness of
\|\phi(\xi)\|
implies the boundedness of dist(
\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
) along a trajectory
\xi(t)
.
This is guaranteed under two mild assumptions (i.e., Assumptions
2
and
3
) that will be imposed later to ensure the consistency of convergence behaviors between dist(
\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
) and
\|\phi(\xi)\|
, so that we can use
\|\phi(\xi)\|
to “represent” the path-following error. The third error signal is the
VFC-following error
:

\beta(q,\dot{q},w)=A\dot{q}-\chi^{\rm s}(Aq,w),

which can be directly used to indicate the satisfaction of VFC (
13
). We will show in the sequel that the convergence behavior of
\|\beta\|
aligns with that of
\|\phi\|
. Therefore, the path-following control design task can be fulfilled by
analyzing

\|\beta\|
, as long as certain consistency of convergence behaviors between the path-following error dist(
\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh}
) and the path-function error
\|\phi(\xi)\|
is guaranteed.

The VFC-following error
\beta

indicates
the distance between
\dot{\zeta}

and
\chi^{\rm s}
, which affects the satisfaction of (
13
). Furthermore, as the part of
\dot{w}
(
12
) always holds, it does not bring any errors. Consequently, we can obtain the
disturbed system of (
9
)
as

\dot{\xi}(t)=\chi(\xi(t))+d(t)

with
d(t)=[\beta^{\top}(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t)),0]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
being the disturbance.
Then the governing equation of
\phi
is

\dot{\phi}(\xi(t))=N^{\top}(\xi(t))(\chi(\xi(t))+d(t))

(14)

with
N(\xi)=[\nabla\phi_{1}(\xi),\nabla\phi_{2}(\xi),\cdots,\nabla\phi_{m}(\xi)]\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times m}
.

With the governing equation (
14
) of path-function error
\phi
, the following subsections will demonstrate the feasibility of using the convergence behavior of VFC-following error
\|\beta\|
to indicate that of path-following error
\text{dist}(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
. The demonstration is carried out considering two different cases: vanishing
d
and non-vanishing but bounded
d
.

III-B
1
Vanishing disturbance

Assumption 2
.

For any positive scalar constant
\epsilon
, it holds that

\inf\{\|\phi(\xi(t))\|:{\rm dist}(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})\geq\epsilon\}>0.

Remark 6
.

Assumption
2
implies that along any continuous trajectory
\xi(t)
, we have
\|\phi(\xi(t))\|\to 0\implies{\rm dist}(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Therefore, if
\|\phi\|
vanishes along a trajectory, then the path-following error also vanishes.
This assumption is
crucial to exclude some pathological cases
[
19
]
, enabling
us to use
\|\phi\|
to indicate
the convergence behaviour of
{\rm dist}(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
when
\|\phi\|

vanishes, which serves as a base for the following proposition to show that vanishing
\|\beta\|
or
\|d\|
leads to vanishing dist
(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
.
∎

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumption
2
, the path-following error dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
will converge to
0
if
\|\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t))\|=\|d(t)\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.
By
[
18
, Theorem 3]
, the governing equation (
14
) of the path-function error
\phi(\xi(t))
is locally input-to-state stable
with
d(t)
being the input
, which implies that the error satisfies
\|\phi(\xi(t))\|\leq\eta(\|\phi(\xi(t_{0}))\|,t)+\varrho(\sup_{r\in[t_{0},t)}\|d(r)\|)
for a class
\mathcal{KL}
function
\eta
and a class
\mathcal{K}
function
\varrho
. Since
\|d\|\equiv\|\beta\|
and
\|\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t))\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
,
\|\phi(\xi(t))\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. By Assumption
2
, we have that dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
converges to
0
as
t\to\infty
.
\hfill\blacksquare

III-B
2
Non-vanishing but bounded disturbance

Assumption 3

(Radial boundedness of
\phi
)
.

It holds that

\|\phi(p)\|\to\infty
as dist
(p,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})\to\infty
for any point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
.

Remark 7
.

Technically, Assumption
3
implies that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
, such that if dist
(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})>\epsilon_{0}
,
then
\|\phi(\xi)\|>\epsilon
. In other words, we have
\{\xi:\|\phi(\xi)\|\leq\epsilon\}\subseteq\{\xi:\mathrm{dist}(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})\leq\epsilon_{0}\}
, indicating that
dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
is bounded if
\|\phi(\xi(t))\|
is bounded along any continuous trajectory
\xi(t)
.
This assumption holds for most practical desired paths, despite counter examples, e.g.,
[
20
, Example 1]
. Therefore, we can
use
\|\phi(\xi)\|
to investigate the convergence behaviour
of dist
(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
when
\|\phi(\xi)\|
is bounded, which serves as a base for the following proposition to show that bounded
\|\beta\|
or
\|d\|
leads to bounded dist
(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
.
∎

Proposition 2
.

Under Assumption
3
, the path-following error dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
is uniformly ultimately bounded if
\|\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t))\|=\|d(t)\|
is uniformly bounded.

Proof.
As
\|d\|\equiv\|\beta\|
, we have
\sup_{r\in[t_{0},t)}\|d(r)\|=\sup_{r\in[t_{0},t)}\|\beta(q(r),\dot{q}(r),w(r))\|
. From the proof of Proposition
1
, the path-function error
\|\phi(\xi(t))\|
is uniformly ultimately bounded by
a
\mathcal{K}
-class function of
\sup_{r\in[t_{0},t)}\|\beta(q(r),\dot{q}(r),w(r))\|
, i.e.,
\varrho(\sup_{r\in[t_{0},t)}\|\beta(q(r),\dot{q}(r),w(r))\|)
,
since the governing equation (
14
) of
\phi(\xi(t))
is locally input-to-state stable
with
d(t)
being the input
.
Applying Assumption
3
, we have
that dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
is uniformly ultimately bounded.
\hfill\blacksquare

From Propositions
1
and
2
, it follows that if the control law designed for (
1
) ensures that the VFC-following error
\|\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t))\|
converges to
0
or remains uniformly bounded as
t\to\infty
, then the path-following error dist
(\xi,\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
exhibits consistent convergence behaviour, provided that mild assumptions are satisfied. Therefore, the path-following task associated with the desired path
\mathcal{P}
in (
10
) can be transformed to the constraint-following task associated with the VFC (
13
), which will be fulfilled in the following subsection.

Based on the above results, the control design for the path-following problem of the mechanical system (
1
) proceeds in two steps. First, neglecting the uncertainty, a class of nominal path-following controls is designed. Second, considering the uncertainty, a class of adaptive robust path-following controls is designed based on the nominal control.

III-C

Nominal path-following control

The nominal system of
(
1
)
can be described as

\begin{split}\bar{M}(q(t),t)\ddot{q}(t)+\bar{C}(q(t),\dot{q}(t),t)\dot{q}(t)\\
+\bar{g}(q(t),t)=\bar{B}(q(t),t)\tau(t).\end{split}

(15)

In this study, the nominal and uncertain portions of a system matrix
(\cdot)
are denoted by
\bar{(\cdot)}
and
\Delta(\cdot)
respectively, hence
(\cdot)=\bar{(\cdot)}+\Delta(\cdot)
. For example,
\bar{M}
represents the nominal portion of
M
,
which is also considered to be positive definite
. The nominal path-following control that steers the nominal system (
15
) to follow
\mathcal{P}
is designed under the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

For any given constant matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
P>0
, there exists a positive constant
\underline{\lambda}>0
such that
\lambda_{m}(PA\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t)(A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t))^{\top}P)\geq\underline{\lambda}
for all
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, where
\lambda_{m}(\cdot)
denotes the minimal eigenvalue of a matrix.

Remark 8
.

Note
PA\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t)(A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t))^{\top}P
is positive semi-definite for all
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
. Assumption
4
simply requires that the minimum eigenvalue remain bounded away from zero. Another implication of Assumption
4
is that the matrix
A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t)
is invertible for all
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
,
a property that does not automatically follow even when
A
and
B
are full rank (as the product may lose rank)
. This assumption excludes tasks where the matrix
A
results in a non-invertible
A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t)
for some
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, as its inverse is required in the control design. A similar assumption can be found in existing CFC studies, e.g.,
[
24
,
23
]
. However, the distinction lies in the fact that Assumption
4
in this study uses a constant
A
that is of full row rank, whereas the corresponding assumption in existing CFC studies involves an
A
that depends on
(q,t)
. This difference stems from the proposed VFC, which greatly relaxes the assumption restriction.
∎

The nominal path-following control is proposed as

\tau(t)=p_{1}(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t),t)+p_{2}(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t),t),

(16)

with

\begin{split}p_{1}(q,\dot{q},w,t)=(A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t))^{-1}[b(q,\dot{q},w)\\
+A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)(\bar{C}(q,\dot{q},t)\dot{q}+\bar{g}(q,t))],\\
p_{2}(q,\dot{q},w,t)=-\kappa(A\bar{M}^{-1}(q,t)\bar{B}(q,t))^{\top}P\beta(q,\dot{q},w),\end{split}

(17)

and

\beta=A\dot{q}-\chi^{\rm s}
,
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a gain parameter
.

Theorem 1

(
Vanishing error for nominal systems)
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
4
, the path-following control (
16
) drives the trajectory of the nominal mechanical system (
15
) to converge to the desired path (
10
), i.e., the path-following error satisfies that
{\rm dist}(\zeta(t),\mathcal{P})\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

By the results in
[
24
, Theorem 2]
, it follows that
\beta(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t))=A\dot{q}(t)-\chi^{\rm s}(Aq(t),w(t))
exponentially converges to
0
as
t\to\infty
. Namely, the trajectory of the nominal mechanical system (
15
) asymptotically follows the constraint (
13
) (c.f., Definition
2
). Then by Proposition
1
, since the VFC-following error converges to 0 as
t\to\infty
, the path-following error dist
(\xi(t),\mathcal{P}^{hgh})
converges to
0
as
t\to\infty
, and thus by Remark
3
, we have
{\rm dist}(\zeta(t),\mathcal{P})\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
∎

III-D

Adaptive robust path-following control

To robustly achieve path-following for the uncertain system (
1
) with the desired path (
10
), this section designs an adaptive robust action
p_{3}
added to the nominal control (
16
) to suppress uncertainty, resulting in a class of adaptive robust path-following controls. Due to the uncertainties, vanishing path-following error is not always possible, while the best achievable performance is typically the boundedness of the path-following error, which is the objective of the
p_{3}
design.

To handle the mismatched uncertainty, we perform the following uncertainty decompositions. Let
H:=\bar{M}M^{-1}-I
, which is employed to represent the uncertainty in
M
. Let

H(q,\sigma,t)=\bar{B}(q,t)\check{H}(q,\sigma,t)+\tilde{H}(q,\sigma,t).

(18)

This is to divide
H
into two portions based on
\bar{B}
, i.e., the matched uncertainty
\bar{B}\check{H}
and the mismatched uncertainty
\tilde{H}
, which lies within and outside the range space of
\bar{B}
, respectively. Throughout the paper, we use
\check{(\cdot)}
and
\tilde{(\cdot)}
to respectively denote matched and mismatched portions.
There are infinite choices for the particular expressions of
\check{H}
and
\tilde{H}
. For example, one may choose any

\check{H}
and let
\tilde{H}:=H-\bar{B}\check{H}
.
However, we chose
\check{H}
and then obtain
\tilde{H}
by (
18
)
as follows

\begin{split}&\check{H}=(A\bar{M}^{-1}\bar{B})^{-1}A\bar{M}^{-1}H,\\
&\tilde{H}=H-\bar{B}(A\bar{M}^{-1}\bar{B})^{-1}A\bar{M}^{-1}H,\end{split}

(19)

where recall that
A\bar{M}^{-1}\bar{B}
is invertible by Assumption
4
. Similar to (
18
), other uncertain system matrices,
\Delta C
,
\Delta g
, and
\Delta B
, can also be decomposed based on the nominal matrix
\bar{B}
. The detailed expressions for these decompositions are omitted here for simplicity. From (
19
), we obtain the following identity regarding the mismatched uncertainty
\tilde{H}

A\bar{M}^{-1}\tilde{H}=A\bar{M}^{-1}[H-\bar{B}(A\bar{M}^{-1}\bar{B})^{-1}A\bar{M}^{-1}H]\equiv 0.

Similarly, it can be shown that the mismatched uncertainties of other uncertain system matrices satisfy analogous identities, i.e.,
A\bar{M}^{-1}\tilde{(\cdot)}\equiv 0
, where
(\cdot)
represents
C
,
g
or
B
.

Assumption 5
.

There exists a (possibly unknown) constant
\rho_{W}>-1
such that for all
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}

\frac{1}{2}\min\limits_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\lambda_{m}(W(q,\sigma,t)+W^{\top}(q,\sigma,t))\geq\rho_{W}

with

W(q,\sigma,t)=\check{B}(q,\sigma,t)+\check{H}(q,\sigma,t)B(q,\sigma,t).

Remark 9
.

Note that
W

is synthesized by
M
and
B
. The inequality of
W
implies that the minimum eigenvalue of symmetric matrix
(W+W^{\top})
over the domain of uncertainty
\sigma
should not be smaller than a constant
\rho_{W}>-1
. For extreme cases,
if
M
has no uncertainty, then the condition reduces to
W=\check{B}
; If furthermore,
B
has no uncertainty, then
W=0
, and the assumption can be removed. Therefore, this assumption simply characterizes
how much uncertainties in
M
and
B
can be tolerated in the control design, similar to
[
3
, Assumption 4]
,
[
23
, Assumption 4]
.
∎

Assumption 6
.

There exists a (possibly unknown) constant vector
\alpha\in(0,\infty)^{k}

with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and a known function
\Pi(\cdot):(0,\infty)^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for all
(q,\dot{q},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\begin{split}&(1+\rho_{W})^{-1}\underset{\sigma\in\Sigma}{\max}\|\check{H}(q,\sigma,t)(-C(q,\dot{q},\sigma,t)\dot{q}-g(q,\dot{q},\sigma,t)\\
&+B(q,\sigma,t)p_{1}(q,\dot{q},w,t))+(\check{B}(q,\sigma,t)p_{1}(q,\dot{q},w,t)\\
&-\check{C}(q,\dot{q},\sigma,t)\dot{q}-\check{g}(q,\dot{q},\sigma,t))\|\leq\Pi(\alpha,q,\dot{q},w,t).\end{split}

(20)

Furthermore, the function
\Pi
can be linearly factored with respect to
\alpha
,

\Pi(\alpha,q,\dot{q},w,t)=\alpha^{\top}\breve{\Pi}(q,\dot{q},w,t),

where
\breve{\Pi}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
.

Remark 10
.

Since
\rho_{W}>-1
, we have
\rho_{W}+1>0
.
Note that the left-hand side of (
20
) aggregates all system uncertainties, thus
the function
\Pi
serves as
an envelope bound that upper-bounds the aggregated uncertainty term over the uncertainty domain
. While the structure of
\Pi
is known, the constant vector
\alpha
is possibly unknown
since it is relevant to the unknown bounding set
\Sigma
. Therefore, Assumption
6
simply aims to provide a functional envelope by
\Pi
to characterize the worst-case effect of system uncertainties.
∎

The adaptive robust path following control is proposed as:

\begin{split}\tau(t)=p_{1}(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t),t)+p_{2}(q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t),t)\\
+p_{3}(\hat{\alpha}(t),q(t),\dot{q}(t),w(t),t).\end{split}

(21)

Here,
p_{1,2}
are given in (
16
), and
p_{3}
is designed as

\begin{split}p_{3}(\hat{\alpha},q,\dot{q},w,t)=-\eta(\hat{\alpha},q,\dot{q},w,t)\upsilon(\hat{\alpha},q,\dot{q},w,t)\Pi(\hat{\alpha},q,\dot{q},w,t),\end{split}

(22)

where

\upsilon=\bar{B}^{\top}\bar{M}^{-1}A^{\top}P\beta\Pi,\quad\eta=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\|\upsilon\|},&\mbox{if }\|\upsilon\|>\mu,\\
\frac{1}{\mu},&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

and
\mu
is a positive scalar constant.
The vector
-\eta\upsilon
defines the “direction” to compensate uncertainties.
The parameter vector
\hat{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is governed by the following adaptive law:

\dot{\hat{\alpha}}=\begin{cases}\ell_{1}\breve{\Pi}\|\breve{\beta}\|-\ell_{2}\hat{\alpha},&\mbox{if }\|\breve{\Pi}\|\|\breve{\beta}\|>\varepsilon,\\
\ell_{1}\breve{\Pi}\frac{\|\breve{\Pi}\|\|\breve{\beta}\|^{2}}{\varepsilon}-\ell_{2}\hat{\alpha},&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(23)

where
\varepsilon
,
\ell_{1,2}
are positive scalar constants,
\hat{\alpha}_{i}(t_{0})>0
and
\breve{\beta}=\bar{B}^{\top}DA^{\top}P\beta
. Hence
\upsilon=\breve{\beta}\Pi
.

Remark 11
.

The piecewise continuous adaptive law (
23
) is of leakage type, and the second term on the RHS is the leak. We can obtain
\hat{\alpha}_{i}(t)>0
for all
t\geq t_{0}
with
\hat{\alpha}_{i}(t_{0})>0
. This is because the first term on the RHS is always non-negative, and by the property of differential inequality, the solution of (
23
) is always no less than that of
\dot{\hat{\alpha}}=-\ell_{2}\hat{\alpha}
with
\hat{\alpha}_{i}(t_{0})>0
, which is always positive. The adaptive parameter
\hat{\alpha}
tends to emulate the unknown
\alpha
in the bound function
\Pi
, which will be proved in Theorem
2
. As
p_{3}
is designed based on
\hat{\alpha}
, it is called adaptive robust action. Consequently, the resulting control
\tau
consisting of
p_{1,2,3}
is called adaptive robust path-following control.
This control design operates free from limitations on the system’s number of degrees of freedom, making it suitable for a wide range of practical applications.

∎

Theorem 2

(
Boundedness for uncertain systems)
.

Under Assumptions
3
-
6
, the adaptive robust path-following control (
21
) ensures that, for the uncertain mechanical system (
1
) with the desired path (
10
), both the path-following error
{\rm dist}(\zeta(t),\mathcal{P})
and the adaptive error
\|\hat{\alpha}(t)-\alpha\|
are uniformly ultimately bounded.

Proof.

See the Appendix.
∎

Remark 12
.

From the proof of Theorem
2
, the boundedness region is primarily determined by the gain parameter
\kappa
, the matrix
P
, and the adaptive-law parameters
\ell_{1,2}
. These four factors collectively influence the extent of boundedness. Notably, for fixed
P
and
\ell_{1,2}
, the expression characterizing boundedness indicates that increasing
\kappa
tends to shrink—and never enlarges—the boundedness region. Hence, larger
\kappa
can enhance path-following accuracy. Moreover, while the main results are derived using the singularity-free GVF, they are also directly applicable to the general GVF presented in
[
18
]
. In fact, such application is even more straightforward, as it circumvents the need for the virtual state
w
.

∎

IV-A

An underactuated planar vertical take-off and landing aircraft system

This section presents simulations on an underactuated planar vertical take-off and landing (PVTOL) aircraft
[

V
Conclusion

This note integrates GVF and CFC to solve the dynamics control problem of geometric path-following for mechanical systems under heterogeneous time-varying uncertainties–a task impossible with either approach alone. The VFC is meticulously constructed to encode the desired path, bridging GVF and CFC. Under mild assumptions, the main results include: i) path-following error convergence matches that of the VFC-following error; ii) nominal VFCFC ensures vanishing errors without uncertainties; iii) adaptive robust VFCFC ensures ultimately bounded errors with uncertainties. Simulations on a PVTOL aircraft validate the approach.

This work opens a new avenue for path-following control that considers the internal dynamics of uncertain systems. Future research may explore extensions such as collision avoidance, input saturation, and
time-varying desired paths
in swarm mechanical systems, for enhanced robotic deployment.
