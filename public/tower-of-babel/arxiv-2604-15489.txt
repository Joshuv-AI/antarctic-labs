A Q-learning-based QoS-aware multipath routing protocol in IoMT-based wireless body area network Journal: Journal of L a T e X Templates
Mehdi Hosseinzadeh, Roohallah Alizadehsani, Amin Beheshti, Hamid Alinejad-Rokny, Lu Chen, Mohammad Sadegh Yousefpoor, Efat Yousefpoor, Muneera Altayeb, Thantrira Porntaveetus, Sadia Din

Abstract
In recent years, the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) has rapidly expanded by leveraging Internet of Things (IoT) technologies to enable intelligent communication among medical devices, systems, and services. This paradigm provides a platform for remote monitoring, automated management, and optimal allocation of medical resources, thereby enhancing the quality of healthcare services and improving patient well-being. However, the dynamic nature of network topology, the energy constraints of sensor nodes, and the need to guarantee Quality of Service (QoS) for diverse medical data–from real-time vital information to routine monitoring data–pose serious challenges to the design of efficient routing methods in IoMT-based WBAN networks. To address these challenges, this research offers a novel Q-learning-based QoS-aware multipath routing method called QQMR. In this method, the sensed data are classified into three categories based on their QoS requirements–emergency, error-sensitive, and normal–so that routing decisions can be made based on the specific needs of each data type. To manage heterogeneous traffic, QQMR uses an adaptive multi-level queuing model in which each node dynamically adjusts the capacity of queues allocated to each packet type according to the occupied volume of the corresponding queue, the data arrival rate, and the available buffer space. To reduce the state space and accelerate convergence, QQMR utilizes a QoS-aware clustering algorithm based on adaptive weighted fuzzy C-means, where each cluster represents a particular QoS requirement, and nodes can belong to multiple clusters with different membership degrees. The clustering output is fed into the Q-learning algorithm to train three independent and specialized learning policies for the different packet types. Consequently, three separate Q-tables are maintained for emergency, error-sensitive, and normal packets, and the primary route for each packet is selected based on the maximum Q-value in its corresponding learning policy. In addition, the backup path is selected from among alternative routes, which have the highest QoS adaptability to the primary learning policy. For performance evaluation, QQMR is compared with three baseline methods: QQAR, EQRSRL, and QPRR. The evaluation results show that, when the node density changes, QQMR increases the packet delivery ratio by 5.45%, while reducing the average end-to-end delay, routing overhead, and energy consumption by 34.79%, 11.17%, and 19.08%, respectively. Moreover, when the packet-sending rate varies, QQMR improves the packet delivery ratio by 2.48%, while reducing the average end-to-end delay, routing overhead, and hop count by 35.02%, 11.73%, and 27.51%, respectively.

To design an efficient routing protocol, smart healthcare services require communication that is reliable, delay-sensitive, energy-aware, and congestion-aware. Reinforcement learning (RL), a subset of machine learning, focuses on how agents learns to make decisions that maximize long-term rewards. In IoMT-based WBANs, RL can be applied to select optimal routes that enhance both reliability and energy efficiency in the data transmission process

The present study proposes a Q-learning-based QoS-aware multipath routing method called QQMR. By combining Q-learning and adaptive QoS-aware clustering, QQMR performs dynamic and specialized routing decisions within the network. In QQMR, data packets are first classified according to their QoS requirements. Then, the network state space is divided into multiple clusters with distinct QoS needs using an adaptive weighted fuzzy C-means algorithm. This clustering process reduces learning complexity and accelerates the convergence of Q-learning. For each packet type, a dedicated learning policy is designed. Based on these policies, both primary and backup routes are determined adaptively with awareness of QoS requirements. The key innovations of this study are as follows:

1.

In QQMR, an adaptive weighted fuzzy C-means algorithm is used to divide the Q-learning state space into subspaces corresponding to QoS characteristics. In this clustering, the feature weights (such as delay, error rate, remaining energy, and buffer capacity) are dynamically adjusted to accurately reflect actual network conditions. This structure reduces the dimensionality of the state space, accelerates the convergence of the Q-learning algorithm, and maintains high decision-making accuracy.

2.

In QQMR, three independent learning policies are designed for the different types of medical packets: emergency, error-sensitive, and normal. Each policy has its own reward function and is trained according to its corresponding QoS requirement–minimizing delay for emergency data, reducing error rate for error-sensitive data, and optimizing energy consumption for normal data. This approach enables targeted decision-making, reduces interference among QoS requirements, and enhances overall network efficiency.

3.

To support dynamic and intelligent buffer management, QQMR utilizes a multi-queue structure with separate queues for each packet type. The capacity of each queue is dynamically adjusted based on its occupied volume, the incoming data rate, and the available buffer space. This adaptive mechanism improves buffer utilization, prevents congestion, and enhances packet delivery rate.

4.

In QQMR, the backup path is not selected solely based on the maximum Q-value. Instead, it is chosen according to its QoS compatibility with the main learning policy. Specifically, the alternative route is selected based on the similarity of its QoS characteristics (such as delay, error rate, or energy) to those of the optimal main path. This approach enhances transmission reliability and stability under topology changes or path failures.

5.

By partitioning the state space into QoS-based clusters and defining independent learning policies, QQMR makes the learning process faster, computationally lighter, and more stable. As a result, the method is well suited for dynamic and large-scale networks. Overall, QQMR effectively balances multiple performance objectives, including delay, energy efficiency, and transmission accuracy.

Related works

In
[
31
]
, the authors proposed a QoS-aware peer routing scheme called QPRR. This protocol improves QoS requirements in body area networks (BANs), particularly for reliability-critical medical data such as continuous monitoring of vital signs and essential physiological parameters. Owing to the limited computational capacity, processing power, and bandwidth of BAN sensors, conventional routing methods cannot maintain stable performance in dynamic environments. This instability can lead to increased latency, energy imbalance, and packet loss. To overcome these challenges, QPRR applies a QoS-aware peer-assisted routing approach to ensure reliable and energy-efficient data transmission even under changing network conditions or node failures. In indoor healthcare environments, routing protocols must ensure high reliability, low energy consumption, and real-time visualization of patient data. Accordingly, QPRR explicitly considers the QoS requirements of BAN data streams and meets medical data reliability. The protocol includes both centralized and distributed components. In the centralized model, patient information and visualization units are managed via a central computing unit. Conversely, the distributed model partitions BAN data across multiple display units, thereby reducing network congestion and ensuring uninterrupted monitoring as patients move. Simulation outcomes indicate that QPRR achieves better throughput, lower routing overhead (fewer hello packets), and reduced packet loss compared with other existing routing strategies.

In
[
32
]
, an efficient QoS-aware routing method called EQRSRL is presented for IoMT applications. Due to the diversity of patient-related data, EQRSRL attempts to provide an adequate level of QoS for heterogeneous IoMT traffic. It improves QoS by establishing secure and stable communication, improving network availability, and reducing transmission delay and packet loss. EQRSRL classifies network traffic into three categories, each handled according to its specific QoS requirements. Additionally, EQRSRL divides the network environment into multiple regions to reduce the exchanged messages among nodes. In addition, route selection considers both QoS metrics and energy constraints to identify optimal paths. EQRSRL integrates a reinforcement learning mechanism (Q-learning) into the route computation process. Simulation results indicate that EQRSRL is feasible for IoMT applications and demonstrates competitive performance compared to other routing protocols. It provides fast and reliable communication paths between patient-connected biosensors and cloud computing platforms.

In
[
33
]
, a Q-learning-based QoS-aware routing approach called QQAR is proposed in IoMT-equipped WBANs. Initially, QQAR employs a two-hop-based link reliability estimation to guarantee communication reliability. Routing decisions rely on two-hop neighbor information to increase the likelihood of successful healthcare data delivery. Then, packets are prioritized according to traffic type to ensure the timely transmission of critical data even under congested networks. QQAR also considers the velocity of two-hop neighboring nodes and incorporates energy balancing to increase the probability of meeting packet deadlines. Additionally, the multi-sink strategy adopted in QQAR enhances overall network reliability. Q-learning is utilized to select appropriate neighboring nodes for decentralized transmission of healthcare data. In addition, the multi-sink load-balancing and congestion-control strategies regulate routing decisions and mitigate network congestion. Simulation results report that QQAR achieves satisfactory performance compared to several existing routing algorithms.

In
[
34
]
, a cluster-based routing mechanism named MGWOQL is designed for WBANs. This approach integrates machine learning (ML) techniques to predict excessive energy consumption and improve cluster-head (CH) selection. Specifically, MGWOQL combines a modified grey wolf optimization (GWO) algorithm with Q-learning to select and update optimal CHs. The protocol employs multiple objective functions to reduce cluster-level energy consumption by carefully selecting the most suitable CH. ML techniques are used to predict cluster energy consumption and lifetime, while the enhanced GWO algorithm, along with Q-learning, determines the optimal CH for data routing. Since WBANs transmit sensitive healthcare data such as ECG and EEG signals, designing an energy-efficient routing mechanism for heterogeneous and dynamic environments is essential. MGWOQL prevents energy wastage during CH selection and updating, thereby maximizing the residual energy across the network. The rationale behind combining GWO and Q-learning is that GWO ensures strong convergence toward near-optimal solutions, whereas Q-learning enhances the search capability for identifying the best routing decisions. Simulation results demonstrate that MGWOQL performs effectively in terms of reducing energy consumption in WBANs.

In
[
35
]
, a high-throughput, thermal-aware routing approach called TAHT is suggested in WBANs. This method utilizes a multi-hop topology and prioritizes stable links for efficient healthcare data transmission by considering factors such as residual energy, link quality, and temperature. By incorporating both link quality estimation and temperature estimation, TAHT selects stable and thermally safe communication paths to enhance throughput and reliability. The protocol operates in several stages: network initialization, link quality calculation, temperature estimation, and data transmission. It reduces redundant transmissions, optimizes routing paths, and manages energy consumption efficiently. Importantly, TAHT places significant emphasis on sensor node temperature to recognize its impact on human health, QoS, and transmission reliability. Simulation results indicate that TAHT outperforms conventional routing protocols in terms of route stability, network throughput, temperature control, and network lifetime.

In
[
36
]
, a reactive routing algorithm called TSFIS-GWO is offered. This routing method integrates the Takagi–Sugeno fuzzy inference system (TSFIS) with the grey wolf optimizer (GWO). TSFIS serves as the core of the routing method, generating real-time solutions and supporting just-in-time (JIT) routing. Meanwhile, GWO is used offline to tune the hyper-parameters of the TSFIS model once before deployment. Thus, TSFIS-GWO represents a hybrid technique that leverages metaheuristic optimization for offline tuning and fuzzy logic for real-time routing decisions. During operation, the tuned fuzzy system continuously receives node and link status information to construct a multi-hop routing tree. The fuzzy rules are automatically adjusted using a GWO-based learning process tailored to the specific requirements of each WBAN application. Notably, this tuning process is conducted only once prior to operational deployment. Simulation results confirm that TSFIS-GWO provides real-time routing solutions and achieves superior performance compared to existing methods.

In
[
37
]
, an adaptive reinforcement learning-based mobility-aware routing method (ARMR) is presented for heterogeneous WBANs. This method introduces a metric called link lifetime estimation (LLE), which predicts how long two nodes will remain within each other’s transmission range, thereby improving routing reliability. In ARMR, nodes with longer predicted link lifetimes are preferred for route construction, enhancing communication stability. LLE is derived from network topology and structural information, enabling ARMR to dynamically adjust neighbor holding time and hello message intervals based on predicted link stability. This adaptive mechanism reduces link disconnections, lost packets, routing holes, and blind paths. In addition, ARMR incorporates an adaptive Q-learning strategy for accurate routing decisions in highly dynamic WBANs. The reward function in ARMR is computed based on three criteria: LLE, energy consumption, and network quality. These criteria are essential for constructing stable and efficient routes. Moreover, ARMR adaptively adjusts the learning rate (
\alpha
) and discount factor (
\gamma
) according to LLE and neighbor stability, enabling effective learning in dynamic scenarios. In addition, ARMR effectively manages communication flows, reducing unnecessary delay and routing overhead, making it suitable for healthcare data transmission. The simulation results show that ARMR achieves superior performance compared to other routing methods.

In
[
38
]
, an energy efficient and reliable routing approach based on reinforcement learning and fuzzy logic, named EERR-RLFL, is suggested for WBANs. Considering the heterogeneity of sensor nodes in WBANs, EERR-RLFL first introduces a node-ranking mechanism. In this mechanism, nodes are categorized into different ranks based on three evaluation perspectives. Each rank is treated as a key metric affecting link quality, which balances energy consumption and effectively utilizes nodes that have completed their sensing tasks. Subsequently, a fuzzy logic-based link quality evaluation algorithm, referred to as FLLQE, is introduced to assess the quality of communication links between nodes. FLLQE considers multiple metrics simultaneously to provide a comprehensive link quality estimation. During data transmission, EERR-RLFL employs a hybrid forwarding technique based on FLLQE. Initially, the transmission time of a candidate node is calculated. Then, a reinforcement learning algorithm is executed to determine the globally optimal routing path. Additionally, EERR-RLFL includes optimization mechanisms aimed at reducing overall energy consumption, balancing energy usage across nodes, and enhancing route reliability. The simulation results indicate that EERR-RLFL outperforms conventional energy-efficient routing protocols in terms of energy efficiency and packet reception rate.

In
[
39
]
, a greedy perimeter stateless routing (GPSR) strategy is offered for wireless networks. GPSR is a decentralized, position-based routing approach that benefits from the geographic coordinates of nodes to forward packets toward the destination. In GPSR, each node makes forwarding decisions based solely on information about its immediate neighbors, using greedy forwarding to select the neighbor closest to the destination. However, when greedy forwarding fails–such as when a packet reaches a local minimum where no neighbor is closer to the destination–GPSR switches to a perimeter forwarding technique. In this mode, packets are routed around the void (routing hole) until greedy forwarding becomes feasible again. This mechanism ensures successful data delivery even in the presence of network voids. Likewise, GPSR offers greater scalability compared with shortest-path algorithms and traditional ad hoc routing protocols because it relies only on local topology information. Furthermore, it adapts efficiently to frequent topology changes and node mobility by continuously utilizing updated neighbor information. As a result, GPSR performs robustly in dense wireless networks and demonstrates strong scalability. However, due to its greedy nature, GPSR may become trapped in local optima under certain conditions. Simulation results indicate that GPSR provides robust performance and high scalability compared to other routing strategies.

Basic concepts

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a key approach in artificial intelligence (AI) that focuses on training intelligent agents to make optimal decisions in dynamic and uncertain environments. In this framework, an agent continuously interacts with its environment and adjusts its behavioral policy based on feedback in the form of rewards or penalties. Through repeated interactions and trial-and-error learning, the agent gradually discovers which actions produce the most desirable outcomes under different conditions. The theoretical foundation of this approach is built on a mathematical model known as the Markov decision process (MDP), in which decision-making depends only on the current state, not on the sequence of past states
[

,

All actions that the agent can take in each state.

to another state, such as

4.

in the given state
{s_{t}}

In RL, the ultimate goal is to extract the optimal policy

that maximizes the expected cumulative future reward. Future rewards are typically discounted using a discount factor (

Network configuration

This section presents the assumptions related to the network and energy models in QQMR.

4.1
Network model

As illustrated in Figure
2
, QQMR is designed for wireless body area networks (WBANs) equipped with the Internet of medical things (IoMT) deployed in large healthcare centers to continuously monitor patients’ health conditions. The primary objective of the proposed method is to collect vital physiological parameters–such as heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature, respiration rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pH–and transmit them to the hospital’s health monitoring system via a sink node.

In this network, two types of communication are defined: communication inside WBANs and communication between WBANs. The communication inside WBANs includes interactions between the biosensors embedded inside and on the patient’s body and the local processing unit (LPU) installed near the patient’s bed. Additionally, communication between WBANs refers to data transmission from the WBAN users to the sink node. This research specifically focuses on the communication between WBANs and the sink node.

In general, the network model in QQMR is a directed graph, denoted as
G=\left(V,L\right)

L\subseteq V\times V
. A communication link between two nodes

) is less than or equal to the communication radius (

Data transmission from the LPU to the sink node can occur either directly or in a multi-hop manner through intermediate WBAN nodes. In addition, QQMR leverages positioning systems to obtain spatial information and monitor nodes’ residual energy levels. This information enables route optimization and efficient energy management.

Once the gathered data reach the sink node, they are forwarded to the hospital server. This ensures that medical staff receive accurate and up-to-date patient information, facilitating timely clinical decisions and improving the quality of healthcare services. Moreover, the approach reduces healthcare personnel workload and enhances responsiveness to emergencies.

5
Proposed method

This research proposes a Q-learning-based QoS-aware multipath routing scheme (QQMR) for WBANs equipped with IoMT. In this approach, the sensed data are classified into three different groups based on their QoS characteristics: emergency packets, error-sensitive packets, and normal packets. Then, QQMR divides the search space into multiple clusters using the weighted fuzzy C-means-based QoS-aware clustering, where each cluster highlights a specific QoS requirement. This clustering reduces the state space and improves the learning speed. As a result, each type of packet investigates only the states relevant to its own requirements. The clustering output is fed into the Q-learning algorithm to train three independent and specialized routing policies for the different packet types. For each cluster, independent learning policies are defined, and three separate Q-tables are used for specialized responsiveness to the specific QoS requirements. The main route for each packet type is determined based on its corresponding dedicated learning policy, while the backup route is selected from other potential paths based on the highest similarity to the main learning policy. Overall, QQMR consists of four phases: discovering the neighboring nodes, QoS-aware packet classification, weighted fuzzy C-means-based QoS-aware clustering, and the design of Q-learning-based multipath routing policies. 5.2.1
Packet queuing and scheduling model

In QQMR, to enable efficient packet management, each node is equipped with an independent multi-queue buffer structure. This structure consists of three separate queues, each of them dedicated to a specific type of packet (
p=1,2,3
). Unlike many traditional models that rely on fixed capacities of queues, QQMR benefits from a dynamic and adaptive model to adjust queue capacities in real time according to network conditions. This approach is adopted in response to the non-uniform distribution of data and the changing demands placed on queues over time.

Accordingly, the total buffer capacity at each node is shared among the queues, and the space allocated to each queue is adjusted based on three factors:

1. Occupied buffer space

At the end of time span
\left[t,t+\Delta t\right]

7
based on the previous occupied volume, i.e.,
B\left(t\right)

(7)

This value is always lower than the total buffer capacity (Equation
8

0\leq B\left(t+\Delta t\right)\leq C

(8)

In QQMR, the sink node is responsible for clustering the nodes using a QoS-aware clustering algorithm based on adaptive weighted fuzzy C-means (WFCM)

]

To enhance adaptability to network changes, clustering is periodically updated at the sink node. The adaptive weighted fuzzy C-means method is a soft clustering technique that permits nodes to belong to multiple clusters simultaneously with different membership degrees. In this method, feature weights are adaptively updated based on network conditions. Algorithm

displays the pseudocode for the QoS-aware clustering process based on adaptive weighted fuzzy C-means.

denotes the number of QoS features, namely delay, available buffer capacity, error rate, and remaining energy. In QQMR, the desired number of clusters is

, corresponding to the emergency cluster, the error-sensitive cluster, and the normal cluster. Each cluster includes a cluster center, denoted by
{\mathbf{v}_{k}}=[{v_{k1}},{v_{k2}},\ldots,{v_{kd}}]\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. In addition, a weighted feature vector,
{\mathbf{w}_{k}}=[{w_{k1}},{w_{k2}},\ldots,{w_{kd}}]
, is defined for each cluster. It specifies the weight of each feature component. The sum of the component weights for each cluster is equal to one, and all weights are non-negative, i.e.,
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}{{w_{kj}}}=1,\quad{w_{kj}}\geq 0
. The most important part of the WFCM-based clustering process is the fuzzy membership degrees of each node in the clusters, denoted by
{u_{ik}}\in[0,1]
. This value
u_{ik}
indicates the extent to which node
{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
belongs to cluster
{c_{k}}
. The sum of the membership degrees of each node in all clusters equals one, i.e.,
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{c}{{u_{ik}}}=1
. Meanwhile, the fuzziness parameter
m
is always greater than one and is typically set to
m=2
.

Due to the different level of importance of features in determining the proximity of each node to the clusters, a weighted distance metric is employed. For node
{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
and cluster
{c_{k}}
, this distance is computed based on Equation
14
.

D_{ik}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{kj}(x_{ij}-v_{kj})^{2}

(14)

Here, the weight
w_{kj}
expresses the importance of feature
j
(i.e.,
{x_{ij}}
) in cluster
{c_{k}}
, such that features, which are critical for a particular cluster, are assigned higher weights. The initial weight of features for each cluster is offered in Table

. Note that at each step, feature weighting for each cluster is carried out dynamically; the details of this process will be described in

The goal of the clustering algorithm is to minimize the cost function mentioned in Equation

The

(17)

6. Adaptive weighting of features

In QoS-aware clustering, each feature has a different level of importance in each cluster. For example:

1.

Emergency cluster:
Delay and available buffer capacity are highly important.

2.

Error-sensitive cluster:
The error rate is critical.

3.

Normal cluster:
The remaining energy has high importance.

Hence, the weighting of features should be in a dynamic fashion for each cluster and be proportional to the current conditions. To achieve this goal, first, the mean value of feature
j
in cluster
{c_{k}}
is obtained from Equation
18
.

{\bar{x}_{kj}}=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{u_{ik}^{m}{x_{ij}}}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{u_{ik}^{m}}}

(18)

Then, the importance degree of each feature (i.e.,
{\mu_{kj}}
) is calculated based on the average value according to Equations
19
and
20
. Depending on the type of feature, there are two cases:

Case 1

Inverse importance (for features where a lower value is more favorable, such as delay).

{\mu_{kj}}=1-\frac{{{\bar{x}}_{ki}}-\underset{j}{\mathop{\min}}\,({{\bar{x}}_{kj}})}{\underset{j}{\mathop{\max}}\,({{\bar{x}}_{kj}})-\underset{j}{\mathop{\min}}\,({{\bar{x}}_{kj}})}

(19)

Case 2

Direct importance (for features where a higher value is more desirable, such as remaining energy).

{\mu_{kj}}=\frac{{{\bar{x}}_{ki}}-\underset{j}{\mathop{\min}}\,({{\bar{x}}_{kj}})}{\underset{j}{\mathop{\max}}\,({{\bar{x}}_{kj}})-\underset{j}{\mathop{\min}}\,({{\bar{x}}_{kj}})}

(20)

The dynamic weight of feature
j
in cluster
{c_{k}}
, i.e.,
w_{kj}^{dynamic}
, is obtained through the normalization of
{\mu_{kj}}
.

w_{kj}^{dynamic}=\frac{{\mu_{kj}}}{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d}{{\mu_{kl}}}}

(21)

This normalization guarantees that
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}{w_{kj}^{dynamic}}=1
.

Then, a learning coefficient is calculated based on the initial weights (
w_{kj}^{init}
) in Table
7
, according to Equation
22
. This learning coefficient is adjusted in such a way that more important features in the initial weights have a greater influence on the final update.

{\wp_{kj}}={\wp_{min}}+\left({\wp_{max}}-{\wp_{min}}\right)w_{kj}^{init}

(22)

Such that
{\wp_{min}}=0.05
means the minimum learning coefficient, and
{\wp_{max}}=0.3
indicates the maximum learning coefficient. As a result, the new weight,
w_{kj}^{new}
, is updated as a combination of the previous weight and the dynamic weight using Equation
23
.

w_{kj}^{new}={\wp_{kj}}{w_{kj}}\left(t\right)+(1-{\wp_{kj}})w_{kj}^{dynamic}

(23)

Finally, it is necessary to normalize the new weights
w_{kj}^{new}
according to Equation
24
so that their sum does not exceed one, i.e.,
\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{kj}^{new}=1
.

w_{kj}=\frac{w_{kj}^{new}}{\sum_{l=1}^{d}w_{kl}^{new}}

(24)

Determination of main and backup paths

In QQMR, for each packet type, the main path is determined through the learning policy corresponding to its QoS requirement. In contrast, the backup path is selected from other potential paths based on the highest similarity to the main learning policy.

Main path selection

For each state (

) in learning policy

where

indicates the set of neighboring nodes in the state space
S_{p}

a
in state
s
from the policy

Backup paths are extracted from two sources:

1.

The second path with the second highest Q-value within the same learning policy

Paths with the highest Q-values in other learning policies (

{H_{3}}=\frac{{E_{i}}}{E_{i}^{\max}}

{a_{backup}}=\arg{\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}_{backup}}}}Score_{{p^{{}^{\prime}}}}(s,a)

5.4.6
Decision-making and update process

The decision-making steps executed at network nodes for each data packet with a specified QoS requirement are as follows:

1.

Selecting the state space:
Determine the QoS cluster associated with the packet and select the state (
s\in S_{p}
).

2.

Recalling Q-table:
Load Table
Q_{p}
(
{Q_{p}}-table
) corresponding to the associated learning policy.

3.

Selecting the main path:
Determine the main path based on the maximum value of
{Q_{p}}\left(s,a\right)
, i.e.,
a_{p^{*}}=\arg\max_{a\in A(S_{p})}Q_{p(s,a)}
.

4.

Extracting the backup path:
Identify the set of backup paths
{\mathcal{A}_{backup}}=\left\{a^{(2)}_{p}\right\}\cup\left\{a^{*}_{p^{{}^{\prime}}\mid p^{{}^{\prime}}\neq p}\right\}
.

5.

Calculating the matching score:
Compute
Score_{p^{{}^{\prime}}}(s,a)
for each backup path using
Score_{p^{{}^{\prime}}}\left(s,a\right)={\beta_{a}}{H_{p^{{}^{\prime}}}}+\left(1-{\beta_{a}}\right){\tilde{Q}_{p^{{}^{\prime}}}}\left(s,a\right)
.

6.

Selecting the backup path:
Select the path with the highest score as the alternative path, i.e.,
{a_{backup}}=\arg{\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}_{backup}}}}Score_{p^{{}^{\prime}}}(s,a)
.

7.

Sending packets:
Transmit the packet via the main path, and if the main path fails, the packet is sent via the backup path.

8.

Calculating the reward function:
Compute the reward based on QoS requirements and the current learning policy.

9.

Updating Q-table:
Only Table
{Q_{p}}
is updated based on the new experience.

6.2
Reasons behind selecting the baseline protocols

To review the performance of QQMR, the three protocols, including QQAR, EQRSRL, QPRR are selected as the baseline protocols. The reasons behind selecting these protocols are the same conceptual structure and considering QoS features in WBAN/IoMT environments. These schemes follow various routing paradigms:

1.

QQAR:
A Q-learning-based routing scheme with one learning policy.

2.

EQRSRL:
A reinforcement learning-based multi-class routing scheme.

3.

QPRR:
A classic QoS-aware routing scheme without reinforcement learning.

In the following, the reasons for selecting these schemes are describes:

1.

QQMR, QQAR, and EQRSRL apply the Q-learning algorithm to make routing decisions, while QPRR is a classic routing algorithm and does not use reinforcement learning algorithms.

2.

QQMR, QQAR, EQRSRL, and QPRR focus on QoS awareness in WBAN environments.

3.

QQMR, QQAR, and EQRSRL prioritize and categorize data traffic into different classes.

The selection of these baseline protocols highlight the effect of innovations of QQMR, including the design of separate learning policies, clustering the state space, and dynamic adaptability.

6.5.1
Packet delivery rate (PDR)

Figure

As illustrated in this figure, when increasing the number of WBAN users from 200 to 1000, the PDR of all compared protocols decreases gradually. This behavior is normal in dense wireless networks because the high number of WBAN users are in competition with each other to access the common channel. Hence, this increases the probability of packet collision, signal interference, buffer overflow, and packet lost. In addition, in high network densities, routing paths include more hops and are unstable. These factors decrease PDR.

However, this comparison shows that the performance of different routing schemes is not the same. QQMR shows the most stable behavior when increasing the network density. It improves PDR by 4.45%, 15.52% and 19.95% in comparison with QQAR, EQRSRL, and QPRR, respectively. In QQMR, when the node density is 200 nodes, PDR is 97%; while its PDR is 93% when the network density is 1000 nodes. Hence, it experiences 4% drop between 200 and 1000 nodes. This proves its stability, which is rooted in the multi-layer structure of QQMR. Firstly, the QoS-aware state-space clustering process decreases the decision space and prevents the interference between different learning policies. As a result, the learning process converges quickly and accurately. Secondly, the use of three independent Q-tables for different traffic classes prevents the effect of congestion of a specific data traffic class on other traffic classes. Thirdly, the adaptive queueing mechanism prevents buffer overflow when increasing network load. This decreases the number of lost packets. Finally, the backup path increases the network stability in the high network density because it continues the data forwarding process when decreasing the quality of the main path.

In contrast, QQAR well uses the Q-learning algorithm and considers QoS requirements but it applies only one learning policy and does not separate the state space. Hence, it experience a weaker performance than QQMR. It decreases PDR from 95% to 87% when the network density increases from 20 to 1000 nodes. In this scheme, a common decision-making framework manages all traffic classes. This lowers the accuracy of the path selection process and increases the interference of policies under high network densities. However, EQRSRL divides the network traffic into different classes and utilizes the reinforcement learning in the routing process but does not cluster the state space and relies on the region partitioning. As a result, it increases the number of control messages exchanges between nodes and experiences weak performance so that its PDR reaches 76%. Finally, QPRR has no learning mechanism and ignores adaptability the WBAN environment. Thus, it has weakest performance (from 93% to 72%) because it cannot quickly act in dense networks and has not adaptability to the fast changes in links and network conditions.

Conclusion

This research introduces a novel Q-learning-based QoS-aware multi-path routing method named QQMR in IoMT-based WBANs. The method combines Q-learning and QoS-aware adaptive clustering to make routing decisions dynamically and specifically. In QQMR, data are initially classified into three groups based on QoS requirements: emergency, error-sensitive, and normal. To manage heterogeneous traffic efficiently, QQMR employs an adaptive multi-level queuing model, where each node dynamically adjusts the queue capacity allocated to each packet type based on the occupied queue volume, data arrival rate, and available buffer space. Then, the state space is divided into several clusters with distinct QoS requirements using a weighted adaptive fuzzy C-means clustering algorithm. This clustering reduces learning complexity and accelerates Q-learning convergence. A specific learning policy is designed for each packet type, and the main and backup paths are determined. For performance evaluation, QQMR is compared with three baseline methods: QQAR, EQRSRL, and QPRR. The evaluation results show that under varying node density, QQMR increases the packet delivery rate by 5.45% while reducing the average end-to-end delay, routing overhead, and energy consumption by 34.79%, 11.17%, and 19.08%, respectively. Additionally, when the packet-sending rate changes, QQMR improves the packet delivery rate by 2.48% and reduces the average end-to-end delay, routing overhead, and number of hops by 35.02%, 11.73%, and 27.51%, respectively. In the future research direction, QQMR will be redesigned using deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques, including DQN, to enhance its flexibility and convergence speed. Furthermore, the state space will be clustered using other metaheuristic techniques such as particle swarm optimization (PSO) and dragonfly algorithm (DA), and the results will be presented.
