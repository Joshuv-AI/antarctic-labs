Treadmill exercise relieves cortical interneuron hyperactivation to prevent stress-induced anxiety-like behaviors in male mice
Authors: Yajie Wang, Yelin Dai, Zhenpeng Gao, Ji-an Wei, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical exercise effectively relieves anxiety disorders, and the underlying mechanisms are mostly studied in the regulation of glutamatergic transmission. The potential role of the GABAergic inhibitory pathway, however, is largely underappreciated. The current study found that chronic restraint stress (CRS) induces hyperactivation of parvalbumin (PV)-expressing interneurons (INs) in the prelimbic (PrL) region of male C57BL/6 J mice, and 14-day treadmill exercise effectively reduces PV-IN activity. Such modulation of the inhibitory transmission restores synaptic structure and functions in cortical pyramidal neurons, and chemogenetic manipulation confirmed the necessary role of PV-IN modulation in exercise-mediated anxiolytic effects. Our work broadens the knowledge of cortical inhibitory circuits in exercise intervention against mental illness and provides more targets for the neuromodulation of anxiety disorders.Subject terms: Prefrontal cortex, Anxiety

Introduction

Physical exercise is one widely accepted lifestyle intervention strategy to prevent mental disorders, including anxiety and depression1–3. The biological mechanism has also been systematically investigated, encompassing potentiation of adult neurogenesis4,5, relief of neuroinflammation6,7, attenuation of oxidative stress8,9, and modulation of cerebrovascular functions10. All these modulations of the brain microenvironment, directly or indirectly, contribute to the improvement of synaptic structural and functional integrity, leading to improvements in neural network and mental illnesses. Our group has already demonstrated various molecular mechanisms related to exercise training, including RNA methylation11, protein lactylation12, and the complement pathway13. These signaling pathways, however, primarily enhance excitatory synaptic transmission in cortical regions, especially the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)14. On the other hand, cortical network activity is further dependent on input from various cortical inhibitory interneurons (INs), and we still lack sufficient knowledge of how these cortical INs are modulated by exercise intervention schemes.

During the occurrence and progression of mental illnesses, inhibitory neural transmission plays a critical role. Studies in both depressed patients and rodent models all implicate altered structure and functions of cortical GABAergic neurons15,16. Indeed, the dynamic regulation of INs’ activity contributes to the homeostatic regulation of the excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance in cortical regions, and this E/I balance is a necessary factor for normal cortical function and prevention of mental illnesses17–20. The hyperactivity of cortical GABAergic INs is frequently associated with mental disorders, including depression21, and the inhibition of GABA INs in mPFC has thus been proposed to exert a rapid antidepressant effect18,20,22,23. A potential future perspective of antidepressant drug development is thus to develop candidate compounds for selectively and effectively modulating cortical INs to relieve their hyperactivity. Recent work in our group has already shown that exercise training attenuates hyperactivation of INs in the motor cortex, potentially via modulation of the mTOR signaling pathway24. Such a chronic training scheme may also counteract the over-pruning of synapses on parvalbumin (PV)-expressing INs in cortical regions, thereby improving cognitive disorders13. Based on this information, we thus hypothesize that exercise training may help to relieve hyperactivation of GABAergic INs in mPFC for preventing anxiety-like behaviors in chronically stressed mouse models.

To verify this hypothesis, we performed a 14-day treadmill exercise training intervention in mice with chronic restraint stress (CRS), as in our previous studies11,12,14. The effectiveness of relieving CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors was associated with the attenuation of hyperactivation of PV-INs in the prelimbic region (PrL) of mPFC. Both ex vivo and in vivo recordings confirmed that exercise training restored cortical inhibitory transmissions. Finally, chemogenetic manipulation of cortical PV-INs confirmed the necessary role of inhibiting cortical GABAergic transmission in the anxiolytic effects of exercise training. These results collectively suggest the previously underappreciated role of cortical PV-INs in exercise-mediated anxiolytic effects and provide more possibilities for neuromodulation to improve anxiety disorders.

Results

Exercise training prevents the overexpression of PV-INs in the PrL

To investigate the potential involvement of cortical PV-INs in the exercise intervention for mental illnesses, we employed the previously established treadmill exercise training protocols in chronically restrained male mice at 6 weeks of age11,12,14, when the cortical network retains relatively high plasticity. The 14-day treadmill training (1 hr per day, 10 m/min for 60 min, Fig. 1A) effectively relieved anxiety-like behaviors without significant effects on general motor behaviors, as supported by a battery of behavioral assays (Fig. 1B–G, Supplementary Fig. S1). Since treadmill exercise alone did not significantly affect behavioral phenotypes in naive (unstressed) mice (Fig. 1B–G, Supplementary Fig. S1), such an exercise training program selectively prevents CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors in mice.

To identify the critical brain region of PV-INs underlying this anxiolytic modulation, we performed the immunofluorescent labeling of both PV and Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA)-labeled perineuronal nets (PNNs). Across different cortical regions, only PrL displayed a higher proportion of PNN-enriched PV-INs, which was attenuated by exercise training, while other brain regions did not show a significant effect (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Fig. S2). To provide further evidence, the quantification of PV-INs activity using the immediate early gene (IEG) cFos was employed (Fig. 1I). Results further showed a higher number of PV-expressing cell density, as well as elevated cFos expression in PV-INs in the CRS group, which was depressed by exercise intervention (Fig. 1J, K). We also examined the other two major types of cortical INs, including somatostatin (SST) and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)-expressing neurons. These INs did not show a significant change in cFos activity upon CRS or exercise training (Supplementary Fig. S3). These data supported the involvement of PV-INs of PrL in exercise-mediated anxiolysis.

Cortical PV-INs are highly engulfed by PNNs’ structure, whose structural and functional integrity are indispensable for PV-INs’ function25,26. We thus further investigated the expression of critical genes in the formation and maintenance of PNNs. Quantitative PCR in mPFC samples indicated that only the Vcan gene showed elevated expression under CRS treatment and was downregulated by exercise training (Fig. 1L). Such patterns are consistent with PV-IN activity in PrL and are further validated at the protein level (Fig. 1M, N). Moreover, the expression of the Vcan gene was negatively correlated with individual performance on the elevated plus maze (Fig. 1O), implying the importance of PNNs in cortical-related anxiety-like behaviors in mice. To find out any causal relationships, we further injected ChABC, which is a drug tool for enzymatically digesting PNNs structure27,28, into bilateral PrL during the first week of CRS (Fig. 2A). The treatment of ChABC effectively removed PNNs structure (labeled by WFA), as well as the expression of PV in PrL regions (Fig. 2B, C). Behavioral phenotyping validated the initial hypothesis by showing that removing PNNs and PV-expressing INs prevented the anxiety-like behaviors induced by CRS, without affecting behaviors in naïve (unstressed) mice (Fig. 2D–I). These pieces of evidence collectively suggest the role of cortical PV-INs upregulation in anxiety-like behaviors.

Exercise training restores excitatory-inhibitory balance in the PrL

Having established the importance of the structural integrity of PNNs and cortical PV-INs in anxiety-like disorders, we next explored neural activity of PrL under the CRS scheme and exercise intervention. Based on the downregulation of PV by exercise intervention, we suspect that treadmill training may relieve the hyperactivity of PV-INs induced by CRS. To verify this hypothesis, we first performed ex vivo electrophysiological recordings on brain slices (Fig. 3A). The attached recording of action potentials (APs) on PV-INs in PrL confirmed their hyperactivation under the CRS scheme and the restoration of normal APs by exercise training (Fig. 3B, C). Further examinations of electrophysiological properties also showed that CRS depolarized the resting membrane potential (Fig. 3D) and amplitudes of APs of PV-INs (Fig. 3E). Meanwhile, CRS also decreased the threshold and risetime of APs of cortical PV-INs (Fig. 3F, G), which also displayed higher input resistance and lower Rheobase values (Fig. 3H, I). Exercise training, on the other hand, effectively restored these parameters (Fig. 3D–I), suggesting that CRS increased PV-IN excitability and that exercise training effectively restored it.

After studying the neuronal activity of individual PV-INs, we next investigated the population activity of these INs by in vivo 2-photon calcium imaging. The genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP6f was expressed in PV-INs of PrL using adeno-associated virus (AAV) with a PV-specific promoter (Fig. 3J, K). The recording of calcium transients from PV-IN populations (Fig. 3L, M) largely agreed with our previous findings from ex vivo electrophysiological recordings. Briefly, CRS significantly potentiated calcium activities of PV-INs, and exercise training effectively restored normal calcium transients (Fig. 3N). Such modulatory effects were mainly attributed to the peak values of single calcium events, rather than to the overall frequency of calcium transients (Fig. 3O, P). Such ex vivo and in vivo evidence illustrates the modulation of PV-INs activity by CRS and exercise training.

Exercise training restores inhibitory transmission in the PrL

Cortical PV-IN activity is critical for maintaining the E/I balance of the cortical neural network, and our previous studies have illustrated the critical role of inhibitory transmissions in cortical structure and function in the motor cortex13,24. We thus further studied whether PV-INs in PrL also contributed to local excitatory and inhibitory transmissions. Using ex vivo electrophysiological recordings from PrL pyramidal neurons (Fig. 4A), we found that exercise training did not affect amplitudes and frequencies of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) (Fig. 4B–D). On the other hand, the inhibitory transmission was significantly affected. In our patch-clamp recording assays, CRS induced higher amplitudes and a higher frequency of spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs), and 14-day exercise training both depressed amplitudes and frequencies of these sIPSCs in PrL (Fig. 4E–G). When we compare the sEPSC and sIPSC from the same recording neuron, ratio of the frequency but not the amplitude showed significant bias toward the inhibition under CRS, plus the restoration by exercise training (Fig. 4H, I). The ratio of the product of amplitude and frequency also showed similar trends of changes (Fig. 4J). These results support the restoration of cortical inhibitory transmissions by chronic exercise intervention.

In addition to dynamic change in cortical inhibitory transmissions, the structure and function of cortical spines can be modulated29,30. We employed in vivo imaging of cortical spines in Thy1-YFP transgenic mice using CRS schemes and an exercise intervention (Fig. 5A). Following previously established protocols in our group24,31, we compared the morphology of dendritic spines of PrL pyramidal neurons before and after exercise intervention (Fig. 5B). Quantitative analysis found that CRS treatment decreased the spine formation and enhanced the spine elimination, leading to below-normal spine density (Fig. 5C–E). Exercise training, on the other hand, effectively restored normal spine dynamics by elevating spine formation and preventing over-pruning (Fig. 5C–E). It is also noted that treadmill training in naïve (unstressed) mice did not significantly alter spine turnover (Fig. 5C–E), suggesting a specific effect of the exercise intervention. As further molecular evidence, we also quantified the expression of critical proteins for maintaining synaptic structures. Results indicated that CRS decreased both pre- and postsynaptic protein expression at excitatory synapses, including PSD95, Synaptotagmin 1, Synaptophysin, and SNAP25, all of which were recovered by exercise training intervention (Fig. 5F, G). Moreover, exercise training also helps to relieve the overexpression of GAD65, which is associated with presynaptic GABA release (Fig. 5F, G). In sum, CRS disrupts the normal inhibitory transmissions of PrL, and exercise training helps to restore homeostasis, probably contributing to the relief of anxiety-like behaviors.

Exercise training alleviates anxiety-like behaviors via suppressing PV-INs hyperactivation

Given these observations that exercise training reshapes inhibitory circuits in cortical regions, we next examined whether restoring inhibitory transmission is necessary for exercise-mediated anxiolysis. We first attempted to mimic exercise effects by directly alleviating the hyperactivation of cortical PV-INs in CRS mice. By expressing the inhibitory chemogenetic receptor hM4Di under the control of PV-Cre transgenic mice, plus daily injection of the specific ligand CNO (Fig. 6A, B), we successfully inhibited the activity of PV-INs in PrL (Fig. 6B–D) and restored the normal activity of excitatory neurons in CRS mice (Supplementary Fig. S4A–C). In the following behavioral phenotyping assays, this manipulation of PV-INs did not affect general motor behaviors but selectively alleviated anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice, as evidenced by a higher preference for the central area, open arms, or the light chamber (Fig. 6E–J). Therefore, the chronic inhibition of cortical PV-INs largely replicated exercise effects in alleviating anxiety-like behaviors.

Lastly, the causal relationship between PV-IN inhibition and anxiolysis was demonstrated by expressing the excitatory chemogenetic receptor hM3Dq in bilateral PrL of CRS mice during treadmill exercise (Fig. 7A, B). The daily injection of receptor ligand CNO effectively potentiated PV-INs activity, even with persistent exercise training (Fig. 7B–D). Such manipulation also abolished the activity of glutamatergic neurons in response to exercise training (Supplementary Fig. S4D–F). Behavioral assays further demonstrated that chemogenetic receptor activation ablated exercise-mediated anxiolytic effects, as evidenced by reduced place preference for the central zone, open arms, or the light chamber in mice with exercise training (Fig. 7E–J). Collectively speaking, our anatomical, physiological, and behavioral results support the role of PV-INs hyperactivation in CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors, and exercise training prevents anxiety-like behaviors via suppressing PV-INs for restoring cortical inhibitory transmissions.

Discussion

In the current study, we identified the previously underappreciated role of cortical PV-INs in exercise-mediated effects in preventing anxiety-like behaviors. Although amounts of evidence have indicated the dysregulation of cortical GABAergic neurons in the pathogenesis of depression and anxiety11,32, and the inhibition of GABAergic transmission was recognized as one potentially rapid antidepressant strategy22,23, we still lack sufficient knowledge of these inhibitory neurons in exercise intervention. A recent study in a mouse depression model reported the improvement of PV-INs morphology and gene expression by treadmill training, but did not provide the causal relationship between PV-INs and antidepressants33. Our work provides in vivo evidence of the restoration of normal neuronal activity in PV-INs in PrL following a 14-day treadmill exercise intervention, along with a chemogenetic manipulation assay demonstrating exercise-mediated PV-IN anxiolysis. These findings largely align with our previous work on the mouse motor cortex, in which exercise training reduces hyperactivation of SST-INs24. Such findings, in conjunction with our recent reports on the potentiation of thalamic inputs to PrL pyramidal neurons14, further our understanding of how exercise intervention, through homeostatic regulation of cortical microcircuits, counters environmental stress.

In mental disorders, including depression and anxiety, cortical GABAergic INs present structural and functional deficits15. In human MDD patients, postmortem studies reported decreased GABA biosynthesis in the PFC34, and functional imaging studies also suggested cortical inhibitory deficits35. In a study using depressive patient-derived GABAergic INs, hyperactivity of these INs was also found21. In the rodent model, transcriptomic studies revealed that GABAergic neurons contribute to neural network activity and stress resilience36. These observations further suggest the possibility of targeting GABAergic transmission in relieving mental illnesses. In rodent models, a recent study reported a rapid antidepressant effect from drug-mediated inhibition of GABAergic INs in cortical regions22. The rapid antidepressant effect of ketamine was also believed to be triggered by its inhibition of cortical INs23. Detailed molecular mechanisms were later found to modulate cell type-specific translational regulation20. Our current data show dysregulation of PV-INs in CRS-treated mice, consistent with previous findings in both human patients and rodent models. Moreover, the targeted regulation of PV-INs by exercise training further underscores its potency as a rapid antidepressant approach, as reported in a recent study37.

Among major subtypes of cortical INs, PV-INs are highly related to mental disorders. In single-cell transcriptomics profiling of human brain samples, the vast majority of differentially regulated genes were enriched in the PV-related pathway38. In rodent models, numerous studies have suggested that stress disrupts PV neurons in mood disorders39. For example, neuroinflammation-induced PV-INs dysfunction and related deficits of oscillation are found in a model of depression40. Our study utilized an in vivo calcium imaging and found potentiation of calcium transients under CRS, as well as exercise-mediated recovery of normal calcium events. These observations, along with the elevated membrane excitability and AP firing frequency of PV-INs in the CRS group, illustrate the functional deficits of these GABAergic neurons and support previous findings of impaired fast-spiking IN function in a mouse depression model41. Since a recent study reported that treadmill exercise improved PV-IN dendritic complexity in a depression mouse model33, our work further provides functional evidence for the recovery of cortical inhibitory transmissions by exercise intervention. In the field of mental illness treatment, the relief of PV-INs hyperactivation was proposed to exert an antidepressant effect. Ketamine, for example, was found to exert a rapid antidepressant effect via downregulating PV expression42. These findings thus imply the possibility of neuromodulation in targeting cortical INs for alleviating mental disorders.

Besides PV-INs, the other major subtype of cortical INs, SST-INs, have been shown to exhibit functional deficits in MDD patients and in rodent depression models16, and our previous work has demonstrated that exercise training regulates cortical SST-INs24. Recent progress has suggested the potency of SST-INs as the new target for treating depression16,43. For the VIP-INs in the cortex, although no direct evidence has suggested their relationship with depression or anxiety, there are some rodent studies indicating the necessary role of cortical VIP-INs in normal control of impulsivity44 or avoidance behaviors45. In this work, we did not find significant changes in neuronal activity in SST-INs or VIP-INs in the PrL following either CRS or exercise training. Future work, however, can still be pursued to study the more sophisticated regulation of IN subtypes by exercise intervention.

In the current study, we also used ChABC to degrade PNNs and relieve the hyperactivation of PV-INs. PNNs are critical for the structural and functional integrity of PV-INs, and cross-species evidence indicates that PNN remodeling is involved in stress and depression46. Therefore, targeting PNN might be a promising strategy for attenuating mental disorders47. For example, the reduction of PNNs structure has been reported to effectively relieve high-fat diet-induced depression48. The antidepressant effect of electroacupuncture is proposed to involve remodeling of PNNs and, consequently, inhibiting PV-INs in the mPFC49. Our results are similar to those of ChABC-mediated cortical-specific removal of PNNs, which effectively prevented anxiety-like behaviors in CRS mice. Since exercise training exerts similar effects in eliminating excess growth of PNNs and hyperactivation of PV-INs, it is postulated that specific molecular mechanisms may link exercise intervention to the structural remodeling of PNNs, thereby helping restore normal PV-INs activity.

The molecular mechanism by which exercise training relieves PV-INs hyperactivity is an interesting question that was not studied in the current work. Our previous work indicated activation of the mTOR pathway by persistent exercise training31, and subsequent studies validated the necessary role of neuron-autonomous mTOR activation in maintaining synaptic integrity24. In a recent report, constitutive activation of the neocortical mTOR pathway, induced by PTEN inhibition, leads to hyperactivation of PNNs and surrounding PV-INs50. Such seemingly contradictory results can be explained by the homeostatic regulation of the mTOR pathway, whose insufficiency or overactivation can lead to tissue abnormalities. On the other hand, the rapid antidepressant ketamine, like exercise training, also activated the brain mTOR pathway for stimulating synaptogenesis51. Since conditional loss of TSC, another mTOR inhibitor, facilitates PV expression and fast-spiking properties, we suspect that modulation of the mTOR pathway may play a crucial role in regulating the homeostasis of PNNs and PV-INs during exercise intervention.

This study has certain limitations and weaknesses. First, the entire study was performed only in male mice. Although our previous works did provide evidence showing that treadmill exercise training also effectively relieves CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors in adult female mice12,14, it is still possible that a sex-dependent mechanism may exist. Given reported human brain transcriptomic data showing higher abundance of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in females compared to males52, it is likely that our current model of PV-INs also applies to female individuals. Second, the underlying mechanisms of the highly specific effects of both CRS and exercise on PV-INs in the mPFC are not comprehensively studied here. Indeed, the existing literature, such as that on ketamine’s antidepressant effect, indicates the central roles of PV-INs’ function15,20,23,42 and of ECM structural homeostasis47. These works, along with our previous findings of molecular pathways, such as mTOR signaling, under chronic exercise training24,31, raise the possibility that PV-INs selectively respond to molecular signaling elicited by CRS or exercise. Further studies can thus be pursued to illustrate the highly responsive “exercise-related neuronal ensemble” in the cortex. Lastly, the modulatory effect on mPFC may vary across sub-regions, as mPFC neurons project to various cortical and subcortical nuclei. In our previous studies, exercise training can potentiate the excitability of VM-projecting neurons14 while inhibiting those mPFC neurons that innervate amygdala nuclei53. Therefore, PV-INs may also respond differentially to our exercise intervention, which was reported to increase mPFC PV expression in a mouse model of chronically unpredictable stress (CUS)33.

In sum, our anatomical, physiological, and behavioral data support the hyperactivation of PV-INs in the PrL during CRS, contributing to anxiety-like behaviors. Chronic treadmill exercise training, on the other hand, effectively recovers normal structural and functional integrity of cortical PV-INs for preventing anxiety disorders. Such a model broadens our understanding of how exercise training reshapes cortical inhibitory transmission for relieving mental illness.

Methods

Animals

Guangdong Jicui Yaokang Biological Co., Ltd. supplied the majority of the male wild-type mice (C57BL/6 N) used in the experiment. Male Thy1-YFP transgenic mice at 4 weeks of age and male PV-Cre mice at 6 weeks of age were initially sourced from the Jackson Laboratory and subsequently maintained in-house through breeding. The mice had unrestricted access to enough food and water, and all experimental animals were kept in groups. The mice were housed in an environment with 12 hours of light (8:00–20:00) and 12 hours of darkness (20:00–8:00), with the temperature controlled at 24 ± 2 °C and the humidity adjusted at 50 ± 10%. All experimental methods and stages were completed in accordance with the Jinan University Animal Guidelines for animal research, and all experimental animals were in conformity with the management regulations of the Jinan University Animal Care and Use Committee.

Chronic stress and exercise

Mice were placed in a plastic piping bag with the tip removed to ensure regular respiration while confining their limbs, thereby inhibiting unrestricted mobility for 3 hours each day. This constraint was maintained for 14 consecutive days. The mice were watched at hourly intervals throughout the restraint period. For the exercise intervention, mice were placed on a treadmill at 8 m/min. After acclimation, the velocity was sustained at 10 m/min. The experimental subjects underwent a daily 1-hour exercise regimen for 14 days. On a stationary treadmill, the control mice were allowed to move freely for an hour. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All animal protocols have been pre-approved by the animal ethical committee of Jinan University in accordance with relevant regulations.

Behavioral assays

Open field test

The experimental configuration included four identical rectangular chambers (50 × 50 × 50 cm), a camera, a 25 W incandescent lamp, and computer-based analysis software. Before each test session, mice were given a 30-minute adaptation period in the experimental environment. Once the trial began, the mouse was placed at the center of the chamber, and the software was activated to record locomotion paths. Each session lasted 5 minutes, and the data were stored for subsequent analysis. Recorded pathways were saved, and locomotor activity was evaluated using EthoVision 7.0 software.

Elevated plus maze test

The elevated plus maze apparatus is located about 75 cm above the floor. It includes two open arms (30 cm long and 5 cm wide), two enclosed arms of identical size, and a central square zone measuring 5 × 5 cm. To begin each trial, a mouse is gently placed in the center of the maze and given 5 minutes of unrestricted exploration. Following this period, the time spent and the path length covered within both the open and enclosed arms are measured and examined.

Light–dark box test

The apparatus comprised a rectangular Plexiglas box (48 × 24 × 24 cm) divided into equal light and dark compartments connected by an opening. The dark compartment, made of black acrylic with a lid, provided a dark environment, while the light compartment, made of white acrylic, was illuminated from above. During trials, mice were placed in the light area and allowed to explore freely for 5 minutes. Behavior was video-recorded, and transitions between compartments, along with time spent in each, were quantified for analysis.

To minimize potentially confounding effect, all behavioral assays follow the same sequence and time interval, and no specification of cage location was designed.

Genotype identification

Ear tissue samples from young mice were incubated in lysis buffer containing proteinase K at 55 °C for 3 hours. Subsequently, 300 μL of phenol-chloroform mixture was added to each tube. After thorough mixing, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 20 minutes. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, mixed with an equal volume of ice-cold absolute ethanol, and incubated at 4 °C for 10 minutes to precipitate DNA. The mixture was then centrifuged again under the same conditions (12,000 rpm, 4 °C, 20 minutes). The supernatant was discarded, and the DNA pellet was collected and dried in a 37 °C oven. The dried pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of sterile double-distilled water and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

PCR amplification was performed using a hot-start Taq DNA polymerase system with the following primer sequences: mouse PV-Cre forward, 5′-AAATGCTTCTGTCCGTTTGC-3′; reverse, 5′-ATGTTTAGCTGGCCCAAATG-3′; mouse Thy1-YFP forward, 5′-ACAGACACACACCCAGGACA-3′; reverse, 5′-CGGTGGTGCAGATGAACTT-3′. PCR products were mixed with 10× loading buffer and separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel. The gel was then visualized and analyzed using a gel imaging system.

Virus injection

Animals were administered 3% tribromoethanol to achieve complete surgical anesthesia. Once anesthetized, each subject was securely positioned within a stereotactic instrument. The cranial hair was carefully removed, and the surgical site was cleansed with disinfectant, allowing for clear visualization of the skull. Stereotaxic coordinates corresponding to the prefrontal cortex were then identified as follows:AP: 2.33 mm; ML: ±0.1 mm; DV: −1.2 mm. Viral delivery was performed using glass micropipettes connected to a microsyringe pump, with injection timing precisely controlled by a microinjection controller. A stereotaxic craniotomy was first performed above the target brain region to allow accurate positioning of the virus-loaded glass electrode at the intended site. The virus was infused slowly at a rate of 50 nL/min. The injection volume at each injection side is 150 nl, with bilateral injections. After injection, the pipette was left in place for 5 min to facilitate adequate diffusion of the viral particles. Following the surgical procedure, mice were placed on a heating pad until they regained consciousness and then transferred to a clean housing cage for postoperative recovery. Subsequent experiments were conducted only after full recovery. The details of virus used were tabulated in Table 1.

Key resources and reagents

Microinjection

chABC was injected into the PrL(AP: 2.33 mm; ML: ± 0.1 mm; DV: −1.2 mm) brain of mice. The chABC was infused slowly at a rate of 170 nL/min. The virus injection volume at each injection point is 500 nl. It is usually a bilateral injection; that is, a mouse is injected with 1000 nl of virus. Due to the enzyme’s susceptibility to degradation, an identical dose of chABC was reinjected at the same site one week after the initial administration.

Intraperitoneal injection

During the 14-day chronic stress (CRS) modeling period, a daily intraperitoneal injection of Clozapine N-oxide dihydrochloride (CNO) (2 mg/kg) was administered. Behavioral tests were conducted following the completion of the modeling protocol.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRI Reagent (BBI). To evaluate transcript levels of target genes, equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA employing the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (TaKaRa). The synthesized cDNA was then amplified using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa) along with sequence-specific primers in a quantitative real-time PCR assay. The mRNA expression of the housekeeping gene GAPDH was concurrently amplified as an internal control for normalization. All qPCR reactions were performed on a CFX384 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Western blotting

Quantified protein samples were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane using a wet transfer method. The membrane was then precisely trimmed according to the target protein’s expected molecular weight, as indicated by a pre-stained protein marker. For blocking, the membrane was incubated with 5% BSA at room temperature for 2 hours. After blocking, the membrane was probed with a specific primary antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Following extensive washing with TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with an appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. After a final series of TBST washes, protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and captured with an imaging system.

Immunofluorescent staining

Following anesthesia induced by 3% tribromoethanol, mice were transcardially perfused with 20 mL of normal saline, followed by 20 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were then carefully extracted and post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 hours. Subsequently, tissues were cryoprotected by immersion in a 30% sucrose solution prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and maintained at 4 °C for 48 hours. Coronary sections of the target brain regions were obtained at a thickness of 40 μm using a cryostat pre-cooled to −24 °C. All sections were stored in PBS at 4 °C prior to use. For immunostaining, brain slices were first incubated in a blocking buffer containing PBS supplemented with 10% CDC blocking agent and 3% Tween X-100 for 2 hours at room temperature.

The sections were then incubated with the primary antibody solution for 48 hours at 4 °C. After incubation, they were rinsed five times with PBS (8 minutes per wash). Subsequently, a 2-hour incubation with the secondary antibody solution was performed at room temperature under light-protected conditions. After secondary antibody incubation, the brain slices were washed three times with PBS (8 minutes each). Finally, the sections were mounted, and fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope. The details of antibody used were tabulated in Table 1.

In vivo 2-photon calcium image

Three weeks after viral expression, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 3% tribromoethanol. The skull surface was exposed and cleaned of connective tissue using 75% ethanol. A metal ring plate was attached to the skull over the target cortical area using biocompatible adhesive and further secured with a mixture of dental cement and 502 glue. Subsequently, a circular craniotomy ~2 mm in diameter was created by removing the skull and dura mater above the target region, which was then covered with a round glass coverslip sealed at the edges with tissue adhesive. After recovery from anesthesia, head-fixed mice were acclimated on the microscope stage for 30 minutes. In vivo imaging was performed with 920 nm excitation and a ×20 water-immersion objective. Neuronal somatic activity was recorded at 2 Hz for 300 consecutive frames. Image processing and registration were performed using ImageJ. Neuronal somata were identified, and signals were extracted using a region-of-interest (ROI) approach, excluding neurons with saturated or undetectable signals. Fluorescence traces were normalized as ΔF/F0 = (F–F0)/F0 × 100%, where F0 represents the average minimum fluorescence during the recording period. For each neuron, the mean calcium peak amplitude, total calcium integral, number of significant calcium transients, and the ratio of transient count to total recording duration were calculated.

Electrophysiology recording

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and swiftly perfused through the heart at 4 °C. Following rapid decapitation, the brain was promptly extracted and immersed in ice-cold, oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF, composition in mM: 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO₄, 10 glucose, 26 NaHCO₃, 2.4 CaCl2, 1.2 MgCl₂, pH 7.4), continuously bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. Coronal brain slices (300 μm thick) containing the target region were prepared using a vibrating microtome under cold conditions. Slices were initially incubated in oxygenated aCSF at 33.6 °C for 30 min, then transferred to room-temperature aCSF for further recovery. For the recording of EPSCs and IPSCs, the internal solution was prepared as follows in aCSF in mM: 125 Cesium Methanesulfonate, 10 TEA-Cl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 1.7 QX314-Cl, 10 Na2-Phosphocreatine, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, pH 7.32. The sEPSC and sIPSC were recorded from the same pyramidal neuron.

To assess neuronal excitability, a series of depolarizing current steps (from −90 pA to 450 pA in 30 pA increments, 1000 ms duration) was injected into neurons in current-clamp mode via glass microelectrodes filled with potassium gluconate-based internal solution (in aCSF in mM: 130 K-Gluconic acid, 10 KCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 10 Na2-Phosphocreatine, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, pH 7.25). In the current work, we mainly discriminate pyramidal neurons from GABAergic neurons based on both the spatial distribution and the morphological features. In brief, we performed whole-cell patch recording from layer 5 of the PrL, a region that mainly consists of pyramidal neurons. Second, the typical shape of soma and apical dendrites can be identified from the bright-field view of microscope before patching cells.

Dendritic spine imaging

Thy1-YFP mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% tribromoethanol. After shaving the scalp and applying ophthalmic ointment to protect the eyes, the skull was exposed and cleaned with 75% ethanol to remove superficial tissue. A double-edged blade was attached above the PrL under a stereomicroscope to guide the craniotomy. A thin circular cranial window (0.5 mm in diameter) was created using a high-speed surgical drill, followed by fine trimming with a microknife. The mouse was then head-fixed on a two-photon microscope stage. Dendritic spines were imaged with a Zeiss LSM780 two-photon microscope equipped with a ×20 water-immersion objective. After imaging, the head plate was removed, residual adhesive was cleared, and the scalp was sutured with surgical thread. Z-stack images were acquired at a depth of 80–120 μm with a step size of 1.1 μm. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software. For each group, 100–150 dendritic spines were selected for quantitative analysis. A “lost spine” was defined as a structure present in the first imaging session but absent in the second; a “newly formed spine” referred to a spine that appeared in the second session but was not detected in the first. Spine density was calculated as the number of spines per micrometer of dendritic length.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data were derived from toxicological experimental animals. Statistical analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 7.0). Sample size was determined based on similar studies from our group and others. Data is presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For datasets following a normal distribution, a paired Student’s t test was applied for two-group comparisons, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons across more than two groups. For data that did not conform to normality, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. A probability (P) value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. No exclusion of animals or samples was carried out. No systemic randomization was performed. However, the investigator during data analysis was blinded to the grouping.
