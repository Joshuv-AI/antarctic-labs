Comparing Ibuprofen and Loop Diuretics for Protein-Bound Uremic Toxins Clearance During Hemodialysis
Authors: Víctor Joaquín Escudero-Saiz, Elena Cuadrado-Payán, María Rodríguez-García, Gregori Casals, Lida María Rodas, Néstor Fontseré, María del Carmen Salgado, Carla Bastida, Nayra Rico, José Jesús Broseta, Francisco Maduell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionProtein-bound uremic toxins (PBUTs), such as indoxyl sulphate (IS) and p-cresyl sulphate (pCS) are associated with cardiovascular mortality in patients undergoing hemodialysis. Because of their high albumin affinity, conventional dialysis techniques achieve poor clearance. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of different competitive binding displacement strategies using ibuprofen and loop diuretics during online hemodiafiltration (OL-HDF).MethodsA longitudinal, cross-over study in 12 patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis was performed. Each patient underwent 6 different dialysis modalities based on postdilutional HDF. The experimental arms included the following: (i) Standard postdilutional HDF (control), (ii) High-flux hemodialysis (HF-HD) (iii) Oral furosemide 1h predialysis, (iv) Oral torasemide 1h predialysis, (v) 1h arterial infusion of furosemide, and (vi) 1h arterial infusion of ibuprofen. Reduction ratios (RR) for IS and pCS were calculated for each session. Statistical analysis was performed using 1-way repeated measures analysis of variance with Bonferroni post hoc correction.ResultsArterial administration of ibuprofen was the only strategy that significantly enhanced PBUT removal, achieving mean RR of 65% for IS and 60% for pCS (P < 0.05 vs. baseline OL-HDF). In contrast, neither oral nor arterial administration of loop diuretics showed a statistically significant increase in the RR of IS or pCS when compared with OL-HDF. No acute adverse events were reported during the pharmacological interventions.ConclusionPrefilter arterial infusion of ibuprofen is the most effective strategy for increasing the intradialytic clearance of IS and pCS during OL-HDF. The lack of efficacy observed with furosemide suggests a ceiling effect in high-volume HDF, where only high-affinity competitors can further displace PBUTs from albumin-binding sites. Pharmacological displacement represents a promising adjunct to convective therapies to optimize protein-bound uremic toxin removal.Keywords: albumin binding sites, competitive displacement, dialytic clearance, indoxyl sulphate, p-cresyl sulphate, protein-bound uremic toxins

Uremic toxins are classified according to their physicochemical characteristics and purification capacity according to conventional hemodialysis techniques, following the current classification of the European Uremic Toxins Working Group1 with 3 well-known major groups as follows: free water-soluble low-molecular-weight molecules (MW < 0.5 kDa, with urea as a prototype molecule), middle molecules (MW: 0.5–60 kDa, with β2 microglobulin as a prototype molecule), and PBUTs.2 PBUTs are a heterogenous group of uremic toxins with high affinity for albumin.1 Among them, IS and pCS are the most studied.3 These toxins are generated through an initial metabolism by the gut microbiota, followed by hepatic metabolism.4 They are considered toxic for different systems,5 highlighting their toxicity to the endothelium6 and therefore the cardiovascular system.3,5 For instance, pCS is considered the most toxic uremic toxin known.1 Observational studies have related IS and pCS to cardiovascular mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing hemodialysis7, 8, 9 and in patients with nondialysis chronic kidney disease.10,11

Because of their high albumin affinity, their clearance with conventional hemodialysis techniques is limited instead of the low-molecular weight (MW) of the free-fraction.12 IS and pCS show albumin affinities of 95% for albumin binding site Sudlow II.13 Nowadays, there are some upcoming strategies for enhancing PBUT clearance during hemodialysis—the use of adsorptive membranes, competitive molecules for albumin binding sites, and the combination of both.3

Displacing agents stand as the most available option in the clinical setting14 but data in humans is scarce. Several molecules have been tested in preclinical models, such as tryptophan, furosemide, ibuprofen,15 long chain fatty acids,16 medium chain fatty acids,17 and lipid emulsion18 with positive results in increasing the free-fraction of different PBUT; but only loop diuretics (furosemide and torasemide) and ibuprofen have been tested with improvement in IS and pCS clearance in patients undergoing hemodialysis.19,20

In a previous study by our research group, we evaluated the most efficient administration route of a displacer molecule to enhance the clearance IS and pCS, concluding that prefilter administration was the most effective one.21 Building on those findings, the primary objective of this study is to compare the main competitive displacement strategies evaluated in clinical trials to date—ibuprofen19 and loop diuretics.20 Secondarily, we aim to investigate whether the displacing efficacy of loop diuretics depends on their specific route of administration, as evaluated previously with ibuprofen.

Methods

Patients

This prospective, single-center study included adult (> 18 years old) patients on maintained OL-HDF prescription for > 3 mo with stable clinical condition. Exclusion criteria were incident patients (< 3 mo on treatment); presence of residual renal function (urine output > 100 ml/24 h); previous history of gastrointestinal bleeding; known adverse reaction to ibuprofen, furosemide, or torasemide; and chronic treatment with any PBUT displacer (loop diuretics, tryptophan, salvianolic acids, or i.v. lipid emulsions). The study protocol was evaluated and approved by the institutional ethics committee of Hospital Clinic of Barcelona (HCB/2024/0173) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment.

Study Design

The study followed a prospective, single-center, crossover design. Each patient underwent 6 different dialysis sessions and technical conditions (Figure 1). A standard OL-HDF session served as the baseline control followed by a HF-HD session. Subsequently, 4 experimental pharmacological interventions were implemented in a randomized crossover design during OL-HDF session, as follows: (i) oral administration of 40 mg of furosemide 60 minutes before the session; (ii) oral administration of 20 mg of torasemide 60 minutes before the session; (iii) a continuous infusion of 800 mg of ibuprofen into the arterial line of the extracorporeal circuit over a 1-hour period; and (iv) a continuous infusion of 40 mg of furosemide into the arterial line of the extracorporeal circuit over 1-hour period. To eliminate potential confounding variables related to the interdialytic interval, a 1-week washout period was strictly maintained between interventions; consequently, all experimental sessions were conducted on the same day of the week for each patient. The sequence of the pharmacological interventions was assigned randomly to each participant to minimize order effects.

Methodological schematic of the study. Flowchart displaying the six parallel technical conditions and pharmacological interventions evaluated in a completely randomized crossover sequence, illustrating the overall study design. HF-HD, high-flux hemodialysis; OL-HDF, online hemodiafiltration.

The prescribed dialysis parameters were postdilution HDF, except from HF-HD arm, bicarbonate buffer, dialysate flow (Qd) 400 ml/min, blood flow (Qb) 450 ml/min, and dialysis time 295 ± 12 min (range 270–300). Dialyzer membranes were 4 FX CorDiax 60 (33.3%; Fresenius, Bad Homburg, Germany), 4 Clearum HS (33.3%; Bellco S.r.l., Mirandola, Italy) and 4 CorAL 60 (33.3%; Fresenius). Net fluid removal was set individually, depending on the patient’s clinical needs. Fresenius 5008 CorDiax or 6008 CAREsystem dialysis monitors were used. The variables collected in each session were as follows: real duration, dialyzer, Qb, recirculation index estimated by the temperature module, arterial, venous, and transmembrane pressures, initial and final hematocrit automatically estimated by the BVM biosensor (Fresenius), initial and final body weights, volume of blood processed, and replacement volume.

Pharmacological i.v. interventions were prepared as follows: 800 mg of ibuprofen or 40 mg of furosemide were diluted in 250 ml of NaCl 0.9%, and they were administered during 60 minutes in the arterial line of the extracorporeal circuit.

Research Instrument

Blood samples for analyses were taken from each patient in the same dialysis session of the week. Laboratory measurements included concentrations of urea (MW 60), creatinine (MW 113), ß2-microglobulin (MW 11,800), myoglobin (MW 17,200), kappa-free Ig light chains (κFLC, MW 22,500), prolactin (MW 23,000), α1-microglobulin (MW 33,000), α1-acid glycoprotein (MW 41,000), lambda-free Ig light chains (λFLC, MW 45,000), and albumin (MW 66,000) in serum at the beginning and at the end of each session to calculate the RR of these solutes. PBUTs, pCS (MW 108) and IS (MW 213), were also evaluated. The final concentrations of ß2-microglobulin, myoglobin, κFLC, prolactin, α1-microglobulin, α1-acid glycoprotein, λFLC, pCS, IS, and albumin were corrected for the degree of hemoconcentration and the volume of distribution (approximate extracellular volume) according to Bergström and Wehle.22

Urea and creatinine were measured by molecular absorption spectrometry, albumin and β2-microglobulin were measured by immunoturbidimetry, and myoglobin and prolactin were measured by indirect enzyme immunoassay; all of them were performed in the Atellica Solution analyser (Siemens Healthineers, Tarrytown, NY). Total IS and total pCS were measured in serum using liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry explained in a previous work from our research group.23 Finally, α1-microglobuline, α1-acid glycoprotein, κFLC, and λFLC were measured by immunonephelometry using the Behring Nephelometer II analyser (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Statistical Analysis

The results are expressed as the arithmetic mean ± SD. For the analysis of the statistical significance of quantitative parameters, the Student t test for paired data and analysis of variance for repeated data followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc comparisons tests were used for the parametric data. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS software version 23 (IBM, Armonk, NY), and graphics were performed using Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 12 patients were included with a mean age of 72.9 ± 16.7 years (41.6% women; range 33–85) on a regular hemodialysis program for 49.2 ± 39.2 mo (range 7–139). Vascular access were 11 native arterio-venous fistula (91.7%) and 1 tunneled central venous catheter (8.3%). The anticoagulation used was low-MW heparin in 7 patients (58.3%) and heparin sodium in 5 patients (41.7%). Underlying renal diseases were nephroangiosclerosis (5 patients), chronic glomerulonephritis (2 patients) systemic diseases (2 patients) unknown etiology (2 patients), and urologic etiology (1 patient).

PBUTs

Pre- and postdialysis IS and pCS values are shown in Table 3, as well as their RR (Figure 3). Baseline RR was 56.9 ± 8.1 % and 51.2 ± 8.4 % for IS and pCS in OL-HDF, respectively. The lowest RR observed was seen with HF-HD with 49.4 ± 7.9 % and 42.3 ± 8.9 % for IS and pCS, respectively. All OL-HDF were statistically superior to HF-HD for IS RR and for pCS RR (Table 4). These differences were statistically superior to the rest of dialysis sessions (Figure 3). Discussion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that the intradialytic clearance of IS and pCS is significantly enhanced by the administration of ibuprofen in the arterial line of the extracorporeal circuit reaching RR of 65% and 60% respectively. Notably, this was the only competitive displacement strategy that remained statistically superior across the 6 dialysis modalities tested. Besides, the oral administration of predialysis loop diuretics did not enhance the clearance of such molecules, nor did the administration of furosemide into the arterial line of the circuit.

PBUTs are considered among the most toxic uremic toxins affecting up to 7 different systems according to the European Uremic Toxin Working Group1 with pCS being regarded as the most toxic uremic toxin known.5 Furthermore, IS and pCS are considered endothelial toxins6,25 and they have been related to an increased risk of cardiovascular events and cardiovascular mortality in patients undergoing hemodialysis7, 8, 9 and with nondialysis chronic kidney disease.10,11 PBUTs exhibit high affinity for Sudlow binding sites I and II of albumin, which is approximately 95% for IS and pCS for Sudlow binding site II.13,26 This makes their removal during HF-HD,27,28 expanded-hemodialysis,29 and OL-HDF27,30 to be less than 50% to 55%. In fact, time-extended hemodialysis has shown the highest RR in clinical scenarios with 66% for IS and 59% for pCS with HF-HD with high mass-transfer area coefficient (KoA) membranes31 and 60% and 52% for IS and pCS respectively, with OL-HDF.27

Because of their known toxicity and their poor intradialytic clearance, some strategies to improve their removal are under development.3 Among these, the use of molecules which bind and compete to the same albumin-site as PBUTs are the most available.14 Different molecules have been tested in preclinical models, but there is only clinical information in the use of loop diuretics and ibuprofen in patients undergoing hemodialysis.19,20 The first published work by Madero et al.19 showed that the administration in the arterial line of 800 mg of ibuprofen enhanced the intradialytic clearance of IS and pCS up to 20.2 ml/min and 14.9 ml/min, respectively, but not when the infusion stopped.19 Moreover, a Spanish group showed that predialysis oral administration of different loop diuretics (furosemide and torasemide) enhanced IS and pCS reduction percentage until 59.1% and 66.3%, respectively, with furosemide, and 58.8% and 61.8% with torasemide during HF-HD.20

This study with different PBUT competitive agents shows that the arterial prefilter administration of ibuprofen is the most effective strategy in enhancing PBUT removal during OL-HDF, increasing the RR of IS from 56.9% to 64.9% and that of pCS from 42.3% to 60.7%. Moreover, all the OL-HDF sessions reached statistical superiority compared with HF-HD. The lack of significant enhancement in IS and pCS RR following loop diuretics administration might be explained for different reasons.

First, ibuprofen presents higher affinity for Sudlow site II compared with furosemide (Ka 2.4-2.7 x 106 M-1)32 and also furosemide binds predominantly to albumin at Sudlow site I (Ka 1.6 x 105 M-1).33 Similar results were observed in an in vitro static model where ibuprofen increased the most the free fraction of pCS and IS compared with furosemide or tryptophan.15 This higher albumin affinity of ibuprofen compared with furosemide might importantly explain the superior effect observed in our work.

Second, the discrepancy between our results regarding loop diuretics and those reported by Sánchez-Ospina et al.20 may be attributed to the baseline dialysis modality. In this work, high-volume OL-HDF with higher blood-flow and dialysis time is used compared with HF-HD performed by Sánchez-Ospina et al.20 (450 ml/min vs. 300 ml/min and 289.4 ± 0.9 min vs. 240 min, without available data of mean dialysis dose). These higher diffusive variables within the slightly increasing effect of convection in PBUTs removal12 achieve a near-maximal removal creating a ceiling effect and masking the displacement effect of loop diuretics. In this scenario, high-affinity competitors could further increase IS and pCS free fraction translating significant clearance enhancement during OL-HDF. The increasing effect of loop diuretics may be present in HF-HD or shorter and/or with lower blood-flow OL-HDF sessions but further studies with this dialysis characteristics are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Finally, another point that could contribute to the lack of effect of loop diuretics might be the insufficient dose to reach the desirable effect. In a previous work from our group, half-doses of ibuprofen did not reach significant effect in enhancing IS and pCS removal,21 suggesting a directly relationship between the displacer dose and its displacement power. Furthermore, during prefilter arterial loop diuretics infusion, a substantial fraction of the drug might undergo rapid dialytic clearance through the dialyzer membrane. This immediate removal, combined with its lower displacement potency for Sudlow site-II-PBUT, likely prevented loop diuretics from reaching the critical concentration required to effectively compete and displace IS and pCS during OL-HDF. Future studies are warranted to evaluate higher doses of furosemide administered via the prefilter arterial line and their potential effect on IS and pCS clearance during OL-HDF. Additionally, the potential role of lower ibuprofen doses in HF-HD, or even the combination of both displacers, remains to be investigated in patients undergoing hemodialysis; however, these currently remain open questions.

PBUTs are eliminated primarily via diffusive mechanisms owing to their low MW.12 OL-HDF enhances PBUT clearance predominantly because of the discrete increase it provides in small solute clearance, rather than through a direct convective drag effect,34 a phenomenon previously documented by Abad et al.35 Another aspect to highlight from this work is that all OL-HDF were superior to HF-HD control not only in IS and pCS removal but in all measured molecules, according to previous works36,37 and within the results of previous clinical trials with high-volume OL-HDF improving surveillance in patients on hemodialysis.38,39

Regarding other PBUT displacing agents, competitive displacement has been demonstrated in in vitro and animal models using tryptophan,15 long-chain fatty acids such as oleic and linoleic acids,18 and, more recently, medium-chain fatty acids including octanoate, decanoate, and dodecanoate.17 Notably, the use of octanoate yielded positive results for IS and pCS clearance in an in vitro dynamic dialysis model with an increased fractional removal of 2.3-fold and 2.5-fold, respectively.17 Interestingly, medium-chain fatty acids are more hydrophilic than long-chain fatty acids, thereby minimizing the associated thrombogenic risk, while offering a superior safety profile with a lower potential to exacerbate dyslipidemia.40 Indeed, medium-chain fatty acids have been shown to induce less inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and steatosis in human liver cell models than long-chain fatty acids.41,42 Consequently, they represent an appealing therapeutic strategy with a dual beneficial effect—enhancing PBUT removal through competitive displacement while simultaneously exerting favorable metabolic effects. Nevertheless, clinical studies in patients on hemodialysis are strictly warranted to confirm these in vivo effects. Currently, an ongoing clinical trial is actively recruiting to evaluate the safety and efficacy of Medialipid 20% (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany), an emulsion containing 94% sodium octanoate and decanoate used in parenteral nutrition (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06595680).

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, the use of a cross-over design—where every patient served as their own control—provides high statistical power to detect differences across all hemodialysis modalities. Second, our analysis was restricted to IS and pCS. Therefore, our results cannot be extrapolated to other PBUTs, although IS and pCS are considered the most relevant and related to cardiovascular toxicity. Third, as previously discussed, it is possible that the doses of loop diuretics evaluated were insufficient to adequately assess their potential beneficial role during OL-HDF. Finally, the serum concentration of the administered drugs (ibuprofen, torasemide, and furosemide) was not measured which can prevent a precise correlation between the degree of albumin-binding displacement and the circulant levels of the competitor. Additionally, although all patients did not present residual renal function, the potential harmful effect of intradialysis prefilter administration of ibuprofen on this function remains to be established in long-term studies.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that pharmacological displacement is a feasible strategy to enhance PBUTs removal during OL-HDF. Although ibuprofen proved to be the most effective competitor in our study, these findings pave the way for the development of safe and targeted methods to optimize the intradialytic clearance of IS and pCS, potentially reducing the associated toxicity. However, their clinical relevance remains unknown, as prospective, long-term studies are warranted to evaluate the potential impact that enhancing IS and pCS removal may have on cardiovascular events or surveillance in patients on hemodialysis.

To conclude, among the strategies tested, only the arterial line administration of ibuprofen significantly increased the RR of IS and pCS beyond the levels achieved by high-volume postdilutional HDF. Direct prefilter infusion is essential for the effective competitive displacement as oral administration of loop diuretics failed to show a significant benefit in OL-HDF. Besides, the absence of significant effect with loop diuretics suggests that high-volume postdilutional HDF may limit the impact of lower-affinity competitive agents such as loop diuretics. These findings highlight the potential of pharmacological intervention to improve dialysis quality, although the long-term safety and the specific impact on residual renal function must be addressed in future larger trials as well as the clinical impact of this improvement in PBUTs removal.
