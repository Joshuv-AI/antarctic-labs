Effect of Socioeconomic Status on Lung Function in the Korean General Population
Authors: Hyonsoo Joo, Yong Suk Jo, Chin Kook Rhee, Joon Young Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocioeconomic status (SES) significantly influences the incidence and severity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. This study investigated the impact of SES on incidence of airflow obstruction and lung function decline within a general population cohort in Republic of Korea (South Korea).MethodsWe utilized data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study Ansan-Ansung Cohort, collected between 2001 and 2014. A total of 10,030 participants were included in the longitudinal analysis. Socioeconomic status was classified using four key indicators: area of residence (urban, n = 5,012; rural, n = 5,018), marital status (single, n = 974; married, n = 8,987), education level (elementary school, n = 3,355; middle or high school, n = 5,287; college or above, n = 1,305), and income (< $1,460, n = 6,413; $1,460–2,920, n = 2,705; > $2,920, n = 729), among participants with available data.ResultsOver a 12-year follow-up period, individuals living in rural areas experienced a more rapid decline in both mean forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) compared to those in urban areas (P < 0.001). Participants with the lowest levels of education and income experienced significantly faster declines in both FVC and FEV1 (all P < 0.001, except for medium vs. high income). The risk of developing airflow obstruction was significantly higher for participants from rural areas, with lower education levels, and lower income.ConclusionThis study demonstrated a significant association between lower SES and incidence of airflow obstruction and progression in Korea. Lower SES, characterized by rural residence, lower educational status, and lower income, was associated with poorer baseline health, higher comorbidity rates, and more rapid lung function decline over a 12-year period.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a significant global health concern and is a leading cause of mortality worldwide.1 The burden of COPD is disproportionately higher in developing countries due to factors, such as high smoking rates, poor air quality, and limited healthcare resources.2 Its prevalence varies widely, influenced by factors, such as age, sex, smoking habits, and socioeconomic status (SES).345 Major risk factors include cigarette smoking, exposure to indoor and outdoor air pollution, and occupational hazards.5

Lower SES has consistently been associated with higher COPD prevalence and severity, possibly because of increased exposure to risk factors and reduced access to healthcare.67 SES influences health behaviors and access to resources, which in turn impact the management and outcomes of chronic diseases, including COPD.8910 Individuals with lower SES are more likely to engage in high-risk behaviors, such as smoking, and have greater exposure to environmental pollutants, both of which contribute to COPD development.111213 Furthermore, limited financial resources often restrict access to preventive care and effective treatment options, exacerbating health outcomes.1415

Recent studies that have utilized the UK Biobank have introduced the Socioeconomic and Environmental Risk Score for assessing additive and cumulative environmental, behavioral, and socioeconomic risk factors,16 reporting it to be a more robust predictor of COPD compared to tobacco smoking alone. Recognizing the impact of SES on COPD is crucial for developing targeted public health strategies to address these disparities and improve patient outcomes.

This study investigated the impact of SES on the incidence of airflow obstruction (AFO) and the decline in lung function within a general population cohort in Republic of Korea (South Korea). Specifically, we examined how various indicators of SES, including education, occupation, family income, and marital status, influence COPD risk and progression, as well as the rate of lung function decline. We hypothesized that lower SES would be associated with a higher incidence of AFO and a greater lung function decline.

METHODS

Study population

This longitudinal observational study utilized data from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study Ansan-Ansung Cohort. The study population included adults aged 40–69 years, residing in urban (Ansan) or rural (Ansung) areas of Korea. Baseline data were collected from 2001 to 2002, with follow-up assessments conducted biennially until 2019. We used data from the first to the seventh follow-up (2013–2014), as spirometry measurements were available up to that point. Structured interviews and standardized questionnaires were used to collect information on lifestyle characteristics, medical history, subjective symptoms, and disease incidence. Details of the study design and protocols have previously been published.17 All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines.

SES classification

SES was classified using four key indicators: area of residence, marital status, education level, and income. Area of residence was categorized as urban or rural. Marital status was classified as either single or married. Education level was grouped into three categories: elementary school, middle, or high school, and college or above. According to the Korean Statistical Information Service (KOSIS), the median monthly household income in Korea in 2023 was approximately 2,000,000 KRW. Based on this standard, we classified income levels into three categories (< 2,000,000 KRW, 2,000,000–4,000,000 KRW, > 4,000,000 KRW) and converted them into US Dollars (USD) (< $1,430, $1,430–2,859, and > $2,859) according to the exchange rate in May 2025 (an exchange rate of 1 USD = 1,399 KRW) (KOSIS. Household income trends. Statistics Korea; 2023. Available at: https://kosis.kr).

Clinical variables

Clinical variables collected at baseline and during follow-up assessments included demographic data, such as age and sex. Lifestyle factors, specifically smoking status and pack-years, were documented. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), were also recorded.

Medical history was thoroughly documented, including information on comorbid conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, and thyroid disease. Symptoms were assessed using the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale and the presence of chronic bronchitis symptoms. Nutritional status was evaluated through dietary intake assessments, measuring total energy intake, protein, fat, and sugar consumption. In addition, exposure to environmental pollutants, such as chemicals and dust, was recorded. All data were collected through structured interviews and standardized questionnaires to ensure consistency and reliability.

Lung function assessment

Pulmonary function tests were conducted at each visit by trained technicians using a standardized spirometer (Vmax-2130, SensorMedics, Yorba Linda, CA, USA), following the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines. Measurements included forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), and forced expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of vital capacity. All spirometry measurements were obtained without the use of bronchodilators to assess baseline lung function. Calibration and quality control procedures were regularly performed to ensure the accuracy and precision of spirometric measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the relationships between SES, baseline characteristics, lung function decline rate, and the incidence of AFO. Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using the Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

A longitudinal analysis of lung function decline over 12 years was conducted using linear mixed models. This analysis assessed differences in the rate of decline in lung function (FEV1 and FVC) according to SES indicators, with models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking history, smoking pack-years, and baseline FEV1. Incidence rates of AFO were calculated as the number of new cases divided by the total person-years at risk and expressed per 1,000 person-years, stratified by SES indicators. The mean follow-up time to AFO was also reported for each SES category to contextualize risk estimates. Propensity score matching (PSM) was additionally performed using key demographic variables, including age, sex, and smoking status, to balance baseline differences between SES groups.

Multivariate Poisson regression was used to examine the risk of developing COPD during the follow-up period, defined as an FEV1/FVC ratio < 0.7. The analysis included individual SES factors as well as their combinations. Models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking pack-years, and smoking history.

Survival analysis was conducted to estimate the time to first AFO, including only participants without AFO at baseline. Cox regression analysis was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) for the risk for AFO, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking pack-years, smoking history, and baseline FEV1, while evaluating the impact of individual SES indicators. To assess the robustness of SES associations, we further applied a stepwise modeling approach. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, baseline lung function, and residential area; Model 2 additionally included education level; Model 3 further included income; and Model 4 incorporated all SES indicators including marital status.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Incheon St. Mary’s Hospital (Institutional Review Board number: OC23ZISI0033). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Incheon St. Mary’s Hospital.

Participants from rural areas had higher rates of breathlessness and chronic bronchitis compared to those from urban areas. They also exhibited poorer nutritional status, with lower protein intake, higher sugar consumption, and higher smoking rates, but biomass exposure was less common. Rural residents experienced higher rates of comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes, (Supplementary Table 1). In terms of marital status, single participants demonstrated higher rates of breathlessness and generally had poorer SES indicators. They also had lower protein intake, although smoking and biomass exposure was less common. Moreover, single individuals had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including diabetes and hypertension (Supplementary Table 2). Participants with lower educational attainment reported more respiratory symptoms and had poorer SES indicators. Their nutritional status was worse, with lower protein intake, and they had higher smoking pack-years, despite a lower current smoking rate. There was also a higher risk of various comorbidities among those with lower education levels (Supplementary Table 3). Individuals with lower income levels had more respiratory symptoms and poorer SES indicators, along with a lower protein intake. While the smoking rate was lower, smoking pack-years were higher in this group. A wide range of comorbidities, including diabetes and coronary artery disease, were more prevalent (Supplementary Table 4). Although absolute values of FEV1 and FVC were lower in the lower SES groups, their %predicted values were relatively higher, which may reflect age- and height-related differences. PSM was performed to adjust for baseline imbalances between SES groups. Owing to substantial group differences, the matching resulted in a notable decrease in sample size and underrepresentation of some SES strata (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Overall, across multiple SES indicators—including rural residence, lower education level, and lower income—participants in the lower SES categories consistently showed poorer baseline health, a higher incidence of respiratory symptoms, worse nutritional status, greater comorbidity burden, and lower lung function compared with those in higher SES categories.

Rates of lung function decline based on SES

The rate of lung function decline over the 12-year follow-up period was compared across different SES indicators. Individuals living in rural areas showed a more rapid decline in both mean FEV1 and FVC compared to those in urban areas (FEV1: −48.6 mL/year vs. −42.7 mL/year, P < 0.001; FVC: −43.1 mL/year vs. −32.3 mL/year, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A and B).

Regarding marital status, the rates of decline in FEV1 and FVC were not significantly different between single and married individuals (Fig. 1C and D). Regarding education, annual FEV1 decline was highest among participants with elementary education (−48.5 mL/year), followed by those with middle/high school (−45.2 mL/year) and college or above (−41.3 mL/year) (P < 0.001). A similar pattern was observed for FVC (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1E and F). In terms of income, participants with low income experienced the greatest FEV1 decline (−46.2 mL/year), followed by medium (−43.9 mL/year) and higher income groups (−40.9 mL/year) (P < 0.001). Similar trends were noted for FVC (Fig. 1G and H).

Incidence of AFO

The incidence rates of AFO also varied significantly across SES indicators (Table 2). Rural residents had a markedly higher incidence compared with urban residents (10.6 vs. 6.02 per 1,000 person-years) and they developed AFO earlier (mean time 6.68 vs. 7.29 years). By education, those with elementary schooling showed the highest incidence (8.66 per 1,000 person-years) and shortest time to AFO (6.66 years), followed by middle/high school (7.92; 6.91 years) and college or higher (7.77; 7.56 years). Participants in the lowest-income group had the highest incidence (8.98 per 1,000 person-years) and earliest onset (6.61 years), whereas those in the highest-income group had the lowest incidence (6.32) and the longest mean time to AFO (8.10 years). Marital status showed smaller differences, with incidence rates of 8.29 per 1,000 person-years among married participants versus 6.76 among unmarried, and mean times to AFO of 6.92 vs. 7.08 years, respectively.

Risk for AFO

The risk of developing AFO during the follow-up period was analyzed based on SES indicators (Fig. 2). Individuals living in rural areas were at higher risk for developing AFO compared to those in urban areas, with HR of 1.64. Elementary school or lower education was also associated with an increased risk compared to those with university education or above (HR, 1.54). Similarly, those with the lowest income levels had a higher risk than those with the highest income (HR, 1.38). The analysis also considered combinations of SES indicators, revealing that individuals with multiple low SES factors had a compounded risk of developing AFO. Those who lived in rural areas, had elementary school or lower education, and the lowest income, were at significantly higher risk for future AFO compared to those who lived in urban areas, had the highest education, and the highest income (HR, 2.00).

Differences in time to first AFO according to SES

The time to first AFO was analyzed according to SES indicators. Kaplan-Meier curves and the results of log-rank tests are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. To examine SES associations with future AFO risk, we performed sequential Cox regression analyses with incremental covariate adjustment (Table 3). In univariate analysis, rural residence and lower income levels were significantly associated with increased risk. In the first multivariable model (Model 1) adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, smoking pack-years, and baseline FEV1, rural residence remained highly significant (HR, 1.439; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.247–1.660, P < 0.001). The addition of education level in Model 2 had minimal impact on the rural residence effect (HR, 1.442; 95% CI, 1.244–1.670), while education itself showed no significant association. Further inclusion of income (Model 3) and marital status (Model 4) did not meaningfully change the results, with rural residence maintaining consistent significance (HR, 1.472; 95% CI, 1.220–1.720, P < 0.001) in the final model. Neither education level nor income achieved statistical significance in any multivariable model, and marital status showed no association.

DISCUSSION

We examined the impact of four SES indicators—place of residence, education, income, and marital status—on the rate of lung function decline and the subsequent risk of developing COPD. Over 12 years of follow-up, we identified 961 new cases of AFO, corresponding to an incidence rate of 8.3 per 1,000 person-years, and observed that lung function declined more rapidly among individuals of lower SES, particularly those living in rural areas, with lower educational attainment, and with lower income. Rural residence was the only SES indicator that remained independently associated with higher AFO risk in the multivariable analysis, whereas the associations with education level, income, and marital status were attenuated. Notably, although education level and income were significantly associated with the risk of AFO in univariate analyses, these associations attenuated and lost statistical significance in the multivariable model. This pattern likely reflects collinearity among SES indicators, as rural residence, lower education, and lower income often cluster together in our study population. The persistence of rural residence as a significant factor after adjustment suggests that geographic location may capture unique influences on respiratory health beyond those explained by education and income alone. In addition, the attenuation of education and income effects may indicate mediation through rural residence. Geographic location can shape access to educational opportunities, employment prospects, and healthcare services, and may serve as a more proximal determinant of environmental exposures and healthcare accessibility that directly affect lung function decline. Finally, the loss of significance for certain SES indicators in the adjusted model may also be related to reduced statistical power when multiple correlated variables are included simultaneously, even in the context of an overall adequate sample size.

In interpreting our findings, it is important to consider potential confounding by demographic and lifestyle factors. Participants in the lower SES group tended to be older and had a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions, which may partly account for their poorer baseline health and faster lung function decline. Although our regression models adjusted for key covariates, including age, sex, and smoking pack-years, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. The lower protein intake observed in lower SES groups may also reflect higher mean age and smaller body size rather than active nutritional deficiency. These differences could be attributed to generational variations in growth and body composition, which in turn influence caloric requirements. Furthermore, the gradient across SES strata—whereby the proportion of males increased, the proportion of never-smokers decreased, and smoking pack-years increased—underscores the importance of accounting for lifetime smoking burden in SES–respiratory outcome associations. While our main analyses incorporated smoking pack-years to address this issue, future stratified analyses could provide deeper insights into the interplay between sex, smoking exposure, and SES.

Interestingly, some higher SES groups exhibited transient increases in FEV1 or FVC at year 2. While these fluctuations may partly reflect random variability, regression to the mean, or unmeasured factors, they could also represent a meaningful biological or behavioral signal. For example, early improvements may arise from temporary lifestyle changes, healthcare access, or other contextual factors that later diminish over time. Therefore, these patterns should be interpreted with caution, and future studies are needed to clarify whether such short-term increases contribute to the mechanisms underlying SES differences in lung function decline.

These findings demonstrate the significant role of SES disparities in the progression of lung function decline and AFO development, emphasizing the need for targeted public health interventions to address these socioeconomic inequalities.

These findings are consistent with those of previous studies indicating a strong link between lower SES and increased risk for COPD. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with lower education and income levels are more likely to develop COPD and experience poorer outcomes.4691618 However, unlike previous studies, which have mostly utilized cross-sectional designs, our longitudinal approach provides stronger evidence of the causal relationship between SES and lung function decline. We incorporated a comprehensive set of SES indicators and examined their collective impact on respiratory health in a general population cohort over a 12-year period.

The association between lower SES and increased risk for COPD can be attributed to several potential mechanisms. First, individuals with lower SES are more likely to engage in high-risk health behaviors, such as smoking, which is a primary risk factor for COPD.1214 While lower SES is often associated with greater exposure to indoor and outdoor pollutants in other populations,19 in our cohort, dust exposure did not differ significantly across SES groups. Therefore, environmental exposures may not be the primary mediating pathway in this study, though other unmeasured exposures related to living or occupational conditions could still contribute. In addition, nutritional deficiencies prevalent among lower SES groups can impair immune function and lung health, exacerbating the risk and severity of COPD.1620 Limited access to healthcare services is another crucial factor. Individuals with lower SES often face barriers to accessing preventive care and early treatment, leading to delayed diagnoses and poorer management of COPD.14 Comorbid conditions are more prevalent in lower SES populations, and can further complicate the course of COPD, which makes it more difficult to manage and treat effectively.21 Moreover, accelerated aging, including deterioration in physical capability, sensory function, physiological function, cognitive performance, and emotional well-being, may be associated with lung function decline in low SES individuals.22

Our findings demonstrate the need for targeted public health interventions to address socioeconomic disparities in incidence and progression of AFO. Improving access to healthcare services, including preventive care and early diagnosis, is crucial for populations of lower SES.815 Educational programs that promote COPD awareness and healthier lifestyles, along with better housing conditions and stricter regulations on occupational hazards, can significantly reduce COPD risks.81523 Public health policies that focus on low-SES areas can reduce the COPD burden and improve health equity, mitigating the adverse effects of SES on COPD outcomes.

This study had several strengths. First, the longitudinal design, with a follow-up period of 12 years, provided robust evidence of the causal relationship between SES and the progression of lung function decline and COPD risk. The large, community-based cohort enhanced the generalizability of the findings to the broader Korean population. In addition, the comprehensive assessment of multiple SES indicators, including education, income, occupation, and marital status, facilitated a nuanced understanding of how various dimensions of socioeconomic disadvantage collectively impact respiratory health.

However, this study also had some limitations. First, spirometry measurements in this study were performed without bronchodilator administration. This may limit the diagnostic accuracy for COPD, as the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease definition requires post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < 0.7 for definitive COPD diagnosis. Therefore, our definition of AFO may overestimate the true incidence of COPD, as some cases of reversible airway obstruction (such as asthma) might have been included. However, this limitation is unlikely to differentially affect the association between SES indicators and AFO development. Moreover, several previous longitudinal studies investigating the incidence of AFO have also relied on pre-bronchodilator spirometry data.2425262728 Second, although we adjusted for major confounders—including age, sex, smoking status, and baseline lung function—in all outcome analyses, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. We attempted PSM using age, sex, and smoking history; however, the large baseline differences between SES groups resulted in substantial sample size reduction and near-complete loss of certain SES strata. For this reason, we retained the unmatched dataset and applied multivariable adjustment in our analyses. The findings were based on a Korean population, and while they provided valuable insights, caution should be exercised when generalizing these results to other populations with different socio-cultural contexts. Despite these limitations, our results significantly contribute to the understanding of how SES influences COPD risk and progression, highlighting the importance of addressing socioeconomic disparities in public health interventions.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated a significant association between lower SES and incidence of AFO and progression in Korea. Lower SES, characterized by rural residence, lower educational status, and lower income, was associated with poorer baseline health, worse nutritional status, poorer lung function, higher comorbidity rates, and more rapid lung function decline over a 12-year period. These findings highlight the crucial role that SES disparities play in respiratory health outcomes, emphasizing the need for targeted public health interventions. Efforts to improve healthcare access, enhance COPD educational programs and healthy lifestyles, and reduce environmental exposures to pollutants are essential.2813 Addressing these socioeconomic factors through comprehensive public health policies is vital for reducing the incidence and severity of COPD and improving health equity.
