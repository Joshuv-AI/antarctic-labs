Field performance of MON 94637 and laboratory evidence supporting a Bt pyramiding strategy against key lepidopteran pests in Brazil
Authors: Leonardo L Miraldo, Joacir do Nascimento, Thais LB dos Santos, Hallison V Vertuan, Luciana V Machado, Augusto C Crivellari, Márcia OMA José, Jessica Fernandez, Erick MG Cordeiro, Paula M Antunes, Samuel Martinelli, Graham P Head, Katherine Caldwell, Uma Kesanapalli, Ester Buiate, Patrick M Dourado, Ramiro FL Ovejero, Geraldo U Berger, Renato J Horikoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDPyramided Bt genes in crops have been widely adopted to delay insect resistance evolution worldwide. The genetically modified (GM) soybean (Glycine max [L.] Merril) MON 94637, expressing the Bt proteins Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2, was designed to improve the level of protection against defoliating caterpillars (Lepidoptera). In this study, we performed laboratory and field studies to assess the efficacy of the Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 proteins in MON 94637 expressed together and in a five‐Bt protein pyramid in soybean against the main lepidopteran pests attacking this crop.RESULTSSoybean leaf tissue expressing a combination of Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2 (MON 94637) or, Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac (MON 94637 × MON 87751 × MON 87701 × MON 94313 × MON 89788) caused 100% mortality of neonates of Chrysodeixis includens, Anticarsia gemmatalis, and Spodoptera eridania across a range of plant growth stages. This Bt soybean also showed high efficacy in bioassays against Cry1Ac‐resistant colonies of Rachiplusia nu and Crocidosema sp. In field tests, MON 96437 soybean (Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2) was highly effective against lepidopteran pest species as assessed by reductions in defoliation rate and pest incidence in comparison to non‐Bt across several locations in Brazil.CONCLUSIONSLeaf bioassays showed that GM soybean expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac will be highly effective against key lepidopteran pests in soybean in Brazil. This is the first pyramid of five‐Bt protein biotechnology soybean, representing a unique innovation for tropical agriculture. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Brazil is one of the largest producers of soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merril) worldwide, accounting for 168.34 million tons distributed across more than 47.6 million hectares in 2024/2025. 1 Currently, an estimation of 99% of Brazil's soybean area is cultivated with genetically modified (GM) varieties, expressing herbicide‐tolerant traits and/or at least one insecticidal protein from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt). 2 The first commercial soybean‐planting area in the country with Bt protein was in 2013/2014 and covered 1.2 million hectares. Over 10 years, the Bt‐expressed soybean adoption rate increased from 4% to 95% in 2024/2025, considering different biotechnologies. 2 Bt soybean has benefitted farmers by maximizing yield, protecting plants from target‐pest damage, and by regionally suppressing pest abundance and reducing the number of insecticides sprayed in both Bt and non‐Bt soybean areas. 3 , 4 The widespread adoption of Bt crops has revolutionized agriculture worldwide over the last 20 years protecting plants from pest damage and maximizing yield. 5

In this context, the high adoption of Bt soybean in Brazil can be explained in part by the high efficacy of MON 87701 × MON 89788 (hereafter referred to as IPRO), which expresses Cry1Ac, against the insect pests Anticarsia gemmatalis (Hübner, 1818) (Lepidoptera: Erebidae), Chrysodeixis includens (Walker, [1858]) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), Chloridea virescens (Fabricius, 1777) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), and Helicoverpa armigera (Hübner, 1808) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae). 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, Cry1Ac soybean provides only low levels of control against Spodoptera species, such as Spodoptera frugiperda (J.E. Smith, 1797) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), Spodoptera eridania (Stoll, 1782) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae), and Spodoptera cosmioides (Walker, 1898) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae). 10 Although Cry1Ac soybean provided protection against Rachiplusia nu (Guenée, 1852) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) in the past, recently field‐evolved resistance to Cry1Ac was reported for both species in Brazil. 11

Bt pyramids are defined as GM plants producing two or more Bt proteins with independent modes of action that target the same insect pest. 12 The expected outcome of pyramiding strategy is enhanced durability of pest efficacy. This approach ensures that if some individuals develop resistance to one Bt protein, they will still be effectively controlled by the other Bt protein(s) expressed by the plant. This outcome, known as ‘redundant killing’, makes pyramiding Bt genes a more effective way to delay the onset of resistance at the field level than deploying single‐mode‐of‐action Bt plants. 12 , 13 , 14 In 2021/2022 season, the MON 87751 × MON 87708 × MON 87701 × MON 89788 (hereafter referred to as I2X), which expresses Cry1Ac (MON 87701), Cry1A.105, and Cry2Ab2 (both in MON 87751) and confers tolerance to glyphosate (MON 89788) and dicamba (MON 87708), was commercially launched in Brazil. 15 The Cry1A.105/Cry2Ab2/Cry1Ac soybean provided high levels of control for A. gemmatalis, C. includens, and H. armigera 16 ; but it also caused high mortality of S. cosmioides and Spodoptera albula (Walker, 1857) (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae). 17 This Bt soybean also suppressed population growth of S. eridania, although minimal impact was observed for S. frugiperda. 17

Recently, a new GM soybean, MON 94637 × MON 87751 × MON 87701 × MON 94313 × MON 89788 (hereafter referred to as I5+), was approved in Brazil. 18 This combination of soybean events was obtained by conventional breeding of five events, expressing five Cry proteins from Bacillus thuringiensis (Berliner) from the transgenes in MON 94637 (Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2 [hereafter referred as SIP3]), MON 87751 (Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2 [hereafter referred to as SIP2]), and MON 87701 (Cry1Ac), and also conferring tolerance to glyphosate, dicamba, 2,4‐D, glufosinate, and mesotrione. 18 The Bt proteins in SIP3, Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2, are chimeric proteins constructed to minimize receptor overlap between them. Specifically, Cry1B.2 contains Cry1Be in domains 1 and 2 and Cry1Ka in domain 3, whereas Cry1A.2 was designed with Cry1Ah in domain 1, Cry1Ac in domain 2, and Cry1Ca in domain 3. 19 In addition, diet bioassays with purified Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 proteins showed each independently high efficacy against major lepidopteran pests such as C. includens, A. gemmatalis, S. eridania, and S. cosmioides. 19

In this study, the Bt‐pyramided soybean I5+ (expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac) was evaluated in laboratory bioassays against the main lepidopteran pests attacking soybean in Brazil, including A. gemmatalis, C. includens, R. nu, Crocidosema sp., and S. eridania. Additionally, individual components (i.e., SIP3) and other commercial combinations (i.e., GTS‐40‐3‐2 [hereafter referred to as RR], IPRO, and I2X) were assessed in field trials under natural infestation.

Efficacy in leaf disc bioassays with neonates

At each stage, the last fully expanded leaves were collected and kept refrigerated until use. For each species (Table 1) and soybean genotype (Table 2), 128 neonates were screened in a 128‐well bioassay tray (BIO‐BA‐128; CD International Inc., Pitman, NJ) containing a solidified 2% agar solution and one soybean leaf disc (1.7 cm diameter) in each well. A single neonate larva (≤24 h old) was placed into each well with a fine brush. Then, plates were sealed and placed in a climatic chamber at 25 ± 2 °C, 70 ± 10% RH, and 14:10 h (L:D) photoperiod. Each replicate consisted of 16 neonates, for a total of 128 neonates tested for each species. Mortality and instar of survivors were recorded 5 days after infestation: larvae that did not molt (progress beyond first instar) were considered dead (shown as Mortality + L1 (%) in Fig. 1). Percentages of mortality were analyzed using a generalized linear model (GLM) with binomial error distribution and logit link function. Goodness‐of‐fit was evaluated with half‐normal plots with simulated envelopes generated by the ‘hnp’ package. 23 Treatment means of percentage mortality were compared by Tukey using the ‘cld’ function from the ‘multcomp’ package in R. 24

Efficacy in leaf tissue with third‐instar larvae

For each species, one neonate per well was initially placed in a 32‐well bioassay tray (BioSupply Comércio de Plásticos, SP, Brazil) containing solidified 2% agar solution and three soybean leaf squares (2.5 × 2.5 cm) per well from the near‐isogenic negative check (non‐Bt). When the larvae reached third instar, they were individually transferred to each treatment (Table 2) in a 32‐well bioassay tray containing a solidified 2% agar solution and three soybean leaf squares (2.5 × 2.5 cm). Then, plates were sealed and placed in a climatic chamber at 25 ± 2 °C, 70 ± 10% RH, and 14:10 h (L:D) photoperiod. Each replicate consisted of 16 third‐instar larvae, for a total of 64 insects tested for each species. The numbers of dead insects were recorded 5 days after infestation to calculate the percentage of mortality. Percentages of mortality were analyzed with a GLM assuming binomial distribution with logit function. Goodness‐of‐fit was checked with a half‐normal‐plot with simulation envelopes using the ‘hnp’ package. 23 Average percent mortality was compared among treatments using Tukey with the ‘multcomp’ package through ‘cld’ function in R. 24

Efficacy in field trials

SIP3 (Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2) efficacy was assessed under natural insect infestation in Brazil. Trials were carried out across six research sites from Bayer Crop Science located at Luís Eduardo Magalhães, BA; Sorriso, MT; Cachoeira Dourada, MG; Santa Cruz das Palmeiras, SP; Não‐Me‐Toque, RS; and Rolândia, PR. Field trials were conducted in the 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 seasons, with planting conducted from September to December in each growing season. There was a total of five and four treatments in 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 trials, respectively, and here we show the comparison across SIP3 (Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2), I2X (Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, Cry1Ac), IPRO (Cry1Ac), RR (used only in 2021/2022 season) and non‐Bt soybean (near‐isogenic negative check). A completely randomized block design with four replicates (plots) was performed per treatment. Each plot consisted of eight rows (8 m length × 4 m width) with 0.5 m spacing between rows. Evaluations were performed within a central area of 15 m2 (5 m length × 4 m width); the border rows were not considered. The percentage of defoliation and the number of larvae per group (classified as Plusiinae [which includes both C. includens and R. nu], Spodoptera spp., and A. gemmatalis) and instar (second/third [≤1.5 cm] or fourth/fifth [≥1.5 cm]) were recorded at V5–V6 (only for 2021/2022), V7–Vn, R1–R2, R3–R4, R5.1, R5.4, and R6 growth stages using a beat sheet (1 m length).

SAS PROC MEANS was used to calculate the mean of each plot for defoliation assessment and Lepidoptera counts (classified as Plusiinae, Spodoptera spp., and A. gemmatalis) of each treatment at each site, stage, and replicate. PROC MEANS in SAS was used to calculate the mean and standard error of each treatment for each characteristic at each stage over replicates at each site. SAS PROC MEANS was used again to calculate the mean measurement for each combination of characteristic, site, and stage. 25 Combinations with a measurement value of zero were excluded from the subsequent analysis due to insufficient variation. RESULTS

Efficacy of MON 94637 (Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2) soybean in a leaf disc bioassay with neonates

The C. includens and A. gemmatalis larvae showed 100% mortality across all Bt soybean trait packages at 5 days after infestation, significantly higher than that found on non‐Bt (Fig. 1(A), (B)). The Crocidosema sp. Cry1Ac‐R strain larvae showed 100% mortality on Bt soybean trait packages I5+, I2X, and SIP2, significantly higher than the mortality observed on IPRO and non‐Bt (Fig. 1(C)). In addition, the mortality observed for V4 (~80%) and R5.5 (~92%) for Crocidosema sp. on SIP3 was statistically lower than that observed for R1, R3, and R5.1 (100%) (Fig. 1(C)). The S. eridania larvae showed 100% mortality on I5+ and SIP3, except at R5.5 for SIP3 (89.84%) (Fig. 1(D)). Additionally, high mortality (>95%) was also observed in I2X and SIP2 at R5.1 and R5.5 for S. eridania (Fig. 1(D)), significantly higher than the mortality observed for those same trait packages at V4 (85.94% and 78.13%, respectively). Similar levels of S. eridania mortality (~70%) were observed for SIP2 and I2X at R3, which were not significantly different from that of I2X at R1 (55.47%) (Fig. 1(D)). The R. nu Cry1Ac‐R strain larvae showed 100% mortality on Bt soybean trait packages I5+, SIP3, I2X, and SIP2, significantly higher than that found on IPRO and non‐Bt (Fig. 1(E)).

Efficacy of MON 94637 (Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2) soybean leaf tissue against third‐instar larvae

The C. includens third‐instar larvae showed 100% mortality across all Bt soybean trait packages, significantly higher than that found on non‐Bt at R3 (1.56%) and R5.1 (2.08%) 5 days post infestation (Fig. 2(A)). The A. gemmatalis larvae showed 100% mortality on I5+, I2X, SIP2, and IPRO, significantly different from that observed in SIP3 (~85%) and non‐Bt (~4%) at both R3 and R5.1 stages (Fig. 2(B)). The Crocidosema sp. Cry1Ac‐R strain larvae showed up to 100% mortality on Bt soybean trait packages I5+, I2X, and SIP2 at R3 and R5.1, significantly higher than the mortality observed on IPRO and non‐Bt at both plant growth stages (Fig. 2(C)). The S. eridania larvae showed high mortality (>93.75%) on I5+ and SIP3 at R3 and R5.1, significantly higher than that found on I2X, SIP2, IPRO, and non‐Bt at both plant growth stages (Fig. 2(D)). The R. nu Cry1Ac‐R strain larvae showed high mortality (>85.42%) on I5+, SIP3, I2X, and SIP2 at R3 and R5.1, significantly higher than that observed on IPRO and non‐Bt (Fig. 2(E)).

Number of larvae per species group

SIP3, I2X, and IPRO nearly always showed similar or lower mean numbers of larvae per group (A. gemmatalis, Plusiinae, and Spodoptera spp.) in comparison to non‐Bt and RR despite the variable lepidopteran pest pressure among locations and soybean growth stages. In general, low numbers of larvae, regardless of insect species group and larval size, were observed in both 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 (Supporting Information, Tables S2 and S4, respectively).

Defoliation rate

In the assessment of the efficacy of SIP3 (Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2), I2X (Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, Cry1Ac), and IPRO (Cry1Ac) under natural lepidopteran infestation, a reduction in defoliation compared to non‐Bt and RR was evident across 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 among locations (Figs 3 and 4). The threshold for insecticide applications to manage lepidopteran caterpillars in soybean during reproductive growth stages is at 15% defoliation. 24 In both crop seasons, the mean defoliation rate observed in SIP3, I2X, and IPRO was below that threshold, except at Rolândia, PR, in 2021/2022 (R6) and 2022/2023 (R5.4 and R6) for IPRO (Figs 3 and 4). The mean defoliation rate on SIP3 was consistently low (<10%) even under high insect pressure (Figs 3 and 4). Similarly, low defoliation (<10%) was also observed for I2X plants among locations at 2021/2022 and 2022/2023. DISCUSSION

Pyramided Bt genes in crops have been widely considered to delay the evolution of resistance in target pests. 12 , 26 Three conditions are crucial for their durability: (i) Each toxin in the pyramid is able to kill 95% of susceptible individuals from a certain target pest, (ii) there is no cross‐resistance between the toxins from the pyramid, and (iii) pyramided Bt genes in plants are not cultivated at the same time as single‐toxin plants that express one or more toxins from the pyramid plants. 26 , 27 In addition, these conditions enable redundant killing, whereby an individual resistant to one toxin produced by the pyramided plant is killed by another toxin in the pyramid. 12 , 28 , 29

Thus, to assess the killing power of pyramiding Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac proteins in a soybean plant (I5+), we evaluated the efficacy of these proteins in several combinations against key target pests in plant tissue assays. C. includens and A. gemmatalis have been listed as the two most abundant pest species in non‐Bt soybean in Brazil. 30 Our findings from leaf disc bioassays with neonates revealed that each soybean trait package caused high mortality of laboratory colonies from C. includens and A. gemmatalis across all plant growth stages. Similarly, high mortality levels were reported for I2X soybean plants expressing Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac, alone or in combination, against susceptible colonies from C. includens and A. gemmatalis across V4, R1–R2, and R5 growth stages. 16 Several studies have shown high susceptibility to Cry1A proteins for A. gemmatalis, which is even more susceptible than C. includens. 7 , 16 , 31 Similar patterns of high susceptibility were also reported to Cry2Ab2 for C. includens, A. gemmatalis. Therefore, soybean expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac will be a valuable tool for the resistance management of C. includens and A. gemmatalis considering that Bt‐commercial proteins are still effective for controlling these pests.

Pyramided plants that express multiple toxins with distinct receptor binding would not only provide high efficacy against insects resistant to Bt proteins used in commercial products but would likely increase the durability of Bt traits relative to single‐mode‐of‐action Bt crops. 26 , 27 , 32 In this context, disabled insecticidal protein (DIP) bioassays with key lepidopteran soybean pests showed that Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 differ in receptor utilization. 19 However, Cry1A.2 has domain 2 in common with Cry1Ac expressed in commercial Bt soybean that could potentially affect, at least in part, its insecticidal activity in a scenario involving resistance to Cry1Ac. For instance, no resistance to Cry1Ac has been reported for primary pests such as C. includens and A. gemmatalis, suggesting that a pyramided soybean expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac will be a valuable tool in pest management. 30 Field‐evolved resistance of secondary soybean pests R. nu and Crocidosema sp. was reported for Cry1Ac soybean in Brazil in 2021. 11 , 21 , 22 Our results showed high mortality levels of SIP3 (expressing Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2) against R. nu Cry1Ac‐R and Crocidosema sp. Cry1Ac‐R in leaf disc bioassays with neonates and third‐instar larvae regardless of plant growth stage, which suggests that soybean expressing Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 is effective for controlling Cry1Ac‐resistant insects. Pyramided crops expressing multiple Bt toxins have shown high levels of control against resistant colonies of several lepidopteran species. 22 , 33 Likewise, soybean plants expressing three or more toxins (I2X and I5+, which both express Cry1Ac) were highly effective against individuals resistant to Cry1Ac, thus meeting the redundant‐killing factor necessary for an effective pyramided crop. Nonetheless, the pyramid effectiveness of I5+ expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac will ultimately depend on the durability of Cry1Ac, Cry1A.105 and Cry2Ab which are currently being exposed for selection pressure in the field. This dependency arises not only from the inherent characteristics of the Cry1Ac, Cry1A.105 and Cry1Ab proteins which constitute components of the I5+ pyramid, but also from the shared domain 2 of the Cry1Ac with Cry1A.2, which may potentially be influenced by resistance to Cry1Ac. Therefore, the adoption of resistance management strategies, such as refuge practices, is fundamental to ensure caterpillar protection provided from I5+ soybean.

The increase in relative abundance of Spodoptera species in Cry1Ac and non‐Bt soybean has been a recent concern for farmers in Brazil. 30 Nonetheless, Cry1Ac soybean continues to be highly effective against several key target pests, and the presence of Spodoptera spp. is expected in Cry1Ac soybean fields due to the low toxicity against this species complex. 10 In contrast, soybean plants expressing the combination of Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac caused high mortality of S. cosmioides, S. albula, and susceptible genotypes of S. frugiperda, while mortality reached up to 82.8% for S. eridania 5 days post infestation. 17 Our results showed similar levels of mortality for first‐instar S. eridania on Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac (I2X) soybean, which varied from 55.47% to 99.22% among the soybean growth stages tested. Moreover, soybean plants expressing Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 (SIP3) showed high levels of mortality against first‐instar larvae of S. eridania, which ranged from 89.84% to 100% depending on the growth stage tested. Nevertheless, the Bt soybean expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac (I5+) showed 100% mortality for S. eridania in plant bioassays with first‐ and third‐instar larvae across all tested plant growth stages, which indicates that this soybean genotype is the first Bt soybean with 100% efficacy against this species in Brazil.

Considering that mid‐size larvae are more tolerant than neonates to Bt proteins, 34 , 35 we tested whether third‐instar larvae could discriminate the toxicity levels of soybean trait packages against key lepidopteran pests. The Bt soybean expressing five Bt proteins (Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac; I5+) showed 100% mortality against all species tested regardless of the plant growth stage (Fig. 2), which indicates high toxicity. Similar levels of mortality were observed for soybean plants expressing two (Cry1A.105 and Cry2Ab2; SIP2) or three (Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac; I2X) Bt proteins against C. includens, A. gemmatalis, and the Cry1Ac‐resistant strains of R. nu and Crocidosema sp. (Fig. 2). In contrast, soybean plants expressing these two‐ and three‐protein combinations showed low mortality for third‐instar S. eridania, as expected based on its efficacy against neonates from this species as shown in this study and previous reports. 17 In addition, Cry1Ac soybean was highly effective against third‐instar larvae from C. includens and A. gemmatalis, whereas low mortality was observed against Cry1Ac‐resistant strains from Crocidosema sp. and R. nu and against S. eridania, as expected. Notably, soybean plants expressing Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 also showed high mortality against late‐instar larvae across lepidopteran pests, which indicates its importance in delivering high mortality, especially for S. eridania, when combined with Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac.

The current defoliation thresholds in soybean for insecticide spraying to manage lepidopteran pests are 30% and 15% during the vegetative and reproductive growth stages, respectively. 36 In the present study, soybean plants expressing the combination of Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 showed lower defoliation than non‐Bt at most locations during the 2021/2022 (Fig. 3) and 2022/2023 (Fig. 4) seasons. Moreover, similar levels of defoliation were observed between soybean plants expressing Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 and those expressing Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac, which highlight the efficacy of Cry1A.2 and Cry1B.2 in comparison to commercial Bt soybean expressing either Cry1Ac alone (which sometimes had unacceptably high defoliation) or Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac under field conditions. Although the soybean line expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1.B2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac (I5+) was not tested under field conditions, its field performance should be at least as good as the line expressing Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac (I2X) based on the laboratory trials reported here. Large‐scale lepidopteran assessment in soybeans fields showed that most lepidopteran larvae found in Cry1Ac soybeans belong to S. frugiperda, S. eridania, and S. cosmioides, 11 and also to R. nu owing to evolution of resistance. 22 , 30 Although low numbers of larvae were observed at 2021/2022 and 2022/2023, the high levels of defoliation observed in Cry1Ac soybean in Rolândia, PR (2021/2022 and 2022/2023) and Sorriso, MT (2022/2023) may have been caused by Spodoptera spp. and R. nu, considering that the Cry1Ac resistance allele frequency in C. includens has remained at low levels since Cry1Ac‐soybean deployment in Brazil. 11 , 37

In summary, I5+ soybean leaf tissues expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac showed high efficacy against C. includens, A. gemmatalis, and S. eridania when infested with either neonates or third‐instar larvae. Moreover, our findings highlight the importance of testing colonies resistant to a single toxin, such as Cry1Ac, on different types of pyramided soybean; here, we showed that the evolution of Cry1Ac resistance in Crocidosema sp. and R. nu in Brazil did not impact the effectiveness of soybean expressing five Bt proteins. The results presented in this study indicate that the Bt soybean expressing Cry1A.2, Cry1B.2, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Cry1Ac will not only provide additional protection against target pests such as R. nu and S. eridania in comparison to current commercial Bt soybean varieties but will also contribute to effective resistance management for primary soybean pests in Brazil.
