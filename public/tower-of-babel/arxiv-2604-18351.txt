Runhao Jiang, Renchi Yang, Donghao Wu

Abstract
. Recommender systems have advanced markedly over the past decade by transforming each user/item into a dense embedding vector with deep learning models. At industrial scale, embedding tables constituted by such vectors of all users/items demand a vast amount of parameters and impose heavy compute and memory overhead during training and inference, hindering model deployment under resource constraints. Existing solutions towards embedding compression either suffer from severely compromised recommendation accuracy or incur considerable computational costs. To mitigate these issues, this paper presents BACO , a fast and effective framework for compressing embedding tables. Unlike traditional ID hashing, BACO is built on the idea of exploiting collaborative signals in user-item interactions for user and item groupings, such that similar users/items share the same embeddings in the codebook. Specifically, we formulate a balanced co-clustering objective that maximizes intra-cluster connectivity while enforcing cluster-volume balance, and unify canonical graph clustering techniques into the framework through rigorous theoretical analyses. To produce effective groupings while averting codebook collapse, BACO instantiates this framework with a principled weighting scheme for users and items, an efficient label propagation solver, as well as secondary user clusters. Our extensive experiments comparing BACO against full models and 18 baselines over benchmark datasets demonstrate that BACO cuts embedding parameters by over 75% with a drop of at most 1.85% in recall, while surpassing the strongest baselines by being up to 346 × 346\times faster.



Technical Report

1.
Introduction

In modern recommender systems, deep learning models
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
have become the go-to approach for recommendations, typically working with embedding tables that map each category feature value, e.g., the ID of a user or item, to a unique vector representation.
However, in industrial-scale scenarios, these embedding tables consume a vast amount of parameters, often reaching hundreds of GB or even TB, due to the need to represent billions of users/items
(
Guo et al. 2021
)
.
For instance, embedding tables deployed in Baidu’s advertising systems may occupy up to 10 TB of storage
(
Xu et al. 2021
)
, while at Meta’s scale, i.e., 3 billion monthly active users worldwide
(
Gupta et al. 2020
)
, the user embedding table in a recommender model can easily reach 715 GB of space for 64-dimensional vectors in 32-bit floating point.
As a consequence, the expansion of embedding tables intensifies storage and hardware demands, thus impeding model scalability and deployment in real production environments
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Coleman et al. 2023
)
.

In recent years,
embedding table compression
(ETC)
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, which seeks to reduce the sizes of embedding tables with minimal degradation in precision, has emerged as a popular choice in industry
(
Wu et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2025
;
Tsang and Ahle 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
and is gaining increasing traction in recommender systems
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Coleman et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2018
)
. Most existing works towards ETC generally follow three compression paradigms:
pre-training
,
in-training
, and
post-training
compressions, where the former generates the mappings for users/items in embedding tables before training recommendation models, while the latter two strategies compress the full embeddings when they are partially or fully learned.
Despite being effective, the in-training/post-training scheme additionally introduces considerable training overhead, and still suffers from substantial memory footprint.

A common treatment for pre-training ETC is to hash the user/item IDs down to a set of buckets with a manageable size through hashing functions
(
Coleman et al. 2023
;
Ghaemmaghami et al. 2022
;
Weinberger et al. 2009
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
.
The users/items mapped to the same bucket will share the same embedding vectors in the reduced embedding tables (a.k.a.
codebooks
).
Although this methodology is computationally efficient, it relies on hashing that is essentially random, which will represent unrelated users/items by the same embeddings, and hence, engendering severe
embedding collisions
and performance degradation
(
Ghaemmaghami et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
.
Subsequent studies alleviate embedding collision issues through the (i) employment of multiple hash functions
(
Shi et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, (ii) incorporation of feature and frequency statistics
(
Coleman et al. 2023
;
Ghaemmaghami et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
, and (iii)
learned hashing
techniques
(
Tsang and Ahle 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024
)
.
These methods either still incur subpar recommendation performance, or require additional user/item information or training costs.
Most importantly, the collaborative signals underlying the user-item interactions are largely overlooked for ETC. Very recently,
Wu et al. 2025
made an attempt to exploit the collaborative information of users/items by reframing the ETC task as a graph clustering problem. They simply apply the highly-efficient
Louvain
algorithm
(
Blondel et al. 2008
)
over the user-item interaction graph to derive clusters of users/items as their buckets, which surprisingly yield remarkable improvements in recommendation quality. Unfortunately,
Louvain
suffers from an inherent drawback of
resolution limit

(
Kim et al. 2022
)
, leading to suboptimal results.

Inspired by the efficacy of leveraging collaborative signals, we make further inroads by presenting
BACO
, a fast and effective solution for ETC via
BA
lanced
CO
-clustering.
Specifically, we first reveal two factors, i.e.,
intra-cluster connectivity
and
cluster-size balance
, that are crucial to clustering for ETC, through an empirical study, which in essence correspond to the embedding collision and
codebook collapse

(
Tsang and Ahle 2023
)
, respectively.
Based thereon, we develop a theoretically-grounded balanced co-clustering framework aiming at optimizing these two factors, and establish its theoretical connections to classic graph clustering algorithms.
Building on the framework, we propose a well-thought-out
hybrid weighting scheme
(HWS) for users and items, and develop a fast optimization solver through local greedy
label propagation

(
Raghavan et al. 2007
)
to circumvent the resolution limit.
To account for the multiple and evolving interests of users,
BACO
further constructs
secondary clusters for users
(SCU) rapidly based upon the primary clusters, thereby overcoming the representation limitation of each user without inducing additional space overhead.
Our comprehensive empirical evaluations and ablation studies manifest that (i) our
BACO
can consistently achieve markedly superior recommendation quality over 18 ETC baselines, while often being highly efficient, and (ii) our proposed HWS and SCU strategies can conspicuously enhance recommendation accuracies even when working with other clustering baselines.

2.
Related Work

We review existing studies on ETC in the sequel and defer reviews on co-clustering and bipartite graph clustering to Appendix
B
.

Hashing-based Methods.

Hashing methods offer a straightforward and efficient means of compressing embedding tables by mapping IDs into a smaller index space using hash functions. For instance, the basic hashing method
(
Weinberger et al. 2009
)
simply employs random function to achieve compression. Although efficient, this approach may introduce collisions.

(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
increases the probability that colliding objects are similar through
LSH
, while
(
Shi et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
employ double hashing combined with other techniques to mitigate collisions.
In contrast,
ROBE

(
Desai et al. 2022
)
employs a more flexible indexing mechanism within compositional embeddings.
Although these hash methods are generally efficient, their weak association with the data makes it difficult to maintain accuracy.

Vector Quantization.

Vector quantization (VQ) maps original embeddings to their most similar meta-embeddings, thereby bridging hash representations and the original data
(
Zhang et al. 2026
)
.
Saec

(
Wu et al. 2020
)
and
MGQE

(
Kang et al. 2020
)
utilize feature frequency to guide the quantization of embeddings. Furthermore,
xLightFM

(
Jiang et al. 2021
)
proposes allocating various numbers of embeddings to different feature categories.
Within multi-codebook frameworks,
LightRec

(
Lian et al. 2020
)
and
LISA

(
Wu et al. 2021
)
aim to differentiate codebooks using distinct mechanisms. In contrast to earlier methods that focus on low-dimensional dense embeddings, recent work
CompresSAE

(
Kasalický et al. 2025
)
introduces a radically different strategy by converting original embeddings into high-dimensional but sparse representations, combining expressive capacity with compression.
Vector quantization introduces significant computational and memory overhead during training, primarily due to the nearest neighbor search and the requirement to retain original embeddings.

Decomposition-based Methods.

Decomposition methods perform soft selection, where each feature aggregates all codebook embeddings using a real-valued index vector.

DHE

(
Kang et al. 2021
)
decomposes the embedding table via hash functions and neural networks, whereas
ANT

(
Liang et al. 2021
)
uses anchor vectors and sparse transformation. Recently, tensor train decomposition (TTD) has been employed to enhance compression by expressing multidimensional data as a product of smaller tensors
(
Wang et al. 2020
;
Yin et al. 2021
;
Xia et al. 2022
)
. However, achieving such high compression ratios entails additional computational costs during decomposition and lookup, potentially slowing down inference.

3.1.
Symbols and Terminology

Symbols.

We model the interactions between a user set
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{|\mathcal{U}|}\}
and an item set
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{|\mathcal{V}|}\}
as a
bipartite network

\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{U}\cup\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where the edge set
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}
consists of all the user-item interactions.
The neighbors of user
u_{i}
(resp. item
v_{j}
) are represented by
\mathcal{N}(u_{i})
(resp.
\mathcal{N}(v_{j})
).
We denote by
\mathbf{B}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{U}|\times|\mathcal{V}|}
the bi-adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}
, where
\mathbf{B}_{i,j}=1
if user
u_{i}
has interacted with item
v_{j}

(u_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
, and 0 otherwise. We use
\footnotesize\textstyle{\mathbf{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\large 0}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}\mathbf{B}\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\hskip 5.95006pt\mathbf{B}^{\top}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\begin{matrix}\mbox{\large 0}\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}\in\{0,1\}^{(|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|)\times(|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|)}
to represent the complete adjacency matrix of
\mathcal{G}
.
The degree of user
u_{i}
(resp. item
v_{j}
) is symbolized by
d(u_{i})
(resp.
d(v_{j})
) and the diagonal degree matrix is denoted by
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|)\times(|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|)}
.

Throughout this paper, we use
\vec{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
(resp.
\vec{\mathbf{v}}_{j}
) to denote the embedding vector of user
u_{i}
(item
v_{j}
).
For notation convenience,
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{U}|\times d}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
represent the embedding vectors of users in
\mathcal{U}
and items in
\mathcal{V}
, respectively, wherein the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{U}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}
) is
\mathbf{U}_{i}=\vec{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}_{i}=\vec{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
). In recommender systems,
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are often referred to as embedding tables of users and items, respectively.
Given a cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
containing users and items, we define
\mathcal{U}_{k}=\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap\mathcal{V}
.
We use
[K]
to denote the set of integers
\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
. Table
1
lists frequently used notions in our paper.

Modularity and CPM.

The
modularity

(
Newman and Girvan 2004
)
quantifies the goodness of a particular division of a network. Formally, given a unipartite network
\mathcal{G}
and clusters
\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}\}
, the modularity is defined by
\footnotesize\textstyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(s_{k}-\gamma\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{d(v_{i})}\right)^{2}}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)}
,
where

\gamma>0
stands for a resolution parameter
(
Reichardt and Bornholdt 2006
)
, and
s_{k}=|\{(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}|v_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\}|
is the actual number of edges within cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
Intuitively speaking,
{\left(\sum_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{d(v_{i})}\right)^{2}}/{|\mathcal{E}|}
can be interpreted as the expected number of edges in cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
In particular, higher (resp. lower) resolutions lead to more (resp. fewer) communities, and a larger modularity value indicates a better partitioning of the network
\mathcal{G}
.

Barber 2007
extended modularity to bipartite networks, formulating
bipartite modularity
as follows:

(1)

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(s_{k}-\gamma\cdot\frac{\sigma^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\sigma^{(v)}_{k}}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)},

where
\sigma^{(u)}_{k}=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}{d(u_{i})}
(resp.
\sigma^{(v)}_{k}=\sum_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}{d(v_{i})}
) denotes the sums of degrees of nodes in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and set
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{V}
).

Problem Statement

Let
\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{(u)}\times d}
(resp.
\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{(v)}\times d}
) be the
codebook
(a.k.a. compressed embedding table) for users (resp. items), where
K^{(u)}\ll|\mathcal{U}|
,
K^{(v)}\ll|\mathcal{V}|
, and each row vector stands for an embedding vector.
The
embedding table compression

f
:
\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[K^{(u)}]
and a mapping
h
:
\mathcal{V}\rightarrow[K^{(v)}]
such that each user
u_{i}
(resp. item
v_{j}

\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}_{f(i)}
(resp.
\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}_{h(j)}
).

The foregoing mappings
f
and

can be equivalently represented by
sketching matrices

\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{U}|\times K^{(u)}}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times K^{(v)}}
, respectively. Each row
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}_{i}
(resp.
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}_{j}
) therein is a one-hot vector, usually called “sketch of user
i
(resp. item
v_{j}
)”
(
Tsang and Ahle 2023
)
. Accordingly,
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}
.

Notice that given sketching matrices
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}
, recommendation models only need to learn codebook
\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}
in the course of training.
As such, the space overhead originally incurred for storing the embedding tables
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
in the recommender systems is reduced from
O((|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|)\cdot d)
to
O(|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|+(K^{(u)}+K^{(v)})\cdot d)
for mappings and codebooks. Given a space budget
B
for the codebooks, i.e., the total number of embedding vectors in codebooks for users and items, the ETC task ensures
K^{(u)}+K^{(v)}\leq B
.

In model training, given a user
u_{i}
(resp. item
v_{j}
), the compressed embedding
\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}_{f(i)}
(resp.
\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}_{h(j)}
) is first retrieved before entering into training. For example, the predicted rating for user
u_{i}
and item
v_{j}
is obtained as
\hat{y}_{i,j}=\langle\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}_{f(i)},\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}_{h(j)}\rangle
, and the loss function like
\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{BPR}}=-\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}{\sum_{v_{k}\in\mathcal{N}(u)}{\sum_{v_{j}\notin\mathcal{N}(u)}}{\ln{\sigma(\hat{y}_{i,k}-\hat{y}_{i,j})}}}+\lambda\cdot\left(\|\mathbf{U}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{V}\|^{2}\right)
, is computed with
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}
accordingly.

3.3.
Construction of Sketching Matrices

There are generally three categories of approaches for constructing the sketching matrices
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}
:
random sketching
,
learned sketching
, and
clustering-based sketching
.

Random sketching simply employs hashing functions as mappings
f
:
\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[K^{(u)}]
and
h
:
\mathcal{V}\rightarrow[K^{(v)}]
. Each user or item is assigned to a random sketch (a random one-hot vector)
(
Weinberger et al. 2009
)
.
Subsequent works
(
Shi et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Desai et al. 2022
;
Tito Svenstrup et al. 2017
)
resort to compositional embeddings, which basically apply two or more hashing functions to the IDs of users and items, such that each user or item is associated with two or more embeddings in the codebook, which can be combined via summation, element-wise multiplication, or concatenation, etc.
Note that this approach yields denser sketching matrices as each row
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}_{i}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}_{j}
comprise multiple non-zero entries.

Instead of generating random sketches, the learned sketching
(
Tsang and Ahle 2023
;
Liang et al. 2024
)
methodology learn the sketch of each user or item by training models to reconstruct sketching matrices.
After every few epochs, sketching matrices
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}
are regenerated upon current embeddings
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}\mathbf{Z}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}
to achieve a better fit.

By interpreting sketching matrices
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}
as node-cluster indicator matrices, e.g.,
\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}_{i,k}=1
if
u_{i}
belongs to the
k
-th user cluster and
0
otherwise, the rationale of clustering-based sketching is to group “similar” users in
\mathcal{U}
and items in
\mathcal{V}
into
K^{(u)}
and
K^{(v)}
clusters, respectively.

GraphHash

(
Wu et al. 2025
)
implements this idea by clustering users and items over the bipartite network structure
\mathcal{G}
, attaining markedly superior effectiveness over random sketching and significantly higher efficiency than learned sketching.

Empirical Study.
To gain deeper insights, we empirically study the correlation between final recommendation performance and the characteristics of sketching matrices produced by various methods from the perspective of clustering. Specifically, we generate sketching matrices using hashing-based methods (
Random
and
Frequency
) and clustering-based methods (

,

From Figure

, when inter-cluster connectivity is excessively high (see methods circled in red), recommendation performance suffers regardless of whether codebook usage (measured by the Gini coefficient) is uniform or uneven (Figures

In contrast,

and

obtain much better recall scores by balancing these two factors.
From the foregoing observations, we can derive the following insight:
the underlying clusters of high-quality sketching matrices should minimize inter-cluster connectivity while avoiding imbalanced cluster sizes.

\mathbf{Y}^{(u)}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(v)}

by mapping the clusters of users and items into consecutive column indices, respectively.

Maximizing Intra-cluster Connectivity.
The first objective in our
BACO
aims to identify clusters
\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}\}
such that the
intra-cluster connectivity
is maximized.
More concretely, users and items that are densely connected via interactions in
\mathcal{E}
should fall into the same clusters since it connotes a high correlation or similarity between users (resp. items). This leads to the maximization of the number of connections within the same clusters, i.e., intra-cluster connectivity, which can be formulated as follows:

(2)

\max_{\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}.

The objective can be further transformed into a trace maximization problem with indicator matrix
\mathbf{Y}
and adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
of
\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}\Leftrightarrow\max_{\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{A}_{i,|\mathcal{U}|+j}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\mathbf{Y}_{i,k}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i,|\mathcal{U}|+j}\cdot\mathbf{Y}_{|\mathcal{U}|+j,k}}

(3)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y})_{k,k}}\Leftrightarrow\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\mathsf{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y}).

Such an optimization task is equivalent to the mincut problem for graph partitioning in the literature
(
Stoer and Wagner 1997
)
, which is proved NP-hard.

Weighted Exclusive Lasso for Size Balance.

As remarked earlier in §
3.3
, in overly imbalanced clusters, substantial users/items with low relevance are likely to be grouped together, which yields severe embedding collision and codebook collapse, and thus, degrades performance.
As a remedy, in
BACO
, we additionally include a weighted exclusive lasso to balance the sizes/volumes of the
K
clusters.

Specifically, instead of treating all users and items equally, we assign a weight
w^{(u)}_{i}
(resp.
w^{(v)}_{j}
) to each user
u_{i}
(item
v_{j}
).
Let
W^{(u)}=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}{{w^{(u)}_{i}}}
and
W^{(v)}=\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{{w^{(v)}_{j}}}
be the total weight of the users and items in
\mathcal{G}
, respectively. Accordingly, we define the volume of a cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
as

(4)

\textsf{vol}(C_{k})=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}{w^{(u)}_{i}}+\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}{{w^{(v)}_{j}}},

which is a summation of the weights of the users and items therein.

Ideally, the volumes of all clusters should be comparable to each other, i.e., minimizing the weighted exclusive lasso:

\displaystyle\min_{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{K}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\textsf{vol}(C_{k})-\frac{W^{(u)}+W^{(v)}}{K}\right)^{2}}\Leftrightarrow\min_{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{K}}{\|\vec{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}\|_{2}^{2}}

(5)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\min_{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{K}}{\mathtt{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\vec{\mathbf{w}}\vec{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y})}.

where
\vec{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{U}|+|\mathcal{V}|}
is a vector containing the weights of users and items, e.g.,
\vec{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=w^{(u)}_{i}
for user
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\vec{\mathbf{w}}_{|\mathcal{U}|+j}=w^{(v)}_{j}
for item
v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
.

Overall Objective.
Combining the foregoing intra-cluster connectivity maximization (Eq. (
3
)) and weighted exclusive lasso minimization (Eq. (
5
)) leads to the overall objective of
BACO
:

(6)

\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\mathtt{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y})-\gamma\cdot\mathtt{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\vec{\mathbf{w}}\vec{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}),

where the coefficient
\gamma
can be used to strengthen the weight of the cluster size balance term.

4.2.
Theoretical Insights

Next, we conduct a theoretical investigation of
BACO
framework, so as to (i) establish its connections to several classic clustering algorithms over bipartite graphs, and (ii) uncover its indirect impact on the downstream user and item embeddings
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
.

Connections to Other Clustering Methods.

We define the Kronecker
\delta
function as follows:

(7)

\delta(u_{i},v_{j})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k},\text{ i.e., }\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\mathbf{Y}_{|\mathcal{U}|+j},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The first intra-cluster connectivity term in Eq. (
6
) can be equivalently expressed by

\displaystyle\mathtt{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y})_{k,k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(u_{i})}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}.

Analogously, the second term
-\gamma\cdot\mathtt{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\vec{\mathbf{w}}\vec{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}\mathbf{Y})
is equal to

-\gamma\cdot\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\vec{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\cdot\vec{\mathbf{w}}_{|\mathcal{U}|+j}\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}.

Along this line, our overall objective in Eq. (
6
) can be rewritten as

(9)

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\left(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot w^{(u)}_{i}\cdot w^{(v)}_{j}\right)\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}.

Together with the above formulation, we are enabled to unify existing clustering algorithms tailored to bipartite graphs including the modularity- and CPM-based
Louvain

(
Blondel et al. 2008
)
and
Leiden

(
Traag et al. 2019
)
, Label Propagation (
LP
)
(
Raghavan et al. 2007
)
, Spectral Co-clustering (
SCC
)
(
Dhillon 2001
)
, and
LPAb

(
Barber and Clark 2009
)
into our
BACO
framework, as summarized in Table
2
, based on their respective optimization functions theoretically analyzed in the following lemmata.

Lemma 4.1.

Given bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}
, the adoption of
Louvain
,
Leiden
, or
LPAb
over
\mathcal{G}
can maximize the following form of the bipartite modularity:
\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\left(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot\frac{d(u_{i})\cdot d(v_{j})}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}
, or the following form of the CPM:
\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\left(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\right)\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}
.

Lemma 4.2.

The optimization objective of both
LP
and
SCC
can be expressed as
\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}
.

It can be observed that the key differences of these algorithms lie in the choices of parameter
\gamma
, weights of users and items, as well as the solver used for optimization.

Impact on User and Item Embeddings.

The overall objecive in Eq. (
9
) can be rewritten as

\max_{\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot w^{(u)}_{i}\cdot w^{(v)}_{j}}.

From the perspective of each user
u_{i}
, if we are given fixed item clusters
\{\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{K}\}
,
u_{i}
is assigned to the user cluster
\mathcal{U}_{k}
when its total
penalized
interaction weight with items in
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is minimized, i.e.,

(10)

\kappa(u_{i})=\underset{1\leq\ell\leq K}{\operatorname{arg}\,\operatorname{max}}\;{\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{\ell}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot w^{(u)}_{i}\cdot w^{(v)}_{j}}}=k.

Accordingly, users
u_{i}
and
u_{l}
are assigned to the same cluster
\mathcal{U}_{k}
if and only if
\kappa(u_{i})=\kappa(u_{l})=k
.
Recall that in the resulting user and item embeddings
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}

and
\mathbf{Z}^{(v)}
(see §
3.2
). The above Eq. (

) implies that users with similar interaction patterns with
K
sets of items are likely to have the same embedding vectors.
A similar conclusion can be made for item embeddings when the user clusters are fixed.

4.3.
Hybrid Weighting Scheme

As pinpointed in Table
2
, previous clustering methods unified into the
BACO
framework all adopt the same weighting functions for users and items in
\mathcal{G}
, either a degree-related function
\frac{d(x)}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}|}}
used in the bipartite modularity or a constant (e.g.,
1
) used in the CPM.
However, this simple strategy neglects the intrinsic distinction of users and items in the context of recommendation systems, and hence, causes flawed cluster assignments.

More precisely, recall that in Eq. (
10
), user
u_{i}
is assigned to cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
if
\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot w^{(u)}_{i}\cdot w^{(v)}_{j}}
is maximal.
When the weight
w^{(v)}_{j}=\frac{d(v_{j})}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}|}}
is adopted for items, user
u_{i}
will be prone to join the cluster of its interacted items with low degrees (i.e., unpopular items) due to the penalty term
-\gamma\cdot w^{(u)}_{i}\cdot\frac{d(v_{j})}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}|}}
, which is counterintuitive. In
BACO
, we view all items interacted by user
u_{i}
equally instead, leading to the following weight for item
v_{j}

(11)

w^{(v)}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{V}|}}.

On the other hand, item
v_{j}
will be merged to cluster
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with maximal
\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot w^{(u)}_{i}\cdot w^{(v)}_{j}}
. In this case, item
v_{j}
will prioritize the cluster of users with lower weights
w^{(u)}_{i}
.
Recall that in real-world scenarios, compared to those who interacted with plenty of items, the interaction with item
v_{j}
from a user
u_{i}
with fewer interactions conveys a stronger preference and interest. Intuitively,
v_{j}
should be more likely to be grouped with
u_{i}
. Given this observation, we resort to the degree-related weight for users, as exemplified below:

(12)

w^{(u)}_{i}=\frac{d(u_{i})}{\sqrt{\sum_{u_{\ell}\in\mathcal{U}}{d(u_{\ell})}}}=\frac{d(u_{i})}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}|}}.

In essence, this label updating rule is to maximize the objective Eq. (

for each target node

4.5.
Generating Secondary Clusters for Users

Although our basic

Complexity Analysis

In Algorithm

To ensure termination over disconnected

T

Similarly, obtaining the secondary cluster labels requires only

6.
Conclusion

This paper presents
BACO
, a simple, fast, and effective framework to compress embedding tables through balanced co-clustering of users and items.
Through maximizing intra-cluster connectivity and enforcing balanced cluster sizes,
BACO
alleviates embedding collisions and codebook collapse, as well as aligns with established graph clustering methodologies.
We further introduce a hybrid weighting scheme, an efficient label-propagation solver that sidesteps resolution limits, and secondary user clusters that expand representational capacity.
Our experiments and ablations demonstrate competitive recommendation quality, substantial parameter reductions, and considerable speedups against strong baselines.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

By the definition of bipartite modularity by
Barber 2007
(Eq. (
1
)), we can derive that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(s_{k}-\gamma\cdot\frac{\sigma^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\sigma^{(v)}_{k}}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}-\gamma\cdot\frac{\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}{d(u_{i})}\cdot\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}{d(v_{j})}}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)}
,
leading to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(s_{k}-\gamma\cdot\frac{\sigma^{(u)}_{k}\cdot\sigma^{(v)}_{k}}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\left({\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}-\gamma\cdot\frac{{d(u_{i})}\cdot{d(v_{j})}}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\left(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot\frac{d(u_{i})\cdot d(v_{j})}{|\mathcal{E}|}\right)\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}.

Likewise, from the definition of bipartite CPM
(
Traag et al. 2011
)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(s_{k}-\gamma\cdot|\mathcal{U}_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{V}_{k}|\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left(\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}-\gamma\cdot\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}}{1}\cdot\sum_{v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}{1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\sum_{u_{i},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\left({\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}-\gamma\cdot{1}\right)}=\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}{\left(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\right)\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}.

As for
LPAb

(
Barber and Clark 2009
)
, it basically leverages label propagation to optimize the modularity by assigning a new label to
u_{i}

l(u_{i})=\underset{1\leq k\leq K}{\operatorname{arg}\,\operatorname{max}}\;{\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\frac{\gamma}{\mathcal{E}}\cdot d(u_{i})\cdot d(v_{j})}}
. It is equivalent to
\max{\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}\left(\mathbf{B}_{i,j}-\gamma\cdot\frac{d(u_{i})\cdot d(v_{j})}{\mathcal{E}}\right)}

which locally maximizes the function and together sum up to an overall objective matching our final form.

(

)
, each step in

k^{\ast}=\underset{1\leq k\leq K}{\operatorname{arg}\,\operatorname{max}}\;{\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap\mathcal{N}(u_{i})}{\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}}

\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(u_{i})}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}
for node
u_{i}

\min_{\mathbf{Y}}\textsf{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}(\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{Y})\Leftrightarrow\min_{\mathbf{Y}}\textsf{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Y})-\textsf{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y})

\max_{\mathbf{Y}}\textsf{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Y})
since
\textsf{Trace}(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Y})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}{d(x)}=|\mathcal{E}|
is a constant. This objective can also be rewritten as
\max_{\mathbf{Y}}{\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(u_{i})}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}\cdot\delta(u_{i},v_{j})}}
by Eq. (

Appendix B
Detailed Related Works

B.1.
Co-Clustering

Co-clustering seeks to reveal associations between row and column clusters through the simultaneous clustering of both dimensions in a data matrix. Initially,
Hartigan 1972
advocated co-clustering of variables and cases, enabling direct interpretation of the resulting clusters.
Dhillon 2001
established a connection between co-clustering and spectral graph partitioning. Regarding the spectral co-clustering algorithm,
Kluger et al. 2003
enabled different cluster numbers per dimension,
Gao et al. 2005
resolved higher-order problems, and
Shi et al. 2010
integrated constraint information. Employing information theory transforms co-clustering into an optimization problem, where different association measures yield distinct algorithms:
(
Govaert 1995
;
Hartigan 1972
)
utilizes a least-squares criterion,
ITCC

(
Dhillon et al. 2003
)
maximizes mutual information, and
BCC

(
Banerjee et al. 2004
)
optimizes the Bregman divergence. Numerous model-based and matrix factorization-based algorithms have also been proposed, as reviewed in detail in previous surveys
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Battaglia et al. 2024
)
. Recently, co-clustering techniques have accelerated improvements in recommender systems. For instance,
Wu et al. 2016
incorporated sampling techniques to accelerate recommendations, while
Feng et al. 2020
improved accuracy by limiting recommended items to the same cluster. However, these methods are typically employed to supplement information and enhance accuracy, with limited focus on addressing the memory constraints of embedding tables.

B.2.
Bipartite Graph Clustering

Bipartite graph clustering is a method for uncovering underlying structural properties in diverse relationship networks
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
. A straightforward approach is to transform the bipartite graph into a unipartite graph, thereby allowing the application of conventional graph clustering methods, e.g.,
(
Newman and Girvan 2004
;
Von Luxburg 2007
)
. Moreover, projection-based methods
(
Melamed 2014
;
Tackx et al. 2017
;
Yang and Shi 2024
)
generate a unipartite graph to yield higher-quality clusters, but this often results in a much denser graph structure. Approaches specifically developed for bipartite graph clustering include spectral clustering
(
Dhillon 2001
;
Kluger et al. 2003
)
, statistical modeling
(
Larremore et al. 2014
;
Yen and Larremore 2020
)
, and graph embeddings
(
Yang et al. 2022
)
. However, the aforementioned methods often involve considerable computational overhead, whereas modularity maximization
(
Blondel et al. 2008
;
Traag et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2022
)
and label propagation
(
Raghavan et al. 2007
;
Taguchi et al. 2020
)
methods are recognized for their computational efficiency. Its influence diffusion-based structure enables continuous and enhanced information exchange between users and items, making it particularly well-suited for recommendation systems. Recently,
Wu et al. 2025
innovatively exploited user-item interaction graphs to compress embedding tables for recommendation tasks. However, their approach simply applies modularity maximization, without adequately considering the unique data characteristics and clustering biases of recommender systems.

Given a set of
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}\}
of
K
disjoint co-clusters, each containing both users and items, we provide the formal mathematical definitions of the
averaged cross-cluster links
(ACCL) and the
Gini coefficient
in Figure
1
as follows:

\textnormal{ACCL}=\frac{\underset{\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{C}}{\sum}\underset{\ u_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{k},v_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}{\sum}{\mathbf{B}_{i,j}}}{\binom{K}{2}},\ \textnormal{Gini}=\frac{2}{K}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\frac{i}{K}-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{i}|\mathcal{C}_{j}|}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}\right).
