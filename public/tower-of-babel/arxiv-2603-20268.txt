McMullen’s Curve, the Weil Locus, and the Hodge Conjecture for Abelian Sixfolds
Amir Mostaed

Abstract
. McMullen’s compact Kobayashi-geodesic curve V V in the Hilbert modular sixfold X L X_{L} meets the Weil locus 𝒲 K \mathcal{W}_{K} of abelian sixfolds with exceptional Hodge classes in H 3 , 3 H^{3,3} in a super-atypical intersection of expected dimension − 2 -2 . We prove V ∩ 𝒲 K V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K} is finite and every point carries a CM structure with field M = K ​ L M=KL of degree 12 12 , via the André–Oort theorem with an independent transcendence proof. The Hodge–Weil classes at these points are absolute Hodge but resist all known algebraicity methods: CM isolation, absent secant geometry, and uncontrolled discriminant obstruct every existing approach. Non-emptiness of V ∩ 𝒲 K V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K} reduces to 2816 2816 explicit equations in ℍ \mathbb{H} , each with finitely many CM solutions, constituting a finite and decidable path toward a new case of the Hodge conjecture.

Introduction

Hodge classes on abelian varieties: the Mumford–Tate group and Weil type

The Hodge conjecture predicts that every rational cohomology class of
type
(p,p)
on a smooth complex projective variety is algebraic.
For abelian varieties the Mumford–Tate group
\MT(A)
governs the
space of Hodge classes: by Mumford’s theorem
[
27
]
, the Hodge
classes on all powers of
A
are exactly the
\MT(A)
-invariants.
Deligne
[
14
, Thm. 2.11]
proved that all such classes are

absolute Hodge
, a powerful rigidity property that nevertheless
falls short of algebraicity.

The deepest open cases occur when
\MT(A)
is small, making the
invariant space large. The theory of
Weil type

[
35
,
25
]

provides the most systematic source of exceptional Hodge classes.
For an imaginary quadratic field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
, a
2g
-dimensional
abelian variety
A
is of
Weil type for
K
if
K\hookrightarrow\End^{0}(A)

acts on
H^{1,0}(A)
with eigenvalues
+i\sqrt{d}
and
-i\sqrt{d}

each of multiplicity
g
. This forces a distinguished
2
-dimensional

\mathbb{Q}
-subspace
W_{K}(A)\subset H^{2g}(A,\mathbb{Q})
of
Hodge–Weil classes
,
exceptional in the sense of Moonen–Zarhin
[
25
]
: they lie outside
the algebra generated by divisor classes when
g\geq 2
.

Positive results on the algebraicity of Weil classes are scarce.
For
g=2
: Weil
[
35
]
treated special cases; Schoen
[
30
]
proved algebraicity for
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})

and a specific polarization class (fourfolds);
Schoen
[
31
]
extended this to arbitrary discriminant
(fourfolds) and trivial discriminant (sixfolds); Markman
[
22
]
settled
the generic fourfold of Weil type with trivial discriminant for
all
K
; and Markman
[
23
, Cor. 1.6.1]
subsequently
resolved the full case, proving algebraicity for every abelian
fourfold of Weil type (all
K
, all discriminants).
For
g=3
: Markman
[
23
, §8.4, §9.3]
proved
algebraicity for discriminant
-1
and all
K
via a
semiregular secant sheaf; outside this locus, the Hodge
conjecture for Weil classes on sixfolds remains completely open.
For related results via the Kuga–Satake construction,
see
[
18
]
.

McMullen’s curve and the Weil locus

The present paper uncovers a new geometrically rigid family of abelian
sixfolds of Weil type, proves their Weil classes are absolute Hodge
yet inaccessible to all known methods, and reduces the existence
question to a finite computation. The family arises from an exceptional
object in the theory of Fuchsian groups.

Among hyperbolic triangle groups
\Delta(p,q,r)\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
the arithmetic ones (commensurable with
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
for a totally
real field
F
) were classified by Takeuchi
[
33
]
: there are
finitely many commensurability classes, all explicit.
For such groups the invariant quaternion algebra

B_{0}:=\mathbb{Q}[\Delta_{0}]
(where
\Delta_{0}=\langle g^{2}:g\in\Delta\rangle
)
splits at exactly one infinite place of the invariant trace field
K_{0}
.

McMullen
[
24
]
studied eleven cocompact triangle groups –
the
Hilbert series
– with the opposite extreme behavior:

B_{0}
splits at
every
infinite place of
K_{0}
,
admitting a matrix model
\Delta_{0}\hookrightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K_{0}})
.
The most remarkable is
\Delta(14,21,42)
. Its invariant trace field is

K_{0}=\mathbb{Q}(\cos\pi/21),\quad[K_{0}:\mathbb{Q}]=6,\quad\Gal(K_{0}/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z},

with integers
\mathcal{O}_{K_{0}}=\mathbb{Z}[t]
,
t=2\cos(\pi/21)
. We set
L:=K_{0}
and write
X_{L}=\mathbb{H}^{6}/\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L})

for the associated Hilbert modular sixfold.

Theorem A

(McMullen
[
24
, Thm. 1.2]
)
.

Every finite-index subgroup of
\Delta(14,21,42)
admits a matrix model
over the ring of integers of its trace field.
In particular,
\Delta_{0}:=\Delta_{0}(14,21,42)
embeds in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L})

as a cocompact subgroup of infinite index
(being a finite-index subgroup of
\Delta(14,21,42)
, which itself
embeds in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L})
with infinite index).

The significance of Theorem
A
is that it places the triangle group

\Delta_{0}(14,21,42)
inside the integer points of a split algebraic
group over a number field — a property shared by arithmetic groups
but achieved here by a non-arithmetic group. It is precisely this
split structure that enables the modular-embedding construction to
produce a holomorphic map into a Hilbert modular variety, rather than
merely into a ball quotient.

Via the Cohen–Wolfart construction
[
11
]
, this embedding yields a

modular embedding

\widetilde{f}_{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}^{6}
, equivariant for

\Delta_{0}
on
\mathbb{H}
and the
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{6})
-twisted action
on
\mathbb{H}^{6}
, where
\sigma_{i}
are the six real embeddings of
L
.
For a suitable finite-index
\Gamma\subset\Delta_{0}
, this descends to a
holomorphic immersion

f:V=\mathbb{H}/\Gamma\longrightarrow X_{L}=\mathbb{H}^{6}/\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L})

into the Hilbert modular sixfold
X_{L}
.

Theorem B

(McMullen
[
24
, Thm. 1.9]
)
.

V\subset X_{L}
is a compact Kobayashi-geodesic curve not contained
in any proper Shimura subvariety of
X_{L}
.

Theorem
B
’s assertion that
V
is not contained in any proper Shimura subvariety of
X_{L}
has a precise translation in Hodge-theoretic terms for the universal abelian sixfold family
\mathcal{A}\to X_{L}
restricted to
V
. For a very general point
v\in V
, the Mumford–Tate group
\MT(A_{v})
is the largest
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic subgroup of
\GL(H^{1}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q}))
fixing all Hodge tensors
(H^{1}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q}))^{\otimes p}\otimes(H^{1}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})^{\vee})^{\otimes q}
for all
p,q\geq 0
.

Theorem C

(Generic Mumford–Tate group)
.

For a Zariski-generic point
v\in V
, the abelian sixfold
A_{v}
satisfies

\MT(A_{v})=\Res_{L/\mathbb{Q}}\SL_{2}.

Thus the generic fiber carries no Hodge class beyond those from real
multiplication and polarization.

The generic Mumford–Tate group is as large as the
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action
permits, so the Hodge ring of a generic fiber is generated by
divisor classes. Points of
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
, by contrast, carry a strictly
smaller Mumford–Tate group — a torus — and a richer Hodge ring
containing the exceptional Weil classes. The transition from generic
to special is the arithmetic heart of the paper.

Each component
f_{i}
of the modular embedding satisfies the
hypergeometric equation
E\bigl(\tfrac{19}{42},\tfrac{3}{7};\tfrac{13}{14}\bigr)
— the same equation for all six
i
, differing
only in monodromy representation. This common ODE is not merely an
analytic curiosity: it is the source of the splitting condition

\ell\equiv 1\pmod{42}
and of the explicit fixed-point
equations that make the Hecke program computable.

Fix an imaginary quadratic field
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
,
d>0
squarefree.
The
Weil locus

\mathcal{W}_{K}\subset X_{L}
is the locus of abelian sixfolds
of Weil type for
K
compatible with the
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action
(Definition
2.3
). By Cattani–Deligne–Kaplan
[
9
]
,

\mathcal{W}_{K}
is a countable union of closed algebraic subvarieties.
Its explicit structure is described in Proposition E below.

Proposition D

(Structure of
\mathcal{W}_{K}
)
.

(i)

Every irreducible component is smooth of codimension
3
in
X_{L}

at every non-CM point.

(ii)

Components are indexed by sign-assignments

\{1,\ldots,6\}=I^{+}\sqcup I^{-}
,
|I^{\pm}|=3
; there are

\binom{6}{3}=20
components, forming four
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})
-orbits
of sizes
6,2,6,6
.

(iii)

For each component, the Hodge–Weil space
W_{K}(A)
is a

2
-dimensional subspace of
H^{6}(A,\mathbb{Q})
whose complexification
lies in
H^{3,3}(A,\mathbb{C})
consisting of
exceptional Hodge classes (Proposition
2.6
).

Part (ii) of Proposition
D
— the four Galois orbits of sizes

6,2,6,6
— reflects the action of
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
on the
set of sign-assignments: the orbit of size
2
consists of the
two “balanced” assignments
(I^{+},I^{-})
stabilised as a set by the
index-
2
subgroup
\langle\sigma^{2}\rangle\subset\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})
, while the three orbits of size
6
account for the
remaining
18
components. Part (iii) identifies the Hodge–Weil
classes as living in
H^{3,3}
, the middle bidegree for a
sixfold— a degree not reached by cup products of divisor classes
when
\mathrm{MT}(A)
is a torus.

Since
\dim V=1
and
\dim\mathcal{W}_{K}=3
, the expected intersection dimension is

\dim V+\dim\mathcal{W}_{K}-\dim X_{L}=1+3-6=-2.

Any non-empty
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is therefore
super-atypical
in the
Zilber–Pink sense
[
29
,
39
]
: a rigid arithmetic phenomenon,
not a generic position.

An expected intersection dimension of
-2
means that in any
one-parameter deformation of either
V
or
\mathcal{W}_{K}
, the perturbed
intersection becomes empty. Any non-empty
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is therefore
isolated in the strongest possible sense: it cannot be produced by
general-position arguments, and the classical tools of intersection
theory give no information about it. This is the context in which
the André–Oort and transcendence methods become essential.

The compositum
M:=KL
is a CM field of degree
12
over
\mathbb{Q}

(
K\cap L=\mathbb{Q}
). For
d\in\{3,7\}
we have
M=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{42})
and

\Gal(M/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/3
.

Main results I: finiteness and explicit height bounds for
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}

The two geometric objects introduced above — McMullen’s geodesic
curve
V\subset X_{L}
and the Weil locus
\mathcal{W}_{K}\subset X_{L}
— meet
in expected dimension
1+3-6=-2
, so any non-empty intersection is
a priori super-atypical. The results of this section make that
rigidity precise. We show first that the curve
V
itself is
arithmetically distinguished: its generic fiber has the largest
Mumford–Tate group the
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action permits, and every
specialization carrying a strictly smaller Mumford–Tate group is
forced to be a CM point. We then show that the intersection

V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is
finite
for every imaginary quadratic
K
,
that every point in it carries a CM structure with endomorphism
algebra equal to the degree-
12
compositum
M=KL
, and that the
resulting CM abelian sixfolds lie in finitely many explicitly
bounded isogeny classes. The key arithmetic input is that
h_{L}=1
,
which makes the Hecke program of Section
5

computationally explicit.

Theorem E

(Finiteness of intersection)
.

For every imaginary quadratic
K
, the intersection
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is finite
(possibly empty). Every point
v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is a CM point with

\End^{0}(A_{v_{0}})=M
, a CM field of degree
12
over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof sketch.

v_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{K}
gives
K\hookrightarrow\End^{0}(A_{v_{0}})
with Weil signature;

v_{0}\in V\subset X_{L}
gives
L\subset\End^{0}(A_{v_{0}})
. Hence

M=KL\subset\End^{0}(A_{v_{0}})
and
[M:\mathbb{Q}]=12=2\dim A_{v_{0}}
, forcing

\End^{0}(A_{v_{0}})=M
. Thus
A_{v_{0}}
is CM.

If
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
were infinite, the forgetful map
\pi:X_{L}\to\mathcal{A}_{6}

sends
V
to a curve
\pi(V)\subset\mathcal{A}_{6}
with a Zariski-dense
set of CM points. By André–Oort
[
34
, Thm. 1.3]
,

\overline{\pi(V)}^{\mathrm{Zar}}
is a special subvariety of

\mathcal{A}_{6}
. Since
\pi\colon X_{L}\to\mathcal{A}_{6}
is a morphism
of Shimura varieties, which is finite onto its image, the preimage of a special subvariety is a finite
union of special subvarieties
[
13
,
16
]
; as
V

is an irreducible component of this preimage,
V
is itself a Shimura
subvariety of
X_{L}
, contradicting Theorem
B
. (See §
3
for the full argument.)

\square

An independent proof via transcendence theory (Wolfart
[
36
,
37
]

and Ax–Schanuel
[
8
, Thm. D]
) is given in §
3.3
.

The proof of Theorem
E
is worth pausing to examine, because it uses two
logically independent routes. The first (via André–Oort and
Tsimerman’s theorem) proceeds by contradiction at the level of
moduli spaces: infinitely many CM points in
V
would force
V
itself
to be a Shimura subvariety, contradicting Theorem
B
. The second (via
Wolfart’s theorem and Ax–Schanuel
[
8
, Thm. D]
) is more direct:
a non-CM point of
V
has
j_{\Gamma}(z_{0})\notin\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, so only
points with algebraic uniformizing coordinates can lie in
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
,
and Wolfart’s theorem identifies these as CM points; there are only
finitely many such. The two proofs illuminate different aspects
of why the intersection is rigid.

Corollary F

(Isogeny classes and height bound)
.

The sixfolds
\{A_{v_{0}}:v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}\}
lie in finitely many isogeny
classes over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. For
d\in\{3,7\}
,

h_{F}(A_{v_{0}})\leq 18^{900}\cdot 42^{30}

(see §
3
for the derivation from
[
2
]
).

The explicit bound
18^{900}\cdot 42^{30}
on the Faltings height is
astronomically large but finite; it is the effective content of
Theorem
E
. It says that even though we cannot yet enumerate

V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
, we know exactly which isogeny class any element would
belong to: one of finitely many, defined over a number field of degree
at most
12
. This transforms the existence question from a geometric
problem into a (very large) finite search.

Main results II: inaccessibility by known methods and reduction to a finite computation

The Hodge–Weil classes
W_{K}(A_{v_{0}})\subset H^{6}(A_{v_{0}},\mathbb{Q})
at any

v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
are absolute Hodge
[
14
, Thm. 2.11]
.
Yet three independent obstructions prevent any known theorem from
proving their algebraicity.

Theorem G

(Inaccessibility of Weil classes)
.

Let
v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
. The classes
W_{K}(A_{v_{0}})
are absolute Hodge
but cannot be shown algebraic by:

(1)

CM isolation and deformation failure.

A_{v_{0}}
is a CM
point, isolated in every positive-dimensional deformation space;
the semiregularity argument of Buchweitz–Flenner used in

[
23
]
requires a family of positive dimension, which
collapses to a point here.

(2)

Absence of
K
-secant structure.
Markman’s construction

[
23
]
is intrinsic to triples

(X\times\widehat{X},\eta,h)
arising from a
K
-secant line on an
abelian threefold
X
. The sixfolds
A_{v_{0}}
do not arise from
such a construction.

(3)

Uncontrolled discriminant.
The
K
-Hermitian form’s
discriminant on
H^{1}(A_{v_{0}},\mathbb{Q})
is not prescribed by the
construction of
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
and is not generically
\pm 1
, so
no existing discriminant-specific theorem
(
[
30
,
31
,
22
,
23
]
) applies.

Each of the three obstructions in Theorem
G
is genuinely independent
of the others. Removing the first (CM isolation) would require a
positive-dimensional family of abelian sixfolds of Weil type through

A_{v_{0}}
; but CM abelian varieties are isolated in their moduli
spaces by definition. Removing the second (absent
K
-secant structure)
would require constructing the secant sheaf
\mathcal{E}
of
[
23
]

without its geometric input — an open problem in sheaf theory.
Removing the third (uncontrolled discriminant) would require a new
algebraicity criterion valid for all discriminants simultaneously.
Any proof of the Hodge conjecture for
A_{v_{0}}
must therefore
bypass all three, not merely one.

Thus any proof of algebraicity for any
v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
would
constitute a genuinely new case of the Hodge conjecture, requiring
either a method to produce cycles at isolated CM points without
deformation, or a new algebraicity criterion for absolute Hodge classes
independent of discriminant.

Despite Theorem
G
, the non-emptiness of
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is decidable in
principle by a finite computation. Section
5
develops:

Proposition H

(Hecke criterion)
.

If there exists a prime
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{42}
and a point

z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
such that
v_{0}:=\widetilde{f}_{0}(z_{0})\in V
is a fixed
point of the Hecke correspondence
T_{\mathfrak{l}}
on
X_{L}
,
and such that the associated Hecke endomorphism
\alpha=\psi\circ\phi
satisfies:

(a)

\alpha
generates
M=L(\sqrt{-d})

(i.e.,
c^{2}-4\ell=-d\lambda^{2}
for some
\lambda\in L^{\times}
,
where
c=\tr_{M/L}(\alpha)
);

(b)

the sign vector
(\mathrm{sgn}\,\sigma_{i}(b))_{i=1}^{6}
for

\alpha=a+b\sqrt{-d}
has exactly three positive entries,

then
v_{0}=\widetilde{f}_{0}(z_{0})\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
. Conversely, every

v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is a CM point (Theorem F), and is expected to arise
from such a Hecke fixed point.

Condition (a) is
not automatic
: the Hecke construction gives

\alpha\notin L
, but the discriminant
c^{2}-4\ell
could lie in

-e\cdot(L^{\times})^{2}
for any squarefree
e\neq d
.
Condition (b) is the Weil signature condition.

Proposition I

(Constant-sign obstruction)
.

If
b\in\mathbb{Q}
(equivalently, if
\sigma_{k}(b)=b
for all
k
), then
the sign pattern
\mathbf{s}=(\mathrm{sgn}\,\sigma_{1}(b),\ldots,\mathrm{sgn}\,\sigma_{6}(b))

is constant
(+\cdots+)
or
(-\cdots-)
, violating the Weil condition

|I^{+}|=3
. Weil-compatible Hecke endomorphisms therefore require

b\in\mathcal{O}_{L}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.

The sign reversal under
\alpha\mapsto\overline{\alpha}
has a geometric
interpretation: it exchanges the two Weil-compatible CM types

\Phi_{I^{+},I^{-}}
and
\Phi_{I^{-},I^{+}}
corresponding to the same
pair of index sets with their roles interchanged. These two types
differ in which half of
H^{1,0}(A_{v_{0}})
carries the

+i\sqrt{d}
-eigenvalue of the
K
-action. In particular, both give
points on the same component of
\mathcal{W}_{K}
, and both yield the same
unoriented CM type.

Theorem J

(Reduction to finite computation)
.

For
\ell=43
(the smallest prime
\equiv 1\pmod{42}
), the question
reduces to solving at most

(\ell+1)\cdot 2^{6}=44\cdot 64=2816

algebraic equations in
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, each degree
\leq 2
in
f_{i}(z_{0})
.
Each equation has either no solution or finitely many CM solutions
(Wolfart’s theorem,
[
37
]
). The computation is finite and explicit.

The prime
43
achieves a simultaneous optimality that the
hypergeometric structure makes precise: it is the smallest prime for
which
\mathbb{F}_{43}^{\times}
contains all
42
nd roots of unity
(since
|\mathbb{F}_{43}^{\times}|=42
), so the six Galois conjugates of

t=2\cos(\pi/21)
are all available as explicit elements of
\mathbb{F}_{43}

without any field extension. This means the
2816
fixed-point
equations can be pre-screened over
\mathbb{F}_{43}
to eliminate the vast
majority before any high-precision numerical work in
\mathbb{H}
.

The CM field
M=KL
controls the geometry of the intersection.

Proposition K

(CM types of
M
)
.

Let
d\in\{3,7\}
so
M=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{42})
.

(i)

\Gal(M/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/3
.

(ii)

There are
64
CM types of
M
, forming
32
conjugate pairs

\{\Phi,\overline{\Phi}\}
; all satisfy
\Phi\neq\overline{\Phi}

(Proposition
4.3
).

(iii)

Exactly
\binom{6}{3}=20
oriented CM types are Weil-compatible
(signature
(3,3)
for
K
), forming
10
conjugate pairs.

(iv)

These
20
oriented CM types are in natural bijection
with the
20
components of
\mathcal{W}_{K}

(Proposition
D
(ii)), both indexed by
sign-assignments
\{1,\ldots,6\}=I^{+}\sqcup I^{-}
,
|I^{\pm}|=3
.

For
\alpha=a+b\sqrt{-d}\in M
,
a,b\in L
,
b\neq 0
, the sign vector

\mathbf{s}=(\mathrm{sgn}\,\sigma_{1}(b),\ldots,\mathrm{sgn}\,\sigma_{6}(b))

satisfies: (i)
\mathbf{s}
and
-\mathbf{s}
give the same unoriented
CM type; (ii) Weil condition
|I^{+}|=3
restricts
\mathbf{s}
to one of

\binom{6}{3}=20
patterns; (iii)
\mathbf{s}
is not determined by the
norm equation alone; conjugation
\alpha\mapsto\overline{\alpha}
reverses all signs.

Open problems.
The full program for proving (or disproving) non-emptiness of

V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
consists of three explicit open problems,
formulated precisely in §
5.7
:

(O1)

Execute the
\ell=43
computation.

Enumerate all solutions
\alpha=a+b\sqrt{-d}\in\mathcal{O}_{M}
to the
system of
2816
fixed-point equations with
a^{2}+db^{2}=43
and

\mathbf{s}(\alpha)
Weil-compatible.

(O2)

Verify the Weil signature.

For any CM point
z_{0}
produced by (O1), compute the CM type of

A_{\widetilde{f}_{0}(z_{0})}
from the period point and confirm
conditions (a)–(b) of Proposition
H
.

(O3)

Prove algebraicity of the Weil classes.

Even granting (O1) and (O2), prove that
W_{K}(A_{v_{0}})
is algebraic,
overcoming the three obstructions of Theorem
G
.

The logical structure of the three problems is serial: (O1) is a
finite computation requiring no new theory; (O2) requires the classical
theory of complex multiplication as in
[
32
]
; and (O3)
requires genuinely new Hodge-theoretic methods. Any mathematician
interested in the Hodge conjecture for abelian sixfolds is invited
to attempt (O1) — a complete and self-contained algorithm is given in
§

McMullen’s curve

The compact Kobayashi geodesic curve

at the heart of
this paper is produced by a modular embedding of the Fuchsian group

\Delta_{0}(14,21,42)

\Delta(14,21,42)
.
The group
\Delta(14,21,42)
belongs to McMullen’s
Hilbert series

[
24
]

splits at every real place of the invariant trace field

forcing a matrix model over the full ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{L}
.
This arithmetic rigidity — absent for the
76
arithmetic triangle groups

, for which

splits at exactly one
real place — is what allows
\Delta_{0}
to embed into
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L})
rather
than merely into
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L}[1/N])
for some integer
N
.
We use the modular embedding to construct the universal family

\mathcal{A}\to V
and to compute its generic Mumford–Tate group
(Corollary
1.6
): for a generic fiber
A_{v}
,

\MT(A_{v})\;=\;\Res_{L/\mathbb{Q}}\SL_{2}.

This computation, which depends on the Zariski density criterion of

[

2

1.1.
Triangle groups and matrix models

Let
\Delta(p,q,r)
be a cocompact hyperbolic triangle group, defined
as the orientation-preserving subgroup of index two in the reflection
group of a hyperbolic triangle with angles
(\pi/p,\pi/q,\pi/r)
,
where
1/p+1/q+1/r<1
.
We regard
\Delta(p,q,r)
as a subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
via the standard
double cover
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
; with this convention it
is generated by elements
a,b,c
satisfying

a^{p}=b^{q}=c^{r}=abc=-I
,
where
-I
is the non-trivial central element of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
The
trace field
of
\Delta(p,q,r)
is

K_{\Delta}\;=\;\mathbb{Q}(\tr\,\Delta)\;=\;\mathbb{Q}\!\left(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{p},\,\cos\tfrac{\pi}{q},\,\cos\tfrac{\pi}{r}\right).

Every cocompact triangle group has a matrix model (i.e., is conjugate
to a subgroup of
\SL_{2}(L^{\prime})
) over some number field
L^{\prime}\supset K_{\Delta}
;
one can always arrange
[L^{\prime}:K_{\Delta}]=2

[
24
, §1]
.
It is unusual, however, for
\Delta(p,q,r)
to admit a model over

K_{\Delta}
itself
[
24
, §1]
.

The algebraic mechanism controlling this is the
invariant
quaternion algebra
.
Set
\Delta_{0}=\langle g^{2}:g\in\Delta\rangle
.
The invariant trace field
L=\mathbb{Q}(\tr\,\Delta_{0})
and the
invariant quaternion algebra
B_{0}
, defined as the

L
-subalgebra of
M_{2}(\mathbb{R})
spanned by
\Delta_{0}
, depend
only on the commensurability class of
\Delta

[
24
, p. 4]
.
By a theorem of Takeuchi,
B_{0}
is a quaternion algebra over
L
.
If
B_{0}
is split (i.e.,
B_{0}\cong M_{2}(L)
as

L
-algebras), then
\SL_{1}(B_{0})\cong\SL_{2}(L)

and the natural inclusion
\Delta_{0}\subset\SL_{1}(B_{0})
provides a matrix
model of
\Delta_{0}
over
L
.
The question of when such a model exists over
K_{\Delta}
(as opposed to
a degree-two extension) is thus governed by whether
B_{0}
splits at
all or just one of the infinite places of
L
.

McMullen
[
24
]
identifies eleven cocompact triangle groups,
called the
Hilbert series
, for which
B_{0}
splits at all infinite
places of
L
and a model over
L
exists.
In the arithmetic case, by contrast,
B_{0}
splits at just one infinite
place of
L
; the two families are therefore disjoint, and together
they provide complementary extremes of the possible splitting behavior

[
24
, p. 8]
.
(The overlap when
\deg L=1
is trivial.)
Takeuchi
[
33
, Thm. 3]
has classified all arithmetic Fuchsian triangle
groups; there are exactly
76
such groups.
The group
\Delta(14,21,42)
does not appear in Takeuchi’s list, as
one verifies directly from Theorem 3 of
[
33
]
. For further results on semi-arithmetic Fuchsian groups, see
[
5
]
.

For the Hilbert series, McMullen establishes the following.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
24
, Thm. 1.1]
)
.

Every group
\Delta(p,q,r)
in the Hilbert series admits a matrix model
over the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K_{\Delta}}
in its trace field
K_{\Delta}
.

The most remarkable group in the Hilbert series is
\Delta(14,21,42)
,
which is the only known triangle group with a
split
invariant
quaternion algebra
B_{0}
.
For this group a stronger integrality statement holds:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
24
, Thm. 1.2]
)
.

Every subgroup
\Gamma
of finite index in
\Delta(14,21,42)
admits
a matrix model over the ring of integers in
\mathbb{Q}(\tr\,\Gamma)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
proceeds by showing that

K_{\Delta}=\mathbb{Q}(\cos\pi/42)
is a quadratic extension of

with Galois group

24

1.5.
Zariski density and the Mumford–Tate group

We now determine the generic Mumford–Tate group of the family

\mathcal{A}|_{V}
.
The key input is the following criterion of McMullen, whose proof
occupies §5 of
[
24
]
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
24
, Thm. 5.1]
)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(L)=(\Res_{L/\mathbb{Q}}\SL_{2})(\mathbb{Q})
.
Then
\Gamma
is Zariski dense in the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic group

\Res_{L/\mathbb{Q}}\SL_{2}
if and only if

(1)

the invariant trace field of
\Gamma
is
L
, and

(2)

\Gamma
is not virtually solvable.

Remark 1.5
.

More formally, Theorem
1.4
asserts Zariski density in
the
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic group
T=\Res_{L/\mathbb{Q}}\SL_{2}
, which satisfies

T(\mathbb{Q})=\SL_{2}(L)
and
T(\mathbb{R})\cong\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{6}
; see
[
24
, §5]
.

Both conditions of Theorem
1.4
hold for every
\Gamma

of finite index in
\Delta_{0}
:
2
2

2

Condition (1), that the invariant trace field of
\Gamma
is
L
,
is stable under passage to finite-index subgroups (the invariant trace
field depends only on the commensurability class).
Condition (2), non-virtual-solvability, holds because
\Delta_{0}
is
cocompact (hence non-elementary) and non-abelian; both properties
persist under finite-index passage.
The Zariski density criterion of Theorem
1.4
is
McMullen’s adaptation of the Margulis–Zimmer criterion for
superrigidity
[
24

: it identifies exactly when
the holonomy of the Gauss–Manin connection is as large as the
RM structure permits, yielding the equality
\MT(A_{v})=\Res_{L/\mathbb{Q}}\SL_{2}

for generic fibers rather than merely the inclusion

.

Upper bound.

2.
The Weil locus

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})
be an imaginary quadratic field.
An abelian sixfold

\mathcal{O}_{L}
-multiplication is of

\End^{0}(A)

\mathcal{O}_{L}

[

]

20

\binom{6}{3}=20

).
Since McMullen’s curve

has dimension

, the expected dimension of

is

§

make precise. For the intersection theory of cycles on

more

[

]
.

Throughout this section we fix an imaginary quadratic field

Since

is totally real, every embedding

2.1.
Abelian sixfolds of Weil type

Let

\mathcal{O}_{L}
-multiplication and

with Riemann form

\varphi
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say

\eta\colon K\hookrightarrow\End^{0}(A)

(i)

\eta
commutes with the
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action on

(ii)

\eta(\sqrt{-d})^{\dagger}=-\eta(\sqrt{-d})
for the
Rosati involution associated to
\varphi

(iii)

the
\mathbb{C}
-linear extension of
\eta(\sqrt{-d})
to

H^{1,0}(A)
has eigenvalues
+i\sqrt{d}
and
-i\sqrt{d}
,
each with multiplicity

[
25

.

Remark 2.2
.

Since
K\cap L=\mathbb{Q}
, the
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action and
\eta(K)
generate a
subalgebra of
\End^{0}(A)

KL

[KL:\mathbb{Q}]=12=2\dim A

Because

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}KL=2\dim A=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}H^{1}(A,\mathbb{Q})

\End^{0}(A)=KL

[

]
.

Set

.
The
Hodge–Weil space
of

where the first map is the natural inclusion of the
K
-exterior power into
the
\mathbb{Q}
-exterior power, and the second is the canonical identification

[
25
, (1.9)]
.
The space

is a rank-
1

K
-submodule of

,
hence
2
-dimensional over
\mathbb{Q}
.
By
[
25
, (1.9)]
, when condition (iii) of
Definition
2.1
holds,

consists entirely
of Hodge classes; these form a

-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-subspace
of
H^{6}(A,\mathbb{Q})
whose complexification lies in

,
i.e.
W_{K}(A)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{C}\subset H^{3,3}(A,\mathbb{C})
.
These classes are

in the sense of

[
25
, (1.4)]
— that is, they are Hodge-invariant but lie outside the

generated by divisor classes—as
proved in
[
25
]
.
Their algebraicity is not known in general
[
25
, (1.9)]
.

2.2.
The Weil locus and its codimension

Definition 2.3
.

The
Weil locus

\mathcal{W}_{K}\;\subset\;X_{L}

is the locus of points
[A]\in X_{L}
for which
A
is of Weil
type for
K
in the sense of Definition
2.1

(in particular,
\eta
is required to commute with the

\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action, as in (i)).
By
[

, Cor. 1.4]

\mathcal{W}_{K}
is a countable union of
closed irreducible algebraic subvarieties of

.

Remark 2.4
.

One may consider

\mathcal{W}:=\bigcup_{K}\mathcal{W}_{K}

over all imaginary quadratic
K
admitting a compatible embedding
into
\End^{0}
as in Definition
2.1
.
This is a countable union of Hodge loci; its density and
accumulation behaviour are more delicate and are not addressed

All arguments below are carried out for the fixed

.

Proposition 2.5
.

Each irreducible component of
\mathcal{W}_{K}
is a smooth subvariety of
codimension
3
in
X_{L}
at every non-CM point.
The components are indexed by sign-assignments

I^{+}\sqcup I^{-}=\{1,\ldots,6\}

|I^{\pm}|=3

\tbinom{6}{3}=20
of them, forming four orbits under
the action of
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
on the set of six embeddings

\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{6}\}

.

Proof.

Codimension.

The period domain of

is
\mathcal{D}=\prod_{i=1}^{6}\mathbb{H}_{i}
,
so
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X_{L}=6

[
24
, §1]
.
At a non-CM point
[A]\in X_{L}
the tangent space decomposes as

T_{[A]}X_{L}\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{i=1}^{6}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}\!\bigl(H^{1,0}_{\sigma_{i}}(A),\,H^{0,1}_{\sigma_{i}}(A)\bigr),

each summand being
1
-dimensional.

Since
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}H^{1}(A,\mathbb{Q})=12
and
[L:\mathbb{Q}]=6
, and since the

\mathcal{O}_{L}
-multiplication is compatible with the principal polarization, the

L
-module
H^{1}(A,\mathbb{Q})
is free of rank
2

[
7
, §9.2]
.
Hence the ring of
L
-linear endomorphisms of
H^{1}(A,\mathbb{Q})
is

\End_{L}(H^{1}(A,\mathbb{Q}))\;\cong\;M_{2}(L).

Any
K
-action
\eta
commuting with
\mathcal{O}_{L}
is in particular

L
-linear, so
J:=\eta(\sqrt{-d})
lies in
M_{2}(L)
.

Fix a sign-assignment
(I^{+},I^{-})
with
|I^{\pm}|=3
.
The operator
J:=\eta(\sqrt{-d})\in M_{2}(L)
satisfies

and
is therefore diagonalizable over
L(\sqrt{-d})
with eigenvalues

\pm\sqrt{-d}
(equivalently, over
\mathbb{C}
with eigenvalues
\pm i\sqrt{d}
).
For
i\in I^{+}
, the condition that the
+i\sqrt{d}
-eigenspace of
\sigma_{i}(J)

contains

is a condition on the period

for
which
H^{1,0}_{\sigma_{i}}(A)
is determined (up to the free choice
of
z_{i}\in\mathbb{H}_{i}
).
For
j\in I^{-}
, the condition that
H^{1,0}_{\sigma_{j}}(A)
is the

-i\sqrt{d}
-eigenspace of
\sigma_{j}(J)
— equivalently, that

is
not

-eigenspace — pins down

once

is fixed.
Thus the three periods

(

) are constrained, giving a

, hence

\codim_{X_{L}}\mathcal{W}_{K}^{I^{+},I^{-}}=6-3=3.

Transversality.

To show
\mathcal{W}_{K}^{I^{+},I^{-}}
is smooth of codimension
3
at a

, we verify that the Jacobian of the
three defining equations has rank
3
.
Since the quotient map
\mathcal{D}\to X_{L}
is étale at non-CM

.
For

be the
(1,2)
-entry of

in the basis

where

spans

then
F_{j}(z^{\prime})=0
if and only if

is the

(-i\sqrt{d})
-eigenline of
\sigma_{j}(J)
, so

\mathcal{W}_{K}^{I^{+},I^{-}}=\{F_{j_{1}}=F_{j_{2}}=F_{j_{3}}=0\}
locally.
At

, we have

\sigma_{j}(J)=\mathrm{diag}(-i\sqrt{d},+i\sqrt{d})

Under the variation

with

(1,2)
-entry of
\sigma_{j}(J)

becomes

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Hermitian pairing between

and
H^{1,0}_{\sigma_{j}}(z)
induced by the
Hodge decomposition and the Riemann form

.
This is non-zero for

Hence

on the

.
Since

are distinct, the differentials

are supported on three different

, hence linearly independent, and
the Jacobian

has rank

.

Orbits.

For the purpose of counting orbits, relabel the six embeddings so that the generator

\sigma
of
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}
acts as
\sigma_{i}\mapsto\sigma_{i+1\pmod{6}}
;
such a labeling exists because
\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})
acts simply transitively on
the set of six embeddings (as

is Galois of degree

with cyclic
group), and we use the labeling of
[
24
, §4]
.
The induced action on
3

\{1,\ldots,6\}
has four

(consecutive, skip-one, and skip-two

) and one of size

(the alternating triples

∎

2.3.
Dimensional analysis

McMullen’s curve
V\subset X_{L}
has
\dim V=1

[
24
, §1]
.
By Proposition
2.5
, each irreducible component

Z\subset\mathcal{W}_{K}
has
\dim Z=3
. Since
\dim X_{L}=6
, the expected
dimension of the intersection is
3
3

3

The expected dimension formula applies the principle that for
subvarieties
V
and
W
of a variety
X
in general position,

\text{edim}(V\cap W)=\dim V+\dim W-\dim X
.
An expected dimension of
-2
means that no solution is predicted
by dimension count: the intersection is
super-atypical

(strictly more than expected from genericity).
This is analogous to the number-theoretic heuristic that a prime
lying in two independent congruence classes modulo
42
has density

1/42^{2}
, not
1/42
: the two conditions over-determine the problem.
Such super-atypical intersections are the subject of
the Zilber–Pink conjecture
[
29
,
39
,
38
]
, of which the
André–Oort theorem is the zero-dimensional special case.
That
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is zero-dimensional (rather than empty)
for generic
K
would already be a strong result; our
Theorem
3.4
shows at least that it is not positive-dimensional.

\text{edim}(V\cap Z)\;=\;\dim V+\dim Z-\dim X_{L}\;=\;1+3-6\;=\;-2.

Any non-empty component of
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
has dimension
\geq 0>-2
,
so the intersection is
super-atypical
: it exceeds the expected dimension
by at least
2
, and its non-emptiness is a rigid arithmetic
phenomenon, not a consequence of general position.

2.4.
Hodge classes on fibers over
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}

Suppose
v\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
is a non-CM point. Let
A_{v}
denote the
corresponding abelian sixfold with
H=H^{1}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})
, Riemann form

\varphi
, and
D_{v}:=\End^{0}(A_{v})\supset KL
.

Proposition 2.6
.

For every non-CM point

, the Hodge–Weil space

is a
2
-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-subspace of
H^{6}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})

consisting entirely of Hodge classes of type
(3,3)
.
These classes are exceptional in the sense of
[
25
, §1.4]
:

W_{K}(A_{v})\;\subset\;B^{3}(A_{v}),\qquad W_{K}(A_{v})\cap D^{3}(A_{v})=0,

where
B^{3}(A_{v})=H^{6}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})^{\Hg(A_{v})}
and
D^{3}(A_{v})\subset H^{6}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})
is the subspace of cup products of three elements of

\NS(A_{v})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
.
In particular,
\Hg(A_{v})\subsetneq\Sp_{D_{v}}(H,\varphi)
.

Proof.

Since
v\in\mathcal{W}_{K}
, condition (iii) of Definition
2.1

holds; by
[
25
, (1.9)]
the space
W_{K}(A_{v})
consists
entirely of Hodge classes, has rank
1
over
K
, hence

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}W_{K}(A_{v})=2
, and
W_{K}(A_{v})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{C}\subset H^{3,3}(A_{v},\mathbb{C})
.

W_{K}(A_{v})
is non-zero: it equals the image of the natural

K
-linear embedding
\bigwedge^{6}_{K}H^{1}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})\hookrightarrow\bigwedge^{6}_{\mathbb{Q}}H^{1}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{6}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})

[
25
, (1.9)]
.

That
W_{K}(A_{v})\subset B^{3}(A_{v})
is immediate: Hodge classes are
by definition fixed by
\Hg(A_{v})
.
Since
W_{K}(A_{v})\neq 0
, the main theorem of
[
25
]
applies:
non-zero Weil classes are exceptional, i.e.
W_{K}(A_{v})\cap D^{3}(A_{v})=0
.
Hence
B^{3}(A_{v})\neq D^{3}(A_{v})
, so
B^{\bullet}(A_{v})\neq D^{\bullet}(A_{v})
.
Since
A_{v}
is of Albert type IV (Definition

),
the Hazama–Murty theorem
[

, (1.8)]
gives

\Hg(A_{v})\subsetneq\Sp_{D_{v}}(H,\varphi)
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The Hodge conjecture for
A_{v}
predicts that every Hodge class—
every element of

\bigoplus_{i}\bigl(H^{2i}(A_{v},\mathbb{Q})\cap H^{i,i}(A_{v},\mathbb{C})\bigr)

—is algebraic. For abelian varieties, classes in
B^{\bullet}(A_{v})
are by definition
Hodge-group-fixed, hence Hodge classes (this is tautological).
By the main theorem of
[
14
]
, every Hodge class on an abelian
variety is an
absolute Hodge
cycle; however, algebraicity remains open.
The subspace
D^{\bullet}(A_{v})
is algebraic: its generators

D^{1}(A_{v})=\NS(A_{v})\otimes\mathbb{Q}
are algebraic by the Lefschetz

(1,1)
theorem, and the cycle class map is a ring homomorphism,
so all cup products of algebraic classes are algebraic.
The remaining obstruction therefore lies in
B^{\bullet}(A_{v})

outside
D^{\bullet}(A_{v})
.
When
\Hg(A_{v})
is the maximal reductive subgroup of

\Sp_{D_{v}}(H,\varphi)
preserving the
K
-eigenspace decomposition
of
H
, one has
B^{3}(A_{v})=D^{3}(A_{v})\oplus W_{K}(A_{v})
and the sole
obstruction is algebraicity of
W_{K}(A_{v})
, requiring an algebraic
cycle of codimension
3
realising a generator

[
25
, (1.9)]
.
For smaller Hodge groups, additional exceptional classes in

B^{3}(A_{v})
may appear.
The algebraicity of
W_{K}(A_{v})
is open for abelian sixfolds of
Albert type IV.

4.3.
Why Markman’s theorem does not apply

Theorem 4.5

(Markman
[
23
, §8.4, §9.3]
)

Let

For every abelian threefold

\mathcal{E}

that deforms

locally
with
(X\times\widehat{X},\eta,h)
over a neighborhood
in the

of Weil type with discriminant
-1

moduli space and the gluing argument of
[
23

\mathcal{E}
deforms over the entire space, implying algebraicity
of the Hodge–Weil classes.

Markman’s argument proceeds as follows.
Let
X
be a polarized abelian threefold and
\widehat{X}
its dual;
set
V:=H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{1}(\widehat{X},\mathbb{Z})

(where

[
23
, §1.2]

A rational
K

to the spinor variety in

\mathbf{P}(S^{+}_{K})
determines an embedding

\eta\colon K\to\End_{\mathbb{Q}}(X\times\widehat{X})
making

(X\times\widehat{X},\eta,h)
a polarized abelian sixfold of Weil type.
Using Orlov’s derived equivalence

[
23
, §1.3]

K

The characteristic class

The semiregularity theorem of Buchweitz–Flenner

[

, §8.4, §9.3]

Observation 4.6
.

Markman’s theorem does not apply to fibers
A_{v_{0}}
over

(1)

A_{v_{0}}
is CM (Proposition

Mumford–Tate group is a torus.
CM points are isolated in every Shimura variety:

A_{v_{0}}

The semiregularity argument requires deforming the sheaf

\mathcal{E}

(2)

Origin of the Hodge–Weil classes.

The Hodge–Weil classes

Fix

5.1.
Hecke correspondences on
X_{L}

Let
\mathfrak{l}\subset\mathcal{O}_{L}
be a prime ideal of norm

N(\mathfrak{l})=\ell
(a rational prime). The

\mathfrak{l}
-Hecke correspondence on
X_{L}
is

T_{\mathfrak{l}}\colon[A]\;\longmapsto\;\bigl\{[A/C]:C\subset A[\mathfrak{l}],\;C\cong\mathcal{O}_{L}/\mathfrak{l}\text{ as }\mathcal{O}_{L}\text{-module}\bigr\}.

On
(\pm\mathbb{H})^{6}
this lifts to the multi-valued map

(1)

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{6})\;\longmapsto\;\bigl\{\,(\sigma_{1}(\gamma)\cdot z_{1},\,\ldots,\,\sigma_{6}(\gamma)\cdot z_{6}):\gamma\in M_{2}^{+}(\mathcal{O}_{L}),\;\det(\gamma)\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathfrak{l}\,\bigr\},

where
\sigma_{i}(\gamma)\cdot z_{i}
denotes the Möbius action of

\sigma_{i}(\gamma)\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on
z_{i}\in\mathbb{H}
, and

\det(\gamma)\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathfrak{l}
denotes equality of ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{L}
.

Remark 5.1
.

Hecke operators on a Hilbert modular variety
X_{L}
are indexed by

ideals

\mathfrak{l}\subset\mathcal{O}_{L}
, not by rational primes.
For a rational prime
\ell
splitting completely in
L
as

\ell\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathfrak{l}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{l}_{6}
(each

), the six operators

are pairwise distinct, and a point of

5.2.
Fixed points are CM points

Proposition 5.2
.

If
[A]\in X_{L}
is a fixed point of
T_{\mathfrak{l}}
for some prime
ideal
\mathfrak{l}\subset\mathcal{O}_{L}
with
N(\mathfrak{l})=\ell
,
then
A
is a CM abelian sixfold with
\End^{0}(A)\supsetneq L
.

Proof.

Since
[A]
is fixed by
T_{\mathfrak{l}}
, there exists an

\mathcal{O}_{L}
-linear isogeny

\phi\colon A\longrightarrow A/C

of degree
\ell=N(\mathfrak{l})
, where
C\subset A[\mathfrak{l}]

is an
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-stable subgroup with

C\cong\mathcal{O}_{L}/\mathfrak{l}
as
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-modules,
together with a principal polarization-preserving isomorphism

\psi\colon A/C\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}A.

Set
\alpha:=\psi\circ\phi\in\End(A)
.

Let
(\,\cdot\,)^{\dagger}
denote the Rosati involution on
\End^{0}(A)

associated to the principal polarization. Since
\psi
is an
isomorphism of principally polarized abelian varieties, it satisfies

\widehat{\psi}=\psi^{-1}
(where
\widehat{\psi}
is the dual isogeny), so

\alpha^{\dagger}=(\psi\circ\phi)^{\dagger}=\widehat{\phi}\circ\widehat{\psi}=\widehat{\phi}\circ\psi^{-1}.

The composition
\widehat{\phi}\circ\phi
equals multiplication by

\deg\phi=\ell
on
A
, hence

\alpha^{\dagger}\circ\alpha=\widehat{\phi}\circ\psi^{-1}\circ\psi\circ\phi=\widehat{\phi}\circ\phi=[\ell].

In
\End^{0}(A)
this reads
\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha=\ell
.

We show that
\alpha\notin L
. Suppose for contradiction that
\alpha\in L\subset\End^{0}(A)
.
Since
L
is totally real, the Rosati involution restricts to the
identity on
L
(the Rosati involution acts as complex conjugation on
any CM subalgebra, and is the identity on the totally real subfield).
Hence
\alpha^{\dagger}=\alpha
, and substituting into

\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha=\ell
(established above) gives

\alpha^{2}=\ell\in\mathbb{Z},

so
\alpha=\sqrt{\ell}\in L
. We show this is impossible for
any rational prime
\ell\notin\{2,3,7\}
.

Since
L=\mathbb{Q}(\cos\pi/21)\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{42})
, the
discriminant of
L/\mathbb{Q}
is divisible only by
2,3,7
.
Every quadratic subfield of
L
must have discriminant dividing

\disc(L/\mathbb{Q})
, so can ramify only at primes in
\{2,3,7\}
.
For any prime
\ell\notin\{2,3,7\}
, the field

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\ell})
has discriminant divisible by
\ell
,
so
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\ell})\not\subset L
and
\sqrt{\ell}\notin L
,
contradicting
\alpha=\sqrt{\ell}\in L
.
Every prime
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{42}
satisfies
\ell\notin\{2,3,7\}
,
so the argument applies to all primes used in the Hecke search.

Hence
\alpha\notin L
, and
\End^{0}(A)\supsetneq L
.

Since
\alpha^{\dagger}\alpha=\ell
and
\alpha\notin L
, the element

\alpha
is not fixed by the Rosati involution. Setting

c:=\alpha+\alpha^{\dagger}\in\End^{0}(A),

the fact that
(\,\cdot\,)^{\dagger}
restricts to the identity on
L

and that
\alpha
commutes with the
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-action (since

\phi
and
\psi
are both
\mathcal{O}_{L}
-linear) implies
c\in L
.
Indeed,
c=\mathrm{tr}_{L(\alpha)/L}(\alpha)
. The minimal
polynomial of
\alpha
over
L
is therefore

X^{2}-cX+\ell=0,\qquad c\in\mathcal{O}_{L},

so
[L(\alpha):L]=2
and
[L(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=12
.

Since
L(\alpha)\subset\End^{0}(A)
is a field extension of
L
of
degree
2
, and
L
is totally real while
\alpha^{\dagger}=c-\alpha\neq\alpha
, the field
L(\alpha)
is a totally imaginary quadratic
extension of the totally real field
L
: that is,
L(\alpha)
is a CM
field of degree
[L(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=12=2\dim A
. Since

\End^{0}(A)
contains a CM field of degree
2\dim A
, the
Mumford–Tate group
\mathrm{MT}(A)
is a torus, so
A
is a CM
abelian variety.
∎

.

Proposition
5.2
gives

5.5.
Why the search terminates: heights, bounds, and finiteness

We now explain why the search described in
§§
5.1
–
5.4
terminates in finite
time regardless of its outcome.
The argument combines three ingredients: the CM type constraint of
Proposition
4.3
(at most
20
oriented Weil-compatible

[

The upshot is that the set of isogeny classes of abelian sixfolds

Height bounds via the Colmez conjecture are in
[
2
]
.

Field of definition and height bound

Every

Since

over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Furthermore, since

Applying

The CM type constraint

We now make explicit the arithmetic constraints that a Hecke
endomorphism

Proposition

with

(8)

\alpha^{2}-c\,\alpha+\ell\;=\;0\qquad\text{over }L,\qquad c:=\tr_{M/L}(\alpha)\in\mathcal{O}_{L},

with

The
trace condition
(
9

the trace

so that

generates

(equivalently, so that

The

constrains the element

appearing in

(3,3)
.
The two examples of §

show that rational

always fail the sign-vector condition and

The norm equation

Since
\End^{0}(A_{v_{0}})=M=L(\sqrt{-d})
(Proposition

The Rosati involution restricts to complex conjugation on

For

(guaranteed by Proposition

)

, so the discriminant of (

which lies in
-d\cdot(L^{\times})^{2}\subset L

Equivalently,
\alpha
generates

if and only if the norm form

represents
\ell
with

\ell=43

\ell\mathcal{O}_{M}=\mathfrak{P}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{P}_{12}

This observation produces a

on the trace

(9)

.
For

and

over

(equivalently

Condition (a) of Remark

\alpha=6+\sqrt{-7}\in\mathcal{O}_{M}
with
N_{M/L}(\alpha)=43

and trace
c=12
.
The discriminant:
c^{2}-4\ell=144-172=-28=-4\cdot 7\cdot 1
.

Again
b=1\in\mathbb{Z}
gives
\sigma_{k}(b)=1>0
for all
k
, sign pattern

(+\,+\,+\,+\,+\,+)
,
|I^{+}|=6
: not Weil-compatible.
Both Examples
5.7
and
5.8
illustrate
the general phenomenon: whenever a solution
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to the norm
equation exists,
b\in\mathbb{Z}
is totally positive in
L
, which always
gives
|I^{+}|=6
and misses all
20
Weil-compatible types.
A Weil-compatible solution requires
b\in\mathcal{O}_{L}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.

Example 5.9

(Sign-pattern constraint for
d=3
)
.

To illustrate the sign-pattern obstruction, consider
b=1+2t\in\mathcal{O}_{L}
,
where
t=2\cos(\pi/21)
is the standard generator of
\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathbb{Z}[t]
.
The six images are

\sigma_{k}(1+2t)\;=\;1+4\cos(k\pi/21)\qquad k\in\{1,5,11,13,17,19\},

which is positive if and only if
\cos(k\pi/21)>-\tfrac{1}{4}
,
i.e.,
k\pi/21<\arccos(-\tfrac{1}{4})\approx 1.823
rad,
i.e.,
k\leq 11
.
Thus
\sigma_{1}(b),\sigma_{5}(b),\sigma_{11}(b)>0
and

\sigma_{13}(b),\sigma_{17}(b),\sigma_{19}(b)<0
,
giving sign pattern
(+\,+\,+\,-\,-\,-)
and
|I^{+}|=3
:
a Weil-compatible sign pattern with
I^{+}=\{1,5,11\}
.

However,
b=1+2t
does
not
satisfy the norm equation

a^{2}+3b^{2}=43
over
L
: the value
43-3\,\sigma_{k}(b)^{2}
is
negative at
k=1
and
k=5
(approximately
-30.7
and
-3.4

respectively), so no
a\in L
with real embeddings can solve the
equation with this choice of
b
.
The two requirements — correct sign pattern
and
norm
equation satisfied — must be met simultaneously, and this requires
a genuine computation in
\mathcal{O}_{L}
.

The programme of §§
5.3
–
5.6

reduces the search for
v_{0}\in V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
to three open computational
and theoretical problems.

Open problem

(O1). Execute the
\ell=43
computation.

For each squarefree
d\in\{3,7\}
and each coset representative

\gamma_{j}
in Step 2 of §
5.3
,
find all elements
\alpha\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{L})\gamma_{j}
satisfying
the fixed-point equations (
2
) with

\alpha=a+b\sqrt{-d}\in\mathcal{O}_{M}
,
a^{2}+db^{2}=43
,
and
\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{1}(b),\ldots,\sigma_{6}(b))
having
exactly three positive entries.
Examples
5.7
–
5.9
show that
rational solutions fail and that the sign-pattern and norm-equation
constraints are jointly non-trivial.

Open problem

(O2). Verify the Weil signature condition.

For any CM fixed point
z_{0}
produced by (O1), compute the CM type

\Phi
of
A_{\widetilde{f}_{0}(z_{0})}
directly from the period point

v_{0}=\widetilde{f}_{0}(z_{0})\in V
.
The sign vector
(\mathrm{sgn}\,\sigma_{i}(b))
is a
necessary

condition for Weil compatibility (Remark
5.6
),
but the CM type is a property of the period and requires independent
verification.
This is condition (b) of Remark
5.3
, the step not
accessible from the Hecke construction alone.

Open problem

(O3). Prove algebraicity of
\widehat{HW}(A_{v_{0}})
.

Even granting (O1)–(O2), two independent obstructions prevent a
direct appeal to known algebraicity results.

First
: Markman’s algebraicity theorem
requires the discriminant of the transcendental lattice to equal

-1
; the discriminant of our construction is uncontrolled.

Second
: even if the discriminant were
-1
, the CM
isolation of
A_{v_{0}}
(Proposition
3.1
) prevents
the semiregularity argument underlying Markman’s proof from
applying: the proof deforms the secant sheaf
\mathcal{E}

over a positive-dimensional family of Weil-type abelian sixfolds,
but
A_{v_{0}}
is an isolated point in that moduli space.
This is the obstruction of Observation
4.6
(1),
independent of the discriminant.
These are genuinely distinct obstructions.
See also Observation
4.6
.

Concluding remarks and outlook

This paper has assembled a program for attacking the Hodge conjecture
for the specific and explicitly described CM abelian sixfolds

A_{v_{0}}
arising over
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
.
Let us collect the logical dependencies.

McMullen’s non-Shimura geodesic curve
V\subset X_{L}
(Theorem
1.3
)
is the geometric input: a curve that is rigid, non-arithmetic, and
parametrized by a hypergeometric differential equation whose monodromy
group is
\Delta_{0}(14,21,42)
.
The Weil locus
\mathcal{W}_{K}\subset X_{L}
(§
2
) provides the
cohomological condition: the
20
-component hypersurface of codimension
3

whose points carry exceptional Hodge classes in
H^{3,3}
.
The expected-dimension calculation
1+3-6=-2
(§
2.3
)
makes any intersection
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
a rigid arithmetic
phenomenon: it cannot arise from general position, and its
non-emptiness is genuinely open (O1–O2 of §
5.7
).
The finiteness of
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
(Theorem
3.4
) converts
this openness into a finite, in principle decidable, computation:
the Hecke search of §
5.3
over
44\times 64=2816

algebraic systems for the prime
\ell=43
.

The Hodge conjecture itself (O3) remains open beyond this computation,
and the three independent obstructions isolated in
Observation
4.6
and §
4.4

show that no currently available method—deformation theory,
spinor geometry, or CM theory alone—suffices to close it.
What the present work provides is a precise and computable

location
: if the Hodge conjecture is to be proved for a
genuinely new family of abelian sixfolds by some future method,
the fibers
A_{v_{0}}
over
V\cap\mathcal{W}_{K}
are among the most
explicitly described candidates in the literature.
They are defined over a fixed degree-
12
number field,
their CM type is one of at most
20
explicitly listed possibilities,
their Faltings height is bounded by
18^{900}\cdot 42^{30}
,
and the algebraic equations they must satisfy are written down in
§
5.3
.

The three open problems O1–O3 thus
represent a division of labor: O1 is a finite explicit computation
requiring an implementation of the Hecke search;
O2 is an arithmetic question about CM types of abelian varieties
with real multiplication, accessible by the theory of complex
multiplication
[
32
]
;
and O3 is the genuinely new Hodge-theoretic question that motivates
the entire program.
Progress on any one of them would be significant.
Progress on all three would resolve an instance of the Hodge
conjecture for abelian sixfolds that is currently out of reach.
