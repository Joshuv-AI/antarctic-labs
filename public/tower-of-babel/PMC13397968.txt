Associations between a psychosocial intervention and quality of life and caregiver-related outcomes in family caregivers of people with dementia: the Danish DemTool trial
Authors: Emma Kjær Pedersen, Ann Nielsen, Dagný Rós Nicolaisdóttir, Laila Øksnebjerg, Karen Tannebæk, Janet Janbek, Gunhild Waldemar, T. Rune Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with dementia often need comprehensive support, depending on the stage of the disease, from both family members and healthcare professionals. Caring for a person with dementia can be stressful and impact the caregiver’s health and wellbeing. Studies show that family caregivers of people with dementia frequently experience stress, depression, and reduced quality of life. We aimed to assess the association between the psychosocial intervention (DemTool) and caregiver wellbeing and quality of life among family caregivers of people with dementia.MethodDemTool trial was a pragmatic, cluster-controlled trial. The intervention was delivered by primary care dementia coordinators across 30 Danish municipalities from 2020 to 2023, with 15 serving as the intervention group and 15 as treatment as usual. The primary outcome measures were the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Caregiver Distress Scale (NPI-D) and the European Quality of Life Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS). Both measures were completed at baseline and follow-up, alongside secondary quality of life outcomes. To estimate group differences in primary and secondary outcomes, we applied analysis of variance (ANOVA) and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).ResultsA total of 245 family caregivers were included in the study (181 in the intervention group and 64 in the treatment-as-usual group). Most participants were females caring for a spouse. Baseline scores for caregiver wellbeing and quality of life were similar across groups. The DemTool intervention was associated with a significant between-group difference in change from baseline in caregiver-related quality of life as measured by the Carer Experience Scale (CES), favoring the intervention. This was evident in the analysis adjusted for baseline scores (p = 0.05) and remained significant when further adjusting for caregiver-related covariates (p = 0.02). No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for the remaining wellbeing and quality-of-life outcomes.ConclusionNo effects of DemTool were found on traditional health-related quality-of-life outcomes. However, a positive association was found between the intervention and the CES, underlining the importance of selecting outcomes that are directly related to the targeted effects of the intervention.Trial registrationThe study protocol was registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov system, registration number: NCT07355829.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07835-7.

Background

Dementia represents a significant global public health challenge, affecting more than 55 million people worldwide [1]. In Denmark, approximately 43,000 persons aged 65 years and above are living with a registered dementia diagnosis (2025), representing a prevalence of approximately 3.4% within the population. The estimated number of those living with dementia is, however, higher at around 103,000 individuals [2, 3]. Dementia is a complex syndrome characterized by a gradual onset and an increasing decline in cognitive functions. It may also include a spectrum of behavioral and psychological symptoms, including depressive mood and aggression [4]. For the people living with dementia, these changes can profoundly affect everyday functioning, sense of self, and participation in social life [5]. As a result, people with dementia often need comprehensive support, from family members [7, 8] and healthcare professionals [6], depending on the stage of the disease. For family caregivers, providing this support can involve emotional and practical demands, as well as changes to their own everyday life and wellbeing [7].

Family caregivers may be defined as individuals who provide unpaid and ongoing assistance to a family member with a chronic illness or disability, or to an older adult who is unable to manage independently [8]. As dementia progresses, it can profoundly affect relationship dynamics, leading to a reduced sense of emotional connection and intimacy [9, 10]. Moreover, a decline in shared activities may exacerbate the challenges family caregivers face in their daily lives [9]. Alongside family members, support from patient organizations and the broader civil society plays an increasingly important role in sustaining the care and wellbeing of people living with dementia [11].

There is a growing number of interventions focusing on improving quality of life and reducing psychological morbidity of family caregivers of people with dementia [12, 13]. High caregiver burden among family caregivers of people with dementia has been associated with reduced quality of life, stress, depression, and increased physical morbidity and mortality [8, 14]. Consequently, supportive interventions targeting caregiver wellbeing are of substantial clinical relevance [15]. Although prior research reports that psychosocial interventions for caregivers are generally effective in producing clinically and socially meaningful improvements in psychological wellbeing, such as reduced caregiver burden and depressive symptoms [15, 16], some interventions have been less successful. Caregiver burden and caregiver-related quality of life are related, but conceptually distinct, constructs and may therefore require different outcome measures [15]. Previous psychosocial intervention studies, including the Danish Alzheimer Intervention Study (DAISY) at the Danish Dementia Research Centre [17], did not find significant effects on family caregivers’ health-related quality of life or depressive symptoms in a randomized controlled trial [18]. This highlights the ongoing need to develop and evaluate evidence-based psychosocial interventions that can support family caregivers in managing their caregiver role and enhance their quality of life [19].

The ‘DemTool – supporting life with dementia’ was developed in Denmark by the Danish Dementia Research Centre with a focus on improving the quality of life of people with dementia and their family caregivers. DemTool was developed to support professional caregivers in Danish municipalities [20, 21]. In Denmark, post-diagnostic dementia support is commonly provided by primary care dementia coordinators, who typically offer counseling, psychoeducation, and support to both people living with dementia and their family caregivers. Primary care dementia coordinators usually come from backgrounds as occupational therapists, nurses, or nursing assistants with specialized training in dementia [22]. Accordingly, DemTool was designed for use by professionals responsible for dementia counseling and psychoeducation in primary care settings. DemTool comprises supportive material for seven activities, including individual counseling and several group-based activities, delivered as lectures, group courses, and support groups. The various activities and tools in DemTool are separate packages that can be offered as single or combined interventions, as they can be tailored to meet the needs and preferences of both groups and individuals. DemTool focuses on educating and counseling people with dementia and their family caregivers through professional oral presentations, supervision, and informational booklets, incorporating evidence from previous research [21]. The interventions and tools in DemTool are outlined in Fig. 1.

DemTool builds, among other sources, on experiences and results from the DAISY study [23–25], as it was developed to be personalized and flexible to accommodate different types and stages of dementia. While DAISY provided valuable insights into supporting family caregivers [18, 24, 25], DemTool builds on this foundation by offering a more adaptable approach tailored to the individual needs of people with dementia as well as family caregivers. Investigating associations between the DemTool intervention and caregiver wellbeing may provide important knowledge for future psychosocial intervention programs targeting family caregivers of people with dementia [26].

Aim

This study aimed to assess the association between the psychosocial intervention (DemTool) and caregiver wellbeing and quality of life among family caregivers of people with dementia (Fig. 1).

Methods

DemTool trial design

DemTool was a pragmatic, non-blinded, non-randomized controlled cluster trial of a psychosocial intervention, delivered by primary care dementia coordinators in 30 Danish municipalities from 2020 to 2023 in a naturalistic setting [20]. From 2017 to 2018, the Danish Dementia Research Centre surveyed seven Danish municipalities to investigate the needs for various psychosocial intervention activities among people with dementia and their family caregivers. The survey further identified the municipalities’ needs for implementing a comprehensive intervention. Experiences gained from this phase and form the DAISY study [25] guided the selection of activities in DemTool and implementation support for the DemTool trial. Although the quality of standard social services following a dementia diagnosis is generally high in Denmark, both for the person with dementia and for the family caregiver [27], one of the main purposes of developing DemTool was to ensure quality assurance and consistency in municipal social support across all Danish municipalities.

The trial was conducted in collaboration with primary care dementia coordinators in the municipalities, where each municipality served as a separate cluster. The DemTool trial included both people with dementia and their family caregivers. This study only examined outcomes among family caregivers. The assessment of DemTool was based on caregiver outcomes at the individual level, with allocation and intervention implemented at the cluster level. Clusters were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or treatment-as-usual group.

Allocation was conducted by the Danish Dementia Research Centre, and guided by region and population size, to ensure a nationally representative sample. Blinding was not feasible in this pragmatic trial due to the organizational level of delivery. Several months later, additional municipalities were invited to participate as treatment-as-usual; as such, allocation was not concealed, and recruitment was not blinded to group assignment.

Primary care dementia coordinators in the intervention clusters received materials and training in the DemTool intervention from the Danish Dementia Research Centre, e.g., reflection sheets and short films for each of the activities. The Danish Dementia Research Centre trained several primary care dementia coordinators from each municipality. However, each municipality selected one primary care dementia coordinator as a facilitator to represent their municipality, ensuring a local expert on the DemTool intervention. The Danish Dementia Research Centre also provided ad hoc supervision for primary care dementia coordinators who required further support and assistance with adjusting and adapting DemTool. The study protocol was registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov system on December 10, 2025, with registration number: NCT07355829.

Clusters

The trial included 15 municipalities in the intervention group and 15 municipalities in the treatment-as-usual group. Eligible clusters included Danish municipalities that responded to Danish Dementia Research Centre with a declaration of interest, outlining their current activities for people with dementia and their family caregivers. For the intervention group, a total of 22 municipalities expressed interest, and 15 were selected for the intervention.

For the treatment-as-usual group, the seven municipalities excluded from the intervention group were initially invited to participate, but four declined. To reach a total of 15 municipalities for the treatment-as-usual group, the remaining ones were recruited through a network of primary care dementia coordinators across all Danish municipalities. Primary dementia coordinators in the 30 municipalities managed recruitment and enrollment processes for family caregivers and people with dementia.

As such, municipalities were not selected through probabilistic sampling or randomized cluster allocation, but through a pragmatic recruitment process based on expressions of interest and implementation feasibility.

Intervention and treatment-as-usual group

Participants in both groups were enrolled based on their caregiving context and support needs. The inclusion criteria for family caregivers were age ≥ 18 years at enrollment, providing care for a family member with dementia, and speaking and understanding Danish. Participants were excluded if they were unable to give informed consent for the trial. Primary care dementia coordinators in participating municipalities identified and invited eligible persons with dementia and family caregivers through their daily community work. Information on non-responders was not systematically collected. For this study, we only included family caregivers who had received one or more caregiver-specific intervention components and fully responded with complete data on primary outcome measures (see Fig. 2).

Caregivers within each cluster received either the DemTool intervention or treatment-as-usual, depending on the cluster allocation. Participants in the intervention group received one or two of the three interventions targeting family caregivers. The extent of intervention was tailored to caregiver burden and needs, as assessed by the local primary care dementia coordinator. The three family caregiver interventions are outlined in Fig. 1, in which the interventions labelled 5, 6, and 7 corresponds to: (1) a support group for family caregivers, aiming to reduce caregiver burden and promote coping through professional and peer support; (2) a group course consisting of six sessions, aiming to provide information on e.g., dementia diseases, everyday life with dementia, and planning the future; and (3) a group course consisting of five sessions, aiming to provide information on dementia diseases, moving into a nursing home, person-centered care, and communication.

Family caregivers in the treatment-as-usual group had access to the available health and social services in their municipality, including support and information from primary care dementia coordinators, support groups, hotlines provided by patient associations, and respite care for the person with dementia in a home or daycare center. The 15 municipalities in the treatment-as-usual group were on a waiting list to receive the DemTool materials, including reflection sheets for people with dementia and their family caregivers, along with PowerPoint presentations and a suggested order for the topics covered in each session for the primary care dementia coordinators. They received the materials after the primary care dementia coordinators collected baseline and follow-up data from family caregivers and people with dementia (Fig. 2).

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes were change from baseline in the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Caregiver Distress Scale (NPI-D) [28] and the European Quality of Life Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS) [29]. Given that caregiver quality of life is a multidimensional construct [30], we included two primary outcomes to assess both the caregiver distress related to neuropsychiatric symptoms and quality of life. Further, we included secondary and exploratory outcomes capturing related concepts of quality of life, such as current mental wellbeing, loneliness, and caregiver-related quality of life. The NPI-D quantifies caregiver distress regarding 12 symptom domains: delusions, hallucinations, dysphoria/depression, agitation/aggression, anxiety, apathy/indifference, euphoria/elation, irritability/lability, disinhibition, and aberrant motor behaviors [28]. Each symptom’s emotional or psychological distress is rated on a 6-point Likert scale. A total distress score is calculated by summing the symptoms across all domains. If a symptom is absent, its distress level is rated as 0 [28]. NPI-D has a score range of 0–60, with higher scores indicating greater caregiver distress [28].

The EQ VAS measures self-rated health on a vertical visual analogue scale from 0 to 100, where the endpoints are labelled ‘The best health you can imagine’ and ‘The worst health you can imagine’ [29]. Higher numbers reflect better subjective health.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes were change from baseline in the 5-level EQ-5D version (EQ-5D-5 L) [31–33], World Health Organization Wellbeing Index (WHO-5) [34, 35], University of California, Los Angeles Three-Item Loneliness Scale (UCLA 3-item) [36, 37], and the Carer Experience Scale (CES) [38–40].

The EQ-5D-5 L assesses five domains: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression [31]. It is often used with the EQ VAS and measures self-reported health-related quality of life. The EQ-5D-5 L rates its domains on a 5-point Likert scale and describes 3125 health states [32]. Completing it generates a one-digit number for each dimension, which can be converted into a utility score. The closer the utility value is to 1, the better the health state. The EQ-5D-5 L utility ranges from − 0.757 to 1 [33].

WHO-5 assesses subjective mental wellbeing over the past two weeks [34, 35]. It consists of five statements, each rated on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 to 5. These ratings represent different levels of wellbeing. The final score can range from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating better wellbeing [34].

The UCLA 3-item scale assesses feelings of loneliness or social isolation via three questions on relational and social connectedness, and self-perceived isolation, rated on a 3-point Likert scale. Scores range from 3 to 9, with higher scores reflecting greater loneliness. Scores of 3 to 5 are classified as “not lonely” and 6 to 9 as “lonely.” [36, 37].

CES is a measure of caregiving experience focusing on six domains: activities outside caring, social support, institutional support, positive aspects of caring, control over caring, and the relationship with the care recipient. CES uses a 3-point Likert scale for attributes indicating an amount or a frequency. A total index score, calculated by adding preference weights, ranges from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates the poorest and 100 the best caregiving experience [38–40].

Procedure for baseline and follow-up assessments

All family caregivers were offered to complete the baseline- and follow-up assessments via online questionnaires, in a printed paper version, or through telephone interviews. Outcome assessors conducting the telephone interviews were not blinded to group allocation.

Baseline data for the intervention group were collected 1–14 days before the first intervention. Follow-up data were collected within a month of the last intervention.

Baseline data for the treatment-as-usual group were collected at the time of inclusion in the DemTool trial. Follow-up data were collected two months after inclusion.

The baseline assessment included information on the characteristics of family caregivers and the person with dementia, as well as the primary and secondary outcome measures. The follow-up questionnaire included the primary and secondary outcome measures.

Statistical analysis and data management

We tested the significance of group differences in change from baseline in primary outcomes and secondary outcomes using analyses of variance (ANOVA) and analyses of covariance (ANCOVA). First, we performed an unadjusted analysis. Then, we conducted an analysis adjusted for baseline scores to account for initial group differences and improve the precision of the between-group comparisons [41, 42]. This adjustment was important, as participants with higher baseline scores may have a limited capacity for improvement. In the fully adjusted analysis, we controlled for follow-up time interval, relationship to the person with dementia, level of education, time since diagnosis, caregiver sex, and caregiver age. These covariates were chosen based on a priori hypotheses and on existing literature suggesting their relevance for caregiver affective outcomes [14, 43], as well as preliminary empirical evidence from our research indicating their influence in this population [44]. Model assumptions were evaluated by examining the normality of residuals and plotting residuals against fitted values. This analysis did not indicate any violations of model assumptions.

Education was split into three categories based on the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) 2011 levels [45]. The coding was as follows: Primary education only (primary, equivalent to ISCED 1-2), completed high school or vocational training (secondary, equivalent to ISCED 3-4), and completed higher education (tertiary, equivalent to ISCED 5, 6, and 7). All data management and statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide, Version 8. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were reported where relevant. We conducted a power calculation, see Appendix 1. This paper follows the CONSORT guidelines [46].

Results

The study encompassed 30 municipality-level clusters, of which 15 received the intervention and 15 were in the treatment-as-usual group. Table 1 delineates the baseline characteristics of family caregivers and people with dementia. A total of 181 caregivers from the intervention group and 64 family caregivers from the treatment-as-usual group were included in the final analysis sample. The mean interval between baseline assessment and follow-up was approximately 60 days in both groups, although variability was greater in the intervention group (SD ± 43 days) than in the treatment-as-usual group (SD ± 1 day).

The mean age of family caregivers was 69 years (SD: 11.6) in the intervention group and 71 years (SD: 9.2) in the treatment-as-usual group. In the intervention group, 78% of family caregivers were females, as 72% were females in the treatment-as-usual group. In the intervention group, 68% were spouses or partners of the person with dementia, whereas 87.5% had that relation in the treatment-as-usual group. In both groups, family caregivers generally possessed a tertiary level of education. See Table 1 for full baseline characteristics.

The family caregivers were caring for people with dementia, with a mean age of 79 years (SD: 8.1) in the intervention group and 76 years (SD: 7.2) in the treatment-as-usual group. Most of the people with dementia were community-dwelling, as of 87% in the intervention group and 83% in the treatment-as-usual group. Nearly half of those in the intervention group had received a dementia diagnosis within the previous year, while those in the treatment-as-usual group had lived with the diagnosis for three years or more. At baseline, the mean EQ-VAS score was 76.0 in the intervention group and 75.7 in the treatment-as-usual group (Table 1).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between the Danish DemTool intervention and quality of life and caregiver wellbeing in family caregivers of people with dementia within a naturalistic community-based setting, compared to treatment-as-usual. Although no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for the primary outcomes (NPI-D and EQ VAS), statistically significant differences in CES scores were observed in the adjusted analysis. As the CES measures caregiver-related quality of life and caregiver experiences, these findings may indicate that the intervention was more closely associated with caregivers’ coping and perceived capacity within their caregiving role than with broader health-related quality-of-life outcomes. One possible explanation for the limited differences observed on broader measures, such as EQ-5D-5 L, EQ-VAS, and WHO-5, is that these instruments may be less sensitive to short-term or caregiver-specific changes. However, such measures may still be highly relevant depending on the aims and focus of the intervention and provide an important basis for comparison across studies and intervention types. As argued in previous reviews, generic health-related quality-of-life instruments may overlook nuanced changes in caregiver-specific domains [47, 48]. Further, a plausible explanation for this pattern is the misalignment between the objectives of the intervention and the characteristics of the more general health-related quality of life scales. DemTool was specifically designed to address aspects of everyday life and relational dynamics between family caregivers and people with dementia, domains insufficiently represented by generic quality of life scales, such as EQ VAS, EQ-5D-5 L, and WHO-5. In contrast, CES directly assesses the relational, emotional, and practical dimensions of caregiving [40], which the intervention aimed to address. The observed differences in CES scores may suggest a closer alignment between the domains targeted by the intervention and those assessed by the scale. As dementia is a complex syndrome which varies in its course from person to person, the burden perceived by each caregiver will therefore differ, and benefits of the intervention will vary as well. Defining the concept of burden can be challenging, making changes difficult to detect and measure [49, 50].

Compared to prior interventions, it is noteworthy that the STrAtegies for RelaTives (START) study [43] demonstrated significant reductions in caregiver distress compared to a treatment-as-usual group. This reduction was attributed to a structured, manual-based coping intervention. START was administered on an individual basis and customized to the caregiver’s specific needs, potentially accounting for its comparatively enhanced efficacy. Although our intervention was adapted to local contexts and executed by dementia coordinators within a community setting, additional individualization may have been necessary to maximize its effectiveness. It is important to note that the DemTool intervention was compared to treatment-as-usual, which might already include high-quality, established social activities, potentially reducing between-group differences. Since the DemTool intervention was delivered by primary care dementia coordinators employed in Danish municipalities, our results may be generalizable to similar settings in other Nordic or welfare-based healthcare systems.

The lack of statistically significant results in several outcome domains also invites reflection on methodological factors. Unequal group sizes and cluster-based delivery may all have reduced statistical power and introduced potential bias. Furthermore, the original sample size calculation was based on individual-level assumptions and did not account for the clustered study design, which may have further affected statistical precision. While efforts were made to ensure comparability across clusters, we cannot rule out the possibility of unobserved differences influencing outcomes due to the lack of probabilistic sampling or randomization between clusters. One important concern is selection bias. Family caregivers who chose to participate may have been more motivated or burdened, which could limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Further, information regarding non-responders was not systematically collected, limiting assessment of potential non-response bias. Attrition between baseline and follow-up may also have influenced the representativeness of the final analysis sample. Moreover, relying on self-reported data carries the risk of reporting bias. Quality of life is subjective and based on individual perception, which means that participants may over- or underreport their experiences. This may potentially inflate, or mask observed differences between groups. Another key limitation is the potential for confounding due to cluster allocation. Since these analyses were performed at the individual level and did not account for the trial’s clustered design, the standard errors might be underestimated. This is a methodological limitation that should be considered when interpreting the results. Interpretation of the findings should also take the multicomponent nature of the intervention into account. Few caregivers (n = 8) participated in both dyadic and caregiver-specific intervention elements. Consequently, the independent contribution of individual intervention components could not be disentangled. In addition, trial registration was completed after study commencement. However, the study was conducted according to a predefined protocol with prespecified outcomes and analyses established before data analysis and publicly available through the trial registration. We cannot rule out systematic differences between clusters, such as variations in municipal support structures, dementia coordinator expertise, or local implementation adherence, as well as temporal or contextual factors related to different recruitment periods, that may have influenced the outcomes. Additionally, because treatment-as-usual clusters were recruited later than intervention clusters, temporal changes in dementia care practices or increasing awareness of supportive approaches across municipalities may have reduced observable between-group differences over time. However, the structured and informed cluster allocation process likely helped minimize the potential impact of cluster-level confounding. Future studies should consider using stratified randomization at the cluster level or mixed-effects models that account for potential intra-cluster variation. Moreover, the relatively short follow-up period limited our ability to detect longer-term benefits, as previous studies, such as the DAISY trial [23] and the START study [43, 51], have found that psychosocial interventions may only show sustained effects after 6 to 12 months. However, this study still provides valuable insights into intervention effects under a naturalistic setting.

As the number of people with dementia is predicted to increase in the future [52], there is a considerable need to implement evidence-based interventions to support people living with dementia and their family caregivers [53, 54]. A better understanding of how to effectively translate interventions from research into real-world practice is needed. Additionally, future studies should investigate which interventions are most effective for specific subgroups of family caregivers, considering factors such as the type of dementia diagnosis, the stage of the disease, and variations in caregiver context, including sociodemographic factors like age, sex, sexuality, and ethnicity. This study highlights the importance of aligning selected outcome measures with the specific domains targeted by the intervention. Therefore, future studies may benefit from incorporating caregiver-specific quality-of-life measures alongside broader health-related quality-of-life instruments, as caregiver-specific measures may be sensitive to changes in caregiving experiences, while broader measures remain valuable depending on the aims of the study and for comparison across intervention studies.

Conclusion

The intervention with DemTool in primary care was associated with statistically significant differences in change from baseline in CES scores between the intervention and treatment-as-usual group, indicating a potential association with caregivers’ coping and perceived capacity within their caregiving role, as well as caregiver-related quality of life. However, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for traditional health-related quality-of-life and wellbeing outcomes.
