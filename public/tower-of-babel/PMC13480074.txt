Screening of autoinflammatory genes in patients with SARS-CoV-2-associated MIS-C
Authors: Raquel Bispo São Pedro, Sâmek D Novaes, Thaís M M Barreto, Andréia S Silva, Ana L Nogueira, Ana P N Bellinat, Sara Nunes, Edson H B Amaral, Alana C M Lessa, Alberto O Moreira, Thayse S de Jesus, Bernardo Mirabal, Sandeep Tiwari, Thiago L P Castro, Natália M Tavares, Marcus V B Oliveira-Sá, Regina C F Ramos, Rodrigo F Carmo, Luydson R S Vasconcelos, Pablo R S Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is a rare but severe complication of SARS-CoV-2 infection, characterized by systemic hyperinflammation. Evidence indicates innate immunity defects may predispose children to MIS-C. Host genetics may influence MIS-C susceptibility and severity, but underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. The aim of this study was to identify rare genetic variants in autoinflammatory genes that may contribute to MIS-C.ResultsWhole-exome sequencing was performed in 21 unvaccinated Brazilian children with MIS-C, stratified as non-critical or critical. Variant prioritization focused on single nucleotide variants with minor allele frequency < 0.001 in autoinflammatory genes. Functional impact was inferred using variant pathogenicity metrics, gene constraint scores, and in silico molecular docking. Four heterozygous missense variants were prioritized in three genes (SH3BP2, CARD14, and ADAM17), all in critical patients. ADAM17 was the most constrained gene, with variants in protein maturation (P18L) and substrate recognition (T663N) domains. Docking analyses showed both substitutions could affected ADAM17 interactions. ADAM17-NOTCH1 binding was predicted to be impaired by in silico analysis.ConclusionsRare deleterious variants in autoinflammatory genes, especially ADAM17, may influence immune regulation and MIS-C severity. Our results highlight genetic pathways underlying MIS-C and provide a framework for mechanistic studies. Understanding how ADAM17 variants affect immune regulation may improve risk stratification and guide targeted therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-00985-0.Keywords: MIS-C, Children, SARS-CoV-2, Genetics, Autoinflammation

Introduction

Although most children infected with SARS-CoV-2 experience asymptomatic or mild COVID-19, a small subset progress to a severe delayed hyperinflammatory condition known as multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) [1]. First identified in early 2020 [2], its incidence has shown marked temporal and geographic variation [3]. In Brazil, 1.1 cases per 100,000 individuals under 20 years were reported that year [4]. Although cases continue to occur, current reports show they are disproportionately concentrated among unvaccinated children [5, 6].

Patients with MIS-C typically present with persistent fever, accompanied by gastrointestinal, hematologic, and mucocutaneous symptoms, along with cardiovascular involvement such as myocarditis, ventricular dysfunction, and coronary abnormalities [3, 7]. Although overall mortality from MIS-C is relatively low, ranging from 1 to 2% in Europe and the United States [8] to nearly 5% in Latin America [9], a substantial proportion of patients progress to critical illness, often requiring invasive mechanical ventilation and/or vasoactive support [3, 7]. Most patients recover completely from MIS-C, but some can exhibit residual cardiac dysfunction [10], neurocognitive symptoms [11], or immune dysregulation [12].

MIS-C shares clinical features with Kawasaki disease (KD) [13], macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) [14], and toxic shock syndrome (TSS) [15]. Although its underlying mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated, current evidence shows a dysregulated immune response to SARS-CoV-2, resulting in systemic hyperinflammation and multiorgan dysfunction [16, 17]. Circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines are significantly increased in patients with MIS-C [18, 19]. Lymphopenia is common, with reductions in CD4 +, CD8 +, and γδ T cells [20–22]. Altered adaptive immunity is further evidenced by expansions of autoreactive B cells and the presence of autoantibodies [23].

Risk factors for MIS-C include host genetics, older age, and preexisting conditions such as obesity, malignancy, or chronic cardiac and respiratory diseases [24–26]. In addition, Black and Hispanic/Latino children are disproportionately affected, and MIS-C tends to be more severe in Latin America compared to high-income countries [27–29]. These observations suggest that, beyond environmental and sociodemographic influences, host genetic background may play an important role in disease susceptibility and severity [29, 30].

The genetic architecture of MIS-C is influenced by inborn errors of immunity (IEI) that predispose individuals to a dysregulated hyperinflammatory response following SARS-CoV-2 infection [31–36]. Early studies reported rare variants in genes such as XIAP, CYBB, and SOCS1 in patients with MIS-C, suggesting potential monogenic susceptibility in a subset of cases [31, 32]. Subsequent studies have implicated components of the type I interferon (IFN) signaling pathway, with variants identified in genes including TLR3, IFNAR1, IFNAR2, IFNB1, and IRF3 in individuals with severe MIS-C [33, 34]. In addition, defects in viral clearance mechanisms have been proposed, supported by reports of loss-of-function variants in the OAS-RNase L pathway (e.g., OAS1, OAS2, OAS3, and RNASEL), which may impair resolution of the initial viral trigger [25].

Given the clinical overlap between MIS-C and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), several studies have also described rare heterozygous variants in genes involved in lymphocyte cytotoxicity, such as PRF1, UNC13D, STXBP2, LYST, and AP3B1, as well as in the HLH-associated gene DOCK8 [37]. These variants have been proposed to contribute to a “threshold model” of disease, whereby partial defects in cytolytic function may promote sustained immune activation and cytokine release. Furthermore, the complement system has emerged as a potential contributor to MIS-C susceptibility, with variants reported in genes such as C6, CFB, FCN3, MASP2, and the factor H gene cluster (CFHR1, CFHR3, CFHR5) [36]. More recent large-scale sequencing studies in diverse populations have expanded the list of candidate genes, including KMT2D, APOL1, TNFRSF13B, G6PD, DDX60, and TMEM154, further supporting a genetically heterogeneous and polygenic model of susceptibility [33, 36, 38].

We previously identified rare deleterious variants in inflammation-related genes, particularly NLRP12, which may impair NF-κB regulation and contribute to the hyperinflammatory state observed in MIS-C [28]. Importantly, NLRP12 encodes a key negative regulator of inflammation, and some of its variants have been previously implicated in autoinflammatory syndromes [39, 40]. These findings indicate that defects in innate immunity predispose children to MIS-C and suggest an autoinflammatory nature for this syndrome. Based on this hypothesis, in the present study we conduct a screen of autoinflammatory genes in Brazilian patients with MIS-C.

The Brazilian population is characterized by a complex pattern of genomic admixture between European, African, and Native American ancestries, as evidenced by the EPIGEN-Brazil initiative [41, 42]. Despite this rich genetic diversity, admixed Latin American populations remain significantly underrepresented in genomic studies, including those investigating host genetic factors in SARS-CoV-2-related conditions such as MIS-C. This study addresses this gap by evaluating rare variants in underrepresented genomic backgrounds.

Material and methods

Samples and ethical considerations

This is a cross-sectional study with a convenience sample of 21 unvaccinated children and adolescents (0–15 years of age) diagnosed with MIS-C in Brazil, from May 2020 to March 2021. The case definition for MIS-C described by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of the United States of America was adopted [43]. Further details on the cohort and the procedures for collecting data are described by Barreto and colleagues (2025) [28]. MIS-C was stratified into non-critical and critical forms. Non-critical cases met the CDC definition but did not require vasopressors or positive pressure ventilation, whereas critical cases met the same criteria but required one or both interventions. An overview of the study workflow is shown in Additional file 1: Figure S1.

The research ethics committee of the Instituto Aggeu Magalhães, Fundação Oswaldo Cruz, Brazil, approved this study (resolution number: 4263871). The parents or legal guardians of the children and adolescents agreed to participate in the study and signed an informed consent form.

Variants detection, prioritization and gene-level constraint analysis

Patients in this study underwent whole-exome sequencing (WES) to identify single nucleotide variants (SNVs) within coding regions. Sequencing depth metrics for this cohort have been previously reported [28], with a median read depth of 126 ×. Copy number variants (CNVs) were not assessed in the present study. Individual ancestry was estimated using ADMIXTURE [44], with reference samples from the 1000 Genomes Project, phase 3 [45]. European (EUR) and African (AFR) ancestries were represented by multiple reference populations with low admixture rates (EUR: GBR, FIN, IBS, TSI; AFR: YRI, LWK, GWD, MSL, ESN), while Native American ancestry was approximated using Native American-related (AMR) populations (MXL, CLM, PEL, PUR), given the absence of large-scale genomic datasets from low admixed Indigenous populations in the 1000 Genomes Project. ADMIXTURE was run for K = 1–5, and K = 3 was selected based on the lowest cross-validation (CV) error (0.525), reflecting the major continental ancestries contributing to the Brazilian population.

Variant functional annotations were carried out with ANNOVAR (version 2019Oct24) [46]. The present analysis focused on very rare variants (minor allele frequency [MAF] < 0.001, as reported in the gnomAD version 4.1.0 exome ‘ALL’ dataset) [47] within the coding sequences of a curated set of genes related to autoinflammatory disorders, as listed in the 2024 update of human inborn errors of immunity (IEI) [48, 49] by the International Union of Immunological Societies (IUIS) (Additional file 2: Table S1). Seven of these genes (IFIH1, IKBKG, NLRP12, NLRP3, NOD2, STAT2, and TBK1) had already been evaluated previously [28] and were therefore excluded from the current analyses, resulting in a final set of 57 genes. The Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) algorithm, version 1.5 [50], which integrates functional, conservation, and regulatory annotations at the variant level, was used to prioritize potentially deleterious variants (PHRED-like scaled score CADD score ≥ 20).

To further contextualize the potential impact of the selected variants, gene-level constraint metrics from gnomAD (version 4.1.0) were incorporated into the analyses. Missense Z-scores, which quantify the depletion of missense variants relative to expectation [51], were used as indicators of intolerance to amino acid substitutions. LOEUF scores, reflecting intolerance to predicted loss-of-function variants [52], were also considered to provide complementary information on overall gene sensitivity to functional perturbation.

Family segregation analyses were not performed due to the unavailability of samples from nuclear family members.

Literature-based variant curation and cross-cohort comparison

To contextualize our findings within the broader landscape of MIS-C genetics, we conducted a structured literature search in PubMed covering the period from March 2020 to April 2026. The search strategy combined the terms “MIS-C”, “genetics”, and “children”. Study selection was based on relevance to MIS-C and availability of variant-level data. We identified 14 studies reporting rare variants in patients with this syndrome, including our previously published analysis of the same cohort [25, 28, 31–38, 53–56]. Variants were compiled and annotated for gene, cDNA variant nomenclature, protein change, SNV-ID, and allele frequency (gnomAD v4.1.0 exome 'ALL'). The curated variants were then screened in our cohort, and their occurrence was recorded at the individual level, including patient ID and MIS-C status.

Molecular docking simulations

Three-dimensional (3D) protein structures were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database [57] and the Protein Data Bank (PDB) repository [58, 59]. The 3D structures of wild-type ADAM17 and its variants, rs144458353 (P18L) and rs1200631089 (T663N), were modeled using the SWISS-MODEL server [60]. To evaluate the quality of the modeled structures, we used PROCHECK from the UCLA-DOE LAB-SAVES version 6.1 platform, which assesses the stereochemical integrity and overall conformational accuracy of protein models using Ramachandran plot analysis [61, 62]. Protein structures were visualized using UCSF Chimera, version 1.17 [63].

Protein–protein docking analyses between ADAM17 and its respective substrates were evaluated using the ClusPro server [64]. For protein–protein docking simulations, ClusPro requires two PDB structure files as input and performs a three-step process: (i) rigid-body docking, which samples multiple conformations using a spherical grid; (ii) clustering based on root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) to identify the most probable complex models; and (iii) energy minimization to refine the docked structures. The software ranks the resulting protein–protein complexes, presenting the highest-scoring models representing distinct interaction modes. Finally, the program ranked the top protein–protein complexes, highlighting the most likely interaction modes.

Intermolecular interaction sites between ADAM17 (wild type, P18L, or T663N variants) and its predicted binding partners were analyzed using the PyMOL molecular graphics system, version 3.0 [65]. In PyMOL, interactions involving amino acid residues at the mutation sites were visualized within a 6 Å distance cutoff to identify local binding patterns. Additionally, LigPlot +, version 2.3 [66], was used to quantify hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic contacts for each complex. It is important to note that these analyses are based on predicted protein–protein interactions and do not directly assess enzymatic activity or substrate cleavage.

Statistical analyses

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess the association of quantitative variables with MIS-C status (non-critical versus critical), while the qualitative data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism software (version 8.4.2), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Variant prioritization and gene constraint assessment

As shown in Additional file 1: Figure S3, in the first step of the variant filtering approach, only SNVs in a set of genes implicated in autoinflammatory disorders were selected, as detailed in the Methods section. The complete list of variants identified in these genes is provided in Additional file 3: Table S3. Assuming that MIS-C is a rare condition, only variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.001 in the gnomAD database (exome ‘ALL’ dataset) were selected. Next, we focused on the top 1% most deleterious variants in the human genome, selecting those with CADD score ≥ 20. This process resulted in 4 heterozygous SNVs in 3 genes, all detected in patients presenting with critical MIS-C (Table 2).

Two missense variants were identified in the ADAM17 gene: rs1200631089 (T663N) in patient 12 (P12) and rs144458353 (P18L) in P18. In addition, 2 rare SNVs predicted to be pathogenic, rs139934973 (P215T) in SH3BP2 and rs1054063201 (S122F) in CARD14, were detected in P10.

Next, we assessed gene-level constraint metrics from gnomAD to provide additional context for the selected variants. As shown in Table 2, missense Z-scores, in which positive values indicate depletion of missense variants relative to expectation and therefore intolerance to amino acid substitutions, were highest for ADAM17 (Z-score = 2.58), suggesting moderate constraint. SH3BP2 (Z-score = 0.13) and CARD14 (Z-score = − 0.25) displayed minimal evidence of missense constraint. In addition, LOEUF scores, reflecting intolerance to predicted loss-of-function variants and used here as a complementary measure of overall gene intolerance, were consistent with the missense constraint patterns. ADAM17 showed moderate intolerance (LOEUF = 0.56), whereas SH3BP2 (0.96) and CARD14 (1.22) appeared relatively tolerant to loss-of-function variation.

These findings identify ADAM17 as the most constrained gene among the candidates, both for missense and predicted loss-of-function variation, highlighting it as the primary target for downstream functional and mechanistic investigation.

Overlap with previously reported MIS-C variants

To further contextualize our findings, we compiled a total of 361 unique variants previously reported in MIS-C patients across 14 studies (see Additional file 4: Table S4) [25, 28, 31–38, 53–56]. Of these, 33 variants were also identified in our cohort. At the patient level, 19 of 21 individuals (~ 90%) carried at least one variant previously described in MIS-C. Only two patients (P3 and P12), both classified as critical, did not carry variants reported in the literature. Notably, patients carrying prioritized variants in autoinflammatory genes (P10 and P18) also harbored variants previously reported in MIS-C, as described in our prior publication [28].

Protein–protein docking simulations

To investigate the potential impact of ADAM17 variation on protein–protein interactions, we performed in silico docking analyses. These analyses are intended to evaluate interaction patterns rather than enzymatic activity. In particular, the prioritized missense variants rs144458353 (P18L) and rs1200631089 (T663N) were analyzed for their effects on substrate binding through molecular docking. ADAM17 encodes a disintegrin and metalloproteinase domain-containing protein of 824 amino acids [67, 68], organized into a signal peptide (1–17 aa), prodomain (18–214 aa), catalytic domain (215–473 aa), disintegrin domain (474–572 aa), cysteine-rich domain (573–671 aa), transmembrane domain (672–694 aa), and cytoplasmic domain (695–824 aa) (Fig. 1A). The P18L variant maps to the prodomain, which is essential for proper folding and activation of ADAM17 [69, 70], whereas the T663N variant lies within the cysteine-rich domain, a region critical for stabilizing substrate interactions and regulating ADAM17 sheddase activity [68, 71].

We modeled wild-type ADAM17 and the variants, confirming stereochemical integrity via Ramachandran plot as shown in additional file 1: Figure S4. A panel of 34 substrates with established roles in immune regulation and inflammatory processes [67, 68, 72, 73] was then analyzed (see Additional file 1: Table S5). Deviations in binding energy were expressed as percent differences relative to wild-type, whereas hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) and salt bridges were quantified as absolute differences in interaction counts (full parameters for all substrates in Additional file 1: Table S5). These docking deviations were not uniform across substrates but concentrated in a subset showing pronounced differences.

As shown in Fig. 1B, for the P18L variant, the largest increases in docking energy were observed for RANKL (+ 10.1%), ALCAM (+ 7.2%), and KL-1 (+ 7.8%), accompanied by marked reductions in hydrogen bonds (e.g., ALCAM: -13 H-bonds) partially offset by additional salt bridges in some cases. Other substrates, including ICAM-1 (-10 H-bonds) and GPVI (-13 H-bonds, partially compensated by + 3 salt bridges), showed interaction losses with minimal changes in docking energy. Compensatory gains in H-bonds were observed for EREG (+ 11), EpCAM (+ 10), and APP (+ 19). For T663N, the most pronounced increases in docking energy were observed for SORT1 (+ 13.6%) and HbEGF (+ 10.8%), accompanied by substantial reductions in H-bonds (SORT1: − 5; HbEGF: − 11) with minimal variation in salt bridges. Moderate energy increases were noted for ALCAM (+ 7.9%) and KL-1 (+ 7.6%), with similar trends in H-bond and salt bridge changes as observed for P18L. Notably, TNF, a canonical ADAM17 substrate [74], showed no major alterations in docking energy or interactions for either variant, suggesting that these substitutions selectively perturb non-canonical substrates without globally affecting TNF processing.

Among affected substrates, NOTCH1 (Notch receptor 1) displayed notable docking changes in response to ADAM17 variants (Additional file 1: Table S5; Fig. 1B). Interestingly, dysregulation of the NOTCH1 pathway has been implicated in MIS-C [75, 76], and ADAM17 mediates ligand-independent cleavage of NOTCH1 to generate the active intracellular domain [77, 78]. In our in silico docking analysis (Fig. 2A), both ADAM17 variants altered interactions with NOTCH1: docking energy increased by + 3.8% for P18L and + 4.0% for T663N, accompanied by reductions in hydrogen bonds (− 7 and − 13, respectively), whereas salt bridges remained unchanged, with the effect more pronounced for T663N (Fig. 2B). Given that T663N resides in the cysteine-rich domain, which is critical for substrate binding, we performed structural inspection of the docked complexes using PyMOL. In the wild-type complex, intermolecular contacts are broadly distributed across the interface, forming a stabilizing network that maintains proximity and complementarity between ADAM17 and NOTCH1 (Fig. 2C). In contrast, T663N forms a less compact interface with fewer stabilizing interactions, consistent with a perturbation of docking geometry.

Collectively, these results indicate that both P18L and T663N could affect substrate docking, with T663N potentially exerting more pronounced structural perturbations, consistent with its location in ADAM17’s cysteine-rich domain.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated rare genetic variants in children with SARS-CoV-2-associated multisystem inflammatory syndrome, focusing on genes previously implicated in autoinflammatory disorders [49]. Identifying risk factors for MIS-C susceptibility or severity is essential to guide prevention strategies and inform potential therapeutic interventions, such as partially restoring impaired immune functions.

Critical cases had longer hospital and PICU stays and differed in ancestry composition, showing higher African and lower European proportions compared with non-critical patients. Although these differences may suggest potential biological contributions, they should be interpreted with caution. Global ancestry estimates do not capture locus-specific effects, and our analysis did not include local ancestry inference or multivariate modeling to account for potential confounders. In addition, ancestry-associated differences may also reflect socio-environmental factors, particularly in highly unequal societies such as those in Latin America [29, 42]. Therefore, these findings should be considered descriptive rather than indicative of causal relationships.

Here, we prioritized four heterozygous nonsynonymous SNVs in three genes (SH3BP2, CARD14 and ADAM17), identified in three patients (~ 14% of the cohort). Although variant prioritization was performed across 21 MIS-C cases, all candidate variants identified were present in patients with the critical form (3 of 12; 25%), suggesting a possible enrichment in this subgroup, although this should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size.

To further contextualize our findings, we evaluated the presence of variants previously reported in MIS-C within our cohort. Overall, the majority of patients (~ 90%) carried at least one variant described in prior studies, indicating substantial overlap with the existing literature. Importantly, patients carrying prioritized variants in autoinflammatory genes also harbored additional variants previously reported in MIS-C. For example, patient P10, who carries prioritized variants in CARD14 and SH3BP2, was previously reported in our prior study as harboring a pathogenic variant in NLRP12, while patient P18, carrying a variant in ADAM17, was also described with a pathogenic variant in IL17RC [28]. These findings indicate that individual patients may carry multiple rare variants affecting distinct components of the immune response. This pattern supports a threshold model of MIS-C pathogenesis, in which susceptibility arises from the combined effects of alterations across interacting pathways, rather than from a single causative variant [25, 28, 34–37]. In this context, the variants identified span key biological processes, including autoinflammatory signaling (e.g., ADAM17, NLRP12), antiviral defense (e.g., the OAS-RNase L pathway), and cytokine regulation (e.g., IL17RC, SOCS1).

Two rare variants were identified in a single patient (P10): rs139934973 (P215T) in SH3BP2 (SH3 domain-binding protein 2) and rs1054063201 (S122F) in CARD14 (Caspase recruitment domain family member 14). SH3BP2 plays a role in hematopoietic cell activation [79], while CARD14 is involved in NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling [80, 81]. Both genes showed low constraint (SH3BP2: Z-score = 0.13, LOEUF = 0.96; CARD14: Z-score = − 0.25, LOEUF = 1.22), suggesting that these variants may influence immune responses under specific conditions, but are unlikely to have broad functional impact under physiological homeostasis. In contrast, ADAM17 emerged as the most constrained gene, showing higher intolerance to both missense (Z-score = 2.58) and loss-of-function variants (LOEUF = 0.56), indicative of potential functional relevance. We identified two rare heterozygous missense variants in ADAM17: rs144458353 (P18L) and rs1200631089 (T663N). P18L alters the prodomain, a region required for proper folding and maturation of the protein [69, 82], while T663N is located in the cysteine-rich domain, which plays a central role in stabilizing substrate interactions and modulating ADAM17’s sheddase activity [68, 71].

Protein–protein docking analyses suggested that both ADAM17 variants (P18L and T663N) may alter interaction patterns with multiple substrates, with T663N producing more pronounced destabilization consistent with its location in the cysteine-rich domain. For T663N, the strongest effects were observed for HB-EGF and SORT1, both showing marked increases in docking energy accompanied by substantial reductions in hydrogen bonds. The alterations in HB-EGF are particularly notable given ADAM17’s established role in EGF-ligand shedding [68, 83, 84]. By contrast, ADAM17 (T663N)-TNF docking remained essentially unchanged relative to wild-type, suggesting that this variation selectively perturbs non-canonical substrates rather than globally impairing enzymatic function.

The T663N variant also affected ADAM17-NOTCH1 docking, with a notable reduction in hydrogen bonds suggestive of impaired ligand-independent cleavage. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that modulation of ADAM protease activity can influence NOTCH1 cleavage, with downstream effects on immune responses [85–87]. Previous studies in MIS-C have reported altered NOTCH1 signaling in regulatory T cells, including CD22 upregulation and mTORC1-dependent destabilization contributing to systemic inflammation [75, 76]. Our findings suggest a possible mechanistic link, in which rare ADAM17 variants such as T663N may impair NOTCH1 processing and perturb Treg-mediated circuits that normally limit excessive immune activation.

Importantly, the docking analyses presented here are based on computational predictions of protein–protein interactions and do not establish causality or confirm pathogenicity. No functional cellular assays were performed to validate the observed interaction changes. While such experiments would be essential to confirm the biological effects of these variants, they were beyond the scope of the present study. Future studies integrating experimental validation will be necessary to determine whether these variants directly impact ADAM17 function and downstream signaling pathways.

The genetic panorama found in the present study highlights the potential involvement of autoinflammatory pathways in MIS-C. Amino acid substitutions in SH3BP2 and CARD14 may influence immune responses under specific conditions, while variants in ADAM17, particularly within key functional domains, could have a stronger impact on immune regulation and systemic inflammation. These genetic observations offer a biologically plausible framework that may help explain, at least in part, the clinical features observed in MIS-C patients, without implying direct causality.

This study has some limitations. Our findings should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the results. In addition, variant prioritization focused exclusively on extremely rare alleles, which may have limited the detection of other potentially relevant genetic contributors. Functional predictions were based on in silico docking analyses, which, although informative, require experimental validation to confirm effects on substrate cleavage and downstream signaling. Protein structures were carefully modeled and passed stereochemical quality assessments, but have not yet been validated through crystallography. Furthermore, while our prioritization analysis focused on genes implicated in autoinflammatory disorders, other genetic or environmental factors contributing to MIS-C may remain undetected. Future studies should explore rare variants in genes involved in broader immune dysregulation, including those previously associated with MIS-C. Integrating these pathways with autoinflammatory mechanisms may provide a more comprehensive understanding of disease susceptibility and heterogeneity.

Conclusions

This study suggests a potential contribution of rare variants in autoinflammatory genes, particularly ADAM17, to the pathophysiology of MIS-C. By integrating genomic data with in silico structural modeling, we identified two missense substitutions in ADAM17 (P18L and T663N) that may perturb substrate interactions, especially within domains essential for protein maturation and sheddase activity. The T663N variant, located in the cysteine-rich domain, showed the most pronounced destabilizing effects, including altered docking with NOTCH1, suggesting potential interference with ligand-independent cleavage and downstream immune regulation. Together, these findings support a model in which rare deleterious variants in ADAM17 may potentially modulate inflammatory signaling cascades, contributing to the severe, multisystem presentation observed in MIS-C.
