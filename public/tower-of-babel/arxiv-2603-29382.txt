Deep Learning-Assisted Improved Differential Fault Attacks on Lightweight Stream Ciphers
Kok Ping Lim, Dongyang Jia, Iftekhar Salam

Abstract
Lightweight cryptographic primitives are widely deployed in resource-constraint environment, particularly in the Internet of Things (IoT) devices. Due to their public accessibility, these devices are vulnerable to physical attacks, especially fault attacks. Recently, deep learning–based cryptanalytic techniques have demonstrated promising results; however, their application to fault attacks remains limited, particularly for stream ciphers. In this work, we investigate the feasibility of deep learning assisted differential fault attack on three lightweight stream ciphers, namely ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM, under a relaxed fault model, where a single-bit bit-flipping fault is injected at an unknown location. We train multilayer perceptron (MLP) models to identify the fault locations. Experimental results show that the trained models achieve high identification accuracies of 0.999880, 0.999231 and 0.823568 for ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM, respectively, and outperform traditional signature-based methods. For the secret recovery process, we introduce a threshold-based method to optimize the number of fault injections required to recover the secret information. The results show that the initial state of ACORN can be recovered with 21 to 34 faults; while MORUS requires 213 to 248 faults, with at most 6 bits of guessing. Both attacks reduce the attack complexity compared to existing works. For ATOM, the results show that it possesses a higher security margin, as majority of state bits in the Non-linear Feedback Shift Register (NFSR) can only be recovered under a precise control model. To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first experimental results of differential fault attacks on ATOM.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, various cryptographic competitions, such as eSTREAM, the Competition for Authenticated Encryption: Security, Applicability, and Robustness (CAESAR) and National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Lightweight Cryptography Project were called for the design and third-party analysis of lightweight cryptography primitives. Lightweight cryptography is designed to ensure secure communication for resource-constrained devices, e.g., Internet of Things (IoT) devices, as traditional ciphers are intended to operate in resource-rich environments. Many cryptanalysis techniques, such as cube attack, side channel attack and fault attack are applied to the lightweight ciphers. Among these attacks, fault attacks have gained significant attention in recent years due to their effectiveness against physical hardware and the ongoing advancement of fault injection techniques. Since IoT devices are commonly deployed in publicly accessible areas, adversaries often have physical access to them, making these devices viable targets for fault attacks
[

]
.

Fault attack was first introduced by Boneh et al.
[
4
]
and targets physical hardware rather than the cryptographic algorithm itself. They applied the attack to RSA and Robin signature schemes by introducing hardware faults to factor the large prime modulus
N
. In the same year, Biham and Shamir
[
3

implemented a variant of fault attack, differential fault attack (DFA), and applied it against the Data Encryption Standard (DES) to recover the 56-bit secret key. The attack works by inducing an error (fault) during the operation of the cipher to obtain the faulty outputs. By analysing the differences between fault-free and faulty outputs, some secret information of the cipher, such as the internal state or secret key can be recovered. Hoch and Shamir
[
8
]
further extended DFA against RC4, LILI-128 and SOBER-t32, introducing DFA on stream ciphers.

Following these foundational works, differential fault attacks were further extended and refined, and subsequently applied to evaluate the security of a wide range of cryptographic primitives. Orumiehchiha et al.

applied the first DFA against WG-8 stream cipher to recover the initial state and secret key of the cipher using 6 faults with the time complexity of

The existing literature shows that differential fault attacks remain a popular cryptanalytic technique for state recovery or key recovery. In addition, the integration of deep learning in fault attack provides a new direction in enhancing the performance of the attack. Given the limited research on deep learning–assisted DFA, the feasibility of such fault attacks remains largely underexplored.
To address this, we propose a deep learning–assisted DFA framework for enhancing differential fault attacks and apply it to ACORNv3, MORUSv2, and ATOM.

Existing Differential Fault Attacks on ACORN and MORUS

Dalai and Roy

By analyzing the occurrence of 1 in the differential keystream, the fault location were identified with a high probability of 97.08%. For equations solving, they used linearization and guess-and-determine strategies to solve the equations and recover the initial state. The linearization strategy required 31 to 88 faults to recover the initial state with an attack complexity of
c\cdot 2^{179.19-1.76n}

Siddhanti et al.

extended DFA to ACORNv3, optimizing the classical differential framework by incorporating both forward and backward state-update equations and fixing selected bits before SAT solving. In their attack, the fault locations were identified using the signature-based method. Due to the large state size of ACORN, the attack was optimized by guessing 20 state bits. Their work showed that the initial state of ACORNv3 can be recovered using 9 faults and 1200-bit keystream, with the time complexity of
2^{25.40}
. Zhang et al.
[
29

also performed DFA on ACORNv3, following the same attack process as in

By incorporating the keystream extension and high probability priority strategies, the accuracy of their proposed method was 99.998%, using 163-bit differential keystream. For the equations solving, a guess-and-determine method was used to recover the full initial state of the cipher. The attack complexity is
c\cdot 2^{146.5-3.52n}
, where
n
is the number of faults such that
26<n<43
faults and where
c
was the time complexity of solving the equations.

In short, most of the existing works of DFA against ACORN requires the guess-and-determine method to recover the state. In addition, some of the works focus on retrieving only linear equations, that require more fault injections to recover the state of the cipher. Additionally, the attacks based on fault model with no control over the fault location rely on traditional signature-based method to identify fault location. Hence, this presents a gap for investigating the use of deep learning for identifying the fault locations and improving the attack complexity. Furthermore, investigation can be conducted by incorporating high degree equations with a threshold-based technique to optimize the number of faults.

Wong et al.
[
24
]
performed a theoretical analysis of DFA on MORUS, targeting the bit-wise AND operation of the keystream generation function, where a random fault is injected into one of the two involved registers.
With these faults, an adversary can reveal the non-zero state bits in the targeted register whenever the differential keystream bits are 1.
Repeated fault injections allow these non-zero bits to be recovered with high probability, while the remaining bits are assumed to be 0 with high confidence.
However, the attack only achieved partial state recovery and and its reliance on precise control over the fault location is a relatively strong assumption; leaving open the the possibility of achieving full state recovery of MORUS with a weaker assumption.

Preliminaries and Background

2.1
Fault Location Identification

Our proposed deep learning assisted fault attack framework works under the fault model of no-control over the fault location. In this model, fault location identification is a critical process, as it can affect the secret recovery process. Consider a toy cipher with 4 bits state,
s_{0}\|s_{1}\|s_{2}\|s_{3}=0\|0\|0\|1
, where the first keystream bit is defined as:

z_{0}=s_{0}\oplus s_{1}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}\oplus s_{2}s_{3}.

(1)

During the attack, a fault is injected at the location
s_{2}
, and thus the value of
s_{2}
is complement. The faulty keystream equation can be rewritten as:

z_{0}^{\prime}=s_{0}\oplus s_{1}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}^{\prime}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3},

(2)

where
s_{2}^{\prime}=s_{2}\oplus 1
. By XOR-ing Equation
1
and Equation
2
, we can obtain the differential:

\begin{split}z_{0}\oplus z_{0}^{\prime}&=(s_{0}\oplus s_{1}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}\oplus s_{2}s_{3})\oplus(s_{0}\oplus s_{1}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}^{\prime}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3})\\
&=s_{1}s_{2}\oplus s_{2}s_{3}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}^{\prime}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime}s_{3}\\
&=s_{1}(s_{2}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime})\oplus s_{3}(s_{2}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime})\\
&=s_{1}\oplus s_{3}.\end{split}

(3)

Substituting the values into the equations, we can find that Equation
3
is valid. Note that in the no control model, the adversary has no knowledge about the fault location. Suppose the fault is injected at
s_{2}
, but the adversary misidentifies the fault location as
s_{3}
, resulting in the following differential equation instead:

\begin{split}z_{0}\oplus z_{0}^{\prime}&=(s_{0}\oplus s_{1}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}\oplus s_{2}s_{3})\oplus(s_{0}\oplus s_{1}\oplus s_{1}s_{2}\oplus s_{2}s_{3}^{\prime})\\
&=s_{2}s_{3}\oplus s_{2}s_{3}^{\prime}\\
&=s_{2}(s_{3}\oplus s_{3}^{\prime})\\
&=s_{2}.\end{split}

(4)

Substituting the values into the equation, it shows that the wrong value is revealed for
s_{2}
due to the misidentification. Thus, it is important to identify the correct fault location, under the no control model, to ensure that the valid differential can be derived. There are a number of methods to identify the fault location. Signature-based method is one of the most popular methods to identify the fault location. One can refer to
[
12
,
13
,
22
]
for the details of signature-based method.

3
Attack Description

Our attacks are conducted in a simulation-based environment, where the fault injection is simulated by code, rather than implementing the attack on a physical device. The attack assumes a known-plaintext attack (KPA) model, where the adversary has access to specific keystream bits.
Besides, the following assumptions are made:

•

The adversary can inject a single-bit bit-flipping fault at the initial state

•

The adversary has no control over the fault location.

•

The adversary can always reset the cipher and run the cipher with the embedded key and initialization vector (IV) to observe the keystream.

The attack consists of two phases, the fault location identification and secret recovery process. The details of these two phases are discussed in the following section.

3.1
Fault Location Identification

The fault location identification is the critical process in our attack, as the fault is injected to unknown location. Without the knowledge of fault location, we cannot obtain the valid differential keystream equations to recover the secret of the cipher. Thus, MLP models are trained to identify the fault location using differential keystream datasets.

Implementation of Multilayer Perceptron Model

Using the generated datasets, we employ multilayer perceptron (MLP) models to identify the fault locations.
The implementation of the models are done with the Keras library provided in the TensorFlow framework, using the Google Colab. The number of neurons in the input layer is the length of the differential keystream, while the number of neurons in the output layer is the size of the internal state of the cipher, that is the number of possible fault location. For the number of hidden layers, number of neurons or other hyperparameters, an exhaustive search is used to optimize the parameters.

The model is compiled with the Adam optimizer and the sparse categorial cross entropy loss function. Since this is a classification problem, accuracy is chosen as the metric. The model is trained for a maximum of 50 epochs and the early-stopping criterion based on validation accuracy is applied. If there is no improvement in validation accuracy for seven consecutive epochs, the training process is stopped, and the weights of the best validation accuracy are restored. The hyperparameters including the number of neurons and hidden layers, dropout rate, and initial learning rate are selected through empirical experiments. The number of neurons is tuned by adjusting the parameters with a power of two, while the dropout rate is adjusted by increasing or decreasing the rate by 0.05.

3.2
Secret Recovery

Once the MLP models are trained, we proceed to apply the differential fault attack on the targeted ciphers. Figure
1
illustrates the process of secret recovery method. The attack starts by obtaining the fault-free keystream of the cipher. Once the fault-free keystream is obtained, the cipher is reset and run to the initial state. At this stage, a single-bit bit-flipping fault is injected to the initial state of cipher, where the fault location is unknown.We then run the cipher with the faulty initial state and obtain the faulty keystream. The computed differential keystream is then fed into the trained model to identify the fault location. When the fault location is correctly identified, we proceed to the next stage of equations construction. In contrast, the cipher is reset and repeat with the previous process when there is an error in fault location identification.

With the correct fault location, we are able to generate the corresponding faulty keystream equations. By XOR-ing the fault-free and faulty keystream equations, the respective differential keystream equations are obtained. A threshold is introduced to determine the minimum number of differential equations for optimizing the number of fault injections. When the number of differential equations is less than the defined threshold, we repeat the fault injection process.Note that the value of threshold varies across ciphers; this will be discussed in Section
4
.

If the number of differential equations has exceeded the threshold, we proceed to solve the equations with Gröbner basis provided in SageMath library. Meanwhile, we set a timer of one minute to determine if Gröbner basis can solve the equations system. If Gröbner basis returns the result within the time, we then verify the recovered secret bits. Otherwise, the current computation is terminated, while the threshold is increased by 5 and the process of fault injection and equations construction are repeated. Increasing the threshold allows more differential equations to be added into the equations system, providing more information to the Gröbner basis for solving the equations system.

The same training and testing datasets are used to generate the fault signature and performing evaluation for the signature-based method. The accuracy of the MLP model is 82.3568%, while the accuracy for the signature-based method is 58.8976%. A total of 400 experiments have been conducted to analyze the performance of the proposed state recovery and key recovery attack on ATOM. Unfortunately, all experiments have encountered the case of wrong identification. Due to the setting of the experiments, we do not proceed to equation solving if there is any error identification occurred. This has shown that the utilization of double key filter provides a high quality of security margin, as the adversary would not be able to collect an all correctly identified fault location to perform the state recovery and key recovery process. If the adversary utilizes the result of wrong identification to generate the equation system and solve the system, it is expected that some of the state bits or the key bits will not be recovered correctly. In these experiments, a new fault is injected to the NRSR of the cipher whenever the number of equations in the system does not exceed the threshold of 234. To reach the threshold of 234, it requires on average 46 faults.

Since the attack based on the model of no control over the fault location cannot recover any initial NFSR state and secret key bit, an experiment is conducted based on the fault model of precise control over the fault locations. In this experiment, the utilization of threshold is excluded. Based on the previous experiments, 46 faults are needed to construct the equation system with at least 234 equations. Conclusion

This work focuses on investigating the feasibility of deep learning assisted differential fault attack against ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM with the fault model of single-bit bit flipping fault with no control over the fault location. We have implemented three MLP models for identifying the fault location on ACORNv3, MORUSv2 and ATOM, where the accuracies of the trained models have improved the traditional signature-based method. Besides, we propose a threshold-based strategy to optimize the number of fault injections. The results show that we can recover the initial state of ACORN with the complexity of negligible and achieve full state recovery on MORUS, that are better than the results of existing works. On the other hand, we have implemented the first DFA on ATOM and managed to recover majority of state bits in NFSR under the fault model of precise control over the fault location. It shows that the design of double key filter has improved the security margin of the cipher. Future works may examine the feasibility of deep learning assisted fault attack under the more relaxed fault attack model of random fault with no control over the fault location. Besides, one may consider to remove the timer used in equation solving, to examine the number of faults needed to recover the state. Furthermore, one can proceed to solve the equation that encounter error in fault location identification, as it is expected that some of the state bits can be recovered correctly.
