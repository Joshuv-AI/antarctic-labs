Spatial immune profiling of local tissues and its correlation with peripheral blood cytokines in lung squamous cell carcinoma patients with immune-related pneumonitis
Authors: Hui He, Yuanyuan Wen, Jiale Yan, Lei Zhou, Haifeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCheckpoint inhibitor-induced pneumonitis (CIP) is a potentially fatal adverse event in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) patients, yet reliable early predictive biomarkers remain elusive. This study aimed to characterize the association between pre-treatment spatial immune landscapes and post-therapy peripheral cytokine fluctuations, in order to identify effective predictive biomarkers for CIP.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 19 LUSC patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) between January 2022 and December 2024, of whom 4 developed CIP. Pre-treatment tumor tissues were obtained at baseline, while peripheral blood specimens were collected at the onset of CIP. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) was employed to analyze the spatial distribution of immune cells and factors, including IL-8, CD66b+, CD8+, and CD163+, in pre-treatment tissues. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to quantify the infiltration densities of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD163+ macrophages within tumor nests and stromal regions. Concurrently, a panel of peripheral blood cytokines [interleukin (IL)-8, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, and IL-35] was measured to compare disparities between the CIP and non-CIP groups.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed in baseline clinical characteristics or routine hematological parameters between the two groups. Cytokine profiling revealed that post-onset peripheral IL-8 and TNF-α levels were significantly lower in the CIP group than in the non-CIP group (P<0.05). mIF analysis demonstrated significantly elevated pre-treatment IL-8 expression accompanied by marked enrichment of CD66b+ neutrophils in the CIP group (P<0.05), whereas CD8+ T-cell infiltration showed no significant difference. IHC further indicated that the infiltration densities of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD163+ macrophages in the stromal regions were significantly higher in the CIP group (all P<0.01); conversely, intratumoral lymphocyte distribution showed no significant disparity, with only CD163+ macrophages significantly increased in the CIP group (P=0.01).ConclusionsIncreased stromal IL-8 expression and CD66b⁺ neutrophil enrichment before treatment may be associated with subsequent CIP development in patients with LUSC receiving ICIs. These findings support a potential role of localized immune microenvironment alterations in CIP and may provide clues for future risk assessment strategies.Keywords: Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), checkpoint inhibitor-induced pneumonitis (CIP), multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF), interleukin-8 (IL-8)

Highlight box

Key findings

• In lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), subsequent checkpoint inhibitor-induced pneumonitis (CIP) was associated with increased pre-treatment stromal interleukin-8 (IL-8) expression and CD66b-positive neutrophil enrichment.

• Peripheral IL-8 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels were lower in the CIP group after CIP onset.

• Immunohistochemistry suggested that CIP-associated immune-cell infiltration was mainly localized to stromal regions rather than tumor nests.

What is known and what is new?

• CIP is a clinically important and potentially severe immune-related adverse event in patients receiving ICIs. Previous studies have suggested that systemic cytokines, myeloid-cell recruitment, and the tumor immune microenvironment may be involved in CIP development, but reliable early predictive markers remain uncertain.

• This study adds tissue-based spatial observations showing that pre-treatment stromal IL-8 upregulation and CD66b-positive neutrophil enrichment may be associated with later CIP occurrence in LUSC. The findings also suggest that local stromal immune alterations and peripheral cytokine changes may not be fully concordant during CIP development.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These results support further investigation of stromal immune features as candidate indicators for CIP risk assessment. At present, these markers should not be considered established predictive biomarkers. Prospective studies with larger cohorts, longitudinal blood sampling, and independent validation are needed before they can be used to guide clinical decision-making or personalized monitoring strategies.

Introduction

Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) is one of the most prevalent histological subtypes of lung cancer. Due to the insidious onset of symptoms, the majority of patients is diagnosed at advanced stages, resulting in a relatively low 5-year survival rate (1). In recent years, the rapid development of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), has fundamentally transformed the clinical treatment landscape for various malignancies, including LUSC, significantly prolonging patient survival (2-4). However, the immune-related adverse events (irAEs) associated with ICIs have emerged as a new challenge in clinical practice, among which checkpoint inhibitor-induced pneumonitis (CIP) is the most lethal and one of the most challenging complications (5-7). Particularly in LUSC patients, who often have underlying conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and limited pulmonary reserve, the occurrence of CIP is associated with a significantly worse prognosis (8-10).

Currently, the clinical diagnosis of CIP relies heavily on radiographic features and exclusionary clinical assessments, yet molecular biomarkers with high specificity and sensitivity remain elusive (11). Although previous studies have attempted to identify predictive indicators through peripheral blood assays, whether systemic circulatory alterations can faithfully and precisely recapitulate the localized spatial immune landscape within pulmonary neoplastic lesions remains a subject of ongoing debate (12).

This study aims to investigate the potential associations between pre-existing localized immune signatures in tumor tissues and subsequent systemic inflammatory responses by integrating pre-treatment lung cancer specimens with post-CIP peripheral blood samples. Utilizing a multidimensional approach—including multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) spatial profiling, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and comprehensive peripheral cytokine assays—we characterize the evolution of the spatial immune microenvironment in CIP patients and elucidate the intrinsic links between localized tissue features and systemic circulatory fluctuations. From this exploratory perspective of “local-systemic” crosstalk, we seek to further clarify the pathogenesis of CIP, thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the early clinical identification of high-risk populations and the development of precision intervention strategies. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0484/rc).

Methods

Patients

Clinicopathological data and blood specimens were retrospectively collected from patients with LUSC who received ICI therapy at Zhoushan Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zhoushan Hospital (No. 2022442). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants or their legal guardians/immediate family members for the collection and analysis of clinical data and specimens. Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (I) age between 18 and 80 years; (II) administration of immunotherapy, specifically PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors; (III) a confirmed pathological diagnosis of LUSC. Conversely, exclusion criteria included: (I) severe extra-pulmonary organ failure; (II) terminal status; and (III) progression of pulmonary lesions attributable to non-immune-related etiologies, such as infectious pneumonia, neoplastic progression, or congestive heart failure. Flowchart of patient selection. CIP, checkpoint inhibitor-induced pneumonitis; ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor; IHC, immunohistochemistry; LUSC, lung squamous cell carcinoma; mIF, multiplex immunofluorescence.

All patients suspected of developing CIP during treatment or follow-up were required to undergo chest computed tomography (CT) scanning. Diagnostic criteria for CIP were established in accordance with the 2025 Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Management of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor-Related Pneumonitis (13), defined by the emergence of novel diffuse parenchymal abnormalities on CT, including ground-glass opacities, consolidation, interlobular septal thickening, reticulation, micronodules, traction bronchiectasis, and architectural distortion. A definitive diagnosis of CIP was rendered only after multidisciplinary discussion among radiologists, pulmonologists, and oncologists, following the systematic exclusion of alternative etiologies such as pulmonary infection, neoplastic progression, or radiation pneumonitis.

Cytokine profiling

Serological specimens were collected from all patients, aliquoted, and preserved at −80 ℃ for subsequent cytokine profiling. Routine hematological assessments and serological sampling were performed prior to the administration of corticosteroids or other anti-inflammatory interventions for irAEs, enabling the comparative analysis of baseline cytokine levels and those recorded at the onset of CIP according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The quantified cytokine panel comprised interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17A, and IL-35.

mIF staining

Multiplex fluorescence staining was performed on pre-treatment LUSC tissues (n=6). Two panels were designed: (I) CD66b (98061-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), IL-8 (HZ-1318, Proteintech), and CD8 (66868-1-IG, Proteintech); (II) CD163 (ET1704-43, Huabio, Hangzhou, China), and IL-8 (HZ-1318, Proteintech). All primary antibodies were diluted at 1:1,000. Briefly, paraffin-embedded sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-induced antigen retrieval (EDTA, pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 5% H2O2, followed by protein blocking [3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) or 10% rabbit serum]. For each cycle, sections were incubated with primary antibodies (1:1,000) overnight at 4 ℃, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and TSA fluorophores (520, 570, or 690). Between cycles, primary/secondary antibody complexes were removed via microwave-assisted elution. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole), and autofluorescence was quenched. Images were captured using a 3DHISTECH scanner and analyzed via Image J. Target cell counts from five random fields per slide were normalized to total DAPI-positive cells and averaged for quantitative analysis.

IHC

IHC staining was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from 6 LUSC patients (3 CIP vs. 3 non-CIP). Briefly, 4 µm sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2.

Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against CD4 (67786-1-Ig, Proteintech), CD8 (66868-1-IG, Proteintech), and CD163 (ET1704-43, Huabio) at 4 ℃ overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

The expression of CD4+, CD8+, and CD163+ was quantified by two independent pathologists blinded to the clinical data. For each slide, five representative high-power fields (200× magnification) were randomly selected. The density of positive cells was expressed as the average number of cells per unit area (14). The hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and IHC slides were scanned by digital pathology slice scanner (KF-PRO-005, KFBIO, Ningbo, China), and then analyzed with K-viewer software (KFBIO, Ningbo, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and SPSS (version 26.0). Categorical clinical data were compared using the Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-squared test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To evaluate the cytokine profiles of the CIP and non-CIP groups at baseline and at the onset of pneumonitis, unpaired t-tests were employed for inter-group comparisons. For the quantitative analysis of mIF and IHC, the Mann-Whitney U test or unpaired t-test was used to assess the differences in tissue-level markers between the CIP and non-CIP groups. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, to explore the association between local immune features and systemic inflammatory status, correlation analyses were conducted between pretreatment stromal IL-8/CD66b expression (quantified by mIF) and peripheral serum IL-8/TNF-α levels measured at CIP onset. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used for statistical analysis. Considering the limited number of available samples, these analyses were exploratory in nature.

Results

Comparison of baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 19 patients with LUSC were enrolled in this study, of whom 4 developed checkpoint inhibitor-induced pneumonitis (CIP group) and 15 remained unaffected (non-CIP group). Statistical analysis of baseline data revealed no significant differences between the two cohorts in terms of gender distribution (P>0.99) or age (71.00±4.24 vs. 71.87±5.30 years, P=0.77). Furthermore, no statistically significant disparities were observed regarding lymph node or distant metastasis (P>0.05), indicating that the baseline clinical characteristics of the two groups were comparable (Table 1). Additionally, the two groups were highly comparable regarding other major clinical features, including the predominance of first-line PD-1 inhibitor-based combination therapy, prior thoracic radiotherapy, history of COPD/emphysema, and prior surgery (Table S1).

Comparison of routine hematological parameters and CRP levels

Laboratory findings indicated no significant disparities in post-treatment routine hematological parameters between the CIP and non-CIP groups. Specifically, no statistically significant differences were observed in white blood cell (WBC) counts, neutrophil counts, lymphocyte counts, red blood cell counts, or hemoglobin (Hb) levels between the two cohorts (P>0.05). Similarly, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, an indicator of systemic inflammation, showed no significant statistical deviation between groups (P=0.86), as detailed in Table 1.

Comparison of serum cytokine profiles between CIP and non-CIP groups

Profiling of multiple serum cytokines revealed significant disparities in specific inflammatory markers between the two cohorts. IL-8 levels were significantly lower in the CIP group compared to the non-CIP group (8.923±0.341 vs. 9.930±1.114 pg/mL; t=−3.011, P=0.008). Similarly, TNF-α levels in the CIP group (15.609 ±0.184 pg/mL) were significantly reduced relative to the non-CIP group (16.429±1.339 pg/mL; t=−2.292, P=0.04). No statistically significant differences were observed for other cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, IL-35, and IFN-γ (P>0.05), as detailed in Table 1.

mIF analysis of the intratumoral immune microenvironment

To characterize the intratumoral immune microenvironment, two mIF panels were designed: Panel 1 (CD66b/IL-8/CD8/DAPI) and Panel 2 (CD163/IL-8/DAPI), as illustrated in Figures 2,3. Quantitative analysis revealed that the density of CD66b+ neutrophils was significantly elevated in the CIP group compared to the non-CIP cohort (0.207±0.200 vs. 0.0133±0.019; P=0.002), which was accompanied by a significant upregulation of IL-8 expression levels (0.183±0.277 vs. 0.003±0.002; P=0.03). Conversely, no statistically significant difference was observed in the infiltration density of CD8+ T cells between the two groups (0.102±0.125 vs. 0.143±0.199; P=0.50). Furthermore, while the abundance of CD163+ M2-type macrophages was higher in the CIP group (1.232±2.493 vs. 0.144±0.217), this disparity did not reach statistical significance due to substantial intragroup variability (P=0.11).

IHC characterization of intratumoral and stromal immune cell densities

This study employed IHC analysis to evaluate the infiltration density of immune cells within the tumor nests of patients in both the CIP and non-CIP groups. As presented in Table 2 and Figure 4 the infiltration of CD163+ macrophages in the intratumoral regions was significantly higher in the CIP group than in the non-CIP group (7.93±9.65 vs. 0.60±0.63, P=0.01). Regarding T-cell populations, although the density of CD8+ T cells was numerically higher in CIP tissues (18.67±17.60 vs. 9.27±7.15), this trend did not reach statistical significance (P=0.07). Furthermore, no significant disparity was observed in the infiltration density of CD4+ T cells between the two cohorts (4.07±3.03 vs. 3.47±2.56, P=0.56). To further characterize the immune microenvironment, the density of immune cells in the peritumoral stromal regions was assessed (Table 3). Within the stroma, the infiltration of CD8+ T cells (85.13±36.97 vs. 40.47±19.05, P<0.001) and CD163+ macrophages (28.27±21.46 vs. 4.2±5.19, P<0.001) was significantly more pronounced in the CIP group than in the non-CIP group. Additionally, the density of CD4+ T cells in the CIP group exhibited a significant increase compared to the non-CIP group (42.6±15.68 vs. 21.27±17.56, P=0.002).

Correlation between local immune features and peripheral cytokines

Correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the association between pretreatment local immune features and peripheral inflammatory cytokine levels at CIP onset. Stromal CD66b expression was negatively correlated with peripheral IL-8 levels (r=−0.829, P=0.042). No significant correlations were observed between stromal IL-8 expression and peripheral IL-8 levels (r=−0.543, P=0.27), between stromal CD66b expression and peripheral TNF-α levels (r=−0.429, P=0.40), or between stromal IL-8 expression and peripheral TNF-α levels (r=0.086, P=0.87).

Discussion

While the implementation of ICIs has significantly improved prognosis for patients with LUSC, the emergence of CIP remains a formidable clinical challenge (2-6). By integrating pre-treatment spatial immune profiling of tumor tissues with peripheral blood cytokine measurements obtained at the onset of CIP, our findings indicate that patients who subsequently developed CIP may exhibit distinct baseline immune microenvironment characteristics, including stromal IL-8 overexpression and myeloid cell enrichment. In contrast, peripheral inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8 and TNF-α were reduced after CIP onset. Together, these observations suggest that immune alterations within local stromal tissues may differ from those observed in the peripheral circulation during CIP development.

mIF analysis showed increased stromal IL-8 expression and greater infiltration of CD66b+ neutrophils in pretreatment LUSC tissues from patients who subsequently developed CIP. These findings suggest that a subset of patients may already exhibit a localized inflammatory microenvironment before initiation of ICI therapy, characterized by stromal IL-8 upregulation and neutrophil enrichment. Similar associations between IL-8-related inflammatory signaling and immune-related pulmonary toxicity have also been reported in previous studies (15-17).

IL-8 (CXCL8) is an important chemokine involved in neutrophil recruitment through CXCR1 and CXCR2 signaling pathways (18,19). Increased stromal IL-8 expression may facilitate the accumulation of neutrophils and other myeloid cells within peritumoral lung tissues (20,21). In patients with LUSC, long-term smoking exposure, squamous cell–associated chronic inflammation, and coexisting pulmonary conditions such as COPD may further contribute to a pro-inflammatory pulmonary microenvironment (22-24). Against this background, stromal IL-8 overexpression together with CD66b+ neutrophil enrichment may reflect a pre-existing inflammatory state associated with increased susceptibility to CIP.

Following ICI treatment, this pre-existing stromal inflammatory microenvironment may become further activated. Although ICIs enhance antitumor T-cell responses, activated lymphocytes may also increase local production of inflammatory cytokines within pulmonary tissues (25,26). In stromal regions already enriched with IL-8-associated neutrophils, this immune activation may further amplify local inflammatory signaling. Activated neutrophils can subsequently release reactive oxygen species, proteolytic enzymes, and additional inflammatory mediators, which may contribute to alveolar epithelial and endothelial injury (27-29). Persistent local immune activation and tissue inflammation may therefore increase susceptibility to immune-related lung injury and facilitate the development of CIP.

We also observed that patients who developed CIP showed higher IL-8 expression within stromal tissues but lower circulating IL-8 levels after CIP onset. Although this pattern may seem paradoxical, it may reflect differences between local tissue inflammation and systemic inflammatory responses. One possible explanation is that IL-8 accumulates within inflamed stromal regions through interactions with extracellular matrix components and glycosaminoglycans, thereby maintaining localized chemotactic signaling (30,31). Under these conditions, CXCR1/CXCR2-expressing neutrophils and other myeloid cells may preferentially migrate from the circulation into pulmonary tissues along the local cytokine gradient (32-34). In addition, local cytokine retention, receptor-mediated consumption, and cellular sequestration within inflamed tissues may contribute to lower detectable IL-8 levels in peripheral blood despite increased tissue expression (35,36). Therefore, elevated stromal IL-8 expression and reduced circulating IL-8 levels may represent different aspects of localized and systemic immune alterations during CIP development rather than truly contradictory findings.

Exploratory correlation analysis further showed an inverse relationship between stromal CD66b+ neutrophil enrichment and peripheral IL-8 levels at the onset of CIP, suggesting that increased local neutrophil accumulation may be associated with reduced circulating IL-8 levels during CIP development. However, because the current study was limited by sample size and lacked functional validation experiments, it remains unclear whether local immune-cell recruitment directly contributes to the observed reduction in peripheral cytokine levels. Further studies incorporating longitudinal cytokine monitoring, migration assays, and mechanistic investigations are needed to better clarify this relationship.

Local immune alterations within stromal regions may also influence surrounding non-malignant cells in the pulmonary microenvironment. In this study, stromal “bystander” cells may include fibroblasts, endothelial cells, resident macrophages, immune cells, and adjacent non-tumor epithelial cells located near inflamed stromal areas (37,38). Although these cells are not the primary targets of antitumor immune responses, they may become secondarily activated by inflammatory cytokines, neutrophil-derived mediators, and activated T-cell signaling. Once activated, these stromal cells may further promote local inflammatory responses through the release of chemokines, adhesion molecules, and additional inflammatory mediators, thereby facilitating continued immune-cell recruitment within pulmonary tissues (39-41). This process may partly explain why CIP manifests as diffuse inflammatory lung injury rather than a strictly tumor-restricted immune response.

Consistent with these observations, the IHC analysis showed that immune-cell infiltration in patients with CIP was largely confined to the peritumoral stromal compartment. Increased numbers of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD163+ macrophages were observed mainly in stromal regions, whereas lymphocyte infiltration within tumor nests was broadly comparable between the two groups. This spatial pattern suggests that immune-cell recruitment during CIP development may occur preferentially in the stromal microenvironment rather than within the tumor nests themselves. The concurrent presence of lymphoid and myeloid cell accumulation in these stromal areas further points to localized inflammatory crosstalk during CIP development (39,42).

The apparent discrepancy between the mIF and IHC results for CD8+ T cells may be explained, at least in part, by differences in their quantification strategies. In the IHC analysis, tumor nests and stromal regions were assessed as separate compartments, allowing compartment-specific changes to be identified. By contrast, the mIF analysis measured CD8+ cells as a proportion of all DAPI-positive cells across the entire imaging field. Since tumor cells accounted for most DAPI-positive nuclei, a localized increase in stromal CD8+ T-cell infiltration could have been diluted in the whole-field calculation. This may have reduced the sensitivity of mIF for detecting regional stromal enrichment.

For patients with LUSC receiving immunotherapy, radiological diagnosis of CIP often occurs only after clinically significant pulmonary inflammation has already developed (3,6,43). In this context, pretreatment evaluation of stromal immune features, including IL-8 expression and CD66b+ neutrophil enrichment, may provide additional information for identifying patients who may be at increased risk of CIP. These findings also support the possibility that a subset of patients may harbor a pre-existing inflammatory stromal microenvironment before initiation of ICI therapy.

Despite these observations, several limitations of the current study should be considered. First, this was a retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, particularly for paired mIF specimens. In addition, peripheral cytokine measurements were not consistently available at baseline because longitudinal cytokine monitoring was not routinely performed in clinical practice. As a result, we could not determine whether the observed cytokine alterations were present before CIP onset. Furthermore, pathological samples obtained during active CIP were unavailable, limiting direct evaluation of dynamic immune changes within pulmonary tissues during disease development. Finally, although our findings suggest a potential association among stromal IL-8 expression, neutrophil enrichment, peripheral IL-8 reduction, and CIP occurrence, the current data are insufficient to establish a causal relationship. Future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal blood sampling, paired tissue analyses, functional assays, and spatially resolved transcriptomic approaches will be important for clarifying the interaction between localized immune microenvironments and systemic inflammatory alterations during CIP development.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that increased stromal IL-8 expression and CD66b+ neutrophil enrichment before treatment may be associated with subsequent CIP development in patients with LUSC receiving ICIs. These results support a potential role of localized immune microenvironment alterations in CIP. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether these stromal immune features may have value in CIP risk assessment.
