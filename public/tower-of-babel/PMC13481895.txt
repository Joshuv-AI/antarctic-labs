Adaptive Pudendal Nerve Stimulation: A Pilot Study on Safety, Technical Feasibility, and Clinical Effect
Authors: Siemen Herroelen, Charles H. Knowles, Sean Doherty, Mo Benjaber, Jacob Kerr, Karen Wendt, Aidan Crawley, Tim Denison, Stefan De Wachter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo evaluate the safety, procedural feasibility, and pilot clinical outcomes for a new implanted device delivering adaptive pudendal nerve stimulation.Materials and MethodsThirteen adult women with treatment‐refractory mixed (n = 8) or urge (n = 5) urinary incontinence were implanted with a Picostim‐DyNeuMo system incorporating an implantable pulse generator and two quadripolar electrode leads. Leads were placed at the trunk and anterior part of the same pudendal nerve with radiological and electrophysiological guidance. Monophasic (pulse duration: 200 µs) stimulation at 15 Hz could be transiently adapted to 40 Hz by an embedded inertial detector (tapping over the device). Outcomes at 6 months included: safety (primary outcome), surgical feasibility, physiological (inc. urodynamic testing), and pilot clinical efficacy (voiding diaries; questionnaires).ResultsPatients were implanted with two leads without technical difficulties. Reported adverse events (including one expected serious adverse device event) and device deficiencies were transient or resolved with intervention. Mean daily incontinence episodes decreased from 5.7 ± 2.8 at baseline to 1.8 ± 2.9 at 6 months. Eight patients were responders (> 50% improvement), with five regaining full continence by the final follow‐up visit. All patients reported symptom improvement (PGI‐I score) and clinically meaningful improvements in quality of life (ICIQ‐OAB‐QoL and ICIQ‐UI‐SF). Seventy percent of patients used the tap function to adapt therapy on a daily basis. Stimulation during awake urodynamics at 6 months showed significant increases in mean maximum cystometric capacity from baseline with basal stimulation and further increments during adaptive stimulation.ConclusionsPudendal nerve stimulation using the Picostim‐DyNeuMo system is safe and technically feasible.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT05241379.

Introduction

The pivotal role of the pudendal nerve (PN) in the maintenance of continence is well‐established in experimental animals and man [1]. In humans, the PN provides volitional motor control of sphincter function but also has an important role in bladder control via afferent and autonomic fiber contributions to pelvic and spinobulbar reflexes [2].

At low frequencies, pudendal nerve stimulation (PNS) leads to rapid inhibition of detrusor contraction and has been therapeutically used since the 1980s in multiple studies of patients with overactive bladder (OAB) by surface and needle approaches to the cutaneous branches of the nerve, for example, the dorsal nerve of the clitoris [3]. Such approaches, using short‐term, intermittent “on demand” stimulation, are mirrored by a variety of more invasive techniques to permit chronic stimulation of the PN trunk (in the region of Alcock's canal) with promising results [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Such studies have generally repurposed existing proprietary neuromodulation systems from other targets, for example, sacral neuromodulation [4, 5, 9, 10, 12], have been limited in follow‐up [12], and have inconsistently documented adverse events [13].

To date, implanted neuromodulation systems employed to treat OAB have been limited to providing a continuous, constant level of basal stimulation. In contrast, adaptive neuromodulation is defined as the ability to dynamically change a neural interface in response to a measured neurophysiologic state to optimize the effect of stimulation in real time [14]. Devices for neuromuscular stimulation capable of environmental sensing and stimulation were first pioneered in the mid‐1990s, for example, implantable cardiac defibrillation. Developments in technology now permit continuous monitoring of the nervous system environment by sensing electrodes that can classify inputs at the device interface such as to control outputs. Examples include a number of brain‐computer interfaces that facilitate deep brain stimulation (for Parkinson's disease and epilepsy) [15], and a new generation of spinal cord stimulation devices [16].

While evidence in experimental animals [17] and man [18, 19] supports a role for patient‐responsive variation in electrical charge delivery to modify continence functions, to our knowledge, this has not been taken forward into the development of an implanted adaptive system in any species.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the safety and technical feasibility of a new fully implanted PNS system with adaptive functionality. The study also explored pilot clinical outcomes and accompanying changes in urethral and bladder physiology associated with stimulation.

Materials and Methods

A single‐center prospective cohort study (NCT05241379) recruited adult women with refractory (failing first‐ or second‐ line treatments) mixed urinary incontinence (MUI) or severe refractory (failing one or more second‐line treatments) urge urinary incontinence (UUI). All participants provided informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local ethics research committee [UZA‐Edge 002220]. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

A new fully implantable neuromodulation system (Picostim‐DyNeuMo) incorporated a rechargeable implantable pulse generator (IPG) with two quadripolar leads (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Under general anesthesia, one lead was placed at the PN trunk close to the ischial spine through an ischiorectal approach, and the other lead was placed at the more distal, anterior part of the PN and was accessed through a gluteal/posterior approach. Pelvic floor muscle (PFM), external anal sphincter (EAS) electromyography (EMG), and radiological guidance (fluoroscopy) were used to accurately place the leads. Two sets of bipolar needle electrodes were placed in the EAS at the 3 and 9 o'clock positions, and a PFM EMG probe (MAPLe) was placed intravaginally to measure the compound muscle action potentials (C‐MAP). The needle was stimulated at 2 mA and 15 Hz until a C‐MAP was found consistent with PNS. The desired responses were simultaneous EAS and PFM EMG activity. With the PN accurately located, a guidewire and introducer were inserted, followed by the lead. Each electrode was stimulated, and when three out of four electrodes provided the desired result, the lead was secured in place using a separately supplied passive anchor, which required fixation to the lead, then sutured to adjacent tissues. The leads were tunneled to the IPG site in the upper‐gluteal region. Radiographic control films were taken to document the fully implanted device (leads+IPG) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2).

Patients were discharged with optimal stimulation settings, which included a monopolar electrode configuration, the lowest effective stimulation amplitude, and the best EMG response. The Picostim‐DyNeuMo system embeds sensors and an algorithm that enable several modes of adaptive stimulation under voluntary (patient‐actuated) and automated control. For this study, a single‐patient‐actuated (accelerometer) function was implemented, enabling the patient to tap once on the IPG in the buttock to induce a defined period of increased charge delivery. Using this function (by tapping) resulted in a switch from basal stimulation (monophasic, pulse width: 200 μs, frequency 15 Hz) to an enhanced stimulation level (as basal but frequency 40 Hz with increased amplitude) for a duration of 30 s.

During a 6‐month follow‐up, patients completed voiding diaries and questionnaires (International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Overactive Bladder Quality of Life Module [ICIQ‐OABqol]; International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire‐Urinary Incontinence Short Form [ICIQ‐UI SF]; Female Sexual Function Index [FSFI]; Patient Global Impression of Improvement [PGI‐I]) at baseline (preoperatively) and at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. Follow‐up visits also included comprehensive EMG evaluations (PFM + EAS + EUS). Data processing was performed using dedicated software. Additionally, urodynamic studies were conducted according to the ICS guidelines [20] at baseline, 1 day after surgery, and at the 6‐month follow‐up. A detailed follow‐up table is provided in Supporting Information S1: Table S2.

Adverse events were reported according to the CONSORT statement for better reporting of harms [21]. Secondary outcome measures included technical feasibility, clinical, and physiological outcomes. Clinical success was defined as more than 50% improvement in mean total daily urine incontinence episodes (UIE).

Statistical analysis (JMP Pro 17) used parametric and nonparametric approaches to compare outcome variables at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months. A mixed‐effects model accounted for repeated measurements and variability between patients.

Results

Patient Population

Thirteen women met the inclusion criteria including eight with refractory MUI (stress and urge incontinence, many of whom had failed stress incontinence surgery and/or sacral neuromodulation and/or botulinum toxin, as well as lifestyle, physical, and pharmacological treatments), and five with refractory UUI (failing SNM ± botulinum toxin and having exhausted lifestyle and drug treatments). Baseline data for the cohort are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S3. One patient left the study for reasons unrelated to therapy, leaving 12 providing outcomes to 6‐months follow‐up.

Safety

Table 1 shows systematically recorded AE's during the 6‐month follow‐up period. There was one serious adverse device event (SADE) categorized as a lead erosion, which fully resolved after the removal of one lead. The patient elected to continue therapy with the remaining lead because it was still providing benefit. 12/13 patients had one or more AE. Postoperative wound discomfort was the most frequent AE (n = 9) followed by undesirable stimulation effects (n = 8), the latter resolved by alterations in programming and/or lowering the stimulation amplitude. The most common device deficiencies were charging‐related (n = 7) and difficulties operating the patient controller (resolved with further education). There were no device infections.

Technical Feasibility

Surgical procedural data are shown in Supporting Information S1: Table S4. Both leads were successfully placed in all 13 patients. On average, three passes (number of times the stimulation needle punctured the skin) were required to locate the PN trunk, and two for the anterior part of the PN. The mean duration for lead placement was 38 min (trunk) and 26.5 min (anterior) but there was an obvious learning curve: in the first five patients, 48 (IQR 19) min were needed to place the trunk lead compared to 32 (IQR 17) min in the later cohort, and for the anterior lead 33 (IQR 15) min were needed for the trunk lead, while only 18 (IQR 8) min were needed in the second half, leading to mean OR time of 180 min for the first five patients, and 105 min for the rest of the cohort. A high proportion of surgical time was spent in siting and securing the passive anchor which proved problematic to both insert into the required depth and then to secure; for three patients, this time also included removal of previous SNM devices. Intraoperative EMG analysis (PFM, EAS, and EUS) favored the trunk lead with higher signal amplitudes compared to the anterior lead. A representative measured urethral pressure increase is shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S3.

Clinical Effect

Mean total UIE decreased significantly at all follow‐up visits (Table 2): with mean reductions from baseline (5.7) of −4.3, −3.8, and −3.9 at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively. After 6 months, there were 8/12 responders to the therapy, defined as a reduction in IUE of at least 50%. In addition, 5/12 patients were categorized as “dry” meaning their symptoms reduced by 97% or more. These findings are further supported by a mixed effects model analysis (Supporting Information S1: Table S5). PGI‐I responses indicated that most patients felt their therapy had made them “very much better” (n = 6), “much better” (n = 1), or “a little better” (n = 5) at 6 months. Most patients (70%) used the adaptive stimulation (tap function) at least once per day, resulting in a mean adaptive use of three times per 30 min segment (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). Study subjects reported that after a period of learning, activation of the tap function could acutely suppress urge exacerbations. Although such reporting was anecdotal (qualitative data only), this finding was reinforced by the demonstration that the higher charge delivery induced by tap (increased frequency and amplitude) could further suppress detrusor overactivity leading to increases in cystometric capacity (see urodynamics results).

Quality of Life

Patients experienced a reduction in ICIQ‐OAB‐QoL and ICIQ‐UI‐SF scores (Figure 1). Using the four predefined ICIQ‐UI‐SF severity grades (ranging from “slight” to “very severe”), 10/12 patients had a reduction of one or more grades. ICIQ‐OAB QoL scores reduced in all patients, with a mean score of 110 points at baseline, to 58,75 at 6 months (mean reduction of 51,25).

Urodynamics

Basal stimulation significantly increased mean maximum cystometric capacity from baseline (224 ± 122 mL) to 305 ± 134 mL at 1 day and 341 ± 168 mL at 6 months. Tap‐induced adaptive stimulation resulted in further increments of MCC at 1 day (358 ± 140 mL) and 6 months (378 ± 175 mL) (Figures 2 and 3).

Discussion

This first‐in‐human study of adaptive PNS using a newly developed Picostim‐DyNeuMo system demonstrates that the surgical procedure is safe and technically feasible. With the caveat of the pilot (exploratory) design, the observed decrease in incontinence episodes (including a positive effect on MUI), improvement in quality‐of‐life measures, and the increased bladder capacity during urodynamic testing also provide some confidence in efficacy (particularly in a treatment‐refractory population).

Safety data were systematically collected and reported according to CONSORT guidance on reporting of harms [21]. This contrasts with previous PNS literature [13], where adverse events were infrequently documented and thence usually only in a brief text narrative. For instance, only one study by Groen et al. reported adverse events using a table format [22]. Data from the current study showed a high number of AEs, including one SADE related to the use of a commercially‐sourced passive anchor. This anchor was advanced onto the lead after siting the electrodes and was then fixed with surgical clips to the lead (by compression) and then to the surrounding tissues with sutures. The anchor was very difficult to advance into the dense tissues adjacent to the ischial tuberosity and led to increased operating time as well as migration, causing the single SADE by erosion. The anchor may also have accounted for reported wound discomfort by other patients. The passive anchor will not be used in future systems.

Other AEs arose from access to the PN (e.g., bruising) or could be extrapolated from those well‐established in the SNM literature (e.g., device migration and unwanted stimulation effects) [23]. Device deficiencies were mainly limited to problems of charging or by difficulties with programming. All were resolved by education, although future device iterations will implement a new charging system.

Our surgical technique aimed to position both leads parallel to the target areas of the PN. One lead was placed using an ischiorectal technique [8], positioning the lead parallel to the trunk of the PN by advancing medially from the ischial tuberosity towards the ischial spine. The other was placed using a modified posterior technique [4], with the goal of positioning it parallel to the anterior part of the PN. Although PFM contraction is commonly used as an indirect method to identify the PN during surgery, incorporating EMG of the sphincter may offer a more accurate and direct approach for locating the PN, thereby reducing the dependence on these indirect indicators [12]. The available literature provides no clear evidence of the superiority of one technique over another [13], and our data also do not allow for a definitive conclusion. Operative durations for PNS in previous literature ranged from 20 to 83−130 min for placement of one lead [12, 24]. Although our technique required a longer surgical time, this reduced quickly with experience and was often attributable to the use of the passive anchor.

Current study data showed a halving of UIEs during follow‐up. However, in addition to reducing symptoms in rUUI patients, we also showed positive results in MUI patients, where both stress and urgency components were addressed. In respect of mechanism, we demonstrate that such symptom reductions are accompanied by relevant changes in bladder physiology. Chronic stimulation using the Picostim‐DyNeuMo device was able to replicate observations made during acute stimulation paradigms using surface DGN stimulation with increases in bladder capacity [4, 6, 7, 22] and suppression of unwanted detrusor contractions (UDCs) [1].

The Picostim‐DyNeuMo device incorporates a novel adaptive stimulation functionality, which allows rapid switching (without reprogramming) to a higher frequency and amplitude of stimulation in response to need (by tapping over the IPG). While previous studies have explored this concept, all required external or intra‐cavity detection of physiological events to cue automated PNS. The presence of extruding wires and/or catheters from the rectum and urethra has previously precluded progress to chronic therapy [19]. Similarly, surface electrodes in the region of the clitoris (as used in other studies) are also impractical in the long‐term [25]. Our data demonstrate for the first time that measurable changes in bladder physiology could be triggered using a fully implanted system suitable for long‐term use. From a clinical perspective, the Picostim‐DyNeuMo system may offer a valuable option for patients with refractory urinary incontinence, particularly those who have not responded to SNM or Botox therapy. The adaptive nature of this system could be refined to further improve patient outcomes by dynamically adjusting stimulation in response to physiological changes.

Limitations

Several study limitations merit mention. First, the small sample size and single‐center design limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the lack of a control group makes inference less reliable—it is not possible to distinguish placebo effects from true effectivity. Third, while a follow‐up period of 6 months is in line with previous literature and is sufficient to prove safety and technical feasibility, it is too short to draw definitive conclusions on the long‐term effects of our therapy and possible late complications. Finally, the primary aim of our study was to evaluate the safety and feasibility, and not to draw strong conclusions on the clinical and physiological efficacy. Our results, though encouraging, should be interpreted with caution, and larger studies are needed to prove the potential efficacy of adaptive PNS.

Conclusions

Adaptive PNS using the Picostim‐DyNeuMo system is safe and technically feasible. With the caveat of the pilot design, this therapy has potential promise, including in MUI and refractory patient populations.
