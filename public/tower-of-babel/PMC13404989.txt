Heterogeneous responses to risk-adjustment reform: evidence from dementia diagnosis in Medicare Advantage
Authors: Sidra Haye, Mireille Jacobson, Julie Zissimopoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn 2020, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) diagnoses were reintroduced into the Medicare Advantage (MA) risk-adjustment model creating financial incentives that may increase diagnostic coding and plan payments. This effect may vary across plans depending on provider integration, administrative capacity, and geographic context, with implications for payments and potential “upcoding.”MethodsUsing 100% Medicare data from 2016–2021, we estimated event study and difference-in-differences models comparing adjusted trends in incident ADRD diagnoses in MA relative to traditional Medicare before and after the policy change.ResultsIncident dementia diagnoses increased in MA relative to traditional Medicare, despite an overall downward trend in diagnosis rates. Increases were larger among enrollees in MA health maintenance organization plans (20.8%) than in preferred provider organization plans (7.6%) and varied across insurers, ranging from 7.2% (United Healthcare) to 21.6% (Kaiser Permanente). Increases were more pronounced in urban than in rural areas.ConclusionReintroducing ADRD into the MA risk-adjustment model was associated with increased diagnostic coding, with substantial variation across plan types, insurers, and geographic contexts. Because ADRD diagnoses increase risk-adjusted payments, larger increases across some plans may reflect coding practices rather than true disease prevalence, highlighting the need for closer monitoring.

Introduction

Since the introduction of Medicare Advantage (MA), considerable policy attention has focused on how payments to MA plans are determined.1-4 Unlike the fee-for-service payment model in traditional Medicare (TM), payments to MA plans are risk-adjusted based on enrollees’ health, which is partly assessed using medical diagnoses from the prior year. While the risk-adjustment payment model discourages plans from enrolling only healthy patients and encourages the enrollment of sicker patients, this model creates financial incentives for plans to increase coding intensity (“upcoding”) to receive higher payments.2,4,5

In 2020, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) reintroduced Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) into the MA risk-adjustment model by adding 2 new hierarchical condition categories (HCCs): HCC-51 (“dementia with complications”) and HCC-52 (“dementia without complications”). Adding it back to the risk-adjustment model implicitly signaled that dementia is a clinically meaningful, nondiscretionary diagnosis that impacts medical spending. To reduce upcoding incentives, however, the 2 HCC codes were assigned equal weights.6

Prior to the 2020 policy change, MA enrollees had lower rates of dementia diagnosis compared with otherwise similar TM enrollees.7 Recent studies demonstrate that including dementia diagnosis in the MA risk-adjustment model led to increased incident dementia diagnoses rates among MA enrollees relative to TM enrollees.8,9 The increase was concentrated among beneficiaries who were more likely to have undiagnosed dementia— specifically, beneficiaries who were Hispanic or Black, were ages 85 years and older, or were dually enrolled in Medicare and Medicaid or received a Part D low-income subsidy.9

However, little is known about how responses to this policy vary across MA plans. Understanding heterogeneity across plan structures and insurers in responses is important for informing the design of risk-adjustment policies and efforts to limit overpayment and overdiagnosis of individuals due to upcoding practices.1,10,11 In 2024, the average Medicare beneficiary had access to 43 different MA plans.12 These plans vary across several dimensions, including the number of enrollees and geographical markets they operate in. A key difference across plans is in operational and benefit design. Medicare Advantage health maintenance organization (MA-HMO) plans, in contrast to preferred provider organization (MA-PPO) plans, offer a narrow network of providers who are integrated with plan operations, have centralized care coordination, and often require that beneficiaries obtain referrals to specialists and prior authorizations for some services.1 These restrictions come at lower cost to beneficiaries, with MA-HMO plans generally having zero premiums and lower cost-sharing. They also provide plans with more control over providers than the MA-PPO plans, potentially impacting diagnosis coding practices. In addition, geographic context may influence responses, as access to providers and diagnostics differs between urban and rural areas. Finally, large plans may have greater administrative capacity for coding and documentation, although reports suggest that coding practices even vary among large MA plans. For example, the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission (MedPAC) finds that coding intensity varies by 15 percentage points across 8 large MA organizations.13 Several other sources have similarly documented this variation across MA plans.1,3,11,14 Understanding these differences is important for addressing equitable access to diagnosis and care.

In this study, we examined how the 2020 reintroduction of dementia diagnoses into the MA risk-adjustment model affected incident dementia diagnoses, with a focus on heterogeneity across plan and market characteristics. We first compared responses between HMO and PPO plans, which differ in their ability to manage care and influence provider behavior. HMOs, which have integrated provider networks, may be better positioned to induce providers to respond to coding incentives than PPOs, which rely on more loosely affiliated provider networks and thereby have less direct influence on provider coding behavior. We then examined differences by several plan characteristics, including presence in urban vs rural areas and plan size. Differences by urbanicity may stem from better access to specialists, diagnostic resources, and health care infrastructure that are needed for dementia diagnosis. At the same time, greater provider density and competition in urban areas may increase provider willingness to adopt insurer-driven coding and documentation practice. Larger plans may be more likely to employ physicians directly, giving them leverage over provider coding behavior as well as having more resources to invest in coding management. Finally, we assessed variation across the 3 largest MA insurers—United Healthcare, Humana, and Kaiser Permanente—which together enroll approximately half of MA beneficiaries.11 These insurers differ in organizational structure, degree of vertical integration, and exposure to regulatory scrutiny of coding practices,3 factors likely to affect the response to risk-adjustment incentives. Kaiser Permanente's tightly integrated model may facilitate more coordinated coding practices, whereas United Healthcare and Humana may rely more heavily on chart reviews and health risk assessments. On the other hand, earlier regulatory scrutiny of United Healthcare may attenuate its response.15

Data and methods

Data

We used claims data for 100% of beneficiaries in TM enrolled in Medicare Parts A, B, and D, and Medicare Encounter data on all beneficiaries enrolled in MA plans with Part D between 2016 and 2021. Information about beneficiary enrollment was obtained from the Master Beneficiary Summary File. Our sample included community-dwelling beneficiaries aged 65 years and older who were continuously enrolled in either TM or MA for at least 2 years. Institutionalized beneficiaries in both insurance types were excluded since dementia diagnosis in this population was not included in the risk-adjustment model.15 Traditional Medicare beneficiaries in accountable care organizations were also excluded as they were subject to the same risk-adjustment model changes in 2020 as MA plans.15 Our analyses includes MA beneficiaries in HMO and PPO plans as at least 90% of MA beneficiaries are enrolled in these plans (see Appendix Figure S1 for details; to access the appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online).

Geographically urban and rural plans were defined using plan enrollee's county from the Master Beneficiary Summary File and US Department of Agriculture classification of urban and rural counties. We identified large plans including United Healthcare, Humana, Kaiser Permanente, and CVS Health based on their market shares (Appendix Table S1 for details). All other plans were categorized as small plans. Given the differences within large plans, such as plan structure (Appendix Table S2 for details), our subgroup analyses focused on heterogeneity among the 3 largest insurers in the market: United Healthcare, Humana, and Kaiser Permanente. While CVS Health is also a major insurer in the MA market, its merger with Aetna coincided with the timing of the change in the risk-adjustment model, making it difficult to isolate the effects of the policy from changes due to the merger. Therefore, we did not examine CVS-Aetna as a separate subgroup.

Outcome

Our primary outcome of interest was the percentage of beneficiaries with a new (incident) diagnosis of dementia. In each year we defined an at-risk population as beneficiaries who had no dementia diagnoses in the prior year. Dementia was defined using the codes from the Chronic Conditions Warehouse (CCW) using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (Appendix Table S3). Diagnosis codes were identified in the inpatient, outpatient, home health care, skilled nursing facility, and carrier files. Since MA risk adjustment is based on a single instance of the specified dementia codes, we required only 1 diagnosis code.

Covariates

We controlled for 5-year age group fixed effects, race/ethnicity, sex, and for dual enrollment in Medicare and Medicaid or in the low-income subsidy program. Race/ethnicity was based on the Research Triangle Institute's improved classification of self-reported Social Security Administration data.16 As certain chronic conditions are associated with higher dementia risk, we also controlled for diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, stroke, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension.17,18 We also included county fixed effects to control, for example, for fixed differences in MA penetration across counties. Consequently, our analysis compares diagnoses among TM and MA beneficiaries within the same county before and after the risk-adjustment change.

Methods

To examine how changes in the risk-adjustment model affected dementia diagnosis rates by plan characteristics, we first examined differences separately by MA plan (HMO or PPO) characteristic relative to TM. We first compared the unadjusted percentage of persons with incident dementia diagnosis per quarter in TM and by MA plan. Quarter of diagnosis was based on the first claim date with an incident dementia diagnosis. Beneficiaries were coded as enrolled in MA each year if they were continuously enrolled in MA for at least the prior year and the study year. Similarly, beneficiaries were coded as in TM if they were continuously enrolled in TM for at least 2 years.

We then estimated event study models to examine adjusted trends in incident dementia diagnosis in MA plans relative to TM before and after inclusion of dementia diagnoses in the MA risk-adjustment model. In addition to the covariates mentioned above, these models included quarter indicators, excluding the indicator for the last quarter of 2018, such that our estimates measured quarterly changes in diagnosis relative to the last quarter before policy implementation (see Methods in Appendix for details). The CMS announced the proposed changes to risk-adjustment methodology in December 2018, and as risk adjustment is based on prior year diagnoses, the policy could affect dementia diagnoses starting 2019.6

We also estimated difference-in-differences models to summarize the overall effect of the policy on annual dementia diagnosis rates. We fitted a linear regression model for annual incident dementia diagnosis with the primary independent variable specified as whether the beneficiary was enrolled in a particular MA plan type interacted with a post-policy indicator, equal to 1 beginning in 2019. We included controls for beneficiary sociodemographic characteristics and chronic conditions, year fixed effects, and county fixed effects (see Statistical Methods in the Appendix for details).15 To examine differences across MA plan types, we estimated separate models for beneficiaries in MA-HMO and MA-PPO plans, and for beneficiaries in 3 large plans relative to TM beneficiaries. We also estimated separate models for MA plans in urban and rural areas relative to TM beneficiaries in urban and rural areas, respectively.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we controlled for observable differences between MA plans and TM beneficiaries, unobserved differences in enrollees may remain.19,20 However, by comparing changes in incident dementia diagnosis rates before and after policy adoption in MA to TM, the methods eliminated unobserved group differences that are constant over time. Second, concerns have been raised about the completeness of MA encounter data, even as MedPAC has noted improvements in data quality over time.21,22 Importantly, data completeness also varies across MA plans, creating challenges for comparing outcomes across MA plans as observed differences in diagnoses may be due to differences in data quality rather than true differences in policy response. Prior work has found that encounter data provide reliable estimates of diagnosed dementia rates.7 A recent study examining the impact of risk adjustment on incident dementia diagnoses found that the estimates were robust to limiting the analyses to contracts with complete data, excluding chart reviews, and excluding 2020.9 As encounter data are used to make payments, it is important to examine the impact of risk-adjustment policy changes using these data, even if some limitations remain. Third, Medicare beneficiaries are allowed to switch their Medicare coverage from MA to TM, or from TM to MA, and switch to a different plan type within MA. Although the empirical strategy requires continuous enrollment in the year of incident diagnosis and the prior year, and switching rates are low,23 the difference between TM and MA would be smaller if many beneficiaries switched from MA to TM after an incident dementia diagnosis.

Results

Among 18 600 869 Medicare beneficiaries in 2018 who met the study criteria, 42.8% were enrolled in TM, 39.2% in MA-HMO plans, and 18.0% in MA-PPO plans (Table 1). Across all groups, the mean age of at-risk beneficiaries was approximately 74 years. The proportion of female beneficiaries was similar, with 57.8% in TM, 56.8% in MA-HMO, and 56.4% in MA-PPO. Compared with TM, MA-HMO beneficiaries were less likely to be non-Hispanic White (61.2% vs 81.4%) and more likely to be Hispanic (18.9% vs 5.7%), non-Hispanic Black (10.9% vs 6.06%), or Asian (5.60% vs 3.68%). MA-PPO beneficiaries were more similar to TM beneficiaries but had higher proportions of non-Hispanic Black (8.28% vs 6.06%) and lower proportions of Hispanic (4.13% vs 5.7%) and Asian (2.14% vs 3.68%) beneficiaries. Relative to TM, both MA-HMO and MA-PPO beneficiaries had higher rates of hypertension (65% and 67.8% vs 60.9%) and hyperlipidemia (58.8% and 63.9% vs 53.1%). Diabetes prevalence was 33.3% in MA-HMO, 28.5% in MA-PPO, and 27.6% in TM. Beneficiaries in MA-HMO plans were more likely to be dually enrolled in Medicare and Medicaid. Among MA-HMO beneficiaries, 16.1% were dually enrolled, compared with 14.1% in TM and 3.1% in MA-PPO plans. In 2018, the year before any expected impact of adding dementia to risk-adjustment models on incident dementia diagnoses, 2.36% of dementia-free TM beneficiaries, 2.33% of MA-HMO beneficiaries, and 2.13% of MA-PPO beneficiaries received an incident dementia diagnosis.

Event study estimates in Figure 1B demonstrate that incident dementia diagnosis rates adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, comorbid conditions, and county fixed effects were slightly lower in MA-HMO plans compared with TM in the 7 quarters before dementia diagnoses were included in risk adjustment relative to 1 quarter prior (F-statistic: 10.98; P < .0001). In 2018 quarter 1–3, 3 quarters prior to policy change, incident dementia diagnosis rates were similar in MA-HMO relative to TM. In the first quarter after policy adoption, the incident dementia diagnosis rates were 0.05 percentage points (P < .01) higher in MA-HMO plans relative to TM. The gap in rates increased through most of 2020 but narrowed in 2021. Nonetheless, rates remained higher in MA-HMO relative to TM in all 3 years after dementia diagnoses were included in the risk-adjustment model.

Figure 1C estimates for MA-PPO plans relative to TM suggest that the adjusted incident dementia diagnosis rates were lower in MA-PPO plans relative to TM in the 7 quarters prior to the policy change (F-statistic: 12.2; P < .0001). In 2019 quarter 1, the first quarter after policy adoption, the incident dementia diagnosis rates were 0.05 percentage points (P < .01) higher in MA-PPO plans relative to TM. While the overall incidence of dementia diagnosis rates in MA-PPO remained higher than TM in years after policy adoption, the difference in rates was more volatile and dropped below TM in 2020 quarter 3 as well as 2021 quarters 3 and 4.

In adjusted difference-in-differences models, annual incident dementia diagnosis rates were 0.49 percentage points (P < .01) higher in MA-HMO plans and 0.18 percentage points (P < .01) higher in MA-PPO plans relative to TM (Table 2).15 Relative to the baseline TM rate of 2.36%, this represents a 20.8% increase in incident dementia diagnoses rates in MA-HMO plans and a 7.6% increase in MA-PPO plans.

The impact of the policy varied by geographic context. Incident dementia diagnosis rates were 0.39 percentage points (P < .01) higher in MA plans relative to TM in urban areas but only 0.17 percentage points (P < .01) higher relative to TM in rural areas after changes in the risk-adjustment model (Table 2). Appendix Figure S2 presents the event study estimates. We found little evidence that responses varied meaningfully by plan size. Adjusting for beneficiary characteristics, the annual incident dementia diagnosis rate was 0.36 percentage points (P < .01) in small MA plans and 0.32 percentage points (P < .01) in large MA plans (Table 2). Event study estimates showed similar patterns over time (Appendix Figure S3). To assess quality or validity in the diagnosis, we also examined the impact of risk-adjustment policy change on “verified” incident dementia diagnosis rates, meaning a beneficiary had more than 1 dementia diagnosis code or dementia symptom code or drug code on separate occasions (see Sample in Supplemental Material). Appendix Table S4 (columns 1 and 2) suggests that, in addition to an increase in single dementia diagnosis codes, verified incident dementia diagnoses increased for both HMO and PPO plans relative to TM but more so for HMO plans. In separate analyses, we also excluded incident dementia diagnoses from chart reviews as prior work suggests that plans used chart reviews to add diagnoses.2,4,5 The estimates without chart reviews are smaller for MA-HMO (0.33 vs 0.49 including chart reviews) and for MA-PPO (0.05 vs 0.18) relative to TM, implying that, while some of the increase may be coming through chart reviews (Appendix Table S4 columns 3-4), a large share of the increase for HMO plans came from other sources.

While the overall impact on small and large plans was similar, substantial heterogeneity emerges across insurers. Among large MA plans, the estimated increase in incident dementia diagnoses ranged from 0.17 percentage points (P < .01) for United Healthcare to 0.45 percentage points (P < .01) for Humana, and 0.51 percentage points (P < .01) for Kaiser Permanente (columns 1, 4, 7 in Table 3). Corresponding event study estimates are presented in Appendix Figure S4. We also analyzed verified dementia diagnoses and diagnoses excluding chart reviews for these insurers. As expected, using verified dementia diagnosis rates narrows the gap in diagnosis rates across 3 large insurers, but not markedly so. However, the estimates without chart reviews were smaller across the 3 large plans. For United Healthcare plans, we found no difference in diagnosis rates relative to TM when we excluded chart reviews (0.00 vs 0.17 percentage points). In contrast, the gap without chart reviews is only 10% to 20% smaller for Humana (0.35 vs 0.45 percentage points) and Kaiser (0.46 vs 0.51 percentage points) relative to TM, respectively.

We performed numerous sensitivity analyses. First, to account for the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on health care use,24,25 we re-estimated models using data for 2017–2019. The estimates for MA-HMO and MA-PPO plans were similar to the main analyses (Appendix Table S4, columns 5-6). Second, we examined the effect of the policy on incident diagnoses of Parkinson's disease as a placebo outcome because it is a chronic condition that was not directly affected by the 2020 risk-adjustment change. The results suggest there were no significant changes in incident Parkinson's diagnoses (Appendix Table S4, columns 7-8). Third, we controlled for county-level MA penetration to account for factors that may influence MA enrollment and found similar results (Appendix Table S4, columns 9-10). Fourth, Appendix Figure S4 shows a sharp increase in incident dementia diagnoses in Kaiser relative to TM in 2020 that may indicate improvement in data quality or other pandemic-related health care use changes rather than the impact of risk-adjustment model changes. Therefore, we conducted analyses without MA beneficiaries in Kaiser and found similar results (Appendix Table S4, column 11). Fifth, the CVS and Aetna merge coincided with the risk-adjustment policy change, which may contribute to an increase in incident dementia diagnoses. Analyses without CVS/Aetna beneficiaries were similar to the main estimates (Appendix Table S4, column 12). Finally, we obtained similar results using various matched samples (Appendix Table S5 and Figure S5).

Discussion

Prior work found that including dementia diagnoses in risk-adjustment models led to a significant increase in incident dementia diagnoses among MA beneficiaries relative to TM beneficiaries, even after accounting for differences in beneficiary characteristics.8,9 Building on this work, our study examines how responses to this policy vary across MA plan types, insurers, and geographic contexts. Understanding plan-level variation is critical, as differences in provider networks, prior authorization practices, coverage rules, and coding practices may influence diagnostic processes.26,27 Insight into the variation in response by plan type supports efforts to improve MA reimbursement design, including efforts to reduce overpayment to plans and overdiagnosis of individuals due to upcoding practices.1,10,11

We conducted separate analyses for MA-HMO and MA-PPO plans, given the known structural differences between these plan types.12 Our findings indicate that incident dementia diagnoses increased in both MA-HMO and MA-PPO plans relative to TM. However, the increase was notably larger in MA-HMO plans, with an approximately 20.8% increase compared with a 7.6% increase in MA-PPO plans. Features of MA-HMO plans, including lower premiums, limits on out-of-pocket spending, and narrow but integrated provider networks,1 may contribute to more consistent and complete documentation of dementia following the risk-adjustment policy change. In contrast, MA-PPO plans offer partial coverage for out-of-network providers, which can lead to less-integrated plan–provider relationships and, consequently, less-standardized dementia coding practices.

Although prior studies have documented higher coding intensity in MA-HMO plans relative to TM,1,3 it is not entirely clear whether this reflects upcoding or improved detection, particularly for conditions such as dementia that are often underdiagnosed.9,28 A larger increase in MA-HMO plans could also indicate better detection among racial and ethnic minority groups, who enroll in MA-HMO plans at higher rates and are also more likely to have undiagnosed dementia. Prior work found that there were larger increases in incident dementia diagnosis among Black and Hispanic beneficiaries after dementia was added to the risk-adjustment model.9 Although we found that the estimated effect was larger for MA-HMO plans even in a matched sample, without evaluating the validity of the new recorded diagnoses, we cannot separate improved detection from plan-specific coding practices. As risk adjustment is based on the presence of a diagnosis in a given year and does not require confirmation or repeated documentation, the increase in diagnosis could reasonably reflect a one-time coding decision. However, the estimated increase in verified dementia diagnoses (and not just initial diagnosis) suggests that many patients receive follow-up care or continued clinical recognition of the condition. This finding is consistent with other work showing that the change in risk adjustment improved access to care for MA beneficiaries with ADRD.29

We also found that increases in dementia diagnoses were larger in urban than in rural areas. This pattern may reflect differences in access to specialists, diagnostic resources, and health care infrastructure, which can influence the ability of providers to identify and document dementia. The observed geographical differences could also reflect the concentration of high-coding-intensity insurers in these geographical areas.

Although we found similar increases in incident dementia diagnoses across large and small MA plans, these estimates may mask well-documented differences in plan structures and coding practices among large plans. Consistent with prior work documenting insurer-level variation in coding practices,3,4,11,13,14,30 we also found substantial heterogeneity in policy impact across large insurers. Rates of verified incident dementia diagnoses were somewhat lower than rates based on a single diagnosis for United Healthcare (0.13 vs 0.17), Humana (0.24 vs 0.45), and Kaiser (0.36 vs 0.51). Furthermore, when we excluded dementia diagnoses coming from the chart reviews, only Humana and Kaiser showed increases in dementia diagnosis.

Finally, the event study results showed that increases in diagnoses peaked shortly after the policy change and declined somewhat in later periods, although remaining above prepolicy levels. This attenuation may reflect disruptions in health care use during the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to a subsequent increase in 2021, as well as stabilization following an initial increase in diagnostic activity.

Conclusion

With MA enrollment now exceeding TM, changes to MA risk-adjustment models have direct consequences for the health care of a growing majority of older Americans and overall Medicare spending. Our findings demonstrate that the reintroduction of ADRD diagnoses into the MA risk-adjustment model in 2020 produced substantial and uneven increases in incident dementia diagnoses, varying systematically by plan type, insurer, and geography in ways that policymakers may not anticipate.

The differential response between HMO and PPO plans raises important questions about whether increased ADRD diagnoses reflect meaningful improvements in detection and diagnosis, particularly among underserved populations, or whether they primarily represent coding responses to financial incentives with limited clinical benefit. These mechanisms would have differential implications for the health outcomes of low-income beneficiaries and racial/ethnic minority groups who are more likely to enroll in HMO plans. Future research that examines whether increased diagnoses translate into greater uptake of dementia-related care will provide essential evidence for CMS to distinguish between risk adjustment working as intended and inflating payments without patient benefit. These findings also underscore that revisions to MA reimbursement methodology must account for the heterogeneity of the MA market. Verifying diagnoses and monitoring disease-specific health care use around newly incentivized conditions may inform CMS refinement of risk-adjustment models.
