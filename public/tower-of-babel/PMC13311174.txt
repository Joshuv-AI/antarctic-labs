Data-driven optimization of salt shock for high-efficiency ectoine production in Halomonas elongata
Authors: Junxiong Yu, Qingfeng Gu, Shuoyan Ji, Hao Liu, Yue Zhang, Chao Li, Yingping Zhuang, Zejian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHalomonas elongata stands as a highly promising industrial chassis, capable of low-cost fermentation under open, non-sterile conditions. However, precisely regulating the fermentation process to breach the production ceiling of ectoine remains a core challenge for its industrial scale-up. This study first designed and evaluated multi-stage salt shock regimens, demonstrating that a gradient salt shock strategy significantly enhances ectoine biosynthesis efficiency. Metabolic flux analysis further confirmed that this strategy triggers a redistribution of carbon flux from energy generation toward product synthesis. Specifically, it significantly enhanced the Entner-Doudoroff pathway and precursor supply while attenuating the Tricarboxylic Acid cycle, thereby achieving high-efficiency ectoine biosynthesis. Subsequently, a machine learning-based ensemble model for key physiological parameters was established using multi-batch fermentation data, enabling accurate prediction of fermentation performance under different salt shock patterns. This model was then coupled with a Genetic Algorithm to optimize the complex multi-stage salt shock process, leading to the identification of an optimal five-stage gradient salt shock strategy. The scaled-up process achieved an ectoine titer of 49.32 g/L with a productivity of 1.76 g/L/h, a yield of 0.38 g/g, and a biomass of 119.30 g/L in 28 h—setting new benchmarks for yield, productivity, and biomass in H. elongata. In conclusion, this work successfully established an efficient salt shock regulation strategy for ectoine biosynthesis and provides a feasible framework for the intelligent optimization of fermentation processes in halophilic microorganisms.Keywords: Halomonas elongata, Salt gradient, Fermentation optimization, Metabolic flux analysis, Ensemble modeling, Ectoine production

Introduction

Driven by the dual objectives of green biomanufacturing and the exploitation of microbial resources from extreme environments, moderately halophilic bacteria are attracting increasing attention as a natural cellular chassis. Their ability to thrive under high salinity effectively prevents microbial contamination during fermentation, thereby simplifying the process and reducing energy consumption, which aligns with the development philosophy of Next-Generation Industrial Biotechnology [1,2]. Early investigations using NMR spectroscopy revealed that tetrahydropyrimidines such as ectoine are the predominant compatible solutes synthesized by a wide range of halophilic bacteria in response to osmotic stress [3]. Among them, ectoine, a core osmoprotectant in these bacteria, has emerged as a highly valuable chemical in cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and biotechnology due to its remarkable ability to stabilize biomacromolecules and protect cells against multiple stresses [4,5]. The hydroxylated derivative, hydroxyectoine, which exhibits enhanced protective properties, is synthesized from ectoine by an evolutionarily conserved ectoine hydroxylase [6]. Recent screening efforts have further identified novel halophilic and halotolerant bacterial species capable of producing ectoine and its derivative hydroxyectoine, expanding the repertoire of potential production hosts [7]. However, wild-type strains often face the challenge of tight coupling between product synthesis and growth during fermentation, making it difficult to achieve sustained high yields. Although halophiles possess an inherent advantage for direct ectoine production, current batch fermentation control strategies have not yet fully exploited their metabolic potential [8], failing to meet the demands of industrial scale-up.

Salinity, as a core environmental signal, not only exerts global effects on cellular growth rate, respiratory metabolism, and morphology but also regulates the expression of key genes involved in ectoine biosynthesis, such as the ectABC operon [9,10]. Although salt shock strategies have been demonstrated to rapidly induce ectoine biosynthesis [11], improper control of the shock intensity and timing can readily cause irreversible osmotic and oxidative stress damage to cells, consequently inhibiting both growth and product accumulation [12,13]. Furthermore, simplistic shock strategies are inadequate for dynamically and precisely steering carbon flux towards optimal distribution within the complex central metabolic network, failing to sustain the anabolic flux for ectoine biosynthesis at high levels [14,15]. Therefore, the rational development of a salt stress strategy that coordinates the complex interplay among cell growth, stress response, and product synthesis constitutes a core scientific challenge for achieving high-efficiency ectoine biosynthesis. Beyond natural producers, heterologous expression of the ectoine biosynthetic pathway has been achieved in non-halophilic bacteria such as Corynebacterium glutamicum, wherein ectoine production is no longer strictly dependent on high-salinity cultivation [16].

In recent years, the rise of data-driven artificial intelligence (AI) technologies has provided powerful tools for addressing the aforementioned challenges and enabling the rational optimization of complex bioprocesses. Unlike traditional kinetic models, which heavily rely on detailed mechanistic knowledge, machine learning (ML) algorithms, such as artificial neural networks (ANNs), can directly learn from high-dimensional, nonlinear fermentation data to construct reliable predictive models without requiring a priori assumptions about the underlying complex mechanisms [17,18]. Further coupling with global optimizers, such as the Genetic Algorithm (GA), enables efficient exploration and automatic optimization within the vast process parameter space, thereby identifying superior process protocols that surpass conventional empirical approaches [19,20]. This “learning + optimization” framework has been widely applied across various stages of fermentation processes. Applications include the offline optimization of culture media and process conditions based on historical data, real-time monitoring and prediction of key variables integrated with soft sensor technology, and providing decision support for intelligent adaptive control, significantly enhancing the efficiency and robustness of biomanufacturing [21].

A central challenge in ectoine production is the inherent trade-off between cell growth and ectoine biosynthesis: while chemostat studies confirm the benefits of high salt and growth rates, sustained high salt in batch culture strongly inhibits growth, reducing productivity [22]. To address this limitation, we devised a multi-stage salt shock strategy that leverages the growth-coupled nature of ectoine production while mitigating the severe growth inhibition caused by initially high salinity. By applying gradual salt shocks throughout the fermentation process, we simultaneously achieved high cell density and high ectoine production, with flux analysis confirming that the ectoine biosynthetic pathway was continuously enhanced throughout the process. Subsequently, a hybrid machine learning and genetic algorithm model was developed to refine the dynamic salinity regimen. Ultimately, validation in a 50-liter bioreactor confirmed that the optimized process significantly boosts ectoine production. This study developed a mechanism-informed, AI-optimized fermentation strategy, offering a scalable and novel paradigm for industrial application.

Materials and methods

Strain, media, and culture conditions

The strain Halomonas elongata DSM 2581T used in this study was kindly provided by the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Plate activation medium (g/L): peptone 10.0, yeast extract 5.0, NaCl 80.0, agar 16.0.

Seed shake-flask medium (g/L): peptone 10.0, yeast extract 5.0, NaCl 80.0, pH 6.9 ± 0.1.

Bioreactor fermentation medium for 5 L fermenter (g/L): peptone 30.0, yeast extract 20.0, glucose 60.0, NaCl (added according to experimental design), antifoam agent 0.5 mL/L. The pH of all media was adjusted to 6.9 ± 0.1 with 2.0 M NaOH solution.

Seed culture: The strain was first inoculated onto a plate containing 30 mL of solid activation medium and incubated at 37 °C for 15 h. Well-grown single colonies were then picked and evenly spread onto a fresh plate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for another 15 h. Subsequently, the bacterial cells on the plate were scraped off using a spreader into a centrifuge tube containing sterile glass beads with 5 mL of sterile 8% saline solution. The tube was vortexed for 30 s to homogenize the cell suspension. This suspension was then used to inoculate a 500 mL shake flask containing 100 mL of seed medium, which was cultivated at 37 °C and 220 rpm for 20 h (5.0% inoculum size).

Fermentation shake-flask cultivation was performed using 500 mL baffled shake flasks at 37 °C and 220 rpm for 15 h, with a working volume of 100 mL and an inoculum size of 5.0%. The salinities in these cultures were set at 10, 50, 80, and 130 g/L, respectively.

Batch fermentation was carried out in a 5 L bioreactor (FUS-5L, Shanghai Guoqiang Bio-chemical Engineering Equipment Co., Ltd.) with a 3 L working volume. The inoculum size was 10%. During fermentation, the temperature was maintained at 37 °C, the initial aeration rate was set at 3 L/min, and the initial agitation speed was 600 rpm. The vessel pressure was controlled at 0.05 MPa. In the late growth phase, the pH was maintained at 7.0 using ammonium hydroxide, and the dissolved oxygen (DO) level was controlled by a DO-stat agitation cascade. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Multi-stage gradient salt shock strategy

Based on the hypothesis that each salt shock can stimulate ectoine biosynthesis, six experimental groups were designed (Fig. S1). Anhydrous NaCl granules were directly added at different time points to achieve a stepwise increase in the salinity of the fermentation broth, thereby continuously stimulating ectoine production. These six groups started with an initial salinity of 1.0% and reached a final salinity of 13.0%. They involved 2-stage (Group A), 3-stage (Groups B and C), 4-stage (Groups D and E), and 5-stage (Group F) salt shocks, respectively (Fig. S1). In Group A, the first salt shock increased the salinity by 5.0%, which was the highest increment among all first shocks, followed by Group C with a 4.0% increase. The initial salt shock for all groups commenced at the 6th hour. Specifically, Group A received shocks at the 6th and 10th hours; Groups B and C were shocked at the 8th, 10.5th, and 13th hours; Groups D and E were subjected to shocks at the 6th, 8th, 10th, and 13th hours; and Group F underwent shocks at the 6th, 7.5th, 9th, 10.5th, and 12th hours. It is noteworthy that prior to each salt shock, the foam level was monitored, and an appropriate amount of antifoam agent was added in advance to prevent rapid foam formation and subsequent broth loss due to foaming after the salt shock. The total fermentation time for all groups was 16 h.

High-density culture methods

The initial medium contained (g/L): peptone 30, yeast extract 20, NaCl 10, glucose 60, NaH2PO4 3.

The feed medium contained (g/L): corn steep liquor powder 100, glucose 500. These two substrates were supplemented separately.

Cultivation was performed in a 50 L bioreactor with an initial inoculum size of 10%. During fermentation, the temperature was maintained at 37 °C, the initial aeration rate was 30 L/min, the initial agitation speed was 400 rpm, and the vessel pressure was controlled at 0.05 MPa. In the late growth phase, the pH was maintained at 7.0 using ammonia solution, and the DO level was controlled above 30% by a DO-agitation cascade. When the fermentation time reached 15 h, corn steep liquor feeding was initiated at a constant rate of 300 mL/h using a peristaltic pump. The glucose feeding rate was adjusted based on its consumption rate to maintain the residual glucose concentration below 10 g/L in the later phase.

Analytical procedures

Biomass

Cell biomass was determined by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). The culture broth was diluted with an isotonic solution (with the same salinity as the medium) to an appropriate range for measurement. The final OD600 value was calculated as the OD reading multiplied by the dilution factor, using a Thermo microplate reader.

For dry cell weight (DCW) measurement, 6.0 mL of culture broth was aliquoted into pre-weighed 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes (recorded as m1). The tubes were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully removed, and the pellet was dried at 90 °C until a constant weight was achieved (recorded as m2). Special care was taken to aspirate the salt solution from the tube walls.

Ectoine and glucose concentrations

Ectoine quantification was performed as follows. A volume of fermentation broth was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube containing glass grinding beads and ground in a cryogenic grinder for 30 min at 75 Hz. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The resulting supernatant was diluted appropriately, filtered through a membrane, transferred to an LC vial, and analyzed using a Shimadzu liquid chromatography system equipped with a WondaSil C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm). The mobile phase consisted of water and acetonitrile (98:2, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 30 °C. Detection was carried out using an SPD-20AV detector at 210 nm.

Glucose concentration was determined using a biosensor (Model: SBA-40C, Shandong Academy of Sciences) at 510 nm.

Metabolic flux analysis

Based on metabolite balances, biochemical constraints, and pseudo-steady-state assumptions for intracellular intermediates, metabolic flux analysis was performed and mass balance equations were established for each intermediate metabolite. The metabolic model used includes the pathways and reactions as described in our previous study [22], incorporating the glucose consumption rate along with the production rates of ectoine and carbon dioxide. The mass balance reactions are expressed in matrix form as S·V = b, where S is the stoichiometric matrix, V is the vector of unknown fluxes, and b represents the net accumulation, input, consumption, or output of metabolites.

The stoichiometric matrix constructed in this study consists of 59 equations, forming a 56 × 59 matrix that constitutes an over-determined system. This system was solved using the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse method.

Ensemble models

Data preprocessing

A data augmentation strategy was employed to address the sparsity of the raw dataset caused by manual offline sampling [23]. For biomass and ectoine, fermentation kinetic equations (see supplementary materials II) were fitted to the experimental data points. Subsequently, the analytical solutions of these kinetic equations over the fermentation timeline were derived to generate high temporal-resolution simulated data. For other key physiological parameters, including the specific growth rate (μ), specific ectoine production rate (qp), specific oxygen uptake rate (qO2), and oxygen uptake rate (OUR), the Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (PCHIP) method was applied to out interpolate between the experimentally measured values. This ensured the smoothness and physiological relevance of the generated trajectories (Fig. 1A). Given the temporal continuity of the fermentation process, the dataset was partitioned by fermentation batch.

Data from Group D were designated as the test set, while the remaining five groups constituted the training set. This approach was adopted to prevent data leakage that could be introduced by random splitting.

Feature engineering and ML models select

In-depth feature engineering was conducted to effectively capture the nonlinear and interactive effects of fermentation time and salinity on the cellular physiological state [17,24]. A total of 43 derived features were generated from these two raw features, encompassing polynomial terms, interaction terms between variables, and rolling statistical features (Supplementary materials). Subsequently, feature importance analysis was performed using 15 commonly used machine learning algorithms, leading to the selection of the 20 most predictive core features. To enhance model robustness, models exhibiting over-reliance on any single feature were excluded. Ultimately, 10 machine learning models and the 20 feature variables were identified, collectively forming the input for the first-stage model, which was designed to predict the key process physiological parameters: μ, qp, qO2, OUR, and Biomass. The input features for the second-stage ectoine yield prediction model comprised the five key physiological parameters predicted by the first-stage model, along with the original time and salinity variables (Fig. 1A).

Genetic algorithm

The GA is a heuristic global optimization algorithm grounded in the principles of natural selection and population genetics [19]. It performs efficient exploration within complex solution spaces by simulating the “survival of the fittest” evolutionary process. GA is particularly suitable for optimization problems where the objective function is nonlinear, multimodal, or non-differentiable, and has demonstrated significant advantages in bioprocess optimization [25]. The core optimization objective of this work was to maximize the ectoine concentration at the end of fermentation (16 h), which was predicted by the aforementioned two-stage fusion model. The optimization aimed to identify an optimal time-varying salinity profile, defined by the following decision variables: the salinity increment of each shock (ranging from 1% to 6% (w/v)), and the timing of each shock (selected within the time window from 1 to 13 h after the initiation of fermentation). Since the number of gradient salt shocks was also a decision variable (set between 3 and 5), we adopted a systematic approach by performing independent GA optimizations for each possible scenario (3, 4, and 5 shocks). For each scenario with a fixed number of shocks (n), the chromosome was encoded as a fixed-length vector consisting of n shock times followed by n salinity increments: [t1, t2, …, tn, ΔS1, ΔS2, …, ΔSn]. This encoding scheme ensured that each optimization problem had a well-defined chromosome structure. After obtaining the optimal solutions for all three scenarios, the strategy yielding the highest predicted ectoine concentration was selected as the final optimal salinity profile. The optimization process was configured with an initial salinity of 1%. Through a series of gradient increases, the final salinity of 13% was reached around the 13th hour and maintained until the 16th hour.

In this study, the initial population size was set to 30, and the maximum number of generations was 50 for exploring the solution space. The crossover probability was set to 0.8 to allow parent chromosomes to exchange genetic information for generating new offspring. The mutation probability was set to 0.2 to introduce population diversity by randomly altering parts of the chromosomes, thereby facilitating escape from local optima. To ensure the physical feasibility of the generated solutions, hard constraints were embedded within the algorithm: the salinity profile must be monotonically increasing over time, and all shock time points must be strictly ordered chronologically. This was implemented to exclude unrealistic process scenarios.

Results and discussion

Two-stage salt shock serves as a proof of concept for enhanced ectoine biosynthesis

To validate the feasibility of a dynamic salt stress strategy for enhancing ectoine biosynthesis, this study first compared the effects of a single salt shock (1.0% to 8.0% NaCl) and a two-stage salt shock (1.0% to 8.0% to 13.0% NaCl) on the physiological metabolism and product synthesis of H. elongata at the 5 L bioreactor scale. Notably, a direct single shock from 1.0% to 13.0% NaCl was not included as a control, because preliminary experiments and prior studies indicated that such an abrupt osmotic shift leads to severe growth inhibition and fails to stimulate ectoine biosynthesis effectively [26].

The results demonstrated that the two-stage shock strategy more effectively stimulated the production potential of H. elongata (Fig. S2). Following the first-stage shock (salinity increased to 8.0% NaCl), an ectoine titer of 3.10 g/L was achieved in 3 h, corresponding to a volumetric productivity of 1.03 g/L/h. The second-stage shock (salinity increased to 13.0% NaCl) further stimulated ectoine biosynthesis, leading to a net production of 4.20 g/L over 3.5 h at a productivity of 1.20 g/L/h, which was 16.5% higher than that in the first stage. This phenomenon suggests that staged osmotic stress can sequentially activate the cellular stress response system. In contrast to a single severe osmotic shock, which may lead to incomplete metabolic reprogramming or irreversible damage [27,26], this gradual strategy likely provides a critical adaptation window for the cells. This window enables a more coordinated upregulation of key genes in the ectoine biosynthesis pathway (e.g., the ectABC operon) and a balanced supply of precursors, thereby sustaining a higher synthetic flux [9].

In terms of overall fermentation performance (Fig. 1B and C), the two-stage shock strategy increased the final biomass, total ectoine yield, and productivity by 18.3%, 25%, and 25%, respectively, compared to the single shock. This result strongly indicates that the dynamic salt stress strategy can more effectively harness the production potential of H. elongata under hyperosmotic conditions. Its success may originate from the strategy's better simulation of natural salinity fluctuations, which allows the cells to prepare for higher osmotic pressure through a “pre-adaptation” mechanism, consequently avoiding the global stress arrest that might be triggered by a single severe stress event [28].

However, the enhanced biosynthetic effect was transient. Cellular ectoine content peaked ∼2 h after the second salt shock, after which the difference from the single-shock condition gradually diminished (Fig. 1D and E). This transient nature underscores a delicate metabolic balance under hyperosmotic stress. Sustained ectoine biosynthesis at high levels requires substantial precursors (e.g., aspartate semialdehyde) and energy (ATP, NADPH). Under prolonged stress, cells appear to reallocate resources, shifting metabolic flux away from the “luxury” overproduction of compatible solutes and back toward essential cellular activities—a typical metabolic trade-off [29]. Consequently, this study not only validated the dynamic salt stress concept but, more importantly, identified the core challenge: converting a transient stress response into a sustained high biosynthetic flux.

Multi-stage gradient salt shock enables sustained stimulation of high-efficiency ectoine biosynthesis

Whether multi-stage salt shock modes could continuously enhance ectoine biosynthesis is shown in Fig. 2. Significant differences in the physiological metabolic changes of H. elongata were observed under the six multi-stage salt shock strategies. Specifically, Groups E and F achieved the optimal cell biomass and ectoine yields, reaching 48.93 ± 1.71 and 50.62 ± 1.61 g/L for biomass, and 18.68 ± 0.82 and 16.30 ± 0.78 g/L for ectoine, respectively (Fig. 2A and B). In contrast, Group A yielded the lowest biomass and ectoine production, reaching only 31.51 ± 1.11 and 11.30 ± 0.58 g/L, respectively (Table 1). The biomass and ectoine yield of Group C were slightly higher than those of Group A but markedly lower than the other four groups. The key to this performance divergence likely lies in the magnitude of the initial salt shock. The high-performance groups (D, E, F) had a maximum initial salinity increase of only 3%, whereas the low-performance groups (A, C) exhibited initial increases of 5% and 4%, respectively. An excessively high initial shock intensity likely exceeded the instantaneous upper limit of the cells' osmotic regulatory capacity, causing sustained damage to their growth machinery and consequently hindering effective recovery and product accumulation during subsequent stresses [27]. In contrast, a moderate initial shock allowed for more efficient transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming, thereby preserving growth potential and establishing a favorable physiological basis for coping with subsequent, higher-level stresses [30].

Regarding the dynamic response of physiological metabolism, the μ exhibited a gradually weakening response to consecutive salt shocks (Fig. 2C), whereas the cellular ectoine content showed a continuous increase (Fig. 2D). This opposing trend clearly reveals that under escalating hyperosmotic stress, cells progressively reallocate limited resources from biomass proliferation to the synthesis of the compatible solute ectoine, which is an essential adaptive response for survival [31]. Furthermore, in most experimental groups, the OUR values of the cells did not decrease significantly during the first two salt shocks. However, upon the third salt shock, a substantial decrease was observed; for instance, Group A exhibited a 55.1% reduction in OUR after the second shock (Fig. 2E). In comparison, the changes in OUR during the later stages were more gradual in Groups D, E, and F. This indicates that the multi-stage continuous shock strategy better maintained cellular energy homeostasis, which is crucial for continuously powering the energy-intensive ectoine biosynthetic pathway (consuming ATP and NADPH) [32]. It should be noted that the specific oxygen uptake rate (qO2) did not decrease significantly after the first salt shock, whereas each subsequent salt shock led to a reduction in qO2 (Fig. 2F). This observation is consistent with previous findings that salt shock exerts an inhibitory effect on cellular respiration [26].

Moreover, at the 16-h mark, the residual glucose concentrations in Groups E and F were 1.12 ± 0.35 and 0.33 ± 0.15 g/L, respectively, significantly lower than those in the other groups (Table 1). This finding aligns with their highest biomass levels, corroborating their efficient substrate utilization capacity and robust overall metabolic activity. As shown in Table 1, Group E achieved the highest productivity of 1.17 ± 0.03 g/L/h and a glucose-to-ectoine yield (Yect/s) of 0.38 ± 0.02 g/g. Compared to batch fermentation at 8.0% salinity, this represents an increase of 4.5% in productivity and 17.8% in yield, respectively [15]. It is important to note that the productivity was calculated based on the total fermentation time, which includes periods without salt shock. This approach may underestimate the true potential of the gradient shock strategy during the high-production phase. When productivity was calculated solely for the active salt shock period, all strategies except Group A exceeded 1.12 g/L/h, with Group E reaching as high as 1.85 g/L/h. This compellingly demonstrates the significant efficiency advantage of multi-stage gradient shocks in eliciting and sustaining a high-production metabolic state in cells.

Metabolic flux analysis elucidates synergistic enhancement of ectoine and oxaloacetate synthesis pathways

To gain deeper insights into the underlying metabolic mechanism by which the gradient salt shock strategy enhances efficient ectoine biosynthesis in H. elongata, MFA was performed on the optimal fermentation protocol (Group E). As shown in Fig. S3, the fermentation process was divided into five consecutive stages (5–6 h, 1.0% NaCl; 7–8 h, 3.0%; 8–10 h, 6.0%; 11–13 h, 9.0%; 13–16 h, 13.0%), corresponding to the stepwise increase in salinity. The carbon balance recovery for each stage ranged between 107.6% and 118.1% (Table 2). This deviation is likely attributable to the complex composition of the complex medium [33]. Nevertheless, the calculated flux distributions reliably reveal the shifting trends in intracellular metabolic flux.

Furthermore, the calculated data for μ, qs, and qp from each stage in Table 2 were incorporated into the constructed metabolic network model for computation. The results, shown in Fig. 3A, indicate that central carbon metabolism underwent systematic reprogramming as the salt shock stages progressed. The fluxes through the Entner-Doudoroff (ED) pathway, the ectoine biosynthesis pathway, the phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) carboxylation reaction, and the synthesis of aspartate (Asp) and glutamate (Glu) all exhibited significant and sustained increases. In contrast, the fluxes through the Pentose Phosphate (PP) pathway and the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) cycle decreased markedly after the first salt shock and did not display a consistent trend thereafter. It is noteworthy that the first salt shock elicited the most drastic alterations in metabolic flux. Specifically, the reaction fluxes of r16 (ED pathway), r30 (Glu synthesis), r34 (Asp synthesis), and r49 (ectoine biosynthesis) increased by 22.9%, 66.9%, 159.5%, and a remarkable 846.8%, respectively. Concurrently, the fluxes of r10 (PP pathway), r19 (TCA cycle), and r28 (Malate to Pyruvate) sharply decreased by 48.0%, 77.1%, and 92.2%, respectively (Fig. 3B). This series of coordinated changes indicates that cells initiated a global redistribution of carbon resources immediately upon the onset of stress.

The flux through the ectoine biosynthesis pathway exhibited the most pronounced enhancement. Compared to the baseline levels in the first and second stages (0.2 and 17.1 mmol/100 mmol glucose, respectively), the flux in the fifth stage reached 40 mmol/100 mmol glucose, representing increases of 199-fold and 133.4%, respectively. Furthermore, after the initiation of the salt shocks, the proportion of glucose catabolized via the ED pathway within the cells also increased significantly. Compared to the first stage (48.1%), the proportion in the second stage was 68.6%, constituting a 42.6% increase (Fig. 3C). Simultaneously, the anaplerotic reaction (PEP carboxylation) for generating oxaloacetate (OAA), a key precursor for ectoine, was enhanced. Conversely, the flux from malate (MAL) to pyruvate (PYR) was significantly attenuated. This effectively channeled carbon flux toward the synthesis of aspartate-family amino acids and ectoine (Fig. 3D and E). Collectively, the metabolic flux analysis in this section not only corroborates our previous findings regarding the regulation of metabolic flux by salinity [22] and the stress response of halophiles to salt shock [26] but, more importantly, it reveals the fundamental advantage of “gradient stimulation” over “single stimulation” in persistently activating the ectoine biosynthetic pathway. While the rapid salinity changes do not fully satisfy the pseudo-steady-state assumption—and therefore the flux values should be interpreted as indicative trends rather than absolute quantities—the consistent patterns observed across stages support the biological validity of this conclusion. This insight provides a theoretical basis and a rational design strategy for engineering microbial cell factories and optimizing ectoine production protocols.

Machine learning-guided salt gradient optimization yields superior fermentation performance

Two-stage ensemble models

To further explore the optimal multi-stage salt shock process, and building upon previous experience in constructing ensemble models for batch fermentation [17], a two-stage ensemble model was developed by training on the fermentation process data from Groups A–E. This model was designed to accurately capture the physiological metabolic response of H. elongata under dynamic salt stress. Compared to individual models, this ensemble approach demonstrated superior robustness and predictive performance, effectively mitigating the risk of overfitting on the small-sample dataset [34]. The predictive performance of the six constructed ensemble models on the test set is shown in Fig. 4. Except for the μ, the fusion models achieved R2 values above 0.92 for the predictions of the remaining five key physiological parameters. Notably, for biomass and ectoine concentration—two variables critical to process economics—the model predictions yielded R2 values greater than 0.97. This indicates that the constructed models successfully captured the core kinetic patterns of cellular growth adaptation and product accumulation under dynamic salt stress. This success underscores the application potential of machine learning as a powerful digital tool for handling data from complex nonlinear bioprocesses [18,21].

Predictive performance of the ensemble models for key physiological parameters on the test set.

It is noteworthy that the fusion model faced challenges in predicting the μ (R2 = 0.82). As observable in Fig. 4, μ exhibited complex, non-monotonic dynamics, in contrast to the relatively smooth profiles of variables like biomass and ectoine. Compared to batch fermentation under constant salinity, this profile displayed a biphasic phenomenon, suggesting the likely occurrence of “diauxic growth” or metabolic reprogramming as cells responded to consecutive salt shocks [26]. Furthermore, the model predictions for all six variables overall produced smoother profiles compared to the experimental data. For data-driven models based on a limited number of fermentation batches, it is challenging to fully capture complex dynamic features [35,36]. Consequently, acquiring high-quality, multi-batch fermentation data remains a critical step towards realizing intelligent biomanufacturing [37,38].

GA-derived strategy and experimental validation

The constructed two-stage ensemble model was embedded into the genetic algorithm as a surrogate model to perform global optimization, with the objective function set to maximize the final ectoine concentration. The resulting five-stage salt shock strategy recommended by the algorithm is presented in Fig. S4. The NaCl concentration was increased stepwise to 3%, 6%, 8.2%, 9.6%, and 13% at 2.4, 4.5, 6.0, 10.5, and 12.2 h of fermentation, respectively, and maintained until the 16th hour. This protocol applies relatively mild and sustained stress during the early exponential growth phase and maintains a moderate salinity level, conducive to both cell growth and ectoine biosynthesis, for an extended period during the exponential phase [15]. This approach facilitates a balanced allocation of metabolic flux between cell growth and ectoine biosynthesis, which aligns well with the physiological understanding gained from prior experiments [15]. To validate this optimized strategy, an independent fermentation experiment was conducted, the results of which are shown in Fig. 5. The model predictions for the dynamic changes in key variables were in strong agreement with the experimental values. Ultimately, the experimentally validated ectoine titer reached 20.30 g/L, representing an 8.7% improvement over the Group E protocol. However, the validation of the optimization results also revealed room for improvement in the fusion model. During the late fermentation stage (after approximately 13 h), discrepancies emerged between the model predictions and experimental data for biomass, OUR, and qp. The predicted values exhibited stagnation or decline, whereas the experimental values demonstrated better sustainability. These discrepancies may stem from the accumulation of minor errors in the predictions of intermediate variables or from certain late-stage physiological phenomena not fully captured by the training data [39]. This also explains why the model's prediction for the final ectoine titer was slightly lower than the experimentally measured value. In summary, the five-stage salt shock strategy optimized by the genetic algorithm in this section significantly enhanced ectoine production, successfully validating the effectiveness of the data-driven optimization strategy.

High-cell-density cultivation under an optimized regime achieves high-titer ectoine production in wild-type H. elongata

To evaluate the scalability of the optimized salt-shock strategy, high-cell-density cultivation of H. elongata was carried out in a 50 L bioreactor, with a focus on testing the machine-learning-recommended optimized shock protocol. Following the fed-batch procedure described in Section 2.3 and a total fermentation time of 28 h, the results presented in Fig. 6 show that under the optimized strategy, the maximum biomass reached 119.30 g/L, ectoine titer attained 49.32 g/L, the Yect/s was 0.38 g/g, and the productivity reached 1.76 g/L/h. Compared with existing literature, the biomass achieved in this study represents the highest level reported to date (Table 3).

Conclusions

This study established a data-driven framework that integrates systematic experiments, metabolic flux analysis, and machine learning to optimize ectoine production in H. elongata. The rationale for multi-stage salt shocks, identified as effective inducers of stress response and ectoine biosynthesis, was further elucidated through flux analysis. This analysis revealed the salt-triggered redirection of carbon flux from the PP pathway and TCA cycle toward the ED pathway, anaplerotic reactions, and ectoine biosynthesis. These quantitative insights directly informed the construction of a data-driven, two-stage ensemble model. Coupled with genetic algorithm optimization, this machine learning core designed an optimal five-stage salt protocol, which experimentally increased ectoine titer to 20.30 g/L. The strategy's scalability and industrial potential were confirmed via high-cell-density cultivation, achieving 119.30 g/L biomass and 49.32 g/L ectoine. This work not only deciphers metabolic regulation in halophiles but also establishes a novel, AI-augmented paradigm for bioprocess optimization.

Future prospects

The salt-shock strategy validated at the 50 L scale not only enhances ectoine production but also establishes a robust and competitive fermentation platform based on H. elongata. Looking forward, this platform holds significant potential for sustainable and integrated biomanufacturing. Future work could focus on replacing refined glucose with more sustainable carbon sources, such as glycerol, agro-industrial residues, or lignocellulosic hydrolysates, thereby improving the environmental footprint and economic feasibility. Furthermore, the approach could be extended to the concerted production of other high-value, growth-coupled compounds (e.g., hydroxyectoine, polyhydroxyalkanoates, or osmo-protective amino acids) within a multi-product biorefinery framework. Downstream valorization of fermentation residues—for instance, as single-cell protein for feed or as biochar for soil amendment—would contribute to a near-zero-waste, circular bio-economy. Together, these directions would transform the platform into a green, closed-loop system that significantly elevates the overall industrial value chain.
