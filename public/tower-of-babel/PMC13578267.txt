A LAMP-based assay for rapid detection of human scabies using clinical skin scraping specimens
Authors: Wonyong Kwun, Hanna Jin, Hyun-Sun Yoon, Min-Ho Choi, Ju Hyeon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScabies, a common parasitic skin disease caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, presents diagnostic challenges because of its diverse clinical manifestations and the limited sensitivity of traditional methods, such as skin scraping. Here, we developed a loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) assay targeting two S. scabiei-specific genes, Sarcoptes scabiei allergen Sar s 3 (Sars3) and Internal Transcribed Spacer 2 (ITS2). To enhance operational simplicity, a direct lysis protocol was employed, enabling rapid template DNA acquisition from skin scraping specimens without conventional DNA extraction. Primer sets were optimized for amplification speed and specificity; reaction temperatures were determined to be 67 °C for Sars3 and 61 °C for ITS2. The assay achieved detection within 35 min, with limits of detection of 103 copies for Sars3 and 102 copies for ITS2. No false-positive signals were observed across the 144 negative control reactions for each target. The assay demonstrated high specificity for non-target DNA from humans and house dust mites. Clinical validation using skin scrapings from patients with classic and crusted scabies yielded consistently positive results, whereas samples from non-scabies controls yielded negative results. These findings indicate that the developed LAMP assay provides a diagnostic platform for scabies, requiring minimal and portable equipment, making it suitable for clinical or point-of-care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59001-z.Keywords: Scabies, Sarcoptes scabiei, Molecular diagnostics, Loop-mediated isothermal amplification, Neglected tropical disease, Direct lysisSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biomarkers, Diseases, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Scabies caused by the parasitic mite Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis is among the most prevalent dermatological conditions worldwide. It affects approximately 200 million individuals and causes > 400 million cases annually. Its high prevalence makes it a significant contributor to the global burden of skin diseases, particularly in low- and middle-income countries1. Accordingly, the World Health Organization (WHO) has classified scabies as a neglected tropical disease (NTD). Infestation typically presents with intense pruritus and a widespread rash and is frequently associated with secondary bacterial infections, sleep disturbances, social stigma, and disruptions to school and work, particularly among children and older adults living in overcrowded households or institutional care environments2.

Over the past two decades, there has been a significant resurgence of scabies across various regions, with prevalence rates ranging from 0.2 to 70%, and the highest burden observed in the Pacific Islands and Latin America3. Increases have been noted in high-income countries, including Germany, France, Norway, and Croatia, primarily among populations with limited access to hygiene facilities, such as migrants and refugees4. Notably, the COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated the situation, with several regional studies reporting increased scabies incidence, likely due to delayed treatment, limited access to healthcare, and increased household contact during the lockdown period. Examples include significant increases in Lazio, Italy, and Adana, Turkey, underscoring the impact of the pandemic on scabies transmission5,6.

Scabies is transmitted primarily through prolonged skin-to-skin contact and outbreaks in institutional settings, such as hospitals, care facilities, and shelters. These outbreaks affect vulnerable patients and impose operational and financial burdens on healthcare systems owing to the need for infection control measures, staff isolation, and temporary ward closures7,8. A study of 70 hospitals in Korea reported that 86% had experienced scabies cases and 60% had encountered major outbreaks, highlighting the urgent need for improved diagnostic and control strategies9.

Accurate diagnosis of scabies remains challenging owing to its diverse manifestations, including nodular, bullous, crusted (Norwegian), and atypical pediatric forms10. Additionally, lesion appearance varies with skin pigmentation, which can lead to frequent misdiagnoses, similar to other dermatological conditions10. Conventional diagnoses rely on clinical signs and patient history. However, visual inspection alone lacks specificity, and the sensitivity of skin scraping remains low, particularly in mild or early-stage cases11. This limited sensitivity is largely attributable to the low mite burden in classic scabies and to sampling error, both of which increase the risk of false-negative results12. Such diagnostic uncertainty can delay appropriate treatment and hinder timely infection control, especially in settings where rapid confirmation is needed to prevent onward transmission. More advanced diagnostic tools, such as video dermoscopy and PCR-based molecular assays, can improve diagnostic accuracy; however, their routine use remains limited by equipment requirements, cost, processing time, and the need for trained personnel2,13. In particular, PCR-based assays generally require DNA extraction, thermal cycling, and laboratory-based workflows, which may pose challenges for rapid routine diagnosis or field application. Therefore, a practical molecular assay that is compatible with routine skin scraping specimens, requires minimal sample processing, and provides rapid results without complex equipment is needed.

Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) addresses these requirements by enabling rapid nucleic acid amplification under isothermal conditions, making it well suited for routine clinical or point-of-care applications. LAMP assays have been successfully applied for the diagnosis of various infectious diseases14–16 and their simplicity and speed make them particularly attractive for use in routine clinical or point-of-care settings. Although LAMP has shown the potential for detecting S. scabiei DNA in animal models17, its adaptation for routine clinical use in human scabies remains underexplored. Accordingly, we developed and validated LAMP assays specifically targeting S. scabiei var. hominis using clinical skin scrapings to establish diagnostic performance in a clinically relevant context.

This study aimed to develop a LAMP-based diagnostic assay for human scabies with high sensitivity, specificity, and operational simplicity to complement conventional diagnostic methods. We designed and screened primers targeting S. scabiei-specific genes and optimized the reaction conditions for speed and accuracy. Diagnostic performance of the assay was evaluated using clinical skin scraping specimens. To further simplify the workflow, we implemented a direct lysis protocol that enables rapid preparation of template DNA from mite-derived material without the need for conventional DNA extraction.

Materials and methods

Target selection and primer design

S. scabiei-specific candidate genes were identified from the whole-genome sequence data18 to obtain highly specific target regions that were not conserved in other mite species commonly found in the human environment. LAMP primers were designed using the NEB LAMP Primer Design Tool (https://lamp.neb.com) with slight modifications to the default settings. Because loop primers were not generated under the default criteria for some target genes, the Tm and GC content parameters were relaxed to facilitate primer design. The Tm ranges were adjusted from 64 to 66 °C to 63–67 °C for F1c/B1c and LF/LB and from 59 to 61 °C to 58–62 °C for F2/B2 and F3/B3, and the GC content range was expanded from 40 to 65% to 35–65%. Seventeen primer sets targeting seven S. scabiei-specific sequences (Sarcoptes scabiei allergen Sar s 1 [Sars 1], S. scabiei allergen Sar s 3 [Sars3], Internal Transcribed Spacer 1 [ITS1], Internal Transcribed Spacer 2 [ITS2], cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 [cox1], NADH dehydrogenase subunit 4 [ND4], intergenic spacer [IGS]) were designed, and the best-performing primer sets were selected for further experiments.

Cloning for plasmid template preparation

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from 20 scabies mites obtained from a skin scraping specimen of a patient with scabies using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Partial sequences of Sars3 and ITS2 were amplified by PCR using a total reaction volume of 20 µl, containing 3 ng of template DNA, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (5 pmol/µL; Table 1), 1.6 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 2 µL of 10X buffer, and 0.4 µL of 50X Advantage 2 Polymerase Mix (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Thermal cycling conditions were as follows; initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 34 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 59 °C for 20 s, and 68 °C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 68 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using the Expin™ PCR SV Kit (GeneAll Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea), cloned into the pGEM-T Easy Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea). Plasmid DNA was extracted using the AccuPrep® Plasmid Mini Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) and sequenced (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea) to confirm the target sequences.

Optimization of reaction conditions for the LAMP assay

Real-time LAMP (rt-LAMP) was performed to optimize the reaction conditions for the LAMP assay. Each 10 µL reaction mixture contained 5 µL of WarmStart® Multi-Purpose LAMP/RT-LAMP 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 1 µL of plasmid containing the Sars3 or ITS2 target sequence (10⁵ copies/µl), 1 µL of primer mix (final concentrations: 1.6 µM FIP, 1.6 µM BIP, 0.2 µM F3, 0.2 µM B3, 0.4 µM LF, and 0.4 µM LB; Table 1), 0.2 µL of LAMP Fluorescent Dye (New England Biolabs), and 2.8 µL of nuclease-free water (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). As a negative control, 1 µL of nuclease-free water was used instead of the plasmid template. Reactions were performed using a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). To determine the optimal reaction temperature, the assays were incubated at 61 °C, 63 °C, 65 °C and 67 °C for 60 min, with fluorescence recorded at 1-min intervals. Each reaction was performed in four replicates. For each target, the temperature conditions that resulted in the fastest amplification without false-positive signals were selected for subsequent experiments.

After selecting the optimal temperature, endpoint LAMP was performed to determine the minimum reaction time required for visible light detection. The reaction mixture contained 5 µL of WarmStart® Multi-Purpose LAMP/RT-LAMP 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 1 µL of plasmid DNA containing the Sars3 or ITS2 target sequence (102-105 copies/µl), 1 µL of the same primer mix above, and 3 µL of nuclease-free water. Nuclease-free water was used as a template control for negative reactions. The reactions were incubated at the selected temperature and terminated at 10-min intervals from 15 to 55 min. After each reaction, 1 µL of 1:10-diluted SYBR™ Green I Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Invitrogen) was added to visualize amplification. The earliest amplification time at which the lowest detectable concentration of the template yielded a positive signal was considered the diagnostic time.

To further evaluate the risk of false-positive amplification, additional 144 reactions for Sars3 and ITS2 were performed using nuclease-free water as a template under the optimized temperature and diagnostic time conditions.

Analytical specificity and detection limit of the LAMP assay

Analytical specificity testing was conducted to assess the potential cross-reactivity with non-target species, including two house dust mite species and human-derived DNA. gDNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) from approximately 500 individuals of Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus and D. farinae and human dermal fibroblasts (HFF-1, ATCC® SCRC-1041™), provided by Prof. Hyun Beom Song (Seoul National University College of Medicine). Reactions were performed using 1 and 10 ng of each gDNA template, with previously optimized primer concentrations and incubation temperatures. Nuclease-free water and 1 ng of S. scabiei genomic DNA were used as negative and positive controls, respectively.

To evaluate the detection limit of the LAMP assay, 10-fold serial dilutions of plasmid DNA containing the Sars3 and ITS2 target sequences were prepared, ranging from 107 to 101 copies/µL. The LAMP reactions were conducted under the same reaction conditions as described above. After 35 min of incubation, 1 µL of 1:10-diluted SYBR™ Green I Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Invitrogen) was added to each reaction tube to visualize amplification, followed by confirmation via gel electrophoresis. All experiments were repeated 10 times to ensure reproducibility.

Clinical validation of the LAMP assay

Skin scrapings were collected from patients with suspected scabies using a sterile blade for routine microscopic examination. Following diagnosis, residual specimens were placed in sterile microcentrifuge tubes and used in this study. To validate the diagnostic method across a range of clinical severities, patients were classified into three groups according to the 2020 International Alliance for the Control of Scabies (IACS) criteria2, specifically Levels A and B. Level A, corresponding to confirmed scabies, was further subdivided into two groups to reflect differences in clinical phenotype and parasite burden: (1) crusted scabies, defined by clinically compatible hyperkeratotic or crusted lesions together with high parasite burden, and (2) typical confirmed scabies, defined by microscopic detection of mites or eggs without clinical features suggestive of crusted scabies. The third group, (3) clinical scabies, included patients who met the IACS Level B criteria despite the absence of microscopically detectable mites or eggs. All patients were treatment-naïve at the time of sample collection. For negative controls, residual skin scrapings were obtained in the same manner as for patients with clinically suspected superficial fungal infections. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of SMG-SNU Boramae Medical Center (30-2020-211). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB owing to the use of de-identified discarded specimens. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

To obtain S. scabiei gDNA directly from clinical skin scraping specimens, STEP lysis buffer (50 mM KCl, 0.25% Triton X-100, 2.5 mM EDTA, and 2.5 mM NaOH) was used19. Approximately 1 mm³ of skin scraping sample, estimated by visual inspection, was incubated with 10 µL of STEP buffer at 95 °C for 5 min. If the specimen comprised powdery skin scales, 20 µL of lysis buffer was used. After a brief centrifugation at 5,000 g for 5 s, 1 µL of the supernatant was used as the template for the LAMP reaction, which was performed under the same conditions as described above. Amplification was evaluated using SYBR Green staining and gel electrophoresis.

Besides SYBR Green staining, amplification results were confirmed using a lateral flow assay (LFA) with the Milenia GenLine HybriDetect system (Milenia Biotec, Gießen, Germany). To enable LFA detection, specific primer pairs were labeled with biotin and fluorescein amidite (FAM). For Sars3, the FIP primer was labeled with biotin at the 5′ end, and the BIP primer was labeled with FAM at the 3′ end. For ITS2, the LF primer carried a 5′ biotin label, and the LB primer was labeled with FAM at the 3′ end. Following LAMP amplification, 7 µL of the reaction product was applied directly to the sample application pad of the test strip, which was inserted vertically into 100 µL of chase buffer and incubated at room temperature for 5 min.

To estimate the genomic copy numbers of Sars3 and ITS2 in clinical skin scraping specimens, qPCR was performed using plasmid DNA-based standard curves. Standard curves for each target were generated using ten-fold serial dilutions of plasmid DNA containing the corresponding Sars3 or ITS2 target sequence. Released gDNA was prepared from eight independent patient-derived skin scraping specimens by direct lysis as described above. Each qPCR reaction was prepared in a total volume of 10 µL containing 5 µL of TB Green® Fast qPCR Mix (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan), 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (2.5 pmol/µL), 1 µL of released gDNA, and 2 µL of nuclease-free water. Amplification was performed using the CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s and 60℃ for 10 s. The copy numbers of Sars3 and ITS2 in clinical samples were calculated from the corresponding plasmid standard curves.

To compare the performance of the LAMP assay with a conventional molecular method, PCR was performed using the identical released gDNA used for the LAMP assay. Because the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (mtCOI) gene is widely used as a molecular marker for S. scabiei detection and identification13,20,21, a 655-bp fragment of this gene was amplified for conventional PCR. Each 10 µL reaction contained 1 µL of released gDNA, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (2.5 pmol/µL; Table 1), 0.8 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 1 µL of 10× buffer, 0.05 µL of Ex Taq polymerase (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), and nuclease-free water. PCR was conducted under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 2 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 3 min.

Results

Optimization of primer performance and amplification conditions

The best-performing primer sets targeting Sars3 and ITS2 were ultimately selected based on their rapid reaction times and absence of false-positive results (Fig. 1). Subsequently, rt-LAMP was performed to optimize the reaction temperature for each primer set (Fig. 2). For the Sars3 primer, detectable amplification by rt-LAMP began within a similar time range (12–15 min) across all temperature conditions; however, only at 67 °C were no false-positive signals observed within 60 min. For ITS2, no false positives were detected at any temperature for up to 60 min. However, the amplification time varied depending on the temperature, with the fastest amplification observed at 61 °C. Therefore, all subsequent experiments were conducted at 67 °C for Sars3 and 61 °C for ITS2.

Analytical specificity and detection limit of the LAMP assay

To evaluate the specificity of the developed S. scabiei primers, a LAMP assay was performed using gDNA from D. pteronyssinus, D. farinae, and human cells. For both Sars3 and ITS2 primer sets, positive signals were observed only with S. scabiei gDNA, as determined by SYBR Green I staining, whereas no amplification occurred in reactions containing non-target gDNA templates (Fig. 5). These results were further confirmed by the presence of characteristic ladder-like bands on the gel. To evaluate the detection limit, 10-fold serial dilutions of plasmid DNA containing Sars3 and ITS2 target sequences were used as templates for the LAMP reaction. Amplification of Sars3 was detected at concentrations > 103 copies, whereas ITS2 was amplified at concentrations as low as 102 copies, which were determined to be the respective limits of detection for each target (Fig. 6A, B). qPCR was then performed using patient-derived skin scraping templates prepared under the same direct lysis procedure used for the subsequent clinical validation. The mean estimated copy numbers of Sars3 and ITS2 were approximately 5.6-fold and 335.6-fold higher than their respective limits of detection, respectively (Fig. 6C).

Clinical validation of the LAMP assay

The effectiveness of the protocol was validated using clinical skin scraping specimens. Among the scabies-positive samples, specimens 1–5, 7, and 9–14 were obtained from patients with classic scabies classified as category A1 according to the IACS criteria. Specimen 6 was classified as category B1, and specimen 8 was obtained from a patient with crusted scabies. Specimens 15–20, which were collected from patients with clinically suspected superficial fungal infections, served as non-target negative controls.

Compared with conventional PCR, which produced visible bands for 12 of 14 scabies specimens (S1 Fig.), Consistent positive results for S. scabiei were obtained from specimens 1–14 using all three detection methods: SYBR Green I staining, gel electrophoresis, and LFA. Positive amplification was observed not only in the crusted scabies specimen (no. 8), which was expected to have a high mite burden, but also in classic scabies specimens, including those without microscopically detectable mites or eggs (no. 6) (Fig. 7). No amplification was observed in specimens 15–20, confirming the specificity of the assay in clinical settings. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the clinical applicability of this assay for the detection of classic and crusted scabies.

Discussion

Here, we developed and validated a LAMP assay for the detection of S. scabiei gDNA from human skin scraping specimens as a diagnostic tool for scabies. The assay demonstrated high analytical specificity and sensitivity with no false-positive results. Reliable amplification was achieved at detection limits of 10³ copies for Sars3 and 10² copies for ITS2 using plasmids containing the corresponding target sequences. Amplification was completed within 35 min. When combined with the rapid DNA preparation protocol using STEP buffer, the total time from tissue lysis to result was < 45 min.

This assay offers a streamlined molecular approach to complement conventional skin scraping microscopy. By detecting specific molecular targets rather than relying on the visualization of intact mites, it serves as a valuable tool, particularly in cases where mite burden is low. Consistent with this rationale, the LAMP assay detected all scabies-positive clinical specimens in this study, including specimens without microscopically detectable mites or eggs and those that did not produce visible amplification by conventional PCR. These findings suggest that the assay may detect mite-derived genetic material remaining in skin lesions, thereby offering an advantage when target DNA is limited.

Another advantage of this workflow is simplified sample preparation. We implemented a direct lysis protocol requiring 5 min of heating, eliminating the need for gDNA extraction, and reducing technical complexity. Although simple water-based lysis may be sufficient when specimens contain abundant mite-derived material, such as in crusted scabies or heavily infested lesions, this condition may not represent routine clinical samples. In many routine clinical settings, the quantity of mite-derived DNA may be limited and not proportional to clinical severity, particularly in early-stage infestations22. Moreover, the quality and depth of skin scrapings can affect the likelihood of capturing the target material23. To improve the amplification success in specimens with limited target DNA, such as skin scrapings containing only mite exuviae, eggs, or scybala rather than whole mites, we introduced a customized STEP lysis buffer to enhance gDNA release. This buffer was specifically optimized for efficient gDNA release from minute amounts of arthropod-derived materials, making it suitable for clinical samples in which the target DNA is extremely sparse. Building on this strategy, recently developed LAMP-CRISPR-Cas12a nucleic acid detection systems24–26 may provide a useful future approach to further improve sensitivity for low-abundance target DNA and enhance specificity through guide RNA-mediated target confirmation.

The choice of detection format is another important consideration for translating LAMP into near-patient use. Although colorimetric LAMP platforms offer advantages, such as visual detection and elimination of post-reaction dyes, we occasionally observed immediate color shifts when crude skin lysates were added to the LAMP reaction, which interfered with the accurate interpretation of the results (S2 Fig). This issue was inconsistent and observed regardless of whether water or STEP buffer was used for lysis, suggesting that pH-related interference from human skin components may be involved. Therefore, we selected conventional LAMP formats combined with SYBR Green or LFA for this study. If this limitation can be addressed in future studies, colorimetric LAMP could enable faster interpretation of results without additional reagents or processing steps.

Beyond the detection format, reagent stability is also important for near-patient implementation. To enhance the near-patient applicability of the assay, we explored a lyophilized reaction format in which all reagents, excluding the DNA template, were premixed and lyophilized. In this format, amplification efficiency was retained for at least one month at 25 °C (S3 Fig), and the assay could be initiated by simple rehydration with water and the addition of template DNA, suggesting practical advantages for outpatient and facility-based use. Because LAMP operates under isothermal conditions, the entire process can be performed using portable battery-powered heating devices. If combined with lyophilized reagents, this approach minimizes the cold-chain requirements and technical burden, supporting its potential use in dermatology clinics, long-term care facilities, and other institutional environments where rapid confirmation can inform timely treatment and infection control measures. Moreover, the simplicity and stability of the system make it promising for screening and outbreak response in congregate care settings in which the laboratory infrastructure is limited. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the practical utility of this assay.

As with any diagnostic study, several considerations remain. The current workflow still relies on skin scraping, and in routine clinical settings, conventional microscopy may offer a shorter turnaround time than this workflow when adequate mite-containing material is obtained by an experienced examiner. However, when screening multiple suspected cases is required, such as during outbreaks in healthcare and long-term care facilities, the molecular workflow developed here can provide operational advantages by enabling standardized processing and high-throughput analysis. Because this assay was developed using a relatively limited number of specimens collected within a single-country setting, conducting further large-scale validation in broader and more diverse clinical cohorts, including specimens representing additional IACS categories, would be valuable to strengthen confidence in its diagnostic performance across different patient populations, disease severities, and geographic regions.

Overall, this study presents a rapid, sensitive LAMP assay for the diagnosis of scabies using human skin scrapings, with potential for routine clinical and point-of-care applications. Its rapid turnaround time, minimal sample preparation, and compatibility with portable equipment support its application in frontline dermatology practice and institutional care settings, where prompt diagnostic confirmation is often required for patient management and outbreak control. With further validation and simplified sampling, this platform could support routine care and outbreak-response screening.
