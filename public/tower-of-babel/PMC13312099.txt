The perceptions and lived experiences of Indonesian nursing students in providing end-of-life care: A qualitative study
Authors: Chalita Diandra Natalie, Yenni Ferawati Sitanggang, Oscar Fidel Antunez Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnd-of-life (EoL) care is a form of service provided to patients in the terminal phase and families to meet physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs so that patients can face death peacefully and with dignity. EoL care can be facilitated by medical personnel, but it is not uncommon for nursing students to be involved as well. Several factors influence nursing students’ ability to provide EoL care to patients.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the nursing students’ perceptions and experiences regarding the provision of EoL care during clinical placements.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted using purposive sampling and in-depth interviews with 10 professional nursing students who had experience providing EoL care. Data were collected with a four-question guide between February and May 2025 and analyzed inductively using thematic analysis, including familiarization, coding, theme development, and reporting. Strategies to enhance trustworthiness included credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability.ResultsFour main themes emerged: 1) Nursing Students’ perspective of EoL Care, 2) Providing holistic Care to Patients and Families, 3) Challenges and Opportunities in Providing EoL Care, 4) Developing Heart of Caring. Participants conceptualized EoL as a holistic, person-centered process emphasizing comfort, dignity, and compassion rather than cure. They demonstrated developing competence in identifying clinical indicators of terminal decline and addressing patients’ physical, psychosocial, and spiritual needs. Students described providing comfort through symptom management, active listening, emotional support, and the facilitation of spiritual practices aligned with patients’ beliefs. Although communication difficulties, emotional strain, and insufficient preparation were significant barriers, they were also perceived as valuable learning opportunities that fostered personal growth, empathy, and professional confidence. Spiritual faith and viewing patients as family members further inspired compassionate, value-driven care.ConclusionOverall, the findings emphasize that experiential and reflective learning during clinical placements are essential in shaping nursing students’ moral sensitivity, resilience, and humanistic orientation. Integrating Structured EoL care education that includes emotional and spiritual dimensions is crucial to preparing future nurses to deliver dignified, compassionate, and holistic care for patients and families at EoL.

Background

End-of-life (EoL) care constitutes a fundamental component of quality healthcare services, aiming to alleviate suffering and preserve dignity for patients with life-limiting illnesses and their families. Globally, an estimated 56.8 million individuals require palliative or EoL care each year, yet fewer than one in five receive adequate services (World Health Organization [WHO], 2020). Furthermore, EoL care remains one of the most complex and emotionally demanding aspects of healthcare practice (Huffman & Harmer, 2019; Kim & Kim, 2021).

Nurses play a central role in EoL care delivery due to their sustained proximity to patients and families during the dying process, positioning them as key providers (Jones et al., 2023; Muliira et al., 2023; Nacak & Erden, 2022). Importantly, nursing students are also frequently involved in caring for dying patients during clinical placements, often before they feel adequately prepared for this role (Abbaspour & Heydari, 2021).

International qualitative studies indicate that nursing students frequently encounter dying patients during clinical placements but report insufficient preparation, emotional distress, and uncertainty in communication and symptom management (Chua & Shorey, 2021; Dorney & Pierangeli, 2021; Jeffers et al., 2022). Students often describe fear of causing harm, difficulty supporting families, and discomfort addressing spiritual concerns, particularly when theoretical instruction is poorly aligned with clinical realities (Cao et al., 2022; García-Navarro et al., 2023).

Although previous studies have explored nursing students’ attitudes toward death and palliative care, much of the literature is quantitative or conducted in Western contexts, limiting insight into students’ lived experiences within culturally and religiously distinct settings (Elzeiny et al., 2025; Ju et al., 2026; Zahran et al., 2022). In Southeast Asia, particularly in Indonesia, empirical qualitative research on nursing students’ firsthand experiences caring for dying patients remains scarce.

Existing Indonesian studies largely address nurses’ knowledge barriers or systemic challenges, rather than students’ emotional, moral, and spiritual meaning-making processes during EoL encounters (Tampubolon et al., 2021; Utami & Putri, 2022). Similarly, international evidence suggests that nurses often struggle with spiritual and cultural aspects of EoL care due to insufficient educational preparation (O'Brien et al., 2019). Poor practice environments and a lack of institutional support have also been linked to suboptimal quality of EoL care (Lasater et al., 2019).

Given Indonesia’s sociocultural and religious diversity, understanding how nursing students interpret and enact EoL care is essential to inform culturally responsive education and supervision. Addressing the gap is urgent, as inadequate preparation may compromise both student well-being and quality of patient care. Thus, this study aimed to explore Indonesian nursing students’ perceptions and lived experiences regarding the provision of EoL care during clinical placements.

Methods

Study Design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design using thematic analysis to explore nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of providing EoL care during clinical placements (Sandelowski, 2000). Qualitative description is appropriate when the purpose is to obtain a rich, practice-oriented understanding of participants’ accounts while remaining close to their expressed meanings and minimizing extensive theoretical abstraction (Bradshaw et al., 2017; Colorafi & Evans, 2016). This approach was considered suitable for examining how nursing students understand, experience, and respond to EoL within real-world educational and clinical settings. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used as a reporting guideline to enhance transparency and rigor (Tong et al., 2007).

Participants

Ten participants were selected based on purposive sampling following the inclusion criteria: (1) Enrolled as a nursing student during the professional stage in the Faculty of Nursing UPH, (2) having completed a course in palliative care nursing during the academic stage, (3) possessing experiences in providing care for patients at the EoL, and (4) willing to be interviewed. The exclusion criteria were: (1) students who had not yet participated in clinical placements involving dying patients; (2) students currently on academic leave; (3) students who declined audio recording or withdrew consent at any stage.

A recruitment flyer was shared and distributed via social media, including the WhatsApp group for nursing students at UPH. Participants received both a written participant information sheet and an oral explanation regarding the study purpose, interview procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. The sample size of 10 participants was deemed sufficient as data saturation was achieved, indicated by repetition of meanings and no emergence of new themes in the final interviews. No eligible students withdrew after providing consent. The number of students who declined participation after viewing the recruitment flyer was not systematically recorded.

Data Collection

Data collection through in-depth interviews was conducted from February to May 2025. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a quiet, private room within the Faculty of Medicine building, as agreed by both parties. The interview guide was developed based on literature concerning EoL care education, nursing students’ clinical experiences, and qualitative interviewing relevant to exploring perceptions and experiences of care provision. The questions were reviewed by two qualitative nursing researchers for content relevance and clarity. The guide consisted of open-ended questions and probing prompts to encourage rich descriptions of lived experience.

Core questions included: “What does the end of life mean to you? Would you tell me about your experiences in providing end-of-life care? How do you feel when looking after patients at the end of life? What do you think would be the barriers for you to provide end-of-life care? Interviews lasted approximately 40-60 minutes and were audio-recorded with participants’ permission. Basic demographic information was collected prior to each interview using a brief questionnaire.

All interviews were conducted by the first author, a female third-year nursing student who had received training in conducting qualitative research and semi-structured interviewing techniques. This minimizes imbalance and social desirability bias. All interviews were conducted in Bahasa Indonesia, the participants’ native language, to ensure depth of expression and conceptual clarity. Field notes were not systematically recorded during the interview. Transcripts were translated into English for reporting purposes, with accuracy checked by bilingual researchers and back translation.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis, an inductive and flexible analytic approach that enables the systematic identification, organization, and interpretation of patterns of meaning across participants’ narratives. Specifically, Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach was applied, encompassing data familiarization, initial coding, theme development, theme review, theme definition, and report production (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Each transcript was read repeatedly by all authors to achieve immersion. Initial codes were generated inductively, compared across transcripts, and grounded into candidate themes. Discrepancies in coding and theme boundaries were resolved through team discussion until consensus was achieved. The authors involved in the data analysis are nurses with prior documented experience in qualitative research; one expert in spiritual and palliative care; another in community health and education; and one familiar with the study’s setting and populations, with prior experience in spiritual care. Data were manually coded using Microsoft Excel; no analytical software was employed for data analysis.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was ensured in accordance with the criteria of credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability (Ahmed, 2024). Credibility was ensured through prolonged engagement with the data, use of open-ended questions, and peer debriefing among the research team during analysis meetings. Researchers encouraged participants to elaborate freely and clarified meanings during interviews to ensure accurate interpretation. During the interview, no non-participants were present. Member checking was conducted with one participant, who reviewed their transcript for accuracy and resonance. Member checking was conducted with one participant to enhance interpretive credibility. Although participant validation was limited, credibility was strengthened through peer debriefing, collaborative coding discussion, prolonged engagement with the data, and maintenance of an audit trail.

Dependability was supported by maintaining a detailed audit trail documenting recruitment, data collection, and analytical decisions. Regular consultation with senior qualitative researchers further enhanced methodological consistency. Transferability was supported by providing thick descriptions of participants and research settings, though findings remain context-specific. Confirmability was achieved by ensuring transparency between raw data, codes, and themes, with regular reviews to minimize researcher bias.

Reflexivity was actively considered throughout the research process. The first author was in the same educational program as the participants; the research team acknowledged the potential influence of insider positioning, shared assumptions, and professional beliefs on data generation and interpretation. To mitigate this, reflexive discussions were conducted throughout analysis, analytic decisions were collaboratively reviewed, and interpretations were repeatedly compared against original transcripts to strengthen confirmability and analytic transparency in accordance with COREQ recommendations (Tong et al., 2007).

Ethical Considerations

Study approval was obtained from the Faculty of Nursing, Universitas Pelita Harapan, Indonesia (Approval number: 053/KEPFON/I/2025, dated February 3rd, 2025). All participants provided verbal and written informed consent for their voluntary participation in this study. Anonymity was maintained to ensure the confidentiality of the participants. Participants were assured that their responses would be used exclusively for research purposes and that all identifying information would be securely stored for at least three years.

Results

The study involved a diverse group of ten participants aged between 20 and 25 years, consisting of three males and seven females. Two participants reported having received formal training in palliative care during their professional program, while the remaining eight had not received any additional preparation in this area. Their experiences in providing end-of-life care varied, ranging from a single encounter to more than ten. The analysis generated four overarching themes and ten subthemes. Themes, subthemes, and participants

Theme 1. Nursing students’ perspective on End-of-Life Care

This theme explores how nursing students conceptualized and constructed their understanding of EoL care, encompassing their ability to identify patients approaching the EoL through clinical manifestations. It further examines how students integrate theoretical knowledge into clinical practice, shaping their attitudes and approaches to EoL care.

Subtheme 1.1. Comprehension of End-of-Life Care

This subtheme reveals that nursing students conceptualized EoL care as a holistic, person-centered approach aimed at promoting comfort and dignity for patients while extending emotional and spiritual support to patients’ family members. They perceived EoL care not as curative, but as a compassionate process focused on alleviating pain and addressing the multifaceted needs (physical, psychological, social, and spiritual) of individuals nearing death. The students underscored the pivotal role of sensitive, empathetic communication in facilitating families’ acceptance of the patient’s condition, highlighting the importance of carefully chosen words to prevent further emotional distress.

“Helping to provide comprehensive care, caring for their needs, comfort, spiritual or perhaps support from their family” (P4)

“The care given to patients who are near death so the focus is not on curing but on how to improve the quality of life, the focus is not only on the physical but also on holistic aspects, such as psychosocial and emotional aspects, are also considered.” (P7)

These perspectives show an emerging awareness of the complexities in supporting patients and families.

Subtheme 1.2. Clinical Indicators and Attributes of end of life

This subtheme highlights nursing students’ developing ability to recognize and interpret clinical indicators that signify a patient’s transition into the EoL phase. Participants identified a range of observable indicators, including persistent pain, declining levels of consciousness, chronic or incurable conditions, and a noticeable decline in respiratory function, often assessed using the ABCDE approach. They also emphasized that these physiological changes require not only clinical discernment but also sensitive communication and psychological readiness for the patient’s imminent death.

“When the patient is deteriorating, such as decreased saturation, can no longer mobilize because of also decreased consciousness. … Talking incoherently, agitated. … Had difficulty sleeping, was restless and … The patient is a bit cranky … he is at the end of his life because he is incoherent and restless.” (P2)

“The care given to patients who have been given the maximum but with incurable diseases, for example, such as cancer and other terminal diseases. … From the diagnosis, then the signs and symptoms that are getting worse, like the results of the examination, show a worsening condition.” (P6)

These quotations show Indonesian nursing students’ increasing clinical acuity and emotional awareness in recognizing signs of terminal decline, reflecting their growing ability to combine scientific observation with empathy in end-of-life care.

Theme 2. Providing Holistic End-of-Life Care

This theme captures nursing students’ comprehensive approach to addressing the diverse and interrelated needs of patients and their families during EoL care. It emphasizes that effective EoL care extends beyond physical management to encompass emotional, psychosocial, and spiritual dimensions of support. The theme further reflects nursing students’ awareness of the nurses’ role in creating a compassionate and respectful environment where patients and families can find solace and meaning amid loss.

Subtheme 2.1. Ensuring Patients’ Comfort through Attentive and Responsive Care

This subtheme focuses on how nursing students understand and fulfill their responsibility to relieve physical suffering in patients at the EoL. It emphasizes their proactive effort to mitigate distressing symptoms such as pain, dyspnea, fatigue, and nausea through vigilant observation, timely interventions, and individualized care strategies. Participants described employing non-pharmacological approaches and continuous monitoring of vital signs to ensure patients’ comfort and safety. The participants stated as follows:

“Well, if reducing pain symptoms can be relaxation, deep breathing, distraction … nausea vomiting by adjusting the position and preparing yellow plastic, if for fatigue, facilitate sleep time.” (P1)

“For example, there is a buildup of sputum, so we give suction. For example, patients who are not accompanied by their families, how can we be more aware, so we often check the patient's monitor, oxygen needs, and also change positions.” (P6)

These narratives reflect nursing students’ growing clinical acumen and ethical awareness, demonstrating their commitment to prioritizing comfort, dignity, and quality of life in the final stages of life through attentive, compassionate, and humanized care.

Subtheme 2.2. Supporting Patients' and Families’ Psychosocial and Emotional Needs

This subtheme highlights nursing students’ pivotal role in addressing the psychosocial and emotional needs of patients during the EoL. Participants emphasized the importance of being present, actively listening, and providing emotional reassurance to both patients and their relatives. They described efforts to motivate patients, facilitate family presence, and foster supportive interactions that help alleviate the emotional burden associated with the dying process.

“Psychosocial is where we provide support to patients so that patients feel peaceful, well. For example, we can just be present and listen to patients; it means a lot to patients and makes them comfortable.” (P5)

“I'm more about listening, chatting, and listening to the patient's family to build trust, … The patient's family will also be happy to be listened to so even though it's a simple thing, it's useful for them.” (P8)

Collectively, these insights reflect nursing students’ emerging capacity to provide compassionate, relational care, underscoring the ethical and humanistic dimensions of EoL practice.

Subtheme 2.3. Facilitating Religious or Spiritual Practices

This subtheme underscores the recognition of spirituality as a central component of holistic care. Nursing students demonstrated respect for and responsiveness to patients' and families’ spiritual needs by facilitating prayer, religious rituals, or moments of spiritual reflection. Such practices enabled students to provide care that was both meaningful and aligned with individual beliefs, particularly during moments of emotional or existential distress.

“Once the patient asked, ‘Where is the mosque?’ Then I told him, but it turned out that his mother said it was too far away because she wanted to stay close and take care of his father. So, we as nurses provided underpads or similar items that could be used for prayer mats.“ (P8)

In some instances, students independently initiated spiritual support, whereas in others they responded to patient or family requests. Participants also acknowledged the facilities provided by the institution, such as a prayer team at Siloam Hospital, which could provide structured spiritual support to patients in the terminal phase.

“If it's spiritual, it's already done because Siloam itself has a prayer team.” (P3)

“Prayer or spiritual support services are provided by Siloam.” (P4)

These narratives illustrate nursing students’ awareness of the importance of integrating spiritual care into practice and reflect their emerging sensitivity to patients’ and families’ individual values, beliefs, and sources of comfort at the EoL.

Theme 3. Challenges and Opportunities in Providing End-of-Life Care

This theme captures nursing students' experiences of navigating the emotional and clinical complexities of EoL care, highlighting how these encounters can foster personal and professional development. It captures students’ reflections on the emotional, communicative, and practical challenges they face in real clinical settings, and how such experiences serve as valuable opportunities for growth and skill refinement.

Subtheme 3.1. Facing Communication Barriers and Difficult Conversations

Nursing students reported that one of the most challenging aspects of EoL care was engaging in meaningful communication with patients and families, particularly when families struggled to accept the patient's condition. They emphasized that differences in background, educational level, and health literacy compounded these communication difficulties, often requiring students to adapt their language and approach to convey sensitive information effectively.

“But it is also difficult to communicate with the family, especially if the family cannot accept the patient's condition. It's also difficult to say, because we don't know how people feel, so in that situation we fulfil the patient's needs with small things, for example giving them tissues or a chair, something like that.” (P8)

“In communication or vocabulary, the patient's background and economic level affect the patient's level of understanding of what we say, because we are also students, so it is not enough, then sometimes we also still explain in medical language.” (P10)

The use of medical terminology by nursing students further illustrates the complexity of these interactions. As described by the participant:

“As a student nurse, I tried to explain to the patient's wife that his husband’s prognosis is decreasing. It started with a decreasing level of consciousness and his increasingly poor breathing, so the ventilator needed to be applied.” (P6)

This quote emphasizes the high-stakes nature of communication around a worsening prognosis. Although Nursing students attempt to explain complex prognostic information, their reliance on medical language may have limited comprehensibility for family members, particularly in emotionally charged situations.

Subtheme 3.2. Psychological Hardiness in Witnessing Patients’ Deterioration and Death

Emotional responses to patient suffering and death emerged as a significant aspect of students’ experiences. Participants described feelings of sadness, fear, helplessness, and guilt as they observed the decline and passing of patients, illustrating the profound psychological impact of EoL care on novice professionals. Participants stated as follows:

“Sometimes I'm tired of taking care of patients and also have to deal with the patient's family who complains … honestly, there is fear, like I'm really scared, I'm afraid that my actions can harm patients.” (P1)

“I feel like I failed as a nurse because I could not make the patient survive.” (P3). “The first is sadness, empathizing and sympathizing, then the second is the guilt that arises like ‘why wasn't I quicker in providing care’” (P4)

These narratives highlight the emotional resilience required in EoL care and the emerging recognition among students of the need to manage their psychological responses to provide compassionate, competent care.

Subtheme 3.3. Transforming Clinical Challenges into Learning Opportunities for Personal and Professional Growth

Rather than being discouraged by the hardships encountered in EoL care, some students reflected on how these experiences contributed to their professional and personal growth. Exposure to emotionally and clinically challenging situations enhanced their empathy, refined practical skills, strengthened confidence in communicating with patients and families, and promoted thoughtful, compassionate care. The participants shared as follows:

“My emotions are more controlled because of previous experience. … I learned from senior nurses, I also learned not to blame myself, I read books specifically for nurse psychology… But the experience that had the most impact on the change was” (P2)

“Because of experience, experience is the best teacher, so make it knowledge so that in the future you can be more skillful.” (P5)

“In the beginning, it was awkward, but the more I get better at communicating, to chat with patients. So thankfully, the nurses here also give me the opportunity and trust to see patients, more practice so there is a lot of experience to learn, which really affects me.” (P6)

These reflections underscore the transformative potential of challenging EoL experiences, illustrating how students gradually develop clinical competence, emotional regulation, and a more nuanced understanding of the ethical and relational dimensions of EoL care.

Theme 4. Developing Heart of Caring

This theme encapsulates the profound emotional, spiritual, and relational commitment that nursing students bring to EoL care. It conveys a deeply humanistic approach to caregiving in which compassion, empathy, and faith intertwine to shape attitudes and guide professional behavior. This theme reveals how caring at the EoL transcends clinical performance, becoming an expression of moral integrity and genuine human connection.

Subtheme 4.1. Treating and placing patients as relatives

This subtheme reflects how nursing students extend the same love, dignity, and respect to dying patients as they would to members of their own family. It captures the emotional closeness and empathy that drive students to treat patients not merely as recipients of care but as individuals deserving of familial warmth and unconditional regard.

“If I'm really tired of being with a patient, I imagine that this patient is my family, I don't want my mother to be scolded by the nurse or scolded, so try to understand their position.” (P6)

“I imagine it's my family, so I can't bear it, but I have to control myself to remain professional and not get too carried away.” (P7)

“I like to hear them tell stories, especially if it's an old lady, so I think of her as my grandmother. So, I treat of them as my own family.” (P9)

These reflections reveal how personal empathy and emotional resonance serve as intrinsic motivators for compassionate care, bridging the professional-personal boundary through human connection.

Subtheme 4.2. Faith-Inspired Compassion: Caring for Patients in the Image of God

This subtheme highlights the influence of spiritual beliefs on students’ caregiving. Providing EoL care becomes a sacred duty, viewed as a reflection of divine love, purpose, and moral responsibility. It underscores how faith and love can inspire deeper patience, hope, and resilience when supporting patients through their final journey. Nursing students perceived their patients as human beings created in the image of God and therefore believed they should be cared for with sincere love and compassion. For some, Jesus Christ served as their role model in providing care—demonstrating love, humility, and selflessness in serving others.

“Christian nurses are serving God…God has served us first, so now we have to serve others because God has done it first.” (P1)

“Spiritually, I see the patient as God's creation, just like me, created in the image and likeness of God, so I treat with totality. And I also help in prayer.” (P3)

“Maybe it's because God teaches us not to discriminate in serving others, and we are also told to do it like it's for God, not for humans, because if we only think for humans, we are really stressed.” (P6)

“The difference is how the Lord Jesus is our example in serving, remembering how God obeyed, wanting to serve is my strength. … Christ is truly an example to serve.” (P8)

Faith also offers emotional strength in challenging moments.

“Maybe it's because we are taught by God not to discriminate in serving others…because if we only think for humans, we are really stressed.” (P6)

This quote reflects how spiritual grounding can buffer against burnout and emotional fatigue, providing a deeper sense of purpose and resilience. Collectively, these accounts demonstrate how spiritual conviction reinforces moral resilience and transforms caring into a faith-driven act of devotion that transcends the boundaries of technical nursing care.

Discussion

This study explores the experiences of professional-stage nursing students in providing EoL care, revealing a multifaceted understanding shaped by emotional, ethical, spiritual, and educational influences. The four interrelated themes demonstrate that, while nursing students conceptualize EoL care as holistic and compassionate, they simultaneously navigate vulnerability, communication challenges, and gaps between theoretical instruction and clinical realities. Importantly, these experiences appear to function not only as stressors but also as formative moments in students’ moral and professional development.

Conceptualization of EoL Care and Recognition of Clinical Indicators

Consistent with prior studies, participants conceptualized EoL care as a holistic, person-centered process emphasizing comfort, dignity, and psychosocial and spiritual support rather than curative intent (Cao et al., 2022). This alignment reinforces existing evidence that nursing students associate EoL care with alleviating suffering and supporting families. However, this study extends prior research by demonstrating that Indonesian nursing students, despite reporting limited formal preparation, were able to recognize concrete clinical indicators of terminal decline, such as changes in consciousness, respiratory deterioration, and escalating symptom burden.

These findings diverge from earlier reports suggesting that nursing students often feel unable to identify EoL trajectories or respond appropriately to clinical deterioration (Dorney & Pierangeli, 2021). One possible explanation is that repeated exposure during clinical placements facilitated experiential learning that partially compensated for curricular gaps. This supports Fristedt et al. (2021), who found that experiential exposure and clinical judgment, and suggests that clinical immersion may foster earlier-than-expected development of EoL awareness, even among novice practitioners.

Participants in the current study expressed a strong need for timely emotional support, often finding comfort in peers or mentors. These findings underscore the potential value of integrating practical training, education in emotional resilience, and structured support systems into nursing curricula to better prepare students for the complex realities of end-of-life care. While these findings indicate a need for structured emotional support, further research is required to examine the effectiveness of specific curricular interventions.

Holistic Care for Patients and Families

The second theme highlights students’ efforts to operationalize holistic care by integrating physical comfort measures with emotional presence and spiritual facilitation. While a meta-analysis has reported deficits in domains such as physical symptom control, communication, and spiritual care (Wang et al., 2022), our findings illustrate how students attempt to bridge these gaps through presence, attentive listening, family facilitation, and spiritual support. The present study, therefore, contributes by demonstrating how nursing students enact holistic care in practice (not merely recognizing its importance), but describing concrete caregiving behaviors within EoL contexts.

Challenges and Opportunities in Providing EoL Care

Consistent with international literature, students reported significant communication challenges, particularly when families struggled to accept terminal diagnoses or when differences in health literacy complicated conversations (Jeffers et al., 2022). Emotional responses, including fear, guilt, sadness, and helplessness, closely mirror findings from Zhou et al. (2022) and Camara et al. (2024), underscoring the universal emotional impact of witnessing death during training.

However, this study extends existing research by illuminating a developmental trajectory wherein repeated exposure to EoL situations gradually transformed emotional distress into psychological resilience, empathy, and reflective capacity. Rather than remaining paralyzed by emotional strain, many students reframed difficult experiences as learning opportunities, aligning with Carvalho et al. (2020) but adding nuance by demonstrating how resilience is cultivated through mentorship, peer learning, and self-reflection. This finding suggests that emotional hardship in EoL care is not inherently detrimental; when appropriately supported, it may promote professional maturation.

Relational and Spiritual Commitment: The Core of Caring

The fourth theme reveals one of the most distinctive contributions of this study: the central role of relational empathy and faith in shaping students’ EoL care practices. Treating patients as relatives and viewing care as faith-inspired moral responsibility emerged as powerful motivators for compassionate engagement. While prior research has noted that nursing students often feel underprepared to provide spiritual care (García-Navarro et al., 2023), this study diverges by demonstrating that spirituality was not perceived as an external skill deficit, but rather as an internalized value guiding practice.

A unique and meaningful finding in this study is the significant role of faith in motivating nursing students and shaping their approach to EoL care. Christian nursing students viewed caring for end-of-life care patients not just as a professional duty but as a spiritual calling, which helped them remain compassionate and resilient. This aligns with Azimi et al. (2024), who found that students with strong spiritual beliefs were more likely to show empathy, compassion, and emotional strength in caregiving. Participants also expressed a desire to provide spiritual comfort through prayer, conversation, or chaplain involvement, supporting Murgia et al. (2022), who emphasized the importance of addressing spiritual needs as part of holistic care, especially in culturally or religiously uniform settings. Students often treated patients as family members, forming deep emotional connections that sustained their motivation, even in stressful situations. This personal and empathetic approach aligns with Sukcharoen et al. (2023), who emphasized the need for nursing education to promote dignity, empathy, and respect for patients’ rights and quality of life. Forming emotional bonds and a sense of familial care were seen as essential to delivering meaningful and compassionate EoL support.

Spiritual beliefs emerged as a powerful motivator in students’ caregiving practices. These insights are supported by Azimi et al. (2024), who found that nurses with strong spiritual beliefs demonstrated greater empathy and emotional strength in caregiving. The integration of faith into clinical practice not only enriches the quality of care but also sustains the caregiver’s well-being. This connection underscores how faith and moral identity are deeply resonant with the mission of our nursing program, which aims to cultivate competent professional nurses equipped with true knowledge, guided by faith in Christ, and shaped by godly character. By fostering these spiritual and moral qualities, the program equips graduates to engage effectively and compassionately. By nurturing these spiritual and moral dimensions, the program prepares graduates to respond effectively and compassionately to rapidly evolving global health needs, both nationally and internationally. This contribution helps broaden understanding of the salience of faith and moral identity.

The balance between emotional closeness and professional boundaries is a recurring theme, suggesting that while empathy is essential, emotional regulation is equally critical in sustaining effective care. These findings align with Sukcharoen et al. (2023), who emphasized the importance of dignity and empathy in nursing education, particularly in palliative settings. By treating patients as family, students not only humanize their care but also cultivate deeper relational bonds that support both the patient’s and caregiver’s emotional well-being. They also acknowledged that emotional support and the presence of loved ones are more meaningful than medical interventions at the end of life, echoing Haroen et al. (2023), who stress that family presence is essential for emotional peace. Thus, healthcare professionals must prioritize family involvement as a core part of quality EoL care.

Implications for Nursing Education and Practice

Across these themes, the findings reinforce the notion that formal training remains uneven, while clinical exposure varies widely. This resonates with the literature that identifies inadequate student preparation in palliative/EoL education (Cao et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). Importantly, this qualitative study’s findings suggest that students engage in meaningful EoL care practices and develop moral-emotional insight even without extensive formal preparation, highlighting the role of experiential learning, reflection, mentorship, and supportive clinical environments. This adds nuance by proposing that educational interventions might focus not only on knowledge acquisition but on facilitating reflective, relational, and spiritually attuned experiences.

Strengths and Limitations

This study offers several notable strengths. It provides context-specific qualitative evidence from Indonesia, a setting where empirical data on nursing students’ end-of-life (EoL) experiences is limited, thereby contributing to global nursing education literature. The research captures rich, in-depth insights into how students integrate faith, empathy, and professional identity in EoL care, highlighting spirituality as a sustaining force rather than a peripheral competency. Methodological rigor was ensured through strategies that enhanced credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability, and the findings offer practical implications for curriculum development, emphasizing experiential learning and culturally responsive spiritual care.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single private university, which may restrict the transferability of findings to other educational or clinical contexts. Participants shared similar religious backgrounds, potentially amplifying the prominence of faith-based interpretations of care and limiting diversity in perspectives. Recruitment through voluntary participation may have attracted students who were more inclined toward reflection or meaning-making. Rather than constituting bias in a statistical sense, this is recognized as a characteristic that shapes narrative data in qualitative inquiry.

Participant validation was limited because the transcript review involved only one participant. Although multiple strategies were used to enhance credibility, broader participant verification may have strengthened interpretive confirmation of findings. Field notes were not systematically recorded, which may have limited contextual interpretation of non-verbal cues. As with all qualitative research, findings reflect subjective experiences and should be interpreted within their cultural and contextual boundaries. The homogeneity of participants’ Christian backgrounds likely contributed to the prominence of faith-based interpretations of EoL care. While this represents a meaningful contribution, it limits the representation of alternative spiritual frameworks common in Indonesia and warrants cautious interpretation.

Finally, although the single-institution design limits institutional variability, it also allows for in-depth exploration of students’ experiences within a clearly defined educational and clinical context. The findings are therefore best understood as transferable to similar faith-based or private nursing programs rather than representative of all Indonesian nursing students.

Conclusion

This study explored Indonesian nursing students’ lived experiences and perceptions of providing EoL care, revealing a complex interplay between knowledge, emotional resilience, spirituality, and professional growth. The findings underscore that while students conceptualized EoL care as a holistic process, they often faced communication barriers, emotional distress, and limited preparedness when engaging with dying patients and their families. Despite these challenges, participants demonstrated emerging competence in symptom management, psychosocial support, and spiritual care, reflecting theoretical integration and humanistic sensitivity. The insights of this qualitative study point to the need for curricular enhancement that explicitly integrates EoL communication training, emotional self-regulation, and culturally responsive spiritual care. By providing students with structured opportunities to reflect, debrief, and practice under supervision, nursing education can transform emotionally demanding experiences into meaningful professional development. Ultimately, nurturing a caring heart remains essential for preparing nurses capable of delivering dignified, compassionate EoL care across diverse cultural contexts
