The Grothendieck Constant is Strictly Larger than Davie–Reeds’ Bound
Chris Jones, Giulio Malavolta

Abstract
The Grothendieck constant K G K_{G} is a fundamental quantity in functional analysis, with important connections to quantum information, combinatorial optimization, and the geometry of Banach spaces. Despite decades of study, the value of K G K_{G} is unknown. The best known lower bound on K G K_{G} was obtained independently by Davie and Reeds in the 1980s. In this paper we show that their bound is not optimal. We prove that K G ≥ K D ​ R + 10 − 12 K_{G}\geq K_{DR}+10^{-12} , where K D ​ R K_{DR} denotes the Davie–Reeds lower bound. Our argument is based on a perturbative analysis of the Davie–Reeds operator. We show that every near-extremizer for the Davie–Reeds problem has Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) weight on its degree-3 Hermite coefficients, and therefore introducing a small cubic perturbation increases the integrality gap of the operator.

1.1
What are hard instances for the Grothendieck problem?

To prove lower bounds on
K_{G}
, we need to identify matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that maximize the ratio in
Eq.

1
.
Prior literature on
K_{G}
has identified a family of interesting instances which we call
Hermite projection games
.
The “matrix” in a Hermite projection game is actually a linear operator on the space
of functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

(which can be discretized into a finite-dimensional matrix at the very end).
A Hermite projection game is defined to be
a linear combination of the operators
\bm{\Pi}_{d}
where
\bm{\Pi}_{d}
projects a function to the degree-
d
part of its Hermite polynomial expansion.
Note that we take the limit
n\to\infty
in a Hermite projection game,
but the coefficients on the matrices
\bm{\Pi}_{d}
are constant with respect to
n
.

For example,
\bm{\Pi}_{1}
achieves value
\frac{\pi}{2}
which is known to be the largest possible value of
Eq.

1
among PSD matrices, the so-called “little Grothendieck inequality”.
The Davie–Reeds operator which achieves the best lower bound on
K_{G}
to date is a Hermite projection game,

A_{DR}=\bm{\Pi}_{1}-\lambda^{*}{\bf I},\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda^{*}\approx 0.19748\,.

A remarkable result of Raghavendra and Steurer shows that Hermite projection games are complete for
K_{G}
: for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a Hermite projection game such that
Eq.

1
is at least
K_{G}-\varepsilon
, regardless of the true value of
K_{G}

[
12
]
.

Our improvement to the Davie–Reeds construction is also a Hermite projection game, specifically

A=\bm{\Pi}_{1}-\lambda^{*}{\bf I}-\varepsilon\bm{\Pi}_{3}

for a small constant
\varepsilon>0
.
Let us give some intuition for why we choose this perturbation of the Davie–Reeds operator using the viewpoint of
K_{G}
through nonlocal games.

The word “game” refers to the interpretation of
Eq.

1
as the gap between the optimal quantum and classical strategies for a 2-player XOR game.
This type of game is parameterized by a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
There are two players, Alice and Bob, who separately receive an index
i_{A},i_{B}\in[n]
such that the probability of receiving pair
(i_{A},i_{B})
is proportional to
|A_{i_{A}i_{B}}|
.
1
1

1

We can assume without loss of generality that the matrix
A
is normalized so that
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|A_{ij}|=1
, since one can easily see from
Eq.

1
that
K_{G}
is scale invariant.

Their goal is to output bits
a,b\in\{\pm 1\}
such that
ab=\sign(A_{i_{A}i_{B}})
.

If no communication is allowed between Alice and Bob, their optimal strategy has bias (i.e., probability of winning minus probability of losing) exactly equal to the denominator of
Eq.

1
.
When Alice and Bob have access to a shared quantum state, instead the bias of their optimal strategy is exactly the numerator of
Eq.

1
.
Therefore,
K_{G}
is equal to the maximum quantum-versus-classical advantage across all possible 2-player XOR games.
The famous
CHSH game
is a simple example with
n=2
showing
K_{G}>1
(there exists a quantum strategy which is strictly better than all possible classical strategies; this is known as a “Bell inequality”).

Consider three different 2-player XOR games which are Hermite projection games:

1.

Alice and Bob receive
n
-dimensional vectors
X,Y
with probability proportional to
e^{-\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2-\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2}|\langle X,Y\rangle|
. Their goal is to output
ab=\sign(\langle X,Y\rangle)
.

2.

Alice and Bob both receive the same
n
-dimensional Gaussian vector
X
. Their goal is to output
ab=-1
.

3.

Alice and Bob play game 1 with probability
1-p
or game 2 with probability
p
.

The operator corresponding to the first game is
\bm{\Pi}_{1}
. The operator corresponding to the second game is
-{\bf I}
. The operator corresponding to the third game is the (rescaled) Davie–Reeds operator
(1-p)\bm{\Pi}_{1}-p{\bf I}
.

The optimal classical strategy for the first game is to output
a=\sign(X_{1})
and
b=\sign(Y_{1})
. The second game is trivially winnable with probability 1. The third game, however, turns out to be strictly harder than game 1 (which is good since this leads to a larger lower bound for
K_{G}
).
At the intuitive level, this is because we have confused Alice and Bob by “negating” the correct answer with probability
p
: when
X
and
Y
are correlated vectors,
we could be in game 1, in which case the goal of Alice and Bob is to answer
ab=1
,
or we could be in game 2, in which case Alice and Bob are supposed to answer
ab=-1
instead.

Extending this intuition,
we should confuse Alice and Bob even more by introducing additional alternations.
For example, a candidate harder game would be
ab=\sign\left(f\left(\frac{\langle X,Y\rangle}{\left\lVert X\right\rVert\left\lVert Y\right\rVert}\right)\right)
for a function
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
that oscillates,
since Alice and Bob would need to know the angle between
X
and
Y
very precisely in order to win this game.
The work of König
[
9
]

suggests a concrete instance of this type, although its analysis remains an open problem.
The candidate instance is an operator on functions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
whose “entries” are (more precisely, the kernel function of the operator is)

A_{G}(X,Y)=e^{-\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2-\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{2}^{2}/2}\sin\langle X,Y\rangle\,.

A_{G}
is similar to a Hermite projection game but it is not quite one.
If we write the Hermite polynomial expansion of
\sin\langle X,Y\rangle

\sin\langle X,Y\rangle=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}c_{\alpha\beta}\He_{\alpha}(X)\He_{\beta}(Y)

then, in
A_{G}
, the terms with
\alpha=\beta
are a Hermite projection game,
while the terms with
\alpha\neq\beta
are additional “off-diagonal matrices” with respect to the Hermite polynomial decomposition.

It can be shown that the Hermite coefficients
c_{\alpha\alpha}
are zero unless
|\alpha|
is odd
and they alternate sign for
|\alpha|=1,3,5,7,\dots

This motivates us to consider Hermite projection games of the form
\bm{\Pi}_{1}-c_{3}\bm{\Pi}_{3}+c_{5}\bm{\Pi}_{5}+\cdots
.
We propose to bridge the gap from the instances
\bm{\Pi}_{1}
and
\bm{\Pi}_{1}-\lambda^{*}{\bf I}
to a candidate hard instance such as
A_{G}

by steadily adding more low-degree Hermite projectors
\bm{\Pi}_{k}
with alternating coefficients.
While the quantitative improvement in
Theorem

1.1
is small, the proof of
Theorem

1.1
confirms the intuition behind this approach, showing that an additional alternation
\bm{\Pi}_{3}
indeed makes the Davie–Reeds game harder.

Preliminaries

We start with some preliminaries on Hermite projection games.

For a measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
and a bounded linear operator
A:L^{\infty}(\mu)\to L^{1}(\mu)
,
define the quantities:

\displaystyle\val(A)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f,g:\Omega\to\{\pm 1\}}\int_{\Omega}(Af)(X)g(X)\,d\mu(X)

\displaystyle\mathrm{sdp}(A)

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\cal H}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g:\Omega\to\mathcal{H}\\
\|f(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1,\|g(X)\|_{\cal H}=1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\langle(Af)(X),g(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,d\mu(X)

where the supremum over
{\cal H}
is over real Hilbert spaces, and we lift
A
to an operator on
f:\Omega\to{\cal H}
by applying it separately to each coordinate of
{\cal H}
.
More precisely, select an orthonormal basis
v_{i}
for
{\cal H}
, represent
f(X)=\sum_{i}f_{i}(X)v_{i}
, and define
Af=\sum_{i}(Af_{i})(X)v_{i}
.
The case where
\mu
is the discrete counting measure on
\{1,2,\dots,n\}

corresponds to finite-dimensional matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

The boundedness of the operator
A:L^{\infty}(\mu)\to L^{1}(\mu)
is exactly equivalent to
\val(A)<\infty
.
On the other hand, while
\mathrm{sdp}(A)
could ostensibly be infinite, the Grothendieck inequality states that
\mathrm{sdp}(A)
is in fact finite
and only a constant factor larger than
\val(A)
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
11
, Theorems 2.4 and 2.5]
)

Let
(\Omega,\mu)
be a measure space and let
A:L^{\infty}(\mu)\to L^{1}(\mu)
be a bounded linear operator.
Then there is a constant
K
such that

\mathrm{sdp}(A)\leq K\val(A)

Furthermore, the best constant

is equal to

defined in
Eq.

1
.

Specializing to the case of Gaussian measure,
let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\gamma=\gamma^{(n)}
be the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian measure.
The space
L^{2}(\gamma)
has the Gaussian inner product
\langle f,g\rangle_{\gamma}=\E_{X\sim\gamma}f(X)g(X)
and the corresponding norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2}=\left(\E_{X\sim\gamma}f(X)^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.
For a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, let
\He_{\alpha}(X)
be the
\alpha
Hermite polynomial (probabilists’ convention)
which together form an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\gamma)
.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
define the notation
\alpha!=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}!
and
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
.

Let
\bm{\Pi}_{k}:L^{2}(\gamma)\to\mathrm{span}\{\He_{\alpha}:|\alpha|=k\}
be the projection operator to the degree-
k
Hermite polynomials.
Our candidate integrality gap instances for the Grothendieck constant are

Hermite projection games
of the form

A=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\bm{\Pi}_{k}

for
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|c_{k}|<\infty
.
The SDP value of a Hermite projection game has a simple formula:
it is just equal to the spectral norm of
A
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\bm{\Pi}_{k}
be a Hermite projection game. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{sdp}(A)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|c_{k}|\,.

Proof.

We first prove the upper bound.
Let
L^{2}(\gamma;{\cal H})
denote the space of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\cal H}
such
that
\E_{X\sim\gamma}\left\lVert f(X)\right\rVert_{\cal H}^{2}<\infty
.
The degree-
k
Hermite subspaces are orthogonal in this space, so

\|Af\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}^{2}\|\bm{\Pi}_{k}f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}\leq\left(\sup_{k\geq 0}|c_{k}|\right)^{2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\|\bm{\Pi}_{k}f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}=\left(\sup_{k\geq 0}|c_{k}|\right)^{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}.

For any
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\cal H}
such that
\|f(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1
, we have
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}\leq 1
. Therefore

\E_{X\sim\gamma}\left\|Af(X)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|Af\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|c_{k}|.

This gives an upper bound for
\mathrm{sdp}(A)
since it holds that

\mathrm{sdp}(A)=\sup_{{\cal H}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g:\Omega\to\mathcal{H}\\
\|f(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1,\|g(X)\|_{\cal H}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\E_{X\sim\gamma}\langle(Af)(X),g(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sup_{{\cal H}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\Omega\to\mathcal{H}\\
\|f(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\E_{X\sim\gamma}\left\lVert(Af)(X)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\,.

For the lower bound, fix some constant
k
. For
n\geq k
, let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space with orthonormal basis

\{e_{S}:S\subseteq[n],\,|S|=k\}
, and define

\Psi(x):=\binom{n}{k}^{-1/2}\sum_{|S|=k}\left(\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)e_{S}.

Notice that

\left\lVert\Psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}=\E_{X\sim\gamma}\|\Psi(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\E_{X\sim\gamma}\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\sum_{|S|=k}\prod_{i\in S}X_{i}^{2}=\binom{n}{k}^{-1}\sum_{|S|=k}\prod_{i\in S}\E X_{i}^{2}=1

by independence. Since
\left\lVert\Psi(X)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
is the average of
\binom{n}{k}
monomials and changing one coordinate affects only
k/n=O_{k}(1/n)
fraction of them, the Efron–Stein inequality yields
\Var(\left\lVert\Psi(X)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2})=O_{k}(1/n)
. Thus

\E_{X\sim\gamma}\left(\left\lVert\Psi(X)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}-1\right)^{2}\leq\E_{X\sim\gamma}\left(\left\lVert\Psi(X)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-1\right)^{2}\to 0.

(2)

That is to say,
\left\lVert\Psi(X)\right\rVert_{\cal H}
converges to 1 in
L^{2}
.
Now define

f(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{\Psi(x)}{\left\lVert\Psi(x)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}}&\left\lVert\Psi(x)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\neq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
0&\left\lVert\Psi(x)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=0.\end{cases}

Then
f
is a feasible solution for the SDP. Moreover,
\left\lVert f-\Psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}=\E_{X\sim\gamma}(\left\lVert\Psi(X)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}-1)^{2}
converges to
0
by
Eq.

2
, which implies that the Hermite weights of
f
converge to those of
\Psi
:

\|\bm{\Pi}_{k}f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}\to\left\lVert\bm{\Pi}_{k}\Psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\gamma;{\cal H})}^{2}=\left\lVert\Psi\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\gamma;{\cal H})}^{2}=1\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\sum_{j\neq k}\|\bm{\Pi}_{j}f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}\to 0.

(3)

By Cauchy–Schwarz and the pointwise bound
\|f(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1
, we have

\operatorname{sign}(c_{k})\,\langle Af(X),f(X)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|Af(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Taking expectations, it follows that

\displaystyle\E_{X\sim\gamma}\|Af(X)\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{sign}(c_{k})\,\E_{X\sim\gamma}\left\langle Af(X),f(X)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sign}(c_{k})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}\,\|\bm{\Pi}_{j}f\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq|c_{k}|\cdot\left\|\bm{\Pi}_{k}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}-\left(\sup_{\kappa\geq 0}|c_{\kappa}|\right)\sum_{j\neq k}\left\|\bm{\Pi}_{j}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\gamma;\mathcal{H})}^{2}.

By
Equation

3
, the above expression converges to
|c_{k}|
. Since
f
is feasible, this is also a lower bound for the SDP value. Since this holds for all
k
, this completes the proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
be a Hermite projection game and let
\val_{A}(f,g)=\langle Af,g\rangle_{\gamma}=\E_{X\sim\gamma}(Af)(X)g(X)
.
Then
\val_{A}(f,g)=\val_{A}(f\circ T,g\circ T)
for all orthogonal matrices
T\in O(n)
and
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We claim that this property holds more generally for operators
A
which are rotation-equivariant, satisfying
A(f\circ T)=(Af)\circ T
for all
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
T\in O(n)
.
To prove this,

\displaystyle\val_{A}(f\circ T,g\circ T)

\displaystyle=\E_{X\sim\gamma}(A(f\circ T))(X)\,g(TX)

\displaystyle=\E_{X\sim\gamma}(Af)(TX)\,g(TX)

\displaystyle(\text{Rotation invariance of }A)

\displaystyle=\E_{X^{\prime}\sim\gamma}(Af)(X^{\prime})\,g(X^{\prime})=\val_{A}(f,g)

\displaystyle(\text{Rotation invariance of $\gamma$})

To use this for Hermite projection games, we note that each of the operators
\bm{\Pi}_{k}
is rotation-equivariant.
One way to prove this is using that the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise operator
U_{t}
decomposes as
U_{t}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-kt}\bm{\Pi}_{k}

and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator is rotation-equivariant
[
2
, Section 1.4]
.
Let
R_{T}
be the rotation operator
f\mapsto f\circ T
.
Since
R_{T}
commutes with
U_{t}
, it preserves each eigenspace of
U_{t}
.
Therefore, it preserves the space of degree-
k
Hermite polynomials,
R_{T}\bm{\Pi}_{k}=\bm{\Pi}_{k}R_{T}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A
be a Hermite projection game and
f,\widetilde{f},g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
.
Then

\left\lvert\val_{A}(f,g)-\val_{A}(\widetilde{f},g)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert\cdot\|f-\widetilde{f}\|_{2}.

Proof.

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\left\lvert\E_{X\sim\gamma}((Af)(X)-(A\widetilde{f})(X))g(X)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{2}\,\lVert A(f-\widetilde{f})\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert\cdot\lVert f-\widetilde{f}\rVert_{2}

since
\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{2}=1
and by definition of the operator norm.
∎

4\left(\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S_{+}}(X)\right)\left(\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S_{-}}(X)\right)\leq\varepsilon\,.

(21)

Therefore, one of the inequalities holds:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S_{+}}(X)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}\\
\displaystyle\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S_{-}}(X)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}\end{cases}

(22)

Without loss of generality, the second inequality holds (else apply
T\in O(n)
which reflects
X_{1}
).

Let
S^{*}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x_{1}|\geq C^{*}\}
.
Then since
|X_{1}|\geq C^{*}
on
S^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle C^{*}\gamma(S_{-}\cap S^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S_{-}\cap S^{*}}(X)\leq\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S_{-}}(X)\underset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:s-minus-small}}}{\leq}\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}

\displaystyle\implies\gamma(S_{-}\cap S^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2C^{*}}

(23)

Next, let
C\geq 0
be such that the set
S_{C}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x_{1}|\geq C\}
has Gaussian measure
\gamma(S_{C})=\gamma(S)=2\Phi(-C)
.
The argument from
Eqs.

12
and
13
established that

\left(\E_{X\sim\gamma}|X_{1}|\cdot\bm{1}_{S}(X)\right)^{2}-\lambda\cdot(2\gamma(S)-1)\leq 4\phi(C)^{2}-\lambda\cdot(4\Phi(-C)-1)\leq\sup_{C^{*}\geq 0}4\phi(C^{*})^{2}-\lambda\cdot(4\Phi(-C^{*})-1)\,.

Furthermore, the loss in both inequalities is at most
\varepsilon
.
Using
Lemma

4.4
on the left inequality,

\gamma(S\triangle S_{C})\leq 4\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,.

(24)

Using
Lemma

4.5
on the right inequality,
we have
|C-C^{*}|\leq 3\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.
Then using
\Phi^{\prime}=\phi
we deduce

\gamma(S_{C}\triangle S^{*})=2|\Phi(-C)-\Phi(-C^{*})|\leq 2\left(\max_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\phi(t)\right)|C-C^{*}|\leq 3\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,.

(25)

Combine
Eq.

24
and
Eq.

25
to get

\displaystyle\gamma(S\triangle S^{*})\leq 7\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(26)

We now define Davie–Reeds strip functions
f_{DR},g_{DR}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
by

f_{DR}(X)=\begin{cases}\sign(X_{1})&X\in S^{*}\\
-f(X)&X\in S\setminus S^{*}\\
f(X)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip 20.00003ptg_{DR}(X)=\begin{cases}\sign(X_{1})&X\in S^{*}\\
g(X)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\,.

By construction,
f_{DR},g_{DR}
are Davie–Reeds strip functions.
It can be seen that the modification set of both
f
and
g
is contained within
(S\triangle S^{*})\cup(S_{-}\cap S^{*})
.
Using
Eqs.

23
and
26
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\|f-f_{DR}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=4\gamma(\{X:f(X)\neq f_{DR}(X)\})\leq 4\gamma(S\triangle S^{*})+4\gamma(S_{-}\cap S^{*})\leq 4\left(7+\frac{1}{2C^{*}}\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,,

\displaystyle\|g-g_{DR}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=4\gamma(\{X:g(X)\neq g_{DR}(X)\})\leq 4\gamma(S\triangle S^{*})+4\gamma(S_{-}\cap S^{*})\leq 4\left(7+\frac{1}{2C^{*}}\right)\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,.

Since
C^{*}\approx 0.25573
, the right-hand side is at most
36\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, and therefore

\left\lVert f-f_{DR}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 6\varepsilon^{1/4}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\left\lVert g-g_{DR}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 6\varepsilon^{1/4}\,.\qed
