A Temperature-Coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–Heat Model for Thermally Driven Phase Separation
Maria Deliyianni, Boris Muha, Andrej Novak

Abstract
We study a diffuse–interface model for thermally driven phase separation in viscous incompressible mixtures. The system couples a convective Cahn–Hilliard equation for the order parameter with a Stokes subsystem for the velocity–pressure field and a heat equation for the temperature. Temperature enters the bulk free energy through a Landau-type coefficient, while the phase field feeds back on the flow through concentration-dependent density and viscosity, yielding a phenomenological temperature-coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–heat system. We motivate the chemical potential by a temperature-dependent Landau free energy and derive a priori estimates for the regularized subproblems. On the analytical side, we prove local-in-time existence of weak solutions for a regularized coupled system. On the numerical side, we propose a fully discrete finite element scheme combining a convex-splitting time discretization for the Cahn–Hilliard equation with an implicit treatment of viscous and thermal diffusion terms and a an implicit Stokes solve. Under impermeable velocity boundary conditions, the Cahn–Hilliard substep conserves mass, in the purely diffusive isothermal case, the convex-splitting discretization is unconditionally energy-stable for the Cahn–Hilliard free energy. Numerical experiments in two dimensions illustrate thermally driven spinodal decomposition, wall-induced phase separation near cooled walls, and phase separation in narrow channels under imposed thermal gradients. The simulations show the qualitative influence of key nondimensional parameters (such as the mass and thermal Péclet numbers, the Cahn number, the density and viscosity ratios, and the gravitational parameter G G ) on pattern formation, interface motion, and flow structure, and confirm that the proposed framework is a robust tool for studying thermally driven phase separation in confined geometries.

Introduction

Water condensation inside battery housings is a practically relevant issue in electric-vehicle systems. Moisture entering through pressure-compensation or ventilation elements may condense on colder internal surfaces, contributing to corrosion, insulation degradation, and accelerated ageing
[
25
]
. Motivated by this application, we study an idealized diffuse-interface model for temperature-triggered phase transition in battery-inspired geometries, and provide a mathematically tractable phase-field framework for qualitative condensation-like behaviour driven by temperature gradients. Since experimental testing over wide ranges of geometry and operating conditions is costly, numerical simulation offers a useful complementary tool.

The Cahn–Hilliard Equation

The Cahn–Hilliard equation (CHE) is a classical diffuse-interface model for
phase separation and coarsening in binary mixtures
[
5
,
17
,
33
,
36
]
.
It describes the evolution of a conserved order parameter

c(\mathbf{x},t)
, which may be interpreted as a concentration difference, volume fraction, or other rescaled composition variable depending on the application.

The evolution is governed by

\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)c=\nabla\cdot\bigl(M\nabla\mu\bigr),

(1.1)

where
M>0
is the mobility and
\mathbf{u}
is the prescribed velocity
field. The chemical potential
\mu
is the variational derivative of the
free-energy functional

F[c]=\int_{\Omega}\left(f(c)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}|\nabla c|^{2}\right)\,dx,

namely

\mu=\frac{\delta F}{\delta c}=f^{\prime}(c)-\epsilon^{2}\Delta c.

Here
f(c)
is the bulk free-energy density, while the gradient term penalizes
spatial inhomogeneity and gives rise to a diffuse interface of finite thickness.

A key aspect of the CHE is the concept of a diffuse interface: the interface between two phases is not sharp but has a finite thickness over which
c
changes gradually. This eliminates the need for explicit interface tracking, making the CHE advantageous for simulating microstructural evolution in complex geometries
[
5
]
. In regions where
c
transitions from one equilibrium composition
c_{\alpha}
to another
c_{\beta}
, the interface is represented smoothly by a narrow transition layer. In contrast, classical sharp-interface models (e.g., Stefan-type formulations or Navier–Stokes systems with an explicit surface-tension boundary condition) treat the interface as a lower-dimensional surface whose motion must be tracked explicitly in time, which becomes cumbersome in the presence of topological changes such as pinch-off, coalescence, or nucleation.

The Role of the Bulk Free-Energy Density

An essential ingredient in the Cahn–Hilliard equation is the bulk free-energy density
f(c)
. Its derivative
f^{\prime}(c)
forms the homogeneous part of the
chemical potential, while the full chemical potential is

\mu=f^{\prime}(c)-\epsilon^{2}\Delta c
. Cahn and Hilliard
[
5
]
originally suggested a singular logarithmic form for this potential:

f_{\text{log}}(c)=\frac{\theta}{2}\left((1+c)\ln(1+c)+(1-c)\ln(1-c)\right)+\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}(1-c^{2}),

(1.2)

where
0<\theta<\theta_{c}
are constants (see
[
8
,
6
]
).

This potential can be challenging to treat numerically due to its singularities near
c=\pm 1
and is therefore commonly approximated by a polynomial of degree four, known as the double-well potential:

f(c)=\frac{\alpha}{4}(c^{2}-1)^{2},\quad\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\quad f(c)=\frac{\alpha}{4}c^{4}-\frac{\alpha}{2}c^{2}+\text{const},

(1.3)

where
\alpha
is a positive constant. The double-well potential has been used extensively in models of spinodal
decomposition and two-phase flow
[
26
,
11
]
, and has also appeared in Cahn–Hilliard-based image inpainting models
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
18
,
32
,
31
]
. As illustrated in Figure
1
, it provides a good approximation of the logarithmic potential away from the singular endpoints.

Essentially,
f(c)
drives the values of the conserved quantity
c
toward one of its wells, i.e., toward energetically stable states. The double-well potential implies that
c=\pm 1
(or
c=0
and
c=1
, depending on scaling) are energetically favorable, thereby promoting phase separation in the system. This potential structure is key for modeling systems where the field
c
oscillates between two distinct phases, such as in binary alloys, mixtures, or other phase-separating media.

Alternative Potentials

In recent years, variants of the Cahn–Hilliard equation based on non-smooth potentials have been studied, particularly in the context of image inpainting problems
[
3
,
4
,
18
]
. One prominent example is the double-obstacle potential, defined as

f_{\text{do}}(c)=\psi(c)+\frac{1}{2}(1-c^{2}),\quad\text{where}\quad\psi(c)=I_{[-1,1]}(c)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }c\in[-1,1],\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.4)

and
I_{A}
is the indicator function of the set
A
. In
[
3
]
, the authors developed an efficient finite-element solver for image inpainting problems and demonstrated that the non-smooth potential can produce superior reconstruction results compared to the smooth double-well potential.

From a modeling perspective, there has been considerable effort to identify physically meaningful forms of the free-energy density
f(c)
, motivated in part by the observation that solutions
c
of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with the double-well potential may not remain bounded within
[-1,1]
. In many applications such unbounded solutions violate physical constraints (e.g., concentration or volume fraction bounds). The singularity of
f_{\text{log}}^{\prime}(c)
, in contrast to the polynomial case, enforces
c\in(-1,1)
at the continuous level, at the price of numerical stiffness.

From a computational standpoint, the violation of bounds can be mitigated by thresholding, where values of
c>1
are reset to
c=1
, and values of
c<-1
are reset to
c=-1
. This approach, employed for instance by Cherfils et al.
[
7
]
, incorporates thresholding in the final steps of the numerical scheme to enforce physical bounds. At the analytical level, a well-posedness result for a more general form of (
1.1
) with degenerate diffusion mobility, where the diffusion coefficient depends on
c
, was established in
[
9
]
. These frameworks provide a rigorous background but typically focus on isothermal settings and do not explicitly resolve temperature-driven phase transitions such as condensation.

Mathematical Modeling of Phase Transitions

Phase-field models describe phase transitions using an order parameter that varies continuously across interfaces. They are based on energy functionals (e.g., Landau-type free energies) with minima corresponding to stable phases (e.g., liquid and vapor). A classical approach postulates a Helmholtz free energy density with a double-well form for the two phases, plus a gradient energy term to penalize interfaces. The Cahn–Hilliard equation arises from this framework as the
H^{-1}
gradient flow of the free energy, governing the evolution of the phase field by free-energy dissipation
[
20
]
.

Fabrizio et al.
[
16
]
formulated a phase-field model for liquid–vapor transitions that enforces thermodynamic consistency. Their model introduces a temperature-dependent Helmholtz free energy and derives the phase-field evolution from the second law of thermodynamics, ensuring energy conservation and proper entropy production.

Jamet et al.
[
22
]
introduced a diffuse-interface formulation for liquid–vapor flows with phase change using a van der Waals-type gradient theory (similar in spirit to the Cahn–Hilliard framework) to model interfacial thermodynamics. In their approach, deviations from the equilibrium chemical potential drive evaporation/condensation, while the hydrodynamics are governed by a stress tensor that includes capillary effects. More recently, Wang et al.
[
35
]
developed a phase-field method for boiling heat transfer by coupling the Cahn–Hilliard equation with the Navier–Stokes and energy equations. Their method introduces a source term in the energy equation to account for latent heat and a velocity divergence correction to handle volumetric expansion due to phase change. While their focus was on boiling, the same framework can be adapted to simulate droplet formation under subcooled (condensing) conditions. The inclusion of a thermal solver ensures that temperature-dependent phase transitions are directly captured: when the local temperature falls below the dew point, the free energy landscape shifts to favor the liquid phase, and condensation ensues.

Recent research has evolved toward more sophisticated mathematical models governed by mass, momentum, energy balance, and diffusion equations, capturing the complex interactions within the condensation of water vapor from humid air. These models address the dynamics of condensing vapor interacting with non-condensable gases under varying flow conditions, such as those in turbulent or laminar regimes within vertical ducts (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
14
,
10
]
).

Another extension is the incorporation of additional fields into the free energy to model multi-phase systems involving gas, liquid, and solid components (e.g., electrodes or separators). Gavish et al.
[
19
]
extended the Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes model to include a solid phase to study salt precipitation in porous media. Although their focus was on salt deposition, the framework informs multi-phase battery models where interactions among liquid water, gas, and solids are critical.

Phase-field models have also been implemented within the lattice Boltzmann framework, which is attractive for complex boundary conditions and parallelization. Dong and Yu
[
12
]
demonstrated how a phase-field approach can be implemented in LBM to simulate droplet formation during condensation. By coupling a phase-field variable with a thermal lattice Boltzmann solver, their approach successfully captured dropwise condensation and boiling in complex geometries relevant to battery thermal management. Because condensation involves thin liquid layers and sharp thermal gradients, adaptive mesh refinement or multiscale methods are often employed. Jamet et al.
[
22
]
used a fine grid near the interface to resolve the phase-change region, while coarser grids handled larger-scale flow features, ensuring numerical stability and accuracy in regions with rapid phase transitions.

These developments demonstrate that diffuse-interface, phase-field formulations are well suited to capture temperature-dependent liquid–vapor transitions in complex geometries. However, most existing studies either focus on idealized thermodynamic settings or on generic boiling/condensation configurations, rather than on battery-relevant housings with realistic boundary conditions and coupling to variable-density Stokes flow. This gap motivates the temperature-coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–Heat model and its analysis presented in this work.

Mathematical Challenges and Analytical Approach

From an analytical perspective, the coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–heat system considered here differs in several essential ways from existing diffuse-interface frameworks, most notably the isothermal Cahn–Hilliard–Navier–Stokes analysis of
[
1
]
. While the overall structure is similar, the introduction of temperature dependence, mixed boundary conditions, and variable material coefficients creates new difficulties that require more delicate treatment.

A first challenge arises from the temperature-dependent free energy. The homogeneous part of the chemical potential depends explicitly on the evolving temperature field, so the phase-field equation is no longer autonomous even at fixed velocity. Classical energy arguments cannot be closed at the level of the Cahn–Hilliard subsystem alone; additional control of the temperature field is required to handle the coupling. Moreover, the temperature enters the bulk potential with only limited regularity, which necessitates a suitable regularization at the analytical level.

A second difficulty stems from the absence of global
L^{\infty}
bounds for the order parameter. Unlike models based on singular potentials, the polynomial free energy employed here does not prevent overshoots of the concentration field. This precludes arguments relying on uniform boundedness of
c
and forces the analysis to be carried out in weaker topologies. We work within the functional framework

H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{-2}(\Omega),

and combine complementary multiplier tests to recover sufficient control of the solution.

A further complication is introduced by the mixed boundary configuration. The presence of traction-free boundaries for the velocity and mixed thermal boundary conditions prevents the imposition of a zero-mean constraint on the chemical potential and introduces boundary terms that are absent in classical settings. This necessitates a careful reformulation of the weak problem and modified energy estimates that remain valid without homogeneous Dirichlet conditions.

Finally, the system exhibits strong coupling between distinct physical mechanisms: phase separation, creeping flow with variable density and viscosity, and heat transport. We address this by analyzing each component operator separately—first the heat equation, then the Cahn–Hilliard system, and finally the Stokes problem—and proving that each is well-defined and continuous with respect to its inputs. These operators are then composed into a single map, and existence of solutions to the full coupled system follows from a standard Schauder fixed-point argument.

Current contribution, overview

In this work we develop and analyse a temperature–coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–Heat model tailored to condensation of humid air in confined geometries. The contribution is twofold, combining a thermodynamically motivated phase-field formulation with a local-existence theory for a regularized coupled system and a numerically simulations based on finite-element implementation.

(i) Temperature–dependent Landau potential for condensation.

Starting from the classical double–well free energy, we introduce a Landau–type bulk potential

f(c,\Theta)=2c^{4}-(1-\beta(\Theta))\,c^{2},

where
\beta(\Theta)=\Theta/\Theta_{S}
encodes the local ratio between the temperature
\Theta
and the saturation temperature
\Theta_{S}
. This choice interpolates, in a single analytic expression, between a single–well landscape for
\Theta>\Theta_{S}
(thermodynamically stable homogeneous vapour) and a symmetric double–well landscape for
\Theta<\Theta_{S}
(phase separation into vapour–rich and liquid–rich regions). In contrast to isothermal Cahn–Hilliard models or purely numerical temperature couplings, the phase behaviour is here controlled directly by the thermodynamic parameter
\beta(\Theta)
, that should be interpreted as a phenomenological proxy for a
temperature-triggered transition. The present model does not include humidity, or latent heat, these effects are absorbed into the choice of the threshold temperature
\Theta_{S}
.

(ii) Coupled CH–Stokes–Heat system with variable density and viscosity.

On the hydrodynamic side, we embed this potential into a diffuse-interface formulation for a two-phase gas-rich/liquid-rich mixture, leading to a
Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes system with:

1.

affine density and viscosity laws
\rho(c)
and
\eta(c)
with fixed reference phase parameters,

2.

gravitational forcing through a buoyancy term
G\,\rho(c)\,\hat{\bm{g}}
,

3.

an advected temperature field satisfying a convection–diffusion equation and entering the bulk energy only through
\beta(\Theta)
.

This structure couples mass diffusion, creeping flow and heat transport at the level of the free energy, rather than via ad hoc source terms, and is flexible enough to accommodate both isothermal spinodal decomposition and non–isothermal condensation/evaporation scenarios.

(iii) Rigorous existence theory under mixed boundary conditions.

On the analytical side, we establish local-in-time existence of weak solutions to the coupled system in two and three space dimensions. The proof analyzes each component operator individually:

1.

the heat operator
\mathcal{H}:\mathbf{u}\mapsto\Theta
is shown to be well-defined and continuous, with a maximum principle ensuring nonnegativity of the temperature;

2.

the Cahn–Hilliard operator
\mathcal{C}:(\mathbf{u},\Theta)\mapsto c
is proven to exist uniquely and depend continuously on both velocity and temperature;

3.

the Stokes operator
\mathcal{S}:c\mapsto\mathbf{u}
is analyzed for variable-coefficient flow with mixed boundary conditions, yielding existence, uniqueness, and continuity.

These operators are composed into a single map
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u},\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u})))
that exploits the triangular coupling structure. A Schauder fixed-point argument applied to
\mathcal{T}
yields existence of weak solutions. The analysis is carried out in the
H^{2}\to L^{2}\to H^{-2}
framework to handle the absence of global
L^{\infty}
bounds on the order parameter.

(iv) Semi-implicit finite–element scheme with an energy-stable Cahn–Hilliard substep.

Building on the continuous theory, we design a first–order semi–implicit finite–element scheme that:

1.

uses an Eyre-type convex splitting of the temperature-dependent free energy for the Cahn–Hilliard step, in the purely diffusive isothermal setting this yields the standard unconditional energy stability of the standalone Cahn–Hilliard substep;

2.

couples this to a Taylor–Hood discretization of the Stokes subsystem with variable coefficients and a backward–Euler discretization of the heat equation;

3.

employs a sequential splitting strategy that is straightforward to implement in existing finite–element software.

We qualitatively compare the hydrodynamic discretization and interface transport against a lid–driven cavity benchmark for two-phase Stokes flow and then explore four classes of scenarios relevant to battery-inspired geometries: isothermal phase separation above and below the transition temperature, gravity-driven phase separation under mixed no-slip/traction-free boundary conditions, and heated-boundary/cooled-boundary transition scenarios. The simulations illustrate how the proposed temperature–sensitive potential and coupled model reproduce qualitative features such as droplet nucleation, coarsening, settling under gravity and wall–induced phase change, providing a mathematically controlled framework for future studies of condensation in electrochemical energy systems.

2.1
Temperature–dependent potential for the Cahn–Hilliard equation

To incorporate temperature effects into the Cahn–Hilliard equation, we modify the classical double–well potential so that the free–energy density depends explicitly on temperature. We consider a binary mixture of air and water, with the order parameter
c
representing a (rescaled) local composition. At the dimensional level we assume that the minima of the double–well potential correspond to the pure phases
c=0
(air) and
c=1
(water). With this choice the reference concentration is
c_{R}=0.5
, and the classical quartic double-well potential can be written in the form

f_{\mathrm{dw}}(c)\;=\;-a\,(c-0.5)^{2}+2a\,(c-0.5)^{4},

for a suitable parameter
a>0
.
For convenience, we shift the order parameter so that the pure phases are symmetric around zero:

c\;\mapsto\;c-0.5,

and, by reuse of notation, we denote the shifted variable again by
c
. This maps
[0,1]
to
[-0.5,0.5]
and yields

f(c)\;=\;-a\,c^{2}+2a\,c^{4}.

(2.1)

To introduce temperature dependence, we let the parameter
a
change sign at the saturation temperature
\Theta_{S}
:

a(\Theta)=\alpha\,(\Theta_{S}-\Theta),

(2.2)

with
\alpha>0
a scaling constant. For nondimensionalization we set
\alpha=1/\Theta_{S}
and introduce the dimensionless temperature

\beta=\frac{\Theta}{\Theta_{S}},

(2.3)

so that

a(\Theta)=1-\beta.

(2.4)

Motivated by this shifted double-well and in order to keep the quartic
coefficient positive for all temperatures, we adopt the Landau ansatz

f(c,\Theta)=2c^{4}-(1-\beta)c^{2}.

(2.5)

For
\beta<1
, the minima of
f(\cdot,\Theta)
are located at

c=\pm\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta}}{2},

so the preferred phase values depend on temperature. The values

\pm 0.5
correspond to the reference case
\beta=0
.
When
\Theta<\Theta_{S}
(i.e.
\beta<1
), the coefficient of
c^{2}
is
negative
and
f(\cdot,\Theta)
has a symmetric double–well shape with two minima away from
c=0
, promoting phase separation. For
\Theta>\Theta_{S}
(i.e.
\beta>1
), the coefficient of
c^{2}
is positive and the potential has a unique minimum at
c=0
, favouring a homogeneous phase.

2.2
System of equations

Diffuse–interface models based on the Cahn–Hilliard and Navier–Stokes (or Stokes) equations are widely used to describe two–phase flows. Following the volume–averaged formulation of Ding et al.
[
11
]
, we take the
volume fraction
of one phase as the order parameter. Let

\phi=\frac{V_{1}}{V_{1}+V_{2}}\in[0,1]

(2.6)

denote the local volume fraction of phase 1 in a representative volume element. The partial mass densities in that element are

\widetilde{\rho}_{1}=\rho_{1}\phi,\qquad\widetilde{\rho}_{2}=\rho_{2}(1-\phi),

and the total density is

\rho=\rho_{1}\phi+\rho_{2}(1-\phi).

(2.7)

The mass balance for phase 1 reads

\frac{\partial\widetilde{\rho}_{1}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{m}_{1}=0,

(2.8)

where
\bm{m}_{1}
is the mass flux of phase 1. We decompose
\bm{m}_{1}
into advective and diffusive parts:

\bm{m}_{1}=\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\bm{u}-\rho_{1}\bm{j}_{1},

(2.9)

with
\bm{u}
the volume–averaged velocity and
\bm{j}_{1}
the (volume) diffusive flux of phase 1. Inserting this into (
2.8
) gives

\frac{\partial\widetilde{\rho}_{1}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\bm{u})-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{1}\bm{j}_{1})=0.

(2.10)

Using
\widetilde{\rho}_{1}=\rho_{1}\phi
and assuming
\rho_{1}
constant,

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\bm{u}\phi)-\nabla\cdot\bm{j}_{1}=0.

(2.11)

Analogously, for phase 2 we obtain

\frac{\partial(1-\phi)}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot[\bm{u}(1-\phi)]-\nabla\cdot\bm{j}_{2}=0.

(2.12)

Adding (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) yields

\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\nabla\cdot(\bm{j}_{1}+\bm{j}_{2}).

(2.13)

Imposing
volume conservation
of the diffusive fluxes,

\bm{j}_{1}+\bm{j}_{2}=\bm{0},

(2.14)

we obtain an incompressible velocity field,

\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=0.

(2.15)

Subtracting (
2.12
) from (
2.11
), and using (
2.15
), leads to the convective Cahn–Hilliard equation for the volume fraction:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\phi=\nabla\cdot\bm{j},\qquad\bm{j}=M\nabla\mu,

(2.16)

with
M>0
the (scalar) mobility and
\mu
the chemical potential.

For consistency with the temperature–dependent potential in Section
2.1
, we introduce the centred order parameter

c=\phi-\tfrac{1}{2}\in[-0.5,0.5],

and, by abuse of notation, write the Cahn–Hilliard equation in terms of
c
. The mixture density then takes the affine form

\rho(c)=\bigl(0.5-c\bigr)\,\rho_{2}+\bigl(0.5+c\bigr)\,\rho_{1},

(2.17)

so that
c=-0.5
corresponds to the pure phase with density
\rho_{2}
, and

c=0.5
to the pure phase with density
\rho_{1}
. Similarly, we define the viscosity by

\eta(c)=\bigl(0.5-c\bigr)\,\eta_{2}+\bigl(0.5+c\bigr)\,\eta_{1},

(2.18)

so that
c=-0.5
corresponds to the pure phase with viscosity
\eta_{2}
, and

c=0.5
to the pure phase with viscosity
\eta_{1}
.

In the model studied in this work, the reference phase parameters
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
are fixed positive constants. Thus
\rho(c)
and
\eta(c)
depend on the order parameter
c
only. For the
battery-condensation interpretation, phase 1 may be viewed as a lighter gas-rich state and phase 2 as a heavier liquid-rich state, typically with
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
. Temperature effects are incorporated exclusively through the coefficient
\beta(\Theta)
in the bulk free energy, and not through
additional temperature dependence in
\rho
or
\eta
.

In the Cahn–Hilliard equation, the mobility
M
controls the rate of diffusive relaxation. Since
M
cannot be derived from first principles and anisotropic transport is not considered here, we take
M
to be a positive constant, following e.g.
[
23
,
11
]
. More elaborate, concentration–dependent mobilities (such as
M(\phi)=\phi(1-\phi)
) would require a significantly more involved numerical treatment and are not pursued in this work.

2.3.1
Nondimensionalization of the Cahn–Hilliard equation

The dimensional convective Cahn–Hilliard system reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{u}c)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(M\nabla\mu),

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f(c,\Theta)}{\partial c}-\epsilon^{2}\Delta c,

(2.21)

with

given by (

). Substituting the nondimensional variables and using
\nabla=L^{-1}\nabla^{*}
,
t=\tau t^{*}
gives

\frac{c_{0}}{\tau}\frac{\partial c^{*}}{\partial t^{*}}+\frac{Uc_{0}}{L}\,\mathbf{u}^{*}\cdot\nabla^{*}c^{*}=\frac{M\mu_{0}}{L^{2}}\,\Delta^{*}\mu^{*},

where incompressibility (
\nabla^{*}\!\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}=0
) has been used to simplify the convective term. Dividing by
c_{0}/\tau
and introducing

Pe=\frac{ULc_{0}}{M\mu_{0}},

we obtain

\frac{\partial c^{*}}{\partial t^{*}}+\mathbf{u}^{*}\cdot\nabla^{*}c^{*}=\frac{1}{Pe}\,\Delta^{*}\mu^{*}.

(2.22)

For the chemical potential, (
2.21
) becomes

\mu_{0}\mu^{*}=\frac{\partial f(c_{0}c^{*},\Theta)}{\partial c}-\epsilon^{2}\frac{c_{0}}{L^{2}}\,\Delta^{*}c^{*}.

Choosing
c_{0}=1
and
\mu_{0}
so that the prefactor of
\partial f/\partial c
is unity, and defining

, we obtain

\mu^{*}=\frac{\partial f(c^{*},\Theta)}{\partial c^{*}}-Ch^{2}\Delta^{*}c^{*}.

Using

, we have

\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}=8c^{3}-2(1-\beta)c,

so that

\mu^{*}=\left[\,8c^{*3}-2(1-\beta)c^{*}\,\right]-Ch^{2}\Delta^{*}c^{*}.

(2.23)

Dropping asterisks for notational simplicity, the nondimensional Cahn–Hilliard system reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{Pe}\,\Delta\mu,

(2.24)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=8c^{3}-2(1-\beta)\,c-Ch^{2}\Delta c.

(2.25)

The nondimensional density and viscosity are

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(c)=\frac{\rho(c)}{\rho_{2}}=(0.5-c)+(0.5+c)\,\lambda_{\rho},

(2.26)

\displaystyle\eta^{*}(c)=\frac{\eta(c)}{\eta_{2}}=(0.5-c)+(0.5+c)\,\lambda_{\eta}.

(2.27)

2.3.2
Nondimensionalization of the Navier–Stokes (Stokes) equations

The dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations for the mixture read

\displaystyle\rho(c)\left(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}\right)

\displaystyle=-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\left(2\eta(c)\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})\right)+\rho(c)\,\mathbf{g},

(2.28)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.29)

where
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\nabla\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})^{\top}\bigr)
is the strain–rate tensor.

Substituting the nondimensional variables and dividing by
\eta_{2}U/L^{2}
yields

Re\,\rho^{*}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}^{*}}{\partial t^{*}}+\mathbf{u}^{*}\cdot\nabla^{*}\mathbf{u}^{*}\right)=-\nabla^{*}p^{*}+\nabla^{*}\cdot\left(2\eta^{*}(c)\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}^{*})\right)+G\,\rho^{*}\,\hat{\mathbf{g}},

where

\displaystyle-\nabla^{*}p^{*}+\nabla^{*}\cdot\left(2\eta^{*}(c)\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}^{*})\right)+G\,\rho^{*}(c)\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(2.30)

\displaystyle\nabla^{*}\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}

\displaystyle=0.

(2.31)

Dropping asterisks, we obtain

\displaystyle-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\left(2\eta(c)\,\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})\right)+G\,\rho(c)\,\hat{\mathbf{g}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

(2.32)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0.

(2.33)

2.3.3
Nondimensionalization of the heat equation

The dimensional heat equation is

\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Theta=\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\Theta).

(2.34)

Using the nondimensional variables and scales,

\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}=\frac{\Delta\Theta}{\tau}\frac{\partial\Theta^{*}}{\partial t^{*}},\quad\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Theta=\frac{U\Delta\Theta}{L}\mathbf{u}^{*}\cdot\nabla^{*}\Theta^{*},\quad\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\Theta)=\kappa\frac{\Delta\Theta}{L^{2}}\Delta^{*}\Theta^{*},

and dividing by
\Delta\Theta/\tau
, we obtain

\frac{\partial\Theta^{*}}{\partial t^{*}}+\mathbf{u}^{*}\cdot\nabla^{*}\Theta^{*}=\frac{1}{Pe_{\Theta}}\,\Delta^{*}\Theta^{*},

with
Pe_{\Theta}=UL/\kappa
. Dropping asterisks, the nondimensional heat equation is

\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Theta=\dfrac{1}{Pe_{\Theta}}\,\Delta\Theta.

(2.35)

2.4
The coupled temperature–dependent condensation model

Collecting the results above and dropping all asterisks, we present the
nondimensional temperature–coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes system in the
form used throughout the analytical part of this work. For the purposes
of Section

Stokes equation is augmented by a time-regularization term
-\alpha\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}

with
\alpha>0
, and the temperature
\Theta
appearing in the chemical
potential is replaced by a spatially mollified field
\hat{\Theta}
. Both

4
. The
system reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)c

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Pe}\,\Delta\mu,

(2.36)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=8c^{3}-2\bigl(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta})\bigr)c-Ch^{2}\Delta c,

(2.37)

\displaystyle-\alpha{\bm{u}}_{t}-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\bigl(2\eta(c)\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})\bigr)+G\,\rho(c)\,\hat{\bm{g}}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(2.38)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.39)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\Theta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Pe_{\Theta}}\Delta\Theta,

(2.40)

where
\rho(c)
and
\eta(c)
are given by the affine mixture relations above. After nondimensionalization by
\Theta_{S}
, the temperature field
\Theta

is already dimensionless. We nevertheless retain the notation

in (

) to emphasize its role as the temperature-dependent coefficient in the Landau potential; in the simplest
choice adopted here,

. The field

is a spatially regularized representative of the evolving
temperature
\Theta
, introduced for analytical purposes; its precise

.

The system is supplemented with the initial conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\bm{x},0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega,

(2.41)

\displaystyle c(\bm{x},0)

\displaystyle=c_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega,

(2.42)

\displaystyle\Theta(\bm{x},0)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega.

(2.43)

and boundary conditions

\displaystyle\nabla c\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(2.44)

\displaystyle\nabla\mu\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(2.45)

for the phase field. Additionally, for the velocity and pressure, on the no–slip boundary

(assumed to have positive measure), we have

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\quad t>0,

(2.46)

and on the traction–free boundary

\displaystyle\left(-p\,\bm{I}+2\eta(c)\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{u})\right)\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{N},\quad t>0.

(2.47)

For the temperature, on the Dirichlet boundary

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\Theta_{D},

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{\Theta},\quad t>0,

(2.48)

and on the thermally insulated boundary
\Gamma_{\Theta_{N}}=\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\Theta}

\displaystyle\nabla\Theta\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0,

3
Mathematical Analysis

We establish local-in-time existence of weak solutions to the coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–heat system introduced in Section
2.4
. The heat operator
\mathcal{H}
depends only on the velocity
\mathbf{u}
, the Cahn–Hilliard operator
\mathcal{C}
depends on both
(\mathbf{u},\Theta)
, and the Stokes operator
\mathcal{S}
depends on the concentration
c
, yielding the composite solution operator

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathcal{S}\big(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u},\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}))\big).

Existence follows by applying the Schauder Fixed-Point Theorem to
\mathcal{T}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Local existence of weak solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, be a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain satisfying

\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{\Theta}\cup\Gamma_{D},\quad|\Gamma_{D}|>0,

and consider the coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–heat system presented in Section
2.4
.
Assume that the initial data satisfy

c_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega),\qquad\mathbf{u}_{0}\in L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega),\qquad\Theta_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega),

and are compatible with the boundary conditions of the system. Assume moreover that the prescribed Dirichlet temperature
\Theta_{D}
is constant in space and time, and that the viscosity
\eta(\cdot)
and density
\rho(\cdot)
are bounded, continuous, and strictly positive.

Fix regularization parameters
\alpha>0
and
\delta>0
. Then there exists a time
T>0
, depending only on
\alpha
,
\delta
, and the norms of the initial data, such that the coupled system admits a weak solution

(c,\mathbf{u},\Theta)\in X_{c}(T)\times X_{u}(T)\times X_{\Theta}(T)

on
(0,T)
, with
\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
, where

\displaystyle X_{c}(T)

\displaystyle:=L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega)),

(3.1)

\displaystyle X_{u}(T)

\displaystyle:=L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}),\quad s\in(1/2,1),

(3.2)

\displaystyle X_{\Theta}(T)

\displaystyle:=L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)),

(3.3)

with

L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega):=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi=0\ \text{for all }\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\right\}.

Proof outline

We establish existence and continuity of the heat operator
\mathcal{H}
(Section
3.1
), the Cahn–Hilliard operator
\mathcal{C}
(Section
3.2
), and the Stokes operator
\mathcal{S}
(Section
3.3
). The composite map
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u},\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u})))
is then shown to be continuous with relatively compact image in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
. Existence follows by applying Schauder’s theorem (Section
3.4
).

Remark 2

(Regularization and numerical implementation)
.

The analytical results in this section are established for a doubly regularized version of the coupled system (
2.36
)–(
2.40
):

1.

Stokes regularization. We add a time derivative term
-\alpha\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}
to the Stokes equation (
2.38
), where
\alpha>0
. This regularization ensures the fixed-point map is well-defined.

2.

Temperature mollification. The temperature
\Theta
appearing in the chemical potential (
2.37
) is replaced by a spatially mollified field
\hat{\Theta}=(\rho_{\delta}*\widetilde{\Theta})|_{\Omega}
, where
\rho_{\delta}
is a standard mollifier with parameter
\delta>0
, and
\widetilde{\theta}
an extension of
\Theta
by zero to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This provides the

regularity needed for the Cahn–Hilliard energy estimates.

In the numerical simulations of Section

we set

and use the unmollified temperature
\Theta
. Extending the analytical results to this case is left for future work.

.

Throughout this section, we adopt the shorthand notation

L^{2}(H^{2})=L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)),\quad L^{\infty}(H^{1})=L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

and similar conventions for other function spaces. Unless stated otherwise,

3.1
The Heat Operator

We begin by analyzing the heat equation for a prescribed divergence-free velocity field
\mathbf{u}
. We establish existence and regularity of weak solutions and prove continuity of the solution operator

\mathcal{H}:\mathbf{u}\mapsto\Theta,

where
\Theta
denotes the temperature field solving the heat subproblem (
2.40
) subject to the mixed boundary conditions (
2.48
)–(
2.49
).

Proposition 3.2

(Existence and regularity of weak solutions to the heat subproblem)
.

Let
R>0
and let
\mathbf{u}
be a prescribed divergence-free (in the weak sense) velocity field
satisfying

\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}),\qquad\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s})}\leq R,

for some
s\in(1/2,1)
, and the boundary conditions (
2.46
)–(
2.47
).
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and let the
initial datum
\Theta_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfy the compatibility condition

\Theta_{0}|_{\Gamma_{\Theta}}=\Theta_{D}
.

Then there exists a time
T_{\Theta}>0
, depending only on

\|\Theta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
\|\Theta_{D}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{\Theta})}
, and
R
, such that
the heat equation (
2.40
) with mixed boundary conditions
(
2.48
)–(
2.49
) admits a unique weak solution
\Theta
on

(0,T_{\Theta})
satisfying

\Theta\in L^{\infty}(0,T_{\Theta};L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{\Theta};H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T_{\Theta};H^{-1}(\Omega)),

and
\|\Theta\|_{X_{\Theta}(T_{\Theta})}\leq R
.
Moreover,
\Theta
satisfies the energy identity

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\Theta-\Theta_{D}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{Pe_{\Theta}}\|\nabla(\Theta-\Theta_{D})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T_{\Theta}).

Proof.

Setting
\widetilde{\Theta}=\Theta-\Theta_{D}
reduces the problem to one with homogeneous boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{\Theta}
.
We construct a Galerkin approximation based on eigenfunctions of the Laplacian with mixed boundary conditions, yielding approximate solutions
\widetilde{\Theta}_{n}
.
Testing the weak formulation with
\widetilde{\Theta}_{n}
and using
\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{u}=0
gives the uniform energy estimate

\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{\Theta};L^{2})}+\|\widetilde{\Theta}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{\Theta};H^{1})}\leq C\|\Theta_{0}-\Theta_{D}\|_{L^{2}}.

The advection term
(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\Theta}_{n},\widetilde{\Theta}_{n})
is controlled using the Sobolev embedding
H^{s}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2d/(d-2s)}(\Omega)
for
s>1/2
when
d=3
, ensuring integrability of the product.
Compactness then yields a strongly convergent subsequence in
L^{2}(0,T_{\Theta};L^{2}(\Omega))
with limit
\widetilde{\Theta}
satisfying the weak formulation and the energy identity.
Uniqueness follows by testing the equation satisfied by the difference of two solutions.

\Box

The next result establishes positivity of the temperature field, which will be used to ensure nonnegativity of the mollified temperature in the Cahn–Hilliard analysis.

Proposition 3.3

(Nonnegativity of the temperature)
.

Let
\Theta
be the weak solution of the heat equation from
Proposition
3.2
, and assume that the geometric conditions of Remark
1
hold.
If

\Theta_{0}\geq 0
in
\Omega
and
\Theta_{D}\geq 0
on
\Gamma_{\Theta},

then

\Theta(x,t)\geq 0\quad\text{for a.e. }(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T_{\Theta}].

Proof.

Define the negative part

\Theta^{-}:=\max\{-\Theta,0\}.

Multiplying (
2.40
) by
-\Theta^{-}
and integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\Theta^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla\Theta^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Theta)\Theta^{-}.

Since
\nabla\Theta=-\nabla\Theta^{-}
on
\{\Theta\leq 0\}
and

\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{u}=0
, we compute

\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Theta)\Theta^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\mathbf{u}\cdot n)(\Theta^{-})^{2}=0,

because
\Theta^{-}=0
on
\Gamma_{\Theta}
(since
\Theta_{D}\geq 0
)
and
\mathbf{u}\cdot n=0
on
\Gamma_{D}
.
Therefore,

\frac{d}{dt}\|\Theta^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\|\nabla\Theta^{-}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=0.

Using
\Theta_{0}\geq 0
, we conclude
\Theta^{-}=0
, and hence

\Theta\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T_{\Theta}].

\Box

Proposition 3.4

(Continuity of the heat solution operator)
.

Let
T>0
be such that
T\leq T_{\Theta}
, where
T_{\Theta}
is the time of existence
obtained in Proposition
3.2
.
Let
\{\mathbf{u}_{n}\}
be a sequence in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
, with
s\in(1/2,1)
, such that

\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{u}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}).

Let
\Theta_{n}=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}_{n})
and
\Theta=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u})

be the corresponding weak solutions of the heat equation.
Then

\Theta_{n}\to\Theta\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Proof.

Step 1: Compactness.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{u}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
, and set

\Theta_{n}=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}_{n})
.
By Proposition
3.2
and the associated energy identity,
the sequence
\{\Theta_{n}\}
is uniformly bounded in
X_{\Theta}
.
The Aubin–Lions lemma therefore yields

\Theta_{n}\to\widetilde{\Theta}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Step 2: Identification of the limit.

Since
\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{u}
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
,
while
\nabla\Theta_{n}
converges weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
and
\Theta_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
by Proposition
3.2
,
we pass to the limit in the weak formulation of
(
2.40
)–(
2.49
).
This identifies the limit as

\widetilde{\Theta}=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}).

Step 3: Continuity.

Since every convergent subsequence has the same limit, we conclude that

\Theta_{n}\to\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u})\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

\Box

3.2
The Cahn–Hilliard Operator

We now analyze the Cahn–Hilliard system for prescribed velocity and temperature fields
(\mathbf{u},\Theta)
. The main challenge is handling the temperature-dependent coefficient
\beta(\Theta)
in the free energy, which requires spatial regularity beyond what the heat equation provides. To address this, we introduce a mollified temperature field.

For a fixed mollification parameter
\delta>0
, we extend
\Theta
by zero outside
\Omega
, denoted by
\widetilde{\Theta}
, and define

\hat{\Theta}:=(\rho_{\delta}*\widetilde{\Theta})\big|_{\Omega},

(3.4)

where
\rho_{\delta}
is a standard smooth mollifier and the convolution is taken with respect to the spatial variables only.
Then
\hat{\Theta}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
for all finite times. Moreover,
since
\rho_{\delta}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Young’s convolution
inequality gives

\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\delta}\|\Theta\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
C_{\delta}
depends only on the mollification parameter. This estimate
will be used in the continuous dependence argument of
Proposition
3.6
.

We establish existence and regularity of weak solutions via Galerkin approximation and compactness arguments, then prove continuous dependence of the solution operator

\mathcal{C}:(\mathbf{u},\Theta)\mapsto c

with respect to both inputs.

Definition 3.1

(Weak solution)
.

Let
T>0
and let
\mathbf{u}\in X_{u}(T)
and
\Theta\in X_{\Theta}(T)
be fixed
prescribed functions, where the spaces
X_{u}(T)
and
X_{\Theta}(T)
are defined in
(
3.2
) and (
3.3
), respectively.

We say that a pair
(c,\mu)
is a weak solution of the Cahn–Hilliard system
on the time interval
(0,T)
if

c\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)),\quad\partial_{t}c\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-2}(\Omega)),\quad\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

and for all test functions

\varphi,\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))

the following identities hold:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\big[\langle\partial_{t}c,\varphi\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}+(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c,\varphi)+\tfrac{1}{Pe}(\nabla\mu,\nabla\varphi)\big]\,dt=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(\mu,\psi)\,dt=\int_{0}^{T}\big[(8c^{3}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c,\psi)+Ch^{2}(\nabla c,\nabla\psi)\big]\,dt.

The initial condition is prescribed as
c(0)=c_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)

Then there exists a time

.

Moreover, the solution satisfies

c\in L^{\infty}(0,T_{CH};H^{1}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{3}(\Omega)).

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Step 1: Galerkin approximation.

We construct a Galerkin approximation by projecting the system onto finite-dimensional spaces. Let
\{\phi_{k}\}
be the eigenfunctions of the Neumann Laplacian satisfying
\partial_{n}\phi_{k}=0
on
\partial\Omega

[
15
]
, and define

V_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}\subset H^{2}(\Omega),\qquad W_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}\subset H^{1}(\Omega).

We seek an approximate solution of the form

c_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{j}(t)\,\phi_{j}(x),\qquad\mu_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{k}(t)\,\phi_{k}(x),

which satisfies the weak formulation: for all
\phi_{j}\in V_{n}
and
\psi_{k}\in W_{n}
,

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}c_{n},\phi_{j}\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}+(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\phi_{j})+\tfrac{1}{Pe}(\nabla\mu_{n},\nabla\phi_{j})=0,

(3.5)

\displaystyle(\mu_{n},\psi_{k})=(8c_{n}^{3}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c_{n},\psi_{k})+Ch^{2}(\nabla c_{n},\nabla\psi_{k}).

(3.6)

Here,
\hat{\Theta}
denotes the regularized temperature introduced in (
3.4
).

The initial condition is prescribed as

c_{n}(0)=P_{n}c_{0},

where
P_{n}
denotes the
L^{2}
-projection onto
V_{n}
.
By standard ODE theory, the resulting finite-dimensional system for
\{g_{j},h_{k}\}_{j,k=1}^{n}
admits a unique local solution in
C^{1}(0,t^{*}(n))
for some
t^{*}(n)>0
.

We now derive uniform estimates independent of
n
, starting with a bound for the time derivative.

Estimate for
\partial_{t}c_{n}
in
H^{-2}
.

Testing (
3.5
) with any
\phi\in V_{n}\subset H^{2}(\Omega)
yields

\langle\partial_{t}c_{n},\phi\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}=-\langle\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\phi\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}-(\nabla\mu_{n},\nabla\phi).

(3.7)

Applying Hölder’s inequality and the embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we obtain

|\langle\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\phi\rangle|\leq C\,\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\nabla c_{n}\|\,\|\phi\|_{H^{2}},\qquad|(\nabla\mu_{n},\nabla\phi)|\leq C\,\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|\,\|\phi\|_{H^{2}}.

Taking the supremum over all
\phi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with
\|\phi\|_{H^{2}}\leq 1
gives

\|\partial_{t}c_{n}\|_{H^{-2}}\leq C\big(\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\nabla c_{n}\|+\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|\big).

(3.8)

We next test the weak formulation with
\mu_{n}
to control
\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|
and
\|\nabla c_{n}\|
.

Multiplying by
\mu_{n}
.

Testing (
3.5
) with
\mu_{n}\in W_{n}
and using (
3.6
), we treat each term separately.

Time-dependent term.

We obtain

\langle\partial_{t}c_{n},\mu_{n}\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\big(4\|c_{n}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+Ch^{2}\|\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}\big)-2\langle\partial_{t}c_{n},(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c_{n}\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}.

Using duality, Young’s inequality, and the elliptic interpolation estimate
\|c_{n}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|c_{n}\|^{2}
, we infer

\big|\langle\partial_{t}c_{n},(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c_{n}\rangle_{H^{-2},H^{2}}\big|\leq\epsilon\|\partial_{t}c_{n}\|_{H^{-2}}^{2}+\epsilon\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}^{2}\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}+C_{\epsilon}\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{W^{2,\infty}}^{2}\|c_{n}\|^{2}.

Here and below,
\epsilon>0
denotes a generic small constant, while
\varepsilon>0
is reserved for small parameters in the final combined estimates.

Convective term.

Using Hölder’s inequality with exponents
(2,3,6)
and the embeddings
\nabla c_{n}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and
\mu_{n}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{3}(\Omega)

|(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\mu_{n})|\leq C_{\epsilon}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\,\|\nabla c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\epsilon\|\mu_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}.

Using the definition of
\mu_{n}
from (
3.6
) and elliptic interpolation, this yields

|(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\mu_{n})|\leq C(\|\mathbf{u}\|,\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{L^{\infty}})(\|c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{6})+\epsilon\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|^{2}+\epsilon\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}.

Dissipative term.

The remaining term satisfies

\frac{1}{Pe}(\nabla\mu_{n},\nabla\mu_{n})=\frac{1}{Pe}\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|^{2}.

Combined estimate.

Combining the above bounds and using (
3.8
), the term
\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|^{2}
can be absorbed, yielding

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\big(4\|c_{n}^{2}\|^{2}+Ch^{2}\|\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}\big)+C\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle~~~~\leq\varepsilon\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}+C(\|\mathbf{u}\|,\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{W^{2,\infty}})(\|c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{6}).

(3.9)

We now test the weak formulation with
c_{n}
to obtain a complementary estimate.

Multiplying by
c_{n}
.

Testing (
3.5
) with
c_{n}\in V_{n}
and integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|c_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{Ch^{2}}{Pe}\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},c_{n})-\frac{1}{Pe}(\nabla(8c_{n}^{3}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c_{n}),\nabla c_{n}).

(3.10)

Expanding the nonlinear gradient gives

\nabla(8c_{n}^{3}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c_{n})=24c_{n}^{2}\nabla c_{n}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))\nabla c_{n}+2(\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta}))\,c_{n}.

Substitution yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|c_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{Ch^{2}}{Pe}\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{24}{Pe}\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{2}{Pe}(\beta(\hat{\Theta})\nabla c_{n},\nabla c_{n})=\frac{2}{Pe}\|\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}+I_{1}+I_{2},

(3.11)

where

I_{1}:=-(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\,c_{n}),\qquad I_{2}:=-\frac{2}{Pe}(\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})\,c_{n},\,\nabla c_{n}).

The term
(\beta(\hat{\Theta})\nabla c_{n},\nabla c_{n})
on the left-hand side is nonnegative. This follows from the nonnegativity of the prescribed temperature
\Theta
(established in Proposition
3.3
), a property preserved by extension by zero and convolution with a nonnegative mollifier, and hence
\hat{\Theta}\geq 0
.

Estimates for
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
.

Applying Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities to the terms
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
defined above, we obtain

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|\leq\epsilon\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}+C_{\epsilon}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2},

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{Pe}\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|\,\|\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})\|\leq\epsilon\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}+C_{\epsilon}\|\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})\|^{2}.

Combining these bounds with (
3.11
), dropping the nonnegative term
(\beta(\hat{\Theta})\nabla c_{n},\nabla c_{n})
from the left-hand side, and choosing
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
sufficiently small, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|c_{n}\|^{2}+\frac{Ch^{2}}{Pe}\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}+C_{3}\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}\leq C(\|\mathbf{u}\|,\|\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})\|)+\frac{2}{Pe}\|\nabla c_{n}\|^{2},

(3.12)

where
C_{3}>0
absorbs the small parameters
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
.

Together with the estimates derived previously, this inequality yields uniform bounds on
c_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
, independent of
n
, which form the basis for the compactness argument.

Step 2: Uniform bounds.

Using (
3.2
) and (
3.12
), and applying standard absorption arguments together with Young’s inequality, we obtain the uniform energy inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}E_{n}(t)+C_{1}\big(\|\nabla\mu_{n}\|^{2}+\|\Delta c_{n}\|^{2}+\|c_{n}\nabla c_{n}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle~~~~\leq C_{2}(\|\mathbf{u}\|,\|\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})\|)+C_{3}(\|\mathbf{u}\|,\|\hat{\Theta}\|)\big(\|c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|c_{n}\|_{H^{1}}^{6}\big),

(3.13)

where

E_{n}(t):=4\|c_{n}^{2}(t)\|^{2}+Ch^{2}\|\nabla c_{n}(t)\|^{2}+\|c_{n}(t)\|^{2}.

Since
c_{n}(0)=P_{n}c_{0}
, with
P_{n}
denoting the
L^{2}
-projection onto
V_{n}
, the projection property together with the embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
implies that
E_{n}(0)
is uniformly bounded in
n
, with a bound depending only on
\|c_{0}\|_{H^{2}}
.

The coefficients in (
3.2
) depend on
\mathbf{u}
and
\Theta
only through their norms. By assumption,

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{X_{u}(T)}+\|\Theta\|_{X_{\Theta}(T)}\leq R.

Since the regularization map
\Theta\mapsto\hat{\Theta}
is linear and bounded from
H^{1}
to
W^{2,\infty}
, and
\beta
is smooth, there exists
C_{\delta}>0
(depending only on
\delta
) such that

\|\hat{\Theta}\|_{W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})\|\leq C_{\delta}R.

Applying a nonlinear Grönwall inequality to (
3.2
) then yields a uniform existence time
T_{CH}>0
depending only on
R
,
\delta
, and
c_{0}
.

Integrating (
3.2
) over
(0,T_{CH})
and using the uniform bound on the right-hand side gives

\nabla\mu_{n}\in L^{2}(0,T_{CH};L^{2}(\Omega))\quad\text{uniformly in }n.

Moreover, from the constitutive relation (
3.6
) and the embeddings
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

\mu_{n}\in L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{1}(\Omega))\quad\text{uniformly in }n.

Combining
\Delta c_{n}\in L^{2}(L^{2})
with
c_{n}\in L^{\infty}(H^{1})
and invoking elliptic regularity for the Neumann Laplacian, we obtain

\|c_{n}\|_{H^{2}}\leq C(\|\Delta c_{n}\|+\|c_{n}\|),\quad\text{and hence}\quad c_{n}\in L^{2}(H^{2}).

Together with the uniform energy estimates obtained above, this yields

c_{n}\in L^{\infty}(H^{1})\cap L^{2}(H^{2}),\quad\mu_{n}\in L^{2}(H^{1}),\quad\nabla\mu_{n}\in L^{2}(L^{2}),\quad\Delta c_{n}\in L^{2}(L^{2}),\quad\partial_{t}c_{n}\in L^{2}(H^{-2}),

which provides the full set of uniform bounds required for the compactness argument.

Step 3: Compactness and passage to the limit.

Using the uniform bounds established above, the Aubin–Lions lemma yields the
existence of a subsequence (not relabeled) and a limit function
c
such that

c_{n}\to c\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(H^{s}),\quad\forall\,s\in[0,2).

By weak compactness, we further obtain

c_{n}\rightharpoonup c\text{ in }L^{2}(H^{2}),\qquad\partial_{t}c_{n}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}c\text{ in }L^{2}(H^{-2}),\qquad\nabla\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\nabla\mu\text{ in }L^{2}(L^{2}),

where the limiting chemical potential satisfies

\mu=8c^{3}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c-Ch^{2}\Delta c.

Passage to the limit.

We now verify convergence of each term in the weak formulation of
Definition
3.1
.

(
a
) The time-derivative and diffusive terms pass to the limit
directly by weak convergence.

(
b
) For the convective term, testing against

\varphi\in L^{\infty}(H^{1})\cap L^{2}(H^{2})
yields

|(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla(c_{n}-c),\varphi)|\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\nabla(c_{n}-c)\|_{L^{3}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{6}}.

Integrating in time and using the strong convergence

c_{n}\to c
in
L^{2}(H^{s})
for
s<2
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}|(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla(c_{n}-c),\varphi)|\,dt\leq C\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\|\nabla(c_{n}-c)\|_{L^{2}(L^{3})}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(H^{1})}\to 0.

Hence,

(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c_{n},\varphi)\to(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla c,\varphi).

(
c
) For the cubic nonlinearity in
\mu
, we write

c_{n}^{3}-c^{3}=(c_{n}-c)(c_{n}^{2}+c_{n}c+c^{2}).

Testing against
\psi\in L^{\infty}(H^{1})\cap L^{2}(H^{2})
and using the Sobolev embedding

H^{3/2+\varepsilon}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
gives

|(c_{n}^{3}-c^{3},\psi)|\leq C\|c_{n}-c\|_{H^{3/2+\varepsilon}}\|c_{n}^{2}+c_{n}c+c^{2}\|\,\|\psi\|.

Integrating in time and applying Hölder’s inequality yields

\int_{0}^{T}|(c_{n}^{3}-c^{3},\psi)|\,dt\leq C\|c_{n}-c\|_{L^{2}(H^{3/2+\varepsilon})}\|c_{n}^{2}+c_{n}c+c^{2}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(H^{1})}.

The first factor converges to zero by strong convergence, while the remaining
terms are uniformly bounded. Consequently,

(8c_{n}^{3},\psi)\to(8c^{3},\psi).

Combining the above limits shows that the pair
(c,\mu)
satisfies the weak
formulation of the Cahn–Hilliard system in the sense of
Definition
3.1
.

Step 4: Higher regularity.

Having identified the weak solution
(c,\mu)
, we return to the constitutive
relation

\mu=8c^{3}-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))c-Ch^{2}\Delta c.

Each term on the right-hand side belongs to
L^{2}(L^{2})
, implying

\mu\in L^{2}(L^{2})
and
\nabla\mu\in L^{2}(L^{2})
.
Differentiating the above identity yields

\nabla\mu=24c^{2}\nabla c-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}))\nabla c+2c\,\nabla\beta(\hat{\Theta})-Ch^{2}\nabla\Delta c.

All terms except the last belong to
L^{2}(L^{2})
, which implies

\nabla\Delta c\in L^{2}(L^{2})
.
Elliptic regularity for the Neumann Laplacian then yields

c\in L^{2}(H^{3})
.

This additional
H^{3}
-regularity ensures compactness of the solution operator

\mathcal{C}:(\mathbf{u},\Theta)\mapsto c(\mathbf{u},\Theta)
.

\Box

, which is essential for the fixed-point argument.

Let

(c_{1},\mu_{1})

Then there exists a constant

\displaystyle\|c_{1}-c_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{CH};H^{1})}^{2}+\|c_{1}-c_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{2})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}(c_{1}-c_{2})\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{-2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\Big(\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{s})}^{2}+\|\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};L^{2})}^{2}\Big).

Proof.

Let
(c_{1},\mu_{1})
and
(c_{2},\mu_{2})
be two weak solutions corresponding to

(\mathbf{u}_{1},\Theta_{1})
and
(\mathbf{u}_{2},\Theta_{2})

Define

\widetilde{c}:=c_{1}-c_{2},\qquad\widetilde{\mu}:=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\qquad\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}:=\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}.

Then
\widetilde{c}
satisfies

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}

\displaystyle=8(c_{1}^{3}-c_{2}^{3})-2\big[(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{1}))c_{1}-(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{2}))c_{2}\big]-Ch^{2}\Delta\widetilde{c}

\displaystyle=8(c_{1}^{3}-c_{2}^{3})-2(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{1}))\widetilde{c}+2(\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{1})-\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{2}))c_{2}-Ch^{2}\Delta\widetilde{c}.

Step 1: Auxiliary bounds.

We first collect estimates for the cubic nonlinearity that will be used

Using the factorization

c_{1}^{3}-c_{2}^{3}=\widetilde{c}\,P(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle\leq C(\|c_{1}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|c_{2}\|_{H^{2}}^{2})\|\widetilde{c}\|_{H^{1}},

\displaystyle\|c_{1}^{3}-c_{2}^{3}\|_{H^{1}}

Step 3: Testing by
\widetilde{\mu}

Testing (

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\widetilde{c},\widetilde{\mu}\rangle

Step 4: Testing by
\widetilde{c}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{Pe}(\nabla[(1-\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{1}))\widetilde{c}],\nabla\widetilde{c})+\frac{2}{Pe}(\nabla[(\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{1})-\beta(\hat{\Theta}_{2}))c_{2}],\nabla\widetilde{c}),

\hat{\Theta}_{i}
with
\delta

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\widetilde{c}\|^{2}+C_{2}\|\Delta\widetilde{c}\|^{2}\leq C_{3}\|\widetilde{c}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+C_{4}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{H^{s}}^{2}.

(3.22)

Step 5: Application of Grönwall’s lemma.

Combining the estimates yields

\frac{d}{dt}\|\widetilde{c}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}_{1}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{2}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\|\widetilde{c}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+C\|\Theta_{1}(t)-\Theta_{2}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T_{CH}).

Applying Grönwall’s lemma gives

\|\widetilde{c}(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq Ce^{Ct}\int_{0}^{T_{CH}}\big(\|\mathbf{u}_{1}(\tau)-\mathbf{u}_{2}(\tau)\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\Theta_{1}(\tau)-\Theta_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big)\,d\tau,

which establishes Lipschitz continuous dependence of
c
on
(\mathbf{u},\Theta)
.
In particular, uniqueness follows by setting
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=0
and
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{2}
.

Integrating the above estimates over
(0,T_{CH})
and using elliptic
regularity yields

\|\widetilde{c}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{2})}^{2}+\|\partial_{t}\widetilde{c}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{-2})}^{2}\leq C\Big(\|\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};H^{s}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T_{CH};L^{2})}^{2}\Big).

\Box

3.3
The Stokes Operator

We now analyze the Stokes subsystem for prescribed concentration
c
from the Cahn–Hilliard analysis. We establish existence and regularity of weak solutions and prove continuity of the solution operator

\mathcal{S}:c\mapsto\mathbf{u}.

We recall the assumptions on the viscosity
\eta(c)
and density
\rho(c)
, which will be used throughout this subsection:

1.

\eta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\eta\geq\eta_{\min}>0
.
In particular, the expression (
2.18
) is valid for
c\in[-0.5,0.5]
,
and
\eta
is smoothly extended to
\mathbb{R}
so that the extension remains
strictly positive.

2.

\rho\in C(\mathbb{R})
and
0<\rho\leq\rho_{\max}
.
Similarly, the expression (
2.17
) is valid for
c\in[-0.5,0.5]
,
and
\rho
is continuously extended to
\mathbb{R}
.

Existence of weak solutions.

For the functional setting, we introduce the spaces

V_{D}:=\{\mathbf{v}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}:\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{v}=0,\ \mathbf{v}|_{\Gamma_{D}}=0\},\qquad L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega):=\{\mathbf{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}:\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\ \text{weakly}\},

which incorporate the Dirichlet boundary conditions and the divergence-free constraint.
We equip
V_{D}
with the norm

\|\mathbf{v}\|_{V_{D}}:=\|\nabla\mathbf{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which is equivalent to the
H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
norm by Korn’s and Poincaré’s inequalities.

Remark 4
.

The proof establishes existence and uniqueness in
L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D})
via Galerkin approximation. Since
V_{D}\subset H^{1}\hookrightarrow H^{s}
, the solution automatically satisfies the required
H^{s}
regularity. Continuity in the
H^{s}
topology is established in Proposition
3.8
via interpolation.

Proposition 3.7

(Existence and regularity of weak solutions to the Stokes subproblem)
.

Let
R>0
and let
c\in X_{c}(T)
be a prescribed concentration field for some

T>0
, where
X_{c}(T)
is defined in (
3.1
).
Assume that the viscosity
\eta
and density
\rho
satisfy the hypotheses stated
at the beginning of Section
3.3
, and let

\mathbf{u}_{0}\in L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)
be given.
Then there exists a time
T_{S}>0
, depending only on

\eta_{\min},\ \rho_{\max},\ \|\mathbf{G}\|,\ \|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\ \text{and }R
,
such that the Stokes system (
2.38
)–(
2.39
), subject to the
boundary conditions (
2.46
)–(
2.47
) and the initial condition

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}
, admits a unique weak solution on
(0,T_{S})
satisfying

\mathbf{u}\in L^{\infty}(0,T_{S};L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D}),\qquad\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D}^{\prime}),

and

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{S};L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq R.

Moreover, since
V_{D}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\hookrightarrow H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}
for
s\in(1/2,1)
, the solution satisfies
\mathbf{u}\in L^{2}(0,T_{S};H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
.

Proof.

Step 1: Galerkin approximation.

Let
V_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{\bm{\varphi}_{1},\dots,\bm{\varphi}_{n}\}\subset V_{D}
, where
\{\bm{\varphi}_{k}\}
forms an
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthonormal basis of
V_{D}
.
We seek an approximate solution

\mathbf{u}_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(t)\,\bm{\varphi}_{j}(x)

satisfying the weak formulation

\alpha(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{n},\bm{\varphi}_{j})+\big(2\eta(c)\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{n}),\mathcal{E}(\bm{\varphi}_{j})\big)=(\rho(c)\mathbf{G},\bm{\varphi}_{j}),\quad\text{for all }\bm{\varphi}_{j}\in V_{n},

(3.23)

with initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{n}(0)=P_{n}\mathbf{u}_{0}
, where
P_{n}
denotes the
L^{2}
-projection onto
V_{n}
.
Standard ODE theory yields a local solution
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\in C^{1}(0,t^{*}(n))
for some
t^{*}(n)>0
.

Step 2: Energy estimates.

Testing (
3.23
) with
\mathbf{u}_{n}
and using the uniform positivity
of
\eta(c)
, the boundedness of
\rho(c)
, and Korn’s inequality, we obtain

\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|^{2}+\eta_{\min}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\|^{2}\leq C\,\rho_{\max}\,\|\mathbf{G}\|\,\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|.

Applying Young’s inequality and Grönwall’s lemma yields a time
T_{S}>0
such that

\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq R\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T_{S}),

uniformly in
n
, and the uniform bound
\mathbf{u}_{n}\in L^{\infty}(0,T_{S};L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D})
.

To estimate the time derivative, we test (
3.23
) with an arbitrary

\bm{\varphi}\in V_{D}
and obtain

\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{V_{D}^{\prime}}\leq C\big(\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{H^{1}}+\|\mathbf{G}\|\big),

which shows that
\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D}^{\prime})
.

Step 3: Weak convergence and uniqueness.

From the uniform bounds, we extract a subsequence such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{u}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}(0,T_{S};L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{2}(0,T_{S};V_{D}^{\prime}).

By linearity of (
3.23
) and continuity of the coefficients
\eta(c)
and
\rho(c)
, we pass to the limit in each term. The limit
\mathbf{u}
therefore satisfies the Stokes subproblem in the weak sense. Uniqueness follows from linearity and coercivity of the Stokes operator.

\Box

Having established existence and regularity, we now prove continuity of the solution operator with respect to the concentration field in the
H^{s}
topology.

Proposition 3.8

(Continuity of the Stokes solution map)
.

Let
T>0
be such that
T\leq T_{S}
, where
T_{S}
is the time of existence obtained in Proposition
3.7
. Let
c_{n}\to c
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
, and let
\mathbf{u}_{n}=\mathcal{S}(c_{n})
denote the corresponding weak solutions of the Stokes subsystem. Then for any
s\in(1/2,1)
,

\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathcal{S}(c)\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}).

Proof.

Step 1: Compactness.

Let
c_{n}\to c
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
, and set
\mathbf{u}_{n}=\mathcal{S}(c_{n})
.
From the uniform estimates established in Proposition
3.7
, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
n
, such that

\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{D}^{\prime})}+\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{D})}\leq C.

Applying the Aubin–Lions lemma to the compact embedding

V_{D}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow V_{D}^{\prime},

we obtain the existence of a subsequence, not relabeled, such that

\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega)).

Step 2: Identification of the limit.

Since
c_{n}\to c
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
and the functions
\eta
and
\rho
are continuous, we have

\eta(c_{n})\to\eta(c),\qquad\rho(c_{n})\to\rho(c)\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

Moreover,

\nabla\mathbf{u}_{n}\rightharpoonup\nabla\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

The strong convergence of
\eta(c_{n})
in
L^{2}
combined with the weak convergence of
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{n})
in
L^{2}
implies that
\eta(c_{n})\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}_{n})
converges weakly in
L^{1}
.
This allows passage to the limit in the weak formulation, yielding

\alpha(\partial_{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\varphi)+(2\eta(c)\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}),\mathcal{E}(\varphi))=(\rho(c)\mathbf{G},\varphi),\qquad\forall\,\varphi\in V_{D}.

Hence,
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=\mathcal{S}(c)
.

Step 3: Lifting to
H^{s}
via interpolation.

To show
\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{u}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
for
s\in(1/2,1)
, we use interpolation. By the Gagliardo–Nirenberg interpolation inequality, for
s\in(0,1)
,

\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)-\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)-\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{1-s}\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)-\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{s}.

Using the uniform bound
\|\mathbf{u}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{D})}\leq C
from Proposition
3.7
, we have for almost all
t

\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)-\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}_{n}(t)-\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{1-s}.

Squaring and integrating over
(0,T)
, then applying Hölder’s inequality, gives

\|\mathbf{u}_{n}-\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s})}^{2}\leq C^{2}T^{s}\|\mathbf{u}_{n}-\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})}^{2(1-s)}.

Since
\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{u}
in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\sigma})
by Step 1, the right-hand side goes to zero, proving that
\mathcal{S}(c_{n})\to\mathcal{S}(c)
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d})
.

\Box

3.4
The Fixed-Point Argument

We now establish existence of weak solutions to the coupled system by
applying the Schauder Fixed-Point Theorem to the composite solution operator

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u}):=\mathcal{S}\big(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u},\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}))\big).

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
R>0
be chosen sufficiently large so that

R\geq\max\{\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\|\Theta_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\},

and define

T=\min\{T_{\Theta}(R),T_{CH}(R),T_{S}(R)\},

where
T_{\Theta}(R)
,
T_{CH}(R)
, and
T_{S}(R)
are the uniform existence
times from Propositions
3.2
,
3.5
, and

3.7
. We work in the space
X_{u}(T)
defined in
(
3.2
), and consider the closed convex set

K:=\{\mathbf{u}\in X_{u}(T):\|\mathbf{u}\|_{X_{u}(T)}\leq R\}.

Self-map property.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in K
and set
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u})
.
By Proposition
3.7
, we have

\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{\sigma}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;V_{D}),\quad\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in L^{2}(0,T;V_{D}^{\prime}),

with bounds independent of
\mathbf{u}
. Using the embedding

V_{D}\hookrightarrow H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}
, we obtain

\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s})}^{2}\leq C\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{D})}^{2}\leq C\big(\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{G}\|^{2}\big)T.

Choosing
T
sufficiently small depending on
R
yields

\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{X_{u}(T)}\leq R
,
and hence
\mathcal{T}(K)\subset K
.

Continuity.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{u}
in
X_{u}(T)
. Then, by Proposition

3.4
,

\Theta_{n}:=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}_{n})\to\Theta:=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u})\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

By Proposition
3.6
,

c_{n}:=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u}_{n},\Theta_{n})\to c:=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u},\Theta)\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)).

Finally, by Proposition
3.8
,

\mathbf{u}_{n}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{S}(c_{n})\to\mathbf{u}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{S}(c)\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}).

Therefore
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u}_{n})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u})
in
X_{u}(T)
,
establishing continuity of
\mathcal{T}
.

Compactness.

Let
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u})
with
\mathbf{u}\in K
. Then

\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{D})}\leq C,\quad\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;V_{D}^{\prime})}\leq C.

By the Aubin–Lions theorem and the compact embedding

V_{D}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow H^{s}(\Omega)^{d}\hookrightarrow V_{D}^{\prime},

it follows that
\mathcal{T}(K)
is relatively compact in
X_{u}(T)
.

By the self-map property, continuity, and compactness established above,
the Schauder Fixed-Point Theorem yields
\mathbf{u}^{*}\in K
such that

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{u}^{*})=\mathbf{u}^{*}
.
Setting
\Theta^{*}=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{u}^{*})
and

c^{*}=\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{u}^{*},\Theta^{*})
, the triplet

(\mathbf{u}^{*},c^{*},\Theta^{*})
is a weak solution of the coupled system
on
(0,T)
with the regularity established in Propositions
3.2
,

3.5
, and
3.7
.

\Box

With the analytical results established for the regularized formulation of the system, we now turn to its numerical approximation. The existence theory above relies on the introduction of auxiliary regularizations, in particular the parameter
\alpha>0
in the Stokes equation and a spatial mollification of the temperature field, which are used to ensure well-posedness of the coupled problem.

In the numerical experiments presented below, we consider a related formulation in which
\alpha=0
and the temperature field is used directly without spatial mollification. The numerical scheme is described in detail in the following section.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section we describe the numerical approximation of the
temperature-coupled Cahn–Hilliard–Stokes–heat system
(
2.36
)–(
2.40
) and present several numerical experiments.
The simulations are performed for the quasi-static model obtained by setting
\alpha=0
in the Stokes equation (
2.38
), consistently with the low-Reynolds-number regime considered in the modelling section.

Spatial discretization is based on conforming finite elements, while time integration uses a first-order semi-implicit splitting: backward Euler for the heat equation, a quasi-static Stokes solve for the velocity–pressure pair, and an Eyre-type stabilized convex-splitting discretization for the Cahn–Hilliard equation. The dimensionless parameters are the mass Péclet
number
Pe
, the thermal Péclet number
Pe_{\Theta}
, and the Cahn number
Ch
. All computations are performed in
FreeFEM++
.

For the numerical part we retain the notation
\Theta
for the
dimensionless temperature field, and
\beta(\Theta)
is evaluated according to
the relation introduced in Section
2.1
.

4.1
Spatial discretization

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a shape-regular triangulation of
\Omega
with mesh size

h
. We define the scalar finite element space

Q_{h}:=\bigl\{q_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\;:\;q_{h}|_{K}\in P_{1}(K),\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\bigr\},

and the associated zero-mean pressure space

Q_{h}^{0}:=\left\{q_{h}\in Q_{h}\;:\;\int_{\Omega}q_{h}\,dx=0\right\}.

For the velocity we use the Taylor–Hood space

V_{h}:=\bigl\{\mathbf{v}_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\;:\;\mathbf{v}_{h}|_{K}\in P_{2}(K)^{d},\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},\ \mathbf{v}_{h}=\mathbf{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\bigr\}.

The pair
(V_{h},Q_{h}^{0})
satisfies the discrete inf–sup condition, and is
therefore suitable for the Stokes subproblem.

The scalar unknowns
c
,
\mu
, and
\Theta
are approximated in
Q_{h}
,
while the pressure is approximated in
Q_{h}^{0}
. The Dirichlet boundary
conditions for the velocity on
\Gamma_{D}
and for the temperature on

\Gamma_{\Theta}
are imposed strongly.

For the thermal problem we also introduce

Q_{h,\Theta_{D}}:=\bigl\{\theta_{h}\in Q_{h}\;:\;\theta_{h}=\Theta_{D}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\Theta}\bigr\},\qquad Q_{h,0}^{\Theta}:=\bigl\{\omega_{h}\in Q_{h}\;:\;\omega_{h}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\Theta}\bigr\}.

4.2
Time discretization and coupling strategy

Let

0=t^{0}<t^{1}<\cdots<t^{M}=T_{\mathrm{fin}},\qquad\Delta t=t^{m+1}-t^{m},

be a uniform partition of the time interval.
Given the discrete solution
(c^{m},\Theta^{m},\mathbf{u}^{m})
at time
t^{m}
,
one time step
t^{m}\mapsto t^{m+1}
consists of the following substeps:

1.

solve the heat equation for
\Theta^{m+1}
,

2.

update the coefficient fields
\rho(c^{m})
and
\eta(c^{m})
, and solve
the quasi-static Stokes problem for
(\mathbf{u}^{m+1},p^{m+1})
,

3.

solve the Cahn–Hilliard system for
(c^{m+1},\mu^{m+1})
.

We describe the three substeps in weak form.

4.2.1
Heat equation

The heat equation (
2.40
) is discretized by backward Euler in time.
Given
\Theta^{m}\in Q_{h,\Theta_{D}}
and
\mathbf{u}^{m}\in V_{h}
, we seek

\Theta^{m+1}\in Q_{h,\Theta_{D}}
such that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\Theta^{m+1}-\Theta^{m}}{\Delta t}\,\omega_{h}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u}^{m}\cdot\nabla\Theta^{m+1})\,\omega_{h}\,dx+\frac{1}{Pe_{\Theta}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Theta^{m+1}\cdot\nabla\omega_{h}\,dx=0

(4.1)

for all
\omega_{h}\in Q_{h,0}^{\Theta}
.
The homogeneous Neumann condition on
\Gamma_{\Theta_{N}}
is incorporated
naturally by the weak formulation.

Once
\Theta^{m+1}
is computed, the temperature-dependent coefficient

\beta^{m+1}(x):=\beta\bigl(\Theta^{m+1}(x)\bigr)

is evaluated pointwise and used in the Cahn–Hilliard step.

4.2.2
Stokes subproblem

Given
c^{m}\in Q_{h}
, we update the density and viscosity fields using the
constitutive laws introduced in (
2.17
)–(
2.18
):

\rho^{m}(x):=\rho\bigl(c^{m}(x)\bigr),\qquad\eta^{m}(x):=\eta\bigl(c^{m}(x)\bigr).

We then solve the quasi-static Stokes problem associated with
(
2.38
)–(
2.39
). We seek

(\mathbf{u}^{m+1},p^{m+1})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}^{0}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}2\eta^{m}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u}^{m+1}):\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{v}_{h})\,dx-\int_{\Omega}p^{m+1}\,\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}G\,\rho^{m}\,\hat{\bm{g}}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{h}\,dx,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}q_{h}\,\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\,dx

\displaystyle=0,

(4.3)

for all
(\mathbf{v}_{h},q_{h})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}^{0}
.
The traction-free boundary condition on
\Gamma_{N}
is encoded naturally in the
weak formulation, while the no-slip condition on
\Gamma_{D}
is imposed
strongly.

At each time step this is a linear saddle-point problem for

(\mathbf{u}^{m+1},p^{m+1})
.

4.2.3
Cahn–Hilliard step

The discrete Cahn–Hilliard step is based on the temperature-dependent free
energy

f(c,\Theta)=2c^{4}-(1-\beta(\Theta))c^{2},\qquad f_{c}(c,\Theta)=8c^{3}-2(1-\beta(\Theta))c.

In order to obtain a convex–concave splitting valid uniformly over the
temperature range considered in the simulations, we introduce a stabilization
parameter
A\geq 0
such that

A+1-\beta(\Theta)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all admissible temperatures }\Theta.

A sufficient choice is, for example,

A\geq\max\{0,\beta_{\max}-1\},

where
\beta_{\max}
is an a priori upper bound for
\beta(\Theta)
in the
simulation.

We then split the bulk energy as

f(c,\Theta)=f^{+}(c)+f^{-}(c,\Theta),

with

f^{+}(c)=2c^{4}+Ac^{2},\qquad f^{-}(c,\Theta)=-(A+1-\beta(\Theta))c^{2}.

Hence

(f^{+})^{\prime}(c)=8c^{3}+2Ac,\qquad(f^{-})_{c}(c,\Theta)=-2(A+1-\beta(\Theta))c.

The convex part
f^{+}
is treated implicitly, while the concave part
f^{-}
is
treated explicitly in
c
and with the already computed temperature

\Theta^{m+1}
.

Given
c^{m}\in Q_{h}
,
\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\in V_{h}
, and

\Theta^{m+1}\in Q_{h,\Theta_{D}}
, we seek

(c^{m+1},\mu^{m+1})\in Q_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{c^{m+1}-c^{m}}{\Delta t}\,\psi_{h}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\cdot\nabla c^{m+1})\,\psi_{h}\,dx+\frac{1}{Pe}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu^{m+1}\cdot\nabla\psi_{h}\,dx

\displaystyle=0,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu^{m+1}\,\phi_{h}\,dx-Ch^{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla c^{m+1}\cdot\nabla\phi_{h}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\bigl(8(c^{m+1})^{3}+2A\,c^{m+1}\bigr)\phi_{h}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}2\bigl(A+1-\beta^{m+1}\bigr)c^{m}\,\phi_{h}\,dx

\displaystyle=0,

(4.5)

for all
\psi_{h},\phi_{h}\in Q_{h}
.
The homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for
c
and
\mu
are treated
naturally in the weak formulation.

Equivalently, the discrete chemical potential is given by

\mu^{m+1}=8(c^{m+1})^{3}+2A\,c^{m+1}-2\bigl(A+1-\beta^{m+1}\bigr)c^{m}-Ch^{2}\Delta c^{m+1}.

At each time level (
4.4
)–(
4.5
) define a
nonlinear algebraic system for
(c^{m+1},\mu^{m+1})
, which we solve by a damped Newton iteration.

Discrete structural properties

The above discretization retains the key structural properties of the
Cahn–Hilliard subproblem.

1.

Mass conservation under no-through-flow.

Assume that the discrete velocity satisfies

\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
Choosing
\psi_{h}\equiv 1
in (
4.4
) gives

\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{\Omega}(c^{m+1}-c^{m})\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\cdot\nabla c^{m+1}\,dx=0.

By integration by parts,

\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\cdot\nabla c^{m+1}\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}c^{m+1}\,\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}c^{m+1}\,\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds.

The boundary term vanishes by the no-through-flow condition. Let

\bar{c}^{m+1}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}c^{m+1}\,dx,\qquad q_{h}:=c^{m+1}-\bar{c}^{m+1}\in Q_{h}^{0}.

Using (
4.3
) with this choice of
q_{h}
and

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds=0
,
we obtain

\int_{\Omega}c^{m+1}\,\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\,dx=0.

Hence

\int_{\Omega}c^{m+1}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}c^{m}\,dx.

In particular, this holds in the purely diffusive tests where

\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\equiv 0
.

2.

Energy decay for the isothermal diffusive Cahn–Hilliard substep.

In the purely diffusive, isothermal case

\mathbf{u}^{m+1}\equiv 0
and
\Theta^{m+1}\equiv\bar{\Theta}
, the above
stabilized Eyre splitting yields the standard discrete dissipation of the
Cahn–Hilliard free energy

\mathcal{E}_{h}(c):=\int_{\Omega}\left(2c^{4}-(1-\beta(\bar{\Theta}))c^{2}+\frac{Ch^{2}}{2}|\nabla c|^{2}\right)\,dx,

namely

\mathcal{E}_{h}(c^{m+1})\leq\mathcal{E}_{h}(c^{m})\qquad\text{for all }\Delta t>0,

see, for example,
[
24
]
.

For the fully coupled non-isothermal simulations considered below, we use the
scheme as a consistent first-order semi-implicit discretization of the model.
We do not claim a global discrete energy law for the fully coupled algorithm, since advection and the time-dependent coefficient
\beta(\Theta)
introduce additional work terms.

4.3
Fully coupled time-stepping algorithm

Collecting the previous substeps, one time step

t^{m}\mapsto t^{m+1}
is performed as follows:

1.

Heat update:

given
(\Theta^{m},\mathbf{u}^{m})
, compute

\Theta^{m+1}\in Q_{h,\Theta_{D}}
from (
4.1
);

2.

Coefficient update:

set

\rho^{m}(x)=\rho\bigl(c^{m}(x)\bigr),\qquad\eta^{m}(x)=\eta\bigl(c^{m}(x)\bigr);

3.

Stokes update:

given
\rho^{m}
and
\eta^{m}
, compute

(\mathbf{u}^{m+1},p^{m+1})\in V_{h}\times Q_{h}^{0}
from
(
4.2
)–(
4.3
);

4.

Cahn–Hilliard update:

given
(c^{m},\mathbf{u}^{m+1},\Theta^{m+1})
, compute

(c^{m+1},\mu^{m+1})\in Q_{h}\times Q_{h}
from
(
4.4
)–(
4.5
).

The resulting splitting is formally first-order accurate in time. The heat and Stokes
subproblems are linear at each time level, while the Cahn–Hilliard update is
nonlinear and is solved by damped Newton iteration. In the experiments below
this strategy proved robust across the parameter regimes considered.

We now turn to the numerical experiments.

4.5

In this experiment we suppress advection by setting
\mathbf{u}\equiv 0
and do
not solve the heat equation. The evolution is therefore governed solely by the
Cahn–Hilliard dynamics with a prescribed constant temperature. This allows us
to isolate the effect of the temperature-dependent bulk potential on phase
separation.

The computational domain is

\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1],

discretized by a structured
100\times 100
mesh. We impose homogeneous
no-flux boundary conditions for both
c
and
\mu
on

\partial\Omega
. The initial phase field
c_{0}(\mathbf{x})
is taken as a
small random perturbation of a constant mean corresponding to an approximate

70{:}30
phase proportion.

We consider two constant temperatures relative to the transition value

\Theta_{S}
appearing in the bulk potential:

\Theta=1.5\,\Theta_{S}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Theta=0.2\,\Theta_{S}.

The corresponding dimensionless parameters are

Pe=1000,\qquad Ch=0.01,\qquad\Delta t=0.01,

see Table
4
. Representative snapshots are shown in
Figures
5
and
6
, while Figure

highlights the

as seen in Figure

\Theta<\Theta_{S}

intended transition between homogeneous mixing and phase separation encoded in
the temperature-dependent Landau potential.

4.6
Experiment 3: Gravity-driven phase separation with variable density and viscosity

The third experiment activates the coupling between the Cahn–Hilliard equation
and the quasi-static Stokes system in the presence of gravity. The goal is to
illustrate the effect of buoyancy when the two phases have different densities
and viscosities.

The computational domain is again

\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1],

discretized by a structured
100\times 100
mesh. We impose homogeneous
no-flux boundary conditions for the phase field,

\nabla c\cdot\mathbf{n}=0,\qquad\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

For the velocity we prescribe

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{1},

\displaystyle\bigl[-p\mathbf{I}+2\eta\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})\bigr]\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4},

where
\Gamma_{1}
denotes the bottom boundary and the remaining sides are
labelled counter-clockwise. Thus the fluid domain has one rigid no-slip
boundary
\Gamma_{1}
and three traction-free boundaries

\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4}
.

The initial phase field
c_{0}(\mathbf{x})
is a random perturbation of a mean
state corresponding to an approximate
70{:}30
phase proportion, and the
initial velocity is
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. The temperature is prescribed as
a spatially uniform constant,

\Theta(x,t)\equiv\Theta_{0}=0.3\,\Theta_{S}\qquad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,2],

so that
\beta(\Theta)
is fixed and the heat equation is not solved in this
test. Consistently with the affine coefficient laws
\rho(c)
and
\eta(c)
used in the numerical scheme, the density and viscosity contrasts are kept fixed
throughout the run. We take

\lambda_{\rho}=0.0009,\qquad\lambda_{\eta}=0.08,

Pe=1000,\qquad Ch=0.01,\qquad\Delta t=0.01.

All parameters are summarized in Table

\Omega=[0,2]\times[0,1],

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{1},
