Enzymatic activity-independent NANS stabilizes LATS2 to drive growth and therapeutic resistance in HR+/HER2- breast cancer
Authors: Jia-Yang Cai, Min-Ying Huang, Shao-Ying Yang, Fang-Lin Zhang, Yin-Ling Zhang, Lisa Andriani, Qian Zhao, A-Yong Cao, Da-Qiang Li, Zhi-Ming Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hormone receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HR+ /HER2-) breast cancer is the most common subtype in females, frequently challenged by resistance to endocrine therapy and CDK4/6 inhibitors. Here we show that N-acetylneuraminate synthase (NANS) is significantly upregulated in female HR+ /HER2- breast cancer patients, inversely correlating with patient prognosis. Functionally, NANS promotes tumor growth and confers resistance to tamoxifen and CDK4/6 inhibitors independently of its canonical enzymatic activity. Mechanistically, NANS recruits ubiquitin-specific protease 7 (USP7) to deubiquitinate and stabilize large tumor suppressor kinase 2 (LATS2), leading to canonical Hippo pathway activation and upregulation of estrogen receptor α and CDK4. Reintroducing LATS2 into NANS-depleted cells partially rescues the impaired growth-promoting and drug-resistant phenotypes in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, the clinical relevance of the NANS-USP7/LATS2 axis is confirmed in patient-derived female HR+ /HER2- breast cancer samples. Together, our findings unveil an enzymatic activity-independent role for NANS in driving therapy resistance, nominating it as a promising therapeutic target.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a highly heterogeneous disease and includes distinct molecular subtypes that are intimately linked to different therapeutic strategies and clinical outcomes1. BC is generally classified into three primary molecular subtypes on the basis of the expression status of estrogen and progesterone receptors (collectively referred to as hormone receptors, HR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). These subtypes include HR-positive and HER2-negative (HR+ /HER2-), HER2-positive (HER2+ ), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)2,3. Among them, HR+ /HER2- is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately two-thirds of all primary BC cases4.

Clinically, endocrine therapy (ET), such as the selective estrogen receptor (ER) modulator tamoxifen, the ER degrader fulvestrant, and aromatase inhibitors that block estrogen production, is the mainstay of treatment for patients with HR+ /HER2- BC5. However, ~ 20% of patients with early-stage disease and nearly all patients with advanced/metastatic disease eventually develop endocrine resistance5. Accumulating evidence has shown that abnormal activation of cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) and their binding partner cyclin D is among the major mechanisms of endocrine resistance6–8. Consequently, the combination of ET with a selective CDK4/6 inhibitor (abemaciclib, palbociclib, or ribociclib) has become the standard first-line treatment for advanced/metastatic HR+ /HER2- BC4,9,10. Although CDK4/6 inhibitors are effective in these clinical settings, nearly all patients eventually develop resistance and succumb to the disease9. However, the molecular mechanisms driving resistance to ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors are still not fully understood and warrant further investigation.

N-acetylneuraminate synthase (NANS) is a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of sialic acids and plays critical roles in brain and skeletal development11,12. Accordingly, NANS mutations disrupt endogenous sialic acid biosynthesis and impair the polysialylation of proteins, thus leading to widespread dysregulation of neuronal proliferation, migration, differentiation, and synaptogenesis12. In addition, mutations in NANS interfere with ribosomal function, compromising protein translation12. Interestingly, the latest emerging evidence indicates that NANS is a potential prognostic marker for patients with prostate13, endometrial14 and colon15 cancers. By analyzing our recently published large-scale multiomics dataset of HR+ /HER2- BC samples16, we found that NANS is highly expressed in HR+ /HER2- BC and that its high expression correlates negatively with patient outcomes. However, the potential biological functions and related mechanisms of action of NANS in cancer progression and therapeutic response remain unexplored to date.

The Hippo pathway is among the most commonly dysregulated signaling pathways in human cancer cells and has been implicated in the initiation and progression of various tumors17–19. As a core component of this pathway in mammals, large tumor suppressor kinase 2 (LATS2) acts as a negative regulator of its downstream transcriptional effectors, Yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), to direct transcriptional programs during numerous biological processes17,19,20. Although LATS2 was initially thought to be a tumor suppressor gene21–24, accumulating evidence has shown that LATS2 exerts tumor-promoting effects in the context of breast cancer25–29. In particular, LATS1/2 promotes ER-positive (ER+ ) breast cancer growth by maintaining ER expression via YAP/TAZ inhibition26,28, as YAP suppresses ER expression and ER+ breast cancer growth27,28. Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of LATS1/2 blocks ER+ breast cancer growth and enhances the sensitivity of ER+ breast cancer cells to the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib28. In addition, LATS2 has been reported to promote the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and invasive capacity of TNBC cells independent of its effects on YAP or TAZ29, and knockout of LATS1/2 in the mouse TNBC cell line 4T1 inhibits tumor growth in mice25. Given the functional importance of LATS2 in breast cancer, an in-depth understanding of its regulatory mechanisms may lead to therapeutic interventions for this disease. Although recent work has shown that LATS2 undergoes both proteasomal30–32 and autophagic33 degradation, its upstream regulatory mechanisms in HR+ /HER2- BC remain largely unknown.

In this study, we report that NANS acts as an oncogene in HR+ /HER2- BC to promote tumor growth and confer resistance to tamoxifen and CDK4/6 inhibitors and that these functions exerted by NANS are independent of its enzymatic activity. Mechanistic investigations revealed that NANS stabilizes LATS2 by enhancing its interaction with ubiquitin-specific protease 7 (USP7), thus activating the Hippo pathway to induce ERα and CDK4 expression. Importantly, the NANS-USP7/LATS2 axis in HR+ /HER2- BC was validated by functional rescue assays, pharmacological inhibition of USP7, and histological analysis of clinical samples. Collectively, these findings suggest that NANS is a potential therapeutic target and a predictive biomarker of resistance to ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR+ /HER2- BC patients.

Results

NANS is highly expressed in HR+ /HER2- BC and is associated with poor patient prognosis

To identify molecular aberrations driving the pathogenesis of HR+ /HER2- BC, we conducted integrative analyses of our recently published large-scale multiomics HR+ /HER2- BC dataset16 and an RNA-seq dataset of HR+ /HER2- BC from TCGA (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). First, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor samples and adjacent normal tissues by analyzing RNA-seq and quantitative proteomics datasets from our center (FUSCC)16 and an RNA-seq dataset from the TCGA. In accordance with the stringent screening criteria (log2FC > 0.7 and adjusted p < 0.05), we found that 85 genes were significantly upregulated in HR+ /HER2- BC tissues (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Second, we analyzed a single–cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset from FUSCC16 with the screening criteria of log2FC > 0.3 and adjusted p < 0.05 to identify DEGs that were specifically expressed in breast cancer cells but not in stromal cells, and found that 38 out of the 85 DEGs identified above were highly expressed in HR+ /HER2- BC cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Third, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to further prioritize functionally relevant genes using an RNA-seq dataset of HR+ /HER2- BC samples from FUSCC16, and found that 6 out of the 38 candidate genes were most strongly associated with HR+ /HER2- BC (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Fourth, we performed Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis using the HR+ /HER2- BC dataset from FUSCC16 and the TCGA dataset and found that high expression of 4 genes, namely, NANS, CERS6 (ceramide synthase 6), MORF4L2 (mortality factor 4 like 2), and HMGB3 (high mobility group box 3), was associated with poor overall survival (OS) in HR+ /HER2- BC patients (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Next, we conducted pan-cancer analyses of NANS expression status in normal and tumor tissues using the TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/) database34 and found that NANS expression was significantly upregulated in tumor samples compared with normal tissues in most cancer types, including breast cancer (Supplementary Fig. S1B). We then analyzed the expression patterns of NANS in normal breast tissues and tissues from the three primary molecular subtypes (HR+ /HER2-, HER2+ , and TNBC) of breast cancer2,3 using proteomic datasets from the CBCGA (Chinese Breast Cancer Genome Atlas)35, alongside CPTAC (Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium) data36 accessed via the UALCAN web portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html). The results revealed that the expression levels of NANS were highest in the HR+ /HER2- subtype in the CBCGA dataset (Fig. 1B) and in the luminal subtype in the CPTAC dataset (Fig. 1C). Similarly, NANS mRNA expression in the HR+/HER2- subtype was significantly higher than that in TNBC across both the TCGA and METABRIC (Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium) datasets, and also significantly higher than that in normal breast tissues within the TCGA cohort (Supplementary Fig. S1C, D). It is worth mentioning that the METABRIC dataset lacks corresponding normal sample data. Consistent with these findings, immunoblotting assays revealed that NANS expression was significantly upregulated in HR+ /HER2- BC cell lines (MCF7 and T47D) compared with that in normal mammary epithelial cell lines and HER2+ and TNBC cell lines (Fig. 1D). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of HR+ /HER2- breast cancer tissues indicated that NANS primarily co-localized with the tumor epithelial marker pancytokeratin (panCK), rather than with fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a marker for stromal cancer-associated fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These results suggest that NANS is localized mainly in tumor cells rather than in stromal components.

To further analyze the relationship between the expression levels of NANS and patient prognosis, we conducted a survival analysis using a large-scale HR+ /HER2- BC cohort from FUSCC16. High NANS expression was associated with poor overall survival (OS), relapse-free survival (RFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) in HR+ /HER2- BC patients (Fig. 1E–G, respectively). Similarly, elevated expression of NANS was correlated with poor prognosis in HR+ /HER2- BC patients in the TCGA and METABRIC datasets, as well as in analyses performed via the Kaplan‒Meier plotter online web portal (Supplementary Fig. S2B–E, respectively).

To validate the above results, we performed RT‒qPCR and immunoblotting assays using tumor samples and paired adjacent normal tissues from HR+ /HER2- BC patients. The results revealed that the expression levels of NANS were significantly greater in tumor samples than in adjacent normal tissues at both the mRNA (Fig. 1H) and protein (Fig. 1I, J) levels. Among the 19 protein samples analyzed, 12 were obtained from the same patients used for the RT-qPCR analysis.

Taken together, these results suggest that NANS is highly expressed in HR+ /HER2- BC tissues and is associated with poor patient prognosis.

NANS promotes the growth of HR+ /HER2- BC cells both in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the biological functions of NANS in HR+ /HER2- BC cells, we stably depleted endogenous NANS or overexpressed Flag-NANS in T47D and MCF7 cells and validated the expression status of NANS in these established cell lines by immunoblotting (Fig. 2A, B). CCK8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that depletion of NANS using two independent sgRNAs targeting NANS (sgNANS #2 and #3) significantly reduced the viability and colony formation ability of MCF7 and T47D cells (Fig. 2C–E). Conversely, ectopic expression of NANS increased the viability and colony formation ability of both MCF7 and T47D cells (Fig. 2F–H). Moreover, reintroduction of NANS into NANS-depleted MCF7 and T47D cells rescued the impaired viability and colony-forming capacity caused by NANS depletion (Fig. 2I, J and Supplementary Fig. S3A, B). Next, we established xenograft tumor models in nude mice to assess the impact of NANS knockdown on the growth of HR+ /HER2- BC cells in vivo. As shown in Fig. 2K–M, knockdown of NANS expression significantly reduced xenograft tumor weight and volume in nude mice. Consistent with these findings, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed that knockdown of NANS decreased the expression levels of the putative cell proliferation marker Ki-6737 in xenograft tumors (Fig. 2N, O). Collectively, these results suggest that NANS accelerates the growth of HR+ /HER2- BC cells both in vitro and in vivo.

NANS confers resistance to tamoxifen and CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR+ /HER2- BC

ET alone or combined with a CDK4/6 inhibitor is the current standard-of-care first-line therapy for patients with HR+ /HER2- BC4,38–40. By analyzing HR+ /HER2- BC datasets from both the FUSCC cohort16 and Kaplan‒Meier plotter web portal41, we found that high NANS expression was associated with poor survival (OS) in patients who received ET (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). These results indicate that NANS may contribute to endocrine resistance in HR+ /HER2- BC.

On the basis of these findings, we next examined whether NANS affects the sensitivity of HR+ /HER2- BC cells to the most commonly used ET drug, tamoxifen (Tam), and the clinically approved CDK4/6 inhibitors abemaciclib (Abema) and palbociclib (Palbo)9,10. CCK-8 cell viability assays revealed that knockdown of NANS expression significantly increased the sensitivity of MCF7 and T47D cells to Tam (Fig. 3A), Abema (Fig. 3B), and Palbo (Fig. 3C), as indicated by the marked decreases in the half inhibitory concentration (IC50) values. Moreover, reintroduction of NANS into NANS‑depleted MCF7 and T47D cells reversed the enhanced sensitivity to tamoxifen (Tam) and abemaciclib (Abema) induced by NANS depletion (Supplementary Fig. S4C, D). Similarly, NANS knockdown significantly sensitized tamoxifen‑resistant (TamR) and palbociclib‑resistant (PalboR) MCF7 cells to their respective drugs (Supplementary Fig. S4E–G).

To further mimic clinical therapeutic settings, we established models with dual-resistance to tamoxifen and palbociclib (TPR) in both T47D and MCF7 cell lines, with their resistance profiles validated via drug sensitivity assays (Supplementary Fig. S4H, I). Notably, NANS depletion markedly sensitized both T47D-TPR and MCF7-TPR cells to combined tamoxifen and palbociclib treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4J, K). Furthermore, NANS deficiency significantly promoted apoptosis and attenuated the migratory and invasive capacities of these dual-resistant cells (Supplementary Fig. S5A–H).

We next generated aromatase‑overexpressing cell lines (MCF7‑aro and T47D‑aro) by stable transfection of aromatase-encoding gene CYP19A1 to assess aromatase inhibitor sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. S5I). In these cells, NANS knockdown markedly increased sensitivity to the aromatase inhibitors letrozole and exemestane (Supplementary Fig. S5J, K). Furthermore, NANS depletion increased cellular sensitivity to multiple combination regimens, including tamoxifen plus abemaciclib (Supplementary Fig. S6A) or palbociclib (Supplementary Fig. S6B), letrozole plus abemaciclib (Supplementary Fig. S6C) or palbociclib (Supplementary Fig. S6D), and exemestane plus abemaciclib (Supplementary Fig. S6E) or palbociclib (Supplementary Fig. S6F).

To validate the above findings, we conducted drug sensitivity experiments on patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from HR+ /HER2- BC patients. The expression levels of NANS in these PDOs were assessed by IHC staining of postoperative pathological tissue sections derived from the same patients to establish PDOs (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. S6G). The results revealed that compared with PDO #221 with low NANS expression, PDO #333 with high NANS expression displayed decreased sensitivity to Tam, Abema, and Palbo (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Fig. S6H). These results collectively suggest that NANS contributes to resistance to Tam and CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR+ /HER2- BC.

NANS exerts its functions in HR+ /HER2- BC independently of its enzymatic activity

In mammals, NANS catalyzes the condensation of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) with N-acetylmannosamine 6-phosphate (ManNAc-6-P) to produce N-acetylneuraminic acid (NeuAc) and, subsequently, cytidine monophosphate (CMP)-NeuAc (the active form of NeuAc)42,43. Given that previous studies have shown that the biological functions of NANS are largely dependent on its enzymatic activity11,12, we next investigated whether the noted functions of NANS in HR+ /HER2- BC depend on its enzymatic activity.

To this aim, we first analyzed the metabolomic dataset from 384 patients with HR+ /HER2- BC in the FUSCC cohort16 to compare the differences in the levels of the catalytic products of NANS (NeuAc and CMP-NeuAc) between patients with high and low expression of NANS. Unexpectedly, we found no significant differences in the levels of NeuAc or CMP-NeuAc between patients with high and low expression of NANS at either the mRNA or protein level (Supplementary Fig. S7A, B). We subsequently conducted gene set variation analysis (GSVA), specifically focusing on cell cycle-related pathways to compare pathway enrichment between patients with high and low NANS expression. The results revealed that cell cycle-related pathways were significantly enriched in the high NANS expression group (Supplementary Fig. S7C). Furthermore, comparisons of responders and nonresponders to endocrine therapy in the HR+ /HER2− breast cancer cohort from the FUSCC16 revealed no differential enrichment of pathways related to sialic acid metabolism between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S7D).

Previous studies have shown that mutation of any of the four sites responsible for enzymatic activity (K131Q, G133V, Y188H, and P189L) in NANS can impair its catalytic activity11. We therefore constructed an NANS expression vector in which all four enzymatic activity sites were mutated according to the above literature (named NANS-Mut) and stably overexpressed wild-type (WT) and mutant NANS in MCF7 and T47D cells to perform functional assays (Supplementary Fig. S7E). The catalytic activity of NANS was assessed by measuring its direct product, Neu5Ac-9-P, using an ELISA kit. Overexpression of wild-type NANS led to a significant increase in intracellular Neu5Ac-9-P levels, whereas expression of the catalytic mutant had no appreciable effects (Supplementary Fig. S7F). These results indicate that these mutations abolish NANS enzymatic activity.

CCK-8 and colony formation assays revealed that mutation of the sites responsible for enzymatic activity in NANS did not significantly affect its ability to promote the viability (Supplementary Fig. S7G) or colony formation ability (Supplementary Fig. S7H, I) of MCF7 and T47D cells. Moreover, ectopic expression of both NANS-WT and NANS-Mut reduced the sensitivity of MCF7 and T47D cells to Tam and the CDK4/6 inhibitors Abema and Palbo (Supplementary Fig. S7J–L). Together, these results suggest that the functions of NANS in HR+ /HER2- BC are not dependent on its enzymatic activity.

With respect to the Hippo pathway, we found that both LATS2 and PAK1 (p21-activated kinase 1) were significantly downregulated in MCF7 cells following knockdown of NANS (Fig. 4C). Immunoblotting assays demonstrated that knockdown of NANS significantly downregulated the expression levels of LATS2, in contrast to PAK1, in MCF7 and T47D cells (Fig. 4D). In addition, overexpression of NANS led to a significant upregulation of LATS2 in MCF7 and T47D cells (Fig. 4E). Notably, neither knockdown nor overexpression of NANS affected LATS1 expression (Fig. 4D, E). These results suggest that NANS positively regulates LATS2 expression in HR+ /HER2- BC cells.

To further investigate how NANS regulates LATS2, we performed RT‒qPCR assays and found that knockdown of NANS did not significantly affect the mRNA levels of LATS2 (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays revealed that knockdown of NANS shortened the half-life of the LATS2 protein (Fig. 4F, G). These results indicate that NANS could increase the protein stability of LATS2. Notably, previous studies have shown that LATS2 undergoes both proteasomal30–32 and autophagic33 degradation in other model systems. However, treatment of T47D and MCF7 cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 but not the lysosome inhibitor Baf-A1 resulted in increased LATS2 protein levels (Fig. 4H, I). These results suggest that LATS2 is degraded in HR+ /HER2- BC cells mainly through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. Moreover, the reduced levels of LATS2 caused by NANS knockdown were partially restored by treatment with MG132 but not with Baf-A1 (Fig. 4J, K). These results highlight that NANS can block the proteasomal degradation of LATS2. In support of this notion, ubiquitination assays revealed that the ubiquitination levels of LATS2 decreased upon overexpression of NANS (Fig. 4L) but increased following NANS knockdown (Fig. 4M). Collectively, these results suggest that NANS blocks the ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation of LATS2.

Next, we investigated whether the NANS-mediated stabilization of LATS2 is dependent on USP7. As expected, immunoblotting and ubiquitination assays revealed that knockdown of USP7 partially attenuated the NANS-induced upregulation of LATS2 (Fig. 5E) and impaired the NANS-mediated deubiquitination of LATS2 (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, in vitro deubiquitination assays demonstrated that USP7 reduced LATS2 ubiquitination and that NANS enhanced the USP7-mediated deubiquitination of LATS2 (Fig. 5G). These results suggest that USP7 contributes to the inhibitory effects of NANS on the ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation of LATS2.

We investigated how NANS regulates LATS2 via USP7. Immunoblotting analyses revealed that neither overexpression nor knockdown of NANS affected the expression level of USP7 (Supplementary Fig. S9D, E). Thus, we hypothesized that NANS stabilizes LATS2 by influencing the interaction between USP7 and LATS2. Reciprocal IP assays demonstrated that ectopically expressed Flag-LATS2 interacted with endogenous USP7 and NANS (Supplementary Fig. S9F), whereas exogenously expressed HA-USP7 bound to endogenous LATS2 and NANS (Supplementary Fig. S9G). Similarly, exogenously expressed Flag-NANS interacted with endogenous LATS2 and USP7 in HEK293T, T47D, and MCF7 cells (Supplementary Fig. S9H). Importantly, endogenous NANS also interacted with endogenous LATS2 and USP7 in T47D and MCF7 cells (Supplementary Fig. S9I).

Next, we generated LATS2 truncation constructs to perform domain mapping experiments. Immunoprecipitation assays revealed that NANS interacts with Flag-LATS2 (301-600 aa), whereas USP7 binds to Flag-LATS2 (601-1088 aa) (Fig. 5H). To validate these direct interactions in vitro, we conducted GST pull-down assays. The results revealed that GST-LATS2 (480-1088 aa) can directly bind full-length His-USP7 and full-length His-NANS and that GST-NANS can interact with full-length His-USP7 (Supplementary Fig. S9J–L). These findings provide strong evidence that NANS, USP7, and LATS2 can directly interact with one another. Moreover, overexpression of NANS enhanced, whereas knockdown of NANS reduced, the interaction between LATS2 and USP7 (Fig. 5I, J). Taken together, these results suggest that NANS forms a ternary complex with USP7 and LATS2 and enhances the interaction between LATS2 and USP7 to stabilize LATS2.

Collectively, these results highlight that NANS promotes growth and confers resistance to tamoxifen and CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR + /HER2- BC partially through the regulation of LATS2 both in vitro and in vivo.

NANS regulates ERα and CDK4 expression by activating the LATS2-mediated canonical Hippo pathway

Previous studies have documented that LATS2 is a central component of the Hippo pathway that phosphorylates its transcriptional effectors YAP and TAZ. Phosphorylated YAP and TAZ are sequestered in the cytoplasm or degraded, thus regulating downstream transcriptional programs involved in numerous biological processes17,19,20. In this context, LATS1/2 are essential for maintaining ERα expression in ER+ breast cancer cells through the negative regulation of YAP/TAZ in a kinase activity-dependent manner26,28, and decreased YAP1 expression in ER+ breast cancer is associated with more aggressive clinicopathologic phenotypes, increased ER expression, and decreased sensitivity to tamoxifen49. In addition, CDK6 is regulated by Hippo signaling in ER+ breast cancer cells50. Thus, we next investigated the effects of NANS knockdown on the activation of the Hippo pathway. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S11A, we found that knockdown of NANS expression significantly reduced the expression levels of LATS2 (but not LATS1), phosphorylated YAP (p-YAP), phosphorylated TAZ (p-TAZ), CDK4, CDK6, and ERα (compare Lane 2 and Lane 1). Moreover, the re-expression of LATS2 in NANS-depleted cells partially restored the reduced expression of p-YAP, p-TAZ, CDK4 and ERα but not that of CDK6 (compare Lane 3 and Lane 2). Furthermore, to assess changes in YAP phosphorylation more sensitively, we performed Phos-tag immunoblotting assays. Consistent with the above results, knockdown of NANS markedly decreased p-YAP levels, accompanied by an increase in the levels of nonphosphorylated YAP. Notably, the re-expression of LATS2 in NANS-depleted cells partially reversed the reduction in p-YAP levels (Supplementary Fig. S11B). These results suggest that NANS regulates CDK4 and ERα expression via LATS2. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed that knockdown of NANS led to a marked increase in the nuclear localization of YAP and TAZ, and these effects were attenuated in NANS-depleted cells with LATS2 re-expression (Supplementary Fig. S11C–F). Notably, knockdown of YAP1 in NANS-depleted cells partially restored the reduced expression levels of CDK4 and ERα caused by NANS knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S11G). Conversely, re-expression of the constitutively active YAP mutant (YAP1−5SA), in which five serine residues (positions 61, 109, 127, 164, and 381) were replaced by alanine51, in NANS-overexpressing MCF7 and T47D cells significantly reduced the altered CDK4 and ERα expression levels mediated by NANS (Supplementary Fig. S11H). Together, these findings suggest that NANS regulates ERα and CDK4 expression by activating the LATS2-mediated Hippo pathway.

As CDK4 plays a pivotal role in the cell cycle transition from the G1 phase to the S phase52, we next examined whether NANS affects cell cycle progression. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that knockdown of NANS led to cell cycle arrest at the G1/S phase (Supplementary Fig. S12A, B). Moreover, immunoblotting revealed the downregulation of G1/S checkpoint proteins, including CDK4 and CDK6, as well as their downstream targets, including phosphorylated retinoblastoma protein (Rb) at serine residues 807 and 811 (phospho-Rb S807/811) and cyclin A29, following knockdown of NANS (Supplementary Fig. S12C). Taken together, these findings suggest that NANS promotes the cell cycle transition from the G1 phase to the S phase.

Finally, we constructed a four-gene signature comprising NANS, USP7, LATS2, and CDK4 using multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis among patients who received endocrine therapy (ET) in the FUSCC cohort. On the basis of this signature, patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups, with the high-risk group exhibiting significantly poorer overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) (Supplementary Fig. S13H, I). We then validated the signature in the METABRIC cohort, in which high-risk patients who received ET similarly demonstrated worse prognosis (Supplementary Fig. S13J, K). These findings suggest that the NANS-USP7/LATS2 axis is clinically relevant in HR + /HER2- BC specimens.

It has been well documented that USP7 is overexpressed in various types of human cancer and is an important regulator of tumorigenesis and an emerging drug target for cancer treatment54. In accordance with these data, selective USP7 inhibitors, such as P5091, exhibit broad-spectrum antitumor activity against multiple malignancies55. On the basis of these results and our findings, we evaluated the effect of the USP7 inhibitor P5091 on drug sensitivity in tamoxifen-resistant and palbociclib-resistant MCF7 cells. P5091 treatment significantly increased the sensitivity of TamR cells to tamoxifen and of PalboR cells to palbociclib, producing effects comparable to those observed with NANS knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S4E–G). Moreover, we examined the effects of the USP7 inhibitor P5091 on drug sensitivity in the PDOs shown in Supplementary Fig. S6G, H. The results demonstrated that the administration of P5091 effectively increased the sensitivity of drug-resistant PDO #333 to Tam and the CDK4/6 inhibitors Abema and Palbo (Fig. 7I, J). These findings were further validated in xenograft models, in which P5091 treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth and enhanced the sensitivity of the tumor to tamoxifen and abemaciclib, with inhibitory effects comparable to NANS inhibition (Fig. 7K–O). Furthermore, to better recapitulate the clinical treatment setting, we employed an exemestane- and abemaciclib-resistant patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model for additional in vivo drug sensitivity studies. The results demonstrated that the addition of P5091 significantly enhanced the sensitivity of the resistant PDX tumors to exemestane and abemaciclib combination therapy (Supplementary Fig. S14A–C). Together, these preclinical data strongly suggest that pharmacological targeting of USP7 can effectively overcome resistance to endocrine and CDK4/6 inhibitor therapies in HR+ /HER2- breast cancer.

Collectively, these results suggest that the NANS-USP7/LATS2 axis is clinically relevant in HR+ /HER2- BC and that targeting USP7 might overcome NANS-mediated resistance to ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors in this disease (Fig. 7P).

Discussion

ET is the cornerstone of systemic treatment for HR+ /HER2- BC patients and substantially reduces both disease recurrence and patient mortality56,57. However, prolonged ET inevitably induces resistance, thus driving extensive research into alternative targeted therapies such as CDK4/6 inhibitors. Clinical evidence has shown that ET combined with CDK4/6 inhibitors significantly prolongs progression-free survival. However, the development of resistance represents a major clinical challenge40,58,59. Therefore, deciphering the mechanisms of resistance to these therapies and discovering therapeutic targets or predictive biomarkers of the therapeutic response are crucial priorities in the management of HR+ /HER2- BC patients. In this study, we report several interesting findings concerning previously unrecognized functional and mechanistic roles of NANS in driving tumor growth and conferring therapeutic resistance in HR+ /HER2- BC cells.

First, we found that NANS is highly expressed in HR+ /HER2- BC and promotes tumor growth and resistance to ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors in an enzymatic activity-independent manner. While sialic acid metabolism has been thoroughly characterized in various tumor contexts, these studies focused mainly on elucidating how sialylation processes contribute to tumorigenesis and disease progression60–62. For instance, cytidine monophosphate N-acetylneuraminic acid synthetase (CMAS), a rate-limiting enzyme in sialic acid metabolism, promotes breast cancer progression through a sialylation-dependent mechanism63,64. Despite its fundamental role in sialic acid biosynthesis, NANS remains poorly characterized in human cancers, with several limited reports in prostate, endometrial and colorectal cancers14,15,65. In this study, we demonstrated that NANS exhibits strong tumor-promoting activity in HR+ /HER2- BC, increasing tumor growth and resistance to ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors (Figs. 2, 3 and Supplementary Figs. S3–S6). Interestingly, these functions of NANS in HR+ /HER2- BC cells are independent of its enzymatic activity (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Second, we discovered that NANS performs its oncogenic functions in HR+ /HER2- BC partially by stabilizing LATS2 both in vitro and in vivo. Dysregulation of the Hippo pathway has been extensively reported in various cancers17–19. LATS2, a core component of this pathway, was initially recognized as a classical tumor suppressor17,20,66–68. However, emerging evidence shows that LATS2 may exhibit oncogenic functions in certain cancer contexts69–71. Notably, multiple studies have demonstrated that LATS2 functions as an oncogenic factor, while its downstream effector, YAP1, acts as a tumor suppressor in HR+ /HER2- BC25–28. Consistent with these results, our study demonstrated that NANS promotes tumor growth and confers resistance to tamoxifen and CDK4/6 inhibitors in HR+ /HER2- BC partially by stabilizing LATS2 (Figs. 4–6 and Supplementary Fig. S8–S10). Previous studies have shown that LATS2 can be regulated by the E3 ubiquitin ligases DCAF172, FZR173, and SIAH230 as well as by the deubiquitinase USP9X32 in human cancer, thereby affecting its protein stability. In this study, we revealed that USP7 acts as a deubiquitinase of LATS2 that mediates its stability and that NANS enhances the interaction between USP7 and LATS2 to stabilize LATS2 (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S9). Importantly, the reintroduction of LATS2 into NANS-depleted cells partially rescued the impaired functional phenotypes caused by NANS knockdown (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S10), and the USP7 inhibitor P5091 effectively reversed NANS-induced resistance to tamoxifen or CDK4/6 inhibitors alone or in combination in the PDO model, cell line-derived xenograft, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S14). Moreover, polychromatic immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining revealed that NANS, LATS2, and USP7 were colocalized in clinical HR+ /HER2- BC samples and that there were positive correlations between the protein expression levels of NANS and LATS2 as well as between those of USP7 and LATS2 (Fig. 7). These results highlight that the NANS-USP7/LATS2 axis is clinically relevant in HR+ /HER2- BC.

Third, we demonstrated that NANS regulates ERα and CDK4 expression by activating the LATS2-mediated canonical Hippo pathway. Previous studies have shown that LATS1/2 promotes ER-positive (ER+ ) breast cancer growth by maintaining ER expression via YAP/TAZ inhibition26,28 and that pharmacological inhibition of LATS1/2 blocks ER+ breast cancer growth and increases the sensitivity of ER+ breast cancer cells to the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib28. In addition, CDK4 overexpression has been detected in approximately 15% of breast cancer cases74, and its overexpression contributes to resistance to both ET and CDK4/6 inhibitors9,75–79. Consistent with previous studies, we demonstrated that NANS regulates ERα expression via the LATS2-YAP pathway (Supplementary Fig. S11). In addition, we report that LATS2 regulates CDK4 expression via the LATS2-mediated canonical Hippo pathway, providing insights into the oncogenic functions of Hippo signaling in breast cancer.

In conclusion, the findings presented here reveal that NANS is highly overexpressed in HR+ /HER2- BC and functions as an oncogene to promote growth and therapeutic resistance by stabilizing LATS2. NANS may serve as a promising therapeutic target and predictive biomarker of the treatment response in HR+ /HER2- BC patients.

Methods

Ethics compliance and consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC). All experiments involving human patient-derived samples were performed with informed consent obtained from all participants. Animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of FUSCC (Approval numbers: FUSCC-IACUC-2024311, FUSCC-IACUC-2025584, FUSCC-IACUC-2026113, and FUSCC-IACUC-2024548).

Cell culture and chemicals

The human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK293T) cell line, human mammary epithelial cell lines (HMEC and MCF10A), and human breast cancer cell lines (MCF7, T47D, HCC1954, MDA-MB-231, LM2-4175, and BT549) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and Shanghai Key Laboratory of Breast Cancer. MCF10A and HMEC cells were maintained in DMEM (#L110, BasalMedia) supplemented with 5% donor horse serum (#16050-114, Gibco), 10 μg/mL insulin (#40107ES76, Yeasen Biotech), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (#10605-HNAE, Sino Biol), and 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone (#40109ES08, Yeasen Biotech). The other cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (#10270-106; Gibco) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (#S110B; BasalMedia).

The tamoxifen-resistant (TamR) MCF7 cell line and its parental counterpart were kindly provided by Prof. Cuixia Yang (Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China)80. The palbociclib-resistant (PalboR) MCF7 cell line and its corresponding parental line were purchased from MEISEN CELL (Shanghai, China). The tamoxifen (Tam) and palbociclib (Palbo) dual-resistant (TPR) MCF7 cell line was established by continuous exposure of PalboR MCF7 cells to tamoxifen for 3–4 months. The tamoxifen- and palbociclib-resistant (TPR) T47D cell line was generated by continuous treatment with both tamoxifen and palbociclib for 3–4 months. To maintain the drug-resistant phenotypes of these cells, MCF7-TamR and MCF7-PalboR cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1 µM tamoxifen (Tam) or 1 µM palbociclib (Palbo), respectively. MCF7-TPR and T47D-TPR cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 0.5 µM tamoxifen (Tam) and 0.2 µM palbociclib (Palbo). MCF7 and T47D cells were stably transfected with the aromatase gene CYP19A1 or corresponding empty vectors to establish MCF7-aro and T47D-aro cell lines for use in aromatase inhibitor sensitivity assays. Cells were cultured in phenol red-free DMEM (BasalMedia, #L140KJ) supplemented with 10% charcoal-stripped fetal bovine serum (#12676-029, Gibco), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and androstenedione (10⁻⁷ mol/L; GC19559, GLPBIO). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma contamination. Detailed information regarding all the reagents used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Clinical samples and datasets

The multiomics HR+ /HER2- BC datasets from Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC), including RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), metabolome, and quantitative proteomics data, and the clinical information of these patients have been described previously16. HR+ /HER2- BC tissue samples and adjacent normal breast tissues were collected from female patients who underwent surgery at the Department of Breast Surgery of FUSCC and did not receive any treatment. All participants provided written informed consent. The experimental procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of FUSCC. An RNA-seq HR+ /HER2- BC dataset from the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) was downloaded from https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/.

Construction of expression vectors

The expression vectors encoding Flag-NANS, HA-NANS, Flag-NANS-Mut, HA-USP7, HA-USP7 (C223S), YAP1 (5SA), Flag-LATS2 (amino acids 1-300), Flag-LATS2 (amino acids 301-600), Flag-LATS2 (amino acids 601-1088), and single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting NANS (sgNANS #2 and #3) and YAP1 (sgYAP1) were synthesized by Syn-Biotech (Suzhou, China). Flag-LATS2 was synthesized by MiaoLing Biol (Wuhan, China). The sequences of short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting USP7 (shUSP7 #1 and #2) were obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich and cloned into the pLKO.1-TRC vector (#10878; Addgene). The sequences of the sgRNAs and shRNAs used in this study are presented in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Plasmid transfection and lentiviral infection

Plasmid transfection was performed using Neofect DNA transfection reagent (#TF201201; Neofect Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For lentiviral infection, the packaging plasmids and target plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells for 48 h. The supernatants were filtered, collected, and used for cell infection along with polybrene (#H9268, Sigma‒Aldrich). After 48 h of viral infection, the transduced cells were subjected to antibiotic selection using 2 μg/mL puromycin (#S7417, Selleck) or 0.5 mg/mL geneticin (G418) (#A600958-0005, Sangon Biotech).

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and tissues using TRIzol reagent (#9109; Takara), followed by reverse transcription using HiScript III RT SuperMix (#R323-01; Vazyme). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays were performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q711-03, Vazyme). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The sequences of the primers used for the qPCR assays are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Immunoblotting and immunoprecipitation (IP) assays

Cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with both protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (#B14002 and #B15003, Bimake). After quantification using a BCA protein quantification kit (#20201ES; Yeasen Biotech), equal amounts of protein were subjected to SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (#IPVH00010; Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and incubated sequentially with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using a chemiluminescent substrate kit (#180-5001E, Tanon). Phos-tag reagents were purchased from GLPBIO (#GK10005), and Phos-tag SDS–PAGE gels were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For exogenous IP assays, cells were lysed using NP-40 lysis buffer and incubated with anti-HA (#GNI4510-HA; Shanghai Genomics Technology) or anti-Flag (#B23102; Bimake) beads for more than 3 h. For endogenous IP assays, cellular lysates were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads (#B23202; Bimake) for 3 h. After being washed three times, the immunocomplexes were subjected to immunoblotting. The information for all antibodies used in this study is provided in Supplementary Data 1.

GST pull-down assays

GST-tagged full-length NANS, His-tagged full-length USP7, and His-tagged full-length NANS were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). After cells were lysed, the proteins were purified via affinity chromatography using glutathione Sepharose for GST-tagged proteins and Ni-NTA resin for His-tagged proteins. GST protein (#11213-HNAE) and GST-tagged C-terminal LATS2 (amino acids 480-1088; #L02-35G) were obtained commercially from Sino Biological. For GST pull-down assays, the proteins were incubated in binding buffer (20 mM Tris pH7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, and 10% glycerol) overnight at 4 °C to allow binding. The beads were then washed three times with wash buffer (10 mM Tris pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% NP-40), resuspended in 2 × SDS loading buffer, and analyzed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 (#93443; Sigma‒Aldrich). After being blocked with 5% BSA, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After being washed with PBST three times, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (#ab104139; Abcam). Images were captured using a Leica fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, USA).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Human breast cancer tissues and mouse xenograft tumor tissues were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by serum blocking for 1 h. The tissues were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (1:200) at room temperature for 1 h. Signal detection was performed using a DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) staining kit (#G1212-200T; Servicebio), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining.

Proteomic analysis

For DIA quantitative proteomics, total proteins were extracted from MCF7 cells (sgNC vs. sgNANS #2, n = 3 biological replicates per group). The peptides were desalted and analyzed via LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (Shanghai Bioprofile Biotech). To investigate LATS2-interacting proteins, cellular lysates from HEK293T cells (pCDH vs. Flag-LATS2) were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag magnetic beads (Bimake). The complexes were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. The protein bands were excised and analyzed via LC-MS/MS using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (Westlake Omics, Hangzhou, China). Protein identification was performed using Fragpipe (v19.1) and the MSFragger search engine against the UniProt Swissprot_Human database (containing 20,456 reviewed proteins). Search parameters included: trypsin digestion with a maximum of two missed cleavages; precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set at 20 ppm; and peptide FDR was set at < 0.01.

In vivo and in vitro ubiquitination assays

For the in vivo ubiquitination assays, HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the indicated plasmids for 48 h, followed by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 μM; #S2619, Selleck) for 6 h. Cells were then lysed using denaturing buffer and subjected to IP assays.

For the in vitro deubiquitination assays, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with V5-tagged ubiquitin and Flag-tagged LATS2 and then treated with MG132 for 6 h. Ubiquitinated Flag-LATS2 was immunoprecipitated, and the isolated protein was incubated with His-USP7 alone or together with His-NANS in deubiquitination buffer (60 mM HEPES, 5 mM MgCl₂, and 4% glycerol, pH7.5) at 30 °C for 4 h. Deubiquitinated proteins were then analyzed by immunoblotting.

Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays

Cells were treated with 100 μg/mL CHX for the indicated durations and then subjected to immunoblotting assays with the indicated antibodies.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and cell viability was determined by measuring the optical density at 450 nm (OD450) using a CCK-8 kit (#40203ES92; Yeasen Biotech).

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were fixed in 70% ethanol for 2 h and subsequently washed with PBS. Cell staining was performed using a cell cycle analysis kit (#40301ES50; Yeasen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was then used to analyze the cell cycle distribution.

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed with an Annexin V–FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (#40302ES60; Yeasen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Collected cells were washed twice with ice‑cold PBS, resuspended in binding buffer, and stained with Annexin V–FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 10–15 min at room temperature in the dark. Flow cytometric analysis was performed within 1 h after staining. The sequential gating strategies for flow cytometry are shown in Supplementary Fig. S15.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers (Matrigel-coated for invasion) placed in 24-well plates. The lower chambers contained DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. Cells were resuspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chambers. After 24 h of incubation, cells that had migrated or invaded were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.2% crystal violet for quantification.

Drug sensitivity assays

For the cell viability assays, cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with or without P5091, letrozole, tamoxifen, abemaciclib, or palbociclib. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the OD450 using a CCK-8 kit. The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined using a three-parameter nonlinear regression model (log[inhibitor] versus response) in GraphPad Prism 9.5.1. For the colony formation assays, the cells were plated in 12-well plates and treated as indicated for 7–9 days. Surviving colonies were then fixed with methanol and stained with 0.2% crystal violet.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs)

PDOs were established following previously described methods81. Briefly, fresh tumor tissues were obtained from HR+ /HER2- BC patients at FUSCC, minced, and digested with collagenase (#C9407; Sigma‒Aldrich) and hyaluronidase (#37326-33-3; Sigma‒Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1–3 h. The digested mixture was filtered through a 100 µm cell strainer and centrifuged. Red blood cells were lysed using the lysis buffer. The resulting organoid pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of basement membrane extract type-2 (#3533-010-02, Trevigen) and seeded into 384-well plates (#GN781900, Greiner). After 5-7 days of drug treatment, morphological changes were observed under a microscope, and organoid viability was assessed using CellTiter-Glo 3D assays (#G9683; Promega).

In vivo xenograft tumor models

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of FUSCC (Approval number: FUSCC-IACUC-2024311, FUSCC-IACUC-2025584, FUSCC-IACUC-2026113 and FUSCC-IACUC-2024548). The maximal tumor size allowed by the IACUC of FUSCC was 2000 mm3 (or 20 mm in diameter). We confirm that the tumor burden in our experiments did not exceed this limit. For cell line–derived xenograft models, a total of 5 × 10⁶ mycoplasma-free MCF7 cells in the logarithmic growth stage were suspended in PBS and mixed with an equal volume of Matrigel (#356334, Corning Falcon). Following subcutaneous implantation of 17β-estradiol pellets (#SE-121, Innovative Research of America), the cell suspension was injected into the mammary fat pads of female BALB/c nude mice. When the tumor volume reached 50–100 mm3, the mice were treated with P5091 (15 mg/kg, dissolved in 6.5% DMSO, 3% Tween-80 and 20% PEG400 in ddH2O, administered daily via intraperitoneal injection), tamoxifen (50 mg/kg, dissolved in corn oil, administered daily via intraperitoneal injection), or abemaciclib (25 mg/kg, dissolved in corn oil, administered every other day via oral gavage). The tumor diameters were measured regularly, and the tumor volumes were calculated using the formula 0.5 × length × width². The experiments were terminated, and mice were euthanized, after 20 days of treatment or earlier if humane endpoints were reached. Humane endpoints were defined as a maximum tumor burden of 2000 mm3 or 20 mm in diameter, rapid weight loss exceeding 10% of the initial body weight, or severe tumor ulceration.

For patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, four- to six-week-old female NCG mice were ovariectomized and allowed to recover from surgery for one week prior to tumor implantation. Androstenedione (100 μg, dissolved in corn oil) was administered subcutaneously to each mouse 5 days per week throughout the experiment to provide a substrate for aromatase. The dual-resistant PDX model was derived from an HR + /HER2- BC patient who developed liver and bone metastases within two months of receiving combined exemestane and abemaciclib therapy. As previously described82,83, the PDX fragments were subcutaneously implanted into the flanks of the NCG mice. Tumor dimensions were monitored twice weekly, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × length × width². When the average tumor volume reached approximately 50–100 mm3, the mice were assigned into four treatment groups (n = 6 mice/group): (1) Vehicle control; (2) Exemestane (15 mg/kg, dissolved in 6.5% DMSO, 3% Tween-80, and 40% PEG400 in ddH₂O, administered daily via intraperitoneal injection) plus Abemaciclib (25 mg/kg, administered every other day via oral gavage); (3) P5091 (15 mg/kg, administered daily via intraperitoneal injection); and (4) the combination of exemestane, abemaciclib, and P5091. All treatments were administered continuously for 20 days. The experiments were terminated at this point, or earlier if mice met any of the predefined humane endpoint criteria, including a maximum tumor burden of 2000 mm3 or 20 mm in diameter, rapid weight loss exceeding 10% of the initial body weight, or severe tumor ulceration. At the endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and xenograft tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed for subsequent analyses.

Bioinformatic analyses

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups were analyzed using the R package limma with a log2FC (fold change) > 0.7 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 as the cutoff criteria. Gene clustering was performed on the basis of similar expression patterns in HR+ /HER2- BC samples from the FUSCC cohort using the WGCNA (weighted gene coexpression network analysis) package in R version 4.3.0. A minimum threshold was applied to construct a dendrogram, and genes with similar characteristics were grouped into the same module. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was conducted to assess the association between the expression of multiple genes and patient survival.

Sex and gender considerations

This study focuses on HR+ /HER2- breast cancer, a disease that predominantly affects females. Therefore, only female human participants, female-derived cell lines, and female mice were included in the experimental design. The sex of the human research participants was determined based on self-report in the clinical records. As this research applies to a single sex, sex- and gender-based analyses were not performed.

Statistics & reproducibility

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments or biological replicates. Differences between the two groups were analyzed using an paired or unpaired Student’s t test, whereas one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves (log-rank test) were used to assess differences in survival. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1, R (version 4.3.0), or ImageJ software (version 1.54). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance (Exact p-values are provided directly within the figures whenever possible; otherwise, significance is denoted as (*, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. All experiments were performed at least three times with consistent results, or representative data are shown. For in vivo studies, mice were assigned to experimental groups to ensure comparable tumor volumes at the start of treatment.
