 Executive Summary

Wastewater from textile industries contains large amounts of reactive dyes and inorganic salts
(such as NaCl and Na2SO4), which pose serious environmental and health threats. Membrane
technology has attracted significant interest for dye desalination due to its high energy efficiency,
ease of operation and maintenance, and the ability to reuse dye-free salt solutions. The objective
of the project was to develop scalable hollow fiber membrane (HFM) modules with high water
and salt permeance, high dye rejection, and high salt/dye separation factor (SF).
 The project was broken down into two budget periods (BPs). During BP1, we
demonstrated technical feasibility using pencil modules containing ~50 cm 2 holey graphene
oxide (HGO) membranes and conducted preliminary process design, techno-economic analysis
(TEA), and life-cycle analysis (LCA). All milestones were met.
1. We developed and optimized amine-functionalized HGO, which exhibited excellent stability
 in aqueous solutions and adhered to both commercial flatsheet and hollow-fiber porous
 supports. The GO nanosheets were etched with hydrogen peroxide to create in-plane
 nanopores (10-30 nm) to reduce tortuosity and enhance water and salt permeance while
 maintaining dye rejection. Then, the obtained HGO was functionalized with a diamine or a
 triamine to improve its stability, adhesion to porous supports, and separation performance.
 UB thoroughly investigated the effect of amine type, content, and reaction time on the
 membrane structure and performance.
2. The aHGO was converted into flatsheet membranes. The processing parameters were
 optimized to improve the membrane separation performance. A membrane
 (1dHGO500/Nylon) was prepared from HGO cross-linked by ethylene diamine (EDA) at a
 mass ratio of 1:1. The membrane (5.7 cm2) showed a selective layer of ~500 nm and superior
 dye desalination performance, i.e., 100 LMH/bar and a CR rejection of >99.5%
3. aHGO was fabricated into HFM modules with areas of 13 and 88 cm 2. The 13 cm2 HFMs
 with 500 nm 1dHGO showed a water permeance of 70 LMH/bar, with ~99% rejection of 200
 mg/L Direct Red 80 (DR) and 9% rejection of 2 g/L Na2SO4. The membrane showed 99.6%
 rejection at 200 mg/L DR, an average water permeance of 75 LMH/bar, 6.6% rejection of 2
 g/L Na2SO4, and highly stable separation properties over 135 h of testing.
4. We identified a typical textile process wastewater composition for the stream entering
 tertiary-stage treatment, developed a preliminary model that extended the flatsheet data to
 bench-scale HFMs, and developed a preliminary process design and an associated TEA. The
 TEA included power costs, water recovery credits, and waste dye disposal costs. The
 sensitivity analysis of the economics on membrane desalination performance showed that
 retentate wastewater disposal and stage-cut dominate WW treatment costs. Trimeric
 researched the inputs to the LCA.
 The BP2 focused on the scale-up of membrane module fabrication and completion of the
TEA and LCA. There was a delay in scale-up fabrication of defect-free bench-scale modules. We
requested a 5-month no-cost extension, but it was not approved. Thus, we had to wrap up the
project at the original deadline (7/31/2025). The details are shown below.
1. We scaled up the synthesis of GO nanosheets to 2.6 g/batch and optimized membrane
 fabrication to reduce the rGO layer thickness by 50% while retaining high dye rejections.
 The flatsheet membrane showed a dye water permeance of 93-107 LMH/bar with
 outstanding dye rejection (>99.2%) across a pH range of 3 to 10, showcasing strong stability.

 Page 4 of 50 Final Report

2. We successfully prepared HFM modules with a surface area of 88 cm 2. When challenged
 with solutions containing mixed dyes (DR, CR, Methylene Blue), the module exhibited a
 stable dye-water permeance of 95 LMH/bar and 99.99% rejection of all dyes. The effect of
 stage-cut on dye desalination performance was thoroughly evaluated. For example, salt
 rejection ranged from 0 to 10% as the stage-cut decreased from 37% to 18%, and DR
 rejection increased from 47 (85% stage-cut) to 97% (6% stage-cut).
3. Bench-scale HFM modules with a surface area of 470 cm 2 were fabricated and showed high
 water and salt permeance and moderate dye rejection. However, due to time constraints, we
 were unable to optimize the module fabrication to achieve dye rejection above 99%.
4. We completed TEAs and LCAs using target rHGO properties, which revealed that the dye
 disposal cost and the water credits are similar for HFMs with rHGO targets and NF
 commercial properties, as dye rejections are similar. Capital and operating costs are lower for
 rHGO targets because NF permeance is lower than rHGO permeance. The processes differed
 in salt rejection across membranes, resulting in significantly more salt in the NF dye-rich
 water sent for incineration, thereby increasing freshwater ecotoxicity. Other tracked
 environmental burdens were similar.

1. Introduction
The textile industry in the U.S. had an output of $76.8 billion in 2018 and is one of the major
manufacturing sectors.1 With $28 billion in textile exports in 2018, the U.S. ranks 4th in textile
exports.1-4 Textile dyeing and finishing is one of the most chemically intensive industries and the
second-largest industrial polluter of clean water (after agriculture). The World Bank estimates
that 17-20% of global industrial water pollution comes from the textile industry. About 1,000-
3,000m3 of wastewater is produced in processing 12-20 tons of textiles. The wastewater contains
a significant number of toxic dyes, chemicals, and salts, which are harmful to the environment.
Figure 1 shows an example process train for wastewater treatment. The industry continually
searches for better solutions to decolorize the 200+ billion liters of effluent produced annually. 4

 One of the key separations is the removal of salts (e.g., NaCl and Na 2SO4) from dyes.
The salts are byproducts or used to enhance the dye uptake by textiles. By separating the dye
from the water and salt, a smaller, more concentrated dye waste stream can be produced, which
can be disposed of more economically. Additionally, the water recovered can be reused in the
process, thus reducing fresh water consumption. Nanofiltration (NF) and tight ultrafiltration (UF)
membranes with pores smaller than dye molecules have attracted significant interest in dye
desalination.5 However, conventional polyamide-based NF membranes exhibit high rejection of
divalent salts (such as Na2SO4) and moderate rejection of monovalent salts (such as NaCl),
resulting in low salt removal efficiency. More importantly, dye solutions usually have high
salinity (such as ~6.0 wt% NaCl and ~5.6 wt% Na2SO4) and thus very high osmotic pressure.
The high pressure required to overcome the significant osmotic pressure difference across the
membranes can make the process cost-intensive. UF membranes have high water permeance and
low salt rejection, while their dye rejections are too low to achieve satisfactory dye recovery.
Therefore, 20-50% of the dye is lost due to the high salt content and discharged into wastewater,
causing significant environmental harm.6 After 3-4 decades of exhaustive refining, these NF and
UF membranes have achieved the best balance of performance and costs, and any significant
improvement to address the challenge of dye desalination is unlikely. Membranes with high
water permeance, >99% dye rejection, and negligible salt rejection are needed for these
applications.
 Graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets have emerged as an exciting platform to construct
ultrathin membranes (<10 nm) with controllable nanochannels, achieving high water permeance
and superior dye/salt SF values. GO nanosheets can also be etched to obtain HGOs with in-plane
pores, resulting in membranes with reduced tortuosity and increased permeability to water and
salts. While HGO nanosheets are hydrophilic, enabling great processability in aqueous
dispersion, they are not stable for long-term underwater operation. Consequently, the nanosheets
are often chemically reduced with hydrazine or crosslinked with polyamines. The reduction

increases hydrophobicity and thus their underwater stability; however, it often decreases the
interlayer spacing, thereby decreasing water and salt permeance.

2. Project Goals/Objectives
 This project aimed to develop high-performance hollow fiber membranes (HFMs) by
synergistically combining chemical etching to form in-plane nanopores and polyamine cross-
linking, enabling the economically viable recovery of valuable dyes, reduced wastewater, and
mitigation of adverse environmental impacts.
 Our core technical activities combined three key innovations: (1) reduced holey graphene
oxide (rHGO) with right-sized nanochannels and low tortuosity exhibiting high water
permeance, low salt rejection, and high dye rejection; (2) porous hollow fiber (HF) supports
based on functional polymers with high packing density; and (3) cross-linkers to covalently bond
the rHGO to the HF support. This project consisted of two budget periods (BPs) to bring the
technology from TRL 2 (Technology concept and/or application formulated) to TRL 4
(Component and/or system validation in a laboratory environment). The BP1 demonstrates
technical feasibility using pencil modules containing ~50 cm 2 rHGO membranes. We optimize
the rHGO to achieve the best dye desalination performance and develop HFMs based on
functional polymers and cross-linkers to yield robust membranes. A preliminary TEA and LCA
model was developed, incorporating membrane performance, process design, and
materials/capital impacts. The BP2 demonstrated the scalability of the technology by developing
bench-scale modules containing 500 cm2 of membrane, resulting in savings of capital expense
and energy consumption. We tested the durability and lifetime performance of the modules,
which were incorporated into the TEA and LCA study.
 Four organizations with complementary expertise collaborated to develop the proposed
technology, including UB, DOE NETL, Helios-NRG, and Trimeric. The lead institution, UB is
the largest and most comprehensive member of the State University of New York system and
New York’s leading public center for graduate and professional education. UB has a
comprehensive research infrastructure to support projects like the one proposed here. DOE
NETL is a government-owned and operated lab that discovers, integrates, and matures
technology solutions to enhance the nation’s energy foundation and protect the environment for
future generations. Helios-NRG is a specialized technology development and consulting group
with proven experience in cross-cutting separations, clean energy, strategic management, and
commercial development. Trimeric serves the private industry and government by providing
process engineering and other specialized technical services, such as Life Cycle Assessments
(LCA).

Subtask 1.1: Preparation of amine-functionalized HGO.

Subtask 1.3: Prepare membranes on flatsheet support to optimize conditions.
 We optimized membrane dye-removal properties by manipulating 3 parameters: reaction
time, amine concentration, and selective layer thickness. First, the reaction time between the
HGO and diamine increases from 1.5 to 6 h, the obtained 1dHGO500/Nylon exhibits pure water
permeance decreasing from 188 to 120 LMH/bar while retaining dye water permeance at 96
LMH/bar (Figure 4a), and CR rejection increases from 76.3% to 99.6% (Figure 4b). Second,
increasing the mass ratio of diamine to GO decreases the pure water permeance before increasing
it for dHGO500/Nylon (Figure 4c), and increases it before decreasing CR rejection (Figure 4d).
The mass ratio of 1 yields a balanced combination of high dye-water permeance (100 LMH/bar)
and high dye rejection (>99.5%). Excessive diamine might cause defects, increasing water
permeance and decreasing dye rejection.

and (b) dye rejection of the 1dHGO500/Nylon membranes. Impact of diamine concentration on
(c) pure water and dye water permeance, and (d) dye rejection of the dHGO500/Nylon
membranes. Effect of the selective layer thickness of 1dHGO on (e) pure water and dye water
permeance, and (f) dye rejection. Experiments were conducted in dead-end cell at 350 rpm with
0.2 g/L dye.

 Third, increasing the 1dHGO thickness decreases water permeance and increases dye
rejection (Figure 4e,f). Additionally, increasing the selective layer thickness beyond 500 nm does
not significantly improve dye rejection. Similar behaviors are observed for the membranes

 We also performed long-term stability testing on the 1dHGO534/Nylon flatsheet

Subtask 6.2. Preliminary LCA
 The objective of this subtask was to define the LCA approaches and identify the key
databases required for the components involved. The detailed results are summarized in Task 11
(Conduct final TEA & LCA).
 Introduction to LCA for Dye Wastewater Treatment. A LCA is an environmental
assessment technique for evaluating the environmental impacts of a technology or product
through its lifecycle stages. The goals of an LCA study are generally: (1) to compile the relevant
material and energy inputs and environmental emissions; (2) to quantify the potential
environmental impacts resulting from the system inputs and outputs; and (3) to use this data to
support decision-making about the process. Performing an LCA early in the design process can
enable environmentally conscious design choices.
 The LCA literature on water treatment processes has typically focused on producing
water for human consumption, particularly seawater desalination. It is less common to apply
LCA to industrial water utilization processes. When LCA is applied, it is often done with the
main goal of comparing the environmental impact of a given quantity of water at a given
quality.8
 For this project, we were developing a membrane technology to treat salty dyewater from
the textile industry. Textile dyeing practices are highly fragmented across the industry, with
thousands of dye types used at various concentrations and combinations to dye a wide range of
fiber types (e.g., cellulose such as cotton, rayon, linen; synthetic fibers; proteins; etc.). Thus,
each facility’s wastewater and associated environmental impact may be unique, requiring
customizable wastewater treatment solutions.
 The objective of the LCA for this project was to quantify the potential environmental
benefits of our proposed new technology (rHGO membrane) for treating salty dye wastewater
effluent from the secondary wastewater treatment stage of a textile dyeing operation. The rHGO
membrane was compared to a more conventional nanofiltration membrane. Because the
application of rHGO technology was in its early conceptual phase, no specific textile wastewater
treatment plant was available to serve as the basis for this LCA. Therefore, this LCA was
performed with many simplifying assumptions. As explained in the section Design Basis
Parameters for LCA, LCA focused on the membrane operation step of the membrane’s life cycle.
This LCA started with a single, simplified water composition and technology performance, then
considered a set of sensitivity cases to illustrate the potential environmental benefits of the rHGO
technology.
 Design Basis Parameters for the Tertiary Processes. In primary treatment, solids are
settled and removed from wastewater. In secondary treatment, biological processes remove
soluble biodegradable organics. In tertiary treatment, non-biodegradable organics, including
fibers, metals, ions, color, odor, etc., are removed. Within each treatment step, multiple unit
operations may be employed. For tertiary treatment, techniques may include membrane
technologies (including the rHGO membrane, NF, and RO), adsorption (such as through clays
and silica), oxidation techniques, electrochemical processes, and more. NF and RO membranes
were used as tertiary treatment to recover dye and to produce a water stream for reuse. The LCA
compared the environmental impacts of these two tertiary treatment options.
 Definition of the rHGO case and fate of the permeate from rHGO. The permeate stream
from the rHGO membrane is dye-depleted, and it contains most of the wastewater’s salt content.
For this analysis, the baseline case assumed that the permeate stream can be recycled without

 Page 19 of 50 Final Report

further treatment by RO, thereby maximizing water recovery. As most textile dyeing plants use
sodium chloride- and sodium sulfate-based salts, recycling the salt-containing permeate water
was potentially viable. An alternative case could be explored in which an RO process treats the
permeate stream, resulting in a concentrated salt stream for disposal.
 The concentrated dyes in the retentate stream cannot be recovered and recycled. Textile
dyeing applications often involve multiple dyes in wastewater, and separating them for reuse is
not considered feasible. Furthermore, other constituents, such as microfiber bits, grease, and
phenols, will be concentrated in the dye retentate and thus require additional processing to
produce a dye suitable for recycling. Proposed options for the disposition of the concentrated dye
retentate stream include incineration, absorbent-based treatment to take up the dye and dispose of
the absorbent as chemical waste, evaporative drying in a pond, or embedding the dye stream in
concrete.
 Design Basis Parameters for LCA. System boundaries. ISO 14040 recommends
achieving the highest resolution in an LCA by focusing on differentiating between the individual
unit processes being compared. Therefore, for the LCA of textile dyeing wastewater treatment,
the focus was on the unit operations associated with tertiary treatment of water leaving the
secondary treatment system, as well as any downstream processing associated with the tertiary
treatment. Upstream processing steps (e.g., primary and secondary wastewater treatment, or
further upstream textile dyeing operations) were not included in the system boundary.
 A published review of the industrial WWT LCA literature concluded that the use of
membranes, compared with other tertiary processes, yields better process water quality but at the
expense of higher energy and chemical consumption.8 Therefore, the LCA comparison between
the novel rHGO technology and the nanofiltration membrane focused on differences in treated
water quality, energy consumption, and chemical consumption. This LCA focused on the life
cycle stages that are presumed to be significant contributors to the life cycle impact:
  Operational inputs: electricity, chemicals
  Operational outputs: permeate water stream, retentate dye stream
 The LCA excluded the life cycle stages for which data are not currently available and/or
that are presumed to be less significant contributors to life cycle impact:
  Manufacturing of the modules: Production and final disposal of membranes were seldom
 considered in previously published water treatment LCA studies. 8 However, the
 environmental impact of wastewater generated during membrane production can be
 significant; in a future analysis, this manufacturing wastewater stream should be
 compared between our proposed technology and the baseline nanofiltration membrane
 technology.
  Maintenance (i.e., cleaning) of the membrane: The water (or solvent) requirements for
 cleaning could be significant and are at present not quantified for our membrane. Water
 cleaning requirements for conventional nanofiltration membrane technology are also
 expected to be significant; therefore, excluding membrane-cleaning water from the
 current LCA is unlikely to materially affect the comparison of life cycle impacts between
 the two tertiary treatment systems.
  Installation and decommissioning of the tertiary treatment process, including the final
 disposal of the membranes. The system construction and system dismantling steps have a
 short duration compared to the process lifetime, so it is typical to allocate the impact of
 these short-duration steps across the total quantity produced, thereby diluting the
 environmental impacts of these two life cycle steps.

 Functional unit. The functional unit (FU) is the measure of the main system function or
performance, enabling comparison across different processes. For this tertiary water treatment
system, the functional unit will be the volume of water to be treated (m 3); this is the same
functional unit used to calculate the TEA costs.
 Life Cycle Inventory (LCI) is the organized input/output data that describes all
materials and energy consumption, emissions, and waste streams associated with the process; the
LCI was developed from the process heat and material balances.
 Impact assessment methodology and environmental categories. Life Cycle Impact
Assessment (LCIA) methods quantify the environmental impacts of processes, such as water use,
climate change, and toxicity. Different emissions that cause the same impact are converted into a
single unit of measure (common equivalence unit) for an impact category.
 The LCA considered the following environmental impact categories, to the extent data
are available for the substances of interest:
  Global Warming Potential/Carbon Footprint, reported in kg CO 2-eq
  Ozone Depletion Potential, reported in kg CFC-11 eq
  Eutrophication Potential (i.e., potential to cause over-fertilization of water and soil,
 increasing aquatic biomass growth, typically due to emissions of nitrogen or phosphorus-
 containing compounds), reported in kg NO3-eq
  Acidification Potential: potential acidification of the air to release of gases such as sulfur
 dioxide and subsequent deposition on the land, reported in kg SO 2-eq
  Water Consumption: an indicator of the relative amount of water used; when based on
 regionalized water scarcity factors, it is reported as m 3 world-equivalent deprived
  Human toxicity (cancer, non-cancer), reported in Comparative Toxic Units, human
 toxicity potential (CTUh)
  Ecotoxicity (freshwater), reported in Comparative Toxic Units, ecotoxicity potential
 (CTUe)
 There are many different LCIA methodologies available. We consulted the following two
LCIA methods regarding the availability of data on the dyes studied.
  The US EPA’s Tool for Reduction and Assessment of Chemicals and Other
 Environmental Impacts (TRACI) is one such impact assessment tool; it provides
 characterization factors for each of the environmental impact categories listed above.
  USEtox represents the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) / Society of
 Environmental Toxicology and Chemistry (SETAC) scientific consensus model for
 characterizing human and ecotoxicological impacts of chemical emissions in life cycle
 assessment. The primary output of the USEtox is a database of recommended and
 indicative characterization factors for human cancer toxicity, human non-cancer toxicity,
 and freshwater ecotoxicity, based on the modeling of environmental fate, exposure, and
 effect parameters for the substances.

Subtask 9.2: Evaluate long-term stability & understand fouling mechanisms
 We developed a systematic approach to evaluate and interpret stability and fouling
behavior in HFM modules. The HFM tests are conducted over 2–3 days. During this time, we
consistently observed stable pure water permeance of 83 LMH/bar at 10 psi and DR water
permeance of 72 LMH/bar, indicating good short-term stability. However, following dye
rejection tests, a significant drop in water permeance was observed. This decline is primarily
attributed to the adsorption of dye molecules onto the membrane surface, which impedes water
flow and highlights the membrane's susceptibility to fouling.
 To mitigate this, the HFMs are routinely sterilized with 0.1 M NaOH after each dye test.
This cleaning step effectively removes the adsorbed dye. Following NaOH washing, a partial
recovery in water permeance is consistently observed—approaching, but not always reaching,
the original values prior to dye exposure.

Subtask 9.3: Develop regeneration techniques
 Two regeneration techniques have been evaluated for small modules, including cleaning
with pure water and a NaOH solution (pH = 10). Both cleaning methods can restore water
permeance, but NaOH solution is more effective than pure water.

Task 10: Final process development and commercialization plan
 Final Process Development. We completed the investigation of actual dye wastewater
conditions and processes. A 280-m3/h wastewater feed flow rate is typical of a medium-sized
dying operation, and 200-mg/L dye and 2000-mg/L salt concentrations are typical for a feed
stream to the tertiary-stage of wastewater treatment.
 The originally proposed process concentrated the dye in the wastewater and recycled it to
the dyeing process, reducing dye consumption. This is a good concept, but difficult to implement
because the vast majority of textile plants use different types and colors of dyes for different
fabrics, and all of these end up mixed together in the wastewater stream, making dye reuse
unrealistic. Hence, several dye disposal options were considered, but not used, including dye
discharge (land-fill, composting, dewatering/landbank) due to dye toxicity; dye sludge for use in
cement due to the potential for dye leaching and the requirement for the cement plant to be

 Page 37 of 50 Final Report

nearby the dyeing plant; and gasification/pyrolysis or underground sequestration due to scale-up
complications and high cost. Adsorbing dye wastewater onto activated carbon was also
considered, but the dye loading onto the carbon is low when wet, and incinerating the carbon is
more expensive and less environmentally friendly than incinerating the dye stream directly to
convert the organic dyes to CO2 and water.
 Based on this, the preferred way to integrate the GO HFMs in the dye wastewater
treatment process is defined in Figure 33. The permeate stream, containing water and a small
amount of salt, is recycled back into the dyeing plant feed stream to replace makeup water,
which is valuable given high water costs. The retentate, which contains the concentrated dye
water, is incinerated. The process is intentionally run at an overall multi-unit, stage-cut of 98% to
maximize the permeate stream and minimize the retentate stream.

 Market Need: The U.S. textile industry had an output of ~$60.9 billion in 2025 and is a
major national manufacturing sector. It supports a supply chain that employs ~460,00 workers
nationwide. With exports of $27 billion in 2025, the U.S. ranks 2nd (after China) in global fiber,
textile, and apparel exports. Textile dyeing and finishing is one of the most chemically intensive
industries and the second-largest industrial polluter of clean water globally (after agriculture).
The World Bank estimates that ~20% of industrial water pollution comes from the textile
industry. About 1,000-3,000 m3 of wastewater is typically generated to process 12-20 tons of
textiles per day. Wastewater contains significant amounts of toxic dyes, chemicals, and salts,
which are harmful to the environment. The industry continually seeks better solutions for safely
disposing of the 200+ billion liters of effluent produced annually. Our target market opportunity

 The key separations of interest are the separation of dyes from water and the removal of
salts (e.g., NaCl and Na2SO4) from dyes. The salts are used to enhance the dye uptake by textiles.
NF and tight ultrafiltration (UF) membranes with pores smaller than dye molecules have
attracted significant interests for dye desalination. However, conventional polyamide-based NF
membranes have low permeability and high rejection of divalent salts (such as Na 2SO4) and
moderate rejection of monovalent salts (such as NaCl), resulting in low salt removal efficiency.
More importantly, dye solutions typically have high salinity (e.g., ~6.0 wt% NaCl and ~5.6 wt%
Na2SO4), resulting in very high osmotic pressure. The high pressure required to overcome the
significant osmotic pressure difference across the membranes can make the process cost-
intensive. UF membranes have high water permeance and low salt rejection, while their dye
rejections are too low to achieve satisfactory dye separation. Therefore, 20-50% of the dye is
often discharged into wastewater and, if left untreated, can cause significant harm to the
environment. After 3-4 decades of exhaustive refining, these NF and UF membranes have
achieved the best balance of performance and costs, and any significant improvement to address
the challenge of dye desalination is unlikely. Membranes with much higher water permeance,
>99% dye rejection, and negligible salt rejection are needed for these applications and are the
target of this project.

4. Conclusions and recommendations
 Conclusions. Table 15A,B summarizes the status of each task and milestone. We were
able to complete most milestones; however, due to time constraints, we were unable to complete
optimization of bench-scale modules (500 cm2) and parametric tests. We requested a 5-month
no-cost extension, but it was not approved.
 In summary, we have successfully demonstrated the potential of rGO-based HFM
modules with superior dye desalination performance. This approach reduced transport tortuosity
while maintaining strong dye retention, enabling membranes with high water permeance, low
salt rejection, and high dye rejection. HFM modules with surface areas of 13 and 88 cm 2 were
prepared and exhibited water permeance >90 LMH/bar, dye rejection >99%, and NaCl &
Na2SO4 rejection <10%, meeting the project targets. Bench-scale modules (~500 cm 2) were also
synthesized; however, due to time constraints for optimization, the modules exhibited high water
and salt permeance but low dye rejection (<90%).
 The TEA and LCA studies indicated that rHGO membranes could provide advantages
over commercial nanofiltration membranes if the target properties are achieved at scale. The
main economic drivers were retentate disposal cost and stage-cut, while the main environmental
benefit came from the low salt rejection of rHGO membranes, which reduces the amount of salt
sent with dye-rich waste streams.

 Lessons Learned. The project provided several important lessons on the design of high-
performance membranes for dye desalination.
a) Membrane performance depends strongly on the careful control of nanosheet structure and
 transport pathways. Optimizing membrane fabrication and module scale-up requires
 systematically balancing multiple variables, including reaction time, amine content, and
 selective-layer thickness.
b) Membrane modules must be evaluated in the context of real feed complexity. Separation
 behavior with real wastewater can differ greatly from that observed in single-solute tests, as
 it contains mixed dyes, salts, surfactants, fibers, suspended solids, grease, phenols, pH-
 adjusting chemicals, and other organic additives.
c) Process designs incorporating membrane systems should be carefully assessed to understand
 how enhanced membrane properties may improve the efficiency of dye desalination.
 The work established a strong scientific foundation for the design of low-tortuosity, dye-
rejecting, and salt-permeable 2D membranes. Future development should focus on improving
large-area coating uniformity, fouling resistance, and realistic wastewater testing. With these

efficient textile wastewater treatment, dye concentration, reuse of salt-containing water, and
