Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells derived platelet lysate promotes diabetic wound healing
Authors: Ying Zhang, Lingna Wang, Zhongyuan Zheng, Haitao Yuan, Xiankun Yin, Chenyu Zhang, Jiaqing Liu, Chao Li, Qun Ma, Long Zhang, Fangfang Zhu, Wenhui Wang, Lu Tie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic wounds represent an escalating clinical challenge driven by the rising global prevalence of diabetes and the lack of effective treatments. Platelets serve as natural reservoirs of diverse growth factors, holding considerable promise for promoting tissue regeneration and wound repair. However, current platelet-derived therapies are constrained by donor dependence, protocol variability, and inconsistent product quality.MethodsThis study established an efficient in vitro protocol for generating functional platelets from human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs). These regenerative platelets were subsequently lysed to produce induced human platelet lysate (ihPL). The contents of key growth factors in ihPL and human peripheral blood platelet lysate (hPL) were compared by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). We established diabetic mice wound model to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ihPL on wound healing. Furthermore, in vitro experiments assessed the effects of ihPL on dermal fibroblasts proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis. Transcriptome sequencing and Western blot (WB) were employed to elucidate the underlying signaling pathways.ResultsHSPCs differentiation into megakaryocytes exceeded 80%, with a subsequent platelet production efficiency of 38.3% ± 5.35%. ELISA revealed that the concentrations of fibroblast growth factor (140.1 ± 0.986 vs. 1.0 ± 0.008) and vascular endothelial growth factor (12.74 ± 5.280 vs. 1.0 ± 0.276) were significantly elevated in ihPL compared with hPL. In diabetic mice, ihPL (89.44% ± 9.83%), hPL (73.60% ± 12.21%), and epidermal growth factor (EGF, 59.72% ± 12.87%) significantly accelerated wound closure compared with the vehicle control group (43.53% ± 16.09%) at day 14 (P < 0.05). Notably, ihPL demonstrated superior efficacy relative to both hPL and EGF (P < 0.05). Histological analysis and Optical coherence tomography angiography confirmed enhanced extracellular matrix deposition and angiogenesis with ihPL versus vehicle control. Furthermore, ihPL significantly promoted dermal fibroblasts proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis in vitro. Transcriptome analyses indicated activation of multiple tissue regeneration-associated signaling pathways and WB further confirmed the upregulation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway representing a potential underlying mechanism.ConclusionThis study establishes a standardized, donor-independent, and immunologically safer cell-based therapeutic strategy for diabetic wound management, with considerable potential for clinical translation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10020-026-01520-6.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers are among the most common and debilitating complications of diabetes, affecting up to 34% of individuals with type 1 or type 2 diabetes during their lifetime (Armstrong et al. 2023). Chronic wounds in diabetic patients often exhibit impairments in re-epithelialization, abnormal extracellular matrix remodeling, and persistent inflammation, leading to prolonged healing time, high recurrence rates, and a higher risk of amputation and mortality (McDermott et al. 2023). Given these challenges, growth factor-based therapies have emerged as promising strategies to enhance diabetic wound healing (Marti-Carvajal et al. 2015; Zheng et al. 2023). Various growth factors, including platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) (Li et al. 2008; Brown et al. 1994), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Chereddy et al. 2015), epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Hajimiri et al. 2016; Gainza et al. 2014), fibroblast growth factor (FGF) (Huang et al. 2016; Miyoshi et al. 2005), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) (Chesnoy et al. 2003), have demonstrated potential in accelerating the healing of diabetic wounds.

Platelets are natural reservoirs of diverse growth factors and cytokines. Upon activation, platelets release a broad array of growth factors and cytokines (Nurden 2011), including VEGF, PDGF, basic FGF (bFGF), EGF, and TGF-β, making them a valuable source of bioactive molecules for promoting diabetic wound healing. These properties have led to the development of platelet-derived therapies, such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy, which have been investigated for their therapeutic potential in enhancing tissue regeneration and accelerating wound healing (OuYang et al. 2023).

However, the clinical application of platelet-derived therapy is hindered by several limitations. These include variability in preparation protocols (Rahman et al. 2024; Pachito et al. 2020), a short shelf life, donor dependence, and inconsistent platelet quality, particularly in diabetic patients whose platelet function is often impaired (Shi et al. 2018; Ajjan et al. 2021; Davi et al. 1990). Moreover, platelet-derived products, such as PRP, are typically heterogeneous mixtures that contain platelets, plasma components, and residual leukocytes, complicating standardization and rendering them only empirical rather than well-defined therapies (Shao et al. 2020; He et al. 2022). These limitations are significant challenges to the clinical implementation of platelet-derived therapies in diabetic foot ulcer management.

To overcome these limitations, we developed a defined and scalable system to generate pure functional platelets from human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in vitro. These in vitro-generated platelets (iPLT) closely resemble peripheral blood platelets (PLT) with regard to morphology, structure, and function. Moreover, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) revealed the enrichment of pathways that are involved in wound healing and angiogenesis in these in vitro-generated platelets, supporting their pro-regenerative potential. We prepared induced human platelet lysate (ihPL) with these pure in vitro-generated platelets, which retains high concentrations of growth factors and enables precise control over product composition. The present study evaluated the therapeutic potential of ihPL in diabetic wound healing. In diabetic mouse models, ihPL significantly accelerated wound closure by promoting extracellular matrix deposition and angiogenesis. We assessed its biological effects on human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs), demonstrating increases in proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis in vitro. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, transcriptomic profiling and pathway enrichment analyses revealed the activation of key regenerative pathways, including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT signaling, which was further validated by Western blot analysis, confirming pathway activation in HDFs.

Collectively, our study provides the evidence of the therapeutic efficacy of in vitro–generated pure platelet, establishing them as a standardized, donor-independent, and immunologically safer cell-based therapy with significant promise for allogeneic application in diabetic wound management.

Materials and methods

Platelet differentiation from HSPCs

Human CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells were purchased from Milestone (Shanghai) Biotechnologies Co. Ltd. (China), and cultured in StemSpan™ Serum-Free Expansion Medium II (SFEM II, StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) at 37 ℃ for 7 days, supplemented with 20 ng/ml TPO, hSCF, hIL6, hIL-3, Flt3 ligand (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) along with SR1 (1 μM) (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA). Medium was changed every other day. On day 8, culture medium was changed with IMDM, supplemented with 20 ng/ml TPO, hSCF and hIL6, alone with chemical compounds KP457 (15 μM), GNF351 (0.5 μM), Y39983 (0.5 μM) and SR1 (1 μM) (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA). Cells were cultured at 39 ℃ for 10 days.

Quantification of platelets

Obtained platelets were resuspended DPBS and counted using BD Trucount™ Absolute Counting Tubes (BD Bioscience, Cat. 340,334), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, platelet suspensions were incubated with PE-conjugated anti-human CD41 antibody (BioLegend) and APC-conjugated anti-human CD42b antibody (BioLegend) for 15 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. After staining, 450 μl of DPBS was added to dilute the samples, which were then transferred into absolute counting tubes and incubated for an additional 15 min. Data acquisition was performed using a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer. Flow cytometric analysis was performed to validate the efficiency of platelet differentiation from HSPCs, using CD41 and CD42b as platelet lineage-specific surface markers (n = 6).

Preparation of platelet lysate

Human platelet lysate (hPL) was prepared with platelets isolated from peripheral blood purchased from Milestone (Shanghai) Biotechnologies Co. Ltd. (China) and ihPL was prepared with platelets differentiated from HSPCs.

For the preparation of hPL, the whole blood from healthy donors (18–60 years of age, at least 150 × 10^9/L platelet count, had not taken any medication recently and were not at risk of transfusion transmissible infections) was drawn into anticoagulated tubes with sodium citrate (INSEPACK, Japan) to avoid platelet activation and degranulation. The preparation of hPL was started within 30 min after whole blood was collected. The peripheral blood was first centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min (acceleration 4, deceleration 0) to obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP). The PRP was then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min to collect the platelet pellet. For the preparation of ihPL, induced platelets were collected from the culture medium at the end of differentiation. Briefly, the collected medium was centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min to remove residual nucleated cells. The supernatant was then transferred to new tubes and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min to pellet the platelets. Finally, platelets from both groups were resuspended in DPBS for subsequent preparation of platelet lysates.

The platelet concentration was frozen and thawed three times at − 80 °C for 1 h and 37 °C until completely thawing. The resulting product was centrifuged at 6000 g for 30 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was platelet lysate, which were filtered by 0.22 μm filters (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The final hPL and ihPL corresponded to lysate derived from 1.11 × 10⁹ platelets per mL in DPBS.

Flow cytometry

The induced platelets were stained with anti-CD41 APC and anti-CD42b PE antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Flow cytometry was performed on a Beckman CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA), and analyzed using FlowJo.

Immunofluorescent staining

The collected platelets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at RT. Following permeabilizing with 0.1% Triton-X-100, samples were blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at RT. Then, platelets were incubated with CD41a Antibody (HIP8) and PF4 antibody overnight at 4 ℃. Samples were washed in PBS and incubated for 1 h protected from light with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies. Following washing with PBS, platelets were mounted with AntiFade Mounting Medium (with DAPI). The labeled-platelets were spun onto slides using a Cytospin centrifuge for microscopy.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of FGF, VEGF, EGF, and TGF-β1 in ihPL and hPL were measured using ELISA kit (ExCell Bio, Suzhou, China). Serum samples of mice were collected on POD 14. VEGF, TGF-β1, PDGF in serum were quantified by using ELISA kits (Fine Test, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance was measured at the corresponding wavelengths using a microplate reader.

Cell culture

HDFs were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity.

Cell viability assay

The viability of HDFs was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Share-bio, Hangzhou, China). HDFs cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured with either FBS or ihPL. After 72 h, the cells were gently washed with PBS and incubated with CCK-8 for 3 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following incubation, the OD value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy2, USA).

EdU staining assay

Cells were seeded on coverslips placed in 24-well plates and incubated with 10 µM 5‑Ethynyl‑2'‑deoxyuridine (EdU, Lumiprobe MD, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 12 h. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% (wt/vol) formaldehyde (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in PBS and incubated with 100 mM Tris (Yuanye Bio-Techonology, Shanghai, China). Cells were then permeabilized using 0.1% TritonX100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Freshly prepared EdU staining solution was applied to the cells for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were stained with DAPI (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 20 min. Coverslips were mounted and allowed to dry overnight. Fluorescent images were captured using Leica DM6 B microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and analyzed using ImageJ.

Scratch assay

HDF cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells/ml in a Culture-Insert 2 Well (Ibidi, Martinsried, Germany). After 24 h of incubation, the insert was gently removed, and the wells were replenished with either fresh serum-free medium or ihPL-supplemented medium. The wound area was monitored and photographed at 0-, 6-, and 12-h post-insert removal, with five distinct fields captured per condition. The images were subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software to quantify the percentage of wound closure.

Western blot analysis

1 × 106 cells were lysed in RIPA Lysis Buffer I (Sangon Biotech, China) supplemented with PhostopTM (Roche, United States) on ice for 40 min. 1 ml ihPL or hPL was lysed in RIPA Lysis Buffer I containing protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min. The total protein concentration was detected by the BCA Protein Concentration Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Subsequently, the samples were mixed with 4 × LDS Sample Buffer (Tanon, China) and heated in a metal bath at 90 ℃ for 5 min. Then the samples were loaded onto 4–20% Bis–Tris gradient precast gel (Tanon, China) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The blots were blocked in 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 and incubated with the following antibodies overnight at 4 °C (Supplemental Table 4). After incubation with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, an ECL kit (Tanon, China) were used to detect the protein signals. Developed membranes were imaged using the Touch Imager XLi (e-Blot). Selected images were exported and quantified using ImageJ.

Mice

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from the animal facility of the Peking University Health Science Center. 8-week-old male B6.BKS(D)-Leprdb/J (db/db) mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). Mice were housed in the animal facility of the Peking University Health Science Center, maintained at 23± 1 °C (50 ± 5% relative humidity) with 12 h light/dark cycles with free access to water and a regular chow diet. All animal procedures associated with this work were conducted after approval by the ethics committee of Peking University (EC file no. 2020422–01).

To establish a STZ-induced diabetic mouse model, the C57BL/6 J mice were intraperitoneally injected with streptozocin (60 mg/kg body weight, 50 mM citrate buffer, pH 4.5, Sigma- Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 consecutive days. After fasted for 6 h, the blood glucose levels were measured by an ACCU-CHEK Aviva blood glucose monitor (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Once blood glucose level reached above 250 mg/dL, daily measurements continued for one week prior to experiments.

Wound healing experiment

Full-thickness excisional wounds were made as previously described (Shi et al. 2018). In brief, wounds were made on shaved back skin by using 6-mm punch biopsy (Acuderminc., Fort Lauderdale, FL, USA) under general anaesthesia. Then a bioclusive transparent dressing (Johnson & Johnson, Milpitas, CA, USA) were applied. Digital photographs of wounds were obtained on the day of surgery and every other day post-injury. Blinded measurements of wound size were performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, version 1.46r, USA). Wound closure rates were calculated as the following formulation: (wound area on day 0 − wound area on day X)/wound area on day 0 × 100%.

The STZ induced diabetic mice were divided into four separate groups and injected subcutaneously around the wound with 100 μL of either ihPL, hPL, EGF, or the vehicle control at the time of wounding, POD (Postoperative Day) 5 and POD 10. The db/db mice were randomly divided into two groups and respectively injected subcutaneously around the wound with 100 μl of ihPL or vehicle control (DPBS) at the time of wounding, POD 5 and POD 10.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)-based angiography (OCTA)

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) provides a non-invasive technique for visualizing blood vessels, which has led to its widespread adoption in recent years (Kalra et al. 2022). By allowing in vivo imaging of capillary blood flow and skin anatomy, OCTA qualitatively and quantitatively assesses vascular responses to skin injuries, thereby helping to determine wound severity and likely healing outcomes. The anesthetized mice were placed flat and their wounds were respectively placed on the camera of the optical coherence tomography angiography instrument (isOCT, 4D-ISOCT Optoprobe, Burnaby, Canada). Subsequently, the target vascular signals around the wound were reconstructed by chromatography and algorithm to form a three-dimensional spatial vascular network and quantitatively assessed using an analysis software (Version 2.0) from OptoProbe Research Ltd. It is recommended to refer to Supplemental Table 2 for detailed definition and description of quantitative vessel features (Liu et al. 2024).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The wound and surrounding tissue were harvested on POD 14 for histological assessments. Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned 5 mm thick. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Serivebio, China) and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed using standard protocols.

For immunohistochemical staining of CD31, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and then incubated with anti-CD31 at 4 °C overnight. A secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) was used to incubate for 45 min at RT. Immunohistochemistry was performed using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) color test kit (BIORIGIN, BN20341, Beijing, China). The cell nuclei were stained using hematoxylin for 30 s. The tissues were differentiated using hydrochloric acid in ethanol for 30 s, rinsed with water for 15 min, and dehydrated using aqueous alcohol and xylene. The glass slides were sealed with neutral resin and observed under a microscope.

All tissues in each section were observed first under 100 × magnification, then 3 fields were selected according to tissue size and expression for analysis at 400 × magnification. Mean optical density in the images was measured using Fiji/ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining assay

HDF cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well and cultured with FBS or ihPL for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by three 2-min washes with PBS. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with PBS containing 3% BSA for 30 min at RT. Primary antibody incubation was carried out using rabbit monoclonal antibodies specific for collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1) and collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1), with overnight incubation at 4 °C. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, ab150081, Abcam) at RT in the dark for 1 h. Cells were finally incubated with DAPI at RT for 10 min, and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Fluorescent images were captured using Leica DM6 B microscope and analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, version 1.46r, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Weng et al. 2024). The extracted RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time, Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis (qPCR) was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq GC (Perfect Real Time, Takara) following the manufacturer's instructions, and the reactions were conducted on an ABI 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System. Data analysis was performed using Expression Suite v1.1, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences used for the amplification of human genes are listed in the Supplemental Table 3.

RNA-sequencing

Platelet samples were isolated and then dissolved in Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The wound tissues of db/db mice receiving vehicle or ihPL were collected on POD 14. The total RNA of platelet samples and wound tissues was extracted by standard extraction methods (Weng et al. 2024). The RNA purification, reverse transcription, library preparation, and sequencing were performed at Berry Genomics Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) using the Illumina Novaseq platform according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HISAT2 software was used to compare Clean Reads with the reference genome to obtain the positioning information of Reads on the reference genome. FeatureCounts software was used for quantification analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.42.0) in R (4.3.1). Heatmaps and volcano plots were generated using the pheatmap package (1.0.12) and the ggplot2 package (3.4.3), respectively. GO enrichment analysis was carried out using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.1), and GSEA analysis was performed using GSEA software (4.3.2). Deconvolution of the cellular composition with MCP-counter The R package software MCP-counter was applied to the normalized log2-transformed FPKM expression matrix to produce the absolute abundance scores for 15 major immune cell types. The deconvolution profiles were then hierarchically clustered and compared across vehicle control and ihPL treatment groups.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the means ± SEM. Data analysis in the work was carried out using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0). The statistical differences between groups were obtained utilizing a two-tailed unpaired t-test. When more than 2 treatment groups were compared, two-way ANOVA with the Newman–Keuls test was used. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Efficient in vitro generation of functional platelets from HSPCs

After two-weeks of differentiation, the generation of megakaryocytes (MKs) was validated by flow cytometry. Across multiple independent batches, more than 80% of live nucleated cells were identified as MKs based on CD41 expression (87.58% ± 5.87%, Figure S1A, B). Following an additional 3 days of maturation, the resulting cell suspension was harvested for further analysis. Flow cytometry revealed that the 38.3% ± 5.35% of events within the platelet-sized gate (Cortin et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2015) co-expressed the canonical platelet markers CD41 and CD42b (Fig. 1B). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed the expression of CD41 and PF4, and these cells exhibited typical platelet features morphologically, including an anucleate structure and extended filopodia (Fig. 1C).

To explore the levels of growth factors relevant to wound healing in the induced platelets and native platelets, the purified induced platelets and native platelets from peripheral blood were comparatively subjected to freeze–thaw cycles to generate ihPL and hPL. The levels of key growth factors of ihPL and hPL were quantified by ELISA (Fig. 1D) and WB (Fig. 1E). Compared with hPL, FGF (ELISA: 140.10 ± 0.99 vs. 1.00 ± 0.01, WB: 0.91 ± 0.56 vs. 3.37 ± 0.79) and VEGF (ELISA: 12.74 ± 5.28 vs. 1.00 ± 0.28, WB: 0.88 ± 0.32 vs. 1.62 ± 0.46) were markedly elevated in ihPL (P < 0.05). In contrast, EGF and TGF-β1 in ihPL were detected relatively lower than hPL (Figure S1C, D). Together, these findings indicate that the induced platelets exhibited a distinct growth-factor profile, with selective enrichment of certain pro-regenerative factors, supporting their therapeutic potential utility in wound-healing applications.

To further characterize molecular features of the induced platelets, we performed bulk RNA-seq to compare induced-platelets with platelets from peripheral blood. The induced-platelets exhibited significant increases in the gene expression of key wound-healing factors VEGFA, bFGF, PDGFA, and PDGFB, indicated by the heatmap and volcano plot analyses (Fig. 1F, G). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed the enrichment of pathways that are related to wound healing and cytokine production in induced platelets (Fig. 1H). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated the upregulation of the platelet α granule pathway, indicating a greater abundance of α granules in the in vitro-generated platelets (Figure S1E), which were known to function in inflammation and angiogenesis, and support wound healing (Blair and Flaumenhaft 2009). Pathways of wound healing and angiogenesis were also significantly upregulated in the in vitro-differentiated platelets (Figure S1E). These results further support the therapeutic potential of induced platelets for wound healing applications.

ihPL facilitates wound healing in diabetic mouse models

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ihPL in diabetic wound repair, we established two mouse models of diabetes-induced cutaneous injury: streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice and diabetic db/db mice.

In the STZ-induced model (Fig. 2A), mice were comparatively treated with ihPL, hPL, vehicle control (DPBS), or positive control (EGF). All treatments accelerated wound closure compared with the vehicle control group, with ihPL showing better efficacy than hPL and both outperforming EGF (Fig. 2B, C). On POD 6, the wound closure ratios were 15.28% ± 9.52% in the vehicle control group, 42.19% ± 6.32% in the ihPL group, 41.59% ± 14.75% in the hPL group, and 38.32% ± 13.70% in the EGF group. Compared with the vehicle control group, ihPL, hPL and EGF all significantly accelerated wound closure (P < 0.01). There was no significant difference among ihPL, hPL and EGF (P > 0.05). On POD 14, the wound closure ratios were 43.53% ± 16.09% in the vehicle control group, 89.44% ± 9.83% in the ihPL group, 73.60% ± 12.21% in the hPL group, and 59.72% ± 12.87% in the EGF group. Compared with the vehicle control group, ihPL, hPL and EGF all significantly accelerated wound closure (P < 0.05). Additionally, ihPL showed better efficacy than hPL and EGF (P < 0.05). In contrast, previous experiments that used human peripheral blood-derived PRP showed impairments in wound healing of diabetic mice (Figure S1F). ihPL treatment enhanced growth factor levels in diabetic models

To explore the effect of different treatments on growth factors levels, serum concentrations of VEGF, TGF-β1 and PDGF were measured in diabetic mouse models on POD14 by ELISA. In STZ-induced diabetic mice, serum PDGF levels were significantly elevated in the ihPL, hPL, and EGF groups compared with the vehicle control group (876.06 ± 215.23 pg/ml, 912.74 ± 169.13 pg/ml, and 1132.02 ± 367.23 pg/ml vs. 211 90 ± 106.15 pg/ml). Notably, significant increases in serum VEGF and TGF-β1 levels were observed exclusively in the ihPL group compared with the vehicle control group (77.21 ± 49.00 pg/ml vs. 0.00 ± 0.00 pg/ml, and 409.50 ± 80.27 ng/ml vs. 288.57 ± 63.98 ng/ml; Fig. 4C). In db/db mice, serum VEGF levels were significantly higher in the ihPL group than in the vehicle control group (69.24 ± 4.38 pg/ml vs. 17.72 ± 14.96 pg/ml; Fig. 4D). Although the differences did not reach statistical significance, the average serum levels of PDGF and TGF-β1 levels were numerically higher in the ihPL group than in the control group (6384.97 ± 1207.62 pg/ml vs. 4549.62 ± 2574.41 pg/ml, 440.83 ± 139.75 ng/ml vs. 329.72 ± 62.75 ng/ml). Collectively, these results indicate that ihPL might enhance the release of key growth factors, thereby facilitating wound healing.

ihPL promotes wound healing with involvement of PI3K/AKT signaling

To identify key signaling mediators of ihPL-induced tissue repair, we further analyzed the transcriptomic data for enriched pathways. PI3K/AKT signaling emerged as a prominently activated cascade, along with mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, all of which are known to be dysregulated in diabetic wounds because of impairments in insulin signaling and oxidative stress (Bonnici et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2023 ) (Fig. 5C).

To further confirm the functional relevance of PI3K/AKT signaling in mediating the regenerative effects of ihPL, HDFs were treated with an AKT inhibitor. CCK-8 and EdU assays revealed that AKT inhibition reduced basal proliferation and attenuated the ihPL-induced increase in cell proliferation (Fig. 7E-G). Consistently, two-way ANOVA demonstrated a significant ihPL × AKT inhibitor interaction, supporting a conditional effect of AKT inhibition on the ihPL-induced proliferative response (Fig. 7E, F). Because AKT inhibition also affected basal proliferation, these data do not support AKT as the sole mediator of the ihPL-induced response. In addition, immunofluorescent staining showed that AKT inhibition significantly reduced the expression of COL1A1(Fig. 7H). Together, these data suggest that AKT signaling contributes, at least in part, to ihPL-associated proliferative and matrix-related responses, although the involvement of other pathways cannot be excluded.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that ihPL rapidly activates the PI3K/AKT signaling in fibroblasts, with concurrent changes in mTOR phosphorylation, and suggest that AKT signaling contributes, at least in part, to ihPL-associated proliferative and matrix-related responses (Fig. 7I). Transcriptomic profiling also indicated enrichment of MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways in addition to PI3K/AKT signaling (Fig. 5C). Accordingly, in the present study, we examined the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis as one candidate pathway associated with ihPL treatment in HDFs.

Discussion

In the present study, we defined a scalable system for generating pure, functional in vitro-generated platelets from human HSPCs. Derived ihPL, which delivered high concentrations of growth factors including VEGF and FGF, enhanced HDFs proliferation, migration, and collagen synthesis in vitro and accelerated wound healing, ECM deposition, and angiogenesis in diabetic mouse models. RNA-seq identified the upregulation of regenerative pathways, and PI3K/AKT signaling was validated as one of the functionally relevant pathways. These findings establish ihPL as a standardized, donor-independent, allogeneic therapy for diabetic wounds.

In contrast to conventional platelet-derived products, our study provided the evidence that in vitro-generated platelets have therapeutic potential for promoting diabetic wound healing. These induced platelets retained abundant growth factors and exhibited higher expression of key wound-healing factors, including FGF and VEGF. The GO analysis further revealed the enrichment of wound healing- and cytokine-related pathways, supporting their pro-regenerative profile. In vitro-generated platelets overcome key limitations of traditional platelet-derived products, including donor dependence and inconsistent platelet quality, offering a standardized alternative. As a cell-free derivative of these platelets, ihPL is mass-producible, long-term preservable, easily transportable, and compositionally stable, making it suitable for off-the-shelf clinical use.

The present study has several limitations. First, although we successfully established an efficient method for platelet production, further optimization is needed, such as engineering starting cells to overexpress wound-healing factors. Second, the study's reliance on a specific mouse model may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader diabetic populations. Validation in clinical trials is needed. Third, the use of time points for evaluating wound healing outcomes may not fully capture the dynamic nature of the healing process. Long-term effects of ihPL on diabetic wound healing, such as scar formation, require further investigation. Fourth, our inhibitor experiments and two-way ANOVA interaction analyses support that AKT signaling contributes to ihPL-associated proliferative responses, but the effect is not exclusively AKT-dependent because AKT inhibition also affected basal cellular activity. Transcriptomic enrichment of MAPK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways further suggests that ihPL-mediated repair involves multiple signaling pathways.

In conclusion, the promising results of applying in vitro-generated platelets lysate in diabetic wound healing highlight their potential as a novel therapeutic strategy. Future studies should focus on optimizing the production protocol, validating the therapeutic targets, and ultimately conducting clinical trials to establish the safety and efficacy of in vitro-generated platelets lysate in human populations.
