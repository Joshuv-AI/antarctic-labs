Newton’s problem of minimal resistance in Lorentz-Minkowski space
Rafael López

Abstract
. We extend Newton’s problem of minimal resistance to the Lorentz-Minkowski space. We derive the functional energy and determine the Euler-Lagrange equation. In contrast to the Euclidean case, this equation is quasilinear elliptic, and thus, a maximum principle holds in this context. We obtain the solutions of separable variables of this equation via separation of variables. Furthermore, we find all radial solutions to the problem, which present conical singularities at the origin. We also analyze the Single Shock Condition.

1.
Statement of Newton’s problem in Lorentz-Minkowski space

The purpose of this paper is to extend Newton’s problem of minimal resistance to the Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{L}^{3}
. In Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and proposed by Newton in his
Principia Mathematica
, the problem consists of finding the shape of a solid body that experiences minimal resistance while moving at a constant velocity through a homogeneous fluid. The literature on this problem is extensive, and from a mathematical viewpoint, we refer the reader to the surveys
[
2
,
4
]
and the references therein. Variants of Newton’s problem have been considered recently regarding the type of bodies, collisions, and fluid
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
7
,
11
,
16
,
17
]

The motivation for extending Newton’s problem to
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is that the metric of
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is not Riemannian which implies important differences. The existence of vectors whose self-inner product may be zero or negative is the reason for the interest in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
in Special Relativity.
First, we provide the necessary context following formally the same approach as in the classical problem. Consider the Lorentz-Minkowski space
\mathbb{L}^{3}
, which is the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Cartesian coordinates
(x,y,z)
, and endowed with the Lorentzian metric
\langle,\rangle=dx^{2}+dy^{2}-dz^{2}
.

The assumptions of the problem in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
are the same as in Euclidean space with the only difference being that the metric used to measure angles is now the Lorentzian metric of
\mathbb{L}^{3}
. Thus, we assume that the fluid is uniform, its particles are non-interacting, move with the same velocity, and when they collide with the (external) surface
S
of the body
\mathcal{B}
, they transfer their normal momentum without friction. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the model satisfies the sine-squared pressure law, which requires the computation of angles between vectors. In
\mathbb{L}^{3}
, there is no a natural notion of angle between arbitrary vectors in general, except when that the vectors are timelike. Therefore, the differences with respect to the Euclidean setting are as follows:

(1)

It is important to specify the direction of the fluid. In
\mathbb{L}^{3}
there are spacelike, lightlike, and timelike directions. In our model, the chosen direction of the fluid is the
z
-axis (time coordinate in Relativity) which is of the timelike type. Let
\vec{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)
.

(2)

The surface
S
of
\mathcal{B}
is spacelike. A spacelike surface in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is a surface whose induced metric is Riemannian. This implies that its normal vector
N
is timelike. This allows us to well-define the angle between
N
and the direction
\vec{e}_{3}
of the particles.

Figure 1.
The model of the resistance problem in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
. The initial velocity of the particles is
\vec{e}_{3}=-\sinh\theta T+\cosh\theta N
, and the final velocity is
\vec{v}_{f}=-\sinh\theta T-\cosh\theta N
.

After determining the direction in which the particles move, and the type of body they collide with, the resistance problem in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
can be formulated as follows:
find the body whose boundary surface is spacelike and offers minimum or maximum resistance to the motion within a fluid whose particles move with constant velocity in the direction
\vec{e}_{3}
.

We specify the mathematical framework of the problem (Fig.
1
). Assume that
S
is the graph of a function
u
defined on a domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where we identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the
xy
-plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We assume that the admissible functions belongs to the class of
C^{1}
piecewise functions on
\overline{\Omega}
. Since
S
is spacelike, it follows that
|Du|<1
in
\Omega
. The unit normal vector of
S
is

(1)

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}}\left(Du,1\right).

We compute the resistance of
\mathcal{B}
as it moves through the fluid. We assume that each particle has unit mass and unit speed, and that the velocity is
\vec{v}_{i}=\vec{e}_{3}
. Since
\vec{v}_{i}
and
N
are timelike and
N
points upwards, there exists
\theta>0
such that

(2)

\langle\vec{v}_{i},N\rangle=\langle\vec{e}_{3},N\rangle=-\cosh\theta.

The number
\theta
is called the angle between
\vec{v}_{i}
and
N
. Consider the plane containing
\vec{v}_{i}
and the final velocity
\vec{v}_{f}
of the particle after colliding with
S
. Let
\{\vec{e}_{x},\vec{e}_{3}\}
be a unit basis for this plane (Fig.
1
). Suppose that the point where the particle collides with
S
is
(x_{0},y_{0},u(x_{0},y_{0}))
, where
\vec{e}_{x}=\frac{Du}{|Du|}
.
Decomposing
\vec{v}_{i}
into its tangential and normal components with respect to
S
, we have

\vec{v}_{i}=-\sinh\theta T+\cosh\theta N.

When a particle collides with
S
, the tangential component of its final velocity
\vec{v}_{f}
remains unchanged, while the normal component changes sign. Thus,

\vec{v}_{f}=-\sinh\theta T-\cosh\theta N.

It follows that
\vec{v}_{f}-\vec{v}_{i}=-2\cosh\theta N
. The resistance contributed by a single particle is the component of its momentum in the direction of
\vec{v}_{i}
, namely,

\langle\vec{v}_{f}-\vec{v}_{i},\vec{v}_{i}\rangle=-2\cosh\theta\langle N,\vec{v}_{i}\rangle=2(\cosh\theta)^{2}.

This identity can be seen as the model in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
satisfying a
hyperbolic cosine-squared law
. Using (
1
) and the relation (
2
), we obtain

(\cosh\theta)^{2}=\frac{1}{1-|Du|^{2}}.

Up to this point, the resistance problem is unconstrained, as
u
is arbitrary except for the spacelike condition
|Du|<1
. To compute the total resistance of the body
\mathcal{B}
, we impose the “Single Shock Property”: after colliding with
S
, particles do not strike the surface again. Consequently, up to constant factors, we have the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
S
be a spacelike surface in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
given by
z=u(x,y)
, where
u
is a
C^{1}
function defined in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The resistance of
S
against a fluid moving along the
z
-direction with constant velocity is

(3)

E[u]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{1-|Du|^{2}}\,dxdy.

Initially, the problem can be formulated as maximizing or minimizing the energy
E
. Recall that a similar situation is the problem of the area of a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
, whereas in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the problem is to minimize (minimal surfaces) but in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
it is to maximize (maximal surfaces).

In order to find solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equation associated with
E
, it is natural to restrict the class of admissible functions. In Section
3
, we prove that linear functions are the only solutions to Newton’s problem that can be expressed by separation of variables. Section
4
is devoted to analyzing the problem of maximizing the energy. In Section
5
, we assume that the solution is radial, finding explicit parametrizations of the solutions. In contrast to the Euclidean case, the radial solution can be defined up to the rotation axis, with a conical-type singularity at the axis. Finally, we study the Single Shock Condition in the Lorentzian context (Section
6
)

2.
Preliminaries and examples

In the Lorentz-Minkowski space, a vector
\vec{v}
is said to be spacelike (resp. timelike, lightlike) if
\langle\vec{v},\vec{v}\rangle>0
(resp.
<0
,
=0
). The modulus of a vector
\vec{v}
is
|\vec{v}|=\sqrt{|\langle\vec{v},\vec{v}\rangle}
. The set of lightlike vectors is the lightlike cone
\{\vec{v}\in\mathbb{L}^{3}:\langle\vec{v},\vec{v}\rangle=0\}
.

Two timelike vectors
\vec{v},\vec{w}\in\mathbb{L}^{3}
cannot be orthogonal, i.e.,
\langle\vec{v},\vec{w}\rangle\not=0
. We say that they are in the same timelike cone (or that they have the same timelike orientation) if
\langle\vec{v},\vec{w}\rangle<0
. This provides a timelike orientation on
\mathbb{L}^{3}
for all timelike vectors once a reference timelike vector is fixed in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
. We fix the timelike orientation induced by the vector
\vec{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)
. The reverse Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for timelike vectors asserts that
|\vec{v}|^{2}|\vec{w}|^{2}<\langle\vec{v},\vec{w}\rangle^{2}
. If
\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}
have the same timelike orientation, the angle between
\vec{v}
and
\vec{w}
is defined as the unique
\theta\geq 0
such that

\cosh\theta=-\frac{\langle\vec{v},\vec{w}\rangle}{|\vec{v}||\vec{w}|}.

See
[
10
]
. An immersion
\Psi\colon S\to\mathbb{L}^{3}
of a surface
S
is called spacelike if the induced metric endowed from
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is Riemannian at all points of
S
. If
S
is the graph of a function
u
, this is equivalent to
|Du|<1
, or equivalently, that the unit normal vector given in (
1
) is timelike. Note that
N
has the same timelike orientation as
\vec{e}_{3}
., and the angle
\theta
between
N
and
\vec{e}_{3}
at each point of
S
is given by

\cosh\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|Du|^{2}}}.

An interesting surface in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is the lightlike cone, which is defined as any translation of
\mathcal{K}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{L}^{3}\colon x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}\}
, as well as, the upper lightlike cone
\mathcal{K}^{+}=\mathcal{K}\cap\{z>0\}
and the lower lightlike cone
\mathcal{K}^{-}=\mathcal{K}\cap\{z<0\}
. The induced metric on
\mathcal{K}
is degenerate.
If the vertex is situated at a point
p
, we denote these by
\mathcal{K}_{p}
,
\mathcal{K}^{+}_{p}
and
\mathcal{K}^{-}_{p}
, respectively.

We calculate the resistance of some spacelike surfaces. The following examples are surfaces of revolution obtained by rotating a planar curve
u=u(r)
contained in the
xz
-plane about the
z
-axis, where
u\colon[0,R]\to\mathbb{R}
. The domain of
u(x,y)=u(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})
is the disk
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
of radius
R>0
centered at the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In polar coordinates, the resistance of
u
is

(4)

E[u]=2\pi\int_{0}^{R}\frac{r}{1-u^{\prime}(r)^{2}}\,dr.

In all examples, we will take the boundary condition
u(R)=0
.

(1)

Horizontal disks. Let
u(x,y)=0
in
B_{R}
. Then

E[u]=\pi R^{2}
.

(2)

Hyperbolic planes. A hyperbolic plane of radius
\rho>0
centered at the origin of
\mathbb{L}^{3}
is defined by
x^{2}+y^{2}-z^{2}=-\rho^{2}
. These surfaces,with two components, are spacelike and play the role of spheres in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
because their Gaussian curvature is constant. Consider the upper component

(5)

u(r)=-\sqrt{\rho^{2}+R^{2}}+\sqrt{\rho^{2}+r^{2}},

which is called a hyperbolic cap of radius
\rho
. Then,
u(R)=0
, and

E[u]=\frac{\pi R^{2}\left(2\rho^{2}+R^{2}\right)}{2\rho^{2}}.

Note that, for a fixed domain
B_{R}
, we have

\lim_{\rho\to 0}E[u]=\infty,\quad\lim_{\rho\to\infty}E[u]=\pi R^{2}.

Thus, as
\rho\to 0
, the hyperbolic cap (
5
) tends to the upper lightlike cone
\mathcal{K}^{+}_{(0,0,-R)}
.

Here, we establish a relation between the resistance of a disk
B_{R}
and a hyperbolic cap in order to compare them with the Euclidean case. Recall that Newton obtained that the ratio between the resistances of a sphere and a disk of the same radius is
1/2
. From the above computations, the analogous result in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
gives

\frac{\mbox{resistance of hyperbolic cap of radius $R$}}{\mbox{resistance of a disk of radius $R$}}=\frac{3}{2}.

(3)

Spacelike cones. Consider a cone
z^{2}=\lambda^{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})
, where
\lambda\in(0,1)
. The parameter
\lambda
represents the slope of the cone, and the condition
0<\lambda<1
ensures that the cone is spacelike. Taking the upper part of the cone

(6)

u(r)=-\lambda R+\lambda r,\quad r\in[0,R],

we have
u(R)=0
. Then

E[u]=\frac{\pi R^{2}}{1-\lambda^{2}}.

Consequently,

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}E[u]=\pi R^{2},\quad\lim_{\lambda\to 1}E[u]=\infty.

For a fixed
R
, if
\lambda\to 1
, the spacelike cone
u
tends to
\mathcal{K}^{+}_{(0,0,-R)}
.

It is clear that the study of extremals (or critical points) of the energy
E
is invariant under rigid motions of
\mathbb{L}^{3}
and under dilations.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex bounded domain. The problem of finding extremals of the energy
E
is invariant under dilations with center
O\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
c>0
and suppose
O=(0,0)
. Given a function
u=u(x,y)
, by the convexity of
\Omega
, we define

v\colon c\cdot\Omega\to\mathbb{R},\quad v(x,y)=cu(\frac{1}{c}(x,y)).

Let
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the dilation defined by
\Phi(x,y)=c(x,y)
. Note that
v\circ\Phi=cu
and
|Dv|^{2}=|Du|^{2}\circ\Phi^{-1}
. By the change of variables formula, and since
\mbox{Jac}(\Phi)=c^{2}
, we have

E[v]=\int_{c\Omega}\frac{1}{1-|Dv|^{2}}dxdy=\int_{\Omega}\frac{c^{2}}{1-|Du|^{2}}\,dxdy=c^{2}E[u].

Since the energy
E
changes by a constant factor, the extremals are the same regardless of the value of
c
.
∎

3.
The Euler-Lagrange equation

We calculate the Euler-Lagrange equation for the functional
E
. We assume that
u
is of class
C^{2}
in its domain. Since the Lagrangian of
E
does not depend on
u
, the Euler-Lagrange equation is

\mbox{div}\frac{Du}{(1-|Du|^{2})^{2}}=0.

Expanding this expression gives the quasilinear equation

(7)

(1-|Du|^{2})\Delta u+4D^{2}u(Du,Du)=0,

or equivalently,

(8)

(1+3u_{x}^{2}-u_{y}^{2})u_{xx}+(1-u_{x}^{2}+3u_{y}^{2})u_{yy}+8u_{x}u_{y}u_{xy}=0.

If
u
is a solution to (
8
), we say that
u
is an extremal of
E
, and its graph
\{(x,y,u(x,y))\colon(x,y)\in\Omega\}
is called a
stationary surface
. The simplest examples of stationary surfaces are spacelike planes
z=u(x,y)=ax+bu+c
, where
a^{2}+b^{2}<1
(spacelike condition). A major difference with the Euclidean case is that (
8
) is an elliptic equation.

Proposition 3.1
.

The Euler-Lagrange equation of the functional
E
is of quasilinear elliptic type.

Proof.

It suffices to compute the eigenvalues of the second-order terms of (
8
), which yields

\lambda=1-|Du|^{2},\quad\Lambda=1+3|Du|^{2}.

Then
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
due to the spacelike condition
|Du|<1
.
∎

This result contrasts with its counterpart in Euclidean space, which we unify in the following presentation. Let
\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}
and define the functional

(9)

E_{\epsilon}[u]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{1+\epsilon|Du|^{2}},\qquad\begin{array}[]{ll}\epsilon=1,&\mbox{Euclidean case},\\
\epsilon=-1,&\mbox{Lorentzian case}.\end{array}

The Euler-Lagrange equation of
E_{\epsilon}
is

(10)

(1+\epsilon|Du|^{2})\Delta u-4\epsilon D^{2}u(Du,Du)=0,

and the eigenvalues of the second order terms are

\lambda_{\epsilon}=1-(2+\epsilon)|Du|^{2},\qquad\Lambda_{\epsilon}=1+(2-\epsilon)|Du|^{2}.

In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the equation (
10
) is of elliptic-hyperbolic type, as indicated in
[
3
]
.

Coming back to
\mathbb{L}^{3}
, and as a consequence of Prop.
3.1
, the maximum principle holds, as well as, the uniqueness of the solutions to the Dirichlet problem (
[
8
, Sect. 10]
). From the examples of spacelike planes, we deduce the following uniqueness result.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution to (
8
) defined on a bounded domain
\Omega
.

(1)

If
u=c
along
\partial\Omega
, then
u=c
on
\Omega
.

(2)

Suppose that
\Omega
is doubly-connected with
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
. If
u=c_{i}
along
\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, and
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
, then
c_{1}\leq u\leq c_{2}
in
\Omega
.

(3)

If
u
attains an interior local maximum or minimum, then
u
is constant in
u
.

As we stated, and without restriction on the class of admissible functions, global minimizers of
E
are obtained when
u
is a constant function. The corresponding stationary surfaces are open sets of horizontal planes, where
E[u]=\mbox{area}(\Omega)
. If we impose constant boundary conditions,
u=c
, and if
u
is not constant, then
u
is necessarily not
C^{2}
at some points of
\Omega
. It is expected that
|Du|\to 1
at the points where regularity is lost because the coefficient of
\Delta u
in (
7
) tends to
0
. Thus, at these points, the surface is tangent to a cone
\mathcal{K}
.

We finish this section by obtaining all stationary surfaces given by separation of variables.

Theorem 3.3
.

Linear functions are the only extremals of the form
u(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)
. In consequence, spacelike planes are the only stationary surfaces given by separation of variables.

Proof.

Suppose that
u(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)
is an extremal. Let
I
and
J
be the domains of
f
and
g
, respectively.
Substituting this into (
8
) gives

(11)

(1-f^{\prime 2}-g^{\prime 2})(f^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime\prime})+4(f^{\prime 2}f^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime 2}g^{\prime\prime})=0.

Differentiating first with respect to
x
, and then with respect to
y
, we obtain

(12)

g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}f^{\prime\prime\prime}=-f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime\prime}.

Suppose that there is
(x_{0},y_{0})
such that

(13)

(f^{\prime 2})^{\prime}(x_{0})(g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}(y_{0})\not=0,

from which we will arrive at a contradiction. Then, there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}}=c=-\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}}

in an open set of
I\times J
around
(x_{0},y_{0})
.
We consider two cases:

(1)

Case
c=0
. Then
f^{\prime\prime\prime}=g^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
, which implies there exist constants
a_{i},b_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq 3
) such that

f(x)=a_{1}\frac{x^{2}}{2}+a_{2}x+a_{3},\quad g(y)=b_{1}\frac{y^{2}}{2}+b_{2}y+b_{3}.

Moreover,
a_{1}b_{1}\not=0
by (
13
). Substituting these expressions into (
11
) yields a polynomial equation in
x
and
y
, whose coefficients must vanish. The coefficients of
x^{2}
and
y^{2}
are
a_{1}^{3}(3a_{1}-b_{1})
and
b_{1}^{2}(3b_{1}-a_{1})
, respectively. This implies
a_{1}=b_{1}=0
, a contradiction.

(2)

Case
c\not=0
. From (
12
), there are constants
a_{1},b_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f^{\prime\prime}=\frac{c}{2}f^{\prime 2}+a_{1},\quad g^{\prime\prime}=-\frac{c}{2}g^{\prime 2}+b_{1}.

Substituting these into (
11
) gives

(6a_{1}-2b_{1}+c)f^{\prime 2}-(2a_{1}-6b_{1}+c)g^{\prime 2}+2(a_{1}+b_{1})+3cf^{\prime 4}-3cg^{\prime 4}=0.

Viewing this identity as a polynomial equation in
f^{\prime}
, all coefficients must vanish because
f^{\prime}\not=0
. We see that the coefficient of
f^{\prime 4}
is
3c
. This yields
c=0
, a contradiction.

As a consequence of these arguments, and from (
13
), we must have
(f^{\prime 2})^{\prime}(g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}=0
identically in
I\times J
. Therefore, either
(f^{\prime 2})^{\prime}=0
or
(g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}=0
. Without loss of generality, assume
(f^{\prime 2})^{\prime}=0
; hence, there is
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f(x)=ax+b
. Substituting this into (
11
), we obtain

(1-a^{2}+3g^{\prime 2})g^{\prime\prime}=0.

This implies
g^{\prime\prime}=0
or
1-a^{2}+3g^{\prime 2}=0
. In both cases, we conclude that
g
is linear. This implies that both
f
and
g
are linear functions, completing the proof.
∎

The same argument proves the following result in the Euclidean space.

Theorem 3.4
.

Planes are the only stationary surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for the energy
E_{1}
, (
9
), of the form
u(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)
.

4.
Maximizers of the energy

In this section, we consider the problem of finding maximizers of the energy
E
. Note that, in principle, this problem is meaningful when recalling that in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
, for the area energy of a spacelike surface, the relevant problem is to find maximizers.
Returning to the examples of cones and hyperbolic caps, we observe that one can construct sequences of spacelike surfaces whose resistance diverges to infinity.

Let

\mathcal{C}(\Omega)=\{u\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}\colon\mbox{$u$ is $C^{1}$-piecewise},|Du|^{2}<1\}.

Proposition 4.1
.

The unconstrained maximum resistance problem has no solution in the class of functions
\mathcal{C}(B_{R})
.

Proof.

Let
u_{\lambda}
be the function defined in (
6
). Then
u_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{C}(B_{R})
, and
\lim_{\lambda\to 1}E[u_{\lambda}]=\infty
.
∎

One can also consider the hyperbolic caps defined in (
5
). As
\rho\to 0
, the resistance tends to infinity.

Proposition
4.1
also holds for any bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
because it suffices to consider a small disk
B_{R}
contained in
\Omega
.

In the Euclidean case, it is natural to restrict the admissible functions to be bounded, satisfying
0\leq u\leq M
for some constant
M
. Even within this family, the infimum of the resistance is
0
. A difference from the Euclidean case is that in
\mathbb{L}^{3}
this boundedness assumption must be refined, because the spacelike condition
|Du|<1
imposes restrictions on the height of the function
u
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain. Let
u\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
be a function such that
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
. If
|Du|<1
on
\overline{\Omega}
, then

|u(p)|<\mbox{dist}(p,\partial\Omega),\quad p\in\Omega.

In particular, if
\Omega=B_{R}
, then
|u|<R
in
\overline{B_{R}}
.

Proof.

Let
p\in\Omega
and let
q\in\partial\Omega
be such that
\mbox{dist}(p,\partial\Omega)=d(p,q)
. Since the segment joining
p
and
q
is included in
\overline{\Omega}
, we define the function
f(t)=u(\alpha(t))
, where
\alpha(t)=(1-t)q+tp
,
t\in[0,1]
. By the mean value theorem,
f(1)-f(0)=f^{\prime}(t_{0})
for some
t_{0}\in[0,1]
. Then, we have

|u(p)|=|f(1)-f(0)|=|\langle Du(\alpha(t_{0})),\alpha^{\prime}(t_{0})\rangle<|\alpha^{\prime}(t_{0})|=\mbox{dist}(p,\partial\Omega).

∎

If
\Omega=B_{R}
, we need to restrict the class to functions that do not attain the value
R
. Given
M
, with
0<M<R
, define

\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})=\{u\in\mathcal{C}(B_{R})\colon 0\leq u\leq M,|Du|^{2}<1\}.

If
u
attains a maximum of
E
in
\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
, then we prove that
u
necessarily satisfies the boundary conditions
u(0)=0
and
u(R)=M
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
be a function that attains a local maximum of
E
in the class of functions
\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
. Then, up to a reflection across a horizontal plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

u(0)=0,\quad u(R)=M.

Proof.

After a reflection across the plane
z=0
, we can assume that
u(0)<u(R)
. Suppose that
u\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
such that
u(0)>0
. For
\epsilon>0
, define the function

v_{\epsilon}(r)=(1+\epsilon)(u(r)-u(0)).

It is clear that
v_{\epsilon}(0)=0
. We prove that, by choosing a suitable
\epsilon>0
, we have
v_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
, and

E[v_{\epsilon}]>E[u]
. Let
\delta=\frac{u(R)-u(0)}{M}<1
and
s_{M}=\max_{r\in[0,R]}u^{\prime}(r)<1
. Then
v_{\epsilon}
is increasing with
v^{\prime}_{\epsilon}=(1+\epsilon)u^{\prime}(r)\leq(1+\epsilon)s_{M}
. The spacelike condition for
v_{\epsilon}
requires
1+\epsilon<\frac{1}{s_{M}}
. Moreover,
v_{\epsilon}(R)=(1+\epsilon)\delta M
. Thus, we also require
1+\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{\delta}
. For any
\epsilon>0
satisfying both conditions, we have
v_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
with
v_{\epsilon}(0)=0
and

E[v_{\epsilon}]>E[u]
. This leads to a contradiction, proving
u(0)=0
.

Similarly, if
u\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
is such that
u(R)<M
, we can define, for
\epsilon>0
, the function

w_{\epsilon}(r)=(1+\epsilon)(u(r)-u(R))+M.

Then, with the same notation for
\delta
and
s_{M}
, we have
w_{\epsilon}(R)=M
,
w_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
and
E[w_{\epsilon}]>E[u]
. This would imply that
u
is not a local maximizer, a contradiction. This proves that
u(R)=M
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

The maximum of the functional
E
in
\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})
is infinite.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
a_{n}=M+\frac{R-M}{n}
and consider the
C^{1}
-piecewise function

u_{n}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{M}{a_{n}}r,&0\leq r\leq a_{n},\\
M,&a_{n}\leq r\leq R.\end{array}\right.

Then
u_{n}
is spacelike and
0\leq u_{n}\leq M
in its domain. Observe that
u_{n}
describes a spacelike cone that tends to the lightlike cone
\mathcal{K}^{+}
in
(0,M)
as
n\to\infty
. We have

\begin{split}E[u_{n}]&=2\pi\int_{0}^{R}\frac{r}{1-u_{n}^{\prime 2}}\,dr=2\pi\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{a_{n}^{2}-M^{2}}\int_{0}^{a_{n}}r\,dr=\pi\frac{a_{n}^{4}}{a_{n}^{2}-M^{2}}\\
&=-\frac{(M(n-1)+R)^{4}}{2n^{2}(M-R)(M(2n-1)+R)}.\end{split}

Hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}E[u_{n}]=\infty
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain and
R>0
such that
B_{R}\subset\Omega
. If
0<M<R
, define

\mathcal{C}_{M}(\Omega)=\{u\in C^{1}(\Omega)\colon 0\leq u\leq M\}.

Then the maximum of the functional
E
on
\mathcal{C}_{M}(\Omega)
is infinite.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{C}_{M}(B_{R})\subset\mathcal{C}_{M}(\Omega)
, the result follows immediately from Prop.
4.4
.
∎

Example 4.6
.

We can find examples of hyperbolic caps where the energy diverges. Indeed, let

be as in the previous proof. Let

The radial case

We study the radial solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equation (

is a locally graph over

Since

\frac{d}{dr}\frac{ru^{\prime}(r)}{(1-u^{\prime}(r)^{2})^{2}}=0.

Therefore, there exists a constant

such that

(15)

\frac{ru^{\prime}(r)}{(1-u^{\prime}(r)^{2})^{2}}=c_{1}.

If

, then

for some constant

, and

In particular,

We now find a parametric representation of

\frac{dr}{dp}=c_{1}(-\frac{1}{p^{2}}-2+3p^{2}).

Then

where we use that fact that

.
Thus, we have
\gamma(p)=(r(p),u(p))
, where

(17)

\displaystyle r(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{p}(1-p^{2})^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle u(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}-c_{1}\left(\log(p)+p^{2}-\frac{3p^{4}}{4}\right).

parametrized by (

)-(

,

.

We now suppose that

is not constant. Since at

, then Cor.

implies that

must lose

) at

. The following result proves the existence of rotational stationary surfaces defined in punctured disks around the origin. We also give a full description of the geometric properties of the radial extremals.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let

and

be positive numbers with

. Then there is a unique extremal of

of radial type,

Moreover:

(1)

The function

(2)

The function

(3)

The curve

(4)

The function

Based on (

6.
The single shock condition

To compute the total resistance of the body
\mathcal{B}
, we have imposed the “Single Shock Property” (SSP). In the Lorentzian context, this condition ensures that, after a particle collides with the surface
S
, the particle does not strike the surface a second time. In the classical problem, under this assumption, the model of energy
E_{1}
in (
9
) can be considered accurate to the physical reality. In
[
3
]
, the authors give a sufficient condition to ensure the SSP, which it is known as the Single Shock Condition (SSC). Assuming the SSC, existence of global minimizers among radial solutions were proved in
[
5
,
6
]
. Considering the class of functions satisfying SSC, minimizers of
E_{1}
do not exist
[
13
,
14
]
. See also
[
1
,
12
]
.

In this section, we define and analyze the SSC in the Lorentzian context. We come back to Fig.
1
. Consider the vertical plane determined by the positive direction
\vec{e}_{x}
and
\vec{e}_{3}
. We follow the same steps as in
[
3
]
. Recall that the final velocity of the particle is
\vec{v}_{f}=-\sinh\theta T-\cosh\theta N
. In terms of the gradient
Du
,

\begin{split}\vec{v}_{f}&=\vec{e}_{3}+2\langle\vec{e}_{3},N\rangle N=\vec{e}_{3}-2\cosh\theta N\\
&=\frac{1}{1-|Du|^{2}}\left(-2|Du|e_{x}-(1+|Du|^{2})\vec{e}_{3}\right).\end{split}

Observe that

\vec{e}_{x}=\frac{Du}{|Du|}
. Then the expression for
\vec{v}_{f}
is

\vec{v}_{f}=\vec{e}_{3}-\frac{2}{1-|Du|^{2}}(Du,1)=\frac{1}{1-|Du|^{2}}\left(-2Du\,\vec{e}_{x}-(1+|Du|^{2})\vec{e}_{3}\right).

If
Du=0
, then
\vec{v}_{f}=-\vec{e}_{3}
and the particle does not hit
\mathcal{B}
again because
S
is a graph on the
xy
-plane. Suppose
Du\not=0
. The slope
s_{p}
of the final velocity
\vec{v}_{f}
is, up to a sign,

s_{p}=\frac{1+|Du|^{2}}{2|Du|}.

In the
xz
-plane we identify
u(x,y)
with
u(x)
and
Du(x,y)
with
u^{\prime}(x)
. If
u^{\prime}(x)>0
(resp.
u^{\prime}(x)<0
), then we say that the SSC is fulfilled if

(27)

u(x)+u^{\prime}(x)(\bar{x}-x)\leq u(\bar{x})\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\bar{x}\leq x,\quad\mbox{(resp. $\bar{x}>x$)}.

By taking
\bar{x}=x-tu^{\prime}(x)
, then (
27

u(x)-u(x-tu^{\prime}(x))\leq\frac{t}{2}(1+u^{\prime}(x)^{2}),\qquad\mbox{for all $t>0$},

or equivalently,

(28)

\frac{u(x)-u(x-tu^{\prime}(x))}{t}\leq\frac{1+u^{\prime}(x)^{2})}{2}\quad(SSC),

for all
t>0
. In case that
u^{\prime}(x)<0
, the same inequality (
28
) holds. Coming back to the function
u(x,y)
, we have the following definition.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
be a function such that
|Du|<1
. The function
u
is said to satisfy the SSC if

(29)

u(x,y)-u((x,y)-tDu(x,y))\leq\frac{t}{2}(1+|Du(x,y)|^{2})

for all
t>0
.

The SSC is a global property in the sense that it must be fulfilled for all
t>0
. If
t\to 0
in (
29
), it would be expectable to get extra information about the derivative of
u
at each point. However, it is not.
Letting
t\to 0
in (
28
), we deduce
u^{\prime}(x)^{2}\leq\frac{1+u^{\prime}(x)^{2})}{2}
, which is trivially fulfilled because of the spacelike condition
u^{\prime}(x)^{2}<1
.

The fundamental difference from the Euclidean case is that any spacelike function

satisfies the SSC regardless the concavity or convexity of the function.

Theorem 6.2
.

If

is a function such that

satisfies the SSC.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case
|Du|\not=0
. Consider the plane determined by
\vec{e}_{x}
and
\vec{e}_{3}
. Then we need to check (
28
) for all points of
\Omega
. Without loss of generality, suppose
u^{\prime}(x)>0
. Let
t>0
. The Mean Value Theorem implies that there exists
\xi
in the segment joining
x
and
x-tu^{\prime}(x)
such that

u(x)=u(x-tu^{\prime}(x))+tu^{\prime}(x)u^{\prime}(\xi),

for some
t_{0}\in[0,t]
.
Then the left hand-side of (
28
) is

\frac{u(x)-u(x-tu^{\prime}(x))}{t}=u^{\prime}(\xi)u^{\prime}(x).

Thus we have to check

u^{\prime}(\xi)u^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1+u^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{2},

that is,
1+u^{\prime}(x)^{2}-2u^{\prime}(\xi)u^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
. But this inequality is fulfilled because

1+u^{\prime}(x)^{2}-2u^{\prime}(\xi)u^{\prime}(x)=(u^{\prime}(x)-u^{\prime}(\xi))^{2}+1-u^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}>0,

and
u^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}<1
.
