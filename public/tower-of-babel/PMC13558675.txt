Tool life and surface quality in GTD-450 milling under dry, MQL, and nanofluid-MQL strategies
Authors: Masoud Saberi, Seyed Ali Niknam, Behnam Davoodi, Ali Hajaliakbari, Ramin Hashemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe machining of iron–nickel-based alloys, such as GTD-450, presents substantial tribological challenges arising from their high-temperature strength and low thermal conductivity. These material characteristics induce rapid tool wear and compromised surface integrity across a range of manufacturing operations. Consequently, improving the machinability of GTD-450 constitutes a critical research imperative, particularly given the limited literature on this alloy despite its widespread industrial applications. These applications span power generation—including steam and gas turbine components such as compressor blades—as well as aerospace and industrial machinery sectors, where GTD-450 is employed in landing gear, high-pressure valves, and pump shafts. One approach to addressing these challenges involves selecting appropriate lubrication strategies, lubricant types, and associated parameters—namely, flow rate, additive components (including reinforcing nanoparticles), and surfactants. To enhance the machinability of GTD-450, the present study systematically investigates and compares the performance of five distinct lubrication-cooling strategies: dry machining, water-immersion cooling, conventional minimum quantity lubrication (MQL), MQL enriched with Al₂O₃ nanoparticles, and MQL enriched with ZnO nanoparticles. The primary response variables are tool flank wear (VB) and workpiece surface roughness (Ra), with feed rate, cutting speed, and depth of cut examined as additional machining parameters. To date, no published work has reported on the use of Al₂O₃ or ZnO nanoparticles to enrich MQL strategies for machining GTD-450. Statistical analysis using analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that the most critical factors influencing VB and Ra are, respectively, feed rate and cutting speed. Among the five lubrication strategies, nanofluid MQL (NMQL) outperformed dry, water immersion, and conventional MQL methods. Specifically, NMQL with ZnO nanoparticles achieved the greatest reduction in tool wear, up to 10% relative to conventional MQL and up to 35% relative to dry machining. Surface roughness (Ra) was also improved, with reductions of approximately 11% relative to MQL and 32% relative to dry machining. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of worn tools indicated that this enhanced performance is attributable to the in-situ formation of a stable triboprotective layer at the tool–chip interface. This layer effectively mitigates severe wear mechanisms, including adhesion and abrasion. The findings confirm that NMQL—particularly with ZnO nanoparticles—represents a highly effective and sustainable strategy for improving the machinability of hard-to-cut superalloys such as GTD-450.Keywords: Superalloy, Machinability, Nanofluid, Al2O3, ZnOSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Iron-nickel-based superalloys are a class of advanced materials essential to high-performance sectors such as aerospace, power generation, and gas turbines1. Among them, precipitation-strengthened superalloys such as GTD-450 are specifically engineered for critical high-temperature applications, including turbine blades and vanes, due to their exceptional high-temperature strength, excellent creep resistance, and high thermal corrosion resistance2. However, the same properties that make GTD-450 ideal for the intended application also make it very difficult to machine, posing serious challenges to manufacturing processes and significantly increasing production costs3. The poor machinability of GTD-450 is due to a combination of its inherent thermophysical properties. First, it maintains high strength at high temperatures and resists plastic deformation during cutting4. Second, its low thermal conductivity prevents efficient heat dissipation in the cutting zone, resulting in high thermal loads concentrated at the cutting tool edge. Third, GTD-450 exhibits a strong tendency to harden, so that each cutting pass induces plastic deformation of the underlying surface, increasing its hardness and making subsequent machining operations more difficult. Finally, its strong chemical affinity with the tool material at high temperatures exacerbates adhesion and severe diffusion5. This confluence of factors results in excessively high temperatures (often exceeding 1100 °C) at the tool-chip interface, leading to catastrophic tool wear, extremely short tool life, and poor surface integrity, characterized by undesirable tensile residual stresses6.

To mitigate these problems, conventional cooling strategies have been widely used. Dry cutting is the first alternative approach to eliminate the use of cutting fluids. Dry machining, although environmentally safe, is often impractical for superalloys such as GTD-450 because it leads to near-immediate tool thermal failure. However, in the absence of lubrication, the higher friction increases the adhesion between the tool and the workpiece. Furthermore, under dry-machining conditions, the tool and workpiece are exposed to high temperatures without cooling, leading to rapid oxidation, diffusion, and wear, thereby reducing tool life. One of the essential functions of cutting fluids is to remove chips from the machining area7. As a result, flood cooling with water-based emulsions has been the traditional industry standard. Cutting fluids play an important role in lubrication, cooling, chip removal, and rust protection in mechanical manufacturing8.

However, this method has several drawbacks: its effectiveness is limited by the material’s low thermal conductivity, and repeated thermal shocks can cause microcracks in the tool coating. In addition, the high costs associated with the preparation, storage, and disposal of cutting fluids, together with significant concerns about environmental and operator health, have prompted the search for more sustainable alternatives. It has been reported that about 16–20% of the lubrication system’s cost can be reduced. Therefore, the use of advanced cooling and quenching techniques is required to achieve better surface roughness and tool life, while maintaining the cutting temperature and cutting force gradient9,10. In response to these limitations, MQL has emerged as a promising and environmentally friendly alternative. MQL is a cooling lubrication technology that lies between dry and wet cutting and is also known as a quasi-dry manufacturing method11. MQL and dry machining techniques have been successfully applied to specific machining processes and are considered environmentally friendly12. The use of MQL in machining can reduce the environmental impact of conventional flooded lubrication systems and improve operator safety13,14. MQL is a type of micro-lubrication designed for near-dry machining. This approach replaces large quantities of traditional lubricants, often water- and mineral-oil-based, with small quantities of an environmentally friendly lubricant, usually derived from vegetable oils and mixed with air15. This cooling-lubrication method represents a shift in strategy from coolant-dominance to lubricant-dominance, delivering small quantities of cutting oil (typically 50–500 mL/h) in a high-velocity compressed air stream16. The resulting aerosol effectively penetrates the tool-chip interface, forming a thin, high-pressure lubricant film that reduces friction at the source17. By minimizing friction, MQL inherently reduces heat generation and is more efficient than post-production heat removal. Several studies have shown that MQL outperforms dry machining and reduces tool wear during machining of various alloys18,20.

Reducing tool wear maintains the cutting edge’s sharpness, thereby improving the quality of the machined surface and ensuring consistent dimensional accuracy of the workpiece. This reduction is particularly essential in high-precision manufacturing sectors, such as aerospace and automotive engine manufacturing, where scrap is significantly reduced21. A key operational benefit is a significant increase in tool life, reducing the number of tool changes. As a result, manufacturing companies can reduce tool acquisition and inventory costs, minimize unproductive machine downtime, and improve overall productivity21. However, tool wear remains an unavoidable phenomenon in machining processes. Failure to promptly detect wear can lead to abnormal cutting conditions that adversely affect machining efficiency and final product quality. The implementation of intelligent tool wear monitoring systems addresses this challenge by predicting tool condition, issuing early warnings, and supporting maintenance decisions. This proactive approach is essential to ensure the efficient and reliable operation of an automated manufacturing system22.

However, the benefits of the MQL technique are limited by the low cooling capacity of the base fluids and by particle clogging in the cutting zone23,24. To further enhance the tribological performance, especially for demanding applications such as superalloy machining, the concept of nanofluid-MQL (NMQL) has been introduced. This advanced technique involves dispersing nanoparticles in the MQL base oil to create a high-performance lubricant25,26. The performance enhancement in NMQL is attributed to several key mechanisms: (i) the “rolling” or “bearing” effect, in which nanoparticles convert sliding friction into rolling friction with lower energy; (ii) the “refining” or “polishing” effect, in which nanoparticles fill the microgrooves on the tool surface and reduce the contact roughness; and (iii) the in situ formation of a protective low-shear “tribofilm” that prevents direct tool-workpiece contact27. Nanoparticles such as the hard ceramic Al₂O₃ and the metal oxide ZnO have shown promise for this purpose.

While research into NMQL for common superalloys such as Inconel 71828,29 is growing, NMQL is among the best-known innovative lubrication techniques that utilize small amounts of nanofluids during machining. The technology aims to provide an optimal combination of lubrication and cooling that, in cooperation with the workpiece and tool, ultimately improves overall machining performance. Physical nanoparticles are lubricant additives that improve lubrication efficiency through enhanced heat transfer and reduced friction. NMQL has been shown not only to minimize tool wear, improve surface finish, and ensure machining performance, but also to contribute to engineering revisions and warrant further research20.

A recent study suggests that using nanofluid with minimal lubrication alone may not be suitable for machining titanium-based composites. Therefore, this study evaluates the effectiveness of each lubrication method by comparing them. The study shows that the use of nanofluids improves surface roughness, tool life, and machining energy consumption compared to other methods30. In another study, Rivatkar et al. demonstrated the superiority of nanofluids over MQL and other methods31. Mirhosseini et al.32 investigated the effects of nozzle geometry and nozzle count on a hybrid NMQL method. They found that increasing the number of nozzles improved the machining performance and hole-making capability of 6061 aluminum. In another study, the machining of 1040 steel under different coolant conditions, including MQL and NMQL, was investigated, and the effects of these methods on improving machining conditions were evaluated33.

The current research addresses a significant gap in the literature on the application of nanoparticle-enhanced coolants to less common yet critical alloys, such as GTD-450. Furthermore, a direct and systematic comparison of the tribological mechanisms of nanoparticles with fundamentally different properties—for example, hard and inert ceramics (Al₂O₃) versus reactive metal oxides (ZnO)—under the severe machining conditions specific to GTD-450 has not yet been reported. As a result, significant unanswered questions remain regarding the machinability of these high-performance materials. In particular, it is important to determine which type of nanoparticles provides better protection against combined thermal and mechanical loads and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms that dominate wear reduction. This study attempts to help bridge these gaps by examining the development of cooling processes from dry, wet, MQL, and NMQL and the impact of each, as well as comparing their performance in GTD-450 machining.

Nano fluid

Two distinct nanofluids—based on Al₂O₃ and ZnO nanoparticles, respectively—were prepared and utilized in this investigation. The nanofluids consisted of an oil-based carrier fluid, sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) surfactant, and the respective nanoparticles. The preparation process involved two stages: first, mechanical mixing of all components with a magnetic stirrer (500 rpm, 60 °C, 30 min), followed by ultrasonic processing for 1 h (20 kHz) to deagglomerate the nanoparticles and achieve a stable suspension. Results

Surface roughness (Ra)

The surface quality of a machined component is a critical determinant of overall cutting tool performance. Surface roughness is a primary quality metric, directly influenced by machining parameters such as cutting speed and feed rate. Throughout this work, surface roughness was assessed at various cutting speeds, feed rates, and cooling conditions, with the findings summarized in Fig. 4. The analysis reveals several key trends. First, dry machining consistently produced the highest surface roughness across all tested conditions. Second, while conventional wet cooling reduced roughness compared with dry machining, MQL achieved a superior surface finish. Third, a general trend of decreasing surface roughness with increasing cutting speed was observed. The incorporation of nanoparticles into the MQL fluid further enhanced the surface finish by up to 5% relative to base MQL, though the magnitude of improvement varied across the test matrix. The underlying mechanisms of these differences are elucidated by examining specific tool-wear modes. In dry-machining tests 1–3, tool-edge chipping occurred. This wear mechanism effectively reduces the cutting tool’s effective sharpness, increases the tool-workpiece contact area, and consequently elevates surface roughness. Conversely, in dry machining tests 7–9, BUE formation was observed. This abrasive phenomenon, absent under effective cooling conditions, resulted in significantly higher roughness than in both wet and MQL-assisted machining. Effect of machining parameters and cooling conditions on tool wear (VB)

Tool wear rate (VB) is a critical parameter for assessing machinability and optimizing machining parameters, as it directly governs cutting tool service life and, consequently, influences both machining costs and production economics. Following experimental machining trials and SEM analysis of the inserts, the VB values were quantified. Figure 5 presents a bar chart summarizing the tool wear rate under each test condition and cooling strategy. Under dry-milling conditions (no cutting fluid), the tool wear rate was consistently higher across all tests. The implementation of wet cooling reduced tool wear, demonstrating its beneficial effect. Notably, the minimum MQL condition yielded a greater reduction in tool wear than conventional wet machining. The incorporation of nanofluids, which augment the heat transfer coefficient of the cutting fluid, further enhanced wear reduction relative to MQL without nanoparticle additives, confirming the efficacy of nano-enhanced cooling techniques. Among the tested conditions, the ZnO-based nanofluid showed the greatest improvement in mitigating tool wear.

SEM and EDS analysis of tool wear mechanisms

Consistent with the primary objective of this study—to quantify the effects of cooling method and conditions on tool flank wear (VB) and surface roughness (Ra)—flank wear of the inserts was measured using SEM following each machining test. The post-test procedure comprised two stages: (i) image acquisition of each cutting edge, and (ii) subsequent flank wear measurement using ImageJ software. All measured flank wear values were compiled and presented graphically. A fundamental requirement for selecting appropriate cutting tool inserts and achieving efficient, cost-effective machining operations is the systematic investigation and understanding of wear mechanisms and their progression during the machining of dissimilar material classes.

Microscopic images were acquired from the worn flank surfaces of tools used in GTD-450 milling. To systematically document microstructural changes associated with flank wear, each cutting edge was treated as a distinct cutting path. The flank surfaces were subsequently evaluated using SEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), including elemental and chemical mapping. All inserts used in the experimental trials were imaged to document wear morphologies specific to each test and machining condition, enabling direct comparisons. The predominant wear mechanisms identified during GTD-450 machining include chipping, abrasion, and BUE formation. The most severe wear occurs under dry machining conditions, where no coolant is applied to either the workpiece or the cutting tool. Elevated temperatures and increased friction under these conditions promote tool wear, facilitating the initiation and propagation of wear mechanisms. At a cutting speed of 160 m/min, edge wear is consistently evident across all three corresponding tests. Observations indicate that the depth of cut does not influence the occurrence or extent of this wear; however, the severity of edge wear decreases with increasing feed rate. Furthermore, while edge chipping is observed in all three tests at 160 m/min, it is absent at higher cutting speeds. Consequently, selecting appropriate machining parameters is critical for controlling edge chipping.

The methodology employed to analyze tool flank wear aligned with the manuscript’s primary focus on evaluating the effects of cooling methods and conditions on both flank wear and surface roughness Ra. After each machining test, the flank wear of the inserts was quantified using SEM. The post-test procedure involved photographing each cutting edge and then processing the images in ImageJ to obtain precise flank wear measurements. Flank wear in dry milling

SEM images obtained under dry machining conditions reveal that BUE formation is a function of cutting speed. At a cutting speed of 240 m/min, BUE presence is consistently observed across all three corresponding tests. Furthermore, as the feed rate increases, the volume, height, and overall extent of the BUE increase proportionally. Elevated machining parameters—particularly cutting speed—lead to higher temperatures in the cutting zone and an increased coefficient of friction. These conditions promote adhesion on both the chip surface and the cutting tool. Consequently, the use of appropriate lubricant and coolant is essential during the machining of such materials. However, coolant application is only an initial mitigating measure; the primary objective is to select the optimal lubrication method and its associated parameters. Given considerations such as cost and environmental impact, the proper choice of lubricant and cooling strategy is critical35. To accurately identify wear mechanisms, map analysis images were employed, with representative examples provided. Test 8 was examined in greater detail across different cutting regimes. As shown in Fig. 6, certain regions of the images exhibit substantially higher iron (Fe) concentrations than others. Given that iron is a primary constituent of the workpiece material, the accumulated edge (BUE) forms preferentially in these regions.

The insert coating consists of a TiAlN/Al₂O₃ multilayer. Accordingly, titanium, aluminum, and oxygen are present in areas where no wear has occurred. Portions of the coating removed due to wear are detached from the cutting site and expelled as debris. In regions where abrasion has occurred, the coating layers are progressively worn away and separated from the tungsten carbide (WC) substrate, leading to tungsten (W) detection in SEM/EDS images. This abrasion phenomenon propagates and intensifies over time. The outermost TiAlN layer is initially removed, exposing the underlying Al₂O₃ layer. As abrasion progresses, the Al₂O₃ layer also detaches from the insert surface, exposing the tungsten carbide substrate. The rate of abrasion increases with proximity to the cutting edge, and the thickness of the degraded coating layer correspondingly increases.

The SEM image of dry-machining test 2 (Fig. 7) shows a combination of adhesion and abrasion mechanisms at the edge, with upper adhesion accounting for 60% of the wear area. The presence of Fe and Cr accumulation on the cutting edge is evidence. Along the thickness of the cutting edge, when the distance is taken from the edge, the areas that are subject to abrasion are visible due to the accumulation of w elements. This results in the formation of a large, accumulated edge. Surface roughness increased in this test, compromising surface smoothness.

An additional wear mechanism identified in at least three dry-machining trials was edge chipping. Tool edge chipping refers to localized fractures and material removal from the cutting edge, which can be induced by thermal shock, excessive mechanical loads, or suboptimal cutting parameters. Microscopic analysis of the tool inserts indicates that this phenomenon occurs more frequently under dry conditions and at lower cutting speeds. For instance, in tests 1–3, conducted under dry machining at a constant speed of 160 m/min with varying depths of cut and feed rates, the only consistent factor was the cutting speed (Fig. 8). The absence of cutting fluid during machining generally results in elevated temperatures and friction, conditions which promote the initiation and propagation of abrasive wear mechanisms. These effects contribute to diminished tool life, increased cutting forces, and elevated workpiece surface roughness36.

Flank wear in wet milling

Using the wet cooling method reduces wear and edge build-up compared to the dry state. However, edge chipping, abrasion, and adhesion still occur during machining. At high speeds, coolant use has significantly reduced edge chipping. The amount of flank wear is lower than in dry machining. Therefore, it can be concluded that edge chipping is primarily the result of inappropriate machining parameters rather than of machining interactions, increased temperature, and friction. Thus, the two principles of selecting the appropriate cooling method (quenching) and choosing optimal machining parameters are of great importance37.

The application of cutting fluid reduces temperature and friction in the cutting zone, the primary drivers of adhesive wear. By reducing these factors, coolants decelerate the initiation and progression of adhesive wear and can suppress the formation of certain high-temperature friction-induced phenomena. For instance, a comparative analysis of SEM images demonstrates that wet lubrication significantly inhibits the rate of BUE formation and growth. Nevertheless, while cutting fluid can prevent or mitigate specific wear modes, it does not eliminate tool wear. As illustrated in Fig. 9, under wet cooling conditions, no significant BUE is observed at the tool edge. However, abrasive wear mechanisms remain active: the TiAlN coating initially delaminates from the substrate due to wear, and the underlying Al₂O₃ layer is subsequently worn away, resulting in overall degradation of the coating system. The micrograph corroborates this sequence of events. Furthermore, although a macroscopic BUE is absent, elemental analysis of localized areas reveals the presence of iron, indicating microscale adhesive material transfer.

Flank wear in MQL milling

Considering that MQL is an environmentally benign cooling method that substantially reduces cutting fluid consumption37, it was employed and benchmarked against alternative cooling strategies in the machining of GTD-450. Flank wear measurements and SEM micrographs unequivocally demonstrate that, across the experimental matrix, MQL reduced VB by 17–24% relative to dry machining and by 6–12% relative to wet machining. Correspondingly, SEM image comparison indicates a comparable reduction in the formation of wear mechanisms, including BUE, abrasion, and adhesion. Consequently, MQL extends cutting tool life by an average of 20% compared to dry machining, while simultaneously reducing cutting fluid consumption. The MQL method prevents tool damage by lowering the temperature within the cutting zone. By forming a cooling and lubricating layer at the tool–chip interface, MQL reduces direct metal-to-metal contact and suppresses BUE formation. BUE is inherently unstable and, when present, diminishes tool life. Conversely, under dry machining conditions, BUE nucleates and grows progressively. As the BUE enlarges, it eventually detaches; during this peeling process, portions of the cutting tool may adhere to the workpiece surface, thereby degrading surface finish. Although MQL mitigates tool wear, several critical observations emerge from SEM analysis. While MQL has partially suppressed BUE growth and formation, both BUE and adhesion remain detectable, as shown in Fig. 13. In wet cooling, the higher fluid volume and greater impingement intensity facilitate more ready removal of BUE. However, optimal utilization of MQL’s lubricious properties reduces the area subject to abrasion. It slows the rate of wear propagation, thereby extending tool service life relative to wet cooling. As previously noted, MQL reduces tool wear, prevents chipping, and minimizes edge buildup compared to dry machining. Nevertheless, localized adhesion persists at small scales in specific regions of the cutting edge, as illustrated in Fig. 14.

Several studies have shown that the NMQL method improves the cooling performance. These particles improve heat transfer and reduce the frictional coefficient39,40. The experimental results show that the use of Al₂O₃ particles prevented adhesion and BUE formation. On the other hand, this cooling method also reduced the wear-affected zone, thereby increasing the cutting tool’s service life. The use of this method also improved BUE prevention. In the comparison between Fig. 15 and images of the same experiment under other cooling conditions, the improvements mentioned are clearly visible, as shown in the image and the explanation of the Fe element. There is no evidence of BUE. In addition to improving thermal conductivity, the use of Al₂O₃ ceramic nanoparticles lowers the cutting zone temperature and prevents BUE formation. These nanoparticles are often spherical and highly wear-resistant, so, like nanoscale bearings, they sit between the chip and tool surfaces and generate rolling friction, increasing life and improving surface roughness.

Overall discussion on the performance of nano-MQL strategies

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of various cooling strategies during milling of GTD-450, with specific focus on tool wear, prevailing wear mechanisms, and Ra. A secondary objective was to analyze the influence of key machining parameters—namely cutting speed, feed rate, and depth of cut—on the aforementioned responses across the different cooling conditions. To quantify the impact of cooling, dry machining was used as the baseline. The performance of each cooling method was subsequently expressed as the percentage improvement relative to this baseline. As summarized in Table 8, conventional wet cooling reduced tool flank wear (VB) by 11–16% compared to dry machining across the experimental matrix, with the maximum reduction of 16% observed in Test 4 (cutting speed: 200 m/min, feed rate: 800 mm/min, depth of cut: 1.5 mm). Transitioning to MQL yielded further wear reduction, achieving 18.8–23.7% relative to the dry baseline. The most significant improvement within this range (23.7%) was recorded in Test 6, indicating that the optimal pulsed application of cutting fluid in the MQL system reduced tool wear by an additional 6–8% points compared to conventional wet cooling. Further enhancement was realized through NMQL. The results confirm that, in general, NMQL reduces tool VB wear by at least 10% relative to base MQL. A detailed analysis reveals that NMQL incorporating Al₂O₃ nanoparticles improved wear resistance by 28–30.9% compared to dry machining and by 5–10% relative to base MQL. The application of NMQL with ZnO nanoparticles resulted in even greater improvement, increasing wear resistance by 31.4–34.9% relative to the dry condition. In practice, implementing this advanced cooling method approximately tripled tool life compared to dry machining.

Surface roughness Ra, a critical parameter for assessing machinability and determining the quality of manufactured components, was analyzed as a secondary output. Minimizing Ra is therefore essential for enhancing final part quality. Under consistent conditions and at identical measurement locations for all machining trials, surface roughness was evaluated. As with the tool wear analysis, dry machining served as the baseline, and the comparative Ra results are presented in Table 9. The data reveal a clear progression in surface finish improvement with advancing cooling technology. Conventional wet cooling reduced surface roughness by 12–20% relative to the dry baseline. The MQL method further improved performance, achieving a 22–27% reduction in roughness relative to dry machining, representing approximately a 10-percentage-point improvement over the wet method. The incorporation of NMQL yielded additional gains. The aggregated data indicate that NMQL reduces roughness by approximately 30% relative to dry machining. A more precise analysis shows that NMQL with Al₂O₃ nanoparticles reduced Ra by 24–32% compared to the dry state and by 3–7% compared to conventional MQL. The use of NMQL with ZnO nanoparticles produced a comparable maximum improvement, enhancing surface smoothness by up to 32% relative to dry machining, with only a marginal difference in performance between the two nanoparticle types.

The comparative analysis of cooling methods reveals a significant correlation between minimizing tool wear and improving surface finish. Specifically, the percentage reduction in tool flank wear relative to the dry-machining baseline is directly proportional to the corresponding percentage reduction in surface roughness (Ra). This relationship is exemplified in Test 1, where the 13% reduction in tool wear achieved with wet cooling was matched by an equivalent 13% reduction in surface roughness. This consistent trend suggests that the primary mechanism through which advanced cooling methods enhance surface smoothness is the effective reduction of tool wear. Consequently, the MQL technique, by substantially reducing wear, improves surface finish. The most favorable outcomes were achieved using MQL enhanced with ZnO nanoparticles (NMQL-ZnO). This method established optimal machining conditions, thereby extending tool life and enhancing surface quality by up to 3 times compared to dry machining.

ZnO nanofluid exhibits superior cooling and lubrication performance due to its unique combination of mechanical (softer, higher thermal conductivity) and tribological (more effective lubricant film formation, better surface repair, and improved rolling mechanism) properties. Therefore, it improves machinability and reduces tool wear more effectively. At the same time, Al₂O₃ is superior in long-term suspension stability and wear resistance at very high temperatures.

Conclusion

The following points summarize the specific contributions of this work to the existing body of knowledge on GTD-450 machining. Several of these findings have not been previously reported for this alloy.

1. ANOVA conducted across all machining conditions consistently identified cutting speed and feed rate as the most significant parameters influencing tool VB and Ra. In contrast, depth of cut exhibited no statistically significant effect (p-values > 0.136 for Ra and > 0.168 for VB). Quantitatively, cutting speed and feed rate together accounted for over 90% of the total variance in both VB and Ra across all cooling conditions. This finding contributes new knowledge to the literature by demonstrating that, for GTD-450, depth of cut can be increased to enhance productivity without compromising surface integrity—a guideline not previously available for this alloy.

2. This study systematically identified and mapped three primary wear mechanisms—abrasion, adhesion, and chipping—on the flank face of tools machining GTD-450. Quantitative image analysis revealed that under dry machining, adhesion and chipping accounted for approximately 60–70% of the worn flank area. In contrast, under NMQL with ZnO nanoparticles, abrasion dominated, accounting for over 80% of the wear area, while adhesion accounted for less than 10%. A novel contribution to the literature is the demonstration that advanced cooling methods—particularly NMQL—progressively transform the dominant wear mode from adhesion and chipping to controlled abrasion. This mechanistic insight has not been reported previously for GTD-450.

3. Under dry machining conditions, a cutting speed of 160 m/min consistently induced edge chipping in all 3 tests (100%), whereas higher speeds (240 m/min) promoted BUE formation, with BUE heights reaching up to 150 μm. Wet cooling reduced chipping to approximately 33% (1 out of 3 tests) at 160 m/min, but did not eliminate it. Notably, MQL completely suppressed chipping across all cutting speeds (0 out of 9 tests). This represents a unique contribution to the literature, as no prior study has established the critical speed–cooling interaction for chipping suppression in GTD-450 milling, nor provided these quantitative thresholds.

4. Relative to dry machining, wet cooling eliminated BUE, improved tool life by 11–13%, and reduced surface roughness by approximately 15%. These quantitative benchmarks contribute to the literature by providing a baseline against which more sustainable cooling methods can be evaluated for this specific superalloy.

5. The MQL method eliminated chipping, reduced tool wear by 18–24%, and improved surface roughness by up to 28% compared to dry machining. This is the first study to report comprehensive MQL performance data for GTD-450, thereby filling a critical gap in the literature on near-dry machining of this superalloy.

6. The experimental results demonstrate that NMQL outperforms dry, wet, and conventional MQL strategies. The single most significant contribution to the literature is the first-ever application and direct comparison of Al₂O₃ and ZnO nanoparticles as MQL additives for machining GTD-450. No prior work has reported using these nanoparticles to enrich MQL for this alloy.

7. NMQL with ZnO nanoparticles consistently outperformed NMQL with Al₂O₃, achieving up to 5% greater reductions in both tool wear and surface roughness in selected tests. This comparative finding provides a new insight into the literature: under the severe thermo-mechanical conditions characteristic of GTD-450 milling, a reactive metal oxide (ZnO) affords superior tribological protection compared with a hard, inert ceramic (Al₂O₃).

8. The results showed that the use of more sustainable methods, in addition to being more environmentally friendly, improved tool wear by up to 35% compared to the dry state, which reduced the cost of the tool. However, the only challenge in using these particles is the relatively high cost of the nanoparticles and the preparation process, which has prevented their wider industrial adoption.

In summary, this study makes multiple novel contributions to the existing literature, as enumerated below:

(i) A comparative assessment of dry, wet, conventional MQL, and NMQL (enriched with Al₂O₃ and ZnO nanoparticles) was conducted for milling of GTD-450.

(ii) Cutting speed and feed rate together account for over 90% of the total variance in both VB and Ra, establishing these as the dominant control parameters.

(iii) Identification and mapping of wear mechanism transformation: adhesion and chipping dominate the worn flank area under dry conditions (60–70%), whereas controlled abrasion prevails under NMQL (over 80%).

(iv) Demonstration that NMQL incorporating ZnO nanoparticles constitutes a highly effective and sustainable cooling strategy, capable of extending tool life and improving surface quality by up to threefold relative to dry machining.

These findings advance the state of knowledge on the sustainable machining of hard-to-cut superalloys and provide actionable, literature-based guidance for industrial applications.
