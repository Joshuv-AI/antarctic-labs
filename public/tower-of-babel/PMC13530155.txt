Study on wind resistance performance of high-rise corridor building based on quasi-steady static aeroelastic analysis method
Authors: Yingke Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the wind-sensitivity problem of high-rise connected corridor buildings caused by their high flexibility and low damping characteristics, this paper systematically investigates the wind-resistant performance of such buildings based on a semi-decoupled static aeroelastic analysis method of steady-state computational fluid dynamics (CFD), combined with finite element numerical simulation. Taking high-rise connected corridor buildings with streamlined airfoil sections as the research object, and adopting typical square and elliptical section buildings as control groups, this study verifies the effectiveness of the two proposed wind-resistant measures: the airfoil profile and the connected corridor. The static aeroelastic analysis considers the coupling relationship between aerodynamics and structures, and ensures the matching between the internal structure of the building and the aerodynamic shape through interpolation functions and rapid model reconstruction. With inter-story displacement and torsion angle adopted as evaluation indicators for wind-resistant performance, this paper systematically compares the differences in wind resistance with and without the proposed wind-resistant measures. This quasi-static wind resistance evaluation method based on static aeroelastic analysis can effectively quantify the peak displacement and inter-story torsion angle of the connected corridor under average wind loads, thereby providing a theoretical basis and data support for optimizing the supporting method of the connected corridor and formulating wind-resistant design parameters.

Introduction

Wind disasters are among the major types of natural hazards. Although their intensity is generally lower than that of seismic loads, they occur considerably more frequently1, making wind loads a major lateral action for various types of structures. This is particularly true for high-rise buildings: frequent and large-amplitude oscillations may cause discomfort to occupants, induce cracking in the main structure, and even lead to fatigue damage in certain structural components. Such damage endangers structural safety and may result in catastrophic consequences2.To meet the landmark functions and usage requirements of modern cities, super high-rise buildings have emerged in dense clusters. Steel-structured corridors are often erected between two towers to facilitate horizontal transportation between the buildings. Super high-rise buildings are characterized by light weight and high strength. However, their inherent properties of lightness, flexibility, low damping, and low natural vibration frequency3 lead to more pronounced wind-induced responses under wind loads. Additionally, their great height and low lateral stiffness result in significant horizontal displacement4,5, torsional deformation6,7, and resonance of the main structure during extreme weather events such as typhoons8,9, which induces excessive bending moments at the building base.

To mitigate the impact of wind loads on structures, additional shape modification measures are often adopted for super high-rise buildings. Currently, common aerodynamic optimization techniques for buildings include corner treatments, spiral configurations, and external flow deflectors. Chen10 investigated the wind-resistant performance of high-rise building models with three different concave shapes on the horizontal plane. Jiang11 discussed the base force coefficients and Strouhal numbers of high-rise buildings with three types of corner modifications and compared them with those of a square model. Li12 investigated the effects of chamfered corners on the aerodynamic performance of high-rise buildings. Tamura13 conducted wind tunnel tests on square prisms with different types of sharp corners, cut corners, and rounded corners. Kim14 deeply explored the mechanism underlying the influence of corner treatments on wind loads. Tanaka15 performed wind tunnel tests on a large number of models with tapered and spiral facades. However, such traditional aerodynamic measures typically involve cross-sectional reductions, leading to a loss in building space utilization. Meanwhile, these methods result in relatively complex building facades, which not only pose difficulties for cladding design but also increase the complexity of structural design and construction. More notably, there is often a conflict between traditional aerodynamic measures and architectural design, restricting architects in spatial design due to the constraints imposed by wind-resistant shape requirements. To preserve the planar shape of practical buildings, aerodynamically optimized cross-sectional profiles are often adopted as alternatives to the conventional square16 or circular17 cross-sections for high-rise buildings. Gu18 investigated the dynamic responses of high-rise buildings with cruciform cross-sections under high wind speeds. Li19 studied the influence of different taper ratios on wind load reduction for rectangular high-rise buildings. Kim20 explored the effects of increasing the number of sides on the response characteristics (rather than other aerodynamic properties) of straight high-rise buildings, as well as the impact of spiral configurations on polygonal cross-section high-rises. Cluni21 conducted wind load analysis on irregularly shaped high-rise buildings. Chen22 researched the aerodynamic interference effects between adjacent twin high-rise buildings. Zhang23 studied the wind effects on complex high-rise buildings with weak connections. Agrawal24 analyzed the wind loads on seven unconventional building shapes under ten wind incidence angles. Noormohamadian25 employed CFD methods to investigate three optimized triangular models. Lee26 explored the influences of open structures and wind directions on the wind field around hollow high-rise buildings.

In terms of research on wind-induced responses of corridor buildings, Qin27 investigated the aerodynamic and structural dynamic characteristics of twin-tower structures, and proposed a new method for the calculation of wind-induced vibration response and wind-resistant design optimization of twin towers28. Wang29 investigated the effects of different corridor layouts on the aerodynamic characteristics of asymmetric twin-tower high-rises. Lou30 studied the wind pressure characteristics of corridors in rectangular high-rise buildings through experimental and numerical simulation methods. Ruan31 explored the mechanical responses of steel-structured corridors under wind loads. Yang32 analyzed the dynamic responses of long-span twin-connected structures subjected to the combined action of wind loads and pedestrian loads. However, these studies focus primarily on the wind-induced effects of the corridors themselves and fail to analyze the influence of corridors’ load-bearing function on the wind resistance of the main buildings from a structural perspective. Thus, this paper proposes an aerodynamically optimized wind-resistant configuration for corridor high-rises, integrating airfoil profiles into the architectural shape design. The proposed configuration inherently exhibits superior aerodynamic performance without requiring additional aerodynamic modification measures.

Currently, although existing studies have modified building facade shapes to achieve wind resistance, research on airfoil cross-sections remains relatively scarce. In addition, systematic investigations into the wind resistance performance of corridor-connected buildings are also limited. Drawing on the aerodynamic principle that changes in airfoil shape can significantly alter lift and drag coefficients, this paper adopts this concept as a starting point to analyze the impact of building shape modifications on surface pressure distribution. Furthermore, the control variable method is used to verify how the presence or absence of corridors affects the displacement between the two towers. The focus of this work is to apply airfoils and corridors as two wind resistance measures. By comparing and evaluating the structural responses before and after the application of wind resistance measures, the wind reduction effect of the measures is analyzed to verify their effectiveness. This study focuses on wind resistance analysis under average loads to verify the effectiveness of the wind resistance measures. Currently, this study does not involve dynamic loads, unsteady flow fields, transient aeroelastic responses and other related fields. The arrangement of the remaining chapters is as follows: “Aerodynamic shape of the corridor building” section elaborates on the principle of the wind-resistant building shape and the load-bearing function of the corridor. “Wind pressure distribution of high-rise corridor buildings” section derives the wind pressure distribution on different building surfaces through computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. “Semi-coupled static aeroelastic analysis of high-rise corridor buildings” section performs static aeroelastic analysis on the high-rise corridor buildings, considers the coupling relationship between aerodynamic and structural behaviors, and analyzes the differences in wind resistance capacity of the structure under the scenarios with and without the corridor. Finally, a discussion is presented in the concluding section.

Aerodynamic shape of the corridor building

The generation of aerodynamic force

The direct cause of the aerodynamic force on an object is the pressure difference across its surface. When the upper surface is curved, the airflow passing over it travels a longer path and attains a higher velocity. In contrast, the lower surface is relatively flat, resulting in a shorter airflow path and a lower velocity. According to Bernoulli’s principle33, for flow at a constant elevation, a higher flow velocity corresponds to a lower pressure. Force-bearing role of the corridor

Corridor building is a kind of complex high-rise building structure system31. Typically, they consist of two high-rise buildings interconnected by an elevated link to meet the requirements of architectural form and functional use. Steel-structured connected corridors are one of the most common structural forms for connected buildings. When rigidly connected to the main buildings, they exhibit high strength and stiffness. However, in practical engineering applications, the function of connected corridors is often limited to spatial connection and facilitating intercommunication among occupants. As a result, sliding connections are generally adopted, with their additional force-bearing role in structural design rarely considered35.

Variations in attack angle induce prominent changes in lift and drag coefficients of airfoils, which originate from altered flow patterns governed by geometric features including camber, thickness, as well as leading- and trailing-edge configurations, further rearranging surface pressure distribution. As illustrated in Fig. 2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{D}$$\end{document} is closely correlated with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{L}$$\end{document} with opposite monotonic trends: within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in [15^\circ ,45^\circ ]$$\end{document}, a higher lift coefficient corresponds to a lower drag coefficient. Both super-tall buildings and aircraft wings feature a large slenderness ratio. Conventional aeronautical airfoils are designed for high lift-to-drag ratio to convert windward drag into upward lift, consistent with the low wind-resistance demand of high-rise buildings. For the twin-tower corridor building, reduced windward drag is converted into leeward lift; the opposite lift forces from two towers act axially on the connecting corridor and counteract one another. Such axial tension lowers the bending moment of main towers and mitigates flexural deformation of the corridor subjected to dead and live loads. Owing to rigid connections between the corridor and towers, the corridor serves as lateral bracing to restrain lateral drift and torsional deformation of the high-rise structure.

Analytical procedures and numerical modelling

The wind-induced response of high-rise buildings is essentially a static aeroelastic problem, which requires consideration of the static aeroelastic deformation of the flexible building structure in aerodynamic analysis. Static aeroelastic analysis investigates the interaction between aerodynamic forces and elastic structural deformation: aerodynamic loads cause structural deformation, which in turn alters the aerodynamic configuration and load distribution, until a static equilibrium is finally achieved. Although a fully coupled solution of aerodynamic and structural analyses with simultaneous equations of the two disciplines yields high accuracy, it is computationally prohibitive. Therefore, this paper adopts a semi-decoupled static aeroelastic analysis approach, in which aerodynamic analysis and structural analysis are performed alternately and iteratively, achieving high computational efficiency while meeting the required accuracy.

The aerodynamic mesh is primarily used for flow field simulation, focusing on the smoothness of surface geometry and the accuracy of flow field capture. It usually adopts dense surface meshes or volume meshes with refinement in high-curvature regions. In contrast, the structural mesh is applied for finite element analysis, emphasizing the transfer of internal stress, strain, and deformation of the structure with a relatively sparse mesh density. For the building structural model in this paper, shell meshes are employed, which can accurately simulate the in-plane stiffness as well as the out-of-plane bending and shear performances of components such as shear walls and cores. This approach can well reflect the bending deformation and shear deformation of the structure under wind loads, which is consistent with the actual force mechanism of the lateral force resistance system of high-rise buildings. In static aeroelastic analysis, these two types of meshes are usually non-matching, and interpolation methods are required to realize the data transfer of aerodynamic forces and structural displacements.

The analysis framework requires the establishment of two models: an aerodynamic model and a structural model, and the difference between them lies in the density of meshing. As shown in the Fig. 3, firstly, CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) calculations are performed on the aerodynamic model. According to the mesh distribution of the aerodynamic model, the loads that can be applied to the surface mesh of the structural model are obtained through the interpolation of aerodynamic loads, which represents the conversion of aerodynamic forces from the aerodynamic model to the structural model. Subsequently, finite element analysis (FEA) is performed on the structural model. The nodal displacements of the structural model are interpolated onto those of the aerodynamic model and then imposed on the aerodynamic geometry to generate the deformed aerodynamic model. This process realizes the displacement mapping from the structural model to the aerodynamic model. The static aeroelastic analysis is considered to reach equilibrium when the vertical displacement and torsion angle of the building top become stable, and the objective function satisfies the specified convergence tolerance of 10−3.Fig. 3Research flow chart.

Aerodynamic analysis is carried out in Fluent. The surface pressure nephograms of high-rise buildings with rectangular, elliptical, and streamlined cross-sections under a wind speed of 30 m/s are compared to analyze the influence of different building cross-sectional profiles on pressure distribution.

This is because the airflow directly impinges on the building surface, resulting in a sharp decrease in kinetic energy, most of which is converted into pressure energy. As shown in the Fig. 6a,b, for the single rectangular cross-section building, there is no significant difference in pressure between the inner and outer sides, with the pressure distribution on both sides being symmetric. Consequently, no pressure difference is generated, and thus no force can be transmitted to the connected corridor. This is attributed to the relatively flat geometry of the inner and outer sides of the building, which fail to guide the airflow. As the airflow passes over the building surface at its original velocity, there is no significant change in pressure.

As shown in Fig. 6c,d, the single building with an elliptical cross-section exhibits no pronounced pressure differential between its inner and outer surfaces, accompanied by a relatively low overall pressure level. This phenomenon arises from the curved contour of the building’s inner and outer walls, which elongates the airflow path. As the flow travels across the building surface, its velocity rises, leading to a pressure reduction in line with Bernoulli’s principle. Nevertheless, owing to the left–right symmetric geometry of the inner and outer profiles of the standalone building, no appreciable pressure difference exists between the two sides. As a result, no net pressure differential is produced, and hence no aerodynamic force can be transferred to the connecting corridor.

As shown in the Fig. 6e,f, for the single streamlined cross-section building, the surface pressure on the inner and outer sides is significantly differentiated: the inner side exhibits higher pressure, while the outer side shows lower pressure. This is attributed to the curved geometry of the outer side, which extends the airflow path, whereas the relatively flat inner side has no significant impact on the airflow velocity, thus maintaining higher pressure. The generation of a pressure difference indicates that the streamlined profile effectively utilizes aerodynamic forces by altering the airflow path, maximizing the lift-to-drag ratio (i.e., converting windward drag into lift to the greatest extent possible). The pressure difference direction of both buildings is from the inner side to the outer side; consequently, through the connecting effect of the steel-structured connected corridor, the wind load on the main buildings is shared.

Semi-coupled static aeroelastic analysis of high-rise corridor buildings

The CFD simulation in the previous section verified the wind pressure distribution of high-rise corridor buildings. In this section, the wind pressure obtained from the aerodynamic model is applied to the structural model for static analysis, aiming to investigate the actual wind-induced response of the structure caused by the lift force generated by the high-rise buildings. Meanwhile, to verify the role of the connected corridor in high-rise buildings, a comparative analysis is conducted on the lateral stiffness and torsional stiffness of the buildings with and without the connected corridor.

Conclusion

Based on the static aeroelastic analysis method of steady-state computational fluid dynamics, this study enables the independent implementation of aerodynamic optimization and structural optimization. It avoids the need to solve the sensitivities of interdisciplinary responses with respect to design variables across disciplines, while simultaneously accounting for the aerodynamic-structural coupling effects by transferring data such as loads, displacements, and shape torsion angles at the coupling interface. Taking high-rise corridor-connected structures with typical rectangular, elliptical, and streamlined cross-sections as the research objects, this study systematically compares the differences in wind resistance capacity between the structures with and without the corridor. The conclusions are as follows:The cross-sectional shape of the building can affect the wind pressure distribution under wind loads. The streamlined cross-section exhibits a more favorable lift coefficient, enabling the connected corridor to share the building load more effectively by leveraging aerodynamic forces. The rectangular building is least affected by wind angles, with its inter-story drift and torsion angle remaining within a stable range.The greater the building height, the larger the inter-story drift. With the increase in building height, the inter-story drift generally exhibits a linear growth trend, whereas the growth rate of the torsion angle gradually decreases by comparison. For example, in the graph of inter-story displacement versus height, the slope remains basically constant, that is, the second derivative is 0; whereas in the graph of torsion angle versus height, the slope decreases gradually, that is, the second derivative is less than 0.The corridor has an obvious inhibitory effect on the structural response under wind loads, which is mainly reflected in the inter-story displacement. For instance, when the wind direction angle is 45° for the building with a streamlined cross-section, the inter-story displacement is reduced by 78.14%. However, the corridor has a limited restraint effect on the torsion angle; under the same working conditions, the maximum reduction of the torsion angle is only 55.38%. Static analysis verifies that the corridor indeed plays a role in restricting building displacement, and when the wind direction is lateral, the lateral stiffness of the building with the corridor is more significant than that without the corridor. This paper investigates the wind resistance performance of high-rise corridor buildings with various cross-sections using a semi-decoupled static aeroelastic approach. Only static wind loads are adopted in the iterative coupling between aerodynamic and structural computations, leading to inherent research limitations. Severe vortex-induced vibration may occur if the vortex shedding frequency coincides with the structural natural frequency, while buffeting induced by incoming turbulence impairs occupant comfort and structural fatigue performance. Since only steady mean wind loads are considered without the time-varying characteristics of fluctuating wind, the present model fails to capture dynamic amplification effects including vortex-induced vibration, buffeting and resonance. For the flexible 250 m-high building with a fundamental frequency of 0.167 Hz, these dynamic responses considerably affect serviceability and fatigue durability. Such dynamic-related discussions cannot be fully supported by the current analytical framework and will be explored in future studies. Unsteady aeroelastic analysis based on Large Eddy Simulation (LES) can overcome the drawbacks of the present steady-state method in dynamic prediction. Follow-up research will implement LES-based unsteady aeroelastic simulation to quantify the influences of buffeting and vortex-induced vibration on internal forces and deformations of connected twin-tower buildings.
