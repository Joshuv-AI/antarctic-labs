Trading in residential energy systems with storage: a kinetic mean-field approach
Margherita Fabini, Andrea Pascucci, Alessio Rondelli

Abstract
We study a stochastic optimal control problem motivated by the operation of a large ensemble of residential storage devices coordinated by an energy aggregator. The aggregator remunerates prosumers in exchange for direct control of their batteries and seeks to jointly (i) reduce local supply-demand imbalances and (ii) exploit intraday price fluctuations through energy arbitrage. The core modeling feature is a kinetic mean-field formulation : the state of charge is treated as a position, the charging/discharging power as a velocity, and the control as an acceleration, thus encoding ramp-rate limitations and producing smooth power trajectories. This leads to a controlled McKean-Vlasov Langevin-type system in which both the drift and the objective functional depend on the time-marginal law of the state, allowing one to capture endogenous interaction effects and population-level stabilization incentives. The performance criterion combines the cost of grid exchange with convex penalties representing degradation and control effort, and includes mean-field terms that promote alignment with the population average; terminal contributions account for residual energy value and end-of-horizon coordination. The resulting control problem is Markovian and hypoelliptic, and naturally connects mean-field control with ultraparabolic operators of kinetic type. This viewpoint provides a coherent bridge between physically constrained storage actuation and law-dependent incentives in large-scale energy management. Numerical experiments based on deep learning solvers are presented to validate the model. From a computational standpoint, the problem is particularly challenging, as it yields a fully coupled forward-backward stochastic system associated with a five-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

Introduction

We investigate a stochastic optimal control problem modeling the strategy of an energy aggregator
managing a distributed network of grid-connected residential prosumers equipped with photovoltaic
panels and battery storage. The framework is grounded in a contractual arrangement wherein the
aggregator directly controls the storage assets in exchange for prosumer remuneration. This
centralized coordination targets two complementary objectives: (i) enhancing grid reliability by
mitigating supply-demand imbalances and smoothing local fluctuations; and (ii) extracting value
through energy arbitrage by leveraging electricity price volatility. The arbitrage component is
particularly relevant when storage is structurally underutilized, a scenario typical of winter
months characterized by reduced solar irradiance. Unlike individual households, the aggregator can
access wholesale markets to monetize intraday price spreads, effectively transforming otherwise
idle storage capacity into a revenue-generating asset. This aggregator-based modeling is
explicitly motivated by recent U.S. regulatory developments, most notably FERC Order No. 841,
which requires organized wholesale electricity markets to admit energy storage resources,
including behind-the-meter and aggregated residential batteries, under market rules tailored to
their operational constraints; see
[
9
]
for background and discussion. This regulatory
shift makes the centralized stochastic optimal control of a large population of grid-connected
prosumers a natural and economically meaningful problem.

To translate these economic and regulatory considerations into a tractable mathematical problem,
we formulate the aggregator’s decision-making process as a continuous-time stochastic control
problem for a large population of interacting systems. The resulting dynamics naturally combine
idiosyncratic uncertainty at the household level with aggregate effects induced by coordinated
operation, leading to a controlled mean-field formulation. More precisely, we consider a stylized
model governed by the following state processes:

•

: electricity price;

•

H_{t}
: net home load (in kW), i.e., household consumption minus renewable generation;

•

•

: battery’s state of charge (SOC), defined as

X^{a}_{t}=X^{a}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}V^{a}_{s}ds;

•

: control process representing the rate of change of power (ramp rate).

We denote by

1.1
A kinetic mean-field approach

Prior to specifying the precise structure of the model coefficients and the objective functional,
we outline the mathematical formulation of the problem. A distinguishing feature of our approach
is the adoption of a
kinetic mean-field
framework. Specifically, the system dynamics are
governed by the controlled Langevin-type equation

\begin{cases}dV^{a}_{t}=b(t,\mathbf{Z}^{a}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}^{a}_{t}],a_{t})dt+{\sigma}(t,\mathbf{Z}^{a}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}^{a}_{t}])dW_{t},\\
dX^{a}_{t}=V^{a}_{t}dt.\end{cases}

(1.2)

Langevin dynamics of the form (

[

They appear naturally in the kinetic theory of gases and fluid dynamics (see, e.g.,

[

In our model the system state is represented through a

•

•

the charging/discharging power

•

the control

Unlike standard models in which power can be adjusted instantaneously, our formulation explicitly
accounts for physical ramp-rate constraints, yielding a smooth evolution of the power output.
Moreover, (

) features

only on the individual state process but also on its time-marginal law. This dependence enables a
consistent description of the collective behavior of a large population of heterogeneous storage
devices under centralized control.

1.2
Related literature and numerical aspects

The optimal management of distributed energy resources has recently attracted considerable
attention across engineering, economics, and applied mathematics, and raises modeling and
mathematical challenges due to physical constraints, market frictions, and large-scale
interactions. Existing contributions adopt complementary perspectives, ranging from decentralized
competitive formulations to centralized coordination mechanisms.

A significant strand of literature focuses on decentralized approaches, often modeled through Mean
Field Games (MFG), where individual agents optimize their strategies against an aggregate signal.
In a purely deterministic setting,
[
14
]
investigates the coordination of micro-storage
devices where the market-clearing price is characterized as the fixed point of a coupled
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman system, broadcast to agents implementing decentralized responses. Moving
to the stochastic framework,
[
1
]
models a large smart grid as a nonzero-sum

N
-player game with endogenous price formation. In their setting, each node buys or sells
electricity based on instantaneous net home load and storage decisions, directly impacting the
spot price. The authors derive the corresponding extended MFG limit with common noise and compare
the decentralized equilibrium with the solution of a central planner, obtaining explicit results
in a linear-quadratic specification.

Complementary to these game-theoretic formulations are centralized control approaches, where a
single entity (e.g., an aggregator) optimizes the system’s global performance. This perspective,
adopted in our work, often requires advanced techniques to handle constraints and dimensionality.
For instance,
[
43
]
addresses the optimal operation of pumped-hydro storage with hard
capacity constraints via a level-set reformulation, though in a single-agent setting
without
mean-field interactions
. In the context of large populations,
[
22
]
develops a
Pontryagin-type analysis for an extended McKean-Vlasov control problem applied to smart grids.
Their work accommodates non-quadratic costs and general filtrations, allowing for jump-type
shocks, thus departing from the standard Markovian linear-quadratic assumptions. Similarly,

[
21
]
proposes a level-set methodology for McKean-Vlasov control under running and
terminal constraints in law. In their electricity storage application, the spot price features a
mean-field price-impact term driven by aggregate net injections, while the controller acts through
constrained injection-withdrawal decisions. One of the differences of our approach lies in the

kinetic
nature of the control problem. Unlike standard formulations that control power
directly and may yield discontinuous strategies, our model controls the ramp rate, ensuring smooth
power profiles that respect physical battery constraints.

We recall that well-posedness results for Langevin backward SDEs (in the non-McKean-Vlasov
setting) were first established in
[
4
]
. Since then, this class of degenerate SDEs and
associated PDEs has been investigated in several works (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
,

[
34
]
,
[
29
]
, and the references therein). The extension to the
McKean-Vlasov framework has been addressed more recently in
[
13
]
, in the infinite
dimensional case, and
[
40
]
.

From a mathematical perspective, the kinetic formulation preserves the Markovian structure of the
problem, allowing us to exploit the extensive theory and recent advances concerning hypoelliptic
operators of ultraparabolic type (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
,
[
37
]
,

[
3
]
). At the same time, the present framework raises a number of new questions that
are interesting in their own right and deserve further investigation. In order to keep the
exposition focused on the main contributions, we only sketch the proof of some results (e.g.
Theorem
2.3

Similarly, the proof of Theorem

From the computational standpoint, the McKean-Vlasov optimality system leads to a coupled
Forward-Backward SDE in which the backward component depends on the law of the forward state,
making classical grid-based schemes impractical. Numerical methods for this class of problems are
still developing (see, e.g.,

]
; we defer the complete proof to forthcoming work
[
41
]
.

We proceed by recalling the definitions relevant to our setting. In
[
27
]

it was shown that, when the diffusion coefficient
\mathbf{a}
is constant,
\mathcal{L}
is invariant with
respect to the left translations in the Lie group
({\mathbb{R}}^{3},\circ)
with composition law defined by

(t^{\prime},v^{\prime},x^{\prime})\circ(t,v,x)=(t+t^{\prime},v+v^{\prime},x+x^{\prime}+tv^{\prime});

moreover,
\mathcal{L}
is homogeneous of degree two with respect to the dilations group

{\delta}_{\lambda}(t,v,x)=(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda v,\lambda^{3}x).

It is then natural to consider the
anisotropic norm

|(v,x)|_{\mathcal{L}}=|v|+|x|^{\frac{1}{3}},\qquad(v,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

Definition 2.4

(
Anisotropic Hölder spaces).

We set

C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}:=L^{\infty}((0,T);C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L}}),\qquad{\alpha}\in(0,3],

where
C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L}}
is the anisotropic Hölder space consisting of all measurable functions

{G}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
for which the following norm is finite:

\displaystyle\|{G}\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L}}}:=\begin{cases}\|{G}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\sup\limits_{(v,x),(v^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\frac{|{G}(v,x)-{G}(v^{\prime},x^{\prime})|}{|(v-v^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})|_{\mathcal{L}}^{{\alpha}}},&{\alpha}\in(0,1],\\
\|{G}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\|{\partial}_{v}{G}\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}_{\mathcal{L}}}+\sup\limits_{v,x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{|{G}(v,x)-{G}(v,x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{\frac{{\alpha}}{3}}},&{\alpha}\in(1,3].\end{cases}

(2.17)

A crucial component of the definition of
intrinsic
regularity is the regularity along the
drift vector field

\mathcal{Y}:=v{\partial}_{x}+{\partial}_{t}

(2.18)

of
\mathcal{L}
: for any function
{F}
defined on
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
we set

\|{F}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{Y},T}}:=\sup_{(t,v,x)\in(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\sup_{s\in(t,T)}\frac{|{F}(s,v,x+(s-t)v)-{F}(t,v,x)|}{|s-t|^{\frac{{\alpha}}{2}}},\qquad{\alpha}\in(0,2].

Following
[
29
]
, we introduce the intrinsic Hölder spaces.

Definition 2.5

(
Intrinsic Hölder spaces).

For
{\alpha}\in(0,3]
, the intrinsic Hölder space
\mathbf{C}^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}
is defined by the norm

\displaystyle\|{F}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}}:=\begin{cases}\|{F}\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}}+\|{F}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{Y},T}},&\hskip 0.0pt\quad{\alpha}\in(0,1],\\
\|{F}\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}}+\|{\partial}_{v}{F}\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}_{\mathcal{L},T}}+\|{F}\|_{\mathbf{C}^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{Y},T}},&\hskip 0.0pt\quad{\alpha}\in(1,2],\\
\|{F}\|_{C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}}+\|{\partial}_{v}{F}\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}_{\mathcal{L},T}}+\|{\mathcal{Y}{F}}\|_{C^{{\alpha}-1}_{\mathcal{L},T}},&\hskip 0.0pt\quad{\alpha}\in(2,3].\end{cases}

(2.19)

Higher order intrinsic Hölder spaces are defined recursively.

Remark 2.6
.

Intrinsic regularity is stronger than anisotropic regularity; in particular, we have the strict
inclusion

\mathbf{C}^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}\subsetneq C^{{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},T}.

Moreover, if
u\in\mathbf{C}^{2}_{\mathcal{L},T}
, then the
Euclidean derivatives
\partial_{v}u
,
\partial_{vv}u
and the directional derivative
\mathcal{Y}u
exist
in the classical sense. Therefore, Theorem
2.3
provides a classical solution to the Cauchy
problem (
2.11
) under the sole assumption that the coefficients are anisotropically regular
(in particular, they may be merely measurable in time).

Even more importantly, for
u\in\mathbf{C}^{2}_{\mathcal{L},T}
the Itô formula holds, which is the fundamental
tool in the analysis of the associated stochastic optimal control problem. By contrast, if
u\in C^{2}_{\mathcal{L},T}
, then
\mathcal{L}u
can, in general, be interpreted only in a weak sense. We notice
explicitly that the existence of the Euclidean derivatives
\partial_{x}u
and
\partial_{t}u
is
ensured only under the stronger regularity assumption
u\in\mathbf{C}^{3}_{\mathcal{L},T}
.

2.2
McKean-Vlasov optimal control problem

In this section we introduce the standing assumptions on the coefficients and state our main
result on the well-posedness of the kinetic McKean-Vlasov optimal control problem (
2.1
).

Assumption 2.7
.

There exists positive constants
{\kappa}
and
\bar{{\alpha}}\in(0,1]
, such that the following conditions
hold for any
t\in[0,T]
,
{z},{z}^{\prime}\in\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and
a,a^{\prime}\in A
.

1.

Non-degeneracy and growth conditions:

\displaystyle{\kappa}^{-1}\leq{\sigma}(t,{z},{\mu})\leq{\kappa},\qquad

\displaystyle|g({z},{\mu})|\lesssim 1,

(2.20)

\displaystyle|b(t,{z},{\mu},a)|\lesssim 1+|a|,\qquad

\displaystyle|{\partial}_{a}b(t,{z},{\mu},a)|\lesssim 1,

(2.21)

\displaystyle|f(t,{z},{\mu},a)|\lesssim 1+|a|^{2},\qquad

\displaystyle|{\partial}_{a}f(t,{z},{\mu},a)|\lesssim 1+|a|.

(2.22)

2.

Regularity:
the diffusion coefficient
{\sigma}
is differentiable in
v
and we have

|{\partial}_{v}{\sigma}(t,{z})-{\partial}_{v}{\sigma}(t,{z}^{\prime})|\lesssim|{z}-{z}^{\prime}|^{\bar{{\alpha}}}_{\mathcal{L}},\qquad|{\sigma}(t,{z},{\mu})-{\sigma}(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu})|+|g({z},{\mu})-g({z}^{\prime},{\mu})|\lesssim|{z}-{z}^{\prime}|.

Moreover,
b,f
are differentiable in
v,a
and satisfy

\begin{split}|b(t,{z},{\mu},a)-b(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu},a)|+|f(t,{z},{\mu},a)-f(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu},a)|&\lesssim|{z}-{z}^{\prime}|,\\
|{\partial}_{v}b(t,{z},{\mu},a)-{\partial}_{v}b(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu},a^{\prime})|+|{\partial}_{v}f(t,{z},{\mu},a)-{\partial}_{v}f(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu},a^{\prime})|&\lesssim|{z}-{z}^{\prime}|^{\bar{{\alpha}}}_{\mathcal{L}}+|a-a^{\prime}|^{\bar{{\alpha}}},\\

(2.23)

The minimizer
\hat{a}=\hat{a}(t,{z},{\mu},p_{v})
is differentiable in
v,p_{v}
and satisfies

\displaystyle|{\partial}_{v}\hat{a}(t,{z},{\mu},p_{v})-{\partial}_{v}\hat{a}(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu},p_{v}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim|{z}-{z}^{\prime}|^{\bar{{\alpha}}}_{\mathcal{L}}+|p_{v}-p_{v}^{\prime}|,

(2.24)

\displaystyle|{\partial}_{p_{v}}\hat{a}(t,{z},{\mu},p_{v})-{\partial}_{p_{v}}\hat{a}(t,{z}^{\prime},{\mu},p_{v}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\lesssim|{z}-{z}^{\prime}|^{\bar{{\alpha}}}_{\mathcal{L}}+|p_{v}-p_{v}^{\prime}|^{\bar{{\alpha}}}.

(2.25)

3.

Lipschitz continuity in the measure argument:

\begin{split}|{\sigma}(t,{z},{\mu})-{\sigma}(t,{z},{\mu}^{\prime})|+|g({z},{\mu})-g({z},{\mu}^{\prime})|&\lesssim\mathcal{W}_{1}({\mu},{\mu}^{\prime}),\\
|f(t,{z},{\mu},a)-f(t,{z},{\mu}^{\prime},a)|+|b(t,{z},{\mu},a)-b(t,{z},{\mu}^{\prime},a)|&\lesssim\mathcal{W}_{1}({\mu},{\mu}^{\prime}).\end{split}

(2.26)

The main theoretical result of the paper is the following

Theorem 2.8

(
Well-posedness for the McKean-Vlasov optimal control problem (
2.1
)).

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.7
, for any
\eta\in L^{2}({\Omega},\mathcal{F}_{0},P)
there
exists a unique strong solution
(\mathbf{Z}^{{\eta}},Y^{{\eta}},{\zeta}^{{\eta}})
to the McKean-Vlasov FBSDE

\begin{cases}dV_{t}=b\left(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}],\hat{a}(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}],\sigma^{-1}(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}]){\zeta}_{t})\right)dt+\sigma(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}])dW_{t},\\

\mathbf{Z}_{0}=\eta,\quad Y_{T}=g(\mathbf{Z}_{T},[\mathbf{Z}_{T}]).\end{cases}

(2.27)

Moreover:

•

there exists a continuous function
u\in\textnormal{Lip}_{0,T}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})\cap\mathbf{C}^{3+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t}

for any
{\alpha}\in(0,\bar{{\alpha}})
and

, such that the processes
\left(u(t,\mathbf{Z}^{{\eta}}_{t})\right)_{t\in[0,T]}

•

(\mathbf{Z}^{{\eta}},\hat{a})

Remark 2.9
.

Even if we restrict our attention to square-integrable solutions, in (

3
Numerical tests

In this section we describe the stochastic dynamics used in the numerical experiments. We consider
a stylized yet fully consistent model, comprising two exogenous processes calibrated to historical
data for the electricity price and the net household load, coupled with controlled dynamics for
the battery power and the state of charge.
The numerical experiments provide encouraging
evidence in support of the proposed approach, as the performance under the optimal control policy
compares favorably with that of the uncontrolled benchmark.

3.1
Electricity price

The electricity spot price
S_{t}
is modeled as a positive mean-reverting process.
Specifically, the log-price follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) dynamics:

d\log S_{t}=\kappa^{S}(t)\bigl(\mu^{S}(t)-\log S_{t}\bigr)dt+\sigma^{S}(t)dW^{S}_{t},

(3.1)

where
\kappa^{S}(t)>0
is a piecewise-constant day/night mean-reversion speed,
\mu^{S}(t)
is the
mean-reversion level, and
\sigma^{S}(t)
is a piecewise-constant day/night volatility profile. The
explicit solution for constant
\kappa^{S}
is

S_{t}=\exp\!\left(e^{-\kappa^{S}t}\log S_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\kappa^{S}e^{-\kappa^{S}(t-u)}\hat{\mu}^{S}(u)du+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{S}(u)e^{-\kappa^{S}(t-u)}dW^{S}_{u}\right),

(3.2)

where
\hat{\mu}^{S}(t):=\mu^{S}(t)-\frac{(\sigma^{S}(t))^{2}}{2\kappa^{S}}
. Hence,
S_{t}
is log-normal
with

E[\log S_{t}]=e^{-\kappa^{S}t}\log S_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\kappa^{S}e^{-\kappa^{S}(t-u)}\hat{\mu}^{S}(u)du,\qquad{\text{var}}(\log S_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}(\sigma^{S}(u))^{2}e^{-2\kappa^{S}(t-u)}du.

(3.3)

The deterministic function
\mu^{S}(t)
captures predictable seasonality at multiple time scales
(intraday peaks and weekly cycles) and represents the time-dependent level to which the log-price
reverts. In the numerical implementation, we approximate
\mu^{S}(\cdot)
by a degree-
2

trigonometric polynomial, while
\sigma^{S}(\cdot)
and
\kappa^{S}(\cdot)
are taken piecewise
constant with two regimes, corresponding to daytime (hours 8-19) and nighttime (all remaining
hours).

The coefficients are calibrated on historical time series of the Italian day-ahead PUN
price
3
3

3

page:

https://www.mercatoelettrico.org/it-it/Home/Esiti/Elettricita/MGP/Esiti/PUN

Source: Gestore dei Mercati Energetici S.p.A.
which represents the reference wholesale price for

dH_{t}=\kappa^{H}(t)\bigl(\mu^{H}(t)-H_{t}\bigr)dt+\sigma^{H}(t)dW^{H}_{t},

(3.4)

where
\mu^{H}(t)
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
2
capturing predictable seasonality,
while
\sigma^{H}(t),\kappa^{H}(t)>0
follow a day/night specification.

We calibrate the model using a historical time series from a residential energy system equipped

3.3
Battery power and cost functional

We adopt the following dynamics for the battery power
V_{t}
, with the convention that
V_{t}>0

corresponds to charging and
V_{t}<0
to discharging:

dV_{t}^{a}=b^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a},a_{t})dt+\sigma^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a}])dW^{V}_{t},\qquad\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a}=(S_{t},H_{t},V_{t}^{a},X_{t}^{a}).

(3.5)

Here

\displaystyle b^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a},a_{t})

\displaystyle=a_{t}{\varphi}(X_{t}^{a})+c_{1}\psi(-X_{t}^{a})\psi(V_{max}-V_{t}^{a})-c_{2}\psi(X_{t}^{a}-X_{max})\psi(V_{t}^{a}-V_{min}),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sigma^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a}])

\displaystyle=\sigma_{V}+\sigma_{V}^{H}|H_{t}|+\sigma_{V}^{V}|V^{a}_{t}|+\sigma_{V}^{\kappa}\left|V^{a}_{t}-E\left[V^{a}_{t}\right]\right|

(3.7)

and the saturation functions

\displaystyle{\varphi}(x)=\max\!\left\{0,\min\!\left\{1,\frac{x}{\delta},\frac{X_{\max}-x}{\delta}\right\}\right\},\qquad\psi(x)=\max\left\{0,\min\left\{1,\frac{x}{{\delta}}\right\}\right\},

attenuate the control action as the state approaches the admissible SOC and power limits,
thereby mitigating boundary violations without enforcing hard state constraints.

We allow
\sigma^{V}
to depend on
(S,H,V,X)
in order to capture state-dependent uncertainty, for
instance random tracking or forecast errors. Specifically, we let volatility increase with
operating stress, i.e., under large net home load and at high charging/discharging power levels.
We could also incorporate asymmetric charging and discharging efficiencies, leading to dynamics of
the form

dX_{t}^{a}=\left(\eta_{c}V_{t}^{a,+}-\eta_{d}^{-1}V_{t}^{a,-}\right)dt,

with

\eta_{c},\eta_{d}\in(0,1)
. Although economically realistic, this refinement leaves the theoretical
structure unchanged and is therefore omitted for simplicity.

For the cost functional (
1.1
), we consider the running cost

f(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{a}],a_{t})=S_{t}(H_{t}+V_{t}^{a})+\lambda_{V}|V_{t}^{a}|^{2}+\lambda_{a}a_{t}^{2}+h_{\textbf{bat}}(X_{t}^{a}-E[X_{t}^{a}])+h_{\textbf{con}}(H_{t}+V_{t}^{a}-E[H_{t}+V_{t}^{a}]),

(3.8)

with
h_{i}(x)=c_{i}((x^{-})^{2}+2(x^{+})^{2})
for some positive constants
c_{i}
, for

i=\textbf{bat},\textbf{con}
. The term
S_{t}V_{t}^{a}
drives the arbitrage mechanism, encouraging
charging at low prices and discharging at high prices. Quadratic penalties model battery
degradation and ramp-rate constraints. The mean-field terms penalize deviations from the
population average, promoting aggregate stabilization.

The terminal cost is

g(\mathbf{Z}_{T}^{a},[\mathbf{Z}_{T}^{a}])=-\gamma S_{T}X_{T}^{a}+\frac{\omega}{2}\left(X_{T}^{a}-E\left[X_{T}^{a}\right]\right)^{2}.

(3.9)

An alternative formulation would replace the quadratic mean-field penalty with a Wasserstein-
2

distance between the empirical SOC distribution and a prescribed target law. This choice would
yield a genuinely distributional control objective, which is conceptually appealing and may be of
independent interest, but we leave it for future work.

3.4
FBSNN and the optimal control

The optimization problem is numerically challenging due to the fully coupled and nonlinear
structure of the associated FBSDE combined with a mean-field type interaction. In particular,
since the running cost depends quadratically on the control, choosing the optimal strategy leads
to a backward component with quadratic growth in
\zeta
and a forward dynamics that depends
explicitly on the backward variables. This leads to a coupled system associated with a
five-dimensional HJB equation.

More precisely, the stochastic control problem is associated to the following mean-field FBSDE

\begin{cases}dV_{t}=b^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},\hat{a}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},\zeta_{t}))dt+{\sigma}^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}])dW^{V}_{t},\qquad\qquad\mathbf{Z}_{t}=(S_{t},H_{t},V_{t},X_{t}),\\
dX_{t}=V_{t}dt.\\
dY_{t}=-f(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}],\hat{a}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}],\zeta_{t}))dt+\zeta_{t}dW_{t},\\
Y_{T}=g(\mathbf{Z}_{T},[\mathbf{Z}_{T}]),\end{cases}

(3.10)

where
\hat{a}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},\zeta_{t}):=-\frac{{\varphi}(X_{t})\zeta^{V}_{t}}{2\lambda_{a}{\sigma}^{V}(\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}])}
and
(S,H)
are the exogenous processes defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.4
).

To solve (
3.10
), we adopt a slight modification of the deep learning approach
introduced in
[
25
]
, inspired by
[
45
]
. The key idea is to approximate the
backward component over the full time horizon by the neural representation

Y_{t}\approx u_{\theta}(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}),

where
u_{\theta}
is a feed-forward neural network with parameters
\theta
.

A key structural feature of our implementation is that the process
\zeta_{t}
is not parameterized
by a separate network. Instead, it is recovered via automatic differentiation:

\zeta_{t}=\nabla_{(S,H,V)}u_{\theta}(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t})\Sigma(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},[\mathbf{Z}_{t}]),

where
\Sigma
is a

3\times 3
diagonal matrix with
({\sigma}^{S},{\sigma}^{H},{\sigma}^{V})
on the diagonal. This design enforces
consistency between
Y
and
\zeta
, keeps the martingale term aligned with the value function
approximation, reduces the number of trainable parameters, and improves numerical stability in the
presence of quadratic growth in
\zeta
.

The dependence on the law
[\mathbf{Z}_{t}]
is handled through a particle approximation. At each training
step, we simulate
N_{\text{sim}}
trajectories and approximate the law of
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
by its
empirical measure,

[\mathbf{Z}_{t}]\approx\frac{1}{N_{\text{sim}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{sim}}}\delta_{\mathbf{Z}_{t}^{(i)}}.

In particular since the dependence on the law is only through the mean we
may just calculate it as empirical average across the simulated batch. In this way, the mean-field
FBSDE is approximated by a large but finite interacting particle system. As
N_{\text{sim}}

increases, the approximation converges in the sense of propagation of chaos.

We discretize the time interval
[0,T]
on a uniform grid
t_{n}=n\Delta t
,
n=0,\dots,N
, with

\Delta t=T/N
. Let

Y_{t_{n}}=u_{\theta}(t_{n},\mathbf{Z}_{t_{n}})

denote the network output evaluated at

(t_{n},\mathbf{Z}_{t_{n}})
. The forward and backward components are then approximated by a
forward

Euler-Maruyama scheme:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{t_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}_{t_{n}}+b_{t_{n}}\Delta t+\sigma_{t_{n}}\Delta W_{n},

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{t_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=Y_{t_{n}}-f_{t_{n}}\Delta t+\zeta_{t_{n}}\Delta W_{n},

where
\Delta W_{n}=W_{t_{n+1}}-W_{t_{n}}
and
b_{t_{n}},\sigma_{t_{n}},f_{t_{n}},\zeta_{t_{n}}
are
evaluated at
(t_{n},\mathbf{Z}_{t_{n}})
(and, when applicable, at the corresponding mean-field terms).

Training amounts to minimizing the mismatch between the network outputs and the one-step
discretized backward dynamics, together with the terminal condition:

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\|Y_{t_{n+1}}-\widetilde{Y}_{t_{n+1}}\right\|^{2}+\left\|Y_{t_{N}}-g(\mathbf{Z}_{t_{N}})\right\|^{2}+\left\|\zeta_{t_{N}}-\nabla g(\mathbf{Z}_{t_{N}})\sigma_{t_{N}}\right\|^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm on

{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\text{sim}}}
. The last term enforces consistency at terminal time also at the gradient
level, which typically improves numerical stability. Given the strong coupling and quadratic
growth of the driver, standard numerical stabilization techniques are employed, such as gradient
clipping, antithetic sampling and a multi-stage learning rate schedule.

The neural network
u_{\theta}:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is parameterized by
\theta
and takes as input
the concatenation of time and state variables
(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{5}
. Its scalar output provides an
approximation of the value process
Y_{t}
. We use a fully connected architecture with an input
layer of dimension
1+4
(time plus state), four hidden layers with 256 neurons each, and a
one-dimensional linear output layer. All hidden layers employ the sine activation function. Smooth
periodic activations are well suited to approximating solutions of semilinear parabolic PDEs,
which are typically smooth in both time and space. In particular, compared with piecewise-linear
activations, sine activations yield smoother gradients, which is advantageous when spatial
derivatives are computed via automatic differentiation; moreover, unlike sigmoid-type
nonlinearities, they are less prone to vanishing gradients. Network weights are initialized via
Xavier (Glorot) initialization, which scales the variance of the initial weights with the layer
width. This choice helps prevent early saturation of the sine activations and promotes stable
signal propagation across depth. Overall, the architecture is tailored to approximate a smooth
value function while preserving the structural coupling of the FBSDE through automatic
differentiation.

4.1
Kinetic FBSDEs

As a first step, we establish a general well-posedness result for kinetic FBSDEs of the
form

\begin{cases}dV_{t}=b(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},{\zeta}_{t})dt+\sigma(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t})dW_{t},\qquad\mathbf{Z}=(V,X),\\
dX_{t}=V_{t}dt,\\

\mathbf{Z}_{0}={\eta},\quad Y_{T}=g(\mathbf{Z}_{T}).\end{cases}

(4.1)

Definition 4.1

(
Decoupling field).

A decoupling field for (
4.1
) is a classical solution to the Cauchy problem (
2.11
)
with

F(t,{z},u,p)=-b(t,{z},p)p-f(t,{z},p),

and terminal condition

.

Remark 4.2
.

Assume that a decoupling field
u
for (
4.1
) exists and that the (forward) SDE

\begin{cases}dV_{t}=b(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t},\sigma(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}){\partial}_{v}u(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}))dt+\sigma(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t})dW_{t},\\
dX_{t}=V_{t}dt\end{cases}

(4.2)

admits a solution with initial datum
\mathbf{Z}_{0}={\eta}
. Applying Itô’s formula to the process

u(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t})
and setting

Y_{t}=u(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}),\qquad{\zeta}_{t}=\sigma(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}){\partial}_{v}u(t,\mathbf{Z}_{t}),

(4.3)

we obtain that the triple
(\mathbf{Z},Y,{\zeta})
solves (
4.1
). Moreover, by (
4.3
) and the
fact that
\mathbf{Z}
admits a density satisfying uniform Gaussian bounds (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
),
we have

\|{\zeta}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\Omega)}=\|\sigma{\partial}_{v}u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2})}.

(4.4)

Notation 4.3
.

We denote by

•

\mathcal{S}^{2}_{0,T}
the space of continuous adapted processes
S

such that

E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|S_{t}|^{2}\right]<\infty.

•

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0,T}
the space of progressively measurable processes

such that

E\left[\int_{0}^{T}|H_{t}|^{2}dt\right]<\infty.

In Theorem
4.5
we prove strong well-posedness for (
4.1
) by extending the classical

four-step scheme
introduced by Ma, Protter, and Yong
[
32
]
. The proof rests on
two key ingredients:

•

the strong well-posedness of the FBSDE on sufficiently small time intervals, a classical result under standard Lipschitz continuity assumptions;

•

Theorem
2.3
, which ensures the existence of a decoupling field for (

).

By combining these ingredients, we construct the solution iteratively and propagate it in time so
as to cover the entire interval
[0,T]
. The argument crucially exploits the intrinsic regularity
properties of the decoupling field, allowing us to extend local well-posedness to arbitrarily long
time horizons and thereby obtain global strong well-posedness.

Assumption 4.4
.

There exists positive constants

and

and

1.

Non-degeneracy and growth conditions:

\displaystyle{\kappa}^{-1}\leq{\sigma}(t,{z})\leq{\kappa},\qquad|b(t,{z},p)|+|g({z})|\lesssim 1,\qquad|f(t,{z},p)|\lesssim 1+|p|.

(4.5)

2.

Regularity:

{\sigma}\in\textnormal{Lip}_{0,T}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
,

and

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
{\eta}\in L^{2}({\Omega},\mathcal{F}_{0},P)
. Under Assumption

) admits

a unique strong solution
(\mathbf{Z},Y,{\zeta})\in\mathcal{S}^{2}_{0,T}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}_{0,T}\times\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0,T}

and

Proof.

We begin by proving strong existence. By Theorem
2.3
, there exists a decoupling field

u\in\textnormal{Lip}_{0,T}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
for (
4.1
). Theorem
2.3
also ensures that
u\in\mathbf{C}^{2+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t}
for any
{\alpha}\in(0,1)
and
t\in[0,T)
. However, this regularity is not
sufficient to ensure strong well-posedness of (
4.2
). The obstruction arises from the
degeneracy of the system. Indeed, the strongest currently available results on regularization by
noise for SDEs of the form (
4.2
), due to Chaudru de Raynal
[
11
]
, require that
the drift coefficient

b(t,v,x,\sigma(t,v,x){\partial}_{v}u(t,v,x))

be Hölder continuous in
x
with exponent strictly larger than
\frac{2}{3}
(and Hölder
continuous in
v
with some positive exponent). Now, the condition
u\in\mathbf{C}^{2+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t}
only
yields
{\partial}_{v}u\in C^{1+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t}
, which in turn implies
{\partial}_{v}u(t,\cdot)\in C^{\frac{1+{\alpha}}{3}}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
in the standard Euclidean sense and this level of regularity is not
sufficient to satisfy the above requirement.

At this stage we invoke the stronger regularity assumption (
4.6
) on the coefficients, which
guarantee that, setting
\hat{{F}}(t,z):={F}(t,z,u(t,z),{\partial}_{v}u(t,z))
, we have

{\sigma},\hat{{F}}\in C^{1+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t},\qquad t\in[0,T),\ {\alpha}\in(0,\bar{{\alpha}}).

(4.7)

This yields higher regularity for the solution. Indeed, under condition (
4.7
), the Schauder
estimates for equation
\mathcal{L}u=\hat{F}
proved in
[
30
]
(see also Proposition 3.9 and
Lemma 3.10 in
[
12
]
) yield
u\in\mathbf{C}^{3+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t}
for every
{\alpha}\in(0,\bar{{\alpha}})
and
every
t\in[0,T)
. In particular,
{\partial}_{v}u\in C^{2+{\alpha}}_{\mathcal{L},t},
and this regularity is enough to
guarantee that (
4.2
) is strongly well posed.

We next address uniqueness. Let
(\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}},\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{{\zeta}})
be a strong solution to
(
4.1
). By the uniform-in-
t
Lipschitz continuity of
u,\sigma
in
{z}
, and of
b,f,g

in
({z},{{\zeta}})
, the standard local theory for FBSDEs (see, e.g., Theorem 1.1 in
[
16
]
)
applies. In particular, there exists
{\delta}>0
sufficiently small such that, for every
s\in[0,T-{\delta}]
, the FBSDE on the interval
[s,s+{\delta}]
with initial condition
\mathbf{Z}_{s}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{s}
and
terminal condition

Y_{s+{\delta}}=u(s+{\delta},\mathbf{Z}_{s+{\delta}})

admits a unique strong solution
(\mathbf{Z},Y,{\zeta})
satisfying
(\mathbf{Z},Y)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}_{s,s+{\delta}}
and

{\zeta}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{s,s+{\delta}}
.

We introduce a partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
such that
t_{k+1}-t_{k}\leq{\delta}
for all
k
:
arguing by backward induction on
k
, we show that

\widetilde{Y}_{t}=u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\widetilde{{\zeta}}_{t}=\sigma(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}){\partial}_{v}u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}),\qquad t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}],

for every
k=0,\dots,N-1
.

Step 1: the last interval.
On the last interval
[t_{N-1},T]
the restriction of
(\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}},\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{{\zeta}})
solves
the local FBSDE with terminal datum
Y_{T}=u(T,\mathbf{Z}_{T})
. By local uniqueness on
[t_{N-1},T]
, this
solution must coincide with the solution constructed via the decoupling field
u
; hence,

\widetilde{Y}_{t}=u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}),\qquad\widetilde{{\zeta}}_{t}=\sigma(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}){\partial}_{v}u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}),\qquad t\in[t_{N-1},T].

In particular,

\widetilde{Y}_{t_{N-1}}=u(t_{N-1},\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t_{N-1}})
a.s.

Step 2: induction.
Assume that for some
k\in\{0,\dots,N-2\}
we already know

\widetilde{Y}_{t_{k+1}}=u(t_{k+1},\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t_{k+1}})
a.s. Then, on the interval
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
,
the restriction of
(\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}},\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{{\zeta}})
solves the local FBSDE with terminal
condition

Y_{t_{k+1}}=\widetilde{Y}_{t_{k+1}}=u(t_{k+1},\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t_{k+1}}).

By local uniqueness on
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
, it follows that this restriction must coincide with the
solution associated with the terminal condition
u(t_{k+1},\cdot)
. Hence,

\widetilde{Y}_{t}=u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}),\qquad\widetilde{{\zeta}}_{t}=\sigma(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}){\partial}_{v}u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}),\qquad t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}],

and in particular
\widetilde{Y}_{t_{k}}=u(t_{k},\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t_{k}})
a.s. This closes the backward
induction.

We have thus shown that
\widetilde{Y}_{t}=u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})
and

\widetilde{{\zeta}}_{t}=\sigma(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}){\partial}_{v}u(t,\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Plugging this
identity into the forward equation of (
4.1
), we see that
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
solves the feedback
SDE (
4.2
). By strong well-posedness of the above SDE, we obtain that
\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}
and

\mathbf{Z}
are indistinguishable; consequently, also
\widetilde{Y}=Y
in
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{0,T}
and

\widetilde{{\zeta}}={\zeta}
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0,T}
. This proves uniqueness for (
4.1

∎
