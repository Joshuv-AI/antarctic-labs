Association of frailty and metabolism with multi-state model in fatty liver disease, diabetes mellitus and all-cause mortality
Authors: Xiaoyan Wang, Chenxi Jin, Qi Zhong, Xiaomeng Wang, Yining Xu, Meixuan Li, Lu Liu, Hui Huang, Anying Liang, Jing Wang, Xianbo Wu, Linna Li, Mengchen Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFrailty is a global public health issue, and metabolic syndrome (MetS) is also associated with a variety of adverse health outcomes. However, the relationship between the combined effects of these two factors and the occurrence and progression of long-term diseases remains unclear.MethodsThis is a population-based cohort study involving 165,555 participants from the UK Biobank. We combined frailty status scores with metabolic condition scores to assess the predictive value. Associations were assessed using multi-state model, corrected Cox models, restricted cubic spline, stratified analysis, Kaplan-Meier analysis.ResultsDuring the 12.7-year follow-up period, 3,585 individuals were newly diagnosed with metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), among whom 170 developed non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM) and 699 died from all causes. Compared with Group 1(neither frailty nor MetS), the HR and 95% confidence intervals for Group 4 (both frailty and MetS) was 2.92 (2.45–3.47) for MAFLD, 4.21 (2.23–7.96) for MAFLD→NIDDM, and 1.22 (0.75–2.02) for MAFLD→Death. The Kaplan-Meier analysis further confirmed these findings. Consistent findings regarding the frailty phenotype and MetS had also been observed in the progression of NIDDM.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a positive association between frailty phenotype and MetS in disease onset and progression, and their combined assessment enables a more effective early risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02201-4.

Introduction

Frailty is recognized as an age-related clinical syndrome characterized by diminished physiological reserve and multisystem dysfunction, resulting in heightened susceptibility to external stressors [1]. This condition affects millions of older adults worldwide and has become an increasingly significant global health concern as populations continue to age [2]. Existing studies indicate that frailty represents a prevalent, medically important state in elderly populations, given its strong association with negative health consequences including falls, disability, hospitalization and mortality [3]. It has been demonstrated that frailty correlates with a greater possibility of developing metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), cirrhosis, liver cancer, liver-related mortality and all-cause mortality [4–6]. To screen for frailty early, the frailty phenotype and frailty index were created [7]. In our article, we employ the frailty phenotype to assess frailty.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a prevalent endocrine disorder driven by the increasing global burden of obesity and characterized by a constellation of metabolic abnormalities, including hypertension, abdominal obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia [8]. The worldwide prevalence of MetS continues to rise, fueled by growing rates of excess weight—particularly visceral adiposity—and type 2 diabetes mellitus [9, 10]. MetS and Non-Insulin Dependent Diabetes Mellitus (NIDDM), share insulin resistance as a key pathophysiological underpinning, frequently co-occur and exacerbate each other, collectively elevating the risk of cardiovascular diseases and hepatic metabolic disorders such as MAFLD [11–13]. Numerous studies highlight associations between metabolic factors and the progression of liver disease, as well as their synergistic effects with weekly binge drinking [12, 13]. Given that MetS encompasses multiple cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors, it contributes to higher morbidity and premature mortality over an individual’s lifespan [14]. Thus, early identification and intervention in patients with metabolic syndrome are of critical clinical importance.

The multi-state model provides an ideal analytical framework for this purpose. It not only assesses the initial risk from the baseline state (health) to various disease states (e.g., MAFLD, NIDDM) (a static perspective) but, more importantly, quantifies the dynamic risks of transitions between different disease states (e.g., MAFLD→NIDDM, NIDDM→MAFLD) and from disease states to death (a dynamic perspective), thereby fully delineating the pathways and drivers of disease progression [15, 16]. Frailty and MetS represent two major global public health challenges that, while not constituting specific diseases, are strongly associated with adverse long-term health outcomes. Despite their individual clinical significance, the synergistic effects of concurrent frailty and MetS on disease progression remain limited. MAFLD and NIDDM, as two closely interrelated metabolic diseases, often present concurrently or sequentially in clinical settings, forming a complex disease progression network [17]. However, existing research has predominantly examined the static associations of frailty or MetS with the incidence risk of either MAFLD or NIDDM in isolation. There is a lack of studies investigating, from a dynamic disease evolution perspective, how these factors influence the mutual transitions between MAFLD and NIDDM and the ultimate outcome of death.

In this study, we aimed to combine assessments of the frailty phenotype and MetS and innovatively apply a multi-state model. We seek to comprehensively investigate their combined effects on the incidence of MAFLD and NIDDM, their mutual transitions, and all-cause mortality from both static (baseline incidence risk) and dynamic (inter-state transition risk) dimensions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study utilized data from 500,000 participants recruited through the UK Biobank (UKB) between 2006 and 2010. All participants completed comprehensive baseline assessments including a touchscreen questionnaire, nurse-conducted interviews, and standardized physical measurements. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant for data collection, subsequent analyses, and record linkage. The study received ethical approval and is part of the UKB project (NHS National Research Ethics Service 16/NW/0274).

Our exclusion criteria were applied sequentially as follows: First, we excluded participants with incomplete data on frailty phenotype or MetS. Second, we removed those with previously diagnosed MAFLD or NIDDM at baseline. Third, we excluded participants aged under 20 years and those lacking complete demographic or lifestyle data (including race, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and sleep patterns). After applying these rigorous exclusion criteria, our final analytical cohort comprised 165,555 eligible participants (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The outcomes of this study were MAFLD and NIDDM. MAFLD was defined as fatty liver and in addition to one of the following three criteria, namely overweight/obesity, presence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or evidence of metabolic dysregulation [18]. NIDDM was defined as non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus, which also commonly referred to as T2DM.

Another important outcome was all-cause mortality, and death outcomes were identified through linkage to national death registries. Detailed information can be found at link https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/~bbdatan/DeathSummaryReport.html.

Frailty phenotype and metabolic syndrome and other covariates

Frailty was assessed using a modified Fried phenotype, with participants classified as frail if they met ≥ 3 of 5 criteria: unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, reduced grip strength, low physical activity (limited to light DIY activities), and slow walking speed - yielding a frailty score ranging from 0 to 5 (Score-frailty; Table S1) [3]. Similarly, MetS was diagnosed when participants fulfilled ≥ 3 of 5 components: central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 88 cm for women/≥102 cm for men), elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL), reduced HDL cholesterol (< 40 mg/dL in men/<50 mg/dL in women), elevated fasting glucose (≥ 100 mg/dL), or hypertension (systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg), resulting in a MetS severity score of 0–5 (Score-MetS; Table S2) [8].

Our analysis incorporated comprehensive socio-demographic and lifestyle variables. Socio-demographic factors included age, sex, and racial background. Lifestyle characteristics encompassed smoking status, alcohol intake, sleep duration, cumulative dietary risk score, body mass index (BMI), and physical activity levels. The dietary risk score (range: 0–9; 0 = healthiest) quantified consumption patterns across nine food groups: fruits, vegetables, total fish, red meat, processed meat, dairy products, cereals, spread type, and water intake (Table S3). BMI categories were defined as underweight (< 19.5 kg/m²), normal weight (19.5–25 kg/m²), and overweight (> 25 kg/m²). Physical activity was considered adequate if participants engaged in either: (1) ≥ 150 min/week of moderate exercise (achieving 50–70% of maximum heart rate [calculated as 220-age]), (2) ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous activity (70–85% maximum heart rate), or (3) an equivalent combination. Additional metabolic biomarkers analyzed included total bilirubin, ALT, AST, creatinine, urea, and CRP levels, all measured with high precision (coefficient of variation < 5%). Detailed blood sample quality control protocols can be found at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/showcase/docs/biomarker_issues.pdf.

Statistical analysis

We initially stratified the dataset according to frailty phenotype and MetS status before collecting baseline characteristics. For missing data handling, we employed mice imputation for continuous variables while excluding cases with missing categorical variables. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables are reported as frequencies with percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, allowing us to systematically evaluate potential differences across participant subgroups.

For longitudinal outcome analysis, we stratified participants into four mutually exclusive groups (Group 1 - Group 4) based on frailty and MetS status: Group 1 (neither condition), Group 2 (frailty only), Group 3 (MetS only), and Group 4 (both conditions). In order to fully assess the relationship between the frailty phenotype and metabolic syndrome in various outcomes, as well as their mutual transformation and disease progression, we constructed a multi-state model. In this study, the multi-state model includes four core states: a baseline state, a MAFLD state, a NIDDM state, and a terminal state defined as death. The model encompasses seven plausible transition pathways, which are predefined as follows: baseline to MAFLD; baseline to NIDDM; baseline to death; MAFLD to NIDDM; MAFLD to death; NIDDM to MAFLD; and NIDDM to death (Fig. 1). To ensure the reliability of the subsequent analysis, we conducted a test of the Markov assumption, which yielded a result of p > 0.05, indicating that the conditions for using the multi-state model were satisfied. Subsequently, We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values, employing the same three-tiered adjustment strategy (unadjusted, age/sex-adjusted, and fully adjusted models, and multivariable adjustment model based on directed acyclic graph (DAG)) (Figure S1). To control potential inflation of Type I error, we performed multiple testing correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. To further evaluate the combined effect of the frailty phenotype and metabolic syndrome, we calculated the composite score of frailty and MetS (0–10) and described the dose-response relationship using a restricted cubic spline (RCS) plot.

Next, we examined potential interactions between frailty phenotype/MetS and various covariates (age, sex, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, BMI, and physical activity) by incorporating additive product terms in Cox proportional hazards models. Comprehensive subgroup analyses were performed, stratifying by: age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years), sex (male vs. female), ethnicity (White vs. Non-White), smoking status (never/former/current), alcohol consumption (never/former/current), sleep duration (< 7/7–8/>8 h), and BMI categories (< 19.5/19.5–25/>25 kg/m²). The cumulative probability of the primary outcome (the specific disease or its progression) across the four exposure groups (Group − 4) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and between-group differences were compared using the log-rank test. The curves are presented as cumulative hazard (1 − survival probability) to visualize the increasing risk of the primary outcome over time. In this analysis, competing risk events (including death from causes unrelated to the primary outcome, as well as loss to follow-up) were treated as standard non-informative censoring. Follow-up time was defined as the time from study enrollment to the earliest of: occurrence of the primary outcome, a competing risk event, or the end of follow-up. To ensure robustness, we ultimately conducted multiple sensitivity analyses: (1) excluding participants with outcome events within the first two follow-up years to address reverse causality; (2) removing those with acute infections to minimize frailty assessment bias; (3) substituting alternative covariates to control for potential confounding; and (4) excluding participants with malignant tumor.

All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R studio software, version 4.3.2 (Core Team. 2023, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

According to baseline statistics, there were 6,027 frail patients, with 77.7% being female, and 38,908 patients diagnosed with MetS, with 49.3% being female. During the median follow-up period of 12.7 years for 165,555 participants, 3,585 (2.17%) developed MAFLD, 5,186 (3.13%) developed NIDDM, and 9,849 (5.95%) participants died. From the perspective of disease transformation and progression, during the follow-up period, among the 3,585 participants with MAFLD, 170 (4.74%) developed NIDDM and 699 (19.50%) passed away. Among the 5,186 NIDDM participants, 239 (4.61%) developed MAFLD and 685 (13.21%) passed away (Fig. 1). Overall, based on baseline characteristics, participants with frailty and metabolic syndrome were more likely to be older, non-white females, and had poorer lifestyle habits and metabolic indicators (Table 1).

Longitudinal horizontal of frailty phenotype and MetS in the multi-state model

We constructed multiple models to evaluate the impact of frailty phenotype and MetS on multiple outcomes and their mutual transitions. After fully adjusting for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle factors, and metabolic markers, we found that individuals with frailty and MetS had a remarkably increased risk. Specifically, compared to the Group 1 in the fully adjusted model, the HR values and 95% confidence intervals of Group 2–4 for Baseline→MAFLD were 1.72(1.42–2.07), 1.69(1.58–1.83), 2.92(2.45–3.47) respectively. The HR values and 95% confidence intervals for MAFLD→NIDDM were 1.42(0.51–3.98), 3.38(2.37–4.81), 4.21(2.23–7.96). The HR values and 95% confidence intervals for MAFLD→Death were 1.11(0.69–1.77), 1.38(1.16–1.64), 1.22(0.75–2.02) respectively (Table 2). Similarly, we also conducted an analysis of the multiple state transitions of NIDDM.

Interaction and stratified analysis

Our interaction analyses revealed distinct patterns between frailty+MetS and various demographic/lifestyle factors in relation to incident outcomes. Specifically, we observed: (1) interactions in age/sex for MAFLD risk; (2) interactions in age/sex/sleep for NIDDM risk; (3) interactions in age/sex/BMI for death risk; (4) interactions in sports for NIDDM→MAFLD; (5) interactions in sex/BMI/sports for NIDDM→Death (Figure S3).

Sensitivity analysis

To validate the robustness of our findings, we performed comprehensive sensitivity analyses by: (1) excluding 1,167 participants who developed outcomes within the first two years of follow-up to mitigate potential reverse causality; (2) removing 5,533 participants with acute infections to minimize confounding from transient health conditions; (3) adjusting for alternative covariate combinations in our models; and (4) excluding 22,496 participants with malignant tumors. These rigorous sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent effect estimates (Figure S4).

Discussion

Our study integrated multidimensional cross-sectional assessments of frailty phenotype and MetS with longitudinal evaluation of their associations with incident MAFLD, NIDDM, and all-cause mortality. Using a multi-state model, we further elucidated the dynamic progression among these conditions by capturing both baseline risks and transition probabilities over time. We also compared predictive performance in the general population and demographic subgroups, showing that combined frailty-MetS indicators consistently outperformed either measure alone. Stratified analyses indicated differential effects, with physical frailty more strongly associated with outcomes in physically inactive individuals, whereas metabolic abnormalities showed age-dependent effects, particularly in adults aged 20–60 years. Together, these findings highlight the importance of lifestyle factors and identify age groups most likely to benefit from targeted preventive interventions.

Our multi-state model identified distinct transitions patterns between MAFLD, NIDDM, and death, offering a dynamic view of disease progression. For instance, the transition from MAFLD to NIDDM was substantially accelerated in individuals with both frailty and MetS (HR = 4.21), indicating a synergistic effect on metabolic deterioration. Conversely, the transition from MAFLD to death, while elevated, showed a less pronounced association in the fully adjusted model (HR = 1.22, 95% CI 0.75–2.02), indicating no clear association between the coexistence of frailty and metabolic syndrome and the transition from MAFLD to death. These findings underscore that MAFLD and NIDDM should be understood not as isolated endpoints, but as interconnected states along a progressive metabolic continuum [15].

From a static perspective, baseline stratification confirmed that individuals with both frailty and MetS (Group 4) had the highest risks of MAFLD (HR = 2.92), NIDDM (HR = 5.94), and mortality (HR = 1.87), supporting the view that combined physical decline and metabolic dysregulation constitute a high-risk phenotype prone to multiple adverse outcomes [19]. Dynamically, the multi-state model further showed that after MAFLD onset, frailty and MetS continued to drive progression to NIDDM and death, highlighting the need for sustained monitoring and intervention after diagnosis.

Our findings demonstrate a significant association between frailty and both the incidence and progression of NIDDM, consistent with prior studies. Frailty is closely linked to NIDDM progression [4] and increasingly viewed not simply as a comorbidity but as a precursor to and accelerator of dysglycemia. Underlying mechanisms include chronic low-grade inflammation (elevated IL-6, TNF-α, CRP impairing insulin signaling) [20], sarcopenia (reducing glucose disposal) [21], mitochondrial dysfunction (compromising insulin secretion) [22], and neuroendocrine dysregulation (disrupting glucose homeostasis) [23]. Emerging evidence suggests a bidirectional loop where diabetes accelerates physical decline and frailty worsens glycemic control [24], forming a “diabetic frailty” vicious cycle [25].

Notably, our study underscores MetS’s role in NIDDM initiation and progression. As a cluster of metabolic abnormalities, MetS increases normoglycemia-to-NIDDM progression risk via shared pathways (insulin resistance, β-cell glucolipotoxicity, chronic inflammation). Visceral adipose dysfunction impairs insulin sensitivity [26], while dyslipidemia (elevated TG, low HDL) exacerbates β-cell apoptosis [27], explaining the 5-fold higher NIDDM risk in MetS individuals [28].

The intrinsic link between frailty, MetS, and NIDDM progression is further supported by their shared pathophysiological underpinnings, namely the inflammatory milieu and insulin resistance common to both frailty and MetS, which not only predispose to NIDDM but also create a substrate for its more aggressive course. Both frailty and MetS were strongly associated with all-cause mortality in our analysis, consistent with previous studies: frailty has been epidemiologically linked to older age, multimorbidity, and increased mortality [6, 29], while higer TyG and TyG-WHtR indices—rooted in insulin resistance—are independently associated with greater all-cause mortality risk in MetS patients [30–33].

Our findings confirm a strong link among frailty, MetS, and MAFLD, with a bidirectional frailty-MAFLD relationship mediated by chronic inflammation and insulin resistance [5, 34–38]. Our multi-state model (a methodological advancement) captures disease transitions (e.g., NIDDM→MAFLD, HR = 3.67 in Group 4) [39] and highlights integrated hepatic-metabolic health strategies. Circulating TSP2 is linked to MetS and MAFLD severity via insulin resistance and fibrosis [40–43], and frailty may relate to both [44].

The key strengths of our study include its novel multidimensional assessment of frailty phenotype and MetS, along with being the first to employ composite indicators for population-based cohort observations. Our multi-state model and rigorous sensitivity analyses further strengthen the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged: insufficient dynamic follow-up may not fully reflect participants’ evolving health status, limited outcome events preclude a multi-state model for MAFLD/NIDDM-to-mortality transitions, and the predominantly White cohort necessitates validation in more ethnically diverse populations. Future research should prioritize randomized clinical trials to evaluate how frailty and MetS collectively influence health outcomes and whether targeted interventions can alter disease transition probabilities.

Conclusions

The state of physical frailty and metabolic conditions are closely related to health status. By integrating these two factors, it is possible to more effectively assess the risks of future illness and the transformation or development of diseases.
