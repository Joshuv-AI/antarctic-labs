Acute pulmonary embolism: clinical and echocardiographic recognition of normotensive shock
Authors: Oliver Hunsicker, Nils Daum, Glenn Hernandez, Eduardo Kattan, Matthias Göpfert, Michelle Chew, Michel Slama, Khodr Tello, Martin W. Dünser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2026 AHA/ACC guideline on acute pulmonary embolism introduces normotensive shock as a clinically relevant pre-cardiopulmonary failure state with implications for treatment escalation. Evidence from cardiogenic shock indicates that tissue hypoperfusion despite preserved arterial blood pressure is associated with substantial mortality, underscoring the need for early recognition. This requires reliable, rapidly obtainable, and broadly applicable criteria. As several proposed indicators are limited by delayed availability or restricted bedside use, shock assessment should integrate concordant clinical signs of tissue hypoperfusion rather than rely on a single marker. Prolonged capillary refill time and skin mottling may complement lactate as bedside markers of abnormal skin perfusion. Critical care echocardiography further supports this multimodal approach by confirming acute right ventricular pressure overload, excluding alternative causes of circulatory failure, and assessing right ventricular adaptation and right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling. Early recognition may identify patients at imminent risk of deterioration who could benefit from timely treatment escalation, including consideration of reperfusion therapy.

The 2026 AHA/ACC guideline on acute pulmonary embolism (PE) introduces a new A–E categorization to guide decisions on admission, anticoagulation, and reperfusion [1]. Unlike earlier guidelines, which limited reperfusion to patients in cardiopulmonary failure, this recommendation extends consideration of reperfusion to pre-cardiopulmonary failure states such as normotensive shock (Category D2). The prognostic relevance of normotensive shock is well supported in the cardiogenic shock (CS) setting, where isolated hypoperfusion without arterial hypotension has been reported in 15% of cardiac intensive care unit patients [2]. Importantly, adjusted hospital mortality in this subgroup was higher than in patients with isolated hypotension and not significantly different from patients with combined hypotension and hypoperfusion. These findings support the concept that hypoperfusion itself, defines a clinically meaningful shock state. Applied to acute PE, this underscores the need for early identification of normotensive patients who already show evidence of impaired tissue perfusion before overt cardiopulmonary collapse develops. Consequently, the clinical and echocardiographic recognition of normotensive shock becomes critically important, as it directly guides treatment escalation in patients at imminent risk.

Clinical recognition of normotensive shock requires criteria that are reliable, rapidly obtainable, and broadly applicable. The 2026 AHA/ACC guideline proposes indicators to identify normotensive shock that closely align with those used in CS and include lactate levels > 2 mmol/L, creatinine increase, oliguria (< 0.5 mL/kg/hour), altered mental status, and a cardiac index (CI) ≤ 2.2 L/min/m2 [1]. The guideline additionally lists MAP < 60 mmHg, although this appears conceptually inconsistent with the designation “normotensive” shock. Given that patients with right ventricular (RV) dysfunction and elevated cardiac biomarkers may deteriorate rapidly, readily obtainable bedside markers of tissue hypoperfusion remain essential for timely risk stratification and management of acute PE. Oliguria and laboratory makers of acute kidney injury, however, have limited value for immediate bedside risk stratification because their assessment is inherently delayed. In contrast, lactate measurement is rapidly available and may provide incremental prognostic information beyond cardiac biomarkers alone [3]. However, the diagnosis of shock should not rely on a single marker but should be based on a multimodal bedside assessment integrating clinical indicators of tissue hypoperfusion [4]. To identify patients with normotensive shock, we suggest adding markers of abnormal skin perfusion such as prolonged capillary refill time (CRT), defined as > 3 s when assessed at the ventral surface of the index fingertip, and presence of skin mottling, both valid clinical indicators of shock to complement the systematic assessment of tissue perfusion (Fig. 1) [5, 6]. This concept is well established in septic shock [7, 8] and is further supported by recent insights from CS, where CRT and skin mottling are increasingly recognized as simple bedside markers of microcirculatory dysfunction with prognostic relevance beyond conventional macrocirculatory variables [9]. Within such a multimodal bedside approach, two or more concordant hypoperfusion signs may increase diagnostic confidence [5]. Beyond clinical hypoperfusion signs, a low CI, such as ≤ 2.2 L/min/m2, may help identify a low-output state and complement multimodal shock assessment, although defining shock by a fixed CI threshold remains inherently challenging. In normotensive PE patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy, 34.1% presented with a CI ≤ 2.2 L/min/m2 despite preserved systolic blood pressure [10]. The normotensive CI-defined shock group showed substantially more moderate or severe RV dysfunction and a higher heart rate. These findings suggest that a normotensive low-cardiac-output state represents a distinct population within the Category D2 in whom RV–pulmonary arterial uncoupling has progressed to the point that compensatory mechanisms can no longer maintain stroke volume adequately. Although these observational findings are based on a highly selected patient population that may not be representative of all PE patients within this haemodynamic classification, identifying normotensive PE patients with a low-cardiac-output state before overt arterial hypotension develops is clinically meaningful. This multimodal clinical approach is strengthened by critical care echocardiography (CCE), which helps recognize and characterize normotensive shock within an integrative haemodynamic assessment. First, CCE can confirm that normotensive shock is primarily attributable to the consequences of acute RV pressure overload caused by PE rather than alternative causes of circulatory failure (e.g., hypovolaemia, sepsis, chronic left heart failure). Second, CCE helps to identify echocardiographic features of chronic RV pressure overload, allowing differentiation between acute RV strain caused by PE and underlying chronic pressure overload due to pre-existing cardiopulmonary conditions. Third, CCE can be used for risk assessment by defining the functional and morphological stage of right ventricular adaptation to acute RV pressure overload; by assessing its secondary effects on left ventricular filling and consequences on stroke volume.

In the heterometric adaptation phase, as reflected by RV dilatation (RV/LV ratio > 0.9), assessment of RV–PA coupling may be particularly informative for evaluating compensatory reserve in patients with normotensive shock. While precise quantification of RV–PA coupling requires invasive pressure–volume analysis, CCE-derived tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) provides a readily available surrogate of right ventricular adaptation to afterload [11]. Even among normotensive PE patients, reduced TAPSE < 16 mm was associated with an increased risk of PE-related death [12]. The TAPSE/pulmonary arterial systolic pressure (PASP) ratio, a non-invasive surrogate of RV-PA coupling metrics, is superior to TAPSE alone to predict adverse outcome in acute intermediate-risk PE [13]. In normotensive PE patients, TAPSE/PASP values < 0.34 have been linked to worse clinical outcomes [14]. When echocardiographic windows are suboptimal or the tricuspid regurgitation envelope is inadequate for reliable PASP estimation, the TAPSE/PASP ratio cannot be reliably determined. TAPSE should then be interpreted alongside alternative parameters of RV–PA interaction. Among these, RV outflow tract spectral Doppler morphology—particularly early systolic notching (ESN)—may provide complementary information on RV–PA interaction in acute PE. ESN reflects early systolic deceleration of pulmonary flow due to premature return of reflected pressure waves and may indicate impaired RV–PA coupling when the right ventricle can no longer adapt to acute increased afterload [15]. Although ESN is not specific for PE, its integration with other echocardiographic markers may improve risk assessment in PE patients. However, further prospective data are needed to refine its clinical utility.

Normotensive shock in acute PE identifies a clinically relevant phenotype with important therapeutic implications. Evidence from CS supports the prognostic importance of hypoperfusion despite preserved arterial blood pressure and underscores the need for early recognition in PE. A pragmatic bedside multimodal assessment should integrate rapidly available markers such as lactate, prolonged CRT, and skin mottling. CCE may further refine risk stratification by confirming acute RV pressure overload, excluding alternative causes of circulatory failure, and assessing RV adaptation and RV–PA coupling to identify patients who may warrant early treatment escalation, including reperfusion therapy.
