CancerSubtyper: a deep learning framework for cancer subtyping through DNA methylation data
Authors: Joung Min Choi, Yat Fei Cheung, Liqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMolecular subtyping is essential for precision oncology, enabling the classification of tumors into biologically and clinically relevant categories. DNA methylation has emerged as a promising biomarker for cancer subtyping, yet its application remains limited by high dimensionality, batch effects, and the lack of automated, user-friendly analytical tools.ResultsHere, we present CancerSubtyper, an end-to-end computational framework for deep learning–based cancer subtyping using DNA methylation data, which is accessible through an intuitive web interface designed to support interactive exploration and downstream analysis. CancerSubtyper integrates two complementary models: a semi-supervised classifier for cancers with well-established subtypes, and a hybrid framework that integrates supervised and unsupervised learning to identify novel subtypes. The framework automatically performs preprocessing, feature selection, batch correction, and cancer subtyping while offering interactive visualization for subtype exploration and validation.ConclusionBy providing an automated, end-to-end workflow accessible through a user-friendly web interface, CancerSubtyper lowers the barrier to large-scale methylation analysis and provides a powerful tool for molecular subtyping and precision oncology research. The framework is freely accessible at https://github.com/ycheung5/cancersubtyper/.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13072-026-00682-1.

Introduction

Molecular cancer subtyping refers to the classification of tumors into biologically distinct categories based on their molecular profiles, rather than relying solely on traditional histopathological features [1]. This molecularly informed approach is crucial as patients diagnosed with the same cancer type often exhibit markedly different clinical outcomes and therapeutic responses [2]. A key factor underlying this variability is tumor heterogeneity — the coexistence of genetically and phenotypically diverse cell populations within a single tumor as well as across patients [3]. Accurate molecular subtyping has therefore become essential for guiding personalized treatment strategies and improving prognostic accuracy [4]. For example, the widely adopted PAM50 classifier introduced molecular subtypes of breast cancer that have become integral to clinical decision-making and treatment planning [4].

Among the various molecular features explored for tumor classification, DNA methylation has emerged as one of the most promising biomarkers for cancer subtyping [5]. This epigenetic modification, involving the addition of methyl groups to cytosines within CpG dinucleotides, regulates gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence [6]. Acting as a molecular “dimmer switch,” methylation fine-tunes transcriptional activity and is frequently dysregulated in malignant cells [7]. Tumors commonly display hypermethylation in promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes and hypomethylation in oncogenes, producing cancer-specific methylation landscapes that distinguish them from normal tissues and other tumor types [8, 9]. Moreover, accumulating evidence shows that distinct methylation signatures also emerge across molecular subtypes within the same cancer type, reflecting underlying biological heterogeneity and providing critical prognostic and therapeutic insights [10, 11].

Despite its promise, harnessing DNA methylation data for cancer subtyping remains technically challenging. One of the primary obstacles is the extremely high dimensionality of methylation datasets, which often comprise measurements from hundreds of thousands of CpG sites. Traditional machine learning (ML) methods struggle to process such complex data effectively without extensive preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, or feature selection [12]. These steps not only are computationally intensive but also demand significant technical expertise — creating a barrier for many biologists and clinicians who lack a background in computer science.

Several web-based tools [13–15] allow researchers to upload their own methylation datasets, but they typically lack automated, end-to-end workflows that integrate preprocessing, feature selection, and deep learning–based subtyping. Moreover, there is currently no framework with web interface specifically designed for multi-class cancer subtype classification. Tools such as MethSurv [13] and MEXPRESS [14] primarily focus on single-CpG analysis, differential methylation, or survival association, rather than comprehensive subtype discovery. Others, like HiTAIC [15], are optimized for tissue-of-origin classification and cannot resolve intra-tumor subtype heterogeneity. This gap highlights the urgent need for a user-friendly, comprehensive framework that enables researchers to seamlessly analyze methylation data and identify cancer subtypes without requiring specialized computational skills.

Another important consideration in framework development is that the optimal subtyping strategy can differ significantly depending on the cancer type. In cancers where molecular subtypes are already well established — such as breast cancer — supervised learning approaches are highly effective for assigning new samples to existing categories. However, for many other cancers, molecular heterogeneity remains incompletely characterized, and known subtypes may represent only part of the biological diversity. In these cases, integrative approaches combining supervised and unsupervised learning are crucial for both leveraging existing knowledge and uncovering novel molecular subgroups. This variability in analytical requirements underscores the need for a flexible, all-in-one framework capable of supporting both classification of known subtypes and exploratory subtype discovery — allowing researchers to tailor their analyses to the specific biological context of each cancer type.

In addition to these methodological considerations, the lack of batch effect correction in most existing tools remains a major limitation. Because the number of publicly available cancer methylome datasets is relatively small, many studies rely on data collected from multiple independent cohorts, often generated across different laboratories, platforms, and time points. Such variations introduce systematic technical differences that are unrelated to the underlying biology. Without proper correction, these batch effects can obscure meaningful molecular signals, distort subtype boundaries, and mislead biological interpretations [16, 17]. Effective batch correction is therefore essential for the robust integration of methylation datasets, particularly given the challenges of assembling sufficiently large and homogeneous cohorts within individual studies.

Furthermore, to fully leverage the results of cancer subtyping analyses, visualization tools that facilitate biological interpretation are indispensable. Most existing frameworks present their results as static, non-interactive plots, which significantly restricts users’ ability to explore the data in depth, compare subtype classifications, or generate new biological hypotheses. In contrast, interactive visualization environments enable researchers to dynamically examine subtype predictions, explore discriminative molecular features, and assess the relationships between subtypes and relevant clinical or genomic variables. Such capabilities are particularly valuable for biomarker discovery and validation of subtype definitions in external cohorts. Moreover, interactive exploration not only enhances interpretability but also promotes collaboration between computational scientists, biologists, and clinicians, bridging the gap between data-driven insights and clinical decision-making.

To address these challenges, we propose CancerSubtyper, an interactive, web-based deep learning framework designed to streamline DNA methylation–based cancer subtyping. CancerSubtyper integrates advanced deep learning models into an automated, end-to-end workflow that eliminates the need for programming expertise. Users can simply upload their methylation datasets, and the framework seamlessly performs data preprocessing, feature selection, batch effect correction, and subtype prediction. Importantly, CancerSubtyper supports two recently presented cancer subtyping strategies: a semi-supervised classification approach optimized for cancers with well-established subtypes (BCtypeFinder [18]), and an integrative framework that combines supervised and unsupervised learning to facilitate the discovery of novel subgroups in less-characterized cancers (CancerSubminer [19]). The framework also offers interactive visualization, enabling researchers to explore cancer subtyping results through UMAP visualization, the distribution of beta values for each feature across the identified subtypes based on the boxplot, correlation with features, comparison with baseline subtyping methods including standard ML methods, and survival analysis based on the Kaplan-Meier plot. By consolidating these capabilities into a single user-friendly interface, CancerSubtyper reduces the barrier to large-scale methylation cancer subtyping analysis and provides a powerful resource for advancing precision oncology research. CancerSubtyper is accessible as a user-friendly web-based deep learning framework, publicly available at https://github.com/ycheung5/cancersubtyper/.

Methods

Overview of CancerSubtyper

CancerSubtyper is an interactive, web-based framework designed to perform deep learning–based cancer subtyping using DNA methylation data as input. The framework was developed to provide an end-to-end solution — from data upload and preprocessing to model execution and interactive visualization — thereby lowering the technical barriers to large-scale methylation-based analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow of CancerSubtyper, while the system architecture of the framework is described in Supplementary Material S1.

Within CancerSubtyper framework, subtype identification is performed using two complementary models that differ in how subtypes are defined and interpreted. BCtypeFinder follows a predefined subtyping system, assigning each sample to established molecular subtype categories based on prior biological knowledge and clinically validated annotations. These subtypes are associated with well-characterized biological and clinical relevance, including distinct methylation patterns, prognostic outcomes, and treatment responses. Accordingly, BCtypeFinder is designed for subtype classification, ensuring consistency with existing clinical frameworks. In contrast, CancerSubminer adopts a data-driven subtype discovery approach while still leveraging predefined subtype labels as an initial reference. The model first learns biologically meaningful feature representations through supervised training guided by known subtypes. It then iteratively refines subtype assignments via clustering and reassignment in the learned latent space, enabling the the identification of refined subtype boundaries and potential novel subgroups. Samples with low classification confidence are re-evaluated and may form new clusters if doing so improves clustering quality and model fit. As a result, subtypes identified by CancerSubminer can be interpreted as refined or extended molecular subtypes: those aligned with known labels preserve established biological meaning, whereas newly identified groups represent candidate subtypes capturing previously unrecognized epigenetic heterogeneity. Data input and preprocessing

CancerSubtyper takes three input files: a source dataset, a target dataset, and an optional metadata file. The source and target datasets are both expected to be comma-separated values (CSV) files containing DNA methylation beta values, where each column represents a CpG site and each row corresponds to a sample. The source dataset should additionally include known subtype labels that are used for model training. The optional metadata file, also formatted as a CSV, may contain relevant clinical variables such as overall survival time and event status, which are incorporated into downstream survival analyses for visualization when available.

All input data undergo a standardized preprocessing pipeline, consistent with the procedures described in prior studies [20]. The pipeline begins by identifying and retaining CpG sites that are shared across all input datasets to ensure compatibility during model training and prediction. CpG sites with more than 20% missing values are excluded to minimize potential bias and improve model robustness. For the remaining missing values, median imputation is applied across samples. To further reduce dimensionality and enhance computational efficiency, the retained CpG sites are grouped into 3,000 clusters using k-means clustering. Within each cluster, the median beta value is calculated and used as the representative feature. This step is referred to as feature selection in our framework, as it reduces the original high-dimensional CpG space into a compact set of representative features by selecting cluster-level summaries that capture dominant methylation patterns while removing redundancy and noise. The number of CpG clusters (3,000) was selected based on prior evaluation in our original BCtypeFinder study [18] using the Elbow method and downstream classification performance, where it provided a favorable balance between dimensionality reduction and predictive accuracy (Supplementary Material S2 and S3). While this value is used as the default setting, CancerSubtyper allows users to customize the number of clusters to accommodate dataset-specific characteristics. In addition, K-means clustering was chosen based on comparative analysis with other feature selection approaches, as it effectively captures co-methylation patterns across samples that are informative for subtype discrimination (Supplementary Material S4). This data-driven grouping has been shown to improve robustness and predictive performance compared to using individual CpG sites or biologically predefined groupings.

Molecular subtyping of cancer with batch effect correction

CancerSubtyper incorporates two state-of-the-art deep learning–based approaches for molecular cancer subtyping: (1) BCtypeFinder [18], a semi-supervised classification model designed for cancers with well-defined subtype categories, and (2) CancerSubminer [19], an integrative framework that combines supervised and unsupervised learning to facilitate prediction based on the representations captured from the known subtype categories and discovery of novel subtypes. Both approaches are built upon a domain adaptation framework with semi-supervised learning and subtype alignment to address batch effects and maximize subtype transferability across heterogeneous datasets.

BCtypeFinder performs semi-supervised cancer subtype classification through a three-stage training process. In the first stage (pre-training), a feature extractor and classifier are trained using labeled source data to learn subtype-discriminative representations. In the second stage (adversarial domain adaptation), a domain discriminator is introduced to minimize the distributional differences between the source and target datasets. The feature extractor is shared across source and target data and is optimized using both classification loss (on labeled source samples) and adversarial loss (on both source and target samples). It is simultaneously optimized to “confuse” the discriminator, thereby learning domain-invariant representations that generalize across cohorts. In the final stage (semi-supervised fine-tuning), pseudo-labels are iteratively assigned to unlabeled target samples based on the highest classification probabilities. These pseudo-labels are incorporated into model optimization during training, progressively improving classification accuracy. Additionally, subtype alignment is integrated into this phase: samples from each domain are grouped by their predicted subtype, and the model minimizes the distance between corresponding cluster centroids across domains, ensuring consistent subtype representation between source and target data.

CancerSubminer is a hybrid subtyping framework that extends beyond classification by integrating supervised learning with unsupervised discovery. The model begins with a supervised training phase, in which a classifier is trained on labeled data to capture features based on the known subtype categories. Similar to BCtypeFinder, the feature extractor is shared across domains and optimized through classification and domain adaptation objectives. The learned feature representations are subsequently subjected to clustering, and samples with low classification confidence are reassigned to refine subtype boundaries and to identify potential novel subtypes. The model is then iteratively retrained with these refined labels, repeating the clustering–reassignment cycle until subtype assignments converge. To correct for batch effects, CancerSubminer employs the same adversarial domain adaptation approach as BCtypeFinder, promoting domain-invariant representations across both labeled and unlabeled data. A semi-supervised fine-tuning step further aligns subtype distributions between datasets. Importantly, CancerSubminer differs from BCtypeFinder in its explicit handling of novel subtypes: samples with high-confidence assignments are used for subtype alignment, while those with low-confidence predictions are grouped separately and evaluated as potential new clusters. The decision to introduce a new subtype is based on improvements in clustering quality, as measured by the silhouette score, and reductions in classification loss. This iterative refinement allows CancerSubminer to identify candidate subtypes that may be absent from the source data but emerge in the target cohort. In CancerSubminer, users can either specify the number of subtypes manually or select an automatic mode, in which the model determines the optimal number of subtypes by choosing the solution that achieves the highest silhouette score.

Both models implement domain adaptation–based adversarial training, semi-supervised learning, and subtype alignment to correct for batch effects and ensure robust cross-cohort performance. During adversarial training, a domain discriminator attempts to distinguish between source and target samples, while the feature extractor is optimized to confuse the discriminator. This adversarial process encourages the extraction of domain-invariant features that are insensitive to cohort-specific biases. In parallel, subtype alignment minimizes the distance between cluster centroids corresponding to each subtype in the source and target domains. Pseudo-labels generated through semi-supervised learning are used to guide alignment for the unlabeled target data. Together, these strategies enable both models to reduce residual batch-specific variations, harmonize subtype representations across datasets, and improve the biological relevance of the resulting classifications. To improve flexibility and accommodate dataset-specific characteristics, CancerSubtyper enables users to adjust parameters related to feature selection (e.g., number of CpG clusters), model architecture (e.g., number of hidden nodes in feature extractor, classifier, and discriminator), and training strategy (e.g., batch size, learning rates, and number of training epochs for each stage). These options are provided through an intuitive user interface, allowing users to easily modify values while retaining recommended defaults (Supplementary Material S5), where the default settings are chosen based on previously validated configurations from the original studies [18, 19], which have demonstrated robust performance across multiple datasets. CancerSubtyper provides an option to utilize pretrained models for subtype identification, allowing users to perform analysis without requiring labeled training data. The pretrained models for both BCtypeFinder and CancerSubminer were trained on the breast cancer DNA methylation dataset used in the prototype analysis and can be directly applied to new datasets for subtype prediction and downstream analysis.

Visualization and downstream analysis

After executing a cancer subtyping model, CancerSubtyper automatically generates a comprehensive suite of interactive visualizations designed to facilitate interpretation, validation, and biological exploration of the results. These visual outputs help users assess subtype separability, evaluate the effectiveness of batch correction, examine the molecular characteristics of identified subtypes, and explore their clinical relevance.

To provide insights into the molecular features underlying each predicted subtype, CancerSubtyper generates correlation heatmaps of CpG clusters. For each subtype, the framework selects the top 30 CpG clusters with the highest Spearman correlation and visualizes their interrelationships. In addition, boxplots are produced to show the distribution of methylation beta values across subtypes for each CpG cluster, illustrating feature-level variation and highlighting distinguishing epigenetic patterns. Since CpGs are grouped into clusters during preprocessing, CancerSubtyper also provides a detailed annotation table listing the CpG components of each cluster, including genomic coordinates (chromosome, strand, and position) and associated gene information retrieved from the UCSC Genome Browser [21]. This resource enhances interpretability and supports downstream biological analyses.

To evaluate the impact of batch effect correction, the framework generates UMAP visualizations based on features extracted from the trained model as well as from the uncorrected raw dataset. Users can color the plots by either subtype or batch to visually compare sample distributions before and after correction. For benchmarking and validation, CancerSubtyper also performs baseline subtype prediction using established methods. When using BCtypeFinder, commonly applied machine learning classifiers — including support vector machines (SVM), random forest (RF), and logistic regression (LogReg) — are executed for comparison. When using CancerSubminer, unsupervised clustering methods such as k-means and NEMO [22] — a state-of-the-art spectral clustering–based subtyping method — are applied. The resulting subtype assignments from all methods are provided in tabular format to facilitate cross-model comparison and external validation.

To assess the clinical relevance of predicted subtypes, CancerSubtyper performs Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis using time-to-event and survival status metadata provided by the user. The framework outputs KM survival curves along with log-rank test results, allowing users to evaluate whether the identified subtypes are associated with distinct prognostic outcomes. All visualizations are consistently generated across models and analysis jobs, ensuring a standardized and interpretable output. Furthermore, each plot supports interactive features such as mouse-over inspection, enabling detailed exploration of sample-level patterns and facilitating hypothesis generation.

Results

To demonstrate the capabilities of CancerSubtyper, we conducted a prototype analysis using a breast cancer DNA methylation dataset. The labeled dataset was derived from the TCGA-BRCA cohort, which comprises 1,060 primary breast tumor samples profiled using the Illumina Human Infinium 450K and 27K platforms, along with established PAM50 subtype annotations [4]. For the unlabeled data, we integrated three publicly available breast cancer methylation datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE69914 [23], GSE75067 [24], and GSE72245 [25], collectively comprising 611 tumor samples.

This analysis illustrates the comprehensive functionality of CancerSubtyper for large-scale cancer subtyping and downstream exploration. The framework identified intrinsic breast cancer subtypes and integrated methylation data from multiple independent cohorts through effective batch effect correction. In addition, CancerSubtyper provided interactive visualizations that revealed subtype-specific methylation patterns and discriminative CpG features. The framework further enhanced interpretability by linking molecular subtypes to clinical outcomes through survival analysis and enabled rigorous benchmarking against conventional machine learning classifiers and unsupervised clustering methods. Together, these results highlight CancerSubtyper as a robust, flexible, and user-friendly framework for integrative cancer subtyping and precision oncology research.

Cancer subtype identification and benchmarking against baseline methods

CancerSubtyper identifies cancer subtypes through two complementary approaches. Using the BCtypeFinder model, the framework assigns each sample to one of the well-defined subtype categories based on supervised classification. Alternatively, with the CancerSubminer model, it performs subtyping through an integrative framework that combines supervised and unsupervised learning. In this approach, the model learns representations informed by known subtype categories and iteratively refines these to redefine subtypes, enabling the discovery of previously unrecognized molecular subgroups.

To support the validation of identified subtypes, CancerSubtyper facilitates direct comparison of its predictions against baseline methods. For BCtypeFinder, standard machine learning classifiers — support vector machines (SVM), random forest (RF), and logistic regression (LogReg) — are used as benchmarks. These models are trained on the labeled source dataset and applied to the unlabeled target dataset in the same manner as the deep learning classifier. For CancerSubminer, unsupervised methods are employed as baselines, including k-means clustering and NEMO, a state-of-the-art spectral clustering–based subtyping algorithm. Visualization of batch effect correction and subtype separation

To visually assess the effectiveness of batch effect correction and evaluate how methylation datasets from different cohorts are integrated, CancerSubtyper provides UMAP visualizations based on both the model-extracted features and the uncorrected original data. Both representations are reduced to two dimensions using UMAP and labeled according to their dataset origin and predicted subtype, allowing for a direct comparison of data structure before and after batch correction.

As shown in Fig. 3, UMAP plots generated from the uncorrected dataset (upper panel) typically display substantial variability across samples, with subtype-specific clusters dispersed and often overlapping due to batch-specific differences. In contrast, the UMAP visualization derived from BCtypeFinder-extracted features illustrates the impact of domain adaptation–based batch effect correction, showing that batch-related variation is substantially reduced and that samples belonging to the same subtype become more closely aligned — even when originating from different cohorts. This visualization enables users to intuitively examine whether batch distinctions have been effectively mitigated and whether subtype-specific groupings are preserved or clarified across datasets. By hovering over a category in the legend (either batch or subtype), users can highlight and examine the distribution of samples within that category, and by hovering over individual data points in the plot, they can view detailed sample- and subtype-specific information.

For analyses performed using the CancerSubminer model (Supplementary Material S7), the framework additionally provides UMAP visualizations that highlight clustering results generated by baseline methods. Alongside the uncorrected dataset, users can visualize sample distributions colored by k-means clustering and NEMO results, allowing direct comparison of how different methods group samples for subtyping. For each subtype, the 30 most highly correlated CpG clusters are selected and visualized in a correlation matrix, where each cell represents the degree of correlation between a pair of clusters (ranging from –1 to 1), with a color gradient indicating both the strength and direction of the relationship. Users can interactively explore these co-methylation patterns by selecting a specific subtype and batch, enabling direct comparisons across datasets. Investigating methylation variation across subtypes

To further investigate methylation variability among molecular subtypes, CancerSubtyper provides an interactive boxplot module that visualizes the distribution of CpG methylation levels across different subtype groups. This functionality enables users to examine methylation intensities and assess how they differ among cancer subtypes, offering valuable insights into potential subtype-specific regulatory mechanisms. Supplementary Material S8 presents an example from our prototype study, where the x-axis represents the molecular subtypes and the y-axis shows the distribution of beta values for each subtype. Users can select specific CpG clusters of interest and filter the results by batch, allowing for a more detailed examination of how methylation patterns vary across cohorts. This interactive visualization provides an intuitive way to assess whether particular CpG clusters display distinct methylation distributions across subtypes, which may indicate their involvement in subtype-specific regulatory processes. Such findings can serve as a foundation for prioritizing candidate biomarkers and guiding downstream functional validation.

Survival analysis to evaluation clinical relevance

To evaluate the clinical significance of predicted cancer subtypes, CancerSubtyper performs Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis with a log-rank test using user-provided metadata that include survival time and event status. This analysis enables stratification of patient samples based on their predicted subtypes and visualizes survival outcomes across distinct molecular categories. By comparing survival curves, users can assess whether subtype assignments are associated with statistically significant differences in overall survival, thereby evaluating the prognostic relevance of the identified subtypes.

Figure 6 shows an example of the survival analysis conducted for breast cancer patients in the prototype study. Users may select specific batches for analysis, provided that valid survival metadata — including both time-to-event and event status — are available. Only cohorts with complete survival annotations are included in the KM plots. Performance evaluation of subtype identification models integrated within CancerSubtyper

To further assess the robustness and performance of the models integrated within CancerSubtyper, we conducted a prototype evaluation using breast cancer DNA methylation datasets. While the primary objective of this study is to present an interactive framework, this analysis provides additional evidence supporting the effectiveness of the underlying deep learning models in both subtype classification and discovery tasks. Among the target dataset, GSE72245 includes subtype labels and was used for classification-based evaluation, while GSE75067 contains survival information and was used for clustering and clinical relevance assessment.

For subtype classification, BCtypeFinder was evaluated on 87 samples from GSE72245 with available subtype labels. The experiment was repeated five times to ensure robustness. Performance was compared against a state-of-the-art DNA methylation–based classification model (meth-SemiCancer) as well as widely used machine learning classifiers, including support vector machines (SVM), random forest (RF), and logistic regression (LR). As summarized in Supplementary Material S9, BCtypeFinder achieved the best performance across all methods, with an average accuracy of 0.830 and F1-score of 0.831, demonstrating strong generalization capability across independent datasets.

CancerSubminer was evaluated using clustering-based metrics that quantify intra-subtype compactness and inter-subtype separation. Specifically, the Silhouette score and Davies–Bouldin index (DBI) were computed on the GSE75067 dataset. CancerSubminer was compared against NEMO, a widely-used subtyping framework, and K-means clustering. The number of subtypes was determined based on recommended procedures for each method or by selecting the configuration with the highest Silhouette score (for K-means). As shown in Supplementary Material S10, CancerSubminer achieved superior clustering performance, with a higher Silhouette score (0.402) and lower DBI (0.826) compared to baseline methods, indicating improved subtype separability and compactness. To further evaluate the clinical relevance of the identified subtypes, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the GSE75067 dataset. The results (Supplementary Material S11) show that subtypes identified by CancerSubminer exhibit statistically significant differences in survival outcomes, as assessed by the log-rank test. This suggests that the discovered subtypes can capture meaningful biological variation with potential prognostic value. Overall, these results demonstrate that CancerSubtyper provides an accessible and automated framework integrating models that achieve improved performance in subtype identification across independent cohorts.

Discussions and conclusion

In this study, we introduced CancerSubtyper, an interactive, end-to-end computational framework that leverages deep learning to perform comprehensive cancer subtyping using DNA methylation data, which is accessible through an intuitive web interface. The framework was designed to address major challenges that have long hindered large-scale epigenomic analyses — including the high dimensionality of methylation data, batch effects across datasets, the lack of automated workflows, and limited interpretability of results. By integrating two complementary subtyping approaches (BCtypeFinder and CancerSubminer), CancerSubtyper provides researchers with a flexible and scalable solution suitable for a wide range of biological contexts.

Several existing tools have been developed to support DNA methylation analysis and cancer-related investigations, including platforms such as MethSurv [13], MEXPRESS [14], and HiTAIC [15]. While these methods provide valuable functionalities for survival analysis, visualization, or specific classification tasks, they are often designed for targeted analyses and lack a comprehensive, end-to-end workflow that unifies data preprocessing, batch correction, and subtype modeling. In this context, CancerSubtyper provides a unified analytical framework that standardizes preprocessing, model execution, and downstream analysis within a single platform. By combining both predefined subtype classification and data-driven subtype discovery in a consistent workflow, the framework enables systematic comparison, interpretation, and application of complementary subtyping strategies. In addition, the integration of interactive visualization and automated analysis pipelines facilitates a seamless transition from model prediction to biological and clinical interpretation, which is not fully supported in prior standalone implementations.

By consolidating deep learning–based subtyping, batch effect correction, visualization, and benchmarking into a single user-friendly interface, CancerSubtyper significantly lowers the technical barriers associated with large-scale methylation analysis. Its automated, end-to-end design democratizes access to advanced computational approaches, enabling researchers and clinicians without programming expertise to perform robust and biologically meaningful analyses. CancerSubtyper is designed to support a broad range of users, including experimental researchers, clinicians, and computational biologists. It is particularly beneficial for researchers working with DNA methylation data who may lack extensive computational expertise, while also accommodating advanced users through customizable parameters and detailed outputs for downstream analysis, thereby enabling flexible application across diverse research settings.

While CancerSubtyper does not currently provide a dedicated interface for directly integrating new models, it is implemented using a Docker-based architecture, which enables extensibility at the system level. We provide a brief additional guidance on integrating new deep learning models into the framework in Supplementary Material S12.

Despite the effectiveness of domain adaptation–based batch correction in improving cross-cohort consistency, we acknowledge that such approaches cannot fully disentangle technical variation from biologically meaningful differences [26]. Cohorts may differ in clinically relevant factors such as age, disease stage, or demographic composition, and aggressive alignment may attenuate these signals [16]. In CancerSubtyper, adversarial training is designed to reduce technical variation while preserving subtype-discriminative patterns, supported by subtype alignment and semi-supervised learning that constrain the feature space using biologically informed structure. Nevertheless, this process involves an inherent trade-off between removing batch effects and retaining biological variation, and results should be interpreted with consideration of cohort-specific characteristics.

Looking ahead, future extensions of CancerSubtyper will include support for multi-omics data integration and additional subtyping algorithms, further expanding its utility as a comprehensive framework for precision oncology and molecular disease stratification. CancerSubtyper is publicly available at https://github.com/ycheung5/cancersubtyper/, with detailed instructions provided on the same webpage and in Supplementary Material S13. To improve accessibility, we provide demonstration videos that guide users through the workflow and illustrate the functionality of CancerSubtyper without requiring local deployment in Supplementary Material S14. Future work will focus on developing a lightweight or cloud-based version to further enhance usability for users without computational expertise.
