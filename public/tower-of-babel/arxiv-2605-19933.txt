A Tight Epidemic Threshold for Competing Stochastic Infection Processes with Mutually Exclusive Immunity
Nicolas Klodt ∗, Martin S. Krejca †

Abstract
Stochastic infection processes are continuous-time Markov chains on graphs that assign each vertex one of multiple states, such as susceptible , infected , or recovered . Depending on the model, vertices changes their state based on random transition rates and the states of their neighbors, resulting in a variety of complex dynamics. The body of rigorous literature is rich for processes that consider a single infection, that is, processes where each state is unique. In contrast, the setting with at least two infections, where the same state exists for different types, allows for far more transition combinations, leaving several interesting models entirely unexplored. One such model is the one where vertices are immune to only one of the infections and lose this immunity upon infection by another one. We address this shortcoming in the literature by defining the IRIR process, in which two SIR processes run on the same graph and each vertex is immune only to its most recent infection. We study the survival time of the IRIR process, that is, the time until no infected vertex remains, with mathematical rigor. Our main result is a tight threshold, known as epidemic threshold , where the survival time rapidly changes from at most quasi-linear in the graph size n n to at least super-polynomial in n n . This result is applicable to perfectly mixed graphs, which are graphs where the density of edges between each non-empty subset of vertices is a given value p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] p\in(0,1] . Introduction

A large and important area in theoretical computer science considers stochastic processes on graphs driven by interactions among the different states of vertices.
Over the last decades, the analysis of such processes has led to various strong guarantees.
Examples include rumor spreading
[
25
,
9
]
, load balancing
[
13
,
5

, voter models
[
7
,
2

[

[

Stochastic infection processes
[
35
]
comprise another important domain that falls into this category and that has been studied with similar rigor.

Stochastic infection processes are motivated by epidemiology and assume that each vertex of a given graph is in one of several states, such as susceptible to an infection (S), infected (I), or recovered (R) and thus immune to an infection.
The dynamics are governed by random rates that determine when vertices transition in continuous time from one state to another, based on the states of their neighbors.
Following a common naming convention, the order of possible transitions is typically reflected by the order of the abbreviation letters in the name of the process, with a repeated letter indicating a loop.
Well-known processes are the SIR process, which results in a type of percolation process, the SIS process, also known as contact process, and the SIRS process.
We note that processes with loops and thus repeated configurations lead to vastly different dynamics from processes without loops.
Thus, in the remainder, we refer to stochastic infection processes only if they allow for repeated configurations, as other models are also covered by other models, such as SIR and percolation.

Since stochastic infection processes usually have exactly one absorbing state, which is quickly reached once no infected vertices remain, an important quantity of a process is the random time that it takes to reach such an absorbing state.
This time is the
survival time
of the process, and it has been subject to extensive research.
Many results work under idealized assumptions, known as mean-field theory, and study approximations of the exact processes.
Nonetheless, popular models have also been studied in full rigor.
For example, the SIS process has been analyzed both for infinite graphs
[
20
,
33
,
34
]
and finite graphs
[
3
,
15
,
4
]

More recently, the SIRS process has received more attention and was also studied in full rigor, on finite graphs,
[
12
,
31
,
16
]
.

Most of these results showcase an
epidemic threshold
, that is, a value of the model’s parameters—typically the infection rate—at which the survival time suddenly changes from at most logarithmic to at least super-polynomial, with high probability.
These epidemic thresholds are usually closely related to the structure of the host graph of the process, and the various results paint a rich picture of graph properties that result in a low or high survival time.
However, this richness of rigorous results only exists for processes that consider a single infection.
For two or more infections, the landscape of fully rigorous results is scarcer, as we detail in
Section

2
.
One substantial reason for this scarcity is the drastic increase in complexity when studying multiple infections, as there are many different choices with respect to what interactions are sensible.
In particular, the interesting scenario of multiple infections with mutually exclusive immunity has not been studied with mathematical rigor at all so far, to the best of our knowledge.
Such mutual exclusion shows up in certain contexts, for example, in the (helpful) Welchia virus that patches a system and thus changes its vulnerability, or as seen by crop rotation, where planting the same crop repeatedly increases the infection risk, making it likely to change the crop and allowing the soil to recover from the former crop but increasing the infection risk of the latter
[
40
,
23

Our Contribution

We introduce a new infection process for two competing infections with mutually exclusive immunity, called
IRIR

To this end, we meticulously derive a suitable potential that transforms the complex configuration space into a supermartingale with drift, which we believe is a value contribution of this work and applicable to other processes as well.
We also provide estimates for the dynamics of a generalization of the
IRIR
process to more than two infections.

Finding a suitable potential.

Our proof of the super-polynomial lower bound is involved.
At its core lies a concentration result for supermartingales with drift (

To this end, we transform the trajectory of the

We note that while this approach is not uncommon, the process of finding a suitable potential is challenging.
In this work, we discuss in detail how we systematically obtain such a suitable potential, which is, to the best of our knowledge, a novel and substantial contribution.
We believe that our strategy is applicable to other processes and thus serves as a good starting point for investigating other processes.

Related Work

We discuss the novelty of our work with respect to existing articles.
Briefly put, the
IRIR
process has not been studied before, and similar models were mostly only studied via mean-field, that is, approximation, approaches, not in full mathematical rigor.
Similarly, while our general proof strategy is not uncommon, we are not aware of precise instructions for deriving suitable potentials systematically.

Novelty of the
IRIR
process.

To the best of our knowledge, the
IRIR
process has not been studied before. However, its properties have similarities to processes studied in prior work. Hence, in the following, we outline several properties of our model and examples of works that studied similar properties. We start by presenting work on processes with multiple infections that compete or cooperate or do both. We then provide an example of a paper that also considers a process where vertices only memorize the last infection. Last, we relate our process to predator–prey systems, which have similar cyclic dynamics.

Processes with multiple infections received a lot of attention in general.
[
30
]
give an overview on the research on multi-strain models in which multiple strains of the same infection spread through a network, where being infected or recovered from one strain gives (partial) immunity to other strains. The authors motivate the different models using real-world examples and discuss the advantages and disadvantages of certain modeling decisions. However, in terms of mathematical rigor, most papers in this area use mean-field approaches in order to calculate the equilibrium states and threshold values for when new equilibria arise.

The dynamics of the IRIR process result in infections that are cooperative in the sense of one infection infecting vertices being purely positive for the other infection, as the susceptible pools are disjoint and grow by recovering from the other infection. In that sense, there has been previous work on cooperative infections that help each other in different ways. For example,
[
6
]
consider two SIR-type infections that infect vertices on the same graph, and being infected by one infection increases the probability to be infected by the other one. The authors show phase transitions via mean-field theory.

Another work that studies cooperation is by
[
36
]
. The authors analyze a system of three infections where one of them cooperates with the other two, while the other two are in competition with each other. The focus is on the effect of having both cooperation and competition in the same model. The analysis uses mean-field calculations and simulations in order to calculate a phase diagram. The system dynamics are related to our extension of the
IRIR
process to more than two infections.
The difference is that our
IRIR
generalization results naturally in an interplay of cooperation and competition, whereas the model by
[
36
]
enforces each individually.

The
IRIR
process can be seen as two SIR infections where vertices only remember the last infection and are thus immune to it.
[
18
]
study a similar infection model and analyze the impact of having memory of the last infection. However, in their model, each infected individual has a
trait
parameter, which changes with each infection, and the age of the infection impacts its effectivity.

Because infections in the
IRIR
process only infect vertices that recovered from the other infection, vertices cycle alternatingly through the two infected and recovered states. This is similar to cyclic predator–prey systems.
The main difference is that predator–prey models feature a high degree of asymmetry, with the predators hunting the prey, and both populations are subject to death-and-life-cycles.
Predator–prey models have been studied extensively, for example, by
[
22
]
and
[
10
]
as well as the references therein.
[
22
]
showed that cyclic three-species predator–prey systems are closely related to the two-species Lotka–Volterra model, which has also been studied extensively. Most papers on both models describe the dynamics over differential equation systems and analyze the existence and stability of equilibria in the mean-field model. In this regard, the analysis of these processes is very close to how SI-type processes are commonly analyzed. Moreover, there are some fully rigorous results, mostly on underlying latices, which investigate the differences of the exact process to the mean-field approximation
[
38
]
. In particular in the cyclic predator–prey model, the stochastic process can behave quite differently to the mean-field approximation, as for example discussed by
[
37
]
. The more complex the models are, the more impact local interactions can have on the global behavior of the dynamics. This highlights the importance of rigorous analysis of such processes that goes further than using mean-field assumptions.

Novelty of our analysis.

Using mean-field theory for the analysis of SIS-type infections is very common.
Similarly, Lyapunov functions are regularly used for showing global stability of equilibrium states. Among such works, closest to our analysis is the work by
[
12

[

Such detailed and systematic instructions are novel, to the best of our knowledge.

At its core, drift analysis is a rephrasing of well-known results for super- and submartingales, such as the optional-stopping theorem.

In this article, we mainly make use of

|X_{t}-X_{t+1}|\cdot\mathbf{1}{\{X_{t}\geq a\}}<c\cdot\mathbf{1}{\{X_{t}\geq a\land t<T\}}+\mathbf{1}{\{X_{t}<a\lor t\geq T\}}
, and

3.

X_{t+1}\cdot\mathbf{1}{\{X_{t}<a\land t<T\}}\leq 0
.

Then for all
t\in\mathds{N}
, we have
\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\leq t}}}\right]\leq t^{2}\cdot\exp\bigl(-\frac{b|\varepsilon|}{2c^{2}}\bigr)
.

An important property of
Theorem

3.1
, using the theorem’s notation, is that we only require drift for the regime
[a,b)
and not over the entire interval
(-\infty,b)
, as the bounded step size prevents the process from stopping earlier anyway once it takes values less than
a
, due to
item

3
.

4
Bounds on the Survival Time of the IRIR Process

Our main result is
Corollary

4.1
, which shows that the
IRIR
process on a jumbled graph
G
of density
p\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
survives with high probability for a time that is at least super-polynomial in the number
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
of the vertices of
G
if the recovery rates are constant, the infection rates are in the order
\frac{1}{pn}
, and the process starts with a linear fraction of infected vertices of each infection.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
,
k\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
with
k\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(n)}}}\right)
,
p\in(0,1]
(possibly dependent on
n
), and let
C
be an
IRIR
process on a
(p,pn/k)
-jumbled graph
G
with
n
vertices.
Let
C
have constant recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*}=c_{1}/n
and
\lambda_{2}^{*}=c_{2}/n
, for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
Furthermore, let
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant such that
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}<(1-c)n
, and let
\varepsilon_{h}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a sufficiently small constant such that
I_{1,0}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
and
I_{2,0}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
.
Last, let
T
be the survival time of
C
.
Then there exists a constant
c_{T}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that for sufficiently large
n
, we have
\mathrm{Pr}\big[{T\leq\exp\bigl(\frac{c_{T}n}{8\ln(n)}\bigr)}\big]\leq 2\exp\bigl(-\frac{c_{T}n}{2\ln(n)}\bigr)
.

We note that an Erdős–Rényi graph
G(n,p)
is almost surely
(p^{\prime},\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\sqrt{np}}}}\right))
-jumbled (
Lemma

A.2
) when
p\leq 0.99
.
Hence,
Corollary

4.1
carries over for
p=k/n\leq 0.99
and
k\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln^{2}(n)}}}\right)
(
Corollary

C.12
).

Since jumbled graphs subsume cliques,
Corollary

4.1
is very much in line with results for the SIS process
[
15
]
on cliques (with one infection), where the process also exhibits with high probability a super-polynomial survival time once the infection rate is at least in the order of
\frac{1}{n}
.
The main difference to the SIS constraint is that we require upper and lower bounds for the infection rates in
IRIR
as both infections depend on each other and an imbalance among the infections may lead to one infection dying out fast which also results in the other one dying out fast.

We show that the constraint
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}<(1-c)n
is tight up to constant factors by showing that the expected survival time of the
IRIR
process is at most
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right)
if
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}>(1+c)n
.
Due to concentration inequalities such as Markov’s inequality, a polynomial upper bound also holds with high probability.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
C
be an
IRIR
process on a perfectly mixed graph
G
with
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices and density
p\in(0,1]
.
Let
C
have constant recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*}=c_{1}/n
and
\lambda_{2}^{*}=c_{2}/n
, for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
Furthermore, let
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant such that
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}>(1+c)n
.
Last, let
T
be the survival time of
C
.
Then
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right)
.

In combination,
Corollary

4.1
and
Theorem

4.2
show that
n
acts as an epidemic threshold for
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}
, that is, a value where the survival time rapidly changes from at most polynomial to at least super-polynomial by just changing the effective infection rates by a constant factor.
This is comparable to
n
being the epidemic threshold for
\frac{\varrho}{\lambda^{*}}
in the SIS process
[
15
]
.

Proof overview.

Due to space constraints, our proofs are in the appendix.
Here, we outline the main idea for proving
Corollary

4.1
, mentioning that
Theorem

4.2
follows from similar but easier arguments.
In the following, we provide a high-level idea before we explain specific important steps in greater detail.

Our aim is to apply
Theorem

3.1
to the
IRIR
process
C
.
This requires us to transform
C
into a real-valued process that has a sufficient drift (
item

1
).
We call a function that performs such a transformation a
potential function
.
Finding such a potential is the main challenge of the analysis.

Our final potential is based on a continuous Lyapunov function, which in turn incorporates information about the equilibrium of the
IRIR
process.
This equilibrium is an idealized configuration where all transition probabilities are equal, which may not be attainable with discrete values.

In an idealized
IRIR
process with continuous values, the equilibrium is a fixed point, meaning that the process cannot escape it.
For the actual, discrete,
IRIR
process, the vicinity of this configuration still leads to configurations from which the
IRIR
process cannot escape quickly with high probability.
This property is well modeled by negative drift.

Given that the equilibrium is based on a continuous view of the
IRIR
process and that the discrete drift of a process can be seen as the difference quotient over a discrete step of the process, we first derive a Lyapunov function
F
whose derivative, that is, its continuous-time drift, is negative in a region around the equilibrium.
We then discretize
F
such that its important properties are still maintained within this region (
Section

4.1
).
Applying
Theorem

3.1
to
C
with this potential (
Section

4.2
) concludes the proof.

4.1
Deriving a Suitable Potential

As explained above, we derive a Lyapunov function that we then discretize into our potential.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
n\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 1}
vertices and density
p\in(0,1]
, and let
C
be an
IRIR
-process on
G
.
Assume that
C
has recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*},\lambda_{2}^{*}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
By requiring all transition rates that change the configuration of
C
to be equal, we calculate the equilibrium state of
C
.
The resulting equilibrium values are, using the notation from
Section

3
,

\displaystyle R_{1}^{*}=\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\text{,\quad}R_{2}^{*}=\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}\text{,\quad}I_{1}^{*}=(n-R_{1}^{*}-R_{2}^{*})\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}\text{,\quad and\quad}I_{2}^{*}=(n-R_{1}^{*}-R_{2}^{*})\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}.

Note that these values are only between
0
and
n
if
R_{1}^{*}+R_{2}^{*}<n
.
Solving for
\lambda_{1}^{*}
yields
\lambda_{1}^{*}>(n-\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}})^{-1}\varrho_{1}
as a threshold for a non-trivial equilibrium to arise.
We obtain an analogous condition for
\lambda_{2}^{*}
.

In order to show that this equilibrium is globally stable, we define a Lyapunov function similar to
[
26
]
and
[
12
]
.
To this end, let
f\colon\mathds{R}^{2}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0},(x,x^{*})\mapsto x^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{x^{*}}-\ln\frac{x}{x^{*}}-1}}\right)
.
We define for all
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
and for the
parameters

x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathds{R}
to be defined later

\displaystyle F_{t}

\displaystyle=F(I_{1,t},R_{1,t},I_{2,t},R_{2,t})=x_{1}\cdot f(I_{1}^{*},I_{1,t})+y_{1}\cdot f(R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t})+x_{2}\cdot f(I_{2}^{*},I_{2,t})+y_{2}\cdot f(R_{2}^{*},R_{2,t}).

The Lyapunov function
F
contains one term for each of the four states that measures how close the number of vertices in that state is to its equilibrium.
In the following, we detail how to choose the parameters in order for the derivative of the Lyapunov function by time to be non-positive everywhere.

Let
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
.
By the definition of
F_{t}
, its derivative with respect to
t
is
0
whenever
R_{1,t}=R_{1}^{*}
and
R_{2,t}=R_{2}^{*}
.
Consequently, requiring that the derivative with respect to
t
is non-positive everywhere, its derivative with respect to
R_{1,t}
and
R_{2,t}
is
0
at all states with
R_{1,t}=R_{1}^{*}
and
R_{2,t}=R_{2}^{*}
.
We use this property in order to derive two constraints for the parameters (
Lemma

B.3
).
These constraints help us fix their values later.

We obtain a third constraint by expressing the derivative with respect to
\delta_{1,t}\coloneqq R_{1,t}-R_{1}^{*}
and
\delta_{2,t}\coloneqq R_{2,t}-R_{2}^{*}
(
Lemma

B.4
) and by choosing the parameters that simplify
F_{t}
and make its derivative with respect to
t
clearly non-positive (
Corollary

B.5
).

Given that we have three constraints and four parameters, we resolve the last degree of freedom in a way that makes all parameters constant for the regime of rates we consider in later part of the proof.

In order to derive a potential from
F
, we require furthermore that configurations that are close to at least one infection dying out to be large, as
Theorem

3.1
stops once the potential becomes too large.
Currently,
F
is large whenever
any
of the states has few vertices in them, whereas having only few recovered vertices does not imply that any infection dies out quickly.
Hence, we adjust the terms for
R_{1,t}
and
R_{2,t}
in
F
such that they never become too large.
This results for all
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
in

\displaystyle K_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{1}f(I_{1}^{*},I_{1,t})+2y_{1}f(2R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*})+x_{2}f(I_{2}^{*},I_{2,t})+2y_{2}f(2R_{2}^{*},R_{2,t}+R_{2}^{*}).

whose derivative is still non-positive everywhere (
Lemma

B.7
).

Last, we require that the derivative is not only non-positive but also smaller than a negative constant (due to
item

1
) for a sufficiently large region of the range.
The function
K
does not satisfy this, as it is
0
whenever
R_{1,t}=R_{1}^{*}
and
R_{2,t}=R_{2}^{*}
.
However, its derivative is already sufficiently small whenever
\delta_{1,t}
or
\delta_{2,t}
are at least linear in
n
, which is a satisfactory regime.
Hence, we adjust
K
slightly in order to make its derivative smaller for small
\delta_{1,t}
and
\delta_{2,t}
without changing it greatly for the other values (
Definition

B.8
).
We call this final Lyapunov function
L
(indexed by the time
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
) and use it as a potential.

4.2
Applying the Negative Drift Theorem

We apply
Theorem

3.1
to the potential
L
derived in
Section

4.1
.
We first derive a result for perfectly mixed graphs and then extend the proofs to jumbled-graphs, requiring only minor adjustments.

Recall that
Theorem

3.1
only requires us to have negative drift for a sufficiently large region of the potential’s range.
To this end, we relate the state space and the potential’s range, deriving useful properties on the states that fall into the regime that are of use for further calculations.
Specifically, the potential is only large when
I_{1,t}
or
I_{2,t}
is small.
We show that there are constants
\varepsilon_{l},\varepsilon_{h},c_{l},c_{h}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
L_{t}<c_{h}n
implies
I_{1,t}>\varepsilon_{l}n
and
I_{2,t}>\varepsilon_{l}n
(
Lemma

C.1
), and additionally
L_{t}>c_{l}n
implies
I_{1,t}<\varepsilon_{h}n
or
I_{2,t}<\varepsilon_{h}n
(
Lemma

C.2
, respectively).
Hence, as long as the potential is less than a specific constant fraction of
n
, neither
I_{1,t}
nor
I_{2,t}
is too small. For the other properties we assume that this condition is satisfied.

Items

2
and
3
of
Theorem

3.1
are easily satisfied by showing that changing the number of infected or recovered vertices changes the potential, up to additive lower-order terms, by the derivative of
L
with respect to the that state (
Lemma

C.4
), which is bounded from above by a constant (
Lemma

C.5
).

For
item

1
of
Theorem

3.1
, we first show that the drift is the derivative of
L_{t}
with respect to
t
normalized by the total rate of change
r_{t}
plus lower-order terms (
Lemma

C.7
).
As
r_{t}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
in the considered states, the drift depends mostly on the derivative of
L_{t}
, which is negative by construction.
We then show that this derivative in the considered regime is at most of order
-n/\ln(n)
(
Lemma

C.8
).
We show this via a case distinction on whether
R_{1,t}
or
R_{2,t}
is close to its equilibrium.
If one of these values is far away, then the drift of
K_{t}
is already in
-\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
, which is almost unaffected by the shift to
L_{t}
.
If both
R_{1,t}
and
R_{2,t}
are close, the drift of
K_{t}
is small, but the shift to
L
yields a derivative in
-\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n/\ln(n)}}}\right)
.

Combining all these results and thus applying
Theorem

3.1
, we obtain a bound on the distribution of the number of (discrete) steps until the infection dies out for perfectly mixed graphs (
Theorem

C.9
).
We extend this result to jumbled graphs by showing that the drift on jumbled graphs within a given parameter range is the same as on perfectly mixed graphs up to lower order terms (
Lemma

C.10
).
All other properties we showed carry over directly.
Hence, the bounds for jumbled graphs and perfectly mixed graphs are the same.
Last, we transfer these results to continuous time by bounding with high probability how much continuous time passes at most between two discrete steps (
Corollary

4.1
).

5
Dynamics for More than Two Infections

We generalize the
IRIR
process to more than two infections and derive the transition equilibria.
With these insights, we speculate on the resulting dynamics, using our insights from
Section

4
.

The
IRIR
process naturally generalizes to more than two infections.
Given
k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 3}
infections, each infection
i\in[1..k]
has its own effective infection rate
\lambda^{*}_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and its own recovery rate
\varrho_{i}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
.
If a vertex infected by infection
i
recovers, it is susceptible to infection by all infections except infection
i
.
Moreover, each vertex is either infected by or recovered from exactly one infection at a time.
Using the same notation as for
IRIR
introduced in
Section

3
but extending the range of indices for all
k
infections, we obtain for each
i\in[1..k]
, abbreviating
[k]_{i}\coloneqq[1..k]\smallsetminus\{i\}
, that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}I_{i,t}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}^{*}I_{i,t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum\nolimits_{j\in[k]_{i}}{(R_{j,t})}-\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}}}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}R_{i,t}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\varrho_{i}I_{i,t}-R_{i,t}\sum\nolimits_{j\in[k]_{i}}{(\lambda_{j}^{*}I_{j,t})}.

(2)

The equilibrium states are exactly those where all derivatives of the equations above are
0
.
This results in some trivial equilibria.
For example, for each
i\in[1..k]
and
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
, we have
\frac{dI_{i,t}}{dt}=0
if
I_{i,t}=0
.
If this is the case for some of the
I_{i,t}
, the problem reduces to finding equilibria in a process with one fewer infection.
Hence, for the remainder, we assume that there is a
t\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for all
i\in[1..k]
, we have
I_{i,t}>0
.
Let
i\in[1..k]
.
Then
equation

1
implies
\sum_{j\in[k]_{i}}{(R_{j,t})}=\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}
.
Summing this equation for all
j\in[k]_{i}
and subtracting the equation for
i
multiplied by
k-2
and then dividing by
k-1
yields

\displaystyle R_{i}^{*}=\frac{\sum_{j\in[k]_{i}}{(\varrho_{j}/\lambda_{j}^{*})-(k-2)(\varrho_{i}/\lambda_{i}^{*})}}{k-1}.

(3)

In order for infection
i
to not die out quickly,
R_{i}^{*}
needs to be positive as otherwise
I_{i}^{*}
is non-positive. Hence, we require

\displaystyle\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}<\frac{1}{k-2}\sum\nolimits_{j\in[k]_{i}}{\frac{\varrho_{j}}{\lambda_{j}^{*}}}.

(4)

In a sense,
\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}
can be seen as the
weakness score
of infection
i
. It says how many vertices susceptible to infection
i
are required in order to sustain the number of vertices infected by
i
in expectation. If the weakness score is too high compared to the weakness scores of the other infections, infection
i
is not able to survive against the other infections.
Equation

4
shows that an infection can only be slightly weaker than the average infection in the process in order to survive, in particular if
k
is large.

An interesting observation is that when all but one infection have the same weakness score, they all satisfy
equation

4
as long as the last infection has a positive recovery rate. Hence, a single strong infection cannot make the other infections die out. Instead, it creates a more competitive environment among the other infections where an infection dies out if it is only slightly weaker than the others.

We get another restriction for survival by summing
equation

3
for all
i\in[1..k]
, resulting in

\displaystyle R^{*}\coloneq\sum\nolimits_{i\in[1..k]}{R_{i}^{*}}=\frac{1}{k-1}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[1..k]}{\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}}.

(5)

As there are only
n
vertices,
R^{*}
needs to be smaller than
n
in order for the equilibrium to possibly be stable. Thus, the overall weighted weakness score (the right-hand side of
equation

5
) of the infections cannot be too large. Noting that
equation

4
is equivalent to
\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{i\in[1..k]}{\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}}<\frac{1}{k-2}\sum_{j\in[k]_{i}}{\frac{\varrho_{j}}{\lambda_{j}^{*}}}
, we see that an infection
i\in[1..k]
has a negative equilibrium value of
R_{i}^{*}
if and only if its addition increases
R^{*}
. Consequently, under idealized assumptions, infections die out until the remaining set
S
of infections minimizes
\frac{1}{|S|-1}\sum_{i\in S}{\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}}
. Note that a single infection alone cannot survive long, hence
|S|\geq 2
. If
\frac{1}{|S|-1}\sum_{i\in S}{\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}}\geq n
, there is no set of infections that is able to survive long and all infections die out. Otherwise, there is a stable state in which all values are positive.

We shift our attention from the equilibrium values of the
R
values to those of the
I
values.
Recall that at the equilibrium, all derivatives from
equation

2
are
0
. Rearranging them yields for all
i\in[1..k]
that

\displaystyle\frac{\varrho_{i}I_{i}^{*}}{R_{i}^{*}}+\lambda_{i}^{*}I_{i}^{*}=\sum\nolimits_{j\in[1..k]}{\lambda_{j}^{*}I_{j}^{*}}.

(6)

Note that the term on the right of
equation

6
is independent of
i
. Hence, the left-hand term is the same for all
i\in[1..k]
. Recalling that
\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}=\sum_{j\in[k]_{i}}{R_{j}^{*}}
, the left-hand term of
equation

6
is equivalent to
\frac{\lambda_{i}^{*}}{R_{i}^{*}}I_{i}^{*}(\frac{\varrho_{i}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}+R_{i}^{*})=\frac{\lambda_{i}^{*}}{R_{i}^{*}}I_{i}^{*}R^{*}
. Thus,
I_{i}^{*}
is proportional to
\frac{R_{i}^{*}}{\lambda_{i}^{*}}
. Using
\sum_{i\in[1..k]}{I_{i}^{*}}=n-R^{*}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{i}^{*}=\frac{R_{i}^{*}/\lambda_{i}^{*}}{\sum_{j\in[1..k]}R_{j}^{*}/\lambda_{j}^{*}}(n-R^{*}).

(7)

All infections combined have
n-R^{*}
infected vertices in the equilibrium. Hence, they have more infected vertices the smaller the overall weighted weakness of the infections is. From this pool of infected vertices, the stronger infections get more vertices. However, infections have fewer infected vertices in the equilibrium when the infection rate is larger and the weakness score is the same. In other words, both an increased infection and recovery rate makes the infection have fewer infected vertices in an equilibrium. Roughly speaking, this is a consequence of the weakness score deciding how many vertices become infected by an infection. Increasing both the infection and recovery rate by the same factor does not change the weakness score, but it makes vertices stay infected for a shorter time, meaning that at the same rate of infecting vertices, fewer vertices are infected at any given time.

All in all, the
IRIR
dynamics with three of more infections differ from those with two infections substantially to some degree.
For two infections, each vertex is susceptible to at most one of the infections at a time.
That is, the dynamics are fully cooperative.
For
k\in\mathds{N}_{\geq 3}
infections, a recovered vertex is susceptible to
k-1\geq 2
infections, which creates a competitive environment among the infections.
This competition is so strong that weak infections die out quickly while the remaining ones continue to thrive.

6
Outlook

While we discuss in
Section

5
the dynamics of an
IRIR
process with more than two infections under idealized assumptions, we are currently missing a fully rigorous analysis in the same spirit as our results in
Section

4
.
In particular, it would be interesting to see whether our predictions translate to the fully rigorous setting.
Moreover, while our discussion for more than two infections predicts how the strongest equilibrium looks like, it does not give estimates for how likely it is.
These probabilities highly depend on the starting configuration and detailed infection interactions, which requires a more careful analysis.

Another interesting direction for future work is the analysis of more complex, heterogeneous graphs.
Currently, the impact of high-degree vertices on the survival time is unknown.
Particularly in the case with more than two infections, the structure of the graph could influence the dynamics drastically.

Another venue to explore is to relax our assumptions.
Our main results determine an epidemic threshold when all equilibrium values are of linear size.
It remains an open problem what happens when this is not the case because one of the infections is much stronger than the other one or because both infections are so strong that there are almost no recovered vertices.

Last, it is interesting to derive a stronger upper bound than
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right)
on the survival time for perfectly mixed graphs if
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}>(1+c)n
.
Most SIRS-type infection processes exhibit an expected survival time of
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(n)}}}\right)
in a similar regime.
However, in the
IRIR
setting, it may be the case that
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(n)}}}\right)
cannot be achieved because
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}>(1+c)n
only implies that
one
of the infections dies out fast. The other infection may then continue, similarly to a percolation process, for longer than
\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln(n)}}}\right)
in expectation.

Appendix A
Mathematical Tools

In this section, we state some mathematical tools we use in our analysis. We start by stating a property of
(p,\alpha)
-jumbled graphs.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
p\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 1}
. If
G
is
(p,\alpha)
-jumbled, then for every two disjoint subsets
W,U\subset V
holds

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|e(W,U)-p|U||W|}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha(|U|+|W|).

This follows directly from expressing the number of edges between
W
and
U
as the number of edges in the graph induced by
W\cup U
minus the edges in the graphs induced by
W
and

\displaystyle R_{1}^{*}=\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\text{;\quad}R_{2}^{*}=\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}\text{;\quad}I_{1}^{*}=(n-R_{1}^{*}-R_{2}^{*})\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}\text{;\quad}I_{2}^{*}=(n-R_{1}^{*}-R_{2}^{*})\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}.

(10)

During the process it is very important how close

Also note that these equilibrium points are only between 0 and

B.1

We aim to use the derivative of the potential to apply drift arguments to get a super-polynomial survival time bound similar to

B.3
Changing the
R
target

We aim to show long survival times using a negative drift theorem. To this end we need a region in the potential space in which the process has a constant negative drift. Currently the derivative is 0 whenever both
R_{1,t}
and
R_{2,t}
are at their equilibrium value (which we call the diagonal). This makes it impossible to find such a region. The idea of how to solve that issue is to change the potential slightly such that the drift becomes negative at the diagonal and stays negative everywhere else. At the diagonal, the derivative of
I_{1,t}
and
I_{2,t}
by
t
is 0. Hence we have to adjust the terms for
R_{1,t}
and
R_{2,t}
. At the diagonal, when
I_{1,t}
is above its equilibrium and
I_{2,t}
below,
R_{1,t}
tends to increase and
R_{2,t}
tends to decrease. To use that fact, we adjust the target for
R_{2,t}
slightly such that is a bit bigger if
I_{1,t}
is below its equilibrium and higher if
I_{1,t}
is above its equilibrium. We do the symmetric adjustment for
R_{1,t}
. We get the following potential function.

Appendix C
Bounding the Survival Times

In this section we derive survival time bounds for the IRIR process. To this end we aim to apply a drift theorem on the potential
L
. For a detailed overview on which properties are needed for that, refer to
Section

4.2
. We start by showing that we have a band in the potential space in which we get some bounds on the
I
values. The following lemma shows that a small potential implies that both of the
I
values are relatively big.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
C
be a
IRIR
process on a graph
G
with
n
vertices and density
p\in(0,1]
. Let
C
have constant recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*}=c_{1}/n
and
\lambda_{2}^{*}=c_{2}/n
for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
. Let there be a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
R_{1}^{*}+R_{2}^{*}<(1-c)n
.

Let
\varepsilon_{l}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant and let

c_{h}\coloneqq\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{c}{c_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{l}})-\ln(\frac{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{2}c})-1}}\right),\frac{c}{c_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{l}})-\ln(\frac{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{1}c})-1}}\right)}}\right).

If
L_{t}<c_{h}n
then
I_{1,t}>\varepsilon_{l}n
and
I_{2,t}>\varepsilon_{l}n
.

Proof.

We show the contraposition of the statement. So we show that if
I_{1,t}\leq\varepsilon_{l}n
or
I_{2,t}\leq\varepsilon_{l}n
then
L_{t}\geq c_{h}n
. Assume that
I_{1,t}\leq\varepsilon_{l}n
, the other case follows using symmetric argumentation.

First note that
f
is non-negative. Hence

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle\geq x_{1}f(I_{1}^{*},I_{1,t})

\displaystyle=x_{1}I_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{2}\lambda_{1}^{*}n}I_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{I^{*}}{c_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0-\ln(\frac{\varepsilon_{l}n}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{c_{1}}n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{l}})-\ln(\frac{n}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c}{c_{1}}n\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{l}})-\ln(\frac{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{2}c})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq c_{h}n.\qed

The next lemma shows the opposite direction to the previous one. To be precise, it shows that a big potential implies that one of the
I
values is very small.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
C
be a
IRIR
process on a graph
G
with
n
vertices and density
p\in(0,1]
. Let
C
have constant recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*}=c_{1}/n
and
\lambda_{2}^{*}=c_{2}/n
for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
. Let there be a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
R_{1}^{*}+R_{2}^{*}<(1-c)n
.

Let
\varepsilon_{h}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant. Then there exists a constant
c_{l}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that if

then
I_{1,t}<\varepsilon_{h}n
or
I_{2,t}<\varepsilon_{h}n

We show the contraposition of the statement. So we show that if
I_{1,t}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
and
I_{2,t}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
then

. There are four terms in

We first upper bound the

\displaystyle x_{1}f(I_{1}^{*},I_{1,t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}I_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2}}{\varrho_{2}\lambda_{1}^{*}n}I_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{I^{*}}{c_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}}-\ln(\frac{I_{1,t}}{I_{1}^{*}})-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{n}{c_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{c\varrho_{2}n/(\varrho_{1}+\varrho_{2})}-\ln(\frac{\varepsilon_{h}n}{n})-1}}\right)

In order to get from the mean-field analysis to an analysis of the original stochastic process, we need to convert the derivatives to steps. The following lemma shows that steps that increase

change the potential term of
R_{1,t}
by as much as the derivative would suggest except for lower order terms.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices and density
p\in(0,1]
and let
C
be a
IRIR
process on
G
with
R_{1}^{*}+R_{2}^{*}<(1-c)n
for a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and with constant recovery rates and effective infection rates in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right)
. Let the parameters be chosen as in
Equation

16
and let
I_{2,t}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
. Then

\displaystyle f((1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*})-f((1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{df((1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*})}{dI_{2,t}}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right).

Proof.

First note that with the given parameter restrictions, all of
I_{2,t},R_{1}^{*},I_{2}^{*}
are in
\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
. Using known bounds for the natural logarithm, we get that for any
x\in\mathds{R}_{>-0.68}
it holds
x-x^{2}\leq\ln(1+x)\leq x
. In particular if
x\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right)
, we get that
\ln(1+x)=x-\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right)
. In particular

\displaystyle\ln(I_{2,t}+1)-\ln(I_{2,t})

\displaystyle=\ln(\frac{I_{2,t}+1}{I_{2,t}})=\ln(1+\frac{1}{I_{2,t}})=\frac{1}{I_{2,t}}-\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right).

(18)

This also implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)\ln(I_{2,t})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})}\cdot\frac{\ln(I_{2,t})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})}\cdot(1-\frac{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)-\ln(I_{2,t})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})}\cdot(1-\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right)).

(19)

Now consider the equation we aim to show. We calculated the derivative in
Lemma

B.9
. We start rearranging the other term by using the definition of
f
and canceling out terms that are the same on both sides of the subtraction.

\displaystyle f((1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*})-f((1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})R_{1}^{*},R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*}}{R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})})+1}}\right)

\displaystyle-R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*}}{R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})})+1}}\right).

(20)

The strategy is now to write the factors in the second term as the factors of the first term plus the appropriate difference. Multiplying things out than cancels the entire first term and just leaves us with the difference. We first calculate the difference of the second terms using
Equation

18
and
Equation

19
.

\displaystyle(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})-(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)}-\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})(\ln(I_{2,t})-\ln(I_{2,t}+1))}{\ln(I_{2,t})\ln(I_{2,t}+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})}(1\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right))(-\frac{1}{I_{2,t}}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{I_{2,t}\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right).

(21)

Doing similar calculation for the third term, we get

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*}}{R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)})})+1}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*}}{R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})})+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)}}{1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)}-\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})}}{1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})(\ln(I_{2,t})-\ln(I_{2,t}+1))}{\ln(I_{2,t}+1)\ln(I_{2,t})}}{1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})(-1/I_{2,t}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right))}{\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})}(1-\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right))}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\ln\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{I_{2,t}\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{I_{2,t}\ln^{2}(I_{2,t})(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-2}}}}\right).

(22)

21
and
Equation

20

\displaystyle xyz

\displaystyle R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ln(\frac{R_{1,t}+R_{1}^{*}}{R_{1}^{*}(1+\frac{\ln(I_{2}^{*})}{\ln(I_{2,t})})})+1}}\right)

Lemma C.4
.

Let
C

IRIR

with
n

\displaystyle L(I_{1,t},R_{1,t},I_{2,t}+1,R_{2,t})-L(I_{1,t},R_{1,t},I_{2,t},R_{2,t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{dL(I_{1,t},R_{1,t},I_{2,t},R_{2,t})}{dI_{2,t}}\pm\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n^{-1}}}}\right).

I_{1,t}
,
R_{1,t}
and

The lemma statement follows directly from
Lemma

A.4

. The difference of the

A.4
only off from the derivative of that term by

The argumentation for

and

∎

Lemma C.7

G

n

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle-2y_{1}\frac{R_{1}^{*}(\ln(I_{2,t})-\ln(I_{2}^{*}))}{2n\ln(n)}(\lambda_{2}^{*}I_{2,t}R_{1,t}-\varrho_{1}\varepsilon_{h}n)

We now show the main technical theorem which is an application of the negative drift theorem. It gives a bound on the distribution of the survival time of an IRIR process on perfectly mixed graphs.

Theorem C.9
.

Let
C
be a
IRIR
process on a perfectly mixed graph
G
with
n
vertices and density
p\in(0,1]
. Let
C
have constant recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*}=c_{1}/n
and
\lambda_{2}^{*}=c_{2}/n
for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
. Let there be a constant
c\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
R_{1}^{*}+R_{2}^{*}<(1-c)n
. Let there be a small enough constant
\varepsilon_{h}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
I_{1,0}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
and
I_{2,0}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
. Let

be the number of steps until the infections in
C
die out. Then there exists a constant

such that for sufficiently large
n

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{\mathrm{d}}\leq t}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}\cdot e^{-c_{T}\frac{n}{\ln(n)}}.

Proof.

The idea is to use the negative drift theorem
Theorem

3.1
on the potential function
L
from
Definition

B.8
. To this end we first show that there is an interval of linear length in the potential space in which all states that fall into this interval have a linear number of infected vertices but one of the infected vertices is below
\varepsilon_{h}n
. Those restrictions are necessary for most of our lemmas to apply. We then apply the lemmas proven in this section to show that in this region the step size is bounded by a constant and the drift is negative. This lets us apply
Theorem

3.1
to bound the survival time.

By
Lemma

C.2
there exists a constant
c_{l}
such that
L_{t}>c_{l}n
implies
I_{1,t}<\varepsilon_{h}n
or
I_{2,t}<\varepsilon_{h}n
. Note that this means that
L_{0}\leq c_{l}n
.

Let
\varepsilon_{l}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant such that the corresponding
c_{h}
from
Lemma

C.1
is at least
2c_{l}
. We then get by applying
Lemma

C.1
that
L_{t}<c_{h}n
implies
I_{1,t}>\varepsilon_{l}n
and
I_{2,t}>\varepsilon_{l}n
.

That means that as long as
L_{t}<c_{h}n
, the conditions for
Lemma

C.5
and
Lemma

C.4
are met and applying both of them together gives us that there is a constant
c_{s}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that the change of
L_{t}
within one step is upper bounded by
c_{s}
.

We now define the process
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathds{N}}
which is the discrete version of
L_{t}
shifted and scaled so that we can apply the negative drift theorem. It is defined as

\displaystyle X_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{\tau_{i}}-c_{l}n}{c_{s}}-1.

We now show that all conditions of
Theorem

3.1
are met. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i\in\mathds{N}}
be the natural filtration of
X_{i}
. Let
a=-1
,
b=\frac{c_{h}n-c_{l}n}{c_{s}}-1
,
c=1
and let
T_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}=\inf\{i\in\mathds{N}\mid X_{i}\geq b\}
. First note that
L_{0}\leq c_{l}n
implies that
X_{0}\leq 0
. For all
i<T_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
it holds that
X_{i}<b
which implies
L_{\tau_{i}}\leq c_{h}n
and therefore
I_{1,\tau_{i}}>\varepsilon_{l}n
and
I_{2,\tau_{i}}>\varepsilon_{l}n
. We use this to show that the three items are fulfilled for all
i<T_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
.

For the first point note that
\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(X_{i+1}-X_{i})\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{(X_{i+1}-X_{i})}\mathcal{F}_{i}}}\right.}}}\right]=\frac{D_{i}}{c_{s}}
. For states with
X_{i}\geq a
we get by
Lemma

C.8
that there is a constant
c_{d}^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
\frac{dL_{t}}{dt}\leq-c_{d}^{\prime}\frac{n}{\ln(n)}
. Together with
Lemma

C.7
and the fact that with
I_{1,\tau_{i}}\geq\varepsilon_{l}n
it holds
r_{\tau_{i}}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
we get that there is a constant
c_{d}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
D_{i}\leq-\frac{c_{d}}{\ln(n)}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{(X_{i+1}-X_{i})\cdot\mathbf{1}{\{X_{i}\geq a\}}\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\ \vphantom{(X_{i+1}-X_{i})\cdot\mathbf{1}{\{X_{i}\geq a\}}}\mathcal{F}_{i}}}\right.}}}\right]\leq-\frac{c_{d}}{\ln(n)}\cdot\mathbf{1}{\{X_{i}\geq a\}}.

The second point follows directly from the fact that
L_{\tau_{i}}
changes by at most
c_{s}
in one step for all
i<T_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
. Hence the step size of

is upper bounded by
c=1
.

This also directly implies the third point as

, hence one step cannot change
X_{i}<a
to

.

Applying
Theorem

3.1
gives us for all
t\in\mathds{N}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}\leq t}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}\cdot e^{-\frac{(\frac{c_{h}n-c_{l}n}{c_{s}}-1)\frac{c_{d}}{\ln(n)}}{2}}.

As for all
i<T_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime}
holds

and

, we know

. Hence using the above inequality we get that there is a constant
c_{T}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that for sufficiently large

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{\mathrm{d}}\leq t}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}\cdot e^{-c_{T}\frac{n}{\ln(n)}}.\qed

e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{8\ln(n)}}
is much smaller than half the expectation of
T_{\mathrm{d}}
when
T_{\mathrm{d}}>e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{4\ln(n)}}
. We get using
Lemma

A.6
that conditioned on
T_{\mathrm{d}}>e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{4\ln(n)}}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X\leq e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{8\ln(n)}}}}}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{X\leq T_{\mathrm{d}}/2r}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-T_{\mathrm{d}}(1/2-1-\ln(1/2))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{6}e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{4\ln(n)}}}.

Plugging that into

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T\leq e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{8\ln(n)}}}}}\right]\leq

Using the fact that Erdős–Rényi graphs are almost surely
(p^{\prime},\sqrt{np})
-jumbled, we get almost surely super-polynomial survival times for them.

Corollary C.12
.

Let
n\in\mathds{N}
, let
k\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
with
k\in\upomega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\ln^{2}(n)}}}\right)
and
p=k/n\leq 0.99
and let
C
be a
IRIR
process on a graph
G=G(n,p)
. Let
C
have constant recovery rates
\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
and effective infection rates
\lambda_{1}^{*}=c_{1}/n
and
\lambda_{2}^{*}=c_{2}/n
for constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
. Let there be a constant

such that
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}<(1-c)n
. Let there be a small enough constant
\varepsilon_{h}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
such that
I_{1,0}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
and
I_{2,0}\geq\varepsilon_{h}n
. Let
T
be the time until the infections in

die out. Then there exists a constant

such that for sufficiently large

almost surely

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\in\upOmega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({e^{\frac{c_{T}n}{8\ln(n)}}}}}\right).

Let
p^{\prime}
be the density of
G
. Not that almost surely
p^{\prime}\in\upTheta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({p}}}\right)
. Using

(p^{\prime},\sqrt{np})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k}=p\frac{\sqrt{k}}{p}=pn/\sqrt{k}.

As almost surely

We obtained a super-polynomial survival time lower bound for the case when
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}<(1-c)n
. In the next theorem we use an easier potential function together with the additive drift theorem to give a polynomial survival time upper bound if
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}>(1+c)n
, showing that this condition gives some kind of threshold behavior.

See
4.2

Proof.

The idea of this proof is to define a simpler potential function than we needed for the other direction and then use an additive drift theorem (
Theorem

A.3
) to bound the expected number of steps until one of the infection dies out. We then bound the time until the second infection also dies out separately. At last we obtain a survival time bound from the bound on the number of steps.

Let
T_{1}^{\prime}
be the number of steps until one of the two infection dies out. Then for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{<T_{1}^{\prime}}
we define the potential (defining
\ln(0)=-1
)

\displaystyle P_{i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}(\ln(I_{1,\tau_{i}})+1)+\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}(\ln(I_{2,\tau_{i}})+1).

Let
r_{i}
be the total rate of change at step
i
and let
e(I_{1},R_{2})
and
e(I_{2},R_{1})
be the number of edges between vertices in these states respectively at step
i
. Note that
r_{i}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n}}}\right)
so let
c_{r}\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
be a constant such that
r_{i}<c_{r}n
for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{<T_{1}^{\prime}}
. We now calculate the drift for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{<T_{1}^{\prime}}
using
\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}>(1+c)n
,
R_{1,\tau_{i}}+R_{2,\tau_{i}}\leq n
and
\frac{1}{x+1}\leq\ln(x+1)-\ln(x)\leq\frac{1}{x}
for all
x\in\mathds{N}_{>0}
(and
\frac{1}{x+1}\leq\ln(x+1)-\ln(x)
for
x=0
using our definition of
\ln(0)
). We get

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}}}\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}e(I_{1},R_{2})}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}(\ln(I_{1,\tau_{i}}+1)-\ln(I_{1,\tau_{i}}))}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\varrho_{1}I_{1,\tau_{i}}}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}(\ln(I_{1,\tau_{i}}-1)-\ln(I_{1,\tau_{i}}))}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}e(I_{2},R_{1})}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}(\ln(I_{2,\tau_{i}}+1)-\ln(I_{2,\tau_{i}}))}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\varrho_{2}I_{2,\tau_{i}}}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}(\ln(I_{2,\tau_{i}}-1)-\ln(I_{2,\tau_{i}}))}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{*}I_{1,\tau_{i}}R_{2,\tau_{i}}}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}\cdot\frac{1}{I_{1,\tau_{i}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\varrho_{1}I_{1,\tau_{i}}}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}\cdot-\frac{1}{I_{1,\tau_{i}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}^{*}I_{2,\tau_{i}}R_{1,\tau_{i}}}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\cdot\frac{1}{I_{2,\tau_{i}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\varrho_{2}I_{2,\tau_{i}}}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\cdot-\frac{1}{I_{2,\tau_{i}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{2,\tau_{i}}-\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+R_{1,\tau_{i}}-\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{2,\tau_{i}}+R_{1,\tau_{i}}-(\frac{\varrho_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{*}}+\frac{\varrho_{2}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}})}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-(1+c)n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{cn}{r_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{c}{c_{r}}.

Furthermore note that for all
i\in\mathds{N}_{<T_{1}^{\prime}}
holds
P_{i}\geq 0
and
P_{i}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right)
. In particular
P_{0}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right)
. We aim to apply
Theorem

A.3
. However, the stopping time in this theorem is defined as the time until the potential hits
0
. Our stopping time might stop earlier than that. To make the drift theorem directly applicable we just redefine the potential of all states at which we stopped to be
0
. This just decreases the drift further and does not break anything else. Applying
Theorem

A.3
we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T_{1}^{\prime}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right).

Let
T_{2}^{\prime}
be the number of steps it takes for the second infection to die out after the first did. Let w.l.o.g.
I_{1}
be the first infection to die out. Then after that no vertex can enter or leave the
I_{1}
state anymore. As each vertex changes states deterministically in a circle, each vertex can change its state at most twice more before the infection dies out completely. Hence
T_{2}^{\prime}\leq 2n
.

While the infection did not die out yet, in each state the total rate of change
r_{i}
is lower bounded by
\min(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})
. Hence the expected length of each step is at most
\min(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})^{-1}
independent of all other steps. In particular, the time until the infection dies out dominates the sum of
(T_{1}^{\prime}+T_{2}^{\prime})

\min(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})^{-1}
. Using Wald’s equation (
Theorem

A.5
) we get that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{T}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(T_{1}^{\prime}+T_{2}^{\prime})\cdot\min(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})^{-1}\in\mathrm{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({n\ln(n)}}}\right).\qed
