A Survey of Advancing Audio Super-Resolution and Bandwidth Extension from Discriminative to Generative Models
Ningyuan Yang ningyuan.yang@stonybrook.edu, Yize Li li.yize@northeastern.edu, Diego A. Cuji diego.cujidutan@stonybrook.edu, Ryan M. Corey corey1@uic.edu, Pu Zhao p.zhao@northeastern.edu, Xue Lin xue.lin@northeastern.edu, Andrew C. Singer andrew.c.singer@stonybrook.edu

Abstract
Audio super-resolution (SR), also referred to as bandwidth extension (BWE), aims to reconstruct high-fidelity signals from low-resolution (LR) or band-limited (BL) observations, an inherently ill-posed task due to the ambiguity of missing high-frequency (HF) content. This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the field, with a particular focus on the paradigm shift from discriminative mapping to modern generative modeling. We first review early discriminative deep neural network (DNN) models, which formulate BWE/SR as a deterministic mapping problem and are prone to regression-to-the-mean effects and spectral over-smoothing. We then systematically review generative approaches, including autoregressive (AR) models, variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), diffusion and score-based models, flow-based methods, and Schrödinger bridges. Across these approaches, we examine key design aspects, including representation domain, architecture, conditioning mechanisms, and trade-offs among reconstruction fidelity, perceptual quality, robustness, and computational efficiency. Furthermore, we discuss emerging directions involving large language models (LLMs) and multimodal foundation models, and highlight open challenges in perceptual evaluation, phase modeling, and real-world generalization. By providing a structured taxonomy and unified perspective, this survey establishes a comprehensive foundation and offers a practical roadmap for advancing BWE/SR from deterministic point estimation toward distribution-aware generative modeling.

2.1

The tasks of

3.3
Spectral Mapping Paradigms

Existing
bwe

,

, and

This setting is typical of specialized systems such as legacy telephony restoration, where the speech bandwidth is extended from

(
nb

–
4

(

(

employs distinct models for upscaling ratios of

and

The bandwidth-agnostic paradigm employs a single universal model trained to handle a wide range of input cutoff frequencies, enabling many-to-one spectral bandwidth mapping within a unified framework. In this regime, input cutoff frequencies are either sampled from a continuous stochastic range or selected from a discrete set, as in NVSR
(

(

, which uses a discrete set

4.2
Evaluation Methods

4.2.1
Objective Metrics

Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR):
Given
N
samples of a reference signal
\mathbf{s}=[s[1],\dots,s[N]]^{\top}

and its estimate
\hat{\mathbf{s}}=[\hat{s}[1],\dots,\hat{s}[N]]^{\top}
, SNR
(
16
)
is defined as

\text{SNR}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{s}-\hat{\mathbf{s}}\rVert^{2}}\right).

(3)

SNR is a standard metric in the signal processing literature, commonly used to evaluate waveform distortion in the time domain.
To better capture frame-level variations, the segmental signal-to-noise ratio (SegSNR)
(
150
)
computes the average SNR over short-time segments of an audio signal.
Let us denote the
l
-th frame of
\mathbf{s}
as
\mathbf{s}_{l}
and the
l
-th frame of

\hat{\mathbf{s}}
as
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{l}
,
l=1,\dots,L
, where
L

represents the total number of frames. SegSNR is defined as

\text{SegSNR}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}10\cdot\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{l}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{l}-\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{l}\|^{2}}\right).

(4)

Both metrics are widely used but have limited sensitivity to perceptual quality in
bwe
/
asr
: because the generated
hf
components are stochastic and may not align sample-by-sample with the reference even when perceptually convincing, high SNR does not guarantee good
hf
reconstruction quality.

Scale-invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR):

SI-SDR
(
85
)
evaluates reconstruction fidelity while explicitly removing sensitivity to global gain mismatch by optimally projecting the estimated signal onto the subspace spanned by the reference signal, which is defined as

\text{SI-SDR}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=10\cdot\log_{10}\left(\frac{\|\alpha\mathbf{s}\|^{2}}{\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}-\alpha\mathbf{s}\|^{2}}\right),

(5)

where

\alpha=\frac{\langle\hat{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{s}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{s}\|^{2}}.

(6)

This metric evaluates reconstruction quality by measuring the energy ratio between the optimally scaled target signal and the residual distortion. Owing to its scale-invariant formulation and robustness to gain mismatch, SI-SDR has become a standard evaluation criterion for speech separation,
se
, and
bwe
/
asr
in contemporary audio research. However, it shares the same fundamental limitation as SNR for
bwe
/
asr
: sample-level alignment between generated
hf
content and the reference is not required for perceptual fidelity, so SI-SDR can underrate perceptually high-quality reconstructions.

Log-Spectral Distance (LSD):

LSD
(
51
)
measures reconstruction quality by computing the average discrepancy between the logarithmic magnitude spectra of the reference and reconstructed signals across frequencies.
Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{T\times F}
and
\hat{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{T\times F}
denote the
stft
of
\mathbf{s}
and
\hat{\mathbf{s}}
, respectively, where
T
and
F
denote the numbers of time frames and frequency bins in the spectrogram.
Their magnitude spectra are defined as
\mathbf{X}=|\mathbf{S}|\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
and
\hat{\mathbf{X}}=|\hat{\mathbf{S}}|\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times F}
. LSD is then computed as

\text{LSD}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\frac{1}{F}\sum_{f=1}^{F}D_{t,f}^{2}},

(7)

with the per-frame, per-frequency log-spectral difference

D_{t,f}=\log_{10}\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{t,f}}{\mathbf{X}_{t,f}}\right).

(8)

To evaluate different spectral regions separately, the
lf
LSD (LSD-LF) is used to assess reconstruction quality of the preserved
lf
region,
while the
hf
LSD (LSD-HF) measures the quality of the extended
hf
region. They are defined as

\text{LSD-HF}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\frac{1}{F_{\text{HF}}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{HF}}}D_{t,f}^{2}},

(9)

\text{LSD-LF}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\frac{1}{F_{\text{LF}}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{LF}}}D_{t,f}^{2}},

(10)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\text{HF}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{LF}}
denote the sets of
hf
and
lf
bins, with
F_{\text{HF}}
and
F_{\text{LF}}
representing the numbers of bins in
hf
and
lf
regions, respectively. Lower scores in LSD, LSD-HF, and LSD-LF imply a more similar spectrum to the target. While LSD correlates better with perceptual quality than time-domain measures like SNR, it often fails to account for the quality of reconstructed signals that are perceptually authentic but not mathematically identical to the reference spectra.

Virtual Speech Quality Objective Listener (ViSQOL):

ViSQOL
(
64
)
is a signal-based, full-reference metric that estimates perceptual quality using a spectro-temporal measure of similarity between reference and degraded signals.
It outputs a Mean Opinion Score-Listening Quality Objective (MOS-LQO), where higher values indicate better quality. ViSQOL supports both speech and general audio: in speech mode (
16
kHz), MOS-LQO ranges from
1
to
5
, while in audio mode (
48
kHz), it ranges from
1
to
4.75
. Unlike SNR-based metrics, ViSQOL is better suited for
bwe
/
asr
evaluation as it reflects perceptual similarity rather than sample-level fidelity.

Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ):

PESQ
(
12
)
is an ITU-T standardized metric that estimates perceptual speech quality by comparing reference and degraded signals. The original PESQ targets
nb
speech (
300
Hz–
3.4
kHz) with scores ranging from
-0.5
to
4.5
, while its
wb
extension (WB-PESQ)
2
2

2

https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.862.2
.
supports
16
kHz speech with scores between
1.04
and
4.64
. In both cases, higher values indicate better quality. However, PESQ is designed for distortions typical of telecommunications channels (e.g., coding artifacts, packet loss) rather than
hf
reconstruction, and can therefore be misleading for evaluating
bwe
/
asr
systems.

Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI):

STOI
(
181

is an objective metric that estimates speech intelligibility by correlating short-time temporal envelopes of reference and degraded signals, producing scores between
0

4.2.2

Mean Opinion Score (MOS):

MOS
(
204

is a subjective evaluation method standardized by the ITU-T for assessing perceived speech and audio quality. It is computed by averaging listener ratings, typically collected on a five-point Absolute Category Rating scale, where
1
denotes bad quality and
5
denotes excellent quality. As a direct measure of human perception, MOS serves as a reliable benchmark and is widely used to complement objective metrics in speech and audio research. In
bwe
/
asr
, MOS evaluations are increasingly conducted via crowdsourcing platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, while automatic MOS predictors, such as MOSNet
(

)
, DNSMOS
(
161
)
,
NISQA
(
142
)
,
and WV-MOS
(
6
)
, have emerged as scalable alternatives to full listening tests.

Preference Tests:

Preference-based listening tests are subjective evaluations in which listeners compare audio samples produced by different systems and indicate their preference. In A/B tests, two audio samples are directly compared in terms of perceived quality or intelligibility, while ABX tests require listeners to identify which of two candidates is closer to a reference signal. These methods enable direct pairwise comparisons and are often used alongside MOS evaluations, providing higher sensitivity to subtle differences between high-performing
bwe
/
asr
systems.

Multiple Stimuli with Hidden Reference and Anchor (MUSHRA):

The MUSHRA test
3
3

3

https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1534-3-201510-I/en
.
is a standardized subjective evaluation methodology designed to assess intermediate-quality audio systems.
In this test, listeners are presented with multiple stimuli, including a hidden reference and one or more anchor signals, and are asked to rate the perceived audio quality on a continuous scale from
0
to
100
.
Compared with MOS-based evaluations, MUSHRA provides finer resolution and improved sensitivity for distinguishing perceptual differences among systems. In
bwe
/
asr
studies, the

/
lr

In practice, LSD and MOS are the most consistently co-reported metrics across the

/
asr
literature—LSD for its direct spectral relevance to the

Transformers have increasingly been adopted for

5.5

Mamba

52

Extensive evaluations show that TRAMBA achieves substantial improvements in speech quality and intelligibility while maintaining a compact model size and enabling highly efficient inference. Furthermore, real-system integration and user studies demonstrate that TRAMBA delivers robust
ssr

6.1
Autoregressive (AR) Model

WaveNet
(
190
)
is a fully probabilistic
ar
model originally developed for audio generation tasks such as multi-speaker speech synthesis, text-to-speech (TTS), and musical audio modeling, where each sample is predicted from all previous samples. This framework is then extended to
bwe
/
asr

(
197
;
57
)
by adopting a conditional WaveNet to model the conditional distribution of a
bb
/
hr
waveform
\mathbf{y}=[y[1],\ldots,y[T]]^{\top}
given a
bl
/
lr
waveform
\mathbf{x}
. The formulation is given as

p(\mathbf{y}\mid h(\mathbf{x}))=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p\left(y[t]\mid y[1],\ldots,y[t-1],h(\mathbf{x})\right),

(11)

where the conditioning term
h(\mathbf{x})
can be either a raw waveform
h(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
or a log-mel spectrogram

h(\mathbf{x})=\log\left({\text{MelFilter}}\left(|\text{STFT}(\mathbf{x})|\right)\right).

(12)

The log-mel spectrogram conditioning has been shown to outperform raw waveform conditioning
(
197
;
57
)
and is widely recognized as an effective intermediate representation for high-fidelity audio generation
(
171
)
.

Figure 5
:
Visualization of a stack of dilated causal convolutional layers.
Dilated causal convolutions with dilation factors of

,
2
,
4

8
are shown, where dilation specifies the spacing between consecutive filter taps, allowing the temporal receptive field to grow exponentially while preserving causality.

WaveNet is built on causal convolutions that enforce temporal causality by conditioning each sample
y[t]
only on past samples, while enabling parallel computation during training and efficient modeling of long sequences.
To expand temporal context without excessively deep stacks or large kernels, WaveNet employs dilated causal convolutions, where the dilation factor increases exponentially across layers, resulting in exponential receptive-field expansion with minimal parameter growth. Fig.
5
depicts dilated causal convolutions for dilation =
1
,
2
,
4
, and
8
.
Each layer uses gated activation units
(
191
)
, which outperform standard ReLU activations in audio modeling. The output distribution is modeled via a categorical softmax over
\mu
-law-companded samples with 256-level quantization
(
160
)
, and it is trained using the
ce
loss. Residual and skip connections further stabilize optimization in deep architectures.

In
(
57
)
, the conditioning log-mel spectrogram is processed to match the temporal resolution of the waveform and injected into WaveNet layers to guide generation. While WaveNet produces high-quality audio, its sample-by-sample generation leads to slow inference. To address this limitation,
197
propose a parallel WaveNet trained via teacher-student distillation, where a non-
ar
student model approximates the
ar
teacher. By leveraging inverse
ar
flows, the student enables parallel generation and significantly improves inference speed. The training objective combines a
kl
divergence term with a spectral-domain power loss to preserve perceptual quality.

Following the WaveNet framework,
56

bwe

(
80
)
, exploits future context for offline scenarios. Unlike the original WaveNet, which performs time-shifted next-sample prediction, both architectures directly generate the
bb
waveform. For the causal configuration, the conditional distribution is modeled as

p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p\left(y[t]\mid x[t-N+1],\ldots,x[t]\right),

(13)

where
\mathbf{x}=[x[1],x[2],\ldots,x[T]]^{\top}
is the input
bl
sequence,
\mathbf{y}=[y[1],y[2],\ldots,y[T]]^{\top}
is the output
bb
sequence, and
N
is the length of the receptive field. For the non-causal version, the distribution becomes

p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p\left(y[t]\mid x[t-N/2],\ldots,x[t+N/2]\right),

(14)

where
N+1
is the length of the corresponding receptive field.
As in WaveNet, both waveforms are

\mu
-law quantized and the model is trained using the
ce
loss.

Following
(
56
)
,
120
propose a
bwe
model based on a
hrnn
(
hrnn

hrnn

bb

bl

dnn

cnn

Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

Figure 6
:
Architecture of an unconditional VAE,
where an encoder infers the latent distribution
\mathbf{z}
parameterized by mean
\bm{\mu}_{\phi}
and variance
\bm{\sigma}_{\phi}
, and a decoder reconstructs the input signal
\mathbf{x}
via latent sampling using the reparameterization trick.

An unconditional variational autoencoder (VAE)
(
33
)
models the generation of an input signal
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
through a latent variable
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The generative process is formulated as

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})=\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z},

(15)

where
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the marginal likelihood of the input signal,
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})
represents the conditional likelihood that maps the latent representation to the input, and
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})
is the prior distribution over the latent variable, which is typically chosen as a standard Gaussian distribution to regularize the latent space.
During training, an encoder network
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})
approximates the intractable posterior over latent variables, while a decoder
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})
reconstructs the input from the latent representation. The model parameters
\theta
and
\phi
are learned by maximizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO) function
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\mathbf{x})
:

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\geq\;\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})}\!\left[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\right]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})\right),

(16)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)
denotes the KL divergence.
The first term of
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;\mathbf{x})
encourages accurate reconstruction of the input signal, while the second term regularizes the latent distribution to match the prior.
Sampling of the latent variable is enabled by the reparameterization trick

\mathbf{z}=\bm{\mu}_{\phi}+\bm{\sigma}_{\phi}\odot\bm{\epsilon},

(17)

where
\bm{\mu}_{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denote the mean and standard deviation predicted by the encoder network, respectively,
\bm{\epsilon}=[\epsilon^{(1)},\ldots,\epsilon^{(D)}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is an auxiliary noise vector with independent standard normal entries

\epsilon^{(d)}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad d=1,\ldots,D,

(18)

and
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication.
Unlike
ar
models which generate samples sequentially,
vae
reconstruct the entire signal
\mathbf{x}
in a single forward pass, enabling efficient and fully parallel inference. Fig.
6
depicts the architecture of an unconditional
vae
.

186

asr
framework that jointly leverages waveform and spectrogram representations. Their approach combines a
vae
-based time-domain module, a U-Net-based spectrogram module, and an attention-based fusion mechanism to integrate complementary information from both domains. This design preserves both temporal and spectral characteristics and demonstrates strong robustness across acoustic domains, including underwater scenarios.

8

(
cvae

bb

bl

cvae
framework, the approach improves both perceptual speech quality and downstream speech recognition performance.

\mathcal{L}_{G}^{\text{hinge}}=-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}[D(\hat{\mathbf{y}})],

(23)

\mathcal{L}_{G}^{\text{LS}}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}[(D(\hat{\mathbf{y}})-1)^{2}],

(25)

A diffusion model comprises two coupled stochastic processes: a forward diffusion process and a reverse sampling process. In the forward process, progressively increasing Gaussian noise is added to a clean signal
\mathbf{x}_{0}
according to

\mathbf{x}_{t}=\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}}\,\bm{\epsilon},

(32)

(33)

\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}

denotes a predefined noise variance schedule. This forward process progressively transforms the data distribution into an isotropic Gaussian distribution. The reverse process is parameterized by a neural network
\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)

\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\,\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\right)+\sigma_{t}\mathbf{z},

(34)

(35)

with
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
and
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\beta_{t}}

Challenges and Overlook

7.1
Discussion

Discriminative
dnn
-based models have driven major progress in
bwe
/
asr
, yet their core limitation is structural: they implement a
deterministic
mapping from a
bl
/
lr
observation to a single
bb
/
hr
estimate. Since
hf
recovery is an inherently one-to-many problem, point-estimation training—often dominated by sample-level or spectral-point losses—tends to collapse conditional uncertainty, leading to a regression-to-the-mean effect. Empirically, the reconstructed audio signals often exhibit spectral over-smoothing and reduced harmonic, transient, and aperiodic micro-structure. Consequently, such models may preserve
lf
fidelity while under-delivering perceptual brightness, detail, and naturalness.

In contrast, generative modeling—including
ar
models,
vae
,
gan
, diffusion and score-based models, flow-based models, and bridge models—reframes
bwe
/
asr
as learning a conditional distribution rather than a deterministic mapping. This probabilistic perspective better reflects the inherent ambiguity of the task: the missing
hf
band is not uniquely determined by the observed
lf
content. A principled model should therefore represent conditional uncertainty and support multiple plausible reconstructions. Consequently, generative approaches can synthesize
hf
details that are consistent with semantic content, timbral structure, and event characteristics across speech, music, and general sounds, rather than regressing toward an average spectrum. This explains the shift toward conditional generative modeling, where conditioning enforces fidelity to the observed band while distribution modeling enhances perceptual realism.

Within the generative family,
gan
were early attempts to improve perceptual sharpness through adversarial learning. However, they have become less dominant in recent
bwe
/
asr
research because adversarial objectives can be unstable and prone to generating
hf
details that sound sharp but are weakly constrained by the observed
lf
content. These trade-offs have motivated increasing interest in generative paradigms with more explicit sampling dynamics and better-controlled conditional generation.

Diffusion, flow, and bridge-based models address this limitation in different ways. Diffusion and score-based models capture complex conditional distributions through iterative denoising, often achieving high perceptual quality and strong mode coverage, but at the cost of multi-step sampling latency
(
58
;
125
)
. Flow-based models instead learn continuous transformations between simple and data distributions, offering a more direct path toward efficient generation and few-step sampling for low-latency inference
(
203
;
69
)
. Bridge models further generalize this view by learning stochastic paths between degraded and clean signals, which is conceptually well aligned with restoration tasks, but they often introduce additional modeling and sampling complexity
(
104
;
105
)
. In this landscape, flow-based approaches are particularly favorable for
bwe
/
asr
as shown in Fig.
1
, because they offer a practical balance between generative flexibility and inference efficiency: they retain the distributional modeling benefits needed for plausible
hf
synthesis while enabling faster sampling than standard diffusion models, making them attractive for real-time audio reconstruction.

7.2
Challenges and Open Problems

Despite the growing success of diffusion, bridge, and flow-based models for
bwe
/
asr
, several challenges remain open. First, high perceptual quality often comes with nontrivial inference cost, especially for iterative diffusion and bridge models, making low-latency or real-time deployment difficult; although flow-based and flow-matching methods improve efficiency, their quality-speed trade-offs remain task- and design-dependent. Second, evaluation remains a major bottleneck, as common objective metrics may not reliably capture perceptual naturalness, artifact suppression, or the realism of hallucinated
hf
details; this limitation is especially pronounced for music and general audio, where perceived quality depends on timbre, texture, and event realism rather than waveform fidelity alone. Third, robustness to real-world degradations remains limited, since many systems are trained on simplified low-pass or downsampling operators and may degrade under codec artifacts, noise, device coloration, unknown bandwidth constraints, and broader domain shifts. Finally, the design space of diffusion schedules, bridge endpoints, flow paths, conditioning mechanisms, and neural representations remains underexplored, leaving open questions about how to balance fidelity, diversity, controllability, and efficiency in next-generation generative
bwe
/
asr
systems.

Additionally, self-supervised and multimodal representation learning may improve robustness and evaluation. Objectives such as contrastive learning, masked audio modeling, and audio-language alignment can produce representations that transfer across restoration tasks and degradation conditions, potentially improving generalization to noise, codec artifacts, device coloration, and unseen bandwidth constraints. In parallel, learned perceptual metrics derived from audio-language or audio-visual models may better capture human judgments of naturalness, timbre, texture, and event realism than conventional signal-based measures, making them useful for evaluation, perceptual fine-tuning, and guided generation.

Overall, future progress in

Joint advances across these directions will be essential for developing robust, efficient, and perceptually faithful systems across speech, music, and general audio.

Conclusion

This survey presented a unified review of
bwe
and
asr

hf

dnn
