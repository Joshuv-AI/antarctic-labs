Performance, safety, and limitations of multimodal large language models in wound image assessment
Authors: Sirin Apichonbancha, Nutcha Yodrabum, Sasima Tongsai, Chantaramon Thanapaisal, Chanya Sinmaroeng, Kengkart Winaikosol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate visual assessment of acute, chronic, and surgical wounds is fundamental to clinical decision-making and is increasingly performed using digital wound photographs in routine and remote care. However, interpretation of wound images remains subjective and variable across clinicians, and the rapid emergence of vision-capable large language models (LLMs) has led to their informal use for wound description and clinical interpretation despite limited task-specific validation. To address this gap, we compared three advanced vision-capable LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini Advanced) for structured wound image assessment using standardized clinical frameworks. From 1,200 clinical wound photographs obtained during routine care, 450 images (150 acute, 150 chronic, and 150 surgical) were randomly selected and independently reviewed by three expert clinicians to establish expert consensus reference standards. Each model received identical prompts incorporating the Bates–Jensen Wound Assessment Tool (BWAT) and the TIMES framework, and outputs were evaluated for diagnostic accuracy, assessment quality, treatment recommendation appropriateness, safety, understandability, and actionability; informational quality was assessed using DISCERN and PEMAT-P. ChatGPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy for diagnosis (51.3%), clinical conclusion (52.4%), and treatment recommendations (53.6%), while Claude performed best for wound sizing (72.0%) and urgency determination (70.9%). Gemini showed substantial limitations, with non-response in 67–68% across several assessment domains and the lowest clinical and safety performance. Overall, ChatGPT-4o most consistently generated accurate, structured, and clinically aligned wound assessments. However, the findings also highlight important limitations in reliability, safety, and real-world applicability, underscoring the need for further validation and explicit safeguards before routine clinical integration.

Introduction

Acute, chronic, and surgical wounds are common across emergency, inpatient, and outpatient settings and remain an important cause of morbidity, resource use, and prolonged follow-up1–3. Accurate visual assessment of these wounds is central to clinical decision-making. For acute traumatic and postoperative wounds, clinicians must rapidly identify signs of infection, dehiscence, or compromised perfusion. For chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers, and pressure injuries, subtle changes in tissue, exudate, or periwound skin can signal progression toward deeper infection or limb-threatening ischemia. These judgments are often based primarily on inspection of the wound surface, whether at the bedside or through clinical photographs.

Despite their importance, visual assessments of acute, chronic, and surgical wounds are subject to variability. Different clinicians may interpret the same wound image differently with respect to depth, tissue composition, infection, and urgency, and formal scoring systems are not applied consistently in routine care4. At the same time, the use of digital images in wound care has expanded. Photographs captured on smartphones or dedicated imaging systems are now frequently used to monitor wounds over time, to seek informal second opinions, and, in some settings, to support remote consultations. These trends raise the question of how computational tools might assist in interpreting wound images in a consistent and reproducible way.

Large language models (LLMs), particularly those with vision capabilities, have recently emerged as a candidate technology for this role. These models can accept both an image and a text prompt and return a detailed, human-readable description. Early studies in dermatology and radiology suggest that vision-capable LLMs can achieve competitive performance on selected image-based tasks and generate clinically oriented narratives5–7. As a result, some clinicians have begun to experiment with using general-purpose LLMs to comment on wound photographs or to cross-check their own impressions, even though these systems were not specifically developed or validated for wound assessment.

This pattern, informal clinical use preceding systematic evaluation, creates a clear need for careful, task-specific assessment. Different LLMs are trained on different data, employ distinct architectures and safety mechanisms, and may exhibit very different behaviors when confronted with complex or atypical wounds8. A model that performs well on one type of visual task may fail on another. In the context of wound care, misclassification or misinterpretation is not benign: underestimating the severity of an acute or surgical wound can delay urgent intervention, while overcalling severity in a stable chronic wound can lead to unnecessary escalation, anxiety, or resource use. Moreover, some models may respond conservatively by declining to answer, whereas others may produce confident but erroneous recommendations. Understanding these patterns is essential before such tools are relied upon in clinical workflows.

Accordingly, there is a need to move beyond anecdotal experience and systematically compare how different vision-capable LLMs interpret real-world wound images. Key questions include their accuracy in identifying wound type and key features across acute, chronic, and surgical cases; the appropriateness and safety of suggested management or urgency; and the clarity and usefulness of the information they provide if used to support patients or less experienced clinicians. Generating comparative data along these dimensions can help clinicians, institutions, and policymakers judge whether, and under what conditions, these models might be safely integrated into wound care, and where substantial room for improvement remains.

The present study was designed to address this gap. We evaluated and compared the performance of several advanced vision-capable LLMs in analyzing clinical images of acute, chronic, and surgical wounds. Using standardized prompts aligned with established wound assessment domains, we examined diagnostic accuracy, assessment quality, safety of recommendations, and informational characteristics of their outputs. Our aim is not to propose these systems as replacements for clinical judgment, but to provide a cautious, evidence-based appraisal of their performance, safety, and limitations as potential tools for wound image interpretation in future practice.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional retrospective study evaluated the performance of vision-capable large language models (LLMs) in interpreting wound images. The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (MU-MOU CoA No. 642/2024). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations, as well as the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, or from their legal guardians when applicable, for the acquisition and use of wound images for research purposes. All images were anonymized prior to analysis to protect participant privacy. All wound images used in this study were de-identified prior to analysis, with removal of any patient-identifiable features or metadata. The study protocol, as approved by the Institutional Review Board, explicitly included the use of de-identified clinical images for evaluation using publicly accessible multimodal large language model platforms operated by third-party providers.

A total of 1,200 wound images were collected between September 2024 and December 2024 from routine emergency, inpatient, and outpatient care. The dataset comprised three broad wound categories: acute wounds, chronic wounds, and surgical wounds. Acute wounds included traumatic wounds and burns presenting during emergency or early clinical care. Chronic wounds included non-healing wounds such as diabetic ulcers, pressure injuries, and other chronic ulcers encountered in routine practice. Surgical wounds included postoperative wounds assessed during follow-up after operative intervention. Representative examples of the three wound categories included in the study are shown in Fig. 1. These categories were used to ensure balanced representation of clinically distinct wound types with different visual and management characteristics.

Because this study involved repeated evaluation of each image across three LLM platforms, followed by structured multi-domain scoring and expert adjudication, a stratified random sample of 450 images was selected for detailed analysis, with equal representation of acute, chronic, and surgical wounds (150 images per category). This sampling approach was chosen to ensure balanced clinical coverage while maintaining feasibility for rigorous expert review across all study domains.

Expert consensus reference standards were established using a structured expert evaluation process. Three clinicians, including two board-certified plastic surgeons and one physician with dedicated wound care experience, independently reviewed all sampled images. A predefined scoring framework was developed prior to analysis, specifying key domains including wound type classification, identification of salient visual features, and overall clinical impression. All reviewers used the same standardized evaluation sheet to ensure consistency across assessments. Discrepancies were then resolved through structured consensus discussion to generate a single expert consensus reference standard for each image. This consensus-based approach was selected to prioritize clinically coherent interpretation for subsequent model comparison.

All LLM outputs were generated and evaluated retrospectively and had no role in patient management or clinical decision-making. All clinical care was delivered independently according to standard clinical practice, without access to or influence from model outputs.

Three commercially available multimodal large language model platforms were evaluated: ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA; 2024 release), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA; 2024 release), and Gemini Advanced 1.5 (Google DeepMind, Mountain View, CA, USA; 2024 release). Each system was accessed through its official subscription platform and evaluated under standard user-facing conditions. The same standardized prompt, requesting wound type identification, description of wound characteristics, a summary clinical impression, and an urgency recommendation, was used across all platforms).

All model evaluations were performed within a one-week interval in October 2024 to minimize the effect of platform updates during the study period. Each wound image was evaluated in a separate new chat session using the full standardized prompt and a single uploaded image, without preprocessing. No prior conversation history or case-specific context was carried over between images. All images were uploaded in the same manner across platforms, and outputs were recorded from the initial model response only. Retries, regenerations, and prompt modifications were not used in the primary analysis. No additional contextual information beyond the wound image and the predefined prompt was provided during evaluation. Because the systems were assessed through their official commercial interfaces, the comparison reflects the performance of commercially deployed multimodal AI platforms under real-world user conditions rather than a platform-independent comparison of underlying models alone (Fig. 2).

The BWAT and TIMES frameworks were used to standardize the descriptive assessment domains requested from each model. In contrast, expert adjudication of diagnostic accuracy, treatment appropriateness, urgency, and safety were anchored in standard wound-care references including Plastic Surgery by Neligan, wound bed preparation principles, and recognized wound infection and surgical site infection guidance9–12. These sources informed reviewer judgments regarding wound classification, tissue viability, infection-related features, wound chronicity, appropriateness of proposed management, and whether escalation or urgent intervention was warranted.

Model performance was evaluated using multiple scoring systems reflecting different dimensions of output quality. Clinical accuracy was assessed using a predefined, semi-quantitative scoring framework. For organ/location and wound-type classification, outputs were scored according to the degree of agreement with expert-defined reference criteria as follows: inaccurate (0–19% agreement), partially correct (20–49% agreement), partially correct (50–89% agreement), and accurate (> 90% agreement). Additional prespecified categories were used for responses that were too broad, non-sequitur, or non-responsive. In this study, the non-response category was used as an observed output classification at the user-interface level and was not assumed to represent a single underlying mechanism. Accordingly, non-response could reflect model inability, platform-level safety filtering, or safety-constrained abstention in the setting of limited contextual information.

For wound-size estimation, reference values were derived from clinically documented wound measurements obtained at the time of image acquisition, including ruler-based photographic documentation or recorded wound dimensions in the clinical record. These measurements were treated as the reference measurements for comparison. The evaluated models were required to estimate wound size based solely on the image input without access to scale references. A rule-based framework was then applied: estimates were considered accurate when deviation from the reference measurement was ≤ 20%, likely accurate when deviation was > 20% but < 50%, inaccurate when deviation exceeded 50%; outputs with no usable estimate were classified as non-response. These thresholds were defined a priori in the study scoring framework and applied consistently across all model outputs. This approach was intended to provide structured, clinically interpretable evaluation rather than purely subjective categorical judgment.

Informational quality was evaluated as a secondary dimension of model output using two validated patient-information instruments. The DISCERN instrument (Sect. 2) was used to assess the quality of treatment-related explanatory information generated by each model13. The Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool for Print (PEMAT-P) was used to generate two continuous percentage scores: an understandability score (PEMAT-U), reflecting how easily information can be processed, and an actionability score (PEMAT-A), indicating how clearly recommended next steps are communicated14. Readability was measured using the Flesch Reading Ease15, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level16, and total word count, which together characterize linguistic complexity, required reading level, and verbosity. These instruments were not used to assess the clinical validity of BWAT- or TIMES-based wound assessment itself, but rather to characterize the clarity, communicative quality, and potential patient-facing usability of the generated responses. These continuous PEMAT scores were used as inputs for latent profile analysis.

Variability in expert scoring of LLM-generated outputs may persist even when an expert consensus reference standard has been established. Latent class analysis (LCA) and latent profile analysis (LPA) were used to explore heterogeneity in model performance beyond aggregate summary statistics. Conventional metrics such as overall accuracy or mean scores may mask variability across individual cases, particularly in multimodal LLMs where performance can be inconsistent. LCA was applied to categorical outcomes (e.g., accuracy levels and DISCERN classifications) to identify latent performance groups, while LPA was applied to continuous PEMAT scores to characterize patterns in understandability and actionability. These approaches enabled identification of distinct performance profiles, such as consistently high-performing, variable, or failure-prone output patterns, which may have clinical implications for reliability and deployment. In both analyses, model selection was based on the Bayesian Information Criterion and clinical interpretability of the resulting classes. Group comparisons across LLMs were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical outcomes and the Friedman test for non-parametric repeated measures, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; 2024 release) and SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; 2019 release). Results

A total of 450 wound images were analyzed. All comparisons between large language models (LLMs) showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.001 for all domains). ChatGPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet consistently outperformed Gemini Advanced, although their relative strengths varied by task.

For diagnostic tasks, ChatGPT-4o and Claude achieved comparable correctness, with 51.3% and 53.8% of images, respectively, classified as accurate, whereas Gemini was accurate in only 9.3% of cases and showed non-response in 68.0% of images. For overall clinical conclusions, ChatGPT-4o yielded accurate outputs in 52.4% and mostly correct outputs in 40.0% of cases, while Claude showed a broader spread across accuracy levels (36.9% accurate, 28.7% mostly correct, and 34.4% partially correct). Gemini produced accurate or mostly correct conclusions in only 30.2% of images and showed non-response in nearly 70% of cases. Wound-type classification showed a similar pattern: ChatGPT-4o correctly identified wound type in 79.6% of images, Claude in 61.6%, and Gemini in 22.2%, with Gemini also showing partially correct outputs in 9.6% and non-response in 67.6% of cases. Framework-based assessments using BWAT and TIMES showed that both ChatGPT-4o and Claude generated almost exclusively accurate or mostly correct descriptions, whereas Gemini gave mostly incomplete or absent responses, with non-response rates of 88.2% for BWAT and 67.8% for TIMES.

In treatment-related recommendations, ChatGPT-4o again demonstrated the best performance, providing accurate advice in 53.6% and mostly correct advice in 41.3% of cases. Claude produced accurate or mostly correct treatment plans in 74.2% of cases but showed non-response in 25.8%. Gemini, in contrast, was accurate in only 5.8% and mostly correct in 26.4% of cases, with non-response in 67.8%. For urgency assessment, Claude slightly exceeded ChatGPT-4o in the proportion of accurate ratings (70.9% vs. 64.7%), but ChatGPT-4o generated fewer non-usable outputs; Gemini again showed limited performance, with 31.8% accurate and 68.2% non-response.

Wound-size estimation showed that Claude produced the highest proportion of estimates within 20% of expert values (72.0%), followed by ChatGPT-4o (62.2%), while Gemini produced accurate size estimates for only 29.6% and showed non-response in 69.8% of cases (Table 1). Safety evaluation highlighted the most striking differences: 94.9% of ChatGPT-4o outputs and 87.1% of Claude outputs were rated as not risky, whereas only 32.0% of Gemini outputs were considered safe, and 67.3% were classified as risky. Because reference values were derived from clinically measured wound dimensions, these results reflect deviation from real-world measurements rather than purely subjective expert estimates.

Informational quality, assessed using DISCERN, again favored ChatGPT-4o. Just over half of its outputs (52.9%) were rated fair and the remainder poor, with all responses being scorable. In contrast, most Claude outputs were rated poor (80.9%) and 19.1% were non-evaluable. Gemini produced scorable but poor information in only 32.2% of cases; in 67.8% of images, its output could not be evaluated because the model did not provide a relevant answer (Table 2).

Readability analyses demonstrated distinct differences in text complexity across models. ChatGPT-4o produced the longest and most detailed outputs, with a median word count of 465 words, and consistently generated graduate-level FKGL scores, reflecting its comprehensive descriptive style. Claude 3.5 Sonnet produced slightly shorter responses, with a median word count of 407 words, and displayed comparable FKGL complexity, indicating a similarly advanced reading level. In contrast, Gemini Advanced frequently produced extremely short responses, with a median of only 26 words, leaving insufficient text for meaningful FRE or FKGL assessment in many cases. Overall, the readability patterns indicated that ChatGPT-4o and Claude generated linguistically dense clinical narratives suitable for professional readers, whereas Gemini’s limited output volume restricted readability evaluation and reduced its practical utility in wound-care communication.

Latent profile analysis further characterized differences in output quality between models by identifying data-driven performance profiles across cases. For PEMAT-A, ChatGPT-4o showed relatively consistent moderate-to-high scoring profiles, whereas Claude demonstrated greater heterogeneity and Gemini showed a predominantly low-scoring pattern (Table 3). For PEMAT-U, ChatGPT-4o again showed relatively consistent high-scoring profiles, whereas Claude exhibited wider dispersion and Gemini showed a dominant very low-scoring subgroup despite smaller higher-scoring profiles (Table 4).

Discussion

This study compared the performance of three vision-capable large language models in interpreting acute, chronic, and surgical wound images. Across all domains, ChatGPT-4o demonstrated the most consistent performance, followed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet, whereas Gemini Advanced showed substantial limitations. These findings are consistent with prior evaluations of multimodal LLMs in medical imaging, where model-specific variability remains substantial even among advanced systems5–7,17,18.

ChatGPT-4o’s superior diagnostic accuracy and descriptive completeness suggest more effective visual–text integration compared with the other models, aligning with reports that newer LLMs can outperform earlier versions on structured image-based clinical tasks5,7. Claude’s moderate but heterogeneous performance reflects patterns previously observed in multimodal evaluations, where models produce high-quality outputs in some cases but fail to generalize consistently across diverse images19–22. Gemini’s high non-response rate parallels known failure modes reported in multimodal LLM studies, particularly in tasks requiring fine-grained morphological interpretation18.

Informational quality findings further support these distinctions. ChatGPT-4o’s favorable DISCERN and PEMAT-P scores are consistent with prior studies demonstrating its ability to generate coherent health-information text suitable for patient education14. DISCERN and PEMAT-P findings should also be interpreted within the scope for which these instruments were designed. In the present study, they were used to characterize the communicative and patient-facing quality of generated responses rather than the core clinical validity of wound assessment itself. Accordingly, these results should be understood as complementary to, rather than substitutes for, expert-based evaluation of diagnostic and management accuracy. Readability patterns observed in this study—graduate-level FKGL scores for ChatGPT-4o and Claude, and minimal evaluable text for Gemini—mirror earlier work showing that insufficient or overly brief LLM outputs reduce interpretability and clinical utility15,16. Readability and output-length findings should also be interpreted cautiously. Metrics such as word count, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, and Flesch Reading Ease are inherently sensitive to prompt phrasing, requested tone, and the implied target audience. Accordingly, these measures in the present study reflect output characteristics under the standardized prompting conditions used here rather than intrinsic or prompt-independent model advantages. These patterns are also consistent with prior research comparing AI- and expert-generated outputs in cleft lip and palate care, where large language models demonstrated substantial variation in diagnostic quality, readability, and treatment-recommendation clarity23. Taken together, the findings from both studies suggest that LLMs exhibit cross-domain behavioral signatures rather than purely task-specific performance characteristics, with certain models showing stable strengths in descriptive completeness while others demonstrate inconsistent or fragmentary outputs that may limit safe clinical adoption.

The use of latent modeling provides additional insight beyond conventional summary statistics. While mean accuracy or percentage correctness describes overall performance, it does not capture whether a model performs consistently across cases or alternates between high-quality outputs and failure modes. In clinical contexts, such variability is critical, as inconsistent performance may limit safe deployment even when average accuracy appears acceptable. By identifying distinct performance profiles, LCA and LPA allow differentiation between models that are consistently reliable and those that exhibit heterogeneous or failure-prone behavior. This distinction is particularly relevant in our findings, where ChatGPT-4o demonstrated relatively stable performance across latent groups, whereas other models showed marked variability or clustering into low-performance groups21,22.

An additional point requiring careful interpretation is the meaning of non-response. In medical contexts, the absence of a recommendation should not automatically be equated with uniformly poor performance. Depending on the platform and context, non-response may represent at least three different phenomena: inability to interpret the image adequately, platform-level safety filtering, or safety-constrained abstention when the system determines that the available information is insufficient for a reliable recommendation. From a patient-safety perspective, such abstention may in some cases be preferable to a confident but incorrect recommendation, as uncertainty communication and abstention are increasingly recognized as important safety behaviors in medical AI under conditions of uncertainty.

This consideration is particularly relevant for Gemini, which showed high non-response rates across several domains. However, regardless of mechanism, a system that frequently withholds clinically relevant output under standardized wound-assessment conditions has reduced practical utility for routine wound-image interpretation. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting observable platform behavior in real-world use, rather than as a pure measure of underlying model competence alone8,21,22,24.

Clinically, these findings underscore that even the best-performing LLMs require supervision. Prior investigations warn that LLMs may produce “plausible-but-incorrect” statements with potential for patient harm8,22. Claude’s inconsistency and Gemini’s failure patterns highlight the importance of model selection and oversight when integrating AI into wound-care workflows. None of the evaluated models can replace clinician judgment, especially for wounds requiring integration of systemic context or rapid escalation, consistent with broader evidence cautioning against unsupervised use of LLMs in clinical decision-making24–26.

This study has several important strengths. First, it directly compares multiple LLMs using a sizeable and clinically diverse set of real-world wound photographs spanning acute, chronic, and surgical wounds. This broad case mix improves the practical relevance of the findings and allows comparison across clinically distinct wound contexts rather than a single wound subtype. Second, all models were evaluated using a unified prompting strategy, which reduced prompt-related variability and enabled a more interpretable head-to-head comparison of model behavior. Third, model outputs were assessed against expert-derived reference standards using a structured evaluation framework that incorporated clinically relevant domains, including diagnosis, treatment recommendation, urgency, safety, readability, and patient-facing informational quality. This allowed the study to move beyond simple descriptive accuracy and examine multiple dimensions of clinical usefulness. Fourth, the use of validated informational-quality instruments, including DISCERN and PEMAT-P, strengthened the assessment of output quality from a patient-communication perspective. An additional methodological strength of this study is the controlled and standardized evaluation procedure used across platforms. Each wound image was assessed in a separate new chat session using the identical full prompt, with no carryover of prior conversational context between cases. All images were uploaded in the same manner across platforms, and only the initial model response was included in the primary analysis, without retries, regenerations, or prompt modification. This session-level isolation reduced the risk of memory-related contamination and strengthened the internal validity of the head-to-head comparison. Finally, the application of latent class and latent profile analyses added methodological depth by identifying underlying performance patterns and between-model heterogeneity that would not have been apparent from aggregate summary measures alone.

In contrast to purely image-based approximation, wound-size estimation in this study was anchored to clinically measured reference values obtained at the time of image acquisition. This allowed a more objective comparison between model-generated estimates and real-world measurements. However, the models themselves did not have access to calibration markers or scale information and were required to infer size directly from visual input. As such, the evaluation reflects the ability of multimodal LLMs to approximate wound dimensions under image-only conditions, rather than true measurement capability. Factors such as perspective distortion, camera distance, and image framing may still influence estimation accuracy.

This study also has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center and relied solely on static wound photographs, which may not capture dynamic or contextual factors relevant to real-world clinical assessment. Second, the evaluation was intentionally performed using images alone, without accompanying clinical history or contextual information. This design does not reflect real-world wound assessment, in which diagnosis, urgency, and management depend not only on visual appearance but also on symptom duration, mechanism of injury, comorbidities, examination findings, and progression over time. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as an evaluation of clinically oriented image-based wound interpretation under constrained conditions, rather than a simulation of fully contextualized clinical decision-making. This image-only design may also have underestimated model performance in scenarios where additional clinical context would normally be available. Third, the evaluation used single-turn prompting, whereas actual clinical or patient-facing use would often involve iterative clarification and the incorporation of additional contextual information. Fourth, although the sampled dataset was balanced and clinically diverse, only a subset of the full image repository was analyzed because each case required repeated model prompting and detailed expert scoring across multiple domains. Future studies using the full dataset or multicenter image collections would therefore be valuable to confirm generalizability across broader clinical settings.

In addition, although the scoring framework incorporated predefined percentage-based and deviation-based thresholds, some evaluated domains remained inherently interpretive and dependent on clinical judgment. Future work could complement expert-based evaluation with external quantitative image-analysis methods, such as wound area or color-feature quantification, for selected visual features. Finally, the development and validation of medical-domain LLMs in wound care remain constrained by the limited availability of openly accessible, high-quality clinical image datasets. Most surgical and clinical textbooks are protected by copyright, and high-fidelity wound images are rarely released as public datasets because of privacy regulations, PDPA restrictions, and institutional governance. Images that do appear online are often censored by automated safety filters or degraded in quality, reducing their utility for training and benchmarking. As a result, progress in domain-specific wound-assessment models may depend on expert-curated datasets, secure institutional data pipelines, or clinician-supervised fine-tuning to achieve reliable and contextually accurate performance. An additional limitation is that the evaluated systems represent commercially available multimodal LLM platforms from 2024, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as time-specific rather than as a benchmark of the enduring or current state of the art. Because commercial AI systems evolve rapidly through model updates, interface changes, and platform-level policy modifications, the comparative performance observed in this study may not remain stable over time.

Conclusion

ChatGPT-4o demonstrated the highest and most consistent performance across diagnostic, descriptive, and informational domains, whereas Claude 3.5 Sonnet showed moderate variability and Gemini Advanced produced substantially limited outputs. These findings highlight both the promise and the current constraints of general-purpose multimodal LLMs when applied to routine wound photographs. Importantly, this study does not evaluate specialized wound-imaging AI systems or comprehensive multimodal wound characterization, but rather the performance of image-prompted LLMs operating on standard 2D clinical images. Although such systems may support documentation and preliminary image-based interpretation, they cannot replace clinician judgment in contexts requiring nuanced synthesis, advanced imaging information, or urgent decision-making. Continued development of domain-specific models, alongside rigorous validation and safety oversight, will be essential for the responsible integration of AI into wound-care practice.
