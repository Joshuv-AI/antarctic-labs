Rapid identification of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii based on MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and machine learning
Authors: Meng Zhou, Liru Zhao, Dongbo Li, Zhongle Cheng¹
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) is a major pathogen in hospital-acquired infections, and rapid detection is critical for guiding antimicrobial therapy and infection control. Conventional antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) has a long turnaround time, limiting early clinical decision-making.MethodsWe developed a rapid CRAB prediction model by integrating routine clinical matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) data with machine learning. Background

Research background and clinical significance

Acinetobacter baumannii is a non-fermentative Gram-negative coccobacillus belonging to the family Moraxellaceae and the Acinetobacter calcoaceticus–baumannii (ACB) complex [1]. It is an important opportunistic pathogen associated with hospital-acquired infections, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs). A. baumannii can survive for prolonged periods in dry environments and readily spreads in high-risk healthcare settings, causing severe infections including pneumonia, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, and skin and soft tissue infections, especially in immunocompromised patients [2]. Owing to its intrinsic resistance to multiple antimicrobial agents and its highly adaptable acquired resistance mechanisms, A. baumannii has become a major global concern in antimicrobial resistance control. The rapid emergence of carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) has further intensified this challenge. Carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii is primarily associated with the dissemination of carbapenemase genes, especially blaOXA-23, blaOXA-51-like, blaOXA-24/40, and blaOXA-58, while metallo-β-lactamase genes such as blaNDM have also been increasingly reported worldwide [3].

Carbapenems have long been regarded as the last line of defense against infections caused by multidrug-resistant A. baumannii. However, the prevalence of CRAB has increased steadily worldwide in recent years [4], and the World Health Organization (WHO) has classified it as a “critical priority” pathogen [5]. CRAB infections are strongly associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and high mortality rates, posing serious challenges to both clinical management and infection control [6].

Currently, CRAB detection primarily relies on phenotypic AST or molecular biological methods. Phenotypic AST typically requires 18–48 h, making it difficult to guide early antimicrobial therapy [7]. Although molecular methods provide faster results, they are limited to known resistance genes and require expensive equipment and reagents, restricting their widespread adoption in primary laboratories [8]. Therefore, the development of a rapid, cost-effective, and widely applicable method for CRAB detection is of substantial clinical importance [9, 10].

Application of MALDI-TOF MS and machine learning in resistance prediction

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) has become a standard tool for bacterial identification in clinical microbiology laboratories. In recent years, increasing numbers of studies have attempted to predict antimicrobial resistance by combining MALDI-TOF MS spectra with machine learning methods [11, 12]. However, most existing studies adopt fixed-interval binning strategies for feature extraction, which may lead to the loss of critical discriminatory peaks. In addition, the lack of model interpretability in some approaches limits their clinical acceptance and biological interpretability.

To address these issues, we introduced a K-means clustering–based dynamic binning strategy during mass spectral preprocessing to better accommodate inter-strain variability in spectral distributions [13]. Furthermore, SHAP was employed to interpret model decision processes, aiming to improve predictive performance while identifying key m/z intervals and statistical features contributing to CRAB discrimination, thereby providing insights into the underlying discriminatory patterns of resistance prediction [14, 15].

Study design and isolate collection

This retrospective study included 301 A. baumannii isolates recovered from clinical specimens collected at a tertiary general hospital in Anhui Province, China, between February 2023 and March 2025. Isolates were classified into the CRAB group (n = 189) and the CSAB group (n = 112) according to imipenem susceptibility testing results. Specimen types included sputum, blood, urine, and other specimen types [16].

The cohort size was determined with reference to previously published MALDI-TOF MS–based antimicrobial resistance prediction studies using machine learning approaches with similar dataset scales [17, 18]. General methodological principles for clinical prediction model development were also considered during study design [19].

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Bacterial identification was performed using the Smart MS 5020 MALDI-TOF MS system (Zhuhai DL Biotech Co., Ltd., Zhuhai, China). Preliminary antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the VITEK 2 Compact automated microbiology system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of imipenem were further confirmed using the broth microdilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M07 guidelines for dilution antimicrobial susceptibility testing of aerobic bacteria [20]. Isolates were categorized as carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) or carbapenem-susceptible A. baumannii (CSAB) according to the imipenem susceptibility breakpoints defined in CLSI M100 (2023 edition) [21]. CRAB isolates were defined as imipenem MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL, whereas CSAB isolates were defined as imipenem MIC ≤ 2 µg/mL. Intermediate-susceptibility isolates (imipenem MIC = 4 µg/mL) were not included in the present study.

MALDI-TOF MS sample preparation and data acquisition

All isolates were cultured on blood agar plates at 35 °C for 18–24 h prior to MALDI-TOF MS analysis. Three independent target spots were prepared for each isolate. Spectral quality was evaluated using multiple objective parameters exported from the Smart MS 5020 system (Zhuhai DL Biotech, China), including the average signal-to-noise ratio (S/N), total peak count, mean peak resolution, and total peak area. Spectra with higher signal intensity, greater peak richness, and better peak resolution were considered to have superior spectral quality [22]. For each isolate, spectra acquired from three independent target spots were manually assessed according to these predefined quality-control criteria and overall spectral characteristics, and the two spectra with better overall quality were retained for downstream analysis to reduce variability caused by low-quality spectra. The two spectra from the same isolate were always kept together during train–test splitting. A total of 602 high-quality mass spectra were obtained, including 378 spectra from CRAB isolates and 224 from CSAB isolates [23].

Data preprocessing and peak processing

Mass spectral data were processed in a Python environment. Isolates were split into training and independent test sets at an 8:2 ratio using stratified sampling to preserve the original class distribution. No isolate contributed spectra to both datasets. This isolate-level partitioning prevented biological information leakage and ensured that the test set was not involved in any preprocessing or model training steps [24]. Training spectra underwent signal-to-noise filtering (S/N ≥ 2), baseline correction, peak detection, and intensity normalization. Only signals within the m/z range of 2,000–20,000 were retained. Peak alignment was performed using the DBSCAN algorithm to ensure feature consistency. Preprocessing parameters and selected features derived from the training set were subsequently applied to the test set to maintain pipeline consistency and prevent information leakage [25, 26]. Feature-selection procedures were uniformly performed on the entire training dataset prior to nested cross-validation. Nested cross-validation was subsequently applied within the training set for hyperparameter optimization. During cross-validation, spectra from the same isolate were always kept together within the same fold to prevent isolate-level information leakage. The held-out independent test set was used only for final performance evaluation.

Binning strategies and feature selection

Fixed binning and K-means clustering–based dynamic binning strategies were compared. Fixed binning divided the entire m/z range into equal-width intervals, whereas dynamic binning adaptively clustered peaks according to their m/z distributions, allowing better preservation of local spectral features [14]. To prevent information leakage and improve reproducibility, K-means-derived bin boundaries were learned exclusively from the training set and then fixed as a standardized template for transforming the independent test set. No recalibration or re-clustering was performed using test-set samples.

The number of bins and the maximum number of retained features for dynamic binning were optimized on the entire training dataset. Differential features were subsequently screened using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction, followed by dimensionality reduction using random forest feature-importance ranking and recursive feature elimination to obtain the optimal feature set [27]. Both binning-parameter optimization and feature-selection procedures were uniformly performed on the entire training dataset prior to nested cross-validation to maintain preprocessing consistency across model comparisons. Additional statistical features, including peak intensity mean, variance, and inter-peak ratios, were constructed to enrich feature representation.

Machine learning model construction and evaluation

Five machine learning models—logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—were constructed [17]. To address class imbalance, class weights inversely proportional to class frequencies were incorporated during the training of LR, SVM, RF, and LightGBM, allowing greater penalization of minority-class misclassification. Class weights were informed by the inverse of class frequencies in the training set and subsequently fine-tuned during model optimization, with higher weights assigned to the minority class (CSAB) to reduce model bias toward the majority class (CRAB) and improve balanced classification performance. This strategy has been widely applied for handling moderately imbalanced datasets in machine learning–based medical prediction studies [28, 29]. For XGBoost, class imbalance was handled via the built-in scale_pos_weight parameter, which adjusts the relative weight of the positive class.

A nested cross-validation framework was applied exclusively within the training set for hyperparameter optimization of the candidate machine-learning models [30]. Importantly, feature-selection procedures were completed prior to nested cross-validation using only the training dataset. All cross-validation procedures were restricted to the training set, while the independent test set was held out and used only for final performance evaluation. Final model performance was evaluated on the independent test set. Evaluation metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and ROC-AUC [11, 31]. To assess model robustness and statistical uncertainty, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for performance metrics were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations on the independent test set.

Model interpretability analysis

SHAP was applied to interpret the optimal machine learning model by quantifying the contribution of individual features to prediction outcomes. Key m/z intervals and statistical features (e.g., peak intensity mean, variance, and inter-peak ratios) associated with CRAB discrimination were identified, providing insight into the model’s decision-making process [24, 32].

Dynamic binning improves spectral feature representation

Under identical preprocessing and modeling pipelines, the dynamic binning strategy achieved a higher mean ROC-AUC (0.97) on the independent test set compared with fixed binning (0.91) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S1). Dynamic binning also demonstrated superior sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy across models, indicating improved spectral feature representation.

Joint optimization of bin number and feature dimensionality

Systematic parameter scanning was performed across combinations of bin numbers and maximum retained feature counts for all five models, and performance heatmaps were generated (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figure S2). Composite performance scores were calculated using weighted metrics: sensitivity (0.4), specificity (0.4), and ROC-AUC (0.2). Greater weights were assigned to sensitivity and specificity because, in the context of CRAB detection, sensitivity reflects the risk of failing to identify resistant isolates, whereas specificity reflects the risk of incorrectly classifying susceptible isolates as resistant. ROC-AUC was assigned a lower weight because it primarily reflects overall discriminatory performance rather than direct clinical misclassification consequences. The composite score was used as a pragmatic criterion for parameter optimization rather than as the sole indicator of final model performance. Optimal and stable performance was achieved with 650 bins and 50 retained features. Although the combination of 700 bins and 80 features yielded a similar composite score, its ROC-AUC was slightly lower. These results indicate that moderate feature compression enhances model generalizability and supports the selection of appropriate binning and feature dimensionality in high-dimensional spectral data.

Performance comparison of different machine learning models

Using the optimal feature set, five machine learning models were constructed and evaluated on the independent test set (Table 2). The LightGBM model achieved the best overall performance, with a ROC-AUC of 0.985 (95% CI: 0.918–0.992), sensitivity of 0.938, specificity of 0.950, accuracy of 0.942, F1 score of 0.945, and precision of 0.949. These results indicate strong discriminatory performance with reasonable robustness across the independent test set. Complete bootstrap-estimated 95% confidence intervals for all performance metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Consequently, LightGBM was selected for subsequent interpretability analysis. Although other models showed comparable performance for some metrics, overall performance—particularly ROC-AUC—was slightly inferior (Fig. 4). SHAP analysis reveals key spectral feature contributions

SHAP analysis revealed that several specific m/z intervals made substantial contributions to CRAB discrimination (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S2). Notably, multiple high-importance SHAP features were clustered within low-molecular-weight spectral regions, particularly around the 6–6.3 kDa and 8.9–9.1 kDa ranges. These discriminative features represent binned spectral intervals rather than discrete isolated m/z peaks. In addition to the spectral features, derived statistical descriptors—including peak intensity mean, variance, and inter-peak ratios—were incorporated into the SHAP analysis. The inclusion of these statistical features further enhanced the stability of model predictions on the independent test set. SHAP summary plots for other models are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Taken together, these results indicate that the classifier integrates multiple aspects of spectral patterns, encompassing both raw m/z signals and higher-level statistical features, to achieve robust CRAB classification.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated the integration of MALDI-TOF MS with machine learning for rapid identification of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB). Our results demonstrated that routinely acquired MALDI-TOF MS data can be leveraged through appropriate feature engineering and model construction to achieve high-accuracy CRAB prediction without additional experimental procedures [33].

When compared with previous studies utilizing MALDI-TOF MS–based resistance prediction, several investigations have reported high predictive performance for pathogens such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa using machine learning and deep learning–based approaches [13, 34, 35]. Relative to these studies, the distinguishing features of the present work include a focus on CRAB, a pathogen associated with a particularly high resistance burden in clinical practice; systematic optimization of binning strategies and feature dimensionality rather than reliance on fixed binning or raw intensity features alone; and the integration of SHAP-based interpretability analysis while maintaining high predictive performance. These methodological refinements may collectively contribute to the observed improvements in specificity and overall model robustness.

Central to these methodological refinements, the K-means clustering–based dynamic binning strategy improved overall predictive performance compared with conventional fixed binning, particularly specificity [36]. Adaptive binning better preserves discriminative information by accommodating the actual distribution of spectral peaks and reducing information dilution and peak misalignment [37]. Previous studies have similarly emphasized that optimization of spectral preprocessing and feature representation is critical for improving antimicrobial resistance prediction performance [38]. Optimization of bin numbers and feature dimensionality balanced predictive performance and model stability, avoiding overfitting while enhancing generalizability [39]. Although K-means-derived bin boundaries were learned from the training set and fixed for test-set transformation, their cross-batch stability was not quantitatively evaluated. Future multicenter studies should validate bin-boundary robustness in independent cohorts before applying the fixed template to new clinical samples. Feature-selection procedures were also performed at the training-set level prior to nested cross-validation rather than independently within each inner fold; although this strategy was adopted to maintain feature-selection consistency given the limited sample size and high-dimensional data structure, fold-wise feature selection may further reduce potential optimistic bias in future studies. Furthermore, although class weights were incorporated to mitigate the moderate class imbalance observed in the training set (189 CRAB versus 112 CSAB), alternative imbalance-handling strategies—including SMOTE oversampling, undersampling, and threshold optimization—were not systematically evaluated [28, 29]. We acknowledge that their systematic comparison may provide additional insight into model robustness and remains an important goal for future research.

The LightGBM model outperformed other algorithms in accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and ROC-AUC, likely due to its capacity to model nonlinear feature interactions and handle high-dimensional, sparse MALDI-TOF MS features [40, 41]. SHAP analysis further enhanced model interpretability by identifying key m/z intervals and statistical features contributing to CRAB discrimination [32]. In the present study, the most discriminative SHAP-associated features were concentrated within several low-molecular-weight spectral regions, particularly around the m/z ~ 6.1 kDa, ~ 5.3 kDa, ~ 9.1 kDa, and ~ 12.3 kDa ranges. Although exploratory UniProt-based protein matching was considered, we recognized a fundamental methodological limitation: our K-means clustering-based dynamic binning strategy generated binned m/z intervals (e.g., 6140.8–6153.3 Da, width ~ 12.5 Da) rather than discrete peaks. UniProt database queries within such intervals returned multiple candidate proteins, precluding definitive peak-to-protein assignment. Precise protein annotation in MALDI-TOF MS studies is generally more feasible when discriminative features correspond to isolated peaks with narrow mass ranges [14]. Therefore, we opted to interpret the SHAP-discriminative features as spectral-level patterns associated with CRAB classification rather than attempting ambiguous protein assignments. The biological significance of these spectral patterns remains to be clarified through high-resolution mass spectrometry or targeted proteomic approaches. Consequently, these SHAP findings represent spectral-level associations rather than validated molecular biomarkers.

Beyond predictive performance, the proposed framework may offer practical advantages in routine clinical microbiology workflows. Conventional phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) generally requires approximately 18–48 h for resistance determination following bacterial isolation [7]. PCR-based molecular assays can shorten detection time to several hours; however, these approaches typically require specialized reagents, dedicated instrumentation, and trained personnel [8]. In contrast, the present framework leverages MALDI-TOF MS spectra that are routinely generated during standard bacterial identification procedures. Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of combining MALDI-TOF MS with machine learning for rapid antimicrobial resistance prediction [12, 42]. Because the subsequent prediction process requires only computational analysis without additional wet-laboratory procedures or reagent consumption, the proposed framework may provide resistance information with limited additional operational burden. Although the operational advantages of the framework were discussed qualitatively, formal health-economic analyses were not performed. Future studies would be warranted to evaluate cost-effectiveness and further clarify the clinical utility of this framework in routine practice. A qualitative comparison of conventional phenotypic AST, PCR-based molecular assays, and the proposed MALDI-TOF MS plus machine learning framework is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Therefore, the proposed framework should currently be regarded primarily as an adjunctive screening or early warning tool rather than a direct replacement for conventional AST. Confirmatory phenotypic susceptibility testing remains necessary for definitive antimicrobial selection and clinical reporting. Nevertheless, by integrating machine learning–based resistance prediction into routine MALDI-TOF MS workflows, preliminary resistance risk assessment could be made available several hours to one day earlier than conventional phenotypic AST without substantially disrupting laboratory operations. This earlier availability of resistance-associated information may be particularly valuable for critically ill patients with suspected CRAB infection, especially in intensive care settings where delays in appropriate antimicrobial therapy are associated with poor outcomes [43]. Although culture-based isolation remains necessary, such early prediction may support more timely optimization of empirical antimicrobial therapy, facilitate earlier implementation of infection control precautions, and contribute to antimicrobial stewardship efforts by reducing unnecessary exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics while awaiting confirmatory susceptibility results [12].

Despite the favorable performance demonstrated, this study has several important limitations. This was a single-center retrospective study, and differences in regional strain epidemiology, resistance profiles, as well as MALDI-TOF MS instrument calibration and operating conditions may affect spectral feature distributions and model generalizability. Consequently, the applicability of the proposed framework to other hospitals, regions, or different MALDI-TOF MS platforms remains to be validated through larger multicenter studies [44]. In addition, molecular epidemiological analyses such as carbapenemase genotyping, MLST, or whole-genome sequencing were not performed in the current study; therefore, lineage confounding cannot be completely excluded, and MALDI-TOF MS spectral patterns may partially reflect clonal background–associated protein expression or phylogenetic relatedness among isolates [3]. The present framework should primarily be interpreted as a phenotype prediction model, and further molecular epidemiological validation will be required to clarify the biological basis of the identified spectral patterns. Interlaboratory standardization of MALDI-TOF MS acquisition protocols, spectral preprocessing pipelines, and model calibration strategies may represent important prerequisites for broader clinical implementation and external reproducibility. Prospective multicenter validation will be essential to determine the clinical utility of this framework across diverse epidemiological settings.

Overall, our dynamic binning–based MALDI-TOF MS feature extraction strategy is scalable, compatible with multiple machine learning models, and may be extended to rapid screening of other pathogens. By balancing predictive performance with interpretability, this framework provides a practical reference for leveraging MALDI-TOF MS data for early CRAB detection in clinical microbiology laboratories.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and systematically evaluated a MALDI-TOF MS–based machine learning framework for rapid prediction of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB). The incorporation of K-means clustering–based dynamic binning improved spectral feature representation and predictive performance compared with conventional fixed binning strategies, while SHAP analysis enhanced model interpretability by identifying key discriminatory features. The proposed framework achieved favorable predictive performance without requiring additional experimental procedures and warrants further multicenter external validation and interlaboratory evaluation before broader clinical implementation.
