Incidental Diagnosis of Hiatal Hernia Simulating a Left Atrial Mass: A Case Report
Authors: Rifah Anjum Samiha, Munim Hasan Choudhury, Zarin Tasnim Huda, Eva Newaz, Mizanur Hosen, Khaleda Parvin, Md. Deluwar Hussen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHiatal Hernia—one of the most frequently encountered gastrointestinal conditions that rarely mimics cardiac pathology on imaging. We present a case of an elderly female where a hiatal hernia appeared as a left atrial mass on transthoracic echocardiography, raising concern for a benign cardiac tumor, while chest radiography revealed a retrocardiac air‐fluid level suggestive of a hiatal hernia. Definitive diagnosis was established with contrast‐enhanced computed tomography of the chest. Our goal is to raise awareness of hiatal hernia as an important differential diagnosis when evaluating an apparent cardiac mass on imaging and to emphasize the value of different imaging modalities, especially chest radiography, in preventing misdiagnosis of this rare presentation in resource‐limited health care settings of developing countries like Bangladesh.

Introduction

Hiatal Hernia is defined as an abnormal protrusion of abdominal contents, most commonly the gastro‐esophageal junction and fundus of the stomach, through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm into the mediastinum [1].

Most patients remain asymptomatic and can be diagnosed incidentally, whereas only 9% of cases typically present with gastrointestinal symptoms like heartburn, regurgitation, dysphagia, and epigastric pain. Advanced and complicated cases may present with symptoms of extrinsic cardiac compression, such as chest pain, arrhythmia, dyspnea [1, 2].

Due to the close anatomical relation of the posterior wall of the left atrium and the esophagus, a Hiatal Hernia may cause extrinsic compression on the left atrium and present as an apparent Left Atrial mass on transthoracic echocardiography, a rare mimic that creates a diagnostic dilemma [3]. This can raise suspicion for serious differential diagnosis, such as primary or metastatic cardiac tumor, atrial thrombus, or vegetations, often requiring prompt investigation and intervention [4].

Imaging plays a pivotal role in the evaluation of an apparent left atrial mass [5]. Transthoracic echocardiography may reveal an echo‐dense, mass‐like structure posterior to the left atrium; however, it is not reliable enough to distinguish true intracardiac lesions from extracardiac structures with a mass‐like appearance [3, 6]. Diagnosis is even more challenging in resource‐limited settings where advanced imaging modalities such as transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are unavailable. In such environments, chest radiography can be valuable, potentially revealing a retrocardiac gas‐fluid level suggestive of a hiatal hernia [7]. Computed tomography of the chest provides precise anatomical delineation, allowing differentiation between a true intracardiac lesion and an extracardiac mass‐like structure, while confirming the definitive diagnosis of hiatal hernia [2]. Management varies from conservative therapy to surgical repair, depending on the severity of symptoms and the presence of complications [1].

We present a case of an elderly female with known cardiac disease who was initially suspected of having a benign cardiac tumor but was ultimately diagnosed with a hiatal hernia following chest radiography and computed tomography.

Case Presentation

An 80‐year‐old female was brought to the emergency department with the sudden onset of chest pain radiating to the epigastrium at rest, lasting less than an hour. The pain was associated with heartburn, regurgitation, and dyspnea. She had a known history of chronic heart failure managed with long‐term digitalis therapy—0.25 mg per day.

On physical examination, the patient appeared pale and restless. Her vitals were stable with blood pressure 130/80 mm of Hg, heart rate 80 beats per minute, temperature 98° Fahrenheit, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. On cardiovascular examination, a holosystolic murmur was audible at the apex. On pulmonary examination, the lungs were clear on percussion and auscultation. On abdominal examination, there was no visible or palpable mass; the upper abdomen was mildly tender, and bowel sounds were audible in all four quadrants.

Discussion

Hiatal hernia is a common clinical phenomenon in which abdominal contents enter the mediastinum through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm, but it rarely mimics an intracardiac mass on echocardiography [2, 8]. The prevalence of this condition increases with age, affecting approximately 55%–60% of individuals older than 50 years. Age‐related loss of flexibility and elasticity leads to muscular weakness, playing a significant role in the development of hiatal hernia [1]. A wide spectrum of symptomology may be observed in the patients, ranging from asymptomatic presentation to gastro‐cardiac manifestations. When the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) becomes lax, it predisposes to gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), presenting with heartburn, epigastric pain, regurgitation, and dysphagia [1]. In more advanced cases, compression to the heart may cause chest pain, shortness of breath, arrhythmia, or mimic ischemic heart disease [2]. We present such a case in which an 80‐year‐old woman presents to the emergency department with the sudden onset of chest pain radiating to the epigastrium at rest. She has a history of chronic heart failure and is on digitalis therapy—0.25 mg daily. Clinical examination reveals pallor, restlessness, and an audible holosystolic murmur at the apex, while other clinical findings, including vital signs, remain within normal limits. Electrocardiography reveals findings consistent with a digitalis effect, and cardiac enzymes remain within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiography reveals an echo dense mass‐like structure suggestive of a left atrial mass raising concern for an intra‐cardiac mass, potentially representing a benign primary cardiac tumor, thrombus, or vegetation [9]. A tumor appears to be a plausible diagnosis in this scenario, given the acute chest pain, presence of a murmur, and the mass as a possible contributing factor to chronic heart failure. Such misinterpretation leads to the planning of invasive cardiac evaluations or even unwarranted cardiac surgery [10]. However, as the patient reports gastro‐esophageal reflex disease like symptoms, subsequent imaging is pursued, which clarifies the diagnosis. Chest radiography in posteroanterior and lateral views demonstrates a retro‐cardiac gas‐fluid level suggestive of gastric herniation, an incidental finding (Figure 3A,B). To confirm whether the mass is of extracardiac or intracardiac origin and to obtain precise anatomical location, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography is performed, revealing a large hiatal hernia with intrathoracic migration of a portion of the stomach (Figure 4A,B). Surgery remains the definitive treatment to cure the condition and prevent further complications. The patient was managed conservatively with omeprazole 40 mg once daily and lifestyle modifications, resulting in symptomatic improvement. She was subsequently referred to a tertiary care center for further management. Therefore, this case emphasizes the crucial role of considering hiatal hernia as a key differential diagnosis when a cardiac mass is identified on echocardiography, even in patients with known cardiac disease. Existing literature highlights the importance of multimodality imaging [2, 5, 6, 11, 12, 13]. But, in resource‐limited hospitals in developing countries such as Bangladesh, where computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging may not be readily available, chest radiography should be routinely performed to exclude hiatal hernia, thereby preventing misdiagnosis, unnecessary invasive procedures, and therapeutic errors.

This case is limited by the inability to follow up with the patient after referral to a higher center. Additionally, we could not perform the upper gastrointestinal barium contrast study that determines the extent of gastrointestinal herniation [2]. Moreover, we could not perform contrast echocardiography to exclude communication between the heart chambers and the mass, nor could we use ingestion of a carbonated beverage during imaging to distinguish an extracardiac source from an intracardiac mass [2, 13, 14].

Conclusion

Although a hiatal hernia is common, it is a rare mimic of an echocardiographic left atrial space‐occupying lesion. This highlights the need to consider it as an important extracardiac differential diagnosis of a left atrial mass. When limited by the unavailability of computed tomography, diagnosis is challenging based on clinical information and transthoracic echocardiography alone; therefore, chest radiography should be routinely performed in suspected cases of left atrial mass, thus preventing misdiagnosis and avoiding unnecessary interventions.
