Midline transperitoneal infrapancreatic approach to the supraceliac aorta
Authors: Hassan Chamseddine, Mahmood Kabeil, Jane Chung, Jonathan Kwong, Kaitlyn Dunphy, Avkash Patel, Jae S Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurgical exposure of the paravisceral and supraceliac abdominal aorta through traditional anterior transperitoneal and lateral retroperitoneal approaches are limited by fragmented operative fields and restricted access to right-sided vasculature. We report the technique of a midline transperitoneal infrapancreatic approach to the supraceliac aorta in a 73-year-old man requiring transaortic paravisceral thrombectomy and aortobifemoral bypass grafting for lower-extremity lifestyle-limiting claudication secondary to extensive paravisceral and aortoiliofemoral atherosclerotic disease. Through a midline transperitoneal incision, the pancreas is reflected anterosuperiorly, and dissection is extended cephalad to obtain supraceliac aortic control, thus allowing uninterrupted contiguous exposure of the entire abdominal aorta within a single operative field. This exposure facilitates secure proximal control and complex aortic reconstruction without the need for medial visceral rotation. This technique demonstrates that extension of the infrapancreatic approach to the supraceliac aorta provides continuous aortic exposure while preserving right-sided vascular access and avoiding extensive visceral mobilization, representing a valuable strategy for the management of complex aortic pathology.Keywords: Supraceliac clamping, Infrapancreatic approach, Inframesocolic approach, Medial visceral rotation, Paravisceral aortic endarterectomy, Transaortic endarterectomy

Surgical exposure of the paravisceral and supraceliac abdominal aorta is traditionally achieved through either an anterior (midline transperitoneal) approach1, 2, 3 or a lateral (retroperitoneal or thoracoretroperitoneal) approach.4, 5, 6 The traditional midline transperitoneal approach to the supraceliac aorta via the lesser sac is easily performed and suitable for simple aortic cross clamping; however, it fragments the surgical field and obscures the paravisceral aorta, which can impair complex reconstructions. It also disrupts the surgical field when the clamp site needs to be inspected or adjusted. Conversely, the lateral approach and the midline transperitoneal medial visceral rotation (MVR) offer enhanced visibility and better access to the paravisceral aorta. However, both are fundamentally limited by inadequate access to the right-sided vasculature, including the right renal and iliac arteries, which may require additional exposure through a separate transperitoneal incision when optimal right-sided access is necessary.

Beyond anatomical constraints, the level of aortic cross-clamping plays a critical role in determining the systemic physiologic response. Supraceliac aortic cross-clamping (SCXC) induces marked hemodynamic disturbances, increased myocardial strain, and hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury.2 Accordingly, when SCXC is not absolutely necessary and adequate exposure of the right-sided vasculature is required, supramesenteric aortic cross-clamping can be performed safely and effectively through a midline transperitoneal infrapancreatic approach (Fig 1).2,3 Herein, we describe a technique in which this approach is extended to the supraceliac aorta, providing uninterrupted contiguous exposure of the entire abdominal aorta within a single operative field to facilitate open paravisceral aortic thrombectomy and aortobifemoral bypass grafting. Case description

A 73-year-old man presented with an 8-year history of bilateral lower-extremity lifestyle-limiting claudication at 50 yards of ambulation without rest pain or tissue loss, which significantly impaired his daily function and quality of life. He had undergone a femoral-femoral crossover bypass grafting 6 years earlier which had subsequently thrombosed, and percutaneous angioplasty of the right external and common femoral arteries at an outside facility 1 year earlier that also failed. His past medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, nonoxygen-dependent chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and previous tobacco use.

On physical examination, femoral pulses were absent bilaterally; Doppler assessments revealed monophasic signals in both the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibialis arteries bilaterally. He had no motor or sensory deficits in either lower extremity. Preoperative ankle-brachial and toe-brachial indices were 0.7 and 0.4 on the right and 0.5 and 0.4 on the left, respectively. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated significant paravisceral and infrarenal atherosclerotic disease, with severe stenosis of the bilateral iliac and common femoral arteries (Fig 2). Chronic mural thrombus and calcific disease were present at the level of the renal arteries (Fig 2, F) and superior mesenteric artery (SMA) (Fig 2, E), which prohibited supra-SMA aortic cross-clamping.

Given the extent of the disease, severity of symptoms, and the failure of previous interventions, a definitive open aortic reconstruction was deemed necessary. As the disease extended to the SMA, supra-SMA clamping was not feasible. After favorable cardiac-risk stratification, the decision was made to proceed with open paravisceral aortic thrombectomy and aortobifemoral bypass grafting, using supraceliac aortic control via a midline infrapancreatic exposure. The operative technique used for supraceliac aortic exposure is described in detail in the Technique section further.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged home in stable condition. At the 3-month follow-up visit, his claudication symptoms were completely resolved with palpable bilateral pedal pulses. Ankle-brachial and toe-brachial indices improved to 1.0 and 0.6 on the right and 0.8 and 0.6 on the left, respectively. Computed tomography angiography at 3 months demonstrated a disease-free paravisceral aorta and a widely patent aortobifemoral bypass graft (Fig 3). At the 7-month follow-up, the patient remains symptom free with unrestricted ambulation.

Technique

Dissection of the supraceliac aorta

A detailed step-by-step demonstration of the technique is presented in the accompanying Supplementary Video (online only).

A midline transperitoneal incision is made to enter the peritoneal cavity. An Omni-Tract retractor (Integra LifeSciences Corporation) is mounted to provide stable exposure. The transverse colon and greater omentum are retracted cephalad with splanchnic retractors positioned at 2 and 10 o'clock position to create a broad intramuscular working window. One or two renal vein retractors, depending on body habitus, are positioned at 12 o'clock position to provide supplementary cephalad retraction (Fig 4). The small bowel is packed into the right abdominal cavity (ie, no evisceration), and the viscera are protected with laparotomy pads. Standard techniques are used to access the infrarenal aorta, and dissection is extended cephalad to the level of the left renal vein (LRV). To minimize the risk of lymphatic leak and subsequent chylous ascites, para-aortic tissues are divided and ligated with silk ligatures rather than bipolar electrosurgical instruments. The LRV is fully mobilized by dividing the left adrenal and lumbar veins. The gonadal vein may also be divided as needed. The LRV is then retracted caudally, providing exposure of the pararenal aorta and the origins of both renal arteries, which are circumferentially dissected free.

Omni-Tract retractor setup demonstrating splanchnic retractors (denoted A) at 2 and 10 o'clock positions for cephalad retraction of the transverse colon and greater omentum, and a renal vein retractor (denoted B) at 12 o'clock position providing supplementary cephalad retraction.

Dissection is continued cephalad along the anterior surface of the suprarenal aorta at a 1 to 2 o'clock orientation toward the SMA. The dense autonomic ganglia and periaortic tissue along the anterolateral surface are divided. It is critical that the pancreas is manipulated minimally. It should not be mobilized; instead, it is retracted anterosuperiorly, in a stepwise fashion, using a renal vein retractor as the aortic dissection is progressed. This retraction is a dynamic process requiring frequent adjustment as exposure of the paravisceral aorta is gained, thus avoiding sudden undue tension on the gland. Unlike MVR, this maneuver leaves the pancreas, spleen, left kidney, and adrenal gland in situ, thereby reducing the risk of organ injury. The left crus of the diaphragm are divided with electrocautery, whereas the aorta is gently retracted to the right using a suction tip to protect it from thermal injury (Fig 5).

Division of the left crus of the diaphragm denoted by the yellow arrow.

Throughout this step, cognizance and identification of the SMA origin and its course by finger palpation or continuous wave Doppler is essential to avoid injury to the vessel. Once the SMA is isolated, the aortic dissection is carried proximally to the celiac axis. Again, full awareness of the celiac axis origin during this phase of the operation is critical to avoid injury to the vessel. The celiac axis is encircled at the origin and the supraceliac aorta dissected and prepared for clamp placement (Fig 6).

Paravisceral aortic thrombectomy and aortobifemoral bypass grafting

For the patient presented in this report, retroperitoneal tunnels were prepared between the aorta and the previously exposed femoral arteries bilaterally. Following systemic heparinization, control was obtained sequentially of the supraceliac aorta, celiac axis, superior mesenteric artery, renal arteries, and common iliac arteries, in that order. An aortotomy was then performed extending from the level of the celiac axis to the infrarenal aorta. Paravisceral aortic thrombectomy was carried out, and the aortotomy was primarily closed with a 5-0 polypropylene suture (Fig 7, A). The clamp was subsequently repositioned to the infrarenal aorta, where the aorta was transected and endarterectomized (Fig 7, B). A 14 × 7 mm woven polyester graft was then anastomosed with a 4-0 polypropylene suture (Fig 7, C). Bilateral common femoral endarterectomy and patch angioplasty were performed, and the 7 mm graft limbs were sewn in an end-to-side fashion on the right and end-to-end on the left using continuous 6-0 polypropylene sutures.

Discussion

The primary challenge in paravisceral aortic surgery remains the trade-off between reliable proximal control and adequate distal exposure. Although the traditional anterior access to the supraceliac aorta through the lesser sac is easily gained, it is fraught with a fragmented operative field and limited visualization of the paravisceral aorta. Under these circumstances, inspection or readjustment of the supraceliac clamp frequently compromises exposure of the pararenal/infrarenal aorta. The lateral approach, on the contrary, provides a contiguous view of the paravisceral segments; however, its inherent limitation in accessing the right-sided vasculature often complicates comprehensive aortic reconstructions, particularly those involving the right renal artery or right iliofemoral system.

The technique reported herein introduces a novel approach for accessing the supraceliac aorta. The safety and clinical utility of the midline transperitoneal infrapancreatic approach to the supramesenteric aorta have previously been established in comparison with traditional SCXC.2 Although technically more demanding, supramesenteric aortic cross-clamping via an infrapancreatic approach was associated with a 0% perioperative mortality and pancreatitis rate and a trend toward lower postoperative lactate levels compared with SCXC.2

Extension of this technique to the supraceliac level is a logical progression—using the same anatomical window to provide the unhindered, contiguous exposure of the entire abdominal aorta, thereby overcoming the limitations of the lateral approach or MVR. This operative continuity is critical during complex aortic procedures for both aneurysmal and occlusive diseases—such as transaortic endarterectomy and paravisceral aortic reconstructions—where simultaneous visualization of both the proximal clamp site and the distal reconstruction is paramount. Furthermore, this technique enables the surgeon to precisely tailor the level of clamping to the patient's specific disease burden, transitioning between supraceliac, supramesenteric and infrarenal control within a single operative filed to minimize hepatic, mesenteric, and renal ischemia.

The midline infrapancreatic approach also represents a significant technical refinement over the classic MVR described by Reilly et al.1 Traditional MVR requires extensive visceral mobilization, hence carrying a notable risk of splenic injury requiring splenectomy (reported up to 20%) and fatal pancreatitis (3%-5%), largely attributed to the traction required to rotate the foregut.1 In contrast, the present technique utilizes the infrapancreatic plane, in which the pancreas is retracted anterosuperiorly without the need for splenic or foregut mobilization. This gentle traction maneuver preserves the spleen in its anatomical position and avoids traction on the SMA, thereby mitigating key contributors to MVR-related morbidity. In our limited unreported experience with seven patients using this technique, no instances of mortality, pancreatitis or splenic injury have been observed (Fig 8). Similarly, in our previous experience obtaining supramesenteric aortic control through the same approach, no intraoperative mortality, splenic injury, or perioperative pancreatitis were observed.2

Conclusions

The extended midline transperitoneal infrapancreatic approach for supraceliac aortic exposure represents a technical evolution in the management of paravisceral aortic disease. It provides the necessary contiguous, uninterrupted surgical exposure of the entire abdominal aorta while potentially mitigating the risks of iatrogenic splenic and pancreatic injury associated with MVR.
