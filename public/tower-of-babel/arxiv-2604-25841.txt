Tight Bounds for some W[1]-hard Problems Parameterized by Multi-clique-width
Benjamin Bergougnoux, Vera Chekan, Stefan Kratsch

Abstract
In this work we contribute to the study of the fine-grained complexity of problems parameterized by multi-clique-width, which was initiated by Fürer [ITCS 2017] and pursued further by Chekan and Kratsch [MFCS 2023]. Multi-clique-width is a parameter defined analogously to clique-width but every vertex is allowed to hold multiple labels simultaneously. This parameter is upper-bounded by both clique-width and treewidth (plus a constant), hence it generalizes both of them without an exponential blow-up. Conversely, graphs of multi-clique-width k k have clique-width at most 2 k 2^{k} , and there exist graphs with clique-width at least 2 Ω ⁡ ( k ) 2^{\Omega(k)} . Thus, while the two parameters are functionally equivalent, the fine-grained complexity of problems may differ relative to them. As our first and main result we show that under ETH the Max Cut problem cannot be solved in time n 2 o ⁡ ( k ) ⋅ f ⁡ ( k ) n^{2^{o(k)}}\cdot f(k) on graphs of multi-clique-width k k for any computable function f f . For clique-width k k an n 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) n^{\mathcal{O}(k)} algorithm by Fomin et al. [SIAM J. Comput. 2014] is tight under ETH. This makes Max Cut the first known problem for which the tight running times differ for parameterization by clique-width and multi-clique-width and it contributes to the short list of known lower bounds of form n 2 o ⁡ ( k ) ⋅ f ⁡ ( k ) n^{2^{o(k)}}\cdot f(k) . As our second contribution we show that Hamiltonian Cycle and Edge Dominating Set can be solved in time n 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) n^{\mathcal{O}(k)} on graphs of multi-clique-width k k matching the tight running time for clique-width. These results answer three questions left open by Chekan and Kratsch [MFCS 2023].

Introduction

The framework of parameterized complexity allows a more detailed study of computational complexity by expressing problem complexity as a function of both input size
n
and one or more parameters. A problem with parameter

subject to hypotheses such as the (Strong) Exponential-Time Hypothesis (ETH and SETH).

(V_{1},V_{2})
of this gadget is associated with a multicolored set
X
of
G
; we say that
(V_{1},V_{2})

selects

X
on this gadget.
Our graph
G^{*}
has a copy of this selection gadget for each edge of
G
.
And these copies are connected in a path-like way such that every partition
(V_{1},V_{2})
of
G^{*}
with
\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\right\rvert=\mathsf{cut}
selects the same multicolored set in each selection gadget.
Moreover, each selection gadget associated with an edge
v_{i}w_{i}
is connected to a combination of five
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadgets forcing the selected multicolored set
X
to contain at most one vertex among
v_{i}
and
w_{i}
.

We start by describing how a single selection gadget works.
The function
\phi
is a bijection such that each of the independent sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{t}
of the graph
G=(U_{1}\cup\dots\cup U_{k^{\prime}},E)
corresponds to a set
S=\phi(i)
from
\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore, we start by adding a set
A_{S}
on
n
vertices forming a clique to the graph
G^{*}
.
In our reduction,
selecting
a vertex
u_{i}^{\gamma}\in U_{i}
into a multicolored set
X
of
G
transfers to placing precisely
\gamma
vertices of
A_{S}
to the side
V_{1}
of a partition
(V_{1},V_{2})
of
G^{*}
, i.e., having the equality
\left\lvert A_{S}\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=\gamma
.

However, the partitions of the clique
A_{S}
crossing the largest number of edges are the ones where precisely half (i.e.,
n/2
) of the vertices of
A_{S}
are in
V_{1}
.
But our aim, for now, is to allow selecting any vertex of
U_{i}
, i.e., to place any number of vertices of
A_{S}
into
V_{1}
and still potentially be able to extend it to an optimal partition of
G^{*}
.
To achieve this, we add a set
B_{S}
of
n
new vertices to
G^{*}
so that
A_{S}\cup B_{S}
forms a clique.
Now no matter what the partition of
A_{S}
is, it is possible to “compensate” it by partitioning
B_{S}
accordingly so that the clique
A_{S}\cup B_{S}
is crossed optimally.
Note that in this case
A_{S}
and
B_{S}
are partitioned in a
complementary
way, i.e.,
\left\lvert A_{S}\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=\left\lvert B_{S}\cap V_{2}\right\rvert
.

Next we ensure that the vertices selected in each of
U_{1},\dots,U_{k^{\prime}}
form an independent set in
G
.
For every edge
u_{i_{1}}^{\alpha}u_{i_{2}}^{\beta}
of
G
, we are allowed to select at most one vertex among the endpoints
u_{i_{1}}^{\alpha}
and
u_{i_{2}}^{\beta}
of this edge.
So in the graph
G^{*}
, we forbid that at the same time, exactly
\alpha
vertices of
A_{\phi(i_{1})}
and exactly
\beta
vertices of
A_{\phi(i_{2})}
are put into
V_{1}
.
In other words, at least one of the following four cases has to apply:

1.

at most
\alpha-1
vertices of
A_{\phi(i_{1})}
are put into
V_{1}
,

2.

or at least
\alpha+1
vertices of
A_{\phi(i_{1})}
are put into
V_{1}
,

3.

or at most
\beta-1
vertices of
A_{\phi(i_{2})}
are put into
V_{1}
,

4.

or at least
\beta+1
vertices of
A_{\phi(i_{2})}
are put into
V_{1}
.

We use an
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadget to implement a disjunction of those cases.
And to capture each of the cases, we also use a
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadget (cf.
\cref
lem:H-if-properties).
An
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadget enforces that at most a certain number of vertices are put into
V_{1}
.
We provide more details on the “at least”-cases later.
Now if we attach the gadgets for all edges of
G
(there may be
\Omega(n^{2})
of them) to the same selection gadget, we get a huge number of “neighborhoods” preventing an expression of low multi-clique-width.

Instead, as standard in lower-bound constructions of this flavor, we use copies of selection gadgets.
Namely, for every edge of
G
, we create a copy of
\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}A_{S}\cup B_{S}
and attach the corresponding five
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadgets to this copy.
It remains to ensure that an optimal partition of the final graph selects the same multicolored set on each the selection gadgets.
Let
m
be the number of edges.
So for every
j\in[m-1]
and every vertex of
B_{S}
in the
j
-th copy, we connect this vertex and its counterpart in the
j+1
-st copy of
A_{S}
by an
F^{\prime}
-gadget (cf.
\cref
lem:F-gadgets) to ensure that these vertices appear on different sides of the partition.
This ensures that any optimal partition
(V_{1},V_{2})
splits
j
-th copy of
B_{S}
and the
j+1
-st copy of
A_{S}
in a complementary way.
Since the
j
-th copies of
A_{S}
and
B_{S}
also need to be split this way, the partitions on the
j
-th copy of
A_{S}
and on the
j+1
-st copy of
A_{S}
coincide, and therefore the same selection is made on all selection gadgets.

As it is now, the multi-clique-width of the construction is very high.
In every copy there is an induced matching of size
k^{\prime}
in the bipartite subgraph with sides
\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}A_{S}
and
\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}B_{S}
: for every set
S\in\mathcal{S}
, it uses one edge between
A_{S}
and
B_{S}
.
Further, from every vertex in
B_{S}
, there is an
F^{\prime}
-gadget to its counterpart in the next copy of
A_{S}
.
And on the level of
cuts of the expression
, each
F^{\prime}
-gadget behaves similarly to an edge of an induced matching: it yields a private neighborhood disjoint from the neighborhoods of the other vertices.
So there is a long sequence of
cuts
with, alternatingly, large induced matchings and large numbers of
F^{\prime}
-gadgets.
To overcome this issue, we finally adapt every copy as follows.

First, we extend the whole construction from the family
\mathcal{S}
to the family
\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
.
And we add an
F^{\prime}
-gadget between every vertex of
A_{S}
and its counterpart in
A_{\overline{S}}
in the same copy to enforce complementary behavior.
Also, to ensure that
at least
a certain number of vertices of
A_{S}
are put into
V_{1}
as desired (cf.
\cref
item:at-least-u,item:at-least-v), we use a
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadget attached to
A_{\overline{S}}
and ensure that
at most
the same number of vertices of
A_{\overline{S}}
are in
V_{1}
.
Second, to decrease the size of an induced matching, in every copy of a selection gadget we make a clique of each set
A_{S}\cup B_{T}
satisfying
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
and
T\in(\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}})\setminus\{\overline{S}\}
.
Observe that because
\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}={[k]\choose{k/2}}
holds, for every set
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
, the only set
T\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
with
S\cap T=\emptyset
is precisely
T=\overline{S}
.
In other words, the set
A_{S}\cup B_{T}
does not become a clique only if
S
and
T
are disjoint.
This characterization will be useful to construct a multi-expression in a moment.
This way, the cut between
\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}A_{S}
and
\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}}B_{S}
of the same copy from a “thickened matching” becomes a “thickened anti-matching”, i.e., there are all edges except for the ones with one endpoint in
A_{S}
and the other in
B_{\overline{S}}
for
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
.

Crucially, we can now create the edges of the graph

in a multi-expression using

Essentially, every vertex of the set

or

or

, respectively.
And we join

with

for every

. Now there is a complete bipartite graph between
A_{S}
and
B_{T}
if and only if there is a value
q\in S\cap T
, i.e., if
T\neq\overline{S}
.
We also ensure that, vaguely speaking, the
F^{\prime}
-gadgets between the
j
-th copy of
B_{S}
and the
j+1
-st copy of
A_{S}
do not belong to the same
cut
of the expression.
Thus we can reuse a constant number of labels for all
F^{\prime}
-gadgets.
At most five
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadgets are attached to every copy and a constant number of reusable labels suffices to create those edges too.

3.3
Construction

The instance
(G^{*},\mathsf{cut})
of
Max Cut
is defined as follows.
Let
m=\left\lvert E\right\rvert
be the number of edges in
G
and let
E=\{v_{1}w_{1},\dots,v_{m}w_{m}\}
be those edges.
We construct the graph
G^{*}
as follows.
We start with two vertices
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
connected by an
F^{\prime}
-gadget.
And we add a vertex
d_{2}^{\prime}
and connect it to
d_{2}
by an
F
-gadget.
The aim of these gadgets is to ensure that in any optimal partition of
G^{*}
, the vertex
d_{1}
is on the one side and the vertices
d_{2}
and
d_{2}^{\prime}
are on the other side.
So the function of the vertices
d_{1},d_{2},d_{2}^{\prime}
is to be the reference vertices for the sides of a partition.
And we proceed as follows for every
j\in[m]
.

For every set
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
, we add the sets

\displaystyle A_{S}(j)=\big\{a^{i}_{S}(j)\,:\,i\in[n]\big\}

and

\displaystyle B_{S}(j)=\big\{b^{i}_{S}(j)\,:\,i\in[n]\big\}

of
n
new vertices each such that
A_{S}(j)
induces a clique and
B_{S}(j)
induces a clique.
For every set
S\in\mathcal{S}
and every index
i\in[n]
, we add an
F^{\prime}
-gadget between the vertices
a_{S}^{i}(j)
and
a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)
.
Also for all pairs
S,T\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
satisfying
T\neq\overline{S}
and all
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
, we add the edge
a_{S}^{i}(j)b_{T}^{i^{\prime}}(j)
.
And we define the sets

\displaystyle A(j)=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}A_{S}(j),

\displaystyle A=\bigcup_{j\in[m]}A(j),

\displaystyle B(j)=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}B_{S}(j),

\displaystyle B=\bigcup_{j\in[m]}B(j).

The pair
v_{j}w_{j}\in E
is an edge of
G
.
So let the indices
i_{1}(j)\neq i_{2}(j)\in[k^{\prime}]
and
\alpha(j),\beta(j)\in[n]_{0}
be such that
v_{j}=u_{i_{1}(j)}^{\alpha(j)}
and
w_{j}=u_{i_{2}(j)}^{\beta(j)}
.
Further let
S_{1}(j)=\phi\big(i_{1}(j)\big)\in\mathcal{S}
and
S_{2}(j)=\phi\big(i_{2}(j)\big)\in\mathcal{S}
be the sets from the family
\mathcal{S}
corresponding to the independent sets
U_{i_{1}(j)}
and
U_{i_{2}(j)}
containing the vertices
v_{j}
and
w_{j}
, respectively.
We add the set

\displaystyle Z(j)=

\displaystyle\left\{z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)\,:\,\alpha(j)\neq 0\right\}\cup\left\{z_{1}^{>\alpha(j)}(j)\,:\,\alpha(j)\neq n\right\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\left\{z_{2}^{<\beta(j)}(j)\,:\,\beta(j)\neq 0\right\}\cup\left\{z_{2}^{>\beta(j)}(j)\,:\,\beta(j)\neq n\right\}

of pairwise distinct new vertices.
Note that each of these four sets consists of at most one vertex: for example, the first set consists of a single vertex
z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)
if
\alpha(j)
is non-zero, and it is empty otherwise.
So the set
Z(j)
contains at most four vertices.
Also note that due to
n^{\prime}>1
, i.e.,
n>0
, the set
Z(j)
consists of at least two vertices.

We add the gadget
H^{\mathsf{if}}_{\left\lvert Z(j)\right\rvert-1,\left\lvert Z(j)\right\rvert}\big(Z(j),d_{2},d_{2}^{\prime}\big)
denoted by
H^{\mathsf{if}}\big(Z(j)\big)
.
Because the vertices
d_{2}
and
d_{2}^{\prime}
may be assumed to be on the same side of the partition of
G^{*}
as argued before, this gadget ensures that at most
\left\lvert Z(j)\right\rvert-1
out of
\left\lvert Z(j)\right\rvert
vertices of
Z(j)
are on the different side (cf.
\cref
lem:H-if-properties), i.e., at least one of them is on the same side as
d_{2}
.
Informally speaking, this employs the distinction of the cases how at least one of the endpoints
v_{j}
and
w_{j}
of the current edge may be not selected.
To handle these cases, we add the following gadgets:

•

If
z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)\in Z(j)
, add
H^{\mathsf{if}}_{\alpha(j)-1,n}\big(A_{S_{1}(j)}(j),d_{2},z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)\big)
denoted by
H^{\mathsf{if}}\big(z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)\big)
.

•

If
z_{1}^{>\alpha(j)}(j)\in Z(j)
, add
H^{\mathsf{if}}_{n-(\alpha(j)+1),n}\big(A_{\overline{S_{1}(j)}}(j),d_{2},z_{1}^{>\alpha(j)}(j)\big)
denoted by
H^{\mathsf{if}}\big(z_{1}^{>\alpha(j)}(j)\big)
.

•

If
z_{2}^{<\beta(j)}(j)\in Z(j)
, add
H^{\mathsf{if}}_{\beta(j)-1,n}\big(A_{S_{2}(j)}(j),d_{2},z_{2}^{<\beta(j)}(j)\big)
denoted by
H^{\mathsf{if}}\big(z_{2}^{<\beta(j)}(j)\big)
.

•

If
z_{2}^{>\beta(j)}(j)\in Z(j)
, add
H^{\mathsf{if}}_{n-(\beta(j)+1),n}\big(A_{\overline{S_{2}(j)}}(j),d_{2},z_{2}^{>\beta(j)}(j)\big)
denoted by
H^{\mathsf{if}}\big(z_{2}^{>\beta(j)}(j)\big)
.

To understand the function of these gadgets, imagine that the vertex
z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)
is on the same side as
d_{2}
.
Then the first gadget ensures that at most
\alpha(j)-1
vertices of
A_{S_{1}(j)}(j)
are placed on the different side.
In particular, this number is not equal to
\alpha(j)
and the vertex
v_{j}
is not selected as desired.
Further, if the vertex
z_{1}^{>\alpha(j)}(j)
is on the same side as
d_{2}
, then the second gadget ensures that at most
n-(\alpha(j)+1)
vertices of
A_{\overline{S_{1}(j)}}(j)
are on the different side.
And because the partitions of
A_{S_{1}(j)}(j)
and
A_{\overline{S_{1}(j)}}(j)
are “complementary” due to
F^{\prime}
-gadgets, at most
\alpha(j)+1
vertices of
A_{S_{1}(j)}(j)
are on the different side of
d_{2}
, i.e.,
v_{j}
is, again, not selected.
The last two gadgets have an analogous function for the endpoint
w_{j}
.

We define the following upper bound on the number of edges crossed by any partition of these new
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadgets:

\displaystyle\mathsf{cut}(j)=

\displaystyle\mathsf{opt}\big(H^{\mathsf{if}}_{\left\lvert Z(j)\right\rvert-1,\left\lvert Z(j)\right\rvert}\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\chi\Bigl(z_{1}^{<\alpha(j)}(j)\in Z(j)\Bigr)\cdot\mathsf{opt}\big(H^{\mathsf{if}}_{\alpha(j)-1,n}\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\chi\Bigl(z_{1}^{>\alpha(j)}(j)\in Z(j)\Bigr)\cdot\mathsf{opt}\big(H^{\mathsf{if}}_{n-(\alpha(j)+1),n}\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\chi\Bigl(z_{2}^{<\beta(j)}(j)\in Z(j)\Bigr)\cdot\mathsf{opt}\big(H^{\mathsf{if}}_{\beta(j)-1,n}\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\chi\Bigl(z_{2}^{>\beta(j)}(j)\in Z(j)\Bigr)\cdot\mathsf{opt}\big(H^{\mathsf{if}}_{n-(\beta(j)+1),n}\big)

(5)

where
\chi
is the characteristic function defined as

\chi\Bigl(x\in X\Bigr)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in X\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We will show that every optimal partition of the final graph
G^{*}
needs to cross exactly
\mathsf{cut}(j)
edges from these new
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadgets.

Finally, for every
j\in[m-1]
, every
i\in[n]
, and every
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
, we add the
F^{\prime}
-gadget between
b_{S}^{i}(j)
and
a_{S}^{i}(j+1)
.
This concludes the construction of the graph
G^{*}
. In the rest of this section, when we refer to a gadget of
G^{*}
, we ignore the
F^{\prime}
-gadgets of
G^{*}
that are included in
H^{\mathsf{if}}
-gadgets. This will allow us to state that an optimal solution must satisfy all gadgets of
G^{*}
.

We define the value
D
used in the definition of the gadgets as

D=m\cdot\Bigl(4k^{\prime}\cdot{n\choose 2}+2k^{\prime}\cdot(2k^{\prime}-1)\cdot n^{2}\Bigr)>1.

Observe that the value
D
is the number of edges of
G^{*}
not contained in any gadget and those are precisely the edges in the set
E_{G^{*}}[A\cup B]
.
First, there are cliques of form
A_{S}(j)
and
B_{S}(j)
with
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
and
j\in[m]
.
And second, there is a complete bipartite graph between
A_{S}(j)
and
B_{T}(j)
for every
S,T\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
with
T\neq\overline{S}
.
So we indeed have

D=\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(A,B)\right\rvert.

(6)

Also note that the values
D
and
C
(cf. (
1
)) are polynomial in
n^{\prime}=n+1
and
k^{\prime}
.
Later we will rely on this to bound the running time required for the construction of
G^{*}
.

We define the target value
\mathsf{cut}
of our
Max Cut
instance as

\mathsf{cut}=N\cdot\mathsf{opt}(F^{\prime})+\mathsf{opt}(F)+\bigl(\sum_{j\in[m]}\mathsf{cut}(j)\bigr)+m\cdot L

(7)

where

\displaystyle L_{1}=k^{\prime}\cdot(2k^{\prime}-2)\cdot n^{2}

\displaystyle L_{2}=2k^{\prime}\cdot n^{2}

\displaystyle L=L_{1}+L_{2}

\displaystyle N=1+m\cdot k^{\prime}\cdot n+(m-1)\cdot 2k^{\prime}\cdot n.

Observe that
N
is precisely the number of
F^{\prime}
-gadgets in
G^{*}
not contained in larger gadgets: one is between
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
, further
m\cdot k^{\prime}\cdot n
are between pairs of form
\big(a_{S}^{i}(j),a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)\big)
, and
(m-1)\cdot 2k^{\prime}\cdot n
more are between pairs of form
\big(b_{S}^{i}(j),a_{S}^{i}(j+1)\big)
.
The definition of
\mathsf{cut}
reflects
that we aim at partitioning every gadget (not contained in a larger gadget) optimally and crossing
m\cdot L
more edges.
As we show in the next lemma, for every
j\in[m]
, the value
L
is the maximum number of edges crossed in the subgraph
G^{*}\big[A(j)\cup B(j)\big]
by a hypothetical partition
(V_{1},V_{2})
that satisfies all the gadgets of
G^{*}
.
For this, we show show that
m\cdot L_{1}
edges are automatically crossed once the
F^{\prime}
-gadgets are satisfied, and crossing
m\cdot L_{2}
further edges is equivalent to partitioning every clique
A_{S}(j)\cup B_{S}(j)
optimally:

Lemma 11
.

Let
j\in[m]
and let
(V_{1},V_{2})
be a partition of
G^{*}\big[A(j)\cup B(j)\big]
satisfying all
F^{\prime}
-gadgets with both endpoints in
A(j)
.
And for every
S\in\mathcal{S}
, let
t_{S}=\left\lvert A_{S}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert
.
Then the following properties hold:

1.

We have
\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\right\rvert\leqslant L
,

2.

The equality
\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\right\rvert=L
applies if and only if for every set
S\in\mathcal{S}
we have
\left\lvert B_{S}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=n-t_{S}
and
\left\lvert B_{\overline{S}}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=t_{S}
.

Proof.

In this proof, by “all
F^{\prime}
-gadgets” we refer to all
F^{\prime}
-gadgets with both endpoints in
A(j)
.
Because
A_{S}(j)
consists of
n
vertices, we have
\left\lvert A_{S}(j)\cap V_{2}\right\rvert=n-t_{S}
for every
S\in\mathcal{S}
.
There is an
F^{\prime}
-gadget between
a_{S}^{i}(j)
and
a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)
for every
i\in[n]
.
An
F^{\prime}
-gadget forces its endpoints to be on different sides of the partition (cf.
\cref
lem:F-gadgets).
As all
F^{\prime}
-gadgets are satisfied, we have
\left\lvert A_{\overline{S}}(j)\cap V_{2}\right\rvert=t_{S}
and
\left\lvert A_{\overline{S}}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=n-t_{S}
.

We define the edge sets

\displaystyle E_{1}=

\displaystyle\big\{a_{S}^{i}(j)b_{T}^{\ell}(j)\,:\,i,\ell\in[n],S\in\mathcal{S},T\in(\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}})\setminus\{S,\overline{S}\}\big\}

\displaystyle\cup

\displaystyle\big\{a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)b_{T}^{\ell}(j)\,:\,i,\ell\in[n],S\in\mathcal{S},T\in(\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}})\setminus\{S,\overline{S}\}\big\}

and

E_{2}=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}}\big\{uv\,:\,u,v\in A_{S}(j)\cup B_{S}(j),u\neq v\big\}.

We claim that the sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
partition the set of edges of
G^{*}\big[A(j)\cup B(j)\big]
.
This follows from the following properties.
Let
S\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
be fixed.
The vertices in
A_{S}(j)
induce a clique and are non-adjacent to vertices in
A(j)\setminus A_{S}(j)
.
Further, there are no edges between
A_{S}(j)
and
B_{\overline{S}}(j)
.
And the set
A_{S}(j)\cup B_{S}(j)
forms a clique.
Finally, for every set
T\in\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}
with
T\notin\{S,\overline{S}\}
, there is a complete bipartite graph between
A_{S}(j)
and
B_{T}(j)
.
So now to analyze the size of
E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})
depending on
(V_{1},V_{2})
, we consider the edges in
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
separately.

We claim that the partition
(V_{1},V_{2})
crosses exactly
L_{1}
edges of
E_{1}
.
Consider arbitrary sets
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
T\in(\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}})\setminus\{S,\overline{S}\}
and integers
i,\ell\in[n]
.
Since
(V_{1},V_{2})
satisfies
F^{\prime}
-gadgets we have

\left\lvert\{a_{S}^{i}(j),a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)\}\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\{a_{S}^{i}(j),a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)\}\cap V_{2}\right\rvert=1.

Therefore, precisely one of the edges
a_{S}^{i}(j)b_{T}^{\ell}(j)
and
a_{\overline{S}}^{i}(j)b_{T}^{\ell}(j)
belongs to
E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})
, no matter what the side of the vertex
b_{T}^{\ell}(j)
is.
With
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\right\rvert=k^{\prime}
, we get

\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\cap E_{1}\right\rvert=k^{\prime}\cdot(2k^{\prime}-2)\cdot n^{2}=L_{1}.

We also claim that
(V_{1},V_{2})
crosses at most
L_{2}
edges of
E_{2}
.
The subgraph
G^{*}[E_{2}]
is a vertex-disjoint union of
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}\right\rvert=2k^{\prime}
cliques on
2n
vertices each.
For a clique of size
2n
, optimal partitions are precisely those which put exactly
n
vertices on each side, and such partitions cross
n^{2}
edges of the clique.
Thus, we have
\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\cap E_{2}\right\rvert\leqslant 2k^{\prime}\cdot n^{2}=L_{2}
and that implies the first claim.

Moreover, we have
\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\right\rvert=L
if and only if every such clique is crossed optimally, i.e., for every
S\in\mathcal{S}
, the equalities

\displaystyle\left\lvert\big(A_{S}(j)\cup B_{S}(j)\big)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=n

and

\displaystyle\left\lvert\big(A_{\overline{S}}(j)\cup B_{\overline{S}}(j)\big)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=n

are satisfied.
Recall that
\left\lvert A_{S}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=t_{S}
and
\left\lvert A_{\overline{S}}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=n-t_{S}
hold.
We conclude that
\left\lvert E_{G^{*}}(V_{1},V_{2})\right\rvert=L
is equivalent to having
\left\lvert B_{S}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=n-t_{S}
and
\left\lvert B_{\overline{S}}(j)\cap V_{1}\right\rvert=t_{S}
concluding the proof of the second claim.
∎

Now we bound the running time of the process.

and

.
The graph

and the value

can be computed in the same time along this process.
∎

Now we are finally ready to prove

4
Algorithm for Hamiltonian Cycle

A
Hamiltonian cycle
in a graph
G
is a cycle visiting each vertex of
G
precisely once.
In the
Hamiltonian Cycle
problem, given a graph
G
, we are asked whether
G
contains a Hamiltonian cycle.

In this section, we show that
Hamiltonian Cycle
can be solved in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
on graphs provided with multi-
k
-expressions.
The crux behind the algorithm can be sketched as follows.
A partial solution of
Hamiltonian Cycle
is usually a path packing of a subgraph created in the expression so far.
Next, every vertex is incident with precisely two edges in any Hamiltonian cycle of the final graph
G
.
So for every endpoint of a path in such a path packing, it suffices to guess one label “responsible” for the creation of the missing incident edge of the sought Hamiltonian cycle later.
Here, for a path consisting of a single vertex, we treat this vertex as two independent endpoints.
With this idea in hand, we are able to adapt the algorithm by Bergougnoux et al.
[
1
]
working on clique-expressions to multi-expressions.

Let us remind the reader that clique-expressions are essentially multi-expressions where no vertex holds multiple labels simultaneously.
To solve the problem, Bergougnoux et al.
[
1
]
study the unique auxiliary multigraph of a partial solution, i.e., a path packing of the current graph.
In this multigraph, the vertex set is equal to the label set
[k]
of the provided clique-expression. And for every path, say with endpoints
u
and
v
, of the path packing, there is one edge in this multigraph between the labels of
u
and
v
—this edge may, in general, be a loop.
Even though the number of possible auxiliary multigraphs can be as large as
n^{\Theta(k^{2})}
—indeed, for every pair of labels the multiplicity of the corresponding edge can vary between
0
and
n
—Bergougnoux et al.
[
1
]
apply the technique of representative sets to argue that what really matters about such an auxiliary multigraph is its degree sequence, i.e., the degree of every label, and the partition of labels into connected components.
This reduces the number of relevant partial solutions to be at most
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}=n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.

In our algorithm for multi-
k
-expressions, every path packing now yields, in general, multiple auxiliary graphs.
Namely, for every path and each of its endpoints, any of its up to
k
labels can potentially be later used at some join-node to create the missing incident edge.
Here, for a path on exactly one vertex, we treat this vertex as two endpoints and thus also get two labels for this path.
Crucially, the number of types of auxiliary multigraphs based on their degree sequences and connected components remains bounded by
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.
In the remainder of this section, we work out the details of this idea and prove the correctness.
Let us remark for clarification that, as Bergougnoux et al.
[
1
]
, we first focus on solving the
Hamiltonian Path
problem, i.e., finding a path visiting every vertex exactly once, and in the very end, show how
Hamiltonian Cycle
reduces to it.
We rely on the notation introduced by Bergougnoux et al.
[
1
]
as much as possible.
To simplify the following definitions, from now on the graph
G
on
n
vertices and a multi-

-expression of
G
are fixed.
In this section, all multigraphs have vertex set
[k]
.
Two multigraphs

are

Lemma 21

Now suppose that we want to add an edge between a vertex containing label
i
and another vertex containing label

On the level of auxiliary graphs, this corresponds to taking two distinct edges, one with endpoints

At a join-node, at most

However, applying the described “combine-operation”

To avoid this, after every single application of this combination, we will apply the operation
{\mathsf{reduce}}

In the following lemma, we will show that this approach maintains representation.
But first, we set up the required notation.

Let

be two labels.
For a red multigraph

, we define the family

of red multigraphs in two steps as follows:

1.

First, the family
A+\{i,j\}
contains
A
as a subset.

2.

Second, let

be two labels and let
\big(e,\{a,i\}\big)
and
\big(e^{\prime},\{b,j\}\big)
be two distinct, i.e., satisfying

, edges of
A
.
Then the multigraph obtained from

by removing the edges

and adding a new edge

.

Let us emphasize that we allow any of

and

to be a loop.
And we also allow

and

to have the same set of endpoints: in this case, both edges have endpoints

and

is thus at least two.
It is important that

are distinct: this way, we remove two edges and replace them with a new one.
For the joining sketched above, this corresponds to the fact that we never connect two endpoints of the same path (i.e., “close a cycle too early”) but rather make a longer path consisting of two old paths and one new edge.

And for a family

of red multigraphs, we define the family

simply as

\mathcal{A}+\{i,j\}=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}A+\{i,j\}.

We also define the family

and for a positive integer

\mathcal{A}+q\cdot\{i,j\}=\Bigl(\mathcal{A}+(q-1)\cdot\{i,j\}\Bigr)+\{i,j\}.

This reflects starting with the family

and applying the operation
+\{i,j\}

q

n^{\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime})}
.
For this, it suffices to show that for every
q\in[n-1]
, the family
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q}
can be computed from
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}
in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime})}
.
So let
q\in[n-1]
be fixed.
First, consider an arbitrary multigraph
A\in\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}
.
Because no path packing of
x
contains more than
n
paths, the multigraph
A
contains at most
n
edges.
Thus, we can construct the family
A+\{i,j\}
by considering at most
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
pairs of distinct edges of
A
, checking whether one of them has an endpoint in
i
and the other has an endpoint is
j
, and in the affirmative case replacing the two edges by a corresponding single one.
For a fixed multigraph
A\in\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}
, this can trivially be done in time polynomial in
n
.
In particular, the size of
A+\{i,j\}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
.
Doing so for every
A\in\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}
, we obtain the family
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}+\{i,j\}
of size
\mathcal{O}\big(\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}\right\rvert\cdot n^{2}\big)\in n^{\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime})}
that can be computed in time
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}\right\rvert\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.
Now the family
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q}
is computed by applying
{\mathsf{reduce}}
to
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}+\{i,j\}
.
By
\cref
lem:size-of-repr, the family
\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q}
is computed in time
\mathcal{O}\big(\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{x}^{q-1}\right\rvert\cdot n^{2}\cdot n\cdot(k^{\prime})^{2}\cdot\log(n\cdot k^{\prime})\big)\in n^{\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime})}
as claimed.

The number of nodes in the multi-
k^{\prime}
-expression of
G
is polynomial in
n
as argued in the beginning.
The above arguments imply that on each node of the expression we spend time
n^{\mathcal{O}(k^{\prime})}=n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.
Altogether, for the root-node
r
of the constructed multi-
k^{\prime}
-expression, we construct the desired family
\mathcal{A}_{r}
in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.
Repeating the process for every edge
uv
of
G
increases the running time by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
concluding the proof.
∎

Let

5
Algorithm for Edge Dominating Set

For a graph
G
, a set
Q\subseteq E(G)
of edges is called an
edge dominating set
of
G
if the induced subgraph
G\big[V(G)\setminus V(Q)\big]
is edgeless, i.e., every edge of
G
shares at least one endpoint with some edge in
Q
, i.e., the set
V(Q)
of endpoints of
Q
is a vertex cover of
G
.
In this section, we show how provided a multi-
k
-expression creating a graph
G
on
n
vertices and an integer
\mathsf{size}
, in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
we can decide whether
G
admits an edge dominating set of size at most
\mathsf{size}
, i.e., solve the
Edge Dominating Set
problem.
To simplify the following definitions, from now on the graph
G
on
n
nodes and a multi-
k
-expression of
G
are fixed.
We will rely on the following folklore equivalent formulation of the problem.
We provide a proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 31
.

A graph
G
admits an edge dominating set of size at most
\mathsf{size}
if and only if there exists a pair
(S,M)
with the following properties:

1.

the set
S
is a vertex cover of
G
,

2.

ths set
M
is a matching in
G
satisfying
V(M)\subseteq S
,

3.

and we have
\left\lvert M\right\rvert+\left\lvert S\setminus V(M)\right\rvert\leqslant\mathsf{size}
.

Figure 8:
(a) An orange edge dominating set of a graph. (b) A purple minimal edge dominating set
Q
. (c) Green vertex cover
V(Q)
and green matching
M
with one edge per connected component of
G[Q]
satisfy
\left\lvert Q\right\rvert=\left\lvert V(Q)\right\rvert-\left\lvert M\right\rvert
.

Proof.

First, let
Q
be an edge dominating set of
G
with
\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\leqslant\mathsf{size}
(see
\cref
fig:eds-equivalence for an illustration).
We may assume that
Q
is an inclusion-minimal edge dominating set of
G
.
Then the subgraph
G[Q]
induced by
Q
forms a vertex-disjoint union of stars on at least two vertices each.
Indeed, if this subgraph contains a simple path of length three, say on vertices
u
,
v
,
w
,
y
, then the set
Q\setminus\{vw\}
is still an edge dominating set; and if it contains a triangle, say on vertices
u
,
v
,
w
, then the set
Q\setminus\{vw\}
is still an edge dominating set contradicting the minimality in both cases.
So let
C
denote the set of connected components of the graph
G[Q]
.
Further, let
S=V(Q)
be the set of endpoints of the edges in
Q
and let
M
be a set containing one edge from every connected component in
C
.
Then
S
is a vertex cover of
G
and
M
is a matching whose endpoints all belong to
S
.
We have

\left\lvert M\right\rvert+\left\lvert S\setminus V(M)\right\rvert=\left\lvert M\right\rvert+\big(\left\lvert S\right\rvert-2\left\lvert M\right\rvert\big)=\left\lvert S\right\rvert-\left\lvert M\right\rvert=\left\lvert S\right\rvert-\left\lvert C\right\rvert=\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\leqslant\mathsf{size}.

Here, the penultimate equality holds since the forest
G[Q]
with
\left\lvert S\right\rvert
vertices and
\left\lvert C\right\rvert
connected components contains
\left\lvert S\right\rvert-\left\lvert C\right\rvert
edges.
This proves one direction of the claim.

Now let
(S,M)
be a pair satisfying the three items of the lemma.
We may assume that
S
contains no vertex isolated in
G
as such vertices can be discarded from
S
without violating any of the three properties.
For every vertex
v\in S\setminus V(M)
, choose an arbitrary edge
e_{v}\in E(G)
incident with
v
.
We define the set
Q
as

Q=M\cup\big\{e_{v}\,:\,v\in S\setminus V(M)\big\}.

First, the set
Q
is an edge dominating set of
G
because the set
V(Q)
of its endpoints contains the vertex cover
S
as a subset.
Second, we have

\left\lvert Q\right\rvert=\left\lvert M\right\rvert+\left\lvert\big\{e_{v}\,:\,v\in S\setminus V(M)\big\}\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert M\right\rvert+\left\lvert S\setminus V(M)\right\rvert\leqslant\mathsf{size}

where the last equality holds by Item
3
concluding the proof of the other direction.
∎

Relying on this equivalence, we now define the partial solutions.
For a node
x
of the expression, a
partial solution
of
x
is a pair
(S,M)
such that
S
is a vertex cover of the graph
G_{x}
and
M
is a matching of
G_{x}
satisfying
V(M)\subseteq S
.

The crucial idea behind bounding the number of relevant footprints (defined in a moment) is that for every vertex in the set
S\setminus V(M)
, it suffices to “guess” one label that will later lead to the creation of an incident edge added to
M
(if such an edge will exist at all).
We formalize this idea using label choices.
A
label choice
for the partial solution
(S,M)
of
x
is a mapping
\phi\colon S\setminus V(M)\to[k]
such that
\phi(v)\in\lambda_{x}(v)
for every vertex
v\in S\setminus V(M)
.
Thus,
\phi
maps every vertex of
S
“not matched” by
M
to one of its labels.
Note that if
S\setminus V(M)
contains a vertex whose label set is empty in
\lambda_{x}
, then no label choice for
(S,M)
exists.

For a partial solution
(S,M)
of
x
and a label choice
\phi
of
(S,M)
, the
footprint
(in
x
) is the triple
(I,\psi,\ell)
such that the following properties apply.

1.

The set
I\subseteq[k]
with
I=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{x}(v)
remembers all labels of vertices in
V_{x}\setminus S
. It is used to ensure at join-nodes that
V_{x}\setminus S
remains an independent set.

2.

The mapping
\psi\colon[k]\to[n]_{0}
with
\psi(i)=\left\lvert\phi^{-1}(i)\right\rvert
for all
i\in[k]
remembers how many vertices are mapped to each label from
[k]
.
First,
\psi
is used to create matching edges at join-nodes and second, it is relevant to keep track of the size of the corresponding edge dominating set.

3.

And the value
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
\left\lvert M\right\rvert=\ell
remembers the cardinality of the matching.

Note that even though a partial solution may have multiple footprints, the footprint becomes unique once we fix the label choice.
The set
\mathcal{S}_{x}
of
footprints of
x
is defined as the set of footprints of all partial solutions of
x
and all their label choices.
Our algorithm will then follow from the following five lemmas which provide the computation of footprints for every type of nodes.
For a leaf-node, the arising graph consists of a single node so we can compute the footprints directly.

Lemma 32
.

Let
(G_{x},\lambda_{x})=v\langle i\rangle
for some vertex
v
and some label
i\in[k]
.
Then there are precisely two footprints of the node
x
, namely
\mathcal{S}_{x}=\Bigl\{\big(\emptyset,\psi_{1},0\big),\big(\{i\},\psi_{2},0\big)\Bigr\}
with

1.

\psi_{1}(i)=1
and
\psi_{1}(j)=0
for all
j\neq i\in[k]
,

2.

and
\psi_{2}(j)=0
for all
j\in[k]
.

Proof.

The graph
G_{x}
contains only one vertex whose label set is
\{i\}
and it contains no edges.
Thus, there are precisely two partial solutions of
x
.
First, it is
\big(\{v\},\emptyset\big)
with the unique label choice mapping
v
to
i
.
Since
V_{x}=\{v\}
, there are no vertices in
V_{x}\setminus\{v\}
.
Thus, the only footprint of this partial solution is
(\emptyset,\psi_{1},0)
.
And second, the partial solution
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
whose unique label choice is the empty mapping.
The only vertex in
V_{x}\setminus\emptyset
is
v
.
So the only footprint of this partial solution is
\big(\{i\},\psi_{2},0)
.
∎

When a node of the expression forgets a label
i
, the label choices mapping some vertex to
i
are “invalidated” so we discard the corresponding footprints.

Lemma 33
.

Let
(G_{x},\lambda_{x})=\rho_{i\to\emptyset}(G_{y},\lambda_{y})
for some label
i\in[k]
and a multi-
k
-labeled graph
(G_{y},\lambda_{y})
.
Then the footprints of the node
x
are given as follows:

\mathcal{S}_{x}=\Bigl\{\big(I\setminus\{i\},\psi,\ell\big)\,:\,(I,\psi,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}_{y},\psi(i)=0\Bigr\}.

Proof.

The unlabeled graphs
G_{x}
and
G_{y}
are equal and only the label sets differ so that we have
\lambda_{x}(v)=\lambda_{y}(v)\setminus\{i\}
for every vertex
v\in V_{x}=V_{y}
.
Hence, the sets of partial solutions of
x
and
y
are equal and only the label choices of those partial solutions differ.

So let first
(I,\psi,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
be a footprint of
y
satisfying
\psi(i)=0
.
Then there exist a partial solution
(S,M)
and a label choice
\phi
of
y
with this footprint.
In particular, we then have
\phi^{-1}(i)=\emptyset
.
The pair
(S,M)
is a partial solution of
x
as well.
Further, for every vertex
v\in S\setminus V(M)
, the properties
\phi(v)\in\lambda_{y}(v)
and
\phi(v)\neq i
imply that we also have
\phi(v)\in\lambda_{x}(v)
.
So the mapping
\phi
is also a label choice of
(S,M)
in
x
.
Finally, we have

\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{x}(v)=\Bigl(\bigcup_{v\in V_{y}\setminus S}\lambda_{y}(v)\Bigr)\setminus\{i\}=I\setminus\{i\}.

So the tuple
\big(I\setminus\{i\},\psi,\ell\big)
is indeed a footprint of
x
, i.e.,
\big(I\setminus\{i\},\psi,\ell\big)\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
.

For the other direction, let
(\widetilde{I},\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\ell})\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
be a footprint of
x
.
And let
(S,M)
be a partial solution and let
\phi
be a label choice of
x
with this footprint.
Then
(S,M)
is a partial solution of
y
and
\phi
its label choice in
y
: indeed, we have
\phi(v)\in\lambda_{x}(v)\subseteq\lambda_{y}(v)
for every vertex
v\in S\setminus V(M)
.
Because no vertex in
G_{x}
contains label
i
, we have
\phi^{-1}(i)=\emptyset
and therefore,
\widetilde{\psi}(i)=0
.
So let
(I,\psi,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
be the footprint of
(S,M)
and
\phi
in
y
.
We then have
\widetilde{\psi}=\psi
and
\widetilde{\ell}=\ell
simply by definition.
We also have
\widetilde{I}=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{x}(v)
and
I=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{y}(v)
.
Then by definition of the graph
G_{x}
, we have
\widetilde{I}=I\setminus\{i\}
.
∎

When a node of the expression adds a label
i
to a label
j
, a label choice may now decide, for every vertex mapped to
i
whether it stays mapped to
i
or “switches” to
j
.
Since for the footprints only the number of such nodes matters, it suffices to branch on the number of vertices, for which such a switch occurs.

Lemma 34
.

Let
(G_{x},\lambda_{x})=\rho_{i\to\{i,j\}}(G_{y},\lambda_{x})
for some labels
i\neq j\in[k]
and a multi-
k
-labeled graph
(G_{y},\lambda_{y})
.
Then the footprints of the node
x
are given as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{x}=\Biggl\{\Big(\widetilde{I},\psi\big[i\mapsto\psi(i)-r,j\mapsto\psi(j)+r\big],\ell\Big)\,:\,

\displaystyle(I,\psi,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}_{y},\widetilde{I}=\begin{cases}I&\text{if }i\notin I\\
I\cup\{j\}&\text{if }i\in I,\end{cases}

\displaystyle r\in\big[\psi(i)\big]_{0}\Biggr\}.

Proof.

The unlabeled graphs
G_{x}
and
G_{y}
are equal and only the label sets differ so that we have

\lambda_{x}(v)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{y}(v)\cup\{j\}&\text{if }i\in\lambda_{y}(v)\\
\lambda_{y}(v)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for every vertex
v\in V_{x}=V_{y}
.
Hence, the sets of partial solutions of
x
and
y
are equal and only the label choices of those partial solutions differ.

First, let
(I,\psi,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
be a footprint of
y
and let
r\in\big[\psi(i)\big]_{0}
be an integer.
Further, let
(S,M)
be a partial solution and let
\phi
be a label choice of
(S,M)
in
y
with this footprint.
In particular, we have
\left\lvert\phi^{-1}(i)\right\rvert=\psi(i)\geqslant r
.
Let now
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
be
r
pairwise distinct vertices from
\phi^{-1}(i)
.
Then
(S,M)
is a partial solution of
x
as well and
\widetilde{\phi}:=\phi[v_{1}\mapsto j,\dots,v_{r}\mapsto j]
is a label choice for
(S,M)
in
x
.
Indeed, for every vertex
v\in S\setminus\big(V(M)\cup\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}\big)
, we have
\widetilde{\phi}(v)=\phi(v)\in\lambda_{y}(v)\subseteq\lambda_{x}(v)
, and we also have
\widetilde{\phi}(v_{q})=j\in\lambda_{x}(v_{q})
due to
i=\phi(v_{q})\in\lambda_{y}(v_{q})
for every
q\in[r]
.
Let
(\widetilde{I},\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\ell})\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
be the footprint of
(S,M)
and
\widetilde{\phi}
in
x
.
We trivially have
\ell=\widetilde{\ell}
.
Further, we have
\widetilde{\psi}=\psi\big[i\mapsto\psi(i)-r,j\mapsto\psi(j)+r\big]
by construction.
Finally, we have
I=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{y}(v)
and
\widetilde{I}=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{x}(v)
.
By definition of
\lambda_{x}
, we then have
\widetilde{I}=\begin{cases}I&\text{if }i\notin I\\
I\cup\{j\}&\text{if }i\in I\end{cases}
.

For the other direction, let
(\widetilde{I},\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\ell})\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
be a footprint of
x
.
And let
(S,M)
be a partial solution and
\widetilde{\phi}
a label choice of
(S,M)
in
x
with this footprint.
Let
r
be the cardinality of the set
\big\{v\in\widetilde{\phi}^{-1}(j)\,:\,i\in\lambda_{y}(v)\big\}
and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
be the elements of this set.
So the vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{r}
are precisely the vertices mapped to
j
by
\phi
such that their label set in
\lambda_{y}
(in fact, in both
\lambda_{y}
and
\lambda_{x}
) contains label
i
.
We define the mapping
\phi=\widetilde{\phi}[v_{1}\mapsto i,\dots,v_{r}\mapsto i]
.
The pair
(S,M)
is also a partial solution of
y
and we now argue that
\phi
is a label choice for
(S,M)
in
y
.
For this consider an arbitrary vertex
v\in S\setminus V(M)
.

•

If
\widetilde{\phi}(v)\neq j
(and in particular,
v\notin\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
), then
j\neq\widetilde{\phi}(v)\in\lambda_{x}(v)
implies
\phi(v)=\widetilde{\phi}(v)\in\lambda_{y}(v)
—indeed, from
\lambda_{y}
to
\lambda_{x}
the label set of a vertex can only gain label
j
.

•

If
\widetilde{\phi}(v)=j
and
i\notin\lambda_{y}(v)
(and in particular,
v\notin\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
), then
\widetilde{\phi}(v)\in\lambda_{x}(v)
implies
\phi(v)=\widetilde{\phi}(v)\in\lambda_{y}(v)
—indeed, the label set of a vertex can change from
\lambda_{y}
to
\lambda_{x}
only if it contained label
i
.

•

Finally, if
\widetilde{\phi}(v)=j
and
i\in\lambda_{y}(v)
(i.e., if
v\in\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
), then we have
\phi(v)=i\in\lambda_{y}(v)
.

So
\phi
is indeed a label choice for the partial solution
(S,M)
in
y
.
Let now
(I,\psi,\ell)\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
be the footprint of
(S,M)
and
\phi
in
y
.
We then have
\widetilde{I}=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{x}(v)
and
I=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus S}\lambda_{y}(v)
.
By definition of
\lambda_{x}
, we then have

\widetilde{I}=\begin{cases}I&\text{if }i\notin I\\
I\cup\{j\}&\text{if }i\in I\end{cases}.

Next, by construction, we have
\widetilde{\psi}=\psi\big[i\mapsto\psi(i)-r,j\mapsto\psi(j)+r\big]
.
In particular, we have
0\leqslant r\leqslant\psi(i)
, i.e.,
r\in\big[\psi(i)\big]_{0}
.
And we also trivially have
\widetilde{\ell}=\ell
.
∎

For a disjoint union of two graphs, the set of partial solutions is simply given by pairwise unions of partial solutions of the two graphs.
And the analogous property applies to label choices.
So for the footprints, we can combine all such pairs as follows.

Lemma 35
.

Let
(G_{x},\lambda_{x})=(G_{y_{1}},\lambda_{y_{1}})\oplus(G_{y_{2}},\lambda_{y_{2}})
where
G_{y_{1}}
and
G_{y_{2}}
are vertex-disjoint graphs.
Then the footprints of the node
x
are given as follows:

\mathcal{S}_{x}=\Bigl\{\big(I_{1}\cup I_{2},i\mapsto\psi_{1}(i)+\psi_{2}(i),\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\big)\,:\,(I_{1},\psi_{1},\ell_{1})\in\mathcal{S}_{y_{1}},(I_{2},\psi_{2},\ell_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{y_{2}}\Bigr\}.

Proof.

First, let
(I_{1},\psi_{1},\ell_{1})\in\mathcal{S}_{y_{1}}
be a footprint of
y_{1}
and let
(I_{2},\psi_{2},\ell_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{y_{2}}
be a footprint of
y_{2}
.
And for every index
a\in[2]
, let
(S_{a},M_{a})
be a partial solution of
y_{a}
and let
\phi_{a}
be a label choice of
(S_{a},M_{a})
in
y_{a}
with footprint
(I_{a},\psi_{a},\ell_{a})
.
Then
(S_{1}\cup S_{2},M_{1}\cup M_{2})
is a partial solution of
x
: because
G_{x}
is a vertex-disjoint union of
G_{y_{1}}
and
G_{y_{2}}
, the set
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is a vertex cover of
G_{x}
and the set
M_{1}\cup M_{2}
is a matching in
G_{x}
.
Furthermore, since the vertices from
G_{y_{1}}
and
G_{y_{2}}
preserve their labels from
\lambda_{y_{1}}
and
\lambda_{y_{2}}
, respectively, in
\lambda_{x}
, the mapping
\phi_{1}\cup\phi_{2}
is a label choice for
(S_{1}\cup S_{2},M_{1}\cup M_{2})
in
x
.
Let
(\widetilde{I},\widetilde{\psi},\widetilde{\ell})\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
be the footprint of
(S_{1}\cup S_{2},M_{1}\cup M_{2})
and
\phi_{1}\cup\phi_{2}
in
x
.
First, we have

\widetilde{I}=\bigcup_{v\in V_{x}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})}\lambda_{x}(v)=\left(\bigcup_{v\in V_{y_{1}}\setminus S_{1}}\lambda_{y_{1}}(v)\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{v\in V_{y_{2}}\setminus S_{2}}\lambda_{y_{2}}(v)\right)=I_{1}\cup I_{2}

because
S_{1}\subseteq V_{y_{1}}
,
S_{2}\subseteq V_{y_{2}}
, and the vertex sets
V_{y_{1}}
and
V_{y_{2}}
are disjoint.
Second, we have
\widetilde{\ell}=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
because
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are disjoint.
And finally, by construction we have

6
Conclusion

We have answered three open questions left by Chekan and Kratsch
[
12
]
about the fine-grained complexity of
Max Cut
,
Hamiltonian Cycle
, and
Edge Dominating Set
with respect to multi-clique-width. For
Max Cut
we ruled out time
n^{2^{o(k)}}
assuming ETH, while a matching algorithm can be obtained by using that multi-clique-width
k
implies clique-width is at most
2^{k}
. In contrast, for
Hamiltonian Cycle
and
Edge Dominating Set
we were able to give algorithms with the same time bound
n^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
as for clique-width, which matches known lower bounds that extend to multi-clique-width. It would be interesting to study further problems that are XP (but likely not FPT) and have tight bounds with respect to clique-width and to determine such tight bounds relative to multi-clique-width and fusion-width. Are there problems that separate two or even all three of these parameters?

The same question can also be posed for problems that are FPT for clique-width. So far, for a small selection of problems, the same tight bounds were obtained for all three parameters
[
30
,
31
,
12
]
. It would similarly be interesting to find separations between them.

The fact that multi-clique-width generalizes treewidth (and clique-width) with only a constant increase in value arguably makes it an attractive parameter for fine-grained parameterized complexity. This would be much strengthened if there were a better way to approximate it, say in FPT time. So far, only a doubly-exponential approximation follows from its relation to clique-with. Can this be improved?
