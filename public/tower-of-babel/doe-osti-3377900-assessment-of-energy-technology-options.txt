Executive Summary
This report documents analysis done by researchers at the National Laboratory of the Rockies
and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory to evaluate the potential for and explore project
concepts of electricity generation and storage additions on the island of Molokai, Hawaii, as
identified in the Community Energy Resilience Action Plan (CERAP) by the Molokai Clean
Energy Hui (MCEH), Sustʻāinable Molokai, and the Hawaii Natural Energy Institute (HNEI).
These electricity generation and storage additions are as follows:

 • Distributed photovoltaics (PV), battery energy storage, and generators for critical
 facilities on the island that can provide backup energy to the facilities during grid
 disruptions and outages of up to 2 weeks
 • A floating PV (FPV) system on Kualapuu Reservoir, the island’s largest waterbody and
 primary agricultural irrigation reservoir
 • Pumped storage hydropower (PSH) systems scaled to act as a significant or primary
 source of energy storage on the Molokai grid.
The analysis of distributed systems for critical facilities estimates PV, battery, and generator
systems can be sized for the hospital, airport, port, and three schools that would be able to cover
100% of their standard load during 14-day outages and would significantly reduce the amount of
energy they would need to purchase from the grid during normal operation. In most cases, the
necessary area for PV panels was identified as being available on facility rooftops or on ground
near the facility. Analysis cost estimates in most scenarios show utility bill savings would offset
the majority of but not all the costs of these systems over a time frame of 25 years.

The analysis shows Kualapuu Reservoir can support an FPV system large enough to serve as the
single largest generation source of most of the grid’s energy needs. For instance, the modeled 9
megawatts direct current (MWdc) FPV plant would be expected to produce 16.6 gigawatt-hours
(GWh) annually on average according to our estimates, which is 42.6% of the Molokai grid’s
forecasted annual energy use in 2050. Similarly, the analysis shows the topography of Molokai
could support a PSH system large enough to serve as the primary energy storage system on the
Molokai grid: A 7.24-MW PSH system would be able to store more than 21 GWh of energy
annually by completing one full charge and discharge cycle daily.

The analysis of a potential FPV system on Kualapuu Reservoir shows the availability of more
than enough developable area for the 9-MWdc system that is desired by the community and
sized to be the largest system in a portfolio of PV systems designed to meet 90% of the Molokai
grid’s net energy demand by a concurrent U.S. Department of Energy sponsored Energy to
Communities (E2C) grid modeling project. As modeled in this analysis, approximately 60% of
the reservoir (21 hectares) is deemed available for FPV, and the desired 9-MWdc project would
require approximately 9 hectares. The system is estimated to generate a marginally higher
amount of electricity annually than a fixed-tilt alternative in a similar location because of the
performance gains due to evaporative cooling seen in experiments on FPV performance. The
system is expected to cost significantly more than ground-mounted alternatives, with the cost on
a $/kilowatts direct current (kWdc) basis being over 26% higher than the fixed-tilt ground-
mounted alternative, which is similar to the benchmark of 25% found in previous National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) research.

However, there are other benefits to the FPV system that the Molokai community is interested in
beyond the trade-off of system cost and generation. The ability of FPV systems to avoid the
development of land with other economic or cultural values is quantified, with the FPV system
avoiding more than 65 hectares (more than 161 acres) of PV array area needing to be developed
for the fixed-tilt ground-mounted alternative. In addition, we find the FPV system could decrease
evaporation losses from the reservoir by more than 30 million gallons (MG) per year,
representing a significant portion of the water the irrigation system uses every year. Lower
evaporation losses could translate to less energy and money being used to pump water into the
reservoir, potentially representing a savings of more than $30,000 a year to the irrigation system.

a single-axis tracking system is also modeled as a comparison. The primary trade-offs

Introduction
The community of Molokai, Hawaii faces energy challenges such as reliance on imported fuel,
aging critical infrastructure, an isolated and aging grid, vulnerabilities to weather-related impacts
to utility lines, and frequent power outages lasting up to multiple days. The average 2024
residential electricity rate for Molokai was $0.50 per kilowatt-hour (kWh), the highest rate in the
state and 3 times higher than the 2024 national average of $0.16 per kilowatt-hour (Hawaiian
Electric 2025; U.S. Energy Information Administration 2025). In addition, Hawaii law mandates
in its renewable energy portfolio standard (RPS) that all electric utilities operating in the state
pursue a 100% renewable energy portfolio by 2045 (Hawaii State Legislature 2015). Executive
Order No. 25-01 establishes a new state policy to accelerate the RPS to achieve 100% renewable
electricity production in the counties of Hawaii, Kauai, and Maui by 2035, including Molokai
(Hawaiian Governor’s Office 2025).

To address these challenges and opportunities, Sustʻāinable Molokai (SM) and the Molokai
Clean Energy Hui (MCEH), in partnership with the Hawaii Natural Energy Institute (HNEI), led
the development of the Community Energy Resilience Action Plan (CERAP), a living document
that outlines priority energy projects identified through robust community engagement, feedback,
and co-design. CERAP 1.0 identified 10 priority energy projects. The community team—
comprising Sustʻāinable Molokai, the Molokai Clean Energy Hui, Hoʻāhu Energy Cooperative
Molokai, and Shake Energy Collaborative—selected three priority energy projects to be further
explored through the Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP) as an integral
part of continuing community energy planning and decision-making during CERAP 2.0. These
priority projects made up the three tasks this ETIPP analysis focused on:

 • Task 1: Distributed photovoltaics (PV), battery energy storage, and generators for critical
 facilities on the island that can provide backup energy to the facilities during grid
 disruptions and outages of up to 2 weeks
 • Task 2: A floating PV (FPV) system on Kualapuu Reservoir, the island’s largest
 waterbody and primary agricultural irrigation reservoir
 • Task 3: Pumped storage hydropower (PSH) systems scaled to act as a significant or
 primary source of energy storage on the Molokai grid.

ETIPP conducted modeling and analysis to evaluate the technical feasibility, costs, risks, and
opportunities of these projects to help inform community decision making. These three primary
tasks were accompanied by a fourth task focused on providing educational materials to the
community team to prepare community members to participate in technical discussions and
decisions to inform the direction of Tasks 1–3. Complementing the ETIPP analysis, MCEH led a
community engagement effort comprising focus groups, workshops, and 1:1 engagements, which
helped inform members of the Molokai community of technical findings and shepherd the
community-based decision making needed to inform the second round of technical analysis.

In March 2024, ETIPP staff from the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) and the Pacific
Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) traveled to Molokai to participate in workshops and site
visits hosted by SM and MCEH. Activities included pilina (relationship) building as well as site
visits to the wastewater facility, elementary school, hospital, fire stations, and Kualapuu
Reservoir. These sites were identified as either critical facilities or central to other priority

 1

projects in the CERAP process and were chosen by MCEH as important sites for lab staff to see
in person. The workshops and site visits provided fundamental insights that guided the ETIPP
analysis.

This report summarizes the key findings from the ETIPP analysis and is provided to support
community and stakeholder decision making on Molokai’s energy future.

Task 1: Critical Infrastructure Backup Energy Analysis
Analysis Approach and Objectives
During the on-island site visit in March 2024, the community team identified the resilience
challenges faced by the community and discussed goals for CERAP 2.0 and this ETIPP analysis.
During the visit, the ETIPP team collected crucial information by touring some of the sites the
community team had identified for critical infrastructure analysis. With support from the
community team, the ETIPP team collected additional operational data and load data of the
facilities and conducted a techno-economic analysis for these facilities to determine possible
solutions to mitigate resilience and outage concerns for the identified facilities.

In contrast to Tasks 2 and 3, which aim to explore community energy goals from the perspective
of the entire island’s energy grid, Task 1 instead aims to explore community resilience goals by
examining the ability to provide backup power during grid disturbances for individual facilities.
Although the decreased reliance on purchasing energy from the grid would to some extent help
meet the energy goals of the entire island, that is not the objective these backup systems are sized
to meet. One critical aspect following this is that these systems are modeled as not selling any
energy to the grid. This allows the dispatch of these facilities to be optimized for resilience,
whereas the requirements of programs offered by the utility that would allow selling power to the
grid would put restrictions on the dispatch of the system that could prevent it from being
managed in a way that provides the most resiliency to these facilities. It does, however, decrease
the economic value of these systems to the facility because they can benefit only from the
avoided costs of purchasing power from the grid—not from the revenues of selling power to the
grid.

Objectives
The objective of this task is to understand distributed energy resource (DER) sizes for backup
energy systems and on-site generation for six critical facilities identified by the community to
survive a 14-day outage. Distributed energy resources are assets used to generate and store
electrical energy; examples include solar PV, battery energy storage systems, and co-generation
plants. The following facilities are included in this analysis:

 • Kilohana Elementary School
 • Maunaloa School
 • Molokai High School
 • Molokai Harbor
 • Molokai General Hospital
 • Molokai Airport.
This is a subset of at least 40 buildings and utilities across the island that provides critical
services and is considered high priority to continue operations during a disaster (MCEH 2023).
The six facilities included in the analysis were selected from an initial list of 12 priority critical
facilities based on availability of energy usage data.

For all facilities except the Molokai General Hospital, the DERs are sized to survive an outage
with 100% of their load online and 50% of their load online as discussed with the community

 3

team during early engagement. This can help the community understand the cost of
implementation and determine the best option for each facility and load management measures
during emergencies.

REopt Overview
The ETIPP team used REopt, a high-level techno-economic analysis tool (Cutler et al. 2017) to
estimate the optimum DER asset sizes for a combination of solar PV, battery energy storage
system (BESS), and diesel generators. The REopt tool takes the hourly or 15-minute load
interval data, location, and tariff information of the facility as its input to determine the most
economically optimum solution for the facility. A variety of constraints can also be added, such
as space availability, energy export constraints, energy goals, and energy backup and outage
survivability considerations to enable the facility to sustain a certain duration of outage. REopt
refers to the Annual Technology Baseline (ATB; “Data | Electricity | 2024 | ATB | NLR” n.d.)
for cost values for technologies over a period of years; default values in the technology section
are derived from the 2023 version of the ATB, which are in 2021 dollar years. Figure 1
summarizes the possible model inputs and outputs in the REopt tool.

Inputs and Assumptions

Load Data
None of the critical facilities on Molokai were equipped with advanced (or “smart”) meters with
data logging capabilities at the start of the project. Thus, REopt’s inbuilt stock load profiles were
used for the analysis. The ETIPP team worked with MCEH to collect monthly utility bills for
these facilities; the monthly energy consumption was used as an input to build and scale the load
profile, derived from matching generic facilities by use. This ensures the total energy, monthly
energy patterns, and peak use (when stated) for the facilities are consistent with those reported on
the monthly bills while providing the time series patterns of diurnal energy use required by

 4

REopt. However, precise operational trends cannot be determined without time series meter
reading data, such as from advanced meters. The modeled load profiles used are the result of
calculations using the available data.

Technology Inputs and Assumptions for All Facilities
This section describes the inputs to the various DER technologies considered in the system
design. Because no Molokai-specific data were available at the time of the analysis, default
numbers based on the ATB were used. These assumptions are recorded next to help understand
cost assumptions and technological specifications used at the time of analysis. As the CERAP
projects move forward, these assumptions can be adjusted by engineering design firms to use
updated or Molokai-specific assumptions to develop more detailed engineering designs. During
this analysis, if a facility has any existing assets, those are described in the overview section.

Solar PV Technology Assumptions
The solar PV systems are assumed to be either ground mounted or roof mounted depending on
the facility. An azimuth of 180 degrees (south facing) with a tilt of 15 degrees from a flat ground
surface is assumed. The system losses are assumed at 14%. The solar irradiance data for PV
modeling were collected from the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB; “NSRDB” n.d.).
The solar PV deployment cost is assumed at $1,790 per kilowatt direct current (kWdc) of solar
PV deployment. An annual operations and maintenance (O&M) cost of $18 per kWdc is
assumed from the ATB (“Data | Electricity | 2024 | ATB | NLR” n.d.). These assumptions are
REopt default inputs as mentioned in the overview. Default values were used because site-
specific costs were not available at the time of analysis.

Battery Energy Storage System Assumptions
The analysis assumed the use of a lithium-ion battery with the following characteristics because
it is currently the most widely used BESS type and can be procured off the shelf from vendors:

 • An internal 97.5% round-trip efficiency with the rectifier and inverter efficiencies of 96%
 • A minimum state of charge maintained at 20%
 • A cost of $910 per kilowatt alternating current (kWac) and $455 per kilowatt-hour
 alternating current (kWhac) and replacement cost after 10 years at $715 per kilowatt
 (kW) and $318 per kilowatt-hour (kWh; from the ATB).
These cost assumptions include cost of inverter along with the respective technology. Note that

Cost Uncertainty
There is much uncertainty around estimates for PV and BESS costs, which have exhibited high
variability in recent years because of the combined effects of technological changes, supply
chain disruptions, rapid increases in demand, high and variable inflation, and changing incentive
and international trade landscapes. This does give the analysis of backup system costs and values
of the systems presented in this section a lower level of confidence than desired given the
importance of these factors. However, this does not affect—or affects minimally—many
important conclusions of the analysis. Outside of these factors, the analysis shows 1) that backup
systems can be sized for these facilities to survive 14-day outages with a high degree of
survivability, 2) the sizing of each generation and storage technology needed to achieve this goal,

 18

and 3) that notwithstanding any issues arising from in-person engineering reviews of roof
suitability, there is enough room on rooftops or land to support the PV needed for these systems.

To examine one potential impact of various cost estimates, we can compare the cost values used
presented previously, which were REopt values at the time of the analysis derived from the 2023
ATB, with values used in Hawaiian Electric Company’s (HECO’s) 2023 Integrated Grid Plan
(IGP) update—which are partially derived from an earlier 2021 version of the ATB but with
adjustments to better reflect Hawaii-specific policy and cost contexts. For PV, the cost value of
2021 USD $1,790/kW used in these results is the 2023 ATB, 2024 estimate for the “commercial”
size class, which is the size class that best matches the size of systems recommended in this
analysis. The corresponding value for the 2024 commercial-scale PV used in the IGP is $1,645
in 2021 $/kW (adjusted from a value of $1,904 in 2024 $/kW). This means the IGP represents a
less conservative value than used here, whereas recent price changes would be more likely to
make these estimates too low.

Because the values in the IGP are listed in nominal dollars, they may be affected by inflation that
was much higher in 2021-24 than was assumed in the IGP. It may be more accurate to compare
the 2021 values in the respective sources, where the values are both in 2021 USD. Here, the
value from the 2023 ATB is $1,754/kW, and the value in the IGP is $2,004/kW. This implies the
IGP views systems of this size class as being 14.3% more expensive because of Hawaii-specific
cost factors. Applying this cost inflation factor to the $1,790/kW value produces an estimate of
$2,046/kW. Following the same procedure for O&M costs leads to an increase to $25/kW
annually from $18/kW.

For BESS, the IGP does not give the cost values for the commercial size class per kW and kWh,
which is necessary to be able to size both peak capacity and storage duration separately as
needed for backup system optimization. We can compare the 2021 cost value of $1,186/kW
given by the IGP for a 2-hour duration commercially sized BESS system to that of $1,460/kW
given in the 2023 ATB. Here, the value used in this study is already more conservative than the
one available in the IGP.

As an example of how backup system value could decrease by using the more conservative IGP-
derived estimate for PV but the more conservative ATB-derived estimate already used, REopt
results were rerun with these assumptions for Kilohana Elementary School. The NPV for the
50% load sized system decreased from $325,561 to $267,851. The NPV for the 100% load sized
system decreased from $209,723 to $127,375. These figures would represent a decrease in net
value of 18% and 39%, respectively. Although these differences represent significant decrease
in value to these facility operators, the change in assumptions does not indicate the value would
come close to switching from positive to negative.

Floating PV on Kualapuu Reservoir was identified as project seven of the CERAP 1.0 Clean

technical and economic feasibility (MCEH 2023). Primary strengths of interest to MCEH and the
Molokai community identified in CERAP 1.0 include the ability to develop solar generation

Kualapuu Reservoir Context and Data
To complete the objectives of this activity, it is necessary to collect as much information and
data about the reservoir that could help inform the answer to the analyses outlined previously. A
summary of relevant information and data is presented in this section.

Kualapuu Reservoir and Molokai Irrigation System Background
Kualapuu Reservoir, identified in CERAP 1.0 as being of interest for FPV development, is by far
the largest reservoir or permanent waterbody on Molokai with a maximum capacity of more than
1,400 million gallons (MG; 5.3 Gigaliters [Gl]) and a surface area of 87.5 acres (35.4 hectares;
Hawaii State Department of Agriculture 2015; US EPA 2014). The next largest permanent
waterbody, the naturally occurring Meyer Lake, has less than 7% of the surface area of Kualapuu
Reservoir—hence the availability of water surface to install FPV on Kualapuu represents the best
opportunity on Molokai by a factor of more than 10 (US EPA 2014). In addition, being formed
by a 50-foot dam on three sides, the reservoir retains most of its surface area even during low fill
levels (Murakami 2012). The reservoir is located centrally on the island, within the census-
designated place of Kualapuu.

Kualapuu Reservoir is one component of the Molokai Irrigation System (MIS), which is the
primary source of agricultural irrigation water on Molokai. The MIS was planned in 1956 by the
U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR), which provided a loan to construct the system through the
Federal Small Reclamation Projects Act (Hawaii State Department of Agriculture 2015).The
MIS conveys water from the rainforest climate of the Waikolu Valley in the island’s windward
highlands to the central Hoolehua Plain where there is a concentration of agricultural activity but
a tropical savanna (bordering on semi-arid) climate that experiences very dry summers. Most of
the Hoolehua Plain receives significantly less than an inch of monthly precipitation on average in
July, August, and September (Giambelluca et al. 2013; Frazier et al. 2016).

The system uses both diversion dams and pumps in the Waikolu Valley to direct water to
Kualapuu Reservoir. Three operational intakes collect gravity-fed surface water that is diverted
using dams throughout the year, providing most of the water to the MIS. In addition, four well
pumps and one booster pump are needed to pump a separate source of diverted water operate
only when needed. Together, between approximately 850 and 1,250 MG annually are redirected
through a 5-mile tunnel through the western edge of the valley and through approximately 4
additional miles of conveyance infrastructure before it arrives at Kualapuu Reservoir (Murakami
2012; Belt, Collins, and Associates 1982). From Kualapuu Reservoir, water is withdrawn to the
distribution system as required by demand via a gravity-fed outtake, where water is gravity-fed
to end users or delivered uphill to some users with a booster pump and an auxiliary holding tank.
The total area served by the distribution was cited as more than 3,300 acres as of 2014 (Hawaii
State Department of Agriculture 2015).

HDOA Concerns
HDOA shared its concerns that the use of the reservoir for an additional purpose could create
tension with its mandated responsibilities to manage the water resource for agricultural purposes
and maintain the safety of the reservoir and dam. Given these requirements, HDOA believes it
would likely be obligated to reasonably ensure a potential FPV system would not interfere with
its primary missions before being able to consider development. Following are areas of particular
concern:

 24

 • Restricted site access to the reservoir: The need for site access to construction and
 operations staff could create opportunities for intentional or unintentional damage to the
 dam or other infrastructure critical to the MIS operation.
 • The possibility that FPV floats and panels could collide with and damage the dam or
 other infrastructure, such as during a natural disaster.
 • The possibility that the presence of FPV on the reservoir could interfere with dam and
 reservoir operations and maintenance.
 Although these concerns are reasonable and the potential for an FPV system to interfere with
the primary uses of the reservoir is a shared concern with members of the community who rely
on the reservoir for irrigation water , it should be noted that FPV systems are commonly
installed on bodies of water that are similarly owned and operated by public entities that must
manage the reservoir for other primary uses. For instance, some of the largest existing FPV
systems in the United States are on retention ponds for drinking water facilities (such as
Sayreville and Canoe Brook, New Jersey) or for recycled wastewater projects (Healdsburg and
Windsor, California) 1. The ability of FPV to coexist with these uses (and save the facility owners
money in the process) has been demonstrated for almost 5 years in the case of the Sayreville and
Healdsburg plants. Although no existing public projects in the United States are specifically for
agricultural irrigation purposes, there are private installations that are, such as the Far Niente
vineyard and a project under construction at Nelson Family Vineyards in California.

This does not remove all potential risks of FPV as an emerging technology, but it does mean it is
not a pilot technology without a track record either. The valid concerns raised by HDOA are
largely things that can have design and engineering solutions, such as locating floats sufficiently
far from infrastructure and using anchoring and mooring solutions less likely to impact dam
infrastructure. To the extent possible, these concerns have been incorporated or discussed in the
analysis methods and results, such as ensuring estimates for potential capacity of an FPV facility
include proper buffers from known infrastructure. Considering these concerns will be crucial for
the acceptance of the project by HDOA and for buy-in from the community.

Technical Potential Analysis
The general process used for a technical potential analysis is as follows:

 1. Use geospatial analysis to remove areas from consideration that are technically infeasible
 for development. Calculate the remaining developable area.

 2. Apply capacity density assumptions to the developable area to calculate the total
 developable capacity in the developable area.

The results of these methods show there is likely twice as much area available to develop an FPV
system in the expected range of 9–12 megawatts direct current (MWdc) to serve the Molokai
grid’s needs in central Molokai. This shows not only that a community-sized FPV system can fit
on Kualapuu Reservoir but also that there is enough available area for flexibility in the specific
design and layout of the system to meet community and HDOA goals—even allowing for the
possibility for additional areas of exclusion to be discovered during site surveys. These steps and
methods as applied to FPV development on Kualapuu Reservoir are described in the following
subsections.

 26

Exclusions and Developable Area Geospatial Analysis
In previous work, NLR has defined the list of qualities that are required for technical feasibility
of inland FPV (Rosenlieb et al. 2025):

 1. The system must be in water that always remains deep enough for the FPV system to
 remain floating, or the waterbody floor must be flat, even, and free from debris so
 the FPV system can be designed to rest on the floor when water levels are low.
 2. The system must be in water that is free from excessively fast water currents.
 3. The system must be in water that is free from ice and snow factors that occur in cold
 climates, such as high levels of snow loading, moving ice, and heavy freeze-thaw
 cycles.
 4. The system must be in a low wake area, either in a waterbody that is free from
 motorized watercraft completely or at a distance from watercraft activity so the
 wakes, depending on size and speed of the watercraft, have time to sufficiently
 disperse.
 5. The system must be located far enough away from dam infrastructure to allow
 normal operations and maintenance of the dam.
This is not intended to be a full list of factors that could affect the feasibility of FPV in any way;
factors such as wind loading and water level variation could make the installation of an FPV cost
more to the extent that it is not economically feasible. However, because these factors have
engineering solutions (such as increased anchoring and mooring requirements), they are not
considered to affect the technical feasibility. Wind is a highly relevant factor for FPV
development on Molokai, and its impact on economic feasibility is assessed in the cost modeling
analysis.

Cold Weather and Wake Factors
Of the five factors listed previously, Kualapuu Reservoir is free from two of them. The cold-
weather factors are not a concern: In the tropical climate of Molokai, the lowest average monthly
low temperature at nearby Hoolehua Airport is 64.3°F in January over the last 30 years of
recorded data (National Centers for Environmental Information 2021). In addition, the reservoir
has controlled access and is not open to watercraft of any kind. Therefore, no areas are
considered “excluded” from feasible FPV development because of these two factors.

Water Depth and Floor Suitability
For the first factor listed, there are no available data to determine whether the reservoir floor is
flat, even, and free from debris to support the grounding of FPV floats at low water elevations.
This information would be provided by bathymetry and sedimentation surveys, of which there is
no record of ever being conducted for Kualapuu Reservoir according to HDOA. Conservatively,
for the purposes of the technical potential analysis, it is assumed there is nowhere on the
reservoir where FPV floats could be grounded and therefore FPV can be developed only where
there is permanent water coverage.

 27

In more than 13 years of monthly elevation data obtained from HDOA—from January 2011 to
May 2024—the lowest water elevation recorded was 10.75 feet. In previous analysis, NLR has
used 1 meter (m) of depth (3.28 feet) as an estimate of the water depth required for an FPV
system to be floating and unaffected by small-scale local variations in water depth (Rosenlieb et
al. 2025). This means even at this low recorded water elevation there is still approximately 7.5
extra feet of depth for areas of the reservoir without other specific obstructions.

The data available suggest the water level has a very low chance of naturally reaching such a low
water elevation (a p-value below 0.005). In addition, in periods of such low water availability
caused by some combination of low passive water flow from the Waikolu Valley diversion dams
or larger-than-expected water use, the reservoir operators would likely enact methods to ensure a
usable level of water in the reservoir—such as increased use of the Waikolu Valley pumps as
well as water conservation requirements on the user side. Because of these factors, we consider
the entire area of the reservoir floor not otherwise known to be obstructed to be permanently
covered with water.

The only known obstructions on the floor of the reservoir that could cause FPV systems to lack
the sufficient water depth are the known areas of heavy sedimentation around the intake and
outtake of the reservoir as well as the pyramidal grate positioned above the outtake. Without any
additional data on the location and magnitude of sedimentation in the reservoir, we assume the
areas of heavy sedimentation are limited to 100 m (328 feet) from the reservoir intake and
outtake. Therefore, 100-m buffers around the intake and outtake are excluded from the
developable area for FPV.

Water Currents
Generally, water currents that exceed 2 meters/second velocity are infeasible for FPV
development (Rosenlieb et al. 2025). These currents would be seen around natural inlets or near
intakes or outtakes designed for high flow rates generally seen in conventional or PSH
applications. Kualapuu Reservoir is not on any natural waterways, and its only inlet is from the
MIS conveyance system. Although data on instantaneous flow rates of the intake are not
available, the capacity of the conveyance system is sized for a maximum flow rate of 21 million
gallons per day, or about 32.5 cubic feet per second—a flow rate that would not typically be
associated with a large tailrace zone (Belt, Collins, and Associates 1982). Although the designed
flow rate capacity of the distribution system is not known, it is expected to be lower than the
conveyance system because it does not need to be sized for high but intermittent flow rates of
heavy precipitation events in the Waikolu Valley. Therefore, any currents that would pose a
threat to the feasibility of an FPV system are assumed to be within the 100-m buffer already used
around the intake and outtake for sedimentation reasons.

Distance From Dam
An engineering document was acquired from the MIS water users advisory board that shows the
extent of the bottom of the reservoir as designed. Because the cross section of the dam structure
is triangular, the cross section of the reservoir is trapezoidal with a larger surface area on the top
than on the bottom—and not all areas of the reservoir that are within the boundaries of the dam
structure at normal fill levels are within the boundaries of the dam structure at low fill levels. The
floor of the reservoir shown in the reservoir design document is the extent of the reservoir that is

entirely within the extent of the dam structure at all water elevations and where the naturally
occurring topography is below the outtake elevation of 770 feet. An FPV system within this
extent should not come into contact with the dam structure at any water level, desirable both for
the FPV system and for the dam. Therefore, the area shown in the engineering document was
georeferenced and digitized to use to represent the extent within the reservoir that can support an
FPV development. An additional 10-m distance from the dam is excluded to allow an extra

additional 10-m buffer applies only to the north, east, and south sides of the reservoir because the
east side of the reservoir is formed by the Kualapuu cinder cone and not a dam structure.

Developable Generation Capacity
From the available developable area of 21.4 hectares, the developable capacity is estimated using
NLR’s capacity density assumption method along with modeled FPV float footprints. This

needed by the community—but the estimation of this number allows an understanding of
whether an FPV system of the size needed can fit on the reservoir and if it might be constrained
by available area. An available area significantly higher than the required size could imply less
risk that any additional exclusions discovered by a site survey would make the development
infeasible. For this section, all capacities noted will be DC nameplate capacities of the PV
modules as opposed to the AC capacity of inverters that ultimately decide the maximum capacity
of AC power that can be sent to the grid. Although the AC capacity is important for grid
modeling context, the DC output of the panels is what directly relates to the amount of area
available for development—whereas the AC capacity can vary depending on the capacity of the
inverters relative to the capacity of the PV panels or the invertor load ration. The inverters of PV
systems of this size are generally located on land and require a small amount of area compared to
the PV panels and are therefore assumed not to be a driving factor for available generation
capacity.

Capacity Density Method
One way to estimate the amount of developable capacity within available area is by using a
capacity density factor. A capacity density is an assumed amount of generating capacity that is
available per unit of available area, making the calculation of potential generation capacity a
simple unit conversion. This method has the advantage of being generalizable with less available
required data on each specific site, such as the shape of available area in addition to the size.
Previous NLR research has analyzed existing FPV installations and found they typically occupy
about 1 hectare per MWdc installed, leading to a capacity density factor of 1 MWdc/hectare
(Spencer et al. 2019). This is not the surface area of the panels or floats but rather the area of the
entire development including safety and maintenance buffers and area between floats—therefore,
this should not be interpreted as the amount of water covered by the FPV floats directly.
Applying this method leads to a developable capacity of 21.4 MWdc on the reservoir.

FPV Float Footprint Method
Although the generalizable nature of the capacity density method is useful for continental or
global-scale analysis where there is not sufficient detail available for every site, for any
individual site it may over- or underestimate the available capacity depending on the shape and
other site-specific factors of that waterbody. To verify this estimate, a discrete model of FPV
float footprints is arranged within the developable area of the reservoir to provide a more
concrete estimation than the abstract capacity density factor.

To model FPV floats, polygons were created and modeled from an existing FPV system. The
existing FPV system chosen as a template is the Canoe Brook plant in New Jersey, for two
reasons. First, as the largest existing FPV system in the United States at 8.9 MWdc, it represents
the most similar system to the multimegawatt scale project that the CERAP 1.0 plan had
envisioned. Second, the float layout and footprint in Canoe Brooks will represent a conservative
estimation of available area. The floats in Canoe Brooks are highly regular and rectangular: The
system comprises two floats, each comprising five equally sized rectangular sections. The layout
of floats is variable and can come in many shapes and sizes, including floats that are assembled
to fit the exact shape of the available area to increase capacity. The design decision for the layout
depends on site-specific factors as well as choice of vendor for the FPV float and racking system.

By estimating capacity using the large rectangular assembly sections of the Canoe Brook system,
the estimate will hold true if there is some economic or operational reason that a denser packing
of floats is not possible. If a denser configuration is used, the available capacity could be higher
than reported here. There are several potential reasons different designs could be preferred. For
example, more densely packed and connected floats could aesthetically occupy less space,
decrease array cabling requirement, and make it easier to perform maintenance by walking
across the entire array without the use of rafts or floating walkways. Separated floats may be
more flexible to the presence of waves and allow easier maintenance if a motorized boat can be
used. These decisions will be necessary during the engineering design with the results of site
survey data available; the use of this site as an example footprint here is meant to represent a
conservative assumption of available capacity and is not intended as an endorsement of any
equipment or design decisions used for the site.

System Performance Modeling
To model FPV system performance and compare it to the performance of a nearby ground-
mounted system, the Renewable Energy Potential model (reV) generation module is used with
the PVWattsV8 module. Two system configurations are considered for both FPV and ground-
mounted PV.

System Configuration
For ground-mounted PV, both fixed-tilt and single-axis tracking systems are considered.
Tracking systems are modeled because they are the most common configuration for large utility-
scale PV development in the United States by a wide margin; however, it should be noted that
the PV system size needed for the grid in central Molokai of 9–12 MWdc is consistent with a
small utility-scale or a large community-scale system for analysis where fixed-tilt configurations
are more common (the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory “Utility-Scale Solar” dataset
aims to capture developments above 5 megawatts alternating current (MWac), for example; Seel
and Kemp 2024). Fixed-tilt configurations have additional benefits in the remote islanded
context of Molokai, where the additional specialized components and maintenance involved for
tracking systems can present additional challenges for system operation as opposed to other areas
of the United States.

For FPV, two common tilt and azimuth configurations are modeled: one with all panels facing
south and one with half the panels facing east and half facing west. Although fixed-tilt ground-
mounted systems always face south in the northern hemisphere, the tilt and azimuth of FPV
panels must be more optimized for other factors such as wind loading and panel density. Low tilt
angles are generally used to minimize wind loading and interrow shading, and at low tilt angles
azimuth is a less important factor. Using east- and west-facing panels can help reduce wind
loading concerns, particularly depending on the predominant wind direction. FPV panels are
assumed to be aligned with the bottom edge of the reservoir, which is 35 degrees off from due
south. A different system design could use panels directly aligned with the cardinal directions for
a small performance gain, though likely at some trade-off with capacity density.

Impact of Cooling
The ability for the evaporative cooling of waterbodies to increase PV performance is well
documented (Ramanan et al. 2025; Anusuya and Vijayakumar 2024; Nisar et al. 2022; Dzamesi
et al. 2024), but the magnitude of these gains of performance depends on PV module, site
meteorology, and float and racking design. The times when evaporative cooling can contribute
the most to panel production are during hot, sunny conditions, which is also when production of
PV modules is high. This means the greatest evaporative cooling gains may be during times that
the PV array DC output is already exceeding the AC output capacity of the system inverters and
would have little to no impact on system output AC generation. It would also follow that the
effects of evaporative cooling on generation would be sensitive to differences in inverter load
ratios, with evaporative cooling having lower impact on higher inverter load ratio systems.
However, in many studies of cooling gains for FPV, PV output is explicitly measured only at the
DC module output level, unclear whether generation estimates are in DC or AC, what inverter
load ratio is used, or an inverter load ratio of 1 is used—which is not typical of built PV systems.
In addition, many experimental studies design FPV systems to maximize panel cooling by
having modules come into direct contact with water or with membranes with high thermal
conductivity, which are not typical nonexperimental FPV installations. A recent study that
examined reported efficiency gains excluding such designs found the efficiency gain of FPV
systems “ranges between 0.1% to 4.45%” (Ramanan et al. 2024). Therefore, while significantly
higher values have been cited in research, because of these factors, NLR has previously assumed
a conservative 3% gain in annual energy production, and annual energy production values for the
FPV systems in this study are multiplied by a factor of 1.03 to approximate evaporative cooling
gains (Rosenlieb et al. 2025; Joshi et al. 2023). This more conservative approach is advocated for
in at least one more recent review of the subject (Chowdhury et al. 2023).

Capital Expenditure Cost Modeling
To understand the cost that an FPV system would pose relative to a ground-mounted system,
versions of NLR’s PV and FPV system cost models are used. The “Q1 2021” cost benchmark
and cost model using the 2020 dollar year is used because it is the most recent available
(Ramasamy and Margolis 2021). The ground-mounted PV cost model used was the “Q1 2023”
version using the 2022 dollar year (Ramasamy et al. 2023). To standardize numbers to the 2022
dollar year, a Consumer Price Index inflation factor was used. Because the 2023 PV cost model
does not include estimates for utility-scale fixed-tilt PV, to estimate fixed-tilt the 2023 PV cost
model was modified by assuming the ratio of material, equipment, and installation labor costs
between fixed-tilt and single-axis tracking systems was the same as the last year NLR included
both in the cost model (Q1 2021; Ramasamy et al. 2021).

The small size and islanded nature of Molokai pose challenges for cost modeling because
shipping, equipment acquisition, and labor rates can be high and difficult to benchmark. The
FPV cost model does include cost inflation factors by state for materials and equipment rental
costs as well as electric and construction labor, and all four of these factors are significantly

 35

higher for the state of Hawaii than for the U.S. average. To approximate the additional costs of
work on Molokai, each of these factors was assumed to be an additional 50% higher relative to
the U.S. average than the Hawaii state average and applied equally to both the FPV and ground-
mounted PV cost models. In addition, PV module and inverter costs were based directly on
quotes provided by local developers. Although this is intended to ensure costs in the Molokai
context are not significantly underrepresented, caution is recommended when interpreting the
absolute cost values presented. However, it is intended that by adjusting the FPV and ground-
mounted PV solar costs using the same method for both technologies should allow for reasonable
understanding of the relative costs of these technologies in comparison to one another.

For FPV systems, cost model components affecting cost—such as water depth, water depth
variation, and wind loading—are set according to site data as available from HDOA and the
American Society of Civil Engineers hazard tool. Of these factors, the wind loading factor is by
far the most impactful, increasing the costs of the anchoring and mooring system by 45%,
compared to the 15% increase caused by the water level variation of the reservoir. For reference,
the 2.9 wind load factor used is moderately higher than the 2.73 factor used in NLR’s FPV cost
model for an installation in Florida, which frequently experiences hurricanes (Ramasamy and
Margolis 2021). Shore anchoring was chosen as the assumption for the anchoring and mooring
system, although some combination of shore and floor anchoring is a likely alternative at
Kualapuu Reservoir to minimize impact on the dam infrastructure. Differences between the two
anchoring systems reported by the cost model were small as a proportion of total system costs
(2%–3%). The system is assumed to have a central inverter on shore, because this is the only
option allowed in the cost model, which represents a potential limitation because string inverter
configurations have been used in FPV applications as well and are quickly gaining market share
for larger PV projects due to technological advancements in string inverter technology. The
trade-offs involved in inverter choice—which include differences in installation costs, operations
and maintenance, inverter efficiency and losses—are beyond the level of detail that can be
analyzed in this desktop study.

Estimated spurline and grid upgrade transmission costs for the Kualapuu Reservoir are based on
the outcome of the concurrent grid modeling effort. The Energy to Communities (E2C) modeling
shows the FPV system will require more grid upgrade costs compared to the ground-mounted
alternative because the alternative is located close to Molokai’s current primary power plant and
much more central to the current grid. Because the location of the reservoir is fixed, there is no
site optimization that can be done to reduce this number—a potential limitation of FPV vs. a
ground-mounted alternative. The total grid upgrade costs incurred by the FPV alternative total
$7.05 million vs. $1.35 million for the ground-mounted alternative (Figure 9). At a system size
of 9 MWdc, this translates to an additional cost of $778/kWdc for the FPV system vs.
$149/kWdc for the ground-mounted alternative.

Evaporative Savings Analysis
Evaporative Losses Estimation
By comparing Kualapuu Reservoir to reservoirs with similar climatic characteristics and water
depth in the AquaPV dataset of estimated evaporation rates at federally owned or controlled
reservoirs in the United States, it is likely the evaporation rate of Kualapuu is in the range of 500
to 620 gallons per square meter annually (“AquaPV” 2025). Even conservatively taking the
lower-end value, this translates to an estimate of 177 MG lost to evaporation each year from the
reservoir. This is lower than a previous estimate of 300 MG a year based on recorded pan
evaporation measurements taken at the reservoir (Santo 2001). However, it is known that pan
evaporation overestimates the evaporation relative to large waterbodies, to correct this effect the
pan evaporation rate can be multiplied by a “pan coefficient” of 0.7. This adjusts the 300

 37

MG/year estimate to 210 MG/year, which is similar to the estimate of 219 MG/year found using
the 620 gallons per square meter estimate for evaporation (Yihdego and Webb 2017). According
to HDOA data, the total amount of water delivered to end users each year from the reservoir
varied between about 740 and 980 MG a year for 2011–2023, meaning evaporative losses from
the reservoir are in the range of 18%–30% of the water delivered to end users every year. This
volume of evaporation is larger relative to the volume of water that is pumped (as opposed to
gravity fed) from the Waikolu Valley to fill the reservoir: In 2023, the recorded volume of
pumped water was just under 100 MG, implying that significantly reducing the evaporation from
Kualapuu Reservoir could have eliminated the need for supplemental pumping entirely.

Evaporation from open waterbodies is driven by a complex combination of factors, but the major
drivers are solar irradiance, wind speed, air temperature, humidity, and waterbody depth. The
recent AquaPV project by researchers at Idaho National Laboratory and NLR contained
evaporation estimates of hundreds of U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Army Corps of
Engineers, and Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) licensed hydropower reservoirs,
and average annual rates range from about 200 gallons per square meter of surface area for
reservoirs in cool climates to more than 1,000 gallons per square meter for reservoirs in the most
arid parts of the intermountain southwest of the continental United States (“AquaPV” 2025;
Rosenlieb et al. 2025). Although the climate of Molokai does not superficially resemble the
southwest of the continental United States, it is conducive to high evaporation. For example, the
average windspeed at nearby Hoolehua Airport is 14.2 mph, significantly higher than Amarillo,
Texas, which is often called the windiest city in America with an average windspeed of 12.9
mph. It also has higher annual average solar irradiation and air temperature. The only
meteorological factor that does not incline Kualapuu Reservoir to high evaporation rates is
humidity, which remains moderately high all year.

Evaporative Loss Reduction Estimation
The evaporative reductions on a waterbody from an FPV system (and for partial covers
generally) are primarily driven by the shade and protection from the winds that the floats cause;
however, other minor elements such as conductive insulation and disruption of convection air
currents have an effect as well (Assouline et al. 2011). Experimental studies have shown model
FPV systems that cover a significant proportion of the surface area of a pan or tank reduce
evaporation by a similar proportion as the proportion of the surface area that is directly covered
by the FPV floats (Abdelal 2021; Al-Widyan et al. 2021). There are no studies that
experimentally test the evaporation savings of various coverages of FPV systems on larger
waterbodies, and studies that model this find a wide range of expected savings. Some modeling
studies have estimated the reduction in evaporation can exceed the proportion of the waterbody
surface area that is covered, such as a 30% coverage leading to a 50% reduction in evaporation
(Bontempo Scavo et al. 2021). However, this estimate assumes the FPV float entirely covers the
surface of the water below the panels, whereas most FPV installations instead have panels
mounted to floats that partially but not completely cover the surface of the water below. This
configuration is acknowledged in the Bontempo Scavo et al. paper; however, estimates for
evaporation for this configuration are not provided. Another recent paper that more explicitly
models these types of floats finds the expected evaporation is less than the proportion of the
waterbody surface area that is covered, particularly at high coverage ratios (Berlioux et al. 2025).

 38

With the range of modeled values cited in the literature—and with factors that influence
evaporation such as choice of float partially controllable in the design process—we choose to
model evaporation savings to be equal to the proportion of the waterbody surface area that is
covered by the FPV system as is supported by experimental studies. The area of the FPV system
that is relevant corresponds to the float footprints as defined in the “FPV Float Footprint
Method” section and not the total FPV system location, including space between floats from one
another and dam infrastructure as included in the “Capacity Density Method” section.

The footprints used in the FPV Float Footprint Method subsection imply a surface area
requirement of approximately 6,900 meters squared per MWdc of PV capacity, although this will
be influenced by the exact array configurations, float vendors, and panel efficiencies assumed.
For a 9-MWdc FPV system on the reservoir, this would occupy about 62,100 meters squared or
6.2 hectares, compared to the 35.4 hectares of surface area as defined by the National
Hydrography Dataset. With a proportion of 0.175 of the reservoir surface area covered, that
would imply an average annual water conservation effect of 31 MG a year, which, by reference,
would represent more than 31% of the volume of water pumped by the Waikolu Valley pumps in
2023.

Next Steps for FPV Development
Transitioning from an abstract understanding of the amount of FPV the reservoir can support to a
more concrete design of float size, shape, configuration of PV panels and locations and types of
anchoring and mooring typically requires the following types of site surveys and studies:

 • Bathymetry study to understand the depth and shape of the reservoir floor, which will
 impact the position of floats and anchoring and mooring solutions.
 • Geotechnical/hydrogeotechnical survey to evaluate geological and soil conditions both
 under and above water. The primary impact is to understand anchoring options and
 locations as different anchor types are adapted for fastening to various rock and soil
 types.
 • Hydrodynamic survey to understand water flows and currents within the reservoir, with
 impacts on location of float and the anchoring and mooring system.
 • A topographic survey to understand the limitations and options for above ground parts of
 the system such as cabling and a central inverter, if a central inverter is to be used.
Although these studies could be requested and pursued individually, it is recommended that a
firm with previous experience with FPV development is hired to conduct these analyses or
advise on the process of selecting contractors to ensure all relevant factors for FPV development
are included. Once an early development concept of the FPV system can be designed using the
results of these studies, work can begin on the analysis required for the licensing and permitting
of the system, such as an environmental impact assessment. The expected costs associated with
these surveys and engineering steps are included in the “engineering and indirect” portion of the
cost analysis in figure 9 above. Another potential analysis that could help quantify the value of
the system—although not included in the cost estimates—would be a more in-depth evaporation
study using the higher-precision measurements provided by the studies above.

This analysis has shown FPV is likely to be technically feasible on Kualapuu Reservoir at the

Task 3: Pumped Storage Hydropower
This section summarizes the pumped storage hydropower (PSH) investigation conducted for the
island of Molokai as part of the ETIPP project. For a more in-depth analysis and detailed
findings, refer to the report titled Molokai Pumped Storage: Concept Development (DeSomber
forthcoming), published by PNNL. This report serves as the primary resource for technical
details and insights into the efforts undertaken to address Molokai’s energy needs.

Pumped Storage Overview
Pumped storage hydropower is a well-established energy storage technology that helps balance
energy supply and demand on electric grids. It operates by using two water reservoirs located at
different elevations. During low energy demand or when variable energy sources such as solar or
wind produce an excess of electricity, water is pumped from the lower reservoir to the upper
reservoir, storing energy as potential energy. When demand exceeds generation potential, the
stored water is released from the upper reservoir back through turbines to the lower reservoir,
generating energy when needed. See Figure 10.

Pumped storage hydropower provides efficient energy storage and grid stabilization, supporting
the island’s goals of offsetting diesel with local energy sources—such as wind and solar—on the
island grid. Although traditional PSH is typically used in large-scale applications, innovative
smaller-scale designs are now being developed to make the technology more accessible in a
variety of geographic and energy environments. A notable example of smaller-scale 2 use is the

Project Purpose and Objectives
The objective of the pumped storage assessment was to examine and identify potential options
for integrating PSH into the Molokai power grid. The concepts presented represent a range of
potential alternatives, paired with various locations. Small-scale concepts are technically
feasible, though challenges result because of the small scale. Large-scale PSH benefits from
economies of scale, whereas small-scale projects require all the same features but generate less
energy. The goal was to offer community members detailed information about PSH
technologies, enabling them to choose the concepts and locations that best align with their values
and energy needs.

As the project progresses to the next phases, it will remain crucial to continue the dialogue with
community members because their input will be vital in selecting the final design strategy. To
date, the ETIPP effort has developed scalable concepts, compared the cost-effectiveness of
alternatives, and routed solutions along previously disturbed areas to minimize environmental
impacts. Each concept includes a detailed design discussion as well as insights into the next steps
of the process.

Conceptual Development of PSH Systems
The conceptual development of PSH systems for Molokai is organized into three categories, each
designed to address specific site conditions and energy objectives. All proposed alternatives have
been sized to meet the island’s anticipated system gross load in 2035, identified in CERAP 1.0 as
7.24 MW. Typically, pumped storage hydropower systems operate with a duration of 8 to 10
hours; for this analysis, an 8-hour duration was selected as the basis for sizing the storage to
match peak load; this results in 57.92 MWh of energy storage. A 10-hour duration would result
in 72.4 MWh of storage. These results are consistent with E2C’s preliminary findings of 59.4 to
74 MWh of required energy storage. However, a more detailed and comprehensive energy
analysis will be necessary during the formal design phase to refine system requirements and
performance expectations. Specifically, the analysis should focus on how the system is charged.
This will involve evaluating whether the generation sources can supply enough power during the
charging period to adequately store water for energy needs.

Summary of Alternatives
Two categories of PSH alternatives resulted from this study. The first category focused on using
existing infrastructure at the Kualapuu Reservoir, a central facility on Molokai serving the

 45

island’s irrigation needs. Molokai residents expressed significant interest in maximizing the
utility of this existing resource. As previously mentioned, PSH systems require a pair of
reservoirs with a difference in elevation. The Kualapuu Reservoir is envisioned to serve as one of
these reservoirs, minimizing the construction requirements.

Through discussion, the importance of water and using previously impacted areas was identified
as a priority. The second set of concepts used large municipal water tanks separated by large
elevation differences. Locations across the island were evaluated, with priority given to sites
where existing water tanks or smaller irrigation reservoirs were already present. These areas
were preferred because they minimized the impact on less disturbed, natural environments. The
enclosed tank system minimizes evaporation and loss to the environment through seepage,
similar to reservoir type systems, minimizing the overall water use of the system.

In general, PSH systems benefit from greater elevation differences (also called the “head”),
because this allows for more energy to be generated per gallon of water. Higher heads reduce the
overall storage volume required, which minimizes the size of reservoirs or the number of tanks
needed. The elevation difference is a critical factor in optimizing system efficiency while
limiting infrastructure requirements. In addition, the length of the penstock plays a key role in
determining the overall cost of the alternatives. Shorter penstocks are typically more cost-
effective because they reduce material requirements and construction expenses. However, this is
contingent on favorable topography; areas with challenging terrain, such as boulders or cliffs,
may complicate construction.

Finally, the diameter of the penstock is directly influenced by the flow rate of water required. To
ensure efficient operation, the flow rate inside the penstock should generally remain below 20
feet per second. As shown in the analysis, systems with larger storage volumes require higher
flow rates, which in turn result in larger penstock diameters to maintain the criterion of 20 feet
per second. These factors must be carefully balanced to ensure optimal system performance and
cost-efficiency. Table 14 illustrates these relationships and provides a general list of criteria
considered for the development of PSH on Molokai.

West End Alternatives
Two possible two-tank concepts were assessed in West Molokai, which has several
promising sites for PSH. However, these concepts are lower performing and less cost-effective
because of lower head heights than alternative locations in Central and East Molokai.

Maunaloa Concept 1: In the first proposal for the Maunaloa region, storage tanks are integrated
near existing agricultural facilities at the lower end, with additional reservoirs situated closer to
the Maunaloa Reservoir. This design features a penstock length of 1 mile, leveraging nearby
roadways to achieve an elevation gain of 370 feet. However, generating 7.24 MW of energy over
an 8-hour period presents challenges with water and penstock requirements. The penstock would
need a diameter of 50 inches and a total water storage capacity of 56 million gallons. To achieve
this, approximately 12 tanks, each with a capacity of 5 million gallons, are needed for both the
upper and lower reservoirs. This is not realistic and poses scalability constraints. This site is
located approximately 7 miles from the Palaau power plant.

Maunaloa Concept 2: The second Maunaloa concept uses a ravine north of the Maunaloa
Reservoir as the primary routing for the penstock, providing an elevation difference of 800 feet.
This increased elevation reduces the necessary penstock diameter to 34 inches and requires only
five tanks per reservoir, making it more resource-efficient than Concept 1. The lower reservoir
tanks would be positioned within the ravine for reduced visual and environmental impact, though
access roads would need to be built for maintenance and operational purposes. Although this
concept outperforms the first in terms of resource optimization, environmental concerns
regarding disturbances in the ravine and construction of access roads should be carefully
considered. This alternative also lies approximately 7 miles from the Palaau power plant.

 • Manilla Camp: The Manilla Camp proposal places lower tanks just above the Manilla

 the Kualapuu Reservoir pipeline. An elevation difference of 1,100 feet is achieved,

 • Kawela: Kawela stands out with the largest elevation gain among all options, providing a

 further evaluation in subsequent design phases. With a favorable length-to-head ratio of
 6.7 (nearing the ideal value of 6), this concept demonstrates efficiency both in design and
 hydraulic performance. Kawela is approximately 6.5 miles from the Palaau power plant.

East End Alternatives
A single option was explored for the eastern portion of the island. This region represents the
farthest distance from the Palaau power plant, which will lead to higher interconnection costs.
Grid upgrades would be required throughout Central and East Molokai to accommodate the
interconnection of an energy project of this size.

Ualapue: The Ualapue concept locates a storage tank in the central part of Ualapue. The area’s
steep yet consistent topography allows for an elevation gain of approximately 1,500 feet over
just over 1 mile. The topography reduces penstock length while maintaining a uniform gradient
to minimize fluctuations in internal water pressure. The system would use a 26-inch penstock
and three 5-million-gallon tanks. Although the length-to-head ratio of 4.1 is lower than the ideal
value of 6, the smooth gradient compensates for this limitation by reducing pressure instabilities.
As a result, Ualapue is considered a technically viable option. Ualapue is approximately 16 miles
from the Palaau power plant.

 For batteries, calendar life depends on factors such as ambient temperature and state of

It is important to note that the design life of the PSH system is 40–50 years, 4 whereas the BESS

guidelines and operational requirements. A minimum rest period of 0.65 hours is required before
and after each discharge cycle. O&M services were not included in this analysis.

Tax Credits and Incentives
There are substantial federal tax credits available that apply to the development of PSH. These
measures, aimed at increasing the economic feasibility of energy storage investments, enable
developers and communities to tap into credits ranging from 6% to 50% of total project costs for
projects that begin construction by the end of 2033 (Boff et al. 2024). The magnitude of these tax
credits varies by factors such as location and the use of domestically produced materials. These
incentives represent substantial funding opportunities, positioning PSH as a key technology for
energy storage and grid resiliency, and have the potential to significantly impact the economic
value of a PSH system to stakeholders on Molokai. Continued analysis and optimization of these
tax credits will be essential to determining the financial viability of pumped storage development
on Molokai. This analysis is a key component of an economic analysis as detailed in Table 18
below.

Licensing Requirements
At the beginning of the project, systems were classified as either open-loop or closed-loop.
Open-loop systems use natural water flows, whereas closed-loop systems are isolated from water
flows in the surrounding environment. This categorization was essential for determining the
appropriate FERC licensing process for the PSH concepts. Open-loop systems typically follow
the “Standard Licensing Process,” which takes an average of 6.8 years (with a variation of ±2.6
years). Closed-loop systems qualify for the “Expedited Licensing Process,” which is estimated to
take approximately 2 years. These licensing timelines represent significant upfront costs and
time commitments.

FERC’s objective is to maintain reliable, efficient, and safe infrastructure for the interstate
transmission of electricity, natural gas, and oil while ensuring public welfare. However, these
grids in Hawaii’s situation operate independently of interstate networks. Recognizing Hawaii’s
unique energy composition, FERC and the state have entered a Memorandum of Understanding
to foster collaboration and share information. This agreement is designed to support Hawaii’s
pursuit of its RPS and the modernization of its grid. It reflects a mutual commitment to
advancing policies and planning that prioritize Hawaii’s energy goals. Though not fully
applicable, certain checks within the FERC licensing process may be useful to develop a safe and
reliable pumped storage system, even though formal licensing requirements may not apply.

Concept Selection
The set of nine PSH concepts needed to be reduced for actionable decisions to follow from the
analysis. The development and downselection of PSH concepts for Molokai emphasized
community collaboration to ensure alignment with local priorities, cultural values, and
environmental considerations. These priorities and values, enumerated in more detail below,
were given by community stakeholders and are primarily based upon those outlined through the
CERAP process. The engagement process involved three main stakeholders: the PNNL design
team, MCEH, and Shake Energy Collaborative. Together, these groups facilitated a feedback
loop that used analysis produced by the lab team for the community led refinement of nine initial
PSH concepts based on community input.

Key themes emerged during discussions, shaping the design criteria:

 56

 • Location. The community strongly supported placing PSH infrastructure closer to
 populated areas to foster accessibility and a sense of ownership. However, concerns were
 raised regarding potential flooding risks associated with systems located above residential
 zones.
 • Scale and cost. Residents expressed interest in community-scale, distributed projects and
 raised questions about how system size impacts cost-effectiveness.
 • Water sources. Emphasizing the Hawaiian phrase “Ola i ka wai” (“water is life”),
 participants viewed water as a vital cultural and environmental resource. They advocated
 for minimal water loss in the designs and closely examined the sources proposed for each
 system.
 • Cultural and environmental impact. Potential impacts on local wildlife—including
 deer populations—and the safety of placing infrastructure in hunting locations were
 highlighted.
 • Infrastructure reuse. Existing infrastructure was identified as an opportunity to
 minimize environmental disruption. Although not covered in this report, the community
 was curious if treated wastewater might be part of the design, illustrating sustainable
 water use.
 • Aesthetics. Desire for designs that minimize visual impact, with preference for
 underground infrastructure to reduce visual impact where feasible.
Following this engagement, the following two concepts were pursued:

 • Kualapuu Reservoir (upper) concept. This concept leverages the existing irrigation
 reservoir at Kualapuu as the upper reservoir, minimizing the need for new infrastructure.
 The lower reservoir, which will house the powerhouse, is located near the Palaau power
 plant, where the grid interconnection will be established. The use of an existing water
 source ensures resource efficiency and reduces environmental impact. In addition, its
 central location fosters community involvement and ownership of the project.
 • Kamiloloa Ahupuaʻa concepts. Three potential designs were explored near Kamiloloa
 Ahupuaʻa, including sites at Manilla Camp and Kawela. These options benefit from their
 relative proximity to the Palaau power plant interconnection, which reduces logistical
 complexity. The topography of this region provides significant head differentials,
 enabling smaller infrastructure requirements such as reduced reservoir volumes and
 narrower penstock diameters—further optimizing project cost and environmental
 footprint. Like the Kualapuu concept, these options are also centrally located, reinforcing
 community engagement and ownership.
Moving forward, continued community dialogue will be essential as final designs are refined,
ensuring culturally and environmentally respectful solutions that meet Molokai’s future energy
goals.

Next Steps
The study is expected to advance the project to a formal design stage. As large capital investment
projects with significant permitting requirements feasibility studies are particularly important for
PSH to be able to demonstrate the ability to satisfy regulatory requirements and acquire funding.
To achieve a comprehensive feasibility study—which should be the primary goal of the 30%
design phase and provide greater accuracy in cost estimates—the following steps would typically
be required for a PSH project and would need to be included in the scope as a minimum:

 1. Energy demand and generation analysis. Conducted over a 3- to 6-month period, this
 analysis will evaluate grid load behaviors to understand energy usage patterns. Key areas
 of focus will include identifying peak demand periods, understanding daily fluctuations,
 and assessing seasonal variability across the grid. In addition, an in-depth evaluation of
 potential generation sources, such as solar and wind power, will be necessary to
 understand capacity factors and anticipated operational behaviors locally. This analysis
 should also explore strategies to ensure Palaau serves effectively as a reliable backup
 generation source. The objectives of this phase are to determine the optimal sizing for the
 PSH system and quantify the capacities of supporting generation infrastructure required
 for efficient system charging, load generation, and reliable grid operations.
 2. Site assessment and feasibility study. This phase establishes a physical layout of all
 major PSH system components, which include penstocks, reservoirs (or tanks), a
 powerhouse, a switchyard, and access roads detailed. Designs for the physical layout of
 equipment within the powerhouse will be developed. A thorough constructability analysis
 will evaluate logistical factors, access routes, staging needs, and construction risks; a
 cost-benefit evaluation will guide the economic viability development of the PSH system.
 3. Civil and hydraulic engineering analysis. This analysis will be necessary to determine
 the feasibility of construction for the selected powerhouse location and the penstock
 routing. Site surveys and geotechnical, hydrological, and environmental studies will
 identify suitable locations for anchoring the foundation of the powerhouse and anchoring
 thrust blocks to support loads caused by changes in direction of the penstock. These
 analyses will evaluate terrain stability, soil and bedrock conditions, and hydrological
 dynamics to ensure safe construction and reliable long-term operation. Hydraulic
 modeling will assess transient pressures, surges, and stresses within the penstock system
 under various operational scenarios, allowing optimization of flow and operational
 efficiency. This phase will also evaluate penstock materials and ratings to ensure costs
 are reduced where appropriate.
 4. Permitting, environmental, and cultural assessments. In parallel with the development
 of site-specific considerations discussed during the conceptual design refinement phase,
 comprehensive environmental and cultural assessments will be conducted over a 3- to 6-
 month period. These assessments are essential to ensure regulatory compliance, safeguard
 Molokai’s unique ecosystems, and respect its cultural heritage while minimizing the

Interactivity Dependencies
PSH Using Kualapuu Reservoir and Floating PV
A PSH alternative using Kualapuu Reservoir and an FPV system on Kualapuu Reservoir would
impose certain limitations on the design and operations of one another. For an FPV installation,
the frequent high water level variation caused by the PSH operation could pose more demanding
requirements on the anchoring and mooring system, which could present as higher capital
expenditures or O&M costs. It could also require the construction of a new intake/outtake
structure on the reservoir, creating an additional location where FPV equipment should not be
installed. For the PSH system, the presence of FPV floats on the water could impose tighter
limits on the minimum water elevation than would otherwise be present, restricting PSH
operation during times of low water elevation in the reservoir.

None of these factors is an insurmountable challenge. The concept of FPV on a reservoir used
for PSH is not unheard of; it is being designed for a concept system in Idaho by Cat Creek
Energy (Cat Creek Energy n.d). However, we believe the potential additional challenges and
constraints imposed would make using Kualapuu for both concepts to be less preferred to
alternatives that don’t—other factors being equal. The cost modeling exercise indicated the
Kualapuu as a lower reservoir PSH alternative should not be preferred. However, considering all
factors, the Kualapuu as upper reservoir was included as one of the community’s two preferred
alternatives. This specific configuration should be evaluated during the engineering survey and
preliminary design phase for both FPV and PSH so any large obstacles can be identified and
evaluated early.

Timing of PSH Grid Storage and Capacity Additions
As reported by Hawaiian Electric, the Molokai grid is currently in a state where it experiences
the “duck curve” phenomenon, where high penetrations of solar generation on the grid cause
large swings in the electricity generation required by other sources as the sun sets in the evening
or otherwise when solar generation on the grid stops producing energy. This existing abundance
of solar generation on the grid and associated grid congestion means in many places on the grid,
new DER installations are not allowed to export to the grid—which is reflected by system export
to the grid not being considered in the economic analysis of our critical infrastructure systems in
Task 1: Critical Infrastructure Backup Energy Analysis. Reducing this phenomenon requires
some way of shifting either electricity demand or supply on the grid, which can take the form of
the addition of solar generation being combined with other generation sources that are either
dispatchable or have complementary generation profiles, the use of policies or incentives to
move grid demand to times of high solar supply on the grid, or the use of energy storage to allow
solar generation to be used by the grid when the PV modules are not generating.

The analysis shows Kualapuu Reservoir can support an FPV system large enough to serve as the
generation source of most of the grid’s energy needs. For instance, the model 9-MWdc FPV
plant would be expected to produce 16.6 GWh annually on average according to our estimates,
which is 42.6% of the Molokai grid’s forecasted annual energy use in 2050. Similarly, the
analysis shows the topography of Molokai could support a PSH system large enough to serve as
the primary energy storage system on the Molokai grid—a 7.24-MW PSH system would be able

 62

to store more than 21 GWh of energy annually by completing one full charge and discharge
cycle daily.

If these concepts are pursued to serve these roles as the largest solar generation and storage
plants on the island, planning the timing of their construction and commissioning so they are
both available to the grid at the same time is important. A large FPV system available before the
completion of PSH (and in the absence of another large storage system) would exacerbate the
duck curve phenomenon and represent significantly more energy than the grid could accept. A
large PSH system without the large solar generating source would not have the excess energy
available that it was designed to store and may not be able to store energy at all when excess
energy on the grid is below the system’s minimum designed pumping capacity. This coordinated
timeline may present challenges related to the different permitting and construction timelines
between the FPV and PSH technologies. With both the federal FERC permitting requirements
and the large civil works required, the timeline to commission a PSH system would be expected
to be considerably longer than that for FPV. As a less mature technology and one that has not yet
been piloted in the state of Hawaii, there are uncertainties in the commissioning timeline of FPV
as well, despite its shorter expected timeline.

Takeaways and Conclusions
The analysis described in this report has found the three CERAP projects analyzed pass the first-
order feasibility criteria that can be assessed in a desktop study, and it would be appropriate to
move forward with engineering surveys and studies required for a full feasibility study. It cannot
be certainly stated the projects as defined are technically feasible because the assessment of site
conditions with specialized tools may reveal unknown project risks, such as the roof integrity of
a facility being unable to support the load of a rooftop PV system or the geological conditions
surrounding Kualapuu Reservoir making anchoring solutions difficult. Just because a project is
technically feasible does not mean it is necessarily desirable—and each project has its own
identified advantages, disadvantages, and potential risks relative to their alternatives. The report
presents as thorough an analysis as possible with available data and represents an important step
in verifying and contextualizing the community goals of CERAP before moving on to further
steps of engineering study and concept development. To the extent that the analysis shows these
projects may help meet the values and goals of the CERAP process, further study of these
projects could be pursued with on-site surveys and studies as relevant to the respective projects.

Beyond the technical feasibility and design of the individual projects, the implementation of the
projects will represent a transformative shift to the Molokai grid and energy system. This large-
scale shift in energy generation technology is ultimately a goal of both the Molokai community
as expressed in CERAP 1.0 as well as by Hawaiian Electric in its Integrated Grid Planning
process and report, although specific pathways differ. The IGP report notes Hawaiian Electric’s
future grid plan for Molokai is subject to change depending on results of the CERAP planning
process. Currently, however, Hawaiian Electric’s IGP considers BESS as the only energy storage
option for Molokai. The IGP also identifies single-axis ground-mounted PV or DERs as the only
solar generation option for the island. This is not an indication that PSH and FPV cannot be
suitable alternatives; however, this analysis does not attempt to clarify the extent to which the
combination of these projects has different impacts on the grid as those listed in the IGP.

The use of Kualapuu Reservoir for FPV and PSH expands the number of potential stakeholders
relative to ground-mounted PV and batteries, including the HDOA, which manages the reservoir,
the MIS system user advisory board, and other MIS system users. Permission to access the
reservoir and the other publicly owned and managed sites identified for critical infrastructure
projects will be required to undergo the site study and survey processes needed to continue with
concept design and final decisions. In addition, because no FPV project has been permitted in the
state of Hawaii, there are uncertainties about how existing regulations for energy and
construction projects could be applied for this first-of-its-kind project. Although no aspect of
permitting energy projects in the state (as described by the Hawaii State Energy Office
Renewable Energy Permitting Wizard 8) would appear to pose a special concern for FPV
projects, there are some additional layers of approval that an FPV system would be subject to
when compared to a ground-mounted alternative such as the need to show the development does
not alter the reservoir’s compliance with dam safety regulations. Even if the FPV project can be
designed to meet all relevant regulations, as expected, the extra steps required during the
permitting process and its first-of-its-kind nature could lengthen its permitting timeline. The

Appendix A: Floating Photovoltaics Operations and
Maintenance Costs
The costs associated with operations and maintenance (O&M) of floating photovoltaics (FPV) is
an area of active research. The exponential growth of global FPV capacity means most FPV
installations do not have long operational histories. For instance, more than 93% of the global 7.7
gigawatts of FPV capacity as of the end of 2023 were less than 5 years old and more than 50%
were less than 2 years old (Şahin et al. 2025). Because of this, values used for FPV O&M costs
are estimates and not empirical values. A recent study that reviewed O&M cost estimates for
FPV found that sources variously cite FPV costs as being lower, similar, or higher than those of
comparable ground-mounted photovoltaic (GPV) systems (Walpita et al. 2025).

During interviews conducted as part of NLR’s cost model, FPV developers did not provide a
consensus on O&M costs, but several suggested reason to believe it could even be lower than
GPV because of less need for vegetation management, lower soiling, and easier soiling
management because of the proximity to water (Ramasamy and Margolis 2021). Similar reasons
were cited in a previous review that independently predicted FPV O&M costs could be lower
than GPV (Sahu et al. 2016). Given other potential challenges with O&M for FPV, such as the
requirement for underwater inspections and maintenance, the NLR cost model review did not
find these reasons to be compelling enough to consider maintenance to be lower cost for FPV
than GPV. The lower O&M cost for FPV systems cited in the study of 15.5 $/kilowatt (kW;
compared to 18 $/kW for GPV) is because of the assumption of lower land leasing costs and not
because of an assumption of lower maintenance costs. However, land leasing costs are
considered in other sources cited here as a component of operational expenditures (OPEX) but
not costs related to normal O&M of the plant. If defined this way for consistency, the NLR FPV
cost benchmark study found OPEX costs of FPV may be lower than GPV if land acquisitions
costs are lower but that O&M costs are similar.

An assumption of similar O&M costs was also determined after a discussion of both advantages
and disadvantages of O&M for FPV in a study of potential on Spanish reservoirs (Micheli 2021).
Other studies have estimated higher O&M and/or OPEX costs for FPV. The largest premium
found in the literature estimated FPV OPEX costs (O&M costs not described separately) were
more than 7 times higher than GPV at 10% vs. 1.5% of capital expenditures (CAPEX) per year;
however, this is an assumption described as a “conservative approach” by the authors (Oliveira-
Pinto and Stokkermans 2020). In several studies, O&M for FPV is cited as twice as high as GPV
($26/kW vs. $13/kW); in the study where this claim originates, it is also an assumed value with
the short explanation of “…[T]he presence of mooring lines and anchors can also mean that the
O&M can be higher, due to the need for inspection underwater” (Martins n.d.). A detailed case
study on five systems in Iran found FPV O&M costs were expected to be higher than GPV, but
by only 2%–4% (Fereshtehpour et al. 2021). In another study, the total OPEX cost (O&M costs
were not reported separately) for FPV systems was listed at $51.64/kW based on offers from
FPV developers, which would represent a significant increase over the U.S. utility-scale GPV
average of $16.12/kW (Tina et al. 2023; Ramasamy et al. 2023). However, the OPEX cost for
GPV in the same paper was also $51.64/kW, suggesting the study understood costs to be similar
between FPV and GPV and the high absolute numbers may be because of assumptions of other
components of OPEX such as leasing or insurance costs.

 72

The most conservative estimates, such as yearly OPEX costs being 10% of CAPEX costs, were
considered broadly inconsistent with NLR’s discussions with FPV developers and the rapid rate
at which it is now being deployed globally. Likely, FPV has several potential benefits and
challenges that affect O&M costs that cumulatively do not generally differ from GPV enough to
radically affect system economic viability. OPEX costs are generally a small portion of the total
levelized cost of photovoltaic (PV) systems, making the economic feasibility of PV systems less
sensitive to changes in OPEX vs. CAPEX. For instance, in NLR’s FPV cost benchmark study,
the total levelized cost of the example 10-megawatts direct current FPV system is $25.93
million, of which OPEX costs of $15.5/kilowatts direct current (kWdc) per year are $4.65
million, or 17.9% (Ramasamy and Margolis 2021).

The literature indicates uncertainty of O&M costs for FPV systems, which is a potential
disadvantage of the FPV alternative. Many FPV developers and vendors offer O&M contracts at
the time of project development. A contract with a preset rate could be one way to mitigate the
risk of unexpected O&M costs. In any case, a reasonable estimate for FPV O&M costs must be
used for the Molokai community to understand the potential trade-offs of the FPV alternative.
Therefore, O&M costs are estimated to be 15% higher than an equivalent fixed-tilt ground-
mounted system. This is a more reasonable conservative estimate than methods that increase
assumed O&M costs by a factor of 2 or more based on the available data. This estimate is also
informed by the range of O&M costs considered by World Bank’s comprehensive FPV market
report cost analysis, where an O&M cost of 15% more than GPV is the highest value considered
in the sensitivity analysis (World Bank n.d.).

The most recent cost benchmark from NLR estimates average U.S. OPEX for “utility-scale”
(larger than 5 megawatts) single-axis tracking PV to be $16.12/kWdc per year in 2022 USD.
Approximately $6.60/kWdc of this is because of non-O&M-related costs, leaving $9.52/kWdc
for O&M costs. A fixed-tilt system would be expected to have lower O&M costs; however, NLR
no longer produces cost estimates for utility-scaled fixed-tilt systems. Extrapolating from the
difference between single-axis tracking and fixed-tilt systems the last year when both estimates
were produced (Q1 of 2021), we would expect costs for fixed-tilt systems to be roughly
$1.50/kWdc less per year, or about $8.00/kWdc per year (Ramasamy et al. 2021). Therefore,
average U.S. O&M costs for FPV are estimated to be approximately $9.60/kWdc, or $12/kWac
for the model FPV system with a 1.25 inverter load ratio.

As an absolute value, this does not consider any factors particular to the state of Hawaii, much
less the Island of Molokai specifically. Stakeholders from Molokai Clean Energy Hui expressed
concern that cost estimates tailored for the United States are often incorrect for the Molokai
context and that, via the input of local PV developers, they understand the O&M costs that
should be expected in Molokai for fixed-tilt GPV. As such, stakeholders may apply the 15% cost
inflation factor to their estimate of the O&M costs for the GPV alternative to estimate the
expected O&M cost of the FPV alternative.
