Isolation of feline-derived scFvs against the VP1-CDE region of feline calicivirus from a phage display library and characterization of their antigen-binding and antiviral potential in vitro
Authors: Yanquan Wei, Hongmei Wang, Zhen Han, Huitian Gou, Dongdong Sun, Yihan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFeline calicivirus (FCV) is a significant causative pathogen of upper respiratory tract disease and virulent systemic infection in feline populations, yet specific targeted antiviral agents for FCV infection remain unavailable.MethodsA feline-derived single-chain variable fragment (scFv) phage display library targeting the VP1-CDE neutralizing region of FCV was constructed using peripheral blood mononuclear cells isolated from cats immunized with recombinant VP1-CDE protein. Positive scFv clones were screened via three rounds of affinity panning and phage ELISA. The scFv-Fc fusion proteins were expressed in eukaryotic cells, and their antigen-binding properties and antiviral potential were characterized through Western blotting, bio-layer interferometry, immunofluorescence assay and viral neutralization assay.ResultsThe constructed library reached a size of 2.1 × 108 independent phages with favorable sequence diversity. Five unique scFv clones, designated 3A5, 3B8, 3H5, 2H5, and 2I5, were identified. All five clones could specifically bind to both recombinant VP1-CDE protein and FCV-infected F81 feline kidney cells. Viral neutralization assays demonstrated that scFv-Fc-3A5 exhibited the most potent neutralizing activity against the genogroup II FCV LZ2023 isolate (IC50= 1.862 µg/mL), scFv-Fc-2H5 showed weak neutralization (IC50= 6.168 µg/mL), and the remaining three clones had no appreciable neutralizing capacity.ConclusionsscFv-Fc-3A5 and scFv-Fc-2H5 are promising candidate molecules for the development of diagnostic reagents and antiviral therapeutics targeting genogroup II FCV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05702-6.Keywords: Feline calicivirus, VP1-CDE protein, Single-chain variable fragment, Phage display technology

Introduction

Feline calicivirus (FCV), a member of the genus Vesivirus within the family Caliciviridae, is a globally distributed pathogen responsible for infectious upper respiratory tract disease in domestic and wild felids [1, 2]. The clinical spectrum of FCV infection ranges from mild signs, including oral ulceration, sneezing, nasal discharge, and coughing, to severe, often fatal systemic disease caused by virulent systemic FCV (VS-FCV) strains, which are characterized by high fever, subcutaneous edema, visceral necrosis, and mortality rates of 50%-79% [3–5]. FCV is a nonenveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus with a genome of approximately 7.7 kb that is organized into three open reading frames (ORFs). ORF2 encodes the major capsid protein VP1, which mediates host cell attachment and entry and constitutes the principal immunogenic determinant of the virion [6]. Structural and functional analyses have delineated six regions (A-F) within VP1, among which the CDE region harbors the dominant neutralizing epitopes [7]. In particular, the E region is critical for engagement of the feline junctional adhesion molecule A (fJAM-A) receptor and for the induction of protective neutralizing antibody responses [8, 9]. Despite considerable progress in preventive vaccination and diagnostic methodologies, the absence of specific antiviral therapeutics continues to represent a critical gap in the clinical management of FCV infection [10].

Single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) are recombinant antibodies composed of the variable domains of the immunoglobulin heavy (VH) and light (VL) chains joined by a flexible (Gly4Ser)3 linker. Owing to their small size, scFvs exhibit superior tissue penetration, reduced immunogenicity, and facile production relative to full-length monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) while retaining the antigen-binding specificity conferred by the three complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) within the VH and VL domains [11–14]. Phage display technology provides a robust and versatile platform for the construction of high-diversity scFv immune libraries and the subsequent affinity-driven selection of antigen-specific clones through iterative rounds of biopanning (target immobilization, library incubation, washing, elution, and amplification) [15, 16]. The filamentous M13 bacteriophage is the vector of choice for such applications because of its nonlytic life cycle and exceptional stability under a wide range of physicochemical conditions [17, 18]. This approach has been successfully harnessed to isolate neutralizing scFvs against a diverse array of viral pathogens, including SARS-CoV-2, rabies virus, and African swine fever virus [19–24].

Despite these technological advances, notable gaps remain in the development of FCV-specific scFvs. First, the majority of existing FCV antibody research has focused on full-length mAbs, with comparatively few studies describing scFvs that target the VP1-CDE neutralizing determinant. Second, feline-derived scFvs are particularly scarce; murine-derived mAbs, while widely employed, carry an inherent risk of immunogenicity when administered to feline patients. Third, the lack of a comprehensively characterized feline-derived scFv library directed against FCVs impedes the development of species-matched rapid diagnostic reagents and therapeutic antibody candidates. To address these limitations, the present study was undertaken with the following objectives: (i) to construct a feline-derived scFv phage display library enriched for binders against the FCV VP1-CDE protein and (ii) to identify and functionally characterize scFv clones with high antigen-binding activity and potent virus-neutralizing capacity. Through iterative panning and a panel of orthogonal validation assays, we report the isolation and in vitro characterization of feline scFv candidates with promising antiviral activity against FCVs.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Competent E. coli BL21(DE3), SS320, NEB5α F’, and M13KO7 helper phage cells were purchased from New England Biolabs. Ni-affinity chromatography resin and protein A resin were supplied by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. HRP-conjugated anti-M13 antibody and HRP-conjugated IgG-Fc antibody were purchased from Sino Biological, Inc. Cy3-conjugated anti-human IgG-Fc was obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich. The pComb3XSS vector, pFUSE-Fc vector, and F81 cells were preserved in our laboratory. Two adult male cats, owned by Lanzhou Aibokang Pet Hospital, were housed at the Lanzhou Institute of Husbandry and Pharmaceutical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The Feline Peripheral Blood Lymphocyte Isolation Kit was obtained from Solarbio (Beijing) Science & Technology Co., Ltd. The FCV LZ2023 strain, which is phylogenetically classified as a genogroup II strain, was preserved in our laboratory, and its VP1 gene sequence was deposited in GenBank (accession No. PZ105981).

Primer design

On the basis of the literature reported by Lu Z et al., feline-derived antibody gene sequences were retrieved from GenBank (accession numbers: KY795030–KY795318) [25]. Relatively conserved sequences were identified by aligning the VH and the variable region of the λ light chain (Vλ). Amplification primers for VH and Vλ were designed via SnapGene software (Table 1). All primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Prokaryotic expression and purification of the recombinant VP1-CDE protein

Total RNA was extracted from the FCV LZ2023 strain via TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 5 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with an oligo (dT) primer. The resulting cDNA was used as a template to amplify the VP1-CDE gene with the primer pair CDE-F (5’-gcggcagccatatggctagcAAGAAGGGAGCCAAACTAG-3’) and CDE-R (5’-tggtggtggtggtgctcgagTGAACCGATGACCTCTTCGC-3’). The recombinant pET-28a-VP1-CDE plasmid was constructed via homologous recombination between the NheI and XhoI sites of the linearized vector via the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). The recombinant product was subsequently transformed into E. coli DH5α, and positive clones were identified via colony PCR via the T7 promoter and T7 terminator primers. Plasmids extracted from positive clones were further subjected to Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotech) to confirm sequence correctness. Heat shock was used to transform E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells, which were spread on 50 µg/mL kanamycin-containing solid LB media and incubated overnight at 37 °C. A single colony was inoculated into kanamycin-containing LB media and cultured overnight. The culture mixture was transferred to fresh LB medium; when the OD600 reached 0.6, 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) was added overnight induction at 16 °C. After induction, the culture was centrifuged to discard the supernatant; the bacterial pellets were resuspended in PBS, ultrasonically disrupted on ice, and the lysate was centrifuged to separate the supernatant and precipitate. The VP1-CDE protein in the supernatant was purified by Ni-affinity chromatography and identified by SDS‒PAGE and Western blotting using anti-FCV feline serum as the primary antibody.

Animal immunization and serum antibody titer determination

Two adult domestic cats, confirmed to be clinically healthy and seronegative for FCV, were immunized with the purified VP1-CDE protein. The immunization regimen consisted of a primary immunization followed by four booster immunizations administered at 14-day intervals. For the primary immunization, 500 µg of VP1-CDE protein was emulsified in an equal volume of Freund’s complete adjuvant and injected subcutaneously at multiple sites over the dorsal scapular and lumbar regions. Subsequent booster immunizations were performed using the same antigen dose emulsified in Freund’s incomplete adjuvant. Blood samples (~ 1 mL) were collected from the saphenous vein 14 days after each immunization to monitor the development of VP1-CDE-specific serum antibodies via indirect ELISA, with VP1-CDE used as the coating antigen (100 ng/well). When the serum antibody titer exceeded 1:16,000 (corresponding to an OD450 sample-to-negative ratio > 10), terminal whole-blood collection was performed. For this procedure, the cats were sedated with dexmedetomidine (20 µg/kg, intramuscular) and butorphanol (0.2 mg/kg, intramuscular) to minimize stress and discomfort. Whole blood (~ 20–30 mL) was drawn via jugular venipuncture, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were immediately isolated via a feline peripheral blood lymphocyte isolation kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated PBMCs were pooled and used to construct the phage display scFv library. Humane endpoints were predefined to include injection site reactions exceeding 2 cm in diameter, persistent fever (> 39.7 °C for > 48 h), or weight loss greater than 15% of the baseline body weight. No cats reached any of these humane endpoints during the study, and all the animals were returned to the owner in good health following terminal blood collection.

Construction of the phage display scFv library

Total RNA was extracted from pooled feline PBMCs via TRIzol™ Reagent, and 5 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA via oligo (dT) primers. PBMCs isolated from the two immunized cats were pooled in equal numbers prior to total RNA extraction. This pooling strategy was employed to maximize the diversity of the recovered immunoglobulin repertoire for library construction. Using the synthesized cDNA as a template, the VH and Vλ genes were amplified separately with two specific primer pairs (FeIGHV-F/FeIGHV-R for VH amplification and FeIGLV-F/FeIGLV-R for Vλ amplification), followed by purification of the target amplicons. Overlapping PCR was performed with the VH and Vλ genes as templates and the FeIGHV-F/FeIGLV-R primer pair to assemble the two fragments via a (Gly4Ser)3 linker and generate full-length scFv genes. The resulting scFv fragments were purified and inserted into the XbaI and EcoRI sites of the pComb3XSS phagemid vector via homologous recombination using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme), with 23 bp overlapping homology arms designed at the 5’ ends of the amplification primers (Table 1). The scFv was expressed as a C-terminal fusion to the gene III minor coat protein (pIII) of the M13 phage, and the recombinant pComb3XSS phagemid vector carrying the scFv gene was electroporated into E. coli SS320 competent cells. The electroporated bacterial suspension was transferred to 30 mL of antibiotic-free 2YT medium and cultured at 37 °C for 1 h. M13KO7 helper phage was added at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10:1 (phage: bacteria), and the mixture was further incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking for 1 h to ensure efficient helper phage infection. A 100 µL aliquot was serially diluted 103–105-fold and plated on 2YT solid media, and single colonies were counted to determine the phage library titer (formula: Library titer = Number of single colonies × dilution factor × total volume of bacterial suspension). Twenty-four single colonies were randomly selected for PCR verification of scFv gene insertion, and PCR products of the expected size were directly sequenced. The remaining suspension was transferred to 300 mL of 2YT medium supplemented with ampicillin and kanamycin and cultured overnight at 37 °C. The next day, the culture was centrifuged to collect the supernatant; one-quarter volume of PEG 8000/NaCl solution was added, followed by a 30 min incubation on ice. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the phage pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS to obtain the primary scFv phage display library.

Panning of the phage-displayed scFv library

The plates were coated with the VP1-CDE protein (1 µg/well) at 4°C overnight, with four VP1-CDE-coated wells and four PBST-coated negative control wells. The next day, the wells were emptied, blocked with 5% skim milk at 37°C for 2 h, and washed three times with PBST. Then, 100 µL of primary phage library was added to each well, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Nonspecific phages were removed by 8–10 PBST washes. One hundred microliters of 0.1 M HCl elution buffer (pH 3.0) was added, followed by incubation for 5 min, and the eluate was immediately neutralized with a 1/4 volume of 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 9.1). The solution was transferred to a 1 mL E. coli NEB5α F’ suspension and cultured at 37 °C for 1 h, after which 2 µL of M13KO7 helper phage was added for another 1 h of incubation. The suspension was transferred to 35 mL ampicillin/kanamycin-supplemented 2YT media. This panning was repeated two to three times; after each round, the eluted phages were serially diluted and plated on 2YT solid medium to determine the recovery ratio and enrichment fold.

Phage ELISA: Plates were coated with VP1-CDE (100 ng/well) at 4 °C overnight and blocked with 5% skim milk at 37 °C for 2 h. Third-round enriched phages were serially diluted and plated; 92 single colonies were randomly picked, and each colony was inoculated into 1 mL of 2YT medium containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin in a 96-deep-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until the bacterial culture reached an OD600 of 0.4–0.6 (approximately 2–3 h). M13KO7 helper phage was added to each well at an MOI of 10:1, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking for 1 h to allow helper phage infection. Kanamycin was added to each well to a final concentration of 50 µg/mL, and the plate was incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The next day, the plate was centrifuged at 4,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant containing the monoclonal phage particles was collected. A 150 µL aliquot of the supernatant was added to each well pre-coated with VP1-CDE and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After five washes with PBST, an HRP-conjugated anti-M13 antibody (diluted 1:8,000 in PBST) was added and incubated for 1 h. Following five further washes with PBST, 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added, the reaction was terminated with 2 M H2SO4, and the OD450 absorbance values were measured via a microplate reader.

Eukaryotic expression of scFv-Fc and Western blot analysis

The selected scFv genes were individually cloned and inserted into the pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc2 eukaryotic expression vector (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) via homologous recombination to generate the recombinant pFUSE-Fc-scFv plasmids. These constructs were transiently transfected into Expi293F suspension cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Five to seven days post-transfection, the culture supernatant was harvested, and the secreted scFv-Fc fusion proteins were purified via protein A affinity chromatography. The molecular weight and purity of the purified proteins were assessed by SDS‒PAGE under reducing conditions. Western blot analysis was performed to confirm the binding specificity of the scFv‒Fc fusion proteins for the VP1‒CDE antigen and native FCV particles. Briefly, the recombinant VP1-CDE protein, FCV suspension, and feline parvovirus (FPV) VP2 protein (negative control) were resolved via SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with each purified scFv-Fc protein (1:500 dilution) as the primary antibody, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG-Fc secondary antibody (1:8,000 dilution). Specific binding was visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

Evaluation of the affinity of the five scFvs-Fc with the VP1-CDE protein via biolayer interferometry (BLI)

The binding affinities of the five scFv-Fc fusion proteins to recombinant VP1-CDE were determined via BLI via an Octet RED96e system (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) at 25 °C with continuous shaking at 1,000 rpm. All the assays were performed in black 96-well microplates with a working volume of 200 µL per well. The assay buffer consisted of PBS supplemented with 0.02% (v/v) Tween-20 and 0.1% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA). Protein A (ProA) biosensors were prehydrated in assay buffer for 10 min prior to use. Each scFv-Fc protein was diluted to 2 µg/mL in assay buffer and captured onto ProA biosensors for 300 s, achieving a stable immobilization response of 1.2–1.8 nm. Following a baseline equilibration step (120 s) in assay buffer, the loaded biosensors were immersed in a twofold serial dilution series of the VP1-CDE analyte, with concentrations ranging from 200 nM to 3.125 nM. The association phase was monitored for 300 s, after which the biosensors were transferred to fresh assay buffer to record the dissociation phase for 600 s. Data were processed and analyzed via Octet Data Analysis HT software (version 12.0). Sensorgrams were reference-subtracted and globally fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to derive the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) for each interaction.

Evaluation of the binding ability between scFv-Fc and FCV via an immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

F81 cells were seeded into 96-well plates. The next day, FCV (MOI = 5) was inoculated per well and incubated for 3 h. Then, the wells were emptied, and the samples were washed three times with PBS. One hundred microliters of prechilled 80% acetone was added to fix the cells at -20 °C for 30 min; the acetone was discarded, and the wells were washed three times with PBST. The cells were blocked with 5% skim milk at 37 °C for 2 h and then washed three times with PBST. Five scFv-Fc constructs were diluted to 0.02 mg/mL with PBS, added to each well, incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, and then washed three times with PBST. A Cy3-conjugated anti-human IgG Fc antibody (1:500) was used as a secondary antibody, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h, followed by three washes with PBST. Then, 50 µL of Hoechst 33,342 nuclear stain was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Virus-free wells served as negative controls.

Viral neutralization test (VNT) of scFv-Fc and IC50 determination

Five scFv-Fc fusion proteins were adjusted to an initial concentration of 75 µg/mL and serially diluted fourfold in DMEM. Each dilution was mixed with an equal volume of FCV suspension containing 100 TCID50 and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The virus‒antibody mixtures were then inoculated onto confluent F81 cell monolayers in 96-well plates, with four replicate wells per dilution. Virus-only (positive) and cell-only (negative) controls were included in each plate. After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, the CPE was observed and recorded under an inverted microscope. The neutralization titer was defined as the reciprocal of the highest scFv-Fc dilution that completely inhibited CPE in all four replicate wells, and the minimum neutralizing concentration (MNC) was calculated accordingly.

To obtain a quantitative measure of neutralization potency, a cell viability-based assay was performed in parallel with the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Briefly, following the 48 h incubation period, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured via a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to uninfected control cells (100% viability) and virus-only control cells (0% viability). Dose‒response curves were generated by plotting percent cell viability against the logarithm of the scFv-Fc concentration, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined via nonlinear regression analysis via a four-parameter logistic model in GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0).

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) from at least three independent experiments unless otherwise stated. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed via two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied.

Results

Prokaryotic expression and purification of the recombinant VP1-CDE protein

The VP1-CDE gene fragment was successfully amplified from FCV LZ2023 cDNA, yielding a specific band of approximately 430 bp (Fig. 1A). The amplicon was subsequently cloned and inserted into the pET-28a(+) vector via homologous recombination to generate the pET-28a-VP1-CDE expression construct (Fig. 1B), and the correct sequence and reading frame were confirmed via Sanger sequencing. Following transformation into E. coli BL21(DE3) and induction with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 °C, the recombinant VP1-CDE protein was expressed predominantly in the soluble fraction of the bacterial lysate. Purification by Ni-affinity chromatography yielded a single prominent band of approximately 18 kDa via SDS‒PAGE, which was consistent with the predicted molecular weight of the His6-tagged VP1‒CDE protein (Fig. 1C). Western blot analysis using anti-FCV feline serum as the primary antibody confirmed that both the purified VP1-CDE protein and the native VP1 capsid protein (~ 65 kDa) in FCV viral lysates were specifically recognized (Fig. 1D). These results demonstrate the successful prokaryotic expression and purification of the immunoreactive VP1-CDE protein suitable for downstream immunization and screening applications.

Determination of the serum antibody titer in immunized cats

To monitor the development of the VP1-CDE-specific humoral immune response, serum samples were collected prior to the first immunization (negative control) and 14 days after each of the five immunizations. An indirect ELISA was performed using the VP1-CDE protein as the coating antigen, and the endpoint OD450 values were determined at a serum dilution of 1:16,000. As shown in Fig. 2, the VP1-CDE-specific antibody titer increased progressively with each immunization, increasing from an S/N ratio of OD450=1.6–1.8 after the first immunization to a ratio > 10 after the fifth immunization. On the basis of this robust and sustained high-titer response, whole blood was collected after the fifth immunization for PBMC isolation and phage library construction.

Amplification of the scFv genes

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, from which the variable heavy (VH) and variable lambda light chain (Vλ) gene fragments were amplified separately. A VH gene fragment of approximately 400 bp and a Vλ gene fragment of approximately 350 bp were successfully amplified (Fig. 3A, B), which was consistent with the expected sizes. Using gel-purified VH and Vλ fragments as templates, overlap extension PCR was performed, and a scFv gene band of approximately 750 bp was successfully obtained (Fig. 3C).

Construction and quality evaluation of the phage-displayed scFv library

The scFv library was subsequently cloned and inserted into the pComb3XSS phagemid vector and transformed into E. coli SS320 competent cells. A schematic diagram of the pComb3XSS-scFv expression cassette is shown in Fig. 4A. The size of the scFv library, determined by serial dilution and plating of the electroporated bacterial suspension, was 2.1 × 108 phages. To perform a preliminary assessment of the diversity of the constructed phage display library, 24 randomly selected colonies were analyzed via colony PCR and subsequent Sanger sequencing. All the clones contained a full-length scFv insert in the correct reading frame, and multiple sequence alignment of the deduced amino acid sequences revealed no identical clones (Fig. 4B), indicating favorable primary sequence diversity of the library, which met the requirements for subsequent panning experiments.

Panning of the phage-displayed scFv library

Three rounds of biopanning were performed on the phage-displayed scFv library on the basis of specific antigen‒antibody binding via the 96-well microplate method. The size of the primary phage library was adjusted to 2 × 1012 phages. The sizes of the input and output phages were determined for each round, and the enrichment fold was used to assess the efficiency of specific antibody isolation and enrichment, with higher values indicating better enrichment performance. The results are presented in Table 2. The phage recovery ratio after elution increased progressively from 3.9 × 10− 9 to 8.1 × 10− 6 over the three rounds, and the enrichment ratio increased from 1.00 to 2,076.92, demonstrating successful specific enrichment of the phage antibody library. Ninety-two single colonies were randomly selected from third-round panning plates for phage ELISA screening, with four parallel negative controls established. Among these clones, 30 presented an S/N ratio of OD450 >10 and were identified as high-affinity positive clones (Fig. 5A). The 30 positive clones were subjected to PCR amplification. Fifteen scFv genes amplified from the positive clones were randomly selected for nucleotide sequencing, and amino acid sequence alignment was deduced. Sequence alignment via SnapGene software revealed five clones with unique scFv amino acid sequences, which were designated 3A5, 3B8, 3H5, 2H5 and 2I5. The VH sequences of the five scFvs were highly conserved, whereas their Vλ sequences presented significant amino acid diversity. The major amino acid variations were concentrated in the CDR3 regions. The CDR3 regions of the Vλ domains of the five scFvs contained 10–13 amino acids, while the framework regions were relatively conserved (Fig. 5B).

Eukaryotic expression of scFvs and evaluation of their binding via Western blotting

The five scFv genes were individually cloned and inserted into the pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc2 eukaryotic expression vector to generate chimeric scFv-Fc fusion proteins (feline VH-Vλ fused to human IgG1 Fc). The recombinant plasmids were then transfected into Expi293F cells. Five days post-transfection, the culture supernatant was collected and purified with Protein A resin. Evaluation of scFv-Fc binding to FCV-infected cells by IFA

To further confirm the ability of the scFv-Fc fusion proteins to recognize native viral antigens in a cellular context, an indirect IFA was performed using FCV-infected F81 feline kidney cells. All five scFv-Fc clones (3A5, 3B8, 3H5, 2H5, and 2I5) produced a specific red fluorescent signal that was localized predominantly to the cytoplasm of infected cells and colocalized with Hoechst 33,342-stained nuclei (Fig. 8). In contrast, no fluorescence was detected in uninfected control cells, confirming that the binding was FCV dependent and free of a nonspecific background. These results demonstrate that all five scFv-Fc proteins specifically recognize FCV antigens expressed during productive viral infection.

Characterization of the viral neutralization activity of the scFv-Fc fusion proteins

The neutralizing activities of the five scFv-Fc fusion proteins against FCV were evaluated via both a conventional CPE-based VNT and a quantitative cell viability assay. In the CPE-based assay, scFv-Fc-3A5 exhibited potent neutralizing activity, with a neutralization titer of 1:64 and an MNC of 1.172 µg/mL. scFv-Fc-2H5 showed weak but detectable activity (titer: 1:16, MNC: 4.688 µg/mL), whereas no neutralization was observed for scFv-Fc-3B8, scFv-Fc-3H5, or scFv-Fc-2I5 at the concentrations tested.

To obtain a more precise and quantitative measure of neutralization potency, dose‒response curves were generated by measuring cell viability following VNT (Fig. 9). The IC50 values, derived from four-parameter logistic curve fitting, are summarized in Table 4. Consistent with the CPE-based results, scFv-Fc-3A5 displayed the strongest neutralizing activity, with an IC50 of 1.862 µg/mL, followed by scFv-Fc-2H5 (IC50 = 6.168 µg/mL). The remaining three clones showed markedly reduced potency, with scFv-Fc-3B8 exhibiting an estimated IC50 of ~ 88.67 µg/mL and scFv-Fc-3H5 and scFv-Fc-2I5 requiring concentrations exceeding 1,000 µg/mL to achieve 50% inhibition.

Discussion

FCV is a globally distributed feline pathogen with a seroprevalence of 90% in some populations [26]. The emergence of VS-FCV strains with > 50% mortality has underscored the urgent need for effective antiviral interventions [27]. While commercial vaccines exist for prophylaxis, specific therapeutics for acute FCV disease are unavailable [28]. In this study, we constructed a feline immune scFv phage display library. Following affinity-based biopanning, five scFv clones with specific binding to VP1-CDE were isolated. Among these compounds, scFv-Fc-3A5 and scFv-Fc-2H5 displayed potent neutralizing activity against the genogroup II FCV strain LZ2023 in vitro. These findings directly support the hypothesis that functional neutralizing scFvs can be recovered from phage display libraries generated from immunized cats and provide a panel of novel feline-derived candidate molecules for the development of FCV diagnostic assays and antiviral therapies.

The feline humoral immune system has received less attention than human or murine counterparts, but foundational studies have established a robust framework for feline antibody engineering. Strietzel et al. provided the first comprehensive characterization of feline IgG subclasses [29], and Lu et al. subsequently reported the full feline immunoglobulin mRNA repertoire and demonstrated successful expression of a felinized monoclonal antibody against FPV, validating species-matched therapeutic development [25]. Murine monoclonal antibodies are inherently xenogeneic in cats and typically provoke anti-mouse antibody responses upon repeated administration, limiting both safety and efficacy [30]. The cost-effective production and tissue penetration of full-length immunoglobulins are also challenges [31]. ScFvs circumvent many of these limitations: they are compact, amenable to high-purity expression, retain antigen-binding specificity, and exhibit reduced immunogenicity [20]. Accordingly, scFvs have demonstrated potent neutralizing activity against a broad spectrum of viral pathogens [12, 19].

Phage display technology adapted to feline antibody repertoires has further accelerated progress. Choowongkomon et al. isolated a feline-derived scFv specific for feline IgG, which has utility in diagnostic platforms [32], and Li et al. engineered a chimeric feline scFv-Fc against feline PD-L1, extending its applications to immuno-oncology [33]. These advances establish a firm technical foundation for the feline scFv library described herein. Phage display offers distinct advantages over traditional hybridoma technology, including reduced development timelines and high-throughput screening capacity. Tajima et al. previously generated a murine monoclonal antibody (4D7) that recognized a broad FCV isolate panel via ELISA but lacked neutralizing activity [34]. In contrast, scFv-Fc-3A5 characterized herein potently neutralized the homologous LZ2023 isolate, with an MNC of 1.172 µg/mL. Combined with its straightforward production workflow, scFv-Fc-3A5 has emerged as a compelling candidate for translational evaluation.

Library size is a critical determinant of phage display success; 107--108 independent phages are generally required to recover high-affinity binders [35, 36]. Our library attained 2.1 × 108 phages with robust sequence diversity, meeting established benchmarks. Solid-phase panning against immobilized VP1-CDE identified 30 high-binding clones (S/N > 10), a yield comparable to that of analogous canine distemper virus studies [37]. The choice of VP1-CDE as an immunogen was guided by prior evidence identifying this region as the principal neutralizing determinant of FCVs [8, 9]. Western blot analysis confirmed that recombinant VP1-CDE was recognized by anti-FCV-positive feline serum, verifying the preservation of native antibody-binding sites. One limitation of our panning strategy is its exclusive reliance on a recombinant subdomain. While these successfully enriched antibodies target the major neutralizing determinant, they may have undersampled clones recognizing complex quaternary epitopes on intact virions. Future selections could incorporate alternating rounds against purified FCV particles to increase the neutralization breadth. Nevertheless, the potent neutralizing activity of scFv-Fc-3A5, validated by both immunofluorescence and quantitative neutralization assays, demonstrates that recombinant antigen strategies can yield functionally relevant antiviral candidates. ScFv-Fc-3A5 emerged as the most potent neutralizing clone (IC50 = 1.862 µg/mL), a value comparable to that of a recently reported Fc-fused nanobody targeting FPV [38]. In vitro engineering strategies, including CDR-targeted mutagenesis and chain shuffling, offer validated routes to further enhance affinity and potency [39, 40]. Notably, sequence alignment revealed that scFv-Fc-3A5 and weakly neutralizing scFv-Fc-3B8 share nearly identical VH domains but differ at three positions within the Vλ domain. These residues represent plausible determinants of the marked disparity in neutralization activity, a testable hypothesis for future studies through site-directed mutagenesis. Targeted engineering of VH and Vλ CDR loops may provide a rational path toward affinity-matured variants with superior antiviral potency.

The scFv‒Fc fusion proteins described are chimeric constructs comprising feline variable domains fused to a human IgG1 Fc scaffold. This design was selected for practical compatibility with widely available protein A resins and anti-human Fc detection reagents, facilitating rigorous characterization in vitro. We recognize that the human Fc domain likely provokes anti-Fc antibody responses in cats upon repeated administration, potentially limiting long-term therapeutic utility. Nonetheless, these chimeric molecules serve as invaluable tools for epitope mapping, diagnostic assay development, and proof-of-concept neutralization studies. For future translational development, the human Fc domain can be seamlessly replaced with a feline IgG1a Fc domain via the same pFUSE vector backbone. Given that complete feline IgG constant region sequences have been characterized and that felinized monoclonal antibodies have been successfully expressed [25], this felinization step is expected to preserve the neutralizing paratope while minimizing immunogenicity and restoring species-appropriate effector functions. Finally, this study represents proof-of-concept validation using the homologous FCV LZ2023 strain (genogroup II). Future investigations will prioritize cross-neutralization evaluation against a phylogenetically diverse panel of FCV isolates, including genogroup I strains, to fully define antiviral activity breadth and translational potential.

Conclusion

In summary, a feline-derived phage-display scFv library was constructed using peripheral blood mononuclear cells from cats immunized with the FCV VP1-CDE protein. Following three rounds of affinity panning, high-affinity scFv clones specific to both recombinant VP1-CDE and the native FCV LZ2023 isolate were successfully isolated, as confirmed by Western blot, biolayer interferometry, and immunofluorescence assays. The viral neutralization test further demonstrated that two of these clones (scFv-Fc-3A5 and scFv-Fc-2H5) exhibited potent neutralizing activity against the homologous FCV LZ2023 strain. These findings establish scFv-Fc-3A5 and scFv-Fc-2H5 as promising candidates for the development of in vitro diagnostic reagents targeting FCV infection.
