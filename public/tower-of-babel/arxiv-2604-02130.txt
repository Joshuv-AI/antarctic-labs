Stable and Efficient Algorithms for the Fermion Determinant
Johann Ostmeyer

Abstract
Some algorithms for the numerically exact treatment of fermion determinants are summarised. This is not supposed to be a review, rather a concise handbook. The audience is expected to have a basic understanding of how to put fermions on a computer. We primarily discuss different ways to work with the fermion matrix in the “sausage” (Green’s function) formulation for quantum Monte Carlo (QMC). We emphasise the need for varied approaches in different space-time volume regimes. In particular, for small spatial volumes we describe a numerically stable method based on dense matrix operations. It is designed specifically to deal with very low temperature regimes. On the other hand, for (relatively) large volumes we describe a highly efficient and scalable sparse matrix approach. These notes might be expanded in the future. As of now, they are not intended to be published in a journal. None of the algorithms are new, they are mostly based on Refs. [ 11 , 9 ] .

2
The “sausage”

We start with a fermionic system of spatial extent
V
and inverse temperature
\beta
.
The fermion dynamics are governed by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{fermion}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}K_{ij}c_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\,,

(1)

where
c_{i}^{\dagger}
and
c_{j}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
are creation and annihilation operators at the spatial positions
i,j
, respectively.
Any potential fields (including Hubbard-Stratonovich transformations) have already been included into the
V\times V
-dimensional hopping matrix
K
.
Throughout these notes we focus exclusively on this part of the (potentially much larger and more complicated) quantum system.

For lattice simulations,
\beta
is discretised into
N_{t}
(imaginary) time slices of extent
\delta\equiv\frac{\beta}{N_{t}}
.
Without derivation and going into detail about a particular physical model, such a system can be described by the real or complex vector fields
\varphi
following the probability density

\displaystyle p(\varphi)

\displaystyle\propto\det M

(2)

governed by the determinant of the fermion matrix

\displaystyle M_{t^{\prime},t}

\displaystyle=\delta_{t^{\prime},t}-\delta_{t^{\prime},t+1}M_{t}\,,

(3)

\displaystyle M_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{K_{t}}\,.

(4)

Anti-periodic boundary conditions in the time direction are implicitly assumed.

K_{t}
is a time-dependent version of the initial hopping matrix
\delta K
with the time dependence included through the fields.
The factor
\delta
has been absorbed into
K_{t}
, so each
K_{t}
approaches zero with increasing
N_{t}
.

M_{t}
is also
V\times V
-dimensional, while the full
M
is
\left(VN_{t}\right)\times\left(VN_{t}\right)
-dimensional.
In block-matrix notation
M
takes the form

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&M_{N_{t}-1}\\
-M_{0}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-M_{1}&1&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&-M_{N_{t}-2}&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(5)

Now observe that

\displaystyle\hat{M}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 1+\prod_{t}M_{t}

(6)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\det M

\displaystyle=\det\hat{M}\,,

(7)

which allows us to define the “sausage”
\hat{M}
using the time-ordered product
\prod_{t}M_{t}\equiv M_{0}M_{1}\cdots M_{N_{t}-1}
.
The inverse of the sausage
\hat{M}^{-1}
is often referred to as the Green’s function
[
3
]
.
Since the probability density mentioned above only depends on the determinant, we never need the full fermion matrix. Instead, we always replace it with the “sausage”
\hat{M}
.

Another important quantity is the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\log\det\hat{M}+\cdots\,,

(8)

that might contain some bosonic contributions and additional fermionic flavours (i.e. multiple distinct fermion matrices) as well as canonical momenta.
Note that this artificial Hamiltonian used for Quantum Monte Carlo simulations already contains the factor
\beta
(making it dimensionless) and is not to be confused with the physical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
of dimension energy.

3.1
Updates

To keep things tractable, we assume that there is a fixed number of real or complex fields with dimension
VN_{t}
each.
Thus, any update of all field variables will involve at least
\Omega(VN_{t})
many operations.
This is also the minimal memory requirement.
On the other hand, the full (not “sausage” version) fermion matrix
M
can be stored using
\ord\left(V^{2}N_{t}^{2}\right)
memory and inverted in a runtime of order
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}^{3}\right)
.
Any algorithm using even more resources is clearly sub-optimal and should be avoided.

Let us briefly review how some of the most common algorithms compare to these theoretical bounds.

3.1.1
The Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm

The HMC algorithm
[
5
]
performs global updates of all the continuous field variables at once.
Therefore runtime and memory requirements of a single HMC step are equivalent to those for a full update from
table

2
.
It goes beyond the scope of these notes to dive into issues of minimal autocorrelation and other tunable properties of the algorithm.
We refer to Ref.
[
15
]
for a hands-on introduction of the HMC and to Refs.
[
4
,
19
]
for the derivation of the HMC formalism in a simple but non-trivial fermionic system.

An HMC step requires one evaluation of the Hamiltonian (
8
) (for the accept/reject step), the molecular dynamics (numerical integration of the equations of motion) and the calculation of fermionic forces
\frac{\partial H}{\partial\varphi}
.
The Hamiltonian evaluation is necessary for any Monte Carlo update and a single evaluation per global update is the theoretical minimum.
The molecular dynamics by themselves scale as
\Theta(VN_{t})
.
As a rule, the update runtime is dominated by the force calculation, a single-time observable measurement.

Thus, the lower bound on HMC complexity is (little surprising)
\Omega(VN_{t})
.
There is no overhead complexity added by the algorithm, so (the fermionic contributions to) the true runtime and memory scalings are identical to those of Hamiltonian and force evaluations, see
table

1
.

3.1.2
The Blankenbecler-Scalapino-Sugar (BSS) algorithm

The BSS algorithm
[
3
]
(Sec. V) performs local updates, so one needs
\Theta(VN_{t})
BSS steps (a sweep) for a full update of all the field variables.
Each accepted local update comes with a runtime of
\Theta\left(V^{2}\right)
.
Rejected updates are
\ord\left(1\right)
, so the overall coefficient can be small.
On the other hand, many rejections come with long autocorrelation times.

Thus, the lower bound on BSS runtime complexity is
\Omega(V^{3}N_{t})
.
This is a natural consequence of the intrinsic use of the “sausage” formalism by the BSS algorithm.
It comes with the additional overhead of an order
\Theta\left(V^{3}\right)
(dense) or
\Theta\left(V^{2}\right)
(sparse) update for every time slice, i.e.
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}\right)
in total.

An important implication is that the BSS algorithm does not profit significantly from sparse matrix multiplications.
The lower bound is fixed to
\Omega(V^{3}N_{t})
by the necessity of a full sweep of local updates.

In contrast to the HMC, the BSS algorithm is applicable to discrete as well as continuous variables.

3.2
Measurements

The computational complexity of measurements is highly observable-dependent.
Here, we provide the bounds for the calculation of all single-fermion observables.
This is equivalent to the determination of the full time-dependent fermionic Green’s function.
In other words, we need the inverse of the
\left(VN_{t}\right)\times\left(VN_{t}\right)
-dimensional fermion matrix
M^{-1}
.
Since there are
V^{2}N_{t}^{2}
elements to
M
and
M^{-1}
, this is the lower bound for runtime and memory.
The naïve inversion requires
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}^{3}\right)
steps, so that once more any algorithm with worse scaling should be avoided.

We remark that the primary bottleneck for measurements is the sheer number of results required.
An observable involving
s
different times, will necessarily scale with
\Omega(N_{t}^{s})
(unless some measurements are dropped, e.g. for reasons of translational symmetry).
This is why the full Green’s function
G(t_{1},t_{2})
scales with
\Omega(N_{t}^{2})
.
On the bright side, observables involving multiple fermions (e.g. two-particle correlators) can be calculated within the same computational complexity class as single fermion observables as long as they do not involve more different times.

Of course, some single-time observables (like the force) and those that are time-independent might be calculable in
\Omega(VN_{t})
.

4
Observables

The vast majority of observables ever needed can be derived in the so-called response formalism relating the observable

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\alpha}

(9)

to some derivative of the Hamiltonian with appropriately chosen
\alpha
. For instance, the force needed for hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) simulations is a response with respect to the main variable, e.g. the Hubbard field
\alpha=\phi
for the Hubbard model.
If
\mathcal{A}
is a physical observable (as opposed to an algorithmic one), we can obtain
\alpha
using the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\beta\mathcal{H}\right\rangle\,.

(10)

A simple example is the total energy of the system for which we have to use
\alpha=\beta
.

It follows that all (relevant) fermionic observables can be written as a derivative of the fermion matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\log\det\hat{M}}{\partial\alpha}

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det\hat{M}}\frac{\partial\det\hat{M}}{\partial\alpha}

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det\hat{M}}\det\hat{M}\tr\left(\hat{M}^{-1}\,\frac{\partial\hat{M}}{\partial\alpha}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\hat{M}^{-1}\sum_{t}\prod_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\alpha}\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\alpha}\right)\,,

(15)

where we used Jacobi’s formula for the derivative of the determinant and subsequently expanded
\hat{M}
using the product definition. For time-dependent observables the derivative only acts on a given time slice, say
t_{0}
, and the outer sum can be dropped

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t_{0}}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}<t_{0}}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial M_{t_{0}}}{\partial\alpha}\right)\,.

(16)

4.1
Shortcut for simple operators

In a sloppy abuse of notation we can relate
K_{t}=\delta\mathcal{H}
and therefore for simple enough physical operators

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M_{t}}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle\approx M_{t}\frac{\partial K_{t}}{\partial\alpha}

(17)

\displaystyle=M_{t}\frac{1}{N_{t}}\hat{\mathcal{A}}\,.

(18)

where
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the operator corresponding to the observable
\mathcal{A}
.
Inserting this result into equation (
15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{t}\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}\leq t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\hat{\mathcal{A}}\right)\,.

(19)

Thus, the expectation value of a fermionic observable can often be obtained by plugging the corresponding operator into equation (
19
).
More rigorously, we take the thermal trace of the operator multiplied by the Green’s function at every discrete time.

A simple example for which this shortcut works very well is the fermion number
\mathcal{A}=n
,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}=\frac{1}{V}\mathds{1}
(a response to the chemical potential
\alpha=\mu
), which reduces to

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{VN_{t}}\sum_{t}\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}\leq t}M_{t^{\prime}}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\tr\left(\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}}M_{t^{\prime}}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{V}\tr\hat{M}^{-1}\,.

(22)

4.2
Matrix element derivatives

Another important class of observables (e.g. the single particle correlator) is described by the response with respect to some specific matrix element
M_{t_{0}x,ty}
of the full fermion matrix.
By Jacobi’s formula this response is, in fact, identical to the corresponding matrix element of the full time-dependent Green’s function, that is inverse fermion matrix.
The corresponding derivative

\displaystyle\left(M^{-1}\right)_{ty,t_{0}x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\log\det\hat{M}}{\partial M_{t_{0}x,ty}}

(23)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\tr\left(\prod\limits_{t^{\prime\prime}=t_{0}-1}^{0}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}^{-1}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod\limits_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\delta_{y}^{x}\right)&\text{if }t_{0}\leq t\,,\\
-\tr\left(\prod\limits_{t^{\prime\prime}\geq t_{0}}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod\limits_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\delta_{y}^{x}\right)&\text{else}\end{cases}

(24)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left[\left(\prod\limits_{t^{\prime}=t-1}^{t_{0}}M_{t^{\prime}}^{-1}+\prod\limits_{t^{\prime}=t}^{N_{t}-1}M_{t^{\prime}}\prod\limits_{t^{\prime\prime}=0}^{t_{0}-1}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{-1}\right]_{yx}&\text{if }t_{0}\leq t\,,\\
-\left[\left(\prod\limits_{t^{\prime\prime}=t-1}^{0}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}^{-1}\prod\limits_{t^{\prime}=N_{t}-1}^{t_{0}}M_{t^{\prime}}^{-1}+\prod\limits_{t^{\prime}=t}^{t_{0}-1}M_{t}^{\prime}\right)^{-1}\right]_{yx}&\text{else}\end{cases}

(25)

can also directly be expressed in the “sausage” formalism. Here
\delta^{x}_{y}
denotes a
V\times V
-matrix that has a single unit entry at the
(x,y)
position and is zero everywhere else. Note that in the special case of
t_{0}=t
the term
\prod_{t^{\prime}=t-1}^{t_{0}}M_{t^{\prime}}^{-1}=\mathds{1}
becomes an empty product because
t^{\prime}\geq t_{0}
is decreasing.

From this relation it also becomes clear why the inverse fermion matrix is sufficient for all fermionic observables that can be expressed in the response formalism.
Applying the chain rule, we can rewrite any derivative as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\log\det\hat{M}}{\partial\alpha}

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial\log\det\hat{M}}{\partial M_{ij}}\,\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial\alpha}

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\left(M^{-1}\right)_{ji}\,\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial\alpha}

(28)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(M^{-1}\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial\alpha}\right)\,,

(29)

that is a linear combination of simple derivatives
\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial\alpha}
with prefactors from
M^{-1}
.

4.3
Discrete time artifacts

To leading order in
\delta
, the expression can be further simplified to

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}\leq t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t}}{\partial\alpha}\right)+\ord\left(\delta\right)\,,

(30)

which is typically sufficient and used in most calculations.
\ord\left(\delta\right)
-improved approximations are readily available

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t}\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}\leq t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t}}{\partial\alpha}+\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}\geq t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t}}{\partial\alpha}\right)+\ord\left(\delta^{2}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}\tr\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}\gtrsim t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}\lesssim t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t}}{\partial\alpha}\right)+\ord\left(\delta^{2}\right)\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\prod_{t^{\prime}\lesssim t}M_{t^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,M_{t}^{1/2}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle M_{t}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}K_{t}}\,.

(34)

Both formulae are second order approximations of the integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\sum_{t}\tr\left(M_{t}^{1-x}\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}\,M_{t}^{x}\,\frac{\partial K_{t}}{\partial\alpha}\right)\,.

(35)

However, higher than second order approximations would exceed the order of the Trotterization in the initial formulation of the fermion matrix and are therefore irrelevant. We only remark that the exact derivative is calculable and should in fact be evaluated if the exponential
M_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{K_{t}}
is already truncated to some finite polynomial. In that case the derivative of the polynomial is to be used instead of the approximations listed above.

4.4
Multiple operators

A similar derivation allows to write a time-dependent product of operators as

\mathcal{A}_{1}(t_{1})\mathcal{A}_{2}(t_{2})\cdots\mathcal{A}_{s}(t_{s})=\\
\tr\left(\prod_{t_{s}^{\prime\prime}\gtrsim t_{s}}M_{t_{s}^{\prime\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t_{1}^{\prime}\lesssim t_{1}}M_{t_{1}^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t_{1}}}{\partial\alpha_{1}}\left(\prod_{t_{1}^{\prime\prime}\lesssim t_{1}}M_{t_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{-1}\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t_{2}^{\prime}\lesssim t_{2}}M_{t_{2}^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t_{2}}}{\partial\alpha_{2}}\left(\prod_{t_{2}^{\prime\prime}\lesssim t_{2}}M_{t_{2}^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{-1}\cdots M^{-1}\prod_{t_{s}^{\prime}\lesssim t_{s}}M_{t_{s}^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t_{s}}}{\partial\alpha_{s}}\right)\\
\quad+\ord\left(\delta^{2}\right)\,,

(36)

or, more readably,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(t_{1})\mathcal{A}_{2}(t_{2})\cdots\mathcal{A}_{s}(t_{s})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\left(1-\hat{M}^{-1}\right)\frac{\partial K_{t_{1}}}{\partial\alpha_{1}}(t_{1})\frac{\partial K_{t_{2}}}{\partial\alpha_{2}}(t_{2})\cdots\frac{\partial K_{t_{s}}}{\partial\alpha_{s}}(t_{s})\right)+\ord\left(\delta^{\nu}\right)\,,

(37)

where the exponent
\nu
depends on the respective approximation of the matrix derivative,
1-\hat{M}^{-1}
acts like the Fermi function, and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K_{t_{i}}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}(t_{i})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{t^{\prime}\leq t_{i}}M_{t_{i}^{\prime}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t_{i}}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}\left(\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}\leq t_{i}}M_{t_{i}^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{-1}

(38)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{K_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{K_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{K_{t_{i}}}\,\frac{\partial K_{t_{i}}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-K_{t_{i}}}\cdots\mathrm{e}^{-K_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-K_{0}}

(39)

can be interpreted as the operator
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(t_{i})\sim\frac{\partial K_{t_{i}}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}(t_{i})
in the Heisenberg picture. While the latter formulation (
37
) is easier to read, the former version (
36
) is typically more efficient to implement (after re-writing the inverses as in eq. (
39
)). Of course, the ‘
\leq
’ in equation (
38
) and the ‘
\lesssim
’ in equation (
36
) can be replaced as desired.

Without going into any further detail, these expressions show that any fermionic quantity in the “sausage”-formalism can be calculated with the knowledge of
\det\hat{M}
and all possible combinations of
\prod_{t^{\prime}>t_{1}}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}<t_{2}}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}
(allowing negative times
t_{1,2}
and inverses
M_{t^{\prime}}^{-1}
). Therefore, an implementation only has to compute said quantities. In the following, we will explore efficient and stable ways to implement them. Depending on the regime, the trade off between efficiency and stability favours some algorithm or another. See
table

1
for a summary.

5
Product accumulation tricks

Usually, the runtime will be dominated by the products
\prod_{t^{\prime}>t_{1}}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}<t_{2}}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}
since computing them for all possible choices of
t_{1,2}
naively scales as
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}^{3}\right)
, or as
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}^{2}\right)
if
t_{1}=t_{2}
(e.g. for force calculations). It is therefore crucial for the runtime to introduce one of the following tricks.

5.1
Dense pre- and suffix calculations

1.

Multiply and store
\prod_{t^{\prime}>t}M_{t^{\prime}}
and
\prod_{t^{\prime}<t}M_{t^{\prime}}
for each
t

2.

Combine
\prod_{t^{\prime}>t_{1}}M_{t^{\prime}}\,\hat{M}^{-1}\prod_{t^{\prime\prime}<t_{2}}M_{t^{\prime\prime}}
as required

Thus, the runtime reduces to
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}^{2}\right)
, or
\ord\left(V^{3}N_{t}\right)
if
t_{1}=t_{2}
.
Memory scales as

A particularly powerful method can be used at low filling for the calculation of observables that are normalised by the particle number.
