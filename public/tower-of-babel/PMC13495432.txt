Low temperature-induced profiling of transcription and alternative splicing during seed germination in rice
Authors: Yue Peng, Ye Shao, Tao Tuo, Jian Zhang, Abdullaev Mirtemir Baxodir Ugli, Mukhammadjon Arabboevich Ergashev, Wangshu Mou, Yingxiang Wang, Dawei Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow-temperature germination (LTG) is a critical agronomic trait for direct-seeded rice as it ensures uniform seedling establishment. Alternative splicing (AS), a key post-transcriptional mechanism, plays a crucial role in plant responses to abiotic stresses; however, the specific AS events and mechanisms involved in LTG remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we conducted RNA-Seq to profile the cold stress response in germinating seeds, and identified 12,558 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in ZH11 seeds after 24 h of low‑temperature treatment during germination. GO annotation showed enrichment in genes associated with circadian rhythm, MAPK and plant hormone signaling, and metabolic processes including nitrogen metabolism. We also detected 12,494 cold-induced differential alternative splicing (DAS) events across 4,812 genes, primarily exon skipping (SE) and intron retention (RI). Cold-induced DAS genes were associated with lipid metabolism and terpenoid biosynthesis implying functions in metabolic adjustment and stress adaptation under cold stress. Furthermore, we identified several potential key regulators of LTG: genes OsCKLP2 and OsSTPK encoding protein kinase, OsPP2C11 encoding protein phosphatase, and transcription factors OsbHLH034, OsKNA2 and OsEREBP26.ConclusionThis study indicates that alternative splicing is involved in rice germination under cold stress. The findings not only advance the theoretical understanding of AS in LTG but also provide valuable genetic resources for breeding cold-tolerant rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13068-0.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.), one of the world’s most important food crops, is a staple food for over half of the global population [1]. The future of food security depends on a sustainable and productive rice sector. Meanwhile, driven by the expansion of rice cultivation and growing shortage of rural labor, the direct seeding method is gaining popularity in both rainfed and irrigated systems owing to its low cost and operational convenience [2, 3]. Currently, direct seeding can be classified into three main types, (a) dry seeding (sowing dry seeds into dry soil), (b) wet seeding (sowing pre-germinated seeds into moist soil), and (c) water seeding (sowing germinated seeds into flooded fields) [4, 5]. Compared to seedling transplant approach, direct seeding not only reduces total labor requirements by approximately 11% to 66% but also enhances nitrogen use efficiency through improved water management [6]. Furthermore, it lowers initial water consumption during planting, leading to a significant increase in production efficiency [5, 7]. Owing to these advantages, direct seeding has become a predominant rice cultivation method worldwide. However direct seeding suffers from concerns like low germination and restricted early growth, leading to poor seedling establishment and consequently reduced yield.

Cold stress is a key abiotic stress, adversely affecting plant growth and development and limiting geographical distribution [8]. As a crop native to tropical and subtropical regions, rice is highly sensitive to cold stress. Globally, cold stress affects over 15 million hectares of rice cultivation across 24 countries, resulting in substantial annual yield losses [9]. The optimal temperature for rice germination is 25–35 °C [10]. When the temperature drops below 15 °C, the germination rate significantly decreases. Low-temperature germination (LTG) represents a key agronomic trait for rice, particularly in systems where it ensures uniform seedling emergence and establishment, thereby contributing to stable yield [11–14]. To date, more than 200 QTLs for LTG have been mapped in rice; however, only a few causal genes have been identified, such as, qLTG3-1, OsSAP16 and qLTG11 [12, 13, 15]. The qLTG3-1 gene, which encodes a glycine-rich protein belonging to the HyP/GRP family in rice, regulates seed germination by adjusting cell vacuolization in tissues [13, 16, 17]. OsSAP16, which containing a zinc finger domain, acts as a regulator of LTG [12]. GENERAL REGULATORY FACTOR14h (GF14h) has been identified as the candidate gene for qLTG11 and plays a vital role in LTG [18]. Additionally, a transposon insertion in the promoter of OsUBC12 (encoding an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme) enhances its expression, thereby increasing low-temperature germinability in japonica rice [14]. Several other genes are also involved in the regulation of LTG. For example, heat shock protein 40 (HSP40) and BURP proteins participate in LTG, as evidenced by the impaired germination of nal11 and burp12 mutants under low temperature compared to ZH11 [19]. OsNAC5 functions as a positive regulator of LTG by directly activating ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 5 (OsABI5) expression [20]. In contrast, OsMYB30 acts as a negative regulator by increasing trehalose levels to suppress α-amylase activity and inhibit LTG [21]. Despite the role of some genes in LTG, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive and require further investigation.

RNA splicing includes two primary modes, constitutive splicing (CS) and alternative splicing (AS). The CS leads to generation of the predominant transcript, and typically produces only one mature mRNA isoform from its pre-mRNA [22]. In contrast, AS refers to the generation of multiple transcripts isoforms from a single pre-mRNA through alternative (other than CS) splicing events [22]. This process may result in a spectrum of structural and functional polymorphisms in both transcripts and proteins [23]. The major types of AS events include: intron retention (RI, an intron between two exons is retained), exon skipping (SE, an exon is spliced out along with its flanking introns), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS, alternative donor or acceptor site, upstream), alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS, alternative donor or acceptor site, downstream), and mutually exclusive exons (MXE, one of two exons, but not both, is present in the mature mRNA) [22, 24]. Nuclear pre-mRNA splicing is catalyzed by the spliceosome, comprised of five major small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs: U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6), which form the catalytic center, along with many additional proteins for its dynamic assembly and precision [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that AS plays a crucial role in plant development and response to biotic and abiotic stresses [26–28]. In Arabidopsis, cold stress triggers rapid AS, modulating expression of hundreds of genes and isoform balance of key regulators [29]. For instance, cold treatment decreases the levels of the non-functional CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) transcript and increases the levels of the functional full-length isoform [30, 31]. Furthermore, C-REPEAT BINDING FACTORs (CBFs), key regulators of cold-responsive genes, also modulate AS during cold acclimation by interacting with subunits of the spliceosome complex [32]. In rice, the serine/arginine-rich proteins OsRS33 and OsRS2Z38 are involved in the seedling cold stress response through splicing regulation [33]. While some AS events and splicing regulators have been characterized during cold stress in plants, the AS events and mechanisms involved in LTG remain poorly understood.

Cold-induced AS has been widely reported in plant seedlings. We therefore hypothesized that cold also induce AS in rice seeds during LTG. This may change the AS patterns of important genes related to seed germination, alter transcript isoform ratios, and affect protein function, ultimately influencing LTG. To test this idea, we used RNA-seq to analyze gene expression and AS in germinating rice seeds under cold stress. Our data showed large-scale changes in both gene expression levels and AS profiles. We further verified several AS events in key regulatory genes, including transcription factors and signaling genes. Notably, we identified several candidate genes important for LTG that displayed changes in both gene expression and AS under cold stress. These results support our hypothesis that AS participates in LTG regulation by affecting the function of key regulatory genes. Overall, our results reveal coordinated transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation during rice seed germination under cold stress, and provide a basis for further studying the role of AS in LTG.

Results

Transcriptome analysis of rice seeds during Low-Temperature Germination (LTG)

Transcriptomic analysis was done to investigate the gene expression profiles during LTG of rice seeds, with and without cold treatment. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated high reproducibility, as the three biological replicates of each group (control and cold-treated) formed tight and distinct clusters (Fig. 1A). A comparison between ZH11 samples under 28 °C and 12 °C conditions revealed a total of 12,558 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 7,267 downregulated and 5,291 upregulated genes (Fig. 1B and C).

Functional enrichment analysis revealed distinct biological profiles of cold-responsive genes. Cold-induced genes were enriched in nucleotide metabolism, biosynthetic processes, energy production, developmental processes, and stress responses (Figure S1A). In contrast, cold-suppressed DEGs were mainly enriched in secondary metabolism, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, transport, cell cycle, cell growth, and stress responses (Figure S1B). KEGG pathway analysis further showed that cold-induced DEGs were associated with primary metabolic pathways, signal transduction, and basic cellular regulation, including starch and sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, nitrogen metabolism, and carotenoid biosynthesis (Fig. 1D). Cold-suppressed DEGs were enriched in secondary metabolism and stress-related metabolic pathways, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, and various amino acid and nucleotide metabolic pathways (Fig. 1E). Together, these results indicate comprehensive transcriptional reprogramming of multiple metabolic and regulatory pathways under low-temperature conditions, including key cold response pathways such as the MAPK signaling pathway, plant hormone signal transduction, and lipid metabolism.

The accuracy of the RNA-seq data for these DEGs was assessed by RT-qPCR analysis of four randomly selected candidate genes from both the cold-induced and cold-suppressed groups (Figure S2). The results showed highly consistent expression patterns, confirming the reliability of the RNA-seq analysis and demonstrating that it reflects the real transcriptomic changes during LTG in rice seeds.

Differential Alternative Splicing (DAS) analysis of rice seeds during LTG

Analysis of five major types of AS events (RI, SE, A5SS, A3SS, and MXE) during LTG in ZH11 was by rMATS tool identified 12,494 significant AS events in 4,812 genes. The distribution of these AS events across different types is summarized as follows: 4,476 SE events in 2,150 genes, 2,778 RI events in 2,046 genes, 2,694 A3SS events in 1,831 genes, 1,863 A5SS events in 1,352 genes, and 683 MXE events in 382 genes (Fig. 2A and B). These results indicate that cold stress induces multiple splicing events within individual genes. Notably, SE and RI were the most abundant events, suggesting their involvement in response to cold stress. Furthermore, among the 12,494 alternative splicing events, 8,876 exhibited increased splicing efficiency and 3,618 exhibited decreased efficiency (Fig. 2C).

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses was done for the five major types of DAS events. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in three categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). For genes with SE events, the most significantly enriched GO terms in BP were catabolic process, DNA metabolic process, transport and lipid metabolic process. In CC, enriched terms included plastid, peroxisome, and cytosol. In MF, enriched terms were hydrolase activity, RNA binding, nuclease activity, and transporter activity, among others (Fig. 3A). KEGG pathway analysis showed that these genes were predominantly enriched in lipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, intracellular signal transduction, RNA processing and degradation, protein and ribosome biogenesis, autophagy, DNA repair, energy metabolism (glycolysis and TCA cycle), nucleocytoplasmic transport, and secondary metabolism (Fig. 3B). Conversely, for genes with RI events, GO enrichment in biological processes included biosynthetic process, carbohydrate metabolic process, and nucleotide metabolic process. In cellular components, enriched terms included plastid, cytosol, and mitochondrion. In molecular functions, enriched terms were nucleotide binding, RNA binding, and hydrolase activity (Fig. 3C). Accordingly, their KEGG pathways are mainly involved in primary carbon and energy metabolism, lipid and fatty acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, amino acid metabolism, autophagy, and DNA repair (Fig. 3D).

Furthermore, genes with MXE events were enriched in GO terms related to plastid, cytosol, lipid metabolism, protein modification process, hydrolase activity, and nucleic acid binding activity (Figure S3A), as well as in KEGG pathways for carotenoid biosynthesis and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Figure S3B). For genes with A3SS events, significant enrichment was observed in GO terms such as mitochondrion, cytosol, catabolic process, reproduction, nucleotide binding, and RNA binding (Figure S4A), as well as in KEGG pathways related to ribosome biogenesis and nucleocytoplasmic transport (Figure S4B). Finally, genes with A5SS events were enriched in GO terms such as mitochondrion, cytosol, embryo development, catabolic process, hydrolase activity, and RNA binding activity (Figure S5A), and in KEGG pathways for fatty acid degradation and propanoate metabolism (Figure S5B). Collectively, these results indicate that genes involved in RNA metabolism, hydrolysis, catabolic processes, and basic cellular metabolism are commonly regulated by multiple types of AS events during LTG in rice.

Analysis of DAS genes for SE and RI events during LTG in rice seeds

Given that signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, and post-translational modification are essential for plants to sense and respond to low-temperature stress [34, 35], these processes may also play an important role in LTG. We focused on genes annotated with representative molecular function terms: enzyme regulator activity and signal transducer activity in SE events, as well as kinase activity and DNA-binding activity in RI events, to further explore the potential regulatory roles of AS in the low-temperature response at the molecular level. Heatmap analysis of the SE events revealed distinct patterns. Within the enzyme regulator activity genes, 60 splicing events occurred across 29 genes. The vast majority (59 events) exhibited increased efficiency, with only a single event showing a decrease. These genes included LOC_Os02g13110 encoding a protein phosphatase, LOC_Os05g23610 encoding a protein phosphatase inhibitor, and LOC_Os05g26890 encoding a G-protein alpha subunit (Fig. 4A). Similarly, for the 51 splicing events in 25 signal transducer activity genes, nearly all (50 events) showed increased efficiency, with only one exception. Key genes in this pathway included: LOC_Os06g31220 and LOC_Os06g26340, encoding mitogen-activated protein kinases; LOC_Os07g33790, encoding a glutamate receptor; and LOC_Os03g50860, encoding a histidine kinase (Fig. 4B).

Heatmap analysis of RI events also showed distinct patterns. Specifically, the 171 splicing events identified in 140 DNA-binding activity genes showed a shift toward decreased efficiency, with 89 events down and 51 up. Affected genes encode key transcription factors and regulators, including the transcription factors LOC_Os12g13170 and LOC_Os08g06110, the auxin response factor LOC_Os02g35140, and a two-component response regulator LOC_Os11g05930 (Figure S6A). Similarly, the 166 genes in the kinase activity pathway encompassed 268 splicing events. A strong bias toward decreased efficiency was observed, with 208 events decreased and 59 increased. These genes encode important kinases, including the CBL-interacting protein kinases LOC_Os01g18800, the PBS1-like protein kinases LOC_Os01g15470, and the leucine-rich repeat-containing protein kinases LOC_Os05g16670 (Figure S6B). These results indicate that genes involved in signal transduction, transcriptional regulation, and post-translational modification undergo extensive AS under low-temperature conditions. SE events in genes related to enzyme regulation and signal transduction mainly showed increased splicing efficiency, whereas RI events in genes associated with DNA binding and kinase activity predominantly exhibited decreased splicing efficiency. These distinct splicing patterns suggest that AS may selectively modulate the transcript isoform profiles of genes involved in low-temperature signaling and regulatory processes during LTG.

Comparative analysis of DEGs and DASGs during LTG in rice seeds

To avoid redundancy and further clarify the coordinated regulatory patterns as well as functional preferences of transcriptional regulation and AS in response to cold stress, we classified cold stress-responsive genes into three categories based on their differential AS (DAS) and differential expression (DE) profiles: genes displaying only DAS (3,306, 21%), genes displaying only DE (11,052, 70%), and genes displaying both DAS and DE (1,506, 9%) (Fig. 5A). These three gene sets were subjected to systematic GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to dissect their functional specialization and coordinated roles in LTG. Each group showed association with a distinct functional profile, DE only genes showed significantly enrichment of 16 terms, DAS-only genes showed enrichment of 26, and the DAS-DE overlapping genes showed enrichment of 20 terms (Fig. 5B-D). Notably, all three groups were co-enriched in transporter activity and carbohydrate metabolic process, which suggests a core functional overlap that may reflect their coordinated involvement in low-temperature adaptation during germination. Beyond this common enrichment, each group displayed unique functional biases that may reflect a division of labor between transcriptional regulation and AS. DE-only genes were specifically enriched in processes related to abiotic stimulus response, transcription factor activity, catalytic activity, oxygen binding, lipid binding, and cell growth, which may be associated with the primary role of transcriptional regulation in global stress adaptation and growth reprogramming (Fig. 5B). In contrast, DAS-only genes were enriched in the protein metabolic process, nucleotide binding, transferase activity, protein modification process, and enzyme regulator activity, which could relate to the potential role of AS in fine-tuning protein homeostasis and enzymatic activity (Fig. 5C). DAS-DE overlapping genes, which are subjected to dual regulation at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, were associated with RNA binding, lipid and DNA metabolic process, post-embryonic development, and hydrolase activity processes that are likely tightly controlled to support precise germination regulation under low temperature conditions (Fig. 5D).

Consistent with the GO enrichment results, KEGG pathway analysis further revealed distinct biological roles of the three gene categories, with potential functional complementarity. DE-only genes were predominantly enriched in pathways related to ribosome biogenesis, phenylpropanoid and diterpenoid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, motor proteins, and DNA replication, processes that are likely important for global metabolic reprogramming and stress tolerance (Figure S7A). DAS-only genes were enriched in RNA degradation, alanine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, nucleocytoplasmic transport, the spliceosome, and transcription factor-related pathways (Figure S7B), which may relate to the role of AS in regulating RNA metabolism and intracellular transport. Importantly, the DAS-DE overlapping genes were mainly associated with glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and inositol phosphate metabolism (Figure S7C), which are core metabolic pathways essential for energy supply and cell signaling during seed germination. Together, these results indicate that cold stress induces extensive AS events enriched in pathways related to redox homeostasis, energy metabolism, and seed germination, processes critical for seed germination under low-temperature conditions.

Significance of AS in the cold stress response was further investigated by analysis of DAS genes (displaying DAS as well as both DAS and DE). We found that under cold stress, a considerable proportion of genes with DAS only are closely related to protein modification processes, with the majority involved in protein phosphorylation (Fig. 5B). Therefore, we selected genes with significant AS changes for RT-PCR validation (Figure S8). These included the protein kinase genes LOC_Os05g51560 (CASEIN KINASE 1-LIKE PROTEIN 2, OsCKLP2) and LOC_Os01g44110 (SERINE/THREONINE-PROTEIN KINASE, OsSTPK), and the protein phosphatase gene LOC_Os02g08364 (PROBABLE PROTEIN PHOSPHATASE 2 C 11, OsPP2C11). Under cold stress, both OsCKLP2 and OsSTPK exhibited RI events. Under normal temperature, OsCKLP2 and OsSTPK each showed two bands, corresponding to the fully spliced mature mRNA and a transcript retaining the fifth intron (I5). Intron retention in I5 of OsCKLP2 introduces a premature termination codon at amino acid position 130, resulting in a truncated protein. The I5 retention event in OsSTPK causes a premature stop at codon 407 and removes the C-terminal disordered region. After cold treatment, the RI isoform of OsCKLP2 decreased significantly, while that of OsSTPK increased significantly. Additionally, OsPP2C11 exhibited an SE event under cold stress, in which the second exon (E2) was skipped (Fig. 6A). This SE caused a frameshift and premature translation termination. These results indicate that the three genes may participate in distinct regulatory pathways or play divergent roles in cold signaling. Specifically, cold stress enhanced the splicing efficiency of OsCKLP2, promoting the production of its functional full-length isoform. In contrast, increased RI in OsSTPK and enhanced SE in OsPP2C11 tend to generate non-functional truncated proteins or trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) degradation.

Similarly, among the genes that exhibit both DAS and DE, a large proportion possess DNA-binding activity and are predominantly transcription factors. For example, under cold stress, LOC_Os02g49480 (bHLH transcription factor OsbHLH034) exhibited an SE event by skipping its sixth exon (E6), LOC_Os03g07940 (AP2/ERF transcription factor OsEREBP26) exhibited an RI event involving retention of its fifth intron (I5). Both events cause a frameshift and premature translation termination, likely leading to non-functional proteins or targeting the transcripts for NMD. In contrast, LOC_Os08g06370 (KANADI 2 transcription factor OsKNA2) underwent an A5SS event that reduced aberrant intron retention at the 5′ end, thereby favoring the production of its functional full-length isoform. These results indicate that cold stress induces distinct AS patterns in transcription factors, leading to changes in their transcript isoform profiles in rice. Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that cold stress suppresses the expression of OsbHLH034 and OsKNA2 but induces the expression of OsEREBP26 (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these results indicate that not only the expression but also the splicing of these transcription factors are regulated by cold, a dual mechanism that may be central to their role in stress adaptation.

To further explore the potential regulatory roles of transcription factors exhibiting both DE and DAS, we constructed co-expression networks for OsEREBP26, OsKNA2, and OsbHLH034 (https://cbi.njau.edu.cn/RiceTFtarget/). Genes with a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) > 0.6 were extracted and intersected with cold-responsive DEGs to identify putative target genes. In total, 139 potential target genes were identified for OsEREBP26, 110 for OsKNA2, and only 7 for OsbHLH034 under cold stress. GO enrichment analysis was performed for the putative target genes of OsEREBP26 and OsKNA2. The results showed that target genes of OsEREBP26 were mainly enriched in development and reproduction pathways. Target genes of OsKNA2 were significantly enriched in translation, DNA metabolic processes, signal transduction, and protein modification processes. In contrast, OsbHLH034 had only a small number of putative target genes, including those encoding transcription factors and transporters (Figure S9). These observations suggest that the three TFs may regulate downstream gene expression in response to cold stress.

Discussion

Low temperature is a major abiotic stress that adversely affects rice production by impairing seed germination, reducing germination vigor, and hindering seedling establishment, ultimately compromising yield and quality [10, 36]. Low-temperature germination (LTG) capacity is a key limiting factor for the broad adoption of direct-seeded rice, a method that is becoming increasingly popular worldwide [37]. Alternative splicing (AS), a key post-transcriptional mechanism, plays a crucial role in plant responses to abiotic stresses [26]. In Arabidopsis, AS increases proteome diversity by generating multiple isoforms from a single gene, through which it regulates mRNA levels and enables plants to rapidly adapt to cold stress [30, 32]. Several key genes including qLTG3-1, OsSAP16, qLTG11, OsUBC12, OsNAC5, and OsMYB30 have been reported to regulate LTG in rice [12–14, 18, 20, 21]. However, such genome-wide regulation of AS during rice seed germination under cold stress remains largely unknown. In this study, we performed RNA-seq to characterize transcriptome and AS in rice seeds under cold stress, and revealed extensive changes in both gene expression and AS profiles.

Differential expression analysis revealed a distinct growth–defense trade-off in rice seeds under cold stress (Fig. 1). This pattern aligns with the adaptive strategy conserved across multiple stress contexts [38], suggesting it may represent a universal plant response to both biotic and abiotic challenges. Future identification of key molecular components in these pathways will significantly improve our understanding of this trade-off. Cold stress also induced widespread alternative splicing reprogramming with enhanced splicing efficiency (Fig. 2). Notably, different AS types preferentially targeted distinct processes: SE events were biased toward signal transduction and RNA metabolism, while RI events tended to affect basal metabolism and transcriptional regulation. These findings suggest that plants coordinate distinct AS types to mount a multi-layered response, potentially linking splicing regulation to physiological adaptation during germination. Elucidating the mechanistic roles of upstream splicing factors and RNA-binding proteins will be a key future direction.

Previous studies have shown that intron retention (RI) often introduces premature termination codons (PTCs) that trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) [39]. In this study, we found that alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), skipped exon (SE) also introduced PTCs in the target genes, suggesting that not only RI but also other AS events introduce PTCs that target the transcripts for NMD-mediated degradation rather than translation into functional proteins. Beyond NMD, AS also plays regulatory roles by producing protein isoforms with altered functions. For example, AS of the protein kinase transcript acts as a molecular switch in rice, regulating the balance between immunity and growth by modulating the relative abundance of its two isoforms, OsMAPKKK18α and OsMAPKKK18β (RI type) [40]. Similarly, AS modulates the function of WRKY TFs (OsWRKY62 and OsWRKY76) that confer immunity against rice pathogens such as Magnaporthe oryzae and Xanthomonas oryzae [41]. In this study, we found that cold stress induces isoform-specific AS in both signaling genes and transcription factors during rice LTG. It enhances productive splicing to generate functional full-length transcripts for the protein kinase OsCKLP2 and transcription factor OsKNA2, while triggering unproductive AS events in OsSTPK, OsPP2C11, OsbHLH034 and OsEREBP26 that produce non-functional truncated proteins or target mRNAs for NMD (Fig. 6). We propose that plants employ divergent post-transcriptional strategies to adapt to cold stress: reinforcing the function of essential genes via improved splicing, while repressing other regulatory genes through unproductive AS. This selective modulation of transcript functionality fine-tunes gene activity and resource allocation, facilitating adaptive growth during LTG. The precise molecular roles of OsCKLP2 and OsKNA2 in LTG regulation warrant further validation through gene editing, overexpression, and isoform-specific functional analyses.

However, the present study was performed using only one rice genotype, ZH11, which may restrict the generalizability of our conclusions. Considering the extensive genetic variation in LTG among rice germplasms, verifying our key results in cold-tolerant and cold-sensitive contrasting genotypes, or comparing them with publicly available transcriptome datasets, would help enhance the robustness and broader applicability of our findings. In future research, we will include more representative rice accessions with contrasting LTG phenotypes and integrate public omics data to explore the conservation and divergence of AS regulation across diverse genetic backgrounds, with the aim of drawing more universal conclusions. Furthermore, as this study only characterizes the early transcriptional response and AS at a single time point, the AS events induced by cold stress in plants are dynamic and change over time [33]. Future studies involving multiple time points will help to further characterize the sequential changes in gene expression and AS events, and provide a more complete view of their temporal regulation during rice seed germination under low temperature.

Materials and methods

Plant material and cold treatment

The rice seeds used in this study were of the japonica variety Zhonghua 11 (ZH11). ZH11 seeds (30 per replicate) were evenly dispersed in Petri dishes lined with two layers of moist filter paper. Germinated seeds with visible plumule emergence were incubated at 28 ℃ in darkness for 24 h. For cold treatment, germinated seeds were incubated at 12 ℃ in darkness for 24 h [21]. Seed germination was performed on moist filter paper in sealed petri dishes, which maintained a saturated internal humidity environment.

Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-PCR), RT- quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from seeds treated at 28 ℃ or 12 ℃ using the M5 HiPer Plant Seeds Super RNA Extraction Kit (Mei5bio, China). RNA purity and integrity were examined by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry, and only high-quality RNA samples free of genomic DNA contamination were used for the next experiment. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using TranScript® Uni One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TRANS, AU311) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reactions were performed at 50 ℃ for 15 min (products for qPCR) or 50 ℃ for 30 min (products for PCR) followed by inactivation at 85 ℃ for 5 s.

To analyze alternative splicing (AS) patterns, key transcription factors and regulatory genes with significant differential AS under cold stress were selected. Primers were designed to span the alternatively spliced exons or introns to ensure specific amplification of target splice variants. Quantitative PCR was performed using the synthesized cDNA as the template, with amplified products ranging from 100 to 500 bp. The PCR products were subjected to 3% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized under UV light to distinguish different AS variants by their fragment sizes. UBQ10 (LOC_Os06g44080) was used as an internal control.

RT-qPCR was performed using PerfectStart® Universal Green qPCR SuperMix (TRANS, AQ631) on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus system, with the cDNA obtained from the above steps as the template. The primer design requirement is that the primers should not span the alternatively spliced exons or intron regions, so as to amplify the total transcripts of the target gene. Gene expression (Fold change) was quantified using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method [32], normalized to UBQ10, with the relative expression of untreated ZH11 set as 1.0. All RT-qPCR primers are listed in Table S1.

RNA isolation and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from ZH11 seeds, and its quality was assessed by measuring concentration and purity (NanoDrop 2000; Thermo Scientific) and evaluating integrity (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer; Agilent Technologies). Specifically, only RNA samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 8.0 were used for subsequent RNA-Seq analysis, which ensures high-quality RNA for reliable sequencing results. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw FASTQ files were processed with fastp for quality control [42]. The clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [43] followed by gene expression quantification with HTSeq-count to generate both FPKM values and raw read counts [44]. Replicate consistency was assessed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) performed on the count matrix using R (v3.2.0).

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 [45] with a threshold of q-value < 0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 2. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed on the OeBiotech Online Platform (https://cloud.oebiotech.cn/), p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Alternative Splicing (AS) analysis

AS events, including SE, MXE, A5SS, A3SS, and RI, were identified from the RNA-seq data using rMATS (v4.0.1) [46]. Differential AS events between cold-treated and control samples were called with the software with default parameters, applying a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and an exon inclusion level change (ΔPSI) of ≥ 0.2.

Statistical analysis

For RT‑qPCR analysis, three independent biological replicates and three technical replicates were performed for each sample. Expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined by Student’s t‑test (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001) in GraphPad Prism version 7.0.

Conclusions

In this study, we used RNA-seq to systematically analyze gene expression and alternative splicing (AS) dynamics in rice ZH11 seeds under cold stress. Functional enrichment indicated that cold-induced DEGs were mainly related to responses to abiotic and endogenous stimuli as well as nucleotide metabolism, whereas cold-repressed DEGs were enriched in diverse metabolic processes. Among the 12,494 differential AS events detected during low-temperature germination, most showed increased splicing efficiency, and multiple AS types frequently occurred within individual genes. We further identified cold stress differentially regulates the AS of key signaling and regulatory genes. Cold improves the splicing efficiency of OsCKLP2 and supports the production of its functional full-length transcript. It also triggers abnormal AS in OsSTPK, OsPP2C11, OsbHLH034, and OsEREBP26, which tend to generate nonfunctional truncated proteins or induce NMD. OsKNA2 is affected differently and undergoes A5SS that reduces abnormal intron retention, helping maintain the formation of its functional transcript. Taken together, our results reveal the important regulatory role of AS during cold adaptation in rice, and expand our understanding of the molecular basis of LTG.
