 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This CRADA provides new funding from SoCalGas and DOE’s BETO and FCTO Offices
 focused hydrogen mass transfer limitations in H2@Scale processes. The project leverages the
 existing hydrogen production capabilities at the Energy Systems Integration Facility (ESIF) and
 SoCalGas’ 700 L bioreactor system designed, built, and delivered to NLR under the first phase
 of this CRADA.

 This Joint Work Statement will cover new hardware modifications between NLR’s electorlyzer
 stack and the SoCalGas bioreactor located outdoors. The new hardware and controls will provide
 researchers with the tools needed to obtain preliminary experimental data for a non-provisional
 application due in July 2019. The IP being developed is expected to improve the productivity of
 the bioreactor and would have wider impacts on other end-use processes using pressurized
 hydrogen (H2).

 In addition, this new funding enhances an existing BETO Biopower award by developing a 10-
 15 kW electrolyzer that is scalable to the MW-class with reduced capital cost and higher
 efficiency aimed at improving H2 mass transfer to downstream processes, like biomethanation.
 Finally due to the close-coupling between the electrolyzer stack and bioreactor, R&D will focus
 on cell retention, nutrient maintenance, optimal water management and process controls.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: The 700 L bioreactor system, fed by NLR’s 250 kW PEM electrolyzer, will be used to
 obtain preliminary data to support the non-provisional patent by July 2019. The modification of
 the system will include adding a parallel H2 supply line from the electrolyzer to the bioreactor.
 This new line will require heat trace and insulation as it will carry both pressurized feed gas and
 water dissolved with H2, This new tubing run will extend from the electrolyzer stack cathode and
 bypass the water knock-out phase separator and desiccant H2 drying system, No signification
 modifications are expected to the bioreactor as the new H2 input line will be connected after the
 mass flow meter. Under this task, new safety systems and controls will be developed for NLR’s
 electrolyzer stack test bed.

 Figure 1. Dynamic gas ratio flow control (green), without implementing NLR IP, showing biological
 load following of hydrogen and carbon dioxide (H2 blue, CO2 orange) and resulting methane
 production (gray).

 Task 1 Work Completed and Outcomes: The biomethanation process requires an input gas
 ratio of 4 hydrogen (H2) molecules for each carbon dioxide (CO2) molecule resulting in the
 production of a methane (CH4) molecule and two water (H2O) molecules. In Figure 1, H2 (blue)
 and CO2 (orange) input flowrates were intentionally varied by the control system and the
 resulting CH4 produced (gray) show promising results as the methane-producing organisms are
 capable of increasing and decreasing their conversion rate up to 98% based on dynamic
 feedstock gases. This data validates the claim the biological conversion is capable of “load
 following” at elevated reactor pressure of 9 bar as gas flowrates are varied like they would be in
 a wind- or solar-powered water electrolysis system. Figure 1 also highlights the struggles of
 maintaining the required 4H2 : 1CO2 ratio (green) using pneumatically-driven proportional
 valves controlling gas flowrates with Coriolis mass flowmeters providing feedback on the 700L
 bioreactor system. Maintaining the 4:1 ratio of the feedstock gases shown here in Task 1 can be
 compared with NLR’s approach to ratio control using electrolyzer stack current highlighted in
 Task 3, with improved ratio control of the gases seen when using stack current to vary H2
 production rate.

 Task 2: This work leverages existing funding originating from the BETO Biopower project, to develop a
 pressurized scaled-down mobile bioreactor. This CRADA specifically includes the design, fabrication
 and integration of an on-board electrolyzer instead of using gas cylinders for the H2 feedstock. The new
 configuration will deliver H2 directly to the bioreactor from a 10 kW PEM electrolyzer stack, bypassing
 the H2 drying system and the H2/H2O phase separation sub-systems. NLR already owns the 10 kW stack,
 which closely matches the H2 production rate required by the scaled-down 30L bioreactor.

 3

 Figure 2. Scaled-down electrolyzer and bioreactor sub-systems installed in the mobile integrated
 energy research apparatus (MIERA).

 Task 2 Work Completed and Outcomes: Under the separate but complementary BETO-funded
 Biopower project (CRD-18-00775 with SoCalGas) a scaled-down 20L bioreactor vessel was
 designed, built, and installed (Figure 2). This project (CRD-19-809) focused on reducing to
 practice NLR intellectual property captured in the non-provisional patents listed below. The
 hydrogen-producing electrolyzer system, designed and built under this project, is capable of up
 to 25 kWdc power level (~0.5 kg H2/hour), while the initial stacks being used in this validation
 testing are rated at 3 – 6kW.
 The polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) stacks have 10 cells and operate at around 2 V per
 cell (~ 20 Vdc) at currents of 150 – 300 Adc. H2 (cathode) pressure from the electrolyzer stack is
 capable of pressures up to 20 bar allowing direct coupling of feedstock gas to the downstream
 bioreactor that has a maximum operational rating of 18 bar. The maximum allowable working
 pressure (MAWP) of the bioreactor vessel is 400 psig (27.6 bar) enabling additional head
 pressure from input gases like H2 to be greater than the operating pressure of 18 bar.

 This scaled-down system, designed and built into a mobile research apparatus, accomplishes the
 optimal configuration described in the non-provisional patents by directly connecting the
 hydrogen product from the cathode of the electrolyzer stack to the mixed gas input to the
 bioreactor (Figure 2), without a pressurized water/gas phase separator and a hydrogen dryer
 found in typical biomethanation systems.

 4

 Task 3: Work accomplished in Task 1 will remove hardware from the electrolyzer that normally
 provides dry H2 to the bioreactor. Now, this task will focus on optimizing feeding additional
 water containing dissolved H2 gas. This additional water will affect nutrient dosing rates, cell
 retention and water management controls. This task focuses on new approaches to maintain cell
 density and optimizing nutrient dosing controls due to the additional water coming from the
 electrolyzer.

 Task 3 Work Completed and Outcomes: Noting the challenges observed in feedstock gas ratio
 control of Task 1 and the design, build, and controls development of Task 2, work completed in
 Task 3 demonstrated the innovations contained in NLR’s patent applications listed below.
 Namely, this task validated the claim that feedstock gas (CO2 and H2) ratio control can be
 accomplished using electrolyzer stack current to obtain the required gas mixture of four
 hydrogen molecules per one carbon dioxide (4H2: 1CO2).

 Research conducted on the scaled-down system showed that the innovations contained in the
 patent applications are effective in accurately controlling hydrogen flow from the electrolyzer to
 the bioreactor. In this case, 80% of the gas sampled by the gas chromatograph was hydrogen
 (blue) and the remaining 20% was carbon dioxide (green), validating the 4:1 H2:CO2 ratio
 control (Figure 3).
