On the Doubling Dimension and the Perimeter of Geodesically Convex Sets in Fat Polygons
Mark de Berg, Prosenjit Bose, Leonidas Theocharous

Abstract
Many algorithmic problems can be solved (almost) as efficiently in metric spaces of bounded doubling dimension as in Euclidean space. Unfortunately, the metric space defined by points in a simple polygon equipped with the geodesic distance does not necessarily have bounded doubling dimension. We therefore study the doubling dimension of fat polygons, for two well-known fatness definitions. We prove that locally-fat simple polygons do not always have bounded doubling dimension, while any ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) -covered polygon does have bounded doubling dimension (even if it has holes). We also study the perimeter of geodesically convex sets in ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) -covered polygons (possibly with holes), and show that this perimeter is at most a constant times the Euclidean diameter of the set. Using these two results, we obtain new results for several problems on ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) -covered polygons, including an algorithm that computes the closest pair of a set of m m points in an ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) -covered polygon with n n vertices that runs in O ⁡ ( n + m ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n+m\log n) expected time.

Introduction

Motivation and background.

In computational geometry, the standard way to model planar objects or regions is by
polygons. Because polygons can have very complicated shapes and/or
interact with each other in complicated ways, developing efficient solutions
for algorithmic problems involving polygons is not always easy. In practice, however, the objects
or regions under consideration are usually fairly well-behaved. The
realistic input models

paradigm
[
11
]
suggests to make this precise by
defining properties of the input that are expected to be satisfied in practice and that
allow for easier and/or more efficient algorithmic solutions.

One such property that has been studied extensively is
fatness
, where the underlying assumption is
that real-world objects or regions are not extremely thin;
see below for more precise definitions. For example, there have been results on motion-planning
in environments where the obstacles are fat
[
25
,
26
]

on various graphics-related problems involving fat
objects
[
1
,
20
,
9
,
8
]
,
on flow problems on terrains consisting of fat triangles
[
7
]
,
on various data-structure problems for fat
objects
[
15
,
21
,
23
]
, and more.
Many of these results use one of the following two fundamental results: (i) the union complexity of
n

constant-complexity fat polygons in the plane is near-linear; see
[
3
]
and
the references therein; and
(ii) any set of
n
constant-complexity disjoint fat objects in the plane admits a hierarchical
decomposition of the plane into constant-complexity cells that each intersect
O(1)
objects
[
12
]
.
Note that both results concern constant-complexity objects and that they
essentially say something about the part of the space outside the objects. We are interested in what
happens
inside
a fat polygon.
In particular, we are interested in properties of the metric space

(P,\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}})
where
P
is a fat polygon (possibly with holes) and
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}
is the geodesic distance.
In other words,
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(p,q)
is the length of a shortest path between two points

and

in
P
.
There has been work on guarding and triangulating fat polygons
[
2
]
,
but the special properties that the metric space
(P,\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}})
may or may not have for fat polygons
have not been studied much, to the best of our knowledge. This is surprising as
problems involving shortest paths in polygons have received widespread attention,
and fatness has been studied and used extensively as well.
The only paper that is closely related to our work is by Bose, Cheong, and Dujmovic
[
5
]
,
who study the perimeter of fat objects, as discussed below.

Fatness variants.

The study of fat polygons was initiated by Matoušek
et al.

[
22
]
,
who studied the union complexity of
\alpha
-fat triangles
, which are triangles whose angles
are at least
\alpha
, for some fixed constant
\alpha>0
. Since then, the concept of fatness
has been generalized in different ways, both to convex and to non-convex objects.
For convex objects, all definitions are equivalent
in the sense that a convex object that is fat under one definition is also fat under the other
definitions
[
27
,
28
]
.
Our interest lies in non-convex objects, since inside a convex object the
geodesic distance is identical to the Euclidean distance. For non-convex objects,
different fatness definitions have been proposed as well, but these are no longer equivalent.
We discuss the most popular ones, focusing on definitions that apply to polygons.

Overmars, Halperin, and Van der Stappen
[

define a polygon

\mathrm{area}(P\cap D)\geqslant\gamma\cdot\mathrm{area}(D)
, for some fixed constant

This definition still allows the polygon to have very thin parts, as shown in

A third definition, by Efrat

Our contribution.

Recall that a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
is
c
-doubling
if every ball of radius
r

in the space
\mathcal{X}
can be covered by
c
balls of radius
r/2
. The

of the metric space is defined to be
\log_{2}c
. If

is a constant, then we say that
the metric space has
bounded doubling dimension
. Spaces of bounded doubling dimension
generalize Euclidean space of constant dimension, and many problems can be solved (almost)
as efficiently on spaces of bounded doubling dimension as they can in Euclidean space.
Unfortunately, if
P
is an arbitrary polygon then the metric space
(P,\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}})
may not have bounded
doubling dimension. The first problem we study is therefore: does the metric space
(P,\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}})

necessarily have bounded doubling dimension when
P
is fat?

For the fatness definition of Overmars
et al.

[
25
]
,
the answer is easily seen to be no, as exemplified by the polygon in
Figure 1
(i):
the ball centered at the red point in the middle of the polygon and whose radius

is the geodesic distance to the top-right vertex, needs
\Omega(n)
geodesic balls of radius
r/2

to be covered. Our first two results, presented in
section 2
, concern the doubling dimension
of locally-fat polygons and
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygons.

•

We show that locally-fat polygons do not necessarily have bounded doubling dimension.
More precisely, we show that for any
n\geqslant 3
there exists a simple polygon
P
with
n
vertices
and a geodesic disk
D
in
P
such that
\Omega(n^{1/3})
geodesic disks of radius
r

are needed to cover
D
, where
r=\mathrm{radius}(D)
.

•

On the positive side, we prove that the doubling dimension of any
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygon
is upper bounded by a constant that only depends on the constants
\alpha
and
\beta
and
not on the number of vertices of the polygon. This result even holds for

(\alpha,\beta)
-covered domains with curved boundaries and holes.

The fact that
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygonal regions have bounded doubling dimension
immediately implies a plethora of results that improve upon known results for non-fat
polygons; we mention several of them at the end of
section 2
.

After studying the doubling dimension of fat polygons, we turn our attention
in
section 3
to the following problem: can the perimeter
\mathrm{per}(R)
of a
geodesically convex polygonal region
R
be bounded in terms of its
geodesic diameter
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathrm{g}}(R)
, or perhaps even in terms of its Euclidean diameter
\mathrm{diam}(R)
?
For arbitrary polygons the answer is clearly no. In the polygon of
Figure 1
(i) the perimeter
\mathrm{per}(P)
of
P
,
which is geodesically convex, is
\Omega(n\cdot\mathrm{diam}_{\mathrm{g}}(P))
, and hence arbitrarily larger than
\mathrm{diam}(P)
. Bose, Cheong, and Dujmović
[
5
]
showed that the same can happen for locally-fat polygons, via the Koch snowflake.
For
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered simple polygons, on the other hand, they showed
that
\mathrm{per}(P)=O(\mathrm{diam}(P))
, where the constant of proportionality only depends on the
constants
\alpha
and
\beta
and not on the number of vertices of the polygon.
The applicability of this result is limited, because it only
bounds the perimeter of the polygon itself and not of geodesically convex sets
inside the polygon. We show that the result also holds in this much more general setting.

•

For any geodesically convex polygonal set
R
in an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygonal domain
P
,
we have
\mathrm{per}(R)=O(\mathrm{diam}(R))
.

A fairly immediate consequence of this result is the existence of an

\varepsilon
-coreset of size
O(1/\varepsilon)
for furthest-neighbor queries in an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered
simple polygon

. This improves on the
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
bound recently proved by
De Berg and Theocharous
[
10
]
for general simple polygons.
The result can also be used to obtain

of
m
points in

whose expected
running time is linear in

.

Notation.

We denote the Euclidean disk of radius

centered at a point

by

by

. For a polygon

From now on, when we speak of the doubling dimension of

2
The doubling dimension of fat polygons

In this section we investigate the doubling dimension of fat polygons.
We first show that locally-fat polygons do not necessarily have bounded
doubling dimension, and then we show that
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered
polygons do have bounded doubling dimension.

2.1
Locally-fat polygons do not have bounded doubling dimension

To prove that locally-fat polygons do not have bounded doubling dimension,
we first construct a family of locally-fat polygons
P_{m}
, for

and
\mathrm{diam}_{\mathrm{g}}(P_{m})\approx 2^{m}

The polygon
P_{m}
will be constructed recursively inside the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
,
in such a way that the shortest path from the lower-left corner
(0,0)

to the lower-right corner

resembles the order-
m
Hilbert curve.

The initial polygon

and a small value

Then slightly inflate these segments to give them width

This shortest path resembles the order-1 Hilbert curve, except that the middle link

Lemma 2
.

The polygon

is locally

Proof.

For simplicity we assume in the computations below that

Let

such that

does not contain

\tfrac{1}{2^{\ell}}

and diameter
\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2^{\ell}}

Let

be the squares of level

containing

Since
P_{m}\not\subset D(p,r)
, we have

. We consider three cases.

•

Case I:
r<\frac{\mathrm{diam}(S_{m})}{2}
.
Then

does not contain
S_{m}
. Moreover, since

is a square at the deepest level,
it doesn’t contain any parts of

This implies that
\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)\sqcap P_{m})\geqslant\tfrac{\pi r^{2}}{4}
.
Hence,

\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)\sqcap P_{m})\geqslant\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)\sqcap S_{m})\geqslant\frac{\pi r^{2}}{4}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\cdot\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)).

•

Case II:
\frac{\mathrm{diam}(S_{m})}{2}\leqslant r<\mathrm{diam}(S_{m})
.
Then
D(p,r)
contains more than
1/4
of the area of

, and so

\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)\sqcap P_{m})>\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2^{m}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\cdot\pi\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2^{m}}\right)^{2}>\frac{1}{8\pi}\cdot\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)).

•

Case III:
r\geqslant\mathrm{diam}(S_{m})
.
Then there exists an
\ell
with
0\leqslant\ell<n
such that
\mathrm{diam}(S_{\ell+1})\leqslant r\leqslant\mathrm{diam}(S_{\ell})
.
Since

, we thus have

.
Moreover, by construction, all points of

within

are connected via a path

. This means

.
Since

, we also have

. Hence,

\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)\sqcap P_{m})\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{2^{\ell+1}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\cdot\pi\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2^{\ell}}\right)^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{8\pi}\cdot\mathrm{area}(D(p,r)),

p_{i},p_{j}
have geodesic distance

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(p_{i},p_{j})>2
and can thus never be covered by the same geodesic disk of radius one.
Hence, the doubling constant of

is at least

.

Note that each

has
O(4^{m})
vertices. Hence, if

denotes the number
of vertices of

then

. Thus, the
doubling constant of
P^{*}_{m}
is
\Omega(n^{1/3})
.
∎

2.2
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered domains have bounded doubling dimension

Recall that a domain
P
, possibly with holes and a curved boundary, is
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered if for
every
p\in\partial P
there exists a triangle
T_{p}\subset P
with

p
as a vertex such that
T_{p}
is
\alpha
-fat—each angle is
at least
\alpha
—and such that each side of
T_{p}
has length at least
\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)
.

Theorem 5
.

Any
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered domain
P
has bounded doubling dimension.
In particular, it is at most
\log c(\alpha,\beta)
, where

c(\alpha,\beta):=\max\left(\left(\left\lceil\frac{48}{\sin\alpha}\right\rceil+1\right)^{2},\left(\left\lceil\frac{16}{\beta\sin\alpha}\right\rceil+1\right)^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Consider a geodesic disk
D:=D_{\mathrm{g}}(p,r)
with center
p
and
radius
r
in
P
. Let
B
be the square of side
3r
centered at
p
.
We partition
B
into a regular square grid
G
with
g\times g
cells, where
g:=\max(\lceil\frac{48}{\sin\alpha}\rceil,\lceil\frac{16}{\beta\sin\alpha}\rceil)
; see
Figure 5
. Let
s
denote the side length of a cell. Then we can see that
s=\frac{3r}{g}\leqslant\frac{m\cdot\sin\alpha}{16}
, where
m:=\min(r,\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P))
.

Figure 5:
Construction of the grid
G
. The geodesic disk
D
is shown in red. The figure is schematic and not to scale.

Now consider the set
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{\mathrm{g}}(q,r/2):q\in G\cap P\}
of geodesic balls of radius
r/2
centered at the grid points inside
P
. Note that
|\mathcal{D}|\leqslant c(\alpha,\beta)
.
To prove
Theorem 5
, it suffices to show that the disks in
\mathcal{D}
cover
D
.
In other words, we need to show that for any point
u\in D

there exists a grid point
q\in G\cap P
with
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(u,q)\leqslant r/2
.
We need the following well-known fact. For completenes, we give a proof.

Fact 5

(restate=triangleingrid,name=)
.

Let
G
be a regular grid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose cells have side length
x
.
Let
T
be an isosceles triangle whose two equal sides have length
t
and
meet at angle
\phi
. If
t\geqslant\frac{4x}{\sin\phi}
, then
T
contains a grid point of
G
.

Proof.

Every point of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is within Euclidean distance
x
—in
fact, within distance
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}x
—of some grid point.
It therefore suffices to show that
r_{\mathrm{in}}
, the radius of the incircle of

T
, is at least
x
.
We have
r_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{area}(T)}{\mathrm{per}(T)}
.
Using
\mathrm{area}(T)=\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sin\phi

and
\mathrm{per}(T)=2t+2t\sin(\phi/2)
we obtain

r_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{t^{2}\sin\phi}{2t(1+\sin(\phi/2))}\geqslant\frac{t\sin\phi}{4}.

Thus, if
t\geqslant 4x/\sin\phi
then
r_{\mathrm{in}}\geqslant x
, which finishes the proof.
∎

We now consider two cases.

•

Case 1:
D(u,r/4)\subset P
.

Since
s\leqslant r\cdot(\sin\alpha/16)\leqslant r/4
,
the disk
D(u,r/4)
contains a grid point

.
Since

we therefore have
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(u,q)=|uq|\leqslant r/4\leqslant r/2
.

•

Case 2:
D(u,r/4)
intersects
\partial P
.

This case is illustrated in Figure
5
(ii).
Let
u^{*}\in\partial P
be a point closest to
u
. Then
|uu^{*}|<r/4

and
uu^{*}\subset P
. Let
T_{u^{*}}\subset P
be an
\alpha
-fat triangle
with vertex
u^{*}
and all of whose edges have length at least
\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)
,
which is guaranteed to exist because
P
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered.

If
r<\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)
then every side of
T_{u^{*}}
has length at least
r
.
Thus,
T_{u^{*}}
contains an isosceles
\alpha
-fat triangle
T
with
apex
u^{*}
and side length

. Because

and

r/4=m/4\geqslant 4s/\sin\alpha
,
the triangle

contains a grid point

by

.
Since

, we thus have

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(u,q)\leqslant|uu^{*}|+|u^{*}q|\leqslant\frac{r}{4}+\frac{r}{4}=\frac{r}{2}.

On the other hand, if

.

We conclude that every

.

3
The perimeter of geodesically convex regions in fat polygonal domains

Recall that Bose et al.
[
5
]
showed that for any
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered simple polygon

there exists a constant
\mu(\alpha,\beta)
, depending only on
\alpha
and
\beta

\mathrm{per}(P)\leqslant\mu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)

Theorem 7
generalizes this result to arbitrary geodesically convex polygonal sets in

, and extends it from simple polygons to general polygonal domains.
As we will see in the next section, this is useful in several applications.
Note that we cannot hope to generalize the result to locally-fat polygons.
Indeed, there are locally-fat polygons for which we do not even have
\mathrm{per}(P)=O(\mathrm{diam}(P))
—the
polygon

from

is an example.

Theorem 7
.

Let
P
be an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygonal domain, possibly with holes.
Let

be a geodesically convex set in

such that

\partial R\setminus\partial P
is polygonal.
Then there exists a constant
\nu(\alpha,\beta)
such that

\mathrm{per}(R)\leqslant\nu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(R)
.

To prove
Theorem 7
, define
\Gamma_{1}:=\partial R\cap\partial P
and

\Gamma_{2}:=\partial R\setminus\Gamma_{1}
, and note that
\mathrm{per}(R)=\|\Gamma_{1}\|+\|\Gamma_{2}\|
. Refer to Figure
6
for an illustration of these sets.
We will bound
\|\Gamma_{1}\|
and
\|\Gamma_{2}\|
separately.

Bounding

Let

We partition

Then

\mathrm{diam}(C)=\sqrt{2}\,s<\beta\sin\alpha\cdot\mathrm{diam}(R).

(1)

Moreover,

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{per}(C)\;=\;g^{2}\cdot 4s\;=\;4g\cdot\mathrm{diam}(R)\;=\;O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha\beta}\cdot\mathrm{diam}(R)\right).

(2)

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

Let

for some cell

emanating from

Assume for a contradiction that there exists a point

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(p,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,S))\leqslant\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2\nu(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\mathrm{per}(\mbox{\sc rch}(S))
, and so

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,S))

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q,p)+\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(p,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,S))

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,C))+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2\nu(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\mathrm{per}(\mbox{\sc rch}(S))

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,C))+\varepsilon\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\mathrm{diam}(\mbox{\sc rch}(S))

\displaystyle\leqslant\ \mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,C))+\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,S)),

which proves that
C
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset.
∎

4
Closest pair in an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygon

Let
Q
be a set of
m
points contained in an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered polygon
P
. The closest-pair problem asks for
a pair of points
p,q\in Q
minimizing the geodesic distance
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(p,q)
. Using Theorem
7

we prove the following lemma, which will allow us to adapt the classical linear-time closest-pair algorithm for
the plane
[
14
]
to the geodesic metric in
P
.

Illustration for the proof of
14
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
P
be an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered simple polygon, and let
S
be a
square that intersects the interior of
P
. Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
be the connected components of

S\cap P
. Then there exists a partition of
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\}
into
t=O(1)

classes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{t}
such that the following holds:

There is constant
M
such that for every class
\mathcal{C}_{j}
and every pair of components

C_{a},C_{b}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
we have

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,y)\leqslant M\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)
for all points
x\in C_{a}
and
y\in C_{b}
.

Proof.

If
S\subset P
then
m=1
and the statement is trivial since any two
points
x,y\in S\cap P
can be connected by a segment of length at most
\mathrm{diam}(S)
.
Hence, we assume that
\partial P
intersects the interior of
S
.
Let
\ell
be the side-length of
S
.
We have two cases.

Case 1:
\ell\geqslant\frac{\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)}{8}
.
Then, for any
x,y\in S\cap P
,

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,y)\leqslant\mathrm{diam}_{\mathrm{g}}(P)\leqslant\mathrm{per}(P)\leqslant\mu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)\leqslant\tfrac{8\mu(\alpha,\beta)}{\beta}\cdot\ell\leqslant\tfrac{8\mu(\alpha,\beta)}{\beta}\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S).

So in this case we may put all components
C_{i}
into a single class and the lemma holds.

Case 2:
\ell<\frac{\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)}{8}
.

Observe that any connected component
C_{i}
of
S\cap P
, is a geodesically convex region. Indeed, if there were
x,y\in C_{i}
such that a shortest path
\pi(x,y)
leaves
C_{i}
,
then
\pi(x,y)
must exit
S
at a point
a
and re-enter
S
at a point
b
.
Let
\pi(a,b)\subset\pi(x,y)
be the subpath of
\pi(x,y)
from
a
to
b
.
Since
C_{i}
is connected, there is a path
\pi^{\prime}(a,b)\subset C_{i}
. Since
P
is
a simple polygon, the cycle
\pi^{\prime}(a,b)\cup\pi(a,b)
does not contain a part of
\partial P
.
But then we can replace
\pi(a,b)
by the part of
\partial S
that lies
inside the cycle, and obtain a shorter path from
x
to
y
, which is a contradiction.
Hence,
C_{i}
must be a geodesically convex, as claimed.
Applying Theorem
7
with
R:=C_{i}
we have
\mathrm{per}(C_{i})\leqslant\nu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(C_{i})
.
Since
C_{i}\subseteq S
, we have
\mathrm{diam}(C_{i})\leqslant\mathrm{diam}(S)
, and hence
for any
x,y\in C_{i}

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,y)\leqslant\mathrm{per}(C_{i})\leqslant\nu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(C_{i})\leqslant\nu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S).

(3)

Since
\partial P
meets the interior of
S
,
every component
C_{i}
satisfies
C_{i}\cap\partial P\neq\emptyset
. For each

i
choose a point
p_{i}\in C_{i}\cap\partial P
and let
T_{i}
be its witness
triangle. Let
S^{*}
be the square with the same center as
S
and with side length
3\ell
,
and let
A:=S^{*}\setminus\mathrm{int}(S)
be the annulus between the two squares.
The maximum Euclidean distance from a point in
S
to a point in
A
is
2\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)
; see Figure
9
.
An edge of
T_{i}
incident to
p_{i}
has length at least
\beta\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)\geqslant 8\ell\geqslant 4\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)
.
This implies that the two sides of
T_{i}
incident to
p_{i}
exit
S^{*}
, and because the angle between them is
at least
\alpha
we can conclude that
\mathrm{area}(T_{i}\cap A)=\Omega(\mathrm{area}(A))
. This implies that we can stab
the collection of objects
\{T_{i}\cap A\}_{i=1}^{m}
, with a constant number of points
q_{1},q_{2},...,q_{t}
.
Our classes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},...,\mathcal{C}_{t}
are now defined by assigning
each component
C_{j}
to the class
\mathcal{C}_{i}
such that
i
is the smallest index for which
q_{i}
stabs
T_{j}\cap A
.

Now fix a class
\mathcal{C}_{j}
and components
C_{a},C_{b}\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
.
Let
x\in C_{a}
and
y\in C_{b}
. To bound
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,y)
, consider the path

\pi=\pi(x,p_{a})\cup p_{a}q_{j}\cup q_{j}p_{b}\cup\pi(p_{b},y)
.
By Inequality (
3
) we have

\|\pi(x,p_{a})\|+\|\pi(p_{b},y)\|=\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,p_{a})+\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(y,p_{b})=2\nu(\alpha,\beta)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S).

Moreover the points
p_{a},p_{b},q_{j}
lie in
S^{*}
, which implies that

|p_{a}q_{j}|\leqslant\mathrm{diam}(S^{*})=3\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)
and
|q_{j}p_{b}|\leqslant 3\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)
.
Therefore
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,y)\leqslant(2\nu(\alpha,\beta)+6)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)
.

Combining the two cases, the lemma holds for all squares
S
.
∎

The algorithm.

We adapt the algorithm by Dietzfelbinger
et al.

[
14
]

that solves the problem in expected linear time in the Euclidean plane.
We process the points of
Q
in random order, and maintain the current minimum distance

\delta
and a grid of squares of side length
s:=\delta/(2M)
, stored in a hash table.
As long as
\delta
is the smallest geodesic distance among the processed
points,
14
implies that each grid cell contains at most
t=O(1)
points.
Indeed, if
|X(S)|>t
then two points of
X(S)
would lie in
components belonging to the same class of Lemma
14
,
implying
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(x,y)\leqslant M\cdot\mathrm{diam}(S)=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\delta<\delta
, a
contradiction.

Now let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
be the points from
Q
, in random order, and
suppose that the insertion of some point
q_{j}
changes the closest pair.
Let
S
be the cell of the current grid that contains the new point
q_{j}
,
and let
q_{i},q_{j}
be the new closest pair. Since
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{g}}(q_{i},q_{j})<\delta
we know that

q_{i}
must lie in cell
S^{\prime}
such that the Euclidean distance between
S
and
S^{\prime}
is
smaller than
\delta
. Thus, to find
q_{i}
we can restrict our
attention to the
O(M^{2})
cells within distance
\delta
of
S
(including
S
itself).
Since each cell contains at most
t
points, only
O(1)
candidates must be checked,
which we can do in
O(\log n)
time using a shortest-path query.
When a smaller distance is found, we update
\delta
and rebuild the grid.
The analysis of the expected running time is identical to the Euclidean case

[
14
]
, except that computing the distance between two given points incurs a logarithmic
overhead (after preprocessing
P
for shortest-path queries in
O(n)
time
[
18
]
).
We obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 15
.

Let
P
be an
(\alpha,\beta)
-covered simple polygon with
n
vertices and
let
Q
be a set of
m
points in
P
. A closest pair of
Q
under the geodesic distance
can be found in expected time
O(n+\kappa(\alpha,\beta)m\log n)
, where

\kappa(\alpha,\beta)
is a constant depending only on
\alpha
and
\beta
.
