Clinical profile and genomic characterization of the 2026 Bundibugyo virus index case in Uganda
Authors: Andrew Nsawotebba, Isaac Ssewanyana, Alisen Ayitewala, Misaki Wayengera, Stephen Kanyerezi, Jonathan Kabazzi, Valeria Nakintu, Aloysious Ssemaganda, Rodney Kisuule, Steven Ssekyondwa, Hellen Rosette Oundo, Ronald Kasujja, Amable Ayebare, Stephen Balinandi, Godfrey Pimundu, Tonny Muyigi, Godrine Mayanja Byekwaso, Ibrahim Mugerwa, Francis Kakooza, Susan Namboozo, Aidah Namakula, Judith Kyokushaba, Moses Joloba, Winters Mutamba, Mudarshiru Bbuye, Bruce Kirenga, Pontiano Kaleebu, Wilber Sabiiti, Reginald Rony Bahatungire, Caroline Achola, Sulaiman Ikoba, Allan Muruta, Bernard Lubwama, Atek Kagirita, Henry Kyobe Bosa, Jane Ruth Aceng, Susan Nabadda, Charles Olaro, Diana Atwine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bundibugyo virus disease remains a high-consequence threat in Eastern and Central Africa, where cross-border mobility, nonspecific early symptoms and delayed recognition can obscure transmission. In this case report, we describe Uganda’s 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease index case: a male patient who traveled from the Democratic Republic of the Congo to Uganda and was admitted to a private hospital in Kampala on 11 May 2026 after more than 2 weeks of vomiting and diarrhea, with epigastric pain, weakness and hiccups. He deteriorated rapidly, developing acute kidney injury, pulmonary edema, hepatic dysfunction, hypoxemia, delirium, atrial flutter, possible disseminated intravascular coagulation and multiorgan failure, and died on 14 May. A posthumous EDTA whole-blood specimen tested at the Central Emergency Response and Surveillance Laboratory was positive for orthoebolavirus RNA and confirmed as Bundibugyo virus by quantitative PCR with reverse transcription. Sequencing achieved 99% genome coverage at ≥100× depth. The 2026 Bundibugyo virus genome formed a distinct lineage approximately equidistant from the 2007–2008 Butalya and 2012 Isiro variants, differing by 216–227 nucleotides (~1.2% sequence divergence). Here, we demonstrate the value of fatality surveillance, private-sector surveillance, diagnostic optimization through national specimen referral and rapid molecular-genomic diagnostics for early detection, transmission chain interruption and public health response coordination.

Main

Ebola disease (EBOD) remains a high-consequence public health threat in Eastern and Middle Africa, where cross-border mobility and nonspecific clinical presentations can delay recognition of early cases, and obscure initial transmission signals1,2. Documented human outbreaks are primarily driven by three orthoebolavirus species, namely Ebola virus (EBOV), Sudan virus (SUDV) and Bundibugyo virus (BDBV), which cause Ebola virus disease (EVD), Sudan virus disease (SVD) and Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD), respectively3,4. The BDBV was first discovered during the 2007–2008 viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF) outbreak in the Bundibugyo District, Uganda. Subsequent molecular analysis revealed that the virus was genetically distinct from previously recognized orthoebolaviruses, a divergence with important implications for diagnostic assay design and genomic surveillance5,6.

On 17 May 2026, the World Health Organization declared BVD a Public Health Emergency of International Concern following an outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and Uganda. As with other orthoebolavirus infections, early BVD clinical manifestations are nonspecific and indistinguishable from severe malaria, bacterial sepsis, typhoid fever, viral hepatitis, severe gastroenteritis and other endemic acute febrile illnesses (AFIs) in and around the region7,8. This substantial overlap in clinical presentation hinders early case detection, particularly in low-resource settings where routine laboratory differentiation of AFI etiologies is limited or unavailable, increasing the risk of uncontrolled community spread. Consequently, rapid molecular laboratory confirmation is therefore central to case classification, infection prevention and control, contact tracing, safe burial decisions and the public health response.

In May 2026, amid heightened regional concern about filovirus transmission in the eastern DRC9, a fatal BVD case involving a male patient who traveled from the DRC to Uganda was confirmed after he was admitted to a private healthcare facility in Kampala10. The patient’s clinical history revealed a complex cross-border pattern of healthcare-seeking behavior: he reportedly sought medical care in the DRC and later at a private clinic in Hoima District, western Uganda, where no blood work was performed before he left, and then self-referred to a private hospital in Kampala. On admission on 11 May 2026, the patient presented with severe vomiting, frequent diarrhea, intermittent epigastric pain, generalized weakness and hiccups; vomiting and diarrhea had reportedly persisted for more than 2 weeks, although the exact symptom onset date was not ascertained. Initial assessment documented marked weakness, epigastric tenderness, bilateral chest crackles, borderline hypoglycemia and preserved oxygen saturation. Consequently, the working diagnoses included acute gastritis, suspected septicemia, severe hypoglycemia and acute kidney injury (AKI).

The patient deteriorated rapidly over the next 3 days. He remained febrile, nauseated, restless and intermittently vomiting, with worsening weakness, reduced urine output (oliguria) and recurrent hypoglycemia requiring dextrose. By 13 May, oliguria had progressed despite intravenous fluids and diuretics, and hemodialysis was initiated. His course was complicated by AKI, pulmonary edema, suspected shock liver or acute hepatitis, hypoxemia requiring oxygen supplementation, and delirium. He was transferred to the intensive care unit on 14 May with sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction, acute fulminant hepatitis, AKI, atrial flutter with a rapid ventricular response and possible disseminated intravascular coagulation. He developed sudden cardiorespiratory arrest, and death was declared after unsuccessful cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Table 1 summarizes the timeline of events from patient presentation to death. The clinical presentation was severe but not immediately specific for BVD, underscoring the difficulty of recognizing filovirus infection when gastrointestinal, renal, and hepatic manifestations predominate and the exposure history is incomplete or unknown. A retrospective epidemiological investigation identified recent travel to Ituri Province, DRC. Following confirmation of EBOD in the DRC, a senior infectious disease consultant, suspecting an epidemiological link to the ongoing outbreak, contacted the Central Emergency Response and Surveillance Laboratory (CERSL) to request VHF testing.

A posthumous EDTA whole-blood specimen of approximately 2.5 ml, collected on 14 May, was transported through Uganda’s national specimen referral system to CERSL in Wandegeya, Kampala. Testing was conducted under emergency-response conditions using biosafety and quality-management procedures for suspected high-consequence pathogens established at the laboratory11. Because postmortem whole blood frequently contains potent PCR inhibitors, RNA extraction was performed using four specimen input volumes (30 µl, 50 µl, 90 µl and 140 µl), which were optimized in a previous SVD outbreak11.

The RealStar Filovirus Screen PCR with reverse transcription (RT–PCR) assay (Altona Diagnostics) detected orthoebolavirus RNA in all four specimen dilutions, as shown in Fig. 1. Species-rank testing using the RealStar Filovirus Type RT–PCR assay (Altona Diagnostics) confirmed BDBV in all four specimen dilutions, with Ct values ranging from 16.50 to 18.59 (Extended Data Fig. 1). EBOV, SUDV, Reston virus (RESTV; Orthoebolavirus restonense) and Taï Forest virus (TAFV; Orthoebolavirus taiense) were not detected. The laboratory turnaround time from sample receipt to reporting results to the hospital and the Ministry of Health was 5 h. The dilution-associated Ct pattern was notable. In the screening assay, the 30 µl input yielded a lower Ct value than the undiluted 140 µl input, with a difference of 4.45 cycles. A similar, although smaller, pattern was observed in the species-rank assay. This finding is consistent with reduced amplification inhibition after dilution, a plausible effect in whole-blood and postmortem specimens containing hemoglobin, immunoglobulins, cellular debris, anticoagulants or other PCR inhibitors11.

Genomic sequencing achieved 99% genome coverage at 100× read depth. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the 2026 Uganda genome forms a distinct lineage relative to both the 2007–2008 Uganda and 2012 DRC BDBV genomes, as shown in Fig. 1. Pairwise genome comparisons identified 219 nucleotide differences between the 2026 genome and the 2007–2008 Uganda genomes, and 216–227 nucleotide differences relative to the 2012 DRC genomes, corresponding to approximately 1.2% sequence divergence across comparable nucleotide sites. As detailed in a recent joint analysis, the sequence is highly similar to genomes recovered from the ongoing DRC outbreak12. Compared to historical references, multiple amino acid substitutions were identified across the viral structural proteins and polymerase (NP, VP35, VP40, GP1,2, VP30, VP24 and L). Notably, no gene deletions were observed, and the GP transcriptional editing site remained intact, indicating that sGP and ssGP expression are expected to proceed normally. Sequence data are available on Pathoplexus (accession PP_006XCJJ.1)13. These genomic findings support the role of near-real-time sequencing in establishing phylogenetic placement, assessing relationships with contemporaneous regional transmission, and strengthening cross-border molecular epidemiology5,14,15.

This case report has limitations. First, it describes a single fatal imported case and therefore cannot characterize the full clinical spectrum, transmission dynamics or case-fatality risk of the 2026 BDBV outbreak. Second, the patient’s exact date of symptom onset and some early clinical details could not be confirmed because the case was recognized retrospectively after clinical deterioration and death. Third, there was an absence of blood work from the clinic in Hoima, limiting reconstruction of the early disease course. Finally, phylogenetic inference depended on the available background genomes at the time of analysis, including public and permitted outbreak sequences, and may be refined as additional genomes become available.

This case demonstrates four operational lessons for filovirus preparedness. First, mortality surveillance can detect cases missed during life. The patient was confirmed after death, following clinical suspicion, cross-border information sharing and postmortem specimen collection. This observation is consistent with Uganda’s recent SVD response, in which an index patient was detected at autopsy through an existing mortality surveillance framework, highlighting the value of postmortem investigation for identifying otherwise missed filovirus transmission events11,16. In settings where patients present late, move between facilities or die before diagnostic confirmation is complete, postmortem testing may identify hidden transmission events and guide safe body handling, burial planning and contact follow-up17. Second, private healthcare facilities must be integrated into national VHF surveillance systems. The index case was identified at a private hospital in Kampala, not at a public isolation unit or a designated outbreak treatment facility. Private facilities are often the first point of care for acute febrile and gastrointestinal illnesses, especially in urban and peri-urban settings. Excluding them from alert systems, specimen referral pathways, training, infection-prevention support and result-feedback loops creates a blind spot in surveillance. Third, rapid molecular confirmation and genomic characterization are complementary. Screening and species-rank quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR) enabled rapid confirmation and classification of the infection as BDBV, distinguishing it from EBOV, SUDV and other VHF-related threats, including the distantly related non-orthoebolavirus filovirus Marburg virus (MARV; Orthomarburgvirus marburgense). Genomic characterization is epidemiologically crucial, confirming phylogenetic linkage to the contemporaneous DRC outbreak and demonstrating divergence from historical BDBV genomic variants. Fourth, AFI surveillance is integral to VHF preparedness. Because early filovirus disease often presents as a nonspecific febrile or gastrointestinal illness that overlaps clinically with other endemic febrile infections, VHF preparedness cannot rely on hemorrhagic signs or late clinical suspicion alone. Strengthening AFI surveillance provides a practical early warning system and frontline mechanism for epidemic intelligence, detecting unusual severity, atypical progression, epidemiological risk, healthcare-seeking across multiple facilities and deaths that warrant escalation for VHF testing. When linked to exposure-risk assessment, private-sector alerting, national specimen referral and rapid molecular confirmation, AFI surveillance can shorten the interval between illness recognition, laboratory diagnosis, isolation, contact tracing and public health response. This is critical because delayed recognition of EBOD has been associated with larger and longer outbreaks1

In summary, Uganda’s posthumous confirmation of a BVD case in a private healthcare facility underscores the importance of an integrated outbreak detection system spanning clinical vigilance, fatality surveillance, specimen referral, rapid RT–qPCR, genomic sequencing and public health communication. As filovirus emergence and cross-border transmission remain recurrent threats in Eastern and Middle Africa, sustained investment in integrating private-sector surveillance, high-consequence pathogen diagnostics, biosafety systems and trained laboratory workforces will be essential to shortening the interval between presentation, confirmation and response.

Methods

Case identification and specimen referral

The case was identified following the clinical deterioration and death of an adult male patient from the DRC who was admitted to a private hospital in Kampala, Uganda, in May 2026. Demographic information, including patient sex and travel history from the DRC, was extracted from a standardized national VHF case investigation form and the electronic case admission record. Given the clinical course, an incomplete epidemiological history and regional concern for VHF, a posthumous EDTA whole-blood specimen was collected before death and referred through Uganda’s national sample transport system (Hub System) to CERSL, Department of National Health Laboratory and Diagnostic Services, Kampala.

Biosafety procedures

Testing was performed under emergency public health response conditions in response to a suspected high-consequence pathogen. Specimen handling was performed using personal protective equipment, including double gloves, a fluid-resistant gown, an apron, a face shield, an N95 respirator and boot covers. Processing was performed in a class III glove box (Könnecke Precision Mechanics) in accordance with institutional biorisk management and quality-management procedures11.

RNA extraction and specimen preparation

To assess possible amplification inhibition in whole blood, four specimen input volumes were prepared: 30 µl, 50 µl, 90 µl and 140 µl. Nuclease-free water was added to diluted preparations to achieve a final extraction input volume of 140 µl. Viral RNA was extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and used immediately for RT–qPCR testing11.

RT–qPCR testing

Broad filovirus screening was performed using the RealStar Filovirus Screen RT–PCR Kit 1.0, and species-rank confirmation was performed using the RealStar Filovirus Type RT–PCR Kit 2.0, which differentiates BDBV, EBOV, SUDV, RESTV and TAFV according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The targeted conserved gene region and the Filovirus sequences used to design the primers and probes of the RealStar Filovirus Screen RT–PCR Kit 1.0 are as described by Rieger et al.18. The RealStar Filovirus Type RT–PCR Kit 2.0 is based on the Filoscreen kit but contains only the primers and probes required for the Orthoebolavirus species rank; however, the manufacturer does not disclose the assay composition. Additional differential testing included assays for MARV and other epidemic-prone VHF pathogens. Reactions were run on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR platform. Each run included non-template controls and manufacturer-provided positive and negative controls. Results were interpreted using manufacturer-defined Ct criteria. Our previous work on the 2025 SUDV detection outlines the detailed RT–qPCR amplification and cycling conditions11.

Genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

Genome sequencing was performed using a targeted viral metagenomics approach on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina) with the Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel. FASTQ files were quality-assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1) and fastp (v1.3.3)19,20. Host DNA reads were removed using Hostile (v2.0.2)21, and the resulting reads were subjected to a custom EboGen pipeline (https://github.com/MicroBioGenoHub/EboGen/). Reads were mapped to the BDBV reference genome (accession NC_014373.1) using BWA (0.7.17-r1188) to generate BAM files22, which were processed with SAMtools (v1.21)21. Variant calling and normalization were performed using freebayes v1.3.8 and bcftools v1.7, annotated with snpEff v5.2, and incorporated into the reference genome to generate a consensus sequence23–25, with low-confidence positions masked and only sites with ≥100× read depth retained. The consensus genome was combined with a background dataset24, comprising publicly available historical BDBV genomes and 2026 DRC outbreak genomes used under restricted-access permission. Multiple sequence alignment was performed with MAFFT v7.525, and a phylogenetic tree was inferred with IQ-TREE (v3.1.2)26,27. The tree was rooted using Sudan virus genomes as outgroups (https://github.com/Kanyerezi30/CPHL-EBOV/), with root placement checked in Gotree28 and visualized in iTOL (v7)29.

Statistics and reproducibility

This study is a descriptive case report of a single fatal imported BDBV case identified through national public health surveillance. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The sample size was determined by the occurrence of the index case and the availability of a posthumous EDTA whole-blood specimen referred for VHF testing during the emergency response. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized because the study did not involve treatment allocation or experimental groups. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment because there were no intervention groups, and laboratory testing was performed for urgent public health confirmation. RT–qPCR testing was performed on a single clinical specimen using four extraction input-volume conditions to assess potential amplification inhibition; these conditions were not biological or technical replicates. Repeat testing was constrained by the emergency-response setting, limited specimen volume and the need to prioritize rapid diagnostic confirmation and sequencing.

Ethics and inclusion statement

The case involved one adult male patient. Patient sex was extracted from the national VHF case investigation form and clinical admission record. Gender identity was not collected as part of the emergency surveillance investigation and was not analyzed because the design of a single-case report does not allow assessment of sex-based or gender-based differences in clinical presentation, diagnosis, outcome or viral genomic characteristics. This work was approved by the Uganda National Health Laboratory Services Research Ethics Committee, which granted a waiver of informed consent because the study utilized de-identified routine surveillance data and posthumous samples collected under national public health emergency-response directives. The report was prepared in accordance with relevant case-reporting standards, including the CARE guidelines, and with the ethical principles for human participant research set out in the Declaration of Helsinki. Specimen handling and molecular testing were conducted strictly within state-approved biosafety networks. This work was conceptualized, designed and executed by the Uganda Ministry of Health through the Department of National Health Laboratory and Diagnostic Services and CERSL. Local scientists led the study design, laboratory testing, genomic sequencing, bioinformatics analysis, data validation and interpretation. Co-authorship was determined to ensure appropriate recognition of researchers, field epidemiologists, laboratory scientists, technologists and public health officials who made substantial contributions. International collaborators provided complementary technical and epidemiological input, while the sequencing infrastructure and EboGen bioinformatics workflow were operated in Uganda. The Ministry of Health retains data sovereignty, and data sharing was undertaken in accordance with national public health priorities, ethical requirements and applicable repository access conditions.
