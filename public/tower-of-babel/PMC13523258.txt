SP-printer: reconstruction of tumor stage-specific microenvironments via phenotype-integrated spatial transcriptomics
Authors: Weihao Deng, Yantao Shi, Hui Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies have rapidly advanced the investigation of tumor microenvironment (TME) mechanisms by enabling high-resolution mapping of mRNA molecules within their native spatial context. However, existing methods often overlook phenotypic indices like malignancy status, limiting their interpretability within the tumor microenvironment.MethodsThis work proposes SP-printer (Spatial-Phenotype printer), a flexible integrating approach that integrates phenotypic and spatial data to quantify stage-specific malignancy levels at each spot. SP-printer first identifies stage-specific metagenes from bulk RNA-seq datasets by using a S-score strategy. These metagenes are then projected onto ST data at pixel level via an information-enhanced mapping strategy, generating spatially resolved stage phenotypes. Finally, the pixel-level mappings are aggregated to spot resolution, enabling compatibility with standard ST workflows and supporting downstream analyses.ResultsSP-printer outperforms state-of-the-art methods in identifying malignant regions across diverse tumor tissues. Specifically, we identified early microenvironment diagnostic markers for primary liver cancer and liver metastasis and evaluated the effects of seven drugs on the tumor microenvironment, providing a comprehensive assessment of their therapeutic potential.ConclusionsSP-printer offers a unified framework to dissect the tumor spatial microenvironment and bridge the gap between cellular context and phenotypic outcomes, thereby enhancing the precision of tumor microenvironment analysis and facilitating informed clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08425-2.

Background

The tumor microenvironment (TME) consists of a variety of resident or infiltrating host cells (e.g. malignant cells, immune cells) and non-cellular components (e.g., secreted factors, extracellular matrix), all of which are the key factors of tumor progression, tumorigenesis, and metastasis [1–3]. Tumorigenesis represents a dynamic infiltrative progression characterized by remodeling of the TME. This process varies greatly by location and tissue, including cellular composition, cell-cell interactions and the continuous interplay between immune cells and malignant cells [4]. The TME dynamically evolves to establish stage-specific ecological niches across progression [5]. For instance, early-stage lung adenocarcinoma exhibits confinement to pulmonary parenchyma with favorable prognosis [6], whereas regionally advanced tumors demonstrate significant expansion requiring multimodal interventions (surgery/chemotherapy/radiotherapy) to curb invasion, portending poorer outcomes and life-threatening risks [7, 8]. Despite these pathophysiological gradients, current histopathological analysis remains constrained by ‌binary tumor/non-tumor classification‌ on tissue sections [9]. This limitation prevents precise stratification of TME-driven biological behavior. Currently, lack of bioinformatic tools to effectively delineate the microenvironment of the specific tumor stage.

Tumor complexity is characterized by the spatial diversity and heterogeneity of cells. Spatial sequencing technologies are fundamentally transforming biomedical research and clinical applications and are disclosing detailed intra- and inter- molecular insights into organized cells from healthy and pathological tissues [10]. The emergence of spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies has shown promise in elucidating the spatial structures of tissues, mapping the subcellular localization of mRNA molecules at the cellular level [11]. Especially, ST enables the spatial localization of cells alongside profiling spatial expression patterns of marker genes, therefore provides a unique chance to explore the diverse molecular heterogeneity under the cellular heterogeneity within the tumor development and progression [12]. However, ST data typically reflect an average of gene expressions from multiple cells in a given tissue region, complicating the precise characterization of specific cellular spots. ST generally needs more comprehensive and integrative considerations to advance the study of the TME mechanisms and help deepen our understanding of tumor biology. Conventional bioinformatics tools for ST analysis typically consider image analysis, cell-type deconvolution, spatial distribution, cell-cell communication, spatial expression patterns, the key regulators in spatial location, and identify cancer cells [13]. Most tools for malignant cell identification in ST data are based on integrating Hematoxylin and Eosin strained (H&E) image and expression data. For example, MUSE characterizes tissue composition through the combined analysis of morphologies and transcriptional states in ST data using a deep learning method; METI maps the cancer cells by integrating spatial transcriptomics, cell morphology and curated gene signatures [14]; SpaRx predicts cellular reactions to drugs by transfer learning from drug responses of cell lines [15], and Cottrazm identifies the tumor boundary connecting malignant and non-malignant cell spots in tumor tissues through integrating ST with H&E image [16]; Cancer-Finder, a novel framework designed to identify tumor regions with high accuracy, leverages a pre-trained model applied to annotated spatial transcriptomics (ST) data [17]; SCTP employs a multimodal fusion method to continuously quantify malignancy on ST Sections. [18]. Despite the outstanding performance of these methods, they don’t consider the clinical phenotype information, which shows a significant gap in phenotype prediction at TME level. In addition, there is currently no effective method to reconstruct tumor stage-specific TME ecosystems, which limits research on the TME driven tumor progression and response to treatment and the identification of potential early targets for clinical treatment.

Indeed, bulk sequencing data can provide clinical phenotype details of a large number of cohorts, which are widely accessible from the public resources like Gene Expression Omnibus [19] and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [20]. Leveraging phenotypic information from bulk data could open a new avenue for TME to drive phenotype prediction. Here, we show SP-printer, an integrated tool framework able to construct the phenotype stage-specific microenvironment based on spatial transcriptomes and bulk RNA-seq data. SP-printer begins by leveraging large-scale bulk datasets with known tumor staging information to identify a robust set of stage-discriminative metagenes (SP-MetageneGenerate). These metagenes are derived through integrative feature selection and represent condensed signatures capturing stage-specific transcriptional programs of the TME. Then, SP-printer employs an information-enhanced mapping strategy to spatially project the stage-specific metagene profiles back onto the ST data at the pixel level (SP-Print). This printing process effectively overlays inferred tumor stage phenotypes onto the spatial canvas, enriching the original ST data with annotations of valid phenotypes. Finally, the pixel-level phenotype mappings are convolved back onto the original spot-level resolution of the ST dataset (SP-SpotDecon), which ensures compatibility with standard spatial transcriptomic pipelines and facilitates downstream analyses such as spatial domain detection, tumor progression trajectory inference, and stage-aware cell–cell communication profiling. To demonstrate the robustness and generalizability of SP-printer, we first collected three cancer datasets in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and applied SP-printer to a collection of diverse spatial transcriptomic datasets. SP-printer consistently achieved superior spatial resolution and phenotype specificity compared with other methods, affirming its capacity to accurately map stage-specific microenvironmental signals. Importantly, we performed an in-depth case study of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by applying SP-printer to four spatial transcriptomic datasets from liver cancer patients with known tumor stages. We reconstruct the dynamic tumor landscape of the HCC microenvironment across tumor stages. Further, we integrated matched single-cell RNA sequencing data, revealing distinct spatial patterns of early immune infiltration. By combining these results with drug response datasets, we proposed stage-aware therapeutic strategies, highlighting candidate drugs and immune-modulating agents with potential efficacy in early-stage HCC.

Methods

Implementation of SP-printer

SP-MetageneGenerate: Identification the stage-specific genes based on bulk datasets

SP-MetageneGenerate takes expression matrix of RNA-seq datasets annotated with phenotype information (e.g., Normal vs. Tumor, Stage I-IV, non-response vs. response to drugs) as input to generate the meta-gene sets.

We first applied standard quality control procedures to remove low-quality samples and genes with low expression levels. For each dataset, we performed differential expression analysis using either the “limma” or “DESeq2” package, depending on whether the input data was normalized or raw count data, respectively. In addition, to further assess the robustness of our framework, we evaluated its performance under small-sample settings and artificially perturbed datasets with Gaussian noise. These analyses are aimed to ensure the feasibility and robustness of the model.

For each gene i, we computed the fold change and corresponding p-value to quantify its association with the phenotype of interest. SP-SpotDecon：Automatic annotation of spots with phenotype information

SP-SpotDecon takes the tumor enrichment image M, generated by SP-Printer, along with the spatial coordinates of each spot as input. For each spot, we aggregate the enrichment scores of the pixels that spatially overlap with or lie within its predefined spot boundary (typically a circular region centered on the spot). The final enrichment score of the spot is computed as the mean average of the corresponding pixel values. Based on phenotypic labels (e.g. tumor stage or drug response), SP-printer automatically assigns each spot to the phenotype with the highest enrichment score. Spots that do not show significant enrichment for any predefined phenotype are labeled as “other”. For simplicity and interpretability, the output can also be represented in a binary manner, where a value of 1 indicates enrichment for a specific phenotype and 0 indicates non-enrichment. A Quantile Threshold is applied during the convolution of pixel-level phenotype mappings back to the original spot-level resolution, with the default value set to 0.4. SP-printer performs mapping and analysis independently for each spatial transcriptomics image rather than jointly across multiple samples. Final biological insights are subsequently derived through integrative analysis of the independently generated spatial mapping results.

Immune cell states mapping

Data preprocessing

Bulk RNA-seq data were downloaded and normalized, and corresponding phenotype data were also collected. The Seurat R package (version 5.0.0) was utilized to process scRNA-seq and ST data. Consistent with typical single-cell data analysis protocols, our first step included removing genes that showed low expression levels in all cells (at least 400 cells), as well as removing cells with low genes and UMIs (at least 1 gene expression detected). Subsequently, the remaining expression data was normalized utilizing the NormalizeData function with the default parameters (scale.factor = 10000). We then identify highly variable genes using the FindVariableFeatures function (selection.method = “vst”). Following this, a transformation of the data was conducted using the ScaleData function, and principal component analysis was applied to these scaled data using the RunPCA function. For the first ten principal components, the next step involved calculating the cell-cell similarity based on the shared nearest neighbor graph with the FindNeighbors function, and the resulting cell-cell similarity matrix was subjected to a binarization process with diagonal elements as zero. Finally, we utilized the RunUMAP and RunTSNE functions to perform dimensionality reduction and visualize cells in low dimensions. For ST data, we first create a Seurat object for each sample within the downloaded data, which contains the histology image of the sample, the coordinates of each spot, and their gene expression. We then utilized the function SCTransform from the R package Seurat to normalize the expression matrix with default parameters.

Cell trajectory inference

Tumor cells exhibit different gene expression profiles when transitioning between different states, requiring a process of transcriptional reconfiguration. Monocle, an advanced bioinformatics tool, is designed to decipher the sequence of gene expression alterations that individual cells undergo during dynamic biological processes [22]. After capturing the overarching trajectory of gene expression changes, Monocle can accurately position each cell along that trajectory. To gain insights into the developmental trajectory and invasive characteristics of tumor cells during metastases, we employed the latest version Monocle3 to calculate the pseudo-time corresponding to each predicted stage I-IV spot with a predicted tumor likelihood greater than 0.5. First, the function learn graph was used to create the trajectory graph. Next, all analyzed cells were ordered according to its progress along this trajectory by the function order cells. The cells can then be colored by pseudo-time to show their order. Finally, the estimated pseudo-time for each spot can be extracted from the output of the function learn graph. Cells with infinite pseudo-time indicate that they were not reachable from the root nodes. By mapping the entire trajectory pseudo-time onto the histological image, we were able to reconstruct the developmental sequence of tumor cells and track their progression from early to advanced stages.

Cellular communication network analysis

We applied R package Cellchat_V2 [23] to evaluate the interaction weights between stage-specific regions, implemented with default parameters. Similarity, Cellchat_V2 was employed to quantify both the interaction weights, and the interaction counts between cell types, based on reference single-cell datasets.

Benchmark different tumor-annotated tools

We benchmarked SP-printer against other tumor detected tools of spatial transcriptomics, including CancerFinder [24], CopyKAT [25], METI [14], and SCTP [18]. All tools were run with default settings specified in their documentation.

Experimental results

SP-printer: construct tumor stage-specific TME based an integrated strategy

SP-printer aims to reconstruct stage-specific tumor microenvironment (TME) ecosystems by integrating TCGA bulk RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and H&E-stained histological images (Fig. 1, Methods). It comprises three core components: (1) identification of stage-specific reference metagenes (SP-MetageneGenerate), (2) determination of the tumor stage-specific TME landscape (SP-Print), and (3) reconstruction of spot-level transfer labels for downstream spatial annotation (SP-SpotDecon). For the SP-MetageneGenerate module, we applied differential expression analysis tools (e.g., Limma and DESeq2) to the bulk RNA-seq data to compute p-values and fold changes (FC). Based on these metrics, we defined a composite score (S-score) for each gene to quantify its stage-specificity. Higher S-scores are assigned to genes that exhibit both large expression changes and strong statistical support, thereby enabling a more balanced and robust prioritization compared to using either metric alone (Fig. S1). Genes ranked by S-score and exhibiting both statistically significant p-values and substantial FCs were selected as candidate metagenes, ensuring that the final set of SP-metagenes exhibits distinct expression patterns across tumor stages (Fig. 1A, Methods). For the SP-Print module, the identified SP-metagenes were then used to annotate tumor stage-specific regions through a customized implementation of the TESLA framework [28]. TESLA enhances gene expression resolution by leveraging histological images, and by integrating tissue morphology with molecular profiles, it enables a comprehensive characterization of the tumor microenvironment across diverse tumor types. For the SP-SpotDecon module, the spatial coordinates of transcriptomic spots were utilized to map the stage-specific tumor microenvironment annotations from the pixel level back to the spot level, thereby improving compatibility with downstream analyses, including tumor progression reconstruction, immune infiltration analysis, and drug–microenvironment interaction profiling (Fig. 1B).

The advantage of ST combined with bulk RNA-seq data enable accurately identifies stage-specific reference metagenes, enabling biologically grounded and progression-aware annotation, which ensures the spatial reconstruction is informed by transcriptomic signatures relevant to tumor evolution. Using a customized TESLA model, SP-printer bridges molecular and morphological information from spatial transcriptomics and histological images, enhancing resolution beyond spot-level. This allows for precise delineation of tumor regions and stage-specific microenvironmental zones. By mapping high-resolution annotations back to the spot level based on spatial coordinates, SP-printer ensures compatibility with widely used downstream analyses such as immune infiltration profiling, tumor heterogeneity modeling, and spatial drug response prediction. Overall, this modular and integrative workflow enables accurate, interpretable, and biologically meaningful reconstruction of dynamic tumor ecosystems across cancer stages.

Benchmarking the identification of tumor malignant cell spots

We performed SP-Printer to delineate the malignant region, normal region, and other region of ST data. We benchmarked SP-Printer against other state-of-the-art methods (Cancer-finder [24], Copykat [25], SCTP, and METI [14]) for identifying malignant regions using 8 samples, including 4 from colorectal cancer (CRC) and 4 from liver metastasis (LM) [29] (methods). Using the pathologist’s segmentation with tumor areas as the gold standard, we use SP-MetageneGenerate to generate meta gene sets for normal and tumor tissues based on bulk RNA-seq data. These gene sets were then used to identify spatial tumor regions by using SP-Print, facilitating comparisons with other methods. We present the tumor likelihood predictions for spots using SP-SpotDecon and binary prediction using baseline approach Cancer-Finder respectively. The SP-printer predictions remarkably outperform Cancer-Finder and show a higher similarity with the pathological segmentation (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2-S4). In addition, SP-printer consistently outperformed existing methods such as CancerFinder, METI, SCTP, and CopyKAT (Fig. 2B, Fig. S2). We further assess the CNV score among the normal spots, malignant spots, and other spots, respectively. We found CNV scores are significant higher in malignant cell spots, where lower CNV scores were observed for normal cell spots (Fig. 2C). In the other cell spots, CNV scores were higher compared to normal cell spots, suggesting that these regions have a malignant tendency. We hypothesize that this could be due to a mixture of different disease stages within the microenvironment (Fig. 2C). In addition, we evaluated the performance of SP-printer through parameter sensitivity, sample-size, and noise perturbation analysis. For the quantile threshold used in the SP-SpotDecon module, the result showed relatively best performance observed when the threshold ranged from 0.3 to 0.4 (Fig. S5A). Robustness analyses under small-sample size and noisy-data conditions demonstrated that SP-printer can maintain stable spatial mapping performance (Fig. S5B). Overall, our results show high consistency between the tumor regions and the annotated tumor regions, with accuracy ranging from 77% to 96% in ST data, indicating that our method is robust.

SP-printer determines tumor stage-specific spatial regions in liver cancer

To demonstrate the efficient application of ST-printer to real spatial transcriptomics data of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) to dissect stage-specific spatial regions within the tumor microenvironment. We collected bulk RNA-seq data of HCC from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and applied SP-MetageneGenerate to generate metagenes for each cancer stage (Fig. 3A-B, Fig. S6-S10). These stage-specific metagenes were then mapped to spatial tissue pixels using SP-SpotDecon (Fig. 3C). Our results revealed distinct molecular features across different stages of HCC when mapped onto spatial tissue regions. Specifically, the metagenes in stage I showed a gradual and blurred transition between tumor and normal regions, suggesting that early-stage tumors closely resemble their normal counterparts at the molecular level. In contrast, both stage II and stage III HCC showed clear and well-defined boundaries between tumor and normal tissue, which suggest that the tumor is not yet highly infiltrative and may be more responsive to interventions. In stage IV, the tumor boundaries became indistinct, and the expression of metagenes extended beyond the tumor core into normal tissue areas (Fig. 3D). Notably, metagenes were detected in normal regions, indicating that the tumor’s influence on the surrounding tissue has become more widespread. Further, we employed the spatial dispersion index (SDI) to quantitatively assess the spatial expansion of metagene expression across the tissue (Fig. 3E). Stage I tumors showed a high degree of spatial dispersion, with expression overlapping significantly with normal tissue. Stage II and III tumors exhibited more localized metagene expression, corresponding to compact and identifiable tumor regions. Stage IV tumors had the highest spatial dispersion, with metagene expression spreading beyond the tumor and into normal areas. These findings highlight the nonlinear evolution of molecular characteristics as tumors progress. In early-stage HCC (Stage I), the transition between tumor and normal tissue is gradual and difficult to define. In mid-stage tumors (Stage II and III), the molecular boundaries are still clear, making them favorable for targeted therapies. However, in late-stage tumors (Stage IV), the tumor’s influence on the surrounding normal tissue increases significantly, indicating extensive remodeling of the tumor microenvironment or potentially early signs of metastatic spread.

SP-printer explores the drug-related tumor microenvironment

To further understand how various drugs affect the TME and which treatment may be most effective at specific tumor stages, we integrated drug response data with spatial transcriptomics using SP-printer. We collected data for seven commonly used drugs in the treatment of HCC, including Sorafenib, Lenvatinib, and others to assess their effects on the HCC’s microenvironment. From these drug response datasets, we generated drug-specific metagenes using SP-MetageneGenerate that reflect the molecular signatures induced by each drug (Fig. 4A). These drug metagenes were then mapped onto spatial tissue pixels using SP-SpotDecon, allowing us to visualize how drug-induced changes in gene expression correlate with tumor regions at different stages of HCC (Fig. 4B). Notably, we observed that the specific targets of the first-line agents Sorafenib, RAF1 and RAF2, as well as the targets of the first-line agents Lenvatinib, FGFR2 and FGFR3, showed consistent enrichment patterns with the metagenes in the tumor regions [30] (Fig. 4C). Further, spatial correlation analysis shows that Sorafenib and Pemigatinib had the strongest correlation with tumor regions, while Lenvatinib and Apatinib had the lowest correlation with tumor regions (Fig. 4D). To further investigate how drug responses correlate with tumor stage-specific microenvironments, we integrated the stage-specific spatial transcriptomics results from the previous section with drug response data. Sorafenib remains the most widely used and effective drug for the treatment of HCC, particularly in earlier stages (stage II and III), where its multi-targeted mechanisms have shown robust clinical outcomes. However, Lenvatinib demonstrates superior efficacy in advanced-stage (stage IV) hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), likely due to its potent anti-angiogenic and immune-modulatory properties [31] (Fig. 4E). Additionally, we validated the downregulated metagenes of Sorafenib, Lenvatinib, and Pemigatinib on HCC TCGA datasets and identified five genes (CYFIP1, DYNC1H1, INTS1, RBM19, and SRSF3) that were significantly upregulated in HCC stages I-III and enriched in tumor region (Fig. 4F, Fig. S11). After drug intervention, these genes showed significant downregulation in response group, suggesting that they may serve as potential early treatment targets for HCC. This finding indicates that these genes play a crucial role in the early progression of HCC and are responsive to treatment, highlighting their therapeutic potential in the early stages of the disease.

SP-printer uncovers shared molecular mechanisms and treatment strategies in hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal cancer liver metastasis

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRC-LM) represent two distinct but clinically relevant forms of liver malignancy. Despite differences in their origin, the liver microenvironment plays a crucial role in both tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. While the treatment of primary HCC has made notable progress with targeted therapies and immunotherapy, the treatment of colorectal cancer liver metastasis remains particularly challenging. The liver’s unique microenvironment, including its immune cell composition, vascularity, and extracellular matrix, often contributes to the difficulty in treating liver metastases effectively. This complexity underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the shared molecular mechanisms between HCC and CRC-LM, which may help identify more effective treatment strategies.

To address this challenge, we utilized SP-printer to integrate bulk RNA-seq data from HCC with spatial transcriptomics of CRC liver metastasis. In contrast to HCC, the bulk characteristic genes at different stages of HCC exhibited clear spatial features. We observed that metagene expression in CRC-LM became progressively enriched in the tumor region, forming a clear boundary between tumor and normal tissue (Fig. 5A, Fig. S12). As the disease progressed, especially in advanced stages, metagene expression was highly concentrated within the tumor, showing a clear separation from the surrounding normal tissue. CNV analysis revealed that the regions enriched with HCC metagenes exhibited progressively higher CNV scores (Fig. 5B). Pseudo analysis on predicted stage I-IV spots showing between a strong concordance between pseudo-time distribution and clinical stage annotation (Fig. S13). To further explore the molecular mechanisms of each stage-specific regions, we deconvoluted the cellular composition in three tissue slides and performed graph-based clustering based on harmony-corrected principal components and annotated the clusters using the cell types with the highest probability and visualized by the UMAP analysis. We found that the clusters were grouped according to the stage-specific regions we annotated and clustered by the progression timeline of the tumor (Fig. 5C). We observed a dynamic shift in the abundance of cDC1s and macrophages across different stages of tumor progression. cDC1s showed a progressive increase in abundance from stage I to stage IV. In contrast, macrophages displayed the opposite trend, with a significant increase in abundance from normal tissue to stage I, and their abundance gradually decreased as the disease progressed through stage II to stage IV (Fig. 5D-E, Fig. S14-S15). These trends suggest a stage-specific immune cell distribution in the liver tumor microenvironment, with cDC1s becoming more prominent in later stages of tumor progression, while macrophages are more abundant in the earlier stages and in normal tissue. To explore early immune molecular mechanisms, we detected cDC1s-specific markers, such as HLA-DPA1 and HLA-DPB1 in Stage I, and ARF3 and ILF3 in Stage II, which were notably upregulated in specific regions of the tissue. These markers were also be upregulated in the corresponding stages of the bulk datasets, highlighting the stage-specific activation of cDC1s during tumor progression (Fig. 5F-G, Fig. S16). In addition, we observed that the abundance of CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T cells increased as cDC1 cells became more abundant. The observed trend highlights the potential for cDC1s to drive T cell-mediated immunity, especially as the tumor progresses through various stages.

To explore whether the HCC-targeting drugs are effective against CRC-LM, we also analyzed the drug response in CRC-LM using SP-printer. By integrating drug-specific metagenes for seven drugs with spatial transcriptomics of CRC-LM, we examined how these drugs affect gene expression patterns in the metastatic liver microenvironment. We found these drug metagenes enriched in tumor regions (Fig. 5I, Fig. S17). Spatial correlation analysis shows that Bevacizuma, Lenvatinib, Sorafenib, Regorafenib and Pemigatinib exhibited a positively linear correlation with tumor stage-specific regions (Fig. 5J). We further intersected the differentially expressed genes induced by Sorafenib, Regorafenib, and Bevacizumab with the stage-specific genes from Stage II-III, we identified S100A6 as a key gene that was consistently upregulated in the tumor regions and was also enriched in stage II-III regions (Fig. 5K). S100A6 has been reported to promotes carcinogenesis of cells [32–34] and highly expressed in pDC1s cells (Fig. 5L). Cell communication analysis showed that pDC1s are regulated by cDC1s, while CD8 + T and CD4 + T cells are also regulated by cDC1s, which is consistent with the observation that the abundance of CD8 + T cells and CD4 + T cells increased as cDC1s cells became more abundant (Fig. 5M). The results suggest that in the treatment of colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRC-LM), once metastasis is detected, drugs such as Bevacizumab, Lenvatinib, Sorafenib, Regorafenib, and Pemigatinib can be used for therapeutic intervention to reduce metastatic symptoms. Furthermore, the findings highlight the key role of cDC1s in modulating the immune response, where their presence promotes the recruitment or activation of T cells, particularly CD8 + T and CD4 + T cells, which are critical for effective anti-tumor immunity. Additionally, S100A6 may serve as both an early diagnostic marker and a drug response biomarker, providing valuable insights into treatment efficacy and disease progression.

Discussion

Despite advances in scRNA-seq and spatial transptomics [35], accurately predicting phenotypes like malignancy at single-cell or spatial resolution remains a major challenge. This limits our ability to resolve tumor heterogeneity and hinders precise tumor microenvironment analysis. In this work, we introduce SP-printer, a flexible approach that brings together phenotypic information from bulk data and spatial spots information from ST data. In a recent work [17], Cancer-Finder detects malignant cells using a pre-trained model. However, the evaluation perspectives of SP-printer and Cancer-Finder are different. SP-printer can provide stage-specific malignancy quantification at each spatial spot, enabling a more fine-grained assessment of tumor microenvironmental characteristics. Given that tumors originate from individual cells and progressively expand spatially, stage-specific quantification using SP-printer provides a more accurate representation of the dynamic progression of tumor tissues. Unlike existing methods, SP-printer integrates batch RNA-seq data to capture phenotypic information, enabling more accurate definition of phenotypic states across different stages. This reference-based integration significantly increases the effectiveness of SP-printer in identifying phenotypic related spatial tumor region, surpassing traditional methods in both scale and precision.

SP-printer represents a flexible and efficient multi-modal integration framework that enables direct mapping of bulk transcriptomic information onto spatial transcriptomics data without the need for pre-training. Unlike deep learning-based spatial annotation models, SP-printer operates in a plug-and-play manner, requiring only bulk RNA-seq datasets with relevant phenotypic labels (e.g., tumor stage, treatment response) and spatial transcriptomic profiles from the tissue of interest. This design greatly lowers the technical barrier for adoption, as bulk transcriptomic datasets are widely available from public repositories such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), GEO, and ArrayExpress. Moreover, the modular nature of SP-printer allows flexible customization for different disease contexts, phenotypic definitions, and downstream analyses, making it highly adaptable to diverse research questions. In practice, the entire analysis can be executed in a user-friendly, one-click workflow, ensuring reproducibility and accessibility for both computational and experimental researchers. These advantages collectively position SP-printer as a broadly applicable tool for integrating large-scale public resources with spatially resolved data to gain novel biological insights.

It is noteworthy that S-score we defined for each gene within SP-printer plays a pivotal role in the prediction model and enables precise quantification of key features within the phenotypic microenvironment. This was further validated through an additional comparison in which reference genes were selected solely based on p-values or fold-change values (Fig. S1). Another key strategy was to rank genes by S-score and select those with high stage-specific expression in the corresponding stage as the final reference gene set, thereby ensuring that the background information carried by the reference genes maximally reflects the phenotypic characteristics of that disease stage.

Beyond its methodological flexibility, SP-printer holds substantial potential for translational applications. By leveraging its ability to integrate stage-specific molecular signatures from bulk data with spatially resolved tumor microenvironments, SP-printer can facilitate the discovery of early diagnostic biomarkers that are both spatially and phenotypically relevant. In our study, this approach successfully pinpointed S100A6 as a candidate marker for early-stage colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRC-LM), with both spatial enrichment and phenotypic specificity. Furthermore, the same framework enables the identification of drug response associated genes and spatial patterns, which may inform therapeutic decision-making. For example, we demonstrated that key drug targets for Bevacizumab, Lenvatinib, Sorafenib, Regorafenib, and Pemigatinib exhibited spatial correlation with stage-specific tumor regions, suggesting their potential utility for early intervention once metastasis is detected. These findings underscore SP-printer’s capability not only as a research tool for understanding tumor progression but also as a practical platform for precision oncology.

Limitations

While SP-printer provides a flexible and robust framework for integrating bulk and spatial transcriptomic data, there are two limitations to note. First, the accuracy of stage or drug response–specific mapping is inherently dependent on the quality of the input bulk RNA-seq datasets; biases or batch effects in public data could potentially affect the spatial results. Although we evaluated the robustness of our model under challenging conditions, including small sample sizes and noisy data. We still encourage the use of as many phenotypically annotated samples as possible in practice to further improve reliability and reduce potential bias. Second, our current approach relies on predefined phenotypic group labels (e.g., tumor stages or drug-response categories) in the bulk data. For datasets lacking phenotype labels, semi-supervised approaches or recent unsupervised approaches can be applied. For example, recent multimodal clustering methods for cancer risk prediction or stratification can be used to generate pseudo-labels, which can then be incorporated into SP-printer for downstream tumor microenvironment reconstruction and analysis.
