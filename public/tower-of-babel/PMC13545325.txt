The human foundations of anatomy at The University of Sydney: One hundred and one years of body procurement
Authors: Rebekah A Jenkin, Kevin A Keay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBody procurement at The University of Sydney has a long history. Anatomy legislation (1881 Anatomy Act) modeled on the British Anatomy Act 1832 legalized procurement of unclaimed bodies from public institutions for anatomical dissection at licensed Schools of Anatomy, effectively conferring the University of Sydney an exclusive license until the 1960s. This study documents the procurement of 7609 bodies between 1883 and 1983, of which 4084 were unclaimed. Until the 1930s, bodies were predominantly from public asylums, with a small number from psychiatric asylums, hospitals and morgues. Donors were uncommon as were female bodies, reflecting prevailing societal antipathy toward anatomical dissection, the characteristics of asylum residents, and academic attitudes to the dissection of the female body. The individuals whose bodies sustained medical education were diverse in origin and circumstances: ex‐convicts, immigrants, miners, Chinese indentured laborers, Indigenous Australians and locally born individuals. Although the law provided a limited right of objection, asylum residents were poorly positioned to exercise it. Mirroring practice across the Western World, their classification as “unclaimed” reflected legal procedure and poverty, institutionalization, separation from family, discriminatory and inaccurate record‐keeping, and Colonial‐era assumptions of social worth. Little consideration was given to their cultural beliefs, preferences and rights. The University shifted to a body donation program in the 1940s as the availability of unclaimed bodies diminished. This shift was rapid; the last unclaimed body was received in 1961.Keywords: anatomy education, asylums, body donation, body procurement, consent

INTRODUCTION

The history of medical education at The University of Sydney has been described and celebrated in several publications. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 These histories have deservedly acknowledged the establishment of the Medical School, its early adoption of (then) innovative pedagogy such as mandating anatomy education for nurses and masseurs (precursors to physiotherapists) and increasing the duration of study to obtain a medical degree to support the development of a local medical workforce. 2 Yet they have omitted the people whose bodies enabled medical education. Anatomical examination (the study of the human body through dissection and later prosection) was central to the medical program. Ready access to sufficient bodies for students to dissect was essential for accreditation of the medical degree by the British Medical Council. 7 The history of the Medical School is therefore also a history of the procurement, use, and disposition of human remains.

The legal foundation for body procurement was the Anatomy Act 1881 (NSW). 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The Act was modeled on the British Anatomy Act 1832. 13 As was evident in the parliamentary debate, 7 , 9 , 10 , 11 the legislation was informed by the scandals of “resurrectionism”, grave robbing and other unsavory acts associated with anatomy in Britain. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 The Act sought to limit the capacity for unsupervised dissection by unlicensed practitioners and to remove the need for grave robbing. 7 , 10 A license, restricted to qualified medical practitioners, surgeons, and medical students, was required to conduct anatomical examination, and the premises at which it could occur were also required to be licensed and associated with a university Effectively, the legislation gave the University an exclusive license to procure unclaimed bodies for anatomical dissection until the 1960s. 9 , 10 , 11 , 19

Bodies could only be procured from the sources proscribed in the legislation; that is, public institutions, excluding prisons. In practice, the Act established a hierarchy of post‐mortem rights; those who died in public institutions, excluding prisons, but including hospitals, morgues, public asylums and psychiatric asylums were vulnerable to having their bodies used for dissection; those who were treated privately were protected. 7 , 9 , 10 , 11 This distinction effectively rendered vulnerable to dissection only those who suffered debility and disability, mental or physical, social isolation or disenfranchisement from family by virtue of time, distance or both; in effect the “lower class”. 8 , 19 Again, mirroring the provisions of the British 1832 Act, consenting donors were anticipated, although they were rare. 19

Bodies had to be “unclaimed” as defined at law; within twelve hours of the individual's death in a public institution no family member had been identified as willing to claim the body and able to make provision for its burial. The timeframe was debated extensively with the initial Anatomy Bill mirroring the 48 hours specified in the British Act. 7 , 9 , 10 , 11 Neither option was particularly reasonable in a location where communications often took days to weeks, and where many asylum residents were born overseas. 19 Once twelve hours elapsed, the medical superintendents of asylums were legally able to send a body to the Medical School. Although there was a statutory right of objection, it was narrow and formal; this could only occur in writing, with two witnesses. Few residents of the public asylums were aware of this option, and even fewer were able to exercise it. Many residents were illiterate, and many were immigrants with English as a second or third language. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Further, until the mid‐1930s, the provisions in the Act were interpreted as excluding friends and companions from claiming bodies, effectively preventing those friends who maintained contact from claiming the body. 19 , 27 , 28

The designation of individuals as “unclaimed” often more accurately reflected legal interpretation, institutional convenience, and poor/inaccurate record keeping. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 26 There were also strong incentives for residents to omit family details when admitted; government policy at the time required those who could pay for care to do so, and thus residents would avoid identifying family to protect them from bearing the costs associated with their care. 29 Institutional authorities were aware that many of those who passed away in public asylums were immigrants, whether by choice or incarceration, and often separated by distance and time from family. The narrative that they were the indigent, uneducated, unemployable, and unworthy members of society misrepresented the reality of their past lives, their current situation, and the impact of Colonial and early 20th‐century policies and practices. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 26

Notably, the legislation was considered advanced for its time; it introduced the first so‐called “decency” clauses in the British Empire which required that bodies were handled with dignity; anatomical examination “conducted in an orderly quiet and decent manner”; and afforded burial in a coffin in consecrated ground according to religious denomination (if known). Written records were mandatory as was reconciliation of registers with death certificates, annual inspections of anatomy facilities, and annual audit of the register. These systems are maintained to this day.

Notwithstanding that the 1881 Anatomy Act of NSW represented a substantial advancement in placing boundaries on body procurement, it perpetuated existing ethical violations in absence of consent and discriminatory application of human rights and dignity. In doing so it continued the practices that had developed throughout the Western world since the 15th century which considered the benefits to medical education and knowledge greater than the loss incurred by those whose bodies were used without their consent. 8 , 18 , 30 , 31 , 32 Indeed, commentary from the time clearly reinforces that the parliamentarians and others who supported the introduction of the Anatomy Act 1881 considered it as imbuing value to the lives of those whom they regarded as having otherwise lived worthlessly, and whose demise in a public institution proved that they were not contributing members of society and thus not deserving of the level of dignity and respect in death they expected for themselves and their peers. 7 , 9 , 10 , 11

The procurement of unclaimed bodies relied heavily on the entrenched attitudes toward those with mental illness, the poor and the destitute. The public asylums of New South Wales housed people who were elderly, infirm, disabled, chronically ill, destitute or unable to work 20 , 21 , 24 , 26 , 33 , 34 with admission by referral from another public institution, or by letter of recommendation (from a member of clergy, local member, or other person of “good” character). 19 , 33 , 34 In the mid‐late 1800s, most individuals confined in “mental/lunatic” asylums under the 1843 Dangerous Lunatics Act, and then the Lunacy Act 1878, 35 were convicts or ex‐convicts with their restraint considered an extension of their penal sentence. 20 Those suffering from mental illness, homelessness and/or addiction were deemed and treated as criminals. Psychiatric asylums thus housed people certified as insane in ways that conflated mental illness, poverty, homelessness, intoxication, cognitive impairment, and perceived criminality. 20 , 21 , 22 , 33 There was overlap between these institutional populations, particularly as the Colony aged and as chronic illness, dementia, and age‐related disability became more common. 20 , 21 , 33 , 34 For many residents, public asylum admission functioned as poor relief, aged care, disability care, or long‐term nursing care. Their presence in these institutions reflected social and economic structures rather than any absence of social value. 20 , 21 , 22 , 26 , 34 , 36

The disproportionate representation of immigrants, particularly those from Ireland, in psychiatric asylums also reinforced prejudice and hostility toward asylum residents. 24 , 25 , 26 Likewise, prejudice against dark‐skinned patients, including “South Sea Islanders, Chinese, African Blacks,” [and], “wanderers' who did not speak English” manifested in attitudes that they were immune to treatment and recovery. 26 The intersection of their isolation and ethnicity rendered them valuable only as anatomical specimens on death, not as individuals deserving of respect and dignity.

Public discourse at the time commonly distinguished the “respectable poor” from the “undeserving poor,” and the privately cared for from those dependent on public institutions. 8 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 31 Parliamentary and professional support for anatomy legislation often implied that the bodies of those who died in public care could acquire posthumous value through service to medical science. 7 , 9 , 10 , 11 This reasoning converted institutional dependency into anatomical availability. It also allowed medical education to be sustained by people who, in life, were least able to resist administrative classification and, in death, least likely to have their cultural, familial, and religious interests recognized. 8 , 18 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 37 , 38

The Anatomy Act 1881 (NSW) was reissued as consolidated legislation in 1901 with only minor changes, 19 , 39 none of which altered the arrangements for procurement of bodies. It then remained materially unchanged until 1977 when a new Anatomy Act was introduced. 39 In the intervening time, the delivery of medical education changed, as did the society of NSW and the expectations of the community about death, and rights to burial, 40 , 41 , 42 dissection, body donation, and consent. 19 , 43 Welfare payments, including burial subsidies, 44 also shifted attitudes with asylum superintendents cognisant that public scrutiny of their designation of bodies as unclaimed might be considered indefensible. 19 These developments ultimately led to an acute shortage of unclaimed bodies in the 1930s and resulted in the Medical School introducing a register of body donors in 1949. 43 Consent to donation by the deceased or their families became mandatory in the 1950s for public donation, although unclaimed bodies from asylums were accepted until 1961. 19 , 43

Those individuals whose bodies sustained medical education in NSW from the commencement of the undergraduate medical program in 1883 are absent from official records of the Medical School, and their contribution to medical education has been overlooked. Their omission diminishes the accuracy and weight of existing histories of the Medical School and perpetuates the discrimination and disenfranchisement enshrined in the anatomy legislation (both in NSW and elsewhere). 8 , 14 , 16 , 18 , 30 , 31

Ignoring past anatomy practices which, if repeated, would be considered unethical today, denies and dehumanizes the contributions of these individuals whose control and sanctity over their bodies was violated. 32 , 37 , 38 , 45 It avoids facing questions relating to practices which even at the time were documented to be questioned or questionable, 9 , 10 , 11 , 18 , 46 including those relating to the retention of Indigenous remains and legacy collections. 37 , 47

It is vital to include these individuals in historical accounts to counter the dehumanizing commodification of their unclaimed bodies as anatomical specimens or tissue for research and education, rather than as the remains of humans deserving of respect and dignity. In essence, procurement practices in anatomy across the world focused on the ends (medical education and advances in scientific knowledge) justifying the means (use of unclaimed bodies or the bodies of executed prisoners as objects and resources). 48 , 49 In doing so, individuals were denied the right to have their cultural and religious beliefs respected and to be recognized as persons with life stories and agency. Their humanity was essentially erased. That these attitudes were common across the Western World does not diminish their impact. The lives and life stories of these individuals have been ignored and lost, with their identity reduced to bare statistics and entries in Anatomy Registers.

This study seeks to address this gap. It examines the procurement of bodies from the commencement of undergraduate teaching at The University of Sydney Medical School in 1883 through to 1983, a period that encompasses the use of unclaimed bodies to the shift to reliance on consenting donors. METHODS

The study received ethical approval from The University of Sydney Research Ethics Committee in 2017 (approval number 2017/898). Permission to access Asylum Records and associated documentation held by the NSW State Archives Collection (State Archives) was granted by the NSW Ministry of Health (approval number H19/34421).

The study used historical records held by The University of Sydney, NSW State Archives and NSW State Library to compile a digital database of individuals whose bodies were received by the Sydney Medical School for anatomical examination between 1883 and 1983. All information presented is deidentified.

The primary source was the Anatomy Registers maintained under the Anatomy Act. Register A was the official reception record and contained the minimum information required by legislation; Register B was a companion record that included additional information and, from the 1940s, details relevant to donor status. The content of both register series varied over time and neither the University nor the State Archives held a complete set for all years before 1965. 19 , 50

Information extracted from the Anatomy Registers included: First Name, Surname, Sex, Age, Date of Death, Date of Reception into the University, Date of Return for Sepulture, Place of Death, and Cause of Death. Cause of death information has not been incorporated in the overall findings reported here. Each individual's origin (place of death) was categorized as: Public Asylum or Hospital; “Mental/Lunatic” (Psychiatric) Asylum; Morgue; Those Aged ≤2 (including stillborn and neonates); “Private” Donor—donated with their/family consent but not registered donors; Donor—Registered on the Medical School Donor Registry, or via other documentation.

Where the Registers were incomplete, additional records were used to reconstruct missing information. These included (1) death certificate transcripts; (2) asylum admission and discharge records (both public and psychiatric); (3) Anglican parish burial records; (4) burial records from Rookwood and Liverpool cemeteries; (5) convict records; (6) passenger arrival lists; and (7) genealogical sources (Ancestry™). The approach was necessarily cumulative: no single source captured all relevant information, and information on many individuals could be compiled only by triangulating several fragmentary records.

Conflicting information was resolved (where possible) by comparing death registration information with asylum admission and discharge records, cross‐referenced with the Anatomy Registers. Transcription errors, anglicization of names, and lost information introduced errors across the records, with known epistemic limitations arising from the imposition of Western‐centric colonial practices and attitudes in record‐keeping 51 reflecting prejudices, particularly against Indigenous Australians, but also against immigrant groups who were over‐represented in the asylums. Discharge records were less accurate and informative than admission records, although valuable in confirming date and time of death, and family contacts (if any). Burial records, particularly those in Rookwood Cemetery, were characterized by frequent errors of names, spelling, age, and date of death.

Where possible, errors were identified and corrections recorded in the database, with the corrected information included in the analysis. Missing or inaccurate recording of age was the most common error—subtracting age at death from successive admission records allowed correction in many cases, and verification of the advanced age of those aged >90.

Findings reported here focus on those individuals whose bodies were unclaimed. Analyses do not include those aged ≤2 except where stated.

Information to identify the country of birth and ethnic origins was collated using death registrations, notations in the Anatomy Records, and names. Anatomy Registers and death certificates used specific terms, for example, “Aboriginal native,” to identify Indigenous Australians; five individuals were identified as Indigenous on death certificates but not in the Anatomy Registers, and cross‐referencing confirmed these identifications. The mistaken identification of individuals from Melanesia as “Aborigines” or “natives” was also corrected. People of color were recorded in historical sources using terms now recognized as offensive; these terms are omitted here. Individuals of Chinese descent were identified through recorded country of birth (China or Hong Kong), surnames, and the repeated use of “Ah” as a recorded first name, a practice reflecting official ignorance or misunderstanding of Chinese naming conventions. 19 , 52

Australian Bureau of Statistics 53 , 54 , 55 historical NSW census data was used to calculate the mean age of death of the NSW population from 1883 to 1983 for comparison with the entire cohort. Data extrapolation due to missing time points is denoted by dots (rather than lines) on Figure 3. Census data from 1851 to 1901 56 was also used to plot the frequency of birthplace and religious practice for the NSW population and compared with two cohorts: the first based on death certificates (1883–1903, n = 386), the second on admission files (1883–1903, n = 124). A spread of census data was used because the nationality and religious practice of the cohort at time of death could not be validly compared with the NSW population at the same time point; those who lived in NSW in 1901 did not necessarily have the same demographic characteristics as those who died in 1901.

The study used names, age, sex, religious practice, country of birth, family and matrimonial status, and occupation as descriptors of the individuals whose bodies were received as these characteristics are strongly associated with ethnicity and socio‐economic status and were collected and reported upon, albeit with previously observed errors and biased classifications. They thus enable comparison with the local population and assisted in rebuilding life histories.

The information extracted was compiled and descriptive analysis conducted using Microsoft Excel for Mac OS (2023) database.

RESULTS

Cohort

Figure 1 details the provenance of all bodies procured under the Act for anatomical examination by the Medical School between 1883 and 1983, tracking the temporal shift in their place of death and consent status.

Plot of the source of bodies as a percentage of the total received annually from 1883 to 1983. The sources are classified as: “Mental/Lunatic" Asylums; Public Asylums, Morgue, Infants (aged ≤2 and stillborn), Private Donors (not on Register but consented directly or via family), Registered Donors.

Between 1883 and 1983, the bodies of 7609 individuals were received. The average retention time was 268 days (median = 231 days); the maximum recorded retention time was 1488 days, not including any human tissue retained in perpetuity. Regulations issued under the Anatomy Act extended permitted retention from six weeks in 1881 to two years by 1960. The first known consented male donor was received in 1916. In 1925, a female donor who died in an asylum was received, with her consent and wish to be sent to the Medical School recorded on her admission record. The first individual known to have registered an intention to donate with the University did so in 1930, well before the formal donor register was launched in 1949. The last unclaimed body was received in 1961, while bodies from morgues were received in small numbers until 1964. 19 , 57

Four thousand and eighty‐four unclaimed bodies were procured between 1883 and 1963 (including those from morgues). Eighty‐seven per cent were individuals who died in public asylums (3570); 342 died in a psychiatric asylum; 92 came from a Sydney or nearby Morgue; and 80 died in a public (19) or infectious diseases (61) hospital.

Public institutions dominated procurement from the beginning of the program until the mid‐twentieth century. In the first decades, the bodies received were predominantly those of elderly men who died in public asylums, with smaller numbers from psychiatric asylums, morgues or hospital sources. A small number of Indigenous Australians who died in nearby public hospitals were received in the early years. This pattern of procuring mainly elderly male bodies from public asylums persisted for much of the first 80 years.

No evidence was found in any of the material reviewed that the Medical School sought to procure bodies for medical education outside the parameters specified in the legislative framework. This included review of extensive correspondence between the University and the Director‐General of Public Health, and between the successive Heads of Anatomy and asylum Medical Superintendents, and the contracted undertakers. 27 , 28 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 As required under the Act, no bodies of prisoners were received and all bodies sent from Morgues were sent with either Coroner's (where no relatives were identified to claim the body) or next‐of‐kin consent 19 , 43 confirming that the Medical School operated within the legal boundaries of the Act. Despite the Anatomy Act permitting a body to be claimed 12 h after death, all bodies were received at least 24 h after death, and mostly 24–48 h after death.

Sex

Figure 2 shows the total number of individuals received each year and the proportion of each sex. Sex was not recorded for five neonates received between 1938 and 1952. The cohort was predominantly male: 5742 males and 1862 females, giving a M:F ratio of approximately 3:1. In 15/101 years, no females were received by the Medical School; 13/15 of these were prior to 1909. The proportion of females changed markedly with the introduction of body donor registration and the cessation of acceptance of unclaimed bodies. The number of each sex achieved parity in the late 1960s, with female donors exceeding male donors until the 1980s, with approximately even numbers of both sexes now received. 61

Annual number received

Annual intake (Figure 2) rose from ten bodies in 1883 to around 100 in 1910, with a similar number received each year for the next decade. More than 140 bodies were received in 1918, followed by a 40% decline in 1919, and further falls thereafter. From the 1920s until the mid‐late 1950s, the Medical School struggled to obtain sufficient bodies to maintain adequate student to body ratios for dissection as student numbers greatly increased 6 , 19 (Anatomy Department, 1926–1973 28 ; Garrett et al. 43 ). In 1948, only thirty‐three bodies were received. Numbers then rose during the next decade, reaching a peak in the mid‐1960s (164 individuals received in 1963) before gradually declining. Fifty‐four body donors were received in 1983, with annual intakes currently also around 50 donors each year. 61

Age

Figure 3 shows the mean age of the deceased whose bodies came into the Medical School, compared to the annual mean age at death for the Australian/NSW populations 53 across the 101 years (estimates for 1885 and 1895 are shown as dots). Both sets of data have been adjusted to exclude those aged less than twenty.

Age was missing for nine individuals (2 female). Most of the cohort were adults aged 18 and over (7526/7600; 99.0%). Four were aged between 3 and 15; seventy were aged two or less including stillborn and neonatal individuals. The average age of all deceased was 71.3 (median = 73); excluding those aged 2 or less the average age was 72.0. The maximum was 110. Females were younger (65.4) on average than males (70.0). Ten individuals were recorded as aged 100 or older at death. Seven of these centenarians died in public asylums between 1901 and 1940, and three were donors who died after 1950. Two were female. Census records from 1881 and 1901, 56 which included residents of asylums on census night, confirm that people of advanced age (as self‐reported) including centenarians were living in NSW. Annual asylum reports also confirm this Norton Manning. 24 Individual identified asylum admission and discharge records provided chronological confirmation of age for six of these seven.

Excluding those aged under twenty, the mean age at death of the cohort was about ten years higher than that of the NSW population, suggesting a consistent longevity characteristic in the deceased who died in the asylums, and reflecting the role of public asylums in aged care. 21 , 34 Annual variation in age was greater in the first three decades, reflecting the small number of bodies received compared to overall state‐wide deaths, and demographic profiles characteristic of particular asylums. Place of death

Table 1 shows the proportion of bodies received from public asylums/hospitals compared to those received from psychiatric asylums and other sources over time, their sex and mean age at death. Particularly evident is the much younger overall age of those individuals who died in the psychiatric asylums and the preponderance of male bodies.

There were marked changes in the source of bodies throughout the 101 years. Between 1883 and 1942, more than 90.0% of bodies were unclaimed and sourced from public asylums and public hospitals. Between 1943 and 1963, private or family consented donations (26.5%) and registered donors (43.9%) became increasingly important, while unclaimed bodies from the psychiatric asylums (14.6%) exceeded those from public asylums (9.1%). No unclaimed bodies from public institutions were received after 1961, although small numbers of bodies were received from morgues in 1961, 1962, and 1964. By 1977, all individuals were registered and consented donors.

Who were they? The people whose bodies were unclaimed

The findings here focus on the individuals aged older than two years who died in the public or psychiatric asylums and whose bodies were received as unclaimed by the Medical School between 1883 and 1961 (3992 individuals). Bodies received from the morgues are excluded due to the paucity of information about these individuals and their lives.

Cohort characteristics

The cohort was heterogeneous in origins, family connections and social lives, and religious/cultural beliefs. They included Indigenous Australians and other people of color, locally born non‐Indigenous people, British, Scottish and Irish immigrants, European immigrants, Chinese men who had lived and worked in Australia for decades, former convicts, former soldiers and sailors. Many had long work histories. Some had spouses, children and other relatives, interstate or overseas, and friends who remained in contact. Their classification as “unclaimed” thus more accurately reflected the impact of Colonial‐era administrative and legal categories than the true absence of social connections. 19

Ethnicity—Based on names and place of birth

Using names, place of birth and other notations extracted from the Anatomy Registers, asylum admission records and death register, individuals of Indigenous, Chinese and other heritage were identified. Recognizing the potential for errors in records, triangulation of all sources was completed to verify heritage.

First names were recorded for all except two adults in the Anatomy Registers—one was recorded as an “Australian Aborigine,” the other as a “Chinaman.” Twenty adults had no surname recorded; most were Indigenous (16). Three individuals were female.

Chinese individuals

One hundred and five individuals whose ancestry appeared to be Chinese were identified. The majority of these had first names recorded as “Ah” (65/105). All except one were male; the only female was a donor. Their ages ranged from thirty‐four to 105. The average age of this group was 76 (median = 78, standard deviation = 11.6, with one missing age). The majority died in public asylums, with a subset having been transferred from country hospitals shortly before death (see section on Chinese individuals below 19 ). Burial records for this group were error‐prone, and most were buried outside customary Chinese practice.

University records indicate that the skulls and brains of 100 individuals of Chinese descent were used in research 65 , 66 and/or were retained as museum specimens. 67 Other documents confirm that some of these remains were from unclaimed bodies procured under the Anatomy Act, 19 although the majority were brought by Shellshear to Australia after his posting in China. The findings confirm that the bodies of Chinese individuals were valued not only for teaching but also for research into racial differences and as museum specimens.

Indigenous Australians

Thirty‐five Indigenous Australian people whose bodies were sent to the Medical School have been identified from the Anatomy Registers, although two identified as “aboriginal” in the Registers were in fact born in the South Sea Islands according to their death certificates. Twenty‐nine died in the period 1884–1921; eight in public hospitals (all except one prior to 1891), nine in psychiatric asylums, and twelve died in public asylums. Five were female. Twenty‐two were aged 50 or younger (mean = 45, median = 40, range 3–110). The majority (20) died of an infectious disease: tuberculosis or related bronchopneumonia (17); diphtheria; a hydatid tumor; acute meningitis. Burial records have been identified for all except one. Most (30) were buried in pauper graves in Rookwood Cemetery, predominantly in the Church of England section, with others buried in pauper graves at Liverpool. University records document that skeletal remains and brains of at least three of these individuals were retained and used for research and/or museum specimens. 19

Other people of color

Seventeen additional people of color were identified from Africa (3), America (1), the West Indies (3), the New Hebrides and South Sea Islands (6), and India (4). Notations in the anatomy registers recorded their ethnicity which was verified using death certificates. In some cases, the retention of their bodies or body parts for research and/or as museum specimens was also recorded. 19 The available death transcripts for all individuals in these three groups record no spouse or family to claim their body.

Social circumstances, 1883–1903

Occupation data showed that most males worked in low‐paid, physically demanding and sometimes dangerous jobs. The most common occupations were laborer (83), miner (28), cook (16), seaman/soldier (13), “pauper” (9), gardener (8), carpenter (8), and shepherd (4). Females were domestic servants, seamstresses or laundresses. These findings parallel the historic census reports; “lower class” men were mainly employed in agriculture, and as laborers, in trades and as miners (until the 1870s) and women in domestic situations as servants, seamstresses and washer women/laundresses. 56 Eight females, but no males, had their occupations recorded as “pauper” in the death certificates; and two in the admission records. Most unknown occupations in the death certificates were for females (69%) compared to males (30.3%). Twelve of the Indigenous individuals had no occupation recorded in their death certificates. Others were variously occupied as “Aboriginal tracker,” “Cattle driver,” “Servant,” and “Pauper.” These findings illustrate the gendered, racist and classist assumptions embedded in official records.

Another important difference between the two sources was in the recording of occupation. Occupation was often absent in death transcripts, whereas admission files recorded the majority of the public asylum residents as having been employed in the lead up to their admission, with acute illness, inability to work due to “rheumatics,” infirmity, advanced age and frailty, or disability such as blindness, their stated reason for seeking admission. Some (male) asylum residents sought admission during the winter months and then were discharged “on request” when the weather warmed up.

Family information differed greatly by source. Death certificates often recorded marital status as unknown or omitted (197/386) of the cohort. No spouse or “issue” details were recorded on any death transcripts prior to 1896, and only twenty records listed descendants, living or deceased. In contrast, the asylum admission records show that only seven individuals (5.6%) had unknown family status. However, even where family details were recorded, the information was sparse, often pejorative; for example, the description of a female as having a “supposed” husband, and of an Indigenous male as having a “Gin,” and likely inaccurate. Where family were recorded, their location was often listed as unknown, interstate, or overseas, with no contact details or address recorded (26.6% of the admission records). A total of thirty‐four (27.4%) individuals had “None” recorded as their family status in asylum records, including two where it was noted that family had left the asylum before details could be taken. Thirty‐eight (30.6%) were single/never married; eleven were widowed with no family (8.9%).

Collectively, these data call into question the official label “unclaimed” and its confirmation of the absence of family.

Burial

The majority received pauper burials. These entailed interment in a simple pine coffin into a grave which usually contained multiple deceased, officiated by an appropriately denominated member of the clergy. Most were buried in the Church of England (198/350) or Catholic pauper sections (107/350) at Rookwood and Liverpool cemeteries (the choice of cemetery was determined by asylum location). Seventeen were buried in Presbyterian graves at Rookwood cemetery, two were buried in the Chinese or “pagan” section of Rookwood cemetery, and twenty‐six had no cemetery denomination noted. Place of burial was not recorded on thirty‐six death certificates, including four of the Indigenous individuals. However, burial records for three Indigenous individuals were independently located, confirming pauper burial. When comparing burial records with admission information, three individuals whose admission records stated that they were of Presbyterian denomination were buried in pauper graves in the Church of England section of the Liverpool cemetery, as were two whose faith was recorded as Lutheran. Burial also failed to respect cultural practice. Chinese individuals were mostly buried in Christian sections despite customary practices, 69 and Indigenous people were also buried in Christian pauper graves with no consideration of traditional practices or cultural beliefs, despite these being documented and reported upon, including in publications to which Medical School staff contributed. 19 , 70 , 71

The existence of burial records does not preclude retention of remains. As noted previously, records confirm that some remains of Indigenous, Chinese, and other bodies, including non‐colored individuals, were retained for museum collections and/or research. 19

DISCUSSION

The findings reported here indicate that The University of Sydney Medical School operated within the legal framework of the 1881 Anatomy Act. Bodies were procured from public institutions; no prisoners were received; the statutory register system was kept and inspected annually; and burial arrangements adhered to the requirements of denomination rites. According to the “official” records, most individuals had no identified family to claim their remains. Notably, despite the Act setting a statutory time of 12 h before a body could be deemed unclaimed, the time between death and reception by the Medical School was longer (≥ 24 h rather than 12 h).

In this sense, the system was lawful. The ethical issue lies not in illegality but in the structure of the law itself. The Act enshrined clear discrimination against asylum residents by making their bodies available for anatomical examination because they were categorized in Colonial‐era records as unclaimed. It thereby gave legal expression to social hierarchies based on entitlement to post‐mortem choice. 8 , 14 , 15 , 18 , 19

The aims of the anatomy legislation included an intention to prevent body snatching and grave robbing and thus to provide a legitimate source of bodies for anatomy education. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 15 Arguably, these aims were achieved. The “decency” clauses were also important but limited. 8 They regulated conduct after the body had entered anatomical custody; they did not secure meaningful consent before use. Dignified handling and burial coexisted with profound denial of agency. The central ethical injury was therefore not disrespect in the dissecting room, but the prior conversion of institutional vulnerability into anatomical entitlement. The Act prevented grave robbing and created a lawful supply of bodies, but did so by transferring risk onto the poor, the elderly, the disabled, migrants, psychiatric patients, Indigenous people, and others dependent on public care just as occurred throughout the Western world. 8 , 18 , 30 , 37 , 38

The questionability of “unclaimed”

This study shows that “unclaimed” is often a misleading category. Death certificates generally confirmed the administrative determination that no family were available to claim the body, but admission records reveal a more complex social reality. Some residents had spouses, children, relatives overseas or interstate, or friends who remained in contact. Some had concealed family details to avoid imposing care costs on relatives. Others had outlived close kin, lost contact through migration, or been institutionalized for years or decades. Administrative records were therefore neither impartial nor comprehensive descriptions of social belonging; they were records used within institutions concerned with Colonial rules and policies about custody, cost, classification, and movement. 19 , 20 , 21 , 26 , 29

The short statutory time frame compounded these problems. In a colony where many residents were born overseas, where communication could take days or weeks, and where family members might live interstate, overseas or in rural districts, determining a body to be unclaimed within a narrow period was administratively efficient but ethically dubious. Friends were excluded as claimants, even when they were the only people maintaining social ties. The legal meaning of “claimed” was therefore narrower than the human reality of connection. 19 , 21 , 26 , 36

Financial incentives also shaped the system. Measures taken by the Colonial government to recoup the costs of asylum care from either the resident or their family contributed to the isolation and disconnection of asylum residents. 29 Many public asylum residents claimed they had no “means” and no family. It was not uncommon for family to leave ill family members at public hospitals and public asylums without providing contact details. 29 , 72 Residents and their families sought to avoid liability for the cost of care, especially in already straightened financial circumstances. 24 , 25 , 29 The findings confirm that many residents stated they had no family nearby or at known locations, just as many stated that they had no means. By doing so, they relieved their families of a financial burden that many could ill‐afford. These assertions were rational responses to Colonial government policies.

Public institutions bore burial costs when family could not be found or could not pay. 29 Sending an unclaimed body to the Medical School transferred burial responsibility and cost to the University. 19 Country hospitals had incentives to transfer elderly or dying patients to city asylums, 40 , 73 and public institutions had incentives to classify bodies as unclaimed where the statutory conditions could be met. The University, in turn, paid undertakers and, from the early twentieth century, fees associated with certification and administration were paid to asylum Medical Superintendents. 19 These arrangements were legally authorized and bureaucratically regular, but they entrenched a system in which poverty and institutional death increased the likelihood of anatomical use.

Asylums, aging, and social vulnerability

The findings from this study and other evidence confirm that the residents of asylums in the 19th and early 20th century in Sydney were varied in their origins, their occupations, family status, and life experience. 19 , 21 , 23 , 24 , 25 Many residents of Sydney asylums in this period were immigrants. Their connections with family and their country of birth were often tenuous, stretched by incarceration, distance, time, life circumstances, and the difficulty of return.

The demographic profile of the cohort reflected the function of public asylums. 21 , 24 , 25 , 36 The predominance of elderly men, many of them immigrants or former convicts, corresponds with the demographics of Colonial and early twentieth‐century New South Wales. 56 Public asylums provided refuge for people who were old, infirm, chronically ill, blind, disabled, unemployed, homeless or without family support. For many residents, asylum admission represented a form of aged care, poor relief, or long‐term nursing care. 21 , 29 , 72 Their deaths in public institutions reflected the absence of alternative supports rather than personal worthlessness or lack of contribution to society. 19 , 21 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 29

Discrimination on the basis of class, ethnicity, education, wealth, sex, ill‐health, or disability was common and largely unchallenged both in the community, and more so within the asylums. 20 , 21 , 22 , 24 , 25 , 26 Those individuals who were admitted to asylums, both in NSW and also in Britain, were presumed to be in some way lesser or deficient members of society, the “undeserving poor”. 18 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 26 Thus, it was rationalized that they in some way deserved their poverty and disadvantage, and in being poor and/or disconnected from family and society, their right to exert control of their bodies after death had been abrogated. Record‐keeping confirmed and promulgated this narrative by omitting key information, anglicizing, and mis‐recording information. 19 In effect, these individuals were regarded as contributing to society only through the use of their body for anatomical examination at death. 18 However, the evidence documented in this study challenges this presumption and suggests that many asylum residents lived active and productive lives prior to their admission. Their “misstep” was to die in a public institution separated from their family, friends, and community.

The evidence of occupation is particularly important. Many residents had worked as laborers, miners, seamen, soldiers, gardeners, carpenters, shepherds, domestic servants, seamstresses, and laundresses. These occupations were central to Colonial and urban economies. 56 , 68 The bodies received by the Medical School were therefore not those of people outside society; they were the bodies of people who had built, serviced, and sustained it, often under conditions of hardship. Their final institutionalization reflected age, illness, disability, and economic precarity. 18 , 19 , 21

The psychiatric asylum population was younger on average and shaped by different but overlapping forms of vulnerability. Nineteenth‐century lunacy legislation conflated mental illness, perceived dangerousness, homelessness, intoxication, attempted suicide, cognitive impairment and social disorder. 20 , 22 , 33 , 34 Certification by medical practitioners and a magistrate could lead to long‐term confinement. Stigma associated with mental illness, combined with costs recoverable from family, reduced family contact and increased the likelihood that bodies would be treated as unclaimed at death. 20 , 29 The bodies of psychiatric patients thus entered anatomical education through a legal system that magnified the disadvantages already imposed by involuntary institutionalization. 20 , 22 , 26 , 29

Gender and anatomical practice

The predominance of male bodies reflects several converging factors: the male‐skewed population of early colonial New South Wales, the sex composition of public asylums, migration patterns, and anatomical teaching assumptions. Public asylums housed more men than women, partly because of the history of convict transportation and male labor migration, and partly because fewer institutional places existed for women. 36 The Medical School appears not to have established routine arrangements for procurement from Newington Asylum, the principal female asylum, until later. 19 More broadly, anatomical pedagogy in the nineteenth century often treated the male body as the normative body for teaching general anatomy, with female bodies regarded as necessary primarily for reproductive anatomy. 74 , 75 The scarcity of female bodies at Sydney is consistent with patterns observed at Melbourne, 76 although British studies of remains buried in sites associated with medical schools 17 and the McGill University study 77 suggest that source, that is, the public institution, determined whose body was unclaimed.

The later increase in female bodies coincided with the donor program. 19 , 43 This suggests that the shift from institutional procurement to consented donation altered not only the ethics of procurement but also the demographic composition of the anatomy laboratory. When bodies were sourced through public asylums, institutional demographics and gendered professional assumptions shaped availability. When donation became normalized, females were no longer structurally excluded in the same way and eventually equaled or exceeded males among donors. 19 , 28 , 61

The links between race, colonialism, and anatomical value

The inclusion of Indigenous Australians, Chinese men and other people of color requires separate ethical consideration. The evidence does not show that Medical School anatomists systematically sought Indigenous bodies for teaching through the ordinary procurement program. Correspondence with asylum superintendents focused on bodies as a resource—seeking to secure supply rather than selecting specific individuals. 19 , 28 Nevertheless, anatomy register notations and museum records show that the bodies of Indigenous Australians, people of color, and “medical curiosities” (people with cognitive or physical disabilities) were regarded as scientifically valuable for research and collections. 19 , 59 Some remains were retained and studied in ways that reflected racial typologies and hierarchies common in nineteenth‐ and early twentieth‐century science. 19 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 78 , 79 , 80

It is also documented that the remains of Indigenous people and other individuals sent by former colleagues, graduates and medical practitioners were received by members of staff at the University, including staff who were part of the Medical School. 46 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 This practice was legal under the Anatomy legislation so long as those performing the research were medically or surgically qualified and the examination occurred at a licensed premise (University) but often occurred without the consent of family, 46 , 78 reflecting the disregard for these people as humans with families, communities and lives to be mourned and valued.

The widespread and devastating impact of colonization on the Indigenous population is well documented. 81 , 84 , 85 However, Indigenous people lived within the Sydney‐town (there were approximately 8000 Indigenous people registered in the 1891 census 56 ) and were admitted to public hospitals and asylums, including psychiatric asylums 23 , 24 , 25 , 82 , 86 . For Indigenous Australians, procurement occurred within the broader impact of colonization, dispossession, disease, institutionalization, and scientific collection. Indigenous people were younger within the cohort and more likely to die from infectious disease, reflecting their poor health and status within the Colony. 82 , 84 , 85 Their family and community connections were poorly recorded, often through administrative categories that did not recognize Indigenous kinship, language, country, or mortuary practice. 78 , 79 , 80 Burial in Christian pauper graves, and the retention of remains for research or museum purposes, disregarded cultural obligations surrounding death and the body which were documented and known. 19 , 70 , 71 , 85

Chinese individuals in the cohort were generally elderly men who had lived for decades in New South Wales. Their institutional deaths cannot be separated from the racialized structures of Colonial labor and exclusion. Restrictive immigration laws, anti‐Chinese sentiment, barriers to family settlement (epitomized in the “White Australia Policy” which included express prohibitions on the settlement of family and purchase of property by non‐white people), rural isolation and poor work conditions shaped their life trajectories. 52 , 87 , 88 , 89 Many were migrants who arrived from the mid‐19th century during the goldrush, or as indentured laborers recruited to fill shortages after the cessation of convict transportation in NSW. 52 , 69 , 87 , 88 , 89 The conditions of their indenture often prevented them from accruing sufficient funds to return to China. 87 Their situation in rural areas also isolated them from the Chinese community in Sydney which actively repatriated remains in accordance with Chinese cultural preferences. 69 As noted, many of the Chinese individuals included in this study were old. They had lived and worked for decades in NSW, yet were still considered “alien,” “pagans” and undeserving of community membership. This study confirms that at least some of these men lived out their lives in the public asylums, removed from their original family, friends and communities, and then further isolated in their old age by transfer from their rural abodes to metropolitan institutions. Their designation as unclaimed often reflected their exclusion from family and community networks produced by policy and prejudice.

The use of Chinese and Indigenous remains in anatomical research also demonstrates how bodies could acquire value precisely through racialization. Anatomical variation was studied and displayed in terms that reinforced stereotypes and hierarchies. 65 , 66 , 67 The body was treated as a specimen, not as the remains of a person embedded in family, culture and community. Acknowledging this history is essential not only as retrospective rejection but as part of continuing institutional responsibility for provenance research, repatriation, truth‐telling, and culturally governed care of legacy collections. 46 , 47 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 83

Donation, consent and institutional change

The shift to body donation at Sydney occurred earlier and more rapidly than in some comparable jurisdictions, 76 , 90 , 91 , 92 but the initial impetus was practical. Declining availability of unclaimed bodies threatened the Medical School's ability to provide anatomical teaching and to meet accreditation requirements. 43 This transition was ethically significant, but it should not be overinterpreted. As Jones 49 noted, historically, the focus of anatomists has been on obtaining human tissue, not on the humans whose bodies were procured. 49 The evidence here supports this priority: changing social circumstances, 44 and a shortage of unclaimed bodies amid increasing student numbers threatened anatomical teaching and medical accreditation. 28 , 43 Public appeals, professional commentary and the eventual donor register reframed donation as altruistic and socially valuable. 19 , 43 The Head of Anatomy, Professor Burkitt, and others emphasized both educational necessity and respectful treatment, seeking to reassure potential donors and families. 19 Only gradually did consent become the defining criterion of legitimacy. This trajectory matters because it reveals that ethical reform may emerge from practical constraint as much as from principled recognition of rights. 49

By 1949 the donor register had been established, and by the mid‐1950s formal consent rules governed acceptance of bodies from members of the public. 19 , 43 , 61 This transition altered relationships between the Medical School and the community. The Medical School placed great emphasis on maintaining community support with the Head of Anatomy answering all correspondence personally. 19 Donors and families could request cremation, non‐Protestant burial rites, and inclusion of objects in coffins. The Medical School recorded individual requests and sometimes met shortfalls in burial costs where private burials were requested. 19 , 61 Some accommodation of religious and cultural preferences was extended even to unclaimed bodies where burial funds existed. These practices reflected changing public expectations and a developing recognition that anatomical use required social trust. 19 , 43

Nevertheless, this historic account cautions against overinterpreting ethical outcomes as evolving directly from ethical values. Consent emerged within a program long structured by need for bodies. 19 , 43 By 1985, uniform human tissue law across Australia required informed consent to the collection and use of all human tissue for education and research, setting a standard that prohibits the use of unclaimed bodies in contrast to that upheld to this day in other countries. 93 The modern ethical legitimacy of body donation depends not only on signed authorization but also on transparency, cultural sensitivity, careful stewardship of remains, and avoidance of coercive or exploitative conditions. 94 , 95 , 96 The Sydney transition shows institutions can move from reliance on vulnerable populations to donation, but it also shows that such transitions may be driven by pragmatic change rather than ethical practice. 19 , 43 , 61

Comparison with other anatomy schools

Sydney's experience was part of a wider British and settler‐colonial pattern. 18 The demand for human bodies for dissection exceeded legal sources for most of the 18th century, restricted as it was to the bodies of executed criminals. 45 , 97 Many jurisdictions, including in Europe, Britain, Canada, the United States, Australia and New Zealand, responded by legalizing the procurement of unclaimed bodies, of those who died in public and charitable institutions including hospitals, workhouses, and asylums. 77 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 98 , 99 This occurred during the 18th century in most European countries 97 and spread across the world in the 19th century. These comparisons place Sydney within a global history in which anatomy advanced through regulated access to the bodies of people with least social power. 49 , 93 , 98 , 100 , 101

Medical Schools established across Australia and New Zealand in the late 19th and early 20th century drew on the British Anatomy Act 1832, and the experiences of other universities both within and outside the Colony in establishing their body procurement processes. 8 , 19 , 61 , 76 , 90 , 91 All implemented procurement pathways based on the collection of unclaimed bodies from public institutions, including public hospitals and asylums or homes. 19 , 76 , 91 , 92 Donation by consent, or with family agreement, was enabled. These laws often appeared as reforms because they curtailed grave robbing and restricted dissection to authorized medical settings. Yet they also shifted the burden of anatomical education onto populations already marginalized by poverty, race, illness, disability, or institutionalization. 8 , 14 , 15 , 18

The University of Melbourne relied on anatomy legislation from the 1860s and, like Sydney, drew bodies from public institutions. Melbourne faced strong public resistance, including objections from asylum residents and refusals by some benevolent asylums to supply bodies. 92 Its early cohort was also predominantly male, shaped by migration and institutional demographics. 76 , 92 Otago Medical School in New Zealand likewise operated under legislation modeled on the British Act and experienced shortages that eventually led to donor bequest strategies. 91 Across Australia and New Zealand, donation became entrenched in the mid‐to‐late twentieth century and was later reinforced by human tissue legislation. 19 , 76 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 102

Institutional truths and ethical repair

The individuals described here are absent from most official histories of the Medical School. This is not a minor omission. Anatomical education depended on their bodies, and the development of medical knowledge and practice in New South Wales was materially sustained by their use. 19 Recognizing them does not negate the achievements of medical education; it clarifies their conditions of operation. The history of anatomy is incomplete if it records the great academics and researchers, facilities, curricula, and graduates but not the people dissected in the anatomy rooms. 18 , 48

Ethical truth‐telling begins with accurate historical recount. It requires identifying individuals where possible, correcting errors in records where appropriate, acknowledging the prejudicial terms and classifications under which they were recorded, and respecting descendant, family and community interests. For Indigenous remains, this work must be led by Indigenous communities and must align with repatriation, truth‐telling and cultural governance. 78 , 81 , 83 For non‐Indigenous individuals, especially those whose families may remain unaware of their post‐mortem use, future work should consider how information can be made available without violating dignity, privacy or cultural expectation. 19 , 47

LIMITATIONS

This study is limited by its focus on one institution and one jurisdiction. Its findings cannot be assumed to represent all Australian or international anatomy schools, although they align with several broader patterns. The archival record is incomplete. Register series are missing for some periods, and the level of detail varies considerably across time and source. Death certificates are unavailable for some individuals because of statutory restrictions, and medical notes for psychiatric admissions remain inaccessible for long periods after death. Some records were destroyed, lost, or never created. 19

A second limitation concerns the character of colonial record‐keeping. 51 Archival documents are rich but not neutral. They reflect the time, priorities, prejudices, policies, and administrative purposes of those who created them. Names were misspelled or anglicized; occupations and family status were omitted or moralized; Indigenous people and people of color were described using racialized terms; and institutional categories such as “pauper,” “idiot,” “imbecile,” “native,” or “pagan” encoded social judgment. Much of the asylum record keeping constituted “book‐keeping” and/or was kept for census purpose. Weekly asylum admission and discharge records focused on numbers, “good” or “bad” behavior, and outcomes (death or discharge or abscondment). The resulting database is therefore not a full and accurate summary of lives but a reconstruction from records produced under conditions of inequality and prejudice. 19 , 21 , 29

A third limitation is interpretive. The study necessarily applies contemporary ethical concerns to nineteenth‐ and early twentieth‐century practices. Those practices were often regarded as legal, routine, and unexceptional. 7 , 103 , 104 Yet they were also questioned at the time, and their routine character does not remove their ethical significance. The study also does not examine in detail the separate but connected history of remains procured for research and museum collections outside the ordinary anatomy register pathway. 32 , 46

FUTURE WORK

This study demonstrates both the richness and fragility of historical records relating to unclaimed bodies. Many individuals can be identified sufficiently to reconstruct aspects of their lives, deaths, and burials. Some may have descendants or communities with legitimate interests in this information. Yet there are few established frameworks governing access to individual‐level information about people whose bodies were used for anatomy, particularly where records were created by officials and anatomists rather than by the individuals themselves. Future work should develop guidelines for ethical management of such information, balancing historical transparency, privacy, dignity and the interests of families and communities. 19 , 47

Further research should also address record correction. Burial records, anatomy registers and death registrations contain errors in names, ages, ethnicity, religion, and burial details. Correcting these records is difficult, but doing so may be an important form of recognition. Provenance work on retained remains and legacy collections must continue, particularly for the remains of Indigenous people and people of color whose remains were collected without consent, and used to perpetuate discrimination, stigma and ignorance, and to justify the destructive impact of Colonization. Such work must be conducted in partnership with affected communities and should include decisions about future care, repatriation, access to information, and public reporting. 19 , 47 , 78 , 80

CONCLUSION

This history of body procurement at The University of Sydney Medical School reveals the human foundations of anatomical education in New South Wales. From 1883 to the mid‐twentieth century, the program relied overwhelmingly on bodies legally classified as unclaimed, most of them from public asylums. The individuals whose bodies were received were elderly, poor, ill, disabled, institutionalized, migrant, Indigenous, Chinese, socially isolated or some combination of these. Many had worked, migrated, married, raised families, served, labored, and contributed to the Colony. They and their lives were not materially different from thousands of others who lived in NSW at the same time. Their misfortune was not that they lacked lives of value, but that they died in institutions where law and administration made their bodies available. 19

The value of the findings reported here, however, is not confined to rejection of these past practices. Rather the findings provide a clear reminder that the individuals whose bodies were used for anatomical examination were people.

The Medical School seems to have operated within the law. The law itself, however, reflected and reproduced Colonial hierarchies of class, race, disability, gender, and institutional status. Its effects were most profound on those least able to object, least likely to have family recognized, and least likely to have their cultural practices respected. The later transition to donation marked an important ethical change, but it was made possible only after decades of anatomical education sustained by unconsented bodies. 19 , 43

Recognizing these individuals is central to the accurate account of the history of medicine. Their bodies helped educate generations of medical practitioners and contributed to the development of the public health system in New South Wales. Their names, lives, and deaths should not remain buried beneath the administrative term “unclaimed.”
