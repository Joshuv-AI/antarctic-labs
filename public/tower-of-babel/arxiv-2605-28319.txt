Dissipative Spectral Form Factor of the Complex Elliptic Ginibre Ensemble across Various Non-Hermiticity Regimes
Gernot Akemann, Sung-Soo Byun, Seungjoon Oh

Abstract
. We study the dissipative spectral form factor (DSFF) at complex time T ​ e i ​ θ Te^{i\theta} for the complex elliptic Ginibre ensemble with non-Hermiticity parameter τ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \tau\in[0,1) . As the matrix dimension N → ∞ N\to\infty , we consider the natural scalings in both the time variable and the non-Hermiticity parameter, namely T = O ⁡ ( N γ ) T=O(N^{\gamma}) and 1 − τ = O ⁡ ( N − α ) 1-\tau=O(N^{-\alpha}) . For all regimes γ ≥ 0 \gamma\geq 0 and α ≥ 0 \alpha\geq 0 , we derive the precise asymptotic behaviour of both the disconnected and connected components of the DSFF. In particular, we explicitly characterise the dip–ramp–plateau structure, including the dip time and the Heisenberg time. In addition, we identify the mesoscopic regime α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) , which interpolates between the behaviour of the DSFF of non-Hermitian random matrices and the spectral form factor (SFF) of Hermitian ensembles. We further provide an explicit description of the phase diagram, in which the ramp exhibits quadratic, linear, or intermediate behaviour depending on the scaling parameters.



1.
Introduction

The spectral statistics of quantum systems have long been shaped by two fundamental conjectures.
The Berry–Tabor conjecture asserts that generic quantum integrable systems exhibit locally Poissonian level statistics, reflecting the absence of significant correlations between neighbouring energy levels.
By contrast, the Bohigas–Giannoni–Schmit conjecture predicts that quantum systems whose classical counterparts are chaotic display local level statistics governed by Hermitian random matrix theory (RMT), in accordance with the relevant symmetry class.
This paradigm of quantum chaos has been extended to open and dissipative quantum systems, where the spectrum becomes complex. In this setting, generic integrable systems are expected to exhibit two-dimensional (2D) Poisson statistics, whereas
open, dissipative
quantum chaotic systems display correlated spectra described by non-Hermitian RMT
[
28
]
.
In this work, we focus on the latter regime.

There are different tools available from RMT to quantify such statistics, on different scales. On a local or microscopic scale, the nearest-neighbour spacing distribution (in radial distance in 2D) is amongst the most popular, due to the Wigner surmise for RMT in 1D. Much more recently, the consecutive spacing ratio has been introduced
[
37
,
4
]
in 1D and in
[
40
]
in 2D, which is particularly convenient in comparison to data. Here, the dependence on the local scale, given by the inverse mean spectral density, drops out without so-called unfolding
[
29
]
. Nevertheless, little rigorous knowledge is available for this quantity so far, see however
[
36
,
7
]
in 1D, even if a surmise is also effective in the Hermitian case in 1D
[
4
]
.
This is despite the fact that, in principle, all local quantities should be accessible from knowledge of the local
k
-point functions. At least in the classical Hermitian ensembles of RMT the latter follow from standard asymptotics of classical orthogonal polynomials and their corresponding kernel, being given by determinantal or Pfaffian point processes
[
15
]
.

An entirely different tool that probes spectral correlations on all scales—microscopic, mesoscopic, and macroscopic—is the spectral form factor (SFF)
[
30
]
, defined as the Fourier transform of the two-point function.
While spectra are typically not universal on macroscopic scales, quantum chaotic systems nonetheless exhibit the characteristic
dip–ramp–plateau
profile predicted by Hermitian RMT; see
[
29
]
and references therein.
The literature on the SFF is extensive, and we refrain from attempting a comprehensive survey.
Perhaps surprisingly, the mathematically rigorous literature on the SFF remains limited; see, for instance,
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
10
]
and
references therein, with early contributions going back to
[
6
]
.
This is largely due to the difficulty of obtaining rigorous asymptotics, as multiple scales arise and must be matched.
To complete the picture, one may also consider other tools probing multiple scales, such as the number variance and related quantities
[
35
, Chapter 16]
, as well as linear statistics more generally.

In this work, we focus on the recently introduced dissipative spectral form factor (DSFF)
[
31
]
, which has been found to be applicable to a large variety of open, dissipative quantum chaotic systems
[
32
]
.
The DSFF is defined as a two-variable Fourier transform of the two-point function of complex eigenvalues in the appropriate non-Hermitian symmetry class.
It also displays a dip–ramp–plateau behaviour; however, the ramp is quadratic instead.
The universality of the results in
[
31
]
has been established for real and complex non-Hermitian random matrices with i.i.d. entries
[
11
]
.
Note that different proposals have also been made, e.g. the dissipative form factor
[
9
]
, a one-parameter Fourier transform of the two-point function of the Liouvillian (rather than the Hamiltonian), and a fidelity-type deformation
[
34
]
.

Given that the study of open dissipative systems in comparison with non-Hermitian RMT has become very popular, see
[
8
, Section 6.2]
for a recent review of quantum chaos from that perspective, our goal is to derive mathematically rigorous asymptotics of the DSFF in 2D, and its interpolation to the SFF in 1D proposed in
[
41
]
.
For that purpose, we consider the simplest ensemble without further symmetries, introduced by Ginibre
[
26
]
, with complex Gaussian matrix entries (GinUE).
Its one-parameter-dependent extension by Girko
[
27
]
and Sommers et al.
[
42
]
, namely the elliptic (or Ginibre–Girko) ensemble, denoted by eGinUE, furthermore allows one to interpolate between the strongly non-Hermitian Ginibre case and the Hermitian limit given by the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE), thereby recovering the SFF in 1D.
Of particular importance here is the weak non-Hermiticity regime introduced by Fyodorov, Khoruzhenko, and Sommers
[
22
,
20
]
.
Previous heuristic results on this interpolation between DSFF and SFF exist in
[
41
]
.
Apart from making these results mathematically rigorous, we also uncover several different scaling regimes for the dip, ramp, and plateau regions, in analogy with recently discovered weak non-Hermiticity regimes for the number variance in the eGinUE
[
2
]
.

1.1.
Elliptic Ginibre ensemble and DSFF

Let us now describe more precisely the model under consideration.
We consider the eGinUE (see
[
8
, Section 2.3]
and references therein) defined by

(1.1)

X:=\frac{\sqrt{1+\tau}}{2}(G+G^{*})+\frac{\sqrt{1-\tau}}{2}(G-G^{*}),\qquad\tau\in[0,1),

where
G
is a complex Ginibre matrix, i.e. an
N\times N
matrix with i.i.d. complex Gaussian entries of mean zero and variance
1/N
.
Let
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
denote the (complex) eigenvalues of
X
.
It is well known that, as
N\to\infty
, the empirical spectral measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{z_{j}}
converges in probability to the elliptic law

(1.2)

\frac{1}{1-\tau^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{S}(z)\,dA(z),\qquad S:=\Big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\Big(\frac{x}{1+\tau}\Big)^{2}+\Big(\frac{y}{1-\tau}\Big)^{2}\leq 1\Big\}.

Here,
dA(z)=d^{2}z/\pi
denotes the normalised area measure.

By definition, the
k
-point correlation function
R_{N,k}
is characterised as follows: for a suitable test function
f:\mathbb{C}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
,

(1.3)

\Big\langle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\leq N\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}f(z_{i_{1}},\dots,z_{i_{k}})\Big\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{k}}f(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\,R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})\,dA(z_{1})\cdots dA(z_{k}),

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the ensemble average.
For the eGinUE, the eigenvalues form a determinantal point process; see again
[
8
, Section 2.3]
. In other words, the
k
-point correlation functions
R_{N,k}
admit the representation

(1.4)

R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\det\big[K_{N}(z_{j},z_{l})\big]_{j,l=1}^{k},

where
K_{N}
is a correlation kernel
which is Hermitian
1
1

1

Such a choice can always be made due to the invariance under multiplying with cocycles.
,

K_{N}(z,w)=\overline{K_{N}(w,z)}
; see (
3.4
) for its representation in terms of the associated orthogonal polynomials.
In particular, the one- and two-point correlation functions, which will play a central role in our analysis, are given by

(1.5)

R_{N,1}(z)=K_{N}(z,z),\qquad R_{N,2}(z,w)=R_{N,1}(z)R_{N,1}(w)-|K_{N}(z,w)|^{2}.

To describe the DSFF for non-Hermitian random matrices, we introduce a
complex time variable

(1.6)

t+is=Te^{i\theta},

where
t,s,T\geq 0
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
.
It will be convenient for our purposes to use both
Cartesian coordinates
(t,s)
and
polar coordinates
(T,\theta)
.

Let
z_{j}=x_{j}+iy_{j}
(
x_{j},y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
) denote the complex eigenvalues of a non-Hermitian random matrix.
We define the associated complex Fourier transform by

(1.7)

Z_{N}\equiv Z_{N}(t,s):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{itx_{j}+isy_{j}}.

The DSFF
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is then defined as

(1.8)

\mathcal{F}_{N}:=\big\langle|Z_{N}|^{2}\big\rangle=\bigg\langle\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}e^{it(x_{j}-x_{k})+is(y_{j}-y_{k})}\bigg\rangle.

In principle, general higher powers

\big\langle|Z_{N}|^{\nu}\big\rangle
,
\nu>0
,
than quadratic can also be defined; see
[
31
, Eqs. (1), (2)]
. When it is necessary to emphasise the dependence on parameters, we write

(1.9)

\mathcal{F}_{N}\equiv\mathcal{F}_{N}(t,s)\equiv\mathcal{F}_{N}(T,\theta)

Using (
1.3
), the DSFF can be expressed in terms of two-point statistics. Separating the diagonal and off-diagonal contributions, we have

(1.10)

\mathcal{F}_{N}=N+\bigg\langle\sum_{1\leq j\not=k\leq N}e^{it(x_{j}-x_{k})+is(y_{j}-y_{k})}\bigg\rangle=N+\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}e^{it(x-x^{\prime})+is(y-y^{\prime})}R_{N,2}(z,z^{\prime})\,dA(z)\,dA(z^{\prime}).

The characteristic dip–ramp–plateau behaviour of the DSFF arises from the interplay between two contributions operating on distinct scales.
This naturally leads to a decomposition of the DSFF into disconnected and connected parts.
More precisely, we write

(1.11)

\mathcal{F}_{N}=\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(d)}+\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(c)},

where

(1.12)

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(d)}:=|\big\langle Z_{N}\big\rangle|^{2},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(c)}:=\big\langle|Z_{N}-\big\langle Z_{N}\big\rangle|^{2}\big\rangle.

These are referred to as the
disconnected
and
connected
parts of the DSFF, respectively.
Using the one- and two-point correlation functions (
1.5
), these terms can be written more explicitly as

(1.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(d)}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{C}}e^{itx_{j}+isy_{j}}R_{N,1}(z)\,dA(z)\bigg|^{2},

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(c)}

\displaystyle=N+\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}e^{it(x-x^{\prime})+is(y-y^{\prime})}\Big(R_{N,2}(z,z^{\prime})-R_{N,1}(z)R_{N,1}(z^{\prime})\Big)\,dA(z_{1})\,dA(z_{2}).

In particular, for the determinantal point process, it follows from (
1.5
) that

(1.15)

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(c)}=N-\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}e^{it(x_{1}-x_{2})+is(y_{1}-y_{2})}|K_{N}(z_{1},z_{2})|^{2}\,dA(z_{1})\,dA(z_{2}).

This representation clarifies the terminology: the disconnected part arises from the product of one-point statistics, while the connected part captures genuine correlations beyond independence.
We remark that in some parts of the literature, the term
N
in the above expression for
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(c)}
is separated and referred to as the
contact
term.

In analysing the asymptotic behaviour of the DSFF for the eGinUE, it is necessary to consider several scaling regimes with respect to the system size
N
.
In particular, two parameters must be scaled appropriately: the complex time parameter introduced in (
1.6
), and the non-Hermiticity parameter
\tau
.

The statistical behaviour of the DSFF under the various scalings constitutes one of the main
results of this work.
A summary of the resulting regimes is presented in Figure
1
, and will be discussed in detail in the next section.

From the elliptic law (
1.2
), one observes that the typical spacing between eigenvalues scales as

(1.16)

\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{N}}&\textup{if }1-\tau\gg\frac{1}{N},\\
\frac{1}{N}&\textup{if }1-\tau=O(\frac{1}{N}).\end{cases}

This reflects a crossover from two-dimensional bulk behaviour to an effectively one-dimensional regime as
\tau\to 1
.

In the study of the eGinUE, it is essential to distinguish different regimes of non-Hermiticity according to the scaling of the parameter
\tau
with the matrix size
N
. Equivalently, these regimes are characterised by the typical fluctuation scale of the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues. We consider the general scaling

(1.17)

\tau=1-\frac{\kappa}{N^{\alpha}},\qquad\kappa>0,\,\alpha\geq 0.

Depending on the value of
\alpha
, the following regimes arise.

•

Strong non-Hermiticity.

The parameter
\tau\in[0,1)
is kept fixed as
N\to\infty
, corresponding to
\alpha=0
in (
1.17
). In this regime, the eigenvalues form a genuinely two-dimensional cloud in the complex plane, and non-Hermitian effects persist
on the macroscopic scale.

•

Mesoscopic non-Hermiticity.
This intermediate regime corresponds to
\alpha\in(0,1)
in (
1.17
). The
fluctuations of the imaginary parts occur on a mesoscopic scale, interpolating between the strong and weak non-Hermiticity regimes. We refer to
[
2
]
for a recent analysis in this setting.

•

Weak non-Hermiticity.

The parameter
\tau
approaches the Hermitian limit on the scale

\alpha=1
in (
1.17
). In this regime, the eigenvalues concentrate near the real
axis, while non-Hermitian effects persist
on the microscopic scale.
Consequently, the local statistics interpolate between those of
the GUE and the GinUE;
see
[
20
,
23
]
. More generally, one may consider
\alpha\geq 1
. The case
\alpha=1
is critical, in the sense that the typical fluctuations of the
imaginary parts of the eigenvalues are of the same order as the mean
eigenvalue spacing. When
\alpha>1
, the model lies closer to the
Hermitian regime, as the
fluctuations of the imaginary parts become negligible
compared to the eigenvalue spacing.

We note that the regimes of non-Hermiticity discussed above are characterised in terms of bulk statistics.
In contrast, for critical edge statistics, the relevant scaling is governed by the critical exponent
\alpha=1/3
in the microscopic limit; see
[
5
,
25
,
3
]
.

Notice that the reciprocal of the typical eigenvalue spacing (
1.16
) scales as

(1.18)

\begin{cases}N^{1/2}&\textup{for strong non-Hermiticity},\\
N^{(1+\alpha)/2}&\textup{for mesoscopic non-Hermiticity},\\
N&\textup{for weak non-Hermiticity}.\end{cases}

As we will see below, this quantity naturally identifies the corresponding
generalised
Heisenberg time

T_{\textup{H}}
in each regime
as the scale, on which universal microscopic fluctuations set in.

2.
Main results

In this section, we present our main results.
We study the large-
N
asymptotics of the DSFF across different regimes of non-Hermiticity and over a range of time scales.
In all cases, we observe a transition in the dominant contribution between the disconnected and connected parts of the DSFF.

More precisely, we analyse the DSFF on general time scales of the form

(2.1)

T=N^{\gamma}\,\mathsf{T},\qquad t=N^{\gamma}\,\mathsf{t},\qquad\gamma\geq 0,

where
\mathsf{T},\mathsf{t}>0
are independent of
N
.
Within this framework, we identify the
Thouless time

T_{\mathrm{Th}}
, at which the DSFF exhibits the dip behaviour, and the
generalised
Heisenberg time
T_{\mathrm{H}}
, at which the DSFF reaches its plateau.
Here,
T_{\mathrm{Th}}
is the time scale needed to explore the full system size, when the effects of quantum localisation will cutoff classical diffusion
[
29
]
.

We recall that the Bessel function of the first kind is defined by

(2.2)

J_{\nu}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}(z/2)^{2k+\nu}}{k!\,\Gamma(\nu+k+1)};

see e.g.
[
39
, Chapter 10]
.
Our first result concerns the regime of strong non-Hermiticity, in which the parameter
\tau\in[0,1)
is held fixed,
corresponding to
\alpha=0
.
Recall that the disconnected component
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}
and the connected component
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}
of the DSFF are defined in (
1.12
).

In the following theorem, we introduce the superscripts
(\mathrm{d})
and
(\mathrm{c})
to denote the disconnected and connected parts, respectively, and the subscript “
\mathrm{s}
” to indicate the strong non-Hermiticity regime.

Theorem 2.1

(
Asymptotics of the disconnected/connected DSFF at strong non-Hermitcity
)
.

Let
\tau\in[0,1)
be fixed
corresponding to
\alpha=0
,
and write

(2.3)

\eta\equiv\eta(\tau,\theta):=\frac{\cos\theta(1+\tau)}{2}+\frac{i\sin\theta(1-\tau)}{2}.

Then we have the following.

(i)

(Dip-ramp regime)
Let
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)&=N^{2-3\gamma}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\textup{s}}^{(\textup{d})}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(N^{-\epsilon_{1}})\Big),\\
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)&=N^{2\gamma}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\textup{s}}^{(\textup{c})}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(N^{-\epsilon_{2}})\Big),\end{split}

where

(2.5)

\mathcal{F}_{\textup{s}}^{(\textup{d})}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{J_{1}(2|\eta\mathsf{T}|)^{2}}{|\eta\mathsf{T}|^{2}}&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi|\eta\mathsf{T}|^{3}}\Big(1-\sin(4N^{\gamma}|\eta\mathsf{T}|)\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\\
\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}\Big)\frac{1}{2\pi|\eta\mathsf{T}|^{3}}\Big(1-\sin(4N^{\gamma}|\eta\mathsf{T}|)\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

and

(2.6)

\mathcal{F}_{\textup{s}}^{(\textup{c})}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}+|\eta\mathsf{T}|^{2}\Big(2J_{0}(2|\eta\mathsf{T}|)^{2}+2J_{1}(2|\eta\mathsf{T}|)^{2}-\frac{J_{0}(2|\eta\mathsf{T}|)J_{1}(2|\eta\mathsf{T}|)}{|\eta\mathsf{T}|}\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}),\\
\displaystyle 1-\exp\Big(-\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

In (
2.4
), the constants
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
are given by

(2.7)

\epsilon_{1}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 2-3\gamma&\textup{for }\gamma\in[0,\frac{2}{5}),\\
\displaystyle 2\gamma&\textup{for }\gamma\in[\frac{2}{5},\frac{1}{2}],\end{cases}\qquad\epsilon_{2}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\gamma&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{3}),\\
\displaystyle 1-2\gamma&\textup{for }\gamma\in[\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2}),\\
\displaystyle 1/2&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

(ii)

(Plateau regime)
Let
\gamma>1/2
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.8)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1}\Phi_{\textup{s}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(\mathsf{e}_{1}(N))\Big),\\
&\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=N-\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1}\Phi_{\textup{s}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(\mathsf{e}_{1}(N))\Big),\end{split}

where

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\textup{s}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\textup{ or }\gamma=1,\,|\eta\mathsf{T}|<2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}+|\eta\mathsf{T}|\sqrt{|\eta\mathsf{T}|^{2}-4}-4\,\textup{arccosh}\frac{|\eta\mathsf{T}|}{2}&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,|\eta\mathsf{T}|>2,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{4}\mathsf{T}^{2}+|\eta\mathsf{T}|^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma>1.\end{cases}

Here, the error term
\mathsf{e}_{1}(N)
is given by

(2.10)

\mathsf{e}_{1}(N)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\log N&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1],\\
\displaystyle N\log N&\textup{for }\gamma>1.\end{cases}

Remark 1

(Dependence on the angular parameter
\theta
)
.

Note that in Theorem
2.1
, the resulting asymptotic formulas may depend solely on
\mathsf{T}
, or may additionally involve the angular parameter
\theta
. In the latter case, the dependence on
\theta
enters through the parametrisation
\eta
defined in (
2.3
); that is,
\theta
always appears together with
\tau
in the combination specified in (
2.3
). In particular, it enters only through the quantity
|\eta\mathsf{T}|
.
Moreover, when
\tau=0
, one has
|\eta|=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and hence the resulting formulas are independent of the angular parameter
\theta
.

By (
1.11
), the following result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.1
.

Corollary 2.2

(
Leading order asymptotics of the DSFF at strong non-Hermitcity
)
.

Let
\tau\in[0,1)
be fixed,
corresponding to
\alpha=0
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.11)

\mathcal{F}_{N}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)\sim\begin{cases}N^{2-3\gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}^{\rm(d)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)&\textup{for }\gamma\in[0,\frac{2}{5}),\\
N^{\frac{4}{5}}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}^{\rm(d)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}^{\rm(c)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)\big)&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{2}{5},\\
N^{2\gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\rm s}^{\rm(c)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{2}{5},\frac{1}{2}],\\
N&\textup{for }\gamma>\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

In Corollary
2.2
, we present only the leading-order asymptotic behaviour of the DSFF. In particular, one observes that when
\gamma<2/5
, the disconnected part is dominant, whereas for
\gamma>2/5
, the connected part provides the leading contribution. Furthermore, when
\gamma>1/2
, the DSFF exhibits a flat (plateau) behaviour

\sim N
, which is constant for fixed
N
;
indeed, in Theorem
2.1
, the error term in this regime is exponentially small.

See Figure
2
for
illustration, presenting our numerics.

We also note that for
\gamma\in(2/5,1/2]
, where the connected part dominates, its behaviour is quadratic by (
2.6
). This quadratic ramp is in contrast to the linear ramp behaviour that arises in the SFF of the GUE.

In the regime of strong non-Hermiticity, part of our result in Theorem

[

, this was further extended to the intermediate regime

T_{\textup{Th}}=O(N^{2/5})

\tau=1-O(N^{-1})

Our next result concerns the mesoscopic scaling regime, in which
\tau\to 1
. In this setting, rather than using the parametrisation
\eta
in (
2.3
) that captures the angular contribution at strong non-Hermiticity, it is more convenient to work with both polar and Cartesian coordinates as introduced in (
2.1
). In particular, by (
1.6
), we have

(2.12)

\mathsf{t}=\mathsf{T}\cos\theta.

Indeed, recalling that in Theorem
2.1
, the
\theta
-dependence of the resulting asymptotic formulas enters through the combination
|\eta\mathsf{T}|
, one may interpret the parameter
\mathsf{t}
as
|\eta\mathsf{T}|
. In the case
\tau=1
, this reduces to
\eta=\cos\theta
.

Accordingly, in the statement of the following theorem, we describe the asymptotic behaviour in terms of the variables
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{t}
.
We then obtain the following result. As before, we introduce the subscript “
\mathrm{m}
” to indicate the mesoscopic non-Hermiticity regime.

Theorem 2.3

(
Asymptotics of the disconnected/connected DSFF at mesoscopic non-Hermitcity
)
.

Let
\tau
be scaled as (
1.17
) with
\alpha\in(0,1).

(i)

(Dip-ramp regime)
Let
\gamma\in[0,(1+\alpha)/2]
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)&=N^{2-3\gamma}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\textup{m}}^{(\textup{d})}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(N^{-\epsilon_{3}})\Big),\\
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)&=N^{\max\{2\gamma-\alpha,\gamma\}}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\rm m}^{(\textup{c})}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(N^{-\epsilon_{4}})\Big),\end{split}

where

(2.14)

\mathcal{F}_{\textup{m}}^{(\textup{d})}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{J_{1}(2\mathsf{t})^{2}}{\mathsf{t}^{2}}&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{t}^{3}}\Big(1-\sin(4N^{\gamma}\mathsf{t})\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,\alpha),\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{t}^{3}}\Big(1-\sin\big(4N^{\gamma}\mathsf{t}+O(N^{\gamma-\alpha})\big)\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma\in[\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}),\\
\displaystyle\exp\Big(-\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}\Big)\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{t}^{3}}\Big(1-\sin\big(4N^{(1+\alpha)/2}\mathsf{t}+O(N^{(1-\alpha)/2}\big)\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\end{cases}

and

(2.15)

\mathcal{F}_{\textup{m}}^{(\textup{c})}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathsf{t}^{2}\Big(2J_{0}(2\mathsf{t})^{2}+2J_{1}(2\mathsf{t})^{2}-\frac{J_{0}(2\mathsf{t})J_{1}(2\mathsf{t})}{\mathsf{t}}\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\mathsf{t}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,\alpha),\\
\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\mathsf{t}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma=\alpha,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}),\\
\displaystyle 1-\exp\Big(-\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}.\end{cases}

In (
2.13
), the constants
\epsilon_{3},\epsilon_{4}>0
are given by

(2.16)

\epsilon_{3}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\alpha-\gamma&\textup{for }\gamma\in[0,\alpha),\\
\displaystyle\alpha&\textup{for }\gamma\in[\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}],\end{cases}\qquad\epsilon_{4}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\alpha&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\min\{2\gamma,\alpha-\gamma\}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,\alpha),\\
\displaystyle\min\{\alpha,1-\alpha\}&\textup{for }\gamma=\alpha,\\
\displaystyle\min\{\alpha,\gamma-\alpha,1+\alpha-2\gamma\}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}),\\
\displaystyle\min\Big\{\alpha,\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\Big\}&\textup{for }\gamma=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\end{cases}

(ii)

(Plateau regime)
Let
\gamma>(1+\alpha)/2
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.17)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1-\alpha}\Phi_{\textup{m}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(\mathsf{e}_{2}(N))\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\textup{ or }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}<2,\\
\displaystyle\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1}\Phi_{\textup{m}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(\mathsf{e}_{2}(N))\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}>2\textup{ or }\gamma>1,\end{cases}\\
&\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=N-\begin{cases}\displaystyle\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1-\alpha}\Phi_{\textup{m}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(\mathsf{e}_{2}(N))\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)\textup{ or }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}<2,\\
\displaystyle\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1}\Phi_{\textup{m}}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(\mathsf{e}_{2}(N))\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}>2\textup{ or }\gamma>1,\end{cases}\end{split}

where

(2.18)

\Phi_{\textup{m}}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1)\textup{ or }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}<2,\\
\displaystyle\mathsf{t}\sqrt{\mathsf{t}^{2}-4}-4\,\textup{arccosh}\frac{\mathsf{t}}{2}&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}>2,\\
\displaystyle\mathsf{t}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma>1.\end{cases}

Here, the error term
\mathsf{e}_{2}(N)
is given by

(2.19)

\mathsf{e}_{2}(N)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\log N&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1)\textup{ or }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}<2,\\
\displaystyle N^{1-\alpha}&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}>2,\\
\displaystyle N^{2\gamma-1-\alpha}&\textup{for }\gamma>1.\end{cases}

As a consequence of Theorem
2.3
, by (
1.11
), we have the following.

Corollary 2.4

(
Leading order asymptotics of the DSFF at mesoscopic non-Hermitcity
)
.

Let
\tau
be scaled as (
1.17
) with
\alpha\in(0,1).

As
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.20)

\mathcal{F}_{N}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)\sim\begin{cases}N^{2-3\gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\rm m}^{\rm(d)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)&\textup{for }\gamma\in[0,\min\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{2+\alpha}{5}\}),\\
N^{\max\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{4-\alpha}{5}\}}\big(\mathcal{F}_{\rm m}^{\rm(d)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+\mathcal{F}_{\rm m}^{\rm(c)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)\big)&\textup{for }\gamma=\min\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{2+\alpha}{5}\},\\
N^{\max\{2\gamma-\alpha,\gamma\}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm m}^{\rm(c)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\min\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{2+\alpha}{5}\},\frac{1+\alpha}{2}],\\
N&\textup{for }\gamma>\frac{1+\alpha}{2}.\end{cases}

As before, we present the leading-order asymptotics of the DSFF. In the mesoscopic regime, the dominant behaviour exhibits a similar transition: for small values of
\gamma
, the disconnected part is dominant, whereas for larger values of
\gamma
, the connected part provides the leading contribution. Here, the precise transition threshold depends on the mesoscopic scaling parameter
\alpha
.

Remark 2

(Behaviour in the ramp regime)
.

As discussed above, the mesoscopic scaling regime exhibits a critical interpolation of the DSFF, in which both linear and quadratic behaviours arise depending on the value of
\gamma
; see (
2.15
) and Figure
3
. The quadratic behaviour is characteristic of strongly non-Hermitian ensembles (GinUE-type), whereas the linear behaviour reflects Hermitian (GUE-type) statistics.

More precisely, for
\gamma\in(0,\alpha)
the statistics are GUE-like, while for
\gamma\in(\alpha,\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2})
they are GinUE-like; see Figure
1
(B). As
\alpha\to 0
, corresponding to strong non-Hermiticity, the interval
(0,\alpha)
shrinks and the linear behaviour becomes negligible. Conversely, as
\alpha\to 1
, approaching the Hermitian limit, the interval
(\alpha,\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2})
shrinks and the quadratic behaviour is suppressed.

In the ramp regime, more refined distinctions appear. On the one hand, when

\gamma\in\Bigl(\min\{\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{2+\alpha}{5}\},\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2}\Bigr]\cap(0,\alpha),

the connected contribution exhibits asymptotically linear growth, in agreement with the GUE ramp. This regime is non-empty only for
\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}
. On the other hand, when

\gamma\in\Bigl(\min\{\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{2+\alpha}{5}\},\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2}\Bigr]\cap\Bigl(\alpha,\tfrac{1+\alpha}{2}\Bigr),

which is non-empty for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the connected part displays quadratic growth, characteristic of the GinUE ramp.

We expect this dichotomy between GUE- and GinUE-type behaviour to be a universal feature of macroscopic observables (such as the DSFF) for the eGinUE. Similar transitions have been observed for other observables, notably the number variance; see
[
2
]
.

As our final main result, we analyse the asymptotic behaviour of the DSFF in the regime of weak non-Hermiticity. As before, we add subscript “w” denoting the weak non-Hermiticity.

Theorem 2.5

(
Asymptotics of the disconnected/connected DSFF at weak non-Hermitcity
)
.

Let
\tau
be scaled as (
1.17
) with
\alpha\geq 1.

(i)

(Dip-ramp regime)
Let
\gamma\in[0,1]
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.21)

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=N^{2-3\gamma}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\textup{w}}^{(\textup{d})}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(N^{-\epsilon_{5}})\Big),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=N^{\gamma}\Big(\mathcal{F}_{\textup{w}}^{(\textup{c})}(\mathsf{T},\theta)+O(N^{-\epsilon_{6}})\Big),

where

(2.22)

\mathcal{F}_{\textup{w}}^{(\textup{d})}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{J_{1}(2\mathsf{t})^{2}}{\mathsf{t}^{2}}&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{t}^{3}}\Big(1-\sin(4N^{\gamma}\mathsf{t})\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,1),\\
\displaystyle\exp\Big(-N^{1-\alpha}\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}\Big)\frac{1}{2\pi\mathsf{t}^{3}}\Big(1-\sin\Big[N\Big(\mathsf{t}\sqrt{4-\mathsf{t}^{2}}+4\,\textup{arcsin}\frac{\mathsf{t}}{2}\Big)+O(N^{1-\alpha})\Big]\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}<2\end{cases}

and

(2.23)

\mathcal{F}_{\textup{w}}^{(\textup{c})}(\mathsf{T},\theta):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathsf{t}^{2}\Big(2J_{0}(2\mathsf{t})^{2}+2J_{1}(2\mathsf{t})^{2}-\frac{J_{0}(2\mathsf{t})J_{1}(2\mathsf{t})}{\mathsf{t}}\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\mathsf{t}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,1),\\
\displaystyle 1-\exp\Big(-N^{1-\alpha}\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathsf{T}^{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big(\mathsf{t}\sqrt{4-\mathsf{t}^{2}}+4\,\textup{arcsin}\frac{\mathsf{t}}{2}\Big)&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}<2.\end{cases}

In (
2.21
), the constants
\epsilon_{5},\epsilon_{6}
are given by

(2.24)

\epsilon_{5}=\min\{2-3\gamma,2\gamma,\alpha-\gamma\},\qquad\epsilon_{6}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min\{2,\alpha\}&\textup{for }\gamma=0,\\
\displaystyle\min\{2\gamma,2-2\gamma,\alpha-\gamma\}&\textup{for }\gamma\in(0,1),\\
\displaystyle 1&\textup{for }\gamma=1.\end{cases}

(ii)

(Plateau regime)
Let
\gamma\geq 1
. Then as
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.25)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{d})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1}\Phi_{\textup{w}}(\mathsf{t})+O(\mathsf{e}_{3}(N))\Big),\\
&\mathcal{F}_{N}^{(\textup{c})}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)=N-\exp\Big(-N^{2\gamma-1}\Phi_{\textup{w}}(\mathsf{t})+O(\mathsf{e}_{3}(N))\Big),\end{split}

where

(2.26)

\Phi_{\textup{w}}(\mathsf{t}):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathsf{t}\sqrt{\mathsf{t}^{2}-4}-4\,\textup{arccosh}\frac{\mathsf{t}}{2}&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}>2,\\
\displaystyle\mathsf{t}^{2}&\textup{for }\gamma>1.\end{cases}

Here, the error term
\mathsf{e}_{3}(N)
is given by

(2.27)

\mathsf{e}_{3}(N)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\log N&\textup{for }\gamma=1,\,\mathsf{t}>2,\\
\displaystyle N\log N&\textup{for }\gamma\in(1,\frac{2+\alpha}{2}),\\
\displaystyle N^{2\gamma-1-\alpha}&\textup{for }\gamma>\frac{2+\alpha}{2}.\end{cases}

We mention that in Theorems
2.1
,
2.3
, and
2.5
, the stated error bounds are indeed optimal.

Note that, as before, the resulting formulas are expressed in terms of
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{t}
. Moreover, for
\alpha>1
, the dependence on
\mathsf{T}
is suppressed, so that the formulas depend only on

. In this regime, the asymptotic behaviour of the DSFF is universal, coinciding with that of the GUE. This reflects the fact that the model is already sufficiently close to the Hermitian setting at the level of DSFF asymptotics.

More precisely, the asymptotics in Theorem
2.5
are consistent with the known results for the spectral form factor of the GUE
[
16
]
. In particular, the regimes
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=1
correspond to
[

, Propositions 16 and 18]
, respectively. Furthermore, the linear ramp behaviour observed in (

) for

agrees with
[
16
, Remark 17]
, where it arises by taking the limit

in the

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.5
, we have the following.

Corollary 2.6

(
Leading order asymptotics of the DSFF at weak non-Hermitcity
)
.

Let
\tau
be scaled as (
1.17
) with
\alpha\geq 1.

As
N\to\infty
, we have

(2.28)

\mathcal{F}_{N}(N^{\gamma}\mathsf{T},\theta)\sim\begin{cases}N^{2-3\gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\rm w}^{\rm(d)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)&\textup{for }\gamma\in[0,\frac{1}{2}),\\

N^{\gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\rm w}^{\rm(c)}(\mathsf{T},\theta)&\textup{for }\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1),\\

Remark 3

(Thouless and Heisenberg time)
.

Combining Corollaries
2.2
,
2.4
, and
2.6
, we observe that, under the general scaling assumption (

T_{\text{Th}}\propto N^{\min\{\frac{2+\alpha}{5},\frac{1}{2}\}},\qquad T_{\text{H}}\propto N^{\min\{\frac{1+\alpha}{2},1\}}.

3.
Finite-
N
analysis

In this section, we prepare several preliminaries for our main results.
For this purpose, we first consider a general point process

\bm{z}=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
in the complex plane, defined through the joint probability distribution

(3.1)

P_{N}(\bm{z})\prod_{j=1}^{N}\,dA(z_{j})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\prod_{j<k}|z_{j}-z_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\omega(z_{j})\,dA(z_{j}).

Here,
\omega(z)
denotes a weight function specifying the ensemble and
Z_{N}
is the normalisation constant.
In particular, the eigenvalues of the complex elliptic Ginibre
ensemble follow the distribution (
3.1
) with the weight function

(3.2)

\omega^{\rm H}_{N}(z):=\exp\Big(-N\frac{|z|^{2}-\tau\operatorname{Re}z^{2}}{1-\tau^{2}}\Big).

The
k
-point correlation function of the ensemble (
3.1
), which can be characterised via (
1.3
), can also be defined in terms of the joint probability distribution by integrating out
N-k
variables:

(3.3)

R_{N,k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})=\frac{N!}{(N-k)!}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N-k}}P_{N}(\bm{z})\prod_{j=k+1}^{N}\,dA(z_{j}).

A remarkable property of the ensemble (
3.1
) is that it forms a determinantal point process. Namely, we have the representation (
1.4
)
where

(3.4)

K_{N}(z,z^{\prime}):=\sqrt{\omega(z)\omega(z^{\prime})}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\phi_{j}(z)\overline{\phi_{j}(z^{\prime})}.

Here,
\{\phi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the set of orthonormal polynomials associated to the weight function
\omega(z)
, i.e.

(3.5)

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\phi_{n}(z)\overline{\phi_{m}(z)}\omega(z)\,dA(z)=\delta_{n,m}.

In particular, for the elliptic weight (
3.2
), the associated planar orthonormal polynomial is given by (see e.g.
[
14
,
13
]
and
[
1
, Lemma 7]
)

(3.6)

\phi^{\rm H}_{N,n}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\tau^{2}}\sqrt{n!}}\Big(\frac{\tau}{2}\Big)^{n/2}H_{n}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{N}{2\tau}}z\Big),

where

(3.7)

H_{n}(x):=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}

is the Hermite polynomial.

For the general ensemble defined in (
3.1
), the associated DSFF is introduced through (
1.8
). By combining (
1.4
), (
1.13
), (
1.14
), and (
3.4
), one can immediately derive exact expressions for both the disconnected and connected parts of the DSFF in terms of the underlying orthonormal polynomials.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

(3.8)

\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(d)}=\bigg|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}F_{jj}\bigg|^{2},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{N}^{\rm(c)}=N-\sum_{j,k=0}^{N-1}\big|F_{jk}\big|^{2},

where

(3.9)

F_{jk}:=\int_{\mathbb{C}}e^{itx+isy}\phi_{j}(z)\overline{\phi_{k}(z)}\omega(z)\,dA(z).

Next, we evaluate
F_{jk}
in (
3.9
) for the elliptic Ginibre ensemble, which we denote by

(3.10)

F^{\rm H}_{jk}:=\int_{\mathbb{C}}e^{itx+isy}\phi^{\rm H}_{N,j}(z)\overline{\phi^{\rm H}_{N,k}(z)}\omega^{\rm H}_{N}(z)\,dA(z).

This quantity can be evaluated explicitly in terms of the generalised Laguerre polynomials, defined by

(3.11)

L^{(\nu)}_{k}(x):=\frac{x^{-\nu}e^{x}}{k!}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}(x^{k+\nu}e^{-x})=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\frac{\Gamma(j+\nu+1)}{(j-k)!\Gamma(\nu+k+1)}\frac{(-z)^{k}}{k!}.

Note that it satisfies the orthogonality relation

(3.12)

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\nu}e^{-x}L_{n}^{(\nu)}(x)L_{m}^{(\nu)}(x)\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(n+\nu+1)}{n!}\delta_{n,m},

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\tau\in[0,1)
. Then we have

(3.13)

F^{\rm H}_{jk}=\exp\Big(-\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{8N}T^{2}-\frac{|\eta T|^{2}}{2N}\Big)\sqrt{\frac{k!}{j!}}\Big(\frac{i\eta T}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)^{j-k}L^{(j-k)}_{k}\Big(\frac{|\eta T|^{2}}{N}\Big),

where
T
and
\eta
are defined in (
1.6
) and (
2.3
), respectively.

We note that Proposition
3.2
remains valid in the Hermitian limit
\tau=1
.
In this case, the identity is well known; see e.g.
[
16
, Proposition 13]
and the references therein.
By contrast, for the planar case
\tau\in[0,1)
, this identity does not appear to be explicitly recorded in the literature, although it was used implicitly in the supplementary material of
[
41
]
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

The complex exponential
e^{itx+isy}
can be absorbed into the weight function, which leads to a convenient translation of variables. Indeed, we have

\begin{split}F^{\rm H}_{jk}&=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\phi^{\rm H}_{N,j}(z)\phi^{\rm H}_{N,k}(\overline{z})\exp\Big(-\frac{Nx^{2}}{1+\tau}-\frac{Ny^{2}}{1-\tau}+itx+isy\Big)\,dA(z)\\
&=\exp\Big(-\frac{1+\tau}{4N}t^{2}-\frac{1-\tau}{4N}s^{2}\Big)\int_{\mathbb{C}}\phi^{\rm H}_{N,j}(z)\phi^{\rm H}_{N,k}(\overline{z})\exp\Big(-\frac{N(x-\frac{it(1+\tau)}{2N})^{2}}{1+\tau}-\frac{N(y-\frac{is(1-\tau)}{2N})^{2}}{1-\tau}\Big)\,dA(z)\\
&=\exp\Big(-\frac{1-\tau^{2}}{8N}T^{2}-\frac{|\eta T|^{2}}{2N}\Big)\int_{\mathbb{C}}\phi^{\rm H}_{N,j}\Big(z+\frac{i\eta T}{N}\Big)\phi^{\rm H}_{N,k}\Big(\overline{z}+\frac{i\overline{\eta}T}{N}\Big)\exp\Big(-\frac{Nx^{2}}{1+\tau}-\frac{Ny^{2}}{1-\tau}\Big)\,dA(z).\end{split}

Using the identity

H_{n}(a+b)=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}H_{l}(a)(2b)^{n-l}

together with the orthonormality (
3.5
) of the family
(\phi^{\rm H}_{N,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we obtain

F^{\rm H}_{jk}=\exp\Big(-\frac{1+\tau}{4N}t^{2}-\frac{1-\tau}{4N}s^{2}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!\,k!}}\sum_{l=0}^{j\wedge k}l!\binom{j}{l}\binom{k}{l}\Big(\frac{i\eta T}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)^{j-l}\Big(\frac{i\overline{\eta}T}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)^{k-l}.

\begin{split}F^{\rm H}_{jk}&=\exp\Big(-\frac{1+\tau}{4N}t^{2}-\frac{1-\tau}{4N}s^{2}\Big)\sqrt{\frac{k!}{j!}}\Big(\frac{i\eta T}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)^{j-k}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{j}{l}\frac{1}{(k-l)!}\Big(-\frac{|\eta T|^{2}}{N}\Big)^{k-l}\\
&=\exp\Big(-\frac{1+\tau}{4N}t^{2}-\frac{1-\tau}{4N}s^{2}\Big)\sqrt{\frac{k!}{j!}}\Big(\frac{i\eta T}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)^{j-k}L^{(j-k)}_{k}\Big(\frac{|\eta T|^{2}}{N}\Big).\end{split}

The case

follows by symmetry using the identity

\frac{(-x)^{m}}{m!}L^{(m-n)}_{n}(x)=\frac{(-x)^{n}}{n!}L^{(n-m)}_{m}(x).

This completes the proof.

Remark 5

(LUE density)
.

As previously mentioned, the function
\rho_{N}
can be identified with the one-point function of the LUE. Thus Proposition
4.4
describes the large-
N
asymptotic behaviour of the LUE average density across all scaling regimes. In the literature, the regimes
I
,
II
,
III
, and
IV
are also commonly referred to as the hard edge, bulk, soft edge, and exterior regimes, respectively, and the corresponding asymptotics have been extensively studied for general rectangular parameter. In particular, the leading term in (
4.36
) is given by the Marchenko–Pastur law, while the correction term coincides with
[
21
, Eq. (52)]
. At the soft and hard edges, it was previously shown in
[
21
, Eq. (73)]
and
[
19
, Eq. (4.27)]
, respectively, that the asymptotics (
4.37
) and (
4.35
) hold up to the second-order term.

However, these previously known results are not sufficient for our purposes, since they are formulated only for fixed scaling limits, and simply combining them does not fully cover the domains required in the present analysis. For instance,
[
21
, Eq. (52)]
states that (
4.36
) holds for
x=4N\mathsf{x}
with fixed
\mathsf{x}\in(0,1)
, whereas we prove that it holds uniformly for
x\in\textup{II}=[c,4N-d\sqrt{N}]
with fixed
c,d>0
.

To prove Proposition
4.4
, we distinguish between the different regimes. For
x\in\textup{I}\cup\textup{II}
, we use the representation

(4.39)

\rho_{N}(x)=N-\int_{0}^{x}f_{N}(u)\,du,

whereas for
x\in\textup{III}\cup\textup{IV}
, we employ

(4.40)

\rho_{N}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}f_{N}(u)\,du.

We first show the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
\alpha,\beta,c,d>0
are positive constants, and let
p_{1}(x)
and
q_{1}(x)
be polynomials. Define

(4.41)

\varphi(x):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sqrt{x-x^{2}}+\textup{arcsin}\sqrt{x}&\textup{ if }0<x<1,\\
\displaystyle\sqrt{x^{2}-x}-\textup{arccosh}\sqrt{x}&\textup{ if }x\geq 1.\end{cases}

Then we have the following.

(i)

For sufficiently large
\lambda>0
, suppose that
c/\lambda\leq a\leq b\leq 1-d\lambda^{-1/2}
, and let
M
be an arbitrary positive integer. Then we have

(4.42)

\int^{b}_{a}\frac{p_{1}(x)}{x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx=F(b)-F(a),

where

F(x)=-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Big(\frac{2i}{\lambda}\Big)^{j}\frac{p_{j}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(j-2)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+(3j-2)/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda^{M+1}}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{\alpha+(M-1)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+(3M+1)/2}}.

Here, the sequence of polynomials
(p_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is defined recursively by

(4.43)

p_{j+1}(x):=x^{\alpha+j/2}(1-x)^{\beta+3j/2}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{p_{j}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(j-2)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+(3j-2)/2}}\Big),\qquad j\geq 1.

(ii)

For sufficiently large
\lambda>0
, suppose that
1+d\lambda^{-1/2}\leq a\leq b
, and let
M
be an arbitrary positive integer. Then we have

(4.44)

\int^{b}_{a}\frac{q_{1}(x)}{x^{\alpha}(x-1)^{\beta}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx=G(b)-G(a),

where

G(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{\lambda^{j}}\frac{q_{j}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(j-2)/2}(x-1)^{\beta+(3j-2)/2}}e^{\lambda\varphi(x)}+O\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda^{M+1}}\Big)\frac{1}{x^{\alpha+(M-1)/2}(x-1)^{\beta+(3M+1)/2}}.

Here, the sequence of polynomials
(q_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is defined recursively by

(4.45)

q_{j+1}(x):=x^{\alpha+j/2}(x-1)^{\beta+3j/2}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{q_{j}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(j-2)/2}(x-1)^{\beta+(3j-2)/2}}\Big),\qquad j\geq 1.

Proof.

We prove only (i), since the proof of (ii) follows by the same argument. Note that by (
4.41
), we have

\frac{d}{dx}\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle x^{-1/2}(1-x)^{1/2}&\textup{ if }0<x<1,\\
\displaystyle x^{-1/2}(x-1)^{1/2}&\textup{ if }x\geq 1.\end{cases}

Then, repeated integration by parts yields

\begin{split}\int^{b}_{a}\frac{p_{1}(x)}{x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx&=-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\Big(\frac{i}{\lambda}\Big)^{j}\frac{p_{j}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(j-2)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+(3j-2)/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\bigg|^{b}_{a}\\
&\quad+\Big(\frac{i}{\lambda}\Big)^{M}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{p_{M+1}(x)}{x^{\alpha+M/2}(1-x)^{\beta+3M/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx,\end{split}

where
(p_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is given by (
4.43
). Thus, it suffices to show the error bound

E(x):=\Big(\frac{i}{\lambda}\Big)^{M}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{p_{M+1}(x)}{x^{\alpha+M/2}(1-x)^{\beta+3M/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx=\frac{1}{x^{\alpha+(M-1)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+(3M+1)/2}}O(\lambda^{-M-1}).

Applying integration by parts to
E(x)
once more, for a positive integer
m
, we obtain

\displaystyle E(x)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=M+1}^{M+m}\Big(\frac{i}{\lambda}\Big)^{j}\frac{p_{j}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(j-2)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+(3j-2)/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\bigg|^{b}_{a}

\displaystyle+\Big(\frac{i}{\lambda}\Big)^{M+m}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{p_{M+m}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(M+m)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+3(M+m)/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx.

It is immediate that the first term on the right-hand side satisfies the desired bound. For the second term, since
x\in[a,b]\subset[c/\lambda,1-d\lambda^{-1/2}]
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\quad\bigg|\Big(\frac{2i}{\lambda}\Big)^{M+m}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{p_{M+m}(x)}{x^{\alpha+(M+m)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+3(M+m)/2}}e^{i\lambda\varphi(x)}\,dx\bigg|\\
&=O(\lambda^{-M-m})\sup_{x\in[a,b]}\frac{1}{x^{\alpha+(M+m)/2}(1-x)^{\beta+3(M+m)/2}}=O(\lambda^{\max\{\alpha-(M+m)/2,\beta/2-(M+m)/4\}}).\end{split}

Choosing

sufficiently large, the above term is also of order
O(\lambda^{-M-1})
. This completes the proof.
∎

(ii), we obtain

(4.47)

\rho_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{4N}\int_{\mathsf{x}}^{\infty}f_{N}^{\textup{IV}}(u)\,du\Big(1+O(N^{-1/4})\Big)=\rho_{N}^{\textup{IV}}(\mathsf{x})\Big(1+O(N^{-1/4})\Big),

where

\displaystyle\rho_{N}^{\textup{IV}}(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{32\pi N}\frac{1}{u(u-1)}\exp\Big[-4N\Big(\sqrt{u^{2}-u}-\textup{arccosh}\sqrt{u}\Big)\Big].

This gives rise to the desired asymptotic formula (
4.38
).

Finally, we establish (
4.37
). For
x\in\textup{III}
, using (
4.7
), we obtain

(4.48)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{N}(x)&=\rho_{N}\big(4N+d\sqrt{N}\big)+(16N)^{1/3}\int_{y}^{dN^{1/6}/2^{4/3}}f_{N}^{\textup{III}}(u)\,du+O(N^{-7/3})\int_{y}^{dN^{1/6}/2^{4/3}}\textup{envAi}_{7/2}(u)^{2}\,du\\
&=\rho_{N}^{\textup{III}}(y)-\rho_{N}^{\textup{III}}\Big(\frac{dN^{1/6}}{2^{4/3}}\Big)+\rho_{N}\big(4N+d\sqrt{N}\big)+\textup{envAi}_{4}(y)^{2}O(N^{-7/3}),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\rho_{N}^{\textup{III}}(u)&:=\frac{1}{(16N)^{1/3}}\Big(\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(u)^{2}-u\operatorname{Ai}(u)^{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{20N}\Big(3u^{2}\operatorname{Ai}(u)^{2}+2\operatorname{Ai}(u)\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(u)-2u\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(u)^{2}\Big)\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{350(2N)^{5/3}}\Big((7-96u^{3})\operatorname{Ai}(u)^{2}-u(74+7u^{3})\operatorname{Ai}(u)\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(u)+37u^{2}\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(u)^{2}\Big).\end{split}

Here, the integral of
f_{N}^{\textup{III}}
follows from the differential equation
\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime\prime}(u)=u\operatorname{Ai}(u)
, while the error term is estimated using

\int\textup{envAi}_{p}(x)^{2}\,dx=\textup{envAi}_{p+1/2}(x)^{2},

which follows directly from the definition (
4.4
). Then, one sees from the asymptotics of the Airy function
[
39
, Eqs. (9.7.5)–(9.7.6), (9.7.9)–(9.7.10)]
that

\rho_{N}^{\textup{III}}(y)+\textup{envAi}_{4}(y)^{2}O(N^{-7/3})=\frac{1}{(16N)^{1/3}}\Big(\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(y)^{2}-u\operatorname{Ai}(y)^{2}\Big)+\textup{envAi}_{3/4}(y)^{2}O(N^{-1}).

Then, using (
4.47
), we obtain the cancellation of the boundary terms:

\displaystyle\rho_{N}(4N+d\sqrt{N})-\rho_{N}^{\textup{III}}\Big(\frac{dN^{1/6}}{2^{4/3}}\Big)=O(N^{-3/4})\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{3}d^{3/2}N^{1/4}\Big].

Combining this with (
4.48
), we obtain (
4.37
), which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix A
Alternative representations of

\Psi_{N}

In this appendix, we present alternative representations of the function
\Psi_{N}
in (
3.18
). For this, we begin with the following elementary lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

We have

(A.1)

\frac{d}{dx}\bigg[e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{k}^{(\nu)}(x)^{2}\bigg]=-e^{-x}L_{N-1}^{(\nu+1)}(x)^{2}.

Proof.

By using (
3.21
) and the elementary parameter-shift identity

(A.2)

L_{k}^{(\nu)}(x)=L_{k}^{(\nu+1)}(x)-L_{k-1}^{(\nu+1)}(x)\qquad(k\geq 0),

with the convention
L_{-1}^{(\nu+1)}\equiv 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\bigg[e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{k}^{(\nu)}(x)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=-e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{k}^{(\nu)}(x)\Bigl[L_{k}^{(\nu)}(x)+2\,L_{k-1}^{(\nu+1)}(x)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=-e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Big[L_{k}^{(\nu+1)}(x)^{2}-L_{k-1}^{(\nu+1)}(x)^{2}\Big]=-e^{-x}L_{N-1}^{(\nu+1)}(x)^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\Psi_{N}
be defined as in (
3.18
). Then it admits the
following alternative representation:

(A.3)

\Psi_{N}(x)=e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(N-k)L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}.

Moreover,
\Psi_{N}
can also be written in the integral form

(A.4)

\Psi_{N}(x)=N\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-u}L_{N-1}^{(0)}(u)^{2}\,du-N(N-1)e^{-x}\Bigl(L_{N-1}^{(0)}(x)^{2}-L_{N-2}^{(0)}(x)\,L_{N}^{(0)}(x)\Bigr).

Proof.

We first establish (
A.3
).
By Proposition
3.4
together with (
3.19
),
it suffices to verify the differential identity

(A.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Big[e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(N-k)L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}\Big]=-e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{k}^{(0)}(x)^{2}.

For this, we rewrite the weighted sum by exchanging the order of summation, which allows us to express it as a double sum over a triangular index set:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(N-k)L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=k+1}^{N}L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}.

Combining this observation with Lemma
A.1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Big[e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(N-k)L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{d}{dx}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}e^{-x}L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}\Big]=-\sum_{m=1}^{N}e^{-x}L_{m-1}^{(0)}(x)^{2},

which leads to (
A.5
).

We next prove (
A.4
).
Starting from the representation (
A.3
), we decompose
\Psi_{N}
into two contributions:

(A.6)

\displaystyle\Psi_{N}(x)=Ne^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}-e^{-x}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}k\,L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}.

Notice here that by using the orthogonality relation (
3.12
) together with the Christoffel–Darboux formula, we have

(A.7)

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}k\,L_{k}^{(-1)}(x)^{2}=N(N-1)\Bigl(L_{N-1}^{(0)}(x)^{2}-L_{N-2}^{(0)}(x)\,L_{N}^{(0)}(x)\Bigr).

On the other hand, by Lemma
A.1
, the first contribution admits an integral representation, which coincides with the remaining term in (
A.4
).
Combining these two evaluations completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Expansion coefficients of the Laguerre polynomials

In this appendix, we recall the recursive formulas developed in
[
24
]
, which allow one to compute the expansion coefficients appearing in Lemma
4.3
.

Let
\nu=4n+2\alpha+2
. Recall that
\xi
is defined in (
4.3
), and that
\zeta
is defined in (
4.26
). It follows from
[
24
, Eqs. (4.7), (5.13)]
that the Bessel-type expansion (
4.29
) extends to arbitrary order
p\geq 0
in the form

(B.1)

2^{\alpha}e^{\nu t/2}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(\nu t)=\frac{J_{\alpha}(\nu\xi(t))}{\xi(t)^{\alpha}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor}\beta_{2k}\Big(\frac{2}{\nu}\Big)^{2k}-\frac{J_{\alpha+1}(\nu\xi(t))}{\xi(t)^{\alpha+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor p/2-1\rfloor}\beta_{2k+1}\Big(\frac{2}{\nu}\Big)^{2k+1}+\frac{\textup{env}J_{\alpha}(\nu t)}{\xi(t)^{\alpha}}O(\nu^{-p}),

while the Airy-type expansion (
4.32
) extends as

(B.2)

\begin{split}(-1)^{n}2^{\alpha}e^{\nu t/2}L_{n}^{(\alpha)}(\nu t)&=\operatorname{Ai}(\nu^{2/3}\zeta(t))\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor}\frac{\gamma_{2k}}{\nu^{2k+1/3}}-\operatorname{Ai}^{\prime}(\nu^{2/3}\zeta(t))\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor p/2-1\rfloor}\frac{\gamma_{2k+1}}{\nu^{2k+5/3}}\\
&\quad+\begin{cases}\displaystyle\textup{envAi}_{-1/4}(\nu^{2/3}\zeta(t))O(\nu^{-p-1/3})&\textup{if }p\textup{ is even},\\
\textup{envAi}_{1/4}(\nu^{2/3}\zeta(t))O(\nu^{-p-2/3})&\textup{if }p\textup{ is odd}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Here,
\textup{env}J_{\alpha}
and
\textup{envAi}_{p}
are given by (
4.27
) and (
4.4
), respectively.

The coefficients
\beta_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
can then be computed systematically as follows.

Fix
t\in(0,1)
. We first describe the coefficients
\beta_{k}
in (
B.1
). Define

(B.3)

h(u):=\Big(\frac{\sinh z(u)}{u}\Big)^{-\alpha-1}\sqrt{\frac{2u}{z(u)}}\frac{dz}{du},

where
z=z(u)
is determined implicitly by the transformation

(B.4)

z-t\coth z=u-\frac{\xi(t)^{2}}{u}.

Next, define recursively

(B.5)

h_{0}(u):=h(u),\qquad h_{n+1}(u):=g_{n}^{\prime}(u)-\frac{\alpha+1}{u}g_{n}(u),

where

(B.6)

\begin{split}g_{2n}(u):=u^{2}\frac{h_{2n}(u)-h_{2n}(i\xi(t))}{u^{2}-(i\xi(t))^{2}},\qquad g_{2n+1}(u):=u\frac{uh_{2n+1}(u)-i\xi(t)h_{2n+1}(i\xi(t))}{u^{2}-(i\xi(t))^{2}}.\end{split}

Then the coefficients
\beta_{k}
are given by

(B.7)

\beta_{2n}=h_{2n}(i\xi(t)),\qquad\beta_{2n+1}=i\xi(t)h_{2n+1}(i\xi(t)).

Comparing (
B.1
) with (

), we obtain

(B.8)

E_{1}=-\frac{2\beta_{1}}{\xi\beta_{0}},\qquad E_{2}=\frac{4\beta_{2}}{\beta_{0}}.

4.3
(i).

Next, we describe the coefficients
\gamma_{k}
in (
B.2
). Define

(B.9)

\mathsf{h}(u):=\Big(1-\mathsf{z}(u)^{2}\Big)^{(\alpha-1)/2}\frac{d\mathsf{z}}{du},

where
\mathsf{z}=\mathsf{z}(u)
is determined by

(B.10)

\frac{1}{4}\log\Big(\frac{1+\mathsf{z}}{1-\mathsf{z}}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{z}t=\frac{u^{3}}{3}-\zeta(t)u.

We then define recursively

(B.11)

\mathsf{h}_{0}(u):=\mathsf{h}(u),\qquad\mathsf{h}_{n+1}(u):=\mathsf{g}_{n}^{\prime}(u),

where

(B.12)

\begin{split}\mathsf{g}_{2n}(u):=\frac{\mathsf{h}_{2n}(u)-\mathsf{h}_{2n}(\sqrt{\zeta(t)})}{u^{2}-\zeta(t)},\qquad\mathsf{g}_{2n+1}(u):=\frac{u}{u^{2}-\zeta(t)}\Big(\frac{\mathsf{h}_{2n+1}(u)}{u}-\frac{\mathsf{h}_{2n+1}(\sqrt{\zeta(t)})}{\sqrt{\zeta(t)}}\Big).\end{split}

The coefficients
\gamma_{k}

(B.13)

\gamma_{2n}=\mathsf{h}_{2n}(\zeta(t)^{1/2}),\qquad\gamma_{2n+1}=\mathsf{h}_{2n+1}(\zeta(t)^{1/2})/\zeta(t)^{1/2}.

Comparing (

(B.14)

F_{1}=-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{0}},\qquad F_{2}=\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{0}}.

A straightforward computation then gives the expressions in Lemma
4.3
(ii).
