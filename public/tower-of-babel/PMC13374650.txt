NUAK1 Inhibition Alleviates Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury via SYNE1-YAP1
Authors: Yangjinming Bai, Tingting Zhao, Qian Wang, Rui Zhang, Zhixing Wei, Yudong Fei, Xingxing Cai, Zhengyang Wu, Ji Yan, Yichao Zhang, Kaiyan Chen, Yuepeng Wang, Yi-Gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Mechanosensitive nuclear signaling contributes to myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, but the substrates and mechanisms of NUAK1 (AMPK-related kinase 5) remain unclear. We investigated whether NUAK1 regulates SYNE1 (Nesprin-1)/linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton-dependent nuclear gating of YAP1 (Yes-associated protein 1) during hypoxia/reoxygenation and ischemia-reperfusion injury.METHODS:We integrated quantitative phosphoproteomics, biochemical assays, phosphosite-mutant analyses, subcellular fractionation, atomic force microscopy, and genetic or pharmacological NUAK1 inhibition in neonatal mouse ventricular myocytes and mouse models of ischemia-reperfusion injury to define the NUAK1-SYNE1-YAP1 axis.RESULTS:Quantitative phosphoproteomics identified a conserved NUAK1-dependent phosphorylation site in Nesprin1-α2/SYNE1 (S434; S8284 in nesprin-1 giant), and biochemical assays supported direct SYNE1 phosphorylation by NUAK1. NUAK1 inhibition reduced apoptotic signaling and SYNE1 stability, enhanced YAP1 nuclear localization, and altered nuclear YAP1 dynamics. SYNE1 phosphosite-mutant and fractionation analyses indicated that NUAK1-SYNE1 restrains stress-induced YAP1 nuclear accumulation. Atomic force microscopy linked this pathway to nuclear mechanical remodeling. In mouse models of ischemia-reperfusion injury, NUAK1 inhibition reduced acute myocardial damage and improved remodeling indices.CONCLUSIONS:NUAK1-dependent SYNE1 phosphorylation shapes nuclear mechanosignaling during ischemic stress. NUAK1 downregulation promotes cardiomyocyte YAP1 nuclear activity and attenuates injury responses.

This study identifies a cardiomyocyte nuclear-envelope pathway through which NUAK1 controls SYNE1/LINC stability and YAP1-dependent stress adaptation during ischemia/reperfusion. Under basal conditions, NUAK1 phosphorylates SYNE1 and restrains HERC2-mediated ubiquitination, maintaining SYNE1 abundance and a stable LINC complex. During ischemia/reperfusion, NUAK1 downregulation or pharmacological inhibition promotes SYNE1 degradation, LINC destabilization, altered nuclear mechanics, and YAP1 nuclear accumulation. These events engage adaptive YAP1-dependent programs that reduce cardiomyocyte injury. The work provides a mechanistic link between kinase signaling, nuclear-cytoskeletal coupling, and cardioprotection, and suggests that acute, time-limited NUAK1 inhibition at reperfusion could augment an endogenous protective pathway while avoiding risks associated with sustained NUAK1 suppression.

Acute myocardial infarction remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Timely reperfusion by primary percutaneous coronary intervention effectively limits ischemic injury, delayed reperfusion and the residual myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, significantly affect long-term outcomes after acute myocardial infarction.1–3 Deepen the mechanistic understanding of ischemic adaptive processes in cardiomyocytes and the discovery of druggable nodes that can be leveraged to reduce acute cell death and improve postmyocardial infarction outcome remain clinically relevant.

Protein kinases represent a major class of druggable enzymes that coordinate cellular stress responses. NUAK1 (also known as AMPK-related kinase 5) and NUAK2 are members of the AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase)–related kinase family and are the known downstream of upstream master kinases such as LKB1 (liver kinase B1), linking important energetic stress signaling to cytoskeletal organization, cell survival, migration, and transcriptional control across physiological and pathological circumferences.4,5 In cancer and other noncardiac systems, NUAK1 signaling has been implicated in prosurvival and proinvasive programs and in the regulation of cellular architecture, hinting potential capacity to integrate biochemical stress with mechanical cues.4 However, direct mechanistic studies of NUAK1 in the myocardium remain limited. Notably, proteomic resources indicate that NUAK1 is robustly expressed in heart muscle, and its expression is much higher as compared with NUAK2, suggesting NUAK1 may represent the predominant functional NUAK family kinase in cardiomyocytes. These observations raise the possibility that NUAK1 might participate in cardiomyocyte stress adaptation during I/R; however, its cardiac substrates and downstream pathways during I/R have not been defined.

Mechanical information in cardiomyocytes is transmitted from the sarcomere and cytoskeleton to the nucleus through the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex, which couples cytoskeletal filaments to the nuclear envelope and lamina, thereby influencing nuclear positioning, chromatin organization, and mechanoresponsive gene expression.6 In the heart, LINC components are essential for nuclear-cytoskeletal coupling and tissue integrity, and perturbations of LINC proteins contribute to cardiomyopathy and conduction abnormalities.7 In particular, SYNE1 (Nesprin-1), a giant spectrin-repeat protein anchored at the outer nuclear membrane, serves as a key KASH (Klarsicht/ANC-1/Syne homology)-domain scaffold within the LINC complex and is highly relevant to striated muscle function.7 Human genetic and animal studies indicate that SYNE1/nesprin-1 disruption could lead to dilated cardiomyopathy, nuclear envelope defects, and impaired myogenesis.8,9 Yet, how LINC components are dynamically regulated under acute I/R stress—and whether kinase signaling can remodel LINC stability to alter cardiomyocyte fate—is not fully understood.

Another central mechanoresponsive transcriptional effector downstream of cytoskeletal and nuclear cues is YAP1 (Yes-associated protein 1), a transcriptional coactivator regulated by the Hippo pathway and by actin-dependent mechanotransduction.10,11 In many nonmuscle tissues, YAP/TAZ (transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif) activity is governed by cell-cell contact inhibition and substrate stiffness, reflecting the integration of junctional signaling with cytoskeletal tension.10,11 YAP signaling has been implicated in cardiomyocyte survival and repair programs, and augmenting YAP activity can enhance regenerative or protective responses in injured myocardium.12–14 These lines of evidence suggest that a cardiomyocyte-specific pathway linking nuclear-cytoskeletal coupling (LINC) to YAP1 may represent an actionable axis for limiting I/R injury—yet the molecular connectors for LINC stability and YAP1 activation during I/R are not fully defined.

Here, we identify NUAK1 as a stress-responsive kinase that remodels cardiomyocyte nuclear-cytoskeletal coupling during ischemic injury. NUAK1 phosphorylates SYNE1/nesprin-1 and regulates its ubiquitin-proteasome turnover, thereby shaping nuclear mechanics and YAP1 nucleocytoplasmic dynamics. Genetic or pharmacological suppression of NUAK1 destabilizes SYNE1, enhances YAP1 nuclear translocation and transcriptional output, and reduces cardiomyocyte death in vitro and myocardial injury in vivo.

Results

NUAK1 Is Primarily Expressed in Cardiomyocytes and Dynamically Downregulated in the Myocardium After Ischemia/Reperfusion Injury

To assess the relevance of NUAK1 in the human myocardium, we analyzed 2 independent human cardiac single-cell RNA-sequencing data sets.15,16 NUAK1 transcripts were predominantly enriched in cardiomyocytes in nondiseased hearts (Figure 1A and 1B). In an independent data set with regional annotation, cardiomyocyte NUAK1 was reduced in the ischemic region (Figure 1C through 1E). In the mouse I/R model, Nuak1 protein levels did not differ between sham-operated hearts and hearts harvested after 30 minutes of ischemia followed by 60 minutes reperfusion within the ischemic zone (IZ; Figure 1F and 1G), nor after 2 hours reperfusion within the IZ (Figure 1H and 1I). At 24 hours after I/R, Nuak1 was markedly reduced, with the lowest abundance in the IZ compared with the border zone and remote zone (Figure 1J and 1K). Consistent with this delayed downregulation, Nuak1 protein remained suppressed at day 1 after I/R but returned toward baseline by days 3 and 7 (Figure 1L and 1M). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed reduced Nuak1 signal in the IZ relative to the remote zone at 24 hours after I/R (Figure 1N and 1O). Thus, Nuak1 is dynamically and transiently downregulated after I/R injury.

We next tested whether hypoxia suppresses Nuak1 transcription or accelerates NUAK1 turnover in neonatal mouse ventricular myocytes (NMVMs). Hypoxia reduced Nuak1 mRNA by 4 hours (Figure 1R), whereas cycloheximide-chase assays showed no obvious acceleration of NUAK1 protein decay (Figure 1P and 1Q). Thus, hypoxia-associated NUAK1 downregulation in cultured NMVMs is initiated mainly at the transcript level.

Cardiomyocyte-Specific Nuak1 Deletion Attenuates Acute Myocardial Injury and Adverse Ventricular Remodeling After I/R

To test the functional role of cardiomyocyte NUAK1, we generated tamoxifen-inducible Nuak1 conditional knockout (cKO) mice and littermate floxed controls and subjected them to I/R with predefined acute and subacute end points (Figure 2A; Figure S1). NUAK1 was efficiently depleted in isolated cardiomyocytes, whereas the residual whole-heart signal remained consistent with nonmyocyte expression (Figure 2D and 2E).

At 24 hours after I/R, Nuak1 cKO mice showed smaller infarcts relative to the area at risk and fewer terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling-positive cardiomyocytes in the IZ (Figure 2B through 2G). By day 7, Nuak1 cKO mice had less fibrosis and improved echocardiographic indices, including higher left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) and favorable changes in left ventricular internal diameter at diastole (LVIDd) and left ventricular anterior wall thickness at diastole (LVAWd) (Figure 2H through 2K). Thus, cardiomyocyte NUAK1 deletion reduces I/R-induced cell death and remodeling.

Function-First Triage and Integrated Phosphoproteomics Prioritize SYNE1 as a Direct NUAK1 Substrate

We used phosphoproteomics and function-first triage to identify NUAK1 effectors whose loss could explain the context-dependent protection seen after NUAK1 suppression (Figure S2).

Anti-Flag immunoprecipitation/liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and phosphoproteomics identified a limited set of NUAK1-responsive candidates, including NCL (nucleolin), USP9X (ubiquitin-specific peptidase 9 X-linked), TXLNB (taxilin beta), SRRM2 (serine/arginine repetitive matrix protein 2), and SYNE1 (spectrin repeat-containing nuclear envelope protein 1) (Figure 3A). First-pass siRNA triage prioritized NCL and SYNE1, but SYNE1 best matched the nuclear-envelope phenotype and tractable phosphosite mapping (Figure S3A through S3D).

Ultrastructural analysis of Nuak1 cKO myocardium showed nuclear-envelope wrinkling, consistent with altered nuclear-cytoskeletal coupling (Figure 3B). The NUAK1-regulated SYNE1 phosphosite mapped to S8284 in nesprin-1 giant, corresponding to S434 in striated muscle-enriched SYNE1α2; we also depicts the NUAK1 T-loop and the GILK motif-derived peptide (GILK peptide) used for NUAK1 inhibition alongside HTH-01-015 (Figure 3C and 3D). Coimmunoprecipitation confirmed a NUAK1-SYNE1 association, and a phospho-specific pSYNE1 S434 antibody detected phosphorylation of wild-type SYNE1 but not the S434A mutant (Figure 3E and 3F). This phosphorylation was reduced by GILK and HTH-01-015 (HTH) and was reproduced with recombinant proteins in vitro (Figure 3H through 3K).

Additional data supported the SYNE1 branch: NCL showed weak, nonreciprocal coimmunoprecipitation, whereas NUAK1-dependent SYNE1 phosphorylation persisted across perturbations and required intact NUAK1 catalytic activity (Figure S3E through S3G).

NUAK1-Dependent Phosphorylation Stabilizes SYNE1 and Influences H/R-Induced Cardiomyocyte Survival

We examined how the NUAK1-SYNE1 axis affects hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) injury and SYNE1 behavior. HTH produced a dose-dependent trend toward lower H/R-induced c-Casp3, and 10 μM was selected for mechanistic studies. Orthogonal NUAK1 suppression by siNuak1 or GILK also reduced c-Casp3 during H/R (Figure 4A through 4D). Under the same conditions, NUAK1 and SYNE1 protein levels declined (Figure 4C, 4I, and 4J), linking stress-induced NUAK1 loss with SYNE1 reduction.

We then tested whether the candidate phosphorylation sites directly encoded the apoptotic phenotype. Re-expression of SYNE1α2 wild type (WT), S434A, or S434D in siNuak1-treated NMVMs directionally attenuated the protective phenotype but did not yield a clear site-specific separation (Figure 4E and 4F). Similar rescue experiments with NCL or SYNE1 variants were also inconclusive (Figure S3H), suggesting that phosphorylation may primarily regulate protein state or stability rather than an immediate binary functional switch.

Consistent with this interpretation, chronic and acute NUAK1 perturbations selectively altered SYNE1 protein state. HTH, GILK, or the S434A construct reduced the remaining SYNE1 end point in CHX-chase assays, supporting a stability mechanism rather than an immediate binary rescue phenotype (Figure 4K through 4P).

To define the degradation route for SYNE1, we compared lysosomal and proteasomal blockade in NMVMs expressing Nesprin1-α2 WT or S434A. MG132, but not chloroquine, markedly increased SYNE1 S434A abundance (Figure 5A), supporting a proteasome-dominant degradation pathway.

We next tested whether acute loss of NUAK1-dependent phosphorylation promotes SYNE1 ubiquitination. Under MG132+CHX turnover-clamp conditions, GILK reduced pSYNE1 S434 and increased K48-linked ubiquitination of SYNE1, while partially weakening the NUAK1-SYNE1 association in the pull-down (Figure 5B). HTH similarly decreased pSYNE1 S434 and increased K48-linked ubiquitination without disrupting endogenous NUAK1-SYNE1 coimmunoprecipitation (Figure 5C), indicating that ubiquitination tracks most closely with loss of phosphorylation.

To identify the ubiquitin machinery controlling SYNE1 turnover, we profiled the SYNE1 interactome. TRIM25 (Tripartite motif-containing protein 25) and HERC2 (HECT and RLD domain-containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2) emerged among the shared candidates (Figure 5D). In an siRNA prioritization screen, Herc2 knockdown reproducibly rescued siNuak1-induced SYNE1 loss, whereas Trim25 knockdown had minimal effect (Figure 5E and 5F).

Finally, we tested HERC2 under H/R stress. siNuak1 reduced c-Casp3, whereas co-silencing Herc2 abrogated this protection and restored SYNE1 protein (Figure 5G and 5H). Herc2 knockdown also attenuated siNuak1-induced K48-linked ubiquitination of SYNE1 (Figure 5I). Thus, HERC2 mediates SYNE1 ubiquitination and degradation downstream of NUAK1 loss.

SYNE1 Downregulation Promotes YAP1 Nuclear Localization and Remodels Nuclear Mechanics in Cardiomyocytes

Because SYNE1 links the cytoskeleton to the nucleus through the LINC complex, we tested whether the NUAK1-SYNE1 axis controls mechanosensitive transcription. Nuak1 cKO hearts showed increased nuclear YAP1 in cTnT (cardiac troponin T)-positive cardiomyocytes (Figure 6A and 6B). In NMVMs, XMU-MP-1 increased YAP1 nuclear accumulation, whereas SYNE1 S434A or S434D expression blunted this response (Figure 6C; Figure S4A). Consistently, siNuak1, siSyne1, and YAP1 overexpression increased the YAP/TEAD target Ctgf (Figure 6D).

RNA-seq showed coordinated Hippo/YAP changes after NUAK1 gain- or loss-of-function. Fractionation, import/export inhibitor experiments, and cross-cell testing supported SYNE1-dependent YAP1 compartmentalization and a muscle-biased response (Figure S4B through S4L).

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching further showed that H/R increased nuclear YAP1 mobility, and siNuak1 or siSyne1 increased the recovery area under the curve (AUC) and early recovery rate (Figure 6K through 6M; Figure S5G). Importazole and leptomycin B experiments supported altered YAP1 nuclear transport balance (Figure S4D).

We measured nuclear mechanics by atomic force microscopy indentation. Force-distance curves were fit with a Hertz model to derive Young modulus (Figure 6N). Compared with H/R+siCtl, siNuak1 or siSyne1 significantly altered nuclear stiffness under H/R (Figure 6O). Together, the fluorescence recovery after photobleaching and atomic force microscopy data indicate that the NUAK1-SYNE1 axis modulates both nuclear YAP1 dynamics and nuclear mechanics during H/R.

YAP1 Is Required for the Cytoprotective Effects of NUAK1 Inhibition, and Cardiomyocyte-Targeted SYNE1 Knockdown Is Sufficient to Confer In Vivo Protection

To test whether YAP1 mediates NUAK1-inhibition-dependent protection, we paired NUAK1 suppression with pharmacological or genetic YAP1 blockade. HTH reduced c-Casp3, whereas K-975 attenuated this benefit (Figure 7A and 7B). Likewise, siYap1 blunted the protection conferred by siNuak1 (Figure 7C and 7D), indicating that YAP1 activity is required for the antiapoptotic effect of NUAK1 inhibition.

We next tested whether the NUAK1-SYNE1-YAP1 relationship persists in vivo. Cardiomyocytes isolated from Nuak1 cKO hearts showed increased nuclear YAP1 and reduced SYNE1 (Figure 7E and 7F). Cardiomyocyte-targeted adeno-associated virus (AAV)2/9-cTnT-shSyne1 similarly reduced SYNE1 and increased nuclear YAP1 compared with AAV2/9-cTnT-shCtl (Figure 7G and 7H).

To determine whether SYNE1 lowering improves I/R outcomes, we delivered AAV2/9-cTnT-shSyne1 or control AAV before I/R (Figure 7I). At 24 hours, shSyne1 mice had smaller infarcts without a significant change in the area at risk/left ventricle (LV) (Figure 7J and 7K). At day 7, shSyne1 mice showed less fibrosis and improved echocardiographic parameters, including higher LVEF and LVFS, with favorable LVIDd and LVAWd changes (Figure 7L through 7O). Thus, cardiomyocyte SYNE1 knockdown is sufficient to recapitulate the in vivo protective phenotype.

Pharmacological NUAK1 Inhibition With HTH Protects the Heart Against I/R Injury In Vivo

To evaluate the translational potential, we administered HTH at reperfusion in C57BL/6J mice subjected to I/R (Figure 8A). At 24 hours, HTH reduced infarct size relative to the area at risk without an overt change in the area at risk/LV (Figure 8B and 8C). It also reduced terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling-positive cardiomyocytes in the ischemic myocardium (Figure 8D and 8E).

At 7 days after I/R, HTH-treated hearts showed less fibrosis and improved echocardiographic indices (Figure 8F through 8I). Context testing showed benefit under hypoxia but liability under normoxia, supporting a time-limited therapeutic window (Figure S2A and S2B).

We also followed tamoxifen-induced Nuak1 cKO mice long term. Some developed dilated cardiomyopathy-like echocardiographic changes at late time points (≥10 weeks), with cardiomyocyte loss, fibrosis, and abnormal nuclear morphology (Figure S2C and S2D). Thus, acute NUAK1 inhibition may be beneficial after ischemic injury, whereas prolonged suppression may be deleterious under nonhypoxic conditions.

Discussion

This study identifies a nuclear-envelope-centered kinase-to-mechanotransduction pathway in cardiomyocyte injury. NUAK1 downregulation or inhibition limits acute I/R injury, reduces SYNE1 stability through K48-linked ubiquitination, and promotes YAP1 nuclear accumulation, positioning SYNE1/LINC as a gate upstream of YAP1.

NUAK1 and NUAK2 are understudied AMPK-related kinases linked to stress adaptation, cell survival, and Hippo-YAP/TAZ signaling, especially in cancer and proliferative settings.17–19 However, cardiomyocytes are mechanically specialized and largely nonproliferative; sustained NUAK1 signaling may therefore constrain injury-responsive transcription.4 In this framework, rapid NUAK1 loss after I/R may release a nuclear-envelope brake and favor repair over cell death. This context dependency is consistent with broader NUAK biology summarized in recent reviews and underscores that therapeutic NUAK1 inhibition may exploit an acute disease-state vulnerability.

A key mechanistic feature is that the NUAK1-regulated phosphosite resides within the SYNE1 adaptive domain. The adaptive domain is a highly conserved, centrally positioned module that interrupts spectrin-like repeat regions of nesprins and has been shown to increase helicity, thermal stability, and cooperativity of folding in larger spectrin-like repeat-containing fragments, while also strengthening dimerization and inhibiting unfolding—properties consistent with a structure that tunes nesprin mechanics under load.20 These observations suggest that adaptive domain phosphorylation can affect nesprin stability, interactions, and force transmission. In parallel, the connection we observe between SYNE1 regulation and ubiquitin-proteasome-related readouts is consistent with the broader principle that E3 ligases can control the functional state of large protein assemblies by modulating oligomerization/complex behavior (as exemplified by HERC2 control of p53 oligomerization).21 Together, these data position adaptive-domain phosphorylation as a switch linking kinase signaling to LINC plasticity.

Our data support a model in which SYNE1-containing LINC architecture acts as a cardiomyocyte nuclear-envelope gate limiting YAP1 nuclear access or residency. Importantly, this behavior is likely cell-type dependent: in contractile cells, SYNE1/LINC may not only transmit force but also set mechanical and transport thresholds for transcriptional access. We probed this cell-type dependence by perturbing NUAK1/SYNE1 across striated and nonstriated cell types. In cardiomyocytes, NUAK1 or SYNE1 silencing increased YAP1 nuclear partitioning and CTGF expression (Figure 6D). C2C12 myogenic cells showed similar YAP1 and YAP/TEAD responses (Figure S4E and S4F). In contrast, analogous experiments in nonstriated muscle cell lines (human embryonic kidney 293T [HEK-293T], National Institutes of Health 3T3 [NIH-3T3], and Michigan Cancer Foundation-7 [MCF-7]) showed attenuated or divergent YAP1 and target-gene responses (Figure S4G through S4L). Together, these comparisons support muscle-biased wiring of NUAK1-SYNE1 to Hippo/YAP output. This differs from canonical nonmuscle models in which LINC-mediated nucleo-cytoskeletal coupling is required for force-driven YAP nuclear entry.22,23 Such force-dependent nuclear-pore gating can promote YAP import, with nuclear-cytoskeletal coupling increasing YAP nuclear import in stiff environments.23 Other nonmuscle studies similarly link nuclear coupling to YAP entry.22–26

However, muscle and nuclear-envelope disease models show a complementary pattern: LINC disruption can enhance YAP nuclear accumulation, consistent with loss of a restraining gate. In LMNA-related congenital muscular dystrophy models, Owens et al27 showed that LMNA mutations drive persistent YAP nuclear localization and similar YAP deregulation in a nesprin-1 ΔKASH congenital myopathy. In parallel, Bertrand et al28 demonstrated that LMNA-mutated muscle precursors show defective mechanosensing and elevated YAP signaling, with partial rescue after YAP knockdown. Extending to the heart, human failing myocardium shows YAP-pathway activation with Hippo-target induction, and similarly observed activation in a mouse desmin-related cardiomyopathy model.29 Consistent with these observations, reviews emphasize that nuclear-envelope defects disproportionately affect cardiac and skeletal muscle, where nuclear mechanics and transport are specialized.30 Together, these data support a context-dependent LINC-YAP model. In many nonmuscle cells, LINC acts as a force conduit permissive for nuclear-pore-mediated YAP import.23 In contrast, in cardiomyocytes (and possibly striated muscle), where nuclei experience chronic organized contractile load, SYNE1/LINC may buffer nuclear mechanics and gate YAP1 access. When SYNE1/LINC is disrupted, this constraint is released and the nuclear transport balance shifts, allowing YAP1 to accumulate in the nucleus. This muscle-biased gate model explains the opposite YAP outcomes across cell types. Prior studies in LMNA-related cardiomyopathy have shown that LINC disruption can be cardioprotective, reducing fibrosis and nuclear damage. Recent work suggests that LINC disruption may also relieve pathological cytoskeletal stress, including reduced perinuclear microtubule compression. Thus, YAP1 gating is 1 protective output of NUAK1-SYNE1 remodeling after I/R, not the sole consequence of LINC perturbation.31,32

YAP1 nuclear accumulation is not merely correlative but functionally required for protection. In H/R cardiomyocytes, HTH protection was attenuated by K-975, a TEAD inhibitor.33 Similarly, siYap1 blunted siNuak1-mediated protection. Together, these experiments place YAP1 downstream of NUAK1 suppression and support YAP/TEAD-dependent protection. This aligns with evidence that cardiac YAP activation improves postmyocardial infarction outcomes.13,14 Mechanistically, the NUAK1-SYNE1 axis also altered YAP1 nuclear behavior. Under H/R stress, NUAK1 or SYNE1 knockdown increased nuclear YAP1 mobility by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching and concomitantly remodeled nuclear mechanical properties by atomic force microscopy indentation, consistent with a nuclear state that is more permissive for YAP1 redistribution and transcriptional engagement. Although nuclear-pore import regulates YAP entry in nonmuscle systems,23 our data suggest that nuclear-envelope scaffolding tunes YAP1 nuclear access through mechanics and transport balance, thereby setting the ischemic YAP transcriptional threshold. Importantly, this pathway is preserved in vivo and is sufficient to confer post-I/R benefit. Nuak1 cKO and AAV-shSyne1 cardiomyocytes showed increased nuclear YAP1 and reduced SYNE1. Moreover, AAV-cTnT-shSyne1 reduced post-I/R fibrosis and improved echocardiographic parameters, supporting SYNE1 reduction as a sufficient protective lever. Together, these findings link NUAK1-dependent SYNE1 proteostasis to YAP1-dependent transcription and cardioprotection.

NUAK1 is unlikely to act as a simple linear injury kinase in vivo. In noncardiac stress-adapted contexts, NUAK1/ARK5 can be prosurvival.34,35 Mouse genetics also indicate that germline Nuak1 or Syne1 loss causes early lethality, indicating that the NUAK1-SYNE1 axis is not dispensable. In cardiomyocytes, NUAK1 suppression is highly context-dependent. After I/R, NUAK1 is transiently and regionally downregulated, with recovery during the first week, suggesting an acute rather than chronic suppression window. In vitro, NUAK1 inhibition protects during H/R but contributes to basal homeostasis outside hypoxic stress. Under normoxia, HTH increased apoptosis and ROS, and NUAK1 re-expression mitigated the proapoptotic effect, whereas prolonged cardiomyocyte Nuak1 deletion produced late detrimental remodeling in some animals. This cell type- and stage-dependent view may also help reconcile our findings with the recent report by Li et al showing that USP9X-mediated activation of ARK5/NUAK1 promotes myofibroblast activation and fibrotic remodeling after myocardial infarction. Here, we define an acute cardiomyocyte NUAK1-SYNE1-YAP1 pathway centered on nuclear-envelope proteostasis. In contrast, postmyocardial infarction USP9X-ARK5/NUAK1 signaling likely operates in fibroblast-rich remodeling contexts. Thus, NUAK1 attenuation may be beneficial in distinct cardiac compartments and windows. These data reinforce that NUAK1/ARK5 is cell type- and stage-dependent, not uniformly deleterious or beneficial. Thus, acute ischemic NUAK1 suppression may be beneficial whereas sustained normoxic inhibition may be maladaptive, making timing and injury state central to translation. Finally, additional regulatory layers remain to be defined. First, we observed that an Lkb1-regulation impaired mutant, NUAK1 T212A, still promoted SYNE1 S434 phosphorylation in our assays, indicating that SYNE1 phosphorylation can persist despite perturbation of a canonical regulatory site and pointing to potential compensatory regulation or residual catalytic output in specific cellular settings.30,36 Our data are in line with recent findings that there were LKB1-dependent phosphorylation of T211, the major mechanism for NUAK1 activation, but also LKB1-independent mechanisms of NUAK1 activity, particularly under conditions of cellular stress.4 Thus, LKB1-independent NUAK1 activity may contribute to SYNE1 phosphorylation under stress. Second, although MYPT1 S445 is widely used as a canonical NUAK1 substrate/readout,37,38 in our stress paradigm the protective phenotype did not track with an overt suppression of this pathway readout (Figure S2A). Consistent with this, our tandem mass tag-based phosphoproteomic analysis did not reveal a significant change in phosphorylation at MYPT1 S445 under the experimental conditions studied. Moreover, several previously reported NUAK1-associated phosphosites (eg, MYPT1 S472/S910 and LATS1 S464) were not confidently detected/quantified in our data set, likely reflecting limited site coverage and ion-sampling constraints inherent to discovery phosphoproteomics. Accordingly, we prioritized phenotype-linked readouts: SYNE1 proteostasis and YAP1 nuclear behavior. This interpretation is conceptually consistent with reports that MYPT1 S445 phosphorylation can be only partially dependent on NUAK1, implying potential contributions from other kinases and context-specific substrate selection.36 Taken together, these observations reinforce that NUAK1 signaling in cardiomyocytes is nonlinear and state-dependent, and that deepened understanding on the upstream regulators and alternative downstream effectors of NUAK1 in hypoxic myocardium will be important for refining mechanism and therapeutic positioning.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, although we mapped a NUAK1-regulated phosphosite on nesprin-1α2 and established a phosphorylation-dependent control of SYNE1 proteostasis, SYNE1 is expressed as multiple large isoforms with distinct domain architectures and subcellular interactions in striated muscle. Determining how the identified phospho-switch relates to other SYNE1 isoforms (including the giant nesprin-1 variants) and whether isoform-specific expression patterns tune LINC signaling in different cardiac regions or disease states will be important for generalizing the mechanism.39 Second, although our data support a ubiquitin-proteasome route involving K48-linked ubiquitination and implicate a HERC2 axis in controlling SYNE1 turnover, the precise ubiquitin ligase machinery, recognition determinants (degrons), and the structural basis by which phosphorylation within the conserved adaptive domain modulates SYNE1 stability remain to be defined.20,21 Addressing these questions will likely require domain-resolved interactomics, structure-informed mutagenesis, and reconstitution approaches to distinguish direct phosphorylation-dependent recruitment/avoidance of ubiquitin ligase complexes from indirect effects mediated by force-dependent conformational exposure.

Third, our study establishes YAP1/TEAD activity as a required downstream effector for the protective phenotype, yet the full spectrum of YAP1-dependent transcriptional programs engaged by NUAK1-SYNE1 remodeling in adult cardiomyocytes remains incompletely resolved, meanwhile, the transcription factors that respond to the corresponding mechanical forces within the cells are not limited to YAP1, other factors such as MRTF are also involved.33,39 Integrating genome-wide transcriptional profiling with chromatin binding assays (eg, TEAD/YAP occupancy) across relevant time points after I/R will help define which components of the YAP response are adaptive versus potentially maladaptive. Fourth, our SYNE1-as-gate model highlights a muscle-biased logic for LINC-YAP regulation that differs from canonical nonmuscle paradigms of force-driven nuclear-pore gating.23 Notably, our preliminary cross-cell comparisons (Figure S3E through S3L) are consistent with such context dependence; however, these experiments were not performed under fully matched mechanical environments, and future work will be needed to disentangle baseline load, cytoskeletal anchorage, and nuclear transport parameters as determinants of the apparent inversion in LINC-YAP behavior. A direct test of this cell-type dependence—comparing cardiomyocytes, skeletal myogenic cells, and nonmuscle cells under matched mechanical regimes—will be essential to delineate which features of striated muscle nuclear coupling (baseline contractile load, anisotropic cytoskeletal anchorage, and nuclear positioning constraints) confer the apparent inversion in LINC-YAP behavior observed across muscle disease models.27–30

Finally, the translational implications are constrained by the pleiotropic roles of NUAK1. Our findings indicate that NUAK1 inhibition can be protective under hypoxic stress but may be deleterious under normoxic baseline conditions, implying a narrow therapeutic window.34,37,38 An additional limitation of the present study is that we did not systematically quantify reperfusion-associated ventricular arrhythmias. Because the early reperfusion phase is well known to be accompanied by abrupt calcium loading and electrical instability, future studies will be needed to determine whether modulation of the NUAK1-SYNE1 axis also influences arrhythmia burden, conduction heterogeneity, or electrical safety in the immediate postreperfusion period. Defining these electrophysiological consequences will be important for fully evaluating the translational potential of acute NUAK1 inhibition. Future work should therefore emphasize timing- and dose-controlled regimens, cardiac-targeted delivery strategies, and clinically relevant comorbidity models to establish whether short-course NUAK1 suppression can be harnessed safely during reperfusion without incurring longer-term liabilities.

Conclusions

In summary, NUAK1 controls a nuclear-envelope proteostasis checkpoint linking SYNE1 stability to cardiomyocyte mechanotransduction. Acute NUAK1 suppression releases this gate, promotes YAP/TEAD-dependent cardioprotection, and reduces I/R injury, whereas sustained inhibition may compromise baseline homeostasis.
