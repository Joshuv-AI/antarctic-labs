Dietary protein, major dietary protein sources, and chronic joint diseases risk
Authors: Yiqian Zhou, Chunlei He, Yihao Fan, Yun Hong, Shuhui Chen, Ding Ye, Xiaohui Sun, Jing Guo, Jiayu Li, Yingying Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesEvidence on dietary protein and chronic joint diseases remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the associations between protein intake and the risk of chronic joint diseases, and to evaluate the mediating roles of inflammation.MethodsDietary protein intake was assessed using 24-hour dietary recall. The primary outcomes, including incident OA, RA, gout, and SpA, were ascertained from UK hospital inpatient records. Cox proportional hazards models estimated associations between protein intake and outcomes. Mediation analysis was conducted using the CMAverse R package.ResultsDuring an average follow-up of 12.19–13.18 years, a total of 23,298 incident cases of OA, 2,070 of RA, 2,934 of gout, and 728 of SpA were documented. Higher total protein intake was associated with a reduced risk of gout (Q4 vs. Q1: HR:0.87, 95%CI 0.79–0.97), vegetable protein intake with lower risks of OA (0.89, 0.86–0.93) and RA (0.76, 0.67–0.87), whereas animal protein intake was related to an increased risk of OA (1.07, 1.03–1.12). Regarding protein-rich foods, whole grains, legumes and cheese intake were associated with lower OA risk. Whole grains, seafood, cheese, and yogurt were negatively associated with RA risk. And red meat was associated with increased RA risk. C-reactive protein was identified as a significant mediator for associations of vegetable and animal protein with OA risk, and of vegetable protein with RA risk.ConclusionHigher total protein intake was linked to a lower risk of gout. Vegetable protein intake was associated with reduced risks of OA and RA, whereas animal protein intake was associated with an increased risk of OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01140-1.

Background

Chronic joint diseases, including osteoarthritis (OA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), gout, and spondyloarthritis (SpA), are typical rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases (RMDs) that represent painful and disabling conditions imposing a substantial and increasing global burden [1–3]. OA alone is estimated to impact over 595 million people globally, ranking as a leading cause of disability, particularly in the elderly [1, 4]. Similarly, RA, SpA, and gout contribute markedly to years lived with disability (YLDs) and reduce quality of life for millions more [4–6]. Thus, identifying modifiable risk factors is essential to the prevention of chronic joint diseases.

Diet plays a crucial role in the development and primary prevention of chronic joint diseases [7]. As a fundamental macronutrient, protein is essential for human physiological function. However, current evidence linking protein intake to chronic joint diseases remains limited and inconsistent. For instance, two cross-sectional studies have suggested that both lower and higher levels of total protein intake may be associated with a higher prevalence of OA [8, 9]. Similarly, for gout, a prospective study among 47,150 men reported total protein consumption as a potential risk factor [10]. In the case of RA, only one cohort study among 82,063 women in the United States found no significant association between total protein intake and RA [11]. Regarding SpA, the available evidence is scarce [7].

Proteins are primarily categorized into plant-based and animal-based types, each with diverse food sources and distinct biological effects on the human body. Regarding vegetable protein, previous studies have reported no significant association with RA risk [11], but it may exert a small protective effect on gout risk [10]. Furthermore, specific plant protein sources like whole grains and legumes have been linked to a reduced risk of knee OA and gout, respectively [12, 13]. As for animal protein, prospective cohort studies have found no link with the risk of RA or gout. However, analyses of specific animal-derived foods reveal more nuanced relationships: positive associations have been observed between meat and seafood intake and gout [10], between beef intake and RA [14], and between processed meat intake and reactive arthritis (ReA) [14]. In contrast, dairy products have been inversely associated with OA and gout risk [10, 12]. Despite these insights, evidence for a comprehensive assessment of the relationships between plant and animal protein intake—including their specific dietary sources—and the risk of chronic joint diseases remains limited or lacking.

The role of diet in modulating inflammation is well-established [15], and anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, such as vegetarian and Mediterranean diets, have garnered significant research interest. Studies have shown that these diets, along with low-calorie regimens, can effectively reduce levels of inflammatory markers, whereas diets high in sugar, saturated fat, and cholesterol can increase them [14, 16]. Given that inflammation is a shared pathogenic mechanism across chronic joint diseases [17], it is likely a key mediator in the relationship between diet and these conditions. Although dietary protein has been associated with changes in various inflammatory markers (such as C-reactive protein, TNF-α and MCP-1) [18–21], whether and to what degree these changes mediate the influence of dietary protein on chronic joint diseases remains to be systematically explored.

Based on this background, our study had two primary objectives: first, to examine the associations between the intake of total protein, plant and animal protein, along with their major food sources, and the risk of chronic joint diseases; and second, to investigate the potential mediating effects of inflammatory biomarkers in these observed relationships.

Method

Study participants

The UKB is a large-scale, population-based prospective cohort that recruited over 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 years during 2006–2010. Recruitment was conducted via invitations mailed through the National Health Service (NHS) registry, and participants attended one of 22 assessment centers distributed across England, Scotland, and Wales. At enrolment, comprehensive baseline information was obtained using self-administered touchscreen questionnaires covering demographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, and dietary intake [22]. In addition, the UKB conducted physical examinations, including blood pressure measurement, carotid ultrasound, arterial stiffness assessment, hearing test, hand grip strength measurement, anthropometry, bone densitometry of the heel, spirometry, 12-lead resting electrocardiogram (ECG), and exercise ECG, and others. Biological samples (blood and urine) were collected, and various assays were performed, including blood count, blood biochemistry, infectious disease testing, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics, proteomics, genome-wide association study (GWAS), and urine biochemistry. Ongoing follow-up of the cohort is achieved through systematic linkage with nationwide health-related databases, such as hospital admission records, primary care data, and death registries. The UKB received approval from the North-West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 21/NW/0157), and written informed consent was secured from all participants prior to participation.

We conducted a prospective cohort study based on the UKB. Figure 1 illustrates the overall study design. As illustrated in Figure S1, among 210,947 participants who completed at least one 24-hour dietary recall assessment, we applied the following exclusion criteria: withdrawal of consent (n = 105), extreme total energy intake (males: <3,347 or >17,573 kJ/d; females: <2,092 or >14,644 kJ/d) [23] (n = 2,529), missing covariate data (n = 34,797), and pre-existing diagnoses of target chronic joint diseases (OA, n = 5,778; RA, n = 601; gout, n = 313; SpA, n = 236) at or before baseline. After these exclusions, the final analytical samples for the main analyses consisted of 167,738 participants for OA, 172,915 for RA, 173,203 for gout, and 173,280 for SpA. For mediation analyses, we further excluded participants with missing biomarker data. The number of exclusions varied by biomarker (OA: n = 6,000–9,595; RA: n = 6,199–9,884), resulting in final subsample sizes of 158,143–161,738 for OA and 163,031–166,716 for RA.

Measurement of dietary protein intake and protein-rich foods

The Oxford WebQ is a self-administered, web-based 24-hour dietary questionnaire developed for large-scale studies. It records intake of over 200 foods and 30 beverages by asking participants to indicate consumption within food groups and subsequently specify the exact type and quantity using standardized portion sizes [24, 25].

Nutrient intakes were automatically derived by linking reported quantities to food composition values, originally from McCance and Widdowson’s tables and subsequently updated to the UK Nutrient Databank. Validation studies have shown moderate-to-strong agreement with interviewer-administered recalls and biomarker assessments, supporting its utility for nutritional epidemiology [26, 27].

For the analysis of protein-rich foods, we focused on animal-based and plant-based protein sources. A total of 25 individual protein-rich food items from the UK Biobank dietary dataset were categorized into 8 animal protein food groups and 3 plant protein food groups, following previously established methods [20].

Dietary data in the UKB were collected through five surveys conducted between 2009 and 2012. The first survey was administered at an assessment centre, and the subsequent four were conducted online. For participants with only one dietary record, dietary intake was based on that single measurement. For those with two or more records, the average intake across all available measurements was used.

Ascertainment of chronic joint diseases

The primary outcomes of this study were four chronic joint diseases: OA, RA, gout, and SpA. These conditions were identified from UK hospital inpatient records using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. Specifically, OA was defined by codes M15–M19 [28]; RA by M05–M06 [29]; and gout by M10 [30]. SpA was defined using a combination of codes covering its heterogeneous subtypes [31, 32], including ankylosing spondylitis (M45) [33], psoriatic arthritis (M07.0–M07.3) [34], reactive arthritis (M02) [35], enteropathic arthritis (M07.4–M07.6), and juvenile idiopathic arthritis (M08–M09) [34]. For each disease, the earliest recorded diagnosis date in the hospital records was used to determine the incidence date.

Measurement of covariates

Based on previous studies [3, 36–38], we considered covariates that may influence the association between dietary protein and chronic joint diseases to control for potential confounding. These included age, sex (Male / female), ethnicity (White / other), education (High / medium / others), household income (<£18, 000 / £18, 000–£30, 999 / £31, 000–£51, 999 / £52, 000–£100, 000 / >£100, 000), body mass index (BMI), smoking status (Never / previous / current), alcohol drinking status (Never / previous / current), physical activity (Yes / no), energy-adjusted fat intake, and energy-adjusted carbohydrate intake and total energy intake [39–42].

Baseline data on sociodemographic and lifestyle factors were obtained through self-administered touch-screen questionnaires [43, 44]. Fat, carbohydrate and total energy intake, like protein intake, were derived from 24-hour dietary recall measurements. Physical activity was defined as participants having regular physical activity, reporting > 150 min of moderate activity per week, > 75 min of vigorous activity per week, or an equivalent combination of moderate-intensity or vigorous-intensity physical activity [45].

Measurement of mediators

Based on these six inflammatory biomarkers’ established roles in systemic inflammation [46] and their availability in the UKB, we selected them for the mediation analysis to assess the inflammatory pathways potentially linking dietary protein intake with chronic joint diseases. These biomarkers included lymphocyte count (10^9 cells/Liter), monocyte count (10^9 cells/Liter), neutrophil count (10^9 cells/Liter), platelet count (10^9 cells/Liter), leukocyte count (10^9 cells/Liter), and C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L). These biomarkers were measured from blood samples collected at baseline and processed according to standard protocols [47]. Lymphocyte, monocyte, neutrophil, platelet, and leukocyte counts were determined using automated hematology analyzers, while CRP levels were quantified by high-sensitivity immunoassays.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). Cox proportional hazards models were employed to evaluate the associations between protein intake (or protein-rich foods) and the risk of chronic joint diseases. Variables were analyzed as either categorical or continuous measures. Results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For the analysis of protein intake, the residual method was applied to adjust for total energy intake [48], thereby deriving energy-adjusted protein intake values. Energy-adjusted protein intake was categorized into quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group. Linear trends across quartiles were assessed by treating them as continuous variables, and associations demonstrating a significant FDR-corrected P-value for trend (FDR-P for trend < 0.05) were selected for further analysis. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, while Model 2 included additional adjustments for ethnicity, education level, household income, BMI, smoking status, alcohol drinking status, physical activity, total energy intake, energy-adjusted fat intake, and energy-adjusted carbohydrate intake. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings, including: (1) excluding participants diagnosed with chronic joint diseases within the first two years of follow-up; (2) restricting the analyses to participants with fairly typical diets during the 24-h dietary assessments; (3) further adjusting for prevalent diabetes (M10–M14) [49], prevalent hypertension (I10–I15) [50], and dyslipidemia (E78) [51]; (4) rerunning the analyses after multiple imputations for missing covariates. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was employed to investigate the potential dose–response relationships between protein intake and risks of chronic joint diseases. RCS curves were fitted with the number of knots selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC); the optimal number of knots was determined as the one with the lowest AIC value.

For the analysis of protein-rich foods, intake values were log10(x + 1) transformed to normalize their distributions. Given the substantial proportion of non-consumers, we categorized each food variable into four groups: non-consumers were assigned to a separate reference group (Group 1, the lowest intake), while consumers were divided into tertiles (Groups 2–4), as described in prior work [38]. Linear trends were assessed using the same method as applied in the energy-adjusted protein intake analysis, with an FDR-P for trend < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The covariates included in these models were similar to those used for protein intake analyses.

Mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential mediating role of inflammatory biomarkers in the relationship between dietary protein intake and chronic joint diseases. First, we estimated the total effect of protein intake on disease risk using Cox proportional hazards models within the subpopulation with available biomarker data; only statistically significant associations were advanced for further analysis. We then assessed the exposure-mediator relationship (Path a) using linear regression models and the mediator-outcome relationship (Path b) using Cox proportional hazards models. Biomarkers that showed significant associations in both pathways were included in a formal causal mediation analysis performed with the CMAverse R package to estimate the natural indirect effect (NIE) and the proportion mediated.

Result

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. During an average follow-up of 12.19 to 13.18 years, 23,298 incident cases of OA, 2,070 of RA, 2,934 of gout, and 728 of SpA were identified. Compared to the non-case group, participants who developed OA were generally older, while those with gout were older, had a higher prevalence of obesity, and included a higher proportion of males (all SMDs > 0.50). In contrast, for RA and SpA, the baseline characteristics were more comparable between cases and non-cases (all SMDs < 0.50).

The distribution of total energy and dietary protein intake among the participants is presented in Table S1. Compared with non-cases, no significant differences across any protein types were observed for the SpA group. Regarding total energy intake, OA and RA cases were more likely to have lower energy intake, whereas gout cases showed higher levels (all P < 0.05). Compared with non-cases, total protein intake was significantly higher in OA cases (P < 0.05), but no significant differences were observed for RA or gout. Vegetable protein intake was consistently lower across the OA, RA, and gout groups compared to non-cases (all P < 0.05). For animal protein intake, OA and gout cases reported higher levels (all P < 0.05), while no significant differences were found for RA.

Associations between dietary protein intake and chronic joint diseases

Associations between dietary protein intake and the risk of chronic joint diseases are presented in Table S2 and Fig. 2. In the model adjusted for age and sex (Table S2), participants in the highest quartile of total protein intake had an 12% higher risk of OA (HR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.16). When protein intake was categorized by source, distinct associations emerged. A higher intake of vegetable protein was associated with a significantly reduced risk of OA, RA and gout. Specifically, the highest quartile was associated with risk reductions of 14% for OA (HR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.83, 0.89), 20% for RA (HR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.71, 0.90) and 31% for gout (HR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.62, 0.76). Conversely, a higher intake of animal protein was associated with an increased risk of OA and gout. The highest quartile was associated with a 16% increased risk of OA (HR = 1.16; 95% CI: 1.12, 1.21) and a 29% increased risk of gout (HR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.16, 1.43).

To account for potential confounders, we further constructed a fully adjusted model (Table S2). The analysis revealed that for total protein intake, a significant association persisted only with gout, and the direction of this association reversed. Specifically, the highest quartile of intake changed from an 10% higher risk in the age- and sex-adjusted model to a 13% lower risk (HR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.79, 0.97) in the fully adjusted model. For vegetable protein, significant inverse associations remained for OA and RA, with the highest quartile associated with an 11% lower risk of OA (HR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.86, 0.93) and a 24% lower risk of RA (HR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.67, 0.87). For animal protein, a significant association was maintained only for OA, albeit weakened, with a HR of 1.07 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.12) for the highest quartile. Notably, after multiple testing corrections, the associations for (1) total protein with gout, (2) vegetable protein with OA and RA, and (3) animal protein with OA remained statistically significant (FDR-P for trend < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, when modeled as a continuous variable, a per IQR increase in these protein intakes also showed significant linear associations with the respective disease outcomes (Table S2). Consequently, we focused subsequent analyses specifically on these associations.

In sensitivity analyses (Tables S3), the vast majority of the aforementioned associations remained significant, except that the P for trend for the associations of total protein with gout (in the analysis excluding participants diagnosed within the first two years of follow-up) and animal protein with OA (in the analysis restricted to participants with fairly typical diets during the 24-hour dietary assessments) was no longer statistically significant.

To further evaluate the dose–response relationships between dietary protein intake and the risk of OA, RA, and gout, we conducted restricted cubic spline analyses (Fig. 3). The results revealed non-linear associations for both vegetable and animal protein intake with OA risk (P for overall < 0.050, P for non-linear < 0.050 for both) (Fig. 3A and B). In contrast, linear associations were observed for vegetable protein intake with RA risk and for total protein intake with gout risk (P for overall < 0.050, P for non-linear > 0.050 for both) (Fig. 3C and D).

Associations of vegetable and animal protein-rich foods with OA and RA

Building on the observed associations between vegetable and animal protein intake and the risk of OA and RA, we further investigated the relationships of specific protein-rich food sources with these diseases. We examined 11 common vegetable- and animal-based protein-rich foods, the distributions of which are presented in Table S4.

For foods rich in vegetable protein, the protective associations observed for vegetable protein as a nutrient were generally consistent at the food level. Specifically, a higher intake of whole grains was associated with a lower risk of both OA (Group 4 vs. Group 1: HR = 0.93; 95% CI: 0.89, 0.96) and RA (HR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.77, 0.99). Similarly, a higher legume intake was inversely associated with the risk of OA (HR = 0.94; 95% CI: 0.90, 0.99). These associations remained statistically significant after multiple testing correction (FDR-P for trend < 0.05) (Table 2 & Table S5).

For animal protein-rich foods, the findings were more complex. Although animal protein as a nutrient was associated with an increased risk of OA, most individual animal protein-rich foods did not show statistically significant associations with OA after FDR correction. Notably, only cheese intake was significantly associated with a reduced risk of OA (Group 4 vs. Group 1: HR = 0.93; 95% CI: 0.89, 0.96; FDR-P for trend < 0.05). Furthermore, while animal protein intake was not significantly associated with RA, food-level analysis revealed significant associations. Higher red meat intake was associated with an increased risk of RA (Group 4 vs. Group 1: HR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.36), whereas higher intakes of seafood (HR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.73, 0.96), cheese (HR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.75, 0.97), and yogurt (HR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.60, 0.97) were associated with a lower risk. All these associations remained significant after FDR correction (all FDR-P for trend < 0.05) (Table 2 & Table S5).

Mediating effects of inflammatory biomarkers on the association between vegetable and animal protein and OA and RA

To explore the potential mechanisms underlying the observed significant associations between vegetable and animal protein intake and the risk of OA and RA, we performed a mediation analysis to assess the role of inflammatory biomarkers. Prior to the formal mediation analysis, we evaluated the prerequisite relationships: (1) the total effect of protein intake on OA and RA (Table S6-S7), confirming the associations remained significant in the sub-population with biomarker data; (2) the associations between protein intake and inflammatory biomarkers (exposure-mediator, Path a, Table S8); and (3) the associations between inflammatory biomarkers and OA and RA (mediator-outcome, Path b, Table S9). For biomarkers significant in both Path a and Path b, we proceeded to estimate the NIE and the proportion mediated (Table S10).

As shown in Fig. 4, in the associations of both animal and vegetable protein with OA, only C-reactive protein demonstrated a significant mediating effect. For vegetable protein, the NIE for CRP was 0.996 (95% CI: 0.995, 0.997), accounting for 9.598% of the total effect. For animal protein, CRP (NIE = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.002, 1.003) accounted for 6.038% of the total effect. Regarding the association between vegetable protein and RA, CRP exhibited the strongest mediating effect (NIE = 0.976, 95% CI: 0.972, 0.980), explaining 16.286% of the total effect. This was followed by neutrophil (NIE = 0.996, 95% CI: 0.995, 0.998; Proportion mediated: 2.657%), platelet (NIE = 0.997, 95% CI: 0.995, 0.998; Proportion mediated: 2.380%), and leukocyte (NIE = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.998, 0.999; Proportion mediated: 1.295%).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study revealed distinct associations between protein intake and chronic joint diseases. Higher total protein intake was linked to a lower risk of gout. Vegetable protein intake was associated with reduced risks of OA and RA, while animal protein intake was associated with an increased risk of OA. In contrast, no significant association was observed between protein intake and SpA.

Total protein

In this study, we observed an inverse association between total protein intake and gout risk, but found no significant associations with other chronic joint diseases. Current evidence on the association between total protein intake and gout remains inconsistent. Aligned with our study, a clinical trial showed that a protein-rich diet reduced serum urate among adults with prehypertension or hypertension [52]. Moreover, it has been also suggested that a low-protein diet may worsen insulin resistance and adverse cardiometabolic profiles, potentially creating a milieu conducive to gout [53]. However, several studies report conflicting evidence. In a cohort study of 47,150 men in U.S., total protein intake was not significantly associated with the risk of gout [10]. Another cohort of 63,257 Singapore Chinese adults reported that the fourth quartile of total protein intake was associated with a 27% increased risk of gout, compared with the first quartile [13]. Evidence on the association between total protein intake and OA or RA is currently limited. Regarding RA, both a prospective cohort study [11] and a Mendelian randomization (MR) study [54] reported no significant association, which aligns with our current findings. As for OA, only one cross-sectional study has indicated a J-shaped association between total protein intake and OA risk [9], and this observation requires further validation in prospective study designs.

The heterogeneity observed across studies on total protein intake and chronic joint diseases may be attributed to variations in dietary patterns and food preferences among different populations. For example, even within animal protein consumption, the primary food sources vary across populations—such as red meat and dairy products in Western diets, compared with seafood, poultry and dairy products in Mediterranean diets. These distinct food sources exert divergent biological effects due to their varying compositions of amino acids, as well as accompanying nutrients and bioactive compounds [39, 55]. Therefore, examining specific protein types and dietary sources is necessary to clarify their respective roles in the development of chronic joint diseases.

Vegetable protein and food sources

We observed that higher intakes of vegetable protein and whole grains were each inversely associated with the risk of RA. This finding, however, contrasts with results from the Nurses’ Health Study cohort of 82,063 U.S. women, which reported no significant association between vegetable protein intake and RA risk [11]. Furthermore, in our study, higher intakes of vegetable protein, whole grains and legumes were inversely associated with the risk of OA. This aligns with existing literature, where a cohort study has suggested that whole grains intake is associated with a reduced risk of knee OA [12], while a MR study has indicated that muesli intake is inversely associated with both knee and spine OA [56]. Regarding gout, we found no significant association between vegetable protein intake and gout risk—a null finding consistent with a previous cohort study [10].

Mechanistically, our mediation analysis indicates that CRP and specific immune cells (neutrophils, platelets, and leukocytes) partially mediate the protective associations of vegetable protein intake with OA and RA. This is biologically plausible, as inflammatory and immune dysregulation are central to both conditions [57–59], and vegetable protein is known to modulate these responses [57, 60]. The combined effects of dietary fiber, antioxidants, and phytochemicals in plant-based foods may further confer joint-protective benefits [55, 61–63]. For instance, dietary fiber-rich legumes and whole grains promote a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing gut microbiota [64, 65], which helps maintain immune homeostasis and reduce systemic inflammation that contributes to joint damage [66]. Additionally, isoflavones in legumes may act as selective estrogen receptor modulators to support cartilage synthesis [67].

Animal protein and food sources

Our analysis revealed distinct associations between animal-derived foods and OA as well as RA. For OA, we observed a positive association with animal protein intake but an inverse association with cheese consumption—the latter finding is supported by a MR study [56]. Regarding RA, our results showed no association between animal protein intake and RA risk, which is consistent with the finding from a large cohort of 82,063 U.S. women [11]. However, a positive association was identified for red meat, while inverse associations were observed for cheese, seafood (including both oily and non-oily fish), and yogurt. These findings contrast with much of the existing literature. For instance, an MR analysis found no significant link between cheese consumption and RA risk [54], and several prospective cohort studies reported no significant association between fish intake and RA [11, 68, 69]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of four cohorts found no association between red meat and RA [70].

Taken together, the inconsistencies between our findings and previous studies may be attributed to three main sources. First, measurement error may arise from differences in dietary assessment methods. In our study, dietary intake was assessed using 24-hour recalls. Unlike food frequency questionnaires (FFQs), 24-hour recalls are less able to capture long term habitual intake. Second, residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. Although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors such as specific medication use such as anti-inflammatory drugs and genetic risk may still bias the estimates. Additionally, covariate adjustment strategies varied considerably across previous studies, contributing to heterogeneous findings. Third, other sources of heterogeneity include differences in study design (e.g., prospective cohort vs. cross sectional) and study populations (e.g., age distribution, ethnicity). These factors may further explain the discrepancies between our findings and those of prior research.

Mechanistically, for the association between animal protein and OA, CRP was identified as a significant mediator—a role analogous to its mediation in the vegetable protein-OA/RA associations, highlighting inflammation as a common mechanism. This is biologically supported by evidence linking specific animal foods-derived components to pro-inflammatory states. For instance, branched chain amino acids (BCAAs), which dominate the amino acid influx into circulation after a red meat meal [39], are associated with elevated inflammatory markers, including high-sensitivity CRP [71]. Furthermore, other constituents commonly found with animal protein foods, such as saturated fat, cooking-derived advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and heme iron, are known to exacerbate inflammation [72, 73]. In contrast, nutrients like ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids in seafood exert anti-inflammatory effects [74]. Regular yogurt consumption has also been linked to reduced serum CRP levels [75], potentially attributable to the activity of probiotic bacteria (such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium) or their metabolites [76, 77].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, as an observational study, causality cannot be established. Second, the cohort was predominantly white, which may limit generalizability. Third, the dietary habits of participants may have changed during follow-up, but this study did not investigate the trajectories of dietary protein intake, and further research is needed. Fourth, the modest number of SpA cases (n = 728) reduced statistical power for this outcome. Fifth, dietary intake was assessed using self-reported 24-hour recalls, which are susceptible to recall bias. Moreover, this method cannot fully reflect long-term dietary habits, although we added sensitivity analyses among those who reported having a fairly typical diet during the 24-hour dietary assessments. Therefore, we averaged multiple waves of dietary data to reduce random errors. Sixth, outcome identification relied on inpatient records, potentially missing mild or undiagnosed cases. Seventh, as with all observational studies, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Specifically, the mediation analysis relies on assumptions regarding the absence of unmeasured confounding between the exposure, mediator, and outcome. Although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of potential confounders, these potential biases may still influence the estimated mediation effects. Finally, although the proportional hazards assumption was met in most analyses, it was violated in some instances. For these, the HRs are interpreted as average effects over follow-up [42, 78].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found an inverse association between total protein intake and gout risk. Vegetable protein intake showed a protective association against both OA and RA, while animal protein intake was positively associated with OA risk. Collectively, these results support the targeting of modifiable nutritional factors for the prevention and management of chronic joint diseases.
