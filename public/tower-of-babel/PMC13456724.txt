A rare case of synchronous bilateral breast cancer with distinct histological subtypes
Authors: Samin Rostamzadeh, Fatemeh Shahrahmani, Seyedehsahel Rasoulighasemlouei, Rahim Mahmodlou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC) is rare, and the occurrence of distinct histological subtypes in each breast is particularly uncommon, presenting diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This report describes a case of SBBC with discordant histology in an elderly woman to highlight the diagnostic challenges.Case presentation:An 80-year-old woman with no prior breast cancer screening presented with a palpable right breast mass. Imaging revealed bilateral lesions, and core needle biopsy (CNB) confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) in the right breast with axillary lymph node involvement. While the initial biopsy of the left breast suggested benign changes, an excisional biopsy was performed due to imaging–pathology discordance, confirming invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated ER and PR positivity, HER2 negativity, and discordant E-cadherin expression, confirming two separate primary tumors. Given the patient’s advanced age and comorbidities, she underwent bilateral mastectomy without chemotherapy. Final pathology confirmed IDC (T2N1M0, grade II) in the right breast and multifocal ILC (T2N0M0, grade II) in the left breast.Discussion:This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of SBBC with different histologic types. The limited sensitivity of CNB for ILC underscores the need for close imaging–pathology correlation and excisional biopsy when discordance exists. Management should follow the principles of unilateral disease but be tailored to patient comorbidities and tumor biology.Conclusion:SBBC with distinct histological subtypes is rare but clinically significant. Bilateral evaluation and accurate histopathological assessment are essential for optimal outcomes.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most common malignancy among women worldwide. Synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC) is a distinct and relatively rare clinical entity, defined by the presence of primary malignant tumors in both breasts occurring simultaneously or within a short interval[1]. The incidence of SBBC is approximately 3% among all breast cancer diagnoses. The most common type of bilateral breast cancer is invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), which accounts for 70–80% of cases, whereas invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) accounts for approximately 10–15%[2].

The occurrence of distinct histopathological subtypes in each breast is particularly unusual and presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges[3,4]. Accurate diagnosis requires careful correlation between imaging and histopathological findings, as discrepancies may lead to misdiagnosis[5,6]. Moreover, due to the lack of standardized treatment protocols for SBBC, clinical decisions predominantly rely on adaptation of guidelines developed for unilateral breast cancer, individualized based on tumor characteristics and patient factors[7,8].

We present a case of an elderly woman with SBBC of different histologic subtypes, consisting of IDC in the right breast and ILC in the left breast, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and treatment considerations in such a rare presentation.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old woman presented following the incidental discovery of a right breast mass. She had no history of breast cancer screening, family history of malignancy, or prior mammography. Her reproductive history included breastfeeding, and she denied the use of hormone replacement therapy. Past medical history included hypothyroidism (on levothyroxine 100 µg daily), thyroidectomy for goiter, knee joint replacement, and cholecystectomy. Her vital signs were stable, and on physical examination, a firm, non-mobile 4-cm mass was palpated at the 7 o’clock position of the right breast, 2 cm from the nipple, along with palpable, firm right axillary lymphadenopathy (0.5 cm in size). A 1-cm, mobile, irregular mass was palpated near the nipple of the left breast. There were no cutaneous changes, nipple retraction, peau d’orange, or nipple discharge. A thorough abdominopelvic assessment was unremarkable, with no palpable masses or organomegaly detected. Laboratory investigations were within normal limits, except for mild anemia.

Breast ultrasonography revealed an indistinct-border, irregular hypoechoic mass measuring 3.3 × 1.9 cm at the 7 o’clock position of the right breast, and a spiculated, irregular, hypoechoic mass measuring 1.1 × 1.8 cm at the 10 o’clock position near the left nipple. No microcalcifications or lymphadenopathy were observed on ultrasound. Mammography revealed a spiculated, high-density mass measuring 1.5 cm in the central-inner left breast, highly suggestive of malignancy (BI-RADS 5), and an indistinct, irregular mass measuring 3 × 2.5 cm in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast, categorized as BI-RADS 4 based on breast imaging features (Fig. 1). The patient underwent core needle biopsy (CNB) using ultrasound guidance for both breast masses and lymph nodes. CNB of the right breast mass confirmed IDC (grades II–III) with lymphovascular invasion and metastatic carcinoma in the right axillary node. The left breast biopsy initially showed benign fibrocystic changes, but due to discordance with imaging, a wire-guided excisional biopsy was performed, revealing ILC, grade II, with lymphovascular invasion. The metastatic work-up was also negative. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong estrogen receptor (ER+) and progesterone receptor (PR+) expression (~70% and ~ 50% nuclear positivity, respectively), HER2−, and a Ki-67 index of 40% in the right breast tumor. E-cadherin expression was positive in the right lesion and negative in the left, confirming two distinct primary tumors with different histology.

This case was discussed during a multidisciplinary tumor board meeting, where it was determined that, given the patient’s advanced age and poor performance status, systemic chemotherapy would not be appropriate. The team collectively decided on a treatment plan involving bilateral mastectomy without adjuvant chemotherapy. After a thorough discussion of the available options, the patient provided informed consent and subsequently underwent bilateral mastectomy with curative intent.

The right mastectomy specimen showed a homogeneous white mass with hemorrhagic areas measuring 4 cm in greatest diameter. All surgical margins were free of invasive carcinoma. Two regional lymph nodes were involved, resulting in a pathologic stage of T2N1M0. Pathological examination confirmed IDC, grade II. The left mastectomy specimen revealed a cystic hemorrhagic area measuring 6.5 cm in greatest diameter and a creamy-colored firm area measuring 1 cm, both in the superomedial quadrant. Histologically, this multifocal tumor was confirmed as ILC, grade II. Lymph node evaluation showed no involvement, resulting in a pathologic stage of T2N0M0. Lymphovascular invasion was present in both tumors without dermal lymphatic invasion. The patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged in stable condition.

Discussion

Bilateral breast cancer can manifest as metastatic lesions originating from a unilateral primary tumor or as two distinct primary tumors. Differentiating between primary bilateral tumors and metastatic spread is crucial, as it directly influences prognosis and therapeutic decision-making. Primary bilateral breast tumors are classified as either metachronous bilateral breast cancer (MBBC) or SBBC, with the latter defined as contralateral tumors diagnosed within three months of the initial breast cancer[10,11]. SBBC is considered a rare but clinically significant entity, particularly when it involves distinct histopathological subtypes[12]. In this case, the presence of IDC in the right breast and ILC in the left breast, along with differing immunohistochemical profiles, confirmed the presence of independent primaries rather than metastatic disease. SBBC is characterized pathologically by a predominance of IDC, while ILC is less frequent overall[3]. The diagnosis of ILC is often challenging due to its subtle clinical and radiographic presentation. SBBC often presents as multifocal tumors, tends to occur more frequently in older individuals, and is characterized by a greater incidence of less aggressive carcinoma types, which may reflect the identification of slow-growing tumors at a subclinical stage[13].

The diagnosis of SBBC requires a comprehensive multimodal approach. While mammography and ultrasonography serve as primary imaging modalities for detecting bilateral lesions, breast MRI has emerged as the most valuable diagnostic tool with superior sensitivity for contralateral disease detection[2,12]. Histopathological examination of surgical specimens with lymph node evaluation and immunohistochemistry provides a definitive diagnosis and molecular characterization[2,13]. In this case, CNB failed to detect malignancy in the left breast despite imaging findings highly suggestive of cancer, reflecting the known limitations of CNB in ILC. The diffuse growth pattern and lack of desmoplastic response in ILC can result in sampling error and false-negative biopsy results. The decision to proceed with excisional biopsy in the setting of imaging–pathology discordance was therefore pivotal, as it established the correct diagnosis and directly influenced definitive surgical management. Treatment in patients with SBBC is generally similar to that in patients with unilateral breast cancer, involving a multimodality approach including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and hormone therapy if indicated. Bilateral mastectomy is the most common surgical procedure in women with SBBC, while systemic chemotherapy is generally reserved for node-positive disease[14,15]. In our case, the potential risks of chemotherapy outweighed the benefits; therefore, surgical management with bilateral mastectomy was prioritized, aiming to achieve local control and reduce disease burden.

To date, only a few case reports have described SBBC involving different histopathologic subtypes. Mehedințu-Ionescu et al.[16] reported a 47-year-old woman with intermediate-grade DCIS and multiple foci of LCIS in the right breast and multifocal LCIS in the left. She underwent skin-sparing mastectomy with immediate reconstruction, whereas our patient’s advanced age and comorbidities required a more cautious surgical approach, underscoring the need for patient-specific management. Similarly, Shah et al[17] described synchronous bilateral IDC of differing histologic grades in a 56-year-old woman managed with bilateral modified radical mastectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy. In contrast, our patient’s advanced age and poor tolerance necessitated a more conservative approach. In another study, Al Marwani et al[18] reported a case of SBBC in a 70-year-old asymptomatic woman. Histopathology revealed IDC in the left breast and mucinous carcinoma in the right, both low-grade and ER-positive, allowing breast-conserving surgery. Similar pathological features were also reported by El Amrani et al.[19], who detected SBBC with mucinous carcinoma in one breast and multifocal ILC in the other, managed with bilateral mastectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy. Itagaki et al[20] described a 78-year-old woman with bilateral oncocytic carcinoma who remained disease-free after bilateral mastectomy. Taken together, these reports highlight the histological heterogeneity of SBBC and the need for comprehensive histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation to confirm independent primaries. SBBC can arise from both common and rare histological subtypes, and its management should be tailored not only to tumor biology but also to patient comorbidities and functional status.

Conclusion

SBBC remains a rare but clinically significant challenge, particularly when distinct histological subtypes are present. This case underscores the diagnostic difficulty of ILC, the necessity of correlating imaging with pathology, and the importance of performing an excisional biopsy when discordance exists. Individualized management, considering tumor biology, patient comorbidities, and functional status, is essential to ensure the adequate and appropriate management of SBBC.
