First Logit Boosting: Visual Grounding Method to Mitigate Object Hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models
Jiwoo Ha, Jongwoo Baek, Jinhyun So

Abstract
Recent Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance across various multimodal tasks that require understanding both visual and linguistic inputs. However, object hallucination — the generation of nonexistent objects in answers — remains a persistent challenge. Although several approaches such as retraining and external grounding methods have been proposed to mitigate this issue, they still suffer from high data costs or structural complexity. Training-free methods such as Contrastive Decoding (CD) are more cost-effective, avoiding additional training or external models, but still suffer from long-term decay, where visual grounding weakens and language priors dominate as the generation progresses. In this paper, we propose First Logit Boosting (FLB), a simple yet effective training-free technique designed to alleviate long-term decay in LVLMs. FLB stores the logit of the first generated token and adds it to subsequent token predictions, effectively mitigating long-term decay of visual information. We observe that FLB (1) sustains the visual information embedded in the first token throughout generation, and (2) suppresses hallucinated words through the stabilizing effect of the “The” token. Experimental results show that FLB significantly reduces object hallucination across various tasks, benchmarks, and backbone models. Notably, it causes negligible inference overhead, making it highly applicable to real-time multimodal systems. Code is available at https://github.com/jiwooha20/FLB

This phenomenon not only reduces model reliability, but also causes serious risks in safety-critical applications, including autonomous driving

Hence, addressing object hallucination remains essential for advancing LVLMs toward reliable real-world deployment.

To mitigate this issue, existing studies can be broadly categorized into three lines of research.
(1)
Retraining-based methods

(2)

(3)
Training-free methods

.
Nevertheless, all three lines exhibit inherent trade-offs: retraining methods are data-intensive and cost-intensive, external ground methods compromise efficiency, and existing training-free methods fail to sustain visual grounding over long generations, leading to persistent hallucinations.
Among the three methods, training-free approaches have recently gained attention for their efficiency and simplicity, as they operate entirely during the inference time without additional supervision or model retraining.
Contrastive Decoding (CD), a representative training-free method, suppresses hallucination by contrasting the logits from the original and perturbed inputs, thereby reducing over-reliance on language priors and statistical biases
15
;
33
;
39
;
8
;
21
.
However, these methods fail to fully address a critical issue,
long-term decay
of visual grounding
24
;
8
.
As text generation progresses, the model’s attention gradually drifts away from visual evidence and becomes dominated by language priors, causing hallucinations to frequently appear in later tokens.
Moreover, CD methods require two forward passes per decoding step, one for the original input and the other for the perturbed version, which doubles inference time.

To overcome these limitations, we propose a simple yet effective training-free technique, named
First Logit Boosting (FLB)
, motivated by the key observation that the first token’s logit inherently encodes the most visually grounded representation in LVLMs.
Specifically, at the first decoding step, the relative logit gap between the ground truth and hallucination tokens is the largest.
By storing the first token logit and reusing it in subsequent decoding steps, FLB continuously reinforces early visual grounding throughout the entire generation process.
As illustrated in
Fig.

1
, FLB leverages two complementary mechanisms that jointly stabilize decoding: (1) a
direct visual grounding effect
where the first token logit serves as a persistent visual anchor retaining visual evidence, and (2) an
implicit visual referencing effect

where the increased likelihood of sentence starting with “The” token encourages the model refer back to entities generated earlier before long-term decay becomes severe, thereby reducing the chance of hallucination.
These dual effects effectively mitigate the long-term decay problem without introducing additional training and external models.
Moreover, unlike previous training-free methods such as CD, which require dual forward passes per decoding step, FLB achieves superior hallucination mitigation using only a single forward pass with negligible inference overhead.
Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose FLB, a lightweight and training-free technique that effectively mitigates object hallucination caused by long-term decay without requiring additional training or external modules.

•

We identify and analyze that FLB’s improvement stems from two complementary effects, a direct visual grounding effect and an implicit visual referencing effect.

•

Extensive experiments demonstrate that FLB achieves state-of-the-art hallucination mitigation performance across various benchmarks and backbone models, while preserving near-baseline inference efficiency.

Related work

2.1
Large Vision-Language Models

LVLMs

This architecture enables LVLMs to effectively integrate multimodal inputs and achieve strong performance on tasks like image captioning and VQA

However, they still suffer from object hallucination, where generated captions mention non-existent objects

;

.

2.2
Mitigating hallucination

Object hallucination in LVLMs arises from multiple causes, and consequently, various mitigation approaches have been explored.
Existing approaches can be roughly categorized into three groups:
(1) training-based methods
28
;
40
,
(2) external grounding methods
38
;
24
;
6
, and
(3) training-free methods
15
;
21
;
13
.
Specifically, training-free approaches typically operate on the logit or latent space during decoding to suppress language priors,
the tendency of LVLMs to rely more on textual context than on visual input, without additional models or retraining.
Among these, CD methods mitigate hallucination by contrasting logits from the original input with those from a distorted input that amplifies language priors.
Different CD variants use distorted inputs such as a noised image
15
, a perturbed instruction
33
, or an unconditioned input
8
.

Despite their effectiveness, prior studies
24
have shown that the influence of language priors grows with sentence length, and existing CD methods fail to fully address this issue.
Moreover, CD methods require additional decoding passes.
To overcome these limitations, we propose a training-free method
which eliminates additional inference passes
while alleviating the reinforcement of language priors that intensify with longer generations.
Unlike previous logit steering methods that steer generation toward a target distribution, FLB instead leverages the earliest decoding distribution, which is the most visually grounded, with the “The” effect reflecting stabilization from reduced uncertainty.

3
Preliminary

3.1
LVLM decoding

The LVLM parameterized by
\theta
consists of a vision encoder and a language model.
The vision encoder processes the visual input
v
and produces a set of visual features,
which are fused with a text prompt
x
to generate a textual response
y
.
The response is generated in an auto-regressive manner,
where each token
y_{t}
at step
t
is sampled according to the following equation:

y_{t}\sim p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid v,x,y_{<t})\propto\exp\big(\text{logit}_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid v,x,y_{<t})\big),

(1)

where
{y}_{<t}
denotes the sequence of previously generated tokens before time step
t
.

\text{logit}_{\theta}(\cdot)
represents the score that the model assigns to each candidate token,
which is normalized into a probability distribution via the softmax function.
When the model assigns high probabilities to tokens that are not grounded in the visual input
– often due to excessive reliance on language priors –
object hallucination occurs in the generated output.

3.2
Contrastive decoding

The CD method was proposed to mitigate object hallucinations in LVLMs.
CD methods generate two output logits: one from original input and another from a distorted input, which induces more stronger language prior.
By contrasting these two logits, CD methods suppress over-reliance on language prior and thereby reduce hallucinations.
Among several variants, Visual Contrastive Decoding (VCD)
15
utilizes negative logit, which is from distorted image input
v^{\prime}
.
The adjusted logits are computed by

\displaystyle p_{\text{\tiny VCD}}(y\mid v,v^{\prime},x)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big[(1+\alpha)\,\mathrm{logit}_{\theta}(y\mid v,x)

\displaystyle-\alpha\,\mathrm{logit}_{\theta}(y\mid v^{\prime},x)\Big]

(2)

where
\alpha
is a parameter to determine the amplification of differences between the two distributions.

Figure 2
:

Comparison of the probability between all ground truth (left) and hallucination (right) words
across token steps for each mitigation method.
As sentence length increases, hallucinated word logits become more dominant; while VCD, ICD, and M3ID fail to suppress this trend, FLB (ours) effectively mitigates hallucinated predictions.

3.3
The limitation of contrastive decoding

CD methods provide a model-agnostic and training-free way to mitigate hallucination.
While these methods are more computationally efficient than retraining- or ground-based methods, they still suffer from two fundamental limitations: (1) the long-term decay of visual grounding and (2) inference inefficiency due to dual forward passes.

Long-term decay.

Previous research
24
;
8
and our own observations consistently show that as generation progresses, the influence of language priors strengthens while visual grounding gradually decays.
This phenomenon originates from the Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)
27
used in most LVLMs, where image tokens are placed at the beginning of the sequence.
As the generation progresses, the relative positional distance between image and textual tokens increases, weakening cross-modal attention
36
.
To empirically confirm this problem, we implement representative CD-based methods – VCD
15
, Instruction Contrastive Decoding (ICD)
33
, and Multi-Modal Mutual-Information Decoding (M3ID)
8
.
We introduce a conditional probability metric conditioned on noun probability to analyze how ground truth and hallucination related logits evolve across decoding steps, as shown in
Fig.

2
.
The results clearly show that, for all CD variants, the average logit of ground truth tokens consistently decreases while that of hallucinated tokens increases as the sequence length grows, demonstrating that none of these methods effectively addresses the long-term decay problem.

Inference inefficiency.

Although CD-based methods avoid retraining, they require two forward passes per decoding step to obtain the original and contrastive logits.
This design roughly doubles the inference time, and the overhead grows linearly with sequence length, which will be demonstrated in
Section

6.3
.

In summary, existing CD-based methods mitigate hallucination locally at each decoding step but fail to preserve visual grounding globally across long sequences. Moreover, their computational overhead at inference time undermines the practical advantages of being training-free. Addressing these two limitations motivates the design of our method introduced in the following section.

4
Method

4.1
First Logit Boosting

To mitigate the amplification of language priors in LVLMs during long sequences, we propose a simple yet effective technique named First Logit Boosting (FLB).
Our key intuition originates from the positional characteristics of LVLMs: the first token is generated immediately after visual tokens, before cross-modal attention begins to decay.
Hence, the first token’s logit is minimally affected by the decay and therefore preserves the original visual information most faithfully.
Specifically, we store the logits of the first generated token given the prompt
x
and visual input
v
:

l_{0}=\mathrm{logit}_{\theta}(y\mid x,v),

(3)

and reuse it throughout subsequent decoding steps.
As the logit in
Eq.

3
remains constant across decoding steps
t
,
the computation needs to be performed only once, incurring negligible additional cost.

Formally, FLB is formulated as follows:

\begin{split}y_{t}\sim\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big[\mathrm{logit}_{\theta}(y\mid v,x,y_{<t})+\,w_{t}\,l_{0}\Big],\end{split}

(4)

where
w_{t}
is a temporal weighting function that gradually increases with decoding step
t
to counteract long-term decay explained in
Section

3.3
.
Specifically,
w_{t}
is given by

w_{t}=\gamma(1-e^{-\lambda t}),

(5)

where
\gamma
denotes the maximum scaling coefficient and
\lambda
controls the rate of change.
Details on weight function and parameter optimization will be provided in
Section

B
.

Since the first token logit does not correspond to the current decoding step,
indiscriminate addition may increase the probability of selecting implausible tokens.
Therefore, we prevent this by applying an adaptive plausibility constraint,
similar to that used in VCD
15
, to filter out tokens with low plausibility scores during prediction.
The candidate set is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{head}}

\displaystyle(y_{<t})=\{\,y_{t}\in\mathcal{V}:

\displaystyle\!p_{\theta}(y_{t}

\displaystyle\mid v,x,y_{<t})\geq\beta\max_{w}p_{\theta}(w\mid v,x,y_{<t})\,\},

(6)

\displaystyle p_{\text{\tiny FLB}}

\displaystyle(y_{t}\mid v,x)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if }y_{t}\notin\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{head}}(y_{<t}),

where
\mathcal{V}
denotes the output vocabulary of LVLM and
\beta\in[0,1]
is a hyperparameter that determines the threshold for the candidate set
\mathcal{V}_{head}
, based on the maximum probability in the original logit distribution.
Finally, the full decoding rule is given by:

\begin{split}y_{t}\sim\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big[\mathrm{logit}_{\theta}(y\mid x,v,y_{<t})+\,w_{t}\,l_{0}\Big],\\
\text{subject to }y_{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{head}}(y_{<t}).\end{split}

(7)

Through this mechanism, FLB obtains two complementary effects: (1) a direct visual grounding effect, where the stored first logit serves as a persistent anchor preserves visual consistency across decoding steps; and (2) an implicit visual referencing effect, named
“The” effect
, where the linguistic nature of the first token (e.g., “The”) stabilizes subsequent noun predictions by encouraging references to previously grounded entities.
The following sections analyze these two effects in detail.

:

This figure shows the logits of the ground truth words (left) and hallucination words (middle) for the first token during caption generation for a case image (right).
The logit of ground truth words are generally higher than that of hallucination words.

4.2
Direct visual grounding effect

The first effect of FLB directly addresses the long-term decay of visual grounding.
As discussed in
Section

3.3
, LVLMs suffer from the long-term decay due to RoPE, which weakens cross-modal attention as the distance between image and text tokens increases.
This section investigates how FLB directly addresses this issue by leveraging the logit of the first token as a persistent visual anchor.
To quantitatively examine the effect, we analyze the logit difference between the ground truth and hallucination tokens contained in the first logit in
Eq.

3
. As shown in
Fig.

3
, the ground truth words exhibit consistently higher logits than the hallucinated ones, demonstrating that the first token retains the strongest visual correlation.
As FLB reuses this logit across all subsequent decoding steps, this margin is repeatedly applied, continuously reinforcing visual signals.

Figure 4
:
Top 20 tokens by logit value for the first token prediction. The list includes common sentence-starting words such as “The”, “In”, and “A”.

4.3
“The” effect

While FLB is originally designed to mitigate visual decay through the direct visual grounding effect, we unexpectedly observe a complementary effect arising from the linguistic properties of the first token.
As illustrated in
Fig.

4
, the first decoding step generates articles such as “The”, “In”, and “A”, which subtly influence the model’s subsequent predictions.
Surprisingly, sentences beginning with “The” exhibit markedly lower hallucination rates, and nouns following “The” are more frequently grounded in visual input than those following “A” or the other starting tokens.
This emergent effect, named
“The” effect
, reveals that linguistic initialization can act as an implicit form of visual grounding.
Even without any modification to the model’s language prior, FLB amplifies this bias toward contextually and visually grounded nouns by reusing the first token logit, leading to more coherent and visually consistent generations.
We empirically validate this effect in
Section

6.4
where we provide a detailed analysis of (i) sentences starting with “The”, (ii) nouns immediately following “The”, and (iii) the reduction in prediction entropy after “The”, jointly offering a comprehensive account of the
“The” effect
.

5
Experiment

In this section, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed FLB in mitigating hallucination.
Since FLB is designed to suppress the influence of language priors that become stronger as sentences grow longer,
its effectiveness in mitigating hallucination increases accordingly.
Therefore, we focused on
generative
tasks, where such effects are more evident, rather than discriminative ones.

5.1
Datasets and evaluation metrics

We conducted main experiments on two standard benchmarks that require long and descriptive caption generation:
the CHAIR
26
and AMBER
30
datasets.
For CHAIR, we used 500 images randomly sampled from the MSCOCO 2014 validation set
18
.
For the AMBER benchmark, we used all 1,004 images from the AMBER dataset.
Captions were generated using the prompt on both benchmarks:

“Please describe this image in detail.”

The generated captions were then evaluated using following metrics
26
;
30
:
(1)
CHAIR
(CHAIR
i
), the proportion of hallucinated objects among all generated objects;
(2)
Cover
, the proportion of ground truth objects among all generated objects;
(3)
Hal
(CHAIR
s
), the proportion of captions containing at least one hallucinated object; and
(4)
Cog
, the proportion of hallucinated objects that are perceptually plausible to humans.

To further evaluate the generalization capability of FLB beyond captioning tasks, we additionally conducted experiments on diverse tasks and interaction styles.
We used two benchmarks: MMHalBench
28
, which includes diverse question types (e.g., object attributes, relations, and contextual reasoning) beyond image captioning, and ConvBench
22
, a three-turn conversation-style benchmark.
Both benchmarks are evaluated using GPT-4V as the judge.

5.2
MLLM backbones

To verify the generality of FLB, we evaluated the two benchmarks on LVLMs employing different types of visual–language alignment.
Specifically, we used two representative models:

LLaVA-1.5 (7B)

20
, which connects vision and language modalities through an MLP-based projection layer,
and
InstructBLIP (7B)

5
, which adopts a Q-former
16
architecture for cross-modal alignment.

5.3
Baselines

To compare FLB with existing training-free hallucination mitigation methods,
we included
Baseline decoding
(vanilla decoding without any hallucination mitigation method) and three representative contrastive decoding techniques as baselines:

VCD

15
,
ICD

33
, and
M3ID

8
.
VCD alleviates hallucination by contrasting logits obtained from an original image and a distorted image,
while ICD performs contrastive decoding using an original instruction and a perturbed instruction.
M3ID reduce hallucinations by minimizing visual information loss using image conditioned input and unconditioned input.
All experiments were conducted with random sampling-based decoding.
Each result was averaged over three runs to ensure consistency.
For each baseline, we adopted hyperparameter configurations that achieved the best performance as reported in the original papers
15
;
33
;
8
.
The hyperparameters of FLB were set to
\gamma=0.3
,
\lambda=0.05
, and
\beta=0.1
,
as detailed in

.

Main result

Across both LLaVA-1.5 and InstructBLIP, FLB outperforms all compared methods, surpassing the prior hallucination mitigation techniques, including VCD, ICD, and M3ID on hallucination-related metrics.

6.2
Qualitative Evaluation

Since FLB directly adds logits from different token positions,
this raises concerns that such cross-position manipulation could distort the natural language generation process and degrade sentence fluency
(e.g., inserting capitalized tokens, such as “The” in the middle of a sentence).
To assess this risk, we conducted experiments to evaluate the impact of FLB on sentence quality.
First, we compared the average number of words and tokens in sentences generated on the AMBER benchmark in
Table

3
. We confirmed that there was no significant difference in sentence length or the number of tokens before and after applying FLB.
Furthermore, across the entire AMBER dataset (approximately 1,000 sentences), no cases were observed in which unexpected capitalized tokens appeared mid-sentence.
This complete absence of failure case indicates that the adaptive plausibility constraint in FLB effectively suppresses excessive logit perturbations, thereby maintaining grammatical consistency.
Second, we adopted the GPT-4-based evaluation framework proposed by
38
to compare sentence-level quality.
As shown in
Table

4
, sentences generated with FLB achieved higher quality scores, confirming that FLB does not compromise grammaticality or expressive fluency.

6.3
Inference speed

We analyzed the impact of various hallucination mitigation methods and FLB on the inference speed of LVLMs.
To evaluate this, we measured the average generation speed on the AMBER generative task.
As shown in
Fig.

5
, CD methods exhibit nearly twice slower inference speed compared to the baseline model without any mitigation.
In contrast, FLB, which only reuses the saved logit of the first token,
shows no degradation in inference speed.
These results demonstrate that FLB achieves hallucination mitigation with negligible computational overhead, maintaining near-baseline efficiency and outperforming existing decoding-based methods that suffer from significant inference latency.

6.4
Ablation study

To investigate the contribution of the two core mechanisms underlying FLB, the direct visual grounding effect and “The” effect’, we conducted an ablation analysis designed to isolate each effect individually.

Table

5
summarizes the results in four configurations:
(1)
Baseline
: vanilla LVLM without hallucination mitigation;
(2)
Direct visual grounding only
: we apply FLB while masking all logits in
l_{0}
except those corresponding to
noun tokens
, thereby activating only the direct visual grounding effect;
(3)
“The” effect only
: we mask all logits in
l_{0}
except the “The” token, which selectively increases the probability of generating sentences that begin with “The”, isolating the implicit visual referencing effect;
(4)
FLB (full)
: the proposed full configuration, in which both effects operate jointly throughout decoding.
As shown in
Table

6.4.1
Sentence level analysis

A key observation from
Section

4.3
is that the first token logit frequently corresponds to sentence-initial articles with “The” having the highest logit (see
Fig.

4
).
We hypothesize that “The” token reduces long-term hallucination by encouraging the model to refer back to entities that have already been visually attended to, rather than sampling novel or ambiguous objects influenced by language priors.
To test this, we divide the captions generated by LLaVA-1.5 into two groups, sentences beginning with “The” and all others,
and measure conditional probability of ground truth and hallucination nouns in the same way as in
Fig.

2
.
As shown in
Fig.

6
, hallucination probability increases sharply in ordinary sentences, reflecting long-term decay driven by language priors, but grows marginally in sentences beginning with “The”.
More importantly, the divergence between the two groups becomes larger at later token positions.
These results suggest that even without applying FLB, the presence of “The” at the beginning of a sentence inherently suppresses long-term decay and reduces hallucination,
possibly by stabilizing the model’s initial linguistic state and mitigating early-stage degradation of visual grounding.

Sentences beginning with “The” show comparable ground truth probability to the overall distribution but exhibit substantially lower hallucination growth, particularly at later token positions where long-term decay intensifies.
The widening gap between two groups demonstrate that “The” token provides a stabilizing initialization that suppresses cumulative hallucination.

6.4.2
Word level analysis

To verify whether this stabilizing effect of the “The” token extends to the nouns that follow it,
we conducted an experiment to examine whether nouns appearing after “The” are indeed less likely to be hallucinated.
Specifically, we compared sentences beginning with the tokens “The” and “A,”
which both serve as articles preceding nouns, and analyzed the nouns following each token.

Table

reports the frequency of ground truth and hallucination nouns following each token.
The results indicate that nouns following the “The” token exhibit a significantly lower hallucination rate than those following the “A” token.
Moreover,
Table

compares the average prediction probabilities of these nouns,
revealing that nouns predicted after “The” have a considerably lower average probability of being hallucinated compared to those following “A.”
This finding suggests that the “The” token helps stabilize noun prediction and suppress the likelihood of hallucination during subsequent word generation.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced FLB, a lightweight and training-free decoding method that mitigates long-term object hallucination in LVLMs by reusing the first-token logit throughout generation.
Unlike prior approaches that introduce additional models or require double inference,
FLB achieves hallucination mitigation through reusing only the first token logit.
FLB leverages two complementary mechanisms, direct visual grounding and implicit visual referencing, and achieves consistent improvements in various LVLMs and hallucination benchmarks.

Limitations and future directions.

Although effective, FLB does not fundamentally resolve the structural limitations of training-free decoding.
In particular, FLB cannot fully model context-dependent visual grounding as it injects visual evidence through the first-token logit rather than adapting to evolving visual semantics.
Furthermore, it does not eliminate the underlying long-term decay introduced by RoPE, but instead mitigates its effect through early-token reinforcement.
Extending FLB with adaptive token-level visual grounding or decay-aware positional mechanisms would be interesting future directions.
