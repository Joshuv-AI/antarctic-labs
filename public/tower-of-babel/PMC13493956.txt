Machine learning–enabled ECG arrhythmia classification: a systematic and educational study from signal processing to decision support
Authors: Negin Melek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocardiogram (ECG) signals play a critical role in the early detection of cardiac arrhythmias, which remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. While deep learning approaches have achieved high classification accuracy, their increasing complexity often limits interpretability and practical applicability. This study presents a systematic and interpretable framework for multi-class ECG arrhythmia classification, examining the effects of signal processing, feature extraction, feature selection, and evaluation strategies on classification performance. Experiments were conducted on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database using two feature representations: (i) morphological and temporal features, and (ii) a compact wavelet-based representation. Sequential Forward Feature Selection (SFFS) revealed that classification performance saturates at approximately 15 features, indicating that most discriminative information is captured within a compact subset. Using this feature space, the support vector machine (SVM) achieved the best overall performance, reaching 98.54% accuracy with stable results across different configurations. The wavelet-based representation further improved performance balance, yielding lower Golden Distance (GD) values (as low as 0.0249), indicating more consistent behavior across evaluation metrics. Overall, the results demonstrate that carefully designed feature extraction and selection enable classical machine learning methods, particularly SVM, to achieve high and reliable performance, providing an interpretable alternative to more complex black-box models in ECG arrhythmia classification.Keywords: ECG signal processing, Feature engineering, Classical machine learning, Arrhythmia detection, Model evaluationSubject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The human heart is a vital organ responsible for continuous blood circulation throughout the body via the cardiovascular system, ensuring the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to tissues and the removal of metabolic waste products1–3. This process is supported by coordinated cardiac structures and regulated blood flow, enabling efficient and stable circulation4–6.

The coordinated function of the heart is governed by electrical impulses that control rhythmic contraction and relaxation7. Cardiac rhythm is initiated by the sinoatrial (SA) node, which acts as the natural pacemaker, and propagates through the cardiac conduction system to ensure synchronized contraction of the heart muscle8,9. These electrical activities, including depolarization and repolarization processes governed by action potentials, are essential for maintaining stable cardiac function, and abnormalities in these processes may lead to arrhythmias10–13.

The electrocardiogram (ECG) is a non-invasive technique widely adopted to record the electrical activity of the heart and evaluate cardiac rhythm14–16. Variations in ECG waveforms, including the P wave, QRS complex, and T wave, provide essential information for identifying arrhythmias and other cardiac disorders.

Cardiac signal analysis is a fundamental component of modern cardiology and biomedical engineering, enabling the assessment of heart rate variability (HRV), QT interval monitoring, arrhythmia detection, and diagnosis of various cardiac conditions17–19. With the advancement of wearable devices and continuous monitoring systems, ECG analysis is increasingly used for real-time health assessment and early diagnosis20–24.

In ECG signal analysis, morphological characteristics and temporal patterns, such as the QRS complex and R–R intervals, are extensively used to extract meaningful features for classification tasks25,26. These features play a crucial role in distinguishing normal and abnormal cardiac rhythms and support the development of automated diagnostic systems.

Several publicly available ECG databases are widely used for developing and evaluating classification algorithms. Among them, the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database is one of the most commonly used benchmark datasets for arrhythmia detection and classification27. Other datasets, such as the Apnea-ECG Database and the Kaggle ECG dataset, further support the development of machine learning-based approaches28,29.

Despite significant progress in ECG-based arrhythmia classification, several challenges remain. Many existing studies focus primarily on maximizing classification accuracy using complex models, often overlooking the interpretability of feature representations and the contribution of individual processing stages. In addition, the relative importance of feature selection versus classifier choice is not systematically analyzed, limiting the understanding of model behavior in practical applications and reducing the transparency of decision-making processes30,31.

Motivated by these challenges, this study adopts a structured and interpretable approach to ECG classification. The aim of this study is to develop a systematic and interpretable framework for multi-class ECG arrhythmia classification, focusing on the interaction between signal processing, feature extraction, feature selection, and evaluation strategies. Related work

Recent research on ECG-based arrhythmia classification has been dominated by deep learning (DL) approaches, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNN) and hybrid architectures31,34,35. These models can achieve high classification performance by learning feature representations directly from raw ECG signals. In addition, recent studies have extended machine learning and deep learning methods to broader clinical and diagnostic problems in cardiovascular signal analysis36. However, despite these advances, the limited interpretability of such models remains an important concern, especially in clinical settings where transparent and explainable decisions are required30,37. This limitation restricts their practical adoption in scenarios where model transparency and clinical validation are essential.

As a result, classical machine learning methods, such as support vector machines (SVM) and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), are still widely used38,39. These methods rely on handcrafted features derived from ECG signals, including morphological, temporal, and frequency-domain characteristics. Since these features are closely related to physiological indicators such as QRS duration and RR interval dynamics, they provide a more interpretable basis for clinical decision-making. Nevertheless, their performance is highly dependent on the quality and relevance of the extracted features, making feature design a critical and non-trivial task.

In addition, recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of statistical and temporal feature-based approaches combined with feature selection techniques for arrhythmia classification. For example, Kaya40 proposed a machine learning-based framework for the detection of Bundle Branch Block (BBB) using higher-order statistical and temporal features, where feature selection methods such as forward selection, backward elimination, and genetic algorithms were employed. Comparative evaluations using k-NN, SVM, and neural networks showed that carefully selected feature subsets can achieve very high classification accuracy on the MIT-BIH dataset. These findings further support the importance of feature quality and selection strategies in improving classification performance, while also highlighting the dependency of results on the chosen feature subset.

Despite this advantage, handcrafted feature-based approaches also face important challenges. One of the most common issues is the curse of dimensionality, where large feature sets introduce redundancy and may reduce classification performance while increasing computational cost41. To address this problem, many studies combine signal processing techniques, such as the discrete wavelet transform (DWT), with feature selection methods to obtain more compact and informative feature sets42,43. Recent work has also explored advanced signal enhancement and denoising strategies to improve ECG processing pipelines44. However, determining an optimal balance between feature richness and model simplicity remains an open challenge.

Among these methods, wrapper-based feature selection approaches, particularly Sequential Forward Floating Selection (SFFS), have been widely used due to their ability to dynamically include and exclude features during the selection process41. This flexibility helps overcome the limitations of simple greedy methods and enables the identification of more representative feature subsets. Nevertheless, these methods may still involve increased computational cost, especially when applied to large feature spaces.

Another important issue in ECG classification studies is model evaluation. Since class imbalance is common in ECG datasets, relying on a single metric may lead to incomplete or misleading conclusions. For this reason, recent studies have increasingly emphasized the use of more comprehensive evaluation measures, including balanced accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Cohen’s kappa25. In this context, the Golden Distance (GD) metric has been proposed as a comprehensive performance measure that combines multiple evaluation criteria into a single index25. This allows a more balanced comparison of classification models by considering different aspects of performance simultaneously, although its adoption in the literature is still relatively limited.

Finally, although many existing studies report strong classification results, fewer studies provide a systematic explanation of how preprocessing, feature extraction, feature selection, and evaluation stages interact within a unified framework. This gap limits the interpretability and reproducibility of existing approaches and highlights the need for more structured and educational frameworks in ECG-based classification studies.

Materials and methods

The proposed methodology is structured as a four-stage pipeline, as illustrated in Figure 1. In the first stage, ECG signals are acquired from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database and imported into the analysis environment. In the second stage, preprocessing is performed to enhance signal quality, including noise reduction and extraction of QRS complexes and R–R intervals. In the third stage, feature selection is conducted using the Sequential Forward Feature Selection (SFFS) algorithm, which iteratively selects the most informative and non-redundant features based on validation performance. This step reduces feature dimensionality while preserving discriminative information. In the final stage, the selected feature vectors are classified using k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) and support vector machine (SVM) classifiers. Model performance is evaluated using both conventional classification metrics and the Golden Distance (GD) metric, which provides a multidimensional assessment of performance.

MIT-BIH arrhythmia database

The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, hosted on PhysioNet and maintained by MIT’s Computational Physiology Laboratory, is a widely used benchmark dataset for studies on cardiac arrhythmias45. The database contains two synchronously recorded ECG channels, Modified Lead II (MLII) and V1. In this study, only the MLII channel was utilized, as it provides more distinct QRS complexes and is commonly used as a reference in arrhythmia classification, ensuring signal quality and comparability with previous studies.

The database consists of 48 recordings, each approximately 30 minutes in duration, provided as raw .dat files. In addition to the signal data, auxiliary files are available for each recording: .atr files contain heartbeat annotations, .hea files provide header information, and .xws files include waveform-related data (available for some recordings). This structure enables access to both raw ECG signals and corresponding annotation data for detailed analysis.

Educational illustrations: demonstration of the Pan–Tompkins algorithm

Although expert annotations from the MIT-BIH database are used for QRS detection in the main analysis, the Pan–Tompkins algorithm—a well-established method for real-time QRS detection—is presented here for illustrative and educational purposes. A representative ECG segment from Record 105 (MLII channel, first 6,000 samples) is used to demonstrate the key processing stages of the algorithm.

The algorithm detects QRS complexes through a sequence of processing stages. First, band-pass filtering (cascaded high- and low-pass filters) is applied to remove baseline drift and high-frequency noise. The signal is then differentiated to emphasize rapid slope changes, squared to enhance high-amplitude variations, and smoothed using moving-window integration to obtain an energy envelope corresponding to the QRS duration. Finally, adaptive thresholding is employed to identify R-wave locations32. This processing pipeline enables accurate real-time QRS detection and provides a reliable basis for subsequent feature extraction.

For visualization purposes, a single-channel ECG segment (Record 105, MLII channel) was selected as a representative example. A 0.15-second Hanning window was applied during the moving-window integration stage, approximating the typical QRS duration while reducing edge effects. In addition, the minimum distance between consecutive R peaks was set to 0.6 s (corresponding to approximately 100 bpm) to prevent false detections.

R-peaks play a central role in ECG analysis33, serving as reference points for cardiac cycle timing and signal segmentation. The intervals between consecutive R-peaks, known as R–R intervals (RRI), are fundamental for heart rate and rhythm analysis, including the detection of arrhythmia, tachycardia, and bradycardia. In this study, RR intervals and RRI are used interchangeably to represent the time difference between consecutive R-peaks. In addition, R-peaks provide temporal reference points for identifying P, Q, S, and T waves, supporting both morphological and dynamic ECG analysis.

Accurate R-peak detection is therefore essential for reliable feature extraction45, segmentation, and subsequent classification tasks. Segmentation enables the isolation of individual cardiac cycles for detailed analysis, whereas inaccurate segmentation may lead to distorted waveform interpretation and reduced classification performance46.

During segmentation, each R-peak defines the onset of a cardiac cycle, partitioning the ECG signal into individual heartbeat units for morphological analysis, feature extraction, and classification47. The resulting R–R intervals (RRI), defined as the temporal differences between consecutive R-peaks, provide key information for heart rate and rhythm analysis, including the detection of tachycardia, bradycardia, and irregular arrhythmias.

Heart rate (HR) is computed from R–R intervals as their inverse in beats per minute, enabling dynamic assessment of cardiac activity. HR analysis captures both instantaneous rate and rhythm stability, forming the basis for heart rate variability (HRV) analysis and providing temporal references for P, Q, S, and T waves48.

Segmentation based on R-peaks and RRI supports systematic analysis of both morphological and temporal ECG features, forming a foundation for monitoring and classification. In this study, RRI values were calculated from consecutive R-peak positions using both direct index differences and the NumPy diff function, yielding identical results; the diff function was adopted for computational efficiency.

Preprocessing and noise management in cardiac signal processing

ECG analysis requires preprocessing prior to segmentation or feature extraction. This stage aims to remove unwanted components and noise, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of subsequent analyses.

In the signal processing literature, noise is defined as any unwanted component added to a signal during recording, transmission, or processing that obscures the true characteristics of the signal50. In ECG signals, such noise can distort waveform morphology and lead to inaccurate analysis, misinterpretation, and incorrect detection of cardiac events51.

The main types of noise affecting ECG signals include low-frequency baseline drift, high-frequency muscle activity (EMG) noise, powerline interference, and electrode motion artifacts. Baseline drift, typically occurring in the 0–0.5 Hz range, arises from respiratory movements, patient posture, or electrode placement, and can interfere with accurate ST-segment analysis. Muscle activity noise is characterized by high-frequency and irregular components caused by patient movements, tremors, or involuntary contractions, which can hinder the detection of P and T waves. Powerline interference is a sinusoidal disturbance at 50 or 60 Hz, depending on the region, and complicates the analysis of high-frequency components. Electrode motion artifacts result from abrupt changes in electrode–skin contact, adversely affecting signal segmentation and waveform detection52.

In preprocessing, the Fourier transform (FT) is employed to analyze the frequency components of ECG signals and to identify different types of noise53. In the frequency domain54, low-frequency baseline drift can be mitigated using high-pass or band-pass filters, while powerline interference, which appears as a peak at a specific frequency, can be effectively suppressed using a notch filter. High-frequency, irregular muscle noise can be attenuated using a low-pass filter.

In this study, a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.5 Hz was applied to suppress baseline drift, while a low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 20 Hz was used to reduce high-frequency muscle noise while preserving the QRS morphology. Additionally, a notch filter in the 49–51 Hz range was employed to eliminate mains interference. All signals were processed in millivolts (mV), as provided by the database.

In addition to conventional filtering approaches, advanced data-driven denoising techniques have recently gained increasing attention in ECG signal processing44. Methods such as wavelet-based denoising, adaptive filtering, and deep learning-based models (e.g., diffusion probabilistic models) have been shown to provide improved noise suppression while preserving subtle morphological characteristics of ECG signals. However, in this study, a relatively simple and well-established filtering strategy was preferred to maintain interpretability, reproducibility, and consistency with the proposed framework. Nevertheless, the integration of advanced denoising techniques represents a promising direction for future research.

This preprocessing stage produced cleaner and more reliable signals, facilitating the accurate determination of characteristic features. In particular, QRS complex detection is critical for identifying heartbeat timing and serves as the foundation for subsequent feature extraction. In this study, QRS locations were not estimated automatically; instead, they were obtained directly from expert annotations in the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, thereby ensuring high reliability for subsequent analysis and evaluation.

The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database records used in this study were sampled at 360 Hz with 11-bit resolution and a ± 10 mV dynamic range. Filtering was performed using third-order Butterworth high-pass and low-pass filters, applied with the forward–backward zero-phase (filtfilt) method to prevent phase distortion.

Feature extraction in cardiac signal analysis

Clinical information derived from electrocardiography (ECG) signals extends beyond direct inspection of the raw waveform and is significantly enhanced through feature extraction across multiple domains. Feature extraction constitutes a fundamental stage in biomedical signal processing and directly influences the performance of classification and diagnostic algorithms. Typically, features are categorized into three groups: time-domain, frequency-domain, and time–frequency-domain features.

In the time domain, the morphological characteristics of the ECG signal—including the amplitudes, durations, and intervals of the P, QRS, and T waves—can be directly measured and provide critical information about cardiac function38. Frequency-domain analysis examines the distribution of signal energy to extract spectral features associated with physiological processes. However, since ECG signals are inherently non-stationary, analyses limited to either the time or frequency domain may be insufficient. In such cases, time–frequency methods enable the simultaneous examination of temporal and spectral components, providing a more comprehensive representation of the signal.

In addition to time-domain analyses, frequency-domain methods play a crucial role in feature extraction from ECG signals. One of the most widely used approaches is the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)53, which computes the frequency spectrum of the signal and enables the analysis of its energy distribution across different frequency bands. The FFT of a discrete signal x(n) is defined as , which provides a representation of the signal in the frequency domain. The Power Spectral Density (PSD), derived from the FFT, quantitatively represents how the signal’s energy is distributed among its frequency components.

Relative power values—particularly within the very low frequency (VLF), low frequency (LF), and high frequency (HF) bands—are widely used in clinical and analytical studies, as they facilitate comparisons across individuals and signals of varying lengths. However, a fundamental limitation of FFT-based analysis is that the resulting spectrum reflects an average frequency distribution over the entire signal. Consequently, short-term or transient events may be overlooked, limiting the ability of FFT to fully capture the dynamic characteristics of non-stationary biological signals.

Because FFT-based methods are limited in handling non-stationary signals, the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) was developed to analyze time-varying frequency components55. In this approach, the signal is segmented into windows of a specified length, and a Fourier transform is applied to each segment to obtain a time–frequency representation. This allows the observation of temporal variations in the frequency content of the ECG signal and facilitates the analysis of transient events.

A key advantage of STFT is its ability to perform frequency analysis on non-stationary signals56. However, its reliance on a fixed window size introduces an inherent limitation: a wide window improves frequency resolution at the expense of time resolution, whereas a narrow window enhances time resolution but degrades frequency resolution. This time–frequency resolution trade-off is particularly critical in biomedical signal analysis, where both low- and high-frequency components must be accurately characterized simultaneously.

The Wavelet Transform (WT) was developed to overcome the time–frequency resolution limitations of traditional methods. By decomposing the signal across multiple scales and shifts, WT provides high temporal resolution for short-duration, high-frequency components and high frequency resolution for long-duration, low-frequency components. This property makes it particularly well-suited for ECG analysis, enabling the effective detection of rapid events such as QRS complexes as well as slower morphological components, including P and T waves. WT can be implemented as either the Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) or the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT). Although CWT provides a highly detailed time–frequency representation, its high computational cost limits its practical applicability. In contrast, DWT offers a more compact and computationally efficient representation, making it more suitable for feature extraction and classification tasks42.

Because wavelet-based features can be high-dimensional and potentially redundant, dimensionality reduction techniques are commonly employed. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) reduces feature dimensionality by retaining components that capture the majority of the variance, thereby enhancing classification performance while reducing computational complexity and mitigating the risk of overfitting43.

Morphological features extracted from electrocardiography (ECG) signals are derived from the fundamental waveforms of cardiac electrical activity. These features include the amplitudes, durations, and intervals of the P wave, QRS complex, and T wave, and they provide direct clinical insight into cardiac function. For example, the peak amplitude and width of the QRS complex reflect the characteristics of ventricular depolarization, while the P wave corresponds to atrial depolarization and the T wave represents ventricular repolarization. Consequently, morphological features serve as a fundamental reference for identifying cardiac arrhythmias and structural abnormalities57.

Temporal features capture timing patterns and sequential relationships within the ECG signal58. These features quantify correlations and temporal variations between consecutive QRS complexes. For instance, feature C11 represents the maximum correlation between a QRS complex and the subsequent QRS complex, C22 represents the maximum correlation between a QRS complex and the preceding QRS complex, and Cxy denotes the maximum correlation between a QRS complex and reference (template) QRS complexes. Such temporal analyses play a critical role in detecting arrhythmias, particularly short-term rhythm disturbances, and, when combined with morphological features, enhance the discriminative power of classification algorithms59.

To further enhance both morphological and temporal representations, wavelet-based decomposition was applied to the ECG signals. This approach provides high temporal resolution for abrupt events such as QRS complexes, as well as high frequency resolution for slower components such as P and T waves.

In this study, three main feature groups were extracted from the ECG signals: morphological, temporal, and time–frequency (wavelet-based) features. Each recording was processed using the wfdb library, and R-wave positions and class labels were obtained directly from the annotation files. The signals were preprocessed using high-pass and low-pass filters to suppress baseline drift and high-frequency noise. Forward–backward zero-phase filtering (filtfilt) was applied to preserve morphological characteristics while avoiding phase distortion.

As a fundamental step in feature extraction, QRS-centered segments were defined around each R wave. Although the physiological duration of the QRS complex is typically 80–120 ms, a wider window was selected to include buffer regions before and after the QRS complex, ensuring robust extraction of morphological, temporal, and wavelet-based features. Accordingly, fixed-length segments of 200 samples were extracted, consisting of 80 samples before and 120 samples after each R wave.

The pre-R interval ensures that the onset of the Q wave is captured, while the post-R interval preserves the complete S wave and the early transition toward repolarization. This R-centered segmentation strategy is consistent with commonly used approaches in the literature and provides a stable and uniform window for subsequent feature extraction.

In preliminary experiments, several window lengths within the selected range were evaluated. Minor variations in window size did not significantly affect classification performance; however, overly short windows failed to capture essential waveform components, whereas excessively long windows introduced redundant information. Accordingly, the selected window length provides a balanced and robust trade-off between physiological relevance and classification performance.

Temporal features were derived from sequences of consecutive RR intervals. Specifically, three consecutive intervals (RR0, RR1, RR2), their mean value (RRm), interval ratios (RR0/RR1, RR2/RR1, RRm/RR1), and the mean of the preceding 20 RR intervals were used as time-based features. These features capture both short- and long-term rhythm variations associated with arrhythmias and enhance the discriminative capability of the classification algorithm.

In addition to morphological and temporal features, a discrete wavelet transform (DWT) was applied to each QRS segment. Four levels of decomposition were performed using the Daubechies 6 (db6) wavelet, producing detail coefficients that capture short-term, abrupt changes and approximation coefficients that represent slower signal variations. These wavelet coefficients were combined with morphological correlation features to construct the morphological feature vector.

Finally, a unified feature vector was constructed for each R wave by combining morphological, temporal, and wavelet-based features, with corresponding class labels representing the five heartbeat classes. All features were extracted on a per-beat (R-wave–centered) basis, without record-level aggregation or averaging prior to classification. This strategy enhances classification performance by jointly representing morphological characteristics, temporal dynamics, and time–frequency information. The resulting high-dimensional feature set was subsequently optimized using feature selection techniques and employed for model training.

Feature conditioning

Before being fed into the classifier, the extracted features were subjected to a feature conditioning stage. The main objective of this step is to optimize the feature space, improve classification performance, reduce the risk of overfitting, and ensure computational efficiency39.

Feature conditioning generally involves two main approaches: feature selection and feature mapping. In feature selection, features are chosen according to predefined criteria without altering their original structure, and redundant and non-informative features are removed. In contrast, feature mapping transforms the original features into a new space; for example, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) linearly combines features to maximize variance, while Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) focuses on maximizing class separability60. These methods enable high-dimensional data to be represented in a more compact and discriminative form.

In this study, rather than explicitly mapping features into a new space using PCA or LDA, feature scaling and feature selection were employed. Specifically, Sequential Forward Feature Selection (SFFS) was applied to identify an informative subset of features while preserving their original representation.

After feature extraction, feature scaling is a critical step to ensure effective classifier performance. ECG-derived features often differ in units and magnitude; for instance, morphological correlation coefficients and RR intervals can vary substantially in scale. Without scaling, features with larger numerical ranges may dominate the learning process, leading to biased model behavior, particularly in distance-based and gradient-based classifiers.

Feature selection and SFFS

In ECG-based classification studies, feature extraction methods such as wavelet transforms, time–frequency analysis, and morphological measurements typically produce a high-dimensional feature space. This space often includes redundant, highly correlated, or weakly informative features, which can increase sensitivity to noise and lead to overfitting, thereby degrading generalization to unseen data. Feature selection addresses this issue by systematically identifying features that effectively discriminate between classes while preserving complementary information when used jointly54.

Unlike filter-based methods, which assess features individually using statistical criteria, wrapper-based approaches integrate the classifier into the selection process and explicitly evaluate feature interactions and their combined contribution to classification performance. To leverage this advantage, Sequential Forward Feature Selection (SFFS) was employed in this study41. Formally, SFFS aims to identify the optimal feature subset given by

Accordingly, SFFS incrementally constructs an optimal feature subset by eliminating redundant and non-informative features while maximizing classifier performance, resulting in a compact and discriminative feature set with improved generalization capability62.

The structure on the right represents complementary features that collectively form a coherent and meaningful whole, each contributing distinct information to the classification model. In contrast, the structure on the left depicts repeated instances of the same feature type, highlighting redundancy caused by duplicated information. Such repetitive features do not introduce new information, increase computational cost, and may negatively affect model performance. As shown in Figure 6, treating features as “puzzle pieces” schematically demonstrates how redundancy disrupts information integrity, whereas complementary features strengthen the overall model.

Figure 7 illustrates the key differences between filter- and wrapper-based feature selection approaches63. Filter methods rank features using statistical criteria independent of the classifier, offering high computational efficiency but ignoring inter-feature dependencies. In contrast, wrapper methods evaluate feature subsets directly based on classifier performance, explicitly accounting for interactions between features. Using sequential search strategies such as SFFS, wrapper approaches identify feature combinations that maximize classification performance, albeit at the cost of increased computational complexity.

In the proposed study, the SFFS method starts with an empty feature set. At each iteration, features that have not yet been selected are evaluated by adding them individually to the current set. For each candidate addition, the model is trained and the resulting classification accuracy on the validation set is computed from the confusion matrix. The feature whose inclusion yields the greatest improvement in validation accuracy is then added to the selected set and removed from the search space. This procedure is repeated until the predefined max_iter criterion is reached. Consequently, the model incrementally identifies the features that contribute most to validation performance, and the final feature subset used for classification consists of those that achieve the highest accuracy.

The k-Nearest Neighbor (k-NN) algorithm is a heuristic classification method that assigns class labels based on geometric proximity in the feature space64. Its fundamental assumption is that samples with similar characteristics are located close to each other. The class of a new sample is determined by the majority label among its k nearest neighbors in the training set. In this study, Euclidean distance was used, as it is widely preferred for continuous and normalized biomedical features. Since k-NN does not involve an explicit training phase, model performance is directly influenced by the structure of the feature space and the selected features. Accordingly, the compact and information-dense feature subsets obtained via SFFS enhance k-NN performance by clarifying neighborhood relationships. For this reason, k-NN was adopted as a simple yet effective benchmark classifier for pattern-oriented tasks such as ECG arrhythmia classification65.

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) are margin-based classifiers that aim to determine the optimal decision boundary (hyperplane) that maximizes the separation between classes66. Through kernel functions, SVMs can transform nonlinear classification problems into linearly separable ones in a higher-dimensional feature space. In this study, the radial basis function (RBF) kernel was employed, as it provides a smooth and flexible decision surface capable of modeling local similarities and within-class variations commonly observed in biomedical signals. SVMs are inherently resistant to overfitting because the decision boundary is determined only by a subset of critical samples known as support vectors. This property, combined with the information-dense feature subsets selected by SFFS, enables an effective representation of morphological and time–frequency differences between classes67. Accordingly, SVM was used as a complementary classifier to contrast margin-based decision behavior with the distance-based k-NN approach.

The k-NN and SVM models were employed as complementary classification approaches because they rely on fundamentally different decision mechanisms. The k-NN classifier assigns class labels based on geometric proximity in the feature space; therefore, the number of neighbors, which is its primary hyperparameter, was initially set to k = 3, a commonly adopted baseline in studies involving small and heterogeneous biomedical datasets. Euclidean distance was chosen as the similarity metric because it effectively reflects neighborhood relationships in normalized continuous feature spaces. The influence of varying the number of neighbors within the range k = 3 to k = 11 on classification performance is examined separately in the Results section68. In addition, the compact and information-dense feature subsets obtained through SFFS improve the definition of neighborhood structures in the feature space, thereby enhancing the spatial discrimination capability of the k-NN classifier.

SVM learns a decision boundary that maximizes the margin between classes, and the RBF kernel was used to provide a flexible representation for nonlinear patterns. The kernel parameter γ was set to auto, and the regularization coefficient C was set to 1000 as initial values, based on ranges commonly reported in the literature. The impact of a wider hyperparameter range on model performance is further evaluated in the Results section69. The decision mechanism of SVM, which relies only on support vectors, combined with informative feature subsets selected by SFFS, enables a more accurate representation of morphological and time–frequency characteristics across classes. Together with k-NN, this approach allows the data space to be analyzed from both geometric proximity and margin-based separation perspectives, providing a comprehensive view of the multiclass arrhythmia classification problem.

The performance of all classification models was evaluated using a repeated holdout validation scheme70. Data splitting was performed at the beat level, where ECG beats were randomly divided into training and test sets without explicit patient-level grouping. Although beat-level splitting may introduce subject overlap between training and test sets, this study focuses on evaluating feature representation and selection behavior under a controlled experimental setting rather than subject-independent generalization. The dataset was initially partitioned into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets. To guide the SFFS procedure, the training set was further divided into an internal training subset (70% of the training data) and a validation subset (30%). Feature selection was conducted exclusively using this internal train–validation split. The optimal feature subset was then used to retrain the model on the full training set. Final performance evaluation was performed on the held-out test set, which remained completely unseen during feature selection and model optimization.

This validation strategy was adopted to ensure a clear separation between feature selection, model development, and final testing, thereby preventing information leakage during the SFFS process. Although cross-validation is widely used, it was not preferred in this study because the objective is to provide a clear and reproducible comparison under a controlled experimental setup. In addition, repeated cross-validation combined with wrapper-based feature selection would significantly increase computational cost without providing additional meaningful insight into model behavior.

The computational complexity of the proposed framework is primarily governed by three stages: feature extraction, feature selection, and classification. Feature extraction is performed on a per-beat basis and scales linearly with the number of ECG beats and the number of extracted features. The SFFS stage represents the most computationally demanding component, as it iteratively evaluates candidate feature subsets using an internal validation set. To control this cost, the maximum number of iterations and the step size were deliberately constrained. The final classification stage using k-NN and SVM introduces relatively low computational overhead compared to feature selection, particularly during inference. Overall, the proposed pipeline exhibits manageable computational complexity and is well suited for offline analysis and educational benchmarking purposes.

Performance evaluation

Because the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database exhibits a highly imbalanced class distribution, performance assessment was not based solely on overall accuracy. Instead, balanced accuracy, Cohen’s kappa, and macro-averaged performance metrics were emphasized to ensure fair evaluation across both majority and minority arrhythmia classes. Within this framework, k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) and support vector machine (SVM) classifiers were implemented and analyzed to examine how different decision mechanisms affect classification performance when identical feature representations are used for ECG-based arrhythmia detection.

In the first stage, a confusion matrix was constructed to analyze correct and incorrect classifications in the test set on a per-class basis. Using this matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score were computed for each class71, and their macro-averaged counterparts (Macro-Precision, Macro-Recall, and Macro-F1) were obtained to summarize class-wise performance. Given the pronounced class imbalance in the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, balanced accuracy was adopted to ensure equal weighting of sensitivity and specificity across classes, thereby preventing dominant majority classes from masking minority-class performance. In addition, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was employed to account for agreement occurring by chance, providing a more reliable measure of classifier consistency beyond raw accuracy72. Together, these metrics enable a comprehensive evaluation of both overall performance and class discrimination capability.

To obtain a more robust and reliable estimate of model generalization, repeated holdout validation was adopted using 10 independent random splits. For each split, the complete pipeline, including feature selection, classifier training, and evaluation, was re-executed. The resulting performance metrics were then aggregated and reported as mean ± standard deviation. This approach explicitly quantifies the variability introduced by data partitioning and provides a more reliable assessment of model stability and generalization.

Although multiple classifier configurations are compared, formal statistical significance testing was not included. Instead, model behavior was interpreted through repeated holdout validation, class-balanced performance metrics, confusion matrix analysis, and the Golden Distance (GD) framework.

In the second stage of performance analysis, the Golden Distance (GD) method was employed to condense multiple evaluation metrics into a single summary value. Metrics defined on the same scale are mapped into a multidimensional space using polar coordinates to form a performance polygon. From this polygon, two quantities are derived: the normalized area value (NAV), representing overall performance, and the distance of the centroid from the origin (DCO), reflecting the balance among metrics. A higher NAV indicates stronger performance, whereas a lower DCO indicates a more balanced distribution.

To eliminate the dependency on metric ordering, NAV and DCO are averaged over all possible permutations of the evaluation metrics. The Golden Distance (GD) is then defined as the Euclidean distance between the averaged coordinates and the ideal reference point (1, 0):

A lower GD value indicates a model that achieves both high overall performance and a balanced distribution across evaluation metrics.

This evaluation enables an objective comparison of the effects of SFFS-selected feature subsets on the classifiers and provides a multidimensional perspective on model strengths and weaknesses. The Results section presents the detailed metric values and corresponding GD outputs and discusses the observed performance differences. Since GD is constructed from multiple normalized metrics and reflects their joint geometric structure, small variations in individual metrics result in gradual rather than abrupt changes in GD. It is important to note that GD is intended for relative comparisons within the same experimental setup, and direct comparison of absolute GD values across different studies is not recommended unless identical datasets, metrics, and normalization procedures are employed.

Results

This section presents the experimental results of the proposed ECG arrhythmia classification framework, emphasizing how preprocessing, feature representation, feature selection, and classifier design collectively influence classification performance. Rather than focusing solely on maximizing accuracy, the analysis aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of how methodological choices at each stage affect class discrimination, robustness, and overall model behavior.

The proposed framework was evaluated using two different feature representations: (i) a handcrafted feature set composed of morphological and temporal descriptors, and (ii) a compact representation in which morphological features were transformed into a wavelet-based form while preserving temporal descriptors. For both representations, the most informative feature subsets were selected using Sequential Forward Feature Selection (SFFS), and classifier performance was evaluated. The evaluation metrics include total accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro precision, macro recall, macro F1-score, Cohen’s kappa, confusion matrices, ROC analysis, and the Golden Distance (GD) metric as a holistic indicator of overall performance.

The first feature representation consists of 203 morphological and 8 temporal features, capturing QRS morphology, amplitude variations, duration characteristics, and temporal dynamics. In the second representation, the morphological component is transformed into a wavelet-based form, reducing it to 47 features while preserving the same 8 temporal descriptors. This transformation compresses the high-dimensional and partially correlated morphological feature space into a more compact and informative representation, reducing dimensionality and computational complexity while retaining discriminative time–frequency characteristics.

To identify compact and informative feature subsets, Sequential Forward Feature Selection (SFFS) was applied using validation-set accuracy as the selection criterion. The impact of subset size was analyzed progressively, and the optimal operating point was determined based on the saturation behavior of validation performance.

Discussion

In this study, the multi-class ECG arrhythmia classification problem is addressed within a holistic framework that aims not only to achieve high accuracy but also to provide a clear and systematic understanding of how signal processing, feature extraction, feature selection, and model evaluation stages influence classification performance. Although very high accuracy rates have been reported on the same datasets in the literature, many of these approaches exhibit reduced interpretability and methodological transparency as model complexity increases34.

In contrast, the proposed framework prioritizes methodological clarity, analytical consistency, and interpretability alongside performance, offering a balanced approach to ECG classification.

The results indicate that morphological and temporal features alone provide strong discriminative capability. However, incorporating wavelet-based representations, which transform morphological information into a more compact and decorrelated form, leads to a significant reduction in feature dimensionality and a more consistent and balanced classification performance. This observation is in agreement with previous studies demonstrating the effectiveness of time–frequency representations in capturing the complex structure of ECG signals35.

The SFFS-based feature selection results in this study show that classification performance saturates at approximately 15 features for both classifiers, indicating that increasing feature dimensionality beyond this point does not yield meaningful performance gains and may introduce redundancy. This observation is consistent with previous studies on the MIT-BIH dataset, which report that a compact subset of morphological and temporal features can achieve high accuracy and stable performance73.

Although feature selection stability across multiple data splits was not explicitly analyzed, the consistent saturation behavior observed for both k-NN and SVM suggests that the selected feature dimensionality reflects an inherent structure of the feature space rather than being dependent on a specific partitioning of the data.

Furthermore, the results indicate that the majority of discriminative information is concentrated within a relatively small subset of features, supporting the effectiveness of SFFS in identifying informative and non-redundant feature combinations. This compact representation not only reduces computational complexity but also contributes to improved generalization by avoiding overfitting associated with high-dimensional feature spaces. In this context, the observed stability across repeated holdout evaluations further reinforces the reliability of the selected feature subset.

Comparative analysis of the k-NN and SVM classifiers shows that both achieve high and stable performance on the SFFS-selected feature space. For k-NN, the best results are obtained at a low neighborhood size (k = 3), while increasing k leads to a gradual decline in total accuracy, macro-level metrics, and Cohen’s kappa. This behavior suggests that larger neighborhood sizes introduce oversmoothing in the decision boundaries, thereby reducing the influence of locally discriminative patterns. Consequently, preserving local structure appears to be critical for maintaining inter-class separability in the proposed feature space.

In contrast, SVM demonstrates consistently high performance across different hyperparameter configurations, with only minor variations in accuracy and macro-level metrics. This behavior suggests that the learned decision boundaries are relatively robust to moderate changes in model parameters. Such stability can be attributed to the compact and well-separated structure of the selected feature space, which enables SVM to effectively exploit global class boundaries.

Although both classifiers achieve comparable performance levels, SVM generally provides slightly higher accuracy and improved stability, particularly when combined with the wavelet-enhanced feature representation. This suggests that, while local neighborhood information is sufficient for effective classification, margin-based methods such as SVM can more effectively utilize the global geometry of the feature space, leading to more stable and reliable decision boundaries.

For SVM, variations in the regularization parameter C and the kernel parameter γ result in only minor changes in performance, indicating that the SFFS-selected feature space provides strong intrinsic discrimination among arrhythmia classes. The consistently high balanced accuracy, macro F1-score, and Cohen’s kappa further suggest that the performance is not significantly affected by class imbalance or chance agreement.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed feature extraction and selection strategy produces a compact and well-separated feature space, enabling both distance-based and margin-based classifiers to achieve reliable and stable classification performance.

A key observation is that the confusion matrix obtained using the SFFS-selected wavelet-based feature subset exhibits a strong diagonal structure with minimal off-diagonal errors. This indicates that the selected feature representation effectively preserves inter-class separability.

These findings suggest that, once a compact and well-separated feature space is established, classification performance is largely driven by the discriminative power of the features. Under such conditions, different classical classifiers can achieve comparable decision behavior, although slight differences in performance and stability may still arise.

The holistic evaluation using the GD metric further confirms that the models achieve not only high accuracy but also balanced and stable performance across multiple evaluation criteria. Unlike many studies that focus primarily on overall accuracy or F1-score, the GD framework provides a multidimensional and more comprehensive assessment of classifier performance. These findings demonstrate that classical machine learning approaches remain a strong and interpretable option for arrhythmia classification on the MIT-BIH dataset.

Instead of maximizing accuracy at the expense of complexity, it focuses on achieving a balanced performance across multiple evaluation metrics, as confirmed by the Golden Distance (GD) analysis.

These results highlight that carefully designed feature-based approaches remain a strong and practical alternative for ECG arrhythmia classification, particularly in scenarios where interpretability, efficiency, and reproducibility are critical.

Despite the promising results, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the evaluation was conducted on a single benchmark dataset (MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to datasets with different acquisition conditions or class distributions. Nevertheless, the proposed feature extraction and SFFS-based selection pipeline is not inherently dataset-specific and can be adapted to other annotated ECG datasets with appropriate preprocessing.

Second, although a repeated holdout strategy with an internal validation split was employed to reduce bias, alternative validation approaches such as k-fold cross-validation could further mitigate dependency on specific data partitions, albeit at the cost of increased computational complexity.

Third, feature extraction and selection were performed in an offline setting, and real-time implementation aspects were not considered. Finally, while classical machine learning models were preferred to emphasize interpretability and methodological transparency, comparisons with deep learning-based approaches were not included and represent an important direction for future research.

Future work

The proposed framework offers several advantages, including interpretability through handcrafted and wavelet-based features, a compact and computationally efficient representation enabled by SFFS, and stable classification performance across different classifiers and evaluation metrics. However, certain limitations should also be acknowledged. The evaluation was conducted on a single benchmark dataset, and the generalizability of the results to different datasets or real-world clinical settings remains to be further validated. In addition, comparisons with deep learning-based approaches were not included, and real-time implementation aspects were not considered. These limitations motivate several directions for future research.

The findings of this study highlight the critical role of feature extraction and selection in determining classification performance. Future research may focus on enriching the feature space by incorporating additional descriptors such as entropy, fractal dimension, and nonlinear complexity measures to better capture the intrinsic dynamics of ECG signals. Integrating these descriptors with morphological, temporal, and wavelet-based features may further enhance discrimination, particularly for arrhythmia classes with subtle inter-class differences.

A systematic comparison between SFFS and alternative feature selection strategies, including heuristic, evolutionary, and embedded approaches, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the trade-offs between performance, dimensionality, and computational complexity. In particular, investigating embedded feature selection mechanisms within classifiers may offer deeper insight into the interaction between feature relevance and decision boundary formation.

In addition, evaluating lightweight deep learning architectures on the SFFS-selected feature space would enable a direct comparison between classical machine learning and deep learning paradigms. Approaches such as CNNs and RNNs have demonstrated promising results in ECG arrhythmia classification; however, they typically require larger datasets and higher computational resources. Future studies could explore the application of such models on diverse ECG datasets to enable automatic feature extraction while maintaining interpretability and reproducibility.

Finally, validating the proposed framework on multiple ECG datasets with varying acquisition conditions and class distributions is essential to assess robustness and generalizability. Incorporating practical considerations such as computational cost, memory requirements, and decision latency will also be important for evaluating the clinical applicability of the approach in real-world deployment scenarios.

Conclusion

This study presents a holistic and interpretable framework for multi-class ECG arrhythmia classification, emphasizing the combined impact of signal processing, feature extraction, feature selection, and evaluation strategies on overall performance. Rather than focusing solely on accuracy, the proposed approach demonstrates how methodological choices at each stage influence classification outcomes.

The results show that morphological and temporal features provide strong baseline discrimination, while wavelet-based representations enable a more compact and decorrelated feature space, leading to improved balance and stability with reduced dimensionality. The SFFS-based feature selection further reveals that a small subset of features is sufficient to achieve high and stable performance, indicating that increasing dimensionality does not necessarily improve generalization.

Comparative analysis of k-NN and SVM indicates that, once a well-structured feature space is established, classical classifiers achieve reliable and consistent performance, with SVM providing slightly improved stability. The use of complementary evaluation metrics, including balanced accuracy, Cohen’s kappa, and the Golden Distance (GD), enables a comprehensive assessment beyond overall accuracy.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that classical machine learning approaches, when supported by carefully designed feature engineering and selection, remain a powerful, transparent, and computationally efficient solution for ECG arrhythmia classification. The proposed framework also offers a reproducible and interpretable reference for future studies aiming to balance performance, efficiency, and methodological clarity. Future work will focus on enriching the feature space with additional descriptors and validating the proposed framework across diverse ECG datasets to further assess its robustness and generalizability.
