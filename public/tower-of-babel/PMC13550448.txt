Secure task offloading framework for industrial edge computing using reconfigurable intelligent surfaces and spectrum agility
Authors: Asma A. Alhashmi, Wajdan Al Malwi, Monir Abdullah, Abed Saif Ahmed Alghawli, Tareq M. Alkhaldi, Khalid N. R. Alharbi, Ahmed Alshammari, Abdulbasit A. Darem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dense Industry 5.0 deployments require IIoT task offloading schemes that are simultaneously scalable, low latency, and resilient to wireless eavesdropping under harsh industrial blockage. This paper proposes a unified secure offloading framework that integrates reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS), frequency hopping spread spectrum (FHSS), and uplink nonorthogonal multiple access (NOMA) within a multiple MEC architecture. We formulate a mixed integer nonconvex max min problem that jointly optimizes device server association, computation allocation, transmit power, FHSS carrier selection, and RIS phase shifts to maximize the minimum secrecy rate while satisfying strict end to end latency and minimum rate constraints. To solve the resulting coupled design, we develop a block coordinate descent algorithm with successive convex approximation, yielding tractable subproblems and a monotonic improvement of the objective. Simulations show that the proposed design improves the minimum secrecy rate by 18.75%, reduces average latency by 41%, and improves energy efficiency by up to 30% relative to five benchmarks, including traditional NOMA, random RIS phases, fixed power allocation, no FHSS, and conventional OMA. These results highlight the benefits of jointly exploiting spatial control (RIS), spectral agility (FHSS), and massive access (NOMA) for secure and reliable IIoT offloading in obstructed industrial environments. Furthermore, by ensuring robust connectivity in these harsh settings, this research directly supports the digital transformation of the mining sector, facilitating secure IoT safety monitoring and automated extraction systems in alignment with Saudi Vision 2030.

Introduction

The adoption of the automated industrial systems relies on deploying the large numbers of Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) devices1; these nodes facilitate real-time monitoring and data acquisition2; and they are fundamentally limited by constrained computational capabilities and energy resources. To bridge this gap; Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) serves as a practical architecture, and it allows the IIoT nodes to offload the tasks to nearby edge servers; this offloading mechanism successfully reduces ongoing processing latency3. However; securing the IIoT-MEC communication links remains a primary challenge in the factory environments; dense layouts; metallic structures, and heavy machinery create the Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS) conditions, and they cause severe signal attenuation4,5. Simultaneously; the broadcast nature of wireless channel exposes transmissions to the passive eavesdropping6,7; these operational factors combine to lower link reliability, and they introduce the significant vulnerabilities within dense deployments. These constraints are particularly visible in case of the smart mining systems; both the underground and open-pit operations depend on secure, low-latency connectivity; and they require this reliable infrastructure to run the worker-safety monitoring, the equipment coordination; and remote process control.

To tackle these physical-layer vulnerabilities, various technologies have been investigated in recent years. Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RISs) offer controllable manipulation of the propagation environment via adaptive phase adjustments, significantly improving coverage and link quality8,9. Frequency-Hopping Spread Spectrum (FHSS) boosts resilience against interception and jamming by rapidly hopping transmission frequencies10. At the same time Non-Orthogonal Multiple Access (NOMA) improves spectral efficiency by letting multiple devices share time-frequency resources11,12. Although effective on their own, the joint design of these techniques for secure uplink transmission is still largely unexplored . Furthermore, most existing IIoT-MEC literature assumes a single-MEC-server architecture. These localized designs are bottlenecked by finite communication and computation resources, making them insufficient for the scalability demands of dense industrial networks13,14. Transitioning to multi-MEC architectures introduces a new set of challenges ; specifically, device-server association, coordinated resource allocation and the fundamental need to ensure physical-layer security without violating strict latency constraints.

Handling these Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) constraints has prompted recent literature to propose sophisticated spatial diversity and interference management strategies. For instance, cell-free massive Multiple-Input Multiple- Output (MIMO) architectures have been combined with graph-theory-based user grouping to mitigate pilot contamination and maximize weighted sum rates15. Rigorous statistical delay analysis via stochastic network calculus has also been applied to distributed architectures to establish tight bounds on delay violation probabilities16. Beyond spatial macro-diversity the integration of RISs has proven to be a transformative approach for balancing strict delay requirements with device energy efficiency. As shown in recent multi-IRS aided Internet of Video Things (IoVT) frameworks, dynamically coupling RIS passive beamforming with power control can drastically cut long-term network power consumption while still guaranteeing delay satisfaction17.

Building upon these structural insights into delay-constrained networks and motivated by the gaps identified above–this paper focuses on the joint design of RIS-assisted FHSS-NOMA uplink transmission, device-server association and computation resource allocation within a multi-MEC IIoT network. Specifically, we aim to maximize the worst-case uplink secrecy performance under stringent latency constraints, directly addressing the following research questions :

Related work

For the sake of simplicity and readability, we divide the related work section into thematic subsections that illustrate the evolution of the field.

Single vs. multiple MEC servers

To provide computational resources for low-power Internet of Things (IoT)/IIoT devices, extensive research has investigated MEC-based offloading and resource allocation. Early studies primarily considered single-MEC-server architectures13,14,18,19, where the devices offload their tasks to a single nearby edge server. More recent studies have shifted toward multi-MEC deployments20,21; these architectures improve scalability by distributing the computational workload across multiple servers. However, the multi-MEC setting introduces a fundamental challenge that is often not adequately addressed: efficient device–server association. Specifically, each device must be assigned to an appropriate MEC server under coupled communication–computation constraints; otherwise, suboptimal association decisions may overload some servers, reduce the offloading efficiency, and ultimately degrade the latency performance.

In this work, we explicitly incorporate the device–MEC association into the optimization problem to enable scalable task offloading in dense IIoT networks. Beyond scalability, security also constitutes a critical challenge in MEC-enabled IIoT networks; this is particularly important in dense industrial environments, where wireless transmissions are highly vulnerable to passive eavesdropping.

Physical-layer security in MEC-enabled IIoT

From various perspectives, security in MEC-enabled IIoT networks has been investigated. Higher-layer approaches, such as the blockchain-assisted edge computing framework in22, enhance authentication, data integrity, and trust management, but do not address vulnerabilities inherent to the wireless transmission medium, including eavesdropping and channel interception. Other works have focused on computation offloading to improve latency and energy efficiency, for example through fuzzy-logic-based schemes in vehicular edge computing23. However, these studies do not consider security or physical-layer threats and are not tailored to industrial IIoT environments. Economic and strategic aspects of MEC resource utilization have also been explored; for instance, Ling et al.24 analyzed blockchain mining offloading using game-theoretic models, without addressing wireless security or latency-critical IIoT requirements.

More closely related, RIS-assisted MEC systems have been investigated for physical-layer security. Gao et al.25 studied secure task offloading in aerial-RIS-assisted MEC networks by jointly optimizing reflection, mobility, and resource allocation. Nevertheless, such works focus on Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV)-mounted RISs and single-server settings, and do not consider dense industrial deployments with multiple MEC servers, advanced multiple access, or spectrum-level security mechanisms. In contrast, this work targets physical-layer security in dense IIoT networks by integrating RIS-assisted propagation control with secure uplink transmission in a multi-MEC architecture. The proposed framework jointly optimizes device–MEC association and communication–computation resources under strict latency constraints, while incorporating spectrum-agile mechanisms such as frequency hopping to further enhance resistance against eavesdropping. Related physical-layer security studies26 have addressed eavesdropping and jamming through secure beamforming and cooperative transmission in air–terrestrial integrated ad-hoc networks. However, such works do not consider MEC-enabled IIoT architectures, task offloading, or latency-constrained edge computing.

NOMA- and RIS-assisted MEC networks

NOMA has been widely recognized for its ability to improve the spectral efficiency and support massive connectivity in MEC-enabled IIoT networks. Chen et al.27 investigated integrated NOMA-based MEC architectures and demonstrated clear gains in spectral efficiency and connectivity. Related studies28,29 further showed that the combination of NOMA and MEC can improve both energy efficiency and computational performance. Learning-based solutions have also been explored; for example, Wang et al.30 and Ju et al.31 employed reinforcement learning to reduce latency and enhance the data-offloading efficiency in NOMA-enabled MEC systems.

The secure offloading in the NOMA-based MEC networks was examined in32; where the physical-layer constraints were incorporated to mitigate the eavesdropping. Despite these advances; the NOMA-based MEC systems still face the challenges in the interference management and the secure transmission; especially in the multi-cell and blockage-prone environments. To address these issues; the RISs have been integrated with the NOMA-MEC frameworks. Truong et al.9 demonstrated the effectiveness of the STAR-RIS-assisted MEC with the uplink NOMA; meanwhile; the RIS-assisted MEC networks were further investigated for the energy-efficient scheduling and the latency reduction in33. Cooperative NOMA-MEC systems integrating RIS and Simultaneous Wireless Information and Power Transfer (SWIPT) were studied in34; additional works35,36 also highlighted the benefits of RIS-NOMA integration for resource allocation and energy efficiency. Nevertheless, most existing studies primarily focus on performance optimization and do not jointly address physical-layer security, multi-MEC architectures, and spectrum-level interference mitigation. In particular, the integration of RIS-assisted NOMA with spectrum-agile mechanisms, such as FHSS, for secure multi-MEC IIoT networks remains largely unexplored.

Furthermore, recent studies have advanced NOMA-assisted MEC designs for latency-sensitive applications, including multi-Base Station (BS) MEC and IoVT task-offloading scenarios37,38. While these works improve the delay performance and resource allocation, they generally do not jointly consider physical-layer secrecy, FHSS-based spectrum agility, RIS phase optimization, and multi-MEC association within a unified industrial offloading framework. Moreover, scaling NOMA in ultra-dense low-latency networks remains challenging, particularly under finite-blocklength operation39. These limitations motivate future extensions toward Rate-Splitting Multiple Access (RSMA); by splitting messages into common and private streams, RSMA provides additional interference-management flexibility, although this comes at the cost of higher decoding and rate-allocation complexity40.

Positioning of This Work and Practical Constraints: The review above indicates that existing MEC-enabled IIoT studies typically address only a subset of the requirements encountered in dense industrial deployments; single-MEC architectures lack scalability, and many multi-MEC works do not explicitly optimize device-server association under coupled communication and computation constraints. Moreover, security-oriented studies often focus on higher-layer mechanisms, or they consider physical-layer security in settings that do not incorporate IIoT task offloading and strict end-to-end delay requirements. Although NOMA- and RIS-assisted MEC designs have been explored to improve spectral efficiency and coverage, their joint design for secrecy-aware uplink offloading in multi-MEC IIoT networks remains limited; importantly, practical constraints that arise naturally in industrial offloading systems are rarely enforced simultaneously.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, existing related works do not jointly account for: (i) finite-horizon operation with time-slotted decisions and frame-averaged secrecy performance; (ii) discrete spectrum access decisions, such as carrier selection or hopping, and the resulting collision-induced interference coupling among devices; (iii) max-min secrecy fairness against multi-antenna passive eavesdroppers under a conservative decoding assumption; and (iv) communication-computation coupled latency constraints that explicitly combine transmission delay and edge execution delay with per-server Central Processing Unit (CPU) capacity limits, alongside binary single-server association.

Motivated by these gaps, this paper investigates a unified RIS-assisted FHSS-NOMA uplink framework for secure task offloading in dense multi-MEC IIoT networks; the proposed formulation jointly optimizes device-MEC association, transmit power control, spectrum selection, RIS phase shifts, and computation resource allocation to maximize the worst-case secrecy rate while satisfying strict latency and minimum rate constraints. In smart mining deployments, these requirements become even more critical, as IIoT communication must remain secure and delay-bounded across autonomous haulage, extraction systems, and mineral-processing infrastructures operating in obstruction-prone environments.

The RIS comprises Q passive elements, each capable of independently adjusting the phase of the reflected signal. In time slot t, the RIS configuration is described by the diagonal phase-shift matrix:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\Theta }(t) \triangleq \operatorname {diag}\!\big (\rho e^{\textrm{i}\theta _1(t)},\dots ,\rho e^{\textrm{i}\theta _Q(t)}\big ) \in \mathbb {C}^{Q \times Q}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _q(t) \in [0, 2\pi )$$\end{document} is the phase shift introduced by the q-th element, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \in [0,1]$$\end{document} denotes the reflection efficiency (assumed constant and identical for all elements). The effective composite channels, incorporating both direct and RIS-reflected paths, are therefore: 3a\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{H}_{i,k}(t)&\triangleq \boldsymbol{h}_{i,k}(t) + \boldsymbol{G}^{(r)}_k(t) \boldsymbol{\Theta }(t) \boldsymbol{h}^{(r)}_i(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}3b\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{H}^{(e)}_{i,j}(t)&\triangleq \boldsymbol{h}^{(e)}_{i,j}(t) + \boldsymbol{G}^{(r,e)}_j(t) \boldsymbol{\Theta }(t) \boldsymbol{h}^{(r)}_i(t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document} These expressions capture the coherent superposition of the direct and RIS-reflected signals; this enables both signal enhancement at intended receivers and strategic degradation at potential eavesdroppers through intelligent phase-shift design.

Unless otherwise stated, the optimization treats the channel matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{H}$$\end{document} as inputs, and it remains completely agnostic to the specific channel generator. In the numerical evaluation, the baseline setup uses frequency-flat Rician block fading with distance-dependent path loss; additionally, a separate robustness comparison instantiates 3GPP TR 38.901 Urban Micro (UMi) and Indoor Factory - Sparse Clutter, Low Base Station (InF-SL) channel models to thoroughly assess performance under urban and harsh industrial propagation conditions.

Signal model and signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) characterization

Device i transmits a complex symbol \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i(t)$$\end{document} with unit power (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[|x_i(t)|^2] = 1$$\end{document}) using transmit power \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i(t)$$\end{document} over the selected subcarrier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_i(t)$$\end{document}. The thermal noise at both MEC servers and eavesdroppers is modeled as circularly symmetric complex Gaussian with zero mean and covariance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ^2 \boldsymbol{I}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma ^2 = N_0 B$$\end{document} is the noise power per subcarrier, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_0$$\end{document} the noise power spectral density and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B = B_{\textrm{total}}/F$$\end{document} the subcarrier bandwidth.

To characterize co-channel interference, we define a collision indicator:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} 1\!\!1_{m,i}(t) \triangleq {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 1, & \text {if } f_m(t) = f_i(t), \\ 0, & \text {otherwise}. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The received signal at MEC server k in slot t is then:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{y}_k(t) = {\sum }_{m=1}^{M} a_{m,k} \, 1\!\!1_{m,i}(t) \sqrt{P_m(t)} \, \boldsymbol{H}_{m,k}(t) x_m(t) + \boldsymbol{n}_k(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{n}_k(t) \sim \mathscr{C}\mathscr{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma ^2 \boldsymbol{I}_N)$$\end{document}. Assuming perfect CSI at the MEC receiver, we employ MRC to maximize the SINR for the desired device. The combining vector for device i at MEC k is:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{w}_{i,k}(t) \triangleq \frac{\boldsymbol{H}_{i,k}(t)}{\Vert \boldsymbol{H}_{i,k}(t)\Vert }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}After applying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{w}_{i,k}(t)$$\end{document}, the instantaneous uplink SINR for device i at its associated MEC server k becomes:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \gamma _{i,k}(t) = \frac{a_{i,k} P_i(t) \Vert \boldsymbol{H}_{i,k}(t)\Vert ^2}{\sigma ^2 + \sum \limits _{m \in \mathscr {M}\setminus \{i\}} a_{m,k} P_m(t) \left| \boldsymbol{w}_{i,k}^H(t) \boldsymbol{H}_{m,k}(t)\right| ^2 1\!\!1_{m,i}(t)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The numerator represents the desired signal power after coherent combining, while the denominator comprises the noise power and the interference from other devices that are: (a) associated with the same MEC server k, and (b) transmitting on the same subcarrier in slot t.

Similarly, the received signal at eavesdropper j is:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{y}_{e,j}(t) = {\sum }_{m=1}^{M} 1\!\!1_{m,i}(t) \sqrt{P_m(t)} \, \boldsymbol{H}^{(e)}_{m,j}(t) x_m(t) + \boldsymbol{n}_{e,j}(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{n}_{e,j}(t) \sim \mathscr{C}\mathscr{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma ^2 \boldsymbol{I}_{E_{\textrm{Rx}}})$$\end{document}. Adopting a conservative worst-case assumption, we presume the eavesdropper can also perform MRC using:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}(t) \triangleq \frac{\boldsymbol{H}^{(e)}_{i,j}(t)}{\Vert \boldsymbol{H}^{(e)}_{i,j}(t)\Vert }, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}yielding the eavesdropper SINR:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \gamma ^{(e)}_{i,j}(t) = \frac{P_i(t) \Vert \boldsymbol{H}^{(e)}_{i,j}(t)\Vert ^2}{\sigma ^2 + \sum \limits _{m \in \mathscr {M}\setminus \{i\}} P_m(t) \left| \boldsymbol{u}_{i,j}^H(t) \boldsymbol{H}^{(e)}_{m,j}(t)\right| ^2 1\!\!1_{m,i}(t)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This formulation provides a rigorous lower bound on the achievable secrecy performance, as it grants the eavesdropper optimal linear processing capabilities and knowledge of the same collision patterns as the legitimate receiver.

Finite-horizon achievable rates and secrecy metric

The instantaneous achievable rates (in bits per second) in slot t are given by Shannon’s formula applied to the respective SINRs: 11a\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R_{i,k}(t)&\triangleq B \log _2\!\big (1 + \gamma _{i,k}(t)\big ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}11b\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R^{(e)}_{i,j}(t)&\triangleq B \log _2\!\big (1 + \gamma ^{(e)}_{i,j}(t)\big ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document} To capture performance over the entire frame, we define the average rates: 12a\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \bar{R}_{i,k}&\triangleq \frac{1}{T} {\sum }_{t=1}^{T} R_{i,k}(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}12b\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \bar{R}^{(e)}_{i,j}&\triangleq \frac{1}{T} {\sum }_{t=1}^{T} R^{(e)}_{i,j}(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{R}_{i,k}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{R}^{(e)}_{i,j}$$\end{document} denote the frame-averaged rates for the legitimate and eavesdropping links, respectively. Assuming non-cooperative eavesdroppers, the secrecy rate is limited by the most capable eavesdropper. Therefore, the finite-horizon achievable secrecy rate for device i when associated with MEC server k is:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \bar{SR}_{i,k} \triangleq \left[ \bar{R}_{i,k} - \max _{j \in \mathscr {E}} \bar{R}^{(e)}_{i,j} \right] ^+, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[x]^+ \triangleq \max (0, x)$$\end{document}. This metric directly quantifies the average secure information rate achievable over the T-slot frame.

Remark 0.1

(Finite Blocklength (FBL) URLLC) For short packets, the FBL normal approximation refines Shannon capacity as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{\textrm{FBL}}\!\approx \!\log _2(1{+}\gamma )-\sqrt{V(\gamma )/n}\,Q^{-1}(\varepsilon )$$\end{document}, where n is the blocklength, V(γ ) is the channel dispersion, and \varepsilon the decoding error probability45. Embedding FBL rigorously into Equation (18) would require re-deriving the secrecy and latency constraints for both legitimate and eavesdropping links, introducing additional non-convex couplings and materially enlarging all BCD subproblems41. Consistent with secure RIS–MEC baselines, we therefore adopt the Shannon model as an analytically tractable upper bound and identify explicit FBL-aware secure offloading as focused future work.

Justification of constraints

Constraint (18b) ensures a uniform secrecy performance floor η across all devices which the objective function seeks to maximize. This max-min fairness criterion is particularly suitable for IIoT environments where all devices require a baseline level of security . Constraint (18c) guarantees that each device achieves a minimum average uplink rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\min ,i}$$\end{document} necessary for reliable data delivery. The latency constraint (18d) couples communication and computation by requiring the total transmission and execution time to remain within the device’s deadline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\max ,i}$$\end{document} , ensuring timely task completion. The server capacity constraint (18e) prevents computational overload at any MEC server, while (18f) enforces the single server association policy. Practical limitations are captured by (18g) (individual power budgets), (18h) (discrete frequency-hopping choices) and (18i) (RIS phase-shift feasibility). Collectively, these constraints model the key resource limitations and operational requirements of a practical RIS-aided MEC system, balancing security, reliability and latency in a unified framework.

The problem () is a mixed-integer non-convex optimization due to the binary association variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{i,k}$$\end{document}, the discrete frequency selections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_i(t)$$\end{document}, the multiplicative coupling of variables in the rate and secrecy expressions and the unit-modulus constraints on the RIS phase shifts. Its solution requires sophisticated algorithmic approaches which we develop in the subsequent section using the BCD method.

Mathematical framework and algorithmic details of the BCD approach

Fundamental principles of BCD

The BCD, also known as nonlinear Gauss–Seidel or alternating optimization, is an iterative method designed for large-scale optimization problems in which the decision variables can be partitioned into multiple blocks. At each iteration, BCD optimizes one variable block while fixing the remaining blocks, thereby reducing a high-dimensional problem into a sequence of lower-dimensional subproblems. Consider the optimization problem19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \min _{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathscr {X}} f(\boldsymbol{x}), \quad \boldsymbol{x} = (\boldsymbol{x}_1,\ldots ,\boldsymbol{x}_B) \in \prod _{b=1}^{B} \mathscr {X}_b, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f:\mathbb {R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb {R}$$\end{document} is continuous and possibly non-convex, and each feasible set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {X}_b \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n_b}$$\end{document} is closed and convex with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum }_{b=1}^{B} n_b = n$$\end{document}. Given an iterate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{x}^{(r-1)}$$\end{document}, the BCD update for block b is defined as20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{x}_b^{(r)} = \arg \min _{\boldsymbol{x}_b \in \mathscr {X}_b} f\!\left( \boldsymbol{x}_1^{(r)},\ldots ,\boldsymbol{x}_{b-1}^{(r)},\boldsymbol{x}_b, \boldsymbol{x}_{b+1}^{(r-1)},\ldots ,\boldsymbol{x}_B^{(r-1)}\right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Under standard regularity conditions, such as block-wise quasiconvexity or uniqueness of the minimizers any limit point of the sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(r)}\}$$\end{document} is a stationary point of (19). For the joint optimization problem in (), a direct solution is intractable due to the mixed-integer structure of the association variables, the non-convex secrecy rate and SINR expressions, the unit-modulus constraints on the RIS phase shifts and the strong coupling among communication, computation, and reflection variables. These properties naturally suggest a block-wise decomposition of the decision variables into association and computation variables, transmission-related parameters and RIS configuration variables. This structure is well aligned with the BCD framework enabling each subproblem to be addressed via convex or SCAs and solved efficiently within an iterative procedure .

Solution Rationale and Block Partitioning Logic: The joint optimization problem () is partitioned into the three distinct blocks: (A) the association and CPU allocation, (B) the transmit power and FHSS selection, and (C) RIS configuration; this approach strategically exploits the inherent structure of the coupled design. This partitioning reflects natural hierarchy of the IIoT-MEC system; and it encompasses the compute admission (A), the PHY/MAC resource control (B), and the environmental propagation shaping (C). Within each block; the targeted convexification techniques are applied to resolve the specific non-convexities: (i) the perspective-based SOCP reformulation is used in (A) to handle the association-dependent computation-latency coupling; (ii) the SCA and linearization are applied in (B) to handle DC secrecy-rate structure and the relaxed FHSS collision probabilities; and (iii) the SDR is employed in (C) to lift non-convex unit-modulus RIS constraints into the higher-dimensional convex space. Convergence, complexity, and practical feasibility

Let the joint problem in () be solved via the proposed three-block BCD-SCA scheme, and denote the iteration-r solution by\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\chi }^{(r)} = \big ( \textbf{A}^{(r)},\textbf{f}^{(r)},\textbf{P}^{(r)},\boldsymbol{\Theta }^{(r)} \big ),$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{A}^{(r)}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{f}^{(r)}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{P}^{(r)}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\Theta }^{(r)}$$\end{document} represent the association, computation/carrier-selection, transmit-power, and RIS phase-shift variables, respectively. The corresponding objective value is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta ^{(r)}$$\end{document}. Since () is a mixed-integer non-convex problem, the proposed method solves a sequence of relaxed block-wise approximations using continuous relaxation, perspective-based conic reformulation, SCA, and SDR. Therefore, the convergence guarantee applies to the relaxed continuous BCD-SCA procedure before final discrete recovery and Gaussian randomization.

The convergence follows from the standard Block Successive Upper-bound Minimization (BSUM)/SCA framework. Specifically, the relaxed feasible set is bounded due to finite transmit-power budgets, CPU capacities, carrier-selection probabilities, and bounded RIS coefficients. For fixed values of the remaining blocks, each subproblem is solved over a convex feasible set: the association/computation block is formulated as a convex second-order-cone-representable problem, the transmit-power/FHSS block is handled through SCA-based convex approximation, and the RIS block is treated using SDR. The SCA surrogates are constructed to be locally tight and first-order consistent at the current iterate, and each accepted block update does not decrease the relaxed objective. Consequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\eta ^{(r)}\}$$\end{document} is monotonically non-decreasing and upper-bounded by the finite bandwidth, power, RIS, and computing resources. Hence, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\eta ^{(r)}\}$$\end{document} converges to a finite limit. Following BSUM/SCA theory, any accumulation point of the relaxed iterative sequence satisfies the stationarity conditions of the relaxed continuous problem. After convergence, deterministic rounding for association and carrier selection, followed by feasibility repair, is applied to recover a feasible discrete policy for the original formulation.

The per-iteration complexity is governed by the three block updates. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_1=\mathscr {O}(MK)$$\end{document} denote the association/computation dimension, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_2=\mathscr {O}(MTF)$$\end{document} the transmit-power and relaxed FHSS dimension, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_3=\mathscr {O}(TQ^2)$$\end{document} the lifted RIS SDP dimension. Using generic interior-point solvers, the association/computation block has worst-case complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}((MK)^{3.5})$$\end{document}, the transmit-power/FHSS block has complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}((MTF)^{3.5})$$\end{document}, and the RIS SDP block has dominant complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}((TQ^2)^{3.5})$$\end{document}. Therefore, for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{iter}}$$\end{document} outer iterations, the overall worst-case complexity is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}\!\left( R_{\textrm{iter}} \left[ (MK)^{3.5} + (MTF)^{3.5} + (TQ^2)^{3.5} \right] \right) .$$\end{document}This polynomial-time scaling is substantially more tractable than exhaustive search over all binary association choices, discrete carrier selections, power allocations, and RIS phase configurations.

From an implementation perspective; the proposed framework is suitable for the centralized industrial edge computing. The computationally intensive optimization is executed at the MEC controller or the edge orchestration unit; while the IIoT devices only receive the low-rate control commands specifying the association, the carrier selection, transmit power; and the offloading decisions. The algorithm can also be warm-started from the previous frame; reducing number of iterations in the slowly varying industrial environments. For very large-scale networks or the extremely large RIS panels; the practical acceleration mechanisms such as the parallel solvers, the RIS element grouping, reduced RIS update frequency, the first-order conic solvers; or the learning-assisted initialization can be adopted. Moreover; the use of the uplink NOMA avoids common/private stream management and the potentially heavier multi-layer Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC) required by RSMA; making the proposed design more suitable for the battery-constrained IIoT devices while leaving the RSMA-based secure offloading as a promising future extension.

2. RSMA vs. NOMA: Theoretical Trade-offs

While the proposed framework utilizes NOMA, recent literature identifies RSMA as a generalized multiple-access alternative40. By splitting messages into common and private parts, RSMA can provide additional interference-management flexibility. However, it also requires rate-splitting optimization and potentially multi-layer SIC, and this inherently increases decoding latency and receiver complexity. Therefore, NOMA is adopted here to keep the IIoT-side processing burden lower, while RSMA-based secure offloading remains a very promising future direction.

Potential of Absorptive Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (ARIS) for Enhanced Security. While this study focuses on standard reflective RIS architectures with unit-modulus constraints, ARIS provides an additional amplitude-control dimension where the reflection coefficients satisfy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _q\in [0,1]$$\end{document}51. In the proposed FHSS-RIS-MEC framework, ARIS could absorb signal energy directed toward compromised spatial regions, thereby further reducing information leakage. Extending the proposed BCD-SCA design to jointly optimize ARIS absorption coefficients is an important future direction.

Conclusion

This work developed the secure and scalable task offloading framework for the Industry 5.0 IIoT networks by jointly integrating the RIS-assisted propagation control, the FHSS-based spectrum agility; and the uplink NOMA within the multi-MEC architecture. A secrecy-latency coupled max-min optimization problem was formulated to capture the key industrial constraints; these constraints include the binary device-server association, the per-server CPU limits, the per-device power budgets, the minimum rate requirements; and the frame-averaged secrecy performance in the presence of the passive eavesdroppers. To handle the resulting mixed-integer non-convexity; a BCD-based algorithm with the convex approximations was developed to iteratively optimize the association and the computation allocation, the power and the carrier selection; and the RIS phase shifts.

The numerical results show that the proposed joint design improves the secrecy performance while reducing the latency and the energy consumption compared with the representative ablation baselines. The gains arise from the complementary roles of the RIS beamforming, the FHSS-based spectral diversity, the NOMA-enabled massive uplink access; and the multi-MEC load balancing. The additional evaluations under the multiplicative fading, different Rician K-factors; and the 3GPP InF-SL channels further demonstrate the profound robustness of the proposed framework in the harsh industrial propagation environments.

The baseline optimization is derived under the idealized perfect-CSI and the continuous-RIS assumptions; while the revised analysis discusses the bounded CSI uncertainty, the finite phase resolution, the switching overhead, the synchronization loss; and the industrial INF channel effects. The future work will integrate these impairments directly into the robust online optimization framework with the discrete RIS control, the practical FHSS synchronization; and the mobility-aware adaptation.

The future extensions will also investigate the RSMA-based secure offloading, the finite-blocklength-aware secrecy constraints; and the ARIS-enabled amplitude and the phase control for the enhanced physical-layer security. By securing the latency-sensitive IIoT communications; the proposed framework strongly supports the smart mining applications such as the IoT safety monitoring and the automated extraction systems; and it stands in direct alignment with the Saudi Vision 2030.
