 ABSTRACT
This report describes an end-to-end pipeline for monitoring and classifying acoustic events in a microreactor
(MR) using distributed acoustic sensing (DAS). The objective of this pipeline is to realize autonomous
supervision of MRs to reduce their operational footprint. MRs promise modular, compact nuclear power,
but their economic viability depends on minimizing operation and maintenance costs through remote,
autonomous monitoring. To address this limitation, we investigated how optical fiber–based remote
sensing could be used to support compact monitoring systems for energy facilities. Distributed optical
fiber sensing, and DAS in particular, is well suited to this role because silica optical fibers are immune
to electromagnetic noise, are tolerant to radiation and high temperatures, and are chemically inert. In
this work, we establish the feasibility of a novel application of established machine learning (ML) event
classification models and statistical anomaly detection algorithms to DAS-derived vibroacoustic data
for industrial system health monitoring. We developed an end-to-end classification pipeline built on the
You Only Look Once (YOLO) object recognition model and evaluated cross-source transfer between
microphonic- and DAS-sourced data. The raw strain rate data were processed using an anomaly detection
algorithm, which leveraged a modified 𝑧-score using the median absolute deviation (MAD) metric. A
commercial DAS system was used to collect acoustic data at the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) at Oak
Ridge National Laboratory. The model trained on DAS data was effective in classifying unseen DAS data,
cross-source classification was inaccurate, and fine-tuning source-derived weights with DAS spectrograms
recovered strong DAS classification. Multiple anomaly detection or “trigger” approaches were explored,
including a time-amplitude root mean square (RMS) trigger and a frequency-based detector. The
RMS-based detector detects stationary events but fails under persistent low-frequency noise interference,
and the frequency-based anomaly detector is robust to the elevated background noise. Deployment at
SNS demonstrated full operation in a nuclear environment, validating the approach end to end as a proof
of concept for MR monitoring and validating DAS hardware as viable for collecting vibroacoustic data
for the detection and classification of MR operation noise. Dedicated DAS-derived datasets are required
to achieve reliable accuracy. For future work, investigating advanced signal processing to bridge existing
acoustic datasets to DAS data is a promising direction worth pursuing.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Microreactors (MRs) are compact nuclear reactors that are factory manufactured, transportable, and
produce power outputs up to 20 MWe [1]. They serve as an energy source for applications poorly suited
to conventional generation, including remote communities and industrial operations independent from
the main electric grid [2]. To advance the commercialization of MRs, they must demonstrate economic
competitiveness against relatively inexpensive fossil fuel alternatives and established terrestrial nuclear
power plants [3]. Shifting from staffed, scheduled maintenance to a remote, largely unmanned model
could reduce operation and maintenance (O&M) costs by more than 80% [4]. Therefore, advanced
instrumentation and machine learning (ML) algorithms that detect and classify irregularities of system
health would advance the economic viability of MRs [3].
Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) is a well-characterized technology for system health monitoring in
railways [5], pipelines [6], and highways [7] as well as geological movement [8] and ocean applications
[9]. DAS instruments use a narrow-linewidth laser to inject coherent optical pulses (5 to 50 ns, or 1 to
10 m) into an optical fiber routed near the equipment of interest and measure the reflection returned from
intrinsic scattering sites along the fiber. This Rayleigh backscatter is recorded by the DAS instrument, and
the time of flight between pulse launch and signal arrival identifies the fiber segment from which each
backscatter profile originates. Acoustic and vibrational disturbances strain the fiber locally, producing
phase changes in the backscattered light that can be resolved to recover the strain rate along a single fiber
with no distributed electronics, local power, or active components (outside of the interrogator itself).
The fiber’s immunity to electromagnetic interference, adaptive algorithms for extreme temperature
environments [10], and tolerance to irradiation [11] make DAS especially attractive for acoustic event
classification in nuclear environments.
Acoustic classification models generally use time-frequency representations, such as mel spectrograms and
mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), to recognize acoustic events [12]. The short-time Fourier
transform (STFT) decomposes each analysis frame into its constituent frequencies. For classification
tasks, the linear-frequency spectrogram is often transformed to the mel scale, which spaces frequency
bands to approximate the nonlinear resolution of human hearing [13]. The mel spectrogram has become
a standard input to modern acoustic classifiers, including the convolutional neural networks that dominate
environmental sound and acoustic scene recognition [14]. The You Only Look Once (YOLO) family of
object detectors formulates detection as a single-stage regression problem, in which one convolutional
passes over the whole image and returns class label and confidence score [15]. This is important because
collapsing classification into a single pass yields the real-time speed required for deployment on streaming
data and edge hardware. However, labeled training data for irregular fault conditions are scarce, and
models trained on microphonic source data have little application to DAS-derived data. A goal of this
report is to demonstrate that standard audio classification ML techniques can be leveraged with DAS
instrumentation for streamlined O&M in the context of nuclear MRs.
In industrial system health monitoring, vibration signals are continuously surveilled to catch the onset
of bearing wear, cavitation, or structural fatigue before failure [16]. There is a large body of work on
this topic [17], and there are commercially available devices [18] that monitor and detect anomalies for
predictive maintenance with traditional sensors. However, there is no currently available software that
automates anomaly detection using DAS instrumentation. The challenge in DAS-based monitoring is that
normal behavior must be characterized, often from a limited or noisy reference. A single fiber-optic cable
(up to 100 km) connected to the interrogator, which can sample thousands of serial channels with ∼5 m
spatial resolution at kilohertz rates, will stream ≥ 1 TB/day. Extracting useful data from DAS therefore

 2

requires advanced signal processing techniques to reduce the size of the data while preserving the relevant
information. Statistics such as the root mean square (RMS) envelope summarize the amplitude of the
signal over a sliding window, and thresholding these quantities yields a lightweight trigger suitable for
real-time operation on high-rate data streams [19]. As environmental conditions evolve and low-frequency
background events occur, the detector must remain sensitive to anomalies while tolerating shifts to the
baseline.
This report documents several experiments conducted to classify acoustic events identified by DAS. First,
we describe a pipeline for batch conversion of acoustic data into standardized mel spectrograms, with
training and classification using an image-based ML model. Second, we present an anomaly detection
algorithm for time series data based on a modified 𝑧-score with the median absolute deviation (MAD) in
the time-amplitude domain, with extensions to frequency-domain features. Finally, we detail a proof of
concept at the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) facility, including hardware deployment, an end-to-end
software pipeline that triggers on abnormal events and saves data, and subsequent frequency band analysis.
This work advances the necessary hardware, signal processing, and ML techniques for DAS-derived strain
rate files for nuclear MR applications.

2.2 CONSTRUCTING A DAS DATASET
It is of interest to investigate whether microphonic data could be used to train object detection models
for DAS data and vice versa. The source data for this study were taken from 50 environmental sound
classifications (ESC-50) [22]. A second copy of these data was recorded with the 40 km Treble+ DAS
system (Terra15 Technologies Pty Ltd., Perth, Australia) to capture the unique background artifacts

 4

introduced by the system. The DAS dataset was created by playing these files on a speaker (S1 Pro+,
BOSE, Framingham, Massachusetts) 1.2 m away from a 1 km fiber spool (Figure 1). All optical fiber used
in this study was SMF-28e+ (Corning Inc., Corning, New York, USA) packaged as either ITU-T G.652.D
compliant OS2 single-mode fiber with a 3 mm jacket or bare fiber coated with with 200 µm dual acrylate.
The DAS system was set to a pulse width of 4.9 m (24 ns) and high-pass filtered at ≥ 100 Hz to eliminate
the DC component of the signal. Each recording in this combined dataset was resampled to a uniform
32-bit, 16 kHz file and then zero-padded or trimmed to 5 s segments. The time series signals for both
the source files and the DAS files were converted to log-mel spectrograms prior to acoustic classification
training (Figure 2). The spectrograms were computed using custom code in Python (v3.14) using the
Librosa library (v0.11.0) with 128 mel bins, 1,024 samples per STFT, and a hop length of 256. Mel-band
power was converted to decibels relative to the maximum value within each 5.0 s window, clipped to a
dynamic range of −80 to 0 dB, and mapped to [0,1] for input to the classification model. These data were
subject to an 80%/20% split for training and validation.

2.3 YOLO26N TRAINING
We trained the head (stage) of a YOLO26n model [23] on the spectrogram dataset. Training used transfer
learning from pretrained weights, standard image augmentation, and early stopping on the validation
split. A custom trainer was used to train the YOLO26n-cls model on single-channel binary spectrogram
arrays rather than the image formats typically used with the ML model. The first convolutional layer of the
pretrained model was converted from three input channels to one input channel by averaging the pretrained
RGB weights across the input channel dimension. The custom dataset loader reads the binary arrays from
class folders, converts them to tensors of shape (1, 𝐻, 𝑊), replaces invalid numeric values, and resizes
them to the target input geometry. The baseline model training process used the AdamW algorithm [24]
with a weight decay of 0.001 and a fixed seed for reproducibility. Model training settings were 50 epochs
(with a patience timeout of 15 epochs), no dropout, and a learning rate of 0.01. Fine-tuning runs used
25 epochs and a reduced learning rate of 0.0001.

To investigate the appropriate parameters in the context of DAS-derived acoustic data, we studied how
varying the RMS window size, 𝑧-score threshold, and choice of reference frame impacted our false
positives (FPs), false negatives (FNs), and F1 error score. Detector performance was quantified by
matching detections against a labeled manifest of event start times, using a ±1 s tolerance window around
each labeled start. A manifest event was counted as a true positive (TP) if at least one detection fell within
its tolerance window and as an FN otherwise. A detection that did not fall within the tolerance window
of any manifest event was counted as an FP. A 6 s lockout period was enforced following each trigger so
that a single sustained event could not register as multiple detections. From these counts, we computed
the F1 score, which is an established metric for evaluating anomaly detection algorithms for system health
monitoring [28]. This metric is especially useful because it depends only on the events and the detections,
penalizing missed events and spurious triggers symmetrically. Initially, we investigated 𝑧-score triggering
on time series data with raw strain rate values. Then, we studied how data representation in the frequency
domain could be used for event detection algorithms by introducing an artificial 100 Hz noise to our clean
data and evaluating time and frequency sourced triggers.

3.2 FIBER DEPLOYMENT AND GAUGE LENGTH IMPACT
A same-session comparison was performed using a single recording session on a combined length of fiber
and evaluated offline across four gauge lengths (1, 2, 5, and 10 m) 4. The spooled condition produced
identical accuracy at every gauge length, indicating that gauge length had no measurable effect on
classification for that topology. The straight condition varied only marginally across gauge lengths (within
roughly 1.4% top-1).
The spooled topology outperformed the straight topology on top-1 accuracy. In the one-point condition,
spooled fiber achieved 58.90% top-1 versus 43.84%–45.21% for straight fiber. The three-point condition
showed the same trend, with spooled fiber at 52.97% top-1 compared with 43.84%–44.75% for straight
fiber.
Top-5 accuracy was substantially less sensitive to topology. In the one-point condition, spooled fiber
reached 82.19% top-5 versus 80.82%–82.19% for straight fiber, and in the three-point condition, 79.45%
versus 79.91%–81.28%. The principal benefit of the spooled fiber therefore appears to be improved top-1
ranking rather than increased inclusion of the correct class among the top-5 candidates; in the three-point
case, the straight topology actually edged out the spooled topology on top-5.

3.3 DEVELOPMENT OF AN ANOMALY DETECTION PIPELINE FOR DAS MONITORING
Windowing the high-rate data stream with an RMS function is useful to minimize the influence of transient
spikes, or outliers, on our data processing. As shown in Figure 8(a), the raw strain rate series contains
sharp, high-amplitude transients against a low-amplitude baseline. Figure 8(b) shows that a 0.25 s RMS
window preserves these peaks with tight temporal localization, whereas a 5 s window suppresses their
amplitude and spreads their energy across a wider span in time. This behavior implies that the window
should be matched to the temporal scale of the acoustic signature of interest. For nonstationary noise
that persists over longer durations, a longer RMS window is acceptable and can improve robustness by
averaging over the event. For brief, stationary noise, a shorter RMS window is preferable, as it retains

the peak amplitude and temporal localization needed for the event to remain distinguishable above the
baseline.
Figure 9 compares two strategies for calibrating the modified 𝑧-score reference values on stitched DAS
RMS data. When the median and MAD are calculated from a fixed 20 s reference window, the MAD
calculation reflects only the artificially quiet background noise of the lab environment (MAD = 1.83 ×
10−9 ), so subsequent events are inflated. When the reference is instead computed over the entire stitched
signal, the transient events are themselves included in the statistics that define the baseline. The median
is essentially unchanged (7.14 × 10−8 versus 7.79 × 10−8 ), but the global MAD rises to 4.38 × 10−9
(compared to 1.83 × 10−9 for the artificially quiet value). The MAD value with the global reference lowers
the peak 𝑧-values recorded for the same physical events and narrows their separation from the baseline.
Consequently, a fixed background reference yields higher-amplitude 𝑧-score peaks. The advantage of the
moving global reference is that it absorbs nonstationary events into its own calibration, systematically
correcting for gradual changes.

 4. DISCUSSION
MRs are attractive because they are modular and small. To make them useful, maintenance and control
must be minimized. Combining continuous anomaly detection with acoustic classification offers a route to
flagging (and identifying) off-normal conditions early and remotely, which could reduce O&M costs and
thereby ultimately advance the widespread adoption of MRs.
Audio classification ML models and anomaly detection algorithms are well researched in the context of
system health monitoring [17]. Wireless sensors that continuously monitor industrial conditions and report
off-normal events are commercially available [18]. This real-time, remote, autonomous monitoring is
advantageous because it moves toward decreasing staffed O&M responsibility and costs. Furthermore,
reactor downtime is a critical limiting factor in the economic feasibility of MRs. In unanticipated
downtime, the lack of predictive maintenance results in surprise faults and unknown time to restoration.
However, even with preventative maintenance and scheduled downtime, this is not an optimized economic
system, as there is too often no necessary maintenance required by the system. Thus, a remote sensing
system is crucial for optimized predictive and preventative maintenance for nuclear systems. Because
of electromagnetic noise, radiation [29], and temperature [11], fiber-optic sensing is an advantageous
hardware instrumentation option compared to traditional sensors. Thus, the key advancement of this
work was to establish the feasibility of applying well-established ML models, such as the YOLO object
recognition model [15], to DAS-derived vibroacoustic data. To the best of our knowledge, there is no
existing model that classifies DAS data in the context of industrial system health monitoring, specifically
for MRs. Because DAS data have a unique noise signature compared to traditional microphonic data, we
had to develop a novel, end-to-end pipeline for our event classification model.
As there is not a readily available dataset for industrial sound signatures recorded by DAS devices, it was
of interest to investigate whether a model trained on microphonic data could be used to classify DAS data
and vice versa. The results presented here indicate that without advanced signal processing techniques,
it is not viable to use variable source data for event classification. However, models trained on DAS data
performed well when validated against unseen DAS data (Table 3). Thus, we also attempted to augment
our training to identify routes for cross-source classification. Spectrogram augmentation did not prove
to be an effective way to make microphonic sourced data usable for DAS validation. Fine-tuning our
source-derived weights with DAS spectrograms was an effective approach to accurately classify unseen
DAS data. The results presented here indicate that established ML models can be used for classification of
DAS data. Based on the experiments conducted in this study, it is not feasible to classify DAS data without
a DAS training dataset. This is an important future direction to investigate, as there are more open-source
industrial sound signature datasets available recorded by microphone compared to DAS hardware. The
results in Table 3 show a mild improvement in cross-domain validation accuracy when spectrogram
augmentation is applied. This indicates there may be advanced signal processing and ML techniques that
could be eventually employed to integrate microphonic data with DAS data.
Accuracy depended on where an event sat on the fiber and in particular differed between straight and
spooled sections because spooling changes how strain couples into the fiber and how adjacent channels
overlap spatially. The gauge length sets the spatial averaging of each DAS channel and trades signal-to-noise
against spatial resolution. These results should not necessarily be interpreted solely as topology-caused.
The straight and spooled measurements occurred at different absolute distances along the fiber, so factors
such as attenuation and coupling could contribute as well. Optimizing gauge length for the deployment
geometry, and accounting for spooled versus straight runs when laying out the fiber, could improve both
classification and anomaly detection.

 20

In the context of O&M, the FN and FP error modes are not equally costly. An FN, where a true event
is missed, represents a permanent loss of information about the monitored system, potentially masking
the onset of a developing fault. An FP, by contrast, merely records an additional data packet that later
can be ignored or deleted. The trigger purpose is to preserve events for later inspection, and the cost
of overrecording is storage, while the cost of underrecording is a missed fault. A straightforward way
to exploit this asymmetry is to have the trigger return the underlying strain rate data packet alongside
each detection so that a human operator, or a downstream classifier, can resolve whether the recorded
excursion was a true event or an erroneous trigger. The 𝑧-score threshold is most useful as a user-facing
sensitivity control rather than a fixed parameter. Lowering the threshold slides the detector toward high
sensitivity, while raising it will only capture major events. Because the appropriate balance depends on the
tolerance for missed events at a given facility, giving the threshold as an adjustable sensitivity setting lets
the operator position the detector along this trade-off curve to suit their context, rather than committing to
the single F1-optimal point identified here.
An important extension of the temporal anomaly detection is the spatial location of the source event
along the fiber. A single physical event may couple into many channels at once, so a simple per-channel
trigger would fire redundantly across the array. If the event signature is similar in its frequency content
across channels but differs in amplitude, the spatial origin can be localized by identifying the channel of
maximum response rather than triggering everywhere the signature appears. Examining the frequency
components channel by channel would allow the detector to confirm that a distributed set of responses
shares a common spectral response, while using the amplitude distribution along the fiber to pinpoint the
location of highest coupling. This would suppress redundant triggers and add a spatial dimension to the
anomaly report.
Using a time-amplitude RMS detector is effective when stationary events occur with low background
noise. However, because the detector sums energy across the spectrum, it cannot distinguish changes in
frequency. To artificially create a noisy industrial background environment, a 100 Hz tone was introduced.
This caused the RMS amplitude 𝑧-score detector to fail. A spectral representation such as the mel
spectrogram addresses this directly by preserving the frequency axis that the RMS detector discards.
Distributing energy across many mel bins and triggering on any bin that exceeds the threshold localizes the
interference more effectively than the time-amplitude trigger. In practice, this suggests a layered strategy.
The RMS detector suffices for events greater than the background noise, while the spectrogram-based
detector is warranted wherever persistent narrowband noise is expected, which, in most operating industrial
settings, is common.
The SNS deployment demonstrated that the full chain functions in an operational accelerator environment.
As a proof of concept, the deployment validated the approach end to end; however, SNS is not a reactor,
and the results should be read as evidence of feasibility rather than as a completed facility monitoring
benchmark.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
This work establishes the feasibility of applying off-the-shelf ML models and statistical anomaly detection
methods to DAS-derived vibroacoustic data for the remote, autonomous health monitoring of MRs. Using
a novel end-to-end pipeline based on the ML model, we showed that classifiers trained on DAS data
accurately classify unseen DAS data. Cross-source classification between microphonic and DAS data
was not viable without adaptation. Spectrogram augmentation proved ineffective, whereas fine-tuning
source-derived weights with DAS spectrograms was an effective route to accurate classification. This
indicates that a DAS training dataset is necessary for accurately classifying DAS spectrograms. Developing
advanced signal processing techniques to make open-source microphone-recorded industrial sound data
usable for DAS classification would be an ambitious and well worthwhile avenue to explore. Alternatively,
building such open-source datasets with DAS data is a more straightforward but labor intensive method.
For anomaly detection, a time-amplitude RMS 𝑧-score trigger is effective for stationary events above a
quiet background but fails in the presence of persistent narrowband interference. A mel spectrogram
detector addresses this by preserving the frequency axis. The 𝑧-score threshold is best used as an
operator-facing sensitivity control, allowing each facility to position the detector according to its tolerance
for missed events. Classification accuracy depended on event location and on spooled versus straight fiber
sections, identifying gauge length as a key tunable parameter for future deployments. Additional natural
extensions include spatially localizing anomalies along the fiber by exploiting shared spectral signatures
and channel-wise amplitude distributions to suppress redundant triggers. Finally, the SNS deployment
demonstrated that the full chain functions in an operational accelerator environment. Although SNS is
not a reactor and these results should be read as evidence of feasibility rather than a completed facility
monitoring benchmark, they collectively establish DAS-based acoustic monitoring as a promising path
toward reduced O&M costs and the wider adoption of MRs.
