Abstract
Higher-derivative quantum corrections are essential components of scalar-tensor effective field theories (EFTs), yet they typically reintroduce the Ostrogradsky ghost instability that the classical theory was designed to evade. This paper resolves this fundamental tension by establishing a rigorous equivalence between two distinct criteria for theoretical consistency. We analyze a general DHOST theory augmented by Gauss-Bonnet and Weyl-squared operators with coefficients that are arbitrary functions of the scalar field and its kinetic term. We then pursue two independent paths: first, we derive a set of differential equations for these coefficients by demanding that the full action remains invariant under the protective gauge symmetry of the classical theory. Second, we perform a first-principles Hamiltonian analysis using the ADM formalism, deriving a separate set of conditions by imposing the primary and secondary constraints required to eliminate the ghost. Our central result is the proof that these two sets of conditions, one algebraic and one dynamical, are mathematically identical. This equivalence demonstrates that the gauge symmetry is the fundamental origin of Hamiltonian stability in the quantum-corrected theory and establishes the symmetry principle as a powerful and practical tool for constructing consistent, ghost-free gravitational EFTs without resorting to a full Hamiltonian analysis.

Introduction

Scalar-tensor theories provide a compelling framework for extending General Relativity, offering pathways to address enduring puzzles in cosmology and the nature of gravity at quantum scales. Within the modern paradigm of Effective Field Theory (EFT), these theories are understood as low-energy descriptions that must inevitably incorporate higher-derivative operators. These operators, arising from the integration of high-energy degrees of freedom, are not optional additions but essential components of a complete theory. However, they introduce a profound and fundamental challenge: generic higher-derivative terms in the Lagrangian lead to equations of motion of higher than second order, which in turn gives rise to the Ostrogradsky ghost

The central tension in constructing viable gravitational EFTs is therefore to reconcile the necessity of higher-derivative quantum corrections with the stringent requirement of ghost-freedom.

At the classical level, this tension is elegantly resolved by the class of Degenerate Higher-Order Scalar-Tensor (DHOST) theories

It is now understood that this degeneracy is not a mere mathematical contrivance but the direct consequence of a subtle gauge symmetry that protects the theory from propagating the unstable mode. The problem, however, re-emerges with force when one considers the next order of EFT corrections, such as the requisite curvature-squared terms. Methodology

One of the longstanding criticisms of Horndeski and DHOST theories concerns the lack of a

In particular, quantum effects may reintroduce higher-derivative operators or spoil the delicate degeneracy structure, potentially leading to ghost-like instabilities in the ultraviolet (UV) completion of the theory. A commonly raised argument in this context is that such pathologies could be avoided if the theory were protected by an underlying symmetry, capable of enforcing the required structure at all energy scales, including the quantum level.

However, the identification of such protective symmetries remains highly nontrivial. In practice, symmetry-based constructions are often restricted to relatively simple and well-known cases, such as shift symmetries or Galileon-like invariances. The absence of an immediately identifiable symmetry in more general scalar–tensor theories should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that no such symmetry exists. Rather, it may simply reflect the limitations of current analytical techniques and human intuition in navigating the vast landscape of possible invariances. In four dimensions, the Gauss-Bonnet scalar Eq. (

\delta_{\epsilon}\left[\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,\beta_{GB}\cdot G\right]=0\,,

the Gauss-Bonnet term with a constant coefficient does not break the classical symmetry.

The variation of the Weyl-squared action with respect to the metric is given by the Bach tensor,

\delta\left[\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,W^{2}\right]=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,B^{\mu\nu}\,\delta g_{\mu\nu}\,.

\delta_{\epsilon}\left[\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,\beta_{W^{2}}\cdot W^{2}\right]=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,\beta_{W^{2}}\,B^{\mu\nu}\,\epsilon(x)\,(\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu})\,.

B^{\mu\nu}(\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu})=0\,.

Hamiltonian Degeneracy Analysis

The next step in this project is to perform an independent Hamiltonian analysis to derive the
degeneracy conditions
and prove their equivalence to these symmetry requirements.
This step provides an independent derivation of the conditions on
\beta_{GB}(\phi,X)
and
\beta_{W^{2}}(\phi,X)
based on a first-principles Hamiltonian analysis.
The goal is to ensure the theory is free of an Ostrogradsky ghost, which arises in higher-derivative theories with non-degenerate kinetic terms.
The method involves performing a 3+1 (ADM) decomposition of the action and demanding that the kinetic matrix for the highest-order time derivatives is degenerate
[
23
,
24
]
.

We decompose the 4D spacetime metric into 3D spatial slices

(5.1)

where
N
is the lapse,
N^{i}
is the shift, and
h_{ij}
is the spatial metric
[
51

52
]
.
The action, when rewritten in these variables, will depend on the velocities (e.g.,
\dot{h}_{ij}
,
\dot{\phi}
) and accelerations (e.g.,
\ddot{h}_{ij}
,
\ddot{\phi}
).
The extrinsic curvature
K_{ij}
contains first time derivatives of
h_{ij}
. The Gauss-Bonnet and Weyl-squared terms contain time derivatives of
K_{ij}
(i.e.,
\ddot{h}_{ij}
) and potentially
\ddot{\phi}
through the
X
-dependence of their coefficients.
The presence of a ghost is determined by the part of the Lagrangian that is quadratic in these accelerations.
Let the accelerations be denoted schematically by
a=(\ddot{\phi},\ddot{h}_{ij})
. The part of the Lagrangian with the highest time derivatives takes the form

\mathcal{L}_{\text{kinetic}}=\frac{1}{2}a^{T}Ha+(\text{linear terms})\,.

(5.2)

Here,
H
is the Hessian matrix of the Lagrangian with respect to the accelerations. It is the “kinetic matrix” for the system.
An Ostrogradsky ghost, a non-physical degree of freedom with negative kinetic energy, appears if and only if this Hessian matrix
H
is invertible (non-degenerate)
[
25
,
2
]
.
Therefore, the fundamental condition for a healthy, ghost-free theory is that the Hessian must be degenerate

\text{Degeneracy Condition:}\quad\det(H)=0\,.

(5.3)

Calculating the full Hessian matrix
H
for the combined system (DHOST + quantum corrections) is an extremely complex task. However, we can deduce the conditions by analyzing the structure of the highest-derivative terms.

The Weyl-squared term,
W^{2}
, is itself quadratic in second time derivatives of the metric (
\ddot{h}_{ij}
or
\dot{K}_{ij}
).
If its coefficient,
\beta_{W^{2}}
, depends on
X
, the Lagrangian would contain terms of the form

\mathcal{L}\sim\frac{\partial\beta_{W^{2}}}{\partial X}\cdot\dot{X}\cdot W^{2}\,.

(5.4)

Since
\dot{X}
contains
\ddot{\phi}
and
W^{2}
contains
(\ddot{h}_{ij})^{2}
, this term is
cubic
in accelerations.
A Lagrangian with cubic or higher dependence on accelerations leads to more than one ghost and an unstable Hamiltonian. To prevent this, we must impose that such terms vanish identically.
This leads to our first degeneracy condition:

\text{Degeneracy Condition 1:}\quad\frac{\partial\beta_{W^{2}}}{\partial X}=0\,.

(5.5)

This implies that
\beta_{W^{2}}
can only be a function of the scalar field
\phi
, i.e.,
\beta_{W^{2}}=\beta_{W^{2}}(\phi)
.

With the first condition imposed, the Lagrangian is now at most quadratic in accelerations. The Hessian
H
can be computed. It receives contributions from:

•

\beta_{W^{2}}(\phi)\cdot W^{2}
: Contributes to the metric-metric (
\ddot{h}
-
\ddot{h}
) block.

•

\beta_{GB}(\phi,X)\cdot G
: Contributes to the metric-metric block and, via its
X
-dependence, to the scalar-scalar (
\ddot{\phi}
-
\ddot{\phi}
) and scalar-metric (
\ddot{\phi}
-
\ddot{h}
) blocks.

A detailed and rigorous calculation, found in the literature on higher-order scalar-tensor theories
[
25

4

26

is degenerate if and only if a second condition is met. This condition establishes a precise relationship between the remaining functional degrees of freedom in
\beta_{GB}
and
\beta_{W^{2}}

The condition is

\text{Degeneracy Condition 2:}\quad\frac{\partial\beta_{GB}}{\partial X}+2\frac{\partial\beta_{W^{2}}}{\partial\phi}=0\,.

(5.6)

Classically, the DHOST theory is already degenerate, which guarantees that the right number of degrees of freedom is being propagates. The new kinetic terms from the quantum corrections threaten to lift this degeneracy and unleash the ghost.
The first condition,
\frac{\partial\beta_{W^{2}}}{\partial X}=0

\phi
coming from
\frac{\partial\beta_{GB}}{\partial X}

Summarizing, the Hamiltonian analysis, by demanding the absence of the Ostrogradsky ghost, imposes two conditions on the functions
\beta_{GB}(\phi,X)
and
\beta_{W^{2}}(\phi,X)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\beta_{W^{2}}}{\partial X}=0\,,

(5.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\beta_{GB}}{\partial X}+2\frac{\partial\beta_{W^{2}}}{\partial\phi}=0\,.

(5.8)

These are the
degeneracy conditions

7
Conclusions

In this work we have addressed the fundamental tension between the necessity of higher-derivative quantum corrections in scalar–tensor effective field theories and the stringent requirement of ghost freedom. Classical DHOST theories are carefully constructed to avoid the Ostrogradsky instability through degeneracy conditions that ensure a consistent Hamiltonian structure. However, the inclusion of higher-curvature operators required by effective field theory considerations, such as Weyl-squared and Gauss–Bonnet terms, can in principle spoil this structure and reintroduce pathological degrees of freedom.

To investigate this issue, we studied whether the protective symmetry underlying the classical theory can be consistently extended to include these quantum corrections. Our analysis followed two complementary paths. First, we imposed that the full action—including curvature-squared operators with general coefficients
\beta_{\mathrm{GB}}(\phi,X)
and
\beta_{W^{2}}(\phi,X)
—remain invariant under the gauge transformations of the classical theory. This requirement leads to a set of coupled differential constraints on the coefficient functions. Second, we performed an independent Hamiltonian analysis using the ADM formalism. By enforcing the primary and secondary constraints necessary to eliminate the Ostrogradsky mode, we derived the conditions required for the dynamical consistency of the theory.
Our central result is that these two sets of conditions coincide. Both approaches require that the Weyl-squared coefficient depends only on the scalar field,
\beta_{W^{2}}(\phi)
, and that the kinetic dependence of the Gauss–Bonnet coupling is fixed through the relation
\partial_{X}\beta_{\mathrm{GB}}=-2\,\partial_{\phi}\beta_{W^{2}}
. This equivalence shows that the gauge symmetry of the classical theory is not merely a formal property but provides the fundamental origin of the stability conditions emerging from the Hamiltonian analysis. In this sense, gauge invariance and Hamiltonian consistency represent two complementary manifestations of the same underlying physical principle.

At the same time, our results reveal an important limitation. Ensuring the preservation of this symmetry at the quantum level requires the coefficient functions to take very specific forms. In particular, the
\beta
-functions cannot appear as generic constants, as typically expected in an effective field theory expansion, but must acquire a highly constrained dependence on the scalar field and its kinetic term. As discussed in Section
4
, this requirement introduces a degree of fine tuning in both the classical parameters and the quantum corrections, indicating that the protective symmetry is not generically stable under radiative effects.

A distinctive aspect of this work is the use of artificial intelligence as a tool to explore this problem. Using the AI system
Denario
, we investigated the existence of symmetry structures capable of preserving the stability of DHOST theories in the presence of higher-derivative corrections. This approach led us to identify a symmetry of the form given in Eq. (
3.5
), which we subsequently showed to be equivalent to the stability constraints obtained through the Hamiltonian analysis presented in Section
5
.

To the best of our knowledge, this work represents one of the first attempts to employ artificial intelligence to tackle fundamental questions in cosmology and gravitational theory. The assumptions adopted throughout the analysis were chosen to ensure the tractability of the problem, but the results presented here suggest several possible directions for future research. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate whether the symmetry structure identified here can be generalized to broader classes of modified gravity theories and whether similar AI-assisted approaches can provide new insights into the theoretical consistency of gravitational effective field theories.

Hence the classical DHOST action is invariant under the local
transformation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta_{\epsilon}\phi=\epsilon(c_{0}-c_{1}X),\\
&\delta_{\epsilon}g_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon(-c_{1}\phi^{\rho}\partial_{\rho})}g_{\mu\nu}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(A.21)

This gauge symmetry is the mechanism that removes the extra scalar
degree of freedom present in generic higher–derivative scalar–tensor
theories, guaranteeing that the classical sector propagates only the
three physical modes (two tensor and one scalar) characteristic of
degenerate scalar–tensor theories.

Appendix B
Explicit computations of the
1-
Loop effective action

We expand the fields around a classical background solution:

g_{\mu\nu}=\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\phi=\bar{\phi}+\varphi\,.

(B.1)

The action is expanded to second order in the fluctuations
(h_{\mu\nu},\varphi)

S[\bar{g}+h,\bar{\phi}+\varphi]=S[\bar{g},\bar{\phi}]+S^{(1)}+S^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(3)\,.

(B.2)

S^{(1)}
vanishes as the background fields satisfy the equations of motion.

S^{(2)}
is the quadratic action governing the dynamics of the fluctuations.

S^{(2)}
is composed of contributions from the Einstein-Hilbert term and the DHOST terms.

Einstein-Hilbert part
(c_{0}R)
:

The expansion of
\sqrt{-g}\,c_{0}R
is standard. To second order in
h_{\mu\nu}
, it gives

S^{(2)}_{\mathrm{EH}}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-\bar{g}}\;h^{\mu\nu}K_{\mathrm{Lich}}h_{\mu\nu}\,,

(B.3)

where
K_{\mathrm{Lich}}
is the Lichnerowicz operator
[
53
]
, a second-order differential operator.

DHOST part
\big(C^{\cdots}\nabla^{2}\phi\,\nabla^{2}\phi\big)
:

This part is significantly more complex. Expanding it yields terms quadratic in
h_{\mu\nu}
,
\varphi
, and their mixings. The expansion involves derivatives up to fourth order.
The resulting structure for the full quadratic action is

S^{(2)}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-\bar{g}}\,\left[\varphi\,K_{\varphi\varphi}\,\varphi+2\,\varphi\,K_{\varphi h}^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}+h^{\mu\nu}K_{hh\,\mu\nu\rho\sigma}h^{\rho\sigma}\right]\,.

(B.4)

The operators have the following properties

•

K_{\varphi\varphi}:
scalar differential operator acting on
\varphi
up to fourth-order derivatives, e.g.
c_{1}(\bar{\Box})^{2}
.

•

K_{\varphi h}:
tensor-valued mixing operator between
\varphi\text{ and }h_{\mu\nu},
up to third-order derivatives.

•

K_{hh}:
fourth-rank tensor operator acting on
h_{\mu\nu}
including EH and DHOST terms, up to fourth-order derivatives.

The operator
K
derived from
S^{(2)}
is singular due to diffeomorphism invariance.
To perform the path integral, we add a gauge-fixing term
S_{\mathrm{gf}}
.
A standard choice is the de Donder gauge condition on
h_{\mu\nu}
.
Gauge-fixing action

S_{\mathrm{gf}}=\frac{1}{2\alpha_{\mathrm{gf}}}\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-\bar{g}}\;G_{\mu}G^{\mu}\,,

(B.5)

where

G_{\mu}=\bar{\nabla}^{\nu}h_{\nu\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\nabla}_{\mu}h\,.

(B.6)

This term modifies the
K_{hh}
part of the kinetic operator, making it invertible.
The introduction of gauge fixing requires the Faddeev-Popov ghost action
S_{\mathrm{ghost}}
.
Ghost action

S_{\mathrm{ghost}}=\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-\bar{g}}\;\bar{c}^{\mu}K^{\mathrm{ghost}}_{\mu\nu}c^{\nu}\,.

(B.7)

The ghost operator is

K^{\mathrm{ghost}}_{\mu\nu}=-\left(\delta_{\mu\nu}\bar{\Box}+\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\right)\,.

(B.8)

The ghosts are vector fields and do not couple to
\varphi
at quadratic level.
The complete action at quadratic order, used for one-loop calculation, is

S_{\mathrm{quad}}=S^{(2)}+S_{\mathrm{gf}}+S_{\mathrm{ghost}}\,.

(B.9)

The one-loop effective action is formally given by

\exp\!\left(i\Gamma^{(1)}\right)=\int\mathcal{D}[\varphi]\mathcal{D}[h]\mathcal{D}[c]\mathcal{D}[\bar{c}]\,\exp\!\left(iS_{\mathrm{quad}}\right)\,.

(B.10)

This gives the formal one-loop correction

\Gamma^{(1)}=\frac{i}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\log K_{\mathrm{gf}}\right)-i\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\log K_{\mathrm{ghost}}\right)\,,

(B.11)

where
K_{\mathrm{gf}}
is the gauge-fixed kinetic operator for the
(\varphi,h_{\mu\nu})
system.

The key takeaway is the structure of
K_{\mathrm{gf}}
: it is a matrix operator containing up to fourth-order derivatives acting on the
(\varphi,h_{\mu\nu})
field space. The subsequent one-loop divergence analysis via heat-kernel methods depends on the coefficients of the highest-derivative terms in this operator.
