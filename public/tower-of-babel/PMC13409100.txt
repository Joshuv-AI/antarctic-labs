Comparison of ultrasound-guided intertransverse process block versus paravertebral block for postoperative quality of recovery in elderly patients undergoing minimally invasive thoracic surgery: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yongwei Su, Xuefei Li, Xia He, Yanhong Li, Yu Cai, Dan Lu, Rurong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThoracic paravertebral block (PVB) is widely used for analgesia but carries risks such as pneumothorax and hypotension. Intertransverse process block (ITPB) is a newer alternative that may offer comparable analgesia with a potentially improved safety profile. However, few studies have compared the two from the patient’s perspective, and none have assessed ultrasound-guided ITPB against PVB for postoperative recovery quality in elderly patients.Methods and analysisThis two-centre, double-blind, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial will enrol 152 elderly patients (age ≥65 years) undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) at West China Hospital and Shangjin Branch of West China Hospital, Sichuan University in China. Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either ultrasound-guided ITPB (n=76) or PVB (n=76). The primary outcome is the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score on postoperative day 1. Secondary outcomes will include (1) QoR-15 scores measured on postoperative days 2, 3, 7 and 30; (2) pain scores at rest and during movement assessed on postoperative days 1, 2 and 3 using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS); (3) worst NRS resting pain score within 30 min of post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) admission; (4) proportion of patients requiring rescue analgesia within 24 hours after surgery; (5) patient satisfaction with analgesia within 24 hours after surgery; (6) total opioid consumption within 24 hours after surgery; (7) incidence of major complications of surgery within 7 days after surgery; (8) length of postoperative hospital stay; and (9) number of days at home within 30 days after surgery.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has received approval from the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval no. 2603), and the Ethics Committee of Shangjin Branch of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval no. 2613). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Study results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberChiCTR2600116524

Introduction

More than 300 million non-cardiac surgeries are performed annually worldwide. As the global population ages, the number of elderly patients requiring surgical intervention continues to rise.1 Lung cancer remains among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) resection is regarded as the most effective treatment for early-stage lung cancer.2 Although minimally invasive, VATS can still damage thoracic muscles and nerves and frequently causes moderate-to-severe pain, particularly during the first 24 hours.3 In elderly patients, reduced organ function and cardiopulmonary reserve and greater sensitivity to anaesthetics often leave postoperative pain undertreated, which impairs sputum clearance and in turn raises the risk of respiratory complications, delays recovery and increases morbidity and mortality.4–6

Poorly controlled acute pain is a significant risk factor for chronic post-surgical pain (CPSP). The incidence of CPSP at 3 and 6 months following thoracic surgery has been reported as 57% and 47%, respectively,7 8 which are the highest among all surgical procedures. In elderly patients, CPSP further undermines quality of recovery and is linked to greater medium-term and long-term morbidity, mortality and social cost. 9Improving analgesia after minimally invasive thoracic surgery, to speed up early recovery and support enhanced recovery after surgery, therefore remains an important challenge.

Thoracic epidural analgesia and paravertebral block (PVB) are recognised as the gold standards for regional analgesia in thoracotomy surgery.10 11 Although PVB has been widely adopted as the standard analgesic approach for VATS,12 13 high-quality comparative evidence remains limited.14 As nerve block techniques have advanced, fascial plane blocks—including the erector spinae plane block (ESPB), serratus anterior plane block (SAPB), and intercostal nerve block (INB)—have been shown to lower the risk of complications such as pneumothorax and hypotension because the needle tip stays away from the paravertebral space, epidural space, spinal cord and lung. Published comparisons of these blocks in minimally invasive lung surgery, however, are heterogeneous in protocol, and their findings are inconsistent.15–19 Most of these studies have focused on pain relief alone rather than on recovery as a patient-centred outcome. Because elderly patients are also more susceptible to block-related complications such as hypotension and hypoxaemia, safer and more effective regional techniques are needed to improve their quality of recovery.

The intertransverse process block (ITPB), first described by Costache et al, involves the injection of local anaesthetic into the tissue plane posterior to the superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL).20 Cadaveric work suggests that PVB acts mainly on the ventral rami and ESPB on the dorsal rami, whereas ITPB reaches the ventral rami at one or two levels while covering the dorsal rami widely.21–23 By acting next to the paravertebral space without entering it, ITPB should in theory carry a lower risk of pneumothorax and haematoma than PVB. Research has demonstrated that ITPB provides effective pain relief after breast surgery24 and is non-inferior to TPVB at 24 hours postoperatively in pulmonary resection via VATS.25 Given that elderly patients are especially vulnerable to hypotension and hypoxaemia after conventional blocks, ITPB may be a viable alternative to PVB for analgesia in VATS.

To help fill this evidence gap for older patients undergoing VATS, we will conduct an investigator-initiated, two-centre, double-blind, randomised controlled trial comparing ultrasound-guided ITPB with PVB for early quality of recovery assessed using the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) in elderly patients undergoing VATS. We hypothesise that ITPB will increase QoR-15 scores more than PVB.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This investigator-initiated, two-centre, double-blind, parallel-group, randomised controlled clinical trial will be conducted at West China Hospital and Shangjin Branch (Chengdu Shang Jin Nan Fu Hospital) of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, in China. The protocol and result reporting will comply with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 Statement, and the study design will adhere to the Standard Protocol Items for Randomised Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines (online supplemental file 3). Recruitment and informed consent

All patients scheduled for VATS will undergo eligibility screening the day before surgery. Study details including objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits will be explained to participants and their legal representatives before the operation. All questions will be addressed to ensure full comprehension. Eligible patients will be informed that their personal information will remain strictly confidential.

Prior to study enrolment, informed consent will be obtained from each patient or their legally authorised representative. Each participant will receive at least 24 hours’ notice before consideration. Voluntariness is a fundamental aspect of the informed consent process. Participants may withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason, without compromising their right to receive optimal medical care. The model participant consent form is provided as online supplemental file 2.

Randomisation and blinding

An independent researcher, not involved in participant recruitment, intervention administration, data collection or statistical analysis, will generate the random sequence using a computer. Allocation will be performed in a 1:1 ratio. Block randomisation with a block size of four will be implemented. Eligible participants will be randomised following completion of the screening process. The randomisation results will be sealed in opaque envelopes labelled with unique screening numbers corresponding to the order in which participants are screened. Each envelope will contain the participant’s random number and assigned group allocation. An independent assistant, who is unrelated to the study, will open a sealed envelope 1 hour before surgery and inform the nerve block operator of the designated nerve block technique for each participant. Patients will remain unaware of their allocated interventions, as interventions are performed after sedation.

Blinding will be maintained for participants, anaesthesiologists, surgeons, ward staff, data collectors and statisticians. Unblinding of participants and their family members is strictly prohibited throughout the study period, including follow-up. Surgeons, nurses, follow-up investigators and statisticians will remain unaware of group allocation. Only the nerve block operators will be informed of group assignments.

Emergency unblinding will be permitted exclusively in cases of serious adverse events, such as anaphylactic shock, local anaesthetic toxicity, severe cardiac arrhythmia, life-threatening conditions or severe medication errors, including incorrect labelling or drug confusion. In these circumstances, researchers can access group allocation information to develop targeted therapeutic strategies.

Standard anaesthetic and analgesic management

Standard monitoring will be applied to all participants, including electrocardiography, peripheral pulse oxygen saturation, blood pressure, end-tidal carbon dioxide, body temperature and bispectral index (BIS). The intraoperative haemodynamic goal is to maintain systolic blood pressure within 20% of baseline and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO₂) at or >94%.

Anaesthesia induction will be performed with sufentanil 0.3–0.5 µg/kg, rocuronium bromide 0.8–1.0 mg/kg (a non-depolarising muscle relaxant) and propofol 1–2 mg/kg. For participants without contraindications, flurbiprofen axetil 5 mg and dexamethasone 0.1 mg/kg will be administered intravenously after successful induction of anaesthesia. After adequate depth of anaesthesia is achieved, tracheal intubation and one-lung ventilation will be achieved using a video laryngoscope-assisted double-lumen endotracheal tube. Anaesthesia maintenance will be achieved with sevoflurane and propofol, maintaining a BIS of 40–60. Intraoperative analgesia will be maintained with remifentanil infusion at 0.1–0.3 µg/kg/h and intermittent boluses of sufentanil. Prophylactic antiemetic ondansetron 0.1–0.15 mg/kg will be administered 30 min before the end of surgery. Sugammadex 2.0–4.0 mg/kg will be administered as a muscle relaxant antagonist at the completion of surgery. The use of esketamine will be prohibited. A restrictive fluid management strategy will be implemented to avoid fluid overload. Blood product transfusion will be performed in accordance with routine clinical practice and the anaesthesiologist’s judgement. Antibiotics and invasive procedures, such as gastric tube and urinary catheter insertion, will be appropriately administered based on each participant’s condition.

Postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) will be provided. The PCIA pump will be set to sufentanil 100 µg and granisetron 9 mg, diluted to 100 mL with normal saline, with a background infusion rate of 2 mL/hour, a bolus dose of 2 mL and a lockout interval of 15 min. Routine postoperative analgesia in the ward will include flurbiprofen axetil 5 mg two times daily. Rescue analgesia will consist of additional PCIA bolus doses. Inadequate analgesia will be defined as an NRS score of ≥4 after two consecutive PCIA boluses, prompting intravenous administration of 100 mg tramadol as rescue analgesia in the ward.

Pressure-controlled ventilation with volume guarantee will be used for all participants during intraoperative mechanical ventilation, from post-intubation to pre-extubation. Tidal volumes will be set at 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight for two-lung ventilation and 4–6 mL/kg for one-lung ventilation, with a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cm H2O. Respiratory rate will be adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 mmHg. At the end of surgery, a standard recruitment manoeuvre will be performed after confirmation of haemodynamic stability by the anaesthesiologist: fraction of inspired oxygen at or below 30%, inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:1, respiratory rate of 8 breaths per minute and PEEP increased by 2–4 cm H2O every 2–4 breaths until plateau pressure reaches 35 cm H2O, followed by three additional breaths at the target plateau pressure.

Following surgery, participants will be transferred to the PACU. Discharge from the PACU to the general ward will occur once participants meet PACU discharge criteria, defined as an Aldrete score >9. Routine postoperative respiratory training will be provided.

Interventions

All nerve blocks will be conducted in the pre-anaesthetic room. Participants will undergo continuous monitoring, receive supplementary oxygen and have intravenous access established. They will be assisted in the lateral decubitus position with the surgical side facing upward, and 1–2 mg of midazolam will be administered intravenously. Following skin disinfection, a 2–5 MHz high-frequency linear ultrasound probe (Mindray UMT-500, China) will be used to scan the 12th rib in the parasagittal plane approximately 5 cm lateral to the midline. The probe will be advanced cephalad until the fifth–sixth intercostal space is identified.

ITPB group: ITPB will be administered using an in-plane technique, as described by Kilicaslan et al.26 The probe will be positioned at the fifth intercostal space for a parasagittal ultrasound scan. After identification of the intertransverse ligament, transverse process, SCTL and pleura, 1–2 mL of 1% lidocaine will be injected for local infiltration. A needle (Stimuplex D, 22G; B. Braun, Germany) will be advanced in-plane in a caudal-to-cranial direction under continuous ultrasound guidance to ensure accurate needle placement. After negative aspiration for blood and air, 15 mL of a mixed local anaesthetic solution (0.5% ropivacaine plus 5 mg dexamethasone) will be administered.

PVB group: the probe will be positioned at the fifth intercostal space with its longitudinal axis perpendicular to the spine. After identification of the spinous process, transverse process and paravertebral space, 1–2 mL of 1% lidocaine will be administered for local infiltration. The needle will be inserted using an in-plane technique. Once the needle tip penetrates the superior costotransverse ligament and negative aspiration for blood and air is confirmed, 15 mL of the same mixed local anaesthetic solution will be injected, with continuous observation for pleural indentation.

All nerve blocks will be performed or supervised by senior anaesthesiologists with specialised experience in regional anaesthesia, each having independently completed at least 50 cases of both ITPB and PVB. All practitioners will undergo standardised training prior to the study and will not participate in subsequent follow-up. Ultrasound images will be continuously acquired during all procedures. Senior anaesthesiologists will regularly monitor nerve blocks and ultrasound images to ensure successful, accurate placement.

Data collection, monitoring and management

Completion of the Case Report Form (CRF) for all participants will commence at the screening phase and conclude 30 days postoperatively. Investigators are required to review the CRF completion guidelines prior to data entry. All data must be recorded objectively, accurately and comprehensively in the CRF, with every item on each page completed. On completing each page, the investigator must sign and date the form. The sequence of CRF pages generally corresponds to the study timeline. Investigators should complete the CRF sequentially, page by page and item by item, as dictated by study progression. The CRF is organised into three sections: screening, intraoperative observation and postoperative follow-up.

During the screening phase, eligible participants will be assigned unique screening numbers. The screening form and preoperative follow-up section will be completed at this stage. Following completion of all preoperative visits and assignment of a random number, the intraoperative observation phase will commence, followed by the postoperative follow-up phase. Study participation for each enrolled participant will conclude once all pages of the corresponding CRF are fully completed. A discharge summary is required to be completed promptly on participant discharge. Follow-up investigators will conduct telephone follow-ups 7 and 30 days after discharge, with all findings documented in the CRF. These data will be double-entered into Excel by two trained research assistants.

This study will develop a standardised operating protocol for these nerve blocks, which should make the technique easier to learn and apply in routine anaesthetic practice. We will also use a structured follow-up strategy to maximise completeness of follow-up.

Throughout the trial, the principal investigator (PI) is responsible for overall data oversight and quality control. Standard operating procedures will be established, and dedicated quality assurance personnel will carry out regular inspections to confirm protocol adherence. All study personnel will complete formal clinical-trial training under senior supervision and must understand their assigned responsibilities, and the study team will remain unchanged throughout. Specific staff are responsible for data collection, entry, verification and statistical analysis with monthly dual-person cross-checks. Once data collection is complete, the PI will notify the institutional ethics committee of trial closure, following the applicable regulations and procedures. All adverse events will be documented in detail, managed appropriately and monitored until the patient’s condition resolves or stabilises. Severe and unexpected adverse events will be reported promptly to the PI, the institutional review board, competent authorities and drug regulatory authorities, as required. The PI will periodically conduct cumulative reviews of all adverse events. When necessary, an investigator meeting will be convened to evaluate the study’s risk–benefit profile. Emergency unblinding may be implemented in double-blind trials to protect subjects’ safety and rights.

Sample size calculation

Preliminary (unpublished) data from our hospital showed that the mean and SD of the QoR-15 score on postoperative day 1 in elderly patients undergoing VATS with intraoperative PVB were 84±12. Consequently, a mean (SD) of 84 (12) QoR-15 score was assumed in the PVB group, and the minimal clinically important difference of the QoR-15 scale was defined as 6.0.28 Using a two-sided hypothesis test with a type I error rate (α) of 0.05, statistical power (1−β) of 80% and anticipated 20% dropout rate, the total sample size calculated using PASS V.15.0 was 152 participants (n=76, per group).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will employ two-sided tests with a significance level (∝) of 0.05. Test statistics and corresponding p-values will be reported for all analyses, with p≤0.05 considered statistically significant. CIs will be provided when appropriate. Analyses will be performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26.0. The primary analytical procedures are described below:

A comprehensive record of all dropouts and study discontinuations, including the reasons for each, will be maintained. The anticipated dropout rate will be capped at 20%. Any rate exceeding this threshold will be subject to further analysis and explanation. Outliers will be identified and confirmed, and missing data will be addressed using appropriate imputation methods. The overall proportion of missing data for all outcome measures in this trial will be <5%, a low rate of missingness. Multiple imputation will be used to handle the limited missing data and reduce bias, and the main analyses will be based on the imputed datasets.

Statistical analyses will be primarily performed on the modified intention-to-treat population, defined as all randomised participants who undergo surgery, do not withdraw informed consent and have available primary outcome data. Additionally, a per-protocol analysis will be conducted for the primary outcome measure.

The normality of quantitative data will be assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, results will be presented as mean±SD, and between-group comparisons will be performed using one-way analysis of variance. For non-normally distributed data, results will be presented as median and IQR, with between-group comparisons conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical data will be summarised as frequencies and percentages, and between-group comparisons will be performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Ordinal data will be analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Generalised estimating equations with robust standard errors will be used to account for repeated measurements of pain and QoR-15 scores.

Multivariate logistic regression will be used to adjust for confounding factors, including surgical type. Prespecified subgroup analyses will be conducted based on age, gender, body mass index, tumour stage, surgical type and operative duration.

Discussion

VATS is now considered the cornerstone of modern clinical practice for lung surgery worldwide.29 Although VATS has been rapidly adopted for its minimally invasive approach, it can still cause moderate-to-severe postoperative pain when analgesia is suboptimal.3 This matters most in elderly patients, in whom poorly controlled pain delays mobilisation, increases pulmonary complications and prolongs hospital stay.4

PVB is widely regarded as the standard analgesic technique for VATS; however, its deep target carries risks such as pneumothorax and vascular puncture.10 11 These concerns have led to the exploration of more superficial fascial plane blocks, including the ESPB, SAPB and INB, which may provide comparable efficacy with improved safety profiles. No consensus has emerged on the best regional technique, which underlines the need for direct comparisons between newer options such as the ITPB and established standards.15–19

Studies in cadavers and healthy volunteers show that local anaesthetic injected at the retro-superior costotransverse ligament during ITPB spreads widely, reaching the intercostal and paravertebral spaces, anterolateral vertebral body (sympathetic chain), erector spinae plane and intervertebral foramina.22–26 A single-level injection can produce multi-level paravertebral spread, giving analgesia comparable with that of multiple-injection techniques in thoracic procedures.30 Compared with PVB, ITPB offers a more superficial needle target with similar analgesia and potentially fewer complications such as pneumothorax and hypotension. Recent trials have reported that after breast surgery, ITPB provides better early pain relief than either PVB or ESPB.23 31 In laparoscopic gastric cancer resection, ITPB has demonstrated more sustained and potent analgesia than the transversus abdominis plane block. 32 It has also been shown to be non-inferior to PVB during the first 24 hours after VATS.25 Evidence also supports moving from multiple-injection to single-injection techniques, with no loss of analgesic potency in thoracic procedures.30 31

Current evidence is limited by a scarcity of comparative trials and a traditional emphasis on narrow clinical metrics rather than comprehensive, patient-centred outcomes. The QoR-15 is an internationally validated tool for assessing overall recovery quality after surgery.27 To our knowledge, this will be the first randomised controlled trial to compare ultrasound-guided ITPB with PVB in elderly patients undergoing VATS using QoR-15 as a patient-centred primary outcome

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study lacks a standardised sensory assessment to confirm block success, as interventions are performed after sedation to maintain blinding. Clinical surrogates, specifically postoperative NRS scores and rescue analgesic requirements, are used to evaluate the efficacy of ITPB and PVB. Second, CPSP will not be measured as a long-term, patient-centred outcome.7 Although this trial addresses the acute phase, progression from acute to chronic pain is an important measure of the overall value of regional technique, and longer follow-up will be needed to show whether the early benefits of ITPB translate into less chronic pain.33 Third, the fixed 15 mL block volume may have constrained the findings. As the efficacy of ITPB and PVB depends on the injectate’s longitudinal spread, further dose-finding studies are needed to define the optimal volume for VATS.34 Finally, the study will be conducted at two centres, which may affect the generalisability of findings to other settings or populations.
