Different treatments regulate starch degradation, ethylene biosynthesis, and energy metabolism involved in the preparation and shelf life of ready-to-eat kiwifruit
Authors: Xianzhi Liu, Senlin Tang, Ning Ji, Jiqing Lei, Cunkun Chen, Pufan Zheng, Bangdi Liu, Rui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid senescence of ready-to-eat kiwifruit limits its commercial expansion. We hypothesized that starch degradation, ethylene biosynthesis, and energy metabolism are key metabolic pathways regulating the edible window of ready-to-eat kiwifruit. This study investigated the effects of alternating temperature, ethanol, ethylene, and control treatments on post-ripening behavior and shelf life of kiwifruit. Fruit were treated until their soluble solids content reached 13%–15%, followed by application of 0.5 μL·L−1 1-methylcyclopropene. Ethanol and ethylene treatments accelerated ripening and shortened shelf life, as reflected by elevated respiration, ethylene bursts, accelerated starch degradation, and enhanced energy metabolism. Conversely, alternating temperature suppressed physiological transitions, maintained energy homeostasis, and extended the edible window. At the molecular level, starch degradation genes (AcBAM3.1/3.5/9), ethylene biosynthesis genes (AcACO1/2), and the energy metabolism-related gene (Acc10529/PFK1) were significantly upregulated in ethanol- and ethylene-treated fruit during the pre-ripening phase. In contrast, fruit exposed to alternating temperature maintained lower expression of these genes throughout storage without impeding normal ripening. This reduction in transcriptional activity was associated with improved cellular energy balance and reduced postharvest spoilage. Overall, these findings provide insights into the physiological and transcriptional responses of postharvest kiwifruit to different ripening treatments and identify candidate pathways influencing shelf life.Keywords: Flavor, Postharvest ripening, Soluble solids content, Transcriptomic analysis

Introduction

The accelerating pace of modern life and changing dietary habits have increased demand for ready-to-eat food products that can be consumed directly or with minimal processing. Owing to their convenience, these products, including ready-to-eat meats, pre-prepared dishes, and ready-to-eat fruits and vegetables, have gained substantial market attention. (Liu et al., 2023; Thomas et al., 2024). Among these, ready-to-eat fruits are particularly valued for their vitamin and dietary fiber content. Ready-to-eat kiwifruit has attracted considerable interest because of its distinctive flavor and nutritional benefits (Chai et al., 2023). Compared with conventional kiwifruit, ready-to-eat kiwifruit (soluble solids content (SSC): 14–20%; pulp firmness: 3.1–14 N (N)) reaches the edible stage more rapidly. However, this stage overlaps with the transition from full ripeness to senescence, resulting in a relatively short shelf life (Yan et al., 2022). Therefore, maintaining fruit quality during this period remains a major challenge to its commercial production.

Few studies have investigated methods for producing ready-to-eat kiwifruit. In our previous study, kiwifruit was ripened with ethylene to reach the edible window and subsequently treated with 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP), an ethylene action inhibitor, to extend shelf life (Yan et al., 2022). Similarly, Chai et al. (2021) demonstrated that 1-MCP effectively prolongs shelf life at 23 ± 1 °C. Although ethylene-induced ripening effectively promotes desirable changes, including flesh softening and sugar accumulation, it may also cause flesh water-soaking and reduce shelf life (Lim & Lee, 2017; Yan et al., 2022). Ethanol has also been reported to promote kiwifruit ripening (Mencarelli et al. 1991). However, its application is limited by noticeable alcoholic odor and increased susceptibility to decay. More recently, Ting-Ting et al. (2023) reported that a variable temperature regime in ‘Xu Xiang’ kiwifruit—15 °C for 24 h, 25 °C for 24 h, and then 15 °C until ripening—accelerated fruit ripening by promoting flesh softening and rapidly increasing SSC while maintaining low spoilage rates during shelf life. These findings suggest that ripening treatments differentially regulate key physiological and metabolic processes. We hypothesized that these effects are largely mediated by starch metabolism, energy metabolism, and ethylene signaling pathways involved in ripening progression.

Previous studies on climacteric fruits, including kiwifruit and apple, have primarily focused on the molecular regulation of ripening from physiological maturity to full ripeness. In kiwifruit, exogenous ethylene upregulates ethylene biosynthesis-related genes, including AdACS1, AdACO1, and AdACO5, promoting endogenous ethylene production and accelerating ripening (Wang et al., 2018). Similarly, in apples, exogenous ethylene upregulates the expression of ethylene receptor genes (ETR1, ETR2, ETR5, ERS1, and ERS2), ethylene response factors (ERF1 and ERF2), and ethylene biosynthesis-related genes (ACS1, ACS3, ACO1, and ACO2), thereby stimulating endogenous ethylene synthesis and fruit ripening (Song et al., 2013). However, the edible window of ready-to-eat kiwifruit spans the period from full ripeness to senescence. Research on ready-to-eat kiwifruit has primarily focused on flavor characteristics and the effects of 1-MCP on postharvest quality (Chai et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023), whereas the molecular mechanisms underlying physiological changes during this critical stage remain poorly understood.

Previous studies have shown that ethylene, ethanol, and alternating temperature treatments can modulate kiwifruit ripening. Our preliminary experiments further indicated that these treatments directly influence the shelf life and quality of ready-to-eat kiwifruit during the edible window. We hypothesized that these treatments regulate ripening and shelf-life stability through distinct mechanisms. Building on our previous study (Yan et al., 2022), we investigated the effects of these treatments on the ripening rate and shelf life of ready-to-eat kiwifruit. Furthermore, we performed transcriptomic profiling and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to examine treatment-specific effects on starch degradation, ethylene biosynthesis, and energy metabolism, thereby providing insights into the molecular basis underlying differences in ripening behavior and shelf-life stability.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

‘Guichang’ kiwifruit (SSC: 9.0 ± 0.6%; dry matter: 18.5 ± 1.0%) were harvested on October 9, 2024, from a commercial orchard in Xiuwen, Guizhou, China (26.84° N, 106.58° E) and immediately transported to the laboratory. Sound fruit of uniform size and free of visible defects were randomly assigned to four treatment groups. Each treatment comprised three biological replicates (n = 3), with 390 fruits per replicate, stored in 60-L sealed plastic boxes (55 × 40 × 31.5 cm) at 85–90% relative humidity (RH). The treatments were as follows: (1) control, maintained continuously at 25 °C; (2) alternating temperature, cycled between 4 °C and 25 °C every 12 h via room transfer; (3) ethanol fumigation at 400 μL·L−1 at 25 °C (Liu et al., 2012) using 12.2 mL anhydrous ethanol (99.5% v/v) on filter paper, replenished after each ventilation cycle; and (4) ethylene treatment at 250 μL·L−1 at 25 °C, generated by injecting 7.6 mL of ethylene based on the net headspace volume (30.52 L) and fruit volume displacement (75.6 mL). Ethylene concentrations were monitored daily by gas chromatography (Yan et al., 2022). To prevent anaerobic conditions, boxes were ventilated every 12 h for 30 s using an axial-flow fan (15 m3·min−1). Headspace O2 and CO2 concentrations were measured before ventilation using a handheld analyzer (Check Point II, Dansensor, Ringsted, Denmark) to ensure that O2 levels remained above 5% throughout the treatment period (Yan et al., 2022). To precisely target the onset of the edible window, all groups were maintained under their respective treatments until fruit SSC reached 14.0 ± 1.0%, the optimal threshold for the senescence-inhibitory effect of 1-MCP (Yan et al., 2022). Then, the fruit were immediately fumigated with 0.5 μL·L−1 1-MCP at 25 °C for 12 h in sealed containers and transferred to ambient conditions (25 °C, 85–90% RH) to simulate postharvest shelf life. The experiment followed a randomized block design with each box as an independent unit. Stratified sampling was conducted from top, middle, and bottom layers at set intervals during treatment (1–8 days) and shelf-life (S1–S13 days) following ISO 874:1980. At each time point, 20 fruits per replicate were used for decay assessment (60 fruits per treatment; n = 3). Six fruits per replicate were analyzed for firmness, SSC, respiration, and ethylene production. Another six fruits per replicate were pooled, peeled, flash frozen, and used to prepare composite pulp samples (n = 3 per treatment), representing each experimental unit.

Determination of SSC, firmness, decay rate, and vitamin C content

Fruit SSC and firmness were determined using six fruits per replicate box. SSC was measured using a handheld digital refractometer (PAL-1, ATAGO, Tokyo, Japan) from juice extracted from the same fruit and expressed as percentage (%). Firmness was assessed using a texture analyzer (EZ-Test, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) following Yan et al. (2022). Measurements were taken at three equatorial points on each horizontally positioned fruit. A P/2 probe was used for outer and inner pericarp tissues, whereas a P/4 probe was used for the core. Test conditions were: pre-test speed, 2 mm·s−1; test speed, 5 mm·s−1; post-test speed, 10 mm·s−1; trigger force, 5 g; and penetration depth, 6 mm. Results were expressed in N. Decay rate was calculated using all 20 fruits per replicate box (60 fruits per treatment) following Choi et al. (2019). Fruit were classified as decayed when visible fungal growth or tissue breakdown occurred with the lesion diameter exceeding 5 mm. Decay rate was expressed as a percentage (%) of the total fruit assessed. Vitamin C (Vc) content was determined using the 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol titration method with minor modifications. Frozen flesh tissue (2 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold (4 °C) 2% (w/v) oxalic acid and filtered. The filtrate was titrated with 0.1% (w/v) 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol (Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) until a stable pink color persisted for 15 s. Vc content was expressed as mg·100 g−1 fresh weight.

Determination of respiration rate, ethylene production rate, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), adenosine diphosphate (ADP), and adenosine monophosphate (AMP) contents

Respiration and ethylene production rates were determined following Yan et al. (2022) and Zhang, Wang, et al. (2024). For each replicate, six intact fruits were sealed in a 1.5-L airtight glass jar and incubated at 25 °C for 2 h. Respiration rate was measured by determining headspace CO2 concentration using a handheld infrared gas analyzer (Check Point II, Dansensor) and expressed as mg CO2·kg−1·h−1. For ethylene production, a 1-mL headspace gas sample was collected with a gas-tight syringe and analyzed using a gas chromatograph (GC-14B, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a flame ionization detector and a Porapak Q column. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at 30 mL·min−1. Injector, column, and detector temperatures were set at 100 °C, 60 °C, and 120 °C, respectively. Ethylene production was quantified against a certified standard and expressed as μL·kg−1·h−1. ATP, ADP, and AMP were quantified using an high performance liquid chromatography system (RIGOL L-3000, Puyuan, Beijing, China) with a Diamonsil C18 column. Frozen tissue (5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of 0.6 mol·L−1 perchloric acid and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was collected, adjusted to pH 6.4–6.8 with 1 mol·L−1 KOH, and incubated on ice for 30 min. Separation was performed using a mobile phase of 0.03 mol·L−1 K2HPO4 and 0.02 mol·L−1 KH2PO4 (A) and methanol (B) under gradient elution at 1.0 mL·min−1 with a 10-μL injection volume: 0 min, 100% A; 7 min, 80% A and 20% B; 9 min, 75% A and 25% B; 10 min, 100% B. Determination of starch content and the activities of α-amylase (AMY), β-amylase (BAM), and total amylase

Starch content and amylase activities (AMY, BAM, and total amylase) were quantified using commercial assay kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) with three biological replicates and two technical replicates per biological replicate. Frozen pulp samples were homogenized in 50 mmol·L−1 sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Starch content was determined at 620 nm (Cary 60, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using a glucose standard curve and expressed as g·kg−1. Amylase activities were measured at 540 nm using a maltose standard curve, normalized to total soluble protein measured by bichinchoninic acid assay, and expressed as U·kg−1. One unit (U) represented the amount of enzyme releasing 1 mg of reducing sugar in min−1·g−1 protein. All standard curves were linear over the range of 0.1–1.0 mg·mL−1 (R2 > 0.99). Recovery rates were 90%–95%, and inter-assay variability was below 5%.

Sensory evaluation, electronic nose, and electronic tongue analyses

Exploratory descriptive sensory evaluation was conducted following Sun and Xu (2023) to contextualize instrumental results. A trained panel of five experts, including three food science professors and two senior graduate students, pre-trained according to ISO 8586:2023, performed assessment based on criteria in Table S1. At each shelf-life sampling point (S0–S13), three representative fruits per treatment were peeled and cut into 1-cm equatorial slices. For blind evaluation, slices were served on white porcelain plates labeled with three-digit random codes. Panelist evaluated three slices per treatment at 25 ± 1 °C for flavor and texture attributes. Volatile profiles were analyzed using a PEN 3 electronic nose (E-nose) system (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with ten metal oxide semiconductor sensors (Table S2). Kiwifruit pulp (5.0 g) was sealed in a 20-mL vial and equilibrated at 25 ± 1 °C for 30 min. Headspace gas was then introduced into the sensor chamber at a constant flow rate. A 60-s automated purge with filtered air was applied before each measurement to stabilize baseline signals and minimize sensor drift. Taste profiles were determined using an SA402B potentiometric electronic tongue (E-tongue) system (Insent Inc., Kanagawa, Japan) equipped with eight lipid membrane sensors detecting sourness, bitterness, astringency, aftertaste-B, aftertaste-A, umami, richness, and saltiness. Pulp (5.0 g) was homogenized with 20.0 mL of deionized water (1:4, w/v) and filtered through two layers of gauze. The filtrate was used as the test solution. Sensors were calibrated with manufacturer standards, and measurements were conducted at 25 ± 1 °C, with automated cleaning between samples to ensure signal stability.

RNA extraction and cDNA library construction

To investigate molecular regulation underlying different ripening rates, transcriptomic analysis was performed at three stages: (1) day 2 (2d), representing early responses and peak induction of starch and ethylene metabolism; (2) S0d, a synchronized edible stage at which all treatments reached the same maturity threshold (SSC: 15%), enabling comparison of convergent molecular profiles; and (3) S3d, representing the post-threshold shelf-life stage for identifying genes involved in metabolic maintenance. Due to accelerated ripening induced by ethanol and ethylene treatments, these groups reached S0d on day 2. In contrast, alternating-temperature fruit required 8 days, whereas control fruit reached S0 at 5 days. Total RNA was extracted from three biological replicates per treatment using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, City, DE, USA). Library construction, purification, and sequencing were conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, poly(A)-selected mRNA (1 μg of total RNA) was isolated, fragmented, and reverse-transcribed into double-stranded cDNA using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The cDNA was end repaired, phosphorylated, ligated with adapters, size selected (300–400 bp), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplified. Libraries were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

RNA-seq analysis

Raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality-filtered using fastp with default settings. The Actinidia chinensis cv. ‘Red 5’ v1.0 reference genome (GenBank Accession: GCA_003024255.1) was used for alignment due to its close genetic relationship with the ‘Guichang’ cultivar of A. chinensis. Clean reads were mapped using HISAT2 v2.1.0, and gene expression levels were quantified and normalized as transcripts per million (TPM). Differential expression analysis was performed in R (v4.5.3) using DESeq2. The Wald test identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with adjusted p-value (p-adjust) <0.05 and |log2 (fold change [FC])| >1 were considered significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on VST-normalized data to evaluate sample clustering and technical reproducibility prior to differential analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed to explore DEG functions and pathways. GO enrichment was conducted using GOATOOLS (v1.3.1), whereas KEGG pathway analysis was performed using the KEGG database (Release 112.0, October 2024), and a hypergeometric test implemented in the Python scipy.stats module.

WGCNA analysis

Transcriptomic analysis included four treatment groups across defined physiological stages. The control and alternating temperature groups were sampled at three stages (2d, S0d, and S3d). However, the ethanol and ethylene groups reached the S0d threshold by day 2; therefore, the 2d and S0d coincided for these two groups, and only one set of triplicate biological replicates was sequenced to represent both stages for each group. In total, 30 transcriptomes were generated (nine samples each for the control and alternating temperature groups, and six samples each for the ethanol and ethylene groups). For co-expression analysis network, 11,448 DEGs were identified using the criteria |log2FC| >1 and p-adjust <0.05. Then, this refined set of highly variable DEGs was used for module construction using the WGCNA package in R. Co-expression modules were generated using the blockwiseModules function with default parameters, except for the soft-thresholding power of 7, a signed TOMType, a mergeCutHeight of 0.25, and a minModuleSize of 30. Gene-trait correlations were visualized using color gradients ranging from blue to red and correlation coefficients ranging from −1 to 1. The module with the deepest color and a value closest to 1 was considered the most strongly associated with the corresponding trait.

Real time-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

RT-qPCR was performed using a LightCycler 480 system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) with the LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master kit (Roche). Primer sequences are listed in Table S3. Actin (GenBank accession no. EF063572) was used as the internal reference (Su et al., 2024). Each sample included three biological replicates, with each biological replicate analyzed in technical triplicates. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analyses

Charts were generated using OriginPro 2026 (OriginLab, City, MA, USA), and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance was used to compare mean values among the four treatment groups, followed by post hoc tests with multiple-comparison corrections selected according to the dataset characteristics. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In this study, S0 was defined as the onset of the edible window and served as a common physiological reference across treatments. Ripening rates differed among treatments, with ethylene- and ethanol-treated fruit reaching S0 on day 2, and alternating temperature and control fruit reaching S0 on days 8 and 5, respectively. These days were therefore used as corresponding relative time points. Absolute time was applied for treatment-period analyses, whereas relative time was used for shelf-life comparisons. Tukey's honestly significantly different test was used for pairwise comparisons of physiological parameters while controlling the family-wise error rate across limited groups. For biochemical and RT-qPCR data, Bonferroni correction was applied for stricter control of Type I error in targeted assays. For RNA-seq data involving large-scale gene testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method was used to balance statistical power and false-positive control, consistent with standard bioinformatics practice.

Results

SSC, firmness of the outer pericarp, inner pericarp and core, decay rate during shelf life, and Vc content of kiwifruit under the three treatments

The headspace gas composition data for the treatment period are presented in Fig. S1. No anaerobic respiration occurred throughout the treatment period. Kiwifruit SSC increased progressively during treatment (Fig. 1A). In the ethanol and ethylene groups, SSC reached 13.38% and 13.83% at 2d, respectively, approaching the edible window. In contrast, the control group achieved an SSC of 14.67% at 5d, whereas the alternating temperature group reached a comparable level at 8d, which was 6, 6, and 3 days later than those of the ethanol, ethylene, and control group, respectively. During shelf life, SSC remained stable at approximately 16% across all groups.

Differences in soluble solids content (SSC) (A), outer pericarp firmness (B), inner pericarp firmness (C), core firmness (D), decay rate (E) and vitamin C (Vc) content (F) in kiwifruit under different treatments. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Significant differences are indicated by different letters (p < 0.05).

Conversely, fruit firmness exhibited the opposite trend (Fig. 1B–D). At 2d, the ethylene group showed the most rapid decline in firmness, with that of the outer pericarp, inner pericarp, and core decreasing to 46.61%, 17.79%, and 13.28% of the control values, respectively. The ethanol group followed, with corresponding firmness values of 62.83%, 52.29%, and 15.79% of the control. At 5d, the alternating temperature group exhibited significantly higher firmness of the outer pericarp (13.93 N), inner pericarp (8.32 N), and core (33.48 N) than the control group (p < 0.05). During shelf life, the firmness of the outer pericarp, inner pericarp, and core firmness remained consistently lower in the control, ethylene, and ethanol groups than in the alternating temperature group, with core firmness remaining relatively high under alternating temperature treatment. At S3d, core firmness in the alternating temperature group was 4.54-, 4.76-, and 5.64-fold higher than that in the control, ethanol, and ethylene groups, respectively. At S1d, inner pericarp firmness accounted for 29.97%, 17.13%, 12.82%, and 21.17% of the core firmness in the control, alternating temperature, ethanol, and ethylene groups, respectively.

No decay was observed in any group during the treatment period (Fig. 1E); however, significant differences in shelf life were detected thereafter (p < 0.05). The ethylene group exhibited the fastest increase in decay rate, reaching 20.00% at S5d, which was significantly higher than that of the control (11.67%), alternating temperature (3.33%), and ethanol groups (11.67%; p < 0.05). The control and ethanol groups reached a decay rate of 20.00% at S7d, whereas the alternating temperature group maintained a relatively low decay rate throughout shelf life, remaining significantly lower than those of the other groups (p < 0.05) and below 20% even at S13d (Fig. 1E). Vc content peaked at 1d in the ethanol (131.59 mg·100 g−1) and ethylene (126.67 mg·100 g−1) groups before gradually declining (Fig. 1F). In contrast, Vc content peaked at 2d in the control and alternating temperature groups (141.16 and 143.48 mg·100 g−1, respectively). However no significant differences in Vc content were observed among treatments throughout shelf life (p > 0.05).

Kiwifruit respiration rate, ethylene production rate, ATP, ADP, and AMP contents, and EC under the three treatments

The respiration and ethylene production rates in the control and alternating temperature groups initially increased and subsequently decreased (Fig. 2). The ethanol and ethylene groups reached peak respiration rates of 12.47 and 13.51 mg CO2·kg−1·h−1 at 2d, respectively, which were 1.24- and 1.34-fold higher than the peak respiration rate of the control group. In contrast, the alternating temperature group reached a peak respiration rate of 8.74 mg CO2·kg−1·h−1 at 6d. Ethylene production rates followed a similar pattern. At 2d, the ethanol and ethylene groups reached peak ethylene production rates of 320.72 and 339.98 μL·kg−1·h−1, respectively, which were 1.12- and 1.18-fold higher than the peak value observed in the control group. During shelf life, respiration and ethylene production rates in the control and alternating temperature groups initially declined and subsequently stabilized. In contrast, the ethanol and ethylene groups exhibited a transient increase before declining, possibly due to the delayed inhibitory effect of 1-MCP on respiration and ethylene production.

Differences in respiration rate (A); ethylene production rate (B); adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (C), adenosine diphosphate (ADP) (D), and adenosine monophosphate (AMP) (E) contents; and energy charge (EC) (F) in kiwifruit under different treatments. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Significant differences are indicated by different letters (p < 0.05).

Energy metabolism-related indicators are shown in Figs. 2C and D. Consistent with the trends observed for respiration and ethylene production, ATP and ADP concentrations in the ethanol and ethylene groups increased markedly during treatment, with no significant differences between the two groups (p > 0.05). Notably, ATP and ADP concentrations in the ethanol group were significantly higher than those in the control and alternating temperature groups during treatment (p < 0.05), reaching 1.58- and 1.63-fold the corresponding levels in the control group at 2d, respectively. In addition, EC in the ethanol group was higher than that in the control and alternating temperature groups (Fig. 2F), with the ethylene group exhibiting a similar trend. Conversely, ATP and ADP concentrations in the control and alternating temperature groups increased gradually, with the control group showing significantly higher ATP and ADP concentrations than the alternating temperature group (p < 0.05). Throughout shelf life, EC, ATP, and ADP concentrations gradually declined in all groups, whereas the AMP concentration initially increased and subsequently decreased (Figs. 2C–F). The ethanol and ethylene groups consistently exhibited higher EC values and ATP, ADP, and AMP concentrations than the control and alternating temperature groups.

Starch content and total amylase, AMY, and BAM activities of kiwifruit under the three treatments

Fig. 3A illustrates the changes in starch content among the treatment groups. Starch content declined throughout the treatment period. Specifically, starch content in the ethanol and ethylene groups decreased from 68.17 mg·g−1 at 0d to 30.78 mg·g−1 and 23.37 mg·g−1 at 2d, respectively, which were significantly lower than those in the control (41.32 mg·g−1) and alternating temperature (54.93 mg·g−1) groups (p < 0.05). At 5d, starch content in the control group decreased to 29.12 mg·g−1, which was significantly lower than that in the alternating temperature group (39.49 mg·g−1; p < 0.05). Throughout shelf life, starch content continued to decline in all groups. However, the alternating temperature group maintained the highest starch content (22.71 mg·g−1) at S1d, which was 82.70%, 274.75%, and 694.06% higher than that in the control, ethanol, and ethylene groups, respectively. These findings suggest that alternating temperature treatment delayed starch degradation in kiwifruit.

Differences in starch content (A) and activities of total amylase (B), α-amylase (AMY) (C), and β-amylase (BAM) (D) in kiwifruit under different treatments. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Significant differences are indicated by different letters (p < 0.05).

As shown in Fig. 3B–D, amylase activity initially increased and subsequently decreased. During treatment, BAM activity in the ethanol and ethylene groups peaked at 6.85 and 8.21 U·g−1, respectively, at 1d, occurring 3 and 4 days earlier than the peaks in the control and alternating temperature groups. Furthermore, BAM activity remained low only in the alternating temperature group, consistent with its higher starch content. In contrast, AMY activity remained relatively low across all groups, whereas total amylase activity exhibited a pattern similar to that of BAM activity, suggesting that BAM played a more important role during ripening. During shelf life, differences in amylase activities among treatment groups were minimal, and the differences between AMY and BAM activities were even less pronounced, suggesting that both enzymes contributed comparably to starch degradation during this period.

Sensory, electronic nose, and electronic tongue analyses

In order to obtain supportive qualitative evidence, a samll panel size sensory evaluation was performed. The result revealed that the control group at S1d (controlS1d) exhibited the highest flavor score, with the ethylene group showing a flavor profile similar to that of the control group. In contrast, fruit in the ethanol group emitted a pronounced alcoholic odor throughout shelf life (Fig. S2, Table S4). The control and ethylene groups developed an alcoholic aroma only during the late stage of storage, whereas the alternating temperature group maintained a normal odor profile throughout shelf life and retained acceptable sensory quality until S13d. In addition, fruit in the alternating temperature group exhibited a distinct starchy flavor during early shelf life, consistent with their higher starch content.

The controlS1d sample, which achieved the highest sensory score, was selected as the flavor reference for E-nose and E-tongue analyses (Fig. 4). For the E-nose dataset, the first two principal components explained 91.0% of the total variance (PC1: 83.7%; PC2: 7.3%), effectively capturing most odor-related information. Notably, samples from the alternating temperature group clustered closely with the controlS1d sample throughout shelf life, whereas the ethanol group exhibited a distinct and clearly separated profile. Similarly, PCA of the E-tongue dataset explained 83.3% of the total taste variance (Fig. 4C). The controlS1d sample, alternating temperature-treated samples (at S5d and S7d), and other core samples formed a dense cluster that was clearly separated from both the ethanol and ethylene-treated samples at S5d (ethyleneS5d). These findings indicate that alternating temperature treatment preserved taste and odor profiles similar to those of the optimal control sample, whereas ethanol fumigation caused pronounced and persistent flavor alterations.

Transcriptomic analysis

Transcriptome profiling and DEG identification

Table S5 summarizes clean reads and data quality, demonstrating the high quality and reliability of the RNA-seq data. PCA showed tight clustering of biological replicates within each treatment group (Fig. S3A), indicating high experimental consistency. DEGs were identified using the criteria |log2FC| >1 and a p-adjust <0.05. At 2d, 790, 1445, and 2427 genes were upregulated in the alternating temperature, ethanol, and ethylene groups, respectively, whereas 485, 2224, and 3201 genes were downregulated, compared with the control group (Fig. S3B). At S0d, the corresponding treatments showed 2034, 949, and 1476 upregulated genes and 1115, 2059, and 2332 downregulated genes (Fig. S3C). At S3d, the numbers of DEGs increased to 6285, 2796, and 2765, comprising 3378, 1218, and 1894 upregulated genes and 2907, 1578, and 871 downregulated genes, respectively (Fig. S3D). Overall, at 2d, S0d and S3d, the largest number of DEGs were observed between the ethylene and alternating temperature groups, whereas the ethylene and ethanol groups yielded comparatively fewer DEGs.

Functions and metabolic pathways associated with DEGs

KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were performed to identify biological pathways and processes significantly affected by the aforementioned treatments. These analyses were based on DEGs from kiwifruit subjected to 2d of alternating temperature, ethanol, and ethylene treatments, as well as at S0d and S3d. GO and KEGG enrichment profiles differed markedly among samples at 2d, S0d, and S3d (Fig. S4). Among the GO terms, carbohydrate metabolic process (GO: 0005975) was enriched in nearly all groups. For KEGG pathways, starch and sucrose metabolism was enriched in all groups at 2d and S0d, indicating treatment-dependent differences in carbohydrate metabolism during ripening. However, this pathway was not enriched in the ethanol vs. control or ethylene vs. control comparisons at S3d. In addition, the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway was enriched in the comparisons of alternating temperature2d vs. control2d, alternating temperatureS3d vs. controlS3d, ethyleneS3d vs. controlS3d, ethanolS3d vs. alternating temperatureS3d, and ethyleneS3d vs. alternating temperatureS3d. Collectively, these findings suggest that postharvest senescence is closely associated with changes in energy metabolism.

WGCNA of DEGs related to ripening and senescence in kiwifruit

To enhance understanding of the regulatory effects of the three treatments on postharvest kiwifruit ripening and senescence, WGCNA was performed to analyze the gene co-expression network of DEGs. A total of 11 co-expression modules were identified (Fig. 5A). Genes within the same module exhibited similar expression patterns, suggesting potential functional relationships. Notably, MEbrown, MEturquoise, MEgreen, MEblack, MEblue, and MEred were strongly correlated with postharvest ripening attributes, including SSC, inner pericarp firmness, ethylene production rate, and starch content, with absolute correlation coefficients exceeding 0.7. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis of the 11,448 DEGs assigned to these modules was conducted to elucidate their biological significance. The analysis revealed that many genes were enriched in pathways related to starch and sucrose metabolism and plant hormone signal transduction (Fig. 5C).

Genes associated with ethylene biosynthesis and signal transduction among DEGs

After excluding genes with a maximum TPM value below 5, 27 genes associated with ethylene biosynthesis and signaling were identified, including 12 involved in ethylene biosynthesis and 15 involved in ethylene signal transduction (Fig. 6A). Genes associated with starch degradation in DEGs

After excluding genes with a maximum TPM value below 5, 20 starch degradation-related genes were identified, including three isoamylase, one phosphoglucan water dikinase, two α-glucan phosphorylase H isozyme (PHS), one α-glucosidase (AGL), two β-glucosidase (BGL), two AMY, eight BAM, and one hexokinase gene(s) (Fig. 6B). In the alternating temperature group, Acc16485 and Acc05785 (PHS), Acc09335 (BGL), and Acc23514 (AMY) were upregulated. In contrast, in the ethanol and ethylene groups, BAM genes Acc15874, Acc16695, Acc28966, and Acc33143 were upregulated, whereas PHS, AGL, BGL, and AMY genes were downregulated. Notably, AcBAM3.1/Acc15874, AcBAM3.5/Acc28966, and AcBAM9/Acc33143 exhibited differential expression at 2d and were selected for further analysis.

Genes associated with energy metabolism among DEGs

We identified 23 DEGs significantly associated with energy metabolism (Fig. 6C). Among the metabolic pathways, glycolytic pathway (EMP) contained the largest number of DEGs (14 genes). Among these, Acc21566 (phosphoglycerate kinase, PGK) and Acc01807 (pyruvate decarboxylase, PDC) showed the most pronounced expression changes during fruit storage and within the edible window. Compared with the control, these genes were significantly upregulated at S0d in the alternating temperature group (p-adjust <0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed in the ethanol or ethylene groups (p-adjust >0.05). Notably, Acc10529, encoding the rate-limiting glycolytic enzyme ATP-dependent 6-phosphofructokinase (PFK), showed contrasting expression patterns among treatments; it was significantly downregulated in the alternating temperature group (p-adjust <0.05), yet significantly upregulated in the ethanol and ethylene groups (p-adjust <0.05). In contrast, the PGK gene Acc21566 exhibited the opposite expression pattern. Key enzymes in the trichloroacetic acid (TCA) cycle and pentose phosphate pathways (PPP), including pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), showed no significant differences in expression among treatments (p-adjust >0.05).

Identification of key transcription factors

After filtering out genes with a maximum TPM below 1, 11,448 genes were partitioned into 11 co-expression modules using WGCNA to identify candidate transcription factors (TFs) regulating starch and ethylene metabolism (Fig. 7). In the BAM regulatory network, the MEblue and MEturquoise modules showed the strongest correlation with the three AcBAM hub genes—AcBAM3.1, AcBAM3.5, and AcBAM9. Network analysis identified TFs co-expressed with AcBAM3.1 (Acc15874), AcBAM3.5 (Acc28966), and AcBAM9 (Acc33143), suggesting potential upstream regulatory roles. Notably, nine TFs were positively correlated with all three AcBAMs. Among them, Acc19274 (WRKY), Acc20613 (B3), Acc25963 (MYB), and Acc30983 (MYB) were identified as core candidate regulators, exhibiting exceptionally high correlations (|r| > 0.95). In the ACO regulatory network, the MEbrown and MEred modules were strongly associated with the two AcACO hub genes—AcACO1 and AcACO2. Seven TFs showed strong correlations (|r| > 0.8) with AcACO expression, including three associated with AcACO2 and six with AcACO1 (Fig. 7E). Notably, all seven TFs were positively correlated with AcACO expression, with Acc21264 (B3) and Acc13371 (E2F/DP) showing the strongest associations (|r| > 0.85).

Discussion

Effects of different treatments on physicochemical parameters

SSC, firmness, starch content, respiration rate, and ethylene production rate are key indicators of kiwifruit ripening. Postharvest ripening is characterized by increased SSC and decreased fruit firmness, and artificial ripening treatments can accelerate these changes. Respiration and ethylene production are important physiological indicators of ripening and senescence. Fruit respiration is closely associated with energy metabolism, supplying energy for normal physiological processes, and increased respiration accelerates substrate consumption, thereby promoting fruit ripening and senescence (Chai et al., 2022).

In this study, during the initial treatment period, ethanol and ethylene significantly increased respiration rate, ethylene production, and ATP/ADP levels, compared with the control (p < 0.05). This metabolic activation promoted rapid, synchronous ripening, evidenced by the accelerated decline in firmness of the outer pericarp, inner pericarp, and core, along with SSC accumulation (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). In contrast, alternating temperature treatment markedly delayed ripening, with fruit requiring 8 days to reach the edible window. This delay was likely due to reduced respiration and ethylene biosynthesis during the low-temperature phase, together with elevated EC levels, which prolonged the treatment period.

During shelf life, clear differences in decay and quality retention emerged. Ethylene-treated fruit exhibited the highest decay rate and shortest shelf life (Fig. 1). This accelerated senescence was associated with a sharp decline in ATP and EC, consistent with previous reports (Lim & Lee, 2017; Yan et al., 2022). Ethanol treatment led to the second-highest decay rate, whereas alternating temperature significantly reduced decay rates during shelf life compared with all other treatments (p < 0.05; Fig. 1E), consistent with Ting-Ting et al. (2023). This improved quality retention was associated with stable EC and sustained ATP homeostasis, both of which can delay senescence (Aghdam et al., 2018). Meanwhile, Vc content showed a general “tip-and-decline” trend across all treatments, suggesting that the treatments had limited effects on intrinsic nutritional quality (Lim & Lee, 2017).

Regarding eating quality, the”Guichang” kiwifruit is often characterized by a starchy taste, alcoholic off-flavors, and a firm core arising from asynchronous softening, which is typically sigmoidal in the pericarp and linear in the core (Burdon et al., 2017; Chai et al., 2023). Although this asynchronous softening was less apparent in early stages due to the compressed ripening period, alternating temperature-treated fruit retained a pronounced firm core and starchy palate until S5d, consistent with higher residual starch content (Fig. 3, Fig. 5A). PCA of E-nose and E-tongue data supported these findings. Flavor profiles of alternating temperature-treated fruit at S5d clustered with those of ethylene-treated and control fruit at S1d, indicating delayed yet convergent flavor development. In contrast, exogenous ethanol fumigation produced a distinct volatile profile throughout shelf life, consistent with sensory results and suggesting that residual ethanol disrupted aroma balance.

Regulatory networks of ethylene biosynthesis, starch metabolism, and energy status

The ethylene biosynthetic pathway is primarily regulated by ACS and ACO (Tomovicova et al., 2024). In kiwifruit, AcACS1 and AcACO1/2 govern autocatalytic system-II ethylene production (Xu et al., 2024). In this study, AcACS transcripts showed minimal variation; therefore, further analysis focused on AcACO genes. Transcriptomic data showed that AcACO1/2 expression in the ethanol and ethylene groups increased sharply at 2d, coinciding with ethylene production peaks (Figs. 2B and 7B). In contrast, alternating temperature treatment suppressed this induction, consistent with lower ethylene production. Notably, after 1-MCP treatment, AcACO1/2 transcripts remained elevated despite inhibited ethylene production, suggesting post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation, including altered mRNA stability or translation efficiency. In addition, WGCNA identified seven novel TFs co-expressed with AcACO1/2, providing candidates for regulatory pathways beyond the known AcWRKY40- and AdNAC2/3-mediated networks (Gan et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2020).

This ethylene-mediated regulatory cascade directly influenced carbohydrate mobilization. Postharvest starch degradation is central to tissue softening and soluble solids accumulation, and residual starch contributes to an undesirable starchy texture (Yang, Jiang, & Ren, 2024). In this study, ethanol and ethylene treatments accelerated early starch degradation, whereas alternating temperature treatment delayed this process, resulting in higher starch retention through S3d (Fig. 3, Fig. 5A). This delayed degradation is consistent with reports that low temperatures can decouple softening from starch breakdown (Yang, Jiang, & Ren, 2024). The strong association between starch degradation and firmness loss across treatments further indicates that starch breakdown is closely linked to fruit softening (Su et al., 2024). At the transcript level, AcBAM3.1, AcBAM3.5, and AcBAM9 were identified as key BAM genes and were significantly upregulated by ethanol and ethylene treatments (p < 0.05; Fig. 3, Fig. 6), consistent with Su et al. (2024). In contrast, alternating temperature suppressed AcBAM expression, delaying SSC accumulation. Notably, plastidial phosphorylase genes, Acc31818 (PHS), Acc16485 (AcPHS2), and Acc05785 (AcPHS1), were upregulated under alternating temperature, resembling compensatory responses reported under chitosan treatment, where BAM suppression coincided with PHS induction (Yang, Zhang, et al., 2024). WGCNA of AcBAMs hub genes identified 30 co-expressed TFs, including AcKAN2 and MYB44 (Boldingh et al., 2020; Su et al., 2024), comprising nine positively and 21 negatively correlated TFs, suggesting a coordinated dual regulatory mechanism fine-tuning postharvest starch catabolism.

Fruit ripening and senescence are closely associated with cellular energy status (Aghdam, 2021), whereas hyperactivated respiration can overstimulate ATP, ADP, and AMP pools, prematurely triggering senescence (Chai et al., 2022). Consistent with these findings, ethanol and ethylene treatments increased respiration rates and adenine nucleotide pools (Figs. 2A and C–E). To explore the metabolic basis, key enzymes in the EMP pathway, TCA cycle, and PPP were examined. Transcript levels of G6PD (PPP) and PDH (TCA cycle) did not differ significantly among treatments (p > 0.05), indicating that carbon flux through the PPP and TCA cycle was largely unaffected. Instead, respiratory regulation appeared to occur mainly through the EMP pathway and was associated with differential expression of ATP-dependent PFK and pyrophosphate-dependent PFP, which regulate Fru-6-P/Fru-1,6-P2 interconversion (Chen et al., 2024). Although activation of the EMP pathway, TCA cycle, and PPP accelerates senescence and quality deterioration in pears (Sun et al., 2023), it delays senescence in broccoli (Li et al., 2017). In the present study, transcriptional regulation within the EMP pathway, particularly through shifts in PFK/PFP activity, likely contributed to treatment-dependent differences in energy balance and shelf-life homeostasis.

Unlike most transcriptomic studies that compare treatments at identical chronological time points, this study examined molecular regulation at both the same chronological time and comparable edible stages (SSC: 13.4–14.7%). At 2d, genes associated with starch degradation (AcBAM3.1/3.5/9), ethylene biosynthesis (AcACO1/2), and energy metabolism (Acc10529/PFK1) were significantly upregulated in the ethanol and ethylene groups. At the onset of the edible window (S0d), these ripening-related genes remained highly expressed. These results indicate that fruit under different treatments exhibit distinct transcriptional profiles despite reaching a similar edible state (SSC: 13.4–14.7%). Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis at S3d (physiological time) revealed persistent and significant differences in gene expression among the four treatment groups, suggesting distinct molecular programs governing postharvest quality evolution.

Conclusion

This study profiled the physiological and transcriptomic responses of ready-to-eat kiwifruit to alternating temperature, ethanol, and ethylene treatments. Both ethanol and ethylene treatments accelerated ripening, as evidenced by rapid starch degradation and increased expression of genes involved in ethylene biosynthesis, starch metabolism, and energy metabolism. In contrast, the alternating temperature treatment suppressed early ripening-related physiological changes while allowing fruit to reach the edible stage. During shelf life, the ethanol and ethylene groups exhibited rapid cellular deterioration, whereas the extended edible window under alternating temperature was associated with higher adenylate EC. Rather than establishing causality, our matrices provide a predictive, physiologically-aligned co-expression framework. Distinct phenotypic trajectories were associated with transcriptional variation in AcBAM3.1/3.5/9, AcACO1/2, and the glycolysis-related gene Acc10529/PFK1. Overall, this study provides a preliminary framework for understanding treatment-specific physiological and molecular responses of ready-to-eat kiwifruit, although the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain to be elucidated.
