Psoriasis patients with psoriatic arthritis demonstrate a reduced regulatory capacity for neutrophil elastase
Authors: Tom Macleod, Sayam R Dubash, Isabel Hyde, Xabier Michelena, Anna Berekmeri, Philip S Helliwell, Sarah R Kingsbury, Philip Laws, Kulveer Mankia, Martin Stacey, Helena Marzo-Ortega, Miriam Wittmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIdentifying which patients with psoriasis (PsO) may develop PsA is a research priority. We have previously shown that elafin, along with IL-36γ, are excellent cutaneous biomarkers for PsO. The protease inhibitor elafin and its target protease, neutrophil elastase (NE), can be detected in both serum and epidermal samples. Our aims were to assess whether known PsO biomarkers are expressed differentially in patients affected by plaque PsO with and without PsA.MethodsProtein expression of NE and elafin were analysed in matched epidermal samples and serum of PsO, PsA patients and healthy controls. Findings were validated in independent PsA and arthritis cohorts.ResultsThe ratio between NE and elafin differed significantly between PsO and PsA patients. Expression of elafin was reduced in PsA samples as compared with plaque PsO patients. The ratio of NE to secretory leucocyte protease inhibitor was also increased in PsA as compared with PsO patients with no PsA, healthy controls and other arthritis patients.ConclusionsPsA patients show a reduced NE inhibitor expression in both the skin and blood compartment compared with those with plaque PsO only. This may suggest differences in the ability to regulated neutrophil activity in PsA patients.

No known measurable biomarkers can identify which psoriasis patients will go on to develop PsA before the development of clinical symptoms.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is an inflammatory arthritis that occurs in up to 30% of patients with psoriasis (PsO) and can be associated with significant impairment in function and quality of life [1]. Time is an important factor associated with disease progression in PsA, highlighting the need for a prompt diagnosis [2]; yet this can be challenging in the early stages, leading to misdiagnosis or patients remaining undiagnosed. To date, no diagnostic criteria exist for PsA, with the diagnosis relying on a combination of clinical signs, symptoms and family history, aided by the ClASsification for Psoriatic ARthritis (CASPAR) [3]. However, skin PsO can be found in the absolute majority of PsA patients preceding the development of musculoskeletal symptoms by up to two decades, making PsO the best biomarker to explore the population at risk of developing PsA. Previous cross-sectional or retrospective studies have shown that a psoriasis clinical phenotype involving the nails, scalp, natal cleft and belly button may have a higher risk of developing PsA [4], but these are not reliable predictors. Lately, a number of screening questionnaires have been developed indicating the desire and clinical need to have some predictive information on who may be at higher risk of developing musculoskeletal involvement [5, 6].

We and others have previously shown that both elafin and IL-36γ are excellent markers for cutaneous psoriatic responses [7–9]. We detected these mediators in epidermal samples by proteomics analysis [10]. IL-36γ is closely linked to neutrophilic recruitment and activation as demonstrated by the successful action of IL-36 receptor inhibition in generalized pustular psoriasis [11]. Elafin is encoded by the gene PI3 and inhibits neutrophil elastase (NE). Elafin is secreted by epithelial cells and is known to play a role in lung pathologies [12, 13]. Importantly, elafin, NE and IL-36γ can reliably be detected by non-invasive epidermal sampling as well as in serum, making them feasible biomarkers in the clinic.

In this study, we aimed to determine whether the above-mentioned, known PsO skin biomarkers along with NE are expressed differentially between those affected by plaque psoriasis only (PsO) and those with associated PsA.

Patients and methods

Study population

This was a translational study performed in the Combined Psoriatic Service at Leeds Teaching Hospitals NHS Trust, UK, a tertiary interdisciplinary clinical set up comprising of specialized dermatology and rheumatology teams [14]. Eligible patients (Supplementary Table S1) included consecutive adults over 18 years, with a clinical diagnosis of plaque PsO (n = 29) or PsA (meeting the CASPAR classification criteria) (n = 24). In addition, healthy volunteers (healthy controls, HC) (n = 25) over the age of 18 years, with no known personal history of any skin or rheumatic diseases were recruited as controls. Exclusion criteria were any current or previous diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, primary or secondary Raynaud’s, acrocyanosis, SSc, lupus erythematosus, PM, DM and vasculitis. Data were collected on demographics, nail (modified Nail Psoriasis Severity Index, mNAPSI) and skin assessments (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index, PASI). PsO patients also completed Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool (PEST) [5] and Early ARthritis for Psoriatic patients (EARP) [6] questionnaires.

Additionally, 262 existing serum samples from patients with dermatologist-confirmed diagnosis of PsO (n = 14, ethics reference 21/SC/0089), rheumatologist-confirmed diagnosis of DMARD-naïve peripheral PsA (n = 106 from the Leeds SpARRO (Leeds Spondyloarthropathy Register for Research and Observation) [15], TICOPA (Tight COntrol of Psoriatic arthritis) [16] and DIAGNOSE (DIAGnosis by NOn-invasive approaches in Skin Epidermal diseases) cohorts, ethics reference numbers RR03/2109; 07/H0903/72 and 21/SC/0089 respectively); OA (n = 59, ethics reference 14/WM/1163); RA (n = 45, ethics reference 09/H1307/98); and HC (n = 38, ethics references 09/H1307/98 and 21/SC/0089) were utilized for a replication analysis.

Epidermal sampling

D-squame adhesive discs of 3.8 cm2 (Cuderm Corporation, Dallas, TX, USA) were used to sample the skin as previously described [17]. Ten tapes were collected from the same location and stored at –80°C. Body location sampled was the elbow area/upper extremities. Non-lesional samples were collected from the volar forearm of participants. Nail samples were taken so the tape covered the nailfold area and adjacent lateral skin areas.

Sample processing and protein extraction

Frozen tapes were submerged in 1.5 ml lysis buffer [20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM EDTA, 1X protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Welwyn Garden City, UK)] for protein measurement. Tubes were left on ice for 30 min prior to sonication (three cycles of 20 s sonication, 20 s on ice), then centrifuged at 15 000 g for 10 min, followed by supernatant collection. Total protein content of tape extracts was measured using the BCA microplate procedure assay kit (Life Technologies Ltd, Paisley, UK).

Protein measurement

Specific cytokines and proteins were measured by sandwich ELISA using commercial DuoSet kits (RnD Systems, Abingdon, United Kingdom) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. IL-36γ was measured using an ELISA developed in-house as previously described [7]. For epidermal samples, concentrations were normalized to each sample’s total protein content as ng/µg.

Statistical analysis

Protein quantification data were analysed with GraphPad Prism software, version 9.4.1. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to determine statistically significant differences between groups. The Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA test was used to correct for variance where standard deviations differed between comparison groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used when assessing correlation between PASI and elafin concentrations. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to analyse and visualize the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of different protein markers.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the NRES Committee North East-York (REC 16/YH/0086). The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consent was given by all study subjects.

Results

Epidermal sampling

Elafin is significantly lower in lesional skin of PsA patients compared with patients with PsO only

The expression of elafin and IL-36γ in lesional skin of patients with either PsO alone or PsO with PsA through tape-stripping was first analysed in order to understand how expression levels compare across these conditions. Interestingly, elafin was significantly lower in PsA lesional skin compared with PsO, with the latter showing on average a 6-fold higher expression as measured by ELISA after normalization to total protein content of the sample (PsA 5.1 ng/µg vs PsO 32.9 ng/µg; mean) (Fig. 1A), while the expression of IL-36γ was not significantly different between PsO and PsA lesional skin (Fig. 1B).

As nail involvement has been associated with PsA, elafin and IL-36γ expression from skin surrounding the nail was measured. As shown in Fig. 2, elafin (Fig. 2A) and IL-36γ (Fig. 2B) are detectable in nailfolds of both involved and uninvolved nails (involved = NAPSI >0). While IL-36γ does not show any difference in expression across condition or nail involvement, elafin is significantly increased in PsO-only nailfolds compared with PsO with PsA regardless of nail disease status. PsA nailbeds also showed a trend for elevated elafin compared with healthy donor nailbeds, but this was not statistically significant. PsO but not PsA non-lesional skin exhibits high levels of elafin that do not correlate with PASI score

We next proceeded to analyse non-lesional skin in more detail. These samples were taken from the ventral forearm in all participants. A statistically significant difference was seen in the expression of elafin but not IL-36γ in non-lesional skin between PsA and PsO, with PsO non-lesional skin measuring 0.3 ng/µg elafin vs 0.044 ng/µg (mean, P = 0.0002) in PsA and 0.015 (mean, P < 0.0001) in HC skin (Fig. 3A and B). As PsA patients may have less severe skin lesions than PsO-only patients, we correlated elafin expression to PASI score to assess whether high levels of elafin are a reflection of more severe skin disease. As shown in Fig. 3C, elafin levels did not correlate with PASI score in PsO patients (r = –0.27, R2 = 0.01); however, in PsA patients there was a moderate increase in elafin at the high end of PASI scores which equated to a slight correlation (r = 0.66, R2 = 0.48). A subset analysis of low-severity PASI score patients (PASI <2.5) clearly shows high elafin levels in non-lesional PsO skin, in strong contrast to PsA, despite the equivalent severity of skin disease (Fig. 3D). Indeed, ROC curve analysis of elafin expression in PsO-only and PsA non-lesional skin shows that measurement of elafin is capable of separating PsO and PsA, with an area under the curve of 0.925 and a Youden index–defined optimal cut-off of 0.051 ng/µg providing 87.5% sensitivity and 87.5% specificity (Fig. 3E).

Serum analysis

PsA serum shows measureable signs of increased neutrophil activity compared with psoriasis

Given that elafin is consistently higher in PsO than PsA skin across both lesional and non-lesional skin, we proceeded to measure elafin concentration in patients’ serum taken at the same time as tapes. Interestingly, the epidermal findings follow the same pattern in serum samples. Figure 4A shows elafin is measurably higher in PsO vs PsA patients. Elafin is an inhibitor of NE, an important mediator of neutrophil-derived inflammation and tissue damage, so we also measured the abundance of serum NE and derived an NE:elafin ratio as a surrogate indicator of neutrophil activity. As shown in Fig. 4B, NE levels are similar between PsO and PsA patients, however the low elafin levels result in a significant difference in NE:elafin ratio between PsA and PsO patients (Fig. 4C). In order to explore these results further, we compared NE expression and NE:elafin ratio between newly diagnosed, DMARD-naïve and established PsA, DMARD-experienced patients (>5 years disease), however neither showed significant differences between these subsets (Fig. 4D–F). Our cohort did not include any PsA patient without symptoms of plaque PsO.

Finally we measured the serum concentration of secretory leucocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI; Supplementary Fig. S1), another protease inhibitor that is important in regulating NE activity, and determined both the NE:elafin ratio (Fig. 5A) and NE:SLPI ratio (Fig. 5B) on existing samples from two additional Leeds cohorts with early PsA (disease duration <12 months) who were biologic DMARD naïve: SpARRO [15] and TICOPA [16]. Discussion

This study sought to address a priority research question in the area of the psoriatic disease continuum: the need to identify biomarkers associated with PsA [18]. A recent meta-analysis concluded that no such biomarker exists at present [19]. While we have not been able to measure predictive value of our here described biomarker nor have we been able to follow a PsO-to-PsA transition with patient samples, our research feeds into such research and forms the basis for further analysis regarding predictive markers.

Based on our previous data on PsO epidermal samples [7, 20], we have chosen the most strongly disease-associated epidermal molecules elafin and IL-36γ but not inflammatory markers without clear ‘disease specificity’; this also applies to CXCL10 which has previously been described to decrease in the transition period from PsO to PsA [21]. Along with the above-mentioned elafin and IL-36γ, we analysed the expression of NE and SLPI and in skin of patients with either PsO alone or PsA through tape-stripping and subsequent protein measurement in order to understand how expression levels compare across these conditions. Our data showed that whilst the expression of NE and IL-36γ was not significantly different between PsO alone and PsA lesional skin, elafin as well as SLPI were significantly lower in PsA lesional skin when compared with PsO. These findings suggest that plaque PsO patients with PsA may harbour a reduced capacity to neutralize NE compared with plaque PsO patients without arthritis.

Elafin and IL-36γ have shown great promise as indicators of psoriatic inflammation [10, 20, 22]. We therefore wished to test whether their diagnostic potential could be applied to PsA, with the intention of measuring elafin and IL-36γ concentration in nailfold-adjacent skin. The nail is a site closely linked with PsA development, and nail changes in psoriasis patients are a well-known risk factor for PsA [23, 24]. The nailbed contains ligament attachment sites and an enthesis, which is a key site of inflammation in the development of PsA joint disease [25]. Given the close anatomical location, it is an attractive notion that inflammatory spillover from enthesitis may be measurable in the adjacent skin prior to the development of clinical symptoms.

Our results, however, show that whilst IL-36γ and elafin are measurable in nailfolds of digits with both involved and uninvolved nails, IL-36γ shows no significant changes in expression. Elafin, however, shows significantly lower levels in PsA nailbeds than in PsO only, yet shows no significant difference between digits with involved or uninvolved nails. Interestingly, a recent publication examining nail clippings in patients with psoriasis and nail changes linked the presence of neutrophils in nail clippings with ultrasound alterations in the nailbed [26]. Neutrophil presence also correlated with NAPSI score, suggesting they may promote nail changes in psoriasis.

Intriguingly, the observation that elafin is reduced in PsA vs PsO carries through into lesional skin, non-lesional skin and serum where elafin is consistently found at lower levels in PsA patients than in psoriasis patients without joint involvement. This highly significant difference in elafin expression between PsA and PsO appears to be independent of PASI severity although overall the PsA cohort had lower PASI scores than the PsO cohort. In contrast to our findings, published data indicate that serum elafin expression correlates with PASI score [27, 28]. Whilst we were not able to correlate serum elafin to PASI scores, our study’s lack of correlation in the skin samples may be a reflection of the localized sampling of non-lesional skin achieved by tape-stripping. While measuring serum biomarkers samples the systemic level of inflammation, tape-stripping remains localized. In this case we sampled non-lesional skin, thus avoiding tissue that is actively involved in the inflammation. This may therefore show the baseline level of biomarkers which may not be as sensitive to fluctuations in disease severity as serum biomarkers.

Our study also demonstrated that the expression balance between NE and its specific inhibitors SLPI and elafin is skewed in patients with PsA as compared with patients with PsO without musculoskeletal involvement. The reduction in elafin and SLPI levels but maintenance of NE levels suggests a higher level of systemic neutrophil activity in PsA patients. However, within this analysis we were not able to measure protease activity itself. This is difficult to perform ex vivo and we aim to address the net activity of NE in future studies using a surrogate NE-specific substrate.

Our observations are interesting regarding the progression of PsO to PsA. They demonstrate significant differences exist in elafin levels between patients with psoriasis alone, and those with PsA (many of whom also have psoriasis). This therefore suggests that at some stage during this transition, PsO patients may experience a drop in elafin production in both lesional and non-lesional skin, and systemically may be subject to an increase in neutrophil activity with potential accompanying tissue damage. Whether that drop precedes the development of PsA, and whether it is mechanistically important or a consequence of PsA development, requires further longitudinal study. Indeed, an increase in neutrophil-derived protease activity may have mechanistic implications given the destructive role they play in the development of joint disease through proteolysis of collagen type IV [29, 30], elastin and extracellular matrix [31] along with PAR activation [32].

Whilst this study did not measure protein levels in SF due to poor availability of fluid within the cohort and difficulty experienced recruiting patients during the COVID-19 pandemic, published proteomic analyses of SF and functional studies of synovial neutrophils have shown increased expression of NE and increased neutrophil activity compared with OA and RA [33–36]. It would be interesting to examine the ratio of elafin and SLPI to NE in the SF of PsA patients to determine whether poor NE regulation extends to affected joints. Furthermore, whilst we cannot determine whether the reduced systemic regulation of NE in PsA patients is responsible for joint damage, it would be interesting to explore the effect of intervening with NE inhibitors such as sivelestat [37]. Indeed, combination of a NE inhibitor with a biologic targeting key pathology-driving cytokines may prove effective in controlling both tissue damage and disease progression.

Whilst this is only a cross-sectional analysis and cannot assess the kinetics of elafin expression or neutrophil activity over the transition from PsO to PsA, the significant differences observed in NE to protease inhibitor ratios warrants further longitudinal study to ascertain whether these factors can predict PsA development in PsO patients. If this were proven, given the ease of detection in patient serum, this measurement may provide a simple method of monitoring a PsO patient’s risk of developing PsA that can be integrated into routine clinical practice.

In conclusion, these data showed an imbalance between the protease NE and its inhibitors elafin and SLPI in PsA as compared with PsO patients who had no PsA. Larger, longitudinal studies are now needed to assess the potential role of the NE/elafin SLPI ratio as a predictive marker for the development of PsA in PsO and its utility in the identification of an ‘at-risk’ population.
