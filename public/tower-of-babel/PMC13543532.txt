Prevalence and associated factors of non-fistulous urinary incontinence among adult women in Bantama, Ghana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Andrew Panyin Vormawor, Rex Mawuli Kwadjo Djokoto, Anthony Amanfo Ofori, Samuel Yaw Adu, Kingsley Afreh Nduro, Timothy Adjei, Victor Boachie Owusu, Edward Anabila Agana, Johnny Arthur-Komeh, Opei Adarkwa, Isaac Kofi Adu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-fistulous urinary incontinence (UI) is a common but understudied condition in sub-Saharan Africa, despite its well-documented negative effects on women’s quality of life. In Ghana, population-based data on its prevalence, associated factors, and impact remain limited.ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of non-fistulous UI, identify associated factors, and assess its impact on quality of life among women aged 20–69 years in Bantama, Kumasi, Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional household survey was conducted among 374 women selected through multistage cluster sampling in the Bantama sub-metropolis. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire incorporating the King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ) to assess UI symptoms and quality-of-life domains. Descriptive statistics and bivariate analyses were performed.ResultsOf the 411 women approached, 374 participated (response rate: 91%). The mean age was 45.2 years. The prevalence of UI was 32.9%, indicating that approximately one in three women experienced urine leakage. Most affected women reported leakage occurring once a week or less; however, increasing frequency was significantly associated with greater interference with daily activities (χ² = 13.86, p = 0.0078). UI was significantly associated with older age, obesity (particularly Class I), higher parity, menopausal status, history of hysterectomy, and childbirth weight ≥ 2.6 kg. Educational level was not significantly associated with UI. Urge incontinence was associated with poorer quality of life, particularly in emotional (“Feeling Bad”) and social (“Personal Relationships”) domains, with increasing severity linked to greater emotional distress and social strain.ConclusionNon-fistulous UI is common among adult women in Bantama and is associated with identifiable reproductive and metabolic risk factors, as well as substantial emotional and social quality-of-life impairments. These findings support the need for routine screening and context-specific UI prevention and management strategies within existing women’s health services in Ghana, particularly for older, multiparous, and menopausal women.Keywords: Urinary incontinence, Prevalence, Quality of life, Ghana, Sub-Saharan Africa, Women's health

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a common condition among adult women worldwide, with reported prevalence varying widely depending on the study setting, population characteristics, and case definition [1]. Population-based studies report UI prevalence ranging from 5% to 70%, with most research indicating rates between 25 and 45% among adult women [2]. Known risk factors include increasing age, parity, obesity, chronic cough, and pelvic floor injury [2, 3]. The condition substantially impacts quality of life, imposing considerable personal and societal economic burdens estimated at over $65 billion annually worldwide [3]. However, most epidemiological studies distinguish between incontinence caused by fistula and other types of urinary leakage [3].

Non-fistulous urinary incontinence refers to involuntary leakage of urine not due to a urogenital fistula an abnormal connection between the urinary tract and the vagina [1]. In practice, non-fistulous UI encompasses stress, urge, and mixed incontinence and is typically associated with pelvic floor dysfunction, detrusor overactivity, or urethral sphincter weakness rather than a traumatic fistula [4]. Previous research from high-income countries has documented that lifestyle factors such as obesity and multiparity significantly increase the risk of non-fistulous UI [5, 6]. However, the condition is often underreported in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where cultural taboos, limited health-seeking behavior, and inadequate screening contribute to the scarcity of reliable data [4, 5].

Although non-fistulous urinary incontinence has been extensively studied in high-income settings, data from sub-Saharan Africa remain limited, with most available research focusing on obstetric fistula-related incontinence [4, 6]. This gap in knowledge has led to the under-recognition of non-fistulous forms of UI among women in low-resource settings, despite their significant impact on physical, psychological, and social well-being. Understanding the prevalence and associated factors of non-fistulous UI among adult women is therefore crucial for developing effective public health interventions and improving women’s quality of life in such contexts.

In Ghana, obstetric fistula has received substantial public health attention, whereas non-fistulous urinary incontinence (UI) remains relatively overlooked [7]. This imbalance has contributed to the perception that UI is uncommon among African women, limiting evidence-informed healthcare planning and resource allocation. Although UI has been studied in Ghana and other African settings, existing evidence is largely facility-based or focused on specific groups such as pregnant or postnatal women, with limited population-based data and insufficient assessment of quality-of-life outcomes. To address these gaps, this household-based study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated reproductive and metabolic factors of non-fistulous UI among adult women aged 20–69 years in Bantama, Ghana, and to assess its impact on quality of life using the validated King’s Health Questionnaire [8].

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional household survey was conducted in Bantama Sub-Metropolitan District, one of the administrative sub-metros within the Kumasi Metropolitan Assembly in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. Kumasi is the second-largest city in Ghana and serves as a major commercial and healthcare hub in the middle belt of the country. Bantama Sub-Metro is characterized by a heterogeneous urban population with varying socioeconomic and reproductive profiles, making it suitable for population-based women’s health research. According to the 2010 Population and Housing Census, Bantama has an estimated population of women aged 20–69 years of approximately 70,472 [9]. The cross-sectional design was chosen to estimate the prevalence of non-fistulous urinary incontinence (UI) and to examine associated factors at a single point in time.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for estimating proportions in cross-sectional studies. An expected UI prevalence of 42% was assumed based on findings from similar African population-based studies [4]. A precision of 5% and a 95% confidence level were applied, yielding a minimum sample size of 374 women. To account for potential non-response, 10% was added, resulting in a target sample size of 411 participants.

Sampling procedure

A multistage cluster sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, Bantama Sub-Metro was purposively selected due to its urban characteristics and population diversity. In the second stage, the sub-metro was stratified into five electoral areas, which served as the primary sampling clusters. Equal allocation was used for logistical simplicity. In the third stage, polling areas within each electoral area were randomly selected as sub-clusters. Households within selected polling areas were systematically sampled, with a maximum of two eligible women recruited per household to minimize intrahousehold clustering. Where more than one eligible woman was present, simple random selection was applied. A detailed flow diagram of participant recruitment is provided, and a geographic map illustrating cluster selection and study areas is recommended for inclusion to enhance transparency and reproducibility.

Participants

Eligible participants were women aged 20–69 years who resided in Bantama for at least six months. This age range was selected because it represents the most reproductively and socially active segment of the female population, consistent with Ghana Statistical Service adult age classifications and prior community-based studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [5, 6]. Women younger than 20 years were excluded to avoid confounding related to adolescent developmental continence issues, while women older than 69 years were excluded primarily due to higher rates of comorbidities that could confound associations with urinary incontinence, and to maintain feasibility of household data collection. Exclusion criteria included a confirmed diagnosis of obstetric fistula or other fistulous incontinence, severe physical or psychological illness limiting participation, and refusal to provide consent.

Data collection

Data were collected between October, 2020 and January, 2021 using a structured questionnaire incorporating the King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ), a validated instrument for assessing the impact of UI on quality of life [8]. The questionnaire consisted of three sections: (1) sociodemographic and reproductive characteristics, (2) assessment of UI prevalence and symptom frequency, and (3) quality-of-life evaluation across eight domains (general health perception, incontinence impact, role limitations, physical limitations, social limitations, personal relationships, emotions, and sleep/energy).

The questionnaire was pre-tested in a comparable urban community outside the study area to assess clarity, cultural appropriateness, and flow, and minor modifications were made accordingly. Data were collected by five trained research assistants, including two urogynaecology nurses and three public health graduates. All data collectors received a two-day training covering study objectives, ethical conduct, interview techniques, and standardized administration of the questionnaire.

For participants unable to read or write, the questionnaire was translated into Twi using a forward–back translation process by bilingual experts to ensure semantic equivalence and cultural appropriateness. Pre-testing was conducted in a similar urban community outside the study area to refine language and clarity. Interviews were conducted face-to-face using paper-based questionnaires. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²) and categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) classifications: normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), Class I obesity (30.0–34.9 kg/m²), Class II obesity (35.0–39.9 kg/m²), and Class III obesity (≥ 40.0 kg/m²) [10].

Associations between UI and explanatory variables were initially assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was applied where expected cell counts were less than five. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology Committee on Human Research, Publication and Ethics (CHRPE/AP/339/20). Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and privacy were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Prevalence of urinary incontinence

Out of a total of 374 participants in the study, 123 individuals reported experiencing urinary incontinence, resulting in a prevalence rate of 32.9%. This indicates that nearly one-third of the respondents are affected by this condition. Frequency of urine leak and its impact on everyday activity

The Table 2 presents the frequency of urine leakage and its impact on participants’ daily activities. Among the 123 respondents, the majority (74.80%) reported leaking urine “about once a week or less,” with 59 of them indicating that it interfered with their daily activities, while 33 reported no interference. As the frequency of leakage increased, a greater proportion of individuals reported interference with daily functioning. Notably, all respondents who experienced leakage “three times a week,” “several times a week,” or “all the time” reported that it interfered with their daily lives, highlighting a clear relationship between more frequent leakage and daily disruption. Only a small number 3 experienced leakage “about once a day,” with 2 reporting interference. A chi-square test revealed a statistically significant association between frequency of urine leakage and its interference with daily activities (χ² = 13.86, p = 0.0078) (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with urinary incontinence

Bivariate analysis identified several significant associations (Table 3). Out of the total participants, 32.9% reported experiencing UI, while 67.1% did not. Age showed a significant association with UI (p < 0.001), with the prevalence increasing steadily with age most notably among those aged 60–69 years (42.3%). BMI was also significantly associated with UI (p = 0.005), particularly among individuals in Class 1 obesity (66.7%), compared to no cases among normal-weight individuals. Parity was a strong predictor (p = 0.001), with the highest prevalence of UI (44.7%) observed among women with five births, and an increasing trend seen with higher parity levels. Similarly, menopausal status had a significant relationship with UI (p = 0.001), with 84.6% of menopausal women experiencing UI compared to 15.5% of non-menopausal women. A history of hysterectomy also showed a significant link to UI (p = 0.004), with a higher proportion of affected individuals among those who had undergone the procedure (22.8%). Quality of life impact

The Table 4 presents the relationship between urge incontinence and quality of life, specifically focusing on two domains: “Feeling Bad” and “Personal Relationship” domains are reported because these showed the strongest associations with UI frequency and severity, highlighting the primary quality-of-life impact in this population. For the “Feeling Bad” domain, the data show a statistically significant association (χ² = 15.3, p = 0.0140), indicating that increased severity of urge incontinence is related to greater emotional distress. For example, 52.0% of participants with a high level of urge incontinence reported feeling bad “slightly,” compared to only 6.7% who felt moderately bad. Similarly, among those who reported feeling “a lot” bad, the largest proportions also had moderate to severe levels of incontinence.

In the “Personal Relationship” domain, the relationship is even more pronounced (χ² = 27.0, p = 0.0002), suggesting that urge incontinence significantly affects interpersonal connections. Discussion

This community-based study found that nearly one in three adult women in Bantama experienced non-fistulous urinary incontinence (UI), underscoring its substantial but often under-recognized burden in urban Ghana. The observed prevalence of 32.9% is comparable to estimates reported in other parts of sub-Saharan Africa, though variations exist by region, study population, and methodology. Studies from West Africa have reported UI prevalence ranging from approximately 20% to 38% among adult women, with higher rates generally observed in community-based surveys compared to facility-based studies. Similar prevalence levels have been documented in East African settings such as Rwanda and Tanzania, where estimates range between 24% and 36%, while studies from Southern Africa, including South Africa, report prevalence figures of up to 40% in some urban populations [4, 5, 11]. These similarities suggest that non-fistulous UI is a common condition among African women, challenging the persistent perception that it is rare in this context.

Differences in reported prevalence across studies may be attributed to variations in age distribution, reproductive history, obesity prevalence, cultural perceptions of UI, and differences in case definitions and measurement tools. The use of a household-based design and a validated quality-of-life instrument in the present study may have facilitated more accurate disclosure of symptoms, particularly in a sociocultural context where UI is stigmatized and often underreported. In contrast, facility-based studies may underestimate prevalence by capturing only women who seek care, thereby excluding those who normalize symptoms or lack access to health services.

Consistent with existing literature, advancing age, higher parity, menopausal status, obesity, and history of pelvic surgery were significantly associated with UI. Age-related pelvic floor muscle weakening, reduced estrogen levels during menopause, and cumulative childbirth-related trauma are well-established biological mechanisms underlying these associations [12, 13]. The strong relationship between obesity and UI observed in this study aligns with evidence linking increased intra-abdominal pressure and chronic inflammatory pathways to pelvic floor dysfunction. These findings reinforce the importance of addressing modifiable risk factors, particularly weight management, in UI prevention strategies.

The association between hysterectomy and UI observed in this study reflects ongoing inconsistencies in the literature. While some studies identify hysterectomy as an independent risk factor due to disruption of pelvic support structures, others suggest that pre-existing pelvic floor dysfunction may confound this relationship [14–16]. The cross-sectional nature of the present study limits causal interpretation; however, the findings highlight the need for longitudinal studies in African settings to better disentangle surgical effects from underlying pelvic pathology.

Beyond prevalence and risk factors, this study demonstrates the substantial emotional and social burden of UI. Increasing frequency and severity of leakage were associated with greater emotional distress and strained interpersonal relationships, findings that mirror studies from both high-income and low- and middle-income countries. These psychosocial consequences emphasize that UI is not solely a biomedical condition but one with significant mental health and social implications, particularly in settings where stigma and silence around pelvic floor disorders are common [17, 18].

From a policy and practice perspective, the findings underscore the need to incorporate UI screening and management into existing women’s health services in Ghana. Given the high prevalence and identifiable risk profile, routine assessment of UI within maternal, reproductive, and menopausal health programs could facilitate early identification and low-cost interventions such as pelvic floor muscle training, lifestyle modification, and counseling. Training healthcare providers to recognize and address UI, alongside community-based education to reduce stigma and promote help-seeking, could substantially reduce the hidden burden of the condition. Integrating UI into broader non-communicable disease and healthy ageing strategies may also enhance continuity of care for women across the life course.

Study limitations

While the study provides valuable insight into the prevalence, factors, and psychosocial impact of urinary incontinence, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; thus, the observed associations between risk factors and UI should be interpreted as correlations rather than cause-and-effect relationships. Longitudinal studies are necessary to establish temporal sequences and confirm causal mechanisms. Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and reporting bias, as respondents may underreport or misclassify urinary symptoms due to embarrassment or social desirability. Third, the absence of clinical evaluation or urodynamic testing limits diagnostic accuracy and prevents differentiation between stress, urge, and mixed incontinence subtypes, which could have provided more nuanced insights into pathophysiology.

Also, some clusters may have fewer cases simply due to population differences. Equal allocation could reduce the precision of estimates for more populous areas and may over- or underestimate associations in subgroup analyses.

Additionally, the sample may not be fully representative of the broader population, particularly regarding socioeconomic and regional diversity, which restricts the generalizability of findings. The study also did not comprehensively assess comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or connective tissue disorders that may independently influence UI risk. Despite these limitations, the study’s robust analytical approach and alignment with prior evidence strengthen the credibility of its conclusions. Future research should employ prospective, clinically validated, and stratified designs to refine understanding of causal pathways, evaluate intervention efficacy, and explore psychosocial outcomes in greater depth.

Future research directions

Longitudinal studies are needed to establish causal relationships and natural history of UI in African populations. Multi-country studies would enhance understanding of regional variations and inform continental health policies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that non-fistulous urinary incontinence affects approximately one-third of adult women in Bantama, Ghana, and is associated with significant emotional and social quality-of-life impairments. Urinary incontinence was significantly associated with advancing age, higher parity, obesity, menopausal status, and selected obstetric factors, underscoring its multifactorial nature. Increasing frequency of urine leakage was linked to greater interference with daily activities and poorer emotional well-being. Together, these findings highlight the substantial burden of urinary incontinence among urban Ghanaian women and its impact beyond physical symptoms.
