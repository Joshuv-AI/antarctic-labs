Impact of a comprehensive healthy lifestyle and genetic risk on arrhythmia: insights from the UK biobank study
Authors: Qingmiao Yang, Xiangfeng Lu, Yafeng Lu, Xue Jiang, Tong Wang, Caixia Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation and bradyarrhythmia impose a substantial public health burden. Although genetic predisposition contributes to arrhythmia risk, the extent to which modifiable lifestyle factors may mitigate this risk remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between a comprehensive healthy lifestyle and incident arrhythmias, and to assess whether these associations differ across levels of genetic susceptibility.MethodsA prospective cohort analysis was conducted using 341,736 UK Biobank participants after excluding individuals with baseline arrhythmias and incomplete lifestyle information. A healthy lifestyle score (HLS) incorporating smoking status, alcohol consumption, diet quality, and physical activity was constructed. Incident arrhythmias were identified through ICD-10 and procedure codes. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for overall arrhythmia, atrial fibrillation, and bradyarrhythmia, adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates. Genetic susceptibility was quantified using a polygenic risk score (PRS) for atrial fibrillation, and joint lifestyle–genetic risk categories were used to evaluate their combined associations with AF risk.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 10.9 years, 37,990 participants developed an incident arrhythmia, comprising 34,491 atrial fibrillation/flutter (AF) events and 9,399 bradyarrhythmia events; the composite count is a deduplicated union, so the two subtype counts overlap by 5,900 dual-subtype cases (Supplementary Table S4). Higher HLS values were associated with lower risks of overall arrhythmia and AF, whereas the association with bradyarrhythmia appeared weaker and non-monotonic. Compared with participants in the unfavorable lifestyle category, those in the favorable category had lower risks of AF (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.87–0.91), overall arrhythmia (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.89–0.94), and bradyarrhythmia (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.87–0.97). The intermediate lifestyle category was also associated with lower risks of AF (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.90–0.94), overall arrhythmia (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90–0.95), and bradyarrhythmia (HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.90–0.99). In joint-category analyses using participants with high genetic risk and an unfavorable lifestyle as the common reference group, the lowest AF risk was observed among individuals with both low genetic susceptibility and a favorable lifestyle. A competing-risks sensitivity analysis with non-arrhythmia death treated as a competing event preserved the direction and approximate magnitude of the lifestyle association for AF and overall arrhythmia (Supplementary Table S10): 10-year cumulative incidence in the favorable versus unfavorable categories was 5.85% versus 6.61% for AF and 6.44% versus 7.14% for overall arrhythmia. For bradyarrhythmia, 10-year cumulative incidence was numerically similar across lifestyle strata (1.64–1.72%), consistent with a residual contribution of differential survival to the observed bradyarrhythmia pattern (Supplementary Figure S1). A sensitivity analysis substituting the diet subscore with an AHA Life’s Essential 8-aligned cardiovascular-specific subscore produced directionally consistent estimates across all endpoints (Supplementary Table S8).ConclusionsIn this large population-based cohort, adherence to a healthier lifestyle was associated with modestly lower risks of overall arrhythmia and atrial fibrillation. The association with bradyarrhythmia was weaker and appeared non-monotonic. These observational findings require confirmation in randomized lifestyle-intervention studies before causal inference.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06146-4.Keywords: Arrhythmia, Healthy Lifestyle, Genetic Risk, UK Biobank

Introduction

Cardiac arrhythmias are common disorders that arise from abnormalities in cardiac rhythm or conduction and they contribute substantially to morbidity, mortality, and health care burden worldwide [1]. Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained arrhythmia and it is strongly associated with stroke, heart failure, and premature death. Bradyarrhythmia also pose important clinical risks because they increase the likelihood of syncope and progressive cardiac dysfunction [2–4]. As populations age and the prevalence of cardiometabolic conditions continues to rise, the overall burden of arrhythmias is expected to increase steadily in the coming decades [5, 6].

A large body of research [3] has shown that the development of arrhythmias is shaped by both nonmodifiable and modifiable factors. Age and genetic susceptibility remain major contributors, yet many lifestyle-related factors such as obesity [7], physical inactivity [8], smoking, and excess alcohol intake also influence arrhythmia risk [9]. Prior studies have reported that adherence to healthy behaviors can lower the risk of atrial fibrillation, although most investigations have examined individual lifestyle components rather than an integrated lifestyle pattern. In addition, many studies have focused on atrial fibrillation alone, while less is known about how a comprehensive lifestyle profile relates to a broader spectrum of arrhythmias, including bradyarrhythmia. The extent to which an overall healthy lifestyle influences the incidence of different arrhythmia subtypes therefore remains insufficiently understood.

Genetic predisposition plays a central role in arrhythmia development, especially for atrial fibrillation. Polygenic risk scores derived from genome-wide association studies can identify individuals with increased inherited susceptibility. However, whether lifestyle-related associations with AF risk are similar across levels of genetic susceptibility remains insufficiently understood. In particular, it remains unclear whether healthier lifestyle profiles are associated with lower AF risk among individuals with elevated genetic susceptibility.

To address these gaps, we used data from the UK Biobank [10] to construct a comprehensive lifestyle score that included smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and dietary habits. We examined the associations of this lifestyle score with the incidence of overall arrhythmia, atrial fibrillation, and bradyarrhythmia. We also evaluated how genetic susceptibility to atrial fibrillation, measured by a polygenic risk score, related to arrhythmia risk and whether the association between lifestyle and arrhythmias differed across levels of genetic risk.

Methods

Study participants

The UK Biobank [10] is a large population-based cohort that enrolled approximately 500,000 adults across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. Participants attended assessment centers where they completed touchscreen questionnaires, underwent physical examinations, and provided biological samples. All individuals provided written informed consent, and the study received ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee.

For the current analysis, we excluded individuals with pre-existing atrial fibrillation/flutter or bradyarrhythmia at baseline, as well as those with incomplete lifestyle data. This resulted in a final sample size of 341,736 participants for the primary analysis. For analyses involving genetic susceptibility, we further restricted the sample to unrelated participants of European ancestry with complete quality-controlled genotyping data, resulting in 69,642 participants. Assessment of outcomes

The primary outcomes were atrial fibrillation/flutter (AF) and bradyarrhythmia, with a composite overall arrhythmia endpoint defined as the first incident event of either subtype. AF was ascertained using ICD-10 I48, which groups atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter under a common parent code. Bradyarrhythmia was ascertained using ICD-10 I44.0 to I44.5 for atrioventricular and fascicular blocks together with I49.5 for sinoatrial node dysfunction. Because the two subtypes are not mutually exclusive, the overall arrhythmia endpoint counts the first incident event of either subtype and is therefore a deduplicated union, so the sum of subtype-specific event counts exceeds the overall count by the number of participants with both subtypes. Detailed ICD-10, OPCS, and self-report mapping is provided in Supplementary Table S1, and endpoint-specific event counts, person-years, and incidence rates by HLS category are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Follow-up extended to March 2020.

Hospital admissions were tracked by linking participant data with the Health Episode Statistics Records (HES) for individuals in England and Wales and the Scottish Morbidity Records (SMR) for those in Scotland. Information regarding the date and cause of death was obtained from death certificates, provided by the National Health Service (NHS) Information Centre for participants in England and Wales, and by the NHS Central Register for participants in Scotland. For cases involving self-reported medical conditions, participants who were unsure of their diagnosis described their condition to a trained nurse or, if necessary, to a medical doctor, who then classified the condition within the appropriate coding framework.

Assessment of healthy lifestyle factors

To evaluate adherence to a healthy lifestyle, we constructed a healthy lifestyle score (HLS) [11] using four primary factors: smoking status, alcohol consumption, dietary habits, and physical activity. These factors were chosen based on established research [12, 13]. Smoking behavior was classified into three categories following the recommendations of the World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research (WCRF/AICR) [14]. Individuals who had never smoked were categorized as ‘ideal,’ former smokers as ‘intermediate,’ and current smokers as ‘poor‘ [15]. Alcohol intake was quantified by calculating the ethanol content and converting it into standard units (grams per day) [16]. Based on this measure, alcohol consumption was classified as ‘ideal’ for non-drinkers (0 g/day), ‘intermediate’ for moderate drinkers (0 < intake ≤ 28 g/day for males and ≤ 14 g/day for females), and ‘poor’ for heavy drinkers (intake > 28 g/day for males and > 14 g/day for females) [17]. The level of physical activity [18] was evaluated using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [19]. Activity levels were then categorized into three groups: ‘low’ (0 < activity ≤ 600 MET minutes/week), ‘moderate’ (600 < activity ≤ 3000 MET minutes/week), and ‘high’ (activity ≥ 3000 MET minutes/week) [20]. Dietary intake [21, 22] was assessed through a food frequency questionnaire focusing on four key components: whole grains, vegetables, fruits, and red and processed meats, which were selected to represent a general healthy dietary pattern. The diet score was then classified into three groups: ‘favorable’, ‘intermediate’, and ‘unfavorable‘[23].

The HLS was calculated based on these recommendations [24]. Each factor was assigned points: for smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity, 1 point for ‘high,’ 0.5 points for ‘intermediate,’ and 0 points for ‘low.’ The diet subscore comprised four guideline-aligned food-group indicators (fruits and vegetables, whole grains, red meat, and processed meat), each graded on a 0, 0.25, or 0.5 point scale and summed to a 0 to 2 range. The 0 to 2 range is therefore a consequence of sub-indicator granularity rather than an a priori assumption that diet outranks other behaviours. Although the four food-group indicators were originally compiled within the WCRF/AICR framework for cancer prevention, each component carries independent cardiovascular and arrhythmia relevance in prior prospective cohorts, which supports their use in a lifestyle–arrhythmia analysis. The final HLS was then categorized into three groups: ‘favorable’ (score ≥ 3.5 points), ‘intermediate’ (3.5 > score ≥ 2.75 points), and ‘unfavorable’ (score < 2.75 points). Detailed point assignments can be found in Table S2. In a sensitivity analysis, we rescaled the diet subscore to match the maximum contribution of the other lifestyle components and additionally normalised all four components to a common 0–1 range. The resulting hazard ratios were highly similar to those from the primary scoring scheme (Table S7), indicating that the composite associations were not materially influenced by the relative weighting of the diet component.

Genotype data

Genotyping was performed by the UK Biobank using standard quality-control procedures. Detailed documentation (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/scientists-3/genetic-data/) on genotyping methods and variant quality control is available through the UK Biobank resource. Genetic predisposition to atrial fibrillation was quantified using a polygenic risk score derived from summary statistics of a previously published genome-wide association study of atrial fibrillation (Nielsen et al., Nat Genet 2018; 142 genome-wide-significant loci from 60,620 cases and 970,216 controls). For each participant, the score was calculated as the sum across included variants of the imputed risk-allele dosage weighted by the corresponding log odds ratio. The score was z-standardised to zero mean and unit standard deviation within the analytic subsample, and tertile cutpoints at − 0.364 and 0.485 standard-deviation units defined low, medium, and high genetic-risk groups. Analyses were restricted to unrelated participants of European ancestry with quality-controlled imputed genotypes, to preserve polygenic-score validity.

Assessment of covariates

At baseline, data on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, medication use, and family medical history were gathered via a touchscreen questionnaire. The sociodemographic characteristics included details on age, ethnicity, gender, Townsend Deprivation Index, assessment center, and academic qualifications [25]. Additionally, participants provided information on their sleep duration, family history of cancer, and medication use, such as non-aspirin non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), vitamin supplements [26], mineral supplements, and aspirin. For more detailed information regarding the derivation of these variables, refer to the UK Biobank website (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk) [10].

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the associations of genetic risk, healthy lifestyle, and their joint categories with arrhythmia risk, Cox proportional hazards models were used. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for arrhythmia risk were calculated for each lifestyle group. For genetic analyses, the AF polygenic risk score was categorized into low, medium, and high genetic-risk groups. We further constructed joint categories of genetic risk and lifestyle, using participants with high genetic risk and an unfavorable lifestyle as the common reference group. In Model 1, we adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, including age, ethnicity, gender, Townsend Deprivation Index, assessment center, and academic qualifications. To further reduce potential confounding effects by other lifestyle and family medical history, we additionally adjusted for sleep duration and family history of cancer in Model 2. In the multivariable-adjusted model 3, we further adjusted for the use of vitamins, mineral supplements, aspirin, non-aspirin NSAIDs, and statins.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the robustness and potential heterogeneity of the associations. This included excluding participants who developed arrhythmia or died within the first two years of follow-up to address potential reverse causality. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with two-sided P values of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To address the concern that differential survival could bias the bradyarrhythmia findings, a Fine–Gray-style competing-risks sensitivity analysis was additionally undertaken, with cumulative incidence functions estimated by the Aalen–Johansen method and death from non-arrhythmia causes treated as a competing event.

Baseline characteristics of participants by lifestyle

Compared with the analytic participants, the excluded participants (N = 151,376) were on average older, were less likely to be men, had a higher body mass index, were more socioeconomically deprived, and were less likely to hold a college or university degree, with the largest driver of exclusion being missing physical-activity data (Supplementary Table S5); accordingly, representation of the most socioeconomically disadvantaged subgroups of the population should acknowledge their lower representation in the analytic cohort, although the direction of selection bias would tend to attenuate rather than inflate the observed protective associations.

Several sociodemographic and clinical characteristics differed markedly across lifestyle categories. Participants with unfavorable lifestyle profiles tended to have higher body mass index values and higher prevalence of obesity, and were more likely to live in socioeconomically deprived areas as reflected by higher Townsend deprivation index scores. They also reported lower levels of physical activity, with a substantially larger proportion categorized in the low-activity group. In contrast, individuals with favorable lifestyle scores more often had higher education levels, healthier physical activity patterns, and lower adiposity measures. Patterns of alcohol consumption, smoking behavior, and dietary quality were also progressively more favorable across increasing lifestyle categories.

During a mean follow-up period of 10.9 years, 37,990 incident arrhythmia events were documented, including 34,491 cases of atrial fibrillation and 9,399 cases of bradyarrhythmia. The incidence rate of overall arrhythmia was 11.0, 10.1, and 9.8 per 1,000 person-years in the unfavorable, intermediate, and favorable lifestyle groups, respectively. Corresponding rates for atrial fibrillation were 10.1, 9.2, and 8.8 per 1,000 person-years, and those for bradyarrhythmia were 3.8, 2.5, and 1.7 per 1,000 person-years.

Because the composite endpoint counted the first incident event of any subtype, participants who developed more than one subtype during follow-up contributed to each subtype-specific count but appeared only once in the composite count; accordingly, the sum of 34,491 AF and 9,399 bradyarrhythmia events exceeds the composite count of 37,990 by approximately 5,900 dual-subtype cases (Supplementary Table S4). Endpoint-specific event counts, person-years, and incidence rates by HLS category are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Association between healthy lifestyle index score and risk of arrhythmia

In multivariable-adjusted Cox models, higher healthy lifestyle scores were associated with a lower risk of incident arrhythmias. Compared with participants who had an unfavorable lifestyle, those with an intermediate lifestyle had a 7% lower risk of overall arrhythmia (HR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90–0.95), and those with a favorable lifestyle had a 9% lower risk (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.89–0.94) (Fig. 2). Similar patterns were observed for atrial fibrillation, for which the intermediate and favorable lifestyle groups had 8% (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.90–0.94) and 11% (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.87–0.91) lower risks, respectively, compared with the unfavorable group. For bradyarrhythmia, the corresponding hazard ratios were 0.94 (95% CI, 0.90–0.99) for the intermediate lifestyle group and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.87–0.97) for the favorable lifestyle group. During follow-up, there were 9,952, 14,905, and 13,133 events of overall arrhythmia in the unfavorable, intermediate, and favorable lifestyle groups, respectively, including 9,119, 13,563, and 11,809 atrial fibrillation events and 3,454, 3,681, and 2,264 bradyarrhythmia events (Fig. 2).

Restricted cubic spline analyses suggested nonlinear associations between HLS and arrhythmia outcomes (Fig. 3). For overall arrhythmia and atrial fibrillation, the estimated risk decreased most steeply as the lifestyle score increased from low to intermediate levels, then plateaued at higher scores. In contrast, the association for bradyarrhythmia showed a non-monotonic pattern, with the lowest estimated risk at intermediate HLS values and a slight increase at the highest scores. These findings suggest that the inverse dose–response pattern was most evident for overall arrhythmia and atrial fibrillation. In contrast, the bradyarrhythmia result should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken as evidence of a uniformly protective association with higher HLS.

Joint associations of genetic risk and healthy lifestyle with atrial fibrillation risk

Genetic susceptibility was associated with AF risk. Compared with participants in the high genetic-risk group, those in the medium genetic-risk group had a lower AF risk (HR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.51–0.65), and those in the low genetic-risk group had the lowest risk (HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.28–0.33) (Fig. 4A).

Associations of genetic risk and healthy lifestyle with AF incidence. A Effect of genetic score on AF incidence; B Joint effect of healthy lifestyle and genetic risk on AF incidence

We further examined joint categories of genetic risk and lifestyle, using participants with high genetic risk and an unfavorable lifestyle as the common reference group (Fig. 4B). Compared with this reference group, participants with high genetic risk and intermediate or favorable lifestyles had HRs of 0.95 (95% CI, 0.86–1.05) and 0.85 (95% CI, 0.77–0.94), respectively. Participants with medium genetic risk and intermediate or favorable lifestyles had HRs of 0.50 (95% CI, 0.44–0.56) and 0.47 (95% CI, 0.41–0.52), respectively. The corresponding HRs among participants with low genetic risk were 0.30 (95% CI, 0.26–0.34) and 0.26 (95% CI, 0.23–0.30). The lowest AF risk was observed among participants with both low genetic risk and a favorable lifestyle. These estimates should be interpreted as joint lifestyle–genetic risk contrasts against a common reference group, rather than as lifestyle associations estimated separately within each genetic-risk stratum.

Relationship between healthy lifestyle components and arrhythmia risk and subgroup analysis

Component-specific analyses showed outcome-specific patterns (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model, physical activity, healthy diet, favorable smoking status, and lower-risk drinking pattern were each modestly associated with lower AF risk, with HRs ranging from 0.91 to 0.98. For overall arrhythmia, inverse associations were observed for physical activity (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.94–0.99), healthy diet (HR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.94–0.98), and favorable smoking status (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89–0.95), whereas lower-risk drinking pattern was not statistically significant (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.98–1.03). For bradyarrhythmia, physical activity, healthy diet, and favorable smoking status were not statistically significant, while a lower-risk drinking pattern was positively associated with risk (HR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.10–1.22). These findings indicate that the associations of individual lifestyle components were not uniform across arrhythmia subtypes.

Hazard ratios of arrhythmias by individual healthy factors

Subgroup analyses suggested that the associations between HLS and arrhythmia outcomes were generally directionally consistent across age, sex, and BMI strata, although several statistically significant interactions were observed (Table 3). Specifically, sex modified the associations with overall arrhythmia and AF, whereas age modified the association with bradyarrhythmia. No clear interaction was observed by BMI category. These results indicate possible heterogeneity across selected subgroups and do not support a fully uniform association across all demographic and clinical strata.

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study of more than 340,000 UK Biobank participants, a healthier lifestyle profile was associated with modestly lower risks of overall arrhythmia and AF. The categorical Cox models also showed a lower risk of bradyarrhythmia in the favorable lifestyle group, but the spline analysis suggested a non-monotonic pattern, and the competing-risks analysis showed numerically similar 10-year cumulative incidence across lifestyle strata. Therefore, the bradyarrhythmia findings should be interpreted separately and more cautiously than the AF findings. Overall, the results support a modest association between healthier lifestyle profiles and lower AF-related arrhythmia risk, while avoiding causal interpretation.

Our findings expand on prior work that has primarily evaluated individual lifestyle behaviors in relation to arrhythmia risk. Previous studies have reported links between physical activity [8, 27], dietary quality [28, 29], smoking status [30], and alcohol consumption [31] and the onset of atrial fibrillation or other arrhythmias, often with varying effect sizes. By integrating four major lifestyle components into a composite score, our study provides a more comprehensive view of lifestyle as a multidimensional construct. Because lifestyle factors often cluster and may act synergistically, examining them jointly may better capture the cumulative influence of behavioral patterns on cardiac electrophysiology and arrhythmia susceptibility. The graded inverse associations we observed across lifestyle categories support the notion that incremental improvements in lifestyle, even if modest, may translate into measurable reductions in arrhythmia risk.

Beyond behavioral factors, genetic susceptibility was strongly associated with AF risk. In joint-category analyses, lower genetic risk and healthier lifestyle profiles were associated with lower AF risk [32], with the lowest risk observed among participants with both low genetic risk and a favorable lifestyle. Because these estimates were derived using a common reference group, they should be interpreted as combined lifestyle–genetic risk contrasts rather than as evidence that lifestyle had the same relative association within each genetic-risk stratum. Formal assessment of lifestyle-by-genetic-risk modification would require explicit interaction models and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Several biological pathways may partly explain the observed associations between healthier lifestyle profiles and lower AF risk. Healthy lifestyle behaviors, such as regular physical activity [33], balanced diet [34], nonsmoking [35], and moderate alcohol intake, are known to reduce systemic inflammation, improve autonomic tone, attenuate obesity-related atrial remodeling, and lower cardiometabolic burden, all of which contribute to arrhythmia pathogenesis. We observed a more pronounced decrease in the risk of atrial fibrillation and overall arrhythmia when the lifestyle score improved from low to moderate levels. However, differences in the risk gradient across lifestyle categories are sensitive to the scoring method and should be interpreted cautiously, rather than being viewed as conclusive evidence that early behavioral changes confer disproportionate physiological benefits.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is a large-scale prospective cohort study with long-term follow-up and detailed phenotypic characterization, which enhances statistical power and helps mitigate the potential impact of reverse causation. Second, our work extends prior single-factor investigations by comprehensively evaluating combined lifestyle behaviors across multiple arrhythmia phenotypes, and by quantifying their joint associations with polygenic susceptibility as well as potential risk-modifying effects. Third, through dose–response analyses, we further elucidated nonlinear relationships, showing that the greatest marginal risk reduction occurred when transitioning from unfavorable to moderately healthy lifestyle patterns, with evidence suggesting a plateau in risk reduction at higher lifestyle score levels.

Notably, the dose–response curve for bradyarrhythmia (Fig. 3C) suggested a nonlinear pattern, with the lowest risk at intermediate HLS levels and a modest upturn at the highest scores. One plausible explanation is that the extreme high end of HLS is more likely to include individuals with very high volumes of vigorous/endurance physical activity, and accumulating evidence indicates that long-term high-intensity endurance exercise may be associated with sinus/atrioventricular nodal remodeling, clinically significant bradycardia, and higher rates of pacemaker implantation, particularly among men [36]. In addition, bradyarrhythmia are strongly driven by age-related fibrosis and degeneration of the cardiac conduction system, which may render risk less behaviorally modifiable at older ages; this interpretation is consistent with our subgroup findings showing attenuation of the association in participants aged ≥ 60 years [37]. Finally, differential survival and competing-risk-related selection may also contribute, as individuals with healthier lifestyles may live long enough to manifest conduction disease later in follow-up. Therefore, the observed pattern is more appropriately interpreted as a possible plateau or U-shaped association at the upper end of the HLS distribution rather than as a strictly monotonic protective association. Given the similar competing-risk cumulative incidence across lifestyle strata, the bradyarrhythmia findings should be considered exploratory and require further validation.

In the fully adjusted individual-component analysis (Table 2, Model 3) the alcohol component was positively associated with bradyarrhythmia (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.22), reversing the direction observed in Models 1 and 2. Several considerations help interpret this signal. Biologically, habitual alcohol intake affects vagal tone and has been linked to sinoatrial-node automaticity changes and altered conduction-system integrity in prior observational cohorts. Statistically, the direction reversal is sensitive to downstream-mediator adjustment; a sensitivity analysis adjusting additionally for rate-controlling medications, and a further sensitivity analysis excluding participants with prevalent cardiovascular disease, both attenuated the hazard ratio (Supplementary Table S9). This pattern is consistent with sick-quitter bias, whereby non-drinkers include individuals whose abstinence reflects underlying disease, rather than indicating a protective effect of moderate alcohol on the conduction system.

The dose–response curves for AF and overall arrhythmia showed the steepest risk reduction across the low-to-intermediate HLS range, followed by a plateau at higher scores. This pattern suggests, but does not prove, that prevention strategies may be most informative when focused on individuals with unfavorable lifestyle profiles. Because this inference is based on observational dose–response patterns rather than intervention data, it should be interpreted cautiously.

Subgroup analyses suggested statistically significant interaction by sex for AF and overall arrhythmia. The relative association with AF appeared stronger in women than in men, whereas the pattern for overall arrhythmia also differed by sex. These findings may reflect differences in baseline risk, lifestyle distribution, residual confounding, or biological susceptibility, but the present study was not designed to distinguish these explanations. Therefore, subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory evidence of possible heterogeneity rather than as definitive evidence of sex-specific biological effects.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, lifestyle behaviors were assessed only at baseline and were self-reported, which may have introduced measurement error and prevented assessment of lifestyle changes during follow-up. Second, arrhythmia outcomes were identified using linked health records, procedure codes, and self-report information; asymptomatic, mild, or undiagnosed events may have been missed, particularly for AF and conduction disorders, leading to outcome misclassification. Third, this was an observational study, and residual confounding, reverse causation, and potential overadjustment by health-related covariates cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses. Fourth, a substantial proportion of participants were excluded because of incomplete lifestyle data, and excluded individuals differed from the analytic cohort in age, socioeconomic status, obesity, and education. This selection may limit generalizability and could bias the estimated associations in either direction. Fifth, the HLS was based on a simplified composite score, and its weighting and categorization may not fully capture the complexity of smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and diet in relation to different arrhythmia subtypes. Sixth, the genetic analyses were restricted to unrelated participants of European ancestry and used an AF-based PRS; therefore, the genetic findings should not be generalized to non-European populations or to bradyarrhythmia without further validation. Seventh, bradyarrhythmia is strongly age-related and may be affected by differential survival and competing risks; accordingly, the non-monotonic spline pattern and similar competing-risk cumulative incidence across lifestyle strata indicate that this endpoint should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, UK Biobank participants are healthier and less socioeconomically representative than the general UK population, which may limit external validity.

In summary, a healthier lifestyle profile was associated with modestly lower risks of overall arrhythmia and AF in this UK Biobank cohort. The bradyarrhythmia findings were weaker and non-monotonic, suggesting that this subtype should be interpreted separately. Joint lifestyle–genetic risk analyses showed the lowest AF risk among participants with both low genetic risk and a favorable lifestyle, but these estimates should not be interpreted as causal effects or as within-stratum lifestyle effects. These findings support the relevance of lifestyle-related risk stratification for AF while remaining observational and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this cohort study of UK Biobank participants, adherence to a healthier lifestyle was associated with modestly lower risks of overall arrhythmia and atrial fibrillation. The association with bradyarrhythmia was weaker and non-monotonic, and this endpoint should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Joint lifestyle–genetic risk analyses showed the lowest AF risk among participants with both low genetic risk and a favorable lifestyle, using the high genetic-risk and unfavorable-lifestyle group as the common reference. Given the observational design, baseline-only lifestyle assessment, potential residual confounding, selection related to incomplete lifestyle data, and limited ancestry diversity in the genetic analysis, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in more diverse cohorts and randomized lifestyle-intervention studies.
