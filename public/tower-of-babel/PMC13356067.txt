Development and Validation of Prescribing Appropriateness Criteria (PAC) for Sri Lankan Older Adults
Authors: Maneesha P. Godakanda Arachchige, Rebekah J. Moles, Sarath Lekamwasam, Margaret Jordan, Benjamin J. Basger, Shukry Zawahir, Timothy F. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInappropriate prescribing in Sri Lankan older adults, contributes to adverse drug events, hospital admissions and increased healthcare expenditure. Although international tools exist to assess prescribing appropriateness in older adults, their direct application in Sri Lanka is limited by differences in clinical practices, resource constraints and variability in medicine availability.ObjectiveWe aimed to develop and validate country-specific prescription appropriateness criteria for Sri Lankan older adults, focusing on prevalent diseases.MethodsA literature review was conducted to develop a preliminary list of criteria, which was scrutinised by three internal reviewers. The criteria were then validated using the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method (RAM). A multidisciplinary Sri Lankan panel (medical specialists, hospital pharmacists and pharmacy academics) completed three rounds of ratings involving 15, 11 and 7 panellists, respectively.ResultsThe preliminary list contained 38 criteria; all were rated appropriate in round one, with clarity amendments suggested for seven criteria. Two additional criteria were proposed and accepted, producing a final set of 40 criteria. The criteria most frequently addressed medicines used for cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus, with additional focus on pain, musculoskeletal and bone health, asthma and neuropsychiatric disorders. Criteria not commonly included in international tools covered gabapentinoid prescribing for pain with renal dose adjustment, corticosteroid-associated glycaemic monitoring with antihyperglycaemic dose adjustment and blood monitoring requirements for methotrexate therapy.ConclusionsThese criteria provide a context-specific framework to support safer prescribing for older adults in Sri Lanka. Their integration into clinical practice and pharmacist-led medication reviews could reduce medication-related problems and improve outcomes.

Key Points

This study developed and validated practical criteria for medical practitioners and pharmacists to support quality prescribing for older adults in Sri Lanka.Criteria were contextualised using current evidence, local guidelines, medicines accessibility, diagnostic limitations and expert consensus, ensuring applicability.The application of these criteria in the Sri Lankan public healthcare system has the potential to improve medication safety and optimise pharmacotherapy in older adults.The criteria may also be used as a research framework to evaluate the quality use of medicines in older adults in Sri Lanka.

Introduction

Sri Lanka, a country in South Asia, has a rapidly ageing population [1]. It is projected that by 2050, 29% of the population will be aged 60 years or older [2]. Among Asian countries, Sri Lanka will exhibit the fastest ageing in the coming decades [3]. Ageing is associated with multimorbidity and the ageing of the population has a significant impact on a country’s health and economy [1]. The prevalence of chronic diseases in the older population in Sri Lanka is higher, compared with other age cohorts, and chronic disease is a leading cause of mortality in this age group [1, 4]. Moreover, owing to the high prevalence of chronic diseases, older adults disproportionately use higher numbers of prescription medicines than their younger counterparts [5, 6]. Hence, appropriate use of medicines by older adults is an essential element of healthcare systems, to mitigate adverse medical, economic and social ramifications [5, 7].

The prevalence of potentially inappropriate prescribing (PIP) among older adults in Sri Lanka has been reported at around 32% [8]. Globally, rates of inappropriate prescribing vary widely, with a range from 27% to 68%, according to findings from multiple studies conducted in different countries [9–11]. Moreover, it is associated with higher rates of mortality, morbidity, adverse drug events and hospitalisation [8, 12]. Although certain pharmacotherapeutic strategies have been used to manage multimorbidity in older patients, inadequate monitoring and review result in adverse events [7].

Inappropriate prescribing occurs when the potential risk of pharmacological treatment outweighs anticipated benefits. This includes misprescribing, overprescribing and underprescribing [13]. Because of the higher prevalence of PIP among older patients, a multitude of decision-making tools for assessing inappropriate prescribing has been developed, often based on a country’s own treatment guidelines, medication formularies and healthcare infrastructure (e.g. Beers Criteria, STOPP/START [Screening Tool of Older Person’s Prescriptions/Screening Tool to Alert to Right Treatment] criteria) [14, 15]. The applicability for implementation of these tools in low- and middle-income countries such as Sri Lanka is limited by infrastructural and resource-related differences, variabilities in disease prevalence, prescribing practices, accessibility to medicines and diagnostic services, different roles and responsibilities of healthcare professionals (e.g. pharmacists) and different community expectations [16, 17]. For instance, the STOPP/START criteria recommend sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors for the management of symptomatic heart failure regardless of ejection fraction or the presence of diabetes. However, their application in the Sri Lankan public healthcare system is currently limited owing to restricted availability in the public sector. As a result, the routine implementation of these recommendations is constrained, and alternative treatment strategies remain the mainstay of care in practice [15, 18]. To overcome these differences, this study aimed to develop and validate country-specific prescribing appropriateness criteria for Sri Lankan older adults grounded in up-to-date evidence and local clinical practices and based on expert consensus.

The application of prescribing appropriateness criteria in older adults requires background knowledge in geriatric medicine, clinical pharmacology and the management of multimorbidity. Given the breadth of the criteria, they are designed to support a structured medication review process. In Sri Lanka, they are particularly relevant for use during comprehensive medication reviews at transitions of care (e.g. hospital admission and discharge), in outpatient clinics and pharmacist-led medication review services. Potential users include hospital physicians, general practitioners and clinical pharmacists involved in the care of older adults.

Methods

This study aimed to develop and validate clinically applicable prescribing criteria for older adults using a structured consensus approach based on the RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method (RAM). The methodology comprised sequential rounds of expert assessment and refinement. Criteria Development

The literature search was conducted from December 2023 to February 2024 using MEDLINE and Google Scholar to identify peer‑reviewed publications reporting the prevalence of chronic diseases in Sri Lanka. The following search terms and combinations were used: “prevalence”, “chronic disease”, “non‑communicable diseases”, “older adults” and “Sri Lanka”. To capture nationally reported epidemiological data not indexed in bibliographic databases, Sri Lankan national health statistics were identified through targeted searches using Google Chrome, including Ministry of Health and Department of Census and Statistics publications. Based on the convergence of findings across peer‑reviewed studies and national datasets, the most consistently reported prevalent chronic conditions among older adults in Sri Lanka were cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, asthma, arthritis, pain and mental illnesses. These conditions formed the primary disease foundation upon which the prescribing criteria were developed [7, 8, 16, 19–21]. The selected diseases were crossmatched with medicines listed in the Sri Lanka Essential list 2022 and approved list of pharmaceutical formulary [18, 22], which aim to optimise medication use or address significant medical issues associated with these conditions. Because of the limited availability of Sri Lankan prevalence data for neurological and psychiatric conditions, medicines relevant to these conditions were selected based on previously established and internationally validated criteria for PIP in older adults. Specifically, the Beers Criteria, the STOPP/START criteria and Australian criteria were used as reference frameworks to inform the inclusion of medicines affecting neurological and psychiatric conditions. [14, 23, 24].

We reviewed the registered medicines list 2024, available in the National Medicines Regulatory Authority Sri Lanka [25] to extend the applicability of our criteria in both public and private healthcare settings. This was necessary because the Sri Lankan public healthcare setting adheres to the essential medicines list (EML), which limits the availability of certain medicines while the private sector has access to a wider range of medicines within the same medication class.

To guide the development of criteria for the optimal use of medicines listed in the EML and the pharmaceutical formulary, we utilised treatment guidelines published by professional medical associations in Sri Lanka [26, 27], the British National Formulary (BNF) [28], the Australian Medical Handbook (AMH) [29] and the Australian electronic Therapeutic Guidelines [30]. The BNF and AMH were specifically chosen because of the absence of comprehensive national treatment guidelines for all disease conditions in Sri Lanka. Furthermore, the BNF serves as the primary information source for Sri Lankan physicians and pharmacists, with the AMH being the next most referenced [8, 16]. We also consulted the electronic Therapeutic Guidelines and research papers published within the last 10 years to corroborate evidence obtained from AMH and BNF.

We followed Australian prescribing appropriateness criteria as a guide to develop our criteria. However, we did not directly adopt or translate them for the local context [31]. Sri Lankan publications from the past decade were scrutinised to identify common drug–drug interactions [7, 32, 33]. Criteria related to drug–drug interactions were developed based on updated evidence [34–36].

Data were scrutinised to develop criteria statements with relevant information and references. The preliminary list was then reviewed by three experts in the field: two Australian clinical pharmacists who are also university academics (BB, MJ), and one Sri Lankan consultant physician who is also a university academic (SL). This review process aimed to further refine and enhance the clarity of the criteria.

Panel Selection

After developing the preliminary list of prescribing appropriateness criteria, we selected a multidisciplinary expert panel to validate the criteria using RAM. The term “validation” in this study refers specifically to an expert consensus-based evaluation of content and face validity, conducted using the RAM. This method integrates available evidence with structured expert judgement to assess the appropriateness, clinical relevance, clarity and applicability of each criterion within the Sri Lankan healthcare context. We selected 15 Sri Lankan panellists to ensure sufficient diversity based on clinical and research expertise in the provision of geriatric care. The panel included 11 physicians with expertise in geriatric care [a geriatric consultant, a psychogeriatrician, physicians who provide postgraduate training in geriatric medicines (n = 4) and physicians who are members of the Sri Lankan Association of Geriatric Medicine (n = 5)], two pharmacy academics (pharmacy academics with training in clinical pharmacy) and two hospital pharmacists (hospital pharmacists with experience in conducting medication review). Panel members were contacted via e-mail and the goal and process were explained to them. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method (RAM), Round One

A survey comprising 38 criteria was distributed to all panel members in July 2024 who were asked to rate each item in terms of pharmacological appropriateness while excluding cost, on a nine-point scale [37]. The appropriateness was defined as ‘the expected health benefit exceeds the expected negative consequences by a sufficiently wide margin that criteria are worth following, regardless of cost’ [37]. Panellists were also given the opportunity to amend wording or provide comments and suggest additional criteria.

RAM, Round Two

In September and October 2024, online meetings were conducted with expert panellists. To address scheduling constraints and ensure maximum participation, two group meetings and two individual online meetings were organised. Meetings were held to discuss the results of the first round and to rerate updated/new criteria and those without consensus. Round two was open to all expert panel member participants from round one and comprised 11 members. The attrition of four panellists occurred because of time constraints and competing professional commitments, which are common challenges in modified Delphi and RAND/UCLA studies conducted over multiple rounds. The four panellists who withdrew (three medical doctors and one hospital pharmacist) represented professional roles already present in the remaining panel, and their withdrawal did not disproportionately affect any single discipline. The final panel retained its multidisciplinary balance, including eight physicians with expertise in geriatric care [including a geriatric consultant, a psychogeriatrician, physicians involved in postgraduate training in geriatric medicine (n = 2) and physicians who are members of the Sri Lankan Association of Geriatric Medicine (n = 4)], two pharmacy academics and one hospital pharmacist.

Prior to the meetings, a copy of the first-round survey results was sent to each panellist. This document included the frequency distribution of ratings of all panellists across the nine-point scale, the overall panel median rating for each criterion and an annotation reflecting how that individual panellist had rated each of the criteria. During the meeting, we discussed items with suggested changes to improve the clarity of criteria and proposed new criteria, supported by a visual representation of the range of scores. The discussions were audio recorded with the consent of panellists.

To maintain methodological rigour and consistency with the RAM, the same structured process was applied during the one-on-one meetings. Panellists were presented with the first-round results and engaged in detailed discussions of items requiring clarification or modification. Importantly, key discussions and perspectives arising from the group meetings were shared visually during the individual sessions to ensure exposure to the full range of viewpoints. This enabled panellists to reflect on collective feedback and reconsider their ratings, thereby preserving the iterative and consensus-driven nature of the method.

RAM, Round Three

In October 2024, a third round was conducted for a single criterion, arising from discussion that occurred during the second round. This comprised an e-mail survey to obtain panel members’ consensus for the changes made to improve the clarity of that particular criterion.

Ethics

Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Ruhuna, Sri Lanka [2023/P/118 (30/11/2023)].

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics Version 30 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New Yor, NY, USA). We assessed the appropriateness of each criterion, adhering to the RAM User’s Manual. Appropriateness was categorised into three levels [37]:Appropriate: panel median of 7–9, without disagreement;Uncertain: panel median of 4–6 OR any median with disagreement;Inappropriate: panel median of 1–3, without disagreement.

The preliminary list consisted of 38 criteria and there was an agreement at round one that all 38 criteria were appropriate. However, panellists suggested alterations to improve the clarity, specificity and clinical applicability of seven criteria (criteria 2, 11, 17, 18, 20, 25 and 26). Additionally, two potentially new criteria were recommended by panellists (criteria 39 and 40, Table 3). All together nine criteria were subjected to round two discussion.

Of the nine criteria discussed in the second round, eight were accepted with amendments and retained (criteria 2, 11, 17, 18, 20, 25, 26, Table 2 and criterion 40, Table 3). Some criteria required minor refinements such as improved clarity on treatment recommendations, and increased specificity on the populations in whom the criterion should be applied or specific medicine use data. For example, the specific population for criteria 11, 26 and 40 were added to provide specificity in their application. More precise wording on medication use was also included for criteria 11 and 20. For criterion 17, clearer wording was provided around escalation of analgesia according to the “pain scale score and type of acute pain” and for criterion 18, defining gabapentinoid discontinuation in the context of “lack of benefit after an adequate trial, with alternative pain control modalities considered”. One osteoporosis-related criterion (criterion 25) was also broadened to include “anabolic medicine and adequate supplements” in addition to anti-resorptive therapy (Table 2). Criterion 39 was accepted as appropriate, but panellists recommended further refinement to improve clarity and clinical applicability. This criterion, originally limited to patients with specific risk factors (e.g. for hyponatraemia), was broadened to include all patients exposed to high-risk medications, reducing ambiguity and improving feasibility in routine practice. In addition, terms such as “regular monitoring” were replaced with more precise wording (e.g. “has had measurement”) to minimise ambiguity in the application of the criterion (Table 3).

Criterion 39 was accepted as appropriate in the third round (Table 3). Following three rounds of validation, the final tool contained 40 criteria (Table 4), supplemented by contextual information on the application of these criteria (Table 5). Additionally, the criteria are accompanied, where applicable, by a rationale to enhance their educational value, facilitating improved understanding and application in clinical practice.

Tables 2 and 3 depict the rating by the IPRAS method and whether there was agreement or disagreement for the original criteria and revised criteria subjected to the second and third rounds. Although 15 panellists participated in Round 1 and 11 in Round 2, the number of ratings (n) shown for individual criteria in Tables 2 and 3 varies slightly because a small number of item-level responses were left unrated by one or more panellists. Where possible, missed items were re-sent for completion; however, a few responses remained missing. All medians and agreement statistics were calculated using the available ratings for each criterion, and missing item-level responses were not imputed.

Discussion

This study developed a set of prescribing appropriateness criteria for prevalent chronic medical conditions in older adults in Sri Lanka. The criteria were developed primarily based on Sri Lankan national guidelines, British, and Australian guidelines and were subsequently validated by a Sri Lankan multidisciplinary panel for use in Sri Lanka. The validation process resulted in the retainment of all 38 criteria from the preliminary list, with the addition of two new criteria. The panel discussion resulted in amendments in seven criteria in the preliminary list to improve clarity, while 31 criteria were accepted without changes. After validation, the resultant criteria set included 40 items. We also developed contextual information to support application of these criteria (Table 5) and recommend that users of the tool obtain additional contextual information from patient medical records, patient and carer interviews, and discussion with other member of the healthcare team to help facilitate appropriate use of medicines. These criteria are designed to provide clinicians with a tool to rationalise prescribing and avoid drug-related problems among older adults in Sri Lanka. The tool comprises two main parts: Table 4 enumerates the criteria whilst Table 5 provides the necessary contextual information for the application of the tool in Sri Lanka.

Our prescribing appropriateness tool was developed specifically for the Sri Lankan healthcare context. Similar to tools such as the STOPP/START Criteria, it has the potential to support decision making at the point of care and inform structured medication review workflows. However, existing international criteria have important limitations when applied in Sri Lanka, mainly because they were developed in healthcare systems with different medicine availability, prescribing patterns, clinical priorities and levels of access to specialist care. In developing the PAC, we considered medicine access in both the Sri Lankan public and private healthcare sectors. Particular attention was given to medicines listed on the Sri Lankan Essential Medicines List for public sector patients, as well as medicines included in the national registered medicines list for patients receiving care in the private sector. Notwithstanding the latter, the availability of medicines in the Sri Lankan public healthcare sector is substantially more limited than the in the healthcare contexts for which the STOPP/START and Beers criteria were developed [14, 15]. For example, several medicines listed in the 2023 Beers Criteria, such as brompheniramine, cyproheptadine, dimenhydrinate, hydroxyzine, amoxapine, nortriptyline, primidone, estazolam, temazepam and indomethacin, as well as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors included in the STOPP/START criteria, are either unavailable or not routinely used in the Sri Lankan public healthcare system [27]. As a result, some recommendations from these international tools may have limited practical relevance in routine local practice. In addition, our tool has a sole focus on high prevalence disease conditions and commonly used medicines in Sri Lanka, unlike other tools that cover a broader range of therapeutic areas [44]. This was a purposeful part of the design of our tool, to maximise utility and applicability in practice, and is similar in approach to Australian prescribing appropriateness criteria [31].

Inappropriate prescribing can be assessed using implicit (judgement-based) and explicit (criterion-based) tools. Implicit tools are primarily patient specific, judgement based and consider the patient’s whole medication regime [45]. Explicit tools such as Beers criteria (USA) [14, 46] and STOPP/START criteria (Europe) [15, 47], provide predefined criteria or lists of medicines to avoid or initiate. Australian criteria consist of both implicit and explicit criteria. [31, 48] Similar to the Australian criteria, our tool consists of both implicit (criterion 18, 25, 37 and 39) and explicit criteria for assessing the appropriateness of medicines.

Our tool includes some unique criteria (Criteria 14, 18, 19 and 23, Table 4) that are not published in other criteria [15, 31, 49–52]. For instance, criterion 18 focuses on prescribing of gabapentinoids, where we emphasise rationalising their use for pain management in older adults in Sri Lanka [53], while criterion 19 provides guidance on dose adjustment of gabapeninoids for patients with reduced renal functions. The inclusion of gabapentinoid-related criteria reflects context-specific concerns in Sri Lanka, where multiple local reports across clinical disciplines highlight emerging misuse of pregabalin and gabapentin in routine practice [53–55]. Although largely based on clinical observations rather than large-scale studies, this consistent signal, together with the high burden of renal disease, supports the need for explicit guidance on appropriate use and renal dose adjustment in older adults [56, 57]. Additionally, another key feature of our tool is the inclusion of a contextual implementation guide, which is shown in Table 5. It facilitates the application of each criterion in the clinical setting by presenting relevant medicines or disease information, instructions for appropriate usage and a clinical rationale. Notably, the guidance is aligned with the medicines available across both the public and private healthcare sectors in Sri Lanka, which improves the applicability and the feasibility within routine practice (Table 5).

Strengths and Weaknesses

We used RAM, a modified Delphi approach, to validate the preliminary list of criteria [37]. RAM combines available evidence with multidisciplinary expert judgement and is particularly useful where high-quality evidence is limited [58]. It has demonstrated face and content validity and has been used previously to develop prescribing appropriateness criteria in geriatric pharmacotherapy [31, 51, 59]. The process included an initial e-mail survey followed by a face-to-face discussion, enabling panellists to clarify their views, resolve misinterpretations, consider new evidence and refine the criteria [31]. Further, we used the statistical method (IPRAS method) to determine agreement and disagreement rather than relying on the predefined classical method. The IPRAS method incorporates asymmetry adjustments, and it is recognised as an analytical method having high sensitivity and high specificity [37].

Because of practical limitations, we could not gather all expert panellists into a single face-to-face onsite interview but instead conducted small group online discussions. We actively facilitated discussion during these meetings. Our criteria only cover commonly occurring medical conditions and related medicines and therefore, will not capture all aspects of inappropriate medication use. A further limitation is the lack of locally available epidemiological data on neurological and psychiatric conditions, which required reliance on existing international prescribing criteria for selecting relevant medicines. This may have an impact on the relevance of these criteria to local disease patterns and prescribing priorities, particularly for neurological and psychiatric conditions. Future studies are required to assess the acceptability, feasibility, reliability and predictive validity of our criteria. Furthermore, we believe that this instrument should be continuously updated according to the updated clinical evidence, treatment guidelines and availability of medicines.

Conclusions

We have developed a validated prescribing appropriateness tool comprising 40 clinically relevant criteria that address prevalent chronic diseases among older Sri Lankan adults. The resulting tool is based on national and international guidelines and has been refined through expert consensus of Sri Lankan clinicians. This tool is intended to support structured medication review workflows, particularly during transitions of care, outpatient follow-up, and the review of high-risk medicines or complex polypharmacy, including pharmacist-led medication reviews. To support its use in busy clinical settings, future research should focus on evaluating its feasibility in real-world practice and exploring more streamlined approaches to implementation, including a pocket guide, mobile applications, or integration into electronic prescribing or clinical decision support systems.
