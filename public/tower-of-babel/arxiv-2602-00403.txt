DROGO: Default Representation Objective via Graph Optimization in Reinforcement Learning
Hon Tik Tse, Marlos C. Machado

Abstract
In computational reinforcement learning, the default representation (DR) and its principal eigenvector have been shown to be effective for a wide variety of applications, including reward shaping, count-based exploration, option discovery, and transfer. However, in prior investigations, the eigenvectors of the DR were computed by first approximating the DR matrix, and then performing an eigendecomposition. This procedure is computationally expensive and does not scale to high-dimensional spaces. In this paper, we derive an objective for directly approximating the principal eigenvector of the DR with a neural network. We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of the objective in a number of environments, and apply the learned eigenvectors for reward shaping.

2.1
Default Representation

We consider linearly solvable Markov decision processes
(
32
;
33

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},r,p,\pi_{d})
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}

r:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{<0}
is the reward function,
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition function, and
\pi_{d}
is the default policy. Let
\mathcal{S}_{T}\subset\mathcal{S}
be the set of terminal states. We assume
\mathcal{S}_{T}
is non-empty. We assume terminal states to be absorbing states and have a negative reward (see Appendix
A
for a detailed discussion).

Let
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{<0}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
be the vector of state rewards, and
\mathbf{P}^{\pi_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
be the transition probability matrix induced by
\pi_{d}
and
p
. The default representation
(
27
;
36
, DR;)
, defined with respect to
\pi_{d}
and denoted by
\mathbf{Z}
, is
\mathbf{Z}=[\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-\mathbf{r}/\lambda))-\mathbf{P}^{\pi_{d}}]^{-1}
, where
\lambda>0
is a hyperparameter. We use
\mathbf{R}
to denote
\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-\mathbf{r}/\lambda))
, and omit
\pi_{d}
as it is clear from context, so we write
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P})^{-1}
. In practice, we symmetrize the DR by replacing
\mathbf{P}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}=(\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}^{\top})/2
to ensure a real eigensystem.

The DR has been applied in a variety of settings. In particular, its principal eigenvector (corresponding to the largest eigenvalue),
\mathbf{e}
, has been used for reward shaping and option discovery.
However, the entries of
\mathbf{e}
can have very small magnitudes, limiting its practical use without additional transformations. For example, simply applying
\mathbf{e}
as a potential function for potential-based reward shaping
(
26
)
gives a shaping reward that is negligible almost everywhere.
To address this,
36
proved, for the discrete setting, that the principal eigenvector of the symmetrized DR is positive, and, in their experiments, used
\log\mathbf{e}
instead, where
\log
here denotes entry-wise logarithm.
Similarly, in this work,
we are interested in the logarithm of the principal eigenvector of the default representation
.

Graph Drawing Objective

Spectral graph drawing
(
15

These methods have been applied in RL since an MDP can be viewed as a graph, where the vertices are the states, and the edge weights are the transition probabilities between states induced by
p

\pi

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\pi}

(
41
;
38
;
12
)
. Specifically, we can retrieve the smallest eigenvector (corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue)

1

Note that this is equivalent to retrieving the principal eigenvector of the matrix’s inverse, as is the case for the DR.
of a matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
through the following objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=c,

(1)

where
\mathbf{u}

2.3
Natural Gradient

Natural gradient descent
(
3
;
2
)
improves convergence properties over standard gradient descent by adjusting the update direction to account for the underlying structure of the parameter space.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
be a parameter space,
and the optimization problem be

\min_{\mathbf{w}}\ell(\mathbf{w})
, where
\ell
is some loss function.
Standard gradient descent makes the simplifying assumption that the distance between two points in
\mathcal{P}
is Euclidean. The descent direction,
\delta\mathbf{w}
, is determined by the following optimization problem, where
\epsilon
denotes some small constant:

\displaystyle\min_{\delta\mathbf{w}}\ell(\mathbf{w})+\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w})^{\top}\delta\mathbf{w}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\delta\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}=\epsilon^{2}.

(2)

That is, we search for the descent direction
\delta\mathbf{w}
that locally minimizes
\ell
, under the constraint that the Euclidean distance between the updated and original parameters is small. The descent direction
\delta\mathbf{w}
is then proportional to
-\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w})
.

Natural gradient descent considers a more general notion of distance, where the distance between
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}+\delta\mathbf{w}
is given by
\sqrt{\delta\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{w})\delta\mathbf{w}}
.
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{w})
is known as the Riemannian metric tensor, which captures the local curvature of
\mathcal{P}
. Note that the Euclidean distance is a special case when
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{I}
. The descent direction is now determined by

\displaystyle\min_{\delta\mathbf{w}}\ell(\mathbf{w})+\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w})^{\top}\delta\mathbf{w}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \delta\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{w})\delta\mathbf{w}=\epsilon^{2}.

(3)

While we still constrain the distance between the updated and original parameters, the distance now reflects the local structure of the parameter space. The descent direction is then proportional to
-\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{w})^{-1}\nabla\ell(\mathbf{w})
.

3
DROGO

Prior work
(
36
)
adopts a computationally expensive procedure to obtain the logarithm of the principal eigenvector of the DR,
\log\mathbf{e}
. Such an approach does not scale to high-dimensional spaces.
To address this, we introduce an objective for directly learning, using only transitions, the logarithm of
\mathbf{e}
. We slowly build up our objective through theory developed in several sections: Section
3.1
describes adapting the GDO for the DR. Section
3.2
describes log-space parameterization to directly learn
\log\mathbf{e}
. Section
3.3
describes applying natural gradient to account for the log-space parameterization. Section
3.4
introduces an alternative constraint and provides theoretical justification. We conclude by introducing the final objective in Section
3.5
.

3.1
Graph Drawing Objective

Recall that we are interested in learning the
logarithm
of the principal eigenvector,
\log\mathbf{e}
, of the symmetrized DR,
(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
. A naive approach is to first learn
\mathbf{e}
using the GDO, and then apply the logarithm. This is a necessary but not sufficient step towards our final objective, as we discuss below.

To construct the GDO for the DR, we have:

Proposition 3.1
.

The principal eigenvector of
(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
is equivalent to the smallest eigenvector of
\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
.

Proof.

Since a matrix and its inverse share the same eigenvectors in reverse order, the principal eigenvector of
(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
is equal to the smallest eigenvector of
\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
. Furthermore, adding
\mathbf{I}
to
\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
only shifts all eigenvalues by 1, and does not affect the set of eigenvectors or their order.
∎

By Proposition
3.1
, for some constant
c
, we can retrieve
\mathbf{e}
by learning the smallest eigenvector of
\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
using GDO:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\mathbf{u}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=c.

(4)

By inspecting Eq.
4
, we gain intuition on what the principal eigenvector of the DR is in fact capturing. Note that
\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}
, where the first term is just the objective for learning an eigenvector of the Laplacian (see Eq.
1
), and the second term
\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}=\sum_{s}\exp(-r(s)/\lambda)u(s)^{2}
can be viewed as a regularization term. Recall that
r(s)
is negative. If a state
s
has a very negative reward,
\exp(-r(s)/\lambda)
is large, and
u(s)
is driven to have a small magnitude. The principal eigenvector of the DR can then be viewed as performing reward-aware regularization on top of an eigenvector of the Laplacian, where low-reward states are driven to have small magnitudes. Our empirical observations match this view.

As in common in prior work
(
41
;
38
;
12
)
, the constraint
\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}=c
can be turned into a quadratic penalty term, giving

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\mathbf{u}+b(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}-c)^{2},

(5)

where
b
is a hyperparameter. We denote the above objective function by
L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u})
.

We now derive the loss function for minimizing Eq.
5
.

Proposition 3.2
.

L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u})
can be written as

\displaystyle L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(s,s^{\prime})\big(u(s)-u(s^{\prime})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{s}\exp(-r(s)/\lambda)u(s)^{2}+b\left(\sum_{s}u(s)^{2}-c\right)^{2}.

(6)

Proof.

\displaystyle L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}+b(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}-c)^{2}.

(7)

It is known that the first term can be written as
(
15
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathbf{P}}(s,s^{\prime})\big(u(s)-u(s^{\prime})\big)^{2}.

(8)

Finally, we rewrite
\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{u}
as summations.
∎

Let
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be the function approximated by a neural network parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and
\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s_{1}),\dots,u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s_{|\mathcal{S}|})]^{\top}
be the vector predicted by the neural network. We have:

Proposition 3.3
.

Given a transition
(s,a,r,s^{\prime})
and an extra state
s^{\prime\prime}
, where
s
and
s^{\prime\prime}
are sampled independently and uniformly at random, and
a
is sampled under the default policy,
\pi_{d}
, we can minimize
L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
by minimizing

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{GDO}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}\big(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)-u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s^{\prime})\big)^{2}+\exp(-r/\lambda)u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +b^{\prime}(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)^{2}-c^{\prime})(u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s^{\prime\prime})^{2}-c^{\prime}),

(9)

where
b^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}
can be treated as hyperparameters.

Proof sketch.
We express Eq.
6
in terms of expectations.
\ell_{\text{GDO}}
is simply a sample-based approximation of the expectations. See Appendix
B.1
for full proof.

We can compute
\ell_{\text{GDO}}
using sampled transitions, then perform backpropagation and gradient descent to update
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. However, recall that some entries of
\mathbf{e}
can have very small magnitudes. By optimizing Eq.
9
, we observe that even small approximation errors can cause the neural network’s prediction,
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)
, to be negative for some low-reward states. This prohibits us from taking the logarithm and using the learned eigenvector in practice.
While increasing
\lambda
can reduce the strength of the reward-aware regularization, extremely large
\lambda
s are required to fully address this problem. However, this comes at the expense of the reward-awareness of
\mathbf{e}
, as
\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
approaches the Laplacian,
\mathbf{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
, as
\lambda\to\infty
.

3.2
Log-Space Parameterization

To guarantee that the learned eigenvector is always positive, while ensuring that it retains certain levels of reward-awareness, we perform log-space parameterization. Specifically, we directly approximate
\log\mathbf{e}
using a neural network.
First, Eq.
5
can be adapted as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}\exp(\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\exp(\mathbf{v})+

\displaystyle b(\exp(\mathbf{v})^{\top}\exp(\mathbf{v})-c)^{2},

(10)

where
\exp
here denotes element-wise exponentiation.
We denote the above objective function by
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v})
. Note that
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v})=L_{\text{GDO}}(\exp(\mathbf{v}))
.

Let
v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
be the function approximated by a neural network parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
. We define
\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
similar to how we defined
\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and define
\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=\exp(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
to be the element-wise exponentiation of
\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
.
Building on Propositions
3.2
and
3.3
:

Proposition 3.4
.

Given a transition
(s,a,r,s^{\prime})
and an extra state
s^{\prime\prime}
, where
s
and
s^{\prime\prime}
are sampled independently and uniformly at random, and
a
is sampled under the default policy,
\pi_{d}
, we can minimize
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
by minimizing

\displaystyle\ell^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}({\boldsymbol{\phi}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(\exp(v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s))-\exp(v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s^{\prime}))\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ +b(\exp(2v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s))-c)(\exp(2v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s^{\prime\prime}))-c)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\exp(2v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s)-r/\lambda).

(11)

Proof.

We replace
u_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)
with
\exp(v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s))
in Eq.
9
.
∎

While it is possible to just approximate
{\ell}^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}
and perform backpropagation and gradient descent to update
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, we perform further analysis on the gradient of
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
. By comparing the gradient of
L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
, we reveal a source of instability for the loss function,
\ell^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}
.

Using the multivariate chain rule, the gradient of
L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
with respect to the
i
-th entry of the parameters,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},

(12)

while the gradient of
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
w.r.t. the
i
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})}{\partial\phi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi_{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})}{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi_{i}}\cdot\left(\exp(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\odot\frac{\partial L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi_{i}}\cdot\left(\exp(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\odot\frac{\partial L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\right)

(15)

where
\odot
is the Hadamard product. Comparing Eq.
12
and
15
, we see that the log-space parameterization introduces an extra multiplicative exponential term
\exp(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
, which scales exponentially with the network output, and can lead to instability in the optimization process. Next, we apply natural gradient to account for this exponential term.

3.3
Natural Gradient

When performing log-space parameterization, we essentially move from solving
\min_{\mathbf{u}}L(\mathbf{u})
to solving
\min_{\mathbf{v}}L(\exp(\mathbf{v}))
, and now we have
\mathbf{v}
as parameters of the optimization problem instead of
\mathbf{u}
. When performing standard gradient descent, we are implicitly assuming that the difference between the original and updated parameters is measured by their Euclidean distance, or equivalently, the size of the gradient step is measured by its Euclidean norm.
While this assumption generally works without issues, assuming the Euclidean distance can be undesirable when we perform log-space parameterization.
Suppose we take a gradient step with a tiny Euclidean norm.
When
\mathbf{v}
has large values, the gradient step causes massive changes in
\exp(\mathbf{v})
, and thus,
L(\exp(\mathbf{v}))
. When
\mathbf{v}
has small values, the same gradient step causes negligible changes.
This is the reason we observe the exponential term,
\exp(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
, in Eq.
15
. To summarize, the
\mathbf{v}
-space’s local geometry is inherently different from that of the
\mathbf{u}
-space.

We can address this problem with natural gradient, by explicitly accounting for the difference in the geometry of the
\mathbf{v}
-space. To do so, we need to identify the distance in the
\mathbf{v}
-space induced by: (1) the relationship between the
\mathbf{v}
- and
\mathbf{u}
-spaces, and (2) the Euclidean assumption on the
\mathbf{u}
-space (see Figure
4
, in the Appendix, for a visual illustration).

Proposition 3.5
.

The distance in the
\mathbf{v}
-space is characterized by the Riemannian metric tensor
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{diag}(\exp(2\mathbf{v}))
.

Proof sketch.
Let
\delta\mathbf{u}
be the change induced in
\mathbf{u}
by
\delta\mathbf{v}
, a small step in the
\mathbf{v}
-space. We identify
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v})
by solving

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{u})^{2}=d_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}+\delta\mathbf{v})^{2},

(16)

where
d_{\mathcal{U}}
and
d_{\mathcal{V}}
are distances in the
\mathbf{u}
- and
\mathbf{v}
-spaces respectively. We provide the full proof in Appendix
B.2
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{v}}
be the standard gradient descent direction. The adjusted natural gradient descent direction is simply
-\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v})^{-1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{v}}=-\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-2\mathbf{v}))\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{v}}
.

We apply the natural gradient to minimize
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
. Incorporating the Riemannian metric tensor to Eq.
15
, the gradient of
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
w.r.t. the
i
-th entry of the parameters,
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, is

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})^{-1}\left(\exp(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\odot\frac{\partial L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{\partial\phi_{i}}\cdot\left(\exp(-\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\odot\frac{\partial L_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})}{\partial\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}\right).

(18)

Note that Eq.
15
and
18
differ only by the sign inside the exponential. Now,
\exp(-\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
ensures that updates to
\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
produce similar effects on
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
regardless of the value
\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
takes. For example, if
v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s)
is very large, for which even a tiny update can drastically change
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
,
\exp(-\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
scales the update to
v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s)
by
1/\exp(v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s))
.

Using the above equation, we now derive a new loss function that incorporates the natural gradient. Note that Eq.
18
is the dot product of two terms, where the first term is the gradient of the neural network’s output w.r.t. its parameters, and the second term is the natural gradient of the objective function,
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
, with respect to the neural network’s output. We first present two propositions for the second term:

Proposition 3.7
.

The natural gradient of
L^{\log}_{\text{GDO}}(\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})
with respect to the output of the neural network parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
, is proportional to

\displaystyle\exp(-\mathbf{r}/\lambda)-\exp(-\mathbf{v}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\odot\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}+b^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}-c^{\prime})\mathbf{1}.

(19)

Proof.
See Appendix
B.3
.

\square

Proposition 3.8
.

The

\displaystyle\exp(-r/\lambda)-\exp(v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s^{\prime})-v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s))

\displaystyle+b^{\prime\prime}(\exp(2v_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s^{\prime\prime}))-c^{\prime\prime}),

where

3.4
Alternative Constraint on the Learned Vector

Despite being difficult to balance, the quadratic norm penalty prevents the learned eigenvector from collapsing to the zero vector. An alternative way of seeing this is to imagine that, without the quadratic norm penalty, a learned vector perfectly aligned with the ground-truth can be expressed as
\alpha\mathbf{e}
for some
\alpha
, and
in this case the objective function is minimized when
\alpha\to 0
. We still have the same issue when learning
\log\mathbf{e}
directly instead of
\mathbf{e}
, as multiplying
\mathbf{e}
by a positive constant
\alpha
would be equivalent to adding the constant
\log\alpha
to all entries of
\mathbf{e}
. When
\alpha\to 0
, all entries of the solution keep drifting to the left on the real number line.

Thus, to drop the quadratic norm penalty, we need to prevent such constant shifts. We do so by fixing the value of a particular entry of the learned vector. In particular, we fix the value of a selected terminal state,
v(s_{T})
, to 0. Intuitively,
v(s_{T})
acts as an anchor that holds the entire vector
\mathbf{v}
in place.
Mathematically, for a terminal state
s_{T}\in\mathcal{S}_{T}
, we solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}\exp(\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\exp(\mathbf{v})\ \text{s.t.}\ v(s_{T})=0.

(22)

While Eq.
22
might make intuitive sense, it is in fact not trivial to show that by minimizing this optimization problem instead, we still approximate the smallest eigenvector of
\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}}

22
, and present:

Proposition 3.10
.

Minimizing Eq.
22
retrieves the logarithm of the column of the DR corresponding to the selected terminal state,
s_{T}
.

Proof.

Using Lagrange multipliers, Eq.
22
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}\max_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\exp(\mathbf{v})^{\top}(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\exp(\mathbf{v})+\mu v(s_{T}).

(23)

Let
\mathbf{b}_{i}
be the standard basis vector with a 1 at entry
i
. Taking the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{v}
and setting it to 0, we have

\displaystyle 2\exp(\mathbf{v})\odot(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\exp(\mathbf{v})+\mu\mathbf{b}_{s_{T}}=0

(24)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})\exp(\mathbf{v})=-\frac{\mu}{2}\exp(-v(s_{T}))\mathbf{b}_{s_{T}}

(25)

\displaystyle\exp(\mathbf{v})=-\frac{\mu}{2}(\mathbf{R}-\tilde{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\mathbf{b}_{s_{T}}

(26)

where

∎

Theoretical analysis reveals that by solving Eq.

s_{T}

Let

Experiments

We verify empirically that optimizing the DROGO objective retrieves the principal eigenvector of the DR. We present an ablation study in Appendix
C.3

4.1
Learning the Principal Eigenvector of the DR

We apply DROGO to approximate the principal eigenvector of the DR using neural networks. To be able to validate our results against the ground-truth, we consider the set of grid world environments used by
36
(see Appendix
C.1
). A neural network takes as input a state, and outputs the logarithm of the corresponding entry in the principal eigenvector.

We generate 200,000 transitions using a uniform random initial state distribution and the default policy, which we choose to be the uniform random policy. We then train the neural networks by minimizing the DROGO loss (Eq.
27
) using mini-batches from the dataset. To demonstrate the robustness of DROGO, we use three different representations of states as input to the neural network: (1) one-hot encoding, where state
s_{i}
is represented as
\mathbf{b}_{i}
, (2)
(x,y)
-coordinates, and (3) pixels. See details in Appendix
C.2
.

To evaluate the quality of the approximation, we compute the cosine similarity between the learned vector and the ground-truth. The cosine similarity is computed between the logarithm of these eigenvectors.
Fig.
2
shows the cosine similarity in four grid world environments for the three state representations. We can see that regardless of the choice of the state representation, DROGO enables approximating the principal eigenvector very well, as the cosine similarity converges to near 1 for all settings. A visual comparison of the learned and ground-truth eigenvectors is in Appendix
C.2
.

4.2
Reward Shaping

While we demonstrate that the cosine similarity between the learned and ground-truth principal eigenvectors is close to 1, this metric does not fully reflect the performance of using these learned eigenvectors. To demonstrate the practical utility of the learned eigenvectors, we perform reward shaping.

In reward shaping, we provide the agent with a shaping reward to aid in learning the optimal policy. We apply the principal eigenvector of the DR for potential-based reward shaping
(
26
)
. Let
\mathbf{v}
be the learned eigenvector, the shaping reward is
\tilde{r}_{t}=\gamma v(s_{t+1})-v(s_{t})
, where
\gamma
is the discount factor. Then, we train a Q-learning
(
39
)
agent using a convex combination of the original environment reward and the shaping reward, resulting in
(1-\beta)r_{t}+\beta\tilde{r}_{t}
, where
\beta\in[0,1]
is a hyperparameter.

We perform reward shaping using the principal eigenvector of the DR in separate experiments for each state representation. We compare against using the principal eigenvector of the SR
(
9
)
, and no reward shaping (plain Q-learning). Applying the principal eigenvectors of the SR and DR for reward shaping is common in RL
(
41
;
38
;
36
)
. We compute the principal eigenvector of the SR in closed form to prevent approximation errors from hurting the performance of the baseline, effectively giving it an advantage. Similar to
36

\gamma=0.99

\epsilon=0.05
for
\epsilon

\beta
(
[0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0]
), and Q-learning’s step size (
[0.1,0.3,1.0]
).

We perform reward shaping in the same environments of Section
4.1
. In these environments, the agent is tasked with reaching the goal state while avoiding low-reward regions on the way.
To measure the effectiveness of different shaping rewards, we evaluate the mean number of steps required to converge to the optimal return (
N_{\text{OPT}}
). As the DR has been shown to give rise to reward-aware behavior, we also measure the mean number of visits to low-reward states during training (
N_{\text{VISIT}}
). The lower
N_{\text{OPT}}
, the more effective the shaping reward. The lower
N_{\text{VISIT}}

We observe that when using the DR with pixels representation for reward shaping in four rooms, there are a small number of seeds with slower learning speed due to small approximation errors in the principal eigenvector, leading to larger

On the other hand, using the DR for reward shaping leads to similar or lower

In general, the DR performs better than the SR. This is because, unlike the SR, the DR captures reward information in the principal eigenvector. During reward shaping, the reward-aware principal eigenvector causes the agent to prefer high-reward paths in the environment, eventually leading to lower

5
Related Work

The SR
(
9
)
represents a state by the expected discounted number of visits to successor states. Specifically, it counts all visits to successor states. The first-occupancy representation
(
24
, FR;)
is a variant that captures only the first visit to successor states. The
\lambda
representation
(
23
,
\lambda
R;)
can be viewed as an interpolation between the SR and FR. Compared to the FR and
\lambda
R, the DR
(
27
)
generalizes the SR in an orthogonal direction by capturing not just the transitions, but also the rewards in the environment.

The SR and its extension in continuous spaces, the successor measure
(
6
)
, have given rise to a wide range of methods for transfer. Such works include successor features
(
5
;
4
;
7
)
, forward-backward representation
(
34
;
35
;
31
)
, proto-successor measure
(
1
)
, and distributional successor measure
(
40
)
.
The DR, unlike the SR, also captures the reward function. Being more specific to environment dynamics comes at the price of less flexible transfer. Without assuming access to environment dynamics, it can be used to directly compute the optimal policy when only rewards at terminal states change
(
36
)
.

Apart from transfer, where the goal is to efficiently compute the optimal policy for downstream rewards, the SR has been applied to better learn from a single reward function. The SR has the same set of eigenvectors as the Laplacian
(
19
;
29
)
, which was shown to be a good basis for approximating the value function
(
21
;
20
)
. As they reflect the geometry of the state space, they have been subsequently applied as a distance metric for reward shaping
(
41
;
38
;
37
)
, an inductive bias for temporally-extended exploration
(
17
;
14
)
, effective state representations
(
16
;
10
)
, and a density model for count-based exploration
(
18
)
.
Given the close connection between the SR and DR,
36
applied the principal eigenvector of the DR to similar applications, and found that the DR gave rise to better performance than the SR due to its reward-aware nature.

The graph-drawing objective
(
15
, GDO;)
has been applied to learn the eigenvectors of the SR (or Laplacian) in RL
(
41
)
. Extensions address problems with the GDO such as arbitrary rotations of the learned eigenvectors
(
38
;
12
)
. However, similar methods do not exist for the DR. DROGO is the first to allow direct learning of the principal eigenvector of the DR.

Apart from its application in computational RL, since the SR can be viewed as a middle ground between model-free and model-based decision making, it has received interest in behavioral and neuroscience studies
(
22
;
29
;
30
;
11
)
. The DR was proposed originally in neuroscience to address, among others, the inflexibility of the SR in replanning tasks
(
27
)
, and has been extended recently to incorporate compositionality
(
28
)
.
Different from these works, we focus solely on computational RL.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present DROGO, which optimizes a variant of the GDO with log-space parameterization and a point-wise constraint using natural gradient, and provide theoretical justification. Empirically, we demonstrate the effectiveness of DROGO in a number of environments with different state representations. We further demonstrate the utility of the learned eigenvectors by performing reward shaping.

In this work, we run our experiments in grid world environments to be able to compare the eigenvectors learned using DROGO against the ground-truth computed in closed form by eigendecomposition.
In the future, it will be interesting to apply the learned eigenvectors for downstream applications, similar to how
14
;
8
used GDO
(
41
;
12
)
for exploration and generalization in RL.

In our reward shaping experiments, we follow a two-stage approach, where we first learn the principal eigenvector before applying it for reward shaping. Such an approach serves our purpose in this paper: to demonstrate the utility of the approximated eigenvectors. However, as
36
demonstrated the utility of the online approximation of the principal eigenvector of the DR in their option discovery experiments, future work can adapt DROGO to learn both the principal eigenvector and the policy simultaneously.

Given the recent prominence of Laplacian-/SR-based research in RL, DROGO unlocks promising new directions by scaling up a reward-aware generalization of the SR.

Appendix A
An Alternative DR Formulation

Figure 3
:

The principal eigenvector of the DR for (top) not treating terminal states as absorbing states
(

C.1
for a detailed explanation of the environments.

We formulate the DR slightly differently than prior work
(
36
)
. Specifically, we handle a terminal state differently. Let
N,T
be the set of indices for non-terminal states and terminal states respectively. For example,
\mathbf{P}_{NN}
denotes the sub-matrix of
\mathbf{P}
that describes the transition probabilities from non-terminal states to non-terminal states. Recall that the DR is defined as

\displaystyle(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P})^{-1}=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-\mathbf{r}_{N}/\lambda))&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-\mathbf{r}_{T}/\lambda))\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}_{NN}&\mathbf{P}_{NT}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{P}_{TT}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{-1}.

(29)

36
allowed terminal states to emit any rewards at the last time step, but used 0 for most of their experiments, which is standard in RL. When the terminal state reward is 0, if we treat terminal states as absorbing states, i.e., transitioning to themselves with probability 1, then both
\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-\mathbf{r}_{T}/\lambda))
and
\mathbf{P}_{TT}
are equal to
\mathbf{I}
, and the DR is not well defined. To address this issue,
36
considered terminal states as states that transition to a unique absorbing state with probability 1, and they removed this absorbing state from the DR matrix.

While this solution worked well for the experiments
36
considered, where they used a small
\lambda=1.3
, we find that the DR formulated in this way is not robust to larger values of
\lambda
. Specifically, as shown on the top row of Fig.
3
, we find that as
\lambda
increases, the principal eigenvector loses the property of having the largest value at the terminal state. A consequence is that, when we use such a principal eigenvector in applications such as reward shaping, the shaping reward encourages the agent to visit states other than the terminal states.

We find that we can handle terminal states differently such that we retain this property. Specifically, we treat terminal states as absorbing states, so
\mathbf{P}_{TT}=\mathbf{I}
. Then, to ensure that the DR is well defined, we require terminal states to have a reward of
-\delta
, where
\delta>0
is a tiny positive constant. We use
\delta=0.001
in our experiments. Using this formulation of the DR, as shown on the bottom row of Fig.
3
, the principal eigenvector is the largest at the terminal state even when
\lambda
is large.

Note that the theorems proved by
36
can be easily adapted to this new formulation of the DR. Specifically, the symmetrized DR under the new formulation is still guaranteed to have a positive principal eigenvector. Finally, note that such formulations of the terminal states are merely part of the algorithm for computing the DR, and do not alter the underlying MDP. When evaluating the agent, for example, we still treat the environment as an episodic task that terminates with a 0 reward.

Proof of Proposition
3.5

Proposition 3.5
.

The distance in the
\mathbf{v}
-space is characterized by the Riemannian metric tensor

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{diag}(\exp(2\mathbf{v})).

(36)

Proof.

Suppose we have an optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}L(\mathbf{u}),

(37)

where
L
is an objective function, and
\mathcal{U}
is the parameter space for the optimization problem.
We assume the Euclidean distance for
\mathcal{U}
. That is, the distance between two points
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
is computed as
d_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2})=\sqrt{(\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2})^{\top}(\mathbf{u}_{1}-\mathbf{u}_{2})}
. Let
\delta\mathbf{u}
be a gradient step. The distance between the updated and the original parameters is
d_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{u})=\sqrt{\delta\mathbf{u}^{\top}\delta\mathbf{u}}=\|\delta\mathbf{u}\|_{2}
, which is just the Euclidean norm of the gradient step.

We can re-parameterize and optimize the logarithm of
\mathbf{u}
instead. We now have the following:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}}L(\exp(\mathbf{v})),

(38)

where
\exp
here denotes element-wise exponentiation, and
\mathcal{V}
is the parameter space for this new optimization problem.

\delta\mathbf{u}
be the change induced in
\mathbf{u}
by
\delta\mathbf{v}
, which is a small change in
\mathbf{v}
. That is,
\delta\mathbf{u}=\exp(\mathbf{v}+\delta\mathbf{v})-\exp(\mathbf{v})
.
Given 1) the relationship
\mathbf{u}=\exp(\mathbf{v})
, and 2) the Euclidean distance for
\mathcal{U}
, the Riemannian metric tensor,
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v})
, can be found by solving the following equation for small
\delta\mathbf{v}

(
2
)
:

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{u})=d_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}+\delta\mathbf{v})=\delta\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{v})\delta\mathbf{v},

(39)

where
d_{\mathcal{V}}
is the corresponding distance in the parameter space
\mathcal{V}
. See Fig.
4
for a visual illustration.

Figure 4
:

d_{\mathcal{U}}
denotes the distance in the
\mathbf{u}
-space, which is assumed to be Euclidean. We aim to find the induced distance in the
\mathbf{v}
-space, denoted by
d_{\mathcal{V}}

Starting from the left-hand side, we have

\displaystyle d^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}+\delta\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=d^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}(\exp(\mathbf{v}),\exp(\mathbf{v}+\delta\mathbf{v}))

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}[\exp(v(s)+\delta v(s))-\exp(v(s))]^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}[\exp(v(s))(\exp(\delta v(s))-1)]^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}[\exp(v(s))(1+\delta v(s)+O(\delta v(s)^{2})-1)]^{2}\quad\quad\text{(Taylor expansion)}

B.6
Proof of Theorem
3.11

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
r(s_{T})=-\delta\ \forall s_{T}\in\mathcal{S}_{T}
, where
\delta>0
. When
\delta\to 0^{+}
, the columns of the DR (
(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P})^{-1}
) corresponding to all terminal states form a basis for the eigenspace of the principal eigenvalue of the DR.

Proof.

Recall that the (non-symmetrized) DR is
(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P})^{-1}=(\operatorname{diag}(\exp(-\mathbf{r}/\lambda)-\mathbf{P})^{-1}
. We assume without loss of generality that
\lambda=1
.
Let
n=|\mathcal{S}|
and
m=|\mathcal{S}_{T}|
.
Suppose we order the states such that the
m
terminal states are ordered last, i.e., they correspond to the last columns of the DR.
Then,
\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix},
for some matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
,
and
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}&-\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{0}&(\exp(\delta)-1)\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}
for some matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
.
When
\delta\to 0^{+}
,
\exp(\delta)-1\approx\delta
, so we write
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
as
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}&-\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{0}&\delta\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}
.

It is easy to see that
\delta
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
with algebraic multiplicity
m
. Note that
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
might have complex eigenvalues, and we order eigenvalues by their magnitudes.
Using the Gershgorin circle theorem, we can show that all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
have non-zero magnitude. It is obvious that for the rows corresponding to terminal states, the Gershgorin discs are centered at
\delta
with radius 0. For a row
i
corresponding to a non-terminal state, the Gershgorin disc is centered at
\exp(-r(s_{i}))-P(i,i)
. Since we assume
r(s)<0\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}
,
\exp(-r(s_{i}))-P(i,i)>1-P(i,i)=\sum_{j\neq i}P(i,j)
, where
\sum_{j\neq i}P(i,j)
is the radius of the disc. Thus, any discs corresponding to non-terminal states do not contain
0
. Then, all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
must have magnitudes greater than
0
.
Since
\delta
can be arbitrarily small,
\delta
is the eigenvalue of
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
with the smallest magnitude, and thus the principal eigenvalue of the DR,
(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P})^{-1}
.

We now study the principal eigenvectors of the DR, which are equal to the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{P}
corresponding to
\delta
. An eigenvector corresponding to
\delta
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}&-\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{0}&\delta\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix}=\delta\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix},

(71)

which gives the equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathbf{x}

(72)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\delta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}

(73)

An eigenvector corresponding to
\delta
then has the general form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\delta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{bmatrix},

(74)

where
\mathbf{y}
can be any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Therefore, the geometric multiplicity of
\delta
is also
m
.

We now derive the form of the columns of the DR corresponding to terminal states. By block-wise matrix inversion, the DR is

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{\prime}&-\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{0}&\delta\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})^{-1}&\frac{1}{\delta}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}.

(75)

Then, the columns corresponding to the terminal states have the form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\delta}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{b}_{j}\\
\frac{1}{\delta}\mathbf{b}_{j},\end{bmatrix}

(76)

where
\mathbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the standard basis vector with a
1
at entry
i
, and
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Comparing Eq.
74
and
76
, we can see that as
\delta\to 0^{+}
, these linearly independent columns of the DR span the same space as the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue
\delta
. Thus, they form a basis for the eigenspace of the principal eigenvalue of the DR.
∎
