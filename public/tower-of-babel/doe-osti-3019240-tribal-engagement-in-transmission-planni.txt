1 Introduction
The purpose of this report is twofold: to provide Tribes with information that helps assess
whether transmission access is important for their energy goals and to provide guidance on
options to engage in transmission planning, if and when doing so has the potential to help the
Tribe achieve its energy goals. Some of the information here might also help non-Tribal
stakeholders in making their engagement with Tribes more productive.

The transmission system moves electricity in bulk from large generators to large distribution
points. It connects to (but is different from) the lower-voltage distribution system, which delivers
electricity to homes, shops, and other end-use customers. Wholesale power transactions,
including large-scale generation and the bulk transfer of electricity between regions, occur on the
transmission system. This white paper deals with Tribes’ ability to use the transmission system
to further their own energy goals. Table 1 summarizes the strategies discussed in this white
paper.

The U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Office of Indian Energy (OIE) notes “Indian Country
contains vast untapped energy resources” (OIE 2023). Individual Tribes express a wide array of

energy needs and goals, ranging from simple access to basic electricity service and energy

Approximately 2.3% of the nation’s transmission miles are on Tribal lands (OIE 2023). Even

Some of the Tribes with the largest land area have little transmission and some such as the
Navajo Nation and the Hopi Tribe have many homes with no access to electricity.

1.1 Energy Sovereignty
The phrase “energy sovereignty” often arises in discussions about energy development with
Tribes (Energy Sovereignty Institute 2025; Midwest Tribal Energy Resources Association
[MTERA] 2025). Tribes differ from utilities and utility stakeholders in that they are sovereign
entities, and this can affect how a Tribe addresses issues related to electricity and transmission.
Strictly speaking, a sovereign entity such as a Tribe is not the same as a “stakeholder” despite
whatever interest it might have in planning outcomes. Its legal position places it on par with a
state government and creates a special relationship between it and the federal government.

Energy’s role in the welfare of a Tribe depends on many things that vary from one Tribe to
another, and “energy sovereignty” refers to the right of a Tribe to decide for itself how to
reconcile these factors toward its own public interest. Although the term lacks its own legal
definition, it is useful to think of energy sovereignty as a sectoral application of the more general
principle of Tribal sovereignty as expressed in the U.S. Constitution and affirmed in numerous
Supreme Court decisions. The Constitution’s Commerce Clause (Article I, Section 8) gives
Congress the power “to regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, among the several States, and
with the Indian Tribes,” and placing Tribes on the same footing as “foreign Nations” establishes
Tribal sovereignty under U.S. law (Gorsuch 2023). 1 This means generally that:

 • Tribes retain sovereignty over their internal affairs.
 • Tribes are free from state jurisdiction.
 • The federal government (not state or local government) regulates commerce between the
 United States and Tribes within the bounds of powers enumerated in the Constitution.
Energy sovereignty has implications for siting generation and transmission, the taxation of
energy development on Tribal land, the role of Tribal utilities and other Tribal energy
enterprises, terms and conditions for outside energy companies doing business with Tribes, and
other commercial matters between Tribal and non-Tribal entities. Federal siting and permitting
has been perhaps the most contentious area of Tribal energy sovereignty.

Although a Tribe might have the sovereign right to determine its own policies and protect its
own land, that right does not change the ability of a non-Tribal counterparty to walk away from a
deal. Nor does it empower a Tribe to require the utility to take actions elsewhere that do not meet
standards for just and reasonable costs, standards decided by the utility’s regulators. For
example, transmission congestion on other parts of the network could affect the cost or
availability of electricity for the reservation, or it might limit the Tribe’s ability to export
electricity to the regional market. Transmission to alleviate congestion must satisfy federal and
state criteria, and Tribal sovereignty may not set these requirements aside.

A Tribe’s ability to act on its energy sovereignty depends on its own institutional capacity. For
most Tribes, these capabilities have grown considerably over recent decades; consequently, a
deal struck 50 years ago might be viewed differently today. For example, when it voted to renew
a site lease for a coal power plant in 2017, the Navajo Nation obtained (among other
concessions) provisions to reserve for the Tribe 500 MW of transfer capacity on the transmission

1.2 Two Regulatory Environments for Transmission
If transmission is important to a Tribe’s energy goals, the first step is to understand which of two
regulatory environments the local transmission utility operates in: RTOs or non-RTO regions. 2
Figure 1 shows where RTOs exist in the United States. National transmission analyses such as
the 2023 Needs Study (GDO 2023) and the National Transmission Planning (NTP) Study (GDO
2024) generally follow RTO footprints in their regional breakdowns; Figure 2 shows where
Tribal lands exist within these transmission planning regions.

For Tribes, a major difference between these two environments is that RTOs are regulated by the
federal government with limited control by individual states. 3 The SPP, MISO, ISO-NE, and
PJM Interconnection are all multistate RTOs under the jurisdiction of the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission (FERC). CAISO and NYISO both operate largely within a single state,
so California and New York have a greater degree of control over the policies affecting
transmission demand and usage—even though FERC remains the final regulatory authority over
the open access transmission tariff (OATT).

The main feature of an RTO is that it combines operations across several transmission-owning
utilities, with the aim of achieving greater efficiency and lower costs. The important functions
are:

 • A day-ahead energy market, which matches available generation with forecasted demand
 for every hour of the next day on a least-cost basis.
 • A real-time energy balancing market, which procures additional generation every 5 or 15
 minutes to reduce the gap between actual demand and actual generation (also procured on
 a least-cost basis).
 • Operating reserves, used to manage frequency and to ensure the grid can keep operating
 in the event of a sudden outage or swing in demand.
 • Resource adequacy, which are measures to ensure that the system has enough resources
 to meet expected demand at all times over the next 10 to 20 years.
Combining these functions across several utility areas can often reduce the cost of each.

Much of the western United States is outside an RTO, but this is changing. CAISO and SPP have
both received approval from FERC to expand their day-ahead and real-time energy markets into

markets for electricity, which could improve a Tribe’s ability to successfully develop and sell
energy resources developed on its lands. Access to the transmission planning process is more
open and transparent in an RTO, but it can also be technically complicated as discussed later in
this report.

1.3 Tribes and Generator Interconnection
Any developer—including a Tribal enterprise—that intends to build a new generator in an RTO
must first get its project into the RTO’s generator interconnection queue. 6 Once the project is
there, the RTO studies the proposed project, the effect the project would have on other parts of
the grid, and the transmission enhancements that would be needed to accommodate the project
while maintaining overall reliably. Some of the transmission enhancement costs are assigned to
the affected transmission owners and included in their general rates, whereas some costs are
assigned directly to the new generator.

Managing the interconnection queue has been a long-standing challenge for RTOs. If a developer
withdraws a project from the queue, there is a ripple effect that compromises all other
interconnection studies that have included the withdrawn project. In 2023, FERC issued Order
2023, which was intended to remedy delays in the interconnection queue (FERC 2023). FERC
found the interconnection rules it issued more than 20 years ago did not sufficiently account for
the characteristics of new technologies with shorter construction cycles, such as natural gas
combustion turbines, wind, solar, and battery storage. These technologies account for more than
75% of generation added in the country over the past decade (U.S. Energy Information
Administration [EIA] 2025a). FERC found, among other things, the old rules created an
incentive for speculative interconnection requests, whereby a developer would secure a position
in the queue before the project was certain. Speculative projects overloaded the study queue,
delayed projects that were not speculative, and ultimately caused additional costs that were
unjust and unreasonable (FERC 2023).

Order 2023 increased financial commitments for generators to enter the interconnection queue,
including larger study deposits, tougher site control requirements, and commercial readiness
deposits. The rule also imposes penalties on parties that withdraw their requests from the
interconnection queue (FERC 2023).

Many Tribes found the new regulation’s financial requirements and penalties disproportionately
burdensome, however. Among other issues, Tribes say raising capital is unusually difficult for
them due to federal ownership of Indian trust land, which affects their ability to use the land as
collateral. They also point to a dearth of lending institutions that have experience dealing with

1.4 Policy Issues Affecting Tribal Energy Goals
External energy policies affect transmission planning. Because of the grid’s natural
interdependence, a Tribe’s ability to achieve its own energy goals could depend on navigating
other federal, state, or utility energy policies.

Policies do not operate in isolation from economics, however, nor do they negate reliability
requirements. Economics and reliability can therefore be important elements of a Tribe’s strategy
to secure its energy goals in the milieu of other external policies.

A Tribe’s energy goals may include the following:

 • Improving the Tribe’s access to reliable electricity service
 • Protecting culturally sensitive Tribal lands
 • Economic development.
These goals exist against a backdrop of increasing system demand. The growth of data centers,
which use large amounts of electricity, is driving an increase in electricity demand in several
parts of the country. Load growth creates opportunities for new generation and for hosting large
new commercial customers, but a Tribe’s ability to compete for both depends on its access to
transmission. The 2023 Needs Study, the NTP Study, and numerous industry studies document
growing electricity demand and the role of transmission in meeting the demand cost-effectively.

Besides commercial demand growth, some Tribes are relatively isolated and face challenges
accessing basic retail electricity service. The Navajo Nation and the Hopi Tribe include large
remote areas where many households have no access to electricity from the grid. Elsewhere, low
incomes and higher electricity rates generally add to an Indian household’s energy burden. The
Office of Indian Energy found in 2023 that energy costs as a share of annual income were 28.3%
higher for households on Indian land than they were for the rest of the country (OIE 2023). This
is especially true where the area is served by lower-voltage transmission lines that have higher
electricity losses per mile.

The evolution of wholesale electricity markets—mostly through RTOs—creates more business
models for Tribes. These models range from simple land lease agreements to full ownership
through a Tribal utility or Tribal business enterprise. A more sophisticated market provides more
opportunities for a Tribe to navigate external energy policies.

1.5 Reliability and Resilience
After “Winter Storm Uri” caused severe grid failures in Texas and South Central states in 2021,
planners increased the importance of resilience as a criterion for the value of new transmission.
Such storm-related impacts affect all electricity customers in the affected region, but a Tribe
could be more vulnerable to outages if its connecting transmission infrastructure is inadequate to
support reliability or to handle extreme events.

Reliability refers to the ability of the grid to deliver electricity to customers consistently,
predictably, and with minimal fluctuations in quality (North American Electric Reliability
Corporation [NERC] 2025). Resilience refers to the ability of the grid to recover from disruptive

 14

events such as hurricanes and polar vortex events (DOE 2025). Both reliability and resilience are
related to the quality of transmission access.

Transmission operators today have better tools to simulate the potential for grid failure. These
models have a high degree of locational specificity, which means planners can often discern
whether electricity customers living on a reservation are more vulnerable to blackouts than
customers elsewhere on the system.

Early engagement in transmission planning (and in utility studies of reliability and resilience)
can help increase a Tribe’s chances of mitigating risks related to reliability and major grid
disruptions. Mitigation by the utility often involves evaluating several options for new
transmission, and these options are usually identified early in a study.

1.6 National Trends for Load Growth
Numerous major utilities have proposed significant transmission expansion to meet expected
load growth (Utility Dive 2025a, 2025b, and 2025c). Transmission studies try to forecast where
load growth will likely create the most urgent need for new lines. RTOs and large non-RTO
utilities usually study their own systems independently, but a few recent studies have examined
the power grid as a single national system. A national framework can test the value of more
transmission connections between planning regions.

DOE’s NTP Study explored the drivers of long-term transmission expansion across the 48
contiguous United States (GDO 2024). In particular, the study tested how different levels of
geographic coordination changed the best options for transmission expansion. 7 After modeling
and analyzing more than 90 potential scenarios, the NTP Study found the “lowest-cost U.S.
electricity system portfolios that meet future demand growth and reliability requirements include
substantial transmission expansion” (GDO 2024, Executive Summary p. 9). This means Tribes
that want to advance their own energy priorities will likely encounter an environment that is
already adding transmission infrastructure to keep up with rising electricity demand.

NTP Study results suggest the following:

 • The need for additional transmission between Oklahoma and points east and southeast
 could triple existing interregional capacity by 2035.
 • The need for additional transmission between Arizona/New Mexico and California was
 57% to 117% of existing capacity.
 • The need for additional transmission between the Dakotas/Nebraska and points east was
 50% to 100% of existing capacity.
The estimated need for more local transmission capacity was especially pronounced in
California, Florida, Michigan, New Mexico, North Carolina, and Oklahoma (GDO 2024; Vol. 2,
p. 94).

institutional coordination, accessible data, and new grid modeling approaches.” The study found
greater regional coordination was especially beneficial in the central part of the country, and two
of the RTOs there—SPP and MISO—have created ongoing working groups to examine
interregional issues. 8

The study also found sectorwide drivers for all types of transmission expansion: upgrading local
networks, expanding the capacity of regional grids, and adding high-volume ties between
regions. Whatever a Tribal project might be, it could likely play a part in a larger regional
transmission plan.

2 Successful Engagement in Transmission Planning
A Tribe’s ability to use these findings (and the findings of other studies) to increase its economic
development opportunities depends on effective participation in transmission planning processes,
building of institutional capabilities, and early identification of preferred zones for generation
and transmission projects.

2.1 Participation in Transmission Planning
An inherent challenge Tribes face is that transmission planning is technically demanding and
time intensive. Consequently, the stakeholder process favors entities that constantly engage with
the transmission utility on many projects: generation developers, electric cooperatives and
municipally owned utilities, ratepayer advocates, industry advocates, and others. For them,
mastering how transmission planning works is an investment that pays off over time. A Tribe
that has never engaged with a utility and has just one transmission project of concern could be at
a disadvantage: All else being equal, the Tribe must acquire the expertise to defend its interests
and interact with other stakeholder interests, often from scratch and over a short time.

Strategic partnerships, early engagement, and training can help overcome a Tribe’s inherent
disadvantage. Partnering with an entity already deep in the transmission planning process allows
the Tribe to leverage its partner’s expertise. A partner is also more likely to identify opportunities
early in the planning process where the Tribe’s interests might be introduced.

Deciding which lines to build and where to build them begins with planning. When a
transmission operator plans its system, it aims to:

 • Minimize the chance of power outages
 • Reduce the cost of generating electricity
 • Reduce the cost of meeting all standards for grid reliability and safety
 • Decrease the amount of energy lost during transmission
 • Reduce the cost of congestion caused by transmission outages
 • Mitigate extreme weather events and unexpected system impacts
 • Reduce the cost of meeting peak demand (FERC 2023).

To achieve these goals with a single set of long-term transmission investments, planners must
also consider laws and regulations by federally recognized Tribes that affect resource mix,
demand, and electrification, including a Tribe’s own policy goals (FERC 2024). Economic and
engineering analyses compare how well different transmission scenarios meet all the necessary
objectives.

Early participation in the planning process increases the likelihood that a Tribe’s needs will be
incorporated into the inputs that inform transmission options. Planners themselves can also
benefit from engagement because knowing in advance about a Tribe’s culturally sensitive areas
or its energy development policies can help avoid conflicts that could otherwise occur later in the
process.

Transmission planning in an RTO is conducted through stakeholder committees in accordance
with the RTO’s federal tariff. Where an RTO covers more than one state, no single state exerts
sole regulatory authority over the process. Outside an RTO, each state regulates transmission
planning by utilities under its jurisdiction.

2.1.1 Regional Transmission Organizations
Every RTO has provisions for stakeholder engagement; Table 4 provides the web links for RTO
planning and stakeholder engagement. Although there are no formal barriers to their doing so,
Tribes generally have not participated in RTO planning processes. (One notable recent exception
is the Morongo Band’s participation in CAISO; see Section 3.1 below.)

Important considerations when engaging with an RTO include the following:

 • An RTO is constrained by the provisions of its OATT. The OATT is the operating
 document describing in detail how the RTO operates. It is a voluminous “living”
 document that is updated by FERC. The RTO cannot take any action affecting a Tribe
 that conflicts with the provisions of its OATT. This does not necessarily prevent an RTO
 from accommodating a Tribe’s interests, but it does mean any action the RTO takes must
 be consistent with the governing OATT.
 • The RTO’s governing board is responsible for reconciling stakeholder needs with
 the OATT. In most cases—SPP and MISO, for example—the governing board is
 independent, meaning its members have no financial ties with any stakeholder. Although
 stakeholders have input into the RTO’s transmission planning processes, the governing
 board has the final say over which projects are ultimately in the RTO’s transmission plan.
 Board decisions may be appealed to FERC, but appeals are evaluated on whether an
 action is just and reasonable in the context of the Federal Power Act, other federal laws,
 the OATT, and previous FERC decisions.
 • The stakeholder process can be chaotic. An RTO has many stakeholders all trying to
 influence outcomes in the direction of their own often conflicting interests. Some
 conflicts are subject to stakeholder votes, but voting is limited to RTO members who sit
 on the committee where the conflict arises. A stakeholder who is not an RTO member
 may observe committee meetings and provide comment—but has no vote.
One significant difference between a Tribe and other participants in RTO planning is that regular
stakeholders usually have a permanent presence in the process. This often includes the right to

 17

vote indirectly on transmission modeling assumptions and proposals for changing business
practices. Voting rights are often assigned by stakeholder category, where a representative
designated for a stakeholder group casts a vote on behalf of all members of the group. For
example, environmental interests have voting representation in MISO’s stakeholder process as a
specific stakeholder category, but at the present time Tribes do not (MISO 2025). 9 A Tribe may
participate informally in MISO’s stakeholder meetings, including those concerning transmission
planning.

One issue that can distinguish observation from voting participation is access to data. In SPP,
access to staff’s interim transmission modeling results is limited to members who have signed
nondisclosure agreements. This is because the detailed input data for the modeling that RTO
staff conducts often include generator-specific information that the owners consider
commercially sensitive. Staff will present a public summary of modeling results—including
proposed lines that pass or fail economic tests—but only members with signed nondisclosure
agreements will have access to the detailed analyses.

All RTOs conduct systemwide transmission planning on a regular, rolling cycle. Short-term
plans look 10 years into the future and repeat every 1 to 3 years. Long-term plans look 20 years
ahead and repeat every 5 years. RTO staff conduct the technical analysis, with the relevant
stakeholder committees providing a review of assumptions, inputs, and results.

In SPP, engagement in the transmission planning process begins with creating a user account in
the RTO’s information systems. There is no charge for registration, and no additional
information is required apart from the registrant’s address, phone number, and email. The
registration page asks for a type of user (e.g., member, market participant, regulator, media) but
there are options for “general public” and “other” which a Tribe or its agent may use. Once the
Tribe has registered, it may then subscribe to the email distribution lists for SPP’s Transmission
Working Group, Economic Studies Working Group, and Strategic Planning Committee, and any
other list of interest. Registration also provides access to SPP’s stakeholder training center. Like
all RTOs, SPP provides a wide array of educational materials to help stakeholders understand the
grid and the markets operated by the RTO. An understanding of how the RTO conducts key
activities can help a Tribe refine its strategy for intervening in the transmission planning process.

2.1.2 Transmission Utilities in Non-RTO Regions
When the utility is not part of an RTO, the state generally has close regulatory control over
transmission planning through the utility’s IRP. Many states have laws establishing IRP
requirements for utilities under state jurisdiction, and the state’s utility regulator oversees these
laws through formal legal proceedings. The IRP process identifies new transmission and new
generation together in a single systemwide analysis.

The degree of control the state exerts over an IRP proceeding depends on the technical expertise
of its regulatory staff. Debates concerning IRP assumptions and methods are usually between the
utility’s experts, the regulator’s experts and trial staff, and experts representing intervening
interests (consumer advocates, natural resource advocates, and others). Specific points that the
utility and intervenors fail to resolve may be decided by the regulator.

A Tribe would need to petition the state regulator for permission to intervene in an IRP
proceeding if it wanted to advocate for its interests. The ease of participation for a Tribe would
therefore be affected by the Tribe’s relationship with the state and with other organizations
intervening in the IRP.

Sometimes, however, it is possible to engage the utility early in IRP planning, before the utility
submits its initial filing to the state. A Tribe may ask the utility’s government affairs officer
about early engagement opportunities. For example, Xcel Energy’s Public Service Company of
Colorado conducted five stakeholder workshops from December 2019 through March 2021 in

 20

preparation for its filing to the Colorado Public Utilities Commission in March 2021. The
workshops included presentations on energy and demand forecasting, resource planning, and the
software the utility used to model its system (Xcel Energy 2021). The company’s filing does not
mention whether Tribes participated in the stakeholder workshops.

2.2 Capacity Building
Tribes can strengthen their energy knowledge by hiring technical staff, working with trusted
experts, and creating hands-on opportunities for Tribal members. For example, 15 Tribes
currently operate their own utilities while others have energy enterprises involved in
development and grid operations.

2.2.1 Multi-Tribal Collaboration
Collaboration among Tribes with similar interests can help support ongoing involvement in
transmission planning. As discussed in Section 2.1, one Tribe that is primarily interested in one
transmission project is at a disadvantage relative to other stakeholders in the planning process.
However, if several Tribes affected by the same RTO can identify common interests that span
several possible projects, a common entity accountable to its member Tribes can become a
permanent presence in the transmission planning process.

Examples of organizations that have represented Tribes in some sort of transmission issue
include the following:

 • MTERA (32 member Tribes in Michigan, Minnesota, and Wisconsin)
 • Oceti Sakowin Power Authority (OSPA) (seven member Tribes in South Dakota)
 • Southern California Tribal Chairmen’s Association (25 member Tribes)
 • Inter-Tribal Council of Arizona (21 member Tribes)
 • ATCE (serving Tribes nationally).
A Tribal collaborative can be a focal point for other capacity-building activities, such as
receiving technical training and disseminating information to member Tribes. It can also
represent Tribal interests in regional state policy forums, placing Tribal interests on a coequal
footing with state interests. 10

2.3 Advance Identification of Preferred Areas for Development
One strategy to protect culturally sensitive areas and to promote economic development is to
direct developer interest to geographic zones that pose no problem for Tribal interests if
disturbed by construction crews. A Tribe can designate preferred development zones without
divulging the exact location of sites that are sensitive. Tribe-designated development zones help
avoid project delays due to problems of land access.

Preferred areas may include transmission corridors across the Tribe’s land. The transmission
utility can provide the minimum right-of-way widths required for operational safety, which the
Tribe can then use to determine suitable alternatives for transmission siting.

Identification of a preferred transmission corridor would not constitute the Tribe’s preapproval
for a specific project; it would only remove culturally sensitive areas as potential obstacles.

2.4 Centralizing Tribal Energy Administration
A Tribe may also consider consolidating administration for energy-related matters under a single
governmental office. This, of course, would be a decision for the Tribal government based on its
assessment of how to achieve its energy goals most effectively. The need for an energy office
would depend on the Tribe’s internal governance, its size, its energy goals, and expected
interactions with external entities.

Unlike a Tribal utility authority or other commercial enterprises, an energy office is part of the
Tribe’s government. It exists to implement the energy policies enacted by the government. The
functions that the Tribal government may assign to the office could include the following:

 • Acting as the Tribe’s single point of contact for outside developers
 • Conducting or commissioning independent studies ordered by the Tribal government
 • Seeking and managing federal grants
 • Representing the Tribe in energy dialogues among other regional officials.
An energy office could also be the Tribe’s technical liaison with RTOs, which are expanding in
the western United States and are affecting a growing number of Tribes. For example, CAISO,
MISO, and SPP each have a regional state committee that communicates directly with the RTO’s
governing board. These advisory committees comprise state officials (usually utility regulators or
state energy officers). 13 Currently no regional state committee includes a Tribal government. If a
Tribal government were to seek membership on a regional committee (or a parallel avenue of

3 Case Studies
3.1 Morongo Band of Mission Indians
The Morongo Band of Mission Indians created a transmission enterprise that has partnered with
utility Southern California Edison (SCE) on a major transmission project crossing the Morongo
Band’s reservation. The agreement establishes remuneration for transmission across Morongo
lands. FERC approved the first-of-its-kind agreement in 2022.

The agreement and its rates culminated 15 years of discussion between the Tribe and the utility.
Under the arrangement, the Tribe achieved the following:

 • Established a transmission company, Morongo Transmission LLC, that is majority owned
 by the Tribe
 • Became, through Morongo Transmission, a participating transmission owner in CAISO
 • Leased from SCE a percentage interest in the transmission project (initially 61.2%)
 • Received FERC approval for a transmission tariff by which it earns revenue on its leased
 share of the transmission project (Morongo Transmission 2020).
The improvements to the aging 230-kV network were part of SCE’s West of Devers transmission
upgrade project. CAISO approved the West of Devers project as a high-value element of its
systemwide transmission plan, estimating it could reduce congestion costs by approximately $27
million per year (CAISO 2016). The upgrades increased the capacity of the lines running through
the reservation from 1,600 megawatts (MW) to 4,800 MW. The agreement between the Tribe,
CAISO, and SCE (struck after CAISO approved the upgrade) saved ratepayers an additional
$500 million; it enabled routing across the Morongo reservation, avoiding the need to run the
lines through a national park or a wilderness area (Jenner and Block 2022).

Morongo Tribal Chairman Charles Martin said of the agreement: “Our Tribe has a deep
connection to the environment, and the agreement by Morongo Transmission to lease capacity on
Southern California Edison’s newly upgraded system will further that legacy by delivering green
energy resources to the Southern California power grid” (Morongo 2021).

3.2 Oceti Sakowin Power Authority
Sioux Tribes in South Dakota have created an enterprise, OSPA, to develop commercial-scale
generation on Tribal land for Tribal revenue. Access to the transmission system operated by SPP

 23

is pivotal to the Tribes’ energy goals because without it OSPA cannot reach its target energy
markets.

OSPA has encountered several transmission access challenges:

 • In 2022, OSPA and its partners withdrew plans for two wind plants on the Pine Ridge
 and Cheyenne River reservations after SPP planners said the projects would require $230
 million in system upgrade costs that OSPA and its partners would have to pay. OSPA
 said the projects would not be economically competitive with the additional cost.
 • OSPA partnered with WAPA and others on the proposed Transmission and Renewables
 Interstate Bulk Electric System (TRIBES) project that would have included grid
 connection points on the reservations. WAPA submitted the project to SPP; however, in
 its 2024 Integrated Transmission Plan, the RTO included only the western leg of the
 project—which does not go through the reservations (see Figure 4).

planning. One of the most important and most difficult questions an RTO must weigh when

Section 1.3 above, speculative projects complicate transmission planning. If a Tribe can

transmission project will have more weight in the planning process.
