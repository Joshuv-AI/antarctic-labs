Adipocyte-derived CRAMP–neutrophil serine protease interaction axis regulates innate cutaneous defense against Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Minghui Ou, Xiaoxing Wang, Mengting Zhu, Yue Liu, Yunpeng Chu, Yuzhu Shi, Changshun Shao, Yufang Shi, Peishan Li, Fei Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDermal adipocytes have emerged as active participants in cutaneous host defense. In parallel, adipocyte hypertrophy and hyperplasia–driven obesity has become a global public health priority and is strongly associated with increased risk and severity of bacterial infections. Here, we established and optimized two complementary S. aureus infection models—epidermal and subcutaneous—and in combination with diet-induced (HFD) and genetic (ob/ob) obesity, to systematically evaluate cathelin-related antimicrobial peptide (CRAMP) expression in adipocytes and its crosstalk with neutrophils. Obese mice displayed impaired cutaneous defense despite marked thickening of the fat layer, characterized by attenuated induction of adipocyte CRAMP and reduced local antibacterial activity. In vitro, CRAMP followed a biphasic pattern during adipocyte differentiation—upregulated at early stages but diminished with advanced maturation and lipid accumulation. Mechanistically, neutrophils processed adipocyte-derived CRAMP via serine proteases to generate shorter peptides with enhanced antibacterial activity. Collectively, these findings identify a CRAMP–neutrophil (serine protease) interaction axis as a key amplifier of cutaneous innate immunity and provide mechanistic insight into obesity-associated susceptibility to skin infection, suggesting potential avenues for targeted intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56289-9.Keywords: Skin infection, CRAMP, Obesity, Adipocytes, NeutrophilsSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

The skin forms the body’s outermost barrier against microbial pathogens, mechanical injury and ultraviolet radiation, and is anatomically organized into the epidermis, dermis and subcutis that act in concert to maintain barrier homeostasis1. The epidermis differentiates from the basal layer through the spinous and granular layers to the stratum corneum, where corneocytes embedded in intercellular lipid lamellae, together with tight junctions, form a highly effective physical barrier. The dermis comprises papillary and reticular compartments containing fibroblasts, blood and lymphatic vessels, nerve fibers and diverse immune cells, and—together with hair follicles, sebaceous and eccrine/apocrine glands—creates specialized niches for immune surveillance. The subcutis is organized into adipose lobules and fibrous septa, providing cushioning, thermoregulation and endocrine functions1. Beneath the reticular dermis, rodents and humans harbor a dense band of adipocytes that envelop mature hair follicles, termed dermal white adipose tissue (DWAT)2.

Antimicrobial peptides/proteins (AMPs) are pivotal innate effectors that enable the skin to meet continuous microbial challenges. Although AMPs are diverse across taxa, mammalian AMPs share the capacity to rapidly kill or inactivate microbes3. Among these, cathelicidins are central to cutaneous defense: in humans, the single cathelicidin gene (CAMP) encodes the precursor CAP18 (also referred to as CAMP)4, which can be proteolytically processed into multiple active fragments, including the 37-residue peptide LL-375; in mice, the homolog is CRAMP (cathelin-related antimicrobial peptide)6. Inflammatory stimuli upregulate CAP18 in keratinocytes, markedly increasing cathelicidin abundance in the skin7.

S. aureus is a Gram-positive pathogen and a major cause of morbidity and mortality in both community- and hospital-acquired infections. The skin is a principal site of S. aureus disease, and approximately 10% of healthy individuals harbor epidermal colonization8. S. aureus virulence factors enable breaching of the epidermal barrier and invasion of the dermis and deeper tissues, driving systemic infection9. Although progress has been made, the host mechanisms that limit dermal invasion remain incompletely defined. Notably, Gallo and colleagues reported in 2015 that dermal adipocytes restrain S. aureus infection by producing cathelicidin: upon infection, preadipocytes rapidly proliferate and expand the dermal fat layer; impaired adipogenesis exacerbates infection; and cathelicidin production by adipocytes is required, as inhibition of adipogenesis reduces cathelicidin expression and adipocytes from Cramp−/− mice lose antibacterial activity10. In parallel, obesity—characterized by adipocyte hypertrophy/hyperplasia—is tightly linked to higher risk and severity of bacterial infections11, underscoring the need to delineate how adipose expansion modulates cutaneous antimicrobial defense.

Neutrophils are central to the early control of bacterial pathogens. Individuals with neutropenia or genetic defects in microbicidal pathways are highly susceptible to infection. Following S. aureus challenge, neutrophils are rapidly recruited to the lesion, where they phagocytose and kill internalized bacteria through multiple mechanisms, including assembly of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and delivery of a repertoire of bactericidal molecules into phagosomes12. Whether, and how, neutrophils modulate adipocyte-mediated antibacterial responses has remained unclear.

Here, we show that both diet-induced and genetic obesity diminish resistance to cutaneous S. aureus infection, coincident with blunted induction of adipocyte CRAMP and reduced local bactericidal activity. We further demonstrate that CRAMP exhibits a biphasic trajectory during adipocyte differentiation—early induction followed by decline with advanced maturation—implicating differentiation stage as a determinant of antimicrobial potential. Mechanistically, neutrophils enhance adipocyte-derived antimicrobial defense by serine protease–dependent processing of CRAMP into shorter, more active peptides. Together, these findings define a CRAMP–neutrophil (serine protease) interaction axis as a critical component of cutaneous innate immunity and suggest testable strategies to mitigate obesity-associated susceptibility to skin infection.

Results

Intradermal adipocytes proliferate and express the antimicrobial peptide CRAMP during cutaneous infection

A prior study reported that dermal adipocytes secrete antimicrobial peptides to kill S. aureus in a subcutaneous infection model10. To further delineate site-specific responses within the skin, we established two murine models of S. aureus infection: epidermal inoculation and subcutaneous inoculation, and optimized inoculum and time points. In the epidermal model, inoculation with 108 CFU followed by 42 h of infection produced overt skin wrinkling; in the subcutaneous model, 107 CFU for 42 h yielded a typical localized abscess (Fig. 1a). Balancing phenotypic clarity and reproducibility, subsequent experiments were performed using a standardized inoculum of 5 × 107 CFU with assessment at 42 h post infection.

Under these conditions, histology and immunofluorescence were performed in both models. Under normal chow diet (NCD), both models induced thickening of the intradermal fat layer and adipocyte hypertrophy, with more pronounced changes in the subcutaneous model (Fig. 1b). Notably, adipocyte CRAMP signal was undetectable in the epidermal model, whereas adipocytes displayed clear CRAMP expression in the subcutaneous model (Fig. 1c). To assess the impact of host metabolic status, we repeated both infections in high-fat diet (HFD)–induced obese mice. Histology again showed fat-layer expansion in both models, with larger effects in the subcutaneous setting (Fig. 1d). Immunofluorescence revealed the same pattern in obese and lean animals: adipocyte CRAMP positivity was observed only in the subcutaneous infection model (Fig. 1e).

Together, these data indicate that the subcutaneous infection model more robustly elicits adipocyte proliferation and CRAMP expression, and is therefore preferable for mechanistic interrogation of adipocyte CRAMP–mediated antibacterial defense. All subsequent mechanistic experiments used the subcutaneous model.

Obesity blunts adipocyte CRAMP induction and compromises resistance to skin infection

To determine how obesity impacts cutaneous antibacterial capacity, NCD- and HFD-fed mice (5 months) were subjected to subcutaneous S. aureus challenge and evaluated at 42 h post infection. Relative to NCD controls, HFD mice developed significantly larger lesions (Fig. 2a). In vivo bioluminescence imaging demonstrated higher bacterial burden at infection sites in HFD mice (Fig. 2b), which was corroborated by CFU enumeration from lesion homogenates (Fig. 2c).

Pathology revealed that even at baseline (naïve), HFD mice had a thicker subcutaneous fat layer with adipocyte hypertrophy; infection further increased fat thickness in both groups (Fig. 2d). By immunofluorescence, CRAMP signal was weak at baseline in both groups; upon infection, adipocyte CRAMP was markedly induced in NCD mice, whereas HFD mice showed only a modest increase and overall significantly lower CRAMP expression than time-matched NCD counterparts (Fig. 2e). These molecular differences paralleled the higher bacterial loads and more extensive tissue injury in HFD mice.

In a genetic model of obesity, ob/ob mice exhibited more severe epidermal damage and significantly higher lesion bacterial burden than wild-type (WT) controls (Fig. 2f–h). Morphometric analysis showed increased adipocyte number and size in naïve ob/ob mice; infection further thickened the fat layer, but adipocyte CRAMP expression was significantly lower in infected ob/ob mice than in infected WT (Fig. 2i, j).

Collectively, both diet-induced and genetic obesity suppress infection-induced adipocyte CRAMP expression following subcutaneous challenge, concomitant with higher local bacterial burden and more severe tissue damage. By contrast, lean/WT mice upregulate adipocyte CRAMP upon infection to augment cutaneous antibacterial defense, whereas obesity—despite increased adipocyte number and size—fails to mount sufficient CRAMP induction, likely contributing to impaired control of infection.

Excess lipid accumulation suppresses CRAMP expression

To probe mechanisms underlying heightened infection risk and severity in obesity, we first induced differentiation of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes in vitro and tracked lipid accumulation and CRAMP dynamics. Oil Red O (Sigma-Aldrich, American) staining showed time-dependent increases in intracellular lipid droplets (Fig. 3a). Western blotting revealed a biphasic trajectory of CRAMP: expression rose early during differentiation, peaked around day 3, then declined progressively; by day 11, when lipid accumulation was maximal, CRAMP was nearly undetectable (Fig. 3b, c). These data indicate a positive association between early lipid accretion and CRAMP, with subsequent suppression as lipid overload develops.

We then compared CRAMP expression in vivo in infected skin from HFD vs. NCD mice and ob/ob vs. WT mice. Western blots showed a reduction of total skin CRAMP in HFD relative to NCD, with an even greater reduction in ob/ob vs. WT (Fig. 3d). Immunohistochemistry further demonstrated diminished adipocyte CRAMP intensity in inguinal fat after infection in both HFD and ob/ob mice (Fig. 3e, f). Concordant with the in vitro data, excessive lipid accumulation suppresses CRAMP expression.

Overall, adipocytes upregulate CRAMP during early adipogenesis to support antibacterial defense, whereas obesity-associated adipocyte over-maturation and lipid overload downregulate CRAMP.

Neutrophil-mediated processing of CRAMP enhances local antibacterial activity

Neutrophils are among the earliest immune cells to arrive at sites of skin infection and exert antibacterial activity via degranulation of proteases, formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and other mechanisms. Adipocytes contribute by secreting antimicrobial peptides. In the subcutaneous model, immunofluorescence revealed marked neutrophil infiltration within the intradermal fat layer in close apposition to adipocytes (Fig. 4a), suggesting potential functional cooperation.

After 40 min at 37 °C, mixtures were plated and colonies enumerated after 20 h to assess NIH 3T3 bactericidal activity. To test whether neutrophils promote CRAMP maturation, bone marrow–derived neutrophils were co-cultured with HEK293T cells transiently transfected with Cramp (Fig. 4b). Western blotting detected CRAMP-specific cleavage fragments under co-culture but not 293T monoculture conditions (Fig. 4c). Because the generation of C-terminal mature cathelicidin peptides requires proteolytic cleavage of the precursor, the appearance of CRAMP cleavage fragments specifically in the co-culture condition indicates that neutrophils promote CRAMP maturation. In NIH3T3 cells stably overexpressing CRAMP, CRAMP cleavage products were likewise observed only during co-culture with neutrophils, not in monoculture (Fig. 4d), confirming reproducibility across cell types.

Functionally, NIH3T3 cells collected after co-culture with neutrophils exhibited significantly enhanced antibacterial activity against S. aureus compared with NIH3T3 monocultures (40 min co-incubation at 37 °C, CFU counted after 20 h; Fig. 4e), consistent with neutrophil-mediated processing of endogenous CRAMP into more active peptides.

Together, spatial proximity, proteolytic processing, and functional assays indicate that neutrophils potentiate adipocyte-derived antibacterial defense by proteolytic maturation of CRAMP into shorter, bactericidal peptides, thereby strengthening early cutaneous protection against infection.

Inhibition of neutrophil serine proteases reduces CRAMP cleavage and antibacterial efficacy

Given that neutrophils are rich in granule proteases with broad substrate spectra13,14, we hypothesized that CRAMP processing depends on neutrophil granule serine protease activity. We added the serine protease inhibitor AEBSF to neutrophil–NIH3T3 (CRAMP-expressing) co-cultures and analyzed CRAMP cleavage and in vitro bactericidal activity (Fig. 5a).

Because CRAMP is secreted, culture supernatants were collected for Western blotting. After co-culture, high-molecular-weight CRAMP decreased while cleavage fragments increased; AEBSF suppressed the generation of these fragments (Fig. 5b). These findings align with intracellular analyses and support granule protease–mediated CRAMP processing. We next evaluated functional consequences. AEBSF itself had no direct effect on S. aureus growth at 200 µM (Fig. 5c). As expected, co-cultured NIH3T3 cells displayed stronger antibacterial activity than monocultures, whereas AEBSF treatment markedly attenuated this enhancement (Fig. 5d).

In summary, convergent biochemical and functional data from cellular and secreted fractions indicate that neutrophil serine protease activity is a key step enabling CRAMP cleavage and amplification of antibacterial effects. Pharmacological inhibition reduces CRAMP processing and diminishes bactericidal function, highlighting a local “granule protease–CRAMP processing–enhanced killing” axis operative early during infection. We note that AEBSF is a broad inhibitor; the specific proteases and their relative contributions remain to be resolved using selective inhibitors and genetic approaches.

Discussion

The global burden of obesity continues to rise. According to the latest WHO estimates, in 2022 roughly one in eight people worldwide were living with obesity; among adults (≥ 18 years), 43% were overweight and 16% were obese (~ 890 million), and the prevalence in children and adolescents has increased substantially since 1990, underscoring obesity as a priority public health challenge (WHO Factsheet: Obesity and overweight). Prior work indicates that obesity promotes cardiovascular disease, cancer, and delayed wound healing by driving systemic inflammation15. Yet the mechanisms by which infections—classically associated with immunosuppression—occur more frequently and present more severely in people with obesity remain incompletely understood. Epidemiologic and causal inference studies link obesity to an increased risk and worse outcomes of bacterial skin and soft tissue infections, particularly cellulitis16,17; in animal models, a high-fat diet increases pathogen burden and reduces antibiotic efficacy18.

Consistent with these observations, we found that both HFD-induced and genetic (ob/ob) obesity diminish resistance to cutaneous S. aureus infection, manifesting as larger lesions and higher bacterial loads, accompanied by blunted induction of CRAMP in adipose tissue. Mechanistically, our data support two complementary dimensions. (i) CRAMP exhibits a biphasic trajectory during adipocyte differentiation—upregulated early, then downregulated with excessive lipid accumulation—suggesting that obesity-associated over-maturation/hypertrophy compromises maintenance of CRAMP expression. (ii) Neutrophils process CRAMP via granule serine proteases to generate shorter, more potent antibacterial peptides; conversely, pharmacologic inhibition of serine proteases reduces CRAMP cleavage and dampens the antibacterial activity of cells, indicating that a “CRAMP–neutrophil (granule protease)” interaction acts as a local amplifier of innate defense (Fig. 5E).

Our findings align with and extend the dermal fat–antimicrobial defense axis previously described. Gallo and colleagues reported that diet-induced obesity depletes dermal adipocyte progenitors while accumulating mature adipocytes and, via TGFβ signaling, suppresses adipogenesis and antimicrobial function, lowering AMP (including cathelicidin) production and increasing susceptibility to S. aureus19. Building on this, we show that, beyond reduced production, the post-translational activation of CRAMP depends on neutrophil granule protease activity. Thus, obesity-related metabolic–immune disequilibrium may concurrently disrupt the production–processing–effector triad of antimicrobial defense, creating a convergent liability for the skin.

These insights have translational implications. From the source side, strategies that restore reactive adipogenesis and CRAMP induction (e.g., optimizing the differentiation window or the local metabolic/inflammatory milieu) may enhance local antibacterial capacity. From the processing side, preserving or judiciously augmenting neutrophil protease–mediated activation—while avoiding collateral tissue injury—could increase the effective activity of CRAMP. Given the upregulated TGFβ axis and AP depletion in obesity19, precise modulation of adipogenesis and TGFβ signaling also emerges as a candidate approach to reduce infection susceptibility. All such strategies require rigorous pharmacologic and infection-model testing to establish safety and efficacy.

This study has some limitations. First, although we observed associations among obesity, reduced CRAMP, and impaired infection control, establishing causal relationships will require genetic approaches, such as adipocyte-specific Cramp deletion and CRAMP mutants that are resistant to proteolytic cleavage, to distinguish between effects on CRAMP production and processing. Second, our in vitro work relied primarily on NIH3T3/3T3-L1 systems; validation in human primary adipocytes/tissues and organoids is needed to assess conservation of CRAMP regulation and proteolytic activation. Third, obesity reshapes the skin microbiome and barrier function—factors that can influence pathogen burden and drug responses18—and we did not systematically stratify for these variables. Fourth, differences in neutrophil recruitment dynamics and granule protease repertoires between dietary and genetic obesity warrant higher spatiotemporal resolution (e.g., single-cell transcriptomics and protease-activity imaging).

Recent work shows that, during skin infection, CXCL12⁺ fibroblasts sense IL-17 to recruit neutrophils to sites of infection20. Neutrophils are enriched for serine proteases with broad substrate and antibacterial activities21, but their role in activating adipocyte-derived AMPs had remained unclear. Our data advance this field by demonstrating that neutrophils cleave adipocyte-secreted CRAMP via serine proteases to generate more active peptides, thereby amplifying local innate defense. This links an upstream “fibroblast–neutrophil recruitment axis” with a downstream “CRAMP processing axis,” and highlights adipocytes as immune-competent cells that contribute to microenvironmental amplification rather than passive energy storage.

Important gaps remain within the “neutrophil–CRAMP axis.” Our pharmacologic data implicate serine proteases but do not identify the key effectors. Given classical neutrophil granule proteases—neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and proteinase-321—future work should combine selective inhibitors with genetic models (single/combined knockouts or neutrophil-specific deletions) to define protease-specific cleavage sites and product spectra. In vivo, direct monitoring of CRAMP cleavage products over time and testing in neutrophil protease–deficient mice are needed to establish causality for antibacterial outcomes. Moreover, obesity may impair both neutrophil recruitment and protease activity; while our data point in this direction, the relative contributions remain to be fully resolved.

In summary, obesity compromises cutaneous innate defense by attenuating CRAMP induction and weakening its neutrophil-mediated activation, identifying a “CRAMP–neutrophil (serine protease)” interaction axis as a critical determinant of obesity-associated susceptibility to skin infection. Defining the plasticity of this axis across obesity phenotypes and human cohorts will guide the development of precise interventions to bolster host antimicrobial capacity.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

The following cell lines were used: NIH 3T3 (mouse fibroblasts; provided by Shandong University), 3T3-L1 (mouse preadipocytes; Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank), and 293T (human embryonic kidney epithelial cells; Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank). NIH 3T3, 3T3-L1, and 293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, American) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Stable Camp-overexpressing preadipocyte lines were generated from NIH 3T3. NIH 3T3 cells were transfected with a Cramp-myc expression plasmid or empty vector using a lipid-based reagent; untransfected cells served as blank controls. After 48 h, cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) until stable lines were established. Overexpression was validated by Western blot and qPCR.

Animals and tissue processing

Experiments were conducted non-blinded with age-randomized allocation. Female B6.Cg-Lepob/J (ob/ob) mice (6–10 weeks; The Jackson Laboratory, USA; stock no. 000632) and sex-/age-matched littermate were used. For diet studies, male C57BL/6 mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD, 40% kcal from fat) or a normal chow diet (NCD) starting at 10 weeks of age for 16 weeks. Sample sizes were estimated from pilot data to ensure adequate statistical power. Mice were housed under SPF barrier conditions (12-h light/12-h dark cycle; controlled temperature and humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water.

Ethical approval

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (approval no. SUDA20231103A04) and followed institutional guidelines. The study was conducted and reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All mice used in this study were adult C57BL/6 animals. At the time of euthanasia, wild-type mice weighed 18–23 g, whereas obese (ob/ob) mice and high-fat diet (HFD)–fed mice weighed 45–50 g. All animals were within the body weight range approved for cervical dislocation by institutional animal care and use guidelines.

At the indicated experimental endpoints, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane until complete loss of reflexes, followed by humane euthanasia via cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel. Death was confirmed prior to tissue collection.

Cutaneous infection model

Dorsal hair was removed prior to infection. Mice received a subcutaneous injection of Staphylococcus aureus Newman-luciferase strain (5 × 107 CFU per mouse). Skin tissues were harvested at 42 h post infection for analysis. For in vivo bioluminescence, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. S. aureus Newman-luciferase–infected mice were injected intraperitoneally with D-luciferin potassium salt (150 mg/kg) 10 min before imaging and scanned using a Tanon bioluminescence imaging system (exposure 75–100 s). Bacterial preparation. Single colonies (BIOSCI, China) were inoculated into TSB and cultured at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 16 h, followed by 1:10 subculture in fresh TSB for 4 h. Bacterial density was quantified by OD and converted to CFU for dosing.

3T3-L1 adipogenic induction and Oil Red O staining

When 3T3-L1 confluence reached 90–100% (Day 0), differentiation was initiated using induction medium: high-glucose DMEM + 10% FBS + 0.5 mM IBMX (MCE, HY-12318) + 10 µg/mL insulin + 10 nM dexamethasone + 60 µM indomethacin. Medium was replaced every other day. Cells were collected on Days 1, 3, 5, …, 15 for protein extraction or lipid staining. Cells were rinsed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, equilibrated in 60% isopropanol for 5 min, stained with Oil Red O working solution for 15 min, and rinsed with deionized water until background cleared. Cells were kept moist in ddH₂O for imaging.

Western blotting

Skin proteins were extracted as described above. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China); proteins in culture supernatants were precipitated by methanol/chloroform. Protein concentration was determined by BCA (Beyotime, China) assay. Samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 16 min, and stored at − 20 °C. Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 12% or 15% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (methanol-activated). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed (TBST, ×3), incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, washed again, and developed with ECL (1–5 min). Images were acquired using a gel documentation system.

Antibodies. Anti-Cathelicidin/CLP (1:1000, Abcam, UK); MYC tag polyclonal (1:1000, Proteintech, China); anti-mouse β-actin (1:2000, Proteintech, China); anti-mouse α-tubulin (1:2000, Proteintech, China); HRP-anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Beyotime, China); HRP-anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA).

Bactericidal assay

TSA plates containing chloramphenicol were prepared in 10-cm dishes. Newman (chloramphenicol-resistant) was grown as above and adjusted by OD₆₀₀. Overexpressing cell lines or sorted neutrophils were co-cultured with preadipocytes for 5–6 h. Neutrophils were then removed, and preadipocytes plus supernatants were collected.

Preadipocytes were dissociated, pelleted, resuspended in 400 µL complete medium, seeded in low-attachment 24-well plates, and mixed with Newman-mCherry at a 1:1 bacteria: cell ratio. Co-incubations were performed for ~ 40 min at 37 °C. In parallel, culture supernatants were diluted 1:100, mixed 1:1 with bacteria, and incubated under the same conditions. After incubation, serial ten-fold dilutions in sterile PBS were spotted (10 µL) onto TSA plates and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h for CFU enumeration. Colonies were manually counted when clearly distinguishable. For high-density spots where colonies overlapped, CFU numbers were estimated based on colony density in defined representative areas and extrapolated to the entire spot. Spots with extremely dense growth were considered too numerous to count (TNTC) and were assigned an upper threshold value(500).

Isolation and culture of neutrophils

Neutrophils. Bone-marrow neutrophils were isolated using Ly6G magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer’s protocol and resuspended in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, American, with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin).

Quantification of skin bacterial burden

Infected skin was minced and digested in RPMI-1640 containing Dispase II (1 mg/ml), collagenases I/II/IV (each 1 mg/ml) and DNase I (0.1 mg/ml) at 37 °C, 180 rpm for 60 min. Digestion was stopped with PBS; suspensions were filtered (70 μm), centrifuged (420 ×g, 5 min), washed, resuspended, and filtered again (200-mesh) to obtain single-cell suspensions on ice.

Infected skin was excised, weighed, and processed to single-cell suspensions. Equal mass-normalized volumes were adjusted to 1 mL. Serial dilutions in sterile PBS were plated on TSA and incubated at 37 °C overnight. CFU were counted the next day and normalized to tissue weight.

Histology and immunofluorescence

Paraffin sections: Tissues were fixed in 4% PFA (24 h), dehydrated, cleared, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm. Sections were used for H&E or immunofluorescence (IF).

Frozen sections: Tissues were fixed in 4% PFA (24 h), cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, frozen at − 80 °C, and sectioned at 12 μm.

Oil Red O: Fixed cell coverslips were rinsed in 60% isopropanol, stained with Oil Red O for 20 min, and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Immunofluorescence: Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked for 1 h (RT) in 3% BSA + 0.2% Triton X-100, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, then with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h (RT). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and imaged by confocal microscopy. Frozen-section IF followed the same procedure without antigen retrieval.

Primary/secondary antibodies and dyes. Statistics

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and are presented as mean ± SEM. Two-group comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests unless otherwise specified. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc tests. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
