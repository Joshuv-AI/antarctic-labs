Urban-scale and urban cluster-scale: how population agglomeration shapes carbon emission intensity
Authors: Haoqing Zheng, Chunli Ji, Huafeng Wang, Songlin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban clusters have become the fundamental spatial units for achieving carbon reduction, yet the pivotal interactive role of urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration in shaping carbon emission intensity (CEI) has been largely overlooked. To address this gap, we develop a dual-scale analytical framework to examine how urban-scale population agglomeration affects CEI and how this relationship is moderated by urban cluster-scale population agglomeration. Based on panel data for 221 cities in 19 urban clusters in China from 2011 to 2022, we employ a two-way fixed effects model for the empirical analysis. The results reveal a robust U-shaped relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI: urban-scale population agglomeration initially reduces CEI but increases it beyond a turning point. More importantly, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration, measured using an inverse-distance-weighted agglomeration index, significantly flattens this U-shaped relationship. Green technological innovation and the co-agglomeration of diverse functional segments are identified as the two main channels through which this moderating effect operates. Heterogeneity analyses show that the moderating effect is more pronounced in eastern coastal and polycentric urban clusters. Moreover, this effect is stronger within a limited geographic range. These findings provide actionable insights for designing coordinated population agglomeration strategies at both the urban and urban cluster scales and for promoting carbon reduction across multiple scales within urban clusters.

Introduction

The rapid concentration of population in cities is one of the most striking long-run trends in human history [1, 2]. In this context, understanding whether urban-scale population agglomeration reduces or increases carbon emission intensity (CEI), commonly measured as total CO₂ emissions per unit of economic output, is of considerable importance for effective carbon management and global sustainable development [3]. However, existing evidence remains uncertain, posing significant challenges for carbon mitigation at the policymaking level. One strand of the literature argues that urban-scale population agglomeration reduces carbon emissions by fostering compact spatial organization, improving resource-use efficiency, and promoting green technological innovation [4–6]. From this perspective, encouraging urban-scale population agglomeration is regarded as an effective pathway for carbon reduction [5, 7, 8]. In contrast, another strand of the literature emphasizes the congestion effects associated with excessive agglomeration, suggesting that rising urban-scale population agglomeration may lead to resource misallocation, higher energy consumption, and ultimately increased carbon emissions [9, 10]. This perspective implies the need to regulate the process of population concentration in cities to avoid surpassing thresholds beyond which agglomeration becomes environmentally detrimental [11–13]. Taken together, these two strands of literature suggest that the effect of urban-scale population agglomeration on CEI is unlikely to be purely linear. Indeed, a growing number of studies have documented nonlinear relationships between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI, including inverted U-shaped and U-shaped patterns [14–16].

In recent years, as intercity linkages have intensified, urban clusters have become the principal spatial carriers and strategic units of regional economic development [17, 18]. As a result, relying solely on the perspective of individual cities to reduce CEI is insufficient; urban clusters have become increasingly critical for carbon mitigation [19]. As an advanced spatial organizational form of urbanization, urban clusters are characterized by increasingly strong interactions and functional linkages among cities [6]. Within such systems, economic agents in one city can benefit not only from local resources but also from knowledge spillovers, a wider variety of intermediate inputs, and larger markets generated by other cities [20–22]. In this context, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration should not merely be understood as the simple sum of urban populations, but rather as a spatially embedded and network-based process shaped by cross-city interactions [23]. Accordingly, some recent studies have begun to shift the analytical focus of carbon reduction from urban-scale to urban cluster-scale population agglomeration. For example, Wang et al. [24] provide evidence that urban cluster-scale population agglomeration significantly reduces CEI by using data from the Yangtze River Delta and the Pearl River Delta urban clusters. However, other studies suggest that the environmental benefits of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration are not always guaranteed, and may vary across stages of development and spatial structures [25, 26].

Although these studies have extended the analysis of population agglomeration and carbon reduction from the urban scale to the urban cluster scale, existing research remains largely constrained by a single-scale perspective. Consequently, limited attention has been paid to how urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration jointly shape CEI. In fact, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration can enable peripheral cities to share knowledge spillovers, labor market pooling, and input sharing benefits generated by population concentration in central cities, while simultaneously alleviating the congestion effects associated with excessive population concentration in those central cities [22, 24]. This creates favorable conditions for improving overall energy efficiency and promoting low-carbon development. However, when central cities are too small to generate sufficiently strong agglomeration economies and comparative advantages, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration may induce the premature outflow of high-quality resources from peripheral cities, weakening the carbon-reduction effects of population agglomeration [26]. Therefore, understanding how to coordinate urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration is crucial for identifying an alternative and effective pathway for carbon mitigation at the urban cluster level. Against this backdrop, this paper uses panel data for 221 cities in 19 Chinese urban clusters from 2011 to 2022 to examine the relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI. To capture the broader spatial context in which each city is embedded, this paper defines urban cluster-scale population agglomeration as the inverse-distance-weighted sum of the population sizes of all other cities within the same urban cluster, where the weights are specified as the inverse of intercity distance to reflect the decay of spatial interactions with distance. Building on this measure, this paper further investigates the moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration in the relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI.

The potential contributions of this work are threefold: First, it moves beyond single-scale analyses by integrating urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration into a unified analytical framework. Building on the existing literature on the impact of urban-scale population agglomeration on CEI, this paper confirms a U-shaped relationship between the two and further examines the moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration in this relationship, thereby extending the analysis of population agglomeration from the urban scale to the urban cluster scale. In doing so, this paper provides a new perspective for understanding how population agglomeration shapes CEI.

Second, by constructing proxy variables for green technological innovation and diverse functional segment co-agglomeration, this paper examines how the interaction between urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration shapes CEI. Furthermore, by exploring heterogeneity across different urban clusters and uncovering the spatial distance decay characteristics across varying spatial radii, this paper identifies the specific conditions under which cross-scale coordination exerts its effects.

Third, our findings reveal that when urban-scale population agglomeration enters an excessive stage, its association with increased CEI can be significantly mitigated as urban cluster-scale population agglomeration increases. This suggests that future carbon mitigation strategies should move beyond the traditional isolated city-centered governance approach and instead coordinate urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration across the entire urban cluster. This would allow urban clusters to serve as the fundamental spatial units for carbon reduction. In the context of China’s continued population concentration in urban clusters, this finding has important practical implications for leveraging the spatial advantages of a large country to advance the carbon peaking and carbon neutrality goals.

Theoretical analysis and hypothesis development

The relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI

Urban-scale population agglomeration refers to the process by which part or all of the population within a given geographic area progressively concentrates in certain cities [27, 28]. Classical agglomeration theory posits that knowledge spillovers, labor market pooling, and input sharing emerge alongside population agglomeration. As widely recognized core components of agglomeration externalities, these factors are theorized to create and reinforce favorable conditions for green technological innovation and the co-agglomeration of diverse functional segments [1, 6, 29, 37]. When urban-scale population agglomeration remains at a moderate stage, these agglomeration externalities are likely to dominate, thereby promoting green technological innovation and functional segment co-agglomeration. However, as urban-scale population agglomeration increases, congestion effects become increasingly prominent [30]. Once urban-scale population agglomeration reaches a turning point and enters an excessive stage, the resulting congestion effects may outweigh agglomeration externalities, thereby potentially exerting adverse impacts on green technological innovation and functional segment co-agglomeration. This asymmetry suggests that the relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI is likely to vary across different stages of agglomeration.

When urban-scale population agglomeration is at a moderate stage, agglomeration externalities dominate and potentially affect CEI through two primary pathways. The first pathway is associated with green technological innovation. As urban-scale population agglomeration intensifies, spatial proximity facilitates face-to-face interactions among workers and accelerates knowledge diffusion, thereby improving the feasibility and efficiency of knowledge recombination [31]. Compared with conventional technological innovation, green technological innovation relies more heavily on the recombination of diverse knowledge and is widely regarded as the outcome of novel knowledge combinations [32]. Indeed, existing studies highlight that knowledge spillovers associated with urban-scale population agglomeration play a critical role in fostering green technological innovation [33]. Such green technological innovation enables the upgrading of production systems and the adoption of cleaner technologies, thereby supporting low-carbon development [34]. As emphasized in the literature, promoting green technological innovation is considered a key factor in reducing CEI [35, 36]. Therefore, when urban-scale population agglomeration is at a moderate stage, it could contribute to reduced CEI by fostering green technological innovation.

The second potential pathway relates to diverse functional segment co-agglomeration. A defining feature of urban-scale population agglomeration is the reinforcement of agglomeration externalities such as labor market pooling and input sharing. With respect to labor market pooling, urban-scale population agglomeration brings together workers with diverse skill levels and professional backgrounds within the same city [37]. This encourages diverse functional segments to spatially co-locate in order to access this rich labor pool [38]. Regarding input sharing, the co-agglomeration of diverse functional segments within cities allows firms to benefit from spatial proximity to intermediate input suppliers, thereby reducing per-unit transaction costs and improving access to producer services [39]. Therefore, the strengthening of labor market pooling and input sharing associated with urban-scale population agglomeration jointly facilitates diverse functional segment co-agglomeration. Due to the presence of forward and backward linkages among diverse functional segments, their co-agglomeration enhances the potential for the reallocation and recycling of resources and energy, thus reducing CEI [40]. For example, by-products or waste generated in one functional segment can serve as intermediate inputs for another, enabling resource and energy recycling and, in turn, lowering CEI [41]. Consequently, when urban-scale population agglomeration is at a moderate stage, it appears to correlate with lower CEI by supporting diverse functional segment co-agglomeration.

As urban-scale population agglomeration enters an excessive stage, congestion effects tend to intensify sharply and become dominant. This not only impedes green technological innovation but is also likely to suppress diverse functional segment co-agglomeration, thereby contributing to an increase in CEI. First, in terms of green technological innovation, when urban-scale population agglomeration is at an excessive stage, due to lags in urban planning, competition for infrastructure and public services becomes intensified [42]. As a result, population-induced congestion effects, such as traffic congestion, overcrowded facilities, and insufficient public services, can become increasingly pronounced [43]. Addressing these challenges typically requires substantial investment in urban governance and infrastructure expansion [44], which could divert financial resources away from activities that would otherwise support green technological innovation. This reallocation of resources constrains green technological innovation and its capacity to reduce CEI.

Second, with respect to diverse functional segment co-agglomeration, when urban-scale population agglomeration is at an excessive stage, competition for limited land and production factors is likely to drive up operational costs of firms [45]. These cost pressures can be particularly pronounced for cost-sensitive production functional segments. Under such conditions, an increasing number of production plants find it difficult to bear the rising costs in central cities and instead relocate to peripheral cities [46]. Consequently, when urban-scale population agglomeration enters an excessive stage, it hinders diverse functional segment co-agglomeration within the same city. The resulting spatial dispersion of functional segments across cities could raise the complexity and cost of cross-regional organization and management, thus entailing a non-intensive spatial structure [47, 48]. Such a spatial configuration reduces efficiency, leading to resource and energy waste and ultimately contributing to increased CEI [46].

To summarize, when urban-scale population agglomeration is at a moderate stage, it is associated with a reduction in CEI by potentially fostering green technological innovation and diverse functional segment co-agglomeration. Conversely, once urban-scale population agglomeration enters an excessive stage, it could hinder these potential pathways, thereby increasing CEI, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration

As intercity interactions and linkages continue to intensify, the capacity of urban clusters to concentrate population has become increasingly pronounced [49, 50]. Against this backdrop, this study extends the spatial scope of analysis from the urban scale to the urban cluster scale and investigates the potential moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration in shaping the relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI.

When urban-scale population agglomeration is at a moderate stage, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration could attenuate the role of urban-scale population agglomeration in reducing CEI. This attenuation primarily arises because, as population agglomeration expands to a broader spatial scale, the geographic distribution of diverse functional segments becomes increasingly dispersed. On the one hand, before urban-scale population agglomeration reaches the turning point into an excessive stage, a low level of spatial functional division in urban clusters makes it more difficult to integrate various resources and capabilities among economic actors compared with co-agglomeration within the same city [51]. However, existing studies emphasize that green technological innovation relies heavily on collaboration among diverse economic actors to access external resources and cope with its inherent novelty, uncertainty, and risk [32, 52]. Consequently, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration could weaken the promoting effect of urban-scale population agglomeration on green technological innovation, thereby moderating its role in reducing CEI. On the other hand, such dispersed distribution also poses challenges for the producer service functional segment in effectively managing other functional segments [46, 53], thus undermining their co-agglomeration. As a result, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration weakens the promoting effect of urban-scale population agglomeration on diverse functional segment co-agglomeration, thus limiting its contribution to carbon mitigation.

When urban-scale population agglomeration is at an excessive stage, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration could mitigate the role of urban-scale population agglomeration in increasing CEI through two primary potential pathways. First, as noted by Portnov and Schwartz [54], an important driving force behind urban cluster-scale population agglomeration is the outward diffusion of population from large cities in response to congestion effects generated by urban-scale population agglomeration. Accordingly, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration helps alleviate congestion effects associated with urban-scale population agglomeration, thereby restoring the pathways that foster green technological innovation and diverse functional segment co-agglomeration, which ultimately support a reduction in CEI.

Second, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration represents not merely a spatial expansion of population agglomeration but also a re-optimization of specialization and cooperation within the urban cluster. This enables cities to access or borrow knowledge spillovers from other cities within the same urban cluster [21, 55], thereby promoting green technological innovation and supporting reductions in CEI. In addition, it facilitates the co-agglomeration of diverse functional segments across cities [48], integrating these functional segments at the urban cluster level and further reducing CEI. Indeed, existing literature suggests that diverse functional segment co-agglomeration within a broader spatial scale can efficiently integrate resource and energy recycling across the entire urban cluster, thus reducing CEI [53].

In summary, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration is expected to play a moderating role in the U-shaped relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI. When urban-scale population agglomeration remains at a moderate stage, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration tends to weaken the CEI-reducing effect of urban-scale population agglomeration. However, once urban-scale population agglomeration enters an excessive stage, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration helps mitigate the CEI-increasing effect of urban-scale population agglomeration, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Variable definitions

Dependent variable

The dependent variable, CEI, is measured as the logarithm of the ratio of total CO₂ emissions to real GDP [62] and is denoted as lncei. Its construction proceeds in three steps:

First, calculation of total CO₂ emissions. Given the limited availability of official city-level data, we follow Liu and Wei [63] and adopt the emission accounting methodology recommended by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC). Consistent with data availability at the city level and following Wang et al. [64], total CO₂ emissions are decomposed into three components: emissions from direct energy consumption (D), emissions from electricity consumption (E), and emissions from heat energy consumption (H). Accordingly, the total CO₂ emissions are expressed as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ total \, CO_{2} \, emissions = D{ + }E + H $$\end{document}

Specifically, emissions from direct energy consumption (D) are obtained by multiplying the consumption of coal gas, natural gas, and liquefied petroleum gas by their respective IPCC-recommended carbon emission factors and summing across them. Emissions from electricity consumption (E) are calculated by multiplying total industrial and residential electricity consumption by the corresponding regional grid emission factors. China’s power system is divided into six regional grids, including North China, Northeast China, East China, Central China, Northwest China, and South China, and annual grid-specific carbon emission factors are published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China. Emissions from heat energy consumption (H) are estimated by first inferring coal consumption based on the total supply of steam and hot water used for district heating in each city and then multiplying the inferred coal consumption by the IPCC-recommended carbon emission factors. Total CO₂ emissions are obtained by adding up emissions from these three sources.

Second, calculation of real GDP. To ensure temporal comparability, nominal GDP is deflated by the Consumer Price Index (CPI) with 2011 as the base year (2011 = 1).

Third, final computation. lncei is computed as the logarithm of the ratio of total CO₂ emissions to real GDP.

To ensure our findings are robust to different measurement methodologies, we employ two alternative measures of CEI in Sect. "Alternative measures of CEI". First, following Chu et al. [65], we reconstruct CEI based on urban industrial CO₂ emissions. This measure is based on the consumption of natural gas, liquefied petroleum gas, and electricity, which is converted into CO₂ emissions using the 2006 IPCC emission factors and then aggregated across energy types. Based on this measure, CEI is defined as the logarithm of the ratio of the reconstructed CO₂ emissions to real GDP, denoted as lncei2. Second, we employ an additional CO₂ emissions dataset from the Carbon Emission Accounts and Datasets (CEADs), which has been widely used in recent research [66]. After obtaining total CO₂ emissions from this source, CEI is measured as the logarithm of total CO₂ emissions divided by real GDP (lncei3).

Instrumental variable

Following Zheng et al. [50], this paper constructs instruments for urban cluster-scale population agglomeration based on geographic characteristics. One is the inverse of the average terrain slope of cities within the same urban cluster, while the other is the inverse of the average geographic distance from the city to other cities in its urban cluster. It is important to note that these two geographic variables are time-invariant cross-sectional features and cannot be directly utilized in panel data analysis. To address this, each instrument is multiplied by the contemporaneous mean of ucpa within the urban cluster, generating time-varying instruments for the empirical analysis, denoted as IV1 and IV2. The validity of the instruments relies on both exogeneity and relevance. First, geographic features are inherently exogenous and have been widely used as instruments in the literature [70]. Terrain slope and intercity distances therefore theoretically satisfy the requirement of exogeneity. Second, because more rugged terrain tends to induce a more dispersed population distribution and greater geographic fragmentation, IV1 is theoretically correlated with urban cluster-scale population agglomeration. IV2 satisfies the relevance condition because greater distances to other cities increase road construction and maintenance costs, constraining population mobility and agglomeration within the urban cluster.

Channel variables

To explore the potential pathways identified in our conceptual framework, we construct two channel variables. First, green technological innovation (gti) is measured as the average of the current and one-period-lagged values of green invention patent applications per 10,000 people. We focus on invention patent applications, rather than utility model or design patents, because they better capture substantive technological novelty [71]. The inclusion of the lagged term accounts for the inherent time delay between innovation inputs and realized outputs. Second, diverse functional segment co-agglomeration (coagg) is used to capture the extent to which producer service and production functional segments are jointly concentrated within a city. Following Duranton and Puga [29] and Zheng et al. [50], we categorize functional segments into the producer service segment and the production segment. Control variables

Following the existing literature [53, 72–74], this paper includes a set of control variables to mitigate potential omitted-variable bias. ① Macroeconomic growth (gdpr). This variable reflects the level of local economic development and is measured by the annual growth rate of GDP. ② Industrial structure (ind). As a fundamental characteristic of the macroeconomic system, industrial structure potentially influences CEI. Compared with the service sector, the manufacturing sector is substantially more energy-intensive and pollution-intensive in China. We measure industrial structure by the ratio of the value added of the service sector to that of the manufacturing sector in GDP. ③ Government intervention (gov). Government intervention may influence urban development and related environmental outcomes. This variable is measured by the ratio of local government general budgetary revenue to GDP. ④ Human capital (lnhc). Human capital captures the concentration of high-skilled labor, which is a key driver of green technological innovation and may affect CEI. We measure human capital by the logarithm of the number of enrolled university students. ⑤ Financial development (lnfin). Financial development facilitates access to capital, thereby supporting green technological innovation and investments in carbon emission mitigation. This variable is measured by the logarithm of the year-end balance of deposits at financial institutions. ⑥ Traffic conditions (tra). Poor traffic conditions and congestion are important sources of energy waste and carbon emissions. This variable is quantified using per capita road area. ⑦ Green area (ga). Green area is closely associated with environmental quality and may contribute to a lower CEI. Therefore, green area is included in the analysis. This variable is measured by per capita park green space.

Next, based on the number of central cities specified in official urban cluster development plans, we further classify urban clusters into polycentric and monocentric urban clusters. Columns (3) and (4) of Table 9 indicate that the moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration is more pronounced in polycentric urban clusters. In monocentric urban clusters, economic activity and population tend to be overly concentrated in a single central city, which may intensify regional polarization and limit the capacity of surrounding cities to share congestion pressures. By contrast, polycentric urban clusters facilitate more balanced intercity coordination and resource allocation among multiple central cities. As a result, under high levels of urban-scale population agglomeration, they are better positioned to strengthen the carbon-mitigating effect of cross-scale population agglomeration.

The moderating role across varying spatial boundaries

To further examine the spatial scope of the moderating effect, we progressively expand the spatial radius used to construct the urban cluster-scale population agglomeration measure from 100 to 500 km and re-estimate the model for each specification. Figure 9 reports the estimated coefficients on the interaction term (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${upa}^{2}\times ucpa$$\end{document}) under different spatial radii. As the spatial radius increases, the absolute magnitude of the estimated coefficients gradually approaches zero, indicating that the influence of neighboring cities decays with geographic distance. This pattern suggests that the moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration is geographically bounded. Specifically, the moderating effect is stronger within a range of approximately 100–300 km. When the radius exceeds roughly 400 km, both the magnitude and statistical significance of the estimated coefficients decline markedly. These findings imply that, when coordinating urban-scale and urban cluster-scale population agglomeration to promote carbon mitigation, the spatial boundaries of urban clusters should not be expanded arbitrarily. Conclusions and recommendations

Main findings

Using panel data for 221 cities across 19 Chinese urban clusters from 2011 to 2022, this study examines the relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI, as well as the moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration. The main findings are summarized as follows.

First, urban-scale population agglomeration exhibits a significant U-shaped relationship with CEI. More importantly, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration significantly flattens this U-shaped relationship.

Second, green technological innovation and the co-agglomeration of diverse functional segments constitute important pathways through which urban cluster-scale population agglomeration exerts its moderating effect on this U-shaped relationship.

Third, the moderating effect of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration is more pronounced in eastern coastal and polycentric urban clusters and is concentrated within a limited geographic range.

Policy recommendations

Based on the above findings, the following policy recommendations are proposed. First, it is essential to accurately recognize and leverage the moderating role of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration in the relationship between urban-scale population agglomeration and CEI. In the top-level design of urban cluster development strategies, policymakers should move beyond the conventional one-size-fits-all approach that emphasizes dispersing population from central cities to sub-central and peripheral cities. The findings of this study suggest an alternative policy logic: when population agglomeration in central cities leads to an increase in CEI, efforts should be directed toward strengthening population agglomeration at the urban cluster scale to mitigate this CEI-increasing effect through broader spatial coordination.

Second, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration should be advanced by optimizing the population distribution structure across the entire urban cluster. While promoting population concentration in central cities, policymakers should guide population flows toward both the core areas of central cities and surrounding peripheral cities to better exploit the integrated agglomeration advantages of the "central city core–peripheral city" structure. Such an approach can help establish a more balanced population distribution structure and allow the carbon-mitigating effect of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration to be more fully realized.

Finally, the development of polycentric urban clusters should be fostered in an orderly manner to mitigate the CEI-increasing effects of urban-scale population agglomeration. In this process, priority should be given to two aspects. The first is to facilitate the flow and integration of green innovation resources across central cities, thereby creating favorable conditions for the formation of green innovation networks in polycentric urban clusters. The second is to support the orderly transfer and upgrading of diverse functional segments, with central cities serving as anchors to foster their cross-city co-agglomeration.

Limitations and future research

Although this study achieves its research objective by examining 19 urban clusters, it still has several limitations. For example, urban cluster-scale population agglomeration may also generate inter-urban-cluster interactions or spillover effects, particularly under China’s ongoing urban cluster development strategy. Exploring the cross-regional spillover effects of urban cluster-scale population agglomeration from a broader spatial perspective is an important topic that we leave for future research.
