Uncovering the relationship between metacognitive ability and learning engagement using a moderated mediation model
Authors: Bowen Liu, Wentao Zou, Ying Peng, Yan Xiong, Yajing Lu, Yuqin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Learning engagement is a crucial factor influencing the quality of college students’ learning. Research indicates that learning engagement is closely related to metacognitive ability, attention, and learning adaptability, but the mechanisms of interaction among these four factors remain unclear. This paper constructs a moderated mediation model to explore the direct effect of metacognitive ability on college students’ learning engagement and the mediating effect of attention while examining the moderating effect of learning adaptability. A total of 349 college students participated in this study. The results indicate that (1) there are significant positive correlations between metacognitive ability, attention, learning adaptability, and learning engagement; (2) metacognitive ability has a direct effect on learning engagement; (3) attention partially mediates the relationship between metacognitive ability and learning engagement; and (4) learning adaptability moderates the relationship between metacognitive ability and attention, with the effect of metacognitive ability on attention being stronger in the high-learning adaptability group. These findings provide theoretical and practical guidance for enhancing college students’ learning engagement.

Introduction

Learning engagement is an important factor affecting the quality of college students’ learning1. In educational practice, it is crucial to pay attention to students’ learning engagement. Studies have shown that learning engagement positively predicts college students’ academic performance and long-term future development2. However, students with lower levels of learning engagement tend to have inappropriate learning behaviours3, a poorer learning status4, less enthusiasm for learning5, poor academic performance6, and higher dropout rates7. Therefore, how to improve college students’ learning engagement in a targeted way is an urgent issue.

Metacognitive ability, attention and learning adaptability play important roles in learning engagement. Metacognitive ability is an advanced thinking ability that is crucial to enable learners to take control of their learning. It tends to occur along with meaningful learning and deep learning8, enabling learners to achieve better academic performance. Moreover, students with higher levels of attention are usually able to enter a learning state quickly9 and engage in immersive learning10. This ability leads to deeper thinking about the learning content. In addition, students with higher levels of learning adaptability tend to exhibit better behavioural, psychological, and environmental adaptability11. They are less influenced by external interferences, which increases their attention and enables them to deeply comprehend and learn new knowledge12. Therefore, an in-depth study of the mechanisms underlying the role of the three factors in learning engagement can help improve college students’ learning engagement.

Importantly, metacognitive ability, attention, learning adaptability and learning engagement appear to be closely related. However, the associations among these variables, particularly the roles of attention and learning adaptability in the metacognition–engagement link, remain insufficiently understood. Guided by self-regulated learning theory, attention control theory, attention restoration theory and cognitive developmental theory, the present study examines a moderated mediation model in which attention serves as a mediator and learning adaptability functions as a moderator. The results of this study is expected to provide further theoretical insight into college students’ learning engagement and may offer practical implications for educational practice.

Literature review and hypothesized model

Metacognitive ability and learning engagement

Metacognitive ability refers to individuals’ awareness of their own thinking and learning processes and their capacity to monitor and regulate them13. It typically involves regulatory activities such as planning, monitoring, and evaluation, which help learners optimize strategy use and cognitive engagement across different tasks and domains. It was first proposed by the American child psychologist Flavell in 197614. Flavell regarded metacognitive ability as a static knowledge structure15, whereas Brown explained metacognition as a dynamic process16. Scholars generally believe that metacognitive ability consists of three elements: “metacognitive knowledge”, “metacognitive experience” and “metacognitive monitoring”. Metacognitive knowledge is knowledge about cognition, which comes from long-term memory14. It includes knowledge of strategies, knowledge of cognitive tasks, and knowledge of self17. Metacognitive experience is the cognitive or emotional experience that accompanies cognitive activities and exists in working memory17, such as feelings of familiarity, satisfaction, and confidence18. Metacognitive monitoring is the process by which an individual consciously focuses on their ongoing cognitive processes. The individual continuously monitors, evaluates, and adjusts these processes as needed19. For example, when solving a math problem, if a student discovers that the solution is incorrect, they pause to review the problem and try another solution.

Learning engagement refers to the sustained behavioural and cognitive investment and positive affective states that students demonstrate in learning activities20,21. Behavioural engagement refers to students’ participation in academic, social, or extracurricular activities22,23. It encompasses not only the student’s engagement in the learning task but also the environment, such as time spent on the task and attendance24. Emotional engagement refers to a student’s emotional response in the classroom and identification with the school. Emotional responses include interest, optimism, boredom, and anxiety20. Cognitive engagement refers to the mental effort and cognitive resources that students invest in learning tasks. It includes the cognitive behaviours demonstrated by students, such as active thinking, problem-solving, and deep processing, when performing learning tasks25.

Self-regulated learning theory views learning as a cyclical, evolving process. In this process, learners control their learning state through metacognition, motivation and behaviour to drive their learning behaviour. They continuously optimize their level of learning engagement based on learning feedback26,27. Malik et al.28 constructed a conceptual model to show that students with high metacognitive ability can significantly enhance their learning engagement. Using the example of a high school student, Zimmerman29 showed that students who lack metacognitive skills fail to regulate their learning effectively and often struggle with academic engagement and performance.

Empirical studies have shown that metacognitive ability is significantly and positively related to students’ learning engagement. Liu et al.30 investigated the relationship among metacognitive ability, learning engagement, and learning burnout among college students (N = 431, 300 females, mean age = 19.61 ± 1.10). Using questionnaire survey data and chain mediation analysis, they found that metacognitive ability was positively correlated with learning engagement, whereas both metacognitive ability and learning engagement were negatively correlated with learning burnout. Wang et al.31 conducted two longitudinal studies of adolescents (N1 = 2,325, N2 = 207; ages 11–15 years) across 20 public schools in the United States. The results suggest that metacognitive ability is positively correlated with math engagement and that high levels of metacognitive ability help maintain students’ learning engagement over time. An et al.32 surveyed 2,989 university students from three Chinese provinces across diverse subject areas (mean age = 20.02 ± 1.44). The results indicated a positive correlation between metacognition and college students’ learning engagement. Moreover, metacognitive ability was shown to not only directly affect learning engagement but also indirectly affect it through learning strategies. Tsai et al.33 surveyed 126 students (81.75% bachelor’s degree holders; 76.98% aged 20–30 years) in the context of massive open online courses (MOOCs). The study found that the stronger the students’ metacognitive skills were, the more that they loved, enjoyed and participated in a course. The study also found that metacognitive ability can indirectly affect learning engagement through learning interest. Moreover, metacognitive ability has a significant positive predictive effect not only on learning engagement but also on different dimensions of learning engagement. Pellas34 surveyed 305 students (210 males) in different subject areas in the context of online learning. The results revealed that metacognitive ability is positively correlated with cognitive and emotional engagement. A high level of metacognitive ability helps learners adapt to the external environment and monitor internal learning engagement. Moreover, metacognitive ability enables students to self-regulate and adjust their behavioural and emotional engagement in the learning process21 to improve their academic achievement35. Based on the above research, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1

Metacognitive ability is positively related to learning engagement.

Mediating role of attention

Attention refers to the ability of mental activity to identify and focus on certain objects10. It is also the selective focus of mental activity on a given object or image. Attention is recognized as an important gateway to information and learning36. Effective attention facilitates the encoding and storage of information.

Studies have shown that metacognitive ability is associated with attention. Murphy and Castel37 conducted a study involving 96 undergraduate students (mean age = 19.32 ± 1.73 years) from the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). The study found that the use of metacognitive regulation can effectively reduce the effects of distraction. Yang et al.38 conducted an empirical neuroscience study involving 57 participants (29 dancers, mean age = 23.1 ± 2.9 years; 28 nondancers, mean age = 22.8 ± 1.6 years). The study revealed that dancers exhibit enhanced dynamic regulation of attention through neural regions associated with metacognition (NCM). Specifically, the study suggested that during a dance performance, dancers need to monitor muscle activity, peer positioning, and movement memory simultaneously, thereby improving their motor coordination and creative expression. Love et al.39 conducted a study involving 46 Australian drivers (93.5% drove a passenger car; 25 males, 21 females; mean age = 40.07 ± 16.58 years). The study found that the level of monitoring and regulation of metacognition can play a significant role in predicting the driver’s attention. Based on the above research, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2

Metacognitive ability is positively related to attention.

Attention is closely related to learning engagement. Qi et al.40 conducted an empirical study that recorded 50 undergraduate students (28 males; aged 18–22 years) in online classroom videos. Specifically, the study captured video clips every 10 s to generate a total of 30,000 clips to analyse students’ learning engagement. The study found that students’ attention, facial expression and behaviour were the core indicators used to assess learning engagement. Lin and Kim41 recruited 68 sophomore students (aged 18–25 years) majoring in art and design at Beijing Polytechnic for a quasiexperimental study on improving learning engagement. The study successfully improved students’ learning engagement by cultivating their attention. Vermeulen and Volman42 conducted a study involving 25 Dutch university social science students (21 males; aged 20–35 years) in an online learning context. The study found that various synchronous online learning activities promoted students’ behavioural engagement by stimulating their attention and focus. This evidence suggests that attention not only has a significant positive correlation with learning engagement but also predicts learning engagement. Focused attention is key to achieving a high level of learning engagement, enabling students to be fully engaged in the learning process. In contrast, when attention declines, students’ learning engagement decreases. This effect leads to students becoming disengaged from the learning process, thus hindering their learning engagement and effectiveness. Based on the above research, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H3

Attention is positively related to learning engagement.

Attention control theory proposes basic functions of the central executive system. These functions include inhibition, which utilizes attentional control to resist interference from task-irrelevant stimuli or responses, and diversion, which utilizes attentional control to maintain attention to task-relevant stimuli to increase learning engagement43,44. Attention restoration theory focuses on cognitive responses. It posits that exposure to nature promotes the recovery of depleted attentional resources, reduces cognitive fatigue, and promotes learning engagement45. Cognitive load theory suggests that during the learning process, students’ cognitive resources are limited, and an overload of information or task difficulty can lead to cognitive overload, forcing learning to cease46. The development of metacognitive ability helps students manage their cognitive load through effective learning strategies, such as information integration and task decomposition. With effective metacognitive monitoring and regulation, students can reduce their ineffective cognitive load and maintain sustained learning engagement.

Rummel and Meiser47 recruited 268 students for an empirical study, which was divided into preexperiments (N = 67), Experiments 1a (N = 95) and 1b (N = 106). The researchers compared the performance of attention allocation strategies under different conditions by randomly grouping them. The study found that metacognition directly affects attention allocation strategies, and changes in attention allocation affect task engagement and performance (e.g., reaction time). Jiang et al.48 recruited 112 college students (95 females; mean age = 21.91 ± 1.81 years) from diverse academic backgrounds for an eye-tracking experiment. The researchers found that learners with stronger metacognitive abilities had more gaze and saccade counts and were thus better able to engage in the task. High-metacognitive ability enables learners to regulate and manage their attention autonomously and reduce distractions. A lack of metacognitive ability may make it difficult for students to achieve effective self-management and monitoring in the learning process. This inability can lead to difficulties in maintaining sustained attention to a task, causing insufficient participation in learning. Based on the above research, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H4

Attention positively mediates the relationship between metacognitive ability and learning engagement.

Moderating role of learning adaptability

Adaptability refers to the degree to which individuals are able to adjust their cognitive, behavioural, and affective functioning in the face of changing environments or situations49. Learning adaptability refers to an individual’s positive attitude towards establishing their educational goals and accomplishing academic tasks, as well as the effectiveness of their efforts to meet these needs and their overall level of adaptation to the learning environment50. Learning adaptability can be viewed as a dynamic process, which reflects a perspective that focuses on the dynamic balance between the individual student and the environment49. With the emergence of various emerging network technologies, learners need to adapt to various learning modes such as the combination of online and offline, personalized learning and collaborative learning modes, as well as the combination of traditional and digital teaching resources. These conditions pose a significant challenge to learners’ learning adaptability51.

Metacognitive ability is associated with learning adaptability. Reinforcement theory suggests that continuous stimulation leads to changes in cognitive and behavioural responses in the brain, enabling adaptation to different environments and demands52. Research has shown that metacognitive ability helps learners adapt to the ever-changing world while allowing them to acquire new knowledge and skills53. Darvish et al.54 conducted a study involving 36 female middle school students (aged 13–15 years) from the city of Tehran, Iran. The study found that training learners in metacognitive strategies can effectively reduce test anxiety and improve adaptability. Ntim55 conducted a study involving 240 s-year students (aged 15–18 years) from four Ghanaian senior high schools. The study found that learners who applied metacognitive and self-regulated learning strategies demonstrated greater adaptability. Colthorpe et al.56 conducted a study involving undergraduate biomedical science students and found that the use of metacognitive learning can help learners adjust and reflect on their learning strategies to better adapt and improve their learning performance.

There is also a correlation between attention and learners’ learning adaptability. A previous study revealed that attention is an important aspect of human adaptability57. Learning adaptability involves students’ regulation of cognitive, behavioural, and emotional aspects. Executive dysfunction in attention deficit patients can affect cognitive and behavioural regulation, thus posing a great challenge to their adaptive capacity12. A psychological and neurophysiological study (N = 12; aged 21–31 years) found that attention enhances the adaptation of the visuomotor system58. Climie et al.59 conducted a study involving 41 children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (34 males; mean age = 10.29 ± 0.80 years). The study showed that children with attention deficits have significantly lower emotion comprehension and adaptive emotional intelligence scores than non-attention-deficit children do.

According to cognitive developmental theory, learning adaptation is the self-adjustment of individuals to adapt to the learning environment and their own learning needs60. This adjustment requires the constant application of metacognitive monitoring and regulation skills. Therefore, students with high learning adaptability develop higher metacognitive ability. In addition, their ability to control and regulate their attention is also enhanced. Tang and Posner57 found through controlled experiments that students with high learning adaptability could improve their attention through metacognitive training and be less disturbed by emotional factors. Students with low learning adaptability struggle to effectively increase their attention through metacognitive abilities because of high stress and low emotional states. Mawarni et al.61 conducted a study involving 31 students (17 females) from the Malang Senior High School in Indonesia. The researchers introduced a metacognitive framework in a secondary school biology classroom and found that learners utilizing metacognitive thinking for learning were able to enhance their adjustment to the biology classroom. Highly adaptable students adapt and manage learning environments effectively, such as “focusing on solving a task rather than small talk”. In summary, learning adaptability may dynamically regulate the strength of the effect of metacognitive ability on attention. Students with high levels of learning adaptability are better able to utilize metacognitive skills to regulate their attention, thereby enhancing their performance in learning activities. In contrast, students with low levels of learning adaptability lack effective metacognitive monitoring and regulation skills, making them more susceptible to distractions (e.g., social media or environmental noise), which results in difficulty in maintaining attention. Based on the above research, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H5

Learning adaptability positively moderates the relationship between metacognitive ability and attention.

Hypothesized model

The literature review suggests that attention may serve as a mediating variable through which metacognitive ability influences learning engagement. Moreover, learning adaptability may serve as a moderating variable through which metacognitive ability influences attention. This study takes college students as the research object to explore the relationships among metacognitive ability, attention, learning adaptability and learning engagement. Based on the above theories, a moderated mediation model is constructed with metacognitive ability as the independent variable, attention as the mediating variable, learning adaptability as the moderating variable, and learning engagement as the dependent variable, as shown in Fig. 1.

Methodology

Participants

In this study, college students from a public university in central China were selected as the target group. A convenience sampling method was used to distribute the questionnaires. The study created an electronic questionnaire and published it on Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/). The questionnaire was distributed to four offline classes. The participants completed the questionnaire in March 2024. The entire process took one week. Among the 366 questionnaires collected, the researchers screened them strictly to ensure the authenticity and validity of the data. Specifically, first, two attention check items were included in the questionnaire to screen out participants who did not answer the questions carefully (e.g., ‘Please select “Strongly Disagree” for this item’). If a participant provided inconsistent answers (e.g., “Strongly Agree”), their questionnaire was deleted (n = 5). Second, the questionnaire had a total of 40 items (5 demographic information items; 33 scale items; and 2 attention check items). Following Huang et al.62 and Meade and Craig63, if a participant’s response time was less than 120 s (40 × 3 s) or more than 800 s (40 × 20 s), their questionnaire was deleted (n = 12). Finally, 349 valid questionnaires were retained, resulting in a response rate of 95.36%. Among the 349 valid questionnaires, participants took 122 s to 750 s to complete them. The demographics of the students who participated in the survey were as follows. In terms of gender distribution, the sample consisted of 93 males (26.65%) and 256 females (73.35%). In terms of grade distribution, the sample consisted of 33 freshmen (9.46%), 35 sophomores (10.03%), 246 juniors (70.49%), and 35 seniors (10.03%). The age range was 18 to 24 years, with an average age of 19.89 years (SD = 0.853). In terms of the distribution of majors, 144 students (41.26%) were majoring in education, 165 students (47.28%) were majoring in art, and 40 students (11.46%) were majoring in literature. This study was reviewed and approved by the Central China Normal University Ethic Committee, Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Approval No. [CCNU-IRB-202409002 A]; approved on 4 September 2024). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and the data were used for research purposes only.

Measurements

In alignment with the methodological aims of this study, a two-round expert review was conducted to establish content validity and iteratively refine the initial item pool prior to large-scale administration. This process constitutes an expert-driven content validation—distinct from a full Delphi study—and is methodologically justified when the objective is to rigorously assess item representativeness and linguistic clarity using a small number of highly specialized experts64,65.

Expert selection criteria. Two senior experts were recruited through purposive sampling based on predefined eligibility criteria. Both experts (a) held doctoral degrees; (b) held associate senior professional titles or above; (c) had demonstrated expertise in the fields of education and psychology, respectively; (d) had extensive experience in cross-cultural adaptation/development of assessment scales and psychometric evaluation; and (e) possessed bilingual academic proficiency in Chinese and English to ensure accurate judgment of item meaning and wording equivalence. In addition, both experts had substantial teaching and research experience related to the constructs measured in this study (metacognitive ability, learning engagement, attention, and learning adaptability). To enhance rigor under a small-expert design, we applied a strict consensus criterion: only items deemed representative by both experts were retained without further revision.

Two-round review procedure. The initial pool comprised 38 items: 12 items for metacognitive ability, 11 items for learning engagement, 7 items for attention, and 8 items for learning adaptability. The expert review materials were distributed in paper form during offline, in-person delivery. Along with the rating questionnaire, we provided an item-development brief that explained the study purpose, the questionnaire dimensions, and the number of items in each dimension. Experts were given one week to complete the review. An expert consultation questionnaire was provided to each expert to rate the representativeness of every item using a 5-point scale (1 = not representative at all; 5 = highly representative). Experts were also invited to provide qualitative comments, including suggestions on wording, ambiguity, construct alignment, redundancy, and cultural appropriateness.

In Round 1, the two experts independently evaluated all 38 items and returned quantitative ratings alongside annotated written feedback. Following data collection, the research team conducted a dual-track synthesis: (i) quantitative ratings were aggregated per item, and items receiving a score ≤ 3 from either expert were flagged; (ii) qualitative comments were thematically coded and mapped to specific items and feedback categories. Items flagged on either quantitative or qualitative grounds—i.e., low representativeness, ambiguous wording, or redundancy—were designated for joint re-evaluation in Round 2.

In Round 2, the experts convened for a structured, face-to-face consensus meeting. They jointly re-examined all flagged items against the original construct definitions and contextual specifications. Decisions followed a prespecified decision tree: (a) deletion occurred only upon unanimous agreement that an item lacked construct relevance or introduced redundancy; (b) rewording was implemented when both experts affirmed the underlying construct validity but identified opportunities to enhance clarity, reduce ambiguity, or improve contextual resonance; (c) retention without change required consensus on both representativeness (≥ 4) and linguistic adequacy. The outcome was a finalized, consensus-validated item set for each scale, fully compliant with the a priori consensus rule.

Pretest for clarity and comprehensibility. Following expert-based content validation, a small pretest was conducted with 10 students. Students were invited to read the questionnaire and indicate whether any items were difficult to understand or confusing in wording. All participants reported that they understood the items, and no additional revisions were required. The final versions of the metacognitive ability scale, learning engagement scale, attention scale, and learning adaptability scale were thus established. All items were scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The final items are presented in the Appendix. This study references publicly published questionnaires, with the sources properly cited, and the use of the questionnaires has been authorized.

Metacognitive ability scale

The state metacognitive questionnaire66 and the metacognitive awareness questionnaire67 were referenced and revised. The validity and reliability of the original instrument have been empirically substantiated among Chinese university students in prior research68. In the first round of review, two items had scores that were less than or equal to 3 points. After the second round of review, the experts agreed that these two items needed to be removed. The final questionnaire contains 10 items. The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) is 0.792, the validity coefficient (KMO) is 0.848, and the factor loading coefficient of each item is greater than 0.4.

Learning engagement scale

The learning engagement scale of college students21 was referenced and revised. The validity and reliability of the original instrument have been empirically substantiated among Chinese university students in prior research69. In the first round of review, one item had a score that was less than or equal to 3 points. After the second round of review, the experts agreed that this item needed to be removed. The final questionnaire contains 10 items. The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) is 0.874, the validity coefficient (KMO) is 0.842, and the factor loading coefficient of each item is greater than 0.4.

Attention scale

The attention scale of college students70 was referenced and revised. The validity and reliability of the original instrument have been empirically substantiated among university students in prior research. In the first round of review, one item had a score that was less than or equal to 3 points. After the second round of review, the experts agreed that this item needed to be removed. The final questionnaire contains 6 items. The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) is 0.820, the validity coefficient (KMO) is 0.798, and the factor loading coefficient of each item is greater than 0.4.

Learning adaptability scale

The college student adaptation scale50 was adapted and revised. The validity and reliability of the original instrument have been empirically substantiated among Chinese university students in prior research71. In the first round of review, one item had a score that was less than or equal to 3 points. After the second round of review, the experts agreed that this item needed to be removed. The final questionnaire contains 7 items. The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) is 0.874, the validity coefficient (KMO) is 0.842, and the factor loading coefficient of each item is greater than 0.4.

Data analysis

SPSS 27 (version 27.0; IBM Corp.) and the PROCESS macro were used to test for common method bias, normality, and multicollinearity and to conduct a descriptive analysis, correlation analysis, and mediation analysis to explore the effects of metacognitive ability and attention on the learning engagement of college students. On this basis, the mediation effect was further used to explore the moderating effect of learning adaptability. Since the data of the four scales in this study originated from the same subjects, common method bias was tested with the Harman univariate test72. The results show that there are 8 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variation interpretation rate of the first factor is 30.926%, which is less than the critical criterion of 40%. These results indicate that there is no obvious common method bias problem in the data of this study. The data were tested for normality, which revealed that the maximum absolute value of kurtosis is much less than 10 and that the maximum absolute value of skewness is much less than 3, which basically conforms to a normal distribution. The multicollinearity test revealed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values of the four variables ranged from 1.671 to 2.194 (< 10), indicating that there was no collinearity problem between the variables.

Descriptive analysis was used to quantify the following four variables: metacognitive ability, learning engagement, attention, and learning adaptability. Correlation analysis tested whether there was a correlation between these four variables. On the basis of the correlation analysis, this study used metacognitive ability as the independent variable, attention as the mediating variable, and learning engagement as the dependent variable. Mediating effect analysis was performed using Model 4 in the PROCESS macro to explore the direct and indirect effects of metacognitive ability and attention on learning engagement. The bootstrap sampling method (5,000 times) was used, and the confidence level was set to 95%. On the basis of the mediating effect analysis, this study used learning adaptability as the moderating variable and standardized each variable. A moderated mediation model was constructed by Model 7 in the PROCESS macro to explore the moderating effect of learning adaptability.

Results

Descriptive analysis and correlation analysis

The results of the descriptive analysis (Table 1) revealed that the mean score of metacognitive ability was 3.835 (SD = 0.437), that of learning engagement was 3.237 (SD = 0.672), that of attention was 3.304 (SD = 0.691), and that of learning adaptability was 3.265 (SD = 0.684). The results of the correlation analysis (Table 1) revealed significant positive correlations between metacognitive ability, learning engagement, attention and learning adaptability. Specifically, metacognitive ability was significantly positively correlated with learning engagement (r = 0.612, p < 0.001, CI = [0.531, 0.680]), attention (r = 0.545, p < 0.001, CI = [0.464, 0.622]), and learning adaptability (r = 0.448, p < 0.001, CI = [0.353, 0.530]). There was a significant positive correlation between learning engagement and attention (r = 0.641, p < 0.001, CI = [0.548, 0.718]) and learning adaptability (r = 0.569, p < 0.001, CI = [0.477, 0.653]). There was a significant positive correlation between attention and learning adaptability (r = 0.574, p < 0.001, CI = [0.493, 0.650]). The correlation coefficients among the four variables were all less than 0.8, further indicating that there was no multicollinearity problem73 and thus verifying the variables were suitable for subsequent regression analysis.

Indirect effect of attention

Discussion

Direct effect of metacognitive ability on learning engagement

The results of the study showed that metacognitive ability has a direct effect on learning engagement, which supports H1. These results are consistent with the findings of An et al.32, Pellas34, Tsai et al.33 and Wang et al.31 Namely, their research revealed that metacognitive ability is significantly positively correlated with learning engagement in different education stages (basic education, higher education), different learning scenarios (MOOCs, online learning) and different cultural backgrounds (Chinese and American). According to self-regulated learning theory, students with high metacognitive ability demonstrate more positive regulation of the learning process, making them better engaged in learning. Students with high metacognitive ability are able to set learning goals that match their abilities and keep moving towards them. Furthermore, students with high metacognitive ability are able to take more learning actions and self-regulate their learning behaviours, so they are less likely to quit halfway through their studies. This ability also helps to increase their learning engagement74. Moreover, students with high metacognitive ability know what cognitive strategies are available for performing a cognitive activity. They can compare the strengths and weaknesses of various cognitive strategies in solving a cognitive task. They also know which cognitive strategies to use under different conditions75. These cognitive activities require students to invest more cognitive effort, which helps them overcome learning difficulties and increases learning engagement. High metacognitive ability enables students to self-monitor and self-adjust the learning process76. On the one hand, students can pay more conscious attention to their learning progress and identify learning problems. On the other hand, students can take appropriate measures to improve their learning performance, which in turn helps improve their learning engagement. This study, which was conducted in the context of traditional offline classrooms with college students from diverse academic disciplines as participants, empirically confirmed the positive correlation between metacognitive ability and learning engagement. The implication of this study is that, in higher education, cultivating college students’ metacognitive ability may help them maintain high levels of engagement when facing learning difficulties, ultimately improving their learning outcomes.

Indirect effect of attention

The results revealed that, first, metacognitive ability is positively related to attention. Second, attention is positively related to learning engagement. Third, attention has a partial mediating effect on the relationship between metacognitive ability and learning engagement. Therefore, H2, H3 and H4 are supported. These results are similar to those of Lin and Kim41, Murphy and Castel37, Jiang et al.48, and Vermeulen and Volman42. Their research revealed the dynamic change in attention and its effects via different methods (experimental and quasiexperimental studies): namely, metacognitive abilities (e.g., self-monitoring, strategy use) can drive learning engagement (e.g., behavioural involvement, task immersion) by increasing the stability of attention (e.g., reducing distractions, extending attention duration). According to attention control theory, attention needs to be controlled and distributed according to metacognitive ability. Focused and sustained attention enables students to think deeply, which in turn promotes learning engagement. During the learning process, students with high metacognitive ability can manage their attention better47. When students have a clear understanding of their learning status and needs, they can more accurately determine the importance of various information and focus their attention on key information. Moreover, students are able to understand and think about the learning content more deeply and thus participate more actively in the learning process. In addition, the ability to monitor and regulate metacognition helps learners maintain stable and persistent attention39. During the learning process, students may encounter various interferences and difficulties, resulting in weakened or distracted attention. At this time, the monitoring ability of metacognition can help learners identify attention problems over time. It can promote regulating strategies (e.g., taking a short break) to restore the state of concentration and maintain learning engagement. This study highlights the partial indirect effect of attention on the relationship between metacognitive ability and learning engagement. The implication of this study is that, in higher education, educators seeking to improve college students’ learning engagement should not only foster students’ metacognitive ability but also optimize attention management.

Moderating effect of learning adaptability

This study revealed that learning adaptability moderated the relationship between metacognitive ability and attention, which supports H5. The moderating effect was more pronounced in the high-learning adaptability group. These results are similar to those of Colthorpe et al.56, Mawarni et al.61, and Ntim55. Their research revealed that highly adaptive learners can flexibly select strategies that align with the learning context based on metacognitive abilities (e.g., task evaluation and strategy monitoring), resist interference from both themselves and the environment, and maintain sustained attention (e.g., remaining focused on problem solving even in noisy environments). According to cognitive developmental theory, students with high learning adaptability are less affected by the external environment. In addition, they exhibit better monitoring and regulation of metacognitive ability, resulting in more focused and sustained attention. Students with high learning adaptability are better at adapting learning strategies to learning tasks and situations, finding ways to improve their learning engagement, and increasing their attention. Moreover, students with high learning adaptability usually have better metacognitive regulation55. They are able to remain calm and focused when faced with learning challenges and difficulties, thus reducing anxiety and distraction. Additionally, students with high learning adaptability usually display interest in and motivation for learning. They are more willing to invest time and energy in learning. This positive attitude towards learning helps them better utilize their metacognitive ability to monitor and adjust their attention. This study revealed that the positive effect of metacognitive ability on attention is not constant and that its strength is significantly moderated by learner adaptability. Especially in changeable or highly challenging learning scenarios, the higher the level of adaptability is, the more significant the effect of metacognition on attention. The implication of this study is that, in higher education, educators should focus on college students’ learning adaptability, helping them maintain a positive attitude and making appropriate adjustments to cope with varying learning modes and resources in a constantly changing learning environment.

Future directions

This study has some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, this study design cannot reveal causal relationships between variables. Future research will follow the same group of participants at multiple time points (e.g., at the beginning, middle, and end of a semester) to collect repeated measurements. Cross-lagged analysis can be used to infer causal relationships (e.g., whether metacognition at the beginning of the semester predicts learning engagement during the semester). Second, the participants were students attending a public university in China. Future research could expand the sample to increase the generalizability of the findings. Future research could also collect participant profiles, such as the students’ GPA and whether they have any learning difficulties or disabilities that may affect the variables studied. Third, although the self-report method is widely used, its subjectivity makes the data susceptible to individual bias, emotional state, and other factors. Future research can build algorithmic models through students’ educational big data to obtain variables such as metacognitive ability and learning engagement. Fourth, future research can explore other mediating and moderating variables to reveal other influences on college students’ learning engagement. Finally, future research could practice the strategies proposed in this study in real-life situations to test the effectiveness of these strategies in improving college students’ learning engagement.

Conclusion

This study constructed a moderated mediation model to examine the associations among metacognitive ability, learning engagement, attention and learning adaptability in a sample of Chinese undergraduates. The results revealed that metacognitive ability, attention, learning adaptability and learning engagement were positively correlated. In the tested model, metacognitive ability was positively associated with learning engagement, and the indirect pathway via attention was significant, suggesting that attention statistically accounted for part of the association between metacognitive ability and learning engagement. In addition, learning adaptability moderated the association between metacognitive ability and attention, such that the association was stronger at higher levels of learning adaptability.

This study has significant theoretical and practical implications. From a theoretical perspective, previous studies have thoroughly explored the relationship between metacognitive ability and learning engagement. However, there is a lack of research on the mechanisms of metacognitive ability, attention, learning adaptability and learning engagement. This study constructed a moderated mediation model that revealed the mediating role of attention and the moderating role of learning adaptability. The model provides theoretical support for improving college students’ learning engagement.

From a practical perspective, the observed associations suggest that metacognitive ability, attention management, and learning adaptability are promising targets for educational practice and future intervention research aimed at supporting learning engagement. First, according to self-regulated learning theory, educators can design specialized learning activities, such as reflecting on learning, making learning plans, and monitoring learning progress, to develop students’ metacognitive ability. Second, according to attention control theory, exposure to the natural environment can promote the recovery of directed attention77. If students have difficulty accessing the natural environment, they can create a quiet, clean study environment to minimize distractions and improve concentration. Students can also use a Schulte grid78 and jigsaw puzzles to train attention. Finally, according to cognitive developmental theory, the design of a course has a significant influence on students’ adaptability. Educators need to fully consider the cognitive characteristics and developmental needs of college students. For example, they should set clear learning objectives and adopt diverse teaching methods that help improve students’ adaptive capacity79. Moreover, students can regularly reflect on their learning methods and learning status, identify deficiencies and make adjustments. In this way, they can adapt to different learning stages and learning environments. Through these practical measures, students can proactively manage their own learning process, thus improving their learning engagement.
