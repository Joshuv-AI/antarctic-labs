Posterior cerebellar degeneration associates with executive dysfunction in multiple system atrophy: a voxel-based and atlas-based morphometric study
Authors: Yoko Taniguchi, Yuto Uchida, Keita Sakurai, Haruto Shibata, Hiroyasu Inoue, Yuta Madokoro, Toyohiro Sato, Teppei Fujioka, Masayuki Mizuno, Masahiro Oomura, Kenichi Oishi, Noriyuki Matsukawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExecutive dysfunction is frequently observed in multiple system atrophy (MSA), yet its neuroanatomical substrates remain incompletely characterized. Emerging evidence suggests that the cerebellum contributes to higher cognitive functions beyond motor control.ObjectiveTo investigate structural cerebellar alterations associated with executive dysfunction in patients with MSA using complementary voxel-based and atlas-based morphometric analyses.MethodsIn this case–control study, 27 patients with clinically established MSA and 19 age-matched healthy controls underwent 3D T1-weighted MRI. Voxel-based morphometry and subsequent atlas-based cerebellar volumetry were performed to identify regional brain volume differences between groups, adjusting for age, sex, total intracranial volume, and MSA clinical subtype (MSA-P vs. MSA-C). Executive function was assessed with the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB). Partial correlations between regional cerebellar volumes and FAB scores were examined within the MSA group.ResultsCompared with healthy controls, patients with MSA showed significant gray matter volume reductions in the bilateral putamen and cerebellar cortex, as well as white matter reductions extending from the brainstem to the middle cerebellar peduncles. Atlas-based analyses demonstrated reduced normalized volumes in bilateral cerebellar white matter and left vermian VIIIA, whereas larger volumes were observed in bilateral vermian Crus II and left lobule IV. Within the MSA group, FAB scores showed nominal positive associations with left VIIIB, bilateral VIIIA, and right vermis IX volumes, without surviving multiple-comparison correction.ConclusionsPosterior cerebellar degeneration may be related to executive dysfunction in MSA. These exploratory findings warrant validation in larger longitudinal studies with detailed cognitive and motor assessments.Keywords: Atlas-based analysis, Cerebellum, Magnetic resonance imaging, Multiple system atrophy, Voxel-based morphometry

Introduction

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by autonomic failure, parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, and pyramidal signs [1]. Motor manifestations constitute a defining feature of MSA and have consequently been the primary focus of clinical and research investigations. Beyond motor symptoms, cognitive impairment has increasingly been recognized as a clinically relevant feature, which can substantially compromise patients' quality of life [2], [3], [4].

Cognitive deficits in MSA are heterogeneous and involve multiple domains, with executive dysfunction emerging as the most consistently observed and prominent impairment [5], [6]. Both neuropathological and neuroimaging findings support the concept that disruption of basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical circuits is a contributor to executive dysfunction, reflecting the central role of fronto-subcortical pathways in executive control [7]. From a network perspective, executive control is not solely supported by basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical circuitry, but rather relies on broader distributed neural networks [8]. The cerebellum is recognized to contribute to cognitive processes via cerebello-frontal networks [9], raising our hypothesis that disease-specific cerebellar degeneration in MSA may influence executive performance.

To date, anatomical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have characterized the structural substrates of MSA at a whole-brain level. At this level of analysis, structural changes have been observed in core subcortical regions such as the putamen, brainstem, and cerebellum, as well as more variable involvement of the frontal lobes and cerebral white matter [10], [11], [12]. However, prior work has been limited by methodological heterogeneity and by the anatomical resolution of approaches used to characterize cerebellar subregions. Whole-brain voxel-based analyses are well suited for detecting spatially distributed or large-magnitude structural alterations without requiring a priori anatomical hypotheses [13]. Because voxel-wise approaches rely on single-voxel estimates and are subject to substantial local variability and multiple-comparison penalties, they may be less sensitive to subtle changes confined to specific anatomical subdivisions, increasing the risk of Type II error [14]. In contrast, atlas-based analysis refers to a region-of-interest–based approach in which cerebellar subregional volumes are quantified using predefined anatomical parcellations, based on the three-dimensional MRI atlas of the human cerebellum delineated by Schmahmann et al. [15]. By averaging signals within these anatomically defined regions, this method improves signal-to-noise ratio and statistical robustness. Such regional aggregation may enhance sensitivity to small but functionally meaningful volumetric variations within cerebellar lobules and the vermis [16]. To leverage the complementary strengths of these approaches, we performed both voxel-wise and atlas-based analyses in the present study.

In this context, we used voxel-based morphometry (VBM) to conduct an exploratory whole-brain group comparison between MSA patients and healthy controls (HC), followed by atlas-based volumetric analyses to assess region-specific associations with executive dysfunctions. The present study aimed to characterize the structural substrates of executive dysfunction in MSA, with a particular focus on cerebellar subregions, using a combined voxel-wise and atlas-based volumetric approach.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this case-control study, we retrospectively enrolled 27 patients (12 men and 15 women; mean age ± SD, 66.1 ± 6.4 years) who met the Movement Disorder Society criteria 2022 for the diagnosis of clinically established MSA [17] at the Department of Neurology, Nagoya City University Hospital (Nagoya, Japan), between January 2013 and February 2022. Nineteen age-matched HC individuals (10 men and 9 women; mean age ± SD, 69.3 ± 7.1 years) were also included. Individuals with a history of head injury, cerebrovascular disease, metabolic disease, severe psychiatric illness, or abnormal findings on brain imaging were excluded. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagoya City University Hospital. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived, and an opt-out procedure was implemented and publicly disclosed in accordance with institutional and national ethical guidelines.

Neurological features and cognitive assessments

Neurological features relevant to the established criteria for the diagnosis of MSA [17] were extracted from medical records within a two-month window before and after the MRI examination. Similarly, global cognitive function, assessed by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and executive function, assessed by the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB), were obtained within the same time window. MMSE is a screening test that primarily assesses orientation, calculation, and language abilities [18], with total scores ranging from 0 to 30, where lower scores indicate greater cognitive impairment. In contrast, FAB has been widely used to assess executive dysfunction in patients with MSA and related disorders, reflecting impairments in frontal–subcortical and cerebello–frontal networks [19]. The FAB score ranges from 0 to 18, with lower scores indicating more severe executive dysfunction [20].

MRI acquisition and processing

MRI scans at baseline were acquired from each subject using a 3 T MR system (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted structural images were scanned by magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition with a gradient echo sequence using the following parameters: repetition time = 7.3 milliseconds, echo time = 2.4 milliseconds, flip angle = 9, inversion time = 900 milliseconds, shot interval = 1900 milliseconds, field of view = 256 × 256 × 176 mm3, matrix size = 256 × 256, and slice thickness = 1 mm, resulting in isotropic voxel dimensions of 1 mm3. Additionally, T2-weighted turbo-spin-echo, fluid attenuated inversion recovery, diffusion-weighted, and T2*-weighted images were acquired to screen for structural or pathological abnormalities.

For the VBM analysis, the 3D T1-weighted images were first segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid. Tissue segmentation and volumetric estimation were performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM12; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Based on the segmented images, a study-specific template was generated with the Diffeomorphic Anatomical Registration Through Exponentiated Lie Algebra (DARTEL) algorithm. Individual GM and WM maps were subsequently normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute standard space and spatially smoothed using an isotropic Gaussian kernel with an 8-mm full width at half maximum, following a previously validated VBM pipeline [21]. Voxel-wise analyses were restricted to the respective GM or WM compartments using explicit masks generated from the segmented tissue maps. In addition, an absolute masking threshold of 0.2 was applied in SPM12 to exclude low-probability voxels at tissue boundaries.

For the atlas-based analysis, the 3D T1-weighted images were parcellated into whole-brain anatomical regions using MRICloud, an automated large-deformation diffeomorphic metric mapping and multi-atlas label-fusion algorithm-based platform available online (https://mricloud.org/). In the present study, we selected the “JHU-NTU-cerebellum_327labels_9atlases_M2_252_V1” segmentation atlas. The cerebellar GM in the present atlas is subdivided into 40 anatomically detailed cerebellar lobules and vermis, including lobules I–X, Crus I–II, and the corresponding vermian subdivisions according to the Schmahmann atlas [15]. These 40 atlas labels are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The remaining internal portion of the cerebellum is labeled as “CerebellumWM”. The resulting parcellation maps were visually reviewed and, when necessary, manually corrected to ensure accurate anatomical correspondence prior to final metric extraction. An example of cerebellar parcellation of this study is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables, according to their distributional characteristics. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons between the MSA and HC groups were performed using Welch's t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Voxel-wise group comparisons were performed in GM and WM, respectively, using two-sample t-tests, with age, sex, total intracranial volume, and MSA clinical subtype (MSA-P vs. MSA-C) included as covariates. The total intracranial volume for VBM was estimated from the tissue segmentation results in SPM12. The initial voxel threshold was set to P = 0.001 uncorrected. A significance level of P value = 0.05 was applied with correction for multiple comparisons using the cluster-level family-wise error (FWE). Similarly, voxel-wise multiple correlation analyses were performed to examine the association between regional GM and WM volumes and FAB scores. The same statistical thresholds were applied (voxel-level P < 0.001 uncorrected, with cluster-level FWE correction at P < 0.05).

In line with our a priori hypothesis, the atlas-based analysis was restricted to cerebellar regions. The volumes of the cerebellar GM, subdivided into 40 regions, and cerebellar WM were normalized to total intracranial volume, calculated as the sum of GM, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid anatomical regions. These normalized volumes were compared between groups using analysis of covariance, with age, sex, and MSA subtype as covariates. The P values obtained from the atlas-based analyses were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were additionally reported as Hedges' g to provide a small-sample bias–corrected standardized estimate of the group differences. Finally, partial correlation analyses were performed to assess associations between the normalized volumes and FAB scores, with the same covariates included. Disease duration, although potentially relevant to regional atrophy, was strongly correlated with age in our cohort, resulting in substantial multicollinearity (variance inflation factor > 10) when both were entered into the same regression model. To maintain statistical stability, we retained age as the primary covariate given its broader generalizability. While uncorrected P values are reported for descriptive purposes, statistical significance was defined based on FDR-corrected P values <0.05. Given the well-recognized impact of motor impairment on performance in motor-dependent cognitive tasks such as the FAB, we sought to account for potential motor confounding using available clinical information. Because this study was retrospective and item-level FAB subscores were not consistently available in the medical records, we could not calculate a modified FAB score excluding motor-dependent subitems such as Motor programming and Inhibitory control (Go/No-Go). Therefore, we performed supplementary analyses using a proxy measure of motor impairment. A motor feature burden score (range 0–4) was constructed based on the presence of the following clinical features documented in the medical records: ataxic gait, limb ataxia, cerebellar dysarthria, and levodopa–resistant parkinsonism. This score was included as an additional covariate in the partial correlation analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB version R2021a (the MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA) and Python version 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE). Reported results include 95% confidence intervals (CI) when applicable.

Results

Participant characteristics

The clinical characteristics of study participants are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed in age or sex between the MSA and HC groups. The mean disease duration in the MSA group was 4.4 ± 4.0 years. The mean MMSE and FAB scores in the MSA group were 27.3 ± 3.0 and 13.3 ± 2.8, respectively. Based on the predominance of motor features, patients were classified as MSA-C (n = 7) or MSA-P (n = 20), according to the presence of three or more cerebellar signs. All patients fulfilled the MRI markers for clinically established MSA [17], including atrophy of the putamen, pons, middle cerebellar peduncle, or cerebellum; the presence of the “hot cross bun” sign; or increased diffusivity in the putamen or middle cerebellar peduncle.

VBM analysis

Compared with HC, patients with MSA exhibited significant GM volume reductions in the bilateral putamen and cerebellar regions, as well as significant WM volume reductions in the brainstem and bilateral middle cerebellar peduncles (Fig. 1). The largest GM cluster was centered in the right cerebellar lobule VIIIA (peak at [10, −48, −60], T = 5.88) and extended across the bilateral cerebellar hemispheres and vermis into the brainstem (cluster size = 11,149, cluster-level FWE-corrected P value <0.05). A significant cluster was also centered in the medial superior frontal gyrus (peak at [−3, 9, 33], T = 5.71), extending into the bilateral putamen and anterior insula (cluster size = 2092, cluster-level FWE-corrected P value <0.05). For WM, the largest cluster, centered in the left cerebellar WM (peak at [−8, −69, −28], T = 4.64) extended anteriorly into the middle cerebellar peduncles and pontine (cluster size = 6992, cluster-level FWE-corrected P value <0.05). In contrast, no significant voxel-wise correlations were observed between regional GM or WM volumes and FAB scores at the predefined statistical threshold.

Atlas-based analysis

Atlas-based volumetry using the JHU cerebellar atlas revealed group differences across several cerebellar regions (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). MSA showed reduced volumes in the bilateral cerebellar white matter and left VIIIA of the vermis (uncorrected P < 0.05), with large effect sizes (Hedges' g ranging from −0.91 to −0.77). Conversely, the bilateral vermian Crus II and left IV lobule demonstrated larger normalized volumes in the MSA group compared with HC, as reflected by positive Hedges' g values ranging from 0.73 to 0.84. To further explore the clinical relevance of regional cerebellar volumes, partial correlation analyses were performed within the MSA group to examine associations between normalized regional volumes and FAB scores, adjusting for age, sex, and MSA subtype (Fig. 2). Positive associations were observed for left lobule VIIIB (r = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.21–0.77, P = 0.003, FDR-corrected P = 0.127), left lobule VIIIA (r = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.12–0.73, P = 0.012, FDR-corrected P = 0.229), right lobule VIIIA (r = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.09–0.71, P = 0.017, FDR-corrected P = 0.229), and right vermis IX (r = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.05–0.69, P = 0.027, FDR-corrected P = 0.272). None of these associations remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Supplementary analyses incorporating the motor feature burden score as an additional covariate yielded similar patterns of association between cerebellar volumes and FAB scores. The observed relationships were generally maintained and, in some regions, appeared more robust after adjustment (Supplementary Fig. 3). Because the findings derived from the atlas-based analyses did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons using the FDR procedure, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Discussion

In this case-control study combining voxel-based and atlas-based morphometry, we investigated the structural substrates of executive dysfunction in MSA with a particular focus on cerebellar subregions. At the whole-brain level, voxel-wise analysis confirmed prominent gray matter reductions in the bilateral putamen and cerebellar cortex, along with white matter degeneration extending from the brainstem to the middle cerebellar peduncles. Complementary atlas-based analyses further suggested region-specific alterations within posterior cerebellar hemispheric and vermian subdivisions. Importantly, within the MSA group, larger normalized volumes in posterior cerebellar regions, including lobules VIIIA, VIIIB, and vermis IX, were associated with better executive performance as assessed by FAB. These converging voxel-wise and atlas-based results support the hypothesis that posterior cerebellar degeneration may contribute to executive dysfunction in MSA.

The VBM findings in the present study were largely consistent with previous neuroimaging reports in MSA. We observed widespread gray matter reductions involving the cerebellum and putamen, as well as white matter degeneration extending from the brainstem to the middle cerebellar peduncles in line with earlier studies [10]. These findings reinforce the established view that MSA is characterized by prominent involvement of the olivopontocerebellar and striatonigral systems [22]. However, while whole-brain voxel-wise analyses are well suited to detect extensive neurodegeneration, they do not fully address our more specific question that motivated the present study—namely, which cerebellar subregions are particularly relevant to executive dysfunction in MSA. Given the functional heterogeneity of the cerebellum and its distinct cerebello-frontal networks [9], a regionally defined, anatomically informed approach was necessary to examine structure–function relationships at the level of cerebellar lobules.

Consistent with results of the VBM analysis, atlas-based analyses demonstrated reduced normalized volumes in bilateral cerebellar WM and posterior subdivisions, such as vermian VIIIA and Crus I, in the MSA group. The large negative effect sizes observed in the left and right cerebellar WM align with the characteristic involvement of the olivopontocerebellar system and degeneration of pontocerebellar fibers in MSA [23]. Vermian VIIIA reductions are also anatomically plausible, as posterior midline cerebellar regions are closely connected with both motor and associative cortical areas [24]. In addition, the reduction observed in left Crus I further supports involvement of posterior cerebellar territories that extend beyond purely motor domains [25]. Although these findings did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, the magnitude and anatomical distribution of the observed effects are concordant with established structural and pathological features of MSA, reinforcing the biological validity of the atlas-based approach.

An unexpected finding of the atlas-based analysis was the observation of relatively larger normalized volumes in selected cerebellar regions, including bilateral Crus II of the vermis and left lobule IV, compared with HC. Several explanations may account for this apparent increase. First, it is important to consider the possibility that the apparent volume increase reflects methodological factors. Atlas-based parcellation in the setting of marked cerebellar atrophy could be susceptible to partial volume effects, misregistration, or boundary misclassification, particularly in small vermian subdivisions [26]. To address this concern, we visually inspected the atlas labels overlaid on individual images in both groups and confirmed that no gross labeling errors or systematic misregistrations were present. Second, the finding may be attributable to alterations in tissue composition rather than true volumetric expansion. Reactive gliosis, microstructural changes, or extracellular fluid shifts might theoretically influence T1-weighted signal characteristics and regional segmentation outcomes. As conventional structural MRI is unable to distinguish these mechanisms, further multimodal imaging approaches would be required to clarify the biological basis of these findings. Third, compensatory structural reorganization cannot be ruled out. It has been proposed that relatively preserved or hypertrophic changes in interconnected regions may reflect adaptive responses within distributed cerebello–cortical networks, particularly in the context of progressive neurodegeneration [27]. Nevertheless, apparent increases in regional cerebellar volumes observed in MSA should be interpreted with caution. In the setting of marked atrophy, automated parcellation methods may be prone to boundary misclassification and partial volume effects, particularly at interfaces with cerebrospinal fluid or adjacent lobules. Therefore, these findings are more likely to reflect methodological artifacts rather than true tissue hypertrophy (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The regional associations observed in the atlas-based analysis between better FAB performance and normalized volumes in posterior cerebellar hemispheric and vermian regions are consistent with the concept of a “Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome,” also known as Schmahmann's syndrome [28]. Accumulating evidence from functional and structural imaging studies has demonstrated that posterior cerebellar regions, particularly Crus I-II and lobules VI–VII, are functionally connected with prefrontal and parietal association cortices, forming distributed cerebello–cortical networks involved in executive control [29]. In this context, our findings extend the framework by suggesting that structural preservation in these posterior cerebellar regions may be linked to better executive performance within clinically defined MSA.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size may have limited statistical power. Second, because of the cross-sectional design, we could not determine the temporal or causal relationship between regional atrophy and executive dysfunction in MSA. The limited sample size and cross-sectional nature of the study prevented rigorous evaluation of the independent effect of disease duration. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the relative contributions of age and disease duration and to determine whether posterior cerebellar degeneration precedes or parallels executive decline. Third, executive function assessed solely using FAB may not capture the full spectrum of executive domains, such as working memory, set-shifting, and complex planning. Certain FAB subscores, particularly Motor programming and Inhibitory control (Go/No-Go), may be spuriously reduced due to primary motor deficits, including cerebellar ataxia, bradykinesia, or rigidity, rather than reflecting pure executive dysfunction. Furthermore, because only total FAB scores were consistently available in this retrospective cohort, a modified FAB score excluding motor-dependent subitems could not be derived. Because standardized motor severity measures were not available, we used a proxy motor feature burden score based on documented clinical features. Although this approach partially addresses potential motor confounding, it does not replace validated scales such as the Unified Multiple System Atrophy Rating Scale. Therefore, residual confounding by motor impairment cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, as this study was retrospective, detailed clinical data regarding years of education and standardized assessments of depressive symptoms were not routinely obtained. Because both educational background and depression may significantly influence cognitive performance, the absence of these variables may have introduced residual confounding and affected the interpretation of cognitive outcomes. Finally, executive dysfunction in MSA is multifactorial [5]. Potential contributing mechanisms include (i) direct propagation of pathology to frontal cortical regions, (ii) disruption of basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical circuits, and (iii) impairment of cerebello-frontal networks. A comparative analysis between MSA-P and MSA-C phenotypes may help disentangle the relative contributions of these mechanisms, as predominant striatonigral versus olivopontocerebellar involvement could differentially impact executive networks [30]. However, the present study was not powered to perform reliable subgroup analyses between MSA-P and MSA-C due to the limited sample size. Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for larger, longitudinal, and multimodal studies to validate and extend the present findings.

Conclusion

Using complementary voxel-wise and atlas-based morphometric approaches, we found that MSA is characterized by prominent structural involvement of the cerebellum, within which region-specific volume variations were observed compared with HC. Notably, regional atrophy in posterior cerebellar subregions may be relevant to executive dysfunction in patients with clinically established MSA. Larger longitudinal and multimodal studies incorporating comprehensive neuropsychological assessment will be essential to validate these associations and to clarify the temporal dynamics linking posterior cerebellar degeneration to executive decline in MSA. The present study provides preliminary structural evidence that may serve as a foundation for future investigations into cerebellar contributions to cognitive impairment in MSA.
