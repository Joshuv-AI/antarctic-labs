 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This work will focus on the development of perovskite radiation detectors fabricated with
 nanocrystal, thick polycrystalline films, or single crystal materials. This will harness NLR’s
 instrumentation and expertise in a variety of research areas including but not limited to metal
 halide perovskites, device characterization, device packaging, accelerated lifetime testing, and
 ink chemistry development.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing DOE developed intellectual property and/or
 capabilities.

 Summary of Research Results:

 The scope of work was written for the first year of the project, and work concluded after 6
 months. Early in this CRADA, Mirion Technology emphasized that rapid improvement in film
 and device figures of merit were the primary goals for the first 6 months, to show traction and
 continue project funding. The very first objective was improving the μ� product of thick
 perovskite films (≥ 10 um) to a minimum requirement of 10-4 cm2 V-1 s-1. Therefore, the
 experimental work began with screening a coarse, broad range of thick film ultrasonic spray-
 coated perovskite compositions from with bandgaps ranging from approximately 1.6 to 2.3 eV,
 as shown in Figure 1. Initial μ� products for films were in the range of 7e-7 to 8E-5 cm2 V-1, as
 measured by time-resolved microwave conductivity (TRMC). Compositions at the lower end of
 the bandgap range gave the highest initial μ� products, so they were used for the remainder of the
 work. The μ� product was improved to ~1E-4 cm2 V-1 (Fig. 1D) through compositional, additive,
 and post-process passivation approaches. Since this value exceeded the required target, the
 remainder of the project moved forward with this optimized ~1.6 eV bandgap perovskite.

 x-axis scale extends to nearly 5000 ns instead of just 500 ns as in the other plots.

 The surface roughness of spray coated perovskite films was a challenge to control and prevent
 device fabrication when perovskite film thickness exceeded ~10 μm due to the buildup of large
 non-uniformities. Polishing, lamination, and hot pressing were evaluated with the goal of
 reducing the surface roughness of the films. Polishing with a non-aqueous process produced a
 mirror finish on spray coated films (Fig. 2A), but the polishing equipment was only available on
 a one-off trial and could not be used throughout the project. Since the polishing equipment was
 unavailable, a film laminator was used frequently to reduce film roughness (Fig. 2B). Perovskite
 delamination from substrates was also a challenge that became more pronounced as the film
 thickness increased (Fig. 2C). Reducing the spray coater deposition rate to more fully remove
 trapped solvent and increasing the duration of temperature ramps during substrate cooling to
 room temperature improved film adhesion. Flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrates
 were also evaluated since they have a coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) more closely
 matched to the perovskite than glass. No delamination was observed on PET for spray coated
 films up to 100 μm thick (Fig. 2C).

 3

      Figure 2 | Post-processing and Substrates. A) Spray coated films were polished with an Allied Multiprep
 polishing tool to achieve a mirror finish, using a 3 μm roughness polishing pad and silicone oil, with the
 platen set to 100 rpm, full load, 2 minutes. B) Spray coated thick perovskite films were passed through a
 Sky hot press laminator set to 140 C to reduce RMS surface roughness, from 4.5 μm to 1.2 μm. C)
 Delamination of spray coated thick films from glass substrates was a challenge that caused poor electrical
 contact. As the film thickness increased from 10 to 50 μm, the delamination could be seen by eye (lighter
 gray regions). Polyethylene terephthalate substrates, with a coefficient of thermal expansion more closely
 matched to the perovskite, were also tested and found to exhibit no delamination at perovskite film
 thicknesses of 100 μm.

 Since spray coating non-uniformities could not be fully resolved by polishing for thick films
 (approaching 100 μm) alternative perovskite deposition and growth methods were evaluated
 (Fig. 3). These included blade coating from a slurry of pre-crystallized perovskite and pressing to
 improve film density (Fig. 3 B-C), using the ~10 μm thick spray coated perovskite as a seed layer
 that was immersed into a saturated perovskite precursor solution to grow a thicker perovskite
 film (Fig. 3 D-E), and confining a saturated perovskite precursor between glass slides and
 gradually reducing the solubility of the salts to grow apparent single crystals (Fig. 3F). The blade
 coated films had improved thickness uniformity without the protrusions that appeared in spray
 coated films but had poor density. Initial blade coated devices had no electrical contact, which
 was resolved by using a hot press on the films (~2 MPa pressure). The spray coated seed films
 with subsequent solution phase crystal growth were too rough for thin contact layers (Fig. 3E).
 The confined single crystals had well controlled surface roughness and excellent density but
 were limited to a few mm2 areas, at most, and a scalable processing route for these materials was
 not identified within the timeline of the project.

 coating produced dense, continuous films but had apparent vertical stacking of grain boundaries
 throughout the film thickness. B) Blade coated slurries produced porous films with poorly
 connected grains. C) Blade coated film density could be improved after hot pressing. D) Spray
 coated films were used as a seed substrate and immersed in a saturated perovskite precursor
 liquid. Solubility of the precursor was gradually reduced to promote perovskite deposition onto
 the substrate. E) A top view of the spray coated seed + solution growth method revealed sharply
 faceted crystallites a few tens of microns in size with improved density relative to the blade
 coated slurries. μ� products for these films exceed the target by an order of magnitude (reaching
 ~3E-3 cm2 V-1 s-1), but surface roughness remained a challenge. F) Single crystal seeds (a few mm
 in size) were grown between glass substrates. Cross sections revealed no apparent grain
 boundaries and surface roughness was well controlled relative to other methods, but scaling to
 large areas was a challenge.

 Experimental work then focused on p-i-n diode development for device demonstrations, as
 shown in Figure 4. The primary challenge was that, despite sufficient μ� products and reasonable
 dark diode characteristics, charge extraction from the devices was limited with AM 1.5g Jsc often
 < 5 mA cm-2. This apparent contradiction between sufficient μ� product and low Jsc is consistent
 with TRMC being a contactless technique and the optimized perovskite compositions having
 large grains (> 1 um); if recombination primarily occurs within grains rather than at grain
 boundaries, the film will have a high μ� product as measured by TRMC but limited charge
 transport through the bulk.

 The primary challenge at the end conclusion of this CRADA is to grow thick, polycrystalline
 perovskite films using scalable deposition methods (e.g. compatible with 10’s of cm2 areas) that
 have high charge extraction under the device’s built-in potential (> 10 mA cm-2 under AM 1.5g
 and at thicknesses > 10 μm). The scope of work is provided below, along with comments, to
 provide additional project insights.

 Mirion Technologies (Canberra) will guide the overall workflow and priorities based on their
 technical milestones. The tasks listed below are a menu of critical path items to facilitate the
 progress towards commercial perovskite radiation detectors.

 6

 TASK 1 – Development of stable electrode materials for perovskite radiation detectors

 • Task 1.1: Corrosion resistance test of metals with suitable work functions (~4.1 – 5.5
 eV) including Au, Ag, Ta, Ti, and Ga or Ga alloys. Metals will be deposited onto a
 substrate and then exposed to iodine vapor. Conductivity measurements before and after
 the iodine exposure will be used to indicate corrosion resistance and down-select
 candidate materials.
 • Task 1.2: Development of electrode interlayers that inhibit diffusion of the metal
 electrode. Electrode metal diffusion into perovskites forms deep traps and shunt paths.
 Electrode materials will be evaluated for long-term stability in accelerated test conditions
 (e.g. heat, illumination, and/or electrical bias). Thin film diodes may serve as a rapid
 screening platform to evaluate a variety of electrode materials. Optionally, time of flight
 secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) will be used to evaluate the diffusion
 depth of metallic contacts into the perovskite.
 • Task 1.3: Promising candidate electrodes and interlayers will be deposited on perovskite
 thick films and shipped to Mirion Technologies (Canberra) for further characterization.

 Task 1 was not addressed within the first 6 months of this project since achieving minimum
 electronic quality and initial device performance were prerequisites.

 TASK 2 – Device and module packaging and stability testing

 • Task 2.1: Perovskites are sensitive to oxygen and moisture. NLR has developed an
 encapsulation process for perovskite films sandwiched between glass sheets to minimize
 the ingress of oxygen and moisture and enable outdoor testing of photovoltaic modules.
 NLR has also developed oxygen and moisture barrier layers deposited via atomic layer
 deposition (ALD), which allow thin barriers (< 1 μm) suitable for charge particle
 detection. Candidate perovskite materials will be evaluated under accelerated conditions
 and with barrier layers.
 • Task 2.2: Candidate perovskite materials will undergo accelerated testing as thin film
 diodes under heat, light, and/or electrical bias to evaluate their relative long-term
 performance.
 • Task 2.3: Bias stability of perovskite materials and/or device stacks will be evaluated.
 Mirion Technologies (Canberra) expects commercially relevant detectors to withstand
 bias of around 1000 V/cm. Currently, the long-term stability of metal halide perovskites
 under high biases is unreported, and perovskite solar cells operate under biases near 1 V.
 NLR has a multichannel potentiostat capable of 10 V applied bias which will be utilized
 for this task; 1000 V/cm bias will be achieved by applying a 10 V bias to 100 μm thick
 films.

 7

 Devices sets were re-tested after a ~3-week interval and were stored in the dark under nitrogen at
 room temperature. The fill factor and open circuit voltage of the devices improved, with some
 trade-off in short circuit current density loss. Long-term device stability was not a priority in this
 phase of the project since initial device performance was the focus.

 TASK 3 – Development of thick perovskite films (> 100 μm)

 • Task 3.1: Thick films will be deposited with ultrasonic spray coating. Optionally, ink jet
 printing will also be evaluated as an alternative deposition method. The deposition
 parameters will be optimized in pursuit of perovskite films with greater than 100 μm
 thickness over areas of at least 5 cm2.

 Perovskite films with thickness greater than 100 μm were deposited over areas greater than 6 cm2
 using a variety of deposition and growth techniques.

 • Task 3.2: Characterization of mobility-lifetime product (μ�). Candidate materials will be
 evaluated with time-resolved microwave conductivity (TRMC) and/or time-resolved
 photoluminescence spectroscopy to obtain mobility-lifetime products, a figure of merit
 for semiconductors radiation detectors.

 The μ� products of many films of varying compositions and deposition processes were evaluated
 quantitatively with TRMC throughout the project.

 • Task 3.3: Wider gap perovskites with a bandgap of > 1.9 eV, containing cesium and
 bromide, will be evaluated to suppress detector noise.

 Perovskites up to 2.3 eV were evaluated, but the lower gap materials had substantially higher
 initial μ� products. The dark current densities of these lower bandgap materials was deemed
 acceptable at this stage of the project, so work on wider gap materials was discontinued.

 • Task 3.4: Solvent systems, including DMSO-DMF and NMP-DMF, will be evaluated for
 their contribution to film morphology and optoelectronic performance.Solvent systems
 were adjusted to accommodate 1 M solubility across a variety of bandgaps, but further
 solvent system evaluation was not needed once the minimum required μ� product was
 reached.
 • Task 3.5: Precursor “additives” to improve the μ� product, film morphology, and
 stability will be evaluated. Generally, these additives are used in addition to the
 stoichiometric perovskite precursor salts but primarily influence film formation or thin
 interfacial layers rather than the final bulk film composition.

 8

 Precursor additives were evaluated and found to substantially alter the film morphology and
 improve the μ� products of ultrasonic spray coated perovskite films.

 • Task 3.6: Thick film uniformity will be evaluated with cross-sectional SEM, optical
 profilometry, and/or photoluminescence imaging.

 Thick films were evaluated with cross-sectional SEM and optical profilometry.

 TASK 4 – Evaluation and suppression of leakage current in perovskite thick films.

 • Task 4.1: Evaluation and suppression of leakage current. Leakage current is a major risk
 for perovskite radiation detection since it gives rise to unacceptably high electronic noise.
 Previously reported perovskite x-ray detectors reached 80 pA/mm2 leakage current after
 implementing blocking layers near the electrodes, but this was still two orders of
 magnitude higher than commercial a-Se and CsI(Tl) detectors. Strategies to suppress
 leakage current such as wider bandgap perovskites, compositional alloying (e.g. Cl- anion
 incorporation), and/or heterostructured perovskites with wider bandgap perovskites
 deposited near the contacts will be evaluated.

 Leakage current of the devices was evaluated but was not a major concern during this stage in
 the project.
