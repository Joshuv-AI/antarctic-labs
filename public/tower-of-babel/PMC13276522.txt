TRP channels as critical pathways in temperature-induced asthma
Authors: Shihan Deng, Xinnan Zhou, Zhihao Wang, Jian He, Qinghua Gao, Lina Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEpidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated that extreme temperatures and abrupt temperature changes increase the incidence and severity of asthma. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects remain largely unclear. Molecular biology research has revealed that transient receptor potential (TRP) channels play a critical role in asthma progression. Current research mainly centers on TRPV1, TRPA1, and TRPV4, while other subtypes remain understudied. Most TRP channels exhibit increased expression in the respiratory system upon stimulation, which can exacerbate asthma symptoms. Notably, sustained abnormal temperatures mainly regulate the protein expression of TRPA1, TRPV1, and TRPM8, which possess different activation temperature ranges. In turn, TRP expression changes affect thermal sensing and thermoregulation, revealing their bidirectional interaction. Reviewing PubMed and Web of Science studies, we suggest extreme temperatures promote asthma development by regulating TRP channel expression. This view reveals the mechanism connecting ambient temperature and asthma, shedding light on novel therapeutic targets and prevention approaches.Subject areas: disease, molecular biology

Introduction

Temperature is a key parameter affecting human health, particularly under extreme high or low conditions. When the human body is exposed to such extremes, core body temperature may deviate beyond the normal physiological range, leading to heat stroke (core body temperature over 40 °C)1 or hypothermia (core body temperature less than 35 °C).2 In addition, exposure to extreme temperatures increases both the incidence and mortality of various diseases, notably cardiovascular3,4 and respiratory disorders.5,6

The respiratory system, comprising the respiratory tract and lungs, serves as the primary interface for gas exchange between the human body and the external environment. Consequently, deviations from the thermal comfort zone often trigger diverse respiratory symptoms. Epidemiological evidence indicates that the incidence, symptom severity, and mortality associated with respiratory diseases—such as rhinitis, asthma, and chronic obstructive lung disease (COPD)—are strongly correlated with temperature fluctuations.5,6,7,8 Among these, asthma, a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways characterized by mucus hypersecretion, airway remodeling, and bronchial hyperresponsiveness, is of particular concern.9,10 Patients often experience recurrent episodes of coughing, wheezing, and dyspnea. The global prevalence of asthma continues to rise, with higher incidence observed in developed countries; however, increasing urbanization in developing regions has also led to a steady upsurge in cases.11,12 This trend underscores asthma as a growing public health challenge.

As the primary pathway for Ca2+ influx, TRP channels determine core cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis by regulating cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration, membrane potential, and downstream Ca2+-dependent enzymes such as PKC and calmodulin. Thus, TRP channels serve as key hubs linking physical/chemical stimuli to the Ca2+ signaling network.13 Meanwhile, TRP channels constitute a family of multifunctional signaling molecules responsive to diverse external stimuli, including temperature, pain, and chemical agents.14,15 These ion channels are widely distributed across various tissues and cell types, primarily localized to cell membranes, where they interact with multiple physiological signaling pathways.16,17,18 The TRP family comprises seven subfamilies, including TRPA (ankyrin), TRPV (vanilloid), and TRPM (melastatin).15 The activation thresholds of several TRP channels have been well characterized: TRPV1 is activated above 43°C,19 and TRPM8 below 27°C.20 Notably, TRPA1 has been reported to respond to both noxious cold and noxious heat.21,22Furthermore, certain TRP channels are implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases. For example, TRPV6, TRPM1, and TRPM8 are associated with tumor progression and may serve as potential biomarkers for cancer detection,23 whereas TRPC3, TRPC6, TRPV1, and TRPV4 have been linked to cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension and arrhythmia.24 TRPV1, TRPA1, and TRPM8 are particularly relevant to respiratory disorders, including asthma.25

Considering the established interrelationships among temperature, asthma, and TRP channel activity, we hypothesize that prolonged exposure to extreme temperatures may alter the expression of specific TRP channels in the respiratory tract. Such modulation could contribute to the initiation and progression of asthma, as well as to variations in disease severity and symptomatology.

Therefore, this review aims to synthesize current evidence on the associations between extreme temperature exposure, TRP channel regulation, and asthma pathophysiology. The studies of epidemiological, clinical, and mechanistic were summarized that elucidate how temperature-dependent TRP channel activation influences airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness. By integrating findings across molecular, physiological, and population levels, this review seeks to clarify potential biological pathways linking environmental temperature fluctuations to asthma development and exacerbation, thereby providing insights for future prevention and intervention strategies.

Literature search strategy

The literature search strategy used in this review is summarized in Figure 1. Guided by the primary objectives and scope of this study, a Boolean search approach was applied to identify relevant publications. The search keywords included: ((“temperature” OR “heat” OR “cold”) AND “asthma”), (“trp∗” AND “asthma”), (“trp∗” AND “lung”), (“trp∗” AND “thermal comfort”), and ((“temperature” OR “heat” OR “cold”) AND “trp∗”). These keywords were organized into three thematic categories reflecting the focus of this review: (1) temperature and asthma, (2) TRP channels and asthma, and (3) TRP channels and temperature. These categories correspond to sections “epidemiological studies on asthma and extreme temperatures”, “TRP channels and asthma”, and “TRP channels and temperature” of the manuscript.

The time window for literature retrieval was limited to the past 5 years to ensure coverage of the most recent and relevant findings. The initial search returned several thousand records. Epidemiological studies on asthma and extreme temperatures

Most of the included studies adopt epidemiological survey designs, drawing on large volumes of health records or case data and applying a range of statistical modeling approaches. Collectively, these studies assess the effects of low temperature, high temperature, and temperature variability on asthma incidence, morbidity, and exacerbation risk. Figure 2 illustrates the main content of this section.

Results of epidemiological studies

Impact of high-temperature and low-temperature exposure on asthma

Among the 40 studies reviewed, 16 reported associations between high-temperature exposure and asthma, while 24 examined the effects of low-temperature exposure. Several studies also assessed the influence of temperature variability, including temperature changes between neighboring days (TCN) and diurnal temperature range (DTR). Individual studies often evaluated multiple thermal indicators simultaneously, such as high temperature, low temperature, TCN, and DTR.

High-temperature exposure has been widely identified as an important contributor to asthma onset and exacerbation. Most evidence is based on outpatient and inpatient records. The association between heatwaves and asthma is consistent and continuous,26 and studies conducted in other regions of the United States have also demonstrated the impact of high temperatures on asthma.27 Studies by Deng, Schinasi, and Hahn, all using hospital visit data, found that elevated temperatures increase asthma-related healthcare utilization.28,29,30 Notably, all three studies emphasized a lag effect, in which asthma-related visits peak several days to weeks after exposure to extreme heat. Findings based on inpatient data yielded similar conclusions.31,32,33 For children, high-temperature exposure significantly increases the risk of asthma-related hospital admission or readmission.34,35

Interactions between temperature and air pollutants have also been highlighted. Jin’s study showed that particulate matter exacerbates heat-related asthma risk, with PM2.5 demonstrating a stronger effect than PM10.33 Questionnaire-based research by Niu found that hot, humid conditions combined with mold exposure during infancy markedly increase asthma risk in early childhood.36 Xu’s global analysis similarly demonstrated that pollutants amplify the adverse impacts of heat.37 Another study by Niu observed that human-induced heat exposure and the urban heat island effect, together with urban pollutants, increase asthma risk.38

Wang’s survey-based study reported an inverse relationship between asthma prevalence and regional GDP.39 This contrasts with earlier findings and may be attributable to the study’s specific target population—urban young parents in China—who generally have higher education levels, better living conditions, and greater awareness regarding allergen avoidance.

Mortality outcomes were also examined. Zhou’s study reported a positive association between high-temperature exposure and asthma-related mortality, although statistical significance was observed only in Jiangsu Province.40 lung function, a key diagnostic indicator, has also been shown to decline following heat exposure.41,42 Consistent with healthcare utilization studies, Lei reported a lag effect, while Xu emphasized the importance of temperature variability.

More studies have investigated low-temperature exposure than high-temperature exposure. Belachew demonstrated a clear linear relationship between average winter temperature and new-onset asthma, with seasonal effects persisting for 1–2 years.43 Specifically, winters colder than the long-term average were associated with increased asthma incidence both during the same season and in subsequent years. Multiple studies similarly found that low temperatures significantly increase asthma-related outpatient visits28,32,44,45,46 and hospitalizations.31,32,47,48,49,50,51,52,53,54 For children, low-temperature exposure also increases the risk of asthma-related readmission.34 Low-temperature impacts also exhibit lag effects, but compared with heat exposure, cold effects tend to arise more rapidly and persist longer.41,50

Several lung function studies demonstrated that abrupt temperature drops exert stronger adverse effects on asthma55 than abrupt temperature rises.41,42 Zhou confirmed that low-temperature-related asthma mortality occurred consistently across two Chinese provinces, whereas high temperature-related mortality was observed in only one.40 Questionnaire-based studies further support the detrimental effects of cold exposure: He and Sun highlighted the role of apparent temperature56 and thermal comfort57 in childhood asthma. Occupational cold exposure has also been linked to increased asthma symptoms58 and Hyrkäs-Palmu reported that patients with severe asthma are particularly vulnerable to cold environments, patients with asthma are also more likely to experience functional impairment and worsening health problems related to cold weather.59,60

Many studies described U-shaped31,46 or J-shaped relationships,40,41,51 indicating that both high and low temperatures elevate asthma risk, with more pronounced effects at more extreme temperatures. However, the temperature corresponding to the minimum risk varies substantially across regions. For example, the minimum hospitalization temperature was 28.6°C in Hong Kong50 but 22°C in Beijing.31 The reason for this difference may lie in the variation in statistical methods. However, more notably, it is attributed to the climatic differences between the study locations: Beijing has a temperate monsoon climate, while Hong Kong has a subtropical monsoon climate. Although the average summer temperatures in the two regions are similar, their average winter temperatures differ by more than 10°C. This leads to differences in the temperature adaptation of long-term residents, especially to low temperatures.

Lag effect is another important characteristic of the impact of extreme temperatures on asthma. Both high and low temperature exposures have a lag effect on asthma. Reduced lung function is a direct indicator of the impact of asthma on the human body. Both cold and heat exposure can reduce lung function-related indicators, with the impact lasting for 3 days, and the impact of cold exposure being more rapid.41 However, conclusions from studies on hospitalization data vary. In a study on hospitalization data in Hong Kong, the impact of cold exposure was more significant and longer-lasting than that of heat exposure.50 In contrast, a study in Beijing showed that the impacts of cold and heat exposure were similar.31 Additionally, an earlier published review also indicated that low temperature exposure has a longer lag effect and a more rapid impact compared with high temperature exposure.61

In summary, both high and low temperatures adversely affect asthma. However, based on current evidence, low-temperature exposure may exert a more substantial and longer-lasting impact than high-temperature exposure.

Effects of TV, DTR, and TCN on asthma

In the past 5 years, temperature variability indicators—TV, DTR, and TCN—have received increasing research attention, often surpassing extreme temperatures as a focus. Among the 40 studies reviewed, 14 specifically investigated these metrics. TV typically refers to the standard deviation of maximum and minimum temperatures across several days.62 DTR is calculated as the difference between daily maximum and minimum temperatures.53,63 TCN is defined as the difference in mean temperature between 2 consecutive days, with positive values indicating warming and negative values indicating cooling.54,64 Growing evidence suggests that increased short-term temperature variability significantly affects asthma and lung function. Elevated TV impairs children’s lung function42 and raises outpatient visits due to asthma,65 a pattern also observed in the general population.62 In cold seasons, higher DTR exacerbates asthma symptoms in both children51,65 and adults.53,66 Additionally, smaller TCN in winter has been shown to worsen children’s lung function.42 In contrast, studies conducted during hot seasons report that higher TCN plays a greater role in triggering asthma.42,64,65 When the TCN >2°C, its adverse effects on children increase rapidly.67 Other research, however, suggests that lower DTR may be more relevant in hot climates.53,66 Similar findings were observed in the study by Li, which also indicated that a higher DTR during the cold season had a similarly substantial impact.67 Yan’s findings highlight that high DTR under elevated temperatures can significantly affect childhood asthma.46

Lag effects have been consistently reported. Zheng’s study indicated that DTR influences asthma with a 5-day lag, whereas TCN shows an immediate (0-day) effect.68 Consistent with this, Hu found that DTR exhibited a 2-week lag in children.46 Lei’s study showed that TCN has a 1-day lag, with effects lasting up to two weeks,64 suggesting that DTR may have a longer cumulative impact.

Despite substantial progress, several knowledge gaps remain. Since ambient temperature is inherently variable and may fluctuate more widely in regions with complex climates or during the transition between hot and cold seasons, variability-related indicators may have an even greater influence on asthma than absolute high or low temperatures. Furthermore, TV, DTR, and TCN exhibit strong seasonal and regional specificity, contributing to inconsistent findings across studies. For example, in Lanzhou, high DTR in warm seasons and low DTR in cold seasons both elevated hospitalization risks,53 whereas in Hong Kong only high DTR in cold seasons showed significant associations.63 Two pediatric studies from Shanghai and Lanzhou consistently reported that greater DTR increased year-round asthma risk, with risk rising proportionally with DTR magnitude.54,65

Interpretations of DTR are season-dependent. In warm seasons, low DTR often reflects stable and comfortable conditions, whereas high DTR may indicate a sudden temperature drop within a day. Conversely, in cold seasons, low DTR may represent sustained cold exposure, while high DTR may indicate potential warming. The extent of exposure is modulated further by lifestyle and environmental factors, such as frequency of outdoor activities and availability of indoor heating or cooling.

TCN demonstrates similarly complex patterns. In cold climates, negative TCN increases asthma exacerbations in the following days.54,55 In warm climates, increased TCN elevates pediatric asthma visits.65 Decreased TCN in cold seasons is associated with reductions in key lung function indicators.42 Compared with DTR, TCN may reflect more intricate physiological and behavioral interactions: small absolute TCN values in extreme climates could signify prolonged exposure to persistent cold or heat, whereas in mild seasons small TCN suggests stable, comfortable conditions.

Overall, the impacts of TV, DTR, and TCN on asthma have been clearly demonstrated, though their magnitude and direction depend heavily on climatic context, seasonal characteristics, and population behaviors. Therefore, temperature variability should be considered an independent and region-specific risk factor in asthma research and public health practice.

The above epidemiological evidence strongly suggests that climatic context and temperature variability play critical roles in the occurrence and exacerbation of asthma. However, the underlying biological mechanisms connecting ambient temperature, seasonal changes, and asthma remain largely unclear. As key temperature sensors and Ca2+-permeable channels in mammals, TRP channels represent a promising molecular link between climatic stimuli and respiratory pathophysiology.

TRP channels and asthma

The TRP channel family mediates transmembrane [Ca2+] flux in response to various external chemical and thermal stimuli.69 Cytosolic free [Ca2+] functions as a critical second messenger that governs cell activation. In the lungs, a variety of immune and structural cell types—including macrophages, mast cells, epithelial cells, airway smooth muscle cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, monocytes, and T lymphocytes—recruit to the airways following injury or infection. Intracellular [Ca2+] regulates a wide range of cellular responses relevant to asthma pathobiology, such as degranulation, ROS production, secretion of lipid mediators, cell proliferation, and airway smooth muscle contraction.70

TRP channels are expressed in almost all cell types, including excitable and non-excitable cells.71,72 Excitable cell membranes are rich in functional voltage-gated ion channels such as [Na2+] and [Ca2+] channels, which can undergo explosive changes in membrane potential and generate action potentials. [Ca2+] rapidly and transiently influx with changes in potential, forming short and sharp transient calcium transients, and producing rapid, synchronous, and all-or-none electrophysiological responses to external stimuli. Non-excitable cells lack voltage-gated [Na2+] and [Ca2+] channels, so they cannot produce all-or-none action potentials. Accordingly, membrane potential mainly modulates [Ca2+] influx through ligand-gated or second messenger-dependent channels, thus altering intracellular [Ca2+] levels.73 In lung tissues, the majority of parenchymal cells are non-excitable cells, including lung epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages. Meanwhile, a subset of sensory nerves belongs to excitable cells. These nerves maintain basal lung physiological functions and trigger critical defensive reflexes such as coughing and sneezing upon stimulation.74 Nevertheless, existing studies on the association between asthma and TRP channels have not clearly distinguished whether TRP channels exert distinct functional patterns in the two types of lung cells. Nevertheless, current studies regarding asthma and TRP channels have not further clarified the specific cell types expressing TRP channels in lung tissue.

TRP channels are broadly expressed throughout the respiratory tract25,75,76 and have gained increasing attention as potential therapeutic targets for respiratory disorders.77,78,79 Bronchial asthma—a chronic inflammatory airway disease,9,10 involves complex interactions among immune cells, airway structural cells, and inflammatory mediators, many of which are tightly regulated by Ca2+ signaling. The TRP channels focused on in the present study include: TRPA1, TRPV1, TRPV4, and TRPM8. Transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) and asthma

TRPA1 is typically activated at temperatures below 17°C.103 In addition, it can be activated or antagonized by various chemical substances such as ATIC.104 Its pore lacks rigorous ion size and charge selectivity, classifying it as a non-selective cation channel. Existing studies consistently demonstrate the detrimental role of TRPA1 in the pathogenesis and progression of asthma. In guinea pig models, van den Berg and his colleague showed that pharmacological antagonism of TRPA1 effectively suppressed allergen-induced AHR, although it did not attenuate airway inflammation.80 In murine models, elevated expression of TRPA1 and CD4+ T cells has been detected in the airways of asthmatic mice, suggesting that TRPA1 may contribute to airway inflammation and immuno-neural interactions.105 TRPA1, together with Dectin-1, has also been implicated as a key pathway mediating β-glucan-induced AHR.106

Mechanistically, activation of TRPA1 has been shown to facilitate the migration, clustering, and synaptic activity of lung neuroendocrine cells (PNECs), thereby aggravating asthma severity.107 Studies by Sun and Zhou demonstrated that exposure to cigarette smoke or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) intensifies airway inflammation in OVA-induced asthma models while concurrently upregulating TRPA1 expression.81,108 Notably, acrolein present in cigarette smoke is indeed a potent activator of TRPA1, whereas the ability of PAHs to activate TRPA1 remains unclear. Nevertheless, inhibition of TRPA1 still attenuated asthma severity in both models, suggesting that other indirect mechanisms may be involved.

Other findings highlight the complexity of TRPA1 signaling. Lai et al. showed that allyl isothiocyanate (AITC) alleviated fibrosis-related protein expression in HDM-induced asthma by modulating TRPA1 expression and reducing Ca2+ influx.109 Conversely, Yap et al. reported that under inflammatory conditions (TNF-α stimulation), AITC enhanced Ca2+ influx in human lung fibroblasts.85 Despite these divergent outcomes, both studies underscore the central role of TRPA1 in Ca2+-dependent inflammatory pathways.

Animal knockout models further support the pathogenic role of TRPA1. TRPA1-deficient rats exhibit reduced leukocyte infiltration and significantly lower eosinophil and neutrophil counts in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) following asthma induction, compared with wild-type (WT) controls.110 Similar reductions in airway inflammation have been observed in TRPA1 knockout mouse models.82,83 In vitro, TRPA1 expression and function in human airway epithelial cells are modulated by inflammatory conditions, including Th1- and Th2-type cytokines. Pharmacological inhibition of TRPA1 reduces neutrophil infiltration, supporting its pro-inflammatory role.84

Importantly, TRPA1 may exert different effects in acute versus chronic inflammatory states. Hajna et al. found that TRPA1 activation protects against LPS-induced acute pneumonia and AHR, potentially through neuropeptide-mediated sensory-nerve defense mechanisms. However, sustained activation under chronic inflammatory conditions, such as cigarette smoke exposure, promotes emphysema formation and respiratory deterioration.111 This suggests a dualistic, context-dependent role of TRPA1. Finally, TRPA1 often interacts with TRPV1 in mediating lung injury and inflammation, indicating potential synergistic or co-regulated pathways.96,112

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) and asthma

TRPV1 is also a non-selective cation channel that can be activated by high temperatures above 43°C and painful stimuli.113 Capsaicin is a potent activator of TRPV1 and is clinically used for cough response testing.114 It is another major TRP channel implicated in asthma pathophysiology, alongside TRPA1. As noted above, TRPV1 and TRPA1 jointly mediate airway inflammation induced by acute ozone exposure.112 Consistently, Liu et al. demonstrated that certain glycosides can simultaneously inhibit TRPV1- and TRPA1-mediated cellular currents and downregulate their protein expression levels, thereby reducing cough and inflammation following lung injury.96 Although both studies investigated TRPV1 and TRPA1 simultaneously, they did not address the interaction mechanism between these two channels. Inhibition of TRPV1 using CZP or siRNA can attenuate inflammation, AHR, and airway remodeling in OVA-induced mouse asthma models.115

Environmental co-exposures also influence TRPV1-dependent asthma responses. In animal models, high humidity combined with NO2,116 PM2.5 combined with O3117 and ozone alone118 exacerbated AHR and inflammation, accompanied by elevated TRPV1 expression. In both studies, antagonism of TRPV1 using CZP mitigated airway inflammation, further supporting the channel’s pro-inflammatory role. In TRPV1 knockout mice, SO2-induced cough responses were significantly reduced compared with WT controls, confirming involvement of TRPV1 in airway sensory activation.119

Several pathways have been identified as potential mechanisms through which TRPV1 contributes to asthma. These include the IL-31/TRPV1 axis,120 the IL-33/TRPV1 pathway,91 the TRPV1/Ca2+/NFAT signaling cascade,92 and the Nrf2 pathway.121

Therapeutic targeting of TRPV1 has produced promising results. Zhang et al. employed photothermal nanoparticles with selective cytotoxicity toward TRPV1-overexpressing cells to PTT in murine asthma models. PTT effectively alleviated asthma symptoms, highlighting TRPV1 as a viable and specific therapeutic target.122 In alignment with epidemiological findings, Deng et al. directly demonstrated in animal models that heat exposure aggravates airway inflammation in asthma, with TRPV1 playing a central role in heat-induced disease exacerbation.123 Such mechanistic evidence complements population-based studies by revealing how extreme temperature exposure can worsen asthma through TRPV1-mediated pathways.

Transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) and asthma

TRPV4 can be activated by a temperature of 27°C, hypotonic conditions, and mechanical stress. Its specific physiological functions are more complex than those of the aforementioned TRP channels.124,125 GSK1016790A and GSK2193874 are the specific agonist and antagonist of TRPV4, respectively.126 Current research on TRPV4 in asthma has produced a substantial but inconsistent body of evidence. Duan and his colleague reported that blocking TRPV4 with HC-067047 significantly attenuates lung pathology and AHR in asthmatic animals exposed to a high-humidity environment combined with formaldehyde, suggesting that TRPV4 may exacerbate asthma through the p38 MAPK pathway.127 Similarly, Bonvini et al. identified a PAR2-TRPV4-ATP-cysLT signaling axis that contributes to bronchospasm in non-atopic asthma, although this pathway appears to be ineffective in allergic asthma.128

Using both animal and cellular models, Wiesner et al. demonstrated that calcium signaling in club cells is a critical mediator of T-helper cell activation during allergic airway inflammation, and that elevated TRPV4 expression amplifies protease-induced inflammatory responses.93 In TRPV4 knockout mice, Al-Azzam et al. further showed that loss of TRPV4 suppresses TGF-β1-induced NOX4 expression and ROS production, indicating a potential role for TRPV4 in oxidative stress-related mechanisms.129 By contrast, Palaniyandi et al. found no association between TRPV4 and allergic asthma or asthma-associated lung fibrosis.130 However, the study by Gombedza et al. found that enhanced matrix accumulation via the TRPV4-dependent pathway may improve the mechanical properties of the airways and promote fibrosis, forming a positive feedback loop that contributes to airway remodeling.94

More recent studies have revealed a possible protective dimension of TRPV4. Orsini et al., using animal models, and Arfath et al., using primary macrophages, both reported that TRPV4 promotes macrophage regulatory activity and mitigates inflammation.95,131 Collectively, these results suggest that TRPV4 exerts context-dependent effects in asthma. Its role may vary according to the specific asthma trigger, the stage of disease progression, and the immune cell types involved. Further mechanistic and phenotype-specific studies will be essential to clarify the precise functions of TRPV4 in asthma pathophysiology.

Other TRP channels and asthma

TRPM8, TRPM2, and TRPP2 have also been implicated in asthma and in lung or bronchial inflammation across several studies. TRPM8, a member of the TRPM family, can be activated by temperatures below 27°C and menthol.20 Meanwhile, TRPM2, another member of the same family, plays important roles in immunity, inflammation, and heat sensation.132 Toluene diisocyanate (TDI) activates the expression of TRPM8 at both mRNA and protein levels in human airway epithelial cells, further increasing the levels of related inflammatory factors. Correspondingly, antagonism of TRPM8 can alleviate this inflammation.88 Activation of TRPM8 markedly increases the gene expression of IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP in bronchial epithelial cells, an effect that can be attenuated by TRPM8 antagonists. Consistently, TRPM8 protein levels in the sputum of patients with asthma are significantly higher than those in healthy individuals.87 Silencing TRPM8 expression suppresses activation of the NF-κB/MAPK signaling pathway, thereby reducing lung inflammation and associated epithelial cell apoptosis.89 TRPM8 may also act synergistically with other TRP channels; for example, bryostatin-1 and bryostatin-3 reduce the expression of both TRPM8 and TRPV1 and subsequently alleviate lung inflammation and tissue injury.90

PM2.5 exposure induces TRPM2 overexpression and promotes Ca2+ influx, which in turn activates NLRP3 and its downstream inflammatory mediators. Notably, scavenging ROS or inhibiting TRPM2 activation effectively mitigates PM2.5-induced lung inflammation.86,133

TRPC3 is highly expressed in the cerebellum, cerebrum, and smooth muscle, and plays a key role in regulating neurogenesis and intracellular and extracellular calcium signaling.134 TRPC1 is a novel physiological Ca2+ channel subunit in arterial smooth muscle cells.135 Overexpression of TRPC1 and TRPC3 (members of the TRPC family) in mouse airway smooth muscle may be involved in AHR and airway remodeling in mouse models of asthma. Treatment of TRPC3 with antagonists or siRNA can reduce the proliferation and migration of ASMCs.136,137 However, Zhang further demonstrated that the effects of TRPC1 and TRPC3 on cell viability and cell cycle progression in ASMCs are mediated by regulating massive Ca2+ influx.138

Similarly, downregulation of TRPP2 expression has been shown to alleviate AHR and lung inflammation in asthma models.139

Antagonism and knockout are common approaches in relevant studies. Pharmacological antagonism directly blocks the biological function of a target protein via specific antagonists. It inhibits protein activity transiently and reversibly without altering the genomic sequence, allowing rapid simulation of phenotypic changes caused by loss of protein function. This approach is suitable for mechanistic validation at the cellular and ex vivo levels. In contrast, gene knockout directly abolishes the target gene at the genomic level, resulting in complete absence of the corresponding protein in cells or organisms and permanent loss of protein function. It can be used to define the physiological and pathological functions of genes at the whole-animal level. In studies by Mueller-Tribbensee et al., both pharmacological antagonism and gene knockout produced identical experimental results, indicating that the use of these two approaches may not influence the final conclusions.140 We summarize the TRP channels mentioned above and their associated signaling pathways in Table 2.

TRP channels and temperature

Organisms perceive external temperature through somatosensory neurons, which transmit this information to the central nervous system and convert it into neural activity. When TRP channels are activated by stimuli including PIP2, Ca2+, cyclic nucleotides, phosphorylation, temperature, and osmotic pressure, they alter membrane potential, mediate ion flux across the membrane, modify enzyme activity, and trigger endocytosis/exocytosis.141 Neurons, including somatosensory neurons, are the fundamental structural and functional units of the nervous system, comprising a cell body and projections (dendrites and axons). Their functions are largely determined by specific patterns of gene expression, including thermo-sensitive TRP channels (Thermo-TRPs).142 Thermo-TRPs mediate temperature sensing at the peripheral terminals of somatosensory neurons over a broad temperature range.143 Experimental modulation of these TRP channels in animal models alters both temperature perception and thermoregulatory behavior, while prolonged exposure to abnormal temperatures can reciprocally modify TRP channel expression. However, ethical and practical constraints limit long-term exposure studies of TRP channels and temperature in humans. Consequently, most current evidence in this field is derived from animal studies.

Regulating TRP channels affects temperature perception

Two main strategies are commonly used to regulate TRP channel expression in animal studies: pharmacological modulation through agonists or antagonists, and direct knockdown of protein expression using gene editing technologies. Yonghak et al. employed both gene editing and pharmacological antagonism to reduce TRPV1 expression in mice, observing a significant increase in body temperature and a marked reduction in heat-dissipating behaviors during heat exposure.147 Similarly, Masuda et al. repeatedly desensitized TRPV1 channels using CZP and reported that treated mice lost escape behaviors and exhibited elevated body temperatures under high-temperature conditions.148 Activation of TRPV1 in gerbils induced a mild preference for lower temperatures, though the effect was not pronounced.146 A similar effect has been observed with TRPM2.

Activation of TRPA1 reduces metabolism and induces cutaneous vasodilation in mice, leading to decreased body temperature.144 In TRPM8 knockout mice subjected to cold stimulation, the number of Fos-positive neurons in hypothalamic regions associated with thermoregulation and metabolism remained unchanged, whereas in WT controls, Fos expression increased.155 Correspondingly, the knockout mice exhibited reduced cold-avoidance behavior, indicating impaired cold perception.151,152

Abd El Hay et al. performed separate genetic knockouts of TRPV1 and TRPM2 in mice. Reduced TRPV1 expression impaired the precision of temperature selection, while TRPM2 knockout mice preferred warmer environments compared with WT animals. Tan et al. also found that mice in which TRPM2 had been genetically deleted showed a striking deficit in their sensation of non-noxious warm temperatures.156 Conversely, overexpression of TRPV1 lowered the response threshold of thermosensitive neurons. In primary DRG neurons cultured in vitro, the proportion of thermosensitive neurons was reduced in TRPV1 knockout mice.154 Additionally, increased TRPV3 expression shifted temperature preference toward warmer environments.157 In human skin cells, TRPM8 mediates innocuous cold perception, whereas TRPV1 and TRPA1 mediate responses to noxious heat.158

TRP channels with overlapping activation temperature ranges may exhibit synergistic effects. Deletion of TRPA1 under the TRPM8-deficient background resulted in a disproportionately greater reduction in cold avoidance behavior in mice compared with TRPM8 single knockout and also affected the proportion of cold-encoding fiber types. These findings indicate that TRPM8 and TRPA1 may act synergistically in the cold-sensing range.159 TRPV1, TRPM3, and TRPA1 triple knockout mice completely lack the acute withdrawal response to noxious heat stimuli (a behavior necessary to avoid burn injury), while maintaining normal nociceptive responses to cold or mechanical stimuli and preserving a preference for moderate temperatures. These findings indicate that the initiation of acute heat-evoked pain responses in sensory nerve endings relies on three functionally redundant TRP channels.22

The impact of temperature on the expression of TRP channels

Organisms exhibit a degree of adaptability to environmental temperature, and temperature-induced modulation of TRP channel expression may constitute one of the underlying mechanisms. Following long-term cold acclimation, TRPM2 gene expression in the hypothalamus of mice decreases, indicating altered hypothalamic temperature sensitivity, whereas short-term cold exposure does not elicit such changes.153 Conversely, prolonged heat exposure significantly increases TRPV1 protein expression while reducing TRPV4 expression in the mouse brain.145 Protein expression level of TRPV1 in the skeletal muscle of mice was increased after active heat acclimation, which upregulated mitochondrial turnover and oxidative phosphorylation efficiency in mouse skeletal muscle, thereby contributing to improved aerobic exercise capacity.160 In Li’s study, heat acclimation increased TRPV1 expression in preoptic heat-sensitive neurons in mice, enhancing their resistance to heat stroke. Similar patterns are observed in cattle, where mutations in TRPM2 and TRPM4 loci exhibit significant geographical and climatic variation, suggesting that ambient temperature exerts selective pressure on gene expression and contributes to adaptive thermoregulation.161,162 Sugimoto et al. reported that chronic heat exposure in NIH3T3cells reduced the mRNA expression level of TRPV1 and increased cellular tolerance to capsaicin.163

Tissue-specific responses are also evident. Cold exposure increases TRPM8 protein expression in BAT149 but decreases it in DRG, accompanied by reduced cold-avoidance behavior.150 These observations indicate that the same TRP channel can exhibit differential expression across tissues following identical thermal stimuli, likely reflecting the distinct physiological roles of different organs or tissues. However, in the same organ, different TRP channels may change simultaneously under the same exposure conditions and act together. After cold exposure, protein expression of TRPV1, TRPA1, and TRPM2 was simultaneously upregulated in BAT of Brandt’s voles, which may contribute to enhanced BAT thermogenesis and cold adaptation.164

Most importantly, a few recent studies have demonstrated that temperature exposure induces changes in lung TRP channel expression, which further affects the progression of asthma. In asthmatic mice with repeated cold exposure, the protein expression level of TRPA1 in the lungs was significantly increased, and antagonizing TRPA1 could markedly alleviate airway injury and AHR.165,166 For combined cold and formaldehyde exposure, simultaneous antagonism of TRPM8 and TRPA1 reduces the levels of lung inflammatory factors, especially IL-1β, in asthmatic mice.167 TRPM8 may serve as the channel mediating cold-induced autonomic nerve responses in the respiratory system,168 and simultaneously triggers inflammatory responses in airway epithelial cells of asthmatic mice through the PKC/NF-κB signaling pathway.169 Furthermore, Liu et al. demonstrated that cold stimulation upregulates TRPM8 expression in mouse lungs, thereby exacerbating inflammation and airway remodeling, whereas inhibiting TRPM8 can suppress the MAPK and NF-κB pathways to alleviate inflammation.170 Heat exposure increases the protein expression level of TRPV1 in the lungs, thereby elevating inflammatory factors in asthmatic mice.171

Discussion

Epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated the adverse effects of extreme temperature exposure on asthma. Both high- and low-temperature exposures increase asthma exacerbations and disease severity, with low-temperature exposure generally exerting a greater impact on asthma-related hospitalizations and mortality.31,32,54 These observations have been corroborated by experimental studies using animal and cell models, where cold exposure impairs airway epithelial barrier function172 and juvenile cold exposure increases asthma prevalence during adolescence.173

In recent years, large temperature fluctuations, such as DTR and TCN, have emerged as research hotspots. Abrupt temperature changes may exert a greater impact on asthma than relatively stable extreme temperatures, possibly due to the physiological mechanisms underlying thermal adaptation. Both higher and lower DTR and TCN significantly affect asthma, with effects depending on reference temperatures and underlying climatic conditions.42,51,53,64,65,66 In cold seasons, smaller TCN reflects multi-day cold exposure, whereas in warm seasons, it indicates prolonged exposure to comfortable temperatures. Larger DTR represents greater daytime temperature fluctuations. Consequently, studies of DTR and TCN must account for climatic context and population activity patterns. For example, low DTR and TCN in hot or cold seasons may represent prolonged exposure to excessive heat or cold; on the contrary, low DTR and TCN in warm seasons represent a mild and less volatile pleasant climate. Combined exposure to temperature extremes and air pollutants further exacerbates asthma, with synergistic effects observed in both epidemiological32,37,45 and biological studies.167,173 Notably, low-temperature exposure, high-temperature exposure, DTR, and TCN all exhibit lag effects, with low-temperature exposure generally having longer-lasting effects than high-temperature exposure,31,32,54 and DTR lagging longer than TCN.46,64,68 Some earlier studies also support this conclusion.174,175

Children and older adults represent vulnerable populations in temperature-related asthma studies.176,177 Among the over 50 studies we reviewed, nearly 20 conducted specialized research on children, and some of the other studies also included separate discussions on children. Older adults are also a vulnerable population for temperature-induced asthma.29,40,41 Future research should define exposure ranges for extreme temperatures, DTR, and TCN across different climate patterns, and incorporate analyses of activity patterns in key populations to generate more precise and actionable conclusions.

Epidemiological evidence alone establishes associations between temperature extremes and asthma but does not reveal underlying mechanisms. Protein expression level of TRP channels may provide the critical biological link. Modulating TRP channel expression alters body temperature regulation144,147 and temperature perception,148,151,152,154,157 while exposure to abnormal temperatures can modify TRP protein expression in various tissues.145,149 Based on the research literature available, the same TRP channel may exhibit differential expression patterns across distinct tissue types: TRPM8 protein expression increases in BAT149 but decreases in DRG after cold exposure.150

Therefore, temperature-mediated inflammatory regulation via TRP channels can be distinguished at three interrelated levels: acute activation, chronic expression changes, and downstream effects. Acute activation by temperature is a rapid, direct gating process, supported by electrophysiological recordings of temperature-induced TRP currents, calcium imaging of transient intracellular Ca2+ elevation, and recapitulated temperature sensitivity in heterologous systems via sole TRP transfection—indicating temperature directly opens channel proteins independent of transcription and translation. Meanwhile, chronic TRP expression changes, a medium-to long-term adaptive response to prolonged temperature exposure, alter TRP mRNA and protein levels; this process is blocked by transcriptional/translational inhibitors and mediated by pathways such as NF-κB. Additionally, downstream inflammatory or neurogenic effects, triggered by TRP activation, include neuropeptide release (CGRP, substance P), increased proinflammatory cytokines, activated NF-κB/MAPK pathways, and tissue/organ-level neurogenic inflammation and abnormalities (vasodilation, plasma extravasation, inflammatory cell infiltration, AHR).

The distinct roles of TRP channels in excitable and non-excitable cells may exert divergent effects on asthma. In the summary of the preceding text, the expression or activation of TRP channels in lung non-excitable cells usually exacerbates asthma-related inflammatory responses.178 Furthermore, study has demonstrated that high expression of TRPV1 contributes to vagal neuron sensitization and aggravates the severity of AHR.179 Therefore, in lung non-excitable cells, the activation of TRP channels may be associated with inflammatory responses; whereas in excitable cells, the activation of TRP channels may sensitize vagal neurons, leading to the exacerbation of AHR. However, this hypothesis currently lacks strong research support.

Several TRP channels have been implicated in asthma pathogenesis. TRPA1,81,105,107,108 TRPV1,91,92,120,121 TRPM8,87,89 TRPM2,86,133 and TRPP2139 show a positive correlation between expression levels and asthma severity. TRPV4, however, exhibits inconsistent effects depending on asthma triggers and immune context.93,95,127,128,130,131

Temperature also modulates TRP channels associated with asthma. Long-term cold adaptation decreases TRPM2 expression in the hypothalamus,153 whereas prolonged heat exposure increases TRPV1 and decreases TRPV4 protein expression in the brain.145 Cold exposure increases TRPM8 in BAT and DRG,149 while its expression level in the DRG decreases.150 These temperature-induced alterations in TRP channel expression in the lungs may contribute to AHR and inflammation, providing a mechanistic explanation for temperature-related asthma exacerbation (Figure 5).

Emerging studies support this mechanism. In mouse models, high-temperature exposure or combined high temperature and NO2 exposure upregulates TRPV1 and triggers asthma-related symptoms.171,180 Cold exposure induces high TRPA1 expression in guinea pig trachea and skin, suggesting that cold-induced cough and AHR may involve activation of cold-sensitive TRPA1 pathways.166 Deng et al. demonstrated that TRPA1, TRPM8, and TRPV1 contribute to temperature-induced asthma in mouse models exposed to heat or cold.123,181

Epidemiological studies have confirmed that co-exposure to temperature and air pollution may exert a greater impact on asthma.36,46,52 In vivo experiments have also demonstrated that certain pollutants can modulate the expression of TRP channels, thereby influencing the progression of asthma.112,116,117 By contrast, co-exposure to low temperature and formaldehyde has been clearly shown to exacerbate allergic asthma via TRPM8 and TRPA1 pathways.167 Furthermore, pollutants such as ozone182 and PM2.5183 are activators of certain TRP channels; therefore, the impact of co-exposure to ambient temperature and pollution may also be an important research direction.

Currently, a small number of studies have confirmed that temperature exposure can regulate the expression of TRP channels, thereby affecting the occurrence and development of asthma. TRPM8,169,170 TRPA1,165,166,167 and TRPV1171 have all been shown to be involved in this process, and both pharmacological antagonism or gene knockout of these TRP channels can significantly reduce asthmatic inflammatory responses or AHR. Future studies can be refined based on existing research to yield more accurate experimental findings. Precisely delineating how exposure to low temperature, high temperature, and large temperature fluctuations modulates the expression of TRP channels and subsequently impacts asthma progression represents a critical gap that needs to be addressed. Meanwhile, experiments typically measure TRP proteins and asthma-related endpoints under controlled temperature conditions; however, active manipulation of TRP proteins under identical temperature conditions is equally essential. That is, the expression of TRP proteins must be regarded as an independent variable to verify the precise contribution of TRP channels to asthma. Co-exposure to temperature and pollutants may also become a key research focus. TRP channels not only act as thermo-sensors but can also be activated by certain chemical stimuli. Whether asthma is further exacerbated when temperature and chemical activators simultaneously affect TRP channels warrants further investigation. Lastly, greater diversity in research models is required. Current studies are largely restricted to animal models, which cannot fully recapitulate human physiology. In particular, mice have a higher thermoneutral temperature than humans, so additional adjustments may be needed in temperature settings. Accordingly, future studies using human in vitro cell models and even human studies will be of great importance.

Overall, as shown in Figure 5, existing evidence suggests that extreme temperatures and temperature fluctuations influence the occurrence and severity of asthma, at least in part, by modulating the expression of specific TRP channel proteins. A small number of studies have begun to support this hypothesis. However, several limitations remain in the current literature:

A large proportion of data in epidemiological studies on temperature and asthma comes from Chinese populations. Although we did not use region as an analytical factor, potential data bias may still exist. Meanwhile, some studies in the epidemiological section adopted questionnaire surveys, which may also introduce potential bias.

Temperature fluctuations vs. extremes: While epidemiological studies consistently indicate that TV may have a greater impact on asthma than simple exposure to extreme temperatures, experimental biological evidence remains limited.

In terms of evidence support, the number of studies on how temperature induces TRP channel activation and changes in protein expression levels is relatively limited, especially regarding the relationship between TRPs and lung tissue proteins. As for the main viewpoint discussed in this paper—“the direct association between temperature, TRP expression, and asthma development”—although it is supported by evidence from three aspects, the number of direct research studies remains relatively scarce.

Tissue-specific TRP expression: TRP channel expression can differ across tissues, suggesting that the same temperature stimulus may elicit distinct responses in different parts of the organism. The combined effect of these heterogeneous responses on asthma remains unclear.

Roles of TRP channels in excitable and non-excitable cells: current studies have not well distinguished the differences in the expression of TRP channels between lung excitable and non-excitable cells, nor have they further discussed the effects and distinctions of TRP channel expression in these two types of cells on a series of asthma-related symptoms such as inflammation, AHR, and airway remodeling.

Regulation of TRP channels: Most studies manipulate TRP channel activity using chemical agonists/antagonists or temperature exposure, rather than directly controlling gene expression. Consequently, observed effects involve a combination of chemical or thermal stimuli plus TRP-mediated inflammatory and immune responses, rather than a fully elucidated causal chain from stimulus to TRP expression, then to downstream asthma pathology.

Based on these limitations, we propose several directions for future research:

Focus on temperature variability (TV, DTR, TCN): these measures of temperature fluctuation warrant greater attention, as current findings remain inconsistent. Future studies should investigate whether similar population cohorts across different climate zones, or different regions within the same climate zone, yield consistent effects of TV, DTR, and TCN on asthma.

Does age have an impact on the expression of TRP channels? Epidemiological studies have shown that the elderly and children are important populations affected by temperature-induced asthma exacerbation. However, there is no evidence at the TRP-related biological level, which is an important research direction.

Integrating temperature variability into TRP research: experiments incorporating TV, DTR, or TCN may produce results that differ from constant temperature exposure, providing insight into how fluctuating temperatures influence TRP channel activity and, in turn, asthma outcomes.

Further distinguish the potential distinct roles of TRP channels in lung excitable and non-excitable cells: the specific hypotheses are whether the expression and activation of TRP channels in non-excitable cells are associated with inflammation, and whether their expression and activation in excitable cells are associated with AHR.

Bridging temperature, TRP channels, and asthma: existing evidence suggests a potential pathway from temperature changes to TRP channel modulation and subsequent asthma exacerbation. However, studies that directly connect these three elements remain scarce. More comprehensive mechanistic research is needed to substantiate this link.

Therapeutic implications: unsuitable temperatures exacerbate asthma symptoms. Investigating whether restoring patients to an optimal temperature range can mitigate symptoms—particularly for individuals who develop asthma following exposure to abnormal temperatures—could inform novel preventive or therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

By integrating multidisciplinary studies on temperature, asthma, and TRP channels, we hypothesize that extreme temperatures may exacerbate asthma by modulating the expression of specific TRP channels, thereby inducing increased AHR and inflammatory responses. Although research that combines these three factors remains limited, sufficient evidence is available to support the plausibility of this hypothesis. This perspective addresses a critical gap in epidemiological studies, which often lack biological mechanistic evidence linking temperature exposure to asthma, and we have identified key supporting studies in this field. By focusing on temperature-induced changes in TRP channel expression and further exploring the regulatory role of TRP channels in asthma, this approach provides a novel framework for investigating the relationship between temperature and asthma. Furthermore, this review highlights the importance of maintaining a comfortable thermal environment for individuals with asthma and identifies potential novel targets for therapeutic intervention.
