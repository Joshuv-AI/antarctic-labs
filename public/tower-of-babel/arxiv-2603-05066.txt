Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning
Michal Nauman, Marek Cygan, Pieter Abbeel

Abstract
RL agents are typically trained under a single, fixed reward function, which makes them brittle to reward misspecification and limits their ability to adapt to changing task preferences. We introduce Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RCRL), a framework that trains a single agent to optimize a family of reward specifications while collecting experience under only one nominal objective. RCRL conditions the agent on reward parameterizations and learns multiple reward objectives from a shared replay data entirely off-policy, enabling a single policy to represent reward-specific behaviors. Across single-task, multi-task, and vision-based benchmarks, we show that RCRL not only improves performance under the nominal reward parameterization, but also enables efficient adaptation to new parameterizations. Our results demonstrate that RCRL provides a scalable mechanism for learning robust, steerable policies without sacrificing the simplicity of single-task training.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved substantial progress in recent years, enabling advances in domains such as language modeling
(
51
;
25
)
and robotic control
(
60
;

. Despite these successes, reward specification remains a bottleneck to practical deployment. Designing effective reward functions typically requires domain expertise and iterative tuning
(
49

, and small changes in reward composition can induce large differences in behavior
(
64

. Moreover, policies trained under a fixed reward offer no flexibility during deployment and adapting to revised reward functions requires retraining
(
44

To address these RL limitations, we propose
Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RCRL)
, a framework that embraces the possibility that many reasonable reward specifications might exist for the same task. Rather than training with a fixed reward function, RCRL collects experience under a single nominal reward parameterization while learning off-policy with a diverse set of counterfactual alternative reward functions. Both the actor and critic are conditioned on this parameterization, enabling the agent to represent and recover reward-specific behaviors while sharing experience collected under the nominal task. Because all updates rely on replayed data generated under
\psi^{\star}
, the learning procedure remains fully off-policy.

The goal of RL is to learn a policy that maximizes expected return in a MDP

In many control tasks, desired behavior is specified indirectly through composite reward functions

Reward-Conditioned RL

3.1
Constructing the Set of Parameterizations
\Psi

A central design choice in RCRL is how to define the set of reward parameterizations
\Psi
that the agent is conditioned on during training. Below, we describe two complementary strategies for constructing
\Psi
, each reflecting a different way of exposing the agent to variation in reward specification.

Parameterized Reward Conditioning.
In the first approach, we generate alternative reward parameterizations by applying controlled transformations to the nominal reward parameterization
\psi^{\star}
. Many composite reward functions used in locomotion and manipulation tasks are expressed as linear or multiplicative combinations of reward components, with parameters determining the relative contribution of each component. This structure naturally admits continuous families of reward parameterizations. Specifically, we define alternative parameterizations
\psi\in\Psi
by applying elementwise perturbations
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
to the nominal parameterization
\psi^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Alternative parameterizations are then obtained via elementwise multiplication
\psi=\psi^{\star}\odot\Delta
.

The perturbation vector
\Delta
is sampled from a predefined distribution supported on the positive real numbers, ensuring that the qualitative structure of the reward is preserved. The spread of this distribution controls the extent of variation in reward parameterizations used during training, with concentrated distributions inducing small deviations, and broader distributions yielding more changes in the nominal reward. To condition the policy and value function on the reward parameterization used for a given update, we provide the perturbation vector
\Delta
, or a transformation thereof, as part of the network input alongside the environment state. As a result, the mechanism allows the agent to learn policies corresponding to a continuum of reward specifications.

Example: Linear Reward Parameterization

Consider a linear reward function
r_{\psi^{\star}}(s,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{i}^{\star}c_{i}(s,a)
. Under reward conditioning, an alternative parameterization
\psi_{i}=\Delta_{i}\psi_{i}^{\star}
preserves this form, where
\Delta_{i}>1
increases and
\Delta_{i}<1
decreases the relative importance of component
i
.

Auxiliary Task Conditioning.
In the second approach, we construct the set of parameterizations
\Psi
using reward functions that correspond to distinct objectives defined within the same environment. Rather than generating variants of a single nominal reward parameterization
\psi^{\star}
, these parameterizations represent qualitatively different tasks applied to the same robot embodiment. For example, when the nominal reward
\psi^{\star}
defines a
run
objective, additional parameterizations in
\Psi
may correspond to related behaviors such as
stand
or
walk
. The motivation for this approach draws from prior work showing that training on multiple related tasks can improve performance even when evaluation is performed on a single target objective, particularly when tasks share state and action spaces
(
35
;
41
;
47
)
.

The improvements observed in multi-task RL can be attributed to two factors: increased state-action coverage, as different tasks induce different trajectories through the environment; and additional reward supervision, where auxiliary objectives provide complementary learning signals or form natural curricula (for example, standing facilitates walking, which in turn supports running). While increasing state-action coverage requires additional interaction or offline data, conditioning on auxiliary task rewards can be achieved entirely off-policy using experience collected under the nominal task. As a result, auxiliary task conditioning captures part of the benefit of multi-task learning while keeping interaction constrained to the single task defined by
\psi^{\star}
.

Reward Conditioning and Training Stability

In this subsection, we describe how, in practice, the agent can be conditioned on arbitrary reward parameterizations, as well as address stability considerations when optimizing over multiple reward specifications.

Conditioning on the Reward Parameterization.
As illustrated in Figure
2
, RCRL conditions the agent on a representation of the reward parameterization
\psi\in\Psi
used in a given update. This requires a mapping from reward parameterizations to representations that are expressive enough to distinguish between different reward specifications. In the simplest setting, where reward parameterizations are defined through continuous perturbations of a nominal configuration, conditioning can be implemented by directly concatenating the reward parameter
\psi
or perturbation vector
\Delta
to the environment state, leading to a conditioned state
z=[s,\psi]
when reward parameters are used and
z=[s,\Delta]
in case of perturbation. In theory, this approach allows for unbounded set of reward parameterizations and can adapt to previously unseen reward configurations at test time.

Alternatively, when the set
\Psi
is finite, we adopt an embedding approach inspired by multi-task RL. In this setting, each
\psi\in\Psi
is associated with a learnable reward embedding stored in a table indexed by the parameterization identity. These embeddings are learned with the value function by backpropagating gradients from the critic loss, following practices in multi-task RL
(
47
)
. This formulation introduces additional degrees of freedom, allowing the model to learn representations of parameterizations that best support learning across the considered reward set. As such, this approach is tailored to a fixed set of known reward parameterizations and enables their representations to be optimized for the learning objective. We validate the importance of conditioning on
\psi
and compare these conditioning strategies through ablation studies in Section
4
.

Training Stability.
Conditioning on multiple reward parameterizations introduces considerations similar to those encountered in multi-task RL, particularly when reward scales differ across parameterizations. In our experiments, we evaluate RCRL using both algorithms that incorporate stabilization mechanisms known to stabilize multi-task RL (eg. reward normalization and categorical RL) and algorithms that do not. In Section
4
, we observe performance improvements across both classes of methods, indicating that RCRL can be applied on top of existing algorithms without requiring additional stabilization beyond those already employed by the base method. We further discuss the stability-related considerations Appendices
A
and
B
.

Experiments

We evaluate the proposed reward-conditioning framework across single-task, multi-task, and vision-based RL settings through a series of experiments designed to address the following research questions:

1.

Does training with multiple reward parameterizations improve performance on the target task defined by the nominal reward configuration
\psi^{\star}
?

2.

Does exposure to alternative reward parameterizations enable zero-shot and few-shot transfer to alternative reward configurations
\psi\in\Psi
?

To further understand the behavior of RCRL, we conduct ablation studies to isolate the contribution of individual design components. Full experimental details are provided in Appendix

4.1
RCRL Configuration

In our experiments, the set of reward parameterizations
\Psi
is constructed using one of two strategies introduced in Section
3.1
: parameterized reward conditioning or auxiliary task conditioning. The strategy used is specified in each experimental subsection. Across all experiments, we fix the conditioning probability to
\alpha=0.5
, so that half of each training batch is updated using the nominal and half using alternative reward parameterizations. For parameterized reward conditioning, alternative reward parameterizations are generated by multiplicatively scaling the nominal reward coefficients. The scaling factors
\Delta_{i}
are sampled from a stratified log-uniform distribution with support
[0.25,4.0]
, where sampling is performed independently below and above
1.0
. For auxiliary task conditioning,
\Psi
is constructed from task-specific reward functions defined within each benchmark. When a benchmark contains
N
tasks that share the same robot embodiment, the reward functions of all
N
tasks are used as conditioning targets for every task. Additional implementation details are provided in Appendices
B
and
C.5

Despite this, RCRL provides a strong initialization, leading to faster adaptation when transferring to new reward configurations.

Zero-shot Transfer.
Finally, we evaluate if RCRL enables zero-shot adaptation to alternative reward specifications, allowing the agent to adjust its behavior at deployment without additional training. Unlike the finetuning experiments, where the agent collects experience under the new reward, zero-shot transfer tests whether conditioning on parameterizations alone is sufficient to induce behavior aligned with alternative objectives. To this end, we train a RCRL-based agent under a nominal reward while exposing it during training to auxiliary reward parameterizations that promote distinct behaviors, including behaviors that are not well represented under the state-action distribution induced by optimizing the nominal task. We evaluate zero-shot transfer on three continuous control tasks from DMC:
cheetah-run
, where auxiliary rewards encourage different running speeds;
hopper-hop
, where auxiliary rewards favor different standing heights; and
humanoid-walk
, where auxiliary rewards modulate action penalties. At evaluation time, we only change the conditioning provided to the policy without updating any network parameters and assess if conditioning alone induces the desired behavioral adjustments.

As shown in Figure
5
, the RCRL-based agent reliably adjusts its behavior to optimize the specified auxiliary reward, exhibiting controllable changes in speed, posture, and actuation strength. Notably, all such behaviors are learned fully off-policy: the agent collects experience exclusively under the nominal reward
\psi^{\star}
, and no additional environment interaction is performed for auxiliary objectives. When evaluated under alternative reward functions, RCRL achieves meaningful performance, whereas standard single-task agents are unable to adapt without retraining. At the same time, RCRL attains performance comparable to full multi-task learning approaches that explicitly collect data under all auxiliary rewards. Together, these results demonstrate that conditioning on reward parameterizations enables the learning of a single, steerable policy whose behavior can be modulated at deployment time, while preserving the simplicity and efficiency of a standard single-task data collection pipeline.

4.4
Ablations

We conduct studies to examine the impact of design choices in RCRL. Specifically, we assess the necessity of conditioning on sampled parameterizations, compare parameterized reward conditioning with auxiliary task conditioning in both single and multi-task RL, and study the effect of varying the ratio
\alpha
of nominal to alternative reward parameterizations. As shown in Figure
6
, removing reward conditioning leads to performance drops of up to
40\%
, with larger degradations under auxiliary task conditioning where reward definitions differ more substantially from the nominal task. We find that RCRL is robust to the choice of
\alpha
, with strong performance across all tested values and best performance when
30\%
-
50\%
of updates use alternative parameterizations. We detail these experimental settings in Appendix
C.3.3
, and provide additional ablation studies in Appendix
D
.

Goal-Conditioned RL.
Goal-conditioned RL (GCRL) defines tasks in terms of goal states, typically using sparse reward functions that indicate success with respect to a given goal
(
37
;
62
)
. In practice, the RL agent is conditioned on a goal representation, enabling a single agent to learn behaviors for multiple goals within the same environment
(
4
;
2
;
24
)
. This conditioning facilitates generalization across goals and can support adaptation to novel goals not encountered during training
(
19
;
20
;
54
;
55
;
9
)
. RCRL is conceptually related, but differs in the conditioning approach. Whereas GCRL conditions policies on discrete or continuous goals that induce sparse rewards, RCRL conditions on reward parameterizations that define dense, composite reward functions. In this sense, RCRL generalizes the conditioning paradigm from goal selection to continuous control over reward preferences. As in GCRL, conditioning allows the agent to modulate its behavior at test time; however, in RCRL this modulation reflects changes in reward weighting rather than changes in target goals.

Domain Randomization and Data Augmentation.
Domain randomization trains a single policy across a distribution of environment parameters, such as physics or dynamics, with the goal of improving robustness to discrepancies between training and deployment conditions
(
68
;
57
;
5
)
. By exposing the agent to randomized environments during training, the policy is encouraged to become invariant to these variations which can improve generalization
(
61
;
16
;
13
)
. A similar principle underlies data augmentation in RL, where transformations of observations encourage invariance to factors such as viewpoint or texture
(
42
;
74
)
. In both cases, the policy is trained without access to the randomized parameters and it must learn representations that treat these variations as irrelevant
(
45
;
59
)
. RCRL differs in that instead of enforcing invariance to reward variation, RCRL explicitly conditions the policy on the reward parameterization, allowing behavior to adapt to different reward functions. From this perspective, RCRL can be viewed as a form of reward augmentation during training where the interaction data is reused to generate multiple learning signals under different parameterizations. When the goal is performance under the nominal reward, this process acts as a regularizer analogous to data augmentation. At the same time, conditioning preserves the ability to recover reward-specific behavior.

Multi-Task RL.
Multi-task RL (MTRL) aims to train a single agent to solve multiple tasks with distinct reward functions and, in some cases, distinct environment dynamics
(
67
;
31
)
. Typical approaches train a single agent by sharing representations across tasks
(
53
;
15
;
31
;
75
)
. This joint training can improve transfer and generalization by encouraging the agent to learn representations that capture the common structure
(
17
;
41
;
47
)
. RCRL differs from MTRL primarily in how experience is collected. While MTRL typically learns multiple tasks using task-specific interaction data and exploration policies, RCRL conditions the policy and value function on multiple reward parameterizations while collecting all experience under a single nominal task. In this sense, RCRL can be viewed as a bridge between single and multi-task RL: it learns multiple reward objectives like MTRL, but does so using a single data stream by reusing shared experience off-policy. From this perspective, RCRL resembles a form of off-policy MTRL, where auxiliary tasks are learned via counterfactual rewards rather than task-specific interaction. Furthermore, as we show in Section
4
, RCRL can also be applied within standard multi-task RL settings to further improve performance.

6
Limitations

While RCRL does not require rewards to be decomposable in a specific way and can be used with additional auxiliary objectives, a key limitation is that auxiliary rewards are learned off-policy under the state-action distribution induced by the nominal policy. As a result, learning behaviors that are poorly supported by this distribution may be difficult without additional exploration. Moreover, there is currently no principled framework for identifying which reward parameterizations are synergistic and which may be conflicting or uninformative. We hypothesize that, in practice, some pairs of reward functions may provide limited mutual benefit or even interfere with the learning. Finally, RCRL introduces negligible overhead, as it only requires computing scalar rewards for alternative reward parameterizations using simple constant-time operations, without requiring any additional optimization steps.

7
Conclusions

We introduced Reward-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RCRL), a framework that leverages structured reward functions to improve data efficiency, robustness, and transfer in reinforcement learning. By training agents off-policy using multiple reward parameterizations while collecting experience under a nominal objective, RCRL enables a single policy to adapt to diverse reward specifications. Across single-task, multi-task, and vision-based settings, we showed that reward conditioning improves performance under the nominal reward, enables efficient finetuning to new tasks, and supports zero-shot behavioral adaptation without additional training. These results demonstrate that explicitly exploiting reward structure provides a powerful and practical mechanism for enhancing learning and generalization in RL.

Impact Statement

This work contributes to improving the robustness, adaptability, and efficiency of reinforcement learning systems by enabling agents to learn and adapt to multiple reward specifications without additional environment interaction. We do not foresee any direct societal impacts specific to this work beyond those generally associated with advances in reinforcement learning research.

Appendix A
Additional Considerations on Training Stability

Conditioning on multiple reward parameterizations introduces challenges similar to those encountered in multi-task RL, particularly when reward functions differ substantially in scale or density. When a single value function is trained across heterogeneous rewards, standard mean-squared error (MSE) objectives can produce imbalanced gradient contributions, causing reward parameterizations with larger numerical magnitude to dominate learning
(
8
)
. This issue is well documented in the multi-task RL literature and can lead to training instability or degraded convergence if left unaddressed
(
67
;
15
;
31
;
75
;
41
;
72
;
47
)
.

In practice, such instability primarily arises when the set of reward parameterizations
\Psi
contains highly diverse rewards with significantly different scales. In our experiments, most reward parameterizations are designed to remain within a controlled range around the nominal reward, and we do not observe instability beyond that of the underlying base algorithm. Moreover, prior work has shown that combining reward normalization with distributional value learning effectively mitigates scale-induced instability. In particular, the
BRC
framework demonstrates that pairing reward normalization
(
3
)
with a categorical critic loss
(
7
;
21
)
enables stable learning across heterogeneous rewards
(
47
)
. Since many modern RL algorithms, such as
SimbaV2

(
43
)
,
BRC

(
47
)
,
XQC

(
52
)
, or
FastTD3

(
64
)
already incorporate reward normalization and categorical critics for single-task training, RCRL can typically be integrated without introducing additional stabilization mechanisms.

Appendix B
Implementation Details

This section provides additional implementation details for the algorithms used in our experiments. We describe how RCRL is integrated into each baseline method, highlighting only deviations from the original implementations, and detail the two proposed reward-conditioning procedures (parameterized reward conditioning and auxiliary task conditioning). Hyperparameters are reported in Appendix
C.5
, and we plan to release our code soon.

B.1
Algorithms

We briefly summarize the algorithmic integrations considered in this work. Unless otherwise stated, all architectural choices, optimization settings, and training procedures follow the corresponding baseline implementations, with modifications limited to the incorporation of reward conditioning.

SimbaV2+RCRL and BRC+RCRL
– The integration of RCRL into
SimbaV2
and
BRC
follows a largely identical procedure, stemming from their use of reward normalization and categorical RL value learning. Both algorithms employ reward normalization and categorical value learning, which enforce bounded return ranges
[V_{min},V_{max}]
and require handling when introducing multiple reward parameterizations. While several approaches are possible, such as designing reward parameterizations that are pre-normalized, we adopt a simpler strategy that minimizes additional reward engineering. Specifically, we compute all alternative rewards during environment interaction and maintain separate running normalization statistics for each reward function used during training. While this approach limits the set of reward parameterizations to a finite collection, it is not a constraint in practice, as a sufficiently large set of alternative reward specifications can still be used. Because reward parameterizations are drawn from a finite set, we employ the embedding-based conditioning strategy described in Section
3
. This is particularly convenient for
BRC
, which already includes an embedding module for task conditioning in its standard formulation; we reuse this module to embed both task identities and reward specifications within each task.

DrQV2+RCRL
– In contrast to
SimbaV2
and
BRC
,
DrQV2
uses a standard mean-squared error critic loss and does not employ reward normalization, which substantially simplifies the integration of RCRL. We therefore apply reward-conditioned training directly using an MSE loss without introducing any additional normalization, allowing us to evaluate whether RCRL remains stable in this setting. Reward parameterizations are sampled from a continuous space, and the corresponding perturbation vectors are used directly as reward embeddings by concatenating them to the state, without introducing a separate embedding module.

B.2
Conditioning

In this subsection, we describe the implementation of the two strategies used to generate the set of alternative reward parameterizations in RCRL: parameterized reward conditioning and auxiliary task conditioning. We focus on how the set
\Psi
is instantiated in practice, how reward parameterizations are sampled during training, and how conditioning information is incorporated into the policy and value networks.

Parameterized Reward Conditioning

For parameterized reward conditioning, the set
\Psi
is defined implicitly as a continuous family of reward parameterizations obtained by multiplicatively perturbing the nominal reward coefficients. During training, for each update that uses an alternative reward, a perturbation vector
\Delta
is sampled independently from a predefined continuous distribution, and the corresponding reward parameterization is constructed as
\psi=\psi^{\star}\odot\Delta
. Below, we present the code used to sample the perturbation vectors. In practice, the benchmarks used in our experiments (ie. DeepMind Control, OpenAI Gym, and HumanoidBench) employ reward functions that are either linear combinations of reward components or multiplicative (or exponentiated) compositions. For linear reward functions of the form
r_{\psi}(s,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{i}~c_{i}(s,a)
the perturbations are applied directly to the linear coefficients
\psi_{i}
. For multiplicative reward functions of the form
r_{\psi}(s,a)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}(s,a)^{\psi_{i}}
the perturbations are applied to the corresponding exponents. This unified parameterization allows the same perturbation mechanism to be applied consistently across different reward structures. Below, we present the code used to sample perturbation vectors during training.

For auxiliary task conditioning, the set
\Psi

Specifically, auxiliary task rewards are constructed by evaluating the reward functions of other tasks that share the same agent embodiment on the collected state-action trajectories. For example, in the DeepMind Control Suite dog benchmarks, tasks such as
dog-run

dog-walk
, and
dog-trot
