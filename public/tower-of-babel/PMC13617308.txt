Refractory Post‐TIPS Hepatic Encephalopathy Complicated by Small Bowel Obstruction: A Therapeutic Dilemma

Ammar Ismail, Amira Alnakeb, Saba Altarawneh, Joseph Depasquale, Neetu Rawal

Abstract

AbstractIntroductionRefractory hyperammonemic encephalopathy in cirrhosis presents a therapeutic challenge, particularly in post‐TIPS patients or with contraindications to standard therapies. We report a fatal case complicated by small bowel obstruction (SBO), highlighting the limitations of current management strategies.Case PresentationA 69‐year‐old male with decompensated NASH cirrhosis and recent TIPS revision for shunt stenosis developed SBO. He presented with severe encephalopathy (GCS 5), seizures, and MRI‐confirmed cerebral edema. Due to SBO, lactulose and rifaximin were not effective. Despite aggressive dialysis with continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD) reducing ammonia from 447 to 160 μmol/L, he progressed to irreversible cerebral edema and met brain death criteria.DiscussionPost‐TIPS hyperammonemia carries high mortality, especially when compounded by SBO. CVVHD effectively lowers ammonia but may fail to prevent neurologic injury if initiated late.ConclusionThis case underscores the need for proactive RRT and novel ammonia‐lowering strategies in high‐risk patients, especially when gut decontamination is not feasible.

1. Introduction

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a devastating complication of cirrhosis, affecting 30%–40% of patients during their disease course [, ]. Post‐transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) HE is particularly severe, with hyperammonemia driving neurologic decline and mortality [–]. While lactulose and rifaximin are first‐line therapies [], their administration is limited in intestinal obstruction or refractory cases similar to our case.

Hyperammonemia exceeding 200 μmol/L poses a critical risk for cerebral edema and irreversible injury []. Renal replacement therapy (RRT), especially continuous modalities like continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD), may mitigate this risk by providing sustained ammonia clearance [, ]. However, evidence remains sparse, particularly in post‐TIPS patients with concurrent small bowel obstruction (SBO).

We present a fatal case of refractory hyperammonemia post‐TIPS, complicated by SBO, to highlight the therapeutic dilemma of managing HE when standard therapies fail, the role of CVVHD in ammonia clearance, and the poor prognosis despite biochemical improvement.

2. Case Presentation

A 69‐year‐old man with a history of decompensated NASH‐related cirrhosis (recurrent ascites, HE, nonbleeding varices s/p banding), portal vein thrombosis (PVT) with superior mesenteric vein (SMV) extension status post‐TIPS and thrombectomy (with recent TIPS revision to 10 mm due to stenosis 2 days prior to admission), hypertension, diabetes, and a recurrent incarcerated right inguinal hernia was brought to the emergency department by his family for acute‐onset altered mental status. The patient was initially nonverbal, unresponsive to commands, and progressively deteriorated to a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 5, necessitating intubation and ICU admission.

Initial management focused on HE with lactulose and rifaximin; however, his mental status continued to decline. On ICU Day 2, new twitching of the hands and eyelids was observed, and video EEG confirmed seizure activity, prompting initiation of IV levetiracetam, propofol infusion, and midazolam infusion for status epilepticus. Concurrently, CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated SBO with a transition point within the right inguinal hernia sac (Figure ). Because nasogastric output was initially low and the abdomen was scaphoid without significant distension, conservative management was pursued given the patient’s prohibitive surgical risk. Lactulose was continued through the nasogastric tube and rectally, while rifaximin was administered through the nasogastric tube. Three days after the CT findings, nasogastric output increased to > 1500 mL/24 h and ammonia levels rose sharply from 264 μmol/L to over 447 μmol/L. Lactulose and rifaximin were therefore discontinued, and RRT was initiated. The patient could not tolerate intermittent hemodialysis (HD) because of high vasopressor requirements and was transitioned to CVVHD.

Because the patient had recently undergone TIPS revision with dilation of the stenotic segment just 2 days prior to presentation, there was early consideration for possible TIPS occlusion in light of worsening hyperammonemia and encephalopathy. As the original procedure was performed externally, TIPS occlusion was deferred to the managing interventional team familiar with the patient’s prior intervention. Plans were initiated to transfer the patient back to the facility where the TIPS procedure had been performed for evaluation and potential TIPS occlusion. However, transfer was delayed and ultimately not possible due to the patient’s hemodynamic instability.

CVVHD was performed for a total period of around 60 h, and although CVVHD was transiently interrupted several times for a total period of around 8 h, to obtain a brain MRI, and for technical issues like filter clotting, ammonia levels dropped from 447 μmol/L to 160 μmol/L over the total period. Despite this, his neurologic status did not improve. Sedation was held for > 48 h, yet brainstem reflexes remained absent. MRI brain, which was performed around 12 h after starting CVVHD, revealed diffuse cerebral edema consistent with severe encephalopathy. EEG demonstrated no cortical activity for a period of 48 h after brain MRI findings. After extensive family discussions regarding his poor neurologic prognosis, care was transitioned to comfort measures, and the patient died shortly after extubation.

3. Discussion

HE is one of the most debilitating complications of cirrhosis, severely impacting patients’ quality of life and healthcare resource utilization []. It marks the transition to decompensated disease, akin to ascites or variceal bleeding []. The prevalence of overt hepatic encephalopathy (OHE) ranges from 10%–14% at cirrhosis diagnosis [, ] to 16%–21% in decompensated cases [, ] and surges to 10%–50% post‐TIPS [, ]. Cumulatively, 30%–40% of cirrhotic patients experience OHE during their disease course, often recurrently [], while minimal or covert hepatic encephalopathy (CHE) affects 20%–80% []. Post‐TIPS HE is particularly challenging, as excessive shunt flow due to large stent diameter can precipitate intractable symptoms [].

SBO represents a clinically challenging scenario that has received limited attention in the literature, particularly when complicated by OHE. Because first‐line therapies for OHE are generally administered enterally rather than intravenously, patients with concurrent SBO may require alternative pharmacologic or nonpharmacologic management strategies. In addition, SBO may itself exacerbate HE. Intestinal stasis can promote bacterial overgrowth and gut microbial alteration, while prolonged contact between luminal contents and the intestinal mucosa may increase ammonia absorption. Disruption of the intestinal barrier may further enhance permeability, bacterial translocation, and portal endotoxemia, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation and contributing to worsening encephalopathy [–].

Current guidelines recommend lactulose and rifaximin as first‐line therapies [], yet evidence suggests they offer no prophylactic benefit against post‐TIPS HE compared to placebo []. Alternative pharmacologic therapeutic options include high‐volume polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350, which may provide greater clinical efficacy than enteral lactulose; however, its use is also limited to enteral administration [, ]. Off‐label metronidazole may be considered as an alternative to rifaximin; however, evidence supporting its use remains limited. One study demonstrating no significant differences between the two agents in clinical outcomes, length of hospital stay, or reduction in serum ammonia levels []. For patients with Grade 3–4 HE who are unable to swallow or are at high risk of aspiration, rectal administration of lactulose enemas represents another therapeutic option []. In refractory cases, shunt reduction or TIPS occlusion may reverse HE [].

HD emerges as a potential salvage therapy, although the literature is limited to isolated case reports. For instance, Kaneda et al. described a cirrhotic patient with chronic kidney disease whose recurrent HE resolved with daily HD but recurred when frequency was reduced []. Similarly, Viguera et al. reported a noncirrhotic patient with hyperammonemia (445 μmol/L) and seizures in whom ammonia levels improved with CVVHD, but clinical outcomes were not reported []. These cases underscore HD’s role in ammonia clearance, particularly when levels exceed 150–200 μmol/L, a threshold linked to intracranial hypertension [].

Intermittent HD is highly effective at rapidly clearing ammonia, making it the preferred modality in many hyperammonemic crises. This is particularly important given that prolonged hyperammonemia increases the risk of irreversible neurologic damage [, ]. However, the rapid solute and fluid shifts associated with intermittent HD may exacerbate cerebral edema, a critical concern in patients with severe HE or acute liver failure [–].

In contrast, CVVHD offers a more gradual ammonia clearance due to its lower blood flow rates (Qb) compared to intermittent HD. While this might suggest reduced efficacy, CVVHD provides the advantage of sustained ammonia removal with fewer treatment interruptions []. More importantly, its slower fluid and solute shifts minimize the risk of worsening intracranial hypertension, making it a safer option in hemodynamically unstable patients or those at high risk of cerebral edema [, ].

Standard CVVHD is primarily designed to remove water‐soluble, non–protein‐bound solutes []. Ammonia, which is water‐soluble and not protein bound so can effectively be cleared by CVVHD; however, there are additional mediators implicated in the pathophysiology of HE including inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐17, along with bile acids and trace elements (e.g., manganese) []. Manganese may be effectively cleared by CVVHD [], but inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐17) have large molecular sizes that limit their efficient clearance, and bile acids are extensively bound to albumin and therefore are not effectively removed [, ].

Although brain MRI performed 12 h after the initiation of CVVHD revealed signs of cerebral edema, serum ammonia levels decreased significantly from 447 μmol/L to 160 μmol/L over a 60‐h period (Figure ). Despite this marked biochemical improvement, there was no corresponding change in neurologic function. As shown in Figure , ammonia levels declined more consistently during the period of relatively uninterrupted CVVHD between hours 37 and 60. In contrast, periods with more frequent CVVHD interruptions coincided with greater fluctuations in serum ammonia levels. These findings suggest that minimizing interruptions in CVVHD may promote more sustained ammonia clearance.

Studies have demonstrated that CVVHD achieves effective ammonia clearance, particularly when higher ultrafiltration rates are employed []. Thus, while intermittent HD remains a choice for rapid ammonia reduction in stable patients, CVVHD may be preferable in critically ill or unstable individuals, where its gentler hemodynamic profile and continuous clearance might offer a therapeutic advantage.

4. Conclusion

This case illustrates the lethal synergy of post‐TIPS hyperammonemia and SBO, where standard HE therapies were precluded and CVVHD, though biochemically effective, failed to reverse neurologic injury. SBO played a significant role in clinical deterioration because of inability to administer first‐line medications and worsening encephalopathy due to stasis and bacterial overgrowth. Relative delay in RRT initiation may make ammonia‐lowering treatments ineffective, as cerebral edema becomes irreversible. While CVVHD’s gradual clearance is safer in unstable patients, its interrupted use (e.g., for imaging) may compromise efficacy. Future studies should define the optimal indications and timing for RRT in patients with severe hyperammonemia including post‐TIPS HE who cannot receive conventional enteral therapy (e.g., intestinal obstruction).
