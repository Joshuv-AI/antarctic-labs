A Rare Case of Mediastinal Hematoma Causing Mass Effect Following Transradial Access for Coronary Angiography
Authors: Prudvi Tarun Betha, Siddharth Bajaj, Harshavardhan Ghadiam
Published: 2026

Transradial access has become the preferred approach for coronary angiography, owing to its lower risk of bleeding, earlier ambulation, and improved patient comfort compared with transfemoral access. Despite these advantages, complications can still occur. The most reported vascular issues include radial artery occlusion, puncture-site hematoma, and less frequently, pseudoaneurysm formation. However, mediastinal hematoma is a rare complication, with a higher rate in transradial than in transfemoral access.1 It typically results from arterial injury during manipulation.

We describe the case of an individual who developed a large mediastinal hematoma following an attempted transradial coronary angiography, requiring both endovascular and surgical management.

Case Presentation

A 77-year-old male patient with a medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia was admitted for elective left heart catheterization following an abnormal exercise stress test performed for evaluation of exertional dyspnea.

Coronary angiography was performed via the right radial artery using a 6-Fr Glidesheath slender (Terumo, Campbell, CA), a 0.035 in × 145 cm Wholey Rosen J-tip curve guidewire (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), and the modified Seldinger technique. Due to the significant tortuosity of the right subclavian artery and resistance to guidewire manipulation, balloon-assisted tracking (BAT) was not attempted. This decision was based on the severity of the subclavian tortuosity encountered and the operator’s preference for prompt access site conversion. Right femoral access was obtained using a 4-Fr micropuncture sheath, later interchanged with a 6-Fr Pinnacle sheath (Terumo, Somerset, NJ). 5-Fr diagnostic catheters were used for angiography, followed by a 6-Fr Launcher EBU 3.5 catheter (Medtronic) and a 0.035 in x 260 cm Cook Safe-T-J guidewire (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN). Angiography showed a 50%-60% calcific lesion in the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery, 20%-30% stenosis in the distal left main, and 60% stenosis in the very distal right posterior descending artery (RPDA). The lesion was assessed with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), which demonstrated a reference vessel diameter of approximately 3.5 mm distally. The pre-procedural instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR ≤ 0.89) was abnormal, confirming the hemodynamic significance of the lesion.

Percutaneous coronary intervention to the LAD was performed with deployment of a 3.5 × 23 mm Xience Sky Point (Abott Vascular Inc, Santa Clara, CA) drug-eluting stent to the proximal LAD, with resolution of stenosis. The very distal RPDA lesion was managed medically, given its location and the small myocardial territory at risk.

Post-procedure, the patient developed mid-sternal chest pain and dyspnea. The electrocardiogram did not show any acute ST-T changes and was unchanged compared to the past. A chest radiograph showed well-demarcated opacification in the right upper hemithorax (Fig. 1A). Computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed a large right posterior mediastinal hematoma measuring 5.2 × 5.2 × 7.5 cm with contrast extravasation from the right subclavian artery branch (thyrocervical trunk; Fig. 1B; Fig. 2A). The bleeding appeared to have resolved spontaneously, resulting in the resolution of chest pain and preserved hemodynamic stability. Over the course of the hospital stay, the patient developed progressive dyspnea and increasing oxygen requirements but remained free of chest pain. Due to worsening respiratory failure, the patient was intubated and referred to our centre. Follow-up CT demonstrated interval expansion of the hematoma to 8.7 × 10.0 × 12.2 cm; however, no active contrast extravasation was seen, and no evidence of airway compression was present.

The transthoracic echocardiogram was unremarkable. Interventional radiology was consulted, and coil embolization of the right thyrocervical trunk was performed (Fig. 2B). Three coils (Concerto Helix Detachable [Medtronic])—2 that were 2 mm x 4 cm, and 1 that was 3 mm x 4 cm—were deployed, and a right-sided chest tube was placed. Thoracic surgery was later consulted, and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was performed for evacuation of the hematoma. Intraoperatively, the hematoma was found to be compressing the trachea and subclavian artery and was successfully evacuated, with no active bleeding identified. A chest drain was placed postoperatively, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was continued throughout the hospital course. The patient was gradually weaned off a high-flow nasal cannula, and the chest drain was removed 2 days after the VATS procedure, without complication. The patient was discharged home in a stable condition.

Discussion

Vascular injury is a recognized complication of both transradial and transfemoral percutaneous coronary interventions. Although most cases present with overt signs, such as localized hematoma or bruising, some patients may exhibit only subtle manifestations until symptoms develop. The transradial approach is generally associated with fewer vascular complications compared to the transfemoral route. This difference may be attributable to the ease of detecting bleeding at the radial access site and the effectiveness of external compression in achieving hemostasis. In contrast, transfemoral access carries a higher risk, as bleeding can be concealed within the retroperitoneal or pelvic spaces, and effective external compression of the femoral artery can be more challenging.

Injury to the subclavian artery or its proximal branches, such as the thyrocervical trunk within the intrathoracic cavity, caused by guidewire-related perforation, dissection, or subintimal passage, can lead to mediastinal hematoma and tracheal compression. The subclavian artery is predominantly intrathoracic in location, and the thyrocervical trunk arises from its first part while it is still within the superior mediastinum, ascending toward the neck to supply various cervical structures. Although thyrocervical trunk injury may present as a cervical hematoma, injury at the origin of the thyrocervical trunk or its proximal branches was reported to result in bleeding directly into the superior mediastinal compartment2 and can track through the fascial planes into the posterior mediastinum, particularly in a supine patient.

Our case highlights the challenges of managing vascular injury after attempted transradial access. A multidisciplinary approach involving cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, vascular surgery, and interventional radiology was essential in ensuring a favourable outcome. Careful management of this complication was crucial, requiring a delicate balance between continuing dual antiplatelet therapy and minimizing the risk of ongoing bleeding.

Very few case reports have described mediastinal hematoma following the transradial approach. In one report, the injury was attributed to branches of the innominate artery, with anticoagulation by heparin further aggravating the leak.3 Another case described a mediastinal hematoma secondary to right subclavian artery injury, causing compression and tamponade of the injured vessel. The patient was managed conservatively without any invasive interventions, but was administered protamine sulfate to reverse the heparin anticoagulation.4 In another instance, mediastinal hematoma with tracheal compression occurred following brachiocephalic artery perforation and pseudoaneurysm formation, which was managed with deployment of a covered stent, temporary withholding of P2Y12 inhibitors, and placement of a silicone tracheal stent.5 In a retrospective analysis, the incidence of mediastinal hematoma was higher with the transradial approach than with the transfemoral approach.1

Several measures could be taken to prevent this injury. The right subclavian artery has a greater predisposition to tortuosity than the left subclavian artery in patients with advanced age, lower body weight, previous stroke, and female sex.6 The pre-procedural evaluation should include assessment of risk factors, such as smoking, body mass index, hypertension, and peripheral vascular disease, which can contribute to subclavian tortuosity and vessel wall fragility. Left radial access (LRA) has been shown to have a higher procedural success rate than right radial access, particularly in older patients and those with underlying risk factors for subclavian tortuosity.7 However, LRA can be ergonomically uncomfortable to both the operator and the patient.

The choice of guidewire also plays an important role in ensuring safe access to the radial and subclavian vessels. Hydrophilic guidewires, although they cause less trauma, carry the risk of inadvertent side branch cannulation due to their slippery coating and their tendency to follow paths of least resistance, particularly in the setting of underlying artery tortuosity. To mitigate this risk, soft-tipped J curve, kinked, or specialized Wholey guidewires can be used during the initial right radial access. In our patient, the Wholey J-tip curve guidewire was used. However, these guidewires may be difficult to navigate through severely tortuous subclavian anatomy and may cause vessel wall injury during manipulation. Therefore, excess torque and force should be avoided during guidewire steering. Maneuvers such as interscapular lift, deep inspiration, or left head rotation could stretch the thoracic outlet and flatten the subclavian curves, but these were unsuccessful in our patient. Additionally, when the guidewire meets resistance, limited angiography should be performed to delineate vascular anatomy and guide advancement, avoiding inadvertent side branch cannulation or perforation.

In this case, limited angiography focused on the subclavian pathway revealed significant tortuosity. This tortuous anatomy alters the natural angulation of side branch ostia, redirecting the guidewire tip toward these branches rather than into the expected course of the vessel during advancement; combined with the tendency of the guidewire J-tip to straighten under tension and manipulation, this likely led to cannulation and guidewire-induced injury to the thyrocervical trunk and subsequent development of mediastinal hematoma. Advanced tracking methods, such as BAT and catheter coaxial manipulation, have been described to facilitate advancement through tortuous arterial anatomy during transradial access, but these should be avoided in severe tortuous anatomy and were not attempted in our patient. Due to the severity of subclavian tortuosity encountered, attempting the BAT was deemed technically challenging and potentially unsafe, whereas catheter coaxial manipulation carried the risk of increased wire tip load or razor effect, increasing the risk of vessel injury. A decision was made to convert to right transfemoral arterial access, through which the procedure was completed successfully. Despite this precaution, the patient subsequently developed a mediastinal hematoma, suggesting that vascular injury had already occurred during the initial guidewire attempt. This experience highlights the fact that injury to proximal subclavian branch vessels, such as the thyrocervical trunk, may occur during guidewire manipulation and may not be immediately apparent clinically.

Operators should maintain a low threshold to change to an alternative access site when guidewire manipulation becomes difficult. Persisting with a difficult approach in the setting of subclavian tortuosity can significantly increase the risk of injury to the subclavian artery or its branches.

Lastly, post-procedural vigilance should be heightened for patients with difficult radial access. The development of chest pain, dyspnea, hoarseness, or hemodynamic instability following transradial procedures should prompt an urgent evaluation for mediastinal hematoma, as early recognition and a multidisciplinary response are crucial to ensure a positive outcome.

Conclusion

In this case report, we highlight the importance of high clinical vigilance for rare but serious vascular complication of mediastinal hematoma during transradial cardiac catheterization. Prompt chest imaging is recommended in the event of post-procedural chest pain to evaluate mediastinal widening, with CT angiography performed as appropriate for further assessment. Management should be guided by hemodynamic stability, airway compromise, and ongoing bleeding. Options include conservative monitoring, endovascular intervention, and surgical evacuation.

Pre-procedural risk factor assessment for subclavian tortuosity, considering LRA in patients with risk factors, maintaining a low threshold for changing the access site in the event of guidewire resistance, and the use of kinked or J-tip hydrophilic guidewires, may minimize vessel wall injury and reduce this complication, but these measures can be ineffective in severely tortuous anatomy.
