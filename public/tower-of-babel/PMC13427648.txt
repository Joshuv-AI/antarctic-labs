Community perceptions of type 2 diabetes causes, symptoms, complications, prevention, and management among market vendors in Butembo, DR Congo: A descriptive cross-sectional study
Authors: Roland Muhindo Muyisa, Esther Nganzi Ushindi, Emile Justin Watumwa, Wivine Masika Kitasuvirwa, Eugène Mbayahi, Fiston Nzanzu Arma, Willy Amani Ngavo, Désiré Kambale Ngesera, Serge Muyisa Sahika, Jacques Katsuva Wahangire, Jean Paul Paluku Mwalitsa

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus is an important public health challenge worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings where health literacy may be limited. This study assessed perceptions regarding the causes, symptoms, complications, and prevention of diabetes among adult market vendors in Butembo, Democratic Republic of Congo.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted between March and April 2025 among adult market vendors recruited from seven major markets in Butembo, Democratic Republic of Congo. Data were collected using a pre-tested structured questionnaire administered face-to-face in French or Swahili using KoboCollect software and analyzed using descriptive statistics in IBM SPSS version 20.ResultsA total of 433 adults participated in the study. Among them, 334 (77.1%) reported having previously heard of diabetes and were included in the perception analyses. Commonly perceived signs of diabetes included weight loss (64.4%) and chronic fatigue (49.4%). High blood pressure (50.3%) and death (48.5%) were frequently perceived as complications. Healthy diet (49.1%) and regular exercise (37.4%) were commonly identified as preventive measures; however, some respondents also reported spiritual or cultural beliefs such as prayer (28.4%) and poisoning-related beliefs. Most respondents (73.7%) reported knowing someone with diabetes, while 52.1% stated that consulting a doctor would be the preferred response if diabetes was suspected.ConclusionParticipants demonstrated mixed perceptions regarding diabetes, combining biomedical explanations with cultural and spiritual beliefs. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), is one of the fastest-growing non-communicable diseases worldwide and represents a major public health challenge. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes in 2024, and this number is projected to increase to 853 million by 2050 if current trends continue [1]. More than 90% of all diabetes cases are type 2 diabetes mellitus, which is largely associated with modifiable lifestyle factors such as unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, overweight, and obesity, although genetic predisposition also contributes substantially to disease risk [1].

Sub-Saharan Africa is experiencing one of the fastest increases in the burden of type 2 diabetes. The number of adults living with diabetes in the region is expected to more than double over the coming decades owing to rapid urbanization, population growth, ageing, nutritional transition, and reduced physical activity [1]. Despite this increasing burden, diabetes remains underdiagnosed in many African countries, and a substantial proportion of affected individuals present only after developing serious complications because of delayed diagnosis, inadequate health literacy, and limited access to preventive healthcare services [2], [3], [4].

The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is also undergoing an epidemiological transition characterized by a growing prevalence of non-communicable diseases alongside persistent infectious diseases. Although national epidemiological data remain limited, recent studies have highlighted increasing numbers of patients with diabetes and significant challenges related to early diagnosis, access to care, treatment adherence, and the management of diabetes-related complications [3], [5], [6]. Improving community awareness of diabetes therefore represents an important component of national prevention and control strategies.

Community perceptions and beliefs play an important role in determining health-related behaviors, including disease prevention, healthcare-seeking practices, treatment adherence, and acceptance of medical advice. In many African settings, biomedical explanations of diabetes often coexist with traditional, cultural, or spiritual beliefs. Studies conducted in Ethiopia, Ghana, and Uganda have reported that diabetes may be attributed not only to hereditary factors, unhealthy diet, obesity, and physical inactivity but also to witchcraft, poisoning, evil spirits, or divine punishment [7], [8], [9]. Such beliefs may delay healthcare seeking, encourage reliance on traditional remedies, and contribute to poor disease outcomes if they discourage timely access to biomedical care [3].

A recent scoping review of studies from sub-Saharan Africa further demonstrated considerable variability in community knowledge and perceptions of diabetes across the region, with persistent misconceptions regarding disease causes, symptoms, prevention, and management despite increasing public awareness campaigns [2]. These findings suggest that culturally appropriate health education interventions remain necessary to improve diabetes literacy and promote evidence-based preventive behaviors.

Although several studies have explored community perceptions of diabetes in other African countries, comparable evidence from eastern Democratic Republic of Congo remains scarce. Understanding local perceptions is particularly important in post-conflict settings such as Butembo, where socioeconomic challenges, limited health resources, and strong cultural belief systems may influence public understanding of chronic diseases. Such information is essential for designing culturally appropriate health education strategies and strengthening community-based diabetes prevention programmes.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess community perceptions of type 2 diabetes mellitus, including its perceived causes, symptoms, complications, prevention, and intended management practices among adult market vendors in Butembo, Democratic Republic of Congo. By identifying both biomedical knowledge and prevailing misconceptions, this study provides locally relevant evidence that may guide culturally sensitive diabetes education and community health promotion initiatives in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.

Methods

Study design

This study was a descriptive cross-sectional survey conducted between 1 March and 30 April 2025 in Butembo, North Kivu Province, Democratic Republic of Congo.

Study population

The target population consisted of adult market vendors and traders working in selected commercial markets in Butembo.

Sampling strategy

Participants were recruited from seven major markets/shopping centres in Butembo: Sokodiza 1, Sokodiza 2, Lichomoya, Kaputu, Linzo, GTB, and Ngwanzu. According to municipal market records, Butembo had approximately 35 formal commercial markets at the time of the study. The seven study sites were selected purposively because they are among the largest and most frequented markets in the town and together serve vendors from all four municipalities of Butembo.

A convenience sampling approach was used. Investigators approached vendors present during data collection hours and invited them to participate. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who were actively working as vendors or traders and who provided verbal informed consent. Data collection was conducted on both weekdays and weekends during morning (08:00–12:00) and afternoon (13:00–17:00) periods to capture vendors working at different times of the day and reduce temporal selection bias. Individuals younger than 18 years, visitors, temporary workers, and persons unable to complete the questionnaire administration because of cognitive or communication difficulties were excluded.

A total of 433 participants were recruited. Among them, 334 participants who reported having heard of diabetes completed the perception-related sections of the questionnaire and were included in the detailed analyses.

A total of 451 individuals were approached during the survey period. Eighteen declined participation or did not complete the questionnaire. Consequently, 433 participants were enrolled. Of these, 334 participants who reported prior awareness of diabetes were included in the analyses of diabetes perceptions and attitudes.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered face-to-face by trained research assistants using KoboCollect software installed on Android tablets. The questionnaire was developed by the research team after a review of the literature on community perceptions of diabetes and existing survey instruments assessing diabetes awareness and beliefs. The instrument was designed to capture participants' perceptions of type 2 diabetes mellitus, which represents the predominant form of diabetes among adults and was the focus of this study.

The questionnaire comprised six sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics; (2) previous awareness of diabetes and sources of information; (3) perceived causes of diabetes; (4) perceived signs, symptoms, and complications; (5) perceived methods of diabetes prevention; and (6) intended actions if diabetes was suspected. Most perception-related questions were multiple-response categorical items, allowing participants to select more than one response where applicable. No composite knowledge or perception score was generated.

The original questionnaire was prepared in French and translated into Swahili, the predominant local language in Butembo. To ensure linguistic consistency, the translated version was reviewed by bilingual investigators familiar with both languages and local cultural expressions.

To establish face and content validity, the draft questionnaire was reviewed by three faculty members with expertise in public health, epidemiology, and internal medicine. Their comments were used to improve the clarity, relevance, wording, and cultural appropriateness of the questionnaire before field implementation.

A pilot test was conducted among 30 adults attending Katwa Municipality who were not included in the final study sample. The pilot assessed the clarity, comprehensibility, sequence of questions, language appropriateness, and feasibility of electronic data collection using KoboCollect. Based on participants' feedback and observations from the pilot, minor modifications were made to improve question wording and response options before commencement of the main survey.

Six data collectors received standardized training on participant recruitment, administration of the questionnaire, ethical considerations, and the use of KoboCollect to ensure consistency in data collection procedures across all study sites. Data were collected through direct interviewer-administered questionnaires to minimize missing data and to facilitate participation among respondents with limited literacy.

Data analysis

Data were exported from KoboCollect and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 20. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for approximate normality using visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots; variables with approximately normal distributions were summarized using means and standard deviations. Analyses related to diabetes perceptions were restricted to participants who reported having previously heard of diabetes (n = 334). In addition to descriptive analyses, exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to examine selected perceptions according to sex, age group, residence, and educational level using chi-square tests where appropriate. Because the study used convenience sampling, inferential findings were interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than population-representative estimates.

Nearly half of the respondents were single (46.9%). Shoe sellers (30.9%) and traders (21.2%) constituted the largest occupational groups. Intended responses if diabetes is suspected

Most respondents (73.7%) reported knowing someone living with diabetes. Consulting a doctor was the most commonly recommended action if diabetes was suspected (52.1%). Other reported responses included stopping sugar consumption (28.1%), doing nothing because diabetes was perceived as incurable (25.7%), consuming bitter foods (23.7%), and using traditional medicines (18.0%). Overall, 37.7% of respondents stated that they did not know what action should be taken if diabetes was suspected (Table 7).

Discussion

This study explored community perceptions regarding the causes, symptoms, complications, prevention, and management of type 2 diabetes among adult market vendors in Butembo, Democratic Republic of Congo.

Sources of information on diabetes

In this study, most respondents reported having previously heard of diabetes, mainly through relatives, radio or television, street awareness campaigns, and social networks. Relatives represented the most common source of information. This finding suggests that informal community networks play a major role in disseminating health information in Butembo. Similar observations have been reported in sub-Saharan Africa, where family members, peers, and community interactions frequently shape public perceptions of chronic diseases [2], [3].

Radio and television were also important information channels among participants. In low-resource settings, mass media often serve as accessible sources of health information, particularly in communities with limited access to formal health education programs [8]. However, a substantial proportion of respondents also reported receiving information from traditional healers and informal market-based discussions. While these sources may improve information circulation within communities, they may also contribute to the persistence of misconceptions and culturally rooted explanations of disease.

Hospitals and healthcare facilities were less frequently cited as sources of information. This finding may reflect limited health promotion activities within healthcare settings or barriers to healthcare access. Strengthening community-based diabetes education through healthcare workers, local media, and outreach campaigns may therefore improve public understanding of diabetes prevention and management.

Perceptions of the causes of diabetes

Participants reported a mixture of biomedical and non-biomedical perceptions regarding the causes of diabetes. Commonly cited biomedical causes included heredity, overweight or obesity, unhealthy diet, and lack of physical activity. These perceptions are generally consistent with established scientific evidence regarding risk factors for type 2 diabetes mellitus [1].

However, many respondents also attributed diabetes to poisoning, witchcraft, evil spirits, or excessive accumulation of wealth. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Ethiopia, Ghana, and Uganda, where diabetes was sometimes perceived as a consequence of supernatural forces, social imbalance, or spiritual causes [7], [8], [9]. These perceptions may reflect the influence of cultural belief systems and traditional explanatory models of illness within the local context.

Importantly, a considerable proportion of respondents stated that they did not know the causes of diabetes. This finding suggests limited community health literacy regarding non-communicable diseases. Because perceptions may influence healthcare-seeking attitudes and preventive practices, community education programs should provide clear and culturally sensitive information about diabetes risk factors and prevention strategies.

Perceived symptoms and signs of diabetes

Weight loss, chronic fatigue, slow wound healing, frequent urination, and excessive thirst were among the symptoms most commonly identified by respondents. These manifestations correspond to commonly recognized clinical symptoms of diabetes mellitus and have also been reported in other African studies assessing community perceptions of diabetes [9], [10].

Nevertheless, several participants also identified symptoms that are not specific to diabetes, including paralysis, convulsions, hearing problems, and mental disturbances. These findings suggest that some respondents may confuse diabetes with other chronic or neurological conditions. Such confusion may reflect limited health education or the coexistence of biomedical and traditional interpretations of disease within the community.

In addition, more than one-third of respondents reported that they did not know the symptoms of diabetes. Limited recognition of early symptoms may contribute to delayed healthcare consultation and late diagnosis of diabetes in community settings. Improving awareness of common warning signs through public health campaigns could therefore support earlier detection and management of the disease.

Perceptions of diabetes complications

Participants frequently perceived high blood pressure and death as complications of diabetes. Some respondents also mentioned anemia, HIV, asthma, and shortness of breath. While diabetes may coexist with other chronic conditions, these responses indicate that some participants may not clearly distinguish diabetes complications from unrelated diseases.

Previous studies conducted in African settings have similarly documented confusion between diabetes and other chronic illnesses, particularly HIV/AIDS [9], [11]. This overlap in perceptions may contribute to stigma, misunderstanding, and fear associated with chronic diseases. It may also reflect limited public understanding of diabetes-related complications such as cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, neuropathy, retinopathy, and diabetic foot disorders.

These findings highlight the importance of improving public education regarding the long-term complications of diabetes and the benefits of early diagnosis and treatment. Community health promotion initiatives should aim to provide clear explanations about the disease and its complications while considering local cultural beliefs and communication practices.

Perceptions regarding diabetes prevention

Healthy diet and regular physical activity were the most frequently identified preventive measures in this study. These findings are encouraging because they align with internationally recommended diabetes prevention strategies [1]. Similar perceptions have been reported in studies conducted in Ghana and other African countries, where respondents recognized the importance of lifestyle-related preventive measures [12].

However, some respondents also believed that prayer, avoiding food prepared by neighbors, or protection against poisoning could prevent diabetes. These findings illustrate the persistence of cultural and spiritual interpretations of disease prevention. Rather than dismissing such beliefs, health promotion programs may benefit from engaging community leaders, religious authorities, and traditional practitioners in culturally appropriate education initiatives.

Integrating culturally respected figures into health communication strategies may improve trust, acceptance, and dissemination of accurate diabetes-related information within communities.

Attitudes toward diabetes management

Most respondents indicated that they would recommend consulting a doctor if someone developed diabetes. This finding suggests that many participants recognize the importance of biomedical care. Nevertheless, several respondents also reported that they would recommend traditional remedies, bitter foods, prayer groups, or dietary restrictions not supported by medical evidence.

Comparable attitudes have been described in studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, where pluralistic health-seeking behaviors involving both biomedical and traditional approaches are common [3], [8], [9]. Such perceptions do not necessarily indicate rejection of biomedical care but may reflect coexistence between traditional and modern explanatory systems.

A substantial proportion of respondents also reported that they did not know what to do if diabetes was suspected. This finding further underscores the need for accessible community-based diabetes education programs focusing on symptom recognition, healthcare-seeking behaviors, and available treatment options.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of a cross-sectional study design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between participants' sociodemographic characteristics and their perceptions of type 2 diabetes. The findings therefore represent participants' perceptions at a single point in time.

Second, participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling approach from seven major markets in Butembo. Consequently, the study sample may not be fully representative of all adults in Butembo, particularly individuals living in rural areas or those not engaged in market activities. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be generalized to the entire population of the city or the Democratic Republic of Congo.

Third, the study relied on self-reported responses, which may have been affected by recall bias or social desirability bias despite efforts by trained data collectors to administer the questionnaire in a standardized and neutral manner.

Fourth, although the questionnaire was developed following a review of the literature, underwent expert review for face and content validity, was translated into the local language, and was pilot tested before implementation, formal psychometric validation, including quantitative measures of validity and reliability, was not performed. Future studies should further validate the instrument using established psychometric methods.

Fifth, the analyses were intentionally limited to descriptive statistics, consistent with the primary objective of characterizing community perceptions of type 2 diabetes. Consequently, potential associations between participants' characteristics (such as educational level, age, sex, or place of residence) and specific beliefs or misconceptions were not explored. Future analytical studies should investigate these relationships to identify population groups that may benefit from targeted health education interventions.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline information on community perceptions of type 2 diabetes among market vendors in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo. It identifies important misconceptions regarding the causes, complications, prevention, and management of diabetes and highlights areas that should be prioritized in culturally appropriate health education and community awareness programmes. These findings contribute to the limited evidence available from low-resource settings and may help guide future public health interventions and research in the region.

Conclusion

This study found that adult market vendors in Butembo reported diverse perceptions regarding diabetes causes, symptoms, complications, and prevention. While some participants identified biomedical explanations and preventive practices, cultural and spiritual beliefs were also commonly reported. These findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive community-based health education initiatives and further research exploring how perceptions may influence healthcare-seeking behaviors.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the Catholic University of Graben (Approval No: PFE.123/UCG; approved on 25 February 2025). The Ethics Committee specifically reviewed and approved the use of verbal informed consent for this face-to-face survey and waived the requirement for written consent because the study was considered minimal risk and no identifying information was collected. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants before questionnaire administration. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequences. Questionnaires were administered face-to-face using KoboCollect software. No audio recordings were made. To ensure confidentiality, no names, telephone numbers, or personal identifiers were collected.
