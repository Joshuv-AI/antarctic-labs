Acceptance and use of GenAI among medical and health sciences students in Saudi Arabia: an extended TAM study
Authors: Mona M Alotaibi, Hind M Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenerative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is increasingly used by medical and health profession students. Despite this trend, most existing evidence remains descriptive, with limited theory-driven work explaining how students’ evaluations translate into intention and actual use in high-stakes learning environments. This study investigated the acceptance and actual use of GenAI among medical and health profession students in Saudi Arabia using a Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). A cross-sectional survey was conducted across multiple public and private Saudi tertiary institutions, yielding 505 valid responses for descriptive analyses and 494 users for confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM). The study tested the core TAM relationships among perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, attitude toward use, behavioral intention, and actual use together with contextual factors such as social influence, trust, and perceived risk. Most core TAM relationships were supported: perceived ease of use strongly predicted perceived usefulness, perceived usefulness predicted both attitude and behavioral intention, and behavioral intention strongly predicted actual use. Perceived ease of use showed a small but significant negative association with attitude. Social influence and trust positively predicted behavioral intention, and trust also positively predicted perceived usefulness. Perceived risk was not significantly associated with behavioral intention and showed only a weak association with attitude. These findings indicate an intention-driven pattern of GenAI adoption grounded primarily in perceived educational value and reinforced by trust and social norms, while risk awareness appears to coexist with selective, self-regulated engagement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09755-x.Keywords: ChatGPT, Medical education, Health profession education, TAM, GenAI

Introduction

The rapid advancement of GenAI technologies is reshaping higher education, with particularly important implications for medical and health profession education. In recent years, GenAI systems— especially large language models (LLMs)—have received increasing attention for their ability to generate natural language responses, synthesize complex information, and support a wide range of learning tasks. Among these systems, ChatGPT has become one of the most prominent and widely adopted tools, due to its accessibility, conversational interface, and perceived versatility across academic contexts.

In medical and health profession education, students are increasingly utilizing ChatGPT to clarify complex biomedical concepts, summarize learning materials, assist with academic writing, prepare for examinations, and explore clinical scenarios [53, 55, 57]. Empirical evidence shows that this adoption is primarily student-driven and often occurs without formal curricular integration or consistent institutional guidance [26]. Simultaneously, the use of GenAI in health education introduces concerns regarding information accuracy, academic integrity, ethical use, and potential over-reliance [24]. These issues are especially significant in high-stakes professional learning environments where educational outcomes are directly connected to future clinical practice [50], [54], [32].

The growing interest in GenAI also aligns with broader efforts to enhance learning through evidence-based educational interventions in medical education. Previous research has showed that innovative and theory-informed instructional approaches can improve learners’ diagnostic reasoning, interpretation skills, and overall competency development [5], [22]. Within this context, GenAI tools such as ChatGPT may be viewed as complementary educational resources that support learning, reflection, and knowledge acquisition when integrated appropriately into medical curricula.

Despite the rapidly expanding interest in ChatGPT, much of the existing literature in medical and health profession education is largely descriptive. Previous studies have mainly reported levels of awareness, usage patterns, perceived benefits, and concerns, often relying on cross-sectional surveys or qualitative analysis of early user experiences. While these studies provide contextual insights, they offer limited explanation of how learners’ evaluations of ChatGPT influence behavioral intentions and actual usage. Therefore, there is a need for theory-driven empirical research capable of explaining the mechanisms underlying the acceptance and use of GenAI tools in medical educational practice.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [19] offers a well-established theoretical framework to address this gap. Developed to explain individuals’ acceptance and use of information systems, TAM proposes that perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) shape users’ attitudes toward technology, which subsequently influence behavioral intention (BI) and actual use (AU). TAM has demonstrated strong explanatory power across various educational technologies, including digital learning platforms and simulation tools in medical education [16, 29–31, 43], . However, the unique features of GenAI systems, such as probabilistic outputs, conversational interaction, and epistemic opacity, suggest that core TAM constructs alone may not fully capture learners’ evaluative processes in this context.

Consequently, recent research has increasingly employed extended TAM approaches that incorporate contextual variables relevant to AI-mediated learning. For ChatGPT, constructs such as social influence (SI), trust, and perceived risk are particularly significant. Social influence reflects the role of peer norms, instructor attitudes, and broader academic expectations that shape adoption decisions [52]. Trust refers to learners’ confidence in the system’s reliability, accuracy, and responsible operation, while perceived risk addresses concerns related to misinformation, academic misconduct, and reduced critical thinking [35]. Integrating these constructs within a streamlined TAM framework allows for a deeper and context sensitive examination of GenAI acceptance while maintaining theoretical coherence.

Although TAM-based studies of ChatGPT are emerging in various educational domains (e.g. [11], [29–31], [33], empirical evidence from medical and health education remains limited, especially outside Western contexts. Moreover, many existing studies focus on behavioral intention as the primary outcome, without empirically examining its relationship to actual use. Given the established distinction between intention and behavior in acceptance theory, this constitutes a significant gap in literature.

In Saudi Arabia this gap is intensified by national initiatives that prioritize digital transformation in education and healthcare [8, 34]. Medical and health profession students in Saudi Arabia are a digitally literate group operating in an environment that encourages technological innovation while simultaneously emphasizing ethical and professional accountability [6]. Examining how these students evaluate and adopt GenAI tools such as ChatGPT therefore provides insights of relevance not only locally, but also to comparable health education systems undergoing rapid digital change.

In this context, the present study examines the acceptance and actual use of ChatGPT among medical and health profession students in Saudi Arabia using an extended TAM. It tests the core TAM pathways connecting perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, attitude toward use, behavioral intention, and actual use, while integrating social influence, trust, and perceived risk as contextual factors.

Literature review

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into education has accelerated significantly in recent years, with applications including automated assessment, adaptive learning systems, and intelligent tutoring tools. In medical and health profession education, this trend has intensified following the emergence of large language models (LLMs). The first year after the public release of ChatGPT saw a rapid increase in scholarly attention to its educational applications. Recent scoping and systematic reviews indicate that, although interest in GenAI has grown rapidly, much of the early literature remains descriptive and methodologically diverse. This highlights the need for theory-driven empirical research to explain how learners evaluate and adopt these tools in practice [14], [41].

Amid the broader expansion of GenAI, ChatGPT has become one of the most prominent and widely used LLM-based tools in medical and health profession education. Empirical studies across various educational contexts report that students primarily use ChatGPT for pragmatic academic purposes, such as clarifying complex biomedical concepts, summarizing learning materials, assisting with academic writing, and supporting examination preparation [23, 44, 45]. Evidence from medical education further indicates that this adoption is largely student initiated and occurs alongside ongoing uncertainty about institutional guidance and pedagogical integration [2]. Additionally, TAM-based studies in higher education show that students’ engagement with ChatGPT is not solely motivated by novelty. Instead, it reflects a deeper evaluative process in which perceived usefulness and ease of use are balanced against concerns about accuracy, reliability, and responsible academic use [3]. These findings suggest that understanding ChatGPT adoption in medical education requires explanatory frameworks that move beyond surface level descriptions and link learners’ evaluations to intention formation and actual usage behavior.

Although research on ChatGPT in medical and health profession education is expanding rapidly, much of the existing literature has focused on descriptive patterns of use and perception [56]. Current evidence often documents patterns of use, perceived benefits, and concerns, but does not systematically model the mechanisms by which these perceptions influence learners’ intentions and translate into actual usage behavior. Survey-based studies among medical students consistently report high levels of awareness and experimentation with ChatGPT, as well as positive evaluations of its usefulness for academic support tasks [23]. However, these studies frequently do not explain why some students develop sustained intentions to use the tool while others remain cautious or disengaged.

Qualitative investigations provide important insights into learners’ initial interactions with ChatGPT, revealing themes of curiosity, efficiency, exploratory learning, and concerns regarding accuracy and ethical considerations [2]. Although these findings enhance understanding of students’ subjective experiences, they do not provide a systematic account of how such experiences are cognitively transformed into evaluative judgments, intentions, and behavior. As a result, the existing literature lacks an integrative explanatory framework that can reconcile positive perceptions of ChatGPT with ongoing concerns, especially in high-stakes medical learning environments.

Recent empirical studies employing technology acceptance frameworks have begun to address this gap; however, methodological limitations persist, with many investigations emphasizing behavioral intention rather than empirically linking intention to actual use, despite the well-established distinction between intention and behavior in acceptance theory [19, 51]. Moreover, constructs such as trust and perceived risk, which are increasingly discussed in the emerging literature but remain insufficiently integrated within coherent explanatory models [14]. Taken together, these limitations point to the need for a theoretically grounded and empirically testable model that can explain how medical and health profession students evaluate ChatGPT, form intentions to use it, and ultimately integrate it into their learning practices. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond descriptive accounts toward established acceptance frameworks capable of capturing both core evaluative beliefs and AI-specific contextual considerations.

The TAM, introduced by Davis [19], offers a concise and empirically validated framework for explaining individuals’ acceptance and use of information systems. According to TAM users’ beliefs regarding a system’s perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) influence their attitudes toward use, which subsequently affect behavioral intention and actual use. Due to its conceptual clarity and consistent empirical support, TAM has been widely applied across educational technologies, including eLearning platforms, simulation environments, and digital learning systems within medical and health profession education [28].

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that PU and PEOU represent the most stable and generalizable predictors of technology acceptance in educational settings, including medical education Ali & Warraich [7, 49]. Specifically, the PEOU to PU and PU to BI pathways have consistently been identified as central mechanisms by which learners assess and adopt new technologies, highlighting the importance of perceived educational value and usability in shaping adoption decisions [28]. These findings support the continued relevance of TAM as a foundational model for examining learners’ interactions with emerging educational technologies.

The widespread adoption of GenAI systems such as ChatGPT has revealed significant limitations in relying exclusively on core TAM constructs. Unlike traditional educational technologies, ChatGPT generates probabilistic, open-ended responses and interacts with users through conversation, introducing evaluative concerns regarding information accuracy, epistemic reliability, and ethical use. These characteristics are especially critical in medical and health profession education, where learning outcomes are closely tied to professional competence and patient safety [20]. However, the emerging literature on ChatGPT and LLMs in medical education remains methodologically diverse and is still dominated by descriptive and conceptual work, highlighting the need for theory-driven empirical models that explain how learners translate evaluative beliefs into intention and use [14].

Accordingly, recent studies have applied extended TAM frameworks that retain the model’s core causal structure while incorporating contextually relevant variables to better capture learners’ evaluations of GenAI tools [3, 28]. Among these extensions, social influence (SI) has been identified as a factor shaping learners’ behavioral intentions through perceived normative expectations in academic and professional settings [37]. Trust-related considerations have been repeatedly highlighted as central to ChatGPT acceptance in health education, reflecting learners’ confidence in output reliability, accuracy, and responsible use—particularly in high-stakes contexts where verification is essential [2], [20]. In contrast, perceived risk (PR) reflects concerns related to misinformation, academic integrity, and over-reliance on AI-generated outputs. Such concerns may temper favorable evaluations of GenAI tools.

In the context of ChatGPT, previous TAM-based studies in higher education demonstrate that learners’ acceptance is influenced not only by functional assessments of usefulness and ease of use, but also by a more complex process in which trust related beliefs and risk perceptions interact with core TAM constructs to inform intention formation [3], [23]. These findings suggest that a theoretically coherent examination of ChatGPT adoption requires integrating AI specific evaluative factors within the established TAM framework, rather than considering them as independent predictors.

Facilitating conditions are often examined in extended acceptance models, particularly within UTAUT-based frameworks, however their conceptual overlap with other acceptance constructs may introduce redundancy in certain empirical contexts. Consequently, in this study, facilitating conditions are treated as contextual consideration rather than as core structural component. This approach preserves model parsimony and discriminant validity while focusing on constructs most directly relevant to learners’ evaluative judgments, intention formation, and actual use of ChatGPT.

Beyond technology acceptance factors, students’ evaluations of educational tools are also shaped by the broader learning environment in which these tools are used. In the context of Saudi medical education, Jarrar et al. [25] argued that students’ perceptions of learning quality are influenced by curriculum structure, instructional processes, and educational outcomes, with process quality playing a significant mediating role. These findings suggest that learners’ evaluations of emerging educational technologies such as ChatGPT may not be determined solely by technological characteristics, but may also reflect their experiences within the wider educational system. Therefore, understanding GenAI adoption requires consideration of both technology-specific beliefs and the educational contexts in which learning occurs.

The educational context in which technologies are introduced may also influence students’ evaluations and adoption behaviors. Research in Saudi medical education has demonstrated that student perceptions, engagement, and academic achievement are shaped by curriculum design and educational models. For example, Mohamed et al. [36] reported significant differences in students’ learning experiences and outcomes across traditional and integrated curricula, highlighting the importance of student-centered educational approaches. These findings suggest that the acceptance and use of GenAI tools may be influenced not only by technology-related beliefs but also by broader educational environments that shape learners’ experiences, expectations, and engagement with innovative learning resources.

The limitations presented above emphasize the need for a more explicitly theory-driven and empirically testable model that can explain how medical and health profession students evaluate ChatGPT, form intentions to use it, and ultimately integrate it into their learning practices. TAM provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated foundation for examining ChatGPT acceptance in medical and health profession education. By retaining TAM’s core pathways and selectively incorporating social influence, trust, and perceived risk as contextual extensions, this study adopts a balanced and theory-driven approach to explaining how learners evaluate ChatGPT, develop intentions to use it, and translate those intentions into actual usage behaviors.

Contextual extensions of the TAM

In medical and health-profession education, learners’ technology-related intentions are shaped not only by individual evaluations of usefulness and ease of use, but also by contextual and psychosocial factors that influence acceptance decisions. In the present study, these contextual factors are modeled as antecedents of intention and use among students with prior experience using ChatGPT.

Social influence (SI) reflects the extent to which learners perceive that important others—such as peers, instructors, or the broader academic community—expect or endorse the use of a particular technology. In academic environments where the appropriate role of GenAI tools is still evolving, perceived normative support may strengthen learners’ willingness to adopt such tools for educational purposes [23, 37].

Methodology

After receiving approval from the IRB at King Saud University (No: KSU-HE-25–1206) and in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, the study was conducted across several public and private tertiary educational institutions in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, providing a broad representation of students’ perspectives on the use of ChatGPT in medical and health profession education. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study. Participation was limited to students enrolled in health-related programs, including medicine, dentistry, pharmacy, nursing, public health, health informatics, emergency medical services, and applied medical sciences. Students from non-health disciplines were not eligible to participate.

Participants represented a range of academic levels, including diploma, undergraduate, internship, master’s, residency/Saudi board, doctoral, and subspecialty fellowship programs. Eligibility for participation required prior awareness of ChatGPT; participants reporting no prior use of the tool were retained for descriptive statistics and internal consistency reliability analyses but were excluded from confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM). This approach aligns with established acceptance research practices, whereby reliability assessment focuses on item coherence, whereas structural modeling of intention and actual use presupposes experiential interaction with the technology.

A convenience sampling approach was employed, with data collected via an online, self-administered questionnaire distributed through institutional mailing lists, student social media groups, and direct faculty invitations. This approach was appropriate given the exploratory and cross-sectional nature of the study, as well as the absence of a centralized sampling frame for students’ informal use of emerging GenAI tools.

Data collection took place between November and December 2025. A total of 518 responses were initially received. Responses were excluded if the participant did not provide informed consent (n = 12) or was enrolled in a non-health-related academic program (n = 1). Following these exclusions, 505 valid responses remained and were included in the final dataset. Detailed demographic characteristics of the sample are presented in the Results section. All participants provided electronic informed consent prior to survey completion. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were informed that their responses would remain confidential and anonymous. These procedural measures were intended to encourage honest responses and reduce response bias. The study protocol received ethical approval from the relevant institutional review board.

Study instrument

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was developed based on the TAM and adapted from previously validated measurement scales widely used in educational technology research. The instrument was designed to assess students’ acceptance and use of ChatGPT within medical and health profession education, with item wording contextualized to reflect actual academic use rather than general attitudes toward artificial intelligence. The questionnaire included items measuring the core TAM constructs: Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU), Perceived Usefulness (PU), Attitude toward Use (ATT), Behavioral Intention (BI), and Actual Use (AU). In addition, three contextual variables frequently examined in extended TAM research—Social Influence (SI), Trust (TR), and Perceived Risk (PR)—were included to capture evaluative considerations that are particularly salient in the context of GenAI tools. Each construct was operationalized using four to five items adapted from prior TAM-based studies, with wording adjusted where necessary to ensure relevance to ChatGPT while preserving the original theoretical meaning of the constructs.

Items related to Facilitating Conditions (FC) were included in the questionnaire for contextual and exploratory purposes only. However, FC was not incorporated into the final confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) or structural equation model (SEM) due to substantial measurement overlap with core TAM constructs observed during preliminary analyses. This decision was taken to preserve discriminant validity and model parsimony and does not affect the interpretation of the core TAM constructs or the contextual variables retained in the final model. In other words, FC items were used to describe the broader implementation context but were not retained as independent latent constructs in the final model.

All items were measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The questionnaire was administered in both English and Arabic to accommodate participants’ language preferences. The original English items were translated into Arabic by members of the research team who are fluent in both languages. The translation was revised by an expert Arabic-English translator with a PhD in Translation and more than 15 years of experience to ensure semantic equivalence and contextual appropriateness. Minor wording refinements were made where needed to improve clarity without altering the theoretical intent of the items. A copy of the questionnaire is provided in Appendix A.

The questionnaire also included one optional open-ended item inviting participants to describe difficulties, challenges, or negative experiences with ChatGPT. Responses to this item were reviewed only as contextual observations and were not subjected to formal qualitative analysis.

Data analysis

Data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0) and R (version 4.5.2). Analyses proceeded in four main stages: descriptive statistics, internal consistency reliability assessment, measurement model evaluation, and structural equation modeling. Descriptive statistics were calculated in SPSS to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and overall response patterns. Internal consistency reliability for each construct was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Participants who reported no prior use of ChatGPT were retained for descriptive statistics and reliability assessment. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modeling (SEM) were performed in R using the lavaan package. Given the ordinal nature of the Likert-scale items, the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator was employed. CFA and SEM were estimated using a users-only subsample (N = 494), as usage-related constructs such as behavioral intention and actual use presuppose experiential interaction with ChatGPT. Model evaluation was based on established fit indices, and hypothesis testing was conducted within the SEM framework.

Consistent with the TAM, perceived ease of use had a strong positive effect on perceived usefulness, supporting H1. Perceived usefulness exerted a significant positive influence on both attitude toward use and behavioral intention, providing support for H2 and H3, respectively. Therefore, H4 was not supported in its hypothesized positive direction; instead, a significant effect in the opposite direction was observed. Discussion

This study aimed to explain the acceptance and actual use of GenAI, specifically ChatGPT, among medical and health profession students in Saudi Arabia using a theory-driven extended TAM. The findings provide strong support for the core TAM pathways, and demonstrate that contextual, AI-specific beliefs meaningfully shape intention formation in this high-stakes educational context. In practical terms, students who found ChatGPT easier to use were more likely to perceive it as useful; those who perceived it as useful reported more favorable attitudes and stronger intentions; and stronger intentions translated into greater actual use. Perceived usefulness was identified as the most influential determinant across the model, exerting substantial effects on both attitude toward use and behavioral intention. Behavioral intention, in turn, strongly predicted actual use. These results confirm that ChatGPT adoption in medical and health education is not merely exploratory or novelty-driven but instead reflects a structured evaluative process in which students weigh educational value, intentional commitment, and subsequent integration into routine learning practices [44].

In addition to the core TAM structure, the inclusion of social influence and trust significantly enhanced the model’s explanatory power. Social influence had a direct positive effect on behavioral intention, indicating that peer norms and perceived academic endorsement remain influential in predominantly self-directed uses of generative AI which aligns with [15, 29–31] and Sallam [46]. Trust also played a dual role, positively influencing both perceived usefulness and behavioral intention, highlighting its importance in shaping learners’ confidence in AI-mediated educational support. These findings differ from several recent studies that reported high use alongside lower trust among students (see [1, 13, 42]. Unlike Al Murshidi et al., [4] and Murthiningtyas et al., [38], perceived risk did not undermine intention to use ChatGPT and showed only a small positive association with attitude, contrary to the hypothesized negative direction.

The findings discussed above suggest that ChatGPT acceptance in medical and health profession education reflects an intention-driven pattern grounded in perceived educational value and reinforced by social and trust-based considerations.

By empirically linking intention to actual use and incorporating AI-specific evaluative beliefs within a streamlined TAM framework, this study advances prior descriptive research and offers a more explanatory perspective on the integration of GenAI tools into contemporary health-professional learning environments.

The core relationships outlined by the TAM were largely supported in this study, confirming TAM’s relevance for examining the adoption of GenAI tools in medical and health profession education. In line with extensive prior research, perceived ease of use shows a strong positive effect on perceived usefulness, indicating that students who experienced ChatGPT as intuitive and effortless were more likely to recognize its value for learning and clinical preparation. This finding aligns with the foundational logic of TAM, whereby usability serves as an enabling condition that enhances users’ perceptions of functional and educational benefit [58], [10].

Perceived usefulness emerged as a central driver of both attitude toward use and behavioral intention which aligns with Paudel & Acharya [40] and Almogren & Al-Rahmi [9]. Students who perceived ChatGPT as beneficial for understanding complex concepts, improving efficiency, and supporting academic tasks were more likely to develop favorable attitudes and stronger intentions to continue using the tool. This pattern highlights the primacy of perceived educational value in shaping acceptance decisions within medical and health profession education, where learners are highly outcome-oriented and sensitive to tools that demonstrably support performance. The strong influence of perceived usefulness observed in the present study may also be interpreted within the broader literature on evidence-based educational interventions. Previous studies in medical education have demonstrated that innovative and theory-based instructional strategies can significantly enhance students’ learning outcomes and competency development [5], [22]. Similar to these educational interventions, students may perceive ChatGPT as useful when it supports understanding of complex concepts, promotes self-directed learning, and facilitates access to educational resources. Thus, the educational value of GenAI may depend not only on the technology itself but also on how it is pedagogically integrated into learning activities.

It should be noted that the standardized path coefficient from perceived usefulness to attitude toward use slightly exceeded unity (β = 1.086). In SEM estimated using WLSMV with ordinal indicators, standardized coefficients may occasionally exceed 1.0 when constructs are highly correlated and scaling properties influence standardized estimates. In the present model, no Heywood cases or improper solutions were observed, and all variance estimates remained within acceptable bounds. The HTMT assessment further indicated a borderline value for PU–ATT, suggesting close empirical proximity between these theoretically adjacent TAM constructs. Accordingly, this coefficient should be interpreted as indicating a very strong association rather than perfect prediction or model invalidity.

Attitude toward use played a key mediating role between evaluative beliefs and behavioral intention which was also reported in Paudel & Acharya [40]. The strong positive association between attitude and intention suggests that affective evaluations of ChatGPT—such as enjoyment, perceived appropriateness, and overall positivity—remain important even in professionally oriented learning environments. Furthermore, behavioral intention translated into actual use with a very strong effect, providing empirical evidence that intention formation represents a meaningful precursor to sustained engagement with ChatGPT in real learning practices. These findings seem consistent with [12] and [18] indicating that the adoption of ChatGPT among medical and health profession students follows a coherent evaluative–intentional–behavioral sequence, consistent with TAM’s theoretical assumptions.

A notable unexpected finding was the significant negative association between perceived ease of use and attitude toward using ChatGPT. Rather than indicating a methodological anomaly, this result can be understood as reflecting the evaluative dynamics of digitally mature, high-stakes learning environments. Among medical and health profession students who are already accustomed to frequent interaction with advanced digital tools, ease of use may be taken for granted and therefore contribute little to positive effect. In the context of GenAI, excessive effortlessness may even prompt cautious or skeptical attitudes related to over-reliance or superficial engagement. This finding should therefore be interpreted as a small, context-specific deviation from the hypothesized TAM pathway and does not alter the central positive role of perceived usefulness in shaping attitudes and intentions.

Importantly, perceived ease of use remained a strong positive predictor of perceived usefulness, suggesting that usability continues to support functional value while exerting a limited—or contextually reversed—direct influence on affective evaluation confirming the finding from [27] and [47].

The findings of the present study highlight the continued relevance of social and trust-related factors in shaping medical and health profession students’ intentions to use ChatGPT. These results confirm Siddiqui et al., [48], Paudel & Acharya [40] and Sallam et al., [47]. who found that social influence exerted a significant positive effect on behavioral intention, suggesting that learners’ adoption decisions are partly embedded within their academic and professional environments. Even though ChatGPT is often used individually and informally, students appear attentive to peer practices, instructors’ attitudes, and broader institutional norms when deciding whether to integrate the tool into their learning routines. This result highlights that acceptance of GenAI in medical education is not purely an individual cognitive process, but also a socially situated one [47].

Similar to the findings reported in Guo et al., [21] and Chen et al., [17], trust emerged as a particularly salient contextual factor, influencing both perceived usefulness and behavioral intention. Students who expressed higher confidence in ChatGPT’s reliability, accuracy, and responsible operation were more likely to view the tool as educationally beneficial and to intend continued use. In high-stakes learning contexts such as medical and health profession education, trust may function as a prerequisite for meaningful engagement with AI-generated content. Rather than acting as a substitute for critical judgment, trust appears to enable students to incorporate ChatGPT selectively and purposefully into their learning activities.

The effects of social influence and trust suggest that the acceptance of ChatGPT extends beyond technical characteristics to encompass broader evaluative judgments about legitimacy, appropriateness, and professional acceptability. These findings support the inclusion of AI-specific contextual variables within extended TAM frameworks and reinforce the view that successful integration of GenAI tools in medical education depends not only on usability and usefulness, but also on learners’ confidence in the tool and the normative climate surrounding its use [21].

Contrary to the hypothesized negative effects, perceived risk did not significantly reduce students’ behavioral intention to use ChatGPT and exhibited only a small positive association with attitude toward use. These findings differ from those of Ortiz-Bonnin and Blahopoulou [39] and Sallam et al. [47], who reported stronger risk-related concerns in relation to ChatGPT use. Although this direction differs from the hypothesized risk-avoidance pathway, it is consistent with an interpretation of perceived risk as risk awareness and critical appraisal rather than deterrence in digitally literate, high-stakes learning contexts [2], [20], where learners continue to engage with GenAI tools while simultaneously expressing concerns about accuracy, ethics, and over-reliance. This finding suggests that concerns related to accuracy, academic integrity, and over-reliance do not necessarily translate into avoidance behavior among medical and health profession students. Instead, perceived risk appears to coexist with continued engagement, reflecting a more nuanced pattern of adoption in which awareness of potential drawbacks does not preclude intentional use. The questionnaire included an optional open-ended item on difficulties or negative experiences with ChatGPT. These responses were reviewed only to provide contextual background for interpreting the perceived risk findings and were not analyzed using a formal qualitative methodology. Therefore, they are not reported as qualitative results and are not used to make claims about the prevalence or relative importance of specific concerns. They should be interpreted only as supplementary contextual observations alongside the quantitative finding that perceived risk did not significantly reduce behavioral intention.

It is crucial to discuss the several theoretical contributions to the literature on TAM and GenAI especially in medical and health profession education. First, the findings seem to confirm the continued relevance of the TAM as a robust explanatory framework for understanding students’ adoption of emerging AI tools. The strong support observed for the core TAM pathways—particularly the relationships among perceived usefulness, attitude, behavioral intention, and actual use—demonstrates that acceptance of GenAI tools follows a coherent evaluative–intentional–behavioral sequence even in the context of probabilistic, conversational AI systems.

The findings highlight the role of perceived ease of use within contemporary, digitally mature learning environments. While perceived ease of use remained a strong antecedent of perceived usefulness, its direct negative association with attitude suggests that usability alone may no longer function as a primary driver of positive affect among advanced learners. This finding extends TAM by indicating that, in high-stakes professional education contexts such as medical education, ease of use may be taken for granted and may even prompt critical distancing when tools appear overly effortless. Accordingly, the affective dimension of acceptance appears increasingly anchored in perceived educational value rather than in usability per se.

Moreover, integrating trust and social influence as contextual extensions of TAM contributes to a deeper understanding of GenAI acceptance. Trust emerged as a dual antecedent of both perceived usefulness and behavioral intention, highlighting its essential role in AI-mediated learning environments where epistemic reliability and ethical considerations are salient. Social influence also exerted a significant effect on intention, highlighting that adoption decisions are socially situated and shaped by perceived norms and professional expectations. Such findings support extended TAM approaches that incorporate AI-specific evaluative beliefs while preserving model parsimony and theoretical coherence.

On the other hand, the study findings provide several practical and pedagogical implications for educators, institutions, and policymakers involved in medical and health profession education. Given the dominant role of perceived usefulness in shaping attitudes and intentions, educational initiatives should emphasize how GenAI tools can support learning outcomes, such as conceptual understanding, efficiency in academic tasks, and reflective engagement with complex material. Positioning GenAI tools as a supplementary learning aid rather than a replacement for clinical reasoning may help align student expectations with responsible use.

The strong translation of behavioral intention into actual use suggests that students who develop positive, informed intentions are likely to integrate GenAI tools into their routine learning practices. Institutions may therefore benefit from guiding intention formation through clear instructional messaging, faculty engagement, and transparent policies. In the Saudi context, these institutional recommendations align with Vision 2030–linked national digital transformation priorities, including the responsible integration of digital health and AI-enabled technologies into healthcare education and practice [8, 34].

Rather than prohibitive approaches, structured guidance on appropriate and ethical use may foster selective and self-regulated engagement with GenAI tools.

The reported influence of social norms and trust further highlights the importance of the institutional climate surrounding the use of GenAI tools. Faculty attitudes, peer practices, and institutional guidance can shape students’ willingness to adopt ChatGPT. Educators and administrators can play a key role by modeling responsible use, discussing limitations openly, and reinforcing norms that encourage critical evaluation rather than uncritical reliance. In this way, trust and social influence can be leveraged to support thoughtful integration of GenAI into medical and health profession education.

It is important to consider the findings within the broader context of educational quality and curriculum design. Previous research in Saudi medical education has shown that students’ perceptions of learning experiences are shaped by curriculum structure and instructional process quality [25]. Consequently, the successful integration of GenAI tools may depend not only on the characteristics of the technology itself but also on the quality of the surrounding learning environment, including instructional design, faculty support, and curriculum implementation. This suggests that institutions seeking to integrate GenAI should consider pedagogical and curricular factors alongside technological adoption strategies.

The findings should also be interpreted within the ongoing transformation of medical education toward more integrated and student-centered learning models. Previous research has shown that curriculum design significantly influences students’ perceptions, engagement, and academic outcomes [36]. From this perspective, GenAI tools such as ChatGPT may represent one component of a broader shift toward learner-centered educational environments that promote self-directed learning, information access, and personalized academic support. Therefore, successful integration of GenAI in medical education may require alignment with curriculum objectives, instructional strategies, and broader educational reform initiatives rather than focusing solely on technology adoption.

Conclusion

This study explored the acceptance and actual use of GenAI, specifically ChatGPT, among medical and health profession students in several Saudi universities. Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) was adopted for this study which, according to the knowledge of the researcher, is among few theory-driven investigations to examine both behavioral intention and actual use of ChatGPT within medical and health professions education. A cross-sectional survey was conducted across multiple public and private Saudi universities, with 505 valid responses for descriptive analyses and 494 users for confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM). The study tested the core TAM relationships among perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, attitude toward use, behavioral intention, and actual use together with contextual factors such as social influence, trust, and perceived risk. The analysis revealed that perceived ease of use strongly predicted perceived usefulness of GenAI, its perceived usefulness predicted both attitude and behavioral intention to use it, and behavioral intention strongly predicted its actual use. Perceived ease of use showed a small yet significant negative association with attitude. Furthermore, the findings indicated that social influence and trust positively predicted behavioral intention to use GenAI tools, and trust also positively predicted their perceived usefulness. On the other hand, results indicated that perceived risk was not significantly associated with behavioral intention and showed only a weak association with attitude. In general, ChatGPT adoption among medical and health-professions students appeared to be shaped mainly by perceived academic value, supported by trust and social influence, while concerns about potential risks did not necessarily prevent continued use.

These findings are hoped to encourage informed integration of GenAI tools in medical and health profession education. Medical and health profession institutions and educators should highlight GenAI's role in promoting conceptual understanding, task efficiency, and reflective learning while framing it as a supplement to clinical reasoning. Sustained integration of GenAI tools in medical and health profession education environments can be achieved through faculty modeling, transparent policies, and ethical guidelines that leverage social norms and trust to support selective, self-regulated engagement rather than prohibition or uncritical reliance.

It is crucial to interpret the findings within the broader educational context. While behavioral intention was modeled through TAM constructs, students’ intentions may additionally reflect the influence of educational experiences, institutional expectations, and professional norms that shape learning behaviors in medical and health professions education. Consequently, the acceptance of GenAI tools should be viewed not only as a technology adoption process but also as part of a broader educational ecosystem.

Despite these interesting findings, the present study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged and addressed in future research. The cross-sectional design captures data at a specific timeframe, thus, it cannot show how students' ChatGPT acceptance might change over time. A longitudinal study to follow the same medical students for 6–12 months is encouraged to explore how their ChatGPT views evolve over time.

Another limitation involves the use of convenience sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although the inclusion of participants from multiple institutions and disciplines partially mitigates this concern, the sample was not selected using probability-based methods. The sample also included a higher proportion of female participants (65.7%), which may partly reflect the inclusion of a women-only institution among the participating universities, while the remaining institutions included both male and female students. Gender was not examined as a potential moderator of the structural relationships. Future research could employ stratified sampling and examine whether ChatGPT acceptance pathways differ across gender, academic level, discipline, or institutional type.

The study relied on self-reported measures collected through a single survey administration. Although anonymity and voluntary participation were used as procedural measures to reduce response bias, the use of a single-source, self-report design may introduce common method variance. No formal statistical test for common method variance, such as Harman’s single-factor test or a common latent factor approach, was conducted. Accordingly, the observed path coefficients and high explained variance values should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies could combine self-reported acceptance measures with objective indicators of actual use, learning performance, or clinical reasoning to provide a more comprehensive assessment of GenAI integration in medical and health profession education.

Finally, although the questionnaire included an optional open-ended item, these responses were not analyzed using a formal qualitative methodology. They were therefore used only as contextual observations, and future mixed-methods studies should apply systematic qualitative coding to better understand students’ concerns and experiences.
