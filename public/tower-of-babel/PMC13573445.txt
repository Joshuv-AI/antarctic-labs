Integrated physiological, hormonal, and transcriptomic analyses reveal pistil responses to pollen sources of varying compatibility in ‘Fengtang’ plum (Prunus salicina)
Authors: Lijun Deng, Binshu Yuan, Xuan Zhang, Yuhuan Feng, Xiaojun Jin, Hui Zhang, Bo Xiong, Guochao Sun, Siya He, Ling Liao, Zhihui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCross-pollination is crucial for commercial plum production, as pollen sources influence pollen tube growth and fruit set. However, the precise mechanisms underlying pollen-pistil interactions remain poorly understood. This study aimed to systematically investigate the dynamic physiological, hormonal, and transcriptomic responses of ‘Fengtang’ plum pistils following pollination with five distinct pollen sources.ResultsFive pollination combinations, including high-compatibility pollens (‘Fenghuang’, ‘Siyue’, and mixed pollen) and low-compatibility pollens (‘Yinhong’ and self-pollen), were established. ‘Fengtang’ pistils exhibited distinct temporal and multi-level responses dependent on pollen source. Physiological analyses revealed that highly compatible pollen induced rapid metabolic mobilization and a robust activation of antioxidant enzymes during the early stages of pollen tube elongation (24–48 h after pollination). Conversely, low-compatibility and self-pollinated pistils exhibited prolonged oxidative stress, characterized by delayed antioxidant responses and higher malondialdehyde accumulation. Hormonal profiling demonstrated the involvement of auxins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, ethylene, salicylic acid, and jasmonic acid in modulating pistil responses. Higher compatibility correlated with improved hormonal balance. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed significant transcriptional reprogramming orchestrating these divergent responses. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling, starch and sucrose metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. Furthermore, weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified hub transcription factors (e.g., ORR21, C3H27, ERF34) as potential regulators mediating these post-pollination pistil responses.ConclusionsOur integrated analysis indicates that pistils can differentially respond to pollen sources through coordinated changes in ROS homeostasis, metabolic status, and hormonal signaling, and provides a candidate framework for understanding compatibility-related responses in fruit tree reproduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09347-3.Keywords: Plum, Pollen source, Pollen-pistil interaction, Antioxidant enzyme, Hormones, Transcriptomic analysis

Introduction

Plum, a member of the Rosaceae family, is an economically significant stone fruit tree. It is highly favored for its nutritional value and richness in dietary fiber and bioactive compounds [1]. As the world’s largest plum producer, China accounted for approximately 2 million hectares of cultivation area and 6.9 million tons of production in 2023 [2]. Most commercially grown plums in China belong to Prunus salicina, commonly known as Chinese or Japanese plum. Similar to many other Rosaceae fruit species, many Chinese plum cultivars exhibit self-incompatibility, which in Prunus is typically controlled by a single multi-allelic S-locus [3]. This gametophytic self-incompatibility (GSI) system operates through S-RNase-mediated degradation of pollen tube RNA in styles, leading to specific arrest of self-pollen tube growth [4]. However, compatibility levels can vary substantially even among different cross-pollen sources, manifesting as differences in pollen tube growth dynamics and fruit set [5]. Consequently, cross-pollination with compatible pollinizer cultivars is essential to ensure adequate fruit set and commercial yield [6].

Successful fertilization fundamentally depends on compatible pollen-pistil interactions [7]. In plum, this process unfolds through three distinct temporal phases. First, the germination phase (0–24 h after pollination, HAP): pollen grains undergo adhesion, hydration, and germination on the stigma, reactivating pollen metabolism and triggering early signaling cascades [8]. Second, the elongation phase (24–72 HAP): The emerging pollen tube then elongates through the style. In incompatible combinations of Prunus, however, pollen tube growth may arrest during this period [9]. Third, the fertilization phase: pollen tubes reach the ovary and accomplish double fertilization, typically within 72–96 HAP in Chinese plum [8]. The pistil provides essential nutritional support, guidance cues, and a permissive physiological environment for pollen tube growth across these distinct phases [10]. Extensive physiological studies have revealed that effective pollen-pistil interactions rely on a unified regulatory network encompassing reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, metabolic support, and hormonal signaling [11]. ROS, derived from both pollen and pistil tissues, function as crucial signaling molecules mediating pollen-pistil communication [12]. In compatible interactions, moderate ROS levels facilitate pollen tube guidance and growth, while excessive ROS accumulation in incompatible interactions triggers programmed cell death (PCD) and pollen tube arrest [13]. The plant antioxidant system, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), maintains ROS homeostasis to support successful fertilization [14]. Concurrently, the pistil must mobilize substantial metabolic resources to support the energetically demanding process of pollen tube growth. Sucrose provides energy and nutrients for pollen tube growth in Arabidopsis thaliana [15]. Metabolomic analyses in Pyrus communis and Paeonia ostii further indicate that differential accumulation of soluble sugars, amino acids, and tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolites distinguishes compatible from incompatible interactions [16, 17]. Cell wall invertase (CWIN) and sugar transporters (SlSUT1/2, SlHT1/2, SlSWEET5b) genes are differentially activated in tomato styles and ovaries during pollination and fertilization to fuel pollen tube elongation [18].

Phytohormones act as core signaling molecules that coordinate post-pollination events [19]. Plant hormone signal transduction pathway is significantly enriched during pollination and fertilization, as reported in studies on oil palm, Rhododendron, and Paeonia ostii [16, 20, 21]. Auxin (IAA), cytokinins, and abscisic acid (ABA) are pivotal regulators of pollen-pistil interactions [22]. IAA stimulates pollen tube growth in Torenia fournieri in vitro, whereas ABA exhibits inhibitory effects [23]. In Petunia hybrida, IAA content increased 1.5-fold within 0–4 h after pollination, facilitating pollen germination [24], while ABA content increases threefold in self-incompatible versus compatible pollinations [25]. Other hormones, including salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA), also show differential accumulation patterns correlated with compatibility status [26].

At the molecular level, recent advances have identified several key genes and transcription factors governing these processes. In compatible pollination of Solanum chacoense, ROS-related genes and AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 5 (ARF5) are specifically upregulated in the early stage of pollination, whereas ETHYLENE-INSENSITIVE 3 (EIN3) and EIN3-like genes in the ethylene signaling pathway are downregulated [27]. Moreover, successful fertilization requires flavonols, as pollen deficient in flavonols fails to produce functional pollen tubes [28]. Arabidopsis The LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES DOMAIN 10 (LBD10) regulates ROS homeostasis by modulating flavonol biosynthesis, thereby maintaining pollen tube growth and integrity [29]. Receptor-like kinases (RLKs) of the CrRLK1L family and their peptide ligands (RALFs) have also been identified as crucial regulators of pollen tube integrity and fertilization [30]. In addition, R2R3-MYB transcription factors, including MYB97, MYB101, and MYB120, have been shown to participate in the regulation of post-pollination pollen tube growth and fertilization [31]. Ethylene-responsive factors (ERFs) mediate the specific recognition of heterologous pollen and the corresponding defense responses in the ovary [27].

Despite these advances, critical knowledge gaps remain that limit our understanding of pollen source-dependent pistil responses in plum and other stone fruit trees. While individual components (physiological changes, hormone profiling, or transcriptomic analysis) have been studied in isolation, the integrated mechanisms by which pistils orchestrate multi-level responses to discriminate among pollen sources of varying compatibility are poorly understood. Previous studies have documented differential pollen tube growth and fruit set in various fruit trees pollinated with different pollen sources [32–34]. However, the corresponding physiological and molecular responses in the pistil remain largely uncharacterized. For ‘Fengtang’ plum (P. salicina), a leading commercial cultivar in southwest China, our previous study observed clear compatibility differences among diverse pollen sources, with ‘Fenghuang’ pollen showing the highest compatibility, followed by ‘Siyue’ and mixed pollen, while ‘Yinhong’ and self-pollen exhibited low compatibility [35]. Nevertheless, how ‘Fengtang’ pistils perceive and respond to these distinct pollen sources, and the underlying physiological and molecular mechanisms driving these differential responses, have not been elucidated.

Building upon these observations, we hypothesize that pollen of higher compatibility triggers a coordinated transcriptional reprogramming in the pistil, leading to enhanced antioxidant capacity and an optimal hormonal balance that supports pollen tube growth, whereas low-compatibility pollen disrupts this homeostasis. To test this hypothesis, this study aimed to comprehensively investigate the dynamic responses of ‘Fengtang’ pistils following pollination with five different pollen sources of varying compatibility. We then performed integrated physiological analysis, endogenous hormone profiling, and comparative transcriptomic analysis to systematically characterize the multi-level responses of pistils to diverse pollen sources, and to identify candidate regulatory factors via weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). These findings will provide novel insights into the regulatory mechanisms of plant fertilization and offer theoretical foundations for optimizing cross-pollination strategies in fruit tree cultivation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

All plant materials used in this study, including the four plum cultivars (‘Fengtang’, ‘Siyue’, ‘Fenghuang’, and ‘Yinhong’), were obtained from the same standardized commercial plum orchard located in Suining City, Sichuan Province, China (latitude 31°30’11’’ N; longitude 105°43’3’’ E; altitude 313 m). The orchard is managed under uniform horticultural practices, and all cultivars were grown under the same environmental conditions. Pollen was collected from each cultivar at the large bud stage within this orchard. Using ‘Fengtang’ as the female parent, five pollination combinations were established: ‘Fengtang’ × ‘Siyue’ (FS), ‘Fengtang’ × ‘Fenghuang’ (FF), ‘Fengtang’ × ‘Yinhong’ (FY), self-pollination (SP), and mixed pollen pollination (MP). The mixed pollen was prepared by weighing dried pollen from each of the four cultivars using an analytical balance and mixing them in equal proportions by weight (1:1:1:1, w/w) to ensure precise compositional uniformity.

Approximately 150 flower branches of ‘Fengtang’ plum (50–60 cm in length) with well-developed flowers were collected at the balloon stage. The branches were maintained in hydroponic culture at 25℃ under laboratory conditions. About 30 branches (1000–1500 flowers total) were assigned to each pollination combination. Flowers were emasculated at the large bud stage. Unpollinated pistils were collected immediately after emasculation, which served as the baseline control (designated as P0 or 0 HAP). Following pollination with the respective pollen sources, the pistils were collected at 24, 48, 72, and 96 HAP. The sampling time points correspond to critical stages of pollen-pistil interaction: 24 HAP (early pollen germination and tube elongation), 48 HAP (penetrating upper style), 72 HAP (reaching lower style), and 96 HAP (approaching fertilization) [33]. Then, the pistil samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃ for subsequent analysis. The entire experiment was conducted with three independent biological replicates. To account for biological variance, each independent biological replicate consisted of a pooled sample of approximately 120 pistils randomly collected from different branches within the same treatment.

Determination of soluble sugar, protein, proline, and malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The soluble sugar content was determined using the anthrone-sulfuric acid method as previously described [36]. Soluble protein content was measured using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue dye-binding assay. Briefly, 0.5 g of sample powder was homogenized in 5 mL of distilled water and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Then, 250 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 2.5 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 solution. After incubation for 2 min, absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Free proline content was assessed using the acid ninhydrin method [37]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid method [38]. All measurements were performed using three independent biological replicates. Each biological replicate was measured with three technical replicates.

Determination of antioxidant enzyme activities

The activities of SOD, POD, CAT, and APX were determined as previously described with minor modifications [39]. For enzyme extraction, 0.5 g of sample powder was homogenized in 5 mL of 0.05 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.8) and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected for enzyme assays. The SOD reaction solution was prepared by mixing 0.05 M PBS (pH 7.8), 130 mM methionine, 750 µM nitro-blue-tetrazolium, 100 µM EDTA-Na2, 20 µM riboflavin, and distilled water (15:3:3:3:3:2.5, v/v). The POD reaction solution consisted of 28 µL of guaiacol, 19 µL of 30% H2O2, and 50 mL of 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.0). The CAT reaction mixture consisted of 0.1 M H2O2 and PBS (pH 7.0) (1:4, v/v). The APX reaction solution contained 0.04718 g of ascorbic acid, and 11.2 µL of 30% H2O2, and 500 mL 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.0). Enzyme activity assays were conducted using three independent biological replicates. Each biological replicate was assayed with three technical replicates.

UPLC-MS/MS analysis of phytohormones

Phytohormone quantification was performed using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS), utilizing three independent biological replicates per treatment point. Approximately 0.1 g of each sample was homogenized in 1 mL of ice-cold 50% aqueous acetonitrile (ACN), sonicated for 3 min at 4 ℃, and extracted on a benchtop rotator for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was purified using C18 reversed-phase solid-phase extraction (RP-SPE) cartridges. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% acetic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% acetic acid (solvent B), delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL·min− 1. The gradient program was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 15% B; 0.5–1.5 min, 15% to 90% B; 1.5–3.0 min, 90% B; 3.0–3.1 min, 90% to 15% B; 3.1–5.0 min, 15% B. The injection volume was 2 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was performed on a Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a heated electrospray ionization source operating in selected ion monitoring mode. Data were acquired using Xcalibur 4.1 software (Thermo Scientific) and processed with TraceFinder™ 4.1 Clinical software (Thermo Scientific).

Transcriptomic analysis

To comprehensively investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying compatibility variations, unpollinated pistils (P0) and pistil samples collected at 24, 48, and 96 HAP from FF, FY, and SP pollination combinations were selected for transcriptomic analysis. The rationale for selecting these three combinations was based on our preliminary compatibility evaluation [35]: FF represents high-compatibility cross-pollination (the highest pollen tube numbers), FY represents low-compatibility cross-pollination (intermediate pollen tube numbers), and SP represents self-incompatibility (the lowest pollen tube numbers). These three combinations thus capture the full spectrum of compatibility responses. The FS and MP treatments, which showed compatibility levels similar to FF, were excluded to focus sequencing resources on maximally contrasting phenotypes while maintaining adequate statistical power.

Total RNA was extracted using the Polysaccharide Polyphenol Plant Total RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (BASEGEN Biotech, Beijing). RNA integrity was verified with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. A total of 30 libraries were constructed, representing 10 sample groups (P0, and FF/FY/SP at three time points) with three independent biological replicates each. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Novogene Co., Ltd., Beijing). Clean reads were aligned to the ‘Sanyueli’ reference genome (https://www.rosaceae.org/Analysis/11745274) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened based on padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted with the clusterProfiler (v4.8.1). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA package (v1.71) in R (v4.2.2) to identify modules significantly correlated with key physiological traits. The co-expression network of hub genes and transcription factors was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.2).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

To confirm the transcriptome data, the expression patterns of 16 selected DEGs were analyzed by qRT-PCR. Specific primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 (Table S1). Total RNA was extracted using the Polysaccharide Polyphenol Plant Total RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (BASEGEN Biotech, Beijing). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the All-in-One 5X RT MasterMix (Applied Biological Materials Inc., Canada). qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix (Applied Biological Materials Inc., Canada). The Actin gene was used as an internal control for normalization. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The qRT-PCR was analyzed using three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate was assayed with three technical replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of three independent biological replicates. Differences among treatments were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, USA). Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test, with a statistical significance level defined as p < 0.05. All histograms were generated using OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab, USA).

Results

Physiological responses in pistils after pollination

Significant physiological differences were observed in ‘Fengtang’ pistils after pollination with different pollen sources. Soluble sugar content decreased sharply within 24 HAP across all treatments (Fig. 1A). At 24 HAP, FY and MP showed higher levels than other treatments, with FS being the lowest. The soluble sugar content was significantly highest in SP and lowest in FF at 72 HAP, but the opposite was true at 96 HAP. Soluble protein content increased markedly at 24 HAP and remained relatively stable thereafter, though FS and FF consistently exhibited lower levels than other treatments (Fig. 1B). Proline levels rose rapidly at 24 HAP, with FF displaying the lowest value (Fig. 1C). Trends diverged subsequently among treatments. Proline in MP gradually decreased, reaching the lowest level (371.77 µg·g− 1) at 96 HAP, while FS showed a slow increasing trend during 48–96 HAP. FF and FY peaked at 48 HAP, whereas SP reached its lowest point at 48 HAP. MDA content in SP, FF, and MP showed an overall increasing trend after pollination, peaking at 72 HAP (Fig. 1D). By 96 HAP, MDA levels in FY and FS were approximately 1.33–1.57 times higher than in FF, SP, and MP.

Antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT, and APX) generally showed an initial increase followed by a decline (Fig. 2). Specifically, SOD activity was strongly induced in FF, FS, and MP during the early elongation phase (24–48 HAP) but decreased markedly thereafter. However, FY and SP exhibited an opposite trend (Fig. 2A). POD activity was highest in FF at 24 HAP, exceeding that of SP, FS, FY, and MP by 44.15%, 36.66%, 35.09%, and 9.79%, respectively, and remained elevated until 72 HAP (Fig. 2B). FS, FY, and SP peaked at 48 HAP, while MP declined gradually after 24 HAP. FY and SP had the lowest POD activities at 96 HAP. CAT activity increased sharply at 24 HAP in FS and FF but decreased in FY, SP, and MP (Fig. 2C). At 48 HAP, the CAT activity of SP was significantly higher than that of the other treatments, followed by a sharp decline thereafter. At 96 HAP, MP exhibited the significantly highest CAT activity. APX activity increased sharply at 24 HAP in FS and FF, reaching levels significantly higher than those in FY, SP, and MP (Fig. 2D). FF peaked at 48 HAP and then gradually declined. FS maintained relatively high APX activity at 72–96 HAP, whereas SP showed the lowest activity at 96 HAP.

Phytohormone profiles and endogenous balance in pistils after pollination

A total of 16 phytohormones were detected by UPLC-MS/MS, including six auxins, four cytokinins, and other hormones. IAA levels increased sharply at 24 HAP, with FF showing the highest value (Fig. 3A). Levels subsequently declined across all groups. FF and MP maintained relatively elevated IAA during 72–96 HAP, whereas SP and FY generally displayed lower concentrations at 96 HAP. Other auxin derivatives, including 3-indoleacetamide (IAM), 3-indolecarboxylic acid (ICA), 3-indolepropionic acid (IPA), 3-indolebutyric acid (IBA), and 3-indoleacetonitrile (IAN), exhibited distinct temporal patterns and treatment-specific differences (Fig. S1). Notably, FF consistently showed higher IAM levels, whereas SP generally displayed lower concentrations of multiple auxin-related compounds. For cytokinins, the zeatin riboside (ZR) contents of FS and FF increased significantly at 24 HAP, then gradually declined before rising again at 96 HAP (Fig. 3B). In contrast, the ZR content of FY showed a continuous upward trend from 24 to 96 HAP. SP exhibited the significantly highest ZR level among all treatments at 72 HAP, whereas MP had a significantly lower ZR level than the other treatments during 72–96 HAP. Dihydrozeatin riboside (DHZR), N6-(Δ2-isopentenyl) adenine (2IP), and N6-(Δ2-isopentenyl) adenosine (IPA) also varied significantly among pollination treatments (Fig. S2). FF and FS exhibited relatively high levels of DHZR and 2IP. The IPA level showed an increasing trend from 24 to 96 HAP, during which FY consistently maintained the significantly lowest level, followed by SP.

Dynamics of key phytohormones in pollinated ‘Fengtang’ plum pistils. A Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, auxin). B Zeatin riboside (ZR, cytokinin). C Abscisic acid (ABA). D 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC, ethylene precursor). E Salicylic acid (SA). F Jasmonic acid (JA)

At 24 HAP, the Abscisic acid (ABA) content decreased in FS and MP. The ABA levels of FS, FY, and SP gradually increased during 24–96 HAP (Fig. 3C). Both FY and SP exhibited relatively high ABA levels throughout the post-pollination period. The 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) content showed a gradual increasing trend during 24–96 HAP (Fig. 3D). The ACC levels of SP and FY were significantly higher than those of the other treatments throughout this period. The salicylic acid (SA) levels of FS, FF, and MP increased sharply at 24 HAP and remained significantly higher than those of SP and FY throughout the period from 24 to 96 HAP (Fig. 3E). FF exhibited the highest SA level at 96 HAP, exceeding that of FS, FY, SP, and MP by 15.62%, 112.36%, 133.14%, and 92.27%, respectively. Jasmonic acid (JA) and jasmonic acid-isoleucine (JA-Ile) decreased initially, with FY and SP exhibiting higher levels at 96 HAP (Fig. 3F, S3A). In contrast, FS and FF exhibited significantly lower dihydrojasmonic acid (H2JA) levels than others at 24, 72, and 96 HAP (Fig. S3B).

The balance between growth-related and stress-related hormonal signals serves as a critical indicator of growth potential. Both the (IAA + ZR)/ABA and (ΣAUXs + ΣCTKs)/(ABA + ACC) ratios of FS, FF, and MP generally showed an initial increase at 24 HAP, followed by a decline (Fig. 4A, B). Notably, the highly compatible FF treatment consistently maintained the highest hormonal balance ratios throughout the post-pollination period, showing a unique rebound at 96 HAP. However, SP exhibited the lowest values among all treatments.

Correlation analysis

To evaluate how these multiple layers of physiological and hormonal responses correspond to the actual biological outcome, we performed a correlation analysis between pollen tube growth, physiological response indicators, and endogenous hormone levels (Fig. 4C). Pollen tube growth data were obtained from our previous study [35], in which FF showed the highest pollen tube numbers and fruit set, FS and MP were intermediate, and FY and SP the lowest, thereby defining the compatibility gradient used here. Antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT, and APX) showed strong positive correlations with pollen tube elongation. MDA and soluble protein content were negatively correlated with pollen tube growth. Conversely, ZR, ABA, ACC, and JA exhibited negative correlations. Hormonal analysis indicated that IAA, IBA, IAN, SA, H2JA, and the hormonal balance ratios (IAA + ZR)/ABA and (ΣAUXs + ΣCTKs)/(ABA + ACC) were significantly positively associated with pollen tube growth. Furthermore, IAA was significantly positively correlated with antioxidant enzyme activities, whereas ICA, 2IP, IPA, ABA, ACC, JA, and JA-Ile were negatively correlated with them. ICA, 2IP, IPA, ABA, and ACC were significantly positively correlated with soluble protein and MDA, while IAA, IBA, and IAN were negatively correlated with them (Fig. S4). A significant negative correlation was observed between IAA and both ABA and ACC in pollinated ‘Fengtang’ pistils, indicating an antagonistic relationship among these hormones during pollen tube growth.

Transcriptomic analysis

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these physiological and hormonal responses, we performed comparative transcriptomic analysis focusing on the most contrasting pollination treatments. Unpollinated pistils (P0), FF, FY, and SP samples at 24, 48, and 96 HAP were selected for transcriptome sequencing. A total of 210.92 GB of clean data was obtained (Table S2). Approximately 91.77%-94.92% of the clean reads were aligned to the ‘Sanyueli’ reference genome. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation among groups (Fig. S5A), with high reproducibility among the three biological replicates (Fig. S5B). Gene expression profiles were homogeneous within groups (Fig. S5C-D).

Analysis of DEGs revealed dynamic transcriptional changes during pollination. When comparing sequential time points, the initial response (24 HAP vs. P0) triggered the largest number of DEGs across all treatments (Fig. S6A). When comparing the three treatments at the same time point, the largest number of DEGs appeared at 96 HAP, followed by 48 HAP, with the fewest at 24 HAP (Fig.S6B), suggesting progressive transcriptional divergence as pollen-pistil interactions develop. Upset plot analysis identified 9,355, 379, and 1,385 DEGs shared in the 24 HAP vs. P0, 48 HAP vs. 24 HAP, and 96 HAP vs. 48 HAP comparisons, respectively (Fig. S6C-E). These likely represent genes commonly involved in pistil responses to the pollination process itself. Furthermore, at each time point, we identified DEGs shared among the three pairwise treatment comparisons (FF vs. FY, FF vs. SP, FY vs. SP), yielding 52, 478, and 991 shared DEGs at 24, 48, and 96 HAP, respectively (Fig. S6F-H). These DEGs may play key roles in mediating the differences in pollen tube growth behavior, although the contribution of developmental or senescence-related transcripts cannot be entirely excluded.

GO enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs were mainly associated with cell wall, response to oxidative stress, carbohydrate metabolism, alpha-amino acid metabolism, signal transduction and transcription regulator activity (Fig. 5A-C, Fig. S7A-C, Table S3). KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in plant hormone signal transduction, starch and sucrose metabolism, MAPK signaling pathway, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, citrate cycle, amino acid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites, monoterpenoid biosynthesis (Fig. 5D-F, Fig. S7D-F, Table S4). The qPCR result of 16 key DEGs confirmed the reliability of transcriptomic patterns (Fig. S8).

Pollen source affects plant hormone signal transduction and MAPK signaling pathway

Consistent with the hormonal profiling, genes involved in plant hormone signaling pathways were differentially activated (Fig. 6). In auxin signaling, FF and FY significantly upregulated TIR1-1/2 and IAA14 at 24 HAP, whereas SP showed higher AFB2 expression. Notably, auxin signaling and response genes, such as the auxin response factors ARF2A/6, the influx carrier LAX2, and the auxin-responsive genes GH3.9, SAUR12, and AUXs, were significantly upregulated in FF_96, correlating with sustained IAA levels. Conversely, ARFE, ARF6, IAA31, and SAUR71/72 showed higher expression in FY and SP. Regarding cytokinin signaling, the expression levels of AHP1/2 were significantly the highest in FF_96. Type-A response regulators (ARR3, ORR9) were highly expressed at later stages (96 HAP), whereas type-B regulators (ORR21) were highly expressed at early stages (24–48 HAP). Notably, SP_96 exhibited a higher expression level of ORR9 than FF and FY. ABA signaling components (PYL8, PP2Cs, and bZIP36/40) and Jasmonic acid components (COI1, TIFY6B) exhibited increasing expression in FY and SP but decreased in FF. Ethylene signaling genes ETR2 and EIN4 were highly expressed in early stages across all treatments, while ERS1, EBF1, and ERFC3-2/92 showed higher expression in SP. In salicylic acid signaling, TGA2.1/2.2/9 and bZIP46 were strongly induced at 48 HAP, with relatively higher levels in FY and SP.

WGCNA and co-expression network

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified gene modules associated with key physiological traits, including pollen tube growth, antioxidant enzymes, and hormonal balance ratios (Fig. S10). The yellow module showed the strongest correlation with these traits and contained eight key structural genes associated with pollination responses, including IAA (PsSY0028482), GH3 (PsSY0006678), MKK3 (PsSY0019745), SUS (PsSY0008267), CYSK (PsSY0003947), PFK (PsSY0007783), CAT (PsSY0014719), and POD (PsSY0025940) (Fig. S9B). Co-expression network analysis revealed 55 transcription factors (TFs) significantly co-expressed with these structural genes. The cytokinin response regulator ORR21 (PsSY0006093) showed high connectivity, interacting with six structural genes. Other key TFs including C3H27 (PsSY0025195), ERF34 (PsSY0023853), Trihelix (PsSY0023756), bZIP18 (PsSY0013824), and GRAS (PsSY0025222) each co-expressed with five structural genes. These TFs are closely associated with hormone signaling and reproductive development, and represent potential core regulators of pistil responses to different pollen sources.

Discussion

The pollen-pistil interaction is critical for successful sexual reproduction in flowering plants. In this study, integrated physiological, hormonal and transcriptomic analyses revealed that pollination with different pollen sources induced distinct temporal response patterns in ‘Fengtang’ plum pistils, which varied depending on the time after pollination and the compatibility of the pollen source. While this study provides a comprehensive temporal map, several limitations must be noted. To optimize sequencing resources, RNA-seq was selectively performed on extreme phenotypes (FF, FY, SP); the absence of transcriptomic data for FS and MP limits our understanding of less polarized compatibility responses. Moreover, the specific functions of the identified hub genes (ORR21, C3H27, ERF34, Trihelix, bZIP18, GRAS) require future transgenic validation. Finally, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that some late-stage transcriptomic shifts reflect generalized, age-dependent pistil senescence rather than specific pollen-induced responses. Future studies utilizing single-cell spatial transcriptomics will help isolate pollen-tube-specific signaling from whole-tissue background noise.

Physiological responses in pollen-pistil interactions

During the initial 24 HAP, the critical phase for pollen hydration, germination, and early signaling, we observed a rapid spike in ROS-scavenging enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT, APX) across all pollinated pistils (Fig. 2). ROS act as signaling molecules in pollen germination, pollen tube tip growth, and fertilization [40]. Elevated ROS levels inhibit pollen tube growth, which can be recovered by ROS scavengers [41]. Thus, this early antioxidant peak likely helps establish a permissive ROS homeostasis necessary for initial pollen-stigma recognition. However, as the interaction progressed into the elongation phase (48–72 HAP) and the fertilization phase (72–96 HAP), a distinct physiological divergence emerged. SOD, POD, CAT, and APX activities of SP and FY pistils declined during 72–96 HAP, mirroring observations in incompatible pollinations of olive [42]. Concurrently, MDA, a marker of oxidative damage, accumulated progressively (Fig. 1D), which may trigger early pistil senescence [43]. Furthermore, the late-stage depletion of nutrient reserves (soluble sugars, proline, and proteins), which are essential structural substrates for elongating tubes [44], likely contributed to the growth arrest observed in FY and SP [35]. Similar nutrient deficiency and antioxidant failure have been reported in abortive ovules of Quercus dentata [45].

Phytohormone dynamics and balance in pollen-pistil interactions

Phytohormones play multifaceted roles during pollination, from pollen tube guidance to fertilization and post-fertilization regulation [22]. Auxin has been proven as a critical regulator for polar growth of pollen tubes [46]. In ‘Fengtang’ pistils, IAA levels peaked at 24 HAP (Fig. 3A), corresponding to the active pollen germination phase. A similar pattern occurs in Nicotiana tabacum, where IAA peaks in the stigma during germination and decreases as tubes penetrate the style [47]. Notably, auxin signaling genes (TIR1, AUX/IAA22, LAX2, SAUR12) remained highly expressed in FF, especially at 96 HAP (Fig. 6), suggesting auxin’s potential sustained role in guiding late-stage fertilization events. Cytokinins also exhibited species- and stage-specific effects. While elevated cytokinins inhibit self-incompatible pollen tubes in Petunia hybrida [25, 48]. ZR is the main form of cytokinin for long‑distance transport in plants [49]. Our study showed that the ZR content in ‘Fengtang’ pistils after pollination is negatively correlated with pollen tube growth. Crucially, WGCNA identified ORR21, a type-B response regulator homologous to cytokinin signaling components in Arabidopsis, as a key hub gene, suggesting that a similar cytokinin signaling module may operate in plum pistils. However, its exact function in plum remains to be determined.

In the low‑compatibility combinations (FY and SP), ABA and ACC levels were significantly higher than those in FF, FS, and MP during the late interaction stage (72–96 HAP), coinciding with floral senescence [50]. ABA and ethylene have been implicated in cooperatively triggering PCD in incompatible pollen tubes [48]. In agreement with this, ABA signaling (PP2Cs, bZIP36/40) and ethylene pathway genes (ERFC3, ERF92, ERS1) were specifically upregulated SP and FY at 96 HAP (Fig. 6). Furthermore, JA signaling genes (COI1, TIFY6B) were highly expressed in SP, which is consistent with the report in Camellia oleifera [51]. By contrast, SA pathway gene NPR5 was highly expressed in FF. Exogenous SA/MeSA promote pollen tube growth via CoNPR1 and CoNPR3.1 in Camellia oleifera [26], suggesting that SA might similarly facilitate compatible interactions in plum, although this has not been directly tested. Rather than acting in isolation, these hormones form an integrated network. Hormonal balance ratios, (IAA + ZR)/ABA and (ΣAUXs + ΣCTKs)/(ABA + ACC), were introduced to assess hormonal homeostasis [20]. The positive correlation between (ΣAUXs + ΣCTKs)/(ABA + ACC) and pollen tube growth (Fig. 4) suggests that maintaining a pro-growth hormonal equilibrium is associated with compatibility.

Transcriptional reprogramming: MAPK, sugars, amino acids and flavonoids

The enrichment of stress-responsive terms in GO analysis suggests that pollination triggers a generalized stress response in ‘Fengtang’ pistils, particularly when confronted with low-compatibility pollen. The MAPK signaling pathway, a central downstream component of ROS and hormone signals, likely plays a key role in this here [52]. In this study, several Rboh genes exhibited higher expression in FF and FY (Fig. S9). In Arabidopsis, these Rboh genes, responsible for ROS production, are phosphorylated by MAPKs and have been implicated as negative regulators of PCD [53, 54]. Therefore, elevated Rboh expression in compatible interactions may help sustain pollen tube growth, though this has not been directly demonstrated in plum. In Arabidopsis, WRKY33 is activated through phosphorylation by MPK3/MPK6 and regulate ROS homeostasis, ethylene biosynthesis, and stress responses [55]. Its strong activation in self-pollinated pistils at 48 and 96 HAP suggests a potentially conserved role in amplifying stress and senescence signals that halt incompatible tube growth. Calcium signaling, mediated by CMLs, also appears involved in early pollen-pistil interactions.

Metabolically, continuous tube elongation into the lower style and ovary demands substantial energy and biosynthetic precursors for proteins, carbohydrate, membranes, and cell walls [56, 57]. In ‘Fengtang’ pistils, carbohydrate metabolism was prominently enriched among DEGs (Tables S3, S4). Sugars serve dual roles as energy sources and signaling molecules. Glucose promotes germination and sucrose deficiency impairs tube growth [58, 59]. Early glucose production was likely fueled by elevated INV1-1/2/3 expression in FF and FY at 24 HAP (Fig. 7). Conversely, low SPS expression in SP suggests limited sucrose availability may contribute to poor tube growth in self-pollinated pistils. Beyond metabolism, sugar signaling influences tube development. In Arabidopsis, HXK- and fructose-mediated signaling have been associated with growth repression [60]. Thus, high SUS3/6/7 and HXK1/2/3 expression in FY and SP may activate similar repressive pathways. Additionally, upregulated starch-degrading genes (BMY, DPE, PHS, AMY) indicate active starch breakdown likely supporting pollen tube energy demands [61]. Interestingly, amino acid biosynthesis genes (RPI3/4, HISs, etc.) were strongly and specifically activated in FF at 96 HAP (Fig. 8). These genes may function at a later stage of pollen-pistil interaction, potentially during pollen tube entry into the ovary [62]. The functional significance of these pathways, however, warrants further investigation.

Cell wall remodeling is essential for successful pollen tube growth [63]. GO annotation revealed significant enrichment of cell wall-related processes in pollinated pistils (Table S3). The upregulation of peroxidase genes involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis further suggests their potential role in cell wall loosening during pollination [64]. Flavonoids play crucial roles in ROS homeostasis and reproductive success [65–67]. In ‘Fengtang’ pistils, key structural genes (CHS, CHI, F3H, DFR, and ANR) showed elevated expression in FF and FY at 24 HAP, but only FF maintained high expression thereafter (Fig. 9). This sustained activation in compatible interactions suggests that flavonoid production may not only facilitate initial pollen-pistil recognition but also contribute to the differential pollen tube growth [68].

Based on these integrated data, we propose a hypothetical temporal model for plum pollen-pistil interactions (Fig. 10). At the early stage (0–24 HAP), pollination appears to trigger a universal priming response characterized by ROS scavenging, initial IAA peaks, and flavonoid production, which may enable general pollen adhesion and germination. During the intermediate elongation phase (24–72 HAP), high compatible interactions (FF) are associated with sustained growth signaling via a favorable hormonal balance and continued nutrient mobilization. In contrast, low compatible interactions (SP, FY) exhibit signs of energetic crisis and ROS accumulation. By the late stage (72–96 HAP), this divergence becomes more pronounced: compatible pistils upregulate sugar, amino acid, and flavonoid biosynthesis potentially supporting fertilization, whereas incompatible pistils display molecular signatures consistent with programmed cell death. We emphasize that this model is based on correlative evidence and serves as a framework for future hypothesis-driven validation.

A conceptual model summarizing candidate processes and regulatory associations underlying differential pistil responses to pollen sources of varying compatibility in ‘Fengtang’ plum

Conclusions

Based on integrated physiological, hormonal, and transcriptomic analyses, this study provides evidence that pistils of ‘Fengtang’ plum exhibit distinct responses to different pollen sources and suggests a potential regulatory framework underlying these interactions (Fig. 10). Highly compatible pollen sources (FF, FS, and MP) were associated with higher antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT, and APX) and more coordinated changes in primary metabolites. In parallel, these pollination combinations tended to show a relatively higher ratio of [(ΣAUXs + ΣCTKs)/(ABA + ACC)], which may reflect a pistil state more permissive for pollen tube progression. By contrast, low-compatibility pollen sources (FY and SP) were associated with higher MDA accumulation and a less favorable hormonal balance. Transcriptomic analyses further revealed pathways potentially involved in these responses, including plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling, and metabolic pathways (starch, sucrose, amino acids, and flavonoids). WGCNA identified candidate regulatory genes, such as ORR21, C3H27, ERF34, Trihelix, bZIP18, and GRAS, that may relate to the differential pistil responses to various pollen sources. Together, these findings provide an integrated descriptive framework for understanding compatibility-associated pistil responses in plum, while the underlying causal mechanisms and the functions of specific candidate genes remain to be clarified through future experimental validation.
