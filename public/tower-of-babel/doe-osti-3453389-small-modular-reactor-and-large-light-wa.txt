ABSTRACT
As the fleet of traditional large light water reactors (LLWRs) around the country continues to age, it
will be necessary to supplement the existing sites with advanced small modular reactors (SMRs),
which will allow the new facilities to benefit from the already established infrastructure. The
complexities of this task require consideration of numerous variables, including security. This study
seeks to highlight high priority elements, including development of security procedures and
infrastructure to ensure the deployment of SMRs at existing sites is accomplished in a safe and
sustainable manner. To demonstrate the challenges of integration, scenario analysis was conducted
using two hypothetical facilities: a representative LLWR (Lone Pine Nuclear Power Plant) and a
representative light water small modular reactor (LWSMR). Two options were explored for
integration of the SMR onto the existing site. First, analysis was conducted for integrating the SMR
within the existing LLWR protected area. Second, analysis was performed to evaluate the addition of
the SMR outside of the original protected area, which requires expanding the protected area to
encompass a new, larger footprint for both facilities. These analyses resulted in recommendations
for considerations and processes to adopt when co-locating an SMR with an established LLWR.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This study examines the physical protection challenges and recommendations for co-locating small
modular reactors (SMRs) with existing large light water reactor (LLWR) facilities, addressing the
growing interest among advanced reactor vendors in leveraging established nuclear sites for cost-
effective deployment. Using hypothetical facilities, specifically the Lone Pine Nuclear Power Plant
(LPNPP) and a light water small modular reactor (LWSMR), researchers conducted scenario-based
tabletop exercises with Scribe3D© modeling to analyze two primary integration strategies:
 • Placing the SMR within the existing protected area (PA)
 • Constructing it outside the PA and expanding the security perimeter around the SMR once
 complete
The analysis evaluated physical protection system (PPS) effectiveness against a design basis threat
consisting of 4-to-8 trained adversaries equipped with military-grade weapons and explosives capable
of coordinated external assault and potential insider assistance.

The study reveals that both colocation strategies present distinct operational and economic trade-
offs that must be carefully balanced. Constructing the SMR within the existing PA offers significant

1. INTRODUCTION
Some small modular reactor (SMR) vendors are considering potential deployment of SMRs with
existing large light water reactor (LLWR) sites. Co-locating an SMR with an existing LLWR will
enable both facilities to utilize existing infrastructure, systems, processes, and procedures. This
presents a unique scenario when considering the design, implementation, and operation of a physical
protection system (PPS) to protect both facility types against a design basis threat (DBT) attack. This
paper aims to identify challenges and design considerations for facilities co-locating an SMR and an
LLWR on the same facility.
One of the primary challenges in co-locating these distinct reactor types lies in the integration of
regulatory compliance frameworks and physical security boundaries. Existing LLWRs operate under
established, highly mature physical security plans that define specific owner controlled areas (OCA),
protected areas (PA), and vital areas (VA). Introducing an SMR to the site requires reconciling these
legacy boundaries with a new, potentially overlapping security perimeter. This report evaluates two
distinct options for collocation of an SMR with an LLWR:
 • Placing the SMR within the existing protected area (PA)
 • Constructing the SMR outside the PA and expanding the security perimeter around the SMR
 once complete

2. DISCUSSION OF CONSIDERATIONS FOR INTERIOR AND
 EXTERIOR COLOCATION OF AN SMR
The complexities of adding an SMR facility to an existing nuclear power plant (NPP) are significant,
and not all aspects are discussed in this report. One major consideration is the challenges of building
inside an existing PA or building outside of it and expanding the PA once the new SMR is
completed. An analysis of these options has been completed previously in, “Modularization of Small
Modular Reactor Facilities: Physical Protection Recommendations,1” so construction phase security
considerations are not discussed here. This evaluation focuses on the security for the complete state
of an SMR added to an LLWR facility.

2.1. Addition of an SMR Inside Existing PA
While SMRs may not be as large as traditional LWRs, their footprint can still be significant. This is
important to take into consideration when planning to add an SMR into an existing PA. Even if
there is sufficient physical space between existing facilities and site boundaries to place an SMR, it is
important to remember that vehicle barrier systems may still need to be adjusted to provide
sufficient stand-off distance to meet regulatory requirements. Additionally, a structure for a new
SMR may fit within the bounds of an existing PA, but placing the new facility in the available space
could significantly impact fields of view and fields of fire for responders, potentially requiring costly
security upgrades, an increase in security full time equivalents (FTEs), and/or significant changes to
security policies and procedures to meet the needs of the modified site.
Beyond the long-term physical footprint, constructing an SMR within an operational PA introduces
near-term operational challenges for the existing facility's security organization. A major nuclear
construction project requires a continuous multi-year influx of hundreds of laborers, heavy
machinery, and material deliveries daily. This volume of activity severely strains existing entry
control locations, necessitating exhaustive search and vetting procedures that can bottleneck daily
operations for the existing facility. Moreover, the presence of unvetted or temporarily escorted
construction personnel in close physical proximity to the existing facility increases the potential
insider threat risk. From a PPS perspective, construction activities, particularly heavy excavation,
structural drilling, and the movement of massive crane systems, can degrade legacy physical
protection systems. As an example, the use of heavy machinery or drilling may cause constant
alarming of vibration sensors or cause other intrusion detection sensors to go out of calibration,
resulting in alarm fatigue for the CAS operator. Additionally, temporary stockpiles of materials and
other equipment could hamper the use of existing pan-tilt-zoom (PTZ) cameras and the ability of
the response force to effectively implement the response strategy. To maintain the regulatory-
mandated security posture of the operating LLWR during the SMR construction phase, security
engineers must design extensive compensatory measures, such as temporary physical barriers,
dedicated construction-access portals, and a surge in security personnel to perform continuous
escort and patrol duties.
While integrating an SMR into an existing PA presents immediate spatial and integration challenges,
establishing a single, unified perimeter offers long-term economic benefits over the multi-decade
lifecycle of the co-located facilities. The most significant financial advantage lies in the optimization
of the response force, which historically represents the largest recurring operational expense in a

PPS. By sharing a single response force, licensees can avoid the overhead of staffing, training, and

Furthermore, a single PA eliminates the need to construct and maintain redundant, high-capital
infrastructure, such as CAS/SAS, duplicative perimeter intrusion detection systems, and separate

drastically reduces long-term maintenance, testing, and technology-refresh costs. Administratively, a
unified PA also streamlines regulatory compliance by consolidating physical security plans,
emergency response drills, and regulatory inspections into a single, cohesive program, thereby
minimizing administrative overhead and reducing compliance costs for both operating units over
their operational lifespans.
All the noted considerations should be carefully reviewed when determining the correct method for
implementing a co-located SMR facility with an existing LLWR.

2.2. Addition of an SMR Outside Existing PA
When planning to add an SMR outside of an existing PA, an operator must consider the cost of the
eventual PA expansion. Any potential excavation can be particularly costly when conducted at a
nuclear site. Significant costs can also be incurred when adding additional fencing, sensors, and posts
for responders to cover the expanded footprint. If the increased footprint is large enough, the
addition of the SMR may also necessitate the hiring of more security personnel, leading to ongoing
costs.
Although constructing the SMR outside the existing PA mitigates direct physical integration
challenges, executing a massive construction project within the surrounding OCA still presents
significant operational and tactical risks to the operating facility. Heavy excavation, trenching, and
grading immediately adjacent to the operational perimeter run a persistent risk of accidentally
severing underground redundant power lines and fiber-optic communication loops, which could
disable operating physical protection systems. Furthermore, the presence of tall construction
equipment, such as crane systems, along with structural staging and material stockpiles right outside
the PA boundary, can severely obstruct the fields of view of perimeter CCTV cameras and elevated
response positions such as bullet and blast resistant enclosure (BBRE) towers. This proximity also
introduces heightened security surveillance concerns; unvetted construction personnel operating
near the perimeter fence obtain a prolonged, high-vantage opportunity to observe rotation patterns,
response tactics, and physical vulnerabilities of the operating LLWR. To counter these
vulnerabilities, security forces must establish enhanced monitoring of the PA boundary, deploy
temporary mobile surveillance units, and potentially re-route response paths, adding operational
complexity and driving up security costs well before the SMR itself is integrated into the facility.
Constructing the SMR outside the active PA yields immediate cost savings during the construction
phase compared to building inside an operating PA. For an in-PA project, every construction
worker, flatbed truck, and concrete delivery must undergo exhaustive, time-consuming searches at
entry control points, creating logistical bottlenecks that delay schedules and drive-up labor overhead.
By building outside the PA, the project avoids the immense operational expense of background-
vetting, badging, and providing continuous armed escorts for hundreds of laborers. Furthermore,
outside-the-fence construction eliminates the need for highly expensive compensatory measures,
such as deploying dedicated, 24/7-armed posts to monitor temporary perimeter breaches or
managing high false-alarm rates on sensors caused by heavy construction vibrations. Ultimately,
isolating the construction zone from the active high-security area supports standard, more cost-

 14

effective industrial construction workflows, resulting in an accelerated schedule that can save tens of
millions of dollars in project financing and labor costs.
Once construction outside the perimeter is complete and before nuclear material arrives onsite for
the SMR, the process of physically and digitally integrating the SMR into the existing PA introduces
complex and high-capital system integration costs. Adding new or more technologies and systems
for the SMR facility could require significant capital investment to upgrade the CAS and SAS.
Security engineers must upgrade alarm display consoles, logic controllers, video management
systems, access control systems, and intrusion detection systems to ingest and process the new
sensors, cameras, and access control devices. Additionally, integrating the SMR requires extending
the legacy facility's safety-grade, uninterruptible power supply (UPS), and backup generator networks
to ensure the SMR's security equipment remains operational during a loss of off-site power. In many
cases, trying to interface modern, highly digital security technologies with the legacy, potentially
outdated security systems of the LLWR can reveal severe compatibility issues, forcing a premature
and exceptionally costly site-wide mobilization to upgrade the entire facility's physical security head-
end software and field equipment.

3. HYPOTHETICAL FACILITY DESCRIPTION
To conduct this analysis and provide recommendations, two hypothetical facilities were used. The
first hypothetical facility is the Lone Pine Nuclear Power Plant (LPNPP). LPNPP is a hypothetical
two-loop pressurized light water reactor (PWR); its primary function is to generate 1,150 megawatts
of electrical power for the national grid.
LPNPP's operational core is housed within several key structures. These include the Reactor
Containment Building, which encases the reactor and primary coolant system; the Auxiliary Building,
containing essential reactor plant auxiliary equipment; the Engineered Safety Features (ESF)
Building, designed to mitigate accident consequences by providing emergency cooling; the Control
Building, a two-story structure housing reactor controls and plant safety systems; and the Fuel
Building, dedicated to the transfer and storage of new and used nuclear fuel. The site also includes a
Condensate Storage Tank, a critical component of the auxiliary feedwater system.
Potential targets at the facility that could lead to high radiological consequences (HRC) if
compromised include the Turbine Driven Auxiliary Feedwater (AFW) Pump in the ESF Building,
the Battery Rooms, Cable Spreading Room, SCRAM Relay Room, and the Control Room within the
Control Building. Other vital areas are the Condensate Storage Tank (CST) and Piping, the Spent
Fuel Pool and Fuel Cell Cooling Pumps in the Fuel Building, the Reactor Containment structure
itself, and the Water Intake structure. These areas house critical equipment and systems essential for
the safe operation and containment of radioactive materials at LPNPP. Figure 3-1 and Figure 3-2
highlight the original LPNPP facility.

The second hypothetical facility used in this analysis is the Light Water Small Modular Reactor
(LWSMR). LWSMR is a hypothetical facility that builds upon previous hypothetical designs,
primarily developed to optimize an onsite response strategy for security and cost-effectiveness. This
facility features consolidated administrative and operational spaces, minimized underground
construction, and strategically placed above-grade support equipment. A significant modification
includes the integration of a "security floor" above operational areas, equipped with numerous gun

critical areas while reducing overall construction and long-term operational costs.
Key design modifications include increasing the size of passive safety injection tanks (PSITs) within
the reactor bays while reducing their overall number, which enhances each reactor's emergency
cooling capabilities. Diesel generators were moved from below-grade to above-grade to improve
resilience against flooding and simplify design.
Potential sabotage targets within the LWSMR facility primarily include the PSITs and the reactor
vessels themselves, as their compromise could lead to a radiological release. The facility's design
strategically places these critical components below-grade within multiple layers of security to
enhance their protection. Figure 3-3 shows the original LWSMR facility.

3.1. Co-located LLWR and LWSMR Facility
As identified in Section 2 of this report, this analysis compared the security considerations for two
different strategies of adding an SMR to an existing LLWR site: co-locating the SMR within the
existing PA and co-locating the SMR outside the existing PA and extending the PA as necessary.
For this analysis, the location for SMR placement was chosen to reduce the changes that a baseline
PPS design and response force strategy for the existing LLWR would have to make to account for
the addition of the SMR. Siting the SMR in a location that naturally integrates with the LLWR
facility's protective footprint allows the existing response plan to envelope the new reactor's vital
areas, yielding significant operational cost savings. By positioning the SMR inside established LLWR
protective strategies, security engineers can utilize the existing response force to secure both
facilities. By leveraging the existing protective force footprint and avoiding a corresponding increase
in FTE security staff, the licensee drastically reduces recurring labor costs, which typically represent
the largest portion of a nuclear facility's physical protection budget. Figure 3-4 and Figure 3-5 show

4. PHYSICAL PROTECTION SYSTEM DESIGN AND ANALYSIS
The design recommendations presented in this report were developed through a series of structured
tabletop exercises using Scribe3D©, a three-dimensional physical protection tabletop tool developed
by Sandia National Laboratories. These exercises allowed security designers to visually analyze line-
of-sight obstructions, test responder positioning, evaluate intrusion detection and assessment
systems, and evaluate combat engagements that arise. The results yielded a holistic framework of
recommendations that optimizes physical protection while minimizing unnecessary capital and
operational expenditures.
To conduct this analysis, a hypothetical DBT was used. This DBT is based on information from the
10 Code of Federal Regulations Part 73.1 (10 CFR 73.1) and an open-source hypothetical DBT. The
adversary team members were assumed to have the following characteristics:
 • Group size of 4-to-8 individuals
 • Ability to conduct a determined, violent external assault, including
 o Attack by stealth or deceptive actions
 o Operate in groups through a single entry point
 o Have multiple groups attacking through multiple entries
 • Military training and skills, willing to kill or be killed, enough knowledge to identify specific
 equipment or locations necessary for a successful attack
 • Information/access from an active or passive insider
 • Land or water vehicles, which could be used for transporting personnel and their hand-
 carried equipment to the proximity of vital areas
 • Land vehicle bomb assault, which may be coordinated with an external assault
 • Ability to conduct a cyber-attack
 • Ability to perform any of the tasks needed to steal or sabotage critical assets
 • Armed with a 7.62-mm rifle and a 9-mm pistol; ammunition; grenades; satchel charges
 containing bulk high explosives, not to exceed 10 kg total; detonators; bolt cutters; and
 miscellaneous other tools2
 • Each able to carry a man-portable total load of 29.5 kg (65 lb.)
 • Assumed run speed of 3 m/s
 • One passive non-violent insider (not included in the adversary group of 4-to-8 individuals)
Since LPNPP is used as a hypothetical training facility, the baseline PPS and response strategy were
not effectively designed to handle the above-mentioned DBT. Tabletop exercises were conducted to

4.1. Baseline LLWR PPS Design
The baseline PPS for the LLWR consists of a perimeter intrusion detection and assessment system
(PIDAS) with two forms of detection: bi-static microwave sensors and active infrared sensors. The
two-fence PIDAS is constructed from standard chain-link mesh, with razor wire capping the inner
fence line. Fixed CCTV cameras provide assessment for the entire PIDAS. The facility interior is
equipped with balanced magnetic switches (BMS) and passive infrared (PIR) sensors along paths to
target locations. Interior CCTV cameras provide assessment where all sensors are located. The
response strategy for the LLWR includes five responders posted in BBRE towers spread across the
PA. The position of the towers provides at least two overlapping fields of fire along the perimeter
and at least one field-of-fire over each entrance to the LLWR facility.

4.1.1. Baseline LLWR Facility – Attack Scenario One
The scenario consisted of a response team with five armed responders. Each of these responders
was equipped with a 5.56-mm assault rifle. The adversary team was comprised of five adversaries
armed with 7.62-mm rifles, explosive charges, and breaching equipment.
The adversary team attacked the facility from the eastern side. One adversary team member
approached the outer PA fence and breached it. As the adversary crossed the isolation zone, three
armed adversaries began to provide suppressive fire onto BBRE towers two and three, and an
additional adversary began suppressive fire on BBRE tower one. One adversary began to breach the
inner PA barrier fence. The suppressive fire allowed the adversary to make it to the base of BBRE
tower 2 and set explosive charges on its legs. The adversary then detonated their explosives,
downing the BBRE tower (in this scenario it was assumed that the adversary had enough explosive
weight do so). After this tower was brought down, two adversaries continued to provide suppressive
fire on on the visible BBRE towers, while one adversary moved up toward the entrance of the
LLWR’s control building. Once this adversary reached the control building, they began to
immediately suppress BBRE tower two. This ensured that two more adversaries could enter into the
facility PA and move toward the office building. Once all three adversaries reached the control
building, they breached the entry door and made their way to the cable spreading room. Due to the
response force not having any internal presence in the facility, the adversaries were able to reach the
cable spreading room and sabotage it.

for leave, vacation, and sick time.

4.3. Co-locating SMR Outside Existing LLWR Protected Area
This section of the report defines the design of an SMR outside an existing PA at an operational
LLWR facility. Figure 3-4 and Figure 3-5 show the location of the SMR on the southeastern corner
of the LLWR facility, placed outside of the original PA. This area was chosen because it provided
the most standoff distance around the facility. It also allowed the eventual integration with response
towers one and two. which enables the facility to leverage the existing response capabilities for the
LLWR to protect avenues of approach from the east and south of the SMR. Additionally, this
provides greater resiliency for the SMR facility and, initially, the same baseline security staffing plan
for an SMR constructed inside the PA and outside of the PA. For this analysis, it was assumed that
the SMR and LLWR would operate under one continuous PA and that the PA would extend around
the SMR. This assumption was made to mitigate any increase in security staffing and allow security
personnel to serve both the LLWR and the SMR.

4.3.1. Expanding PA Around New SMR – Attack Scenario One
Subject matter experts developed an attack scenario that considered the adversaries attacking the
facility from the south side of the SMR. The adversary force, comprised of five adversaries equipped
with 7.62 assault rifles, targeted this vector to reduce the likelihood of contending with the five
responders posted around the co-located LLWR. The adversary team began by breaching the
PIDAS on the south side of the SMR facility. Once the perimeter fence was breached (requiring
between 12 and 17 seconds), the adversary team moved up and advanced on the south side of the
SMR, directing heavy suppressive fire at the south-facing ports of the SMR blisters. Immediately
upon reaching the skin of the building, one adversary began a mechanical breach of the south
personnel entrance. However, during this movement, response units six and seven began engaging
the advancing adversaries from protected gun ports directed along the skin of the south side of the
SMR. This combined engagement from protected positions allowed the responders to neutralize all
adversaries and prevent entry. The positioning of the blisters on the corner of the building and the
angle of the gunports on the blisters rendered the adversaries incapable of suppressing the inner
ports along the skin of the building. In addition to the gunport placement, the gunports in the
blisters were designed to be muzzle holes with viewing glass above the muzzle holes. This design
makes it much more difficult for the adversaries to direct effective suppressive fire on the blisters.
The scenario concluded with subject matter experts deciding the response team had won. This
outcome was determined because the response force successfully neutralized the adversary team at
the facility skin, preventing any breach of the SMR. Given this successful defense, the response
force and their existing security strategy were deemed effective at preventing radiological sabotage.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
When placing an SMR onto an existing LLWR facility, numerous factors should be considered. One
example is the increased number of sabotage and theft targets that may be located on one facility.
Additionally, some SMR vendors are developing multi-unit, multi-building SMR facilities, and this
may increase the impacts to the design and implementation of a PPS for a co-located facility. In
general, previous analysis has shown that the less cluttered a protected area (e.g., fewer auxiliary
buildings, fewer pieces of equipment distributed around the site, etc.) the easier it is for the response
to efficiently secure the site. The analysis completed in this study focused on the addition of an SMR
design that minimizes the above-ground footprint and is squared off to reduce angles that could be
exploited by approaching adversaries. The incorporation of such an SMR reduced the complexity of
adaptation required for the existing security structure and procedures.
The addition of an SMR to the site of an existing LLWR will require unique and individualized
assessment and planning whenever it is attempted due to the unique qualities of each existing LLWR
site as well as the unique designs of SMRs. Furthermore, the greater the integration of security-by-
design (SeBD) principles in the development of the SMR design, the simpler it will be to incorporate
the new facility into an existing security architecture. The LWSMR used in this study was optimized
through repeated analysis to reduce the personnel and resources required to protect the facility’s
assets. The small footprint of this hypothetical design had a reduced impact on the coverage of the
existing security force when compared with some designs currently being investigated or promoted
by vendors in the SMR industry. In some respects, the design of the LWSMR to provide the
capability for responders to efficiently and effectively engage both internally and externally to the
facility enhanced the security of the existing LLWR site.

5.1. Colocation of SMR with LLWR Recommendations
When considering the addition of a co-located SMR, an operator should aim to place the new
structure such that there is the greatest overlap with the existing security architecture, to include
existing responder fields-of-view and fields-of-fire. The example LLWR and LWSMR used in this
analysis had several attributes that lent themselves to efficient integration. The LLWR had a large PA
that offered the option of placing the new LWSMR within the existing boundary. In this
configuration, the existing towers for overwatch of the LLWR afforded considerable protection of
the combined facilities without the need for significant adaptation. One tower was repositioned and
two other towers were rotated to ensure sufficient coverage of the facility exterior. The blisters and
gun ports incorporated in the LWSMR design provided the necessary additional fields-of-view while
minimizing the additional number of responders needed to provide security for the combined
facilities. In the case of adding the LWSMR outside of and expanding the existing PA, a similar
principle was followed with similarly successful results. In both cases, despite the addition of a
building with a footprint of approximately 29,000 square feet, only two additional responders were
required to successfully defend against the DBT.
The following list summarizes recommendations for integration of SMRs on existing LLWR sites:
 1. When possible, select an SMR design that is optimized for a minimal footprint
 a. Reduce the number of auxiliary buildings required for SMR operations
 b. Square the building to remove the potential for adversaries to exploit alcoves for
 cover and concealment

 41

 2. When possible, select an SMR design that has integrated security features (e.g., gun ports,
 blisters, security floor, etc.)
 a. SeBD principles like these ensure the added SMR is already equipped with features
 that provide a robust defense against the DBT
 b. This will reduce the number of additional security personnel needed to provide
 defense of the new facility and, therefore, reduce the ongoing costs associated with
 integration of the new SMR
 3. Conduct scenario analysis with scaled, three-dimensional models to determine the most
 efficient location for the deployment of the new SMR
 a. When possible, select a location that provides the greatest overlap with existing
 security infrastructure (i.e., intrusion detection, overwatch by response positions)
 b. Position the new SMR such that personnel entries or roll-up doors are viewable by
 existing response positions
 c. Assess the potential for vulnerabilities introduced by the addition of the SMR
 4. Consider the technology of the SMR to be included and how the operational activities of the
 new SMR will impact the operational activities of the existing LLWR
 a. Frequency of refueling
 b. Fuel handling processes required by the new technology
 c. Whether the location of existing LLWR infrastructure with respect to the new SMR
 will create new targets or attack vectors (e.g., does proximity of water tanks or water
 lines for the LLWR create the potential for exploitation by the adversary to cause
 interaction with new fuel types or coolants like molten salts)
 5. Utilize security infrastructure from the LLWR to protect the SMR (e.g., integrate the SMR
 inside of the LLWR PA boundary)
 a. These measures can help reduce the total number of CAS operators, the total
 number of responders, and the overall total number of security personnel needed.
 b. This can also reduce the total cost to maintain and implement the PPS. By leveraging
 similar infrastructure, the facility can implement the same performance testing
 programs, maintenance programs, search programs, access control procedures, and
 insider threat mitigation programs. This can lead to reduced regulatory engagement
 costs and reduced costs for overall manpower needed to operate the security system.

5.2. General Colocation Recommendations
The discussion in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 demonstrates that construction phase considerations
represent one of the most significant differentiators between inside-PA and outside-PA co-location
approaches, with impacts that extend well beyond the construction timeline into long-term
operations of the facility and the security system. While the analysis in Section 4 showed minimal
differences in required staffing between the two approaches (adding only one armed responder
regardless of SMR placement) the construction phase presents substantially different risk profiles
and cost implications that must factor into colocation decisions.

 42

As detailed in Section 2.1, constructing an SMR within an existing PA introduces immediate
operational challenges that can compromise the security posture of the operating LLWR. The multi-
year influx of hundreds of construction workers, heavy machinery, and daily material deliveries
creates persistent entry control bottlenecks and insider threat risks that require compensatory
measures. The degradation of existing physical protection systems from construction vibrations,
equipment obstructions, and alarm fatigue necessitates costly temporary security enhancements and
potential increases in security staffing during the construction phase. However, Section 2.1 also
highlighted that these short-term challenges yield significant long-term benefits through unified
security infrastructure and shared response forces, potentially saving millions annually in operational
costs.
Conversely, the outside-PA construction approach analyzed in Section 2.2 avoids many immediate
operational disruptions to the existing facility but introduces different risks, including potential
damage to underground security infrastructure and visual obstruction of existing security systems.
While this approach offers substantial cost savings during construction by eliminating the need for
extensive worker vetting and compensatory security measures, it ultimately requires expensive
system integration efforts when the facilities are unified under a single PA. The construction phase
analysis reveals that the choice between colocation approaches should be driven not only by the
minimal operational security differences demonstrated in Section 4, but more significantly by an
organization's capacity to manage construction-phase security challenges and their long-term
operational cost priorities. Future colocation projects should conduct detailed construction impact
assessments alongside operational security analysis to fully understand the total cost and risk
implications of each approach.
One option that was not considered in this report was siting an SMR outside of an existing LLWR
facility and not unifying the security system and other supporting infrastructure. This is an option
that some have considered to leverage existing electrical infrastructure and an existing OCA that
would surround both the SMR and LLWR. One of the possible drawbacks to this option is the
increase in cost over the lifetime of both facilities. By not unifying the security system and response
strategy for both facilities, each facility will have its own separate security system and security
program. This may result in significant long-term expenses to implement effective security systems
and programs for two facilities compared to implementing one program for both.
Finally, as the analysis presented in Section 4 indicates, by integrating an SMR that is designed for
optimal footprints and integrated security technologies, options for colocation siting are less
impacted by the overall cost to implement and maintain the security system. The larger driver for
choosing the colocation option becomes the cost-tradeoffs between constructing the operating
reactor inside an existing and operational PA or outside of an existing PA and unifying the security
system after the SMR is constructed. Therefore, vendors and future utilities should consider all of
these potential factors when choosing the correct colocation option for their needs.
