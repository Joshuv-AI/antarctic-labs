Screening of cytochrome CYP26A1 expression in different bone tumors
Authors: Belal Al-zu’bi, Fatemah O F O Alshammari, Randa AlQaisi, Ahmed A Al-abadleh, Anas O Satari, Sameeh Al-Sarayreh, Yousef M Al-saraireh, Mohammad Salem Hareedy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCytochrome P450 26A1 (CYP26A1) is a retinoic acid metabolizing enzyme which is overexpressed in many cancers. However, its expression in bone tumors has not been characterized.MethodsImmunohistochemistry was used to evaluate CYP26A1 expression in a human bone disease spectrum comprising duplicated cores of 95 cases of benign, intermediate and malignant bone tumors alongside normal bone samples. STRING database was utilized to perform CYP26A1 protein–protein interaction and functional enrichment analyses.ResultsCYP26A1 staining was not identified in the normal bone samples analyzed. Collectively, CYP26A1 was expressed in 31.6% (30/95) of bone tumors, with cytoplasmic localization, while no immunoreactivity was detected in normal tissues. Compared with benign (18.8%) and malignant tumors (27.1%), intermediate bone tumors expressed CYP26A1 significantly more frequently (66.7%). This difference in expression between tumor categories was statistically confirmed (p < 0.05, Cramér’s V = 0.382). Among intermediate tumors, CYP26A1 was detected with high frequency and weak staining intensity in giant cell tumors (GCTs, 14/20 cases), with immunoreactivity localized mainly in osteoblast-like and osteocyte-like stromal cells. Malignant bone tumors predominantly showed focal or an absence of CYP26A1 immunoreactivity. However, strong CYP26A1 immunoreactivity was displayed in the limited chordoma and adamantinoma cases examined, while osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma displayed no immunoreactivity in general. Interestingly, functional enrichment analysis confirmed the established association of CYP26A1 with retinoid metabolic pathways.ConclusionsCYP26A1 expression was most frequent in giant cell tumors of bone. While also detected in a subset of other bone tumors, expression was limited or absent in most benign and malignant lesions, and entirely absent in normal bone tissue. These findings provide novel descriptive insight into CYP26A1 distribution and support further investigation into its role in retinoid-related pathways in bone tumor biology.Keywords: CYP26A1, Bone tumors, Immunohistochemistry, Benign and malignant bone lesions

Introduction

Bone and joint tumors are uncommon but highly aggressive tumors predominantly affecting children, adolescents, and adults. Despite accounting for less than 1% of total tumors, about 3,770 new cases of bone tumors were diagnosed with an overall annual rate of approximately 1.0 new cases per 100,000 population, and about 2,190 deaths were reported with an annual death incidence of 0.5 per 100,000 population in the USA [1, 2]. Therapeutically, different multimodal strategies are used to treat these tumors including neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy, complete surgical resection of the tumor, and, in certain cases, radiation. However, the overall therapy outcomes remain poor, especially for patients with recurrent or metastatic disease. Moreover, despite an increase in 5-year survival rates for localized bone tumors to roughly 60–70%, severe long-term toxicity and lower survival rates are significantly associated with the current therapies used for advanced cases [2–4]. Such challenges highlight the urgent need for new molecular targets and physiologically based treatment approaches for bone tumors.

Cytochrome P450 26A1 (CYP26A1) is an enzyme of the cytochrome P450 superfamily that participates in the oxidative metabolism of retinoic acid (RA) and also contributes to the synthesis of steroids, cholesterol and other types of lipids along with its peers CYP26B1 and CYP26C1. CYP26A1 mediates the oxidative degradation of RA, hence modulating intracellular levels and influencing RA signaling pathways critical for cellular proliferation and differentiation [5–9]. RA homeostasis is essential for gene regulation and developmental functions, and the disruption of retinoid balance has been associated with tumor development. Aberrant CYP26A1 expression has been associated with reduced RA levels and tumor development in many tumor types including breast, pancreatic, colorectal and cervical cancers [10, 11]. Furthermore, inhibiting CYP26-dependent RA metabolism enhances RA-triggered activities in preclinical tumor models, supporting the theory that modulation of CYP26 affects tumor biology [12]. However, data on CYP26A1 expression in bone tumors is currently limited.

RA signaling pathways are essential for osteogenic differentiation, mesenchymal cell fate, and skeletal development. As bone tumors and their malignant potential may originate from the disruption of these developmental processes, it is rational to investigate CYP26A1 expression in bone tumors. Retinoids have been shown to play an important role in bone tumor models, with CYP26A1 regulating intracellular RA levels. Its aberrant expression was found to induce cell growth, and impair differentiation and retinoid resistance [13]. On this basis, this study aimed to evaluate CYP26A1 expression in a wide range of human bone tumors, as well as to examine CYP26A1 protein–protein interaction and functional enrichment. Mapping these expression patterns is a vital initial step toward developing hypotheses about potential physiological importance, which will require further confirmation in future functional and clinical research.

Materials and methods

Tissue specimens

Before commencing this investigation, the Institutional Review and Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Mutah granted ethical permission (Reference No. 43025, date: June 15, 2025). In this study, a commercially supplied human bone disease tissue microarray (BO2081, TissueArray.Com LLC) was utilized. The tissue array includes a wide range of benign and malignant bone diseases, alongside normal bone tissue. This cohort consisted of duplicate cores of 95 patient cases (190 tissue cores) to ensure full tissue representation. In particular, the array includes cases of osteosarcoma (25 cases, 50 cores), chondrosarcoma (10 cases, 20 cores), and Ewing sarcoma (2 cases, 4 cores). The other 48 cases (96 cores) include a diverse range of less frequent bone diseases, such as mixed sarcoma subtypes, rare primary bone tumors and other bone-associated neoplastic or reactive lesions (please refer to Table 1 for more information on each subtype or reactive lesion). Additionally, 10 (20 cores) ordinary bone tissues were utilized to facilitate comparative analysis. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, all specimens requiring decalcification underwent the same processing procedure, including formalin fixation followed by decalcification in 10% nitric acid for approximately 12–24 h prior to paraffin embedding. The heterogeneous case composition allows for a comprehensive analysis of CYP26A1 protein expression profiles across a large set of malignant bone tumors, rare bone neoplasms, and non-neoplastic bone controls (normal tissues) under controlled experimental conditions.

Immunohistochemistry

A tissue microarray containing formalin-fixed, wax-embedded tissue cores was de-waxed and rehydrated through graded xylene and ethanol solutions. After rehydration, tissue cores underwent an antigen retrieval process by microwaving in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) for 20 min. After cooling at room temperature and a triple wash in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), tissue cores were immersed in 5% hydrogen peroxide solution for five minutes to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Tissue cores were then washed in PBS three times and incubated with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min to inhibit non-specific binding. Consequently, tissue cores were incubated with a rabbit monoclonal CYP26A1 (EPR11841, ab172474, Abcam) at an optimized concentration of 5 μg/mL for one hour at room temperature. After washing in PBS, a specific polymer goat anti-rabbit IgG reagent (ImmPRESS, MP-7451; Vector Laboratories) was applied for 30 min. To visualize the immunoreactivity, tissues were treated with the chromogen 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Following a wash in distilled water, tissue cores were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated and mounted with resin. To confirm antibody specificity, a colon cancer tissue, known to express CYP26A1, was included as a positive control. Negative controls were performed by omitting the CYP26A1 primary antibody. Staining results were semi-quantitatively and independently assessed by two experienced pathologists who were blind to the pathological diagnosis. Disagreements amongst observers were settled through collaborative review to reach a consensus score. Since CYP26A1 is a cytosolic enzyme responsible for intracellular metabolism of RA, cytoplasmic and membranous staining patterns were considered as positive expression, and no nuclear immunoreactivity was found. The staining scores were recorded as follows: negative (< 5% positive cells), weak (5–33%), moderate (34–66%) and strong (> 67%). The final percentage score for each case was calculated by averaging the results from duplicate cores. Because of the limited size of tissue microarray cores and the inherent intratumoral heterogeneity, a percentage-based scoring system was adopted without applying composite scoring methods such as the H-score to maintain consistency and avoid overinterpretation of focal staining patterns.

Protein–protein interactions (PPI) network

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was used to perform PPI networking to elucidate the CYP26A1 interaction profile. Only humans were included in the analysis, and an absolute minimum interaction score of 0.7 (high confidence) was needed. Both functional and physical protein associations were included, and k-means clustering was performed with the number of clusters set to three to determine functional modules within the network.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8 (USA). The data were presented as frequencies and percentages of CYP26A1 expression in the different samples used.

Results

Prevalence of CYP26A1 expression in normal versus bone tumor categories

The expression of CYP26A1 was exhibited as patchy membranous and cytoplasmic staining without significant immunoreactivity observed in the nucleus (Fig. 1). Such expression was confirmed by using appropriate staining controls, where the negative control showed no expression, whereas the positive control colon cancer tissue exhibited typical membranous and cytoplasmic immunoreactivity, denoting CYP26A1 expression. Approximately 31.6% (30/95 cases) of bone tumor tissues displayed heterogeneous CYP26A1 expression, whereas the remaining cases (68.4%, 65/95) showed no immunoreactivity. Notably, no CYP26A1 immunoreactivity was detected in all normal bone samples analyzed (Table 1).

Importantly, the highest frequency of CYP26A1 expression was found in the intermediate bone tumor cases (giant cell tumors, GCTs), accounting for 66.7% (14/21). In contrast, only 18.8% (3/16) and 27.1% (13/48) of benign and malignant bone tumors respectively were CYP26A1-positive. Statistical analysis confirmed that CYP26A1 expression was significantly associated with tissue category (normal vs intermediate vs benign vs malignant, χ2 test, P < 0.05), with a moderate relationship (Cramér’s V = 0.438) (Fig. 2). A similar pattern of association was also reinforced by re-comparing benign, intermediate and malignant bone lesions without including normal tissue cases. (P < 0.05, Cramér’s V = 0.382) (Fig. 2).

Patterns of CYP26A1 expression within specific bone lesions

Expression patterns should be regarded cautiously and provided descriptively without inference for tumor types represented by very small case numbers (n < 3), as shown in Table 1. Benign bone tumors displayed a limited CYP26A1 expression with predominantly weak staining intensity in general (Fig. 3). Cases of chondroblastoma, chondroma, and osteochondroma had no CYP26A1 expression at all. Aneurysmal bone cyst showed limited positivity (1/6 cases), while osteofibrous dysplasia demonstrated higher relative positivity (2/3 cases) with weak and moderate staining intensity (Table 1).

Intermediate lesions were represented by GCTs and epithelioid osteoblastoma (Table 1). Among these, giant cell tumors demonstrated the highest frequency of CYP26A1 expression (14/20 cases, 46.7%), with predominantly weak staining intensity (13/14 cases) and only one case showing strong immunoreactivity (Fig. 3). Cellular localization analysis revealed that CYP26A1 immunoreactivity was restricted to tumor stromal cells (osteoblasts and osteocytes), while osteoclast-like giant cells remained consistently negative. Conversely, no CYP26A1 immunoreactivity was detected in epithelioid osteoblastoma (1 case).

In malignant bone tumors, the expression of CYP26A1 was mainly absent or limited (Fig. 3). In particular, there was no CYP26A1 immunoreactivity displayed in Ewing’s sarcoma. Moreover, moderate expression was demonstrated in a subset of chondrosarcoma cases (3/10), while minimal expression was observed in osteosarcoma (1/26, weak staining). Conversely, chordoma (2/2 cases), squamous cell adamantinoma (2/2 cases), and classical adamantinoma (5/6 cases) showed strong CYP26A1 immunoreactivity (Table 1).

Anatomical distribution of CYP26A1 expression in GCTs

As GCTs were the most common expressers of CYP26A1, a sub-analysis of the anatomical distribution of CYP26A1 expression was undertaken (Table 2). Given the limited subgroup sizes, statistical analysis between CYP26A1 expression and anatomical site was not feasible, and these observations are descriptive only. However, CYP26A1 expression was more frequently found in the femur (35.7%), followed by the tibia and humerus (21.4% each). In addition, CYP26A1-negative cases were more uniformly distributed in the sacrum and radius.

PPI Functional enrichment and clustering

PPI functional enrichment analyses were used to position CYP26A1 within established retinoid biochemical routes, rather than inferring cancer-specific molecular processes or reflecting evidence particular to bone tumors. The interaction network of CYP26A1 consisted of 11 nodes and 39 edges, with an average node degree of 7.09 and a PPI enrichment p-value of 15.66 × 10−12. Such analysis showed significantly more interactions than expected by chance and yielded high connectivity and intermodular communication with the average local clustering coefficient of 0.741.

Three primary protein clusters were determined using k-means clustering (Fig. 4). The first cluster (1) included nine protein genes, namely CYP26A1, ALDH1A2, DHRS3, RDH11, LRAT, ALDH1A3, ALDH1A1, ADH5, and RDH10, which are responsible for retinol metabolism. The second cluster (2) involves CYP2C8, known for its role in the metabolism of various endogenous substrates, including fatty acids, steroid hormones and vitamins. The third cluster (3) includes only alcohol dehydrogenase 6 (ADH6). Overall, the PPI network reflects previously established roles of CYP26A1 in retinol metabolism, drug metabolism, tyrosine metabolism and detoxification of chemical carcinogenesis.

Discussion

The current study presents a descriptive immunohistochemical analysis of CYP26A1 protein expression across a spectrum of benign, intermediate, and malignant bone tumors and should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. CYP26A1 expression was detected in a subset of bone tumors, with the highest frequency observed in intermediate lesions, particularly giant cell tumors. Conversely, most benign and malignant tumors showed limited or absent expression. No CYP26A1 immunoreactivity was detected in the analyzed normal bone tissues. These findings indicate that CYP26A1 expression varies across tumor categories, with a predominance in specific lesion types rather than a uniform association with benign or malignant status, without implying functional causality.

CYP26A1 is a key hydroxylase enzyme that oxidatively degrades all-trans RA. This controls cellular RA concentration responsible for downstream signaling mechanisms mediated by retinoid X receptors (RXRs) and retinoic acid receptors. Since RA affects bone remodeling, osteoblast differentiation and skeletal development, tight regulation of RA signaling mechanisms is essential. Although disruption of RA metabolic processes has been linked to cancer biology, the direction and functional implications of such disruption seem to be extremely context-dependent [5, 14, 15]. Despite limited data on CYP26A1 expression in normal bone tissue, this study’s finding that CYP26A1 immunoreactivity was not detected in the normal bone samples examined is in line with earlier investigations demonstrating that mature, homeostatic bone tissue requires a low RA-catabolic demand under homeostatic conditions [16, 17].

Another important finding of the current study was the high frequency of CYP26A1 expression in GCTs categorized as intermediate lesions. These tumors are particularly distinguished by a cellular microenvironment consisting of proliferative stromal cells and osteoclast-like giant cells that are responsible for local bone remodeling. RA signaling pathway was functionally found to regulate both osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation, suggesting a possible connection between RA metabolism and GCT [16]. RA was also found to include osteoclast growth as well as inhibiting its RANKL-induced differentiation, further suggesting RA's regulatory effect on osteoclasts [18]. Such alteration of RANKL/osteoprotegerin ratio also modulates osteoblast–osteoclast interplay, indicating the possibility of RA effect on bone resorption [19]. In this study, we identified CYP26A1’s presence in osteoblast-like cells, while we did not find it in osteoclast-like giant cells. This can be explained by a compartment-specific pattern at the tumor’s cellular level. Such identification in osteoblast-like cells may be consistent with RA’s role in bone resorption, since stromal cells represent the part of the tumor responsible for recruiting osteoclasts. Hence, CYP26A1 expression in the GCTs may co-exist with RA regulatory mechanisms, but this remains elusive.

In the studied array, CYP26A1 expression was detected in certain subtypes of malignant bone lesions. Osteosarcoma and chondrosarcoma cases showed weak and moderate expression, respectively. On the other hand, CYP26A1 was strongly expressed in cases of adamantinoma and chodrodma. This variability in expression in different malignant bone lesions may align with the altered enzyme expression related to the RA pathway in other tumor entities. The oncogenic effect of RA signaling has been confirmed through the disruption of mechanisms related to cell cycle regulation, cell apoptosis, cell stemness, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [18, 19]. Thus, the differences in CYP26A1 in bone malignancies might indicate the variation of RA pathway activity across the various tumor subtypes. Despite the novelty of the present findings, they remain preliminary and observational, as CYP26A1 enzymatic activity and RA levels were not experimentally assessed here. It is noteworthy to mention that such variability in the expression of CYP26A1 was found in other cancers such as breast, colon and head and neck malignancies [20–22]. This may underscore the context-dependent role of CYP26A1 in the tumor microenvironment, indicating that the involvement of the RA pathway may vary between epithelial tumors and mesenchymal bone tumors.

Translationally, the retinoid cascade continues to attract the interest of researchers in bone oncology. In bone cancer models, RA has been shown to exhibit anti-cancer and anti-metastatic effects by decreasing tumor metastatic dissemination and controlling cancer-associated macrophage polarization [23]. Moreover, pharmacologic inhibition of CYP26 enzymatic activity has been studied as a strategy to improve RA-based differentiation therapies, as CYP26 enzymes are known regulators of retinoid resistance in several tumors [5, 23]. Although these approaches remain experimental, they highlight the significance of the retinoid metabolic pathway in cancer therapies [19].

There are several limitations that should be addressed in the current study. Limited sample sizes allowed only for the study of certain bone tumor subtypes. For this reason, bone tumors were broadly classified into benign, intermediate, and malignant groups according to the WHO classification, rather than stratification on the basis of lineage differentiation (e.g., osseous, chondroid, fibro/myofibroblastic, and giant cell–rich categories). Moreover, only the protein expression of CYP26A1 was investigated by immunohistochemistry rather than the enzymatic activity of CYP26A1 or local RA concentrations. Additionally, there was no independent molecular validation (e.g., Western blotting) of antibody specificity, despite support obtained from proper positive and negative immunohistochemical controls. Accordingly, the current data should be regarded as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. On this basis, future studies should be focused on exploring the functional and clinical importance of CYP26A1 in bone tumors, including the assessment of CYP26 isoenzymes, transcriptomic analyses, RA receptor profiling, and molecular evaluation of retinoid pathways. Combined assessment of CYP26A1 with CYP26B1 and CYP26C1, alongside direct measurement of RA levels, should be explored to better understand the importance of retinoid metabolism in bone tumor biology.

In summary, CYP26A1 expression was found in several bone tumors. The highest frequency of expression was found in intermediate lesions, particularly in GCT entities. Conversely, CYP26A1 expression was either absent or limited in benign and malignant bone tumor categories. Notably, all normal bone cases examined were completely negative for CYP26A1 expression. In the benign entity, limited positivity was observed in aneurysmal bone cyst and osteofibrous dysplasia. Regarding malignant lesions, chondrosarcomas, adamantinomas, and chordoma cases showed moderate to strong positivity. While this study disruptively investigates CYP26A1 patterns of expression in several bone tumors, further studies are needed to address the role of the RA pathway in the biology of bone tumors.
