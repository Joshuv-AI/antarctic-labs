Clinical outcomes and parental satisfaction of two stainless steel crown systems in pediatric patients: a retrospective observational cohort study
Authors: Başak Günay, Narmin Soylu, Merve Bayram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStainless steel crowns (SSCs) are widely used for restoring severely carious primary molars. However, clinical evidence comparing different commercially available SSC systems remains limited. This study aimed to compare the clinical success and parental satisfaction of two SSC systems, Kids Crown and NuSmile, in pediatric patients.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study included children aged 2–8 years who received at least one SSC under general anesthesia and had a minimum follow-up of 6 months. Clinical evaluations were performed by a single calibrated examiner and assessed crown retention, marginal adaptation, occlusion, proximal contact, gingival health, oral hygiene status, and biological or mechanical complications. Parental satisfaction was evaluated using a 5-point Likert-scale questionnaire. Non-parametric statistical tests were applied with a significance level set at p < 0.05; all tests were two-sided.ResultsA total of 65 children (246 crowns) were included, with a mean follow-up of 20.12 ± 13.31 months. Both SSC systems demonstrated high clinical success, with overall crown retention exceeding 97%. Good marginal adaptation was observed in 98.3% of crowns and was significantly more frequent in the Kids Crown group than in the NuSmile group (p = 0.038). No significant intergroup differences were found for retention, occlusion, proximal contact, gingival index, or oral hygiene status. Parental satisfaction scores were high in both groups, particularly regarding durability and overall satisfaction.ConclusionsBoth Kids Crown and NuSmile SSC systems showed high clinical success and parental acceptance in pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia. Although marginal adaptation favored Kids Crown, the overall clinical relevance of this difference appears limited.Trial registrationThe trial was registered retrospectively on May 8, 2025 (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07393854).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08998-w.Keywords: Stainless steel crowns, Pediatric dentistry, Primary molars, General anesthesia, Parental satisfaction

Introduction

Dental caries is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases affecting children worldwide and often leads to extensive destruction of primary molar tooth structure when not addressed early [1]. Preservation of primary dentition is essential not only for masticatory function and speech development but also for maintaining arch integrity and preventing future malocclusion, psychological impacts, and impaired quality of life [2].

General anesthesia enables comprehensive, pain-free, and high-quality dental care, particularly in uncooperative children with behavioral problems or disabilities [3]. The choice of restorative material is particularly critical in children undergoing dental rehabilitation under general anesthesia, where retreatment carries significant clinical and economic implications. Considering the risks associated with repeated general anesthesia exposure and the limited opportunity for reintervention, the use of highly durable and predictable restorative materials is essential [4].

Stainless Steel Crowns (SSCs) are indicated for use in both primary and permanent posterior teeth with enamel defects such as hypoplasia, in cases of failed previous direct restorations, for coronal restoration following pulpotomy and pulpectomy procedures, in children with high caries risk, and in treatments performed under general anesthesia [5]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported high clinical success rates for SSCs in primary molars, with pooled estimates approaching 96% and reported outcomes exceeding 97% in clinical settings, highlighting their superior longevity compared to intracoronal restorations [6–8].

In vitro and ex vivo studies comparing different SSC brands report that SSCs are exposed to oral factors such as mastication, saliva, tooth brushing, acidic challenges, and biofilm, which may alter surface properties and affect clinical performance. In addition, hardness, wear resistance, and fatigue resistance are important for long-term success. Differences in manufacturing, alloy composition, and design among brands may lead to variability in these properties [9, 10]. Clinical studies comparing different brands in the literature are limited to pre-veneered SSCs, and these studies have demonstrated that variations in crown thickness, contour, and alloy composition may influence marginal adaptation and handling characteristics [11–13].

Periodontal and gingival health are essential components of overall oral health. As a soft tissue, the gingiva is highly susceptible to irritation and inflammation induced by mechanical, microbial, or chemical factors. SSCs may influence the microbial balance within the oral cavity. In addition, inadequately adapted crowns and poor oral hygiene can contribute to increased plaque accumulation, alterations in the microbial flora, and subsequently lead to gingivitis or periodontitis [14].

Although SSCs are generally considered restorative materials with similar properties, variations among different commercial brands in manufacturing processes, alloy composition, and surface finishing procedures may lead to differences in surface characteristics. These differences may particularly influence surface parameters such as roughness and polishability [9]. Toxicity, durability, and cost are key factors that parents consider before consenting to any restorative technique. Clinicians treating pediatric patients frequently experience parental influence during the selection of dental restorations, and it has been reported that parents are increasingly involved in clinical decision-making processes. Therefore, meeting parental expectations has become one of the important determinants in the selection of pediatric dental restorations [15].

Parents’ treatment expectations include not only functional requirements but also aesthetic demands. [16]. Although esthetic full-coverage restorations such as zirconia and pre-veneered crowns have gained increasing attention in pediatric dentistry, each option has important clinical limitations. Pre-veneered crowns may be associated with veneer chipping over time, whereas zirconia crowns require higher cost and increased technique sensitivity [17, 18]. In this context, SSCs remain the most commonly used full-coverage restorative option for severely carious primary molars due to their well-documented durability, low cost, and ease of application [18–20]. Although different SSC systems share similar indications, variations in design and manufacturing characteristics may influence clinical fit and long-term performance [9, 11–13]. This is particularly important in patients treated under general anesthesia, where maximizing restoration longevity and minimizing the need for retreatment are critical considerations [4].

Among the commercially available SSC systems, Kids Crown and NuSmile are widely used in pediatric dentistry due to their availability in multiple sizes, pre-contoured designs, and favorable clinical handling characteristics [21, 22]. In routine clinical practice, clinicians often select SSC brands based on personal experience, institutional habits, or product availability rather than evidence-based criteria. Therefore, comparative evaluation of different SSC systems may provide clinically meaningful evidence to support decision-making in crown selection. Although both in vitro studies and in vivo investigations on pre-veneered SSCs have demonstrated differences among brands in terms of physical and mechanical properties—including hardness, wear resistance, corrosion behavior, and marginal adaptation—no clinical study to date has evaluated the in vivo performance and parental satisfaction of different SSC systems applied to posterior teeth [9]. This gap in the literature is particularly critical, as it may directly influence clinical decision-making, especially in high-risk settings such as treatment under general anesthesia. Therefore, the present observational study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes and parental satisfaction associated with two commercially available SSC systems—Kids Crown and NuSmile—in children undergoing dental rehabilitation under general anesthesia. The study was based on the null hypothesis (H0) that no significant difference exists between the two SSC brands in terms of clinical outcomes and parental satisfaction. In contrast, the alternative hypothesis (H1) proposed that the structural and compositional differences reported in the in vitro literature would translate into measurable clinical differences and influence parental perceptions of treatment outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective comparative observational study with cross-sectional clinical evaluation, comparing the clinical success and parental satisfaction of two commercially available SSC systems—Kids Crown and NuSmile—in pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia. The study protocol was registered and approved as a clinical investigation prior to data collection. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [23]. The primary outcome of this study was the clinical outcome of SSCs assessed using predefined clinical criteria. The secondary outcome was parental satisfaction, evaluated using a structured questionnaire.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Bezmialem Vakıf University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 08.06.2023-E.110543). All participants were under the age of 16; therefore, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians during the follow-up appointments, at which clinical evaluations were performed and satisfaction questionnaires were administered. The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national ethical guidelines.

Study population and setting

The study was conducted at the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Bezmialem Vakıf University Faculty of Dentistry, Istanbul, Turkey. The study population was identified from pediatric patients who underwent dental treatment under general anesthesia between 2020 and 2022, had at least one SSC (Kids Crown or NuSmile) placed, and had a minimum postoperative follow-up period of 6 months. Only cases with complete and sufficient clinical records for outcome assessment were included. Cases were excluded if patients had chronic systemic diseases or physical or mental conditions that could interfere with reliable clinical evaluation. All eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria were actively invited to the clinic for comprehensive clinical examinations and parental satisfaction surveys. Clinical evaluations and parental questionnaires were completed during a single follow-up visit conducted within a defined one-month assessment period.

This study specifically focused on pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia, as this group represents an important subset of pediatric dentistry characterized by extensive treatment needs and/or difficulties in behavioral management. Performing treatments under general anesthesia eliminated the potential influence of patient cooperation on clinical procedures. This allowed SSCs to be placed under standardized conditions with optimal moisture control and high precision, ensuring that the outcomes primarily reflected the intrinsic performance of the materials rather than behavioral variables. In addition, the inclusion of children treated under general anesthesia enabled all SSCs to be placed in a single session. Consequently, although follow-up durations varied between patients, all SSCs within the same child were exposed to the oral environment for an equal duration at the time of evaluation. Furthermore, considering the risks associated with repeated general anesthesia exposure and the limited opportunities for reintervention, the long-term clinical performance of highly durable and predictable restorative materials is of critical importance.

Sample size and power analysis

The minimum required sample size was determined using an a priori power analysis based on previously published clinical data evaluating gingival outcomes of full-coverage restorations [24]. Due to the absence of direct in vivo comparative studies between different SSC systems, effect size estimation was based on the most comparable available clinical data. Unlike other clinical parameters that yield binary outcomes (presence/absence), the Gingival Index (GI) was specifically selected as the reference parameter because it is a scorable variable. A minimum of 27 participants per group was required to achieve 80% power at a two-sided significance level of 5%. Sample size calculations were performed using G*Power software (version 3.0.10; Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Kiel, Germany).

In accordance with the registered clinical protocol, a minimum total sample size of 54 pediatric patients was therefore established. Ultimately, 65 children fulfilling the eligibility criteria were included in the study, exceeding the minimum required sample size and thereby increasing the robustness and reliability of the comparative analyses.

Clinical evaluation

The clinical procedures were performed between 2020 and 2022 by experienced instructors who are specialists in pediatric dentistry. To ensure consistency, all operators followed a standardized clinical protocol in accordance with the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) guidelines [25] and the standard techniques described in McDonald and Avery’s Pediatric Dentistry [26] for the placement of SSCs under general anesthesia.

All clinical evaluations were performed by a single researcher (B.G.), a pediatric dentistry specialist and an experienced instructor to ensure consistency and minimize variability. To minimize measurement bias, all clinical evaluations were performed under standardized conditions. Examinations were conducted under dental unit lighting using a dental mirror and a curved Explorer. Due to differences in the tooth numbering coding on the crowns used in the SSC systems, the investigator could not be blinded. Radiographic assessments were not routinely performed as part of the follow-up evaluation to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure to the pediatric patients, in accordance with the As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) principle, unless clinical signs or symptoms indicated a specific need for further investigation. Selection bias was reduced by including all eligible patients who attended follow-up within the defined assessment period. However, the observational design and non-randomized allocation may introduce residual confounding.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which indicated non-normal distribution. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, frequency, and percentage. Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for comparisons involving more than two groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, Fisher’s Exact test, or Fisher–Freeman–Halton Exact test, with Continuity (Yates) correction applied when appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were two-sided. No imputation was performed for missing data. No interaction analyses were performed. Follow-up duration varied between participants and was accounted for descriptively; no time-to-event analyses were performed.

Results

A total of 65 pediatric patients meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis (28 females [43.1%] and 37 males [56.9%]), with a total of 246 treated teeth (Fig. 1).

The mean age of the participants was 75.2 ± 19.7 months, with a median age of 69 months. The dmft values varied between 3 and 19 (mean 10.11 ± 3.12; median 10).

All clinical evaluations were performed during a single follow-up visit, with postoperative durations ranging from 6 to 60 months, with a mean of 20.12 ± 13.31 months and a median of 18 months. Clinical outcomes

When the clinical outcomes of the study were evaluated, crown retention was found to be 98.5% in the Kids Crown group and 97.3% in the NuSmile group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p > 0.05).

A statistically significant difference was observed in marginal adaptation, with the Kids Crown group demonstrating a higher rate of optimal crown-tooth adaptation (100%) compared to the NuSmile group (96.3%) (p = 0.038).

No significant differences were found between groups in terms of occlusion, proximal contact, GI or oral hygiene index (p > 0.05). The incidence of clinical complications, including crown perforation, sensitivity, fistula formation, mobility, and opposing tooth wear, was low across both groups, with no statistically significant differences detected (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Discussion

This retrospective observational study compared the clinical performance and parental satisfaction of two commercially available SSC systems—Kids Crown and NuSmile—in pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia. Both systems demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes and strong parental acceptance, with excellent retention rates and satisfactory periodontal and functional outcomes. However, only minor differences in clinical outcomes were observed between the two systems. These findings support the role of SSCs as a reliable full-coverage restorative option for extensively carious primary molars in high-risk pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia [21, 22].

The literature reports high success rates regarding SSC retention. In the study by Mathew et al. [27] a 100% crown retention rate was reported for SSCs, whereas Murali et al. [29] reported a rate of 96.7%. In the present study, the overall SSC group demonstrated a crown retention rate of 98%, which is consistent with the high retention rates reported in the literature. Although there is no study in the literature directly comparing the retention rates of the two SSC systems, in our study the retention rates were found to be 97.3% for NuSmile crowns and 98.5% for the Kids Crown group. The difference observed between the two brands was not statistically significant, indicating that both systems exhibit similarly successful clinical performance in terms of retention. However, this slight variation may be attributed to the significant differences in follow-up durations between the groups.

Previous studies have reported that all crowns demonstrated 100% good marginal integrity [16, 27, 29]. In the present study, the overall rate of good marginal adaptation in all SSCs was 98.3%. When evaluated by brand, Kids Crown showed 100% good marginal adaptation, whereas NuSmile demonstrated a rate of 96.3%. Although this difference was statistically significant, it should be interpreted in light of the fact that marginal adaptation was assessed clinically using visual inspection and explorer examination without routine radiographic confirmation. Because SSC margins are commonly positioned slightly subgingivally, minor marginal discrepancies may not always be detectable through clinical examination alone. In addition, operator technique, the difference in follow-up duration between the two groups, the lack of examiner blinding may also have contributed to the observed outcomes. Despite these limitations, both SSC systems demonstrated very high rates of clinically acceptable marginal adaptation. These findings suggest that both systems provide satisfactory clinical performance in pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia.

Restoring functional contact relationships following restorative treatment is one of the key determinants of clinical success [27]. In this context, occlusion was maintained in 93.8% of SSCs in our study. When evaluated by brand, this rate was 93.3% in the Kids Crown group and 94.4% in the NuSmile group. These high rates indicate that both SSC systems demonstrate successful clinical performance in terms of maintaining occlusal contact. The statistically non-significant difference observed between the two crown systems may be associated with factors such as crown placement technique, appropriate crown selection, and careful occlusal adjustments performed prior to cementation.

In the present study, all SSCs maintained proximal contact with the adjacent tooth at a rate of 94.2%. This rate was found to be 91.8% in the Kids Crown group and 97.2% in the NuSmile group. The difference between the two groups was not statistically significant. This variation may be attributed to factors such as the anatomical form of the crowns, operator experience, contour adjustments made during crown adaptation, and patient-related factors. Overall, both SSC systems demonstrated high success in establishing and maintaining proximal contact.

In our study, crown perforation, surface staining, and antagonist tooth wear were also evaluated. These complications were observed at very low rates, with perforation occurring in only 2 crowns, staining in 1 crown, and antagonist tooth wear in 1 case. No statistically significant difference was found between the two SSC systems for these parameters. These low complication rates suggest that both SSC systems are generally fabricated from materials with high mechanical durability and stable behavior in the oral environment. In addition, adequate surface smoothness and polishability properties may help explain the rare occurrence of staining and surface defects.

The present study also evaluated crowns in terms of sensitivity, swelling, presence of fistula, percussion sensitivity, and mobility. These clinical parameters reflect not only the mechanical performance of the restoration but also the outcome of the pulpal treatment performed. When assessed according to these criteria, a high level of success was observed in all SSCs, and no statistically significant difference was found between the two systems for any clinical parameter. The low complication rates observed may be associated with appropriate case selection, correct clinical application, and good marginal adaptation. The absence of a statistically significant difference between the groups suggests that these clinical outcomes are influenced not only by brand-related characteristics but also by factors such as operator technique, patient selection, pulpal treatment success, and follow-up duration. In particular, sensitivity and percussion tenderness are directly related to the underlying pulpal condition and cannot be explained solely by the restorative material. Similarly, swelling and fistula formation are more commonly associated with endodontic failure or existing infection. However, the long-term maintenance of successful endodontic treatment also requires a restorative material that provides adequate marginal seal and prevents microleakage. Overall, these findings indicate that both SSC systems demonstrate successful and reliable clinical performance with respect to these parameters.

Good to moderate fitting restorations with well-contoured crown margins have been reported to be associated with good oral hygiene, healthy gingiva, and minimal plaque accumulation [30, 31]. However, it has also been suggested that plaque accumulation cannot be completely prevented even around well-adapted crown margins, and that gingivitis observed in teeth restored with SSCs is mainly related to bacterial plaque accumulation rather than mechanical irritation caused by the presence of the crown [31]. In our study, when gingival and oral hygiene assessments were evaluated, the proportion of individuals with a normal GI was found to be 66.4%. This rate was 64.9% in the Kids Crown group and 68.2% in the NuSmile group. In terms of oral hygiene evaluation, the proportion of individuals with no plaque was 66.8%, with 66.4% in the Kids Crown group and 67.3% in the NuSmile group. The absence of a statistically significant difference between the groups for both parameters suggests that SSC systems exhibit similar clinical effects on gingival health and oral hygiene. However, it is well known that gingival condition and plaque control are influenced not only by restorative material but also by daily oral hygiene habits, dietary habits, and parental motivation. In pediatric patients in particular, consumption of sugary and sticky foods, toothbrushing frequency, and parental guidance regarding oral care may play a decisive role in these clinical outcomes.

In our study, SSCs were evaluated in terms of color, shape, size, durability, retention, and overall satisfaction, and in accordance with previous studies in the literature, generally high satisfaction scores were obtained [27, 32]. Consistent with previously reported findings, the lowest satisfaction scores across all categories were observed in the color parameter [32].This finding suggests that although SSCs demonstrate high performance in terms of durability, retention, and overall clinical success, their metallic appearance may be perceived as a disadvantage, particularly by parents with higher aesthetic expectations. Nevertheless, despite lower scores in the color category, overall satisfaction remained high. This may indicate that parents prioritize treatment success over aesthetic concerns. This tendency may be more pronounced in cases involving severely carious teeth and treatments performed under general anesthesia, where long-term durability and clinical success are prioritized over esthetics.

Although there is no direct study in the literature comparing SSC systems in terms of parental satisfaction, no statistically significant difference was found between the Kids Crown and NuSmile Crown groups in our study regarding color, shape, size, durability, retention, and overall satisfaction. In the color category, slightly higher mean scores were observed in the Kids Crown group. This slight difference may be attributed to variations in surface finish and polishing characteristics between the two systems, which could influence parental aesthetic perception. The similarity in overall satisfaction scores between the groups suggests that both SSC systems demonstrate comparable performance in terms of parental perception. This finding indicates that parental satisfaction is more closely associated with treatment success and the absence of complications rather than minor differences in crown design or material properties.

In our study, the relationship between parental satisfaction and demographic variables was examined. The findings revealed that the educational level of the parent and their role in the family (mother/father) did not create a statistically significant difference in satisfaction scores. This indicates that the functional and esthetic success of the restoration is independent of the sociocultural background of the parent. In addition, the fact that university-educated parents had numerically lower color satisfaction scores compared to primary school graduates suggests that esthetic expectations may increase with higher levels of education. In the evaluation according to parental age, parents over 35 years of age were found to have statistically significantly higher satisfaction with the shape, durability, and retention categories of the restorations compared to younger parents. These findings suggest that older parents tend to evaluate the technical success and longevity of the restoration in a more positive manner.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study was conducted in a single university center under general anesthesia, which may limit the generalizability of the results to routine clinical settings or different patient populations. While our findings provide valuable information regarding SSC performance in pediatric patients requiring comprehensive dental treatment under general anesthesia, multicenter studies involving diverse clinical settings and cooperative patients are needed to enhance the external validity of these findings.

The effect size used in the a priori power analysis was derived from a study evaluating the most comparable clinical data available in the literature, owing to the absence of in vivo studies directly comparing different SSC systems. In addition, the GI was selected as the reference parameter for sample size calculation due to its ordinal, score-based nature and its ability to reflect graded changes in periodontal status. However, the use of a GI-based effect size introduces certain methodological limitations, as differences in study design and outcome measures between the reference study and the present investigation may have influenced the accuracy of the sample size estimation.

One of the limitations of this study is the inclusion of a relatively wide age range (2–8 years), which may introduce variability in dietary habits, masticatory forces, and oral hygiene status. This heterogeneity may particularly influence clinical parameters such as oral hygiene and gingival indices. Although oral hygiene status was assessed and found to be comparable between groups, this does not fully exclude the possibility of residual confounding. Patient-level factors, including variability in oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and the number of restorations per patient, could not be fully controlled.

With respect to diagnostic assessment, in accordance with the ALARA principle, radiographic evaluation of the SSCs could not be performed to avoid unnecessary radiation exposure in pediatric patients; therefore, the assessment of the SSCs was based solely on clinical examination findings. This situation may have limited the detection of certain subclinical failures or complications such as secondary caries or periapical pathology that cannot be identified solely through clinical examination. Additionally, although a statistically significant difference in marginal adaptation was observed between the NuSmile and Kids Crown groups, the absence of radiographic evaluation may have limited the precise assessment of subgingivally extending crown margins, thereby restricting the detection of minimal marginal discrepancies. According to the AAPD guidelines [33], radiographs are a crucial adjunct to clinical examination for accurate diagnosis, but they must be used judiciously to minimize radiation exposure in pediatric patients. Furthermore, examiner blinding was not possible due to manufacturer-specific crown coding differences between the two SSC systems, which may represent a methodological limitation that could increase the risk of detection bias in clinically assessed outcomes. However, marginal adaptation was evaluated using a binary clinical approach as “no gap detected with explorer” and “marginal gap detected with explorer,” and efforts were made to minimize subjective interpretation.

Given the retrospective design, intra-patient clustering may have occurred, as multiple teeth from the same individual were included in the analysis, potentially violating the independence assumption. Since this clustering effect was not formally modeled, the precision of the estimated outcomes may have been affected. Although appropriate statistical methods exist to account for such clustering, they could not be applied due to the low frequency of events and the presence of zero-event outcomes, which led to model instability.

In addition, the significant difference in follow-up duration between the study groups may have influenced the assessment of clinical success. Although a minimum postoperative period of 6 months was ensured for all cases, outcomes were assessed at a single time point, and restorations with longer follow-up may have had a higher likelihood of failure detection, potentially biasing the estimation of long-term outcomes. Importantly, follow-up duration was also found to differ significantly between the study groups; this may have affected the comparability of outcomes, as unequal observation periods inherently increase the likelihood of detecting time-dependent complications. The absence of precise event timing further precluded time-to-event analyses.

Moreover, the limited number of adverse clinical outcomes and resulting sparse data structure precluded the use of regression-based approaches, including logistic regression and the estimation of effect sizes such as odds ratios with confidence intervals. Consequently, multivariable adjustment for potential confounders was not feasible. In addition, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied; therefore, isolated statistically significant findings should be interpreted with caution.

The questionnaire used to assess parental satisfaction is widely accepted in the pediatric dentistry literature and has been frequently used in studies comparing full-coverage restorations such as SSC and zirconia crowns. However, this instrument may have limited sensitivity in detecting subtle differences between two SSC systems. In addition, parental satisfaction was assessed as a patient-reported outcome, whereas child self-reported measures were not included.

Conclusions

This study provides comparative clinical evidence on two commercially available ssc systems in pediatric patients treated under general anesthesia. Both Kids Crown and NuSmile demonstrated high clinical success, with crown retention rates of 98.5% and 97.3%, respectively, and high parental satisfaction (mean overall score: 4.7 ± 0.8). A statistically significant difference was observed in the clinically assessed marginal adaptation outcome; however, this finding should be interpreted in light of the fact that the assessment was based solely on clinical examination without routine radiographic confirmation.

These findings support the continued use of SSCs as a durable and effective restorative option for primary molars, particularly in challenging clinical conditions such as treatment under general anesthesia, where long-term predictability is essential.

Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter designs with standardized follow-up intervals to enhance the generalizability of the results. Incorporating both clinical and radiographic evaluations, where ethically appropriate, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of subclinical complications. Additionally, future studies should include validated child self-reported outcome measures alongside parental satisfaction to capture a more holistic view of patient-centered outcomes.
