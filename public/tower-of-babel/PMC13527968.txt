HIV self-testing and its associated factors among nursing and medical university students: A cross-sectional study in EasternUganda
Authors: Winnie Nambi, Rose Chalo Nabirye, Samuel Olowo, Brenda Nabawanuka, Tracy Alexis Kakyo, Munanura Turyasiima, Gloria Nakato, Mary Aleni, Ivan Lume, Prossy Nabachenje, Joshua Epuitai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe uptake of HIV testing services is sub-optimal among young people. HIV self-testing (HIVST) offers opportunities to improve the uptake of HIV testing services among young people. The study was conducted to determine preferences for HIVST, its uptake, and the factors associated with uptake of HIVST among young adults.MethodsThe study was a descriptive cross-sectional design. Data collection was done using self-administered questionnaires. We interviewed 384 university students and collected information about their HIV testing practices and preferences. Logistic regression analysis was used to determine the factors associated with HIVST among young people. The study obtained ethical clearance from a Research and Ethics Committee.ResultsThe median age of the participants was 24 (interquartile range: 22–28). Uptake of HIVST was 55% among participants. A similar proportion (56%) of young people preferred HIVST over facility-based HIV testing in the future. Privacy (46%), confidentiality (7%), and convenience (32%) were the most common reasons given for preference for HIVST. Participants, who preferred health facility-based HIV testing, cited the need for counselling services (21%) and the perceived accuracy and credibility (59%) of results from health facility-based testing. HIVST was significantly associated with increasing age (AOR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.80–0.98), the fourth year of study (AOR = 2.90, 95% CI:1.01–8.31), students of anaesthesia program (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI:0.17–0.95), Muslim religion (AOR = 0. 07, 95% CI:0.01–0.41), consistent use of condoms (AOR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.15–0.88), and having multiple sexual partners (AOR = 3.22, 95% CI:1.49-7.00).ConclusionYoung people preferred HIVST over health facility-based testing because of privacy, confidentiality, and convenience. Addressing concerns about the accuracy of test results from HIVST and the need for counseling services may improve the uptake of HIVST among young people.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13740-6.

Background

Globally, 1.3 million new HIV infections occurred and its prevalence was estimated at 41 million people in 2024 [1]. The burden of HIV disproportionately occurs in Eastern and Southern Africa [1]. In Uganda, HIV prevalence was reported at 1.5 million people in 2024 [2]. Adolescent and young adults from 15 to 24 years have a higher risk of acquiring new HIV infections compared to older age groups [3]. In Uganda, about 37,000 new HIV infections were reported in 2024, of which 45% were among young people between the age of 15–24 years [2]. The Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) has proposed 95-95-95 targets to be attained by 2030 [4]. The 95-95-95 targets strives to ensure that 95% of people living with HIV (PLHIV) are aware of their HIV status, 95% of them are on antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of those on ART have achieved viral load suppression [4]. These UNAIDS targets are projected to avert about 28 million new infections, 21 million AIDS related mortality and ultimately end the HIV/AIDS epidemic by 2030 [4].

In 2024, 87% of PLHIV globally were aware of their status which falls short of the 95% UNAIDS target [5]. This may partly be attributable to suboptimal HIV testing practices which is more common among young people [6]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), only 36% of young adults between 15 and 24 years had tested for HIV [7]. In Eastern and Southern Africa, 29% of adolescent girls and 19% of adolescent boys had tested for HIV in the past year [8]. In Uganda, rates of HIV testing were lowest among young people compared to other age groups [9].

The UNAIDS has proposed countries to adopt fast track responses to accelerate progress towards meeting the 95-95-95 targets [4]. UNAIDS has proposed self-and home-based HIV testing as one of the fast track approaches instead of the provider-initiated and health facility-based HIV testing [4]. HIV self-testing (HIVST) involves individuals testing themselves for HIV at home or in various locations in the community using oral or blood testing kits [5]. HIVST kits are highly sensitive, and therefore, they are primarily used for screening [5]. Therefore, individuals, who test positive, are required to seek further testing, counselling and HIV treatment and care in health facilities [5].

The use of HIVST among young people varies globally [10]. In SSA, HIVST among young people varied from 1 to 100% [10]. HIVST was less than 50% across most settings in SSA [10]. In South Africa, history of ever using HIVST among university students was 17–19% while it was 15% in Uganda [10]. Although uptake of HIVST was low among young people, they reported high acceptability to use HIVST ranging from 49% to 100% in SSA [10]. Similarly, young people prefer HIVST to health facility-based HIV testing [10]. In Nigeria, 69% of young people preferred HIVST compared to 24% who wanted facility-based HIV testing [11]. In Uganda, 93% of university female students were willing to use HIVST [12].

Socio-demographic and sexual behaviours are associated with the use of HIVST [10]. In SSA, young people who were of older age, higher level of education, female gender, and single marital status were associated with increased likelihood to use HIVST [10, 13, 14]. Prior history of HIV testing, having multiple partners, and high-risk sexual behaviours were associated with willingness to use HIVST [12, 14]. HIVST tends to address youth-specific barriers inherent in traditional HIV testing services such as stigma, provider bias, lack of access, concerns for privacy and confidentiality [15, 16]. Young people in Nigeria, Zimbabwe and Malawi preferred HIVST because it fostered respect, privacy, convenience, confidentiality, and comfort [15, 16].

In Uganda, the Ministry of Health recommends HIVST, while testing kits are available in public and private outlets [5]. Although some studies have explored HIVST uptake among university students in Uganda [12, 17], there is limited focus on nursing and medical students. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the preferences for HIVST, uptake of HIVST, and its associated factors among young people in a public University.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

We used a cross-sectional study to determine the HIVST preferences and practices among young people. The study was conducted at Busitema University, Faculty of Health Sciences. The university being a public higher institute of learning attracts a large number of students from different socioeconomic, cultural, and religious backgrounds. The Faculty of Health Sciences is located in Eastern Uganda, where it offers undergraduate and postgraduate programs. The undergraduate programs were nursing, anaesthesia, medicine, and surgery, while postgraduate programs are in the area of public health and master of medicine programs.

Study population

The study population comprised students from a public university. Participants were eligible if they were at the Faculty of Health Science, Busitema University and they were pursuing clinical disciplines including a Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN), Bachelor of Anaesthesia (BNA), and Bachelor of Medicine and Surgery (MBChB). University students who were critically sick were excluded from the study. We used consecutive sampling to select the participants for the study. Participants were recruited from social media platforms, university and residential premises. The sample size, determined using the Kish-Leslie formula, was 384. This represented 85% of the student population in the Faculty of Health Sciences.

Data collection tool

Data was collected between 01st November 2022 to 31st December 2022. We used a self-administered questionnaire for data collection. Participants filled out the questionnaires designed on the Kobo toolbox. The questionnaire had sections for socio-demographics, sexual behaviors, and HIV testing practices. The questionnaire had open-ended questions on the challenges participants faced while seeking HIV testing services, and the reasons for preferring HIVST or provider-based testing in the facilities. The questionnaire was developed based on findings from other studies [11, 18]. The questionnaire was pretested at the Faculty of Engineering and Technology, Busitema University.

Study variables

The dependent variable in the study was HIV self-testing. Participants were asked if they tested for HIV during their first sexual encounter with their last or current partner (Appendix I). Participants who admitted testing for HIV were asked to indicate whether they tested at home or at the health facility. HIV self-testing was considered as those who tested for HIV at home when they had their first sexual encounter with a current or previous sexual partner. Additionally, participants were asked about their preferences for HIV testing (self-testing at home or healthcare provider testing in the healthcare facility settings) if they were to enter into a new relationship in the future.

The independent variables included age, gender, marital status, employment status (employed/unemployed), year of study, program of study, religion, sexual risky behaviors, and HIV testing practices. Risky sexual behaviors were considered as those behaviors that increased the risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections including HIV. These behaviors included number of sexual partners in the last 12 months, age at sex debut, inconsistent use of condoms, non-disclosure of HIV status, broken condoms, and whether they were sexually active. HIV testing practices investigated were ever testing for HIV, the number of times HIV was tested, and HIV testing in the last sexual encounter.

Data analysis

Data was analysed using STATA version 15 software. Categorical variables were analysed using frequencies and percentages, while the median and interquartile range were used to describe continuous variables that were not normally distributed. Logistic regression at the bivariate and multivariate levels was used to determine the factors associated with HIV self-testing among university students. The probability value (p-value) of less than 5% indicated a significant association, while the 95% confidence level (CI) was used to infer the magnitude of the association. P-values which were less than 0.2 at the bivariate level and variables relevant to HIV self-testing were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Factors associated with HIV self-testing were those that were significant in the multivariate logistic regression. We did not perform any model diagnostics during data analysis.

Preferences for HIV testing options

Similar to the high proportion of young people who self-tested for HIV at home, more than half of the participants (56%, n = 213) preferred to test themselves at home if they were to enter into new sexual relationships in the future (Table 3). Privacy and convenience were the most common reasons given for preferring HIV self-testing. Individuals who preferred provider-based HIV testing in health facilities did so because of concerns about the accuracy of the self-test results and the need for pre-test and post-test counseling.

Factors associated with HIV self- testing among medical/nursing students

In the adjusted analysis, age, program of study, year of study, religion, number of sexual partners, and inconsistent use of condoms were significantly associated with self-testing for HIV (Table 4). An increase in age significantly reduced the odds of HIV self-testing by 11%. Students who were pursuing the BNA program were significantly less likely to test for HIV at home compared to students who were pursuing the MBChB program. Consistent use of condoms and the Muslim religion significantly reduced the odds of self-testing for HIV. Students in year four of study compared to those in year five, and having 2 or more partners in the last year increased the odds of HIV self-testing.

Discussion

The study was conducted to determine HIV testing preferences, uptake of HIVST, and its associated factors among young people at a public university. Overall, 55% of the young people had tested themselves at home, which was congruent with their preferences to use HIVST in the future. HIVST was preferred over provider-based HIV testing because of the perceived privacy and convenience for HIVST. These study findings should be interpreted in light of their limitations. This was a cross-sectional study, and as a result, it was difficult to infer causality. Measurement bias could have affected the operationalisation of some study variables including the outcome variable. Our study may be subject to selection bias considering that we used consecutive sampling, while recall bias was possible especially for events in the past. Although anonymity was maintained during data collection, social desirability bias was likely especially given the sensitivity of HIV as a topic. Nevertheless, preferences for HIVST were similar to the expressed actual behaviors to use HIVST which suggests that social desirability bias was minimal in the study. The study provides important implications that may improve HIV testing practices among young people. Ultimately, improved HIV testing may contribute to the attainment of the UNAIDS goals to end the HIV/AIDS epidemic by 2030 [13].

In our study, more than a half (55%) of the participants had used HIVST kits which indicates a high uptake of HIVST in our setting. This was higher than the 19% reported among university students in Central Uganda [12], and the uptake of HIVST in most of the African settings [10]. In Kenya, only 19% of young people were aware of HIVST which explains why there is a low uptake of HIVST in most African settings [14]. Our study noted high uptake of HIV self-testing perhaps because the participants were medical and nursing students who may have had some level of knowledge regarding HIV testing [19].

Despite the low uptake of HIVST in most settings [10], studies have indicated that young people generally prefer HIVST to traditional provider-based testing [14, 19]. Our study findings indicated that 56% of young people preferred HIV self-testing which was consistent with preferences and acceptability to use HIVST in most of the studies [12, 17, 20]. Qualitative studies have equally reported that young people find HIVST services to be more preferable, acceptable, feasible, and appropriate [16, 18]. However, 77% of adolescents and young girls in Kenya preferred provider-aided HIV testing to HIVST [13]. This may perhaps be attributed to the profile of the participants including their level of education, empowerment, and wealth index. Variation in preferences for HIVST highlights that one size may not fit all and that it is critical to consider the socio-demographic profile of young people when promoting HIVST practices.

One of the major findings in our study was that young people valued HIVST because it was perceived to be convenient amidst maintenance of privacy and confidentiality, a finding which was consistent with previous studies [16, 21]. The HIVST is seen to address youth-related barriers that affect the uptake of HIV testing services among young people [16, 18]. Barriers such as provider bias, discrimination, stigma, and negative judgment from older clients in clinic-based HIV testing are seen to be no longer of much concern in HIVST [16]. This is because adolescents and young people can test themselves for HIV in the privacy of their homes without judgment, discrimination, or mistreatment as it may happen in health facilities [16]. Furthermore, self-testing for HIV is seen to eliminate health system bottlenecks such as long waiting times, distant health facilities, limited HIV testing services, and shortage of personnel which health system problems may deter young people from testing for HIV [22]. HIVST may enable young people to have confidential HIV results thereby preserving their dignity and autonomy while accessing HIV testing services [16, 17]. The perceived convenience and privacy from HIVST may therefore increase HIV testing among high-risk populations [23].

Some of the young people in our study preferred provider-based HIV testing over HIVST because it was seen to provide more accurate and reliable test results. Although the accuracy of test results in HIVST is equivalent to that from health facility-based HIV testing [13, 24], previous studies have underscored young peoples’ scepticism regarding the credibility of test results obtained from HIVST [19, 25]. Concerns over accuracy in HIVST were even more pronounced for oral-based HIV test kits than in blood-based HIV test kits [26]. Similar study in Uganda noted that young people had mistrust regarding the perceived accuracy of HIVST [27]. In Uganda, however, the Ministry of Health recommends HIVST to be primarily used as a screening tool [5]. This means that confirmatory tests should be carried out in health facilities for individuals with concerns regarding their test results [5]. Therefore, it is important to emphasise the role of HIVST as a screening tool to address fears and scepticism regarding the accuracy of results from HIVST [22].

Previous studies have highlighted much concern for the limited pre-test and post-test counseling services in HIVST [20, 22]. Our study findings were consistent with studies that cited perceived limited counseling services as a major deterrent for uptake of HIVST [16, 19]. Failure to receive counseling after positive tests result from HIVST has been perceived to be linked to gender-based violence, emotional distress, suicide, and self-harm though evidence to that effect is currently lacking [19, 20, 25]. In Uganda, guidelines emphasise that HIVST does not confirm HIV status but that it serves to screen for HIV [5]. As a result, individuals, who test positive during HIVST, have opportunities for counselling and further care in health facilities [5]. HIVST approaches that include virtual counseling by healthcare providers through the telephone have been suggested to provide immediate counselling for young people before and after the HIVST [26]. Post-test counseling over the telephone could reduce maladaptive behaviors in cases of positive test results from HIVST [26].

The predictors for the uptake of HIVST in our study were consistent with those in the literature [12, 19, 28]. Participants with multiple sex partners were more likely to have HIVST, a finding which was similar to that in Uganda among university students [12]. The increased odds of HIVST among participants with multiple sex partners were plausible because of the need to test each new partners before sexual encounter. Our study was consistent with findings in Nigeria where each unit increase in age significantly reduced the likelihood of using HIVST [28]. The decreased likelihood of HIVST among those who were relatively older maybe likely because of higher sexual activity among younger people than their older counterparts. Similar to our study findings, previous studies have reported religion, program of study, and year of study to be associated with HIVST [12, 19]. In our study, students doing anaesthesia compared to those in the medicine program were less likely to use HIVST, while those in fourth year of study were more likely to use HIVST than those in fifth year.

Conclusion

Young people preferred HIV self-testing over provider-based HIV testing in the health facility. Privacy, confidentiality, and convenience were the most common reasons given for preferring HIVST over clinic-based HIV testing. Young people who preferred clinic-based HIV testing wanted to receive counseling services and the perception that the results in the health facilities were accurate, and reliable. Addressing fears of test accuracy in the HIVST and the lack of counseling can promote increased preference to use HIVST. This has wider implications for the uptake of HIV testing, linkage to care, viral load suppression, and reduced AIDS-related mortality. Individuals who were of increasing age, pursuing BNA program compared to MBChB program and those who were Muslims compared to Christians were significantly less likely to use HIVST. HIVST was significantly more likely among young people with having multiple sex partners, fourth year of study, and consistent condom use.
