Behavioral Changes in Children Are Uncommon 4 Weeks After Procedural Sedation and Analgesia With Intranasal Dexmedetomidine and Nitrous Oxide
Authors: Anna Nikula, Malin Ryd‐Rinder, Stefan Lundeberg, Mitja Lääperi, Maaret Castrén, Lisa Kurland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPainful procedures requiring sedation and analgesia are commonly performed in pediatric emergency departments. However, there is little knowledge about the possible delayed effects of procedural sedation and analgesia on children's behavior. The aim of the current study was to examine children's behavioral changes 4 weeks after procedural sedation and analgesia with intranasal dexmedetomidine and nitrous oxide. Parents of the children who participated in a clinical trial comparing intranasal dexmedetomidine and nitrous oxide for procedural sedation and analgesia during painful procedures were interviewed 4 weeks after procedural sedation and analgesia. The Post Hospitalization Behavior Questionnaire was used for the parental interviews by phone to evaluate changes in children's behavior. Behavioral changes were reported in 13 of 128 (10.2%) children, of which eight experienced negative behavioral changes and five positive changes. Young age was associated with negative behavioral changes; median age was 5.7 years in children with negative behavioral changes and 9.4 years in children with no behavioral changes (p‐value 0.0062). Children with negative behavioral changes were less likely to accept the same method for procedural sedation and analgesia in the future (p‐value 0.049) as were their parents (p‐value 0.012). Parents of children with negative behavioral changes were less satisfied with the general management of procedural sedation and analgesia than parents whose children did not experience negative changes (p‐value 0.024). No association with higher pain scores during the procedure and negative behavioral changes was seen. Behavioral changes are uncommon 4 weeks after procedural sedation and analgesia with intranasal dexmedetomidine or nitrous oxide for painful procedures such as fracture reduction.

Introduction

Approximately one fifth of children require a painful procedure during their emergency department (ED) visit [1]. To ensure the wellbeing of the children during these painful procedures, sedation and analgesia are commonly provided [1, 2, 3]. To understand the possible effects of procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) on children's behavior is important; it may also have an impact on their attitude towards future procedures or healthcare visits. However, the potential delayed effects of PSA on children are little investigated.

Prior studies have shown that behavioral changes are common 1–2 weeks after PSA with intravenous drugs commonly used for PSA (ketamine with or without midazolam and propofol with fentanyl) [4, 5, 6]. As many as approximately one in five children were reported to experience negative behavioral changes within that time period [4, 5, 6]. Separation anxiety, sleep disturbances, and aggression towards authority were those most commonly reported. We studied the effects of broadly available inhaled nitrous oxide (N2O) [2, 7], and intranasal dexmedetomidine, which use for PSA is still limited most likely due to lack of evidence [8]. To our knowledge, there are no previous reports on possible delayed effects of PSA with N2O or intranasal dexmedetomidine. A study investigating children's behavioral changes after general anesthesia showed a significant reduction in behavioral changes from 1–2 to 4 weeks after anesthesia [9]. We, therefore, chose to assess children's behavior 4 weeks after PSA in order to evaluate the possible longer‐term effects of PSA, which to our knowledge has not been studied previously.

The aim of the current study was to examine children's behavioral changes 4 weeks after PSA with intranasal dexmedetomidine or N2O for fracture reduction and to further investigate the association between pain intensity and post‐PSA behavioral changes.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was a planned follow‐up study of the prospective, randomized, open‐label, parallel‐group clinical trial (Eudra CT 2016‐003773‐17) which investigated the non‐inferiority of intranasal dexmedetomidine 2.0 μg/kg to 50% N2O for analgesia and sedation during a painful procedure among children 3–15 years of age [10]. This study was conducted at an urban, tertiary, pediatric ED between August 2017 and October 2020. The follow‐up interview took place 4–4.5 weeks after PSA. This study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board Stockholm, Sweden.

Participants

In total, 148 children, 3–15 years of age, who completed the clinical trial were eligible for this follow‐up study. Parents of all these children were asked to participate in the follow‐up interview. Signed consent was acquired separately for the clinical trial and the current follow‐up study.

Interventions

The highest level of pain during PSA for fracture reduction was assessed with an observational Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability scale (FLACC) [11] by the trial physician (AN, SK). The ED physician performing the reduction scored the feasibility of the procedure on a scale of 1–5 (1 = very easy, 5 = very difficult). Child and parental satisfaction in relation to PSA was evaluated with a questionnaire (Appendix S1) directly after PSA.

The follow‐up interview by phone with the parent present during PSA was scheduled to take place 4 weeks after PSA. The time for the interview was agreed directly after PSA. The trial physician (AN) conducted all interviews. If a parent was not reached at the scheduled time, three more attempts were made over the next 3 days. If a parent was still not reached within that timeframe, the interview was not conducted, hence not included in the study. The interviews were conducted in Swedish.

Post Hospitalization Behavior Questionnaire (PHBQ) [12] (Appendix S2) was used during the interviews to assess behavioral changes in children. PHBQ was primarily developed to identify behavioral changes in children after hospitalization and general anesthesia and has previously been used to investigate behavioral changes after PSA [4, 6]. PHBQ includes 27 items within six subcategories: general anxiety, separation anxiety, eating, aggression, apathy and sleep. Parents were asked to rate their child's behavior in all 27 items as “much less than before,” “less than before,” “same as before,” “more than before,” or “much more than before.” In addition to the predetermined questions in PHBQ, parents were asked whether their child had any memory of PSA. Parents were not informed of the questions before the interview.

Data Analysis

We describe the data using counts, percentages, medians (Md) and interquartile ranges (IQRs). We compared the patients with behavioral changes to patients without behavioral changes. Patients with positive and negative changes were analyzed separately. The comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests and Fisher's tests. p‐values below 0.05 were considered significant. The analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 [13].

Discussion

Few children had negative behavioral changes 4 weeks after PSA with intranasal dexmedetomidine or N2O for fracture reduction. Young age, lower acceptance for the same method of PSA in the future by child or parent, and lower parental satisfaction were associated with negative behavioral changes. Interestingly, there was no association with higher pain levels during the procedure and negative behavioral changes.

Considerably fewer children in the present study had post‐PSA negative behavioral changes than previously reported [4, 5, 6]. The previous studies used intravenous ketamine with or without midazolam and intravenous propofol with fentanyl for PSA [5, 6], hence the results of these studies cannot be easily compared to the results of the present study. To our knowledge, N2O and intranasal dexmedetomidine's effect on children's behavior has not previously been reported. The longer follow‐up period in the present study, 4 weeks in comparison to 1–2 weeks, could explain the smaller number of negative behavioral changes. A reduction in negative behavioral changes from 1 week to 1 month has been shown after general anesthesia [9, 14]. We acknowledge that 4 weeks is still a relatively short period and whether the behavioral changes reported 4 weeks after PSA affect subsequent procedures or future healthcare visits cannot be answered by the current study. Our finding that only a few children experience negative behavioral changes 4 weeks after PSA with intranasal dexmedetomidine and N2O gives further support for the use of these drugs for PSA during painful procedures in children.

We found young age associated with negative behavioral changes. A similar association has been reported after general anesthesia [9], however, previous studies investigating behavioral changes after PSA in the ED did not find any association between young age and negative behavioral changes [4, 5, 6]. Our finding could be explained by the difficulty to prepare young children for the procedure since their cognitive capacity to understand and process information about such a complex event may be limited. Further studies are needed with a focus on young children to find more optimized methods for PSA for this group.

Similar to the previous studies [4, 5, 6], fracture reduction was performed in close proximity in time to the acute trauma, which makes it impossible to conclude whether the negative behavioral changes were a consequence of PSA or the trauma. To our knowledge, there are no studies investigating behavioral changes in children after trauma without a procedure or hospitalization. Hence, to understand whether the behavioral changes are related to PSA or the trauma itself requires further study. Furthermore, there are additional factors, both as part of and beyond the health care situation, which may affect a child's behavior.

Interestingly, positive behavioral changes in children were also reported. This was unexpected as previous studies of PSA in the ED have not reported positive behavioral changes [4, 6]. However, positive changes have been reported to a similar extent as those in the current study after general anesthesia [9]. In general, lower pain scores and higher acceptance for the same method of PSA in the future were seen among children with positive behavioral changes, although there was no statistical difference when compared with children without positive changes.

One of the goals set for PSA according to the American Academy of Pediatrics [15] is amnesia. More than one third of the children did not have any memory of the procedure. However, many parents could not report on their child's memory of the procedure as they had not discussed this specifically with their child. Therefore, no accurate information on amnesia produced by intranasal dexmedetomidine or N2O can be provided.

Limitations

The strengths of this study were the high response rate, interviews conducted by the same person, and that the children whose parents were interviewed were representative of all children enrolled in this follow‐up study. However, these results cannot be generalized to PSA with other drugs than intranasal dexmedetomidine or N2O.

One of the limitations was that we did not interview the children. The reason for this was that the youngest children in this study were 3 years old and to interview such a young child is challenging. Additionally, we assessed that a child's interview should be conducted face to face, which would have required a follow‐up visit. This would have caused an additional burden to the families and may have negatively impacted the participation rate. Furthermore, to our knowledge there is no validated questionnaire for children to evaluate their behavior; PHBQ was designed for parents.

To study children's behavioral changes, a pre‐ and post‐assessment would be preferable. However, we could not obtain pre‐PSA PHBQ scores as children in this study went through procedural sedation and analgesia within a short timeframe after a trauma; therefore, it was not possible for the parents to fill in a questionnaire beforehand without the added stress of the situation.

Parents were not aware of the interview questions before the interview, and this may have affected the number of behavioral changes reported. It is possible, that especially older children did not report some behavioral changes, for example, nightmares to their parents. On the other hand, knowing the questions beforehand could have led to over analyzing and reporting of non‐significant behavioral changes. Unfortunately, one third of the parents could not answer the question whether their child had any memory of the procedure because they had not asked their child this specific question. Therefore, no conclusion about children's recollection about the PSA and the impact of amnesia on behavioral changes could be made.

In conclusion, behavioral changes are uncommon 4 weeks after PSA with intranasal dexmedetomidine or N2O for painful procedures such as fracture reduction. There was no association with higher pain level during PSA and negative behavioral changes.
