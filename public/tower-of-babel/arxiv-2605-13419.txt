Solving the Sylvester equation in Banach modules
Bogdan D. Djordjević

Abstract
For given unital complex Banach algebras 𝒜 1 {\mathcal{A}}_{1} and 𝒜 2 {\mathcal{A}}_{2} , let 𝔐 \mathfrak{M} be a Banach module acting between them. Let a ∈ 𝒜 1 a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1} , b ∈ 𝒜 2 b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2} , and c ∈ 𝔐 c\in\mathfrak{M} be provided such that σ 𝒜 1 ​ ( a ) ∩ σ 𝒜 2 ​ ( b ) ≠ ∅ \sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}(a)\cap\sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}(b)\neq\emptyset . In this paper we completely characterize the consistency of the Sylvester equation a ​ x − x ​ b = c . ax-xb=c. Precisely, we establish verifiable sufficient and necessary solvability conditions, and we provide some formulas for particular solutions x ∈ 𝔐 x\in\mathfrak{M} when the equation is solvable. Moreover, we characterize the uniqueness of the solutions.



MSC 2020 Classification: 46H25; 46H10; 46H30; 47D03; 47A08.

Keyphrases: Sylvester equation; Banach modules; Banach algebras; Operator matrices.

1
Introduction

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
be unital complex Banach algebras. A Banach space
\mathfrak{M}
defines a Banach
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2})-
module, if it is simultaneously a left Banach module for
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
, and a right Banach module for
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, with respect to some (fixed) multiplications
\cdot_{1}:{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\mathfrak{M}\to\mathfrak{M}
, and
\cdot_{2}:\mathfrak{M}\times{\mathcal{A}}_{2}\to\mathfrak{M}
. It is inherently understood that the sub-multiplicativity is preserved within such structures, that is,

\|a_{1}\cdot_{1}c\cdot_{2}a_{2}\|_{\mathfrak{M}}\leq\|a_{1}\|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}\cdot\|c\|_{\mathfrak{M}}\cdot\|a_{2}\|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}},

for any
a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
,
c\in\mathfrak{M}
, and that the associative law holds:

\left(a_{1}\cdot_{1}c\right)\cdot_{2}a_{2}=a_{1}\cdot_{1}\left(c\cdot_{2}a_{2}\right)\in\mathfrak{M}.

It is also assumed that
\mathfrak{M}
is not a trivial module, meaning that, if
c\neq 0_{\mathfrak{M}}
, then
1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}\cdot c\neq 0_{\mathfrak{M}}
and
c\cdot 1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\neq 0_{\mathfrak{M}}
.

With respect to the previous notation, let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
,
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, and
c\in\mathfrak{M}
be given elements. In this paper we study and solve the algebraic Sylvester equation

ax-xb=c

(1)

for the unknown solution(s)
x\in\mathfrak{M}
: we establish both sufficient and necessary solvability conditions, and provide several formulas for particular solutions. In addition, we obtain new necessary and sufficient conditions under which the solution is unique. To the best of our knowledge, these results are original and represent a significant progress compared to the existing findings in the available literature.

Sylvester equations: Known results

The Sylvester equation (
1

The most well-known criterion regarding the existence and uniqueness of a solution lies in spectral properties of
a
and

a
and
b
are bounded linear operators given in the corresponding Banach spaces, provided such that their spectrums are disjoint, then for any given bounded linear operator
c
, there exists a unique bounded linear operator
x
(in the respective operator space), such that (
1
) holds. Moreover, when
a
,
b
, and
c
are matrices (or operators in finite-dimensional vector spaces), then there exists a unique solution
x
to (
1
), if and only if the spectra of
a
and
b
are disjoint. For this reason, in the bounded operator (and matrix) setting, the equation is said to be
regular
whenever the spectrums of
a
and
b
are disjoint, and it is called
singular
otherwise. Regular Sylvester equations and their generalizations in matrices and operators have been extensively studied in the available literature, see e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
28
]
,
[

]
,
[
32
]
, and numerous references therein.

Conversely, singular Sylvester equations lack such results. There are some special cases in which the equation has been solved successfully: for instance, when
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{M}
are finite-dimensional, i.e., when the equation (
1
) is provided in matrices, solvability conditions have been established and the solution set has been described completely, see
[

,

]

These results usually rely on the existence of Riesz points in the respective spectra, or demand some stronger properties, like self-adjointness, Drazin-Koliha invertibility, von Neumann regularity, etc. The difficulty with the singular equation is that the nonempty spectral intersection for

1

1.2
Banach module setting

Throughout this paper we are working with fixed unital complex Banach algebras
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, and a fixed Banach
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2})-
module
\mathfrak{M}
. We denote this convention as
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2},\mathfrak{M})
. For easier notation, we simplify the indexing and write

a_{1}\cdot_{1}c\cdot_{2}a_{2}\equiv a_{1}ca_{2},\quad a_{i}\cdot_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}b_{i}\equiv a_{i}b_{i},\quad c\in\mathfrak{M},\ a_{i},b_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\quad i=1,2.

Similarly, we write
\|\cdot\|
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathfrak{M}}
, or
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}
, etc. The spectrum of
a_{i}
within the respective algebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, will be written as
\sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}}(a_{i})
, or as
\sigma(a_{i})
if there is no chance of confusion. The algebra of bounded linear operators over
\mathfrak{M}
is denoted as
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})
. For a given
L\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})
, the image (range) of the operator
L
is denoted as
{\mathcal{R}}(L)
. Finally, for any
a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
, the set
(a_{i})^{\prime}
denotes the set of elements in
{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
which commute with
a_{i}
. For more on Banach (and Hilbert
C^{*}-
) modules consult
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
25
]
, and numerous references therein.

Accordingly, we assume that
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
,
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, and
c\in\mathfrak{M}
from (
1
) are also provided and fixed, and we focus on inspecting the consistency of (
1
). It is straightforward to see that, even in the Banach module setting
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2},\mathfrak{M})
, if
a
and
b
have disjoint spectrums then there always exists a unique solution
x\in\mathfrak{M}
corresponding to the given
c\in\mathfrak{M}
. Indeed, by introducing the bounded multiplication operators in
\mathfrak{M}

Ax:=ax
and
Bx:=xb
, it follows that
\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(A)\subset\sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}(a)
and
\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(B)\subset\sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}(b)
, ergo

\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)=\emptyset\Rightarrow\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(A)\cap\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(B)=\emptyset.

Moreover, the operators
A
and
B
commute, and can be embedded into the same maximal commutative Banach subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}
of
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})
that contains them. Furthermore, the said commutative algebra
{\mathcal{B}}
preserves their spectrums. Then, due to the Gel’fand transform (see
[
7
]
,
[
26
]
, and
[
29
]
), it follows that

0\notin\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(A)-\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(B)\supset\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}}(A-B)=\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(A-B).

In other words, if
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)=\emptyset
, then for every
c\in\mathfrak{M}
there exists a unique
x\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

(A-B)x=c\Leftrightarrow ax-xb=c,

i.e., the equation (
1
) is
regular
. Keeping the notation consistent with
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
, and
[
30
]
, when
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)\neq\emptyset
, the equation (
1
) is said to be
singular
.

Due to the previous discussion, in this paper we focus only on the case when
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)\neq\emptyset
. As opposed to the previously mentioned papers that treat the singular equation, the approach in this paper is completely different: rather than conducting a rigorous spectral analysis for the elements
a
and
b
, we introduce and study a matrix algebra
\mathcal{M}
which is generated by
a
,
b
, and
c
. This way, the existence of a particular solution, as well as its uniqueness, are closely related to the existence of a nonprimary square root (and its modifications) of a certain matrix which belongs to the said matrix algebra
\mathcal{M}.
Though this idea resembles the Roth’s removal rule, the advantage of our matrix algebra lies in the fact that we keep the “if and only if” statements, rather than the “only if” one. Moreover, Roth’s removal theorem does not make much sense when
c=0
, whereas our results work with an ease in this case (see Section
3
). By employing the said construction, we reduce the problem to a simple (and verifiable) consistency criterion for a two-equation system, see Theorem
4.2
at the end of the paper.

Throughout this paper, we assume that
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2},\mathfrak{M})
is fixed and provided as above. Moreover, it is understood that the elements
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
,
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, and
c\in\mathfrak{M}
are provided as well, such that
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)\neq\emptyset
. By studying this two-sided module setting, we simultaneously enclose the problem in several different types of classical operator algebras and modules: bounded, compact, (semi-)Fredholm, finite-rank, with finite or semi-finite traces, and even some more advanced structures, like the unital quasi *-algebras and non-commutative
\ell_{p}
spaces, see e. g.
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
26
]
.

2
Transferring the problem

In this section we rephrase the initial problem in the following way. By the spectral mapping theorem, the equation (
1
) is mathematically equivalent to

(a+\lambda 1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}})x-x(b+\lambda 1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}})=c,

(2)

for an arbitrary scalar
\lambda
. Thus for any given angle
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
we can choose
\lambda
to be a sufficiently large positive number, such that
\sigma(a+\lambda)\cup\sigma(b+\lambda)
is contained in the sector

\{z:0\neq z,-\alpha,<\arg z<\alpha\}
. For convenience, for a given
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
, we denote that sector as

\Lambda_{\alpha}:=\{z:0\neq z,-\alpha,<\arg z<\alpha\}.

When necessary, we will assume that
\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)\subset\Lambda_{\alpha}
, without the loss of generality. Moreover, by
\mathbb{H}_{r}
we denote the open right complex half-plane with positive real parts:

\mathbb{H}_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z>0\}=\bigcup_{0<\alpha<\pi/2}\Lambda_{\alpha}.

2.1
The regular equation
a\bar{c}+\bar{c}b=c

By transforming the equation (
1
) into (
2
) above, the following analysis immediately follows:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
be such that
\sigma(a)\in\mathbb{H}_{r}
. Then the function
t\mapsto e^{-ta}
,
t\in[0,+\infty)
, vanishes when
t\to+\infty
.

Proof.

By the Cauchy integral formula, it follows that

e^{-ta}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}e^{-tz}(z-a)^{-1}dz

where
\gamma
is a contour surrounding
\sigma(a)
, and lies entirely in
\mathbb{H}_{r}
as well. Then the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality for the dual norms
\|\cdot\|_{1}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
gives

\|e^{-ta}\|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\gamma}\|(z-a)^{-1}\||dz|\cdot\sup_{z\in\gamma}\left|e^{-tz}\right|.

The function

z\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\gamma}\|(z-a)^{-1}\||dz|

is continuous on the compact
\gamma
, therefore, it is uniformly continuous and thus it is bounded by some
C_{\gamma}>0
. On the other hand, due to
\left|e^{z}\right|=e^{\operatorname{Re}z}
, and
\operatorname{Re}z>0
for every
z\in\gamma
, we have

\|e^{-ta}\|\leq C_{\gamma}\cdot\max_{z\in\gamma}e^{-t\operatorname{Re}z},

which vanishes when
t\to+\infty
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
such that
\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)\subset\mathbb{H}_{r}
. Let
c\in\mathfrak{M}
be arbitrary. Then the regular Sylvester equation

a\bar{c}+\bar{c}b=c

(3)

has the unique solution
\bar{c}\in\mathfrak{M}
given as

\bar{c}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ta}ce^{-tb}dt.

(4)

Proof.

Since
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(-b)=\emptyset
, by the previous discussion it follows that the equation (
3
) is indeed regular and with a unique solution
\bar{c}
for a given
c
. On the other hand, it is obvious that the integral in (
4
) is absolutely convergent, therefore it can be treated as a Bochner integral with respect to the standard scalar Lebesgue measure on
[0,+\infty)
, and as such it subjects to the fundamental theorem for the Bochner integral: in other words, we have

\displaystyle a\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ta}ce^{-tb}dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ta}ce^{-tb}dt\ b=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-ta}ce^{-tb}\right)^{\prime}_{t}dt=-\left.\left(e^{-ta}ce^{-tb}\right)\right|_{t=0}^{t\to+\infty}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c-\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-ta}ce^{-tb}=c.

∎

The previous result also follows from the multiplication operator analysis: let
A,B\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})
be the appropriate multiplication operators in
\mathfrak{M}
,
Ay=ay
and
By=yb
. Then
A
and
B
commute in
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})
, and

\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(A)\cup\sigma_{{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})}(B)\subset\sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}(a)\cup\sigma_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}(b)\subset\mathbb{H}_{r}.

It follows by the virtue of the Gel’fand transform for commutative Banach algebras that

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ta}ye^{-tb}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tA}e^{-tB}ydt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t(A+B)}dt\ (y)=(A+B)^{-1}y,\quad y\in\mathfrak{M}.

Therefore, the expression
e^{-t(A+B)}
is well-defined in
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathfrak{M})
, and so is the integral
\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t(A+B)}dt=(A+B)^{-1}
, by Lemma
2.1
.

2.2
The equation
az=wb

In addition to obtaining the consistency conditions for (
1
), it is important to inspect the uniqueness of the solution. Since the problem is linear, the uniqueness of the solution to (
1
) is equivalent to the uniqueness of the solution to the corresponding homogeneous equation (also known as the intertwining problem, see e.g.
[
9
]
and
[
27
]
):

ax=xb.

(5)

In other words, if
ax=xb\Rightarrow x=0_{\mathfrak{M}}
, then there is at most one solution to
ax+xb=c
. Indeed, if there were at least two different solutions, say
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, then
x_{1}-x_{2}
would solve (
5
), which is impossible. Therefore, it is significant to study the equation (
5
) as well. As opposed to the matrix case (see
[
17
]
), it is quite simple to construct a singular homogeneous Sylvester equation with the unique trivial solution:

Example 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
be a noncommutative unital Banach algebra, and let
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
be any nontrivial unital Banach algebra. Let
\mathfrak{M}
be any nontrivial Banach
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2})-
module, i.e.,

1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}\cdot m=0_{\mathfrak{M}}\Rightarrow m=0_{\mathfrak{M}},\quad m\cdot 1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}=0_{\mathfrak{M}}\Rightarrow m=0_{\mathfrak{M}}.

For a nonzero scalar
\lambda
, let
b=\lambda 1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}
, and let
a
be any element in
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
such that the linear pencil
a_{\lambda}:=a-\lambda 1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}
is singular in
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
(say, let it be a right zero divisor), but in the manner that
a_{\lambda}
is not a left zero divisor. Then
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)=\{\lambda\}
and

ax=xb\Leftrightarrow a_{\lambda}x=0\Rightarrow x=0.

\clubsuit

Surprisingly enough, obtaining a particular solution to (
2
) (equivalently, to (
1
)), can be transferred to solving a generalized form of (
5
) via the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
a
and
b
be invertible in
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, respectively. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

There exists a particular solution
x\in\mathfrak{M}
to (
1
).

(b)

There exist
c_{1}
,
c_{2},
and
y\in\mathfrak{M}
, such that
c=c_{1}-c_{2}
and
(c_{1}+y)b=a(c_{2}+y)
.

In that case, a particular solution can be chosen to be

x:=a^{-1}(c_{1}+y)\equiv(c_{2}+y)b^{-1}.

(6)

Proof.

(a)\Rightarrow(b):
If
x
is a particular solution, then
c_{1}:=ax
,
c_{2}:=xb
, and
y:=0
(or any solution to the homogeneous equation (
5
)). Then
c=ax-xb=c_{1}-c_{2}
and

(c_{1}+y)b=axb+yb=axb+ay=a(c_{2}+y).

(b)\Rightarrow(a):
Conversely, by choosing
x
as in (
6
), it directly follows that it is a solution to (
1
).
∎

Remark 2.1
.

From
c=c_{1}-c_{2}
, it follows that
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
determine each other uniquely (if one is known then so is the other). On the other hand, the element
y
is any solution to the homogeneous Sylvester equation (
5
). Given that we seek any particular solution in this lemma, it suffices to choose
y=0
without the loss of generality.

In order to find a particular solution in the form (
6
), one ought to address the generalized intertwining problem (or the generalized homogeneous Sylvester equation)

az=wb

(7)

for the unknown pair
(z,w)\in\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{M}
. The elements
z
and
w
are uniquely determined by one another, via the relation
z=a^{-1}wb
. Therefore we are going to analyze the set

\Omega=\{(z,w):az=wb\}.

(8)

Note that its “diagonal” is precisely the solution set for the homogeneous Sylvester equation (
5
):

\Omega_{0}:=\{(z,z):az=zb\}=\{(z,w)\in\Omega:w-z=0\}.

(9)

Moreover, the set
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}
is nonempty, because for any chosen
z
there exists (a unique)
w
, given as
w=azb^{-1}
, such that (
7
) holds. Within the set
\Omega
, due to Lemma
2.3
, we are interested in the existence of the set

\Omega_{c}=\{(z,w)\in\Omega:w-z=c\}.

(10)

Corollary 2.1
.

With respect to the previous notation, there exists a particular solution to (
1
) if and only if
\Omega_{c}\neq\emptyset
.

3
The matrix algebra
\mathcal{M}

For the fixed
({\mathcal{A}}_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{2},\mathfrak{M})
, we introduce the following algebraic construction: we assume there exists a complex Banach space
\mathfrak{K}
, which is a Banach
({\mathcal{A}}_{2},{\mathcal{A}}_{1})-
module (note the reverse order of
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
). This is always possible: for any given Banach space
\mathfrak{B}
, choose
\mathfrak{K}:=\{0_{\mathfrak{B}}\}
, and define the respective multiplications as
a_{2}\cdot 0_{\mathfrak{B}}=0_{\mathfrak{B}}\cdot a_{1}=0_{\mathfrak{B}}
, for every
a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{i}
,
i=1,2
. In that case, the module
\mathfrak{K}
is said to be
trivial
. This, of course, need not be the only choice for
\mathfrak{K}
: it can be provided in a manner that it is a proper nontrivial Banach space. However, it will soon be shown that the choice of
\mathfrak{K}
does not affect our calculations: we can always use just the zero vector from any
\mathfrak{K}
without the loss of the generality.

Nonetheless, the space
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{K}\times{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
as such exists, and can be embedded into a
2\times 2
matrix algebra
\mathcal{M}
, in the way that

(a,m,k,b)\mapsto\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&m\\
k&b\\
\end{array}\right]\in\mathcal{M},

for every
(a,m,k,b)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{K}\times{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
. Precisely, the algebra
\mathcal{M}
is equipped with the matrix multiplication
\circ
, defined as

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}&m_{1}\\
k_{1}&b_{1}\\
\end{array}\right]\circ\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{2}&m_{2}\\
k_{2}&b_{2}\\
\end{array}\right]:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}a_{2}&a_{1}m_{2}+m_{1}b_{2}\\
k_{1}a_{2}+b_{1}k_{2}&b_{1}b_{2}\\
\end{array}\right],

for
(a_{i},m_{i},k_{i},b_{i})\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\times\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{K}\times{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
,
i=1,2
. The ordered pair
\left(\mathcal{M},\circ\right)
represents a semigroup with the unit
1_{\mathcal{M}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&0_{\mathfrak{M}}\\
0_{\mathfrak{K}}&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]
, i.e., it is a monoid. When both
a
and
b
are invertible in the respective algebras, the inverse of the matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&m\\
k&b\\
\end{array}\right]
is given as
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a^{-1}&-a^{-1}mb^{-1}\\
-b^{-1}ka^{-1}&b^{-1}\\
\end{array}\right]
.The set of invertible matrices in
\mathcal{M}
is denoted as
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
. As before, we will often omit the notation
\circ
when the matrix multiplication is obvious.

3.1
(Non)-primary square roots

Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
be given elements in the respective algebras. Denote by
N_{0}^{\pm}
the matrices

N^{\pm}_{0}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&\pm b\\
\end{array}\right],

(11)

and by
N_{0}^{2}
:

N_{0}^{2}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a^{2}&0\\
0&b^{2}\\
\end{array}\right].

(12)

In that sense, there exists the set of square roots of
N_{0}^{2}
in
\mathcal{M}
:

\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}=\{N\in\mathcal{M}:N^{2}=N_{0}^{2}\}\neq\emptyset,

(13)

where
N^{2}=N\circ N
.
Since the sets
\sqrt{a^{2}}
and
\sqrt{b^{2}}
are nonempty, where

\sqrt{a^{2}}=\{\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}:\xi^{2}=a^{2}\},\ \sqrt{b^{2}}=\{\eta\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}:\eta^{2}=b^{2}\},

we say that
N
is a
primary
square root of
N_{0}^{2}
if
N
is contained in the set

\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi&0\\
0&\eta\\
\end{array}\right]:\ \xi\in\sqrt{a^{2}},\ \eta\in\sqrt{b^{2}}\right\},

and is
nonprimary
otherwise. We define the set:

\left(\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\sim}_{-}:=\left\{N\in\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}:N\textrm{ is similar to }N_{0}^{-}\right\},

(14)

with “similarity” being understood in the sense that there exists an invertible matrix
U\in\mathcal{M}^{-1}
such that

N=U\circ N_{0}^{-}\circ U^{-1}
. Since it contains
N^{-}_{0}
, the set
\left(\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\sim}_{-}
is nonempty.

3.2
Coupled homogeneous equations

For given elements
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
and
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, assume the matrices
N_{0}^{\pm}
and
N_{0}^{2}
are defined as (
11
) and (
12
), respectively. Since these block-diagonal matrices are symmetric with respect to the entities
a
and
b
, it is convenient to, in addition to the equation (
5
), consider the adjoint equation in
\mathfrak{K}
:

by=ya

(15)

for the unknown
y\in\mathfrak{K}
. The equation (
15
) is solvable for
y=0_{\mathfrak{K}}
, regardless of whether
\mathfrak{K}
is trivial or not. Therefore, we are going to study the coupled system of the homogeneous equations

\begin{cases}ax=xb,\\
by=yb\end{cases}

(16)

and we are going to inspect the existence of the solution
(x,y)\neq(0_{\mathfrak{M}},0_{\mathfrak{K}})
. As opposed to the matrix case (see e.g.
[
17
]
), it is quite possible to have a system (
16
), in which one of the equations has only the trivial solution, while the other one allows nontrivial solutions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
a
and
b
be invertible in their respective algebras, such that
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)\neq\emptyset
, and
\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)\subset\mathbb{H}_{r}
. Then:

(a)

Every solution
x
to (
5
) (resp. every solution
y
to (
15
)) defines a similarity matrix
U=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{1}&u_{2}\\
u_{3}&u_{4}\\
\end{array}\right]
, such that
u_{2}
solves (
5
),
u_{3}
solves (
15
), and
U^{-1}N^{-}_{0}U\in\left(\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\sim}_{-}
. Moreover, if
(x)\neq 0_{\mathfrak{M}}
, (resp. if
y\neq 0_{\mathfrak{K}}
), then the said similarity matrix
U
can be chosen such that
U^{-1}N^{-}_{0}U\in\left(\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\sim}_{-}\setminus\{N_{0}^{-}\}
.

(b)

Let
N\in\left(\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\sim}_{-}
, and let
U=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{1}&u_{2}\\
u_{3}&u_{4}\\
\end{array}\right]
be an invertible matrix such that
UNU^{-1}=N^{-}_{0}
. Then
u_{2}
solves (
5
) while
u_{3}
solves (
15
).

Proof.

(a)

Let
x
be a given solution to the respective equation
ax=xb
. Denote by
N_{x}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&x\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]
. Then there exists a unique
\bar{x}
such that
a\bar{x}+\bar{x}b=x
, given via (
4
). This implies that

a\bar{x}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ta}axe^{-tb}dt=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ta}xbe^{-tb}dt=\bar{x}b.

On the other hand, Roth’s removal rule implies that

N_{x}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-\bar{x}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&\bar{x}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right],

and

\left(N_{x}\right)^{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&x\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&x\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a^{2}&ax-xb\\
0&b^{2}\\
\end{array}\right]=N_{0}^{2}.

Therefore
N_{x}\in\left(\sqrt{N_{0}^{2}}\right)^{\sim}_{-}
, and
\bar{x}
solves the equation (
5
) while
0_{\mathfrak{K}}
is a solution to (
15
). Specially, if
x\neq 0
, then
\bar{x}\neq 0
and
N_{x}\neq N_{0}^{-}
. Similarly, by choosing

\ {}_{y}N:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
y&-b\\
\end{array}\right]

the same method applies and gives the similarity matrices
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&0\\
\pm\bar{y}&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]
, where
b\bar{y}+\bar{y}a=y
and
\bar{y}
is determined via (
4
), ergo,
b\bar{y}=\bar{y}a
.

(b)

Conversely, let
N
be a square root of
N_{0}^{2}
which is similar to
N^{-}_{0}
. Then there exists an invertible matrix
U=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}u_{1}&u_{2}\\
u_{3}&u_{4}\\
\end{array}\right]
, such that
N=U^{-1}N^{-}_{0}U
. This gives

N_{0}^{2}=N^{2}=U^{-1}(N^{-}_{0})^{2}U\Leftrightarrow UN_{0}^{2}=N_{0}^{2}U.

The latter implies that

u_{2}b^{2}=a^{2}u_{2}
and
b^{2}u_{3}=u_{3}a^{2}
. Let
au_{2}=u_{2}b+v
, for some
v\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then
a^{2}u_{2}=au_{2}b+av
, and similarly, from
u_{2}b=au_{2}-v
it follows that
u_{2}b^{2}=(au_{2}-v)b
. Equating the two gives

a^{2}u_{2}-u_{2}b^{2}=au_{2}b+av-au_{2}b+vb=av+vb=0,

which implies that
v=0
, since
\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)\subset\mathbb{H}_{r}
. Analogously, it holds that
u_{3}a=bu_{3}
.

∎

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
a
and
b
be such that
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)\neq\emptyset
, and
\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)\subset\mathbb{H}_{r}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

There exists a pair
(x,y)\in\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{K}\setminus\{(0_{\mathfrak{M}},0_{\mathfrak{K}})\}
which solves the system (
16
).

(b)

There exists a nonprimary square root for
N_{0}^{2}
which is similar to
N_{0}^{-}
.

Proof.

(a)\Rightarrow(b):
If
x
is a nonzero solution to (
5
), then the matrix
N_{x}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&x\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]
from the previous Lemma
3.1
provides a nonprimary square root for
N_{0}^{2}
. If
x=0
and
y
is a nonzero solution to (
15
), then
\ {}_{y}N=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
y&-b\\
\end{array}\right]
from the same Lemma provides a nonprimary square root for
N_{0}^{2}
.

(b)\Rightarrow(a):
Conversely, let
N
be a nonprimary square root of
N_{0}^{2}
, which is similar to
N_{0}^{-}
. Then there exists an invertible matrix
U
such that
N=UN_{0}^{-}U^{-1}
. If

and
u_{21}=0
, then

would be block-diagonal, i.e., it would be a primary square root of

, which is not the case. Therefore at least one entry out of

is not zero. Then Lemma
3.1

(b)
applies and the proof is complete.
∎

Example 3.1
.

Let
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be a given nonzero function, and let
\widehat{h}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be its Fourier transform. Denote by
C_{h}
the convolution operator
C_{h}(f):=f*h
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and by
\mu_{\widehat{h}}
the multiplication operator
\mu_{\widehat{h}}(f):=\widehat{h}f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is clear that the two bounded operators
\mu_{\widehat{h}}
and
C_{h}
are unitarily equivalent in
{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
by the virtue of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
:

\left(\mu_{\widehat{h}}\ \mathcal{F}\right)(f)=\mathcal{F}\left(C_{h}(f)\right)=\left(\mathcal{F}\ C_{h}\right)(f),\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Therefore by Theorem
3.2
the operator matrix
N_{0}^{2}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu^{2}_{\widehat{h}}&0\\
0&C^{2}_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]
has a nonprimary square root which is similar to
N_{0}^{-}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&0\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]
. Indeed, the matrix

N_{\mathcal{F}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&\mathcal{F}\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]

is a nonprimary square root for
N_{0}^{2}
, similar to the matrix
N_{0}^{-}
by the means of the similarity matrix

U_{\pm}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{id}&\pm\overline{\mathcal{F}}\\
0&\operatorname{id}\\
\end{array}\right],

where

\overline{\mathcal{F}}:=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t\mu_{\widehat{h}}}\mathcal{F}e^{-tC_{h}}dt,

and
\operatorname{id}
is the identity operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, that is, the unit in
{\mathcal{B}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.

There can exist some other nonprimary square roots of
N_{0}^{2}
, depending on the choice for
h
: suppose that the function
h
is chosen in the manner that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\sin(\xi t)h(\xi)d\xi=0,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

For instance, it suffices to let
h
be an even function. Then we have

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(h)(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{it\xi}h(\xi)d\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-it\xi}h(\xi)d\xi=\mathcal{F}(h)(t)=\widehat{h}(t),

which implies

\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(h*f\right)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(h)\cdot\mathcal{F}^{-1}(f)=\widehat{h}\cdot\mathcal{F}^{-1}(f),

i.e.

\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}-\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\right)(f)=0,\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Since
h\neq 0
it follows that
\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}\neq-\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}
, and

\displaystyle\mu_{\widehat{h}}^{2}\mathcal{F}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}^{2}=\mu_{\widehat{h}}^{2}\mathcal{F}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}^{2}-\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}+\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{\widehat{h}}(\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h})+(\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h})C_{h}=0.

Denote by

N_{\left(\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}+\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}\right)}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}+\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right].

We have

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}+\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}+\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}^{2}&\mu_{\widehat{h}}^{2}\mathcal{F}^{-1}-\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}^{2}\\
0&C_{h}^{2}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}^{2}&0\\
0&C_{h}^{2}\\
\end{array}\right],

ergo
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}+\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]
is a nonprimary square root of
N_{0}^{2}
. It also holds that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{id}&-\mathcal{F}^{-1}\\
0&\operatorname{id}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&0\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{id}&\mathcal{F}^{-1}\\
0&\operatorname{id}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}+\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}\\
0&-C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right],

thus concluding that
N_{\left(\mu_{\widehat{h}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}+\mathcal{F}^{-1}C_{h}\right)}
and
N_{0}^{-}
are indeed similar.
Also note that

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&0\\
0&C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{id}&\mathcal{F}\\
0&\operatorname{id}\\
\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{id}&\mathcal{F}\\
0&\operatorname{id}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&0\\
0&C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&0\\
\end{array}\right],

so
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{id}&\mathcal{F}\\
0&\operatorname{id}\\
\end{array}\right]
commutes with
N_{0}^{+}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\widehat{h}}&0\\
0&C_{h}\\
\end{array}\right]
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let

be a complex unital Banach algebra. Then the equation

Proof.

[
23

, while

On the other hand, due to the claim

\xi\in\mathfrak{M}
which solves

(A^{2}+AB+B^{2})(\xi)=a^{2}\bar{c}+a\bar{c}b+a^{3}\bar{c}b^{-1}.

By a direct verification we see that
\xi=a\bar{c}b^{-1}
is a good (and hence the only) candidate for the solution, ergo

a^{3}u-ub^{3}=a^{2}\bar{c}+a\bar{c}b+a^{3}\bar{c}b^{-1}\Leftrightarrow au-ub=a\bar{c}b^{-1}.

By Lemma
4.2
it follows that all conditions enlisted in (
42
) are true, hence, the pair
(u,v)
indeed solves all equations (
38
)–(
41
).
∎

4.1
The parameter
v-u
: a quadratic matrix equation

Within this subsection we obtain a condition for the difference
q:=v-u
, which will be consistent with the equations (
38
)–(
41
). Though one might be tempted to seek an explicit form for the missing parameter
q
, it should be noted that the system

\begin{cases}u+v=a^{-1}cb^{-1};\\
v-u=q\end{cases}

is always solvable for
(u,v)
and with a pairwise unique solution, which would imply that (
1
) is always solvable with a unique solution (possibly provided by (
25
)). Since this is not the case, the premise that
q
can be explicitly written down needs to be discarded. Instead, we do the next best thign as demonstrated below.

By premise, the elements
a
and
b
are invertible in the respective algebras, with their spectrums contained in
\mathbb{H}_{r}
. Thus the sets
\sqrt{a}
and
\sqrt{b}
are non-empy, and every
\xi\in\sqrt{a}
and every
\eta\in\sqrt{b}
is invertible as well, where again

\sqrt{a}=\{\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}:\xi^{2}=a\},\quad\sqrt{b}=\{\eta\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}:\eta^{2}=b\}.

Then the element
-b
also has square roots, where the setwise equality holds:

\sqrt{-b}=i\sqrt{b}.

Denote by

N_{1}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right],\quad N_{2}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}-r\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right].

Since
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(-b)=\emptyset
, there exist unique
e_{1}
and
e_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

ae_{1}+e_{1}b=-\bar{c},\quad ae_{2}+e_{2}b=-\bar{c}-r.

Then by Roth’s removal theorem:

N_{i}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-e_{i}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&e_{i}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right],\quad i=1,2,

which implies that the matrices
N_{i}
have square roots in
\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{0}
: indeed, one can take

N_{i}^{1/2}(k_{1},k_{2}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-e_{i}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}(-1)^{k_{1}}\sqrt{a}&0\\
0&(-1)^{k_{2}}i\sqrt{b}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&e_{i}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right],

where
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are integers independent from one another. This demonstrates that the sets

\sqrt{N_{i}}:=\{\Sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{0}:\Sigma_{i}\circ\Sigma_{i}=N_{i}\},\quad i=1,2,

are non-empty and comprise out of invertible elements in
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{-1}
as well. Ergo, the
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{-1}-
quadratic equation

YN_{1}Y=N_{2}

(47)

is solvable in
\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{0}
: by employing invertibility, (
47
) is mathematically equivalent to

N_{1}^{1/2}YN_{1}^{1/2}N_{1}^{1/2}YN_{1}^{1/2}=N_{1}^{1/2}N_{2}N_{1}^{1/2},\quad N_{1}^{1/2}\in\sqrt{N_{1}}-\textrm{arbitrary},

(48)

and the latter reads

\left(N_{1}^{1/2}YN_{1}^{1/2}\right)^{2}=N_{1}^{1/2}N_{2}N_{1}^{-1/2},

(49)

which is solved for any
Y
of the form

Y=N_{1}^{-1/2}ZN_{1}^{-1/2},

(50)

where
Z
is an arbitrary square root of the expression
N_{1}^{1/2}N_{2}N^{1/2}
, i.e.,

YN_{1}Y=N_{2}\Leftrightarrow Y=N_{1}^{-1/2}ZN_{1}^{-1/2},\quad Z\in\sqrt{N_{1}^{1/2}N_{2}N_{1}^{1/2}},\ N_{1}^{1/2}\in\sqrt{N_{1}}.

(51)

With this observation in mind, we proceed with the main result of this paper. For the sake of completeness, we formulate it in its full form.

Theorem 4.2

(Solvability and particular solution:
u
and
v
version)
.

Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
,
b\in{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
, be given in the manner that
\sigma(a)\cup\sigma(b)\subset\Lambda_{\pi/4}
, and that
\sigma(a)\cap\sigma(b)\neq\emptyset
. Let
c\in\mathfrak{M}
be arbitrary, and denote by
\bar{c}
the unique solution to the equation
a\bar{c}+\bar{c}b=c
. Moreover, let
r:=a^{-1}\bar{c}b+a\bar{c}b^{-1}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

The equation (
1
) is solvable, i.e., there exists an
x\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

ax-xb=c.

(b)

There exist elements
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{M}
which solve any two (all) of the following equations:

av+ub=\bar{c}.

(52)

au+vb=\bar{c}+a^{-1}\bar{c}b+a\bar{c}b^{-1}.

(53)

u+v=a^{-1}cb^{-1}.

(54)

a^{3}v+a^{2}vb+ub^{3}+aub^{2}=0.

(55)

(c)

There exist elements
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{M}
which solve any one of the equations enlisted in (
52
)–(
55
), while their difference
q:=v-u
solves:

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&q\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&q\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}-r\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right].

(56)

If the equation (
1
) is solvable, then with respect to the mentioned parameters
u
and
v
, the expressions

x_{v}:=-(a^{2}vb^{-1}+av);\quad x_{u}:=a^{-1}ub^{2}+ub

(57)

define one and the same particular solution to (
1
).

Proof.

(a)\Longleftrightarrow(b)
: This follows from Corollary
3.2
, Lemma
4.1
, and Theorem
4.1
.

(a)\Rightarrow(c):
By Corollary
3.2
and Theorem
4.1
, the equation (
1
) is solvable if and only if any two (all) the conditions enlisted in (
52
)–(
55
) are true. Then, from (
52
) and (
53
) it follows that

N_{0}^{-}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-u\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&v\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]

while simultaneously

N_{0}^{-}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-v\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}-r\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&u\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right].

Equating the two gives (
56
).

(c)\Rightarrow(a):
Assume that (
56
) is satisfied. Then as before we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-u\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&v\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]=\\
=&\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&-v\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}-r\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&u\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right].\end{aligned}

(58)

If (
52
) is true, then the left-hand side of the latter is equal to
N_{0}^{-}
, implying that the right-hand side is also equal to
N_{0}^{-}
. However, this concludes that (
53
) is also true, and by Lemma
4.1
, the equation (
1
) is solvable. The same reasoning holds if one assumes that (
53
) is true: it follows from (
58
) that (
52
) must also be true.

Now assume that (
54
) is true. Then
u=a^{-1}cb^{-1}-v
, and substituting this into
v-u=q
implies that
q=2v-a^{-1}cb^{-1}
. Then from (
56
) we have

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}-r\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&2v-a^{-1}cb^{-1}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&2v-a^{-1}cb^{-1}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-\bar{c}-2vb+a^{-1}c\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&2v-a^{-1}cb^{-1}\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&2av-cb^{-1}-\bar{c}-2vb+a^{-1}c\\
0&-b\\
\end{array}\right],

and this equality is true if and only if

2av-2vb=cb^{-1}-a^{-1}c-r=cb^{-1}-a^{-1}c-a^{-1}\bar{c}b-a\bar{c}b^{-1}.

Writting
c
as
a\bar{c}+\bar{c}b
gives

2av-2vb=a\bar{c}b^{-1}+\bar{c}-\bar{c}-a^{-1}\bar{c}b-a^{-1}\bar{c}b-a\bar{c}b^{-1}=-2a^{-1}\bar{c}b,

i.e., (
44
) is true. By Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.1
, this is equivalent to the consistency of (
1
).

Finally, assume that (
55
) holds. Then we have

0=a(a^{2}v+ub^{2})+(a^{2}v+ub^{2})b\Leftrightarrow a^{2}v+ub^{2}=0\Leftrightarrow a^{2}v=-ub^{2}.

On the other hand, since
v=q+u
, where
q
satisfies (
56
), it follows that

a^{2}q+a^{2}u=-ub^{2}\Leftrightarrow a^{2}u+ub^{2}=-a^{2}q.

Now we have

a^{2}(u+v)+(u+v)b^{2}=2a^{2}u+a^{2}q+2ub^{2}+qb^{2}=a^{2}u+ub^{2}+qb^{2}=qb^{2}-a^{2}q.

Finally, from (
56
) it follows that

-\bar{c}-r=-\bar{c}-qb+aq\Leftrightarrow qb-aq=r\Leftrightarrow b^{2}q-a^{2}q=ar+rb=a^{-1}cb+acb^{-1}

where we used
b^{2}q-a^{2}q=(A+B)(B-A)(q)
in terms of the multiplication operators
Aq=aq
and
Bq=qb
. Therefore, equating the two expressions for
qb^{2}-aq^{2}
gives

a^{-1}cb+acb^{-1}=a^{2}(u+v)+(u+v)b^{2}.

The latter is a regular Sylvester equation, and a direct verification shows that
a^{-1}cb^{-1}
solves the equation, implying that (
54
) is true, and the proof is complete.

The expressions (
57
) follow immediately from (
25
) in Theorem
3.3
.
∎

Due to (
47
)–(
51
), the equation (
56
) is indeed solvable for some
Y\in\mathcal{M}_{0}^{-1}
, provided via (
50
). Therefore, if among all such solution matrices
Y
there are some of the form

Y_{q}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}&q\\
0&1_{{\mathcal{A}}_{2}}\\
\end{array}\right]

then (
1
) is solvable, and one has a regular system
u+v=a^{-1}c^{-1}
,
v-u=q
, which defines a particular solution via (
57
). Depending on how many different matrices
Y_{q}
there are, one has different choices for a particular solution to (
1
).
