Real spectral shift functions for pairs of contractions and pairs of dissipative operators Thanks: The research on § 3-5 is supported by Russian Science Foundation [grant number 23-11-00153].
M.M. Malamud, H. Neidhardt and V.V. Peller

Abstract
. Recently the authors solved a long-standing problem and showed that for an arbitrary pair of contractions on Hilbert space with trace class difference has an integrable spectral shift function on the unit circle 𝕋 {\mathbb{T}} and an analogue of the Lifshits–Krein trace formula holds. It is also known that it may happen that there is no real-values integrable spectral shift function. In this paper we find conditions under which a pair of contractions with trace class difference has a real-valued integrable spectral shift function. We also consider a similar problem for pairs of dissipative operators. Finally, we find an application of the results in question to dissipative Schrödinger operators.



to the blessed memory of Heinz Langer

1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to find conditions, under which a pair of contractions with trace class difference possesses a real-valued integrable spectral shift function. We also study the problem to find conditions for the existence of a real-valued spectral shift function for a pair of dissipative operators with trace class difference.

It was physicist I.M. Lifshits who introduced in
[
Li
]
the notion of spectral shift function for pairs of self-adjoint operators with trace class difference and offered a trace formula. In that paper he associated with a pair of self-adjoint operators
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
, whose difference is of trace class
1
1

1

We refer the reader to the book
[
GK
]
for an introduction to the trace class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
and Schatten–von Neumann classes
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.
, a real-valued integrable function

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{A_{0},A_{1}}
called the
spectral shift function for the pair
\{A_{0},A_{1}\}
and offered the trace formula

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}\big(f(A_{1})-f(A_{0})\big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(t)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{A_{0},A_{1}}(t)\,{\rm d}t

(1.1)

for sufficiently nice functions
f
on the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
. It turned out, however, that mathematically the reasonings in
[
Li
]
were not mathematically rigorous.

Later M.G. Krein in
[
Kr1
]
offered a mathematically rigorous approach and considered the most general situation. He proved that for an arbitrary pair of self-adjoint operators
\{A_{0},A_{1}\}
with trace class difference that exists a
unique
function
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{A_{0},A_{1}}
in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
such that trace formula (
1.1
)
holds for sufficiently nice functions
f
. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
must be
real-valued
.
We are going to call formula (
1.1

Lifshits–Krein trace formula
.

In the same paper
[

Krein showed that

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{A_{1},A_{0}}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\operatorname{Im}(\log(\Delta_{A_{1}/A_{0}}(t+{\rm i}y)))\quad\mbox{for a.e.}\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where the
perturbation determinant

\Delta_{A_{1}/A_{0}}
is defined by

\Delta_{A_{1}/A_{0}}(\zeta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\det(I+(A_{1}-A_{0})(A_{0}-\zeta I)^{-1}),\quad\zeta\not\in{\mathbb{R}},

(see also
[
Ya
]
, Ch. 8 and
[
Sch
]
, Ch. 9; we also mentioned here the surveys
[

]
and
[

]
on spectral shift function).

Later Krein extended in
[
Kr3
]
formula (
1.1
) to the class
\mathcal{W}_{1}({\mathbb{R}})
of functions whose derivative is the Fourier transform of a complex Borel measure.

Krein also observed in
[
Kr1
]
that the right-hand side of (
1.1
) makes sense for arbitrary Lipschitz functions
f
and posed the problem to describe the class of functions, for which formula (
1.1
) holds for all pairs of self-adjoint operators with difference of trace class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
.

However, Farforovskaya proved in
[
F
]
that trace formula (
1.1
) cannot be generalized to the class of all Lipschitz functions. Indeed, it was shown in
[
F

that there exist a Lipschitz function

on
{\mathbb{R}}
and self-adjoint operators
A_{1}
and

such that
A_{1}-A_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
, but
f(A_{1})-f(A_{0})\not\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
.

Later it was shown in
[

]
and
[

that (

) holds for functions

in the (homogeneous) Besov space
B_{\infty,1}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
. It was also shown in
[
Pe1
]
and
[
Pe2

that if trace formula (

locally belongs to the Besov space
B_{1,1}^{1}
.

Krein’s problem was completely resolved in
[
Pe3
]
where it was shown that trace formula (

(A_{0},A_{1})
of not necessarily bounded self-adjoint operators with trace class difference if and only if

is an
operator Lipschitz function

In

An analog of the result of
[

]
for unitary operators was obtained in

. Namely, it was shown in

, for which formula (

\{U_{1},U_{0}\}
of unitary operators with
U_{1}-U_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
coincides with the class
{\rm OL}_{\mathbb{T}}
of operator Lipschitz functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

The problem to find analogs of the above trace formulae for functions of contractions and maximal dissipative operators has been under consideration since long ago.

Recall that an operator
{\mathbb{T}}
on Hilbert space is called a
contraction
if
\|T\|\leq 1
.
A densely defined operator
L
is called
dissipative
if
\operatorname{Im}(Lx,x)\geq 0
for an arbitrary vector
x
in the domain
{\rm dom}(L)
of the operator
L
. A dissipative operator
L
is called maximal dissipative if it has no proper dissipative extension.

Recall that the Sz.-Nagy–Foiaş functional calculus (see
[
SNF
]
)
associates with each function
f
in the
disk-algebra

{\rm C}_{\rm A}
(i.e., the space of functions analytic in the disk
{\mathbb{D}}
and continuous in its closure) for a contraction
T
on Hilbert space establishes the linear and multiplicative functional calculus

f\mapsto f(T),\quad f\in{\rm C}_{\rm A},

that satisfies the von Neumann inequality

\|f(T)\|\leq\|f\|_{{\rm C}_{\rm A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\max\{|f(\zeta)|:~|\zeta|\leq 1\},\quad f\in{\rm C}_{\rm A}.

There were many attempts to generalize the Lifshits–Krein trace formula to the case of functions of contractions. In other words, the problem is for a pair of contractions
(T_{0},T_{1})
with trace class difference, to establish the existence of an integrable function
\boldsymbol{\xi}

on
{\mathbb{T}}
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}\big(f(T_{1})-f(T_{0})\big)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(\zeta)\boldsymbol{\xi}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta

(1.3)

for sufficiently nice functions
f
. Another related problem was to describe the maximal class of functions

f
, for which trace formula (
1.3
) holds for arbitrary pairs
(T_{0},T_{1})
of contractions with trace class difference.

Similar problems for pairs of maximal dissipative operators also remained open for a long time.

The first result obtained for nonself-ajoint and nonunitary operators was obtained by Heinz Langer
[
La
]
who considered the case of contractions whose spectra are subsets of the open unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
that includes the case of strict contractions
and proved in this case the existence of an integrable spectral shift function. Actually, he considered in
[
La
]
even a more general situation that involves operators that are not necessarily contractions.

We would also like to mention partial results by Rybkin
[
R1
,
R2
,
R3
,
R4
]
,
Adamyan and Neidhardt
[
AN
]
and M.G. Krein
[
Kr4
]
. Note here that in his papers
[
R2
,
R3
,
R4
]
Rybkin under additional assumptions established the existence of
a complex-valued spectral shift function that is A-integrable but not necessarily
Lebesgue integrable.

We also mention here that in
[
AN
]
the existence of a real-valued integrable spectral shift function
for a pair of contractions
(T_{0},T_{1})
was established under the stronger assumption than
T_{1}-T_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k}(T_{1}-T_{0})\log\big(1+(s_{k}(T_{1}-T_{0}))^{-1}\big)<\infty

(1.4)

(it is assumed that the function
x\mapsto x\log(1+x^{-1})
takes value 0 for
x=0)
.
It follows easily from our Theorem
2.5
in § 2 that condition (
1.4
) is not necessary for the existence of a real-valued integrable spectral shift function.

The longstanding problem to obtain an analogue of the Lifshits–Krein trace formula for functions of contractions was solved completely in
[
MNP1
]
(see the earlier paper
[
MN2
]
, in which such a trace formula was obtained under an additional assumption) and in
[
MNP2
]
by using completely different approaches.
In
[
MNP1
]
Krein’s approach based on perturbation determinants was used while the paper
[
MNP2
]

develops an idea of Birman and Solomyak
[
BS1
]
that uses double operator integrals.

Note also that in
[
BS1
]
the authors did not obtain the existence of an integrable spectral shift function for pairs of self-adjoint operators with trace class difference. They rather established the existence of a spectral shift measure and their method did not give the absolute continuity of that measure. The crucial point in
[
MNP2
]
to obtain the absolute continuity of the spectral shift measure was an application of the Sz.-Nagy–Foiaş theorem that says that the minimal unitary dilation of a completely nonunitary contraction has absolutely continuous spectral measure.

Note that unlike the case of pairs of self-adjoint operators with trace class difference a spectral shift function for pairs of contractions with trace class difference is never unique. If
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a spectral shift function for a pair of contractions with trace class difference, then all spectral shift functions for the same pair admits parametrization

\{\boldsymbol{\xi}+h\}
, where
h
ranges over the Hardy class
H^{1}
.

A question naturally arises of whether it is possible to select a real-valued spectral shift function. In general the answer is negative: there exist pairs
(T_{0},T_{1})
of contractions with trace class difference, for which
there is no real-valued integrable spectral shift function, see
[
MNP1
]
. However, as shown in
[
MN2
]
, in the case of a finite rank perturbation a real-valued integrable function always exists.

On the other hand, it was established in
[
MNP2
]
that for an arbitrary pair
\{T_{0},T_{1}\}
of contractions with trace class difference, there exists a
real-valued
A-integrable spectral shift function in which case
in formula (
1.3
) the right-hand side should be understood in the sense of A-integral.

Finally, let us mention here that the maximal class of functions
f
for which trace formula (
1.3
)
holds for arbitrary pairs
(T_{0},T_{1})
of contractions with trace class difference coincides with the class of operator Lipschitz functions analytic in
{\mathbb{D}}
, see
[
MNP2
]
.

The Lifshits–Krein trace formula was also generalized in
[
MN2
]
,
[
MNP1
]
and
[
MNP2
]
to the case of pairs of maximal dissipative operators. If
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
are maximal dissipative operators with trace class difference, then it was shown in
[
MNP1
]
and
[
MNP2
]
that there exists an integrable spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
on the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
such that the trace formula

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}\big(f(L_{1})-f(L_{0})\big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(t)\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\,{\rm d}t

(1.5)

for rational functions
f
with poles in the open lower half-plane.

On the other hand, if instead of the condition
L_{1}-L_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
we impose the resolvent condition

({\rm i}I+L_{1})^{-1}-({\rm i}I+L_{0})^{-1}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},

there exists a spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)|}{1+t^{2}}\,{\rm d}t<\infty

(1.6)

and trace formula (

) holds for rational functions
f
with poles in the open lower half-plane,
see
[
MNP2
]
.

In this paper we are going to find conditions under which a pair of contractions with trace class difference has a
real-valued
integrable spectral shift function. Recently Chattopadhyay and Sinha showed in
[

that if

and

are contractions with trace class difference and

We are going to essentially improve this result in §

It is also appropriate to mention here that it was shown in

that if

In §

of maximal dissipative operators with trace class resolvent difference. We find new sufficient conditions under which the pair

In this paper we improve the results of our note

2.
Real-valued integrable spectral shift functions for pairs of contractions

In this section we obtain a condition on a pair
(T_{0},T_{1})
of contractions with trace class difference, under which this pair has a real-valued integrable spectral shift function. To establish the main result of this section, we prove Theorem
2.1
. Obviously, if we know that
X
and
Y
are operators on Hilbert space satisfying the conditions
{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq X\leq I
and
{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq Y\leq I
and
Y-X\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
, then for
\sigma\in(0,1)
, the operator

Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}
does not have to belong to
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
. However, Theorem
2.1
shows that if we impose a certain additional assumption, we can make the conclusion that
Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
.

We are going to introduce the following piece of notation. Suppose that
Q
is a densely defined linear operator on Hilbert space that extends by continuity. We are going to use the the same notation
Q
for the unique extension of
Q
to a bounded linear operator.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
p>0
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
.
Suppose that
X
and
Y
are operators on Hilbert space such that
{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq X\leq I
,
{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq Y\leq I
,

\operatorname{Ker}X=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
and
\operatorname{Ker}Y=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. If
Y-X\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
and the operator

Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}
is densely defined and extends to an operator of class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
for some nonnegative

\alpha
and
\beta
such that

\alpha+\beta\in(1-\sigma,1]
, then
Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.

Proof.
We use the notation
\operatorname{clos}(Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha})
for the closure of the operator
Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}
, which belongs to
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
by the assumption.
The following elementary identity is well known (see e.g.,
[
BS2
]
, Ch. 10, Sect. 4).

c_{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{\sigma-1}}{t+s}\,{\rm d}t=s^{\sigma-1},\quad c_{\sigma}=\frac{\sin\pi\sigma}{\pi},\quad s>0,\quad 0<\sigma<1,

It follows easily and it is well known (see e.g. Bir-Sol, Ch.10) that

Y^{\sigma}=c_{\sigma}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{\sigma-1}Y(tI+Y)^{-1}{\rm d}t\quad\mbox{and}\quad X^{\sigma}=c_{\sigma}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{\sigma-1}X(tI+X)^{-1}{\rm d}t.

It is easy to verify that

(tI+Y)\big(Y(tI+Y)^{-1}-X(tI+X)^{-1}\big)(tI+X)=t(X-Y),

and so

Y(tI+Y)^{-1}-X(tI+X)^{-1}=t(tI+Y)^{-1}(X-Y)(tI+X)^{-1}.

Thus,

\displaystyle Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=c_{\sigma}\int^{\infty}_{0}t^{\sigma}(tI+Y)^{-1}(X-Y)(tI+X)^{-1}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=c_{\sigma}\int^{1}_{0}t^{\sigma}\big((tI+Y)^{-1}Y^{\beta}\big)\operatorname{clos}\big(Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\big)\big(X^{\alpha}(tI+X)^{-1}\big)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+c_{\sigma}\int^{\infty}_{1}t^{\sigma}(tI+Y)^{-1}(X-Y)(tI+X)^{-1}\,{\rm d}t.

Since
Y
is non-negative, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\left\|t^{\sigma}(tI+Y)^{-1}\right\|\leq t^{\sigma-1},\qquad t\in(0,\infty).

(2.1)

Clearly,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\big\|Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}\big\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{\sigma}\left\|\int^{1}_{0}t^{\sigma}\big((tI+Y)^{-1}Y^{\beta}\big)\big(Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\big)\big(X^{\alpha}(tI+X)^{-1}\big)\,{\rm d}t\right\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}

\displaystyle+c_{\sigma}\left\|\int^{\infty}_{1}t^{\sigma}(tI+Y)^{-1}(X-Y)(tI+X)^{-1}\,{\rm d}t\right\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}.

(2.2)

Clearly, the first term on the right of (
2
) can be estimated
in terms of

\displaystyle c_{\sigma}\left\|\int^{1}_{0}t^{\sigma}\big((tI+Y)^{-1}Y^{\beta}\big)\big(Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\big)\big(X^{\alpha}(tI+X)^{-1}\big)\,{\rm d}t\right\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\leq c_{\sigma}\int^{1}_{0}\left\|(tI+Y)^{-1}Y^{\beta}\right\|\left\|t^{\sigma}Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\right\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}\left\|X^{\alpha}(tI+X)^{-1}\right\|\,{\rm d}t.

(2.3)

The elementary inequality

\frac{x^{\alpha}}{t+x}\leq t^{\alpha-1}\quad\mbox{for}\quad x\in[0,1],~t\in(0,1]

immediately implies that

\left\|{X^{\alpha}}(tI+X)^{-1}\right\|\leq t^{\alpha-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|(tI+Y)^{-1}{Y^{\beta}}\right\|=\left\|\big({Y^{\beta}}(tI+Y)^{-1}\big)^{*}\right\|\leq t^{\beta-1},

and so the right-hand side of (
2
) is less than or equal to

\big\|Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\big\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}c_{\sigma}\int^{1}_{0}t^{\sigma}t^{\alpha+\beta-2}\,{\rm d}t\leq\frac{c_{\sigma}}{(\alpha+\beta+\sigma-1)}\big\|Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\big\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}\;.

Next,

c_{\sigma}\left\|\int^{\infty}_{1}t^{\sigma}(tI+Y)^{-1}(X-Y)(tI+X)^{-1}{\rm d}t\right\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}\\[5.69046pt]
\leq c_{\sigma}\int^{\infty}_{1}\big\|t^{\sigma}(tI+Y)^{-1}\big\|\cdot\|X-Y\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}\left\|(tI+X)^{-1}\right\|{\rm d}t

(2.4)

Obviously, for
t\geq 1
,

\big\|(tI+Y)^{-1}\big\|\leq t^{-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\big\|(tI+X)^{-1}\big\|\leq t^{-1}.

Thus, the right-hand side of (
2.4
) is less than or equal to

c_{\sigma}\|X-Y\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}\int^{\infty}_{1}t^{\sigma-2}{\rm d}t\leq\frac{c_{\sigma}}{1-\sigma}\|X-Y\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}.

Combining inequality (
2
) with estimates (
2
) and (
2.4
), we arrive at the conclusion
Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.
\blacksquare

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
p>0
.
Suppose that

{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq X\leq I
and
{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq Y\leq I
. If
Y-X\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
and

X
is invertible, then
Y^{\sigma}-X^{\sigma}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.

Proof.
If
X
is invertible,
then
X^{-\alpha}
is a bounded operator for every
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence,

(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
. Put
\beta=0
. The result follows from Theorem
2.1
.
\blacksquare

For a contraction
T
, we consider the defect operators

D_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(I-T^{*}T)^{1/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{T^{*}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(I-TT^{*})^{1/2}.

In what follows we agree that if
R
is a linear operator defined on a dense subset of a Hilbert space and it extends to a bounded linear operator, we use the same notation
R
for its unique extension to the whole space.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
p>0
. Suppose that

T_{0}
and
T_{1}
are contractions such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}D_{T_{0}}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}

(2.5)

and for some
\alpha\geq 0
,
\beta\geq 0
satisfying
\alpha+\beta\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1]
, the operators

D_{T_{1}^{*}}^{-2\beta}(T_{1}-T_{0})D_{T_{0}}^{-2\alpha}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{T_{1}}^{-2\beta}(T_{1}^{*}-T^{*}_{0})D_{T_{0}}^{-2\alpha}\quad

(2.6)

are densely defined and extend to operators of class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.
Then

\displaystyle D_{T_{1}}-D_{T_{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}\quad\mbox{and}\quad D_{T_{1}^{*}}-D_{T^{*}_{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}.

(2.7)

Remark 1.
Actually,
\operatorname{Ker}D_{T_{0}}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
if and only if
\operatorname{Ker}D_{T^{*}_{0}}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
, and so (
2.5
)
can be replaced with the condition

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}D_{T^{*}_{0}}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}.

(2.8)

Indeed, (
2.5
) is equivalent to the fact that
\operatorname{Ker}(I-T_{0}^{*}T_{0})=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
while (
2.8
) is equivalent to the condition

\operatorname{Ker}(I-T_{0}T^{*}_{0})=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. It remains to use the well-kown fact that
1
is an eigenvalue of
T_{0}^{*}T_{0}
if and only if it is an eigenvalue of
T_{0}T^{*}_{0}
.

Remark 2.
Clearly, each of the inclusions in (
2.6
) implies that
T_{1}-T_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.

Proof.
Put
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}I-T_{1}^{*}T_{1}=D^{2}_{T_{1}}
and
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}I-T^{*}_{0}T_{0}=D^{2}_{T_{0}}
. It is easily seen that

Y-X=T^{*}_{0}T_{0}-T_{1}^{*}T_{1}=-(T_{1}^{*}-T^{*}_{0})T_{0}-T_{1}^{*}(T_{1}-T_{0})\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}.

(2.9)

Clearly, we have the obvious commutation relation

T_{0}D^{2}_{T_{0}}=D^{2}_{T^{*}_{0}}T_{0}

which implies that

\displaystyle T_{0}q(D^{2}_{T_{0}})=q(D^{2}_{T^{*}_{0}})T_{0}

(2.10)

for an arbitrary polynomial
q
, and so (
2.10
) also holds for an arbitrary continuous function
q
. In particular,

T_{0}D_{T_{0}}^{\alpha}=D_{T^{*}_{0}}^{\alpha}T_{0},\quad\text{for every}\quad\alpha\geq 0.

Hence,

T_{0}f=D_{T^{*}_{0}}^{\alpha}T_{0}D_{T_{0}}^{-\alpha}f,\qquad f\in\operatorname{Range}X^{\alpha},

and so

D_{T^{*}_{0}}^{-\alpha}T_{0}f=T_{0}D_{T_{0}}^{-\alpha}f,\qquad f\in\operatorname{Range}X^{\alpha}.

(2.11)

It follows from (
2.9
) that

\displaystyle Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}f

\displaystyle=-Y^{-\beta}T_{1}^{*}(T_{1}-T_{0})D_{T_{0}}^{-2\alpha}f

\displaystyle-Y^{-\beta}(T_{1}^{*}-T^{*}_{0})T_{0}D_{T_{0}}^{-2\alpha}f,\quad f\in\operatorname{Range}X^{\alpha}.

Together with (
2.11
) this yields

\displaystyle Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}f

\displaystyle=-T_{1}^{*}D_{T_{1}^{*}}^{-2\beta}(T_{1}-T_{0})D_{T_{0}}^{-2\alpha}f

\displaystyle-D_{T_{1}}^{-2\beta}(T^{*}_{0}-T_{1}^{*})D_{T_{0}^{*}}^{-2\alpha}T_{0}f,\quad f\in\operatorname{Range}X^{\alpha}.

Note that because of (
2.6
) both operators on the right hand side of this identity are bounded
and
\operatorname{Range}X^{\alpha}
is dense in
{\mathscr{H}}
. Hence,

Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}=-T_{1}^{*}D_{T_{1}^{*}}^{-2\beta}(T_{1}-T_{0})D_{T_{0}}^{-2\alpha}-D_{T_{1}}^{-2\beta}(T^{*}_{0}-T_{1}^{*})D_{T_{0}^{*}}^{-2\alpha}T_{0}.

Hence, with an account of condition (
2.6
) one gets
Y^{-\beta}(Y-X)X^{-\alpha}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.

Applying Theorem
2.1
with
\sigma=1/2
ensures the inclusion

Y^{1/2}-X^{1/2}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
which means that
D_{T_{1}}-D_{T_{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.
Interchanging the roles of
T_{1}
and
T_{1}^{*}
, we find that

D_{T_{1}^{*}}-D_{T^{*}_{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
.
\blacksquare

The following lemma was established in
[
MNP2
]
. We submit a proof here for completeness.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(T_{0},T_{1})
be a pair of contractions with trace class difference. Suppose that
conditions (
2.7
) hold for
p=1
. Then the pair
(T_{0},T_{1})
has a real integrable spectral shift function.

To prove the lemma, we need the construction of Schäffer matrix dilation. Let
T
be a contraction on a Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}
. We consider the Schäffer matrix dilation
U^{[T]}
on the two-sided sequence space

\ell^{2}_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathscr{H}})=\bigoplus_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathscr{H}}_{j}
,
{\mathscr{H}}_{j}={\mathscr{H}}
,
of
{\mathscr{H}}
-valued sequences, see
[
SNF
]
, Ch. 1, § 5. We identify
{\mathscr{H}}
with the subspace of sequences

\{v_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
such that
v_{j}={\boldsymbol{0}}
for
j\neq 0
.
This dilation does not have to be minimal. However, an advantage of this dilation is that it allows us to consider unitary dilations of contractions on
{\mathscr{H}}
on the single space
\ell^{2}_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathscr{H}})
.

The block matrix representation of
U^{[T]}
has the form:

\displaystyle U^{[T]}=\left(\begin{matrix}\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\iddots\\
\cdots&{\boldsymbol{0}}&I&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\cdots\\
\cdots&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&I&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\cdots\\
\cdots&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&D_{T}&-T^{*}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\cdots\\
\cdots&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&T&D_{T^{*}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\cdots\\
\cdots&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&I&{\boldsymbol{0}}&\cdots\\
\cdots&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&I&\cdots\\
\iddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots\end{matrix}\right).

(2.12)

Here the entry
T
is at the
(0,0)
position. In other words, the entries

U^{[T]}_{j,k}
of
U^{[T]}
are given by

U^{[T]}_{0,0}=T,\quad U^{[T]}_{0,1}=D_{T^{*}},\quad U^{[T]}_{-1,0}=D_{T},\quad U^{[T]}_{-1,1}=-T^{*},\quad U^{[T]}_{j,j+1}=I\quad\mbox{for}\quad j\neq 0,~-1,

while all the remaining entries are equal to
{\boldsymbol{0}}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Consider the Schäffer matrix dilations
U^{[T_{0}]}
and
U^{[T_{1}]}

of
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
.

It follows easily from (
2.12
)
and (
2.7
)
that
U^{[T_{1}]}-U^{[T_{0}]}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
. By Krein’s theorem
[
Kr2
]
, the pair

\Big(U^{[T_{0}]},U^{[T_{1}]}\Big)
has a real spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
, i.e., the following trace formula holds

\operatorname{trace}\Big(f\big(U^{[T_{1}]}\big)-f\big(U^{[T_{0}]}\big)\Big)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(\zeta)\boldsymbol{\xi}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta

for an arbitrary trigonometric polynomial
f
.

It is easy to see that if
f
is an analytic polynomial, then the
(0,0)
entries
f\big(U^{[T_{1}]}\big)_{0,0}
and
f\big(U^{[T_{0}]}\big)_{0,0}

are given by

f\big(U^{[T_{1}]}\big)_{0,0}=f(T_{1})\quad\mbox{and}\quad f\big(U^{[T_{0}]}\big)_{0,0}=f(T_{0}).

It follows that for an arbitrary analytic polynomial
f
,

\operatorname{trace}\big(f(T_{1})-f(T_{0})\big)=\operatorname{trace}\Big(f\big(U^{[T_{1}]}\big)-f\big(U^{[T_{0}]}\big)\Big)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f^{\prime}(\zeta)\boldsymbol{\xi}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta.

Thus,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a real integrable spectral shift function of the pair
\{T_{0},T_{1}\}
.

\blacksquare

3.
The case of dissipative operators

As we have mentioned in the introduction if
(L_{0},L_{1})
is a pair of maximal dissipative operators with trace class resolvent difference, i.e.,

(L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},

(3.1)

trace formula (
1.5
) holds with a spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
satisfying (
1.6
).

In this section we find a sufficient condition for the existence of a
real-valued
spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
satisfying (
1.6
), see Theorem
3.1
below.

Recall also that if we replace condition (
3.1
) with the condition

\displaystyle L_{1}-L_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},

(3.2)

then trace formula (
1.5
) holds with an
integrable
spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Recall that both results were obtained in
[
MNP2
]
.

However, it turns out that under condition (
3.2
), an
integrable
real-valued spectral shift function
does not have to exist. Moreover, such a function cannot exist if

\operatorname{trace}(L_{1}-L_{0})\not\in{\mathbb{R}}
, see Theorem
3.2
below.

With each maximal dissipative operator
L
we associate the quadratic form

{\mathfrak{T}}^{\operatorname{Im}}_{L}[f]=\operatorname{Im}(Lf,f)
,
f\in{\rm dom}(L)
.
This form is non-negative. Though it is not necessarily closable.

In what follows we always assume that the symmetric form
{\mathfrak{T}}^{\operatorname{Im}}_{L}[f]
is closable,
and so by the first representation theorem (see
[
Ka
, Ch.VI, Th. 2.1]
),
its closure
\operatorname{clos}{\mathfrak{T}}_{L,\operatorname{Im}}
corresponds to a nonnegative self-adjoint operator,
which we denote by
\operatorname{Im}L
.

If
{\rm dom}(L)={\rm dom}(L^{*})
, then the operator
\operatorname{Im}L
is just the Friedrichs extension of the
symmetric operator
\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}(L-L^{*})\geq 0
(see
[
Ka
, Ch.VI, Theorem 2.9]
).

If the numerical range of
{\mathfrak{T}}^{\operatorname{Im}}_{L}[f]
is contained in the sector

S_{\vartheta}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:\operatorname{Im}z\geq\cot\vartheta|\operatorname{Re}z|\},

then the form
{\mathfrak{T}}^{\operatorname{Im}}_{L}[f]
is necessarily closable
and the operator
\operatorname{Im}L
definitely exists, it is nonnegative and self-adjoint (see
[
Ka
, Ch.VI, Th. 1.27]
).

Recall that if
T
and
R
are linear operator in Hilbert space with domains
{\rm dom}(T)
and
{\rm dom}(R)
,
the operator
R
is said to be
dominated by

T
if

\displaystyle{\rm dom}(T)\subset{\rm dom}(R)

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle\|Rx\|^{2}\leq a\|Tx\|^{2}+b\|x\|^{2},\quad x\in{\rm dom}(T),

(3.4)

for some positive numbers
a
and
b
. If
R
is dominated by
T
, the number

{\rm a}_{T}(R)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\inf\{a>0:~\mbox{ there exists positive}~b~\mbox{ such that }~(\ref{1v2})~\mbox{ holds }\}

is called the
T
-bound of
R
.

It is well known (see
[
BS2
]
, § 3.4) that if
T
and
R
are closed operators satisfying (
3.3
), then inequality (
3.4
) holds for some positive numbers
a
and
b
.

It is also easy to verify that if
L
is a maximal dissipative operator, then an operator
R
is dominated by
L
if and only if the operator
R(L+{\rm i}I)^{-1}
is bounded.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
be
maximal dissipative operators satisfying (
3.1
) such that

{\rm dom}(L_{0})={\rm dom}(L_{1})
and
\operatorname{Ker}\operatorname{Im}L_{j}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
,
j=0,1
.
Assume also that both forms
\mathfrak{T}^{\operatorname{Im}}_{L_{j}}
are closable
and the operator
(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}
is dominated by
L_{j}+{\rm i}I
and by
L_{j}^{*}-{\rm i}I
, i.e.,
the operators

(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}\quad\text{are bounded},\quad j\in\{0,1\}.

(3.5)

Assume in addition that

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Im}L_{1})^{-1/2}\big(L_{1}-L_{0}\big)(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}

(3.6)

in the sense that the operator in (
3.6
) is densely defined and extends by continuity to a trace class operator.

Then the pair
(L_{0},L_{1})
has a real-valued
spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
satisfying the condition

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)|}{1+t^{2}}\,{\rm d}t<\infty.

(3.7)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Consider the Cayley transforms of
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
defined by

T_{0}=(L_{0}-{\rm i}I)(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad T_{1}=(L_{1}-{\rm i}I)(L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}.

(3.8)

Let us show that the contractions
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
satisfy the hypotheses
of Theorem
2.5
with
\alpha=\beta=1/2
and
p=1
.

It follows easily from (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle T_{1}-T_{0}=-2{\rm i}\big((L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\big).

(3.9)

Combining this identity with (
3.1
),
we see that
T_{1}-T_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
. Put

\displaystyle G_{j}=(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{G}_{j}=(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2},\quad j\in\{0,1\}.

(3.10)

By (
3.5
), the operators
G_{j}
are bounded. Since

(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}\subset\big((\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}\big)^{*},

the operators
(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}
are also
bounded.

With this notation, one can easily deduce from (
3.8
) that

\displaystyle D^{2}_{T_{j}}

\displaystyle=I-T^{*}_{j}T_{j}=2{\rm i}\big((L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L^{*}_{j}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle-2{\rm i}(L^{*}_{j}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=4\big((L^{*}_{j}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}\big)(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=4\big((\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\big)^{*}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}=4G_{j}^{*}G_{j}.

(3.11)

Since
\operatorname{Ker}\operatorname{Im}L_{j}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
, it obviously follows that
\operatorname{Ker}G_{j}=\operatorname{Ker}G_{j}^{*}G_{j}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
, and so

T_{0}
satisfies (
2.5
).

Next, (
3
) implies that
2|G_{j}|=2(G_{j}^{*}G_{j})^{1/2}=D_{T_{j}}
,
j\in\{0,1\}
, and hence the polar decomposition of
G_{j}
is
G_{j}=V_{j}|G_{j}|=2^{-1}V_{j}D_{T_{j}}
where
V_{j}
,
j\in\{0,1\}
, is unitary.

Similarly,
(
3.10
) that

\displaystyle D^{2}_{T_{j}^{*}}

\displaystyle=I-T_{j}T^{*}_{j}=2{\rm i}\big((L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L^{*}_{j}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle-2(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}{\rm i}(L^{*}_{j}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=4(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\operatorname{Im}L_{j}(L^{*}_{j}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=4(L_{j}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(\operatorname{Im}L_{j})^{1/2}(L_{j}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}=4\widetilde{G}_{j}\widetilde{G}_{j}^{*},

(3.12)

It follows that
|\widetilde{G}_{j}^{*}|=(\widetilde{G}_{j}\widetilde{G}_{j}^{*})^{1/2}=2^{-1}D_{T_{j}^{*}}
,
j=0,1
.

We also need the antipolar representation of
\widetilde{G}_{j}
:

\widetilde{G}_{j}=|\widetilde{G}_{j}^{*}|\widetilde{V}_{j}=2^{-1}D_{T_{j}^{*}}\widetilde{V}_{j}
(see
[
BS2
, Ch. 8]
,
[
Ka
]
). Note that the
\widetilde{V}_{j}
,
j\in\{0,1\}
, are unitary. Thus, combining the polar and the antipolar decompositions
for
G_{0}
and
\widetilde{G}_{1}
and keeping (
3.10
) in mind, we easily obtain

\displaystyle 2D_{T_{0}}^{-1}=G_{0}^{-1}V_{0}=(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}V_{0}

(3.13)

and

\displaystyle 2D_{T_{1}^{*}}^{-1}=\widetilde{V}_{1}(\widetilde{G}_{1})^{-1}=\widetilde{V}_{1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{1})^{-1/2}(L_{1}+{\rm i}I).

(3.14)

Multiplying (
3.9
) by
D_{T_{1}^{*}}^{-1}
on the left and by
D_{T_{0}}^{-1}

on the right, using representations (
3.13
) and (
3.14
)
for
D_{T_{0}}^{-1}
and
D_{T_{1}^{*}}^{-1}
and keeping (
3.6
) in mind, we obtain

\displaystyle D^{-1}_{T_{1}^{*}}(T_{1}-T_{0})D^{-1}_{T_{0}}=2^{-1}{\rm i}D^{-1}_{T_{1}^{*}}\big((L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\big)D^{-1}_{T_{0}}

\displaystyle=2^{-1}{\rm i}D^{-1}_{T_{1}^{*}}(L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}(L_{1}-L_{0})(L_{0}+iI)^{-1}D^{-1}_{T_{0}}

\displaystyle=2^{-1}{\rm i}\widetilde{V}_{1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{1})^{-1/2}(L_{1}-L_{0})(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}V_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}

by (
3.6
). Similarly,

D^{-1}_{T_{1}}(T_{1}^{*}-T^{*}_{0})D^{-1}_{T^{*}_{0}}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}.

Thus,
T_{0}
and
T_{1}
satisfy (
2.6
) with with
\alpha=\beta=1/2
.
By Theorem
2.5
, the pair of contractions

\{T_{0},T_{1}\}
has an integrable real-valued spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm c}
.
We can define now the function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
by

\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\rm c}\left(\frac{t-{\rm i}}{t+{\rm i}}\right),\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Clearly, (
3.7
) is satisfied and trace formula (
1.5
) holds
for an arbitrary rational function
f
with poles in
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}=\{\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}:~\operatorname{Im}\zeta<0\}
. That is,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a real-valued spectral shift function for the pair
\{L_{0},L_{1}\}
.

\blacksquare

Finally, we deduce
from Theorem 5.6
[
MN2
]
the following result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
are maximal dissipative operators satisfying (
3.2
) and such that

\operatorname{trace}(L_{1}-L_{0})\not\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then the pair
(L_{0},L_{1})
does not possess a real-valued integrable function.

Moreover, if
L_{1}-L_{0}
is dissipative, then the condition
\operatorname{trace}(L_{1}-L_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}
is equivalent to the existence of an integrable real-valued spectral shift function.

Before we proceed to the proof, let us write the following special case of trace formula (
1.5
):

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}\big((L_{1}-zI)^{-1}-(L_{0}-zI)^{-1}\big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)}{(t-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}t

(3.15)

Proof.
Put
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}L_{1}-L_{0}
.

It is easy to see that under (
3.2
) identity (
3.15
) implies that

\displaystyle\operatorname{trace}\big((L_{1}-zI)^{-1}V(L_{0}-zI)^{-1}\big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)}{(t-z)^{2}}\,{\rm d}t.

(3.16)

Setting here
z=-iy
, multiplying both sides by
y^{2}
and passing to the limit as
y\to\infty
, we find that

\operatorname{trace}V=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\,{\rm d}t.

Both conclusions in the statement of the theorem follow immediately from this formula.
\blacksquare

4.
An application to dissipative Schrödinger operators

In this section we apply Theorem
3.1
to the pair
\{L_{0},L_{1}\}
of maximal dissipative Schrödinger operators, where

L_{0}=(1+{\rm i})D^{2}+{\rm i}I\qquad\text{ and}\qquad L_{1}=(1+{\rm i})D^{2}+M_{{\rm i}+q}

(4.1)

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that the potential
{\rm i}+q
satisfies
\operatorname{Im}q\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}
. Here
D=-{\rm i}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
and by
M_{\varphi}
we understand multiplication by
\varphi
, i.e.,

M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f.

In other words,

L_{1}f=-(1+{\rm i})f^{\prime\prime}+({\rm i}+q)f.

For a compact operator
T
on Hilbert space, we consider the sequences
\{s_{j}(T)\}_{j\geq 0}
and

\{\lambda_{j}(T)\}_{j\geq 0}

the sequences of singular values and the sequence of eigenvalues of
T
counted with algebraic multiplicities and we arrange them so that

s_{j+1}\leq s_{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\lambda_{j+1}|\leq|\lambda_{j}|,\quad j\geq 0.

If
T
has finitely many eigenvalues, we extend the finite sequence of its eigenvalues with infinitely many zero terms.

We are going to use the following fact (see
[
BS2
]
, § 3.10, Th. 2):

Let
A
and
B
be compact operators on Hilbert space. Then

\lambda_{j}(AB)=\lambda_{j}(BA),\quad j\geq 0.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are compact self-adjoint operators on Hilbert space.
Then

s_{j}(T_{1}T_{2})=s_{j}(T_{2}T_{1}),\quad j\geq 0.

Proof.
Clearly,

\lambda_{j}\left((T_{1}T_{2})^{*}(T_{1}T_{2})\right)=\lambda_{j}\left(T_{2}T_{1}^{2}T_{2}\right)=\lambda_{j}\left(T_{1}^{2}T_{2}^{2}\right)

Similarly,

\lambda_{j}\left((T_{1}T_{2})(T_{1}T_{2})^{*}\right)=\lambda_{j}\left(T_{1}T_{2}^{2}T_{1}\right)=\lambda_{j}\left(T_{1}^{2}T_{2}^{2}\right)

Thus,

s_{j}(T_{1}T_{2})^{2}=\lambda_{j}\left((T_{1}T_{2})^{*}(T_{1}T_{2})\right)=s_{j}(T_{2}T_{1})^{2}
.
\blacksquare

We need the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
q
be a nonnegative function in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and let
R
be a continuous positive-definite function on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
.
Suppose that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)R(x,x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

Then the integral operator
K
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
defined by

(Kf)(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(s)^{1/2}R(s,t)q(t)^{1/2}f(t)\,{\rm d}t

is a nonnegative operator of trace class and

\|K\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\operatorname{trace}K=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)R(x,x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

Note that in the case when the function
q
is continuous the result is well-known, see e.g.
[
GK
, Section 3.10]
. We give two different proofs of Theorem
4.2
.

The first proof of Theorem
4.2
.
First of all, it suffices to assume that both
R
and
q
are compactly supported. Indeed, let
w
be a continuous function
w
on
{\mathbb{R}}
such that

0\leq w(t)\leq 1\quad\mbox{for}\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}};\qquad\operatorname{supp}w=[-2,2]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad w(t)=1\quad\mbox{for}\quad t\in[-1,1].

We can define the operators
K_{n}
,
n\geq 1
by

(K_{n}f)(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(s)^{1/2}R_{n}(s,t)q(t)^{1/2}f(t)\,{\rm d}t,

where

R_{n}(s,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}w\Big(\frac{s}{n}\Big)R(s,t)w\Big(\frac{t}{n}\Big).

Clearly,
R_{n}
is a positive definite function with compact support.

By the assumption that the conclusion of the theorem holds for compactly supported
R
and
q
, we can conclude that
K_{n}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
and

\|K_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\operatorname{trace}K_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(x)R_{n}(x,x)\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

It is easy to se that the sequence
\{K_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
converges in the weak operator topology and

\mbox{w-}\lim_{n\to\infty}K_{n}=K
. Also, it is clear that
(K_{n}f,f)\leq(Kf,f)
for
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and
n\geq 1
.
It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{trace}K_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j\geq 1}(K_{n}f_{j},f_{j})=\sum_{j\geq 1}(Kf_{j},f_{j})=\operatorname{trace}K,

where
\{f_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(R)
.

Thus, we assume that both
R
and
q
are compactly supported. It follows that the operator
K
belongs to the Hilbert–Schmidt class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}
. Let us establish that
K\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
.

Consider an infinitely smooth function
v
on
{\mathbb{R}}
such that

v(t)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}};\qquad\operatorname{supp}v=[-1,1],\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t){\rm d}t=1.

Put
V_{n}(t)=nv(nt)
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Consider the function
\Phi_{n}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defined by

\Phi_{n}(s,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{n}(u)q^{1/2}(s-u)R(s-u,t-u)q^{1/2}(t-u)\,{\rm d}u.

It is easy to see that
\Phi_{n}
is a compactly supported infinitely smooth function and
\Phi_{n}
is positive definite for every
n
.

Consider now the integral operator
T_{n}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
defined by

(T_{n}f)(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{n}(s,t)f(t)\,{\rm d}t.

Since
\Phi_{n}
is a compactly supported infinitely smooth function, it follows that
T_{n}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
for every
n\geq 1
. It follows from
[
B
]
that

\operatorname{trace}T_{n}=\|T_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{n}(s,s)\,{\rm d}{s}.

It is easy to verify that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{n}(s,s)\,{\rm d}{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(s)R(s,s)\,{\rm d}x.

It is also clear that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|K-T_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}}=0.

This easily implies that
K\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
.

Once we know that
K\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
, we can easily conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|K-T_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=0.

(4.2)

Indeed, this is a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
R
be a trace class operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and

(Rf)(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k(s,t)f(t)\,{\rm d}t,

for a function
k
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
. Let
R_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, be the operators defined by

(R_{n}f)(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k_{n}(s,t)f(t)\,{\rm d}t,

where

k_{n}(s,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{n}(u)k(s-u,t-u)\,{\rm d}u.

Then
R_{n}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|R_{n}-R\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=0.

Let us first complete the proof of Theorem
4.2
. Since
\Phi_{n}
is a smooth function with compact support, it is well known (see
[
B
]
), that

\operatorname{trace}T_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{n}(s,s)\,{\rm d}s.

To complete the proof, we observe that it follows from (
4.2
) that

\operatorname{trace}K=\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{trace}T_{n}.\quad\blacksquare

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.
Clearly, it suffices to prove the result for rank one operators
R
in which case the result is trivial.
\blacksquare

Let us proceed now to the second proof of Theorem
4.2
.

First we complement Theorem 3.5.1 from
[
GK
]
. Suppose that
\{A_{n}\}_{1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of operators on Hilbert space of class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, that converges in the norm to a bounded linear operator
A
and

\sup_{n}\|A_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}<\infty.

(4.3)

It is well known that in this case
A
must be in
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
and its
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}
norm is less than or equal to the supremum in
(
4.3
). The following lemma shows that if we in addition to this assume that the sequence
\{|A_{n}|\}_{1}^{\infty}
of the moduli of
A_{n}
is monotone, we can make a stronger conclusion.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that in addition to the above assumptions the sequence
\{|A_{n}|\}_{1}^{\infty}
is monotone. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|A-A_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}=0.

(4.4)

Moreover, if the operators
A_{n}
are nonnegative, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|A_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}=\|A\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{p}}

(4.5)

Proof.
Assume to be definite that the sequence
\{|A_{n}|\}
is increasing. Then
s_{j}(A_{n})\uparrow s_{j}(A)
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}

as
n\to\infty
.
Therefore, applying the monotone convergence theorem, we obtain (
4.5
).

It we replace now
A_{n}
with
A-A_{n}
and observe that the resulting sequence is also monotone,
we arrive at (
4.4
).
\blacksquare

The second proof of Theorem
4.2
.

It suffices to assume that the function
q
is supported on a compact interval
[a,b]
.
Choose a monotone sequence
\varphi_{n}(\cdot)\in C[a,b]
approximating
q
from below in
L^{1}[a,b]
:

\varphi_{n}(\cdot)\in C[a,b],\quad\varphi_{n}(t)\leq\varphi_{n+1}(t)\leq q(t)\quad and\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\varphi_{n}-q\|_{L^{1}}=0

(4.6)

Define the operators
K_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, by setting

(K_{n}f)(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{n}(s)^{1/2}R(s,t)\varphi_{n}(t)^{1/2}f(t)\,{\rm d}t,

(4.7)

Clearly,

\|K_{n}-K\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}}^{2}=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}|(\varphi_{n}(s)\varphi_{n}(t))^{1/2}-(q(s)q(t))^{1/2}|^{2}\cdot|R(s,t)|^{2}\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(4.8)

Further, since the integral operator
K_{n}
given by (
4.7
) is non-negative with continuous kernel, and

\int_{a}^{b}\varphi_{n}(t)R(t,t){\rm d}t<\infty
, it is of trace
class,
K_{n}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
, and

\|K_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\operatorname{trace}K_{n}=\int_{a}^{b}\varphi_{n}(t)R(t,t){\rm d}t,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

(4.9)

(see
[
GK
, Chapter 3.10]
).
In turn, it follows from (
4.9
) and (
4.6
) that

\sup_{n}\|K_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\sup_{n}\int_{a}^{b}\varphi_{n}(t)R(t,t){\rm d}t=\int_{a}^{b}q(t)R(t,t){\rm d}t<\infty.

(4.10)

Combining this estimate with relation (
4.8
) and applying
[
GK
, Theorem 3.5.1]
yields
the inclusion
K\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
and the following estimate

\|K\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}\leq\sup_{n}\|K_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}\left(=\int_{a}^{b}q(t)R(t,t){\rm d}t\right).

Thus, condition (
4.3
) with
p=1
is verified and due to (
4.8
)
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|K_{n}-K\|=0
.
Thus, we can apply Lemma
4.4
to the sequence
\{K_{n}\}_{1}^{\infty}
which together with
(
4.9
)–(
4.10
) yields

\|K\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|K_{n}\|_{{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{trace}K_{n}=\int_{a}^{b}q(t)R(t,t){\rm d}t=\operatorname{trace}K.\quad\blacksquare

Let us return now to the Schrödinger operators
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
as in (
4.1
).

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

and let
\operatorname{Im}q\geq 0
. Then the pair of maximal dissipative operators

(L_{0},L_{1})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
has a real-valued spectral shift function
\boldsymbol{\xi}
satisfying
(
3.7
).

Proof.
We divide the proof in several steps.

(i) First, we observe that in accordance with the Sobolev imbedding theorem,

W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})
is continuously imbedded into the space
C_{\rm b}(\mathbb{R})

of bounded continuous functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
endowed with the sup-norm. Moreover,
for each
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists a constant
b(\varepsilon)>0
such that
the following estimate holds:

\|f\|_{C_{\rm b}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\varepsilon\|f\|_{W^{2,2}}+b(\varepsilon)\|f\|_{L^{2}}=\varepsilon\|D^{2}f\|_{L^{2}}+b(\varepsilon)\|f\|_{L^{2}},\quad f\in W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}).

(4.11)

Therefore the assumption
q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
implies that

{\rm a}_{D^{2}}(M_{q})=0

, i.e.,

\|qf\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|q\|_{L^{2}}\|f\|_{C_{\rm b}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\varepsilon_{0}\|L_{0}f\|_{L^{2}}+b(\varepsilon_{0})\|f\|_{L^{2}},\quad f\in{\rm dom}(L_{0})=W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}),

(4.12)

where
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon\|q\|_{L^{2}}
. This inequality means that the (dissipative) multiplication
operator
M_{q}

is dominated by
L_{0}
with
L_{0}
-bound equal to 0, i.e.,
{\rm a}_{L_{0}}(M_{q})=0
.
Therefore, the Kato-Rellich theorem applies and ensures that the operator
L_{1}
is closed on
{\rm dom}(L_{0})
and

{\rm dom}(L_{1})=\text{{\rm dom}}(L_{1}^{*})={\rm dom}(L_{0})\ =W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}).

(4.13)

In turn, this relation implies that the operator
K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)(L_{1}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}
is bounded.

(ii) Below we use the notation
{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t})
for the domain of a quadratic form
\mathfrak{t}
.
At this step we consider the form
\mathfrak{t}_{|q|}
,

\mathfrak{t}_{|q|}[f]=\big\|\sqrt{|q|}f\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad f\in{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{|q|})=\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}):\ \sqrt{|q|}f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\}

and the form
\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}}
,

\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}}[f]=\|Df\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})}^{2},\quad{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}})=W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}).

Let us show that for each
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists a constant
c(\varepsilon)>0
such that
the following estimate holds:

\mathfrak{t}_{|q|}[f]\leq\varepsilon\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}}[f]+c(\varepsilon)\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\qquad f\in{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}})=W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}).

(4.14)

Now instead of estimate (
4.11
), we need another
Sobolev imbedding theorem:
for each
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists a constant
c(\varepsilon)>0
such that
the following estimate holds:

\|f\|_{C({\mathbb{R}})}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\|Df\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+c(\varepsilon)\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},\qquad f\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}).

(4.15)

Using the assumption
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, applying estimate (
4.15
),
and setting
C_{q}=\|q\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{t}_{|q|}[f]

\displaystyle=\|qf^{2}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|q\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\|f\|_{C_{\rm b}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}\left(\varepsilon\|Df\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+c(\varepsilon)\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\right),\quad f\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}),

This estimate shows that
{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{|q|})\supset{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}})=W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
and
proves estimate (
4.14
).

By the KLMN theorem (
[
RS
, Theorem X.17]
), the form
\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}}+\mathfrak{t}_{|q|}
is semibounded below and closed on the domain

{\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}}+\mathfrak{t}_{|q|})={\rm dom}(\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}})=W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, the operator associated with the form
\mathfrak{t}_{D^{2}}+\mathfrak{t}_{|q|}
is
{D^{2}+|q|I}
,

{\rm dom}({D^{2}+|q|I})=W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})
.

It follows that both operators
M_{\sqrt{|q|}}(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1/2}
and
M_{\sqrt{|q|}}(L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1/2}
are bounded.

(iii) It is easily seen (and well known) that the resolvent
(D^{2}-zI)^{-1}
of the selfadjoint operator

D^{2}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
admits the following integral representation

(D^{2}-zI)^{-1}f=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}G(x,t;z)f(t){\rm d}t,\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(4.16)

where
G(x,t;z)
is the Green’s function of the Hamiltonian
D^{2}
,

G(x,t;z)=-\frac{1}{2{\rm i}\sqrt{z}}\begin{cases}e^{{\rm i}(x-t)\sqrt{z}},&\,\text{if f }t\leq x,\\
e^{-{\rm i}(x-t)\sqrt{z}},&\,\text{if }t\geq x.\end{cases}

(4.17)

the function
G(x,t;z)
is the Green’s function of the Hamiltonian
D^{2}
.

Let us show that

M_{q}(D^{2}-zI)^{-1}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
. To this end, we show that the operator
R
defined by

\displaystyle Rf

\displaystyle=|q|^{1/2}(D^{2}+I)^{-1}(|q|^{1/2}f)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|q(x)|^{1/2}G(x,t;-1)|q(t)|^{1/2}f(t){\rm d}t,\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

(4.18)

belongs to
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
. First, we observe that for each
t>0
, the (bounded) operator
(D^{2}+tI)^{-1}

is nonnegative because so is

D^{2}
. Therefore, the operator
R
is also nonnegative.

Next, it follows from (
4.17
) that

G(x,t;-1)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}e^{-(x-t)},&\,\text{if }t\leq x,\\
e^{(x-t)},&\,\text{if }t\geq x.\end{cases}

(4.19)

and so,
G(x,x;-1)=\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, denoting by

k_{R}
the kernel of the integral operator
R
defined by
(
4
) and keeping the assumption
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
in mind,
we can easily obtain

\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}k_{R}(x,x)\,{\rm d}x=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|q(x)|^{1/2}G(x,x;-1)|q(x)|^{1/2}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{2}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|q(x)|\,{\rm d}x<\infty.

(4.20)

Since the operator
R
is nonnegative, inequality (
4.20
) implies by Theorem
4.2

the required membership of
R=M_{|q|^{1/2}}(D^{2}+I)^{-1}M_{|q|^{1/2}}
in
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
.

Similarly, we get from (
4.19
) combined with the assumption
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

that the operator
M_{|q|^{1/2}}(D^{2}+I)^{-1}
belong to the Hilbert-Schmidt
class
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}
.

(iv)
Next, since
\operatorname{Im}q\geq 0
, we can select a branch of the square root of
q

such that
\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{q(x)}>0
. Therefore,

R_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}M_{q^{1/2}}(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}=M_{\varkappa|q|^{1/2}}(L_{0}+iI)^{-1}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2},

where
\varkappa(x)=q^{1/2}(x)/|q^{1/2}(x)|
,
|\varkappa(x)|=1
.

Next, we have the factorization

(L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}=-\left(M_{q^{1/2}}(L_{1}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}\right)^{*}\left(M_{q^{1/2}}(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}\right),

(4.21)

with both factors bounded.

Keeping in mind that the operator
K=\left((L_{0}+{\rm i}I)(L_{1}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}\right)
is bounded (step (i)), we obtain

\displaystyle R_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}M_{q^{1/2}}(L_{1}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(M_{q^{1/2}}(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1})\left((L_{0}+{\rm i}I)(L_{1}^{*}-{\rm i}I)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=(M_{q^{1/2}}(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1})K\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}.

Combining both inclusions
R_{j}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}
,
j=0,1
, with identity (
4.21
), we obtain

(L_{1}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}-(L_{0}+{\rm i}I)^{-1}=R_{1}^{*}R_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}.

(4.22)

Thus, condition (
3.1
) of Theorem
3.1
is verified.

(v)
Next, it follows from (
4.1
) that

\operatorname{Im}L_{0}=D^{2}+I\geq I\qquad\text{ and}\qquad\operatorname{Im}L_{1}=D^{2}+I+\operatorname{Im}q\geq I

and due to (
4.13
)
\text{dom}\operatorname{Im}L_{1}=\text{dom}\operatorname{Im}L_{0}
and
since both operators
\operatorname{Im}L_{0},\ \operatorname{Im}L_{1}
are positive, this implies that

\text{dom}(\operatorname{Im}L_{1})^{1/2}=\text{dom}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{1/2}
(see e.g.,
[
RS
]
, Theorem X.18 and
[
Sch
]
, Ex. 10.8.7).
In turn, this equality ensures the existence of a bounded operator
K_{1}

with bounded inverse
K_{1}^{-1}
that satisfies the condition

(\operatorname{Im}L_{1})^{-1/2}=K_{1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}
.

On the other hand, it follows from (
4.1
) that
L_{1}-L_{0}=M_{q}
. Inserting in (
3.6
) this equality together with
the previous identity turns it into the following one

(\operatorname{Im}L_{1})^{-1/2}M_{q}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}=K_{1}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}M_{q}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}.

(4.23)

which we have to verify this condition.
Since
K_{1}
is invertible,
condition (
4.23
)
is equivalent to the fact that

\left(M_{|q|^{1/2}}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}\right)^{*}\left(M_{|q|^{1/2}}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}\right)\\[5.69046pt]
=\operatorname{clos}{\left((\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}M_{|q|^{1/2}}\right)}\left(M_{|q|^{1/2}}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}\right)\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},

where we use the notation
\operatorname{clos}A
for the closure of a closable operator
A
.
In turn, this condition is equivalent to the condition

R=\left(M_{|q|^{1/2}}(\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}\right)\operatorname{clos}{\left((\operatorname{Im}L_{0})^{-1/2}M_{|q|^{1/2}}\right)}\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}

(4.24)

which follows immediately from the membership

R\in{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}
established at step (iii).
\blacksquare
