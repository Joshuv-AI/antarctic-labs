Impact of heatwave exposure on chronic kidney disease risk among middle-aged and older adults: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Huan Yang, Hong Wang, Lei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major global public health concern. The increasing morbidity of extreme heat events, attributed to global climate change, may pose a substantial risk to renal health. Nonetheless, the body of evidence examining the relationship between heatwave exposure and CKD remains sparse, especially among middle-aged and older demographic groups.MethodsThis study employed data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, analyzing information from 6,718 participants aged 45 years and older during the follow-up period from 2011 to 2015. Kidney function was evaluated using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at both baseline and endpoint surveys. Heatwaves were characterized by various temperature thresholds (e.g. the 90th,95th, and 97.5th percentiles) and durations of 2 to 4 consecutive days. Logistic regression models were utilized to examine the association between heatwave exposure and the morbidity of CKD, with sensitivity analyses conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings.ResultsOur study identified consistent and positive correlations between heatwave exposure and the risk of CKD across various definitions. We observed that higher intensity and prolonged duration of heatwaves were associated with increased odds of developing CKD, suggesting a dose–response relationship. Through clustering analysis, we identified geographic heterogeneity, distinguishing “dual high-risk zones” characterized by both high CKD prevalence and extended heat exposure, and “heatwave-dominant zones” marked by lower CKD prevalence but prolonged heat exposure. Furthermore, under conditions of high-intensity heatwaves, specifically HW11(97.5th percentile for 3 days) and HW11 (97.5th percentile for 4 days), significant interactions were observed among participants with pre-existing chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease.ConclusionExposure to heatwaves constitutes an independent environmental risk factor for CKD among middle-aged and older adults. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating climate-related risks into strategies for the prevention of chronic kidney disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05112-8.Keywords: Heatwaves, Chronic kidney disease (CKD), Environmental exposure, Public health

Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a prevalent chronic condition marked by the gradual deterioration of renal function. Between 1990 and 2017, the global prevalence of CKD rose by 33%. By 2019, it was estimated that there were approximately 697 million cases of CKD worldwide [1, 2]. The predominant etiologies of CKD include diabetes, glomerulonephritis, hypertension, and genetic disorders. Given its significant rates of disability and mortality, CKD not only poses a serious threat to individual health but also imposes a considerable economic burden on society.It has thus emerged as a major global public health challenge [3]. As a result, safeguarding kidney function in high-risk populations has become a critical priority in both national and international public health policies.

According to data from the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), the year 2024 marks the conclusion of the hottest decade on record, with the global near-surface average temperature surpassing the pre-industrial baseline by 1.55 °C (± 0.12 °C) [3]. This trend underscores the intensification of global warming. As the climate continues to warm, environmental heat exposure increasingly threatens human health, particularly affecting the renal system. The kidneys are integral to regulating body temperature and fluid balance, rendering them particularly susceptible to heat-related illnesses [4]. Research indicates that occupational heat stress elevates the risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) and may even lead to chronic kidney damage, especially under conditions characterized by high temperatures and dehydration. In such scenarios, the renal proximal tubules are identified as the primary site of potential injury, suggesting that the effects of heat stress on the kidneys are both specific and consistent [5–8]. Moreover, heat stress may exacerbate the progression of CKD, particularly among populations subjected to prolonged exposure to elevated temperatures [9, 10]. A large-scale population study in Taiwan, China, demonstrated a significant association between heat-related diseases and the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD), with heatstroke patients exhibiting a markedly elevated risk of CKD compared to the control group [11]. Further research has corroborated these findings, indicating that extreme heat events are significantly associated with an increase in emergency department visits for renal conditions, including AKI, nephrolithiasis, and urinary tract infections [12]. As global temperatures continue to escalate, regions characterized by high ambient heat are anticipated to encounter an increased morbidity of CKD [13].

While prior research has suggested a possible connection between heat exposure and compromised kidney function, the majority of these studies have predominantly focused on populations with occupational exposure. This study focuses on the association between the intensity and duration of heatwave exposure and the prevalence of chronic kidney disease. There are significant differences in research content and exposure definitions compared with studies in Taiwan, China that explore the risk of chronic kidney disease onset related to heat-related illnesses (heatstroke). Consequently, epidemiological evidence concerning this association is still sparse, particularly among non-occupational groups. This study seeks to address this gap by utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the relationship between heatwave exposure and the risk of CKD among middle-aged and older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design and study population

The data for this study were sourced from the China Health and Retiree Survey (CHARLS). CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort study targeting Chinese middle-aged and elderly populations aged 45 and above. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011,followed by biennial follow-ups, and multiple rounds of data collection have been completed by the time of this study. The study employed a rigorous multi-stage stratified probability proportional sampling (PPS) design, ensuring national representativeness. Its detailed study protocol, sampling framework, and quality control procedures have been systematically reported in previous literature and official documents. Readers can refer to the main text, supplementary materials, and references for complete information [14].

In light of the fact that biological samples, including serum creatinine, were collected exclusively during the 2011 (Wave 1) and 2015 (Wave 3) surveys, this study utilized data spanning from 2011 to 2015 to ensure the comparability and completeness of the research dataset. Initially,17,709 participants were included in the 2011 baseline survey. Participants were sequentially excluded based on the following criteria: (1) absence of serum creatinine test data in either Wave 1 or Wave 3; (2) a baseline diagnosis of CKD, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m²; (3)In 2015, follow-up data or outcome data were missing; and (4) information on key covariates was missing. This study did not impute missing data, primarily because the proportion of missing data was low and met the characteristics of completely random missing data. Direct use of complete case analysis could avoid potential bias introduced by imputation, ensuring the authenticity and reliability of the analysis results. Figure 1 presents a flowchart detailing the study design. After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 6,718 participants satisfied the inclusion criteria and comprised the final analytical sample for this study. This sample provides a robust foundation for examining the longitudinal association between heatwave exposure and changes in renal function among middle-aged and elderly populations.

Assessment of CKD events

In this study, the eGFR was employed as a metric for assessing renal function to determine the status of CKD. The eGFR was calculated using the creatinine equation developed by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) and published in 2012 [15].This study conducted two rounds of surveys in 2011 (baseline) and 2015 (follow-up), measuring serum creatinine levels and calculating the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) accordingly. All exposure indicators were determined based on temperature data from before the baseline survey and the year of the baseline, ensuring that the exposure window preceded the outcome assessment time to avoid post-exposure bias. According to internationally recognized diagnostic criteria, an eGFR of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m² is classified as indicative of CKD [16].

Assessment of heat wave

In this investigation, we sourced daily temperature data from the (Resource and Environment Science Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (RESDC) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Meteorological data for each city were acquired from a principal meteorological monitoring station within the respective region [17]. Environmental exposure was evaluated based on the environmental conditions of the city where participants resided. Due to the absence of detailed residential location data, it was assumed that participants residing in the same city experienced similar levels of environmental exposure. Currently, there is no unified international standard for heatwaves, with varying research approaches in selecting temperature metrics. Some studies use daily average temperature, while others employ daily maximum or minimum temperatures. Epidemiological evidence indicates that daily average temperature more consistently reflects overall heat exposure levels throughout the day. It outperforms daily maximum or minimum temperatures in predicting health impacts of heatwaves, making it the preferred metric for assessing heatwave exposure. Therefore, this study adopts daily average temperature as the core indicator for defining heatwaves to enhance the scientific rigor and reliability of exposure assessments. To thoroughly assess the potential health impacts of heatwaves, this study utilized daily average temperature as the primary metric and defined heatwaves by integrating multiple temperature thresholds and durations. The daily average temperature is regarded as a relatively optimal indicator; empirical evidence suggests that it surpasses the daily maximum temperature in forecasting the health impacts of heatwaves [18]. In our study, we established temperature thresholds based on the percentiles (90th, 92.5th, 95th, and 97.5th percentiles) of the daily average temperature for each city throughout the study period. Given that more stringent high thresholds, such as the 99th percentile, are commonly used in environment-related fields to define extreme heatwaves, the thresholds selected in this study can simultaneously cover both high-temperature events and relatively extreme high-temperature events, enabling a more comprehensive exploration of the impact of different heat levels on the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD). These thresholds were integrated with varying durations to define heatwaves, encompassing high-temperature events persisting for ≥ 2 consecutive days,≥3 consecutive days, and ≥ 4 consecutive days. This combinatorial approach yielded a total of 12 distinct heatwave definitions (4 temperature thresholds × 3 durations), thereby enhancing the robustness of exposure assessment (Table 1) [19]. Subsequently, this study adopted the cumulative value of heatwave days under various definitions in 2011 as a quantitative indicator of heatwave exposure to assess the level of long-term exposure to high-temperature events among study subjects. The heatwave determination threshold in this study was not based solely on a single year but was set according to percentile standards derived from the region’s long-term climatic background (10–20 years of historical temperature distribution), thereby reflecting local climatic variability and population heat adaptation levels, which reduces the instability that may arise from using only single-year exposure data. Specifically, heatwave days were defined as a regional-level exposure proxy indicator based on urban daily average temperatures and allocated according to individual residential locations, with the cumulative value being the sum of all days in 2011 that met the corresponding heatwave definition. If a heatwave definition was “a maximum daily temperature ≥ 35°C lasting 3 days or more”,and a city experienced three high-temperature events in 2011 that met this standard, lasting 3 days,4 days, and 3 days respectively, then the cumulative number of heatwave days for that city in the year was 10 days. Individuals residing in that city were all considered to have a long-term heatwave exposure of 10 days.

Covariates

This study incorporated multiple covariate data collected from the 2011 baseline survey to control for potential confounding factors. Demographic variables included sex, age, marital status, and place of residence, while body mass index (BMI) was included as a key anthropometric indicator. Furthermore, data on participants’ lifestyle behaviors were gathered, with a particular focus on smoking status and alcohol consumption. The model also incorporated health-related confounding variables, including self-reported chronic disease histories such as diabetes, heart disease, and stroke, while hypertension was included as an independent risk factor for separate analysis to avoid path interference between variables. These covariates are considered likely to affect the trajectory of renal function changes and were therefore adjusted as essential control factors in the statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Initially, a descriptive statistical analysis was conducted on the baseline characteristic variables. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and inter-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage), and the chi-square test (χ² test) was employed to assess differences in variables between participants with and without CKD.

This study employed a longitudinal cohort research framework, excluding subjects without follow-up data, and utilized a logistic regression model to analyze the association between heatwave exposure and chronic kidney disease (CKD) prevalence. The rationality of this analytical approach was demonstrated through a cohort study design. All heatwave-related abbreviations in the study followed a unified naming convention, denoting all heatwave definitions at the same temperature threshold as “HW+percentile threshold” rather than using a single heatwave indicator. For instance, HW90 refers to heatwave definitions based on the 90th percentile temperature threshold. Three models were developed through the sequential incorporation of covariates: Model 1 was unadjusted, with no covariates included; Model 2 was adjusted for demographic variables, such as age, gender, and place of residence; and Model 3 was further adjusted for health-related variables based on Model 2,including stroke, heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, self-reported kidney disease, smoking status, and alcohol consumption behavior.

This study conducted stratified analysis by age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and hypertension status to identify potential effect modifiers. Interaction terms were included in the regression model to test whether the association between heatwave exposure and chronic kidney disease (CKD) differed statistically across subgroups. Subsequently, sensitivity analyses were conducted employing two distinct approaches: (1) defining the outcome as a 30% reduction in eGFR and (2) transforming heatwave exposure into a binary variable, in order to evaluate the robustness of the study findings. All statistical analyses were executed using R software (Version 4.3.1;R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Two-tailed tests were applied, with a p < 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of sample

Table 2 delineates the baseline characteristics of the study cohort, comprising 6,718 individuals, of whom 54.5% (n = 3,659) were female, with a mean age of 58.21 years. Throughout the follow-up period,300 participants were diagnosed with CKD. Participants were stratified based on CKD status, revealing that those who developed CKD were statistically more likely to be older, possess lower educational attainment, and exhibit a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and stroke (p < 0.05). Furthermore, individuals who developed CKD were exposed to a significantly greater number of heatwave days (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the cumulative exposure to heatwave days under 12 heatwave definitions showed statistically significant differences between the groups, with the number of heatwave days in the CKD group being significantly higher than that in the non-CKD group (all P < 0.001). This suggests that in subsequent studies, the cumulative number of heatwave days under different heatwave definitions will be used as exposure indicators to further analyze their association with the risk of CKD onset.

Association between heat wave exposure and CKD

Figure 2 depicts the relationship between heatwave exposure and the risk of developing CKD. After controlling for potential confounders—such as age, sex, educational level, marital status, place of residence, hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, smoking, and alcohol consumption—the logistic regression analysis revealed a positive association between all twelve definitions of heatwave exposure and CKD risk. Furthermore, the impact of heatwave exposure on CKD risk was found to intensify with higher temperature thresholds (ranging from the 90th to the 97.5th percentile) and longer durations of heatwave events (spanning 2 to 4 consecutive days), indicating a dose–response relationship.However, under the same temperature threshold, no extension of heatwave duration from 2 days to 4 days was observed, with the odds ratio showing a continuous and monotonic increasing trend, except for a slight rise at the 97.5th threshold. Therefore, these results can only robustly support the trend of increased CKD risk with elevated temperature thresholds.

Results of the subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Figure 4 illustrates the findings from subgroup analyses evaluating the relationship between heatwave exposure and the risk of CKD. The analyses were stratified by variables including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and place of residence. Additionally, interaction terms were examined. The association between heatwave exposure and CKD risk was consistently positive across all subgroups, as indicated by odds ratios greater than 1. Notably, under conditions of high-intensity heatwaves-specifically HW11 (97.5th-3d) and HW12 (97.5th-4d)-significant interactions were identified among participants with hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease (p-value of interaction <0.05). This study employed a causal framework based on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to guide variable selection and model construction. In this DAG, heatwave exposure was defined as the exposure variable, chronic kidney disease (CKD) as the outcome variable, and age, sex, body mass index (BMI), urban/rural residence, lifestyle, socioeconomic status, and chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke) as confounding factors. To avoid collision bias, we did not adjust for variables that might act as collision factors in the causal pathway, thereby minimizing bias and ensuring robust estimation of the association between heatwave exposure and CKD.

Discussion

It is anticipated that global climate warming will result in an increased frequency ofextreme heat events, which could have significant implications for human health, particularly affecting kidney function [20]. This study employed a systematic analytical approach, utilizing data from a large, nationally representative cohort encompassing multiple regions of China, to assess the association between heatwave exposure and the morbidity of CKD. The findings consistently indicated a significant positive association between heatwave exposure and CKD risk, irrespective of the heatwave definition used, including HW90, HW95,and HW97 percentile thresholds with durations ranging from 2 to 4 days. These results suggest that heatwaves may constitute an independent risk factor for CKD. Additionally, the robustness of our findings was substantiated through two distinct types of sensitivity analyses. Initially, employing a ≥ 30% decline in eGFR as an alternative endpoint for CKD revealed significant associations across all definitions of heatwaves. Subsequently, when heatwave exposure was considered as a binary variable, the direction and magnitude of the associations remained consistent with the primary analysis, thereby further reinforcing the validity of our conclusions. Collectively, the evidence suggests that heatwave exposure constitutes an independent environmental risk factor for the development of CKD.

In our study, we observed that, for a constant duration of heatwaves, the ORs for CKD risk increased significantly when heatwaves were defined using higher temperature percentiles (e.g. HW97.5). This indicates that the intensity of heatwaves is a critical determinant of renal health outcomes. Furthermore, at a constant temperature threshold (e.g. HW97.5), the risk of CKD increased progressively with the duration of heatwave exposure, from 2 to 3 and then 4 days. This time-accumulative effect suggests that the kidneys may exhibit a form of “cumulative sensitivity” to prolonged heat exposure, potentially mediated by mechanisms such as heat stress response, dehydration, and the depletion of the body’s compensatory recovery capacity. The study focuses on the short-term cumulative effects of heatwaves, conceptualizing them as intermittent environmental events with short-term health impacts. The exposure definition adopted aims to reflect the combined effects of heatwave intensity and duration in the short term. These findings elucidate the association between heatwaves and kidney health from the perspective of short-term cumulative exposure, providing epidemiological evidence for the rationality of this exposure metric. These findings align with previous studies [21–23], which have similarly demonstrated that both the intensity and duration of heatwaves can influence the extent of kidney function impairment. Collectively, this evidence supports the existence of a dose–response relationship, further reinforcing the credibility of heatwaves as an environmental risk factor for CKD. Additionally, these insights provide a scientific basis for setting intervention thresholds and response levels in heatwave early warning systems.

The clustering analysis conducted in this study has identified two distinct “high-risk” regional patterns in China: (1) dual high-risk zones, characterized by both elevated CKD prevalence and extended durations of heatwave exposure, and (2) heatwave-dominant zones, where CKD prevalence is comparatively low, yet heatwave exposure is significantly prolonged. The former (regions with overlapping environmental stressors and health vulnerabilities) face dual pressures of high heat exposure loads and weak population health protection capabilities, constituting high-risk vulnerable areas for chronic kidney disease (CKD) and serving as priority regions for public health interventions and enhanced early prevention and control of chronic diseases. The latter (regions with currently lower CKD disease burden), although exhibiting relatively stable incidence risks at present, will see increasingly prominent cumulative effects of long-term heat stress due to frequent extreme heat events and escalating duration and intensity of heatwaves under continuous climate warming. The CKD incidence risk may significantly rise in the future, necessitating early warning systems and routine health protection strategies to address long-term disease burden pressures caused by climate change. These spatial clustering results underscore significant geographic heterogeneity in the distribution of heatwave exposure and CKD prevalence across China.These findings are consistent with earlier research. One study found a strong link between high temperatures and increased CKD hospitalizations in China, especially in subtropical cities [24–26]. Another study showed higher CKD risks from extreme urban temperatures compared to rural areas [24–26]. The aforementioned differences may be closely related to urban heat island effect and high humidity environment. Urban areas are characterized by dense population, concentrated buildings, and insufficient green spaces, which readily form a significant heat island effect, leading to persistently elevated temperatures. Concurrently, higher air humidity inhibits human heat dissipation, further exacerbating heat stress-induced damage, collectively contributing to the increased incidence and hospitalization risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) among urban populations. Identifying “dual high-risk zones” highlights the need for strategies that integrate climate adaptation and heat mitigation with traditional chronic disease management. “Heatwave-dominant zones” should be prioritized for prevention, as they face intense heat exposure that could rapidly increase CKD morbidity with future climate changes.

Heatwave-induced kidney injuries can present clinically with symptoms such as hematuria, mild proteinuria, leukocyturia, elevated levels of urinary neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and decreased urine specific gravity [24–26]. Pathological observations predominantly reveal chronic glomerular ischemia, along with tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, renal interstitial inflammation, and only minor vascular lesions [27]. These effects are likely mediated through various physiological pathways. Under conditions of sustained high temperatures, the body endeavors to regulate heat through increased perspiration, leading to systemic dehydration and a reduction in blood volume. This process results in hemoconcentration and elevated plasma osmolality, which can cause a temporary increase in serum creatinine levels. Additionally, it activates the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), resulting in renal arteriolar vasoconstriction and decreased renal plasma flow [28–31]. These hemodynamic changes lead to glomerular hypoperfusion, elevated intraglomerular pressure, and mechanical stretch stress, which collectively facilitate the development of glomerulosclerosis [31, 32]. Concurrently, increased heat exposure can activate the TGF-β1 signaling pathway and the NLRP3 inflammasome, initiating tubulointerstitial inflammation. Furthermore, heat stress is strongly linked to pyroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction, which together expedite renal fibrosis and establish a pathological foundation for the progression of CKD [33, 34].

Moreover, engaging in moderate - to - high intensity physical activity under heat stress conditions may heighten the risk of exercise - induced muscle injury, thereby augmenting the susceptibility to heat - related renal damage [35]. Considering that obese individuals demonstrate diminished thermoregulatory ability, reduced tolerance to heat stress, and a greater vulnerability to metabolic disorders and elevated renal workload, this study incorporated obesity as a stratification factor to clarify its role in modulating the impact of heatwave-induced renal injury. Beyond the direct physiological effects, indirect risk factors commonly present during heatwaves may further exacerbate renal impairment through synergistic mechanisms. These factors encompass the use of non - steroidal anti - inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for the management of musculoskeletal pain associated with labor - intensive activities, a higher incidence of gastrointestinal infections, and electrolyte imbalances such as hypokalemia or hypernatremia, all of which have the potential to compromise renal perfusion or intensify tubular injury [36]. Our study revealed a significant association between exposure to heatwaves and the morbidity of CKD during the follow-up period, aligning with previous research findings [37, 38]. The evidence shows that heatwaves’ impact on renal function goes beyond short - term physiological disruptions, involving complex biological interactions and additive effects that may increase CKD susceptibility. These mechanisms suggest that extreme heat is a biologically plausible critical environmental risk factor for CKD.

Our study confirms that, among Chinese adults aged 45 and older, heatwave intensity and duration are significantly associated with increased CKD risk. By filling gaps in understanding heatwaves as a CKD environmental risk factor, this research adds valuable evidence. With global climate change, heat - related CKD burden is expected to rise, emphasizing the need for comprehensive strategies to protect kidney health. Urban planning should focus on improving microclimatic conditions in high - risk areas. Previous research shows insufficient shading is linked to faster renal function decline [39]. Our clustering analysis further identified regions experiencing both prolonged heatwave exposure and high CKD prevalence. These areas should be prioritized in urban health planning, with a focus on enhancing green infrastructure, such as parks, green roofs, and vegetated spaces, to mitigate ambient heat exposure. Our findings further elucidate a dose–response relationship between heatwave exposure and the risk of CKD. Previous research has demonstrated that behavioral interventions, such as scheduled cooling breaks, electrolyte replenishment, and the adjustment of work hours, can effectively mitigate occupational heat stress [40, 41]. Our stratified interaction analysis reveals that chronic diseases can heighten the susceptibility to heat exposure. Consequently, public health policies and clinical interventions ought to prioritize individuals with chronic conditions during extreme heat events. By concentrating on surveillance and targeted care for these individuals, we can more effectively alleviate the health impacts of high temperatures. These findings underscore the necessity for coordinated environmental and health policies to address the burgeoning issue of heat - related chronic kidney disease in the context of climate change.

Despite the strengths of this study, which include the utilization of a nationally representative sample, the application of multiple heatwave definitions, and the adoption of a prospective cohort design that enhances both generalizability and robustness, several limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, individual heatwave exposure was defined using city-level temperature monitoring data.This approach lacked high-resolution measurements, behavioral information, participant mobility, and temporal changes in exposure, potentially introducing exposure misclassification bias. Secondly, to ensure accurate outcome assessment, only participants who underwent serum creatinine testing in 2015 were included. While this strategy enhanced the validity of the CKD definition, it may have introduced selection bias, which could affect the representativeness of the risk estimates. Third, despite our efforts to adjust for major confounding variables in our models, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely dismissed, as shown in Fig. 6. This is particularly relevant given the lack of data on longitudinal kidney function measurements, medication use (such as herbal remedies or antipyretic/analgesic drugs), and ambient air pollution levels. Notably, many Chinese cities experience relatively high levels of ambient air pollution, which may act as an important confounder for the association between heatwave exposure and chronic kidney disease. This may limit the generalizability of our findings to regions with lower air pollution levels [42]. Fourth, stratified analysis has obvious limitations: In terms of sample size, after stratification by age, gender, BMI, and hypertension status, the sample sizes of each subgroup are split, with some subgroups having smaller sample sizes, which may reduce the statistical power and make it difficult to accurately detect subtle differences in the association between heatwave exposure and CKD within subgroups. In terms of the number of results, the combination of 12 heatwave definitions and 4 stratification variables generates a large number of stratified analysis results. Certain subgroups demonstrate suboptimal result stability owing to inadequate sample sizes and are susceptible to false positives or false negatives, thereby augmenting the complexity of result interpretation. Fifthly, the study population in this research is confined to Chinese adults, and the generalizability of the results to non - Chinese populations remains undetermined [5–41]. Based on this, future studies ought to integrate high - resolution environmental exposure data, comprehensive behavioral records, and biomarkers to more effectively clarify the potential causal mechanisms of the association between heatwave exposure and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Simultaneously, the design of stratified analysis should be optimized, and the sample size should be rationally expanded to enhance the reliability of subgroup analysis results.

Notwithstanding certain constraints, this study employed a nationally representative dataset, providing a distinctive and valuable avenue to investigate the influence of climatic factors on renal health. By integrating high - resolution meteorological data with longitudinal health follow - up data of Chinese adults aged 45 and above, we systematically evaluated the association between heatwave exposure and the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) under diverse heatwave definitions. Our findings present empirical evidence from the Chinese population, corroborating the hypothesis that environmental heat exposure is a significant exogenous risk factor for the onset of CKD.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides systematic evidence linking different heatwave exposure definitions to the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD), suggesting a potential positive correlation between the two.The findings indicate that extreme heat, as a potentially intervenable environmental factor, warrants inclusion in comprehensive CKD prevention and management strategies.Future research could further utilize longitudinal cohort data to investigate population-specific differences in heatwave adaptation and evaluate the effectiveness of relevant climate health interventions.

This study still has certain limitations: First, the analysis was conducted based on observational data; although some confounding factors were controlled, the influence of unmeasured confounding variables on the results cannot be entirely excluded, making it difficult to directly infer causal relationships. Second, there is some heterogeneity in the definition and measurement methods of heatwave exposure, which may affect the assessment of association effects. Finally, the study did not thoroughly examine differences in heatwave exposure adaptation among different populations; future research could conduct more targeted stratified analyses to further validate and expand the conclusions of this study.
