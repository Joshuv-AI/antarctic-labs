Chebotarev geodesic theorem: split case.
Alberto Acosta Reche

Abstract
We study the prime geodesic theorem (PGT) in congruence classes of SL 2 ⁡ ( ℤ ) \SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) . We generalize previous work of Luo and Sarnak [ 28 ] and of Soundararajan and Young [ 45 ] , and prove that the geodesic analogue of the Chebotarev density theorem holds with exponent 25 / 36 + ε 25/36+\varepsilon . In particular, we deduce that the PGT holds with exponent 25 / 36 + ε 25/36+\varepsilon for any congruence subgroup of SL 2 ⁡ ( ℤ ) \SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) .



1
Introduction.

1.1
Prime geodesic theorem.

Let
\{\pm I\}\subset\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be a discrete cofinite subgroup. Let
\{P\}_{\Gamma}
range over hyperbolic
\Gamma
-conjugacy classes and let
X\geq 1
be a parameter, to be taken large. The prime geodesic theorem (PGT), is concerned with the asymptotics as
X\to\infty
of the sum

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
N(P)\leq X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{\log N(P)}{\nu_{\Gamma}(P)}.

(1)

Here
\nu_{\Gamma}(P)
is the positive integer such that
P=P_{0}^{\nu(P)}
for a
\Gamma
-primitive hyperbolic element
P_{0}\in\Gamma
. The quantities
\log N(P)
are the lengths of the closed geodesics of
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
, and the conjugacy classes
\{P\}_{\Gamma}
can be considered as analogues of rational prime powers. In this sense,
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X)
can be considered as a geodesic analogue of the second Chebyshev function
x\mapsto\sum_{p^{k}\leq x}\log p
.

In analogy with the prime number theorem, the PGT asserts that

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X)\sim X\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as }\hskip 10.00002ptX\to\infty.

(2)

When
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is compact this result goes back to Huber, see
[
15
, Satz 9]
. We can be much more precise in (
2
). Recall that the small eigenvalues of the Laplacian of
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
are those eigenvalues which lie in
[0,1/4)
. Let
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j}
denote the finitely-many small eigenvalues of
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
counted with multiplicity. In particular,
0
is always an eigenvalue with multiplicity one. Then we can write

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X)=\sum_{j}\frac{X^{1/2+\sqrt{1/4-\lambda_{j}}}}{1/2+\sqrt{1/4-\lambda_{j}}}+E_{\Gamma}(X).

(3)

When
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is compact Huber proved that
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O(X^{5/6})
, see
[
16
, Satz III]
. Selberg observed
[
41
, page 179]
that his trace formula could be used to derive results about the distribution of lengths of prime geodesics, but he did not publish a proof of the PGT. Sarnak followed Selberg’s idea and provided a direct proof that
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O(X^{3/4}(\log X)^{3})
for any cofinite discrete group
\Gamma
, see
[
38
, Theorem 3.4]
. Iwaniec later refined this proof and obtained
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O(X^{3/4})
, see
[
20
, Theorem 10.5]
. By a less direct argument using the theory of the Selberg zeta function, Hejhal treated the general case of a multiplier system of arbitrary real weight and proved that
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O(X^{3/4}(\log X)^{1/2})

[
13
, Theorem 3.4]
in that generality.

We consider the estimate
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O(X^{3/4})
to be the baseline bound for the PGT. For a general cofinite group
\Gamma
this estimate is yet to be improved. By analogy with the prime number theorem, one is lead to expect that the bound
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O_{\varepsilon}(X^{1/2+\varepsilon})
holds for any
\varepsilon
. This is believed to be true, but a proof is far from reach at the moment. If proven, this estimate would be optimal up to an arbitrarily small power of
X
. Indeed, it is known that

E_{\Gamma}(X)=\Omega_{\pm}(X^{1/2-\varepsilon})

for any
\varepsilon>0
, see
[
13
, note 18 in page 503]
.

The baseline bound on
E_{\Gamma}(X)
has been improved in particular cases of
\Gamma
. The case that has attracted the most attention is
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
because of its connection with class numbers of quadratic orders. Indeed, Sarnak showed
[
39
]
that

\Psi_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}(X)=\sum_{2<t\leq X^{1/2}+X^{-1/2}}\sum_{du^{2}=t^{2}-4}h(d)\log(\epsilon_{d}^{2})=2\sum_{2<t\leq X^{1/2}+X^{-1/2}}\sum_{du^{2}=t^{2}-4}\sqrt{d}L(1;\chi_{d}),

(4)

where the inner sums are over positive integers
d
dividing
t^{2}-4
and such that the quotient is a square. Here,
h(d)
and
\log\epsilon_{d}
are the class number and the regulator, respectively, of the unique quadratic order of discriminant
d
,
\chi_{d}
is the quadratic character (not necessarily primitive) associated to the discriminant
d
, and
L(s,\chi_{d})
is the corresponding Dirichlet series. The second equality follows from the class number formula.

When
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, improvements to the baseline bound were given, chronologically, by Iwaniec, by Luo and Sarnak, and by Cai, see
[
18
]
,
[
28
]
and
[
3
]
, respectively. The bounds they proved for
E_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}(X)
were
O_{\varepsilon}(X^{35/48+\varepsilon}),O_{\varepsilon}(X^{7/10+\varepsilon})
and
O_{\varepsilon}(X^{71/102+\varepsilon})
, respectively.

The last improvement was due to Soundararajan and Young, see
[
45
, Theorem 1.1]
. Combining the work of Luo and Sarnak with a clever use of Equation (
4
), Soundararajan and Young proved that
E_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}(X)=O_{\varepsilon}(X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon})
. Here
\theta>0
is any subconvexity exponent in conductor aspect for quadratic Dirichlet characters. By work of Conrey and Iwaniec
[
6
, Corollary 1.5]
we can take
\theta=1/6+\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
. Thus, Soundararajan and Young were able to prove that
E_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}(X)=O_{\varepsilon}(X^{25/36+\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
. This estimate remains the best unconditional bound for the error term in the PGT at the moment of writing this paper.

Since there are no nonzero small eigenvalues in the case of
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the result of Soundararajan and Young simply says that

\Psi_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}=X+O_{\varepsilon}(X^{25/36+\varepsilon}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0.

Regarding other cofinite groups, Luo, Rudnick and Sarnak claimed in the last paragraph of
[
27
]
that the arguments in
[
28
]
could be generalized to prove that
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O(X^{7/10+\varepsilon})
holds for any congruence subgroup
\Gamma
, but they did not provide further details.

Later on Cherubini, Wu and Zábrádi generalized the arguments of Soundararajan and Young to the case where
\Gamma=\Gamma(q)
is a principal congruence subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus, assuming the claim of Luo, Rudnick and Sarnak, they proved that
E_{\Gamma(q)}(X)=O_{q,\varepsilon}(X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon})
for any
q\geq 1
, see
[
5
, Theorem 1.10]
.

Finally, the baseline bound has also been improved for certain cocompact groups built from quaternion algebras. Let
\mathcal{O}
be a maximal order in
B
, an indefinite quaternion division algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
\nrd:B^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
denote the reduced norm map, and let
\mathcal{O}^{1}:=\{x\in\mathcal{O}:\nrd(x)=1\}
. Via the isomorphism
B\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}\simeq M_{2}(\mathbb{R})
we can view
\mathcal{O}^{1}
as a cocompact discrete group of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Using the Jacquet-Langlands correspondence, Koyama deduced from the result of Luo, Rudnick and Sarnak that
E_{\mathcal{O}^{1}}(X)=O_{\varepsilon}(X^{7/10+\varepsilon})
holds for any
\varepsilon>0
, see
[
24
]
. More recently, Tang, Wu, Yang and Yang have generalized the bound of Koyama to principal congruence subgroups of
\mathcal{O}^{1}
, see
[
46
]
.

Chebotarev geodesic theorem.

Let
\{\pm I\}\subset\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
be two cofinite discrete groups, such that
\Gamma^{\prime}
is normal in
\Gamma
. In his PhD thesis Sarnak compared the normal covering
\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
to a Galois extension of number fields
K/F
, see
[
38
, section 3]
. The analogy is summarized in the following table.

In particular, the geodesic analogue of the Frobenius map simply sends a
\Gamma
-primitive hyperbolic conjugacy class
\{P\}_{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
to the conjugacy class of
P\modulo\Gamma^{\prime}
inside
\widetilde{\Gamma}:=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\Gamma
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a conjugacy class of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. In view of the table above, the geodesic analogue of the Chebotarev density theorem is the asymptotic

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
N(P)\leq X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{\log N(P)}{\nu_{\Gamma}(P)}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(P\modulo\Gamma^{\prime})\sim\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{|\widetilde{\Gamma}|}X

(5)

as
X\to\infty
. Following Sarnak we call a result like this a
Chebotarev geodesic theorem
(CGT). As with the PGT, one can give a much more precise version of (
5
).

Let
f
be a class function on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and introduce the twisted Chebyshev sum

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
N(P)\leq X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{\log N(P)}{\nu_{\Gamma}(P)}f(P\modulo\Gamma^{\prime}).

(6)

If we define the quantity

\langle f,\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\rangle_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}:=\frac{1}{|\widetilde{\Gamma}|}\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\Gamma}}f(g),

then Equation (
3
) can be generalized to

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f)=\langle f,\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\rangle_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}X+\sum_{j}c_{j}(f)\frac{X^{1/2+\sqrt{1/4-\lambda_{j}}}}{1/2+\sqrt{1/4-\lambda_{j}}}+E_{\Gamma}(X;f),

(7)

where the
j
-sum is over the nonzero small eigenvalues of
\Gamma^{\prime}
, and each of the coefficients
c_{j}(f)
can be expressed as a sum of inner products of
f
with characters of representations of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. Sarnak proved that
E_{\Gamma}(X;f)=O_{f}(X^{3/4}\log(X)^{3})
, see
[
38
, Theorem 3.16]
. Following the proof of Theorem 10.5 in
[
20
]
one can remove the factor
\log(X)^{3}
without any difficulty. As with the PGT, we consider the estimate
E_{\Gamma}(X;f)=O_{f}(X^{3/4})
to be the baseline bound for the CGT. It seems that even in the case of congruence subgroups of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
the baseline bound has not been improved.

1.3
Results.

In order to state our main result, we need to introduce a somewhat ad-hoc notion of subconvexity exponent.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
\theta>0
is
admissible
if for any
q\geq 1
and any Dirichlet character
\chi_{1}
of level
q
, there exists
A>0
, possibly depending on
q
, such that

|L(s;\chi_{1}\chi_{2})|\ll_{q,\theta}\mathfrak{q}(\chi_{2})^{\theta}|s|^{A}

holds for
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
and any quadratic Dirichlet character
\chi_{2}
.

In this definition
\mathfrak{q}(\chi_{2})
is the conductor of
\chi_{2}
. By the work of Petrow and Young we know that the exponent
\theta=1/6+\varepsilon
is admissible for any
\varepsilon>0
, see
[
32
]
and
[
33
]
. The generalized Lindelöf hypothesis predicts that
\theta=\varepsilon
is admissible for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
q
be a positive integer,
\Gamma
a congruence subgroup of level
q
,
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma(q)
and
f
a class function on
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\Gamma
. In this case the geodesic analogue of the Frobenius map is simply reduction modulo
q
. Note that, by work of Kim and Sarnak
[
23
, second appendix]
we know that the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of
\Gamma(q)
satisfies
\lambda_{1}\geq 1/4-(7/64)^{2}
. Similarly as before define

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f):=[\{\pm I\}\Gamma:\Gamma]\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
N(P)\leq X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{\log N(P)}{\nu_{\Gamma}(P)}f(P\modulo q),

(8)

where the factor
[\{\pm I\}\Gamma:\Gamma]
accounts for the fact that the group
\Gamma
does not necessarily contain
-I
. Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any congruence subgroup
\Gamma
, any class function
f
on
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and any admissible exponent
\theta>0
we have

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f)=\langle f,\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\rangle_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}X+O_{f,\theta,\varepsilon}(X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon}).

Since
\theta=1/6+\varepsilon
is admissible we deduce unconditionally that

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f)=\langle f,\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\rangle_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}X+O_{f,\varepsilon}(X^{25/36+\varepsilon}).

This result extends the estimate of Soundararajan and Young
[
45
, Theorem 1.1]
to an arbitrary class function
f
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
, whereas the original result applied only to constant functions on
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})
. To illustrate the generality of Theorem
1.2
, let us consider two types of class functions.

Suppose that
\{\pm I\}\subset\Gamma
and let
f=\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}
. Then
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}})=\Psi_{\Gamma}(X)
as defined earlier in (
1
).

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\{\pm I\}\subset\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be a congruence subgroup. Then for any admissible
\theta>0
we have

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X)=X+O_{\Gamma,\theta,\varepsilon}(X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon}).

Thus, the PGT holds with error term
E_{\Gamma}(X)=O_{\varepsilon}(X^{25/36+\varepsilon})
for an arbitrary congruence subgroup. This result is a strengthened version of
[
27
, Corollary 1.2]
.

Another interesting family of class functions
f
is the one consisting of characteristic functions of fibers of the trace map. When
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the resulting Chebyshev sums are particularly interesting, since they are averages of class numbers with congruence conditions. Indeed, from (
4
) it follows that for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
we have

\Psi_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}(X;\mathbbm{1}_{\Tr^{-1}(a)})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2<t\leq X^{1/2}+X^{-1/2}\\
t=a\modulo q\end{subarray}}\sum_{du^{2}=t^{2}-4}h(d)\log(\epsilon_{d}^{2})=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2<t\leq X^{1/2}+X^{-1/2}\\
t=a\modulo q\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{du^{2}=t^{2}-4}\sqrt{d}L(1;\chi_{d}).

(9)

Theorem
1.2
allows us to evaluate these sums very accurately.

Corollary 1.4
.

For any positive integer
q
, any congruence subgroup
\Gamma
of level
q
, any
a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
and any admissible exponent
\theta>0
we have

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\mathbbm{1}_{\Tr^{-1}(a)})=\frac{|\{\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}\mid\Tr(\gamma)=a\modulo q\}|}{|\widetilde{\Gamma}|}X+O_{q,\theta,\varepsilon}(X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon}).

Corollary
1.4
can be seen as the geodesic analogue of Dirichlet’s theorem on arithmetic progressions. It generalizes and improves on the results of
[
4
]
and
[
7
]
in the
X
-aspect. These two papers considered only the case
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and obtained a worse bound for the error term in the
X
-aspect.

Remark 1.5
.

If
q\geq 3
, then
\{\pm I\}\not\subset\Gamma(q)
. Thus, in the case of congruence subgroups of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
our setup is a little bit more general than that of
[
38
, Theorem 3.16]
. The drawback of this small subtlety is that, in order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we need to deal with Maass forms of both weights
0
and
1
, whereas Sarnak worked only with Maass forms of weight
0
. The reward, however, is that we are able to deduce Corollary
1.4
as stated. If we had worked with
\Gamma^{\prime}=\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q)
instead of
\Gamma(q)
we would only have been able to study the combined sums
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\mathbbm{1}_{\Tr^{-1}(a)})+\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\mathbbm{1}_{\Tr^{-1}(-a)})
, instead of each of them separately.

Before stating other results it is convenient to introduce a definition.

Definition 1.6
.

We say that
(\Gamma,\chi)
is a
congruence character of level
q
if
\{\pm I_{2}\}\Gamma(q)\subset\Gamma\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\chi:\Gamma\rightarrow S^{1}
is a character which is trivial on
\Gamma(q)
.

By a formal argument, Theorem
1.2
is reduced to the study of the sums
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)
where
(\Gamma,\chi)
is a congruence character of level
q
, see Section
2
. We let
k\in\{0,1\}
be defined implicitly by the equation
\chi(-I)=(-1)^{k}
. Let
\{u_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis of Maass cusp forms of weight
k
for
(\Gamma,\chi)
. Let the eigenvalue of
u_{j}
be parametrized as
\lambda_{j}=1/4+t_{j}^{2}
, where either
t_{j}\geq 0
or
\text{Im}(t_{j})>0
.

By adapting the proof of
[
18
, Lemma 1]
we can show that

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=\delta_{\chi=1}X+\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq T}\left(\frac{X^{1/2+it_{j}}}{1/2+it_{j}}+\frac{X^{1/2-it_{j}}}{1/2-it_{j}}\right)+O_{q}\left(\frac{X}{T}\log^{2}X\right)

(10)

for parameters
X\geq 10
and
1\leq T\leq\sqrt{X}(\log X)^{-2}
, see Section
5
. Thus, the Chebyshev sums
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)
are connected with the exponential sums

\mathcal{S}(T,X):=\sum_{0\leq t_{j}\leq T}X^{it_{j}}.

(11)

By a weak version of Weyl’s law (see (
43
) below) the trivial bound on
\mathcal{S}(T,X)
is

\mathcal{S}(T,X)\ll_{q}T^{2},

(12)

which combined with (
10
) recovers the baseline bound
E_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=O_{q}(X^{3/4}\log X)
.
Following the prediction of
[
31
, Conjecture 2.2]
we expect that for any
X,T\geq 1
we have

\mathcal{S}(T,X)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}T(XT)^{\varepsilon}.

If true, this estimate would imply the Chebotarev geodesic theorem with the error term
O_{q,\varepsilon}(X^{1/2+\varepsilon})
. Unfortunately, a bound of such strength is far from reach at the moment. On the positive side, we do have two very useful estimates on
\mathcal{S}(T,X)
. The first of them generalizes Equation (58) in
[
28
]
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(\Gamma,\chi)
be a congruence character of level
q
. Then, for
1\leq T\leq X^{1/2}
we have

\mathcal{S}(T,X)\ll_{\varepsilon,q}T^{5/4}X^{1/8}(XT)^{\varepsilon}.

(13)

The second estimate generalizes
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
(\Gamma,\chi)
be a congruence character of level
q
. Let
\theta>0
be an admissible exponent. Then, for
1\leq T\leq X^{1/2}
we have

\mathcal{S}(T,X)\ll_{\varepsilon,q,\theta}X^{1/4+\theta/2}T^{1/2}(XT)^{\varepsilon}.

(14)

This result is closely connected to
[
45
, Theorem 3.2]
, which we are also able to generalize.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be a congruence subgroup of level
q
. Let
f
be a class function on
\widetilde{\Gamma}:=\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma
. Let
\theta>0
be an admissible exponent. Then, for any
u\geq 1
we have

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;f)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f)=\langle f,\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\rangle_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}u+O_{f,\theta,\varepsilon}(u^{1/2}X^{1/4+\theta/2+\varepsilon}).

Proof of Theorem
1.2
assuming Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
.

Interpolating between bounds (
13
) and (
14
) we deduce that

\mathcal{S}(T,X)\ll_{q,\theta,\varepsilon}TX^{1/6+\theta/6+\varepsilon}

when
1\leq T\leq X^{1/2}
. Integration by parts then leads to

\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq T}\left(\frac{X^{1/2+it_{j}}}{1/2+it_{j}}+\frac{X^{1/2-it_{j}}}{1/2-it_{j}}\right)\ll_{q,\theta,\varepsilon}X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon}.

Therefore, letting
T=X^{1/3-\theta/6}
in (
10
) we obtain

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=\delta_{\chi=1}X+O_{q,\theta,\varepsilon}(X^{2/3+\theta/6+\varepsilon}),

which finishes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

∎

Remark 1.10
.

By following the discussion in
[

1.7

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1/2}T^{1/2}X^{1/4}(NX)^{\varepsilon}

(15)

\displaystyle+N^{-1/2}\int_{(1/2)}|s|^{-A}\left(\sum_{t_{j}\geq 0}|\mathcal{L}(s;|u_{j}|^{2})|\omega(t_{j})\exp(-|t_{j}|/T)\right)\,d|s|,

The task now is to give, for some choice of orthonormal basis

\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

\pi_{j}

\pi_{j}

\overline{\pi}_{j}

[
21
]
. By work of Iwaniec
[
19
]
and of Hoffstein and Lockhart
[
14
]

(1+|t_{j}|)^{-\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}\omega(t_{j})|\rho_{j}(1)|^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}(1+|t_{j}|)^{\varepsilon}.

By work of Gelbart and Jacquet
[
11
]
, who generalized previous work of Shimura

]
, we can write

L(s;\pi_{j}\otimes\overline{\pi_{j}})=\zeta(s)L(s;\Ad(\pi_{j})),

where
L(s;\Ad(\pi_{j}))
is the standard
L
-function associated to

, a cuspidal automorphic form of

. The spectral average of

can be estimated by using the spectral large sieve inequality of Deshouillers and Iwaniec

(18)

for some
A>0
whose value is not important for this discussion. By Weyl’s law, this estimate is as strong as the Lindelöf hypothesis on average. Another application of (

) and the previous discussion gives

\sum_{|t_{j}|\leq T}\omega(t_{j})^{2}|\mathcal{L}(s;|u_{j}|^{2})|^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{2+\varepsilon}|s|^{A},

(19)

where the value of
A>0
may be larger. Luo and Sarnak obtained this estimate in the case when
\Gamma=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Inserting it in (

) and optimizing the value of
N
finishes the proof of Theorem
1.2
in this case.

The main obstacle in generalizing the argument of Luo and Sarnak is the following: given an arbitrary congruence pair
(\Gamma,\chi)
, we need to construct an orthonormal basis

whose Rankin–Selberg series are expressible in terms of

-functions. We solve this problem in Section

can be chosen in such a way that for each

there is a cuspidal automorphic form
\pi_{j}
of
\GL(2)
such that

\omega(t_{j})\mathcal{L}(s;|u_{j}|^{2})\approx\sum_{\psi\modulo q}L(s;\pi_{j}\otimes\overline{\pi_{j}}\otimes\psi),

(20)

see (
110
) and (
120
) below. After this structural identity is established we can adapt the argument of Luo and Sarnak and prove (
19
) for an arbitrary congruence character. Inserting this estimate in (
15
) and optimizing the value of
N
we can finish the proof of the general case of Theorem
1.7
, see Section
8
.

The proofs of Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.9
are quite similar to each other, and they are both based on the arguments of
[

]
. In Section

we define the
Bykovskii–Zagier

, where

, see (

) for the definition. The Dirichlet series

has analytic continuation past a simple pole at

with residue

\text{Res}_{s=1}Z(s;t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
\Tr(P)=t\end{subarray}}\frac{2\log N(P_{0})\chi(P)}{\pi\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})(N(P)^{1/2}-N(P)^{-1/2})},

Thus, the hyperbolic contribution to the Selberg trace formula can be expressed as a weighted sum over residues of

. This observation goes back to Zagier
[

. We can express the Bykovskii–Zagier series in terms of Dirichlet

-functions, roughly as

Z(s;t)\approx\sum_{\psi\modulo q}\frac{L(s,\psi)}{L(2s,\psi)}L(s,\psi_{D}\psi),

see the proof of Proposition

for a fundamental discriminant

and

is the quadratic Dirichlet associated to the discrimimant

Z(s;t)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}|t|^{2\theta+\varepsilon}|s|^{A}

(21)

Remark 1.11
.

Our proof of Theorem

[

2
Reduction to congruence characters.

Let
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}\subset\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be cofinite discrete groups. It may be the case that
-I\notin\Gamma_{2}
. Note that
[\Gamma_{2}:\Gamma_{1}]<\infty
since both groups are cofinite. A class function
f
on
\Gamma_{1}
can be induced to a class function on
\Gamma_{2}
by defining

\Ind_{\Gamma_{1}}^{\Gamma_{2}}(f)(\delta):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\backslash\Gamma_{2}\\
\gamma\delta\gamma^{-1}\in\Gamma_{1}\end{subarray}}f(\gamma\delta\gamma^{-1}).

The next lemma shows that the sums
\Psi(X;\cdot)
introduced in (
8
) are compatible with induction of class functions.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
X\geq 1
we have

\Psi_{\Gamma_{2}}(X;\Ind_{\Gamma_{1}}^{\Gamma_{2}}(f))=\Psi_{\Gamma_{1}}(X;f).

(22)

This lemma generalizes Theorem 2.2 from
[
47
]
, where it was assumed that
-I\in\Gamma
. We recall the proof for the convenience of the reader.

Proof.

For brevity we write
g=\Ind_{\Gamma_{1}}^{\Gamma_{2}}(f)
in this proof. For a hyperbolic element
P\in\Gamma_{1}
we let
\nu_{1}(P)
be the positive integer such that
P=P_{0}^{\nu_{1}(P)}
for a primitive element
P_{0}
of
\Gamma_{1}
. Let
C_{\Gamma_{1}}(P)
be the centralizer of
P
in
\Gamma_{1}
. Since we have

C_{\Gamma_{1}}(P)=\begin{cases}\{\pm P_{0}^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{ if }-I\in\Gamma_{1},\\
\{P_{0}^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\},&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

it follows that

\nu_{1}(P)=\frac{[\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{1}:\Gamma_{1}]}{2}\times[C_{\Gamma_{1}}(P):\langle P\rangle].

Similarly, if
Q\in\Gamma_{2}
is hyperbolic and we write
Q=Q_{0}^{\nu_{2}(Q)}
for a primitive hyperbolic element
Q_{0}
of
\Gamma_{2}
, then

\nu_{2}(Q)=\frac{[\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{2}:\Gamma_{2}]}{2}\times[C_{\Gamma_{2}}(Q):\langle Q\rangle].

In particular, any hyperbolic
P\in\Gamma_{1}
can be considered an element of both
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, and we have

\nu_{2}(P)=\nu_{1}(P)\cdot[C_{\Gamma_{2}}(P):C_{\Gamma_{1}}(P)]\cdot\frac{[\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{2}:\Gamma_{2}]}{[\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{1}:\Gamma_{1}]}.

(23)

The identity in (
22
) is equivalent to

[\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{2}:\Gamma_{2}]\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{Q\}_{\Gamma_{2}}\\
N(Q)=X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{g(Q)}{\nu_{2}(Q)}=[\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{1}:\Gamma_{1}]\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{P\}_{\Gamma_{1}}\\
N(P)=X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{f(P)}{\nu_{1}(P)}

(24)

for every
X>1
. Using the definition of
g
we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{Q\}_{\Gamma_{2}}\\
N(Q)=X\\
\Tr(Q)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{g(Q)}{\nu_{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{Q\}_{\Gamma_{2}}\\
N(Q)=X\\
\Tr(Q)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\nu_{2}(Q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}\\
\gamma^{-1}Q\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\end{subarray}}f(\gamma^{-1}Q\gamma)

(25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{P\}_{\Gamma_{1}}\\
N(P)=X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}f(P)\cdot\frac{\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\backslash\Gamma_{2}\mid\gamma P\gamma^{-1}\in\Gamma_{1}\text{ and }\{\gamma P\gamma^{-1}\}_{\Gamma_{1}}=\{P\}_{\Gamma_{1}}\}}{\nu_{2}(P)}.

It is easy to check that the inclusion
C_{\Gamma_{2}}(P)\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{2}
induces a bijection

C_{\Gamma_{1}}(P)\backslash C_{\Gamma_{2}}(P)\longleftrightarrow\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\backslash\Gamma_{2}\mid\gamma P\gamma^{-1}\in\Gamma_{1}\text{ and }\{\gamma P\gamma^{-1}\}_{\Gamma_{1}}=\{P\}_{\Gamma_{1}}\},

so that (
25
) can be simplified to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{Q\}_{\Gamma_{2}}\\
N(Q)=X\\
\Tr(Q)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{g(Q)}{\nu_{2}(Q)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{hyp. }\{P\}_{\Gamma_{1}}\\
N(P)=X\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}f(P)\cdot\frac{[C_{\Gamma_{2}}(P):C_{\Gamma_{1}}(P)]}{\nu_{2}(P)}.

If we combine this identity with (
23
) we deduce (
24
), finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

q

\Gamma

Lemma 2.2

(Artin)
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Every character in

is a linear combination with rational coefficients of characters of representations induced from one-dimensional representations of cyclic subgroups of

.

Since characters of representations of a finite group span the space of class functions we deduce the following: there exist subgroups

, characters
\chi_{i}
on
\widetilde{\Gamma_{i}}
and constants

such that

f=\sum_{i}c_{i}\Ind_{\widetilde{\Gamma_{i}}}^{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\chi_{i}.

(26)

By Frobenius reciprocity we have

\langle f,\mathbbm{1}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}\rangle_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\delta_{\chi_{i}=1}.

Let
\Gamma_{i}
be the pullback of
\widetilde{\Gamma_{i}}
via the projection
\Gamma\rightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;f)=\sum_{i}c_{i}\Psi_{\Gamma_{i}}(X;\chi_{i}).

If for some

\Ind_{\Gamma_{i}}^{\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{i}}(\chi_{i})=\chi_{i}^{+}+\chi_{i}^{-}.

Therefore, using Lemma

\Psi_{\Gamma_{i}}(X;\chi_{i})=\Psi_{\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{i}}(X;\chi^{+}_{i})+\Psi_{\{\pm I\}\Gamma_{i}}(X;\chi^{-}_{i}).

Background.

Refenrences for the material in this section are
[

, Section 4]
and
[

, Chapter 9]
.

For
\gamma\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
z\in\mathbb{H}
we let

j_{\gamma}(z)=\frac{c(\gamma)z+d(\gamma)}{|c(\gamma)z+d(\gamma)|}.

(30)

The slash operator
|_{k}
is defined for

\gamma\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
h:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

(h|_{k}\gamma)(z):=h(\gamma z)\left(\frac{c(\gamma)z+d(\gamma)}{|c(\gamma)z+d(\gamma)|}\right)^{-k}=h(\gamma z)j_{\gamma}(z)^{-k}.

(31)

This formula provides a right action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
on functions on
\mathbb{H}
. The Laplacian of weight
k
is denoted by
P
, so that in cartesian coordinates we have

P:=-y^{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})+iky\partial_{x}.

(32)

Let
d\mu(z)
denote the usual volume form on
\mathbb{H}
, which in Cartesian coordinates is expressed as

d\mu(z)=\frac{dx\,dy}{y^{2}}.

We let
\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})
denote the volume of a fundamental domain of
\Gamma
with respect to this measure. We denote by
L^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
the Hilbert space of functions
h:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
,
which obey the transformation property

h|_{k}\gamma=\chi(\gamma)h,\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma

and are square integrable on
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
, that is

\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}|h(z)|^{2}d\mu(z)<\infty.

The inner product is computed with respect to probability measure, so that

\langle h_{1},h_{2}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}h_{1}(z)\overline{h_{2}(z)}d\mu(z).

(33)

Note that this normalization differs from that in other sources.

Definition 3.1
.

Given two congruence characters, we write
(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})\subset(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})
when
\Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}
and
\chi_{2}\mid_{\Gamma_{1}}=\chi_{1}
.

The normalization (
33
) is convenient because whenever we have an inclusion of congruence characters
(\Gamma^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})\subset(\Gamma,\chi)
, then the natural inclusion

L^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Gamma^{\prime},\chi^{\prime})

is an isometry.

3.1.1
Fourier expansion.

The
\Gamma
-stabilizer of the cusp at
\infty
is of the form
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:x\in\ell\mathbb{Z}\}
for some
\ell=\ell(\Gamma)\geq 1
which divides
q
. Since
\chi
is trivial on
\Gamma(q)
we have

\chi(\begin{pmatrix}1&\ell\\
0&1\end{pmatrix})=e(-\alpha),

(34)

for a unique
\alpha\in\{0,\ell/q,2\ell/q\ldots,1-\ell/q\}
. Suppose that
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{H})
has the following three properties:

•

u|_{k}\gamma=\chi(\gamma)u
for
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

•

Pu=(1/4+t^{2})u
for some
t
. We can and will assume that, either
\text{Re}(t)>0
or else
\text{Im}(t)\geq 0
.

•

|u(z)|\leq y^{A}+y^{-B}
for all
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{H}
and some
A,B\geq 0
.

Then we have a Fourier expansion

u(z)=\rho(u;0^{+})\frac{y^{1/2+it}+y^{1/2-it}}{2}+\rho(u;0^{-})\frac{y^{1/2+it}-y^{1/2-it}}{2it}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Q}-\{0\}}\frac{\rho(u;n)}{|n|^{1/2}}W_{\frac{k\sgn(n)}{2},it}(4\pi|n|y)e(nx),

(35)

where the function
W_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
is the Whittaker function, as in
[
10
, Equation (4.19)]
, and where
(y^{1/2+it}-y^{1/2-it})/(2it)
is interpreted as
y^{1/2}\log y
when
t=0
. The Fourier expansion (
35
) has the following properties:

•

It converges absolutely, uniformly on compacta of
\mathbb{H}
.

•

\rho(u;n)=0
unless
n\in\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{\ell}
.

Note that the Fourier coefficients
\rho(u;n)
do not depend on the congruence character
(\Gamma,\chi)
, only on the function
u
.

3.1.2
Spectral decomposition.

The Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
has an orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)=L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)\oplus L^{2}_{\text{res}}(\Gamma,\chi)\oplus L^{2}_{\text{cont}}(\Gamma,\chi),

(36)

which arises from the spectral resolution of the operator
P
. The spaces
L_{0}^{2}
,
L^{2}_{\text{res}}
and
L^{2}_{\text{cont}}
are called, respectively, the cuspidal, the residual and the continuous parts of the spectrum of
P
. We will recall some information about each of these spaces.

The residual part.

If
\chi
is the trivial character, then
L^{2}_{\text{res}}(\Gamma,\chi)
is one-dimensional space of constant functions. Otherwise, if
\chi
is nontrivial, then
L^{2}_{\text{res}}(\Gamma,\chi)=\{0\}
is the trivial zero-dimensional space. Note that
k=1
implies that
\chi
is nontrivial. These facts depend on the fact that
(\Gamma,\chi)
is a congruence character.

The continuous part.

The continuous part of the spectrum is spanned by the Eisenstein series
z\mapsto E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;1/2+it)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We now recall how these are defined.

Note that, since
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the cusps of
\Gamma
are the points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
. We let
[\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma}
denote the
\Gamma
-orbit of cusps containing
\mathfrak{a}
, that is

[\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma}:=\{\mathfrak{b}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q}):\mathfrak{b}=\gamma\mathfrak{a}\text{ for some }\gamma\in\Gamma\}.

There are only finitely-many different
\Gamma
-orbits. For a cusp
\mathfrak{a}
of
\Gamma
we let
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}\}
be its stabilizer. This group is of the form
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\{\pm T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
T_{\mathfrak{a}}
is a parabolic element with positive trace. We let
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
be any matrix such that
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\infty=\mathfrak{a}
and

\sigma^{-1}_{\mathfrak{a}}T_{\mathfrak{a}}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

We define
\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}\in[0,1)
by the equation

\chi(T_{\mathfrak{a}})=e(-\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}}),

in particular
\alpha=\alpha_{\infty}
. We say that
\mathfrak{a}
is a
singular cusp
of
(\Gamma,\chi)
when
\chi(T_{\mathfrak{a}})=1
. We let
\kappa
denote the number of different
\Gamma
-orbits of singular cusps of
(\Gamma,\chi)
. We choose representatives
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{\kappa}
of these orbits with the condition that, if
\infty
is singular, then it is chosen as a representative of its class. Let
f_{s}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
denote the function
f_{s}(z)=\text{Im}(z)^{s}
. Then, to a singular cusp we can attach the Eisenstein series
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s)
, depending on
(z,s)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{C}
, defined for
\text{Re}(s)>1
by the absolutely convergent series

E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}\backslash\Gamma}\overline{\chi(\gamma)}\left[f_{s}|_{k}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\gamma)\right](z).

(37)

By construction, it follows that the identity

E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)|_{k}\gamma=\chi(\gamma)E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)

holds for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Also, we note that the definition of
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z,s)
does not depend on the choice of
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}
. On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{b}=\gamma\mathfrak{a}
for
\gamma\in\Gamma
, then we have

E_{\mathfrak{b}}(z;s)=\overline{\chi(\gamma)}E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s),

(38)

so that
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s)
depends very mildly on the representative of the equivalence class
[\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma}
. We will use the more complete notation
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s;\Gamma,\chi)
when we want to emphasize the dependence on
(\Gamma,\chi)
. The Eisenstein series can be continued meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}
. On the region
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
the only possible singularity can occur at
s=1
(we use here that
(\Gamma,\chi)
is a congruence character). When
\chi
is the trivial character there is a simple pole at
s=1
with residue

\text{Res}_{s=1}E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s)=\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}.

If
\chi
is nontrivial, then
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;\cdot)
is holomorphic at
s=1
. The Eisenstein series
E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s)
has a Fourier expansion at
\infty
as in (
35
). For simplicity we will write
\rho_{\mathfrak{a},t}(n)
instead of
\rho(E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;1/2+it);n)
.

We can arrange the Eisenstein series in a column vector
\mathcal{E}(z;s):=(E_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(z;s))_{i}
of size
\kappa\times 1
. Then, there exists a square matrix
\Phi(s)
of size
\kappa\times\kappa
such that the identity

\mathcal{E}(z;s)=\Phi(s)\mathcal{E}(z;1-s)

(39)

holds away from possible poles. The matrix
\Phi(s)
is called the
scattering matrix
of
(\Gamma,\chi)
, and its entries can be expressed in terms of the constant term of the Fourier expansions of the different Eisenstein series at the different singular cusps. We know that
\Phi(s)
is unitary on the critical line
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
. The
scattering determinant
is the meromorphic function

\phi(s):=\det\Phi(s),

(40)

which is nonzero and holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the line
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
. The logarithmic derivative of this function expresses the contribution of the continuous part of the spectrum to the Selberg trace formula, see Lemma
3.4
further down. Thus, for analytic applications it is important to control the size of
\phi^{\prime}/\phi
. It turns out that for a congruence character
(\Gamma,\chi)
the scattering determinant can be expressed explicitly in terms of completed Dirichlet
L
-functions. Recall that, if
\psi
is a primitive Dirichlet character such that
\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}
, the completed Dirichlet
L
-function of
\psi
is given by

\Lambda(s;\psi):=\pi^{-\frac{s+k}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+k}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\psi(n)}{n^{s}}

when
\text{Re}(s)>1
, and otherwise is defined by analytic continuation.

Lemma 3.2
.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,\kappa\}
there is a primitive Dirichlet character
\psi_{i}
with
\psi_{i}(-1)=(-1)^{k}
, a finite set of primes
S_{i}
, and for each
p\in S_{i}
a rational function
H_{i,p}(X)
such that

\phi(s)=\prod_{i=1}^{\kappa}\left(\frac{\Lambda(2s-1;\psi_{i})}{\Lambda(2s;\psi_{i})}\prod_{p\in S_{i}}H_{i,p}(p^{-s})\right).

Proof.

The formula follows from the discussion in
[
37
, Paragraph 2.7]
.
∎

From Lemma
3.2
it follows easily that

\int_{|t-T|\leq 1}\left|\frac{\phi^{\prime}(1/2+it)}{\phi(1/2+it)}\right|\,dt\ll_{q}\log T.

(41)

The cuspidal part.

We know that
L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
decomposes discretely as an orthonormal sum

L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)=\overline{\bigoplus_{j\geq 1}}\mathbb{C}u_{j},

(42)

where
Pu_{j}=\lambda_{j}u_{j}
for some
\lambda_{j}>0
and where each eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
appears with finite multiplicity. As is customary, we write
\lambda_{j}=1/4+t_{j}^{2}
. When
\lambda_{j}\neq 1/4
we need to make a choice of sign for
t_{j}
. We will assume that, either
\text{Im}(t_{j})>0
, or else
t_{j}>0
.
We recall some facts about these eigenvalues.

1)

If
k=0
, then since
\lambda_{j}>0
we have that
t_{j}
is either real or purely imaginary with
|\text{Im}(t_{j})|<1/2
. The Selberg eigenvalue conjecture predicts that
t_{j}
is always real. The best progress towards this conjecture is that
|\text{Im}(t_{j})|\leq 7/64
, or, equivalently, that
\lambda_{j}\geq 1/4-(7/64)^{2}
. The proof can be found in the second appendix to
[
23
]
.

2)

If
k=1
, then we know that
\lambda_{j}\geq 1/4

[
10
, Equation (4.40)]
, so we always have
t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. When
t_{j}=0
the function
y^{-1/2}u_{j}(z)
is an holomorphic cusp form of weight
1
.

Quantitatively, we need the following coarse version of Weyl’s law

\#\{t_{j}\in[T,T+1]\}\asymp_{q}T,

(43)

which follows from Theorem 2.28 in
[
13
]
and Equation (
41
) above. At the cusp
\infty
the cuspidal eigenfunction
u_{j}
has a Fourier expansion as in (
35
), with the particularity that
\rho(u_{j};0^{+})=\rho(u_{j};0^{-})=0
. If the choice of orthonormal basis
\{u_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is understood from the context we will write
\rho_{j}(n)
instead of
\rho(u_{j};n)
.

3.2
Smoothing the exponential sums.

The exponential sums
S(T,X)
with sharp cut-off are not directly accesible to trace formulae, since test functions on the spectral side are always smooth. To overcome this difficulty, Iwaniec introduced the smooth sums

\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X):=\sum_{t_{j}\geq 0}X^{it_{j}}\exp(-t_{j}/T).

(44)

Following the appendix to
[
28
]
, we can write

\mathcal{S}(T,X)=\int_{-1}^{1}\widehat{g}(\xi)\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X\exp(-2\pi\xi))\,d\xi+O(T\log T),

for a certain function
\widehat{g}(\xi)
which satisfies
\int_{-1}^{1}|\widehat{g}(\xi)|\,d\xi=O(\log T)
. In this argument the only property of the sequence
t_{j}
that is used is the weak version of Weyl’s law in (
43
).

Proposition 3.3
.

It is enough to prove the bounds of Theorem
1.7
and Theorem
1.8
for
\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X)
instead of
\mathcal{S}(T,X)
.

3.3
Selberg trace formula.

We say that a function
h
, defined on an horizontal strip
|\text{Im}(z)|\leq 1/2+\delta
for some
\delta>0
, is
admissible
if it is even, holomorphic on the strip and for some
C>0
satisfies the bound
|h(z)|\leq C(1+|z|)^{-2-\delta}
there.
Its inverse Fourier transform is denoted by
g
, so that

g(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{irt}h(t)\,dt,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pth(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{irt}g(r)\,dr.

(45)

Given
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
we define
k(\theta):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta.\end{pmatrix}
.
Given an elliptic element
R\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, there is a unique
\theta(R)
satisfying
0<|\theta(R)|<\pi
and such that
R
is conjugate to
k(\theta(R))
in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. If
R\in\Gamma
is elliptic, we let
m(R)
denote the cardinality of the centralizer of
R
in
\Gamma
. Note that
m(R)<\infty
.

Lemma 3.4

(Selberg, Hejhal)
.

Let
(\Gamma,\chi)
be a congruence character,
k\in\{0,1\}
and
h
an admissible function. Then the following equality holds

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{Res}}(h)+\Lambda_{\text{Cusp}}(h)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\text{Id}}(h)+\Lambda_{\text{Hyp}}(h)+\Lambda_{\text{Ell}}(h)+\Lambda_{\text{Par-Cont}}(h).

where each
h\mapsto\Lambda_{*}(h)
is a distribution on admissible functions given as follows.

\Lambda_{\text{Res}}(h)=\delta_{\chi=1}h(i/2),\hskip 10.00002pt\Lambda_{\text{Cusp}}(h)=\sum_{j}h(t_{j}),

\Lambda_{\text{Id}}(h)=\begin{cases}\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(r)r\tanh\pi r\,dr,&\text{ if }k=0,\\
\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(r)r\coth\pi r\,dr,&\text{ if }k=1.\end{cases}

\Lambda_{\text{Hyp}}(h)=\sum_{\text{hyp.}\{P\}_{\Gamma}}\frac{\chi(P)\log N(P_{0})}{N(P)^{1/2}-N(P)^{-1/2}}g(\log N(P)),

\Lambda_{\text{Ell}}(h)=\begin{cases}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ell.}\{R\}_{\Gamma}\\
0<\theta(R)<\pi\end{subarray}}\frac{i\chi(R)}{m(R)\sin\theta(R)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{g(u)}{e^{u/2+i\theta(R)}-e^{-u/2-i\theta(R)}}\,du,&\text{ if }k=0,\\
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ell.}\{R\}_{\Gamma}\\
0<\theta(R)<\pi\end{subarray}}\frac{i\chi(R)}{2m(R)\sin\theta(R)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(u)\left(1-\frac{i\sin 2\theta(R)}{\cosh u-\cos 2\theta(R)}\right)\,du,&\text{ if }k=1.\end{cases}

When
k=0
we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{Par-Cont}}(h)

\displaystyle=-g(0)\sum_{\text{nonsing. }\mathfrak{a}}\log|1-e(\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}})|+\frac{1}{4}h(0)\Tr(I-\Phi(1/2))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}(1/2+it)\,dt-\kappa\left(g(0)\log 2+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(r)\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}(1+ir)\,dr\right),

and when
k=1
we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{Par-Cont}}(h)

\displaystyle=-g(0)\sum_{\text{nonsing. }\mathfrak{a}}\log|1-e(\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}})|-\frac{h(0)}{2}\sum_{\text{nonsing. }\mathfrak{a}}(1/2-\alpha_{\mathfrak{a}})+\frac{h(0)}{4}\Tr(I-\Phi(1/2))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}(1/2+it)\,dt-\kappa\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}g(u)\tanh(u/4)\,du+g(0)\log 2+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(r)\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}(1+ir)\,dr\right).

All sums and integrals converge absolutely when
h
is admissible.

Proof.

After a few simple manipulations, the formula is a particular case of Theorem 6.3 in chapter 9 of
[
13
]
.
∎

Note that
\Lambda_{\text{Res}}(h)=0
when
k=1
, since
\chi\neq 1
in that case.

3.4
Kloosterman sums.

Recall that
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:x\in\ell\mathbb{Z}\}
, with
\chi(\begin{pmatrix}1&\ell\\
0&1\end{pmatrix})=e(-\alpha)
, where
\alpha\in\{0,\ell/q,2\ell/q\ldots,1-\ell/q\}
. For any
n,m\in\mathbb{Q}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we define the Kloosterman sums

S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c):=\begin{cases}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[\gamma]\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}\\
c(\gamma)=c\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi(\gamma)}e\left(\frac{ma(\gamma)+nd(\gamma)}{c}\right),&\text{ if }m,n\in\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{\ell},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(46)

The trivial bound is
|S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)|=O_{q}(c)
, but thanks to Weil’s bound on classical Kloosterman sums we can do much better.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
m,n\neq 0
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}\gcd(qm,qn,c)^{1/2}c^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Of course, we can assume that
m,n\in\ell^{-1}\mathbb{Z}-\alpha/\ell
. We let
m^{\prime}:=qm,n^{\prime}:=qn
, which are nonzero integers. After a small manipulation we find that

S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)=\frac{\ell^{2}}{q^{2}}\sum_{\tilde{\gamma}\in\tilde{\Gamma}}\overline{\chi(\tilde{\gamma})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,d\text{ mod }cq\\
ad=1\modulo c\\
\begin{pmatrix}a&(ad-1)/c\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\tilde{\gamma}\text{ mod }q\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m^{\prime}a+n^{\prime}d}{cq}\right).

(47)

We will estimate each inner sum separately. We may assume that
c=c(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo q
, since otherwise the inner sum vanishes. We can write
q=\prod_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{r_{i}}
, where
t\geq 1
, and where each
p_{i}
is prime and each is
r_{i}
positive for
1\leq i\leq t
. Write also
c=c_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}^{s_{i}-r_{i}}
, where
(c_{0},q)=1
and the product ranges over the primes dividing
q
. We have

cq=c_{0}\prod_{i}p_{i}^{s_{i}}
. We define

c_{0}^{\prime}=c_{0},\hskip 10.00002ptc^{\prime}_{i}=p_{i}^{s_{i}}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq t,

so that

cq=\prod_{i=0}^{t}c_{i}^{\prime},\hskip 10.00002pt(c_{i}^{\prime},c_{j}^{\prime})=1\text{ for }i\not=j.

We define also

R_{i}=\prod_{j\neq i}c_{j}^{\prime},

and let
\overline{R_{i}}
be any integer which is the inverse of
R_{i}
when considered modulo
c_{i}^{\prime}
. From the Chinese remainder theorem we see that each inner sum in (
47
) factors as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,d\text{ mod }cq\\
ad=1\modulo c\\
\begin{pmatrix}a&(ad-1)/c\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\tilde{\gamma}\text{ mod }q\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m^{\prime}a+n^{\prime}d}{cq}\right)=S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{0}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{0}};c_{0})\times\prod_{i=1}^{t}S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{i}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}};c;p_{i}^{s_{i}}),

where

S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{0}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{0}};c_{0})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,d\text{ mod }c_{0}\\
ad=1\text{ mod }c_{0}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}a+n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}d}{c_{0}}\right)

is the classical Kloosterman sum and for
1\leq i\leq t
we have

S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{i}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}};c;p_{i}^{s_{i}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,d\text{ mod }p_{i}^{s_{i}}\\
(a,d)=(a(\tilde{\gamma}),d(\tilde{\gamma}))\text{ mod }p_{i}^{r_{i}}\\
ad=1+b(\tilde{\gamma})c\text{ mod }p_{i}^{s_{i}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}a+n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}d}{p_{i}^{s_{i}}}\right).

The Weil bound gives

S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{0}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{0}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{0}};c_{0})\ll_{\varepsilon}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime},c_{0})^{1/2}c_{0}^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

For
1\leq i\leq t
we write
(m^{\prime},n^{\prime},p_{i}^{s_{i}})=p_{i}^{u_{i}}
as well as
m^{\prime}=m_{i}^{\prime}p_{i}^{u_{i}}
,
n^{\prime}=n_{i}^{\prime}p_{i}^{u_{i}}
. If
s_{i}<2r_{i}+u_{i}
, we can estimate the sum
S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{i}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}};c;p_{i}^{s_{i}})
trivially (our bounds are allowed to depend on
q
). Thus, we can assume that
s_{i}\geq 2r_{i}+u_{i}
for the remainder of this proof. Under this assumption we have
p_{i}\mid c
, so that
w:=1+b(\widetilde{\gamma})c
is a unit modulo
p_{i}^{s_{i}}
, and both
a(\widetilde{\gamma})
and
d(\widetilde{\gamma})
are units modulo
p_{i}^{r_{i}}
. With this notation we can rewrite

S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{i}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}};c;p_{i}^{s_{i}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\modulo p_{i}^{s_{i}}\\
a=a(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo p_{i}^{r_{i}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}a+wn_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}\overline{a}}{p_{i}^{s_{i}-u_{i}}}\right)=p_{i}^{u_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\modulo p_{i}^{s_{i}-u_{i}}\\
a=a(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo p_{i}^{r_{i}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}a+wn_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}\overline{a}}{p_{i}^{s_{i}-u_{i}}}\right).

If
v_{i}=\lceil(s_{i}-u_{i})/2\rceil
and
a_{1}=a(1+xp^{v_{i}})
, then its inverse modulo
p_{i}^{s_{i}-u_{i}}
is given by
\overline{a_{1}}=\overline{a}(1-xp^{v_{i}})
. It follows that

\displaystyle S^{\tilde{\gamma}}_{i}(m^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}},n^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}};c;p_{i}^{s_{i}})

\displaystyle=p^{u_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\modulo p_{i}^{v_{i}}\\
a=a(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo p_{i}^{r_{i}}\end{subarray}}e\left(\frac{m_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}a+wn_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}\overline{a}}{p_{i}^{s_{i}-u_{i}}}\right)\sum_{x\modulo p^{s_{i}-u_{i}-v_{i}}}e\left(\frac{x(m_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}a-wn_{i}^{\prime}\overline{R_{i}}\overline{a})}{p_{i}^{s_{i}-u_{i}-v_{i}}}\right)

(48)

\displaystyle\leq p^{s_{i}-v_{i}}\#\{a\modulo p_{i}^{v_{i}}:a^{2}m_{i}^{\prime}=wn_{i}^{\prime}\modulo p^{s_{i}-u_{i}-v_{i}}\}

\displaystyle\ll p_{i}^{v_{i}+u_{i}},

where we have used Hensel’s lemma in the last step. Putting together the bounds for
i=0
and
1\leq i\leq t
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.5
Preliminary version of the Bruggeman–Kuznetsov trace formula.

Fix an orthonormal basis
\{u_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
of
L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
as in the discussion after (
42
), so that
Pu_{j}=(1/4+t_{j}^{2})u_{j}
. Fix also representatives
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{\kappa}
of the equivalence classes of singular cusps of
(\Gamma,\chi)
. Recall that
\ell\geq 1
is the width of the cusp
\infty
, so that
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:x\in l\mathbb{Z}\}
: Recall also the Fourier coefficients introduced in (
35
). Note that we write
\rho_{j}(n)
instead of
\rho(u_{j};n)
, as well as
\rho_{\mathfrak{a},t}(n)
instead of
\rho(E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\cdot;1/2+it);n)

The following preliminary version of the Bruggeman–Kuznetsov is already very convenient for some applications, like upper bounding the Fourier coefficients on average.

Lemma 3.6
.

For positive
m,n\in\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{\ell}
and for any real number
r
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\overline{\rho_{j}(m)}\rho_{j}(n)}{\cosh\pi(r-t_{j})\cosh\pi(r+t_{j})}+\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\frac{\ell}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\overline{\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(m)}\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(n)\,dt}{\cosh\pi(r-t)\cosh\pi(r+t)}

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Gamma\left(1-\frac{k}{2}-ir\right)|^{2}}{4\pi^{3}}\left(\delta_{m=n}+\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)}{c\ell}I\left(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{c};r\right)\right).

In this sum we have

I(x;r)=-2x\int_{-i}^{i}(-i\zeta)^{k-1}K_{2ir}(\zeta x)\,d\zeta,

where the contour is a semicircle contained in the right half-plane.

Proof.

The same proof as that of
[
10
, Proposition 5.2]
is valid in our context. We can also quote
[
36
, Lemma 3]
, specialized at
\sigma=1
, which deals with the case of arbitrary real weight. Note that our normalization of the inner product of
L^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
as well as that of the Fourier coefficients differs from these two references.
∎

It is convenient to introduce the spectral weights

\omega(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi}{\cosh\pi t},&\text{ if }k=0,\\
\frac{\pi t}{\sinh\pi t},&\text{ if }k=1.\end{cases}

(50)

Note that for
|\text{Im}(t)|\leq 7/64
we have

|\omega(t)|\asymp(1+|t|)^{k}\exp(-|t|/T).

We can use Lemma
3.6
to upper bound averages of Fourier coefficients.

Lemma 3.7
.

For positive
m,n\in\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{\ell}
and for
T\geq 1
we have the following upper bound:

\sum_{|t_{j}-T|\leq 1}\omega(t_{j})|\rho_{j}(n)\rho_{j}(m)|+\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\int_{||t|-T|\leq 1}\omega(t)|\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(n,t)\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(m)|\,dt\ll T.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
T\geq 10
, and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we can assume that
n=m
, so that the summands and integrands on the left hand side are nonnegative. We are going to integrate equality (
49
), multiplied by
r^{k}e^{\pi r}
, over
r\in[T-2,T+2]
.
Note that

\int_{T-2}^{T+2}\frac{r^{k}e^{\pi r}\,dr}{\cosh\pi(r-t)\cosh\pi(r+t)}\gg T^{k}e^{-\pi T}

(51)

whenever
||t|-T|\leq 1
. Regarding the special function
I(x;r)
, we can use the trivial bound

I(x;r)\ll x\int_{-i}^{i}K_{0}(x\text{Re}(\zeta))\,d\zeta\ll x|\log x|

where we used that
K_{0}(x)\ll|\log x|
as
x\to 0^{+}
, see
[
30
,
(10.31.2)
]
. Using Lemma
3.5
we get

\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)}{c\ell}I\left(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{c};r\right)\ll_{q,m,n}1.

Thus, after multiplying equality (
49
) with
m=n
by
r^{k}e^{-\pi r}
and integrating over
r\in[T-2,T+2]
we obtain

T^{k}e^{-\pi T}\left(\sum_{|t_{j}-T|\leq 1}|\rho_{j}(m)|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\int_{||t|-T|\leq 1}|\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(m)|^{2}\right)\ll_{q,m,n}\int_{T-2}^{T+2}r^{k}e^{\pi r}|\Gamma(1-k/2-ir)|^{2}\,dr\ll T,

where we have used Stirling’s formula. The proof of the lemma is finished after noting that
\omega(t)\asymp T^{k}e^{-\pi T}
when
||t|-T|\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma 3.8

Let

For

and

, Theorem 2]
. The result was later extended to arbitrary

and arbitrary Dirichlet character

.

3.6
Bruggeman–Kuznetsov trace formula.

Let
\varphi\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
satisfy, for some
\delta>0
, the bounds

\varphi(x)\ll x^{1/2+\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{\prime}(x)\ll x^{-1+\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\ll x^{-2+\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as }x\to 0^{+},

(52)

as well as the bounds

\varphi(x)\ll x^{-1/2-\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{\prime}(x)\ll x^{-1/2-\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\ll x^{-3/2-\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as }x\to\infty.

(53)

Let us write

\varphi_{*}(x):=\varphi(x)-\int_{0}^{1}\xi xJ_{k}(\xi x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{k}(\xi y)\varphi(y)\,dy\right)\,d\xi.

For
\text{Im}(r)<1/2
define

h(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{2\pi i}{\sinh(\pi t)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(J_{2it}(y)-J_{-2it}(y))\varphi(y)\frac{dy}{y},&\text{ if }k=0,\\
\frac{2\pi i}{\cosh(\pi t)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(J_{2it}(y)+J_{-2it}(y))\varphi(y)\frac{dy}{y},&\text{ if }k=1.\end{cases}

(54)

We are now ready to state the Bruggeman–Kuznetsov trace formula.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let the notation be as in Lemma
3.6
. Suppose that
\varphi\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
satisfies the bounds (
52
) and (
53
) for some
\delta>0
. Then, for positive
m,n\in\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{Z}-\frac{\alpha}{\ell}
we have the equality

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\sum_{j}\overline{\rho_{j}(m)}\rho_{j}(n)\omega(t_{j})h(t_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\frac{\ell}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(m)}\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(n)\omega(t)h(t)\,dt

(55)

\displaystyle=\delta_{m=n}\frac{i^{k}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{k}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx+\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)}{c\ell}\varphi_{*}\left(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{c}\right),

where all sums and integrals converge absolutely.

After some manipulations,
[
36
, Equation (84)]
shows that (
55
) holds for a class of test functions which includes
C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
. In the discussion below we will use an approximation argument to extend the identity in (
55
) to all
\varphi\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
satisfying the bounds (
52
) and (
53
) for some
\delta>0
. We start by collecting a couple of lemmas.

Lemma 3.10
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

J_{2it}(x)\ll(1+|t|)^{-1/2}e^{\pi|t|}

(56)

uniformly in
x>0
.

Proof.

Recall Poisson’s formula for the
J
-Bessel function
[
30
,
(10.9.4)
]

J_{2it}(x)=\frac{2(x/2)^{2it}}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(2it+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{1}(1-r^{2})^{2it-\frac{1}{2}}\cos(xr)\,dr.

By Stirling’s formula it is enough to show that the integral is bounded by
(1+|t|)^{-1/2}
uniformly on
x>0
.
It is convenient to change variables so that

\int_{0}^{1}(1-r^{2})^{2it-1/2}\cos(xr)\,dr=\int_{0}^{1}r^{-1/2}(2-r)^{-1/2}e^{i(2t\log r+2t\log(2-r))}\cos(x(1-r))\,dr.

Consider the phase function
\phi_{\pm}(r)=2t\log r+2t\log(2-r)\pm xr
. Note that

|\phi_{\pm}^{\prime\prime}(r)|\asymp|t|/r^{2}
for
0<r\leq 1
. Let
0<A\leq 1
. Thus, by Van der Corput’s lemma
[
12
, Proposition 2.6.7]
we have

\int_{A}^{A+y}e^{i\phi_{\pm}(r)}\,dr\ll A(1+|t|)^{-1/2}

whenever
0<y\leq A
, uniformly in
A
. By integration by parts, it follows that

\int_{A}^{2A}r^{-1/2}(2-r)^{-1/2}e^{i(2t\log r+2t\log(2-r))}\cos(x(1-r))\,dr\ll A^{1/2}(1+|t|)^{-1/2},

for
A\in(0,1/2]
. The lemma follows after summing over
A=2^{-i}
with
i\geq 1
.
∎

Define the following seminorms on
C^{2}(0,\infty)
:

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}:=\sup_{x\in(0,1]}|\varphi(x)|x^{-1/2-\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\|\varphi\|_{2,\delta}:=\sup_{x\in(0,1]}|\varphi^{\prime}(x)|x^{1-\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\|\varphi\|_{3,\delta}:=\sup_{x\in(0,1]}|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)|x^{2-\delta},

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}:=\sup_{x\in[1,\infty)}|\varphi(x)|x^{1/2+\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\|\varphi\|_{5,\delta}:=\sup_{x\in[1,\infty)}|\varphi^{\prime}(x)|x^{1/2+\delta}\hskip 10.00002pt\|\varphi\|_{6,\delta}:=\sup_{x\in[1,\infty)}|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)|x^{3/2+\delta}.

Note that these seminorms are monotonically increasing in
\delta
. We will use these seminorms to control the geometric and spectral side of the Bruggeman–Kuznetsov. We have the following approximation result.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\varphi\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
be such that
\sum_{i}\|\varphi\|_{i,\delta_{0}}<\infty
for some
\delta_{0}>0
. Then for any
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
there exists a sequence
\varphi_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{6}\|\varphi-\varphi_{n}\|_{i,\delta}\to 0.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{0},\psi_{\infty}\in C^{\infty}(0,\infty)
be functions such that
0\leq\psi_{0},\psi_{\infty}\leq 1
and also

\psi_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }x\leq 1/2,\\
1,&\text{ if }x\geq 1,\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ as well as }\hskip 10.00002pt\psi_{\infty}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }x\leq 1,\\
0,&\text{ if }x\geq 2.\end{cases}

For
N\geq 10
define

\varphi_{N}(x):=\varphi(x)\psi_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1}),

so that

\varphi_{N}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }x\leq(2N)^{-1},\\
\varphi(x),&\text{ if }N^{-1}\leq x\leq N,\\
0,&\text{ if }x\geq 2N.\end{cases}

One can prove that

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\varphi_{N}\|_{1,\delta}

\displaystyle\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta_{0}},

(57)

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\varphi_{N}\|_{2,\delta}

\displaystyle\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}(N^{-1/2}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta_{0}}+\|\varphi\|_{2,\delta_{0}}),

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\varphi_{N}\|_{3,\delta}

\displaystyle\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}(N^{-1/2}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta_{0}}+\|\varphi\|_{2,\delta_{0}}+\|\varphi\|_{3,\delta_{0}}),

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\varphi_{N}\|_{4,\delta}

\displaystyle\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta_{0}},

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\varphi_{N}\|_{5,\delta}

\displaystyle\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}(N^{-1}\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta_{0}}+\|\varphi\|_{5,\delta_{0}}),

\displaystyle\|\varphi-\varphi_{N}\|_{6,\delta}

\displaystyle\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}(N^{-1}\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta_{0}}+\|\varphi\|_{5,\delta_{0}}+\|\varphi\|_{6,\delta_{0}}).

We will only explain the bound for
\|\cdot\|_{3,\delta}
, the remaining estimates being very similar. When
0<x\leq 1
the derivatives of
\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1})
vanish, so we have

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)-\varphi_{N}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)(1-\psi_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1}))-2N\varphi^{\prime}(x)\psi^{\prime}_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1})-N^{2}\varphi(x)\psi^{\prime\prime}_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1}).

When
0<x\leq 1
, the quantity
(1-\psi_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1}))
vanishes unless
x\leq N^{-1}
, thus we have

\sup_{x\in(0,1]}|\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)(1-\psi_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1}))|x^{2-\delta}\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}\|\varphi\|_{3,\delta_{0}}.

Similarly, the derivatives of
\psi_{0}(xN)
vanish unless
(2N)^{-1}\leq x\leq N^{-1}
, so that we have

\sup_{x\in(0,1]}N|\varphi^{\prime}(x)\psi_{0}^{\prime}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1})|x^{2-\delta}\ll N^{\delta-\delta_{0}}\|\varphi\|_{2,\delta_{0}},

as well as

\sup_{x\in(0,1]}N^{2}|\varphi(x)\psi^{\prime\prime}_{0}(xN)\psi_{\infty}(xN^{-1})|x^{2-\delta}\ll N^{-1/2+(\delta-\delta_{0})}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta_{0}}.

This proves the inequality for
\|\cdot\|_{3,\delta}
in (
57
). Note that
\varphi_{N}
is supported on
[(2N)^{-1},2N]
, but is only of class
C^{2}
. Fix
N\geq 10
. Choose
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
with support contained in
[0,1]
and satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(x)=1
. For small
\varepsilon>0
define
v_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-1}v(\varepsilon^{-1}x)
. Consider the convolution
\varphi_{N}*v_{\varepsilon}
, which is
C^{\infty}
with support contained in
[(2N)^{-1}-\varepsilon,2N+\varepsilon]
. If
0<\varepsilon<(3N)^{-1}
, then we have

\sum_{i=1}^{6}\|\varphi_{N}-\varphi_{N}*v_{\varepsilon}\|_{i,\delta}\ll N^{3/2+\delta}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\sup_{x\in[1/6N,3N]}|(\varphi_{N}-\varphi_{N}*v_{\varepsilon})^{(j)}(x)|.

We know that
(\varphi_{N}*v_{\varepsilon})^{(j)}
tends uniformly to
\varphi^{(j)}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, for
0\leq j\leq 2
. Thus, if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, the bounds of (
57
) hold when
\varphi_{N}
is replaced by
\varphi_{N}*v_{\varepsilon}
. This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.9
.

We start by estimating the spectral and geometric sides of (
55
) in terms of the seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{i,\delta}
,
1\leq i\leq 6
. To estimate the geometric side first note that
J_{k}(x)\ll\min(x^{k},x^{-1/2})\ll\min(1,x^{-1/2})
. If
0<\xi\leq 1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{k}(\xi y)\varphi(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\varphi(y)|\,dy+\int_{1}^{\xi^{-1}}|\varphi(y)|\,dy+\xi^{-1/2}\int_{\xi^{-1}}^{\infty}y^{-1/2}|\varphi(y)|\,dy

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}+\xi^{-1/2+\delta}\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}

for any
0<\delta<1/2
. This implies that

\int_{0}^{1}\xi xJ_{k}(\xi x)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{k}(\xi y)\varphi(y)\,dy\right)\,d\xi\ll_{\delta}x\left(\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}+\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}\right),

so that

\varphi_{*}(x)=\varphi(x)+O_{\delta}\left(x(\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}+\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta})\right)

for any
\delta>0
(recall that the seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{i,\delta}
are monotonically increasing in
\delta
). Similarly,

\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{k}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\ll_{\delta}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}+\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}

for any
\delta>0
. Using Lemma
3.5
we obtain

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{k}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\right|+\sum_{c\geq 1}\left|\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(m,n;c)}{c\ell}\varphi_{*}\left(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{c}\right)\right|\ll_{n,m,q,\delta}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}+\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}

(58)

for any
\delta>0
. To bound the spectral side we follow
[

]
. We want to prove the estimate

h(t)\ll_{\delta}(1+|t|)^{-2-\delta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{6}\|\varphi\|_{i,\delta}\right)

(59)

for any
\delta>0
. This is clear for
|t|\leq 1
, so we can assume that
|t|\geq 1
. By Bessel’s equation
[

,

]
we have

x^{2}J_{2it}^{\prime\prime}(x)+xJ_{2it}^{\prime}(x)+(x^{2}+4t^{2})J_{2it}(x)=0,

and after integration by parts we see that

\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{2it}(y)\varphi(y)\frac{dy}{y}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{2it}(y)\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}y}\left(\frac{y\varphi(y)}{y^{2}+4t^{2}}\right)\,dy+\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{2it}(y)\frac{d}{dy}\left(\frac{\varphi(y)}{y^{2}+4t^{2}}\right)\,dy.

) and the definition of the seminorms

we can estimate

\displaystyle e^{-\pi|t|}(1+|t|)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{2it}(y)\frac{d}{dy}\left(\frac{\varphi(y)}{y^{2}+4t^{2}}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle\ll\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(y)|}{|t|^{2}}+\frac{y|\varphi(y)|}{|t|^{4}}\,dy+\int_{1}^{|t|}\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(y)|}{|t|^{2}}+\frac{y|\varphi(y)|}{|t|^{4}}\,dy

\displaystyle+\int_{|t|}^{\infty}\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(y)|}{y^{2}}+\frac{|\varphi(y)|}{y^{3}}\,dy

\displaystyle\ll_{\delta}|t|^{-2}\|\varphi\|_{2,\delta}+|t|^{-4}\|\varphi\|_{1,\delta}

\displaystyle+|t|^{-3/2-\delta}\|\varphi\|_{5,\delta}+|t|^{-5/2-\delta}\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}

for
|t|\geq 1
and any
\delta>0
. In the same way one can show that

\displaystyle e^{-\pi|t|}(1+|t|)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{2it}(y)\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}y}\left(\frac{y\varphi(y)}{y^{2}+4t^{2}}\right)\,dy

\displaystyle+|t|^{-5/2-\delta}\|\varphi\|_{4,\delta}+|t|^{-3/2-\delta}(\|\varphi\|_{5,\delta}+\|\varphi\|_{6,\delta})

for
|t|\geq 1
and any
\delta>0

h(t)
from (
54

59
) with Lemma
3.7
we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left|\overline{\rho_{j}(m)}\rho_{j}(n)\omega(t_{j})h(t_{j})\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa}\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\overline{\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(m)}\rho_{\mathfrak{a}_{i},t}(n)\omega(t)h(t)\right|\,dt

(60)

\displaystyle\ll_{m,n,q,\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{6}\|\varphi\|_{i,\delta},

valid for any
\delta>0
. We are now ready to conclude the proof of Proposition
3.9
. After substracting the right-hand side from the left-hand side we can write the identity in (

J(\varphi)=0
for a linear functional
J

58
) and (

|J(\varphi)|\ll_{\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{6}\|\varphi\|_{i,\delta},

(61)

for any
\delta>0
. Suppose that
\varphi_{0}\in C^{2}(0,\infty)

) and in (

) for some

Fourier coefficients of Eisenstein series.

In the proof of Theorem

Accordingly, for any

The purpose of this section is to estimate

4.1
Hecke congruence subgroup.

Consider
N\geq 1
and let
\psi
be a Dirichlet modulo

. Let
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
be primitive characters modulo

respectively, such that

\chi_{1}\overline{\chi_{2}}=\psi

E_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}(z;s):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(c,d)=1}\frac{(q_{2}y)^{s}\chi_{1}(c)\chi_{2}(d)}{|cq_{2}z+d|^{2s}}\left(\frac{|cq_{2}z+d|}{cq_{2}z+d}\right)^{k},

.
This definition is valid for

then we have a very neat Fourier expansion

see

\lambda_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}(n;s):=\chi_{2}(\sgn(n))\sum_{ab=|n|}\chi_{1}(a)\overline{\chi_{2}}(b)\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{s-\frac{1}{2}}.

In particular, on the critical line these coefficients satisfy
|\lambda_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}(n;1/2+it)|\leq\tau(n)

Let
\psi
a Dirichlet character modulo
N

given by

. The singular cusps of

are described in

\mathfrak{a}=\frac{1}{uf}

,

. The Eisenstein series associated to such a cusp can be related to the Eisenstein series associated to Dirichlet characters as follows:

\displaystyle E_{\frac{1}{uf}}(z;s;\Gamma_{0}(N),\psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(fN^{\prime\prime})^{s}}\frac{1}{\varphi((f,N/f))}\sum_{q_{1}\mid\frac{N}{f}}\sum_{q_{2}\mid f}\,\,\,\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi_{1}\modulo q_{1}\\
\chi_{2}\modulo q_{2}\\
\chi_{1}\overline{\chi_{2}}\simeq\psi\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi_{1}}(-u)\frac{L(2s,\chi_{1}\chi_{2})}{L(2s,\chi_{1}\chi_{2}\chi_{0,N})}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\mid f\\
(a,q_{2})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\mid\frac{N}{f}\\
(b,q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(a)\mu(b)\chi_{1}(b)\chi_{2}(a)}{(ab)^{s}}E_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}\left(\frac{bf}{aq_{2}}z;s\right),

see
[

. Here

and
\chi_{0,N}
is the trivial character modulo
N
. Also, the notation
\chi_{1}\overline{\chi_{2}}\simeq\psi
means equality of the induced characters modulo
N
. Comparing this equation with (
64
) and (
65
) we see that for
m\geq 1
we have

\rho\left(E_{\frac{1}{uf}}(\cdot;1/2+it;\Gamma_{0}(N),\psi);m\right)\ll_{\varepsilon,N}\frac{m^{\varepsilon}}{L(1+2it;\chi_{1}\chi_{2})\Gamma(1/2+it+k/2)}\ll_{\varepsilon,N}e^{\frac{\pi}{2}|t|}(1+|t|)^{-k/2+\varepsilon}m^{\varepsilon}.

Squaring and summing over the different cusps we deduce that

\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma_{0}(N),\psi)\ll_{N,\varepsilon}e^{\pi|t|}(1+|t|)^{-k+\varepsilon}m^{\varepsilon}

(66)

for

.

4.2
General case.

Let
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{\kappa}
be representatives of the inequivalent singular cusps of
(\Gamma,\chi)
. For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we let

\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi):=\text{span}\{E_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\cdot;1/2+it;\Gamma,\chi):1\leq i\leq\kappa\}.

When
t\neq 0
, the spanning set
\{E_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\cdot;1/2+it;\Gamma,\chi):1\leq i\leq\kappa\}
is actually a basis of
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi)
. This can be deduced by computing the constant term of the Fourier expansions of the different Eisenstein series at the different cusps and using that the scattering matrix
\Phi(s)
is invertible on the critical line. Thus, if
t\neq 0
we can introduce an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi)
by letting

\langle E_{\mathfrak{a}_{i}}(\cdot;1/2+it;\Gamma,\chi),E_{\mathfrak{a}_{j}}(\cdot;1/2+it;\Gamma,\chi)\rangle:=\delta_{i=j}\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})},

(67)

which agrees with
[
48
, Equation (8.1)]
up to normalization. Looking at (
63
) we recognize that

\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma,\chi)=\sup_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi)}\frac{|\rho(f;m)|^{2}}{\langle f,f\rangle}.

(68)

That is, the quantity
\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma,\chi)
is the squared
L^{2}
-norm of the linear functional
f\mapsto\rho(f;m)
on the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi)
.

Until the end of this section we use the notation
\Gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{a}):=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{i}:\gamma\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}\}
and
[\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma_{i}}:=\{\gamma\mathfrak{a}:\gamma\in\Gamma_{i}\}
, where
i\in\{1,2\}
and
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
. We also let
\sigma_{i}(\mathfrak{a})
denote a scaling matrix for
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
with respect to the group
\Gamma_{i}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that we have
(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})\subset(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})
for two congruence characters. Then, for
t\neq 0
, the natural inclusion
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})
is an isometry.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{a}
be a singular cusp for
(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})
. The map
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{-1}\mathfrak{a}
establishes a bijection

\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{2}\leftrightarrow[\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma_{2}}.

Passing to
\Gamma_{1}
-orbits we have

[\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma_{2}}=\bigsqcup_{\delta\in\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}[\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a}]_{\Gamma_{1}},

where we note that

\Gamma_{2}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})=\delta^{-1}\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\delta,\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{1}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})=\delta^{-1}\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\delta\cap\Gamma_{1},

(69)

for
\delta\in\Gamma_{2}
. We also have the identity

\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}[\Gamma_{2}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{1}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})]=\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}[\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\cap\delta\Gamma_{1}\delta^{-1}]=[\Gamma_{2}:\Gamma_{1}].

(70)

We let
\sigma_{i}(\mathfrak{a})
denote a scaling matrix for
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
with respect to the group
\Gamma_{i}
. Note that we can choose
\sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})
,
\sigma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})
so that

\sigma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}[\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})]^{1/2}&0\\
0&[\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})]^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}.

(71)

From the definition (
37
) of the Eisenstein series we have

\displaystyle E_{\mathfrak{a}}(z;s;\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}\overline{\chi_{2}}(\delta)\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{1}}\overline{\chi_{2}}(\gamma)[f_{s}|_{k}(\sigma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}\delta\gamma)](z)

(72)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}}\overline{\chi_{2}}(\delta)[\Gamma_{2}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{1}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})]^{s}E_{\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a}}(z;s;\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1}),

where we have used (
71
) and the fact that
\sigma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}\delta=\sigma_{2}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})^{-1}
for
\delta\in\Gamma_{2}
. Identity (
72
) is proved first for
\text{Re}(s)>1
and extended to
s\in\mathbb{C}
away from poles by analytic continuation. The proof of the lemma is finished by combining (
72
) for
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
with the definition of the formal inner product (
67
) and using (
70
).
∎

If we combine the previous lemma with (
68
) we obtain the following fact.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that
(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})\subset(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})
for two congruence characters. Then for
m\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
we have

\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})\leq\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1}).

Suppose now that
\Gamma_{1}
is normal in
\Gamma_{2}
. In this case, if
\delta\in\Gamma_{2}
then we have
\delta^{-1}\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})\delta=\Gamma_{1}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})
. It follows that

[\Gamma_{2}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{1}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})]=[\delta^{-1}\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a})\delta:\delta^{-1}\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})\delta]=[\Gamma_{2}(\mathfrak{a}):\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})].

(73)

Also, from the definition (
37
) we see that

\displaystyle E_{\mathfrak{a}}(\cdot;s;\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})|_{k}\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{1}}\overline{\chi(\gamma)}[f_{s}|_{k}\sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}\gamma\delta]=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}(\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a})\backslash\Gamma_{1}}\overline{\chi(\gamma)}[f_{s}|_{k}\sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{a})^{-1}\delta\gamma]

\displaystyle=E_{\delta^{-1}\mathfrak{a}}(\cdot;s;\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1}),

so that
\Gamma_{2}
acts on
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})
. Since we are assuming that
\Gamma_{1}
is normal in
\Gamma_{2}
, it is clear that the action of
\Gamma_{2}
preserves
\Gamma_{1}
-equivalence classes of cusps. Thus, the action of
\Gamma_{2}
on
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})
is unitary with respect to the inner product (
67
). Using (
72
) and (
73
) it is easy to recognize that
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})
is the
\chi_{2}
-isotypic component of
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})
, that is,

\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})=\{f\in\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1}):f|_{k}\gamma=\chi_{2}(\gamma)f\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma_{2}\}.

It follows that if
\chi_{2}
and
\chi_{2}^{\prime}
are different characters extending
\chi_{1}
to
\Gamma_{2}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{2},\chi_{2})
and
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{2},\chi^{\prime}_{2})
are orthogonal with respect to the inner product on
\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{1},\chi_{1})
.

We now consider a particular case of this situation. Suppose that
\Gamma_{1}=\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q)
and
\chi_{1}=\sgn_{q}^{k}
is the character which is trivial on
\Gamma(q)
and has parity
k
. Also, suppose that
\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{d}(q)
. Since

\Gamma_{1}\backslash\Gamma_{2}=\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma_{d}(q)\simeq\{\pm 1\}\backslash(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times},

we deduce that there is an orthogonal decomposition

\mathcal{E}_{it}(\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q),\sgn_{q}^{k})=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\modulo q\\
\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{E}_{it}(\Gamma_{d}(q),\psi).

It follows that for any
m\in\mathbb{Q}-\{0\}
we have

\Sigma(m;t;\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q),\sgn_{q}^{k})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\modulo q\\
\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}\end{subarray}}\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma_{d}(q),\psi).

Combined with Corollary
4.2
we have arrived at the following conclusion.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\Gamma,\chi)
be a congruence character of level
q
and weight
k
. Then, for any
m\in\mathbb{Q}-\{0\}
we have

\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma,\chi)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\modulo q\\
\psi(-1)=(-1)^{k}\end{subarray}}\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma_{d}(q),\psi).

Finally, note that

\begin{pmatrix}q&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\Gamma_{d}(q)\begin{pmatrix}q&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\Gamma_{0}(q^{2}),

from where it follows easily that

\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma_{d}(q),\psi)=q^{-1}\Sigma(qm;t;\Gamma_{0}(q^{2}),\psi).

(74)

Combining this relation with (
66
) and the asymptotic
\omega(t)\asymp e^{-\pi|t|}(1+|t|)^{k}
we deduce the following result.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(\Gamma,\chi)
be a congruence character of level
q
. Then, for
m\in q^{-1}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
\varepsilon>0
we have

\omega(t)\Sigma(m;t;\Gamma,\chi)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}(m(1+|t|))^{\varepsilon}.

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)

In this paragraph we extend the explicit formula for

We start by generalizing

Lemma 5.1

Recall that for a hyperbolic element

the error term coming from the contribution of nonprimitive conjugacy classes. By (

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\

for all

\pi_{\Gamma}(x+y)-\pi_{\Gamma}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\\

Now we generalize equations (23), (24) and (25) from

6
Newforms and orthonormal basis of
L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
.

When
(\Gamma,\chi)
is an arbitrary congruence character of level
q
, it is not obvious how to find a basis
\{u_{j}\}_{j}
related to Hecke newforms. In this section we show that there exists a basis of
L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi)
with the following property: for each
j
there is a newform
f_{j}
such that
u_{j}
belongs to the sum of oldspaces of the twists
f_{j}\otimes\psi
, where
\psi
ranges over Dirichlet characters modulo
q
. For a more precise statement see Corollary
6.5
below.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\cup i[0,7/64]
and consider the finite-dimensional space

\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi):=\{u\in L_{0}^{2}(\Gamma,\chi):Pu=(1/4+t^{2})u\}.

(78)

Recall that we have fixed the weight
k\in\{0,1\}
such that
\chi(-I)=(-1)^{k}
, and we have dropped it from the notation. Let
\sgn_{q}^{k}
be the character on
\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q)
which is trivial on
\Gamma(q)
and has weight
k
. For conciseness write
\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma(q)):=\mathcal{C}_{it}(\{\pm I\}\Gamma(q),\sgn_{q}^{k})
. Then we have a natural inclusion
\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma,\chi)\subset\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma(q))
which is an isometry when the inner products are normalized as in (
33
).

6.1
Newforms, oldspaces and twists.

We recall some familiar facts about newforms and oldspaces. A detailed exposition for these results can be found in sections 4.5 and 4.6 of
[
29
]
. Even though Miyake treats holomorphic modular forms, the theory for the nonholomorphic case is completely analogous.

Consider the congruence pair
(\Gamma_{0}(M),\psi)
, where
\psi
is a Dirichlet character of conductor dividing
M
. For any
n\geq 1
the Hecke operators
T(n;M,\psi)
are defined on
\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma_{0}(M),\psi)
by the formula

(T(n;M,\psi)u)(z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n\\
(a,M)=1\end{subarray}}\psi(a)\sum_{b\modulo d}u\left(\frac{az+b}{d}\right).

(79)

We use the abbreviated notation
T(n)
when
M
and
\psi
are clear from the context. These operators preserve
\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma_{0}(M),\psi)
, and the effect on the Fourier expansion is given by the formula

\rho(T(n)u;m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\mid(n,m)\\
(a,M)=1\end{subarray}}\psi(a)\rho(u;mn/a^{2}),

(80)

where
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. In particular, for any
n\geq 1
we have

\rho(T(n)u;1)=\rho(u;n).

We say that
f\in\mathcal{C}_{it}(\Gamma_{0}(M),\psi)
is a
newform
if it is an eigenfunction of
T(n;M,\psi)
for all
n\geq 1
. In that case we say that
M
is the
conductor
of
f
, denoted
\mathfrak{q}(f)=M
, and we also say that
\psi
is the
nebentypus
of
f
. By the strong multiplicity one property (see
[
34
]
)
f
is also an eigenfunction of the involution
Q_{s,k}
introduced in
[
10
, Proposition 4.5]
, with eigenvalue

We say that two multiplicative functions

g_{1}=g_{2}
. Using the computation from
[
10
, Lemma 8.2]
(including the corrections from
[
48
, Section 12]
), together with the first Barnes lemma
[
30
,
(5.13.3)
]
, one can deduce that

I(s,\beta,1)=\pi^{-s}\frac{\Gamma^{2}\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+k}{2}+\beta\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+k}{2}-\beta\right)}{\Gamma(s)}.

(116)

In particular, we have
I(1,it,1)=\omega(t)
, where
\omega(t)
was introduced in (
50
). It follows that

\text{Res}_{s=1}\mathcal{L}(s;v_{1},\overline{v_{2}})=\frac{\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle}{\omega(t_{j})}

(117)

when
g_{1}=g_{2}
. We let
g\in\mathcal{T}_{f_{j}}(q)
and use this equality when
v_{1}=v_{2}=u_{j}^{g}
. By (
107
) and orthogonality we have

\langle u_{j},u_{j}\rangle=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{T}_{f_{j}}(q)}\langle u_{j}^{g},u_{j}^{g}\rangle,

so we can deduce that

\text{Res}_{s=1}\mathcal{L}(s;|u_{j}|^{2})=\frac{1}{\omega(t_{j})},

(118)

finishing the first part of the proof of Theorem
7.1
. We now start working towards the proof of the inequality in (
109
).

7.2
Good primes and adjoint
L
-function.

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it}(q)
be newforms, of nebentypus
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
respectively. Recall that these normalized newforms correspond to automorphic cuspidal representations
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
of
\GL_{2}/\mathbb{Q}
, of conductor dividing
q^{2}
. For a prime
p\nmid q
write

1-\lambda_{g_{i}}(p)X+\psi_{i}(p)X^{2}=(1-\alpha_{i,p}X)(1-\beta_{i,p}X).

We know that there is an
L
-function

L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})=\prod_{p}L_{p}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}}),

called the Rankin-Selberg convolution of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, with the following properties:

•

For primes
p
such that
(p,q)=1
we have the explicit formula

L_{p}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta_{1}}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta_{2}}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{2}\overline{\beta_{1}}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{2}\overline{\beta_{2}}p^{-s})}.

•

L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})
has meromorphic continuation to the complex plane, with at most a simple pole at
s=1
.

•

In general, for any prime
p
we have an expression

L_{p}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha_{1,p}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{2,p}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{3,p}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{4,p}p^{-s})},

where, by the second appendix to
[
23
]
we know that
\max(|\alpha_{i,p}|,|\alpha_{i,p}|^{-1})\leq p^{7/32}
.

The precise computation of
L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})
at primes
p\mid q
depends on the local
p
-adic component of the automorphic representations
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
, see
[
21
]
, as well as Sections 1 and 2 in
[
11
]
. We warn the reader that the convention in those sources is that
L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})
is the completed
L
-function, whereas for us it is just the product over finite primes. One can check that the Dirichlet series
L^{(q)}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})
in (
112
) is given by the product

L^{(q)}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})=\prod_{p\nmid q}L_{p}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}}),

so that in particular we have

q^{-\varepsilon_{1}}|L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})|\ll_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}|L^{(q)}(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})|\ll_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}q^{\varepsilon_{1}}|L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})|

(119)

on the half-plane
\text{Re}(s)\geq 7/32+\varepsilon_{2}
, for any
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
.

Suppose that
g_{1}=g_{2}\otimes\psi
, where
\psi
is a Dirichlet character modulo
q
. By work of Gelbart and Jacquet
[
11
]
, which generalizes previous work of Shimura
[
44
]
, we know that

L(s;g_{1}\otimes\overline{g_{2}})=L(s;\psi)L(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi),

(120)

where
\Ad(f_{j})
is the adjoint lift of
f_{j}
from
\GL(2)
to
\GL(3)
.
Let
\delta\in\{0,1\}
be such that
\psi(-1)=(-1)^{\delta}
and let
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
be such that
(-1)^{\epsilon}=(-1)^{\delta+k}
. Let us write

\gamma(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi):=\pi^{-\frac{3s}{2}-\epsilon-\frac{\delta}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+\delta}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+\epsilon}{2}+it_{j}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+\epsilon}{2}-it_{j}\right).

(121)

If we define

\Lambda(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi):=\gamma(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)L(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi),

(122)

then we have the functional equation

\Lambda(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)=W(\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)\mathfrak{q}(\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)^{1/2-s}\Lambda(1-s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\overline{\psi}),

(123)

where
|W(\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)|=1
and the conductor
\mathfrak{q}(\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)
divides
q^{4}
. Furthermore, the completed
L
-function
\Lambda(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)
is entire unless the following condition holds: there exists a Dirichlet character
\eta\neq 1
such that
f_{j}\otimes\eta=f_{j}

[
11
, Theorem 9.3]
. If this condition holds we say that the newform
f_{j}
is
exceptional
. Otherwise, we say that
f_{j}
is
regular
. Accordingly, we write

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q)=\mathcal{H}^{\text{reg}}_{it_{j}}(q)\bigsqcup\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}_{it_{j}}(q)

where

\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}_{it_{j}}(q)=\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q):f=f\otimes\eta\text{ for some }\eta\neq 1\}.

Denote the collection of newforms of spectral parameter
|t_{j}|\leq T
by

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(q;T)=\bigcup_{|t_{j}|\leq T}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q),

and define similarly
\mathcal{H}^{\text{reg}}(q;T)
and
\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T)
. We will treat separately the contributions to the inequality in (
109
) of these two types of newforms.

7.2.1
Norm of arithmetically normalized newforms.

Recall that the newform
f_{j}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q)
is normalized so that
\rho_{f_{j}}(1)=1
, see (
82
) and (
88
). Let
v_{1}=v_{2}=v
, given by
v:=f_{j}|_{k}\begin{pmatrix}q^{-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
. Note that
v\in V_{f_{j}}(\Gamma_{d}(q))
. Also, in this case we have
\rho(v;n/q)=q^{-1/2}\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)
for
n\geq 1
. Thus,

\mathcal{L}(s;v,\overline{v})=q^{s-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|^{2}}{n^{s}}.

If we apply (
117
) to
v_{1}=v_{2}=v
we deduce that

\text{Res}_{s=1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|^{2}}{n^{s}}=\frac{\langle f_{j},f_{j}\rangle}{\omega(t_{j})}.

(124)

By multiplicativity of Hecke eigenvalues we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|^{2}}{n^{s}}=\left(\sum_{n\mid q^{\infty}}\frac{|\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|^{2}}{n^{s}}\right)L^{(q)}(s;f_{j}\otimes\overline{f_{j}}).

(125)

By the bound
|\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|\ll_{\varepsilon}n^{7/64+\varepsilon}
from the second appendix to
[
23
]
, we know that

\sum_{n\mid q^{\infty}}\frac{|\lambda_{f_{j}}(n)|^{2}}{n^{s}}\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}

(126)

on
\text{Re}(s)\geq 7/32+\varepsilon
. Combining (
124
), (
125
) and (
126
) with (
119
) and (
120
) we obtain

q^{-\varepsilon}\frac{\langle f_{j},f_{j}\rangle}{\omega(t_{j})}\ll_{\varepsilon}L(1;\Ad(f_{j}))\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}\frac{\langle f_{j},f_{j}\rangle}{\omega(t_{j})}

(127)

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Also, by
[
14
]
we know that

((1+|t_{j}|)q)^{-\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}L(1;\Ad(f_{j}))\ll_{\varepsilon}((1+|t_{j}|)q)^{\varepsilon},

(128)

the upper bound having been established previously by Iwaniec
[
19
]
in the case
k=0
. Combining (
127
) and (
128
) we arrive at the following lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

For
j\geq 1
and any
\varepsilon>0
we have

((1+|t_{j}|)q)^{-\varepsilon}\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{\langle f_{j},f_{j}\rangle}{\omega(t_{j})}\ll_{\varepsilon}((1+|t_{j}|)q)^{\varepsilon}.

7.2.2
Contribution from exceptional newforms.

By Weyl’s law (
43
) we have

|\mathcal{H}^{\prime}(q;T)|\asymp_{q}T^{2}.

On the other hand, by the following lemma we know that the subfamily
\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T)
is quite thin.

Lemma 7.3
.

For any
T\geq 1
we have

|\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T)|=O_{q}(T).

(129)

Proof.

The idea is that exceptional newforms are parametrized by Hecke Größencharacters of quadratic extensions of
\mathbb{Q}
. Let
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T)
and consider
\eta\neq 1
such that
f\otimes\eta=f
. By elementary considerations
\eta
is a quadratic Dirichlet character of conductor dividing a power of
q
. Fix one of the finitely many possibilities for
\eta
and let
H
be the quadratic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
attached to it. This means that if
D_{H}
is the discriminant of the quadratic extension we have

|D_{H}|=\mathfrak{q}(\eta),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\sgn(D_{H})=\eta(-1).

Let
\mathbb{A}_{H}^{\times}
denote the group of ideles over
H
. Recall that
\mathbb{A}_{H}^{\times}
contains
H^{\times}
embedded diagonally as a discrete subgroup. By work of Labesse and Langlands
[
25
, Proposition 6.5]
we know that there exists a unitary Hecke Größencharacter
\theta
of
\mathbb{A}_{H}^{\times}/H^{\times}
such that the standard
L
-functions attached to
f
and
\theta
agree. We write
f=f(\theta)
in this case. By comparing conductors of the respective
L
-functions we see that if
f=f(\theta)
for
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q)
, then
\mathfrak{q}(\theta)\mid q^{2}
. Let us examine the archimedean components of
\theta
. First note that the gamma factor of the
L
-function attached to
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q)
is of the form

\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s+it_{j}+k_{1})\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s-it_{j}+k_{2}),

(130)

where
\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s):=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{0,1\}
are such that
k_{1}+k_{2}=k\modulo 2
.
We need to distinguish two cases.

Suppose first that the quadratic extension
H
is imaginary. There is only one infinite place of
H
, which is complex. Thus, the archimedean component
\theta_{\infty}
of
\theta
is a unitary character of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. There exist
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\theta_{\infty}(z)=|z|^{ir}(z/|z|)^{m},\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{C}.

The gamma factor of
\theta
is

\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s+ir/2+|m|/2),

where

\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s):=2(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s).

Comparing with (
130
) we see that
f=f(\theta)
is only possible if
t_{j}=r=m=0
and
k=1
. By the finiteness of the class number, the number of characters
\theta
of
H
with fixed archimedean component and fixed conductor is bounded independently of the archimedean component. It follows that

|\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T):f=f(\theta)\text{ for some }\theta\text{ on }\mathbb{A}_{H}^{\times}/H^{\times}\}|=O_{q}(1)

in this case.

Suppose otherwise that
H
is a totally real quadratic extension. Then there are two real places
v_{1},v_{2}
, and we let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
be the components of
\theta
at these places. Thus,
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
are unitary characters on
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. Then, for
i\in\{1,2\}
there exist
r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
m_{i}\in\{0,1\}
such that

\theta_{i}(x)=|x|^{ir_{i}}(x/|x|)^{m_{i}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}.

The gamma factor of the
L
-function attached to
\theta
is given by

\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s+ir_{1}+m_{1})\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s+ir_{2}+m_{2}).

Comparing with (
130
) we deduce that
r_{1}=-r_{2}=\pm t_{j}
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{H}
denote the ring of integers of
H
, let
\mathcal{O}^{\times}_{H}
denote the group of units of
\mathcal{O}_{H}
and define

\mathcal{O}_{H}^{\times}(q^{2}):=\{u\in\mathcal{O}_{H}:u=1\modulo q^{2}\}.

Let
\iota_{i}
denote the embedding
\iota_{i}:H\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
corresponding to the place
v_{i}
.
Since the conductor of
\theta
divides
q^{2}
, we have

\theta_{1}(\iota_{1}(u))\theta_{2}(\iota_{2}(u))=1\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{O}_{H}^{\times}(q^{2}).

(131)

By Dirichlet’s unit theorem we know that
\mathcal{O}_{H}^{\times}(q^{2})
is finitely generated of rank one. Let
u\in\mathcal{O}_{H}^{\times}(q^{2})
be an element which is not a root of unity, and define
a_{i}:=\log|\iota_{i}(u)|
, so that
a_{i}\neq 0
. Note that
a_{1}=-a_{2}
since
u
is a unit. Recall that we know that
r_{1}=-r_{2}
. From (
131
) we deduce that

r_{1}\in\frac{\pi}{2a_{1}}\mathbb{Z}.

Thus, if
f=f(\theta)\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{it_{j}}(q)
for some Größencharacter
\theta
on
H^{\times}\backslash\mathbb{A}^{\times}_{H}
, then
t_{j}
lies in an arithmetic progression. Also, by the finiteness of the class number, the number of characters
\theta
of
H
with fixed archimedean component and fixed conductor is bounded independently of the archimedean component. It follows that

|\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T):f=f(\theta)\text{ for some }\theta\text{ on }\mathbb{A}_{H}^{\times}/H^{\times}\}|=O_{q}(T)

in this case. Since there are only finitely many possibilities for
H
, the proof of the lemma is finished.
∎

Let
f\in\mathcal{H}_{it_{j}}^{\text{excep}}(q)
. Note that the archimedean analytic conductor of
L(1/2+ir;\Ad(f)\otimes\psi)
is
\asymp(1+|r|)(1+|r-2t_{j}|)(1+|r+2t_{j}|)
. By work of Li
[
26
]
we know good upper bounds for
L
-functions at the edge of the critical strip, in particular we have

L(1+\varepsilon_{2}+ir;\Ad(f)\otimes\psi)\ll_{q,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}((1+|r|)(1+|r-t|)(1+|r+t|))^{\varepsilon_{1}}

for any
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
. It follows by the Phrägmen–Lindelöf principle that

L(s;\Ad(f)\otimes\psi)\ll_{q}(1+|t|)^{1/2+\varepsilon}(1+|s|)^{A},

for some
A>0
and
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
. When we combine this estimate with Lemma
7.3
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 7.4
.

There is
A>0
such that for any
T\geq 1
and any
\psi\modulo q
we have

\sum_{f_{j}\in\mathcal{H}^{\text{excep}}(q;T)}|L(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)|^{2}\ll_{q}T^{2+\varepsilon}(1+|s|)^{A},

for
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
.

7.2.3
Regular newforms.

The purpose of this section is to prove the following estimate, which complements Corollary
7.4
.

Proposition 7.5
.

There is
A>0
such that for any
T\geq 1
and any
\psi\modulo q
we have

\sum_{f_{j}\in\mathcal{H}^{\text{reg}}(q;T)}|L(s;\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)|^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon,q}T^{2+\varepsilon}(1+|s|)^{A}

whenever
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
.

We will follow the original argument of
[
28
, Pages 220-221]
. We let
t_{j}
be the spectral parameter of
f_{j}
, so that
f_{j}\in\mathcal{H}^{\text{reg}}_{it_{j}}(q)
. We can break up the sum over
f_{j}
into dyadic pieces with respect to the spectral parameter and assume that
T\geq 100
and that
T\leq t_{j}\leq 2T
. Also, since the conductor
\mathfrak{q}(\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)
is a positive divisor of
q^{4}
, it can take only finitely many values. Thus, by a further partition of the family we can assume that
\mathfrak{q}(\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi)
is a fixed divisor of
q^{4}
, which we denote simply by
\mathfrak{q}
. Finally, it is enough to consider the case
|s|\leq T^{1-\varepsilon}
, since we can use the convexity bound on the complementary range
|s|\geq T^{1-\varepsilon}
.

For concinesses we abbreviate
F_{j}:=\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi
and write
L(s;F_{j})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)/n^{s}
. By the assumption that
f_{j}
is regular we know that
L(s;F_{j})
is an entire
L
-function. Also, we know that it satisfies the functional equation (
123
). Therefore, we can use the approximate functional equation
[
17
, Theorem 5.3]
with the function
G(u):=e^{-u^{2}}
and deduce that, for any
X>1
, we have

L(s;F_{j})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)}{n^{s}}V_{s}\left(\frac{n}{X\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}};F_{j}\right)+\epsilon(F_{j},s)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\overline{\lambda_{F}(n)}}{n^{1-s}}V_{1-s}\left(\frac{nX}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}};F_{j}\right),

(132)

where
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
,
|\epsilon(F_{j},s)|=1
and

V_{s}(y;F_{j}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(3)}y^{-u}G(u)\frac{\gamma(s+u;F_{j})}{\gamma(s;F_{j})}\frac{du}{u}.

(133)

The gamma factor
\gamma(s;F_{j})
was defined in (
121
). We will only need to apply (
132
) for values
1/2\leq X\leq 2
. Note that with our assumptions on the size of
s
and
t_{j}
, the archimedean analytic conductor of
L(s;F_{j})
is comparable to
|s|T^{2}
. By
[
17
, Proposition 5.4]
we have

V_{s}(y;F_{j})\ll_{B}\left(1+\frac{y}{|s|^{1/2}T}\right)^{-B}

for any
B>0
. Recall also that
\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)\ll_{\varepsilon}n^{7/32+\varepsilon}
(any polynomial bound would work here). It follows that

L(s;F_{j})=\sum_{n\leq(\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}|s|}T)^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)}{n^{s}}V_{s}\left(\frac{n}{X\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}};F_{j}\right)+\epsilon(F_{j},s)\sum_{n\leq(\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}|s|}T)^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{\overline{\lambda_{F}(n)}}{n^{1-s}}V_{1-s}\left(\frac{nX}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}};F_{j}\right)+O_{\varepsilon,B}(T^{-B}),

(134)

for any
B>0
. If we use the very rapid decay of
G(u)=e^{-u^{2}}
in Equation (
133
) we can see that

V_{s}(y;F_{j})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Re}(u)=\varepsilon\\
|u|\leq\log T\end{subarray}}y^{-u}G(u)\frac{\gamma(s+u;F_{j})}{\gamma(s;F_{j})}\frac{du}{u}+O_{\varepsilon,B}(y^{-\varepsilon}T^{-B}).

Note that
V_{s}(y;F_{j})
depends on
F_{j}
via the spectral parameter
t_{j}
. We want to make this dependence as explicit as possible. By Stirling’s formula we have

\log\Gamma(s)=(s-1/2)\log s-s+\frac{\log 2\pi}{2}+O(|s|^{-1}).

Using the power series expansion of
s\mapsto\log(1+s)
as well as the bounds
|s|\leq T^{1-\varepsilon}
,
|u|\leq\log T
and
|t_{j}|\asymp T
we deduce that

\displaystyle\log\Gamma((s+u+\epsilon)/2\pm it_{j})-\log\Gamma((s+\epsilon)/2\pm it_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{u}{2}\log((s+\epsilon)/2\pm it_{j})+O((\log T)/T)

\displaystyle=\frac{u}{2}(\log t_{j}\pm i\frac{\pi}{2})+O(\log T/T^{\varepsilon}).

Recalling (
121
) it follows that

V_{s}(y;F_{j})=W_{s}(y/t_{j})+O_{\varepsilon,B}(y^{-\varepsilon}T^{-B}),

(135)

where

W_{s}(y):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Re}(u)=\varepsilon\\
|u|\leq\log T\end{subarray}}(y\pi^{3/2})^{-u}G(u)\frac{\Gamma((s+u+\delta)/2)}{\Gamma((s+\delta)/2)}\frac{du}{u}.

(136)

Note that
W_{s}(y)
is independent of the spectral parameter
t_{j}
. Inserting (
135
) into (
134
) it follows that

L(s;F_{j})=\sum_{n\leq(\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}|s|}T)^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)}{n^{s}}W_{s}\left(\frac{n}{Xt_{j}\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}}\right)+\epsilon(F_{j},s)\sum_{n\leq(\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}|s|}T)^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{\overline{\lambda_{F}(n)}}{n^{1-s}}W_{1-s}\left(\frac{nX}{t_{j}\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}}\right)+O_{q,\varepsilon,B}(T^{-B}).

(137)

We have proved this formula for
T\leq|t_{j}|\leq 2T
and
X\in(1/2,2)
. We can use the variable
X
to relax the dependence on
t_{j}
. Indeed, if we take absolute values, square both sides of (
137
) and integrate
X
over
(1/2,2)
with respect to Lebesgue measure we obtain

|L(s;F_{j})|^{2}\ll\int_{1/4}^{4}\left|\sum_{n\leq(\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}|s|}T)^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)}{n^{s}}W_{s}\left(\frac{nr}{T\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}}\right)\right|^{2}\,dr+O_{q,\varepsilon,B}(T^{-B}).

After summing over
T\leq t_{j}\leq 2T
we have

\sum_{T\leq|t_{j}|\leq 2T}|L(s;F_{j})|^{2}\ll\int_{1/4}^{4}\sum_{T\leq t_{j}\leq 2T}\left|\sum_{n\leq(\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}|s|}T)^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)}{n^{s}}W_{s}\left(\frac{nr}{T\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}}\right)\right|^{2}\,dr+O_{q,\varepsilon,B}(T^{-B}).

(138)

Lemma 7.6
.

For any sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n}
and
N\geq 1
we have

\sum_{T\leq t_{j}\leq 2T}\left|\sum_{n\leq N}\lambda_{F_{j}}(n)\alpha_{n}\right|^{2}\ll_{q,\varepsilon}(NT)^{\varepsilon}(T^{2}+N^{2})\sum_{r\leq N}\left(\sum_{u\leq\sqrt{N/r}}|\alpha_{ru^{2}}|\right)^{2}.

(139)

Proof.

Recall that
F_{j}=\Ad(f_{j})\otimes\psi

Bykovskii–Zagier series.

In this section we collect some preliminaries about the Bykovskii–Zagier series which we need for the proofs of Theorems
1.8
and
1.9
.

9.1
The sum
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)
as a sum of residues.

Let
\{P\}_{\Gamma}
be a hyperbolic
\Gamma
-conjugacy class and choose a representative
P
. For
\text{Re}(s)>1
define the Dirichlet series

Z_{P}(s):=\ell^{-s}\sum_{\gamma\in C_{\Gamma}(P)\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}}\frac{1}{|c(\gamma^{-1}P\gamma)|^{s}},

which only depends on the
\Gamma
-conjugacy class of
P
. The following lemma will allow us to express the sum
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)
as a sum of residues of these Dirichlet series. In our arguments it plays a role which is analogous to the role played by the class number formula in
[
45
]
.

Lemma 9.1
.

The series
Z_{P}(s)
converges absolutely for
\text{Re}(s)>1
and has meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
. On the region
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
its only singularity is a simple pole at
s=1
of residue

\text{Res}_{s=1}Z_{P}(s)=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\log N(P_{0})}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})(N(P)^{1/2}-N(P)^{-1/2})}.

(164)

Proof.

Let
t:=\Tr(P)
so that
|t|>2
. Recall that
N:=N(P)>1
is defined implicitly by the identity
|t|=N^{1/2}+N^{-1/2}
and let
\Delta:=t^{2}-4=(N^{1/2}-N^{-1/2})^{2}
. Let
\Phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty))
and consider the pair-point invariant

k(z,w):=\Phi\left(\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{\text{Im}(z)\text{Im}(w)}\right).

We can consider the automorphic kernel attached to the conjugacy class
\{P\}_{\Gamma}
, defined by

K_{P}(z;\Phi):=\sum_{\gamma\in C_{\Gamma}(P)\backslash\Gamma}k(\gamma z,P\gamma z).

Recall the Eisenstein series
E(z;s):=E_{\infty}(z,s;\Gamma,\mathbbm{1}_{\Gamma})
introduced in (
37
), given explicitly as

E(z;s)=\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma}\text{Im}(\ell^{-1}\delta z)^{s}=\ell^{-s}\sum_{\delta\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma}\text{Im}(\delta z)^{s}.

We consider the inner product of
K_{P}(z;\Phi)
and
E(z;s)
. If we unfold with respect to the Eisenstein series we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}K_{P}(z;\Phi)E(z;s)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\ell^{-s}\int_{\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\mathbb{H}}\sum_{\gamma\in C_{\Gamma}(P)\backslash\Gamma}k(\gamma z,P\gamma z)\,y^{s}d\mu(z)

(165)

\displaystyle=\ell^{-s}\sum_{\gamma\in C_{\Gamma}(P)\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{H}}k(z,\gamma^{-1}P\gamma z)y^{s}\,d\mu(z).

Let us write

\gamma^{-1}P\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
c\neq 0
since
|t|>2
and
a,b,c,d
are integers. If we let

\sigma_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}|c|^{-1/2}&a|c|^{-1/2}\sgn(c)\\
0&|c|^{1/2}\end{pmatrix},

then a direct computation shows that

\sigma_{1}^{-1}\gamma^{-1}P\gamma\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\sgn(c)\\
\sgn(c)&t\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, performing the change of variables
z\mapsto\sigma_{1}z
we see that

\int_{\mathbb{H}}k(z,\gamma^{-1}P\gamma z)y^{s}\,d\mu(z)=|c|^{-s}\int_{\mathbb{H}}k\left(z,\begin{pmatrix}0&-\sgn(c)\\
\sgn(c)&t\end{pmatrix}z\right)y^{s}\,d\mu(z).

Furthermore, by the change of variables
z\mapsto-\overline{z}
we see that

\int_{\mathbb{H}}k\left(z,\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&t\end{pmatrix}z\right)y^{s}\,d\mu(z)=\int_{\mathbb{H}}k\left(z,\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&t\end{pmatrix}z\right)y^{s}\,d\mu(z).

Thus, coming back to (
165
) we deduce that

\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}K_{P}(z;\Phi)E(z;s)\,d\mu(z)=Z_{P}(s)V(s;t;\Phi),

(166)

where

V(s;t;\Phi):=\int_{\mathbb{H}}k\left(z,\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&t\end{pmatrix}z\right)y^{s}\,d\mu(z).

Note that if we let

\sigma_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{-t+\sqrt{t^{2}-4}}{2}&\frac{-t-\sqrt{t^{2}-4}}{2}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

then
\det(\sigma_{2})=\Delta^{1/2}
and, furthermore, we have

\sigma_{2}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&t\end{pmatrix}\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}N^{1/2}&0\\
0&N^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, after changing variables
z\mapsto\sigma_{2}z
we have

\displaystyle V(s;t;\Phi)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{s/2}\int_{\mathbb{H}}k(z,Nz)\frac{y^{s}}{|z+1|^{2s}}\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{s/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi\left(\frac{|(N-1)z|^{2}}{Ny^{2}}\right)\frac{y^{s}}{((x+1)^{2}+y^{2})^{s}}\frac{dx\,dy}{y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{s/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\Delta(1+x^{2}))G(x;s)\,dx,

where

G(x;s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{s}}{((1+xy)^{2}+y^{2})^{s}}\frac{dy}{y}.

It is clear that this integral converges absolutely on
\text{Re}(s)>0
, so that
V(s;t;\Phi)
is holomorphic on
\text{Re}(s)>0
. All the previous manipulations are justified when
\Phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty))
is nonnegative and
s=\sigma>1
. Thus, from (
166
) we deduce that the series defining
Z_{P}(s)
is absolutely convergent for
\text{Re}(s)>1
.

The function
V(s;t;\Phi)
has meromorphic continuation to
\mathbb{C}
, see
[
50
, Proposition 4]
. By varying the test function
\Phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty))
in equation (
166
) we deduce the assertion of the lemma regarding the meromorphic continuation of
Z_{P}(s)
. To compute the residue at
s=1
, recall that
\text{Res}_{s=1}E(z;s)=\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})^{-1}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\text{Res}_{s=1}\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}K_{P}(z;\Phi)E(z;s)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}K_{P}(z;\Phi)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{C_{\Gamma}(P)\backslash\mathbb{H}}k(z,Pz)\,d\mu(z).

Recall that
C_{\Gamma}(P)=\{\pm P_{0}^{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for a
\Gamma
-primitive hyperbolic element
P_{0}
. Letting
\sigma_{3}
such that
\sigma_{3}^{-1}P\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}N^{1/2}&0\\
0&N^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}
and changing variable
z\mapsto\sigma_{3}z
we see that

\displaystyle\text{Res}_{s=1}\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}}K_{P}(z;\Phi)E(z;s)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{1}^{N(P_{0})}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Phi\left(\frac{|(N-1)z|^{2}}{Ny^{2}}\right)\frac{dx\,dy}{y^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log N(P_{0})}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\Delta(1+x^{2}))\,dx.

On the other hand, it is not difficult to show that
G(x;1)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(x)
, which by parity considerations implies that

V(1;t;\Phi)=\frac{\pi\Delta^{1/2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(\Delta(1+x^{2}))\,dx.

Comparing with (
166
) and taking
\Phi
such that
V(1;t;\Phi)\neq 0
we obtain

\text{Res}_{s=1}Z_{P}(s)=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\log N(P_{0})}{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\Delta^{1/2}},

as desired.
∎

It is a natural step to combine the series
Z_{P}(s)
attached to the different conjugacy classes
\{P\}_{\Gamma}
for which
\Tr(P)
takes a fixed value. When
|t|\geq 3
we define

Z(s;t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}\\
\Tr(P)=t\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi}(P)Z_{P}(s)=\ell^{-s}\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{a(c;t)}{c^{s}},

(167)

for certain coefficients
a(c;t)
. By Lemma
9.1
the series
Z(s;t)
has meromorphic continuation to
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
with at most a single pole at
s=1
. Furthermore, by formula (
164
) we have

\text{Res}_{s=1}Z(s;t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
\Tr(P)=t\end{subarray}}\frac{2\log N(P_{0})\chi(P)}{\pi\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})(N(P)^{1/2}-N(P)^{-1/2})},

(168)

where we have used that
\Tr(P)=\Tr(P^{-1})
and
\chi(P^{-1})=\overline{\chi(P)}
. In particular, we can express the sum
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)
as

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2}\sum_{2<t\leq X^{1/2}+X^{-1/2}}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}\text{Res}_{s=1}Z(s;t).

(169)

9.2
Average over
t
of the coefficients of
Z(s;t)
.

Let us analyze the coefficients
a(c;t)
more closely. For two elements of
\Gamma
, let us write
P_{1}\sim_{\ell}P_{2}
if they are conjugate by an element of
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&\ell\mathbb{Z}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\}
, and let us denote the resulting equivalence classes of
\Gamma
by
\Gamma/\sim_{\ell}
. The coefficients
a(c;t)
are given by

a(c;t)=a^{+}(c;t)+a^{-}(c;t),

where

a^{\pm}(c;t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[P]\in\Gamma/\sim_{\ell}\\
c(P)=\pm c,\,\Tr(P)=t\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi(P)}.

Note that the map
P\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&\ell\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}P
establishes a bijection between the two sets

\{[P]\in\Gamma/\sim_{\ell}:c(P)=\pm c,\Tr(P)=t\}\longleftrightarrow\{[P]\in\Gamma/\sim_{\ell}:c(P)=\pm c,\Tr(P)=t\pm c\ell\}.

Since
\chi(\begin{pmatrix}1&\ell\\
0&1\end{pmatrix})=e(-\alpha)
, it follows that we have
a^{\pm}(c;t+c\ell)=e(\pm\alpha)a^{\pm}(c;t)
. Thus, we see that

t\mapsto a^{\pm}(c;t)e(\mp\alpha t/c\ell)
is periodic modulo
c\ell
.

The coefficients
a^{\pm}(c;t)
can be expressed in terms of Kloosterman sums.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
c\geq 1
. Then

a^{\pm}(c;t)=\frac{\chi(\pm I)}{c\ell}\sum_{n\modulo c\ell}S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}\left(\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell},\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell};c\right)e\left(\mp\frac{(n-\alpha)t}{c\ell}\right).

(170)

Proof.

Since
-I\in\Gamma
, it follows that
a^{-}(c;t)=\chi(-I)a^{+}(c;-t)
. Thus, it is enough to prove the lemma for
a^{+}(c;t)
.
Recalling the definition of Kloosterman sums from (
46
) and grouping the summands by their trace modulo
c\ell
we see that

\displaystyle S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}\left(\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell},\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell};c\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[\gamma]\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}\\
c(\gamma)=c\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi(\gamma)}e\left(\frac{(n-\alpha)(a(\gamma)+d(\gamma))}{c\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\modulo c\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[\gamma]\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}\\
c(\gamma)=c\\
\Tr(\gamma)=t\modulo c\ell\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi(\gamma)}e\left(-\frac{\alpha t}{c\ell}\right)e\left(\frac{nt}{c\ell}\right).

By a direct computation
\Tr\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&xl\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\gamma\right)=\Tr(\gamma)+xc(\gamma)l
and
c\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&xl\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\gamma\right)=c(\gamma)
. It follows that the natural projection map
\Gamma/\sim_{\ell}\rightarrow\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}
induces a bijection

\{[\gamma]\in\Gamma/\sim_{\ell}:c(\gamma)=c,\Tr(\gamma)=t\}\longleftrightarrow\{[\gamma]\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma/\Gamma_{\infty}:c(\gamma)=c,\Tr(\gamma)=t\modulo c\ell\}.

Thus, we obtain

S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}\left(\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell},\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell};c\right)=\sum_{t\modulo c\ell}\left(a^{+}(c;t)e\left(\frac{-\alpha t}{c\ell}\right)\right)e\left(\frac{nt}{c\ell}\right).

The lemma follows from this identity by Fourier inversion.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
9.2
we can obtain precise asymptotics for partial sums of the coefficients
a(c;t)
over the variable
t
. The following result is a generalization of
[
45
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 9.3
.

If
c,z\geq 1
, then

\sum_{t\leq z}a(c;t)=(1+\chi(-I))\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}z+O_{q,\varepsilon}(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}).

(171)

Proof.

It is enough to look at the partial sums of
a^{+}(c;t)
, since the case of
a^{-}(c;t)
is analogous and we have
a(c;t)=a^{+}(c;t)+a^{-}(c;t)
. By (
170
) we have

\sum_{t\leq z}a^{+}(c;t)=\frac{1}{c\ell}\sum_{n\modulo c\ell}S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}\left(\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell},\frac{n-\alpha}{\ell};c\right)\sum_{t\leq z}e\left(-\frac{(n-\alpha)t}{c\ell}\right).

When
\alpha=0
, the term
n=0
gives the contribution

\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c\ell)}{c\ell}\sum_{t\leq z}1=\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c\ell)}{c\ell}z+O_{q}(1).

When
n-\alpha\neq 0
we have the familiar bound

\sum_{t\leq z}e\left(-\frac{(n-\alpha)t}{c\ell}\right)\ll||(n-\alpha)/c\ell||^{-1}

where
||x||
is the distance from
x
to the nearest integer. Using this estimate together with the bound for Kloosterman sums from Lemma
3.5
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{t\leq z}a(c;t)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c\ell)}{c\ell}z+O_{q,\varepsilon}\left(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}\sum_{1\leq n\leq\lfloor c\ell/2\rfloor}\left(\frac{((n-\alpha)q,c)^{1/2}}{n-\alpha}+\frac{((n+\alpha)q,c)^{1/2}}{n+\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c\ell)}{c\ell}z+O_{q,\varepsilon}(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

as desired.
∎

9.3
Main term of the
t
-average.

Suppose that
\alpha=0
and
k=0
. In this case the cusp
\infty
of
(\Gamma,\chi)
is singular. Recall the Eisenstein series
E(z;s)=E_{\infty}(z;s;\Gamma,\chi)
, given as

E(z;s)=\ell^{-s}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma}\overline{\chi}(\gamma)\text{Im}(\gamma z)^{s},

where
l\geq 1
is the width of the cusp
\infty
. We know that

\int_{0}^{1}E(\ell(x+iy);s)\,dx=y^{s}+\phi_{\infty,\infty}(s)y^{1-s},

(172)

where

\phi_{\infty,\infty}(s):=\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(s-1/2)}{\Gamma(s)}\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s),

(173)

and

\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s):=\ell^{-2s}\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c^{2s}},

see
[
20
, Theorem 3.4]
for the case of trivial
\chi
, the proof of the general case being the same. Thus,
\phi_{\infty,\infty}(s)
is the
(\infty,\infty)
-entry in the scattering matrix for
(\Gamma,\chi)
. By (
172
) and (
173
), the function
\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)
inherits the holomorphicity properties of
E(z;s)
. In particular
\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)
is holomorphic on
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
except for a possible simple pole at
s=1
of residue

\text{Res}_{s=1}\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)=\frac{\delta_{\chi=1}}{\pi\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}.

In order to handle an average over
c
of the main term of (
171
) using Mellin inversion we need to control the growth of
\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)
on vertical strips.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
s=\sigma+it
. Then, for
\sigma\geq 1/2
and
|t|\geq 1
it holds that

\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}|s|^{\max(1-\sigma,0)+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

From Section 2.7 in the proof of
[
37
, Theorem 1]
, it follows that
\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)
is a linear combination of functions of the form

\frac{L(2s-1,\chi)}{L(2s,\chi)}\prod_{p\in S}P_{p}(p^{s}),

where
L(s,\chi)
is the Dirichlet
L
-function attached to a character
\chi
,
S
is a finite set of primes, and
P_{p}(X)
is a rational function for each
p\in S
. The lemma follows by standard bounds for Dirichlet
L
-functions.
∎

Remark 9.5
.

The same bound is true for a general cofinite group
\Gamma
and a character
\chi:\Gamma\rightarrow S^{1}
. The idea is to apply the Phrägmen-Lindelöf principle. First, one uses the bound
S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)\ll_{\Gamma}c
to deduce that
\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)
converges absolutely on
\text{Re}(s)>1
. Since the scattering matrix
\Phi(s)
is unitary on
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
, its
(\infty,\infty)
-entry
\phi_{\infty,\infty}(s)
satisfies
|\phi_{\infty,\infty}(s)|\leq 1
on
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
. By (
173
) and Stirling’s formula we deduce that
|\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)|\ll|s|^{1/2}
on
\text{Re}(s)=1/2
. As an outcome of a proof of the analytic continuation of
E(z;s)
one obtains some information about the growth of
\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}
on vertical strips. For example, from (6.11), (6.12) and (6.15) in
[
20
, Proposition 6.1]
it follows that
|\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(s)|=O_{q,\varepsilon}(\exp(|s|^{8+\varepsilon}))
for
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
and
|t|\geq 1
(Iwaniec’s proof assumes that
\chi
is trivial, but the proofs in the general case are similar). At this point one can apply the Phrägmen-Lindelöf principle to deduce the bound of Lemma
9.4
.

We can now evaluate the sum over
c
of the main term from (
171
).

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
\omega\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
be such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\omega(v)\,dv=1
, and let
V>0
be a parameter. Then

\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2V}\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}\omega(c\ell/V)=\delta_{\chi=1}+O_{q}(V^{-1}).

(174)

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\omega}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega(v)v^{s}\,dv/v
be the Mellin transform of
\omega
. On any fixed vertical strip we have
|\widehat{\omega}(s)|\ll_{A}(1+|s|)^{-A}
for any
A>0
. By Mellin inversion it follows that

\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2V}\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}\omega(c\ell/V)=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{4\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)\widehat{\omega}(s)V^{s-1}\,ds,

where
\sigma>1
. Moving the contour to the left is justified by Lemma
9.4
and the decay of
\widehat{\omega}(s)
. If we move the contour to
\text{Re}(s)=0
and pick up the residue at
s=1
when
\chi
is trivial we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2V}\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}\omega(c\ell/V)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\chi=1}+\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{4\pi i}\int_{(0)}\psi_{(\Gamma,\chi)}\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)\widehat{\omega}(s)V^{s-1}\,ds

\displaystyle=\delta_{\chi=1}+O_{q}(V^{-1}),

as desired.
∎

9.4
Estimate for
Z(s;t)
on the critical line.

In this section we estimate
Z(s;t)
on the critical line. Let

Z^{\pm}(s;t):=\ell^{-s}\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{\pm}(c;t)}{c^{s}}.

It is clear that

Z^{-}(s;t)=\chi(-I)Z^{+}(s;-t),

(175)

and by (
167
) we have

Z(s;t)=Z^{+}(s;t)+Z^{-}(s;t).

Recall Definition
1.1
regarding admissible exponents.

Proposition 9.7
.

Let
\theta>0
be any admissible exponent. There exists
A>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
the bound

Z^{\pm}(s;t)\ll_{q,\theta,\varepsilon}(1+|s|)^{A}t^{2\theta+\varepsilon}

holds uniformly for
|t|\geq 3
,
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
and
|s-1|\geq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By (
175
) it is enough to consider the case of
Z^{+}(s;t)
. We write
\gamma_{1}\sim_{q}\gamma_{2}
if the matrices are conjugate by an element of
\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&q\mathbb{Z}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\}
, and let
\Gamma/\sim_{q}
denote the corresponding equivalence classes. Note that we can write

Z^{+}(s;t)=\frac{\ell^{1-s}}{q}\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{b(c;t)}{c^{s}},

where

b(c;t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}[P]\in\Gamma/\sim_{q}\\
c(P)=c,\,\Tr(P)=t\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi(P)}.

Recall the group
\widetilde{\Gamma}:=\Gamma(q)\backslash\Gamma
. Since
\chi
is trivial on
\Gamma(q)
, we can group matrices by their reduction modulo
q
and write

Z^{+}(s;t)=\frac{\ell^{1-s}}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\gamma}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}\\
\Tr(\widetilde{\gamma})=t\modulo q\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi\left(\widetilde{\gamma}\right)}\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{b(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma})}{c^{s}},

where

b(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma}):=\left|\{[\gamma]\in\Gamma/\sim_{q}:\gamma=\widetilde{\gamma}\modulo q,c(\gamma)=c,\Tr(\gamma)=t\}\right|.

We can evaluate the cardinality
b(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma})
by classifying equivalence classes
[\gamma]\in\Gamma/\sim_{q}
according to the residue class
a(\gamma)\modulo qc
. Recall that

\begin{pmatrix}1&yq\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-yq\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a+yqc&*\\
c&d-yqc\end{pmatrix}.

It follows immediately that

b(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma})=\delta_{\{c=c(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo q\}}\times|\{a\modulo qc:a=a(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo q\text{ and }a^{2}-at+1=-b(\widetilde{\gamma})c\modulo qc\}|.

For each
c\geq 1
we write
c=c_{0}c_{q}
where
(c_{0},q)=1
and
c_{q}\mid q^{\infty}
. By the Chinese remainder theorem we can write

b(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma})=b_{0}(c_{0};t)\times b_{q}(c_{q};t;\widetilde{\gamma};c_{0}\modulo q),

where

b_{0}(c_{0};t):=\left|\{a\modulo c_{0}:a^{2}-ta+1=0\modulo c_{0}\}\right|

and, for
r\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,

b_{q}(c_{q};t;\widetilde{\gamma};r):=\delta_{\{c_{q}r=c(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo q\}}\times\left|\{a\modulo qc_{q}:a=a(\widetilde{\gamma})\modulo q\text{ and }a^{2}-at+1=-b(\widetilde{\gamma})rc_{q}\modulo qc_{q}\}\right|.

Let us define

Z_{q}(s;t;\widetilde{\gamma};r):=\sum_{c\mid q^{\infty}}\frac{b_{q}(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma};r)}{c^{s}},

as well as

Z_{0}(s;t;\psi):=\sum_{(c,q)=1}\frac{b_{0}(c;t)\psi(c)}{c^{s}},

where
\psi
is a Dirichlet character modulo
q
. Then by orthogonality of characters we have

Z^{+}(s;t)=\frac{\ell^{1-s}}{q\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\gamma}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}\\
\Tr(\widetilde{\gamma})=t\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi(\widetilde{\gamma})}\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}Z_{q}(s;t;\widetilde{\gamma};a)\sum_{\psi\modulo q}\overline{\psi(a)}Z_{0}(s;t;\psi).

(176)

Write
t^{2}-4=Df^{2}
where
D
is a fundamental discriminant. According to
[
49
, Proposition 3]
we have

\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{0}(c;t)}{c^{s}}=\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}L(s;\psi_{D})\sum_{d\mid f}\mu(d)\left(\frac{D}{d}\right)d^{-s}\sigma_{1-2s}(f/d).

Here
\psi_{D}(\cdot)=\left(\frac{D}{\cdot}\right)
is the Kronecker symbol and
\sigma_{\nu}(m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid m\\
d>0\end{subarray}}d^{\nu}
. Since
c\mapsto\psi(c)
is completely multiplicative, it follows by standard manipulations with Dirichlet series that

Z_{0}(s;t;\psi)=H(s;t;\psi)\frac{L(s;\psi)}{L(2s;\psi)}L(s;\psi_{D}\psi)

where
H(s;t;\psi)
is a Dirichlet series depending on
t
,
q
and
\psi
which is holomorphic on
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
and satisfies

H(s;t;\psi)\ll_{q}\log(3+|s|)

in the same region, independently of
t
. By the definition of admissible exponent
\theta>0
we have, for some
A>0
, the bound

L(s;\psi_{D}\psi)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}(t^{2}-4)^{\theta+\varepsilon}(1+|s|)^{A},

where
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
. We deduce that, for some
A>0
, we have

Z_{0}(s;t;\psi)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}(t^{2}-4)^{\theta+\varepsilon}(1+|s|)^{A}

(177)

in the region
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
. It remains to bound
Z_{q}(s;t;\widetilde{\gamma};r)
. It is easy to check that if
c\mid q^{\infty}
, we have
b(c;t;\widetilde{\gamma};r)\ll_{q}(t^{2}-4,c)^{1/2}
. If we write
t^{2}-4=\prod_{p}p^{e_{p}}
, then we have

Z_{q}(s;t;\widetilde{\gamma};r)\ll_{q}\prod_{p\mid q}\left(e_{p}+\frac{1}{1-p^{-1/2}}\right)\ll_{q,\varepsilon}t^{\varepsilon}

(178)

in the region
\text{Re}(s)\geq 1/2
. The proof of Proposition
9.7
is concluded by combining (
176
) with (
177
) and (
178
).
∎

10
Applications of the Bykovskii-Zagier series.

10.1
Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

Let
2\leq u\leq X
. For conciness, let
q(y)
be the function
q(y):=y^{1/2}+y^{-1/2}
. By equation (
169
), we can express the difference
\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;\chi)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)
as

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;\chi)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2}\sum_{q(X)<t\leq q(X+u)}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}\text{Res}_{s=1}Z(s;t).

Let
V>0
be a parameter to be chosen later, and let
\omega\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
satisfy
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\omega(x)\,dx=1
. Consider the approximation

\mathcal{S}_{V}:=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2V}\sum_{c\geq 1}\sum_{q(X)<t\leq q(X+u)}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}a(c;t)\omega(c\ell/V).

(179)

If we execute first the summation over
c
and use Mellin inversion we obtain

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{4\pi i}\int_{(\sigma)}\left(\sum_{q(X)<t\leq q(X+u)}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}Z(s;t)\right)\widehat{\omega}(s)V^{s-1}\,ds,

where
\sigma>1
. Moving the contour to
\sigma=1/2
and picking up the residue at
s=1
we get

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;\chi)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)+\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{4\pi i}\int_{(1/2)}\left(\sum_{q(X)<t\leq q(X+u)}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}Z(s;t)\right)\widehat{\omega}(s)V^{s-1}\,ds.

Note that
q(X)\sim X^{1/2}
and
q(X+u)-q(X)\sim uX^{-1/2}/2
. Bounding
Z(s;t)
via Proposition
9.7
we get

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;\chi)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)+O_{q,\varepsilon}(uX^{\theta}V^{-1/2+\varepsilon}).

(180)

We now come back to (
179
) and execute the summation over
t
. Using summation by parts and Lemma
9.3
it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{q(X)<t\leq q(X+u)}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}a(c;t)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha=0}(1+\chi(-I))\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}\int_{q(X)}^{q(X+u)}(t^{2}-4)^{1/2}\,dt+O_{q,\varepsilon}(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}X^{1/2})

\displaystyle+O_{q,\varepsilon}\left(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}\int_{q(X)}^{q(X+u)}1\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha=0}(1+\chi(-I))\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{2c\ell}u+O_{q,\varepsilon}(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}X^{1/2}).

Therefore, the sum
\mathcal{S}_{V}
can be evaluated as

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{\pi\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})(1+\chi(-I))}{2}\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{2c\ell V}\omega(c\ell/V)u+O_{q,\varepsilon}(V^{1/2+\varepsilon}X^{1/2}).

If we use (
174
) we can simplify this formula to

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\delta_{\chi=1}u+O_{q,\varepsilon}(V^{1/2+\varepsilon}X^{1/2}+uV^{-1}).

(181)

Comparing (
180
) and (
181
) we obtain

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;\chi)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=\delta_{\chi=1}u+O_{q,\varepsilon}(uX^{\theta}V^{-1/2+\varepsilon}+V^{1/2+\varepsilon}X^{1/2}).

If we optimize the error term by letting
V=uX^{-1/2+\theta}
we arrive at

\Psi_{\Gamma}(X+u;\chi)-\Psi_{\Gamma}(X;\chi)=\delta_{\chi=1}u+O_{q,\varepsilon}(u^{1/2}X^{1/4+\theta/2+\varepsilon}),

as desired.

10.2
Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Let
X\geq 1
sufficiently large, let
1\leq T\leq X^{1/2}
and consider
\beta
defined by
2\beta=\log X+i/T
. We are going to apply the Selberg trace formula (Lemma
3.4
) with the test function

h(t)=\frac{\sinh(\pi+2i\beta)t}{\sinh(\pi t)}.

Recall that there are no small eigenvalues in the case
k=1
. On the spectral side we get

\Lambda_{\text{Res}}(h)+\Lambda_{\text{cusp}}(h)=\delta_{\chi=1}h(i/2)+\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X)+O_{q}(1+\delta_{k=0}X^{7/64}\log X),

where we used (
162
).
The Fourier transform of
h
is easily computed to be

g(x)=\frac{-i\sinh(2\beta)}{4}\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(x/2)-\cosh^{2}(\beta)},

recall (
45
). Note that
\text{Re}(4\cosh^{2}(\beta))\sim X
and
\text{Im}(4\cosh^{2}(\beta))\sim X/T
. It follows easily that

\int_{0}^{\infty}|g(x)|\,dx\ll X\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u+u^{-1}+2-4\cosh^{2}(\beta)}\frac{du}{u}\ll\log X.

Also, we know that

\int_{-R}^{R}\left|\frac{\phi^{\prime}}{\phi}(1/2+it)\right|\,dt+\int_{-R}^{R}\left|\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\Gamma}(1+it)\right|=O(R\log R),

recall (
41
) and Stirling’s formula. At this point it is straightforward to check that

\Lambda_{\text{Id}}(h)=O(\log X),\hskip 10.00002pt\Lambda_{\text{Ell}}(h)=O\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(u)|\,du\right)=O(\log X),\hskip 10.00002pt\Lambda_{\text{Par}-\text{Cont}}(h)=O(\log X+T\log T).

Thus, an application of the Selberg trace formula to the congruence character
(\Gamma,\chi)
and the test functions
h
produces the identity

\delta_{\chi=1}h(i/2)+\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(P)\log N(P_{0})}{N(P)^{1/2}-N(P)^{-1/2}}g(\log N(P))+O(T\log T+\log X+\delta_{k=0}X^{7/64})

(182)

It remains to evaluate the hyperbolic contribution. Note that
\Tr(P)
and
N(P)
are related by
\Tr(P)=N(P)^{1/2}+N(P)^{-1/2}
. It follows that

g(\log N(P))=-\frac{i\sinh(2\beta)}{\Tr(P)^{2}-4\cosh^{2}\beta}.

Accordingly, let us write

f(t):=-\frac{i\sinh(2\beta)}{t^{2}-4\cosh^{2}\beta}.

(183)

We have
f(2\cosh(x/2))=g(x)
. It follows that

\int_{2}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(x)\sinh(x/2)\,dx=\frac{h(i/2)}{2}+O(1).

(184)

Using (
168
) we deduce that

\Lambda_{\text{Hyp}}(h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P\}_{\Gamma}\\
\Tr(P)>2\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(P)\log N(P_{0})}{N(P)^{1/2}-N(P)^{-1/2}}g(\log N(P))=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2}\sum_{t>2}f(t)\text{Res}_{s=1}Z(s;t).

Introduce a parameter
1\leq V\leq X^{1/2}
, and let
\omega\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,\infty))
be such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\omega(x)\,dx=1
. Consider the double sum

\mathcal{S}_{V}:=\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2V}\sum_{t>2}\sum_{c\geq 1}a(c;t)f(t)\omega(c\ell/V),

(185)

which approximates the hyperbolic contribution. If we argue as in Section
10.1
, we can execute the sum over
c
, apply Mellin inversion, move the contour to the left and pick up the residue at
s=1
, obtaining

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\Lambda_{\text{hyp}}(h)+O\left(V^{-1/2}\int_{(1/2)}\sum_{t>2}\left|f(t)Z(s;t)\widehat{\omega}(s)\right|\,|ds|\right).

If we estimate
Z(s;t)
by Proposition
9.7
, and bound
f(t)
directly from the explicit formula (
183
) we obtain

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\Lambda_{\text{hyp}}(h)+O_{\varepsilon}(V^{-1/2}X^{1/2+\theta+\varepsilon}).

(186)

On the other hand, we can deal with (
185
) differently by first summing over
t
. Using Lemma
9.3
and summation by parts we arrive at

\displaystyle\sum_{t>2}a(c;t)f(t)

\displaystyle=-(1+\chi(-I))\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}\left(\int_{2}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(t)t\,dt\right)+O_{\varepsilon}\left(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}\int_{2}^{\infty}|f^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=(1+\chi(-I))\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{c\ell}\left(\int_{2}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt\right)+O(1)+O_{\varepsilon}\left(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}\int_{2}^{\infty}|f^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\right).

From the definition of
f
it is easy to check that
\int_{2}^{\infty}|f^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\ll T
. It follows that

\sum_{t>2}a(c;t)f(t)=(1+\chi(-I))\delta_{\alpha=0}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{2c\ell}h(i/2)+O_{\varepsilon}(c^{1/2+\varepsilon}T),

where we have used (
184
). After summing over
c
we get

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\delta_{\alpha=0}(1+\chi(-I))\frac{\vol(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})\pi}{2V}\sum_{c}\frac{S_{(\Gamma,\chi)}(0,0;c)}{2c\ell}\omega(c\ell/V)h(i/2)+O_{\varepsilon}(V^{1/2+\varepsilon}T).

(187)

Finally, applying Lemma
9.6
we obtain

\mathcal{S}_{V}=\delta_{\chi=1}h(i/2)+O_{\varepsilon}(X^{1/2}V^{-1}+V^{1/2+\varepsilon}T).

(188)

If we put together (
186
) and (
188
) we see that

\Lambda_{\text{Hyp}}(h)=\delta_{\chi=1}h(i/2)+O_{\varepsilon}(X^{1/2+\theta+\varepsilon}V^{-1/2}+V^{1/2+\varepsilon}T).

We can optimize the error term by taking
V=X^{1/2+\theta}T^{-1}
, obtaining

\Lambda_{\text{Hyp}}(h)=\delta_{\chi=1}h(i/2)+O_{\varepsilon}(X^{1/4+\theta/2+\varepsilon}T^{1/2}).

Combining this formula with (
182
) we arrive at the estimate

\mathcal{S}_{0}(T,X)=O_{\varepsilon}(X^{1/4+\theta/2+\varepsilon}T^{1/2}).

By Proposition
3.3
, this bound implies Theorem
1.8
.
