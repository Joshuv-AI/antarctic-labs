Minkowski-Type Wasserstein Metrics and Barycenters for Location-Scale Mixtures with Application to Domain Adaptation
Songyan Luo, Yunxin Zhang

Abstract
Discrete optimal transport (OT) typically relies on pointwise matching between empirical measures, incurring computational costs that scale at least quadratically with the sample size. To circumvent this limitation, we introduce a mathematical framework for OT between finite location-scale mixture models. By defining a specific function class grounded in generalized Minkowski inequalities and characterizing OT maps between multivariate location-scale families, we extend Wasserstein-type metrics and barycenters to these mixture models under the assumption of identifiability. Furthermore, we prove that restricting joint couplings to a specific mixture structure reduces the continuous multimarginal OT problem to a discrete transport problem over mixture components. Computing transport plans between these components rather than individual samples reduces the computational complexity to linear scaling with respect to the sample size. Empirical evaluations on the VisDA-C benchmark confirm that this strategy achieves competitive accuracy compared to existing empirical OT approaches, while substantially reducing the computational cost and memory footprint.

Introduction

Despite the widespread success of machine learning (ML) across various disciplines, traditional ML methodologies still exhibit inherent limitations when deployed in complex real-world scenarios. Achieving robust and reliable performance typically necessitates massive amounts of annotated training data, which incurs substantial computational and financial costs. While the standard independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption simplifies model training and theoretical analysis, the pervasive issue of domain shift
[
28
,
3
]
often degrades the generalization and robustness of models applied across disparate data distributions. To mitigate this issue, various transfer learning paradigms, ranging from domain adaptation (DA)
[
13
]
to domain generalization
[
19
]
, have been proposed. Conventional DA focuses on transferring knowledge from a single labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain. However, practical applications frequently aggregate labeled data from multiple heterogeneous sources, motivating the more complex paradigm of multi-source domain adaptation (MSDA)
[
24
]
. Driven by this broader context, this paper establishes a theoretical framework whose underpinnings accommodate both two-marginal settings (i.e., standard DA between a single source and a target) and multimarginal settings (i.e., MSDA). Nevertheless, recognizing the distinct algorithmic complexities inherent in multi-source integration, our practical algorithmic design and empirical evaluations in this work are strictly focused on the foundational two-marginal DA scenario.

To address the distribution shift inherent in DA, optimal transport (OT) has emerged as a principled mathematical framework
[
4
]
. By defining robust metrics between probability distributions — most notably the Wasserstein and Gromov-Wasserstein distances
[
34
]
— OT provides a geometrically meaningful way to align source and target domains. Compared to traditional information-theoretic measures (e.g., Kullback-Leibler divergence), OT-based metrics are particularly effective in capturing the underlying geometry of the data space. Recent methodologies have further expanded the theoretical and practical efficacy of OT. For instance, in the space of Gaussian mixture models (GMMs), Delon and Desolneux
[
6
]
introduced a Wasserstein-type distance that restricts the set of couplings, significantly alleviating computational burdens. Furthermore, in the context of MSDA, Wasserstein barycenters have been successfully employed to aggregate knowledge from multiple heterogeneous sources
[
22
]
, highlighting the practical utility of barycentric formulations in transfer learning.

Despite its theoretical elegance, discrete OT typically relies on pointwise matching between empirical measures, incurring computational costs that scale at least quadratically with the sample size
[
26
]
. This computational bottleneck restricts the applicability of empirical OT to large-scale datasets. To circumvent this limitation, representing data distributions via parametric or semi-parametric mixture models offers a viable alternative. A classical and widely adopted choice is the GMM
[
21
]
, whose origins trace back to Pearson. In this work, we theoretically consider a broader and unified class: the finite location-scale mixture model (LSMM)
[
12
]
. Serving as universal approximators for probability densities, LSMMs can capture complex, multimodal data distributions through a combination of simpler parametric components. This paradigm shift substantially reduces the representation complexity from the number of individual samples to the number of mixture components. While our theoretical framework is established for finite LSMMs, we specifically leverage GMMs in our algorithmic implementation and empirical evaluations to exploit their computational tractability.

The standard
p
-Wasserstein distance (associated with the cost function
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{p}
for
p\geqslant 1
) serves as a cornerstone in OT, providing a profound geometric tool for comparing probability measures. Fundamentally, the metric properties of the
p
-Wasserstein distance are intimately connected to the classical Minkowski inequality. Recognizing that the Minkowski inequality can be generalized to accommodate a much broader class of functions
[
14
]
, a significant mathematical opportunity arises: establishing a generalized Wasserstein-type metric and its corresponding barycenter theory over finite LSMMs for this broader function class. In this article, we address this challenge by proposing a comprehensive mathematical framework. By defining a specific function class grounded in generalized Minkowski-type inequalities and characterizing the OT maps between multivariate location-scale families, we extend Wasserstein-type metrics to identifiable finite LSMMs. Then, we prove that by restricting joint couplings to a specific mixture structure, the continuous multimarginal OT problem reduces to a tractable discrete transport problem over mixture components.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

1.

Theoretical Formulation of Metrics and Barycenters
: Grounded in generalized Minkowski-type inequalities, we introduce a specific function class to construct extended Wasserstein spaces, defining Wasserstein-type metrics and barycenters for identifiable finite LSMMs.

2.

Continuous-to-Discrete Reduction and Bounding
: We prove that by restricting joint couplings to specific mixture structures, both two-marginal and multimarginal continuous OT problems between finite LSMMs can be reduced to tractable discrete OT problems. Furthermore, we establish inequalities bounding these discrete transport costs against their original continuous counterparts.

3.

Characterization of Costs for Affine Optimal Maps
: We provide a complete characterization of translation-invariant cost functions that admit affine OT maps. Specifically, we prove that the optimality of symmetric positive definite affine maps requires the cost function to be separable, and that the presence of any cross-coupling further reduces the cost to a quadratic form.

4.

Analytical and Closed-Form Formulas
: For general multivariate location-scale families, we derive analytical expressions for the generalized transport costs. When instantiating the framework with GMMs and specific functions from our proposed class, we provide closed-form formulas, expanding the analytical tools available for parametric OT.

5.

Algorithmic Efficiency in DA
: Building upon these theoretical foundations, we propose a scalable domain adaptation framework instantiated via GMMs. By computing transport plans between mixture components rather than individual samples, the computational complexity is reduced to linear scaling with respect to the sample size. Empirical evaluations demonstrate significant reductions in computational cost and memory footprint while maintaining competitive accuracy compared to existing empirical approaches.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces preliminary concepts of Wasserstein spaces, barycenters, and OT for DA. Section 3 defines the specific function class and formulates the generalized metrics for finite mixture models. Section 4 establishes the reduction of continuous multimarginal OT problems to discrete OT problems. Section 5 characterizes translation-invariant cost functions admitting affine optimal transport maps and provides analytical or closed-form derivations for multivariate location-scale families. Section 6 details the algorithmic implementation of the proposed framework for domain adaptation and presents the experimental results. Finally, Section 7 concludes the paper.

Definition 4
(
\mathcal{A}
-mixture).

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a subset of
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, called dictionary of atoms. We denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
the set of finite mixtures of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
{\bm{\nu}}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
if and only if there exist
J\in\mathds{N}_{+}
,
({\bm{\mu}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{J})\in\mathcal{A}^{J}
and
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{J})\in\Delta^{J}
such that

{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}{\bm{\mu}}_{j},

which means

F_{{\bm{\nu}}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}F_{{\bm{\mu}}_{j}}(\bm{x}),\quad\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega,

where
F_{{\bm{\nu}}},F_{{\bm{\mu}}_{1}},\dots,F_{{\bm{\mu}}_{J}}
are corresponding cumulative distribution functions. Then
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
.

To define a metric on the set of finite mixtures and derive essential properties, the following identification assumption is required.

Assumption 1
(Identifiability).

We say that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
is identifiable, if for any two mixtures of
\mathcal{A}
,

{\bm{\nu}}_{i}=\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{J_{i}}\lambda_{i,j_{i}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i,j_{i}},\quad\lambda_{i,j_{i}}>0,\;\;\!\forall\,j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\},i\in\{0,1\},

where the atomic distributions
({\bm{\mu}}_{0,1},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{0,J_{0}})
are pairwise distinct, and similarly for
({\bm{\mu}}_{1,1},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{1,J_{1}})
. Then
{\bm{\nu}}_{0}={\bm{\nu}}_{1}
if and only if
J_{0}=J_{1}
, and the indices in the sums can be reordered such that
\lambda_{0,j}=\lambda_{1,j}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{0,j}={\bm{\mu}}_{1,j}
for all
j=1,\dots,J_{0}
.

Classical examples of identifiable mixtures are the set of Gaussian or Cauchy mixtures
[

]
, more identifiable mixtures can be found in
[
16
]
. To define metrics between mixtures, we begin with the following metric assumption on the dictionary of atoms.

Assumption 2

The function

\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 5

Lemma 8

Without loss of generality, we assume that

{\bm{\nu}}_{i}=\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{J_{i}}\lambda_{i,j_{i}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i,j_{i}}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A}),{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}=(\lambda_{i,1},\dots,\lambda_{i,J_{i}})\in\Delta^{J_{i}}
. Let us define

\MWp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}\left(\inf_{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}\Pi(\mathcal{A})\cap\Pi({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1}))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1})\right).

(4)

Then, under
1
, (
4
) is equivalent to (
3
), i.e.
\MWp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})=W_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})
with
W\coloneqq\Wp_{\varphi}^{d}
. Moreover, the sets of minimizers of (
4
) is equal to the set of measures
{\bm{\gamma}}
which can be written as

{\bm{\gamma}}=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}w_{j_{0},j_{1}}^{\ast}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}},

(5)

with
\bm{w}^{\ast}\coloneqq(w_{j_{0},j_{1}}^{\ast})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j_{i}\leqslant J_{i}\\
i\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}
a minimizer of (
3
), and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\in\Gamma_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}},{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}})
for all
j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\},i\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

Our proof combines the approaches of
[
6
]
and
[
10
]
, extending the latter’s result from the specific case of
\varphi(x)=x^{p}
with
p\geqslant 1
to the collection of functions
\MI
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the measures
{\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}}
(respectively
{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}}
) are all distinct, and that
{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}\in\Delta^{J_{i}}_{+}
for all
i\in\{0,1\}
.
First, let
\bm{w}^{\ast}\coloneqq(w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j_{i}\leqslant J_{i}\\
i\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}
be a solution to (
3
). For any
{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\in\Gamma_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}},{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}}),j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\},i\in\{0,1\}
, let us define
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\ast}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j_{i}\leqslant J_{i}\\
i\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}
. There holds
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}\Gamma_{\varphi}^{d}(\mathcal{A})\cap\Pi({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\varphi(\MWp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1}))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\varphi(\Wp_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}},{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}}))

\displaystyle=\varphi(W_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})).

Second, for any
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}\Pi(\mathcal{A})\cap\Pi({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})
. Then, there exists
J\in\mathds{N}_{+}
such that
{\bm{\gamma}}\in\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}
with
{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\in\Pi(\mathcal{A})
. Using that
{\proj_{0}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}=\nu_{0}
and
{\proj_{1}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}=\nu_{1}
where
\proj_{i}
stand for the projection map
(x_{0},\dots,x_{i},\dots)\mapsto x_{i}
for any
i\in\mathds{N}
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}{\proj_{0}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\lambda_{0,j_{0}}{\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}}.

Using the fact that for all
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
,
{\proj_{0}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
and using the identifiability
1
, we obtain that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
, there exists
j_{0}^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,J_{0}\}
such that
{\proj_{0}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}={\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}^{\prime}}
, and the set
\Lambda_{0,j_{0}}\coloneqq\{j:{\proj_{0}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}={\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}},1\leqslant j\leqslant J\}
is a nonempty set for each
j_{0}\in\{1,\dots,J_{0}\}
. Similarly, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
, there exists
j_{1}^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,J_{1}\}
such that
{\proj_{1}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}={\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}^{\prime}},\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{1,j_{1}^{\prime}}
, and the set
\Lambda_{1,j_{1}}\coloneqq\{j:{\proj_{1}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}={\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}},1\leqslant j\leqslant J\}
is a nonempty set for each
j_{1}\in\{1,\dots,J_{1}\}
. Now for each
j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\},i\in\{0,1\}
, we can define
w_{j_{0},j_{1}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{0,j_{0}}\cap\Lambda_{1,j_{1}}}\lambda_{j}
, and

{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}^{\prime}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{w^{\prime}_{j_{0},j_{1}}}\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{0,j_{0}}\cap\Lambda_{1,j_{1}}}{\bm{\gamma}}_{j},&\mbox{if }w_{j_{0},j_{1}}^{\prime}>0;\\
{\bm{\gamma}}_{j_{0},j_{1}},&\mbox{if }w_{j_{0},j_{1}}^{\prime}=0.\end{cases}\in\Pi({\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}},{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}}).

Then
{\bm{\gamma}}=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}w^{\prime}_{j_{0},j_{1}}{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}_{j_{0},j_{1}}
and
(w^{\prime}_{j_{0},j_{1}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j_{i}\leqslant J_{i}\\
i\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}\in\Pi({\bm{\lambda}}_{0},{\bm{\lambda}}_{1})
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{0}.\bm{x}_{1}))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{x}_{0}.\bm{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}w^{\prime}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{0}.\bm{x}_{1}))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}_{j_{0},j_{1}}(\bm{x}_{0}.\bm{x}_{1})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{j_{=}1}^{J}w^{\prime}_{j}\varphi(\Wp^{d}_{\varphi}({\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}^{\prime}},{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle\geqslant\varphi(\Wp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})),

which conclude
\MWp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})\geqslant\Wp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})
.

Finally,
\MWp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})=W_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},{\bm{\nu}}_{1})
, and (
5
) is then a straightforward consequence of this proof.

4.2
Multimarginal problems

When the atomic metric
W
lacks a Euclidean structure, the mere issue of defining the barycenter of points becomes a challenging task. So for the general multimarginal problems, let us define the corresponding barycenter firstly.

Lemma 15
(Borel barycenter map).

Let
\varphi\in\MI
and
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a locally compact Polish space. Then, given any integer
K\geqslant 2
and weights
{\bm{\tau}}\coloneqq(\tau_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}\in\Delta^{K}
, there exists a Borel map
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}:\mathcal{X}^{K}\to\mathcal{X}
that associate
(\bm{x}_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}
to a minimum of
\bm{x}\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{k}))
. Such maps will be called
Borel barycenter map
.

Moreover, if for all
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\bm{y}\mapsto\varphi(d(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
is a strictly convex function with respect to
\bm{y}
on
\mathcal{X}
, then
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}
is a continuous function on
\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}^{K}
and uniquely determined, where the product metric
d_{\mathcal{Z}}
is defined by any norm
h
on
\mathds{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{K}
such that

d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime})\coloneqq h(d(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{0}^{\prime}),\dots,d(\bm{x}_{K-1},\bm{x}_{K-1}^{\prime})),

for all
\bm{X}\coloneqq(\bm{x}_{0},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1}),\bm{X}^{\prime}\coloneqq(\bm{x}_{0}^{\prime},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

The first part of this proof extends the argument of
[
18
]
to our generalized setting. For simplicity, we denote

f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{y},\bm{X})\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{y},\bm{x}_{k})),\quad\forall\,\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X},\bm{X}\in\mathcal{Z}.

Since
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a locally compact Polish space, applying Theorem A.5 in
[
36
]
with

A\coloneqq\left\{(\bm{x}_{0},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1},\bm{x})\in\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathcal{X}}:f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{x},\bm{X})\leqslant f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z},\bm{X}),\forall\,\bm{z}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}.

shows the existence of a Borel section
f
from
\pi_{\mathcal{Z}}(A)
to
\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathcal{X}}
of the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{Z}}:\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{Z}
. Then
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}\coloneqq\pi_{\mathcal{X}}\circ f
is a Borel barycenter map, where
\pi_{\mathcal{X}}:\mathcal{Z}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the projection.

Secondly, if
\bm{y}\mapsto\varphi(d(\bm{x},\bm{y}))
is a strictly convex function for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}
, then
f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{y},\bm{X})
is a strictly convex continuous function with respect to
\bm{y}
on
\mathcal{X}
, so its global minimum point is unique. We therefore can define

T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{X}}f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{y},\bm{X}).

For fixed
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{Z},\varepsilon>0
, and any
\bm{X}^{\prime}\coloneqq(\bm{x}_{0}^{\prime},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}
, we denote
\bm{z}\coloneqq T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X}),\bm{z}^{\prime}\coloneqq T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X}^{\prime})
. If
d(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})\geqslant\varepsilon
, since the strict convexity of
f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\,\cdot\,,\bm{X})
and
\varphi\in\MI
, then there exists
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that
\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{X}))>\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z},\bm{X}))+\varepsilon_{1}
. According to
lemma

6
, for any
\bm{y}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\left\lvert\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{y},\bm{X}))-\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{y},\bm{X}^{\prime}))\right\rvert\leqslant\varphi^{-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{x}_{k}^{\prime})))\right).

It is straightforward to verify that
d_{\mathcal{Z}}
defines a norm on
\mathcal{Z}
. By the equivalence of norms in finite dimensions, there exists
\varepsilon_{2}>0
such that for any
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime})<\varepsilon_{2}
,

\max_{k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}}{d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{x}_{k}^{\prime})}<\varphi^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{K}\varphi(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{3})\right),

which implies

\varphi^{-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{x}_{k}^{\prime})))\right)<\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{3}.

Now if there exists
\bm{X}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime})<\varepsilon_{2}
and
d(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})\geqslant\varepsilon
, then

\frac{2\varepsilon_{1}}{3}>\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{X}))-\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z},\bm{X}))+\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z},\bm{X}^{\prime}))-\varphi^{-1}(f_{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{X}^{\prime}))>\varepsilon_{1}.

This is a contradiction, so
d_{\mathcal{Z}}(\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime})<\varepsilon_{2}
implies
d(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime})<\varepsilon
, therefore
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}
is a continuous function and uniquely determined.

Theorem 16
(Barycenter and multimarginal problem).

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a locally compact Polish space, and
\varphi\in\MI
. Given an integer
K\geqslant 2
,
({\bm{\mu}}_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}^{d}(\mathcal{X})^{K}
and weights
{\bm{\tau}}\coloneqq(\tau_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}\in\Delta^{K}
, there exists a measure
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}\in\Gamma({\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1})
minimizing

{\bm{\gamma}}\mapsto\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{k}))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{x}_{0},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1}).

Moreover, let
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}:\mathcal{X}^{K}\to\mathcal{X}
be a Borel barycenter map (as in
lemma

15
). Then, the measure
{\bm{\mu}}_{B}\coloneqq{T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}
is a barycenter of
({\bm{\mu}}_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}
. Furthermore, if this map is unique, any barycenter
{\bm{\mu}}_{B}
is of the form
{\bm{\mu}}_{B}={T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}
.

Proof.

This proof extends the argument of
[
1
,
18
]
to our setting. Under the assumptions that
\varphi\in\MI
and that
\mathcal{X}
is locally compact, the existence of a solution to the multimarginal problem follows directly from
lemma

1
.

Let
{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}
be a solution of the multimarginal problem, and set
{\bm{\mu}}_{B}\coloneqq{T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}
. For simplicity, we write
\bm{X}\coloneqq(\bm{x}_{0},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1})
. Then, by the definition of the Wasserstein distance associated with
\varphi
, for each
k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
,

\varphi(\Wp_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\mu}}_{B}))\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K}}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}(\bm{X}),

and consequently,

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(\Wp_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\mu}}_{B}))\leqslant\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}(\bm{X}).

(6)

Furthermore, for any
{\bm{\nu}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varphi}^{d}(\mathcal{X})^{K}
, let
\pi_{k}\in\Gamma({\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\nu}}_{k})
be an optimal transport plan between
{\bm{\mu}}_{k}
and
{\bm{\nu}}
for each
k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
.
By the disintegration theorem, for any
k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
, there exists a family of conditional measures
\{{\bm{\mu}}_{k}^{\bm{y}}\}
defined for
{\bm{\nu}}
-almost every
\bm{y}
, which satisfies
\pi_{k}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}_{k},\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{y})={\bm{\mu}}_{k}^{\bm{y}}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}_{k}){\bm{\nu}}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{y})
. We then set,

{\bm{\theta}}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{X},\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{y})\coloneqq{\bm{\mu}}_{0}^{\bm{y}}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}_{0})\times\dots\times{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1}^{\bm{y}}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}_{K-1})\times{\bm{\nu}}(\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{y}),

and let
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}
be the law of the
K
first marginals of
{\bm{\theta}}
. Then, by the construction of
{\bm{\theta}}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(\Wp_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\nu}}))=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K+1}}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y})))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{X},\bm{y})

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\inf_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K+1}}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{z})))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{X},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K+1}}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X}))))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{X},\bm{y})

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K}}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X}))))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}^{K}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})))\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(\Wp_{\varphi}^{d}({\bm{\mu}}_{k},{\bm{\mu}}_{B})).

where the last inequality follows from (
6
). Since
{\bm{\nu}}
is arbitrary, we conclude that
{T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}_{B}
is a barycenter.

Moreover, by choosing
{\bm{\nu}}
to be a barycenter, (
7
) becomes an equality, which implies that for
{\bm{\theta}}
-almost every
(\bm{X},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{X}^{K}\times\mathcal{X}
,

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y})))=\inf_{\bm{z}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{z})))=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})))).

Thus, if the barycenter map
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}
is unique, then
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})=\bm{y}
for
{\bm{\theta}}
-almost everywhere, which implies that
{T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}}_{\#}{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}={\bm{\nu}}
. Furthermore, the optimality of
{\bm{\nu}}
, together with (
6
), shows that
{\bm{\nu}}
is a solution to the multimarginal problem.

Now the theory naturally extends to multimarginal problems by the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a locally compact Polish space whose Borel barycenter map
T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}
is uniquely determined and continuous function for any integer
K\geqslant 2
and any weight vector
{\bm{\tau}}=(\tau_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}\in\Delta^{K}
.

Definition 17
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
satisfy
3
. For any integer
K\geqslant 2
,
\varphi\in\MI
,
{\bm{\tau}}\coloneqq(\tau_{k})_{0\leqslant k\leqslant K-1}\in\Delta^{K}
, and
{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we define

\Wp_{\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}({\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1})\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}\left(\inf_{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Pi({\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1})}c_{\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{X})\right),

(8)

where
\bm{X}=(\bm{x}_{0},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1})\in\mathcal{X}^{K}
and

c_{\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tau_{k}\varphi(d(\bm{x}_{k},T_{B}^{{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{x}_{0},\dots,\bm{x}_{K-1}))).

(9)

Since
T_{B}
is continuous, then, by
lemma

1
, there exists at least one solution to the above problem. The set of minimizers of (
8
) is denoted by
\Gamma_{\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}({\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1})
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be a dictionary of atoms. Similarly, we introduce further notation:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varphi,K}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1}\in\mathcal{A}}\Gamma_{\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}({\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1}),

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1}\in\mathcal{A}}\Pi({\bm{\mu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\mu}}_{K-1}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\Gamma_{\varphi,K}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\Gamma_{\varphi,K}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}(\mathcal{A}))\subseteq\mathcal{M}\Pi_{K}(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\mathcal{M}(\Pi_{K}(\mathcal{A}))\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{K}).

Following an approach similar to that in the previous subsection, we define the multimarginal transport problem.

Definition 18
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
satisfy
3
, and let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be a dictionary of atoms. For any integer
K\geqslant 2
,
\varphi\in\MI
,
{\bm{\tau}}\coloneqq(\tau_{k})_{0\leqslant\tau\leqslant K-1}\in\Delta^{K}
, and
{\bm{\nu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\nu}}_{K-1}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
, we define

\MWp_{\mathcal{A},\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}({\bm{\nu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\nu}}_{K-1})\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}\left(\inf_{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}\Pi_{K}(\mathcal{A})\cap\Pi({\bm{\nu}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{\nu}}_{K-1})}c_{\varphi}^{d,{\bm{\tau}}}(\bm{X})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{X})\right),

(10)

5
Multivariate location-scale families

To facilitate practical implementation, particularly within the subsequent domain adaptation framework, this section specializes our theoretical analysis to multivariate location-scale families. Our contributions are twofold:
theorem

23
establishes the precise conditions under which affine transformations are optimal transport maps, while
theorem

30
provides explicit analytical formulas for the optimal cost. Notably, for specific function families such as GMMs, these reduce to closed-form formulas.

Location-scale measures are generated from affine transformations of a given probability measure. By considering the multivariate version, we define the corresponding set of atoms as follows.

Definition 20
(Affine location-scale atoms).

We define the set of affine location-scale atoms generated from
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathds{R}^{n}})
as

\mathcal{ALS}({\bm{\mu}})=\left\{T_{\#}{\bm{\mu}}:T(\bm{x})=A\bm{x}+\bm{b},\forall\,\bm{x}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}},A\in{\mathds{R}^{n\times n}},\bm{b}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}},\det(A)\neq 0\right\}.

We then rewrite a lemma on affine location-scale measures, which related to the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance.

Lemma 21
(
[
9
]
).

Let
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathds{R}^{n}})
with mean
\bm{m}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}
. Let
{\bm{\mu}}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{1}\in\mathcal{ALS}({\bm{\mu}})
, and there exists
T_{i}:{\mathds{R}^{n}}\to{\mathds{R}^{n}}
with
A_{i}\in{\mathds{R}^{n\times n}},\bm{b}_{i}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
such that

T_{i}(\bm{x})=A_{i}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{i},\;\;{\bm{\mu}}_{i}={T_{i}}_{\#}{\bm{\mu}},\quad\forall\,\bm{x}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}},i\in\{0,1\}.

Then,
{\bm{\mu}}_{i}
has mean
\bm{m}_{i}
and covariance
\Sigma_{i}
defined by

\bm{m}_{i}\coloneqq A_{i}\bm{m}+\bm{b}_{i},\Sigma_{i}\coloneqq A_{i}\Sigma A_{i}^{\mathrm{T}},\quad\forall\,i\in\{0,1\}.

Moreover, the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein distance squared between
{\bm{\mu}}_{0}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{1}
satisfies

\W({\bm{\mu}}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{1})^{2}\geqslant\left\lVert\bm{m}_{0}-\bm{m}_{1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\Tr{\Sigma_0+\Sigma_1-2(\Sigma_0^{\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}},

with equality if and only if the transport map

T:{\mathds{R}^{n}}\to{\mathds{R}^{n}},\quad T(\bm{x})=\Sigma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Sigma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_{1}\Sigma_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}_{0})+\bm{m}_{1},

is such that
T_{\#}{\bm{\mu}}_{0}={\bm{\mu}}_{1}
.

Remark 22
.

If
A_{0},A_{1}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}\coloneqq\{\diag(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):x_{j}\geqslant 0,\forall\,1\leqslant j\leqslant n\}
, or more generally, if
A_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}A_{1}=A_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}A_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{\geqslant 0}^{n}
, then the inequality in
lemma

21
is tight. On the other hand, if this inequality is tight for all
A_{0},A_{1}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{++}^{n}
, then
{\bm{\mu}}
is a shift of a spherically invariant distribution almost surely
[
5
]
.

Let us consider more general cases in which the affine transformation constitutes an optimal transport map.

Theorem 23
(Characterization of Costs for Positive Definite Affine Optimal Maps).

Let
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{n}})
be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{n}
, whose density is strictly positive on a non-empty open set
U\subseteq{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. Let
\mathscr{P}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}_{++}^{n}\times{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. For each
(A,\bm{b})\in\mathscr{P}
, we define
T_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{x})\coloneqq A\bm{x}+\bm{b}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{A,\bm{b}}\coloneqq{T_{A,\bm{b}}}_{\#}{\bm{\mu}}
.
Let the cost function
c:{\mathds{R}^{n}}\times{\mathds{R}^{n}}\to\mathds{R}
be translation-invariant, i.e.,
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})\coloneqq\tilde{c}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})
, where
\tilde{c}\in C({\mathds{R}^{n}})
is twice continuously differentiable except on a finite set
\mathscr{S}\subset{\mathds{R}^{n}}
.
Assume that for each
(A,\bm{b})\in\mathscr{P}
, there exist upper semicontinuous functions
u_{A,\bm{b}}\in L^{1}({\bm{\mu}})
and
v_{A,\bm{b}}\in L^{1}({\bm{\mu}}_{A,\bm{b}})
such that
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})\geqslant u_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{x})+v_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{y})
for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, and that

C_{c}({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}}_{A,\bm{b}})\coloneqq\inf_{{\bm{\gamma}}\in\Pi({\bm{\mu}},{\bm{\mu}}_{A,\bm{b}})}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}\times{\mathds{R}^{n}}}c(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\bm{\gamma}}(\bm{x},\bm{y})<+\infty.

(i)

Necessity
: Suppose that for any
(A,\bm{b})\in\mathscr{P}
,
T_{A,\bm{b}}
is an optimal transport map with respect to
c
. Assume that there exists at least one matrix
A
with mutually distinct eigenvalues for
n\geqslant 2
, and
A\neq[1]
for
n=1
, such that
\{A\}\times{\mathds{R}^{n}}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
.
Then,
\tilde{c}
is a globally convex function that is separable with respect to the orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
A
. Specifically, if
A=QDQ^{\mathrm{T}}
is the spectral decomposition, there exists a convex function
c_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\tilde{c}(\bm{z})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}((Q^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{z})_{i})
for all
\bm{z}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, where
(\,\cdot\,)_{i}
denotes the
i
-th component of a vector. Furthermore, if
n\geqslant 2
, each
c_{i}
is globally twice continuously differentiable, i.e.,
c_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
.

(ii)

Sufficiency
: Conversely, if
\tilde{c}(\bm{z})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}((Q^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{z})_{i})
for all
\bm{z}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, where each
c_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
is convex, then for any SPD matrix
A
diagonalized by
Q
and any
\bm{b}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, the affine map
T_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{x})=A\bm{x}+\bm{b}
is an optimal transport map with respect to
c
between
{\bm{\mu}}
and
(T_{A,\bm{b}})_{\#}{\bm{\mu}}
. If, in addition, each
c_{i}
is strictly convex, then
T_{A,\bm{b}}
is the unique optimal transport map.

(iii)

Cross-Coupling and Quadratic Costs
: Additionally, suppose the hypotheses of (i) hold. If
\mathscr{P}
contains another matrix
\bar{A}
such that
\{\bar{A}\}\times{\mathds{R}^{n}}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
, let
\bar{A}_{Q}\coloneqq Q^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{A}Q
be its representation in the
Q
-basis. If the
(i,j)
-th entry of
\bar{A}_{Q}
is non-zero for some
i\neq j
, then
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
must be quadratic functions sharing the same second derivative, i.e., there exists a constant
\kappa\geqslant 0
such that
c_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t)=c_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\kappa
for all
t\in\mathds{R}
.

Proof.

The proof is divided into the following steps.

Step 1: First-Order Condition and Regularity Bootstrapping.

Fix an arbitrary
\bm{b}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
and let
T(\bm{x})=A\bm{x}+\bm{b}
. By Kantorovich duality
[
34
, Theorem 5.10]
, there exists a
c
-convex function
\psi
such that the global inequality
\psi^{c}(\bm{y})-\psi(\bm{x})\leqslant c(\bm{x},\bm{y})
holds for all
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, and the duality relation
\psi^{c}(T(\bm{x}))-\psi(\bm{x})=c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))
holds for
{\bm{\mu}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
.

Let
X_{\mathscr{S}}\coloneqq\{\bm{x}\in U:(\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b}\in\mathscr{S}\}
. Since
\mathscr{S}
is finite and the kernel of
\mathrm{I}-A
has dimension at most 1 (because
A
has distinct eigenvalues for
n\geqslant 2
, and
A\neq[1]
for
n=1
),
X_{\mathscr{S}}
is a finite union of affine subspaces of dimension at most 1. For
n\geqslant 2
, this implies the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{n}(X_{\mathscr{S}})=0
. For
n=1
,
A\neq[1]
implies
\mathrm{I}-A
is invertible, so
X_{\mathscr{S}}
is finite and
\mathcal{L}^{1}(X_{\mathscr{S}})=0
. Thus,
U^{\prime}\coloneqq U\setminus X_{\mathscr{S}}
is an open set of full Lebesgue measure in
U
.

We now establish the regularity of
\psi
. Since
\tilde{c}\in C({\mathds{R}^{n}})
is twice continuously differentiable outside the finite set
\mathscr{S}
, it is locally Lipschitz continuous on
{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. Because the optimal map
T(\bm{x})
is continuous, it is locally bounded on
U
. Since the supremum in the
c
-transform is attained at
T(\bm{x})
, this local boundedness, combined with the local Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{c}
, guarantees that the
c
-convex potential
\psi
is locally Lipschitz on
U
. By Rademacher’s theorem,
\psi
is differentiable
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e.

Let
\Omega_{\bm{b}}\subseteq U^{\prime}
be the set of full measure where
\psi
is differentiable and the duality relation holds. For any fixed
\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\bm{b}}
, define the auxiliary function
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\coloneqq c(\bm{x}^{\prime},T(\bm{x}))+\psi(\bm{x}^{\prime})
. The global inequality implies
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\geqslant\psi^{c}(T(\bm{x}))
for all
\bm{x}^{\prime}
, while the duality relation ensures
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})=\psi^{c}(T(\bm{x}))
. Thus,
f_{\bm{x}}
attains a global minimum at
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{x}
.

Since
\bm{x}\in U^{\prime}
(an open set), it is an interior minimum. Furthermore, because
\bm{x}\notin X_{\mathscr{S}}
, the point
\bm{x}-T(\bm{x})\notin\mathscr{S}
, meaning
c(\,\cdot\,,T(\bm{x}))
is continuously differentiable at
\bm{x}
. Since
\psi
is also differentiable at
\bm{x}
, Fermat’s theorem for interior extrema dictates that
\nabla f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})=\bm{0}
. This yields

\nabla\psi(\bm{x})=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x})),\quad\forall\,\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\bm{b}}.

(14)

Notice that the right-hand side is a
C^{1}
vector field on
U^{\prime}
because
\tilde{c}\in C^{2}({\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus\mathscr{S})
. Since
\psi
is locally Lipschitz, it belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,\infty}_{\text{loc}}(U^{\prime})
, and its weak gradient
\nabla\psi
exists almost everywhere.
Since
\nabla\psi
coincides almost everywhere with the
C^{1}
vector field
-\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))
on
U^{\prime}
, it follows immediately
that
\psi
is classically continuously differentiable on
U^{\prime}
, and

\nabla\psi(\bm{x})=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))
holds everywhere on
U^{\prime}
.
The
C^{1}
-regularity of the right-hand side then guarantees that
\psi\in C^{2}(U^{\prime})
.

Step 2: Second-Order Condition and Globalization.

Since
\psi\in C^{2}(U^{\prime})
, we can differentiate (
14
) everywhere on
U^{\prime}
to obtain the Jacobian matrix relation:
\nabla^{2}\psi(\bm{x})=-\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))-\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))A
. Concurrently, since
f_{\bm{x}}\in C^{2}(U^{\prime})
attains a local minimum at
\bm{x}
, its second-order necessary condition requires the pointwise Hessian to be PSD:
\nabla^{2}f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x})=\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))+\nabla^{2}\psi(\bm{x})\succcurlyeq 0
. Substituting the former into the latter cancels
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}c
, yielding
-\nabla^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{y}}c(\bm{x},T(\bm{x}))A\succcurlyeq 0
. Using
c(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\tilde{c}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})
, this becomes
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{x}-T(\bm{x}))A\succcurlyeq 0
. The symmetry of
\nabla^{2}\psi(\bm{x})
further implies that
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{x}-T(\bm{x}))
commutes with
A
.

To globalize this, let
\chi(\bm{z})
be the indicator function of the set of points
\bm{z}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
where
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})
either does not exist, does not commute with
A
, or
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})A
is not PSD. From the local conditions, for every
\bm{b}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, we have
\chi((\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b})=0
for
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in U
. Thus,
\int_{U}\chi((\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}=0
.

Integrating this over all
\bm{b}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
and applying Tonelli’s theorem to swap the order of integration, we obtain:

0=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}}\left(\int_{U}\chi((\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{b}=\int_{U}\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}}\chi((\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{b}\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}.

For any fixed
\bm{x}\in U
, we perform the change of variables
\bm{z}=(\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b}
. The Jacobian determinant of the map
\bm{b}\mapsto\bm{z}
is
(-1)^{n}
, so
\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{b}=\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{z}
. The inner integral becomes
\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}}\chi(\bm{z})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{z}
, which is entirely independent of
\bm{x}
. Substituting this back yields:

0=\int_{U}\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}}\chi(\bm{z})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{z}\right)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{x}=\mathcal{L}^{n}(U)\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}}\chi(\bm{z})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{z}.

Since
U
is a non-empty open set,
\mathcal{L}^{n}(U)>0
. Therefore,
\int_{{\mathds{R}^{n}}}\chi(\bm{z})\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\bm{z}=0
, meaning the commutativity and PSD properties hold for
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e.
\bm{z}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. Because
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}
is continuous on
{\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus\mathscr{S}
, these properties must hold everywhere on the open set
{\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus\mathscr{S}
.

Step 3: Distributional Separability and Global Convexity.

Since
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})
commutes with
A
on
{\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus\mathscr{S}
, and
A=QDQ^{\mathrm{T}}
has distinct eigenvalues,
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})
is simultaneously diagonalized by
Q
. Let
\bm{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
and define
\hat{c}(\bm{t})\coloneqq\tilde{c}(Q\bm{t})
. By the chain rule,
\nabla^{2}\hat{c}(\bm{t})=Q^{\mathrm{T}}\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(Q\bm{t})Q
is a diagonal matrix for all
\bm{t}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}
.

This implies that all mixed classical second derivatives vanish:
\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}^{2}_{t_{i}t_{j}}\hat{c}(\bm{t})=0
for all
i\neq j
on
{\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}
. Note that any distribution supported on a finite set
Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}
must be a finite linear combination of Dirac
\delta
distributions and their derivatives. However, the mixed partial derivative
\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}^{2}_{t_{i}t_{j}}
of a continuous function cannot contain such singular distributions. Indeed, if
\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}^{2}_{t_{i}t_{j}}\hat{c}
were to contain a Dirac
\delta
mass
\delta_{\bm{t}_{0}}
(or its derivatives) at some point
\bm{t}_{0}\in Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}
, integrating this relation would directly contradict the continuity of
\hat{c}
around
\bm{t}_{0}
. It follows that the distributional mixed derivatives
\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}^{2}_{t_{i}t_{j}}\hat{c}
must vanish globally on
{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. By Schwartz’s theorem on distributions,
\hat{c}
is globally separable:
\hat{c}(\bm{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(t_{i})
. The PSD condition
\nabla^{2}\hat{c}(\bm{t})D\succcurlyeq 0
outside a finite set ensures
c_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})\geqslant 0
, proving global convexity.

For
n\geqslant 2
, we can further show that
c_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Fix an arbitrary
t_{i}\in\mathds{R}
. Since
\mathscr{S}
is a finite set,
Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}\subset{\mathds{R}^{n}}
is also finite. Thus, the slice

\Xi_{t_{i}}\coloneqq\left\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{i-1},t_{i+1},\dots,t_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathds{R}^{n-1}:(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\in Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}\right\}

is finite. Since
n\geqslant 2
, the space
\mathds{R}^{n-1}
is uncountable. We can therefore choose a point
(t_{1}^{*},\dots,t_{i-1}^{*},t_{i+1}^{*},\dots,t_{n}^{*})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathds{R}^{n-1}\setminus\Xi_{t_{i}}
. By construction, the point
\bm{t}^{*}\coloneqq(t_{1}^{*},\dots,t_{i-1}^{*},t_{i},t_{i+1}^{*},\dots,t_{n}^{*})^{\mathrm{T}}
does not belong to
Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}
. Since
{\mathds{R}^{n}}\setminus Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}
is open, there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
\bm{t}^{*}
such that
V\cap Q^{\mathrm{T}}\mathscr{S}=\emptyset
. On this neighborhood
V
, the function
\hat{c}
is of class
C^{2}
. Since
\hat{c}(\bm{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}(t_{k})
, we have

c_{i}(x)=\hat{c}(t_{1}^{*},\dots,t_{i-1}^{*},x,t_{i+1}^{*},\dots,t_{n}^{*})-\sum_{\ell\neq i}c_{\ell}(t_{\ell}^{*})

for all
x
in a neighborhood of
t_{i}
. Since the first term on the right-hand side is
C^{2}
with respect to
x
in a neighborhood of
t_{i}
, and the second term is a constant, it follows that
c_{i}
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of
t_{i}
. Since
t_{i}\in\mathds{R}
was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that
c_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
when
n\geqslant 2
.

Step 4: Sufficiency and Uniqueness of the Optimal Map.

Assume
\tilde{c}(\bm{z})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}((Q^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{z})_{i})
for all
\bm{z}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, where each
c_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
is convex. Let
A=QDQ^{\mathrm{T}}
be any SPD matrix and
\bm{b}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. We construct the Kantorovich potential
\psi
by defining its gradient:
\nabla\psi(\bm{x})\coloneqq-\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},T_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{x}))=-\nabla\tilde{c}((\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b})
. The Jacobian of this vector field is
-\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}((\mathrm{I}-A)\bm{x}-\bm{b})(\mathrm{I}-A)
. Since
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}
and
\mathrm{I}-A
are both diagonalized by
Q
, they commute, making the Jacobian symmetric. By Poincaré’s lemma, a global potential
\psi\in C^{2}({\mathds{R}^{n}})
exists.

To prove that
T_{A,\bm{b}}
is an optimal transport map, it suffices to show that for any fixed
\bm{x}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, the objective function
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
— redefined here as
c(\bm{x}^{\prime},T_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{x}))+\psi(\bm{x}^{\prime})
— attains its global minimum at
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{x}
. Without loss of generality, we can rotate the coordinate system by
Q^{\mathrm{T}}
so that
A=\diag(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is diagonal with
a_{i}>0
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
\tilde{c}(\bm{z})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(z_{i})
. In this rotated coordinate system, the potential
\psi(\bm{x}^{\prime})
decomposes as
\psi(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime})
, where each
\psi_{i}
satisfies
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})=-c_{i}^{\prime}((1-a_{i})x_{i}^{\prime}-b_{i})
. Consequently,
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime})
, where

f_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime})\coloneqq c_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i}x_{i}-b_{i})+\psi_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime}).

For each fixed
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, differentiating
f_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime})
with respect to
x_{i}^{\prime}
yields

f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})=c_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i}x_{i}-b_{i})-c_{i}^{\prime}((1-a_{i})x_{i}^{\prime}-b_{i}).

Let
q_{1}\coloneqq x_{i}^{\prime}-a_{i}x_{i}-b_{i}
and
q_{2}\coloneqq(1-a_{i})x_{i}^{\prime}-b_{i}
. Their difference is given by
q_{1}-q_{2}=a_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{i})
. Since
c_{i}
is convex,
c_{i}^{\prime}\in C^{1}(\mathds{R})
is non-decreasing. We analyze the sign of
f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})
in two cases:

•

If
x_{i}^{\prime}>x_{i}
, then
q_{1}>q_{2}
, which implies
c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{1})\geqslant c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{2})
, and thus
f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})\geqslant 0
.

•

If
x_{i}^{\prime}<x_{i}
, then
q_{1}<q_{2}
, which implies
c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{1})\leqslant c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{2})
, and thus
f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})\leqslant 0
.

This shows that
x_{i}
is a global minimum of
f_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime})
. Since this holds for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the point
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{x}
is a global minimum of
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
, which proves that
T_{A,\bm{b}}
is an optimal transport map.

Now, assume in addition that each
c_{i}
is strictly convex. Then
c_{i}^{\prime}
is strictly increasing. In this case, the inequalities above become strict:

•

If
x_{i}^{\prime}>x_{i}
, then
q_{1}>q_{2}
, which implies
c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{1})>c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{2})
, and thus
f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})>0
.

•

If
x_{i}^{\prime}<x_{i}
, then
q_{1}<q_{2}
, which implies
c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{1})<c_{i}^{\prime}(q_{2})
, and thus
f_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})<0
.

This establishes that
x_{i}
is the unique global minimum of
f_{i}(x_{i}^{\prime})
, and consequently,
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\bm{x}
is the unique global minimum of
f_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
. This implies that the
c
-subdifferential of
\psi
at
T_{A,\bm{b}}(\bm{x})
is the singleton
\{\bm{x}\}
for
{\bm{\mu}}
-a.e.
\bm{x}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. By
[
34
, Theorem 5.10]
, the optimal transport map is unique.

Step 5: Cross-Coupling Implies Quadratic Costs.

Suppose
\mathscr{P}
contains another matrix
\bar{A}
such that
\{\bar{A}\}\times{\mathds{R}^{n}}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
. By the exact same globalization argument in Step 2, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})
must commute with
\bar{A}
for
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e.
\bm{z}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
. Let
\bm{t}=Q^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{z}
and
\hat{c}(\bm{t})\coloneqq\tilde{c}(Q\bm{t})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}(t_{k})
. The Hessian in the
Q
-basis is the diagonal matrix
\nabla^{2}\hat{c}(\bm{t})=\diag(c_{1}^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}),\dots,c_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t_{n}))
.

The commutativity condition
\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})\bar{A}=\bar{A}\nabla^{2}\tilde{c}(\bm{z})
transforms into
\nabla^{2}\hat{c}(\bm{t})\bar{A}_{Q}=\bar{A}_{Q}\nabla^{2}\hat{c}(\bm{t})
for a.e.
\bm{t}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, where
\bar{A}_{Q}\coloneqq Q^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{A}Q
. Comparing the
(i,j)
-th entry of both sides yields

c_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})(\bar{A}_{Q})_{ij}=(\bar{A}_{Q})_{ij}c_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t_{j}),\quad\mathcal{L}^{n}\text{-a.e. }\bm{t}\in{\mathds{R}^{n}},

where
(\,\cdot\,)_{ij}
denotes the entry in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of a matrix. If
(\bar{A}_{Q})_{ij}\neq 0
for some
i\neq j
, we can divide by it to obtain
c_{i}^{\prime\prime}(t_{i})=c_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t_{j})
for a.e.
t_{i},t_{j}\in\mathds{R}
. Since the left-hand side depends only on
t_{i}
and the right-hand side only on
t_{j}
, both must equal a common constant
\kappa
. Because
n\geqslant 2
(since
i\neq j
), we know from Step 3 that
c_{i},c_{j}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
, so this equality holds everywhere. The convexity of
c_{i}
ensures
\kappa\geqslant 0
. Consequently,
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
are quadratic functions sharing the same second derivative
\kappa
. Note that any linear or constant terms in
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
do not affect the optimality of the transport map, as they merely add a constant to the total transport cost. Therefore, no further relationship between the lower-order terms of
c_{i}
and
c_{j}
can be deduced.

Remark 24
(Regularity Improvement for
n\geqslant 2
).

When
n\geqslant 2
, the conclusion that
c_{i}\in C^{2}(\mathds{R})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
implies that the cost function
\tilde{c}
is actually twice continuously differentiable on the entire space
{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, meaning the potential singular set
\mathscr{S}
must be empty. This reveals that the existence of affine optimal transport maps for a full-space set of translations is so restrictive that it smooths out any isolated singularities of the cost function.

Remark 25
(Weaker Conditions on the Translation Set).

The assumption that
B_{A}={\mathds{R}^{n}}
can be relaxed to
B_{A}
being merely dense in
{\mathds{R}^{n}}
, provided we additionally assume that

is not an eigenvalue of

for

. Under this condition, the matrix

is invertible, which maps the full-measure set

to a full-measure set

Remark 26
(Rigidity to the Squared Euclidean Distance).

Building upon
theorem

23

6
Domain Adaptation via Optimal Transport between Mixtures

We will present a domain adaptation framework based on the theory discussed in the previous sections and compare it with similar algorithms to demonstrate its effectiveness.

6.1
Fitting mixture models

In this work, we assume that the data can be effectively modeled using mixture models. For the sake of simplicity, we select the location-scale family
DLS({\bm{\mu}}_{g_{n}})
, which is actually the diagonal Gaussian mixture model. Other mixture models that satisfy
1

Let

where
P\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lambda_{j}P_{j},P_{j}\coloneqq p_{g_{\bm{m}_{j},\Sigma_{j}}}

[
7

. In our method, we model the unlabeled domain using a single Gaussian mixture, whereas for the labeled domain, we fit a separate Gaussian mixture for each label and then integrate them. For completeness, we adopt the pseudocode from

6.2
Optimal transport between Gaussian mixtures

Referring to the ideas in
section

2.3
, we first construct the optimal transport problem from the source domain
\mathcal{P}^{S}
to the target domain
\mathcal{P}^{T}
. Based on the fitting in the previous section, we may assume that
\mathcal{P}^{S}=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\lambda_{0,j_{0}}{\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{T}=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}\lambda_{1,j_{1}}{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}}
with
{\bm{\lambda}}_{i}\coloneqq(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{J_{0}})\in\Delta^{J_{0}}
,
{\bm{\mu}}_{i,j_{i}}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}_{i,j_{i}},\Sigma_{i,j_{i}})
for all
i\in\{0,1\},j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\}
.
According to
theorem

14
, we only need to solve a discrete optimal transport problem, whose cost matrix can be determined by
theorem

30
. For any
{\bm{\eta}}\coloneqq(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n})\in\mathds{R}^{n}_{>0}
,
{\bm{\lambda}}\coloneqq(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\in\Delta^{n}_{+}
, and
\varphi\in\MI\cap C^{2}(\mathds{R}_{\geqslant 0})
, we define the cost matrix

C^{\varphi,{\bm{\eta}},{\bm{\lambda}}}\coloneqq\left[C^{\varphi,{\bm{\eta}},{\bm{\lambda}}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\right]_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{0,1\}\\
j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\}\end{subarray}}\coloneqq\left[\varphi(\Wp_{\varphi}^{{\bm{\lambda}},{\bm{\eta}}}({\bm{\mu}}_{0,j_{0}},{\bm{\mu}}_{1,j_{1}}))\right]_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{0,1\}\\
j_{i}\in\{1,\dots,J_{i}\}\end{subarray}}.

The corresponding problem is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\ast}\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{w}\coloneqq(w_{j_{0},j_{1}})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j_{0}\leqslant J_{0}\\
1\leqslant j_{1}\leqslant J_{1}\end{subarray}}\in\Pi({\bm{\lambda}}_{0},{\bm{\lambda}}_{1})}\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}C^{\varphi,{\bm{\eta}},{\bm{\lambda}}}_{j_{0},j_{1}}w_{j_{0},j_{1}}.

Let the total number of data points in the source and target domains be
N
, and define
J\coloneqq\max\{J_{0},J_{1}\}
. Compared to the optimal transport problem involving all data points, it is worth mentioning that the reduction in time complexity from
O(N^{3}\log N)
to
O(N\cdot J^{3}\log J)
and in space complexity from
O(N^{2})
to
O(N\cdot J^{2})
(where typically
J\ll N
) represents a transition from a computationally intensive task to a real-time operation. This advancement enables Wasserstein-based clustering and distribution alignment in large-scale machine learning systems.

For simplicity, in the subsequent numerical experiments, let
\varphi=\varphi_{p}
with
p\geqslant 1
,
and let
\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{n},\eta_{i}=1
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

6.3
Estimation methods

Based on the optimal matching solution
\bm{w}^{\ast}
obtained in the previous section, we aim to establish a classifier
h
in the target domain or an effective mapping
T:{\mathds{R}^{n}}\to{\mathds{R}^{n}}
that aligns
\mathcal{P}^{S}
with
\mathcal{P}^{T}
. The literature posits four primary methodologies for this estimation, as outlined below.

6.3.1
Maximum a posteriori classifier
h_{MAP}

In the GMM-based Optimal Transport (GMM-OT) framework, the matching matrix
\bm{w}^{\ast}\in\mathds{R}^{L\times L}
is interpreted as a joint probability distribution over the latent component spaces of the source and target domains. Specifically, for the
j_{0}
-th source component and the
j_{1}
-th target component, the entry
w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}}
represents the joint probability
\Prob(J^{S}=j_{0},J^{T}=j_{1})
, where
J^{S}
and
J^{T}
are categorical random variables that mapping each data point to its latent generating component in the source and target GMMs, respectively.

Let
Y^{S}
and
Y^{T}
be the random variables that assign each data point to its label in the source and target domains, respectively, and let
X^{T}\sim\mathcal{P}^{T}
. Similar to the covariate shift hypothesis
[
31
]
, we assume that

\displaystyle\Prob(Y^{T}|J^{S}=j_{0},J^{T}=j_{1})=\Prob(Y^{S}|J^{S}=j_{0})

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Prob(Y^{T}|J^{T}=j_{1})=\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}\Prob(Y^{S}|J^{S}=j_{0})\Prob(J^{S}=j_{0}|J^{T}=j_{1}),

for all
j_{1}\in\{1,\dots,J_{1}\}
.
Then, using label propagation
[
30
]
, the estimated label of the
j_{1}
-th target component can be defined as follows:

\hat{\bm{y}}^{T}_{j_{1}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1,j_{1}}}\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J_{0}}w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}}\bm{y}^{S}_{j_{0}},

where
\bm{y}^{S}_{j_{0}}
is the label of the
j_{0}
-th source component. Next, following
[
23
]
, we can use maximum a posteriori estimation to establish a classifier in the target domain:

\displaystyle h_{MAP}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\argmax_{y\in\{1,\dots,L\}}\Prob(Y^{T}=y|X^{T}=\bm{x}^{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{y\in\{1,\dots,L\}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}\Prob(Y^{T}=y|X^{T}=\bm{x}^{T},J^{T}=j_{1})\Prob(J^{T}=j_{1}|X^{T}=\bm{x}^{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\argmax_{y\in\{1,\dots,L\}}\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}\frac{\lambda_{1,j_{1}}g_{\bm{m}_{1,j_{1}},\Sigma_{1,j_{1}}}(\bm{x}^{T})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}_{1}=1}^{J_{1}}\lambda_{1,j^{\prime}_{1}}g_{\bm{m}_{1,j^{\prime}_{1}},\Sigma_{1,j^{\prime}_{1}}}(\bm{x}^{T})}\hat{\bm{y}}^{T}_{j_{1}},

where the last equality holds because classes and components are structurally congruent within the data points under the usual settings.

6.3.2
Conditional mean mapping
T_{mean}

The mean mapping strategy, as discussed by
[
6
, Section 6.3]
, is derived from the barycentric projection. For a given source sample
\bm{x}^{S}
, the transported point is defined as the conditional expectation under the optimal transport plan
{\bm{\gamma}}^{\ast}
:

T_{mean}(\bm{x}^{S})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}^{T}\sim{\bm{\gamma}}^{\ast}(\,\cdot\,|\bm{x}^{S})}[x^{T}].

While
T_{mean}
provides a deterministic and continuous transformation, it frequently fails to maintain the global structural properties of the target distribution. Specifically, the mapping may lead to a "shrinkage" effect where the transported points converge toward the global mean, thus failing to accurately populate the distinct modes of
\mathcal{P}^{T}

[
6
]
.

6.3.3
Stochastic mapping
T_{rand}

To circumvent the limitations of mean-based methods,
T_{rand}
introduces a probabilistic assignment mechanism
[
6
, Section 6.3]
. A source point
\bm{x}^{S}
is mapped to the
j_{1}
-th target component via the affine map
T_{j_{0},j_{1}}
with a probability
P_{j_{0},j_{1}}(\bm{x}^{S})
defined as

P_{j_{0},j_{1}}(\bm{x}^{S})\coloneqq\frac{w^{\ast}_{j_{0},j_{1}}g_{\bm{m}_{0,j_{0}},\Sigma_{0,j_{0}}}(\bm{x}^{S})}{\sum_{j_{0}^{\prime}=1}^{J_{0}}\lambda_{0,j^{\prime}_{0}}g_{\bm{m}_{0,j_{0}^{\prime}},\Sigma_{0,j_{0}^{\prime}}}(\bm{x}^{S})}.

Theoretically,
T_{rand}
ensures that the push-forward distribution
{T_{rand}}_{\#}\mathcal{P}^{S}
exactly recovers
\mathcal{P}^{T}
. However, from a practical optimization perspective, the stochastic nature of the index sampling
(j_{0},j_{1})
introduces significant variance and irregularity in the mapping, which may degrade the stability of subsequent classification models.

6.3.4
Importance weighted mapping
T_{weight}

The
T_{weight}
strategy, proposed by
[
23
, Section 4.2]
, regularizes the transport process by combining deterministic component identification with weighted mass distribution. The procedure is formalized as follows:

1.

Maximum a posterior selection: the label of the source component which most likely to have generated the observation
\bm{x}^{S}
is determined by

\hat{j}_{0}\coloneqq\argmax_{j_{0}\in\{1,\dots,J_{0}\}}\frac{\lambda_{0,j_{0}}g_{\bm{m}_{0,j_{0}},\Sigma_{0,j_{0}}}(\bm{x}^{S})}{\sum_{j_{0}^{\prime}=1}^{J_{0}}\lambda_{0,j^{\prime}_{0}}g_{\bm{m}_{0,j_{0}^{\prime}},\Sigma_{0,j_{0}^{\prime}}}(\bm{x}^{S})}.

2.

Multicomponent Transport: the point is mapped to all the
j_{1}
-th target components satisfying
w^{\ast}_{\hat{j}_{0},j_{1}}\geqslant\tau
, where
\tau
is a sparsity threshold.

3.

Weight assignment: each generated target point
T_{\hat{j}_{0},j_{1}}(\bm{x}^{S})
is assigned an importance weight
w^{\ast}_{\hat{j}_{0},j_{1}}
.

T_{weight}
yields a piece-wise affine transformation that inherits the geometric properties of the GMM components. By enforcing a form of group-sparsity (where samples from the same source cluster are transported collectively), this method preserves class-discriminative features.

Conclusion

In this paper, we establish a general mathematical framework for optimal transport (OT) between identifiable finite location-scale mixture models. Our theoretical contributions are threefold. First, by defining a specific function class grounded in generalized Minkowski-type inequalities, we extend traditional Wasserstein-type metrics and barycenters to these mixture models. Second, we provide a characterization of translation-invariant cost functions that admit affine OT maps. Specifically, we prove that the optimality of symmetric positive definite affine maps requires the cost function to be separable, and that the presence of any cross-coupling further reduces the cost to a quadratic form. Third, we prove that restricting joint couplings to specific mixture structures reduces the continuous multimarginal OT problem to a tractable discrete formulation over mixture components, accompanied by bounding inequalities against their original continuous counterparts.

From a computational perspective, these theoretical foundations translate into scalable algorithms for machine learning applications. By shifting the transport paradigm from pointwise empirical matching to component-level matching, we propose an efficient domain adaptation framework instantiated via Gaussian mixture models. This approach decouples the core OT computation from individual data points, reducing the computational complexity to scale linearly with the sample size. Empirical evaluations on the VisDA-C benchmark demonstrate that our method achieves significant reductions in both computational overhead and memory footprint, while maintaining competitive classification accuracy compared to existing empirical OT approaches.

Looking forward, this generalized framework opens several avenues for future research. First, while our current theoretical analysis relies on the identifiability of mixture models, extending these generalized metrics to non-identifiable or more complex distributions remains an open mathematical challenge. Second, although our algorithmic design and empirical evaluations focus on the foundational two-marginal domain adaptation setting, a natural progression is to develop and evaluate practical algorithms for multi-source domain adaptation, thereby leveraging the multimarginal barycenter theory established herein. Finally, integrating this efficient transport mechanism into the training objectives of deep generative models may offer a principled approach to refining latent space alignment in large-scale representation learning.
