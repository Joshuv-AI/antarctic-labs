Distributionally-Robust Learning to Optimize
Vinit Ranjan, Jisun Park, Bartolomeo Stellato

Abstract
We propose a distributionally robust approach to learning hyperparameters for first-order methods in convex optimization. Given a dataset of problem instances, we minimize a Wasserstein distributionally robust version of the performance estimation problem (PEP) over algorithm parameters such as step sizes. Our framework unifies two extremes: as the robustness radius vanishes, we recover classical learning to optimize (L2O); as it grows, we recover worst-case optimal algorithm design via PEP. We solve the resulting problem with stochastic gradient descent, differentiating through the solution of an inner semidefinite program at each step. We prove high-probability bounds showing that the true risk of the learned algorithm is at most the in-sample L2O optimum plus a slack that shrinks with the sample size, and is no worse than the worst-case PEP bound. On unconstrained quadratic minimization, LASSO, and linear programming benchmarks, our learned algorithms achieve strong out-of-sample performance with certifiable robustness, outperforming both worst-case optimal and vanilla L2O baselines.

Introduction

Applications in machine learning, signal processing, and optimal control increasingly rely on solving large-scale optimization problems using first-order methods for their low computational cost per iteration and modest memory requirements
[
9
,
68
]
.
Building on classical gradient descent
[
27
]
, modern variants of first-order methods apply proximal operators
[
60
]
and operator splitting techniques
[
13
,
15
]
to solve nonsmooth and constrained problems efficiently.
This has led to robust open-source solvers such as PDLP
[
6
,
7
]
for linear programs (LPs), OSQP
[
75
]
for quadratic programs (QPs), and SCS
[
58
,
59
]
, COSMO
[
28
]
for semidefinite programs (SDPs) to name a few.
Yet a fundamental challenge persists: practical performance depends critically on hyperparameters like step sizes, and tuning them well remains largely a manual effort prone to trial and error.

Classical analysis derives worst-case convergence bounds over a given function class.
The performance estimation problem (PEP)
[
24
,
77
]
automates this approach by solving semidefinite programs to obtain tight worst-case guarantees.
These bounds yield worst-case optimal constant step sizes
[
9
]
, while recent work develops time-varying step sizes with improved worst-case rates
[
3
]
.
However, worst-case analysis is inherently pessimistic: it guards against pathological instances that rarely arise in practice, yielding conservative algorithms that underperform on typical problems.

Machine learning offers an alternative by exploiting structure in a dataset of problem instances.
Learning to optimize (L2O)
[
5
,
45
,
18
]
, also known as amortized optimization
[
4
]
, unrolls algorithm iterations and tunes hyperparameters to minimize average loss on training data, often achieving substantial practical speedups over hand-tuned alternatives.
However, L2O provides no theoretical guarantees on out-of-sample performance: learned algorithms may fail on problems outside the training distribution
[
18
]
.
Recent efforts enforce guarantees via nonlinear systems theory
[
55
,
56
,
54
]
or PAC-Bayes bounds
[
25
,
71
,
76
]
.
The former reintroduces worst-case conservatism through dynamical systems analysis;
the latter yields bounds whose tightness depends sensitively on the prior and posterior used during training.

In this work, we propose
distributionally-robust learning to optimize
(DR-L2O), a framework that combines computer-assisted worst-case analysis with data-driven L2O.
Given a dataset of problem instances, we minimize the worst-case expected loss over algorithm parameters such as step sizes, where the worst case is taken over a Wasserstein ambiguity set centered at the empirical distribution.
This builds on the recent distributionally robust PEP of
[
61
]
, which evaluates this risk for a fixed algorithm; we instead
minimize
it, turning a performance certificate into a learning objective.
A single Wasserstein radius controls the trade-off between data-driven and worst-case design: as the radius vanishes we recover L2O; as it grows we recover worst-case optimal design via PEP.
We solve the resulting problem with stochastic gradient descent, where each iteration solves an inner SDP and differentiates through its solution via implicit differentiation of the KKT conditions.

1
illustrates this trade-off.
L2O minimizes the empirical loss but degrades on out-of-sample instances, the worst-case design is conservative on both, and DR-L2O sits between the two in-sample yet dominates both out-of-sample.
Our contributions are as follows:

1.

DR-L2O framework.

We formulate the distributionally-robust learning to optimize problem (

We prove that varying the radius continuously interpolates between (

2.

Scalable solution method.

We solve (

3.

Out-of-sample guarantees.

We prove that, for an appropriately chosen radius, the distributionally robust risk upper-bounds the true risk uniformly over algorithm parameters with high probability (

Combined with the interpolation result, the true risk of the learned algorithm is at most the in-sample L2O optimum plus a slack proportional to the radius, and at most the worst-case PEP bound (

4.

Numerical experiments.

On unconstrained quadratic minimization, LASSO, and linear programs, we show that DR-L2O learns algorithms with strong out-of-sample performance and certifiable robustness, outperforming both worst-case optimal and vanilla L2O approaches.

Related works

Worst-case analysis and its limitations

The performance estimation problem (PEP)
[
24
,
77
]
and control-theoretic analysis
[
44
]
automate worst-case convergence analysis for first-order methods in convex optimization, while verification frameworks
[
65
,
64
]
extend these ideas to parametric quadratic and linear optimization.
These approaches have enabled the discovery of worst-case optimal algorithms including the optimized gradient method (OGM)
[
38
]
, OptISTA
[
36
]
, the accelerated proximal point method (APPM)
[
39
]
, and accelerated methods with silver step sizes
[
21
,
3
]
.
However, worst-case bounds are often far from typical performance.
Average-case analysis
[
73
,
80
]
offers tighter characterizations when problem distributions are known, while data-driven evaluation
[
72
,
35
,
61
,
37
]
provides performance bounds on observed instances.
Yet these approaches do not directly address how to
learn
hyperparameters that balance robustness with empirical performance.

Learning to optimize (L2O)

L2O
[
5
,
45
,
18
]
, also termed amortized optimization
[
4
]
, learns algorithm hyperparameters from a dataset of problem instances.
This has achieved practical success, from learned proximal methods that solve LASSO in only a few iterations
[
48
]
to scalable approaches for neural network training
[
20
]
.
However, L2O is essentially empirical risk minimization, offering no guarantees on out-of-sample performance.
Two strategies have emerged to address this gap.
The first imposes structural constraints that ensure convergence:
[
19
]
identified algorithm structures that are both convergent and trainable,
[
63
]
and
[
33
]
developed safeguarded formulations guaranteeing convergence for every instance, and
[
47
,
55
,
56
]
derived necessary conditions for convergent learned algorithms. While effective, these structural approaches limit learning capacity.
The second strategy incorporates distribution-aware objectives:
[
81
]
proposed meta-learning for better generalization,
[
74
]
developed out-of-distribution-robust training via data alignment,
[
71
,
76
]
employed PAC-Bayes bounds, and
[
70
]
used worst-case convergence rates as a regularizer in the learning objective.
Both strategies have limits: structural constraints reduce learning capacity, while distribution-aware objectives either reintroduce worst-case conservatism or rely sensitively on PAC-Bayes priors and posteriors.

Distributionally robust optimization (DRO)

DRO
[
42
]
handles uncertainty by optimizing over ambiguity sets, which are collections of distributions consistent with partial information.
When only data samples are available, data-driven DRO
[
79
,
42
]
constructs ambiguity sets as metric balls centered at the empirical distribution, using Wasserstein distance
[
57
,
41
]
or divergences such as Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
[
34
]
.
This framework naturally quantifies distributional uncertainty from finite samples, providing the mechanism we exploit to interpolate between L2O and worst-case PEP.

Problem setup

We consider a
problem instance

z=(f,x^{0})
consisting of a convex optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{x}{\textnormal{minimize}}&f(x),\end{array}

where
f\colon{\mathbf{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbf{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
is convex, proper, and lower semi-continuous, and
x^{0}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
is an initial point.
We assume that the problem has a solution
x^{\star}
with optimal value
f^{\star}=f(x^{\star})
.

An
algorithm

\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
maps a problem instance to a sequence of approximate solutions
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)=\{x^{k}\}_{k=0,1,\ldots}
, where
\theta\in\Theta
represents the algorithm parameters such as step sizes.
We restrict attention to first-order algorithms, which use only subgradient information of
f
.

Assumption 1

(First-order algorithms)
.

For any
z=(f,x^{0})
, the sequence
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0,1,\dots}=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)
satisfies the span condition

x^{k}\in x^{0}+\mathop{\bf span}\{g^{0},\dots,g^{k-1},g^{k}\},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,

where
g^{i}\in\partial f(x^{i})
for
i=0,\dots,k
.
Note
x^{k}
is
\partial f(x^{k})
-dependent when the algorithm involves proximal step
x^{k}={\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\eta f}(x^{k-1})
,
{\it i.e.}
,
g^{k}=x^{k-1}-x^{k}\in\partial f(x^{k})
.

We measure algorithm performance on instance
z
by a
loss function

\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}
.
Given an iteration budget
K
, common choices of
\ell
are the function-value gap
f(x^{K})-f^{\star}
and the distance to optimality
\|x^{K}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
, evaluated at the
K
-th iterate
x^{K}
.

Let
{\mathbf{P}}
be a probability distribution over the set
\mathcal{Z}
of problem instances.
Our goal is to
find the optimal algorithm parameter
\theta^{\star}
minimizing the
risk
, defined as the expected loss under
{\mathbf{P}}
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\textnormal{minimize}}&\mathcal{R}(\theta,{\mathbf{P}}):=\underset{z\sim{\mathbf{P}}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\big(\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\big).\end{array}

(1)

3
Performance guarantees

We now describe two approaches to measuring algorithm performance that serve as building blocks for our framework: worst-case guarantees via PEP
[
24
,
77
]
and data-driven probabilistic guarantees via Wasserstein DRO
[
61
]
.

3.1
Worst-case guarantee

The worst-case loss
\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))
upper-bounds the risk
\mathcal{R}(\theta,{\mathbf{P}})
for any distribution
{\mathbf{P}}
supported on
\mathcal{Z}
.
It is evaluated as the optimal value of the following performance estimation problem (PEP):

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\\
\textnormal{subject to}&z=(f,x^{0})\in\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{X},\end{array}

(PEP)

where
\mathcal{F}
is the function class and
\mathcal{X}
is the set of initial iterates.
When the function class
\mathcal{F}
admits interpolation conditions,
e.g.
, smooth convex or convex Lipschitz functions, (
PEP
) admits a tractable formulation as a convex conic program
[
24
,
77
,
67
]
.

Following
[
24
,
77
]
, (
PEP
) admits an equivalent SDP via a lifted representation.
Given the iterates
\{x^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(f,x^{0})
, we define the Gram matrix
G=P^{T}P\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}
with

P=\begin{bmatrix}x^{0}-x^{\star}&g^{0}&\cdots&g^{K}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d\times(K+2)}\text{ where }g^{k}\in\partial f(x^{k}),

and the function-value vector
F=(f(x^{0})-f^{\star},\dots,f(x^{K})-f^{\star})\in{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
.
Each component of (
PEP
) is linear in
(G,F)
:
the loss is
\ell(G,F)=\mathop{\bf tr}(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{T}G)+b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{T}F
,
the initial condition is
\mathop{\bf tr}(A_{0}^{T}G)+b_{0}^{T}F+c_{0}\leq 0
,
and the interpolation constraints of
\mathcal{F}
are
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(G,F)\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{M}
with

\big(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(G,F)\big)_{m}=-\mathop{\bf tr}\big(A_{m}(\theta)^{T}G\big)-b_{m}(\theta)^{T}F,\qquad m=1,\dots,M,

(2)

where the LMI coefficients
A_{m}(\theta)\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K+2}
and
b_{m}(\theta)\in{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}
depend on the algorithm parameters
\theta

[
37
]
.
We denote by
\Xi^{\theta}
the feasible set of
(G,F)
pairs:

\Xi^{\theta}=\left\{(G,F)\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}\left|\;\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(G,F)\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{M},\;\mathop{\bf tr}(A_{0}^{T}G)+b_{0}^{T}F+c_{0}\leq 0\right.\right\}.

The explicit SDP and its Lagrangian dual are given in
Section

A.1
.

3.2
Data-driven probabilistic guarantee

Since the true distribution
{\mathbf{P}}
is unknown, we approximate the risk in (
1
) from a finite dataset.
Given i.i.d. samples
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{N}=\{\hat{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim{\mathbf{P}}^{N}
, the empirical distribution is
\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\hat{z}_{i}}
, where
\delta_{z}
is the Dirac measure at
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
The corresponding empirical risk is

\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=(1/N){\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\hat{z}_{i})\big).

With finite samples, however, the empirical risk may be a poor estimate of the true risk
\mathcal{R}(\theta,{\mathbf{P}})
.
Running
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
on each sample
\hat{z}_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
yields a lifted representation
(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\in\Xi^{\theta}
, inducing the lifted empirical distribution
\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta}=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})}
over
\Xi^{\theta}
.
To mitigate the bias of the empirical risk,
[
61
]
proposes a data-driven framework that yields a probabilistic performance guarantee robust to sampling variability.
The guarantees are derived from a Wasserstein distributionally robust variant of the performance estimation problem (
DRO-PEP
):

\!\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}):=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\mathcal{R}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})\\
\textnormal{subject to}&{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta}),\end{array}

(DRO-PEP)

where the ambiguity set
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta})
is the
1
-Wasserstein ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta}
:

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta})=\left\{{\mathbf{Q}}^{\theta}\,\left|\;\mathrm{supp}\,{\mathbf{Q}}^{\theta}\subseteq\Xi^{\theta},\;W_{1}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta},{\mathbf{Q}}^{\theta})\leq\varepsilon\right.\right\},

with
W_{1}
induced by the norm
\left\|(G,F)\right\|=\sqrt{\|{G}\|_{F}^{2}+\|{F}\|^{2}}
.
We refer to
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
as the
DRO risk
.

Tractable formulation

By
[
61
, Theorem 2]
, (
DRO-PEP
) has a tractable formulation as a convex conic program; see
Section

A.2
for details.
For
\theta\in\Theta
and
\varepsilon>0
, (
DRO-PEP
) also admits the following equivalent robust optimization formulation:

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{\bf tr}(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{T}G_{i})+b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{T}F_{i}\\
\textnormal{subject to}&(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{(G_{i},F_{i})-(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})}\|\leq\varepsilon\\
&(G_{i},F_{i})\in\Xi^{\theta},\hfill i=1,\dots,N.\end{array}

(DRO-PEP-P)

The equivalence comes from the strong duality between the dual of (
DRO-PEP
) and its bi-dual, which is exactly (
DRO-PEP-P
).
The proof of the strong duality result is deferred to
Section

A.3
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\theta\in\Theta
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Then (
DRO-PEP-P
) is a bi-dual of (
DRO-PEP
), and their optimal values coincide.
Furthermore, (
DRO-PEP-P
) has an optimal solution.

The key departure of this work from
[
61
]
is to minimize, rather than evaluate, the DRO risk over
\theta
, turning a performance certificate into a learning objective.

4
Distributionally-robust learning to optimize

We now minimize the DRO risk
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
over
\theta
to learn optimal algorithm hyperparameters.
Unlike
[
61
]
, which uses the DRO risk only to certify performance of a fixed
\theta
, we treat it as a learning objective.
When the learning objective is the worst-case loss, the corresponding learning problem is
[
37
]

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\textnormal{minimize}}&\underset{z\in\mathcal{Z}}{\sup}\;\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big).\end{array}

(OPT-PEP)

However, worst-case optimal algorithms can underperform on typical instances.
At the other extreme, learning to optimize (L2O) minimizes the empirical risk
[
18
]
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\textnormal{minimize}}&\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\hat{z}_{i})\big).\end{array}

(L2O)

When the loss
\ell
is differentiable, (
L2O
) is solved efficiently with stochastic first-order methods.
However, it provides no out-of-sample generalization guarantee: the learned algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\star}}
may fail on unseen instances.
To balance the two extremes, we minimize the DRO risk, yielding the learning problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\textnormal{minimize}}&\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}),\end{array}

(DR-L2O)

which we call
distributionally-robust learning to optimize
.

4.1
Solution method

Problem (
DR-L2O
) is non-convex in
\theta
, since the entries of
\widehat{G}_{i}
,
\widehat{F}_{i}
,
A_{m}(\theta)
, and
b_{m}(\theta)
are non-convex quadratic polynomials in
\theta
.
Still, the DRO risk
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is the optimal value of the convex conic program (
DRO-PEP-D
) parametrized by
\{(A_{m},b_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, whose solution map is differentiable using the approach of
[
2
]
.
We compute the gradient
d\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})/d\theta
via the chain rule and apply stochastic gradient descent to obtain
\theta^{\star}
.
See
Figure

2
for a summary of the solution method and
Appendix

C
for a detailed description of the gradient evaluation and solution algorithm; the latter also establishes that (
DR-L2O
) attains its minimum over
\Theta
(Remark
3
).

, we have interpolation LMI coefficients

through back-propagation using chain rule.

5
Theoretical guarantees of learned optimizer

In this section, we show that (
DR-L2O
) not only interpolates between (
OPT-PEP
) and (
L2O
), but also yields out-of-sample and out-of-distribution guarantees. Throughout this section, we assume that the parameter set
\Theta
is compact.
This holds in practice because of finite floating-point precision.

Assumption 2
.

The parameter set
\Theta
is compact, i.e., closed and bounded.

5.1
Robustness certificate

We first show that our learned algorithm enjoys a robust out-of-sample guarantee, a consequence of the data-driven Wasserstein DRO framework
[
57
]
.
Here, we extend the finite-sample guarantee of
[
61
, Section 3.2]
from a fixed
\theta\in\Theta
to a uniform statement over
\theta\in\Theta
.
The proof is provided in
Section

B.1
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
2
holds.
Let
N
and
K
be fixed.
For each
\beta\in(0,1)
,
there exists
\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta)>0
and
L>0
such that

{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left(\begin{array}[t]{l}\underset{z\sim{\mathbf{P}}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\big(\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\big)\end{array}\leq\begin{array}[t]{ll}\underset{{\mathbf{Q}}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta)}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta})}{\sup}&\underset{(G,F)\sim{\mathbf{Q}}^{\theta}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\big(\ell(G,F)\big),\end{array}\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta\right)\geq 1-\beta,

where
q
is the dimension of
(G,F)

and

\varepsilon(\beta)\lesssim\left(\frac{\log(1/\beta)+\mathrm{dim}(\Theta)\big(\log N+\log\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)\big)}{N}\right)^{1/q}+\frac{2L}{N}.

Remark 1

(Out-of-distribution (OOD) guarantee)
.

The DRO risk provides a performance guarantee that is also robust to the choice of true distribution, in the sense that the same bound holds for any
{\mathbf{Q}}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta^{\star}})
, not just the true distribution.

5.2
Interpolating behavior of (
DR-L2O
)

We now show how (
DR-L2O
) interpolates between (
L2O
) and the worst-case optimal design (
OPT-PEP
) as the radius
\varepsilon
varies.
All proofs are presented in
Section

B.2
.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose
2
holds,
\varepsilon>0
,
and let
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
be an optimal solution of (
DR-L2O
).
Then the mapping
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is monotonically nondecreasing.
Furthermore,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}),\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\underset{\theta\in\Theta}{\inf}\,\underset{z\in\mathcal{Z}}{\sup}\,\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big).

As
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, problem (
DR-L2O
) loses its distributional robustness and reduces to (
L2O
).
For large enough
\varepsilon
, the optimizer learned via (
DR-L2O
) is robust to essentially all distributions supported on
\Xi
, becoming worst-case optimal.

We now establish certified performance bounds for the true risk of our learned algorithm.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
2
holds.
Given
\varepsilon>0
,
let
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
be an optimal solution of (
DR-L2O
).
For any
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon=\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta)
as in
Theorem

1
, the true risk
\mathcal{R}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{P}})
of the optimizer learned via (
DR-L2O
) satisfies the following with probability at least
1-\beta
:

\mathcal{R}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{P}})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip}(\ell),\qquad\mathcal{R}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},{\mathbf{P}})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big),

where
\mathrm{Lip}(\ell)
is a Lipschitz constant of the loss function
\ell
.

The first bound certifies that the true risk of the optimizer learned via (
DR-L2O
) is within
\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip}(\ell)
of the in-sample optimum of (
L2O
), providing an out-of-sample guarantee that (
L2O
) alone lacks.
The second bound validates that the true risk is no worse than the worst-case optimal bound from (

6.3
Total variation (TV) inpainting

We consider the
\ell_{1}

[

.
For a grayscale image represented as an
m\times n

[0,255]

[0,1]

\mathcal{K}
of known pixel locations, the goal is to reconstruct the image
U

The corresponding linear program is

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{U}{\textnormal{minimize}}&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}U_{i+1,j}-U_{i,j}\\
U_{i,j+1}-U_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{1}\\

We solve this LP using primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG)
[

adjusted for linear programs (PDLP)
[

We first cast the problem in standard LP form by introducing slack variables for the

where

encodes the LP’s linear cost and box constraints,

is the indicator of the constraints; see
Section

D.3
for the explicit form and the corresponding PDHG iteration, whose step sizes
\theta=\{(\tau^{k},\rho^{k},\sigma^{k})\}_{k=0}^{K-1}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{3K}
we learn.
For the performance loss, we use the Lagrangian duality gap
L(x,u^{\star})-L(x^{\star},u)
; since this class falls outside the standard (
PEP
) interpolation conditions, we extend them to general linear operators in
Section

A.4
.
For in-distribution we use Olivetti faces
[
69
,
14
]
with 10% pixels blacked out; for out-of-distribution we use color images from Tiny ImageNet
[

,

]

).

7
Conclusion

We presented a distributionally-robust algorithm design framework for first-order methods.
By minimizing the distributionally robust risk, our learned algorithms enjoy out-of-sample and out-of-distribution guarantees that classical L2O approaches lack.
The framework extends naturally to risk-averse training with other risk measures, such as conditional value-at-risk
[
66
]
; the corresponding DRO-PEP formulation already appears in
[
61
, Corollary 2.1]
.
A major limitation is scalability: unrolling algorithms for more iterations requires solving a much larger conic program at each gradient step.
A natural remedy is to use GPU-accelerated first-order solvers
[
52
,
46
,
51
,
16
,
17
]
.

A.1
Tractable convex conic formulation of (
PEP
)

The performance estimation problem (
PEP
) admits the following tractable formulation:

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{maximize}&\ell(G,F)=\mathop{\bf tr}(A_{\mathrm{obj}}^{T}G)+b_{\mathrm{obj}}^{T}F\\
\textnormal{subject to}&\mathcal{S}(G,F)\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{M}\\
&\mathop{\bf tr}(A_{0}^{T}G)+b_{0}^{T}F+c_{0}\leq 0\\
&G\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2},\,F\in{\mathbf{R}}^{K+1}.\end{array}

The Lagrangian dual of the problem above writes as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[t]{ll}\textnormal{minimize}&-c_{0}\tau\\
\textnormal{subject to}&-\mathcal{S}^{*}(y)+\tau(A_{0},b_{0})-(A_{\mathrm{obj}},b_{\mathrm{obj}})\in{\mathbf{S}}_{+}^{K+2}\times\{0\}\\
&\tau\geq 0,\,y\in{\mathbf{R}}_{+}^{M},\end{array}

where

is an adjoint mapping of

defined as
\mathcal{S}^{*}(y)=-\sum_{m=1}^{M}y_{m}(A_{m},b_{m})
.
For the most of the first-order methods, strong duality holds true from
[
77
, Theorem 6]
.

the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
involves either gradient step of
L
-smooth function
f
:

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\nabla f(x^{i}),

or proximal step of convex, proper, and lower semi-continuous
g
:

x^{k+1}={\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta g}(x^{k}),

and
\nabla f(x^{\star})
and
x^{\star}-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\eta g}x^{\star}
are uniformly bounded over choices of
f
and
g
.
Then the mapping
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
is Lipschitz, i.e., there exists
\bar{L}>0
such that

\left\|\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{1}}(z)-\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{2}}(z)\right\|\leq\bar{L}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|,

for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
and
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the grammian representation
G\in{\mathbf{S}}^{K_{0}}_{+}
is defined as
G=P^{T}P
where
P\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d\times K_{0}}
is a horizontal stack of vectors of the forms:
x^{i}-x^{\star}
,
\nabla f(x^{i})
, or
{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta g}(x^{i})
.
We denote by
G=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)=G(\theta)
,
P=P(\theta)
, and
x^{i}=x^{i}(\theta)
to emphasize the
\theta
-dependency of each terms.
For the sake of generality, let
x^{\star}=0
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.
Since

\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{1}}(z)-\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{2}}(z)=P_{1}^{T}P_{1}-P_{2}^{T}P_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\big((P_{1}-P_{2})^{T}(P_{1}+P_{2})+(P_{1}+P_{2})^{T}(P_{1}-P_{2})\big),

we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{1}}(z)-\mathcal{A}_{\theta_{2}}(z)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|P(\theta_{1})-P(\theta_{2})\right\|_{F}\left\|P(\theta_{1})+P(\theta_{2})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|P(\theta_{1})-P(\theta_{2})\right\|_{F}\max_{\theta\in\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\}}\max_{i}\Big\{x^{i}(\theta)-x^{\star},\,\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta)),\,{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta g}(x^{i}(\theta))\Big\}.

It remains to show that
\theta\mapsto P(\theta)
is Lipschitz continuous and the vectors
x^{i}(\theta)-x^{\star}
,
\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta))
, and
{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta g}(x^{i}(\theta))\}_{i=0}^{K}
are uniformly bounded for
i=0,\dots,K
.

We first prove the latter claim by the following recursive statement:
If
x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}
is bounded, then
x^{k+1}(\theta)-x^{\star}
is bounded for these two cases:
x^{k+1}(\theta)=x^{k}(\theta)-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\nabla f(x^{k}(\theta))
or
x^{k+1}(\theta)={\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta))
.
Note that we impose the initial condition that
x^{0}(\theta)-x^{\star}
is uniformly bounded with
\theta\in\Theta
.
Also, from
L
-Lipschitz of
\nabla f
,
\left\|\nabla f(x^{k}(\theta))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|\leq L\left\|x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|
so
\nabla f(x^{k}(\theta))
is bounded as well.
For the first case, we have

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\big(x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\big)-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\left(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\right)-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|+\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\big(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\big)\right\|+\left|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\right|\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|+\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|^{2}}+\left|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\right|\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|+\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|^{2}}+\left|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\right|\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|+L\,\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|x^{i}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}}+\left|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\theta_{k,i}\right|\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|.

For the second case,

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\big({\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{\star})\big)+\big({\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{\star})-x^{\star}\big)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{\star})\right\|+\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{\star})-x^{\star}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta)-x^{\star}\right\|+\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}(x^{\star})-x^{\star}\right\|,

where the inequality comes from
{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta^{k}g}
being a firmly-nonexpansive operator.
Therefore, all iterates are bounded uniformly over
\theta\in\Theta
, given a fixed
K
.
Since
G=P^{T}P
with
P
stacking these bounded iterates and gradients, the support set
\Xi^{\Theta}=\bigcup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Xi^{\theta}
is bounded.

Now it remains to show the Lipschitz property of
\theta\mapsto P(\theta)
.
We similarly use induction to prove that,
if
\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|\leq L_{k}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|
for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
and any choice of
f
and
g
, then there exists
L_{k+1}>0
such that
\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})\right\|\leq L_{k+1}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|
for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
and any
f
and
g
as well.

For the first case (
f
-related update), we have

\displaystyle x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})

\displaystyle=x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{2}))\Big)

\displaystyle=x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{2}))\big)

\displaystyle=x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)\big(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)\Big)\nabla f(x^{\star})-\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{2}))\big)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)\big(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\big)\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big(\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{2}))\big)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\big((\theta_{1})_{k,i}-(\theta_{2})_{k,i}\big)^{2}}\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\theta_{2})_{k,i}^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{1}))-\nabla f(x^{i}(\theta_{2}))\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|+L\,\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|x^{i}(\theta_{1})-x^{\star}\right\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\|+L\,\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left\|x^{i}(\theta_{1})-x^{i}(\theta_{2})\right\|^{2}}.

For the second case (
g
-related update), we have

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{1}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{2})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{1}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))+{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{2})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{1}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))\right\|+\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{2})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|+\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{2})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))\right\|.

If
g
is an indicator of closed convex set, therefore
{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta g}
is always a projection mapping, then

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})\right\|\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|.

For other cases, we have

\left\|{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{1})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{2})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))\right\|\leq|(\theta_{1})_{k}-(\theta_{2})_{k}|\left\|\frac{x^{k}(\theta_{2})-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{(\theta_{2})_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta_{2}))}{(\theta_{2})_{k}}\right\|.

Note that for any
\alpha>0
,

\frac{x-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\alpha g}(x)}{\alpha}\in\partial g\big({\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\alpha g}(x)\big).

We have already shown that the iterates are uniformly bounded over
\theta\in\Theta
and the choice of
g
.
Therefore, the iterates lie on compact set of
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
.
As we can restrict the domain of
g
to a compact set,
\partial g({\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\alpha g}(x))
for any
x
within such compact set is always locally bounded
[
8
, Proposition 16.17]
.
We can choose finite covering of
\Theta
in terms of such neighborhoods, which translates to a finite covering of compactly-restricted domain of
g
.
Within such compactly-restricted domain,
\partial g
is uniformly bounded over
\theta\in\Theta
,
which results in

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\|\frac{x^{k}(\theta)-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\theta_{k}g}(x^{k}(\theta))}{\theta_{k}}\right\|\leq M<\infty.

Therefore,

\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})\right\|\leq\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|+\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|R,

for some universal constant
R>0
over
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
and
g
.

We may conclude that there exists
L_{k+1}>0
such that, for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\Theta
and functions
f
,
g
,

\left\|x^{k+1}(\theta_{1})-x^{k+1}(\theta_{2})\right\|\leq L_{k+1}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|,

given
\left\|x^{k}(\theta_{1})-x^{k}(\theta_{2})\right\|\leq L_{k}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|
.
This results in
\left\|P(\theta_{1})-P(\theta_{2})\right\|_{F}\leq\bar{L}\left\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\|
for
\bar{L}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}L_{k}
.
∎

Remark 2

(Assumption in
Lemma

4
)
.

We have seemingly nontrivial assumption on
Lemma

4
that, given
\eta>0
,
\nabla f(x^{\star})
and
x^{\star}-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\eta g}x^{\star}
being uniformly bounded over choices of
f
and

.
This can be implied by the condition that there exist
x_{f}^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x}f(x)
and
x_{g}^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x}f(x)
such that
x_{f}^{\star}
and
x_{g}^{\star}
are not too far away from
x^{\star}
, as

\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})\right\|=\left\|\nabla f(x^{\star})-\nabla f(x_{f}^{\star})\right\|\leq L\left\|x^{\star}-x_{f}^{\star}\right\|,

and

\left\|x^{\star}-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\eta g}(x^{\star})\right\|=\left\|(x^{\star}-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\eta g}(x^{\star}))-(x_{g}^{\star}-{\mathop{\bf prox}}_{\eta g}(x_{g}^{\star}))\right\|\leq\left\|x^{\star}-x_{g}^{\star}\right\|.

We follow the measure concentration result of
[
26
]
to derive the radius
\varepsilon
guaranteeing that DRO loss upper-bounds true risk with probability at least
1-\beta
, as in
[
61
]
.
Choose
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\beta,\theta)
as the follows:

\varepsilon(\beta,\theta)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}N}\right)^{\left((K+2)(K+3)/2+(K+1)\right)^{-1}}&N\geq\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}}\\
\left(\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}N}\right)^{1/a}&N<\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}},\end{cases}

(4)

where
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
are some appropriately chosen constants depending only on
\theta
, due to its dependency on true distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{\theta}
, which is the pushforward measure of
{\mathbf{P}}
by mapping
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
.
From this, we get the following measure concentration inequality for each
\theta\in\Theta
:

Lemma 5
.

Let
\beta\in(0,1)
.
For each
\theta\in\Theta
, there exists
\varepsilon(\beta,\theta)>0
as in (
4
) such that

{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon\left(\beta,\theta\right)}\big(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta}\big)\right)\geq 1-\beta.

Proof.

Proof follows directly from
[
26
, Theorem 2]
.
∎

We now prove
Theorem

1
using the covering number argument on compact
\Theta
to extract
\theta
-independent radius
\varepsilon
of Wasserstein ambiguity set.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

For any
\delta>0
, there exists a finite covering
C_{\delta}
of compact set
\Theta
in a sense that,
|C_{\delta}|<\infty
and for any
\theta\in\Theta
, there exists
\theta_{\delta}\in C_{\delta}
such that
\left\|\theta-\theta_{\delta}\right\|\leq\delta
.
According to
Lemma

5
, there exists
\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)=\sup_{\theta_{j}\in C_{\delta}}\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|,\theta_{j})>0
such that

{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta_{j}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)}\big(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta_{j}}\big)\right)\geq 1-\beta/|C_{\delta}|,\qquad\forall\theta_{j}\in C_{\delta}.

From the union bound, we have

{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta_{j}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)}\big(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta_{j}}\big),\;\forall\theta_{j}\in C_{\delta}\right)\geq 1-\beta.

Now, define

\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta)=\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)+2L\delta.

For any
\theta\in\Theta
, there exists
\theta_{j}\in C_{\delta}
such that
\left\|\theta-\theta_{j}\right\|\leq\delta
.
From
Lemma

4
, we have

W_{1}\left(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta},\,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta_{j}}\right)\leq L\left\|\theta-\theta_{j}\right\|\leq L\delta,

and

W_{1}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta},\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\theta_{j}}\right)\leq L\left\|\theta-\theta_{j}\right\|\leq L\delta.

From triangle inequality of Wasserstein distance,

\displaystyle W_{1}\left(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta},\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}\left(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta},\,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta_{j}}\right)+W_{1}\left(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta_{j}},\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\theta_{j}}\right)+W_{1}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta_{j}},\,{\mathbf{P}}^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq L\delta+\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)+L\delta=\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta).

Therefore, we get

{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta)}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta}),\;\forall\theta\in\Theta\right)\geq 1-\beta.

Finally, for any
\delta>0
, we get

{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta}\in\mathcal{U}_{\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta)}\big(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta}\big),\;\forall\theta\in\Theta\right)\geq{\mathbf{P}}^{N}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\theta_{j}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)}\big(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}^{\theta_{j}}\big),\;\forall\theta_{j}\in C_{\delta}\right)\geq 1-\beta,

for
\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta)=\varepsilon(\beta/|C_{\delta}|)+2L\delta
.

From
[
78
, Corollary 4.2.13]
, the covering number
|C_{\delta}|
of
\Theta
is upper bounded as

|C_{\delta}|\leq\left(\frac{2\,\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)}{\delta}+1\right)^{\mathrm{dim}(\Theta)}.

Then

\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta)\geq\varepsilon\left(\beta(2\,\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)/\delta+1)^{-\mathrm{dim}(\Theta)}\right)+2L\delta,

so the choice of
\delta
with tightest
\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta)
will give the best bound on
\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta)
.
From (
4
), we have

\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,\delta)\geq\left(\frac{\log(1/\beta)+\mathrm{dim}(\Theta)\log(2\,\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)/\delta+1)}{N}\right)^{1/q}+2L\delta,

where

is the dimension of
(G,F)
.
Naively choosing

yields

\overline{\varepsilon}(\beta,1/N)\lesssim\left(\frac{\log(1/\beta)+\mathrm{dim}(\Theta)\big(\log\,\mathrm{diam}(\Theta)+\log N\big)}{N}\right)^{1/q}+\frac{2L}{N}.

∎

Proof of Proposition

Throughout this subsection, we omit
\theta
-dependency and write

and

as

and

, for simplicity.

Proposition 3

(Proposition 8.5 of
[
42
]
)
.

Consider the

-Wasserstein ambiguity set

\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\left\{{\mathbf{Q}}\mid W_{1}({\mathbf{Q}},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where
{\mathbf{Q}}
is a probability measure supported on a closed set
\Xi
.
Then, if

\mathop{\bf E{}}_{Y\sim\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}}(|\ell(Y)|)<\infty

\begin{array}[t]{ll}\underset{{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})}{\sup}&\underset{Y\sim{\mathbf{Q}}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\big(\ell(Y)\big)\end{array}\leq\underset{Y\sim\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\big(\ell(Y)\big)+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip}(\ell).

Note that from
[

, DRO loss interpolates (

) and (

) in the following sense: the problem (

) either converges to (

This property is directly stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

For any algorithm parameter
\theta\in\Theta
and probability distribution
{\mathbf{Q}}
supported on
\Xi
,

\mathcal{R}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})\leq\sup_{(G,F)\in\Xi}\,\ell(G,F),

for any
\varepsilon^{+}\geq\varepsilon\geq 0

From
\{{\mathbf{Q}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{Q}})\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon^{+}}({\mathbf{Q}})
, we get

\mathcal{R}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}}).

If
\Xi
is compact, a maximizer
(G^{\star},F^{\star})\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{(G,F)\in\Xi}\ell(G,F)
exists.
Therefore, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,{\mathbf{Q}})=\sup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{Q}})}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{(G,F)\sim\widetilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}}\big(\ell(G,F)\big)\leq\sup_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{Q}}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{Q}})}\sup_{(G,F)\in\Xi}\ell(G,F)=\sup_{(G,F)\in\Xi}\ell(G,F).

∎

We now prove
Proposition

2
.

Proof of
Proposition

2
.

First, from
Lemma

6
,

\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\,\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big),

for any
\theta\in\Theta
and
\varepsilon^{+}\geq\varepsilon>0

Since
\Theta
is a compact set, each terms in inequality above has respective minimizers in
\Theta
.
First of all,

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}),\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

For
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
, we have

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}),\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

Similarly, with respect to
\theta_{\varepsilon^{+}}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
,

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta_{\varepsilon^{+}}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}),\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

Lastly,

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon^{+}}(\theta_{\varepsilon^{+}}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{(G,F)\in\Xi^{\theta}}\,\ell(G,F),

we observe that
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is monotonically nondecreasing and upper-bounded by
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big)
.

Furthermore, as
\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
implies
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
,
the following holds true for all
\varepsilon>0
.
The left inequality follows from
Lemma

6
(since
\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
for all
\theta
, taking
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}
on both sides gives
\inf_{\theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
).
The right inequality follows from
Proposition

3
(applying Proposition 8.5 of
[
42
]
to each
\theta
and taking
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}
on the right-hand side):

\displaystyle\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip}(\ell).

Applying
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get the desired result.

Note that mapping
(\varepsilon,\theta)\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is (jointly) continuous in
(\varepsilon,\theta)
.
Also,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big),

and
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is monotonically nondecreasing for each
\theta\in\Theta
.
Then according to Dini’s theorem,
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
converges uniformly for
\varepsilon\to\infty
,
i.e.
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\,\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big)-\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\right)=0.

As
\Theta
is compact,
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))
exists and is an
\varepsilon
-independent term.
Therefore, uniform convergence allows interchanging the limit and the infimum:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\lim_{\varepsilon\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big),

which completes the proof.
∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem
2

We now prove
Theorem

2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

According to
Lemma

6
,

\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\,\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big),

holds true for all
\theta\in\Theta
.
From
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
, we get

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\text{PEP}}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\,\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\star}_{\text{PEP}}}(z)\big)=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big).

Taking
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}
consecutively from smaller to larger terms, we get

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\ell\big(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)\big).

Applying
Proposition

3
with
Y=\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z)
,

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\sup_{{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{z\sim{\mathbf{Q}}}\left(\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\right)\leq\mathop{\bf E{}}_{z\sim\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}}\big(\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\big)+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip(\ell)},

holds for every
\theta\in\Theta
.
Take
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}
on the left-hand side of the inequality to have

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\mathop{\bf E{}}_{z\sim\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}}\big(\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\big)+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip(\ell)},

and also take
\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}
on the right-hand side to get

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{z\sim\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N}}\big(\ell(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\big)+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Lip(\ell)}.

We conclude the proof by upper-bounding true risk
\mathcal{R}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},{\mathbf{P}})
by DRO risk
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
with probability at least
1-\beta
, using
Theorem

1
.
∎

Remark 3

(Well-posedness of (

))
.

The entries of

,

,

, and

.
By

2
, the Slater condition holds for (

(Assumption
2
) implies it holds uniformly over

.
Hence
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is continuous by Berge’s maximum theorem

, Chapter 6]
, and problem (

.

Algorithm

1
describes the solution method for the learning problem (
DR-L2O
).

Algorithm 1

Stochastic Gradient Method for Distributionally-robust L2O (DR-L2O)

Input:

training data
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{N}
,
parameter initialization
\theta^{0}
,
mini-batch size
n
,
iteration counter
k=0
.

repeat

Sample mini-batch
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
from training data
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{N}

Define mini-batched empirical distribution
\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n}
of
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}

Evaluate
d\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n})/d\theta
using
Lemma

7
.

Update
\theta^{k+1}\leftarrow\verb|AdamWUpdate|(\theta^{k})
.

k\leftarrow k+1
.

until

\theta^{k}
converges

In order to evaluate the gradient of
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n})
given a dataset
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
of
n
samples,
we use the approach of
[
2
,
1
,
32
]
to differentiate the solution mapping of conic linear problem in terms of problem parameters.
Let
\mathcal{T}\big(\mathcal{S},\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\big)
be a solution mapping of the problem (
DRO-PEP-D
) and let
\mathcal{L}
be an objective function of (
DRO-PEP-D
).
Then the DRO risk
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{N})
is composed of solution mapping and
\mathcal{L}
as follows:

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n})=\mathcal{L}\Big(\mathcal{T}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\theta},\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n})\big)\Big).

We finally apply the chain rule to obtain the gradient with respect to
\theta
, as the following:

Lemma 7
.

Given
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
, the gradient of
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n})
evaluates as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\theta\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n})\right)=\left.\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dt}\right|_{t=\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta},\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}))}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}}{\partial(A_{m},b_{m})}\right|_{(\mathcal{S}_{\theta},\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}))}\frac{d(A_{m}(\theta),b_{m}(\theta))}{d\theta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathcal{T}}{\partial(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})}\right|_{(\mathcal{S}_{\theta},\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n}))}\frac{d\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\hat{z}_{i})}{d\theta}\Bigg).

Proof.

We interpret the risk
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n})
as the parametric conic program (
DRO-PEP-D
) with parameters
(\widehat{G}_{i},\widehat{F}_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,n
and
(A_{m},b_{m})
for
m=0,1,\dots,M
.
Using the chain rule on

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(\theta,\widehat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{n})=\mathcal{L}\Big(\mathcal{T}\big(\mathcal{S}_{\theta},\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{n})\big)\Big),

gives the desired result.
∎

Training procedure

For each experiment, we fix the algorithm structure and learn the step-size schedule
\theta=\{\theta^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
for each horizon
K
and each of (
DR-L2O
), (
OPT-PEP
), and (
L2O
).
For (
DR-L2O
) and (
L2O
), we use minibatched stochastic gradient descent with the AdamW optimizer
[
40
,
50
]
, with weight decay cross-validated over
\{0,10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3}\}
, combined with a linear warm-up followed by a cosine annealing learning rate schedule
[
49
]
.
For (
OPT-PEP
), we follow the approach of
[
37
]
: their
[
37
, Theorem 3.2]
gives an explicit gradient of the worst-case rate with respect to the step size, and we apply gradient descent with the same cosine annealing schedule.
All experiments run for 1000 iterations, with 10% of the iterations used for a linear warm-up to the maximum learning rate, which is cross-validated from
\{10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3}\}
.

When learning the schedule, we do not use the performance loss at the last iterate as the training objective as this can hinder gradient information flow to earlier stepsizes
[
18
]
.
Instead, we use an exponentially decaying weighted summation for the training loss.
Letting
\ell^{k}
represent the loss at iterate
k
, we minimize the sample analogue of the surrogate objective:

\underset{z\sim{\mathbf{P}}}{\mathop{\bf E{}}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\ell^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(z))\right].

We choose
w_{k}=0.9^{K-k}
, and show a plot of the exponential decay in Figure
6
.

Figure 6:
A plot showing the exponential weight decay for our training objective with weights
w_{k}=0.9^{K-k}
for
K=15
.

Additionally, during the learning procedure, in order to remove numerical issues with any step size component becoming negative, we
square
the step size before plugging it into the first order method.
We initialize the values so that the square of the input step size is our desired value but we backpropagate the gradients to the square root.

Parameter selection

For each experiment, we set a maximum value of
K
and learn a separate schedule for each
k=1,\dots,K
used as the learning horizon.
The Wasserstein radius
\varepsilon>0
from
Section

3.2
controls the probability of constraint satisfaction; in practice it is treated as a hyperparameter tuned by cross-validation
[
29
,
61
]
.
We cross-validate over
\varepsilon\in\{10^{-2},10^{-1},1,5,10\}
on a validation set and choose the schedule minimizing the empirical risk.
A separate test set is used for final loss evaluations.
