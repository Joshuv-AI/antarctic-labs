Abstract
Duodenal perforation secondary to gastrostomy tube migration is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening
complication. This report aims to describe its clinical presentation, diagnostic assessment, and surgical management,
highlighting the importance of recognizing abdominal symptoms after tube replacement. A 43-year-old woman with
pulmonary lymphangitic carcinomatosis, extrinsic esophageal compression, dysphagia, and chronic malnutrition
underwent surgical gastrostomy using a 22-French Foley catheter. Two months later, the catheter was replaced without
complications. The following day, she developed abdominal pain. Noncontrast abdominal computed tomography
demonstrated pneumoperitoneum, free intraperitoneal fluid, and the catheter tip within the duodenal bulb. Subsequent
imaging with contrast administered through the gastrostomy tube revealed intraperitoneal extravasation, supporting
the diagnosis of gastrointestinal perforation. Emergency exploratory laparotomy confirmed a perforation in the third
portion of the duodenum, with contamination by gastric, biliary, and pancreatic contents. Surgical treatment
comprised closure of the previous gastrostomy site, primary duodenal repair, pyloric cerclage, cholecystectomy,
Roux-en-Y gastrojejunostomy, and feeding jejunostomy. Recovery was favorable, and the patient was discharged after
approximately 15 days, tolerating jejunal enteral nutrition. This case underscores the need to suspect tube migration
and duodenal injury when abdominal pain occurs after gastrostomy tube replacement. Early imaging assessment and
timely intervention are essential to guide management. Surgical treatment should be individualized according to the
injury and clinical context. Appropriate device selection, secure external fixation, and verification of tube position are
important preventive measures, particularly when Foley catheters are used. The underlying malignancy remained the
principal determinant of long-term prognosis.
Keywords: Gastrostomy; Duodenal perforation; Catheter migration; Foley catheter.

1. Introduction
Gastrostomy is a widely used route of enteral access for long-term nutritional support in patients unable to maintain
safe oral intake because of neurological or oncological conditions or swallowing disorders. Placement can be performed using
percutaneous endoscopic, radiological, or surgical techniques, with percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy being the most
commonly used approach because of its low associated morbidity and mortality and relative technical simplicity (Rajan et al.,
2022).
Despite being considered a safe procedure, gastrostomy is not without complications. These are generally classified as
minor or major. Minor complications include insertion-site infection, peristomal leakage, tube obstruction, and partial tube
dislodgement, all of which are relatively common. Major complications, including peritonitis, necrotizing fasciitis, gastrocolic
fistula, buried bumper syndrome, and hollow viscus perforation, are uncommon but carry substantial morbidity and mortality
(Zouk & Batra, 2021; Menni et al., 2023). Recent cohort studies have reported early complication rates ranging from 6% to
20%, depending on the population studied, the placement setting, and the patient’s nutritional and immune status (Ahmad et

Gastrostomy tube–related duodenal perforation is an uncommon and potentially severe complication whose true
incidence is difficult to establish, as much of the available evidence comes from isolated case reports. The pathophysiological
mechanisms described include distal migration of the tube tip through the pylorus into the duodenal bulb or second portion of
the duodenum and pressure necrosis caused by an overinflated balloon against the duodenal wall. The use of Foley catheters as
replacement tubes without an adequate external fixation system also facilitates displacement through gastric peristalsis (Dalex
et al., 2024; Theocharidou et al., 2025). These mechanisms may act independently or in combination, and delayed recognition
increases the risk of peritonitis and abdominal sepsis.
The clinical presentation is generally nonspecific and includes diffuse abdominal pain, distension, nausea, vomiting,
or intolerance to enteral feeding, which may delay diagnosis, particularly in patients with cognitive or neurological impairment
who are unable to communicate their symptoms (Shehata et al., 2024). Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography is
the diagnostic modality of choice, as it can identify an abnormal position of the tube tip or balloon, pneumoperitoneum, fluid
collections, or contrast extravasation (Dalex et al., 2024; Theocharidou et al., 2025; Cuadros Mendoza et al., 2025).
Management of this complication is not standardized and depends on the size of the defect, the degree of peritoneal
contamination, and the patient’s clinical condition. Reported approaches range from minimally invasive endoscopic
management to open or laparoscopic surgical repair with primary closure and an omental patch, generally yielding favorable
outcomes when diagnosis and treatment are timely (Theocharidou et al., 2025; Karveli et al., 2023; Cozma et al., 2024).
Nevertheless, given the rarity of this condition, the evidence guiding clinical decision-making remains based primarily on case
series and isolated case reports.
This report aims to describe its clinical presentation, diagnostic assessment, and surgical management, highlighting
the importance of recognizing abdominal symptoms after tube replacement.

2. Methodology
This descriptive clinical case study (Toassi & Petry, 2021; Risemberg et al., 2026a) uses a qualitative (Risemberg et
al., 2026b; Pereira et al., 2018) and narrative approach (Ogassawara et al., 2025) to report a duodenal perforation following
gastrostomy tube replacement in a 43-year-old female patient. The clinical course is presented chronologically, including the
indication for gastrostomy, symptom onset after tube replacement, imaging and intraoperative findings, surgical management,
and postoperative outcomes.
The findings were contextualized through a narrative discussion of literature on gastrostomy-related complications
and complex duodenal injuries (Rajan et al., 2022; Zouk & Batra, 2021; Karveli et al., 2023), emphasizing clinical lessons
without establishing the superiority of a specific surgical technique.
The report complied with applicable ethical and institutional requirements. Written informed consent (ICF/TCLE)
was obtained from the patient for publication of the clinical information and accompanying images. Confidentiality was
maintained, and identifying information was omitted.

3. Results and Discussion
Case Report
A 43-year-old female patient with a history of pulmonary lymphangitic carcinomatosis associated with an exophytic
growth pattern causing extrinsic esophageal compression presented with dysphagia to both solids and liquids. Prolonged

The procedure was performed in April of the same year, without intraoperative or immediate postoperative
complications. Following tube placement, the patient received enteral nutrition through the gastrostomy for approximately two
months, with partial improvement in her nutritional status. In June, she presented to the emergency department for elective
replacement of the 22-French Foley gastrostomy catheter, which was performed without apparent complications. The
following day, she developed severe abdominal pain and returned to the emergency department.
Noncontrast abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed multiple foci of pneumoperitoneum associated with free
intraperitoneal fluid, as well as the distal tip of the Foley catheter positioned within the duodenal bulb (Figure 1). These
findings prompted further abdominal CT imaging with contrast administered through the gastrostomy tube, which
demonstrated no passage of contrast into the small bowel and concomitant extravasation into the peritoneal cavity. These
findings supported a presumptive diagnosis of duodenal perforation.

4. Discussion
Gastrostomy tube–related duodenal perforation is an uncommon complication for which the available evidence
consists primarily of isolated case reports, reflecting its low incidence and the absence of large prospective series (Dalex et al.,
2024; Theocharidou et al., 2025; Karveli et al., 2023). The present case adds to this limited evidence and illustrates how a
procedure generally considered low risk, such as gastrostomy tube replacement, can result in a potentially fatal complication if
the replacement tube migrates into the duodenum.
In the present case, the most likely mechanism of perforation was distal migration of the Foley catheter tip through the
pylorus into the duodenal bulb and subsequently into the third portion of the duodenum during or after tube replacement,
facilitated by the lack of an external fixation system inherent to Foley catheters. This mechanism is consistent with reports in
the literature, which identify the use of inadequately secured Foley catheters as replacement tubes, together with gastric
peristalsis, as a major factor associated with transpyloric migration and duodenal wall injury (Rajan et al., 2022; Zouk & Batra,
2021).
Additionally, the patient’s chronic malnutrition and compromised overall condition, secondary to malignant
esophageal obstruction, may have contributed to reduced physiological tolerance of the complication and an increased risk of
device-related adverse events. This is consistent with recent cohort studies associating patients’ nutritional and immune status
with a higher rate of gastrostomy-related complications (Ahmad et al., 2023; Shehata et al., 2024; Cuadros Mendoza et al.,
2025).
Timely diagnosis in this case was achieved through sequential computed tomography imaging. An initial noncontrast
scan demonstrated pneumoperitoneum and free fluid, followed by a scan with contrast administered through the gastrostomy
tube itself, which confirmed the absence of contrast passage into the small bowel and its extravasation into the peritoneal
cavity. This diagnostic strategy is consistent with the literature, which recognizes contrast-enhanced computed tomography as

the imaging modality of choice for assessing tube position, the extent of peritoneal contamination, and the magnitude of

2023). Early recognition of these findings was instrumental in preventing a delay in surgical management, a factor consistently
associated with worse outcomes in published cases.
Regarding management, although minimally invasive endoscopic approaches have been described for gastrostomy
device–related complications such as buried bumper syndrome, these techniques primarily address migration of the tube into
the gastric wall and are not applicable in the presence of frank hollow viscus perforation with active peritoneal contamination,
as occurred in the present case (Menni et al., 2023; Cozma et al., 2024; Fasulo et al., 2025; Kitchin et al., 2022). Emergency
open surgical management was therefore selected. The extent of the duodenal injury, contamination of the peritoneal cavity
with gastric, biliary, and pancreatic contents, and the preexisting gastrostomy in the anterior gastric wall justified an approach
more extensive than simple duodenal repair. This included pyloric cerclage to achieve functional exclusion and reduce the
passage of gastric contents through the duodenal repair site, a Roux-en-Y gastrojejunostomy to restore gastrointestinal
continuity, and a jejunostomy to provide secure access for enteral nutrition. Such combined strategies, aimed at diversion and
decompression of the affected segment, have been described as valid options for complex duodenal injuries at high risk of
dehiscence (Karveli et al., 2023).
The patient’s favorable postoperative course supports the appropriateness of the selected surgical approach in the
setting of peritoneal contamination and preexisting nutritional compromise. Nevertheless, the medium- and long-term
prognosis remains guarded, primarily because of the progression of the underlying lung malignancy rather than the surgical
complication itself. As a limitation, this single case report does not permit generalizable conclusions regarding the true
incidence of this complication or the superiority of any surgical technique. Its principal contribution is therefore to expand the
available descriptive evidence on this uncommon complication and to emphasize the importance of early clinical suspicion in
patients presenting with abdominal pain after manipulation of a gastrostomy tube.

5. Conclusion
Duodenal perforation secondary to gastrostomy tube migration is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication
that should be considered in patients presenting with abdominal pain or feeding intolerance after tube replacement. This case
highlights the importance of early recognition, imaging confirmation, and timely surgical intervention in the presence of
perforation with peritoneal contamination. The favorable postoperative outcome illustrates the feasibility of an individualized
surgical approach addressing the duodenal injury, gastrointestinal continuity, and nutritional access, without establishing the
superiority of a specific technique. Appropriate device selection, secure external fixation, and verification of tube position after
replacement remain essential preventive measures, particularly when Foley catheters are used as temporary substitutes.
