Minute-scale red phosphorescence in carbon nanodots
Authors: Fan Ding, Fu-Kui Li, Yan-Wei Hu, Shi-Yu Song, Wen-Bo Zhao, Qing Cao, Chong-Xin Shan, Kai-Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRed phosphorescent materials with long-lived triplet excitons are highly desirable for optoelectronic and bio-related applications but remain limited by short emission lifetime. Here we show ultralong red phosphorescent carbon nanodots, which exhibit minute-scale phosphorescence. Through combining excited-state engineering assisted by machine learning optimization, the obtained red carbon nanodots achieve the phosphorescence lifetime of 353 second, with emission persistence exceeding 4000 second. Spectroscopic analysis reveals the coexistence of a fast radiative transition channel from 3(n, π*) to 1n2 and a slow transition channel from 3(π, π*) to 1(π, π*), where the latter dominates the ultralong phosphorescence. As a proof of concept, a wearable passive light-emitting-diode indicator based on the carbon nanodots is demonstrated, enabling visual signaling in time-limited environments after power-off.Subject terms: Materials for optics, Nanoparticles, Optical materials

Introduction

Phosphorescence arises from the radiative recombination of triplet excitons to the singlet ground state and is intrinsically enabled by the spin-forbidden nature of the transition1. In contrast to persistent luminescence, where excitation energy is stored in deep defect traps and released over hours, phosphorescence represents an intrinsic excited-state relaxation process with well-defined spectral characteristics2. The spin-forbidden transition is therefore the fundamental origin of long phosphorescence lifetime3. However, owing to spin-orbit coupling and non-radiative relaxation, room-temperature phosphorescence typically remains limited to the millisecond-to-second regime4–6.

Since Francis Perrin first clarified in 1929 that phosphorescence proceeds through an intermediate triplet state prior to emission7, a century of research has established that efficient triplet exciton emission in organic systems generally requires cryogenic temperatures or inert environments8,9. In recent years, significant progress has been achieved with organic luminescent materials and carbon nanodots (CNDs), where heteroatom doping10,11, heavy-atom effects12,13, and rigid matrix confinement14,15 have enabled efficient room-temperature phosphorescence. As a result, phosphorescence lifetime has been extended from milliseconds to several seconds, with reported values for blue phosphorescent CNDs reaching up to 5.72 s, enabling emerging applications in bioimaging16,17, information encryption and optoelectronics18,19. Despite these advances, achieving ultralong-lived phosphorescence especially in red region remains exceptionally challenging.

From a quantum-mechanical perspective, phosphorescence lifetime exceeding 100 s is theoretically permissible when the triplet radiative transition is strongly spin-forbidden20,21. However, in most phosphorescent systems, triplet radiative transition is weakly allowed due to orbital angular momentum mixing, resulting in relatively fast T1-S0 transitions22,23. Notably, transition channel involving3(π, π*) to 1π2 exhibits orders of magnitude slower radiative decay than that of3(n, π*) to 1n2, yet such slow channels are rarely isolated or stabilized. This fundamental trade-off suggests that suppressing, rather than enhancing, specific triplet decay pathways may be essential for realizing intrinsic ultralong phosphorescence. Instead, if a triplet exciton could decay predominantly through a strongly forbidden channel, analogous to a defect-trapped excitons, ultralong intrinsic phosphorescence may become achievable.

In this work, we report ultralong red phosphorescent CNDs with minute-scale lifetime at room temperature. We constructed a dual-channel triplet relaxation landscape in which a fast3(n, π*) to 1n2 pathway governs early-time emission, while a highly spin-forbidden3(π, π*) to 1π2 transition dominates long time emission decay and determines the ultralong lifetime. With the assistance of machine learning (ML), the resulting red phosphorescent CNDs exhibit a phosphorescence lifetime of up to 353 s and emission persistence exceeding 4000 s, enabling passive wearable LEDs as visual indicators for time-limited operation in extreme environments.

Two representative triplet manifolds,3(n, π*) and3(π, π*), are compared in terms of orbital angular momentum change (ΔL), SOC strength (H^soc), and the corresponding radiative transition rate (k). The transition between3(n, π*) and 1n2 involves a nonzero angular momentum change (ΔL = 1), resulting in appreciable SOC via angular momentum conservation and a relatively fast phosphorescence decay, corresponding to a short lifetime (t1). In contrast, the transition3(π, π*) between 1π2, exhibits negligible angular momentum change (ΔL ≈ 0), leading to strongly spin-forbidden transition and a significantly reduced radiative transition rate, which gives rise to a much longer intrinsic lifetime (t2). The right energy-level diagram schematically illustrates the evolution pathway of the excited states responsible for the distinct phosphorescence lifetime. In the ground state, electrons occupy the π and n orbitals. Upon photoexcitation, electrons are promoted to the singlet excited states, forming either1(π, π*) or1(n, π*). Subsequent intersystem crossing (ISC) occurs between the singlet and triplet manifolds, accompanied by orbital reconfiguration and spin flipping10,24. During ISC, transitions between1(π, π*) and3(n, π*), as well as between1(n, π*) and3(π, π*), involve a nonzero change in orbital angular momentum, which effectively facilitates efficient population of triplet states25–29. To obtain optimal CNDs, ML-guided optimization framework was employed in this work to identify synthesis conditions favorable for minute-scale red phosphorescence, as shown in Fig. 1b. To achieve excellent performance in both wavelength and lifetime, we developed a strategy that prioritizes long lifetime over long wavelength by assigning additional rewards. The figure of Q is scientifically defined as a product of a wavelength factor (ωλ) and a lifetime factor (ωτ), allowing simultaneous evaluation of phosphorescence wavelength and lifetime. Higher weights are assigned to long wavelength (λ ≥ 590 nm) and long lifetime (τ ≥ 50 s). Through the definition of Q, the two desired properties are converted into calculable specific values, providing clear targets for subsequent data processing and model training. We then performed label-based classification on the collected experimental dataset of CNDs, and the screened samples served as the core data for ML training, thereby reducing training bias and improving prediction accuracy. Data with longer wavelength and longer lifetime were fed back to the model for regression analysis, ultimately determining the optimal preparation conditions and obtaining the desired CNDs. Figure 1c outlines the feature engineering and experimental workflow underlying ML training. Six features including temperature, heating time, precursor ratio, rhodamine content, and solvent composition are encoded as independent features defining a multidimensional parameter space. Supplementary Table 1 shows the ranges of the six synthesis parameters used for machine learning. The importance of the six feature values is shown in Supplementary Fig 1. The features are not randomly chosen but are determined based on the reported references, our previous work31, and the property regulation rules of red phosphorescent CNDs. Thermal decomposition synthesis is then performed to generate red phosphorescent CNDs, a dataset is obtained through multiple sets of experiments.

Figure 1d shows the obtained red phosphorescent CNDs with a peak centered at 590 nm. The photoluminescence emission peak of the red phosphorescent CNDs is located at 580 nm (Suppl. Fig. 2). Figure 1e shows that the decay exhibits a long-lived component and can be fitted with a characteristic lifetime of 353.24 s, demonstrating minute-scale phosphorescence at room temperature. The phosphorescence lifetime was determined by three-exponential decay fitting using Origin software, with a high fitting reliability (Suppl. Table 2). Figure 1f presents a visual demonstration of the ultralong red phosphorescent CNDs, where the phosphorescent pattern (353 s) remains clearly visible under dark conditions after excitation is removed. This lifetime represents the longest reported value in the field of red phosphorescent CNDs to date. For the photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) in Fig. 1g, five tests were conducted, and the result showed an average PLQY of 10.2%. Similarly, five tests were also performed for the phosphorescence quantum yield, and the average value was determined to be 2.7% (Suppl. Fig. 3). CNDs exhibited no obvious attenuation in phosphorescence intensity during multiple repeated photoexcitation-luminescence cycles, demonstrating excellent stability of the CNDs (Suppl. Fig. 4).

To establish a quantitative synthesis-property relationship for ultralong red phosphorescent CNDs, an ML-assisted framework was constructed to correlate synthesis parameters with phosphorescence performance. The distribution of synthesis temperature as a function of Q is visualized in Fig. 2a, revealing a clear dependence of high-Q samples on elevated reaction temperatures. Samples with larger Q values are predominantly concentrated in the higher temperature regime, indicating that temperature plays a key role in the formation of ultralong red phosphorescent CNDs. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to determine the correlation among the synthesis parameters (Fig. 2b). The low linear correlation observed between all feature pairs confirms the independence and complementarity of the six selected features. The same holds true in regression analysis (Suppl. Fig. 5). Based on the defined quality factor, a classification model was first trained to categorize samples into different performance levels. Six classification models, including extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Gaussian naive Bayes (GauNB), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), decision tree regression (DTR), and random forest (RF), were selected for training and were evaluated using four common performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score). Higher scores closer to 1 indicate better model performance. Among the six selected models for comparison, the XGBoost model exhibits the best performance (Suppl. Fig. 6). The confusion matrix in Fig. 2c demonstrates high classification accuracy across all classes (0, 1, and 2 represent samples with Q of 5, 10, and 50), with minimal misclassification, indicating that the selected descriptors are sufficient to distinguish phosphorescence quality. Correspondingly, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves in Fig. 2d yield high area-under-curve (AUC) values for all classes, confirming the robustness and reliability of the classification model. Beyond classification, regression models were employed to quantitatively predict phosphorescence lifetime in the class with Q of 50. Figure 2e and Suppl. Fig. 7 compare the performance of various ML models; the XGBoost model achieves the optimal performance in terms of the coefficient of determination (R2) while maintaining a relatively low root mean square error (RMSE). The predicted versus observed lifetime plotted in Fig. 2f further validates the strong predictive capability of the optimized model. The detailed hyperparameter settings of the optimal XGBoost models are provided in Suppl. Table 3. To elucidate the physical significance behind the model predictions, SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) analysis was performed, as shown in Fig. 2g. The results indicate that higher reaction temperatures, longer heating durations, and higher precursor ratios result in higher SHAP values, suggesting that these conditions exert a positive promotional effect on phosphorescence performance. Figure 2h–k show SHAP dependence plots, further clarifying the nonlinear influence of key parameters. Increasing temperature contributes positively to lifetime enhancement up to an optimal range, beyond which the effect saturates. The SHAP decision plots in Figs. 2l–n indicate that temperature provides a dominant positive contribution to lifetime, whereas the precursor ratio and RhB content exhibit threshold- and interval-dependent effects, respectively. In contrast, reaction time shows a highly nonlinear influence arising from multivariate coupling. The parallel coordinates plot in Fig. 2o visualizes the multidimensional synthesis space, mapping how coordinated parameter combinations converge toward regions of ultralong phosphorescence, thereby providing a global correlation network for rational CND design. The parallel coordinates plot is generated based on a full factorial experimental design matrix of the synthesis parameters, covering a total of 226,800 theoretical conditions (Suppl. Table 4). For better visualization, 5000 data points are randomly selected from the dataset to construct the parallel coordinates plot, showing the relationship between parameters and performance. Suppl. Fig. 8 compares the predicted and measured phosphorescence lifetimes of seven CND samples, yielding a prediction accuracy of over 70%.

Guided by the ML predictions, red phosphorescent CNDs with ultralong lifetime were synthesized and systematically characterized to elucidate their structural, chemical, and photophysical origins. To further verify the generalizability of the machine learning model, four representative samples were selected from the full prediction dataset, and their key synthesis parameters are detailed in Suppl. Table 5. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images shown in Fig. 3a–d reveal that all four representative samples consist of uniformly dispersed CNDs without apparent aggregation. The corresponding sizes of the CNDs range from 3-5 nm, with an average size of approximately 4 nm. High-resolution TEM images confirm that the lattice spacing of the CNDs is approximately 0.21 nm, suggesting the presence of locally ordered graphitic domains32,33. Figure 3e shows the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the CNDs, from which stretching vibrations of P = O (1272 cm−1), C = N (1490 cm−1), N-H/C = O (1600 and 1750 cm−1), C-H (2960 cm−1), and N-H/O-H (3200–3600 cm−1) can be observed, confirming the coexistence of heteroatom-containing groups34–36. The same phenomenon was also detected in the other samples (Suppl. Fig. 9). The chemical composition of the composite material was then studied using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and the corresponding XPS survey spectra of the CNDs confirmed the presence of O, N, C, Br, and P (Fig. 3f). The same elements were also detected in the other samples (Suppl. Fig. 10). The C1s high-resolution spectra show characteristic peaks at 284.6, 286.9, and 288.8 eV, which correspond to C-C/C = C, C-N, and N-C = N bonds (Fig. 3h), the presence of C-N and N-C = N bonds provides significant driving force for the electron spin flip37–39.

To investigate the inner structure of the CNDs, 13C NMR and 1H NMR spectra were recorded, as shown in Fig. 3g. In the 13C NMR spectra, the signals presented in the range of 110–140 ppm can be ascribed to sp2 conjugated aromatic C atoms. The signals from C = O groups (155–170 ppm) are also detected. In the 1H NMR spectra, aromatic H centered at 6.0 ppm and 8.5 ppm can be observed clearly, other H signals C-H (4.5-6.5 ppm) were also detected30. Figure 3i presents the UV absorption spectra and phosphorescence spectra of samples #1, #2, #3, and #4. All the CNDs exhibit pronounced absorption features associated with π-π* and n-π* transitions, indicating the formation of hybrid excited state within the CNDs. CIE coordinates of the samples is shown in Suppl. Fig. 11. Among these, sample #1 exhibits the longest lifetime and shows a higher absorption intensity corresponding to the n-π* transition. In the phosphorescence spectra, sample #1 displays a longer emission wavelength, this shift arises from the presence of more rhodamine molecules attached to the CND surface. The photoluminescence excitation spectra are shown in Suppl. Fig. 12, with the excitation wavelength centered at around 340 nm. Without RhB, the CNDs have a phosphorescence emission peak at 450 nm. RhB exhibits a broad absorption band in the 400–550 nm region, which shows distinct spectral overlap with the phosphorescence emission band of the CNDs (Suppl. Fig. 13). An increase in temperature leads to the reduction of both phosphorescence and fluorescence intensities (Suppl. Fig. 14), while the emission peak position remains unchanged, verifying that the emission originates from phosphorescence (Suppl. Fig. 15). The other samples also displayed analogous phosphorescence emission profiles (Suppl. Fig. 16). Time-resolved phosphorescence decay curves (Fig. 3j) demonstrate ultralong emission persistence for all samples, with lifetimes extending to several thousand seconds. The lifetime of other selected samples was also detected, which also exhibited ultralong lifetimes (Suppl. Fig. 17). Figure 3k illustrates the excited state evolution responsible for the ultralong phosphorescence of the CNDs. Upon photoexcitation, electrons are promoted from n and π orbitals to the singlet excited states, generating mixed1(n, π*) and1(π, π*) configurations40–43. The orbital mixing between n and π states introduces a nonzero change in orbital angular momentum, which activates the originally spin-forbidden singlet-triplet ISC. This process is further enhanced by the heavy-atom-induced spin-orbit coupling, enabling efficient population of both3(n, π*) and 3(π, π*) triplet manifolds25,44. For radiative recombination, 3(n, π*) transition to the ground state is allowed, giving rise to a fast phosphorescence channel. In contrast, 3(π, π*) to S0 transition preserves similar orbital symmetry and remains strongly spin-forbidden, resulting in an extremely slow radiative decay pathway. The dominance of this forbidden channel effectively traps triplet excitons and governs the minute-scale phosphorescence lifetime observed experimentally. Figure 3l compares the CNDs developed in this work with those reported in previous studies45–61. While most prior materials are confined to either short wavelengths or short lifetimes, the CNDs reported here simultaneously achieve red phosphorescence and minute-scale lifetimes, significantly outperforming most phosphorescent materials reported previously.

The minute-scale red phosphorescent CNDs enable a distinct operation mode for wearable LEDs as visual indicators that is particularly advantageous for time-limited tasks in extreme environments, where continuous power supply is unreliable or impractical62. Figure 4a shows photographs of the red phosphorescent CNDs, upon UV illumination, the CNDs emit red light with uniform intensity that gradually decays over several minutes after the UV light is turned off, and the emission remains visible even after 10 min. To translate this mechanism into a practical device, a multilayer wearable LED was constructed by integrating the CNDs into a multilayer excitation-emission architecture (Fig. 4b). The fabricated luminescent device consists of excitation, luminescent, supporting, and heat-dissipating layers, this architecture enables efficient excitation while maximizing afterglow output and device stability.

The practical on-body operation of the device is illustrated in Fig. 4c. Under electrical excitation, bright red emission is observed, while upon switching off the power supply, the device seamlessly transitions into a passive afterglow mode (Fig. 4d), sustaining phosphorescence without any external energy input. This source-free LEDs are particularly suited for extreme or hazardous environments, such as high temperature zones, radiation-exposed areas, underground rescue sites, or oxygen-limited spaces, where electronic components and power systems may fail rapidly63. The persistent phosphorescence of the CNDs ensures that visual indicators remain operational even after power interruption, providing continuous location marking and safety signaling throughout the remaining working period. Figure 4e reveals sustained phosphorescence emission of the LEDs over thousands of seconds, with the decay behavior confirming the ultralong lifetime of the afterglow (Fig. 4f). In Fig. 4g, although the spectral peak shifts slightly with time, the overall emission band remains stable and the intensity decreases slowly, ensuring the stability and reliability of the devices, with detectable signals still present at 45 minutes. In addition, spatially resolved afterglow patterns (Fig. 4h) demonstrate that visual information is preserved during decay, supporting applications in passive displays and long-duration visual communication.

Discussion

In summary, we have achieved ultralong red phosphorescence in CNDs by engineering the intrinsic triplet-state relaxation pathways. Guided by ML-assisted optimization, the CNDs exhibit a record phosphorescence lifetime of 353 s with emission persistence exceeding 4000 s, significantly surpassing previously reported phosphorescent nanomaterials. A dual-channel triplet decay mechanism is established, in which a weakly allowed 3(n, π*) transition contributes to early emission, while a strongly spin-forbidden 3(π, π*) transition pathway dominates long-time recombination and governs the ultralong lifetime. Benefiting from the exceptional phosphorescence performance, passive LED indicators are demonstrated for visual signaling after power-off in time-limited extreme environments. This work provides a new strategy for triplet-exciton management in carbon-based phosphors and advances their applications in low-energy optoelectronics and persistent photonic systems.

Methods

The precursors used in this study include Urea (H2NCONH2, purity > 99%), tricyclohexylphosphonium bromide (CyPh3PBr, purity > 97%), and Rhodamine B (C28H31ClN2O3, purity > 95%). The former two chemicals were obtained from Aladdin Chemistry Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China), while Rhodamine B was purchased from Tianjin Guangfu Fine Chemical Research Institute. Note that all the chemicals used in this work were analytical grade without further purification.

Preparation of CND database

First, CO(NH2)2, CyPh3PBr bromide, and Rhodamine B were accurately weighed according to different preset mass ratio combinations, then transferred into an agate millstone and ground thoroughly at room temperature for 30 min until a homogeneous fine powder was formed. Mixed at different ratios and ground for 30 min, followed by reaction at 130–200 °C for 4–13 h. Finally, 241 CND samples were prepared in the laboratory to form a database for ML. Subsequently, the ground mixture was transferred into a clean, dry stainless steel reaction kettle with a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) liner. The sealed kettle was placed in a programmable constant-temperature drying oven for thermal reaction at 130–200 °C for 4–13 h. After the reaction, the kettle was naturally cooled to room temperature. The product was collected, obtaining pure CND samples. A total of 241 CND samples with different synthesis parameters (reagent ratios, reaction temperatures and times) were prepared in the laboratory. These samples were further characterized to obtain comprehensive performance data, forming a complete database for subsequent machine learning (ML) research.

Preparation of Minute-Scale Red Phosphorescent CNDs

Firstly, 6.75 g of CO(NH2)2, 6.75 g of CyPh3PBr bromide, and 0.01 g of rhodamine B were weighed separately. The solid mixture was placed in an agate mortar and ground thoroughly at room temperature for 30 min to obtain a uniform fine powder. Subsequently, the ground mixture was transferred into a clean and dry PTFE liner, which was then sealed and placed into a stainless steel autoclave. The sealed autoclave was heated in a forced-air drying oven at 185 °C for 12 h. After the reaction was completed, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The product was collected and purified to obtain CNDs sample.

Training of Machine Learning (ML) models

Six different types of ML models (Naive Bayes (NB), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Random Forest (RF), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were trained to predict the lifetime categories of CNDs. All models were constructed directly in Jupyter Notebook using the Scikit-Learn package, an open-source library. The models were trained on 80% of the initial dataset and tested on the remaining 20%. Hyperparameter optimization was performed using 5-fold cross-validation, and model performance was evaluated based on accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The XGBoost model with the best performance was selected for further research.

Structural characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were acquired on a Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN microscope operated at 200 kV to examine the morphology and microstructure of the CNDs. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were collected using a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to analyze the elemental composition and chemical states of the CNDs.

Optical characterization

Fluorescence and phosphorescence spectra and phosphorescence lifetime decay profiles were obtained using a Hitachi F-7000 spectrophotometer. Photoluminescence quantum efficiency and low-temperature spectra were measured with an Edinburgh FLS1000 fluorescence spectrophotometer. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Hitachi UH 4150 spectrophotometer. Time-dependent emission spectra of the wearable LED indicator were measured with a QE Pro spectrometer (Ocean Insight).
