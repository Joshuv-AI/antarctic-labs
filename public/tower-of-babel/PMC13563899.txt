Measles vaccination status and evidence of immunity among medical students in Catalonia (Spain), 2023–2025: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Angela Domínguez, Núria Soldevila, Diana Toledo, Irma Casas, Marta Aldea, Andreu Prat, Rosa Bartolomé, Eva Borràs, Pere Godoy, Working Group on Vaccine Preventable Diseases in Medical Students
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasles is a highly contagious infectious disease. The importance of vaccinating healthcare workers (HCW) and medical students as future HCW is underlined by the numerous measles outbreaks in health institutions. We aimed to assess the factors associated with complete vaccination, and measles serological status in relation to vaccination history among medical students in Catalonia (Spain).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among medical students attending four university teaching-units during the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 academic years. Medical students completed a questionnaire and provided blood samples for measles IgG testing. Associations between positive serology, vaccination status, and independent variables (age, sex, country of birth, degree year, future speciality, vaccination attitudes, and measles vaccination history) were assessed using the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsOf the 255 participants, 79.2% were female, 92.5% were aged ≤ 24 years and 94.9% had received a complete schedule of two doses of measles vaccine. A complete vaccination schedule was associated with being a 5th-year student (aOR 5.26; 95% CI 1.43–20.48). Serological results were positive in 200 students (78.7%), and associated with none of the independent variables. Positive serology was observed in 79.3% and 58.3% of Spanish-born and non-Spanish-born students, respectively (aOR 0.38; 95%CI 0.12–1.27), but the difference was not statistically significant.ConclusionsA high proportion of medical students in our sample had received two doses of measles vaccine. In line with public health recommendations, medical students as future HCW should be required to provide documented proof of two doses of measles-containing vaccine prior to starting clinical training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09779-3.Keywords: Measles, Vaccination, Seroprevalence status, Medical students

Introduction

Measles virus infection is a highly contagious infectious disease [1]. Precise data on the global disease burden prior to the introduction of measles vaccine are limited, but it has been estimated that the disease causes over two million deaths each year [2]. In 2024 worldwide, 476,412 measles cases were reported, with the European region recording the highest number of measles cases in 25 years [3], accounting for 31.7% of the total number of cases worldwide [4].

A review of observational studies reports median vaccine effectiveness of 92.5% and 94.1% for one and two doses, respectively [5]. Measles vaccination has been estimated to have prevented 58.7 million deaths globally between 2000 and 2024 [6]. Vaccination is not only extremely effective in preventing measles, it is also among the most cost-effective public health interventions [7].

In Catalonia (Spain) the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine at the age of 12 months was included in the recommended immunization schedule in 1980, and the two-dose schedule at 15 months and 11 years, was introduced in 1988. Since 2008 the first dose is administered at 12 months and the second dose at 3–4 years. On this basis, it can be assumed that all persons born in 1988 (aged 35 years in 2023) and subsequently should have received two doses of the measles vaccine.

The COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a marked reduction in reported cases of measles, likely due to the implementation of non-pharmaceutical measures aimed at controlling SARS-CoV-2 transmission; however, vaccination coverage also declined [6]. Following the COVID-19 lockdown period, coverage for the second MMR vaccine dose dropped by 2.2% in Catalonia [8]. By 2024, MMR vaccination coverage in Catalonia reached 96.7% for one dose and 94.6% for two doses [9].

In the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, measles cases and outbreaks have increased in several European countries [10, 11]. In Spain, during the period 2014–2020, a total of 84 cases were reported among workers in healthcare settings, representing 9.7% of all reported cases, with health science students accounting for 15.5% of those cases [12]. In 2024, 40.0% of the 30 reported outbreaks occurred in healthcare settings, accounting for 83.2% of cases [13].

Healthcare workers (HCW) are at a 2–19-fold greater risk of measles infection than the general population [14], implying a risk for both HCW and patients. HCW born after the measles virus was circulating widely —until 1978 in Spain [15]— may not have acquired natural immunity and so may be susceptible to developing measles when exposed to the virus. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that all HCW who expect to be in contact with patients should provide evidence of immunity on signing an employment contract or entering a training programme [16]. Recommended in Spain is that persons who start their activity in healthcare settings, including health science students, demonstrate that they have received two MMR vaccine doses [17]. Medical students transitioning to clinical settings are a key target for preventive strategies, as they are exposed to numerous biological hazards, including airborne containing infectious agents such as the measles virus [18, 19]. Therefore, ensuring medical student immunity is crucial for both personal safety and infection control strategies [14].

This study among medical students from four university teaching units in Catalonia (Spain) aimed to assess the factors associated with complete measles vaccination and measles serological status.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional epidemiological study was conducted to assess measles antibody seroprevalence and vaccination status among medical students at four university teaching units in Catalonia (Spain) during the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 academic years.

Participants were defined as 3rd to 6th year medical students receiving clinical training at any of the participating university teaching units (Hospital Clínic de Barcelona-Universitat de Barcelona, HC-UB; Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron-Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona, HVdH-UAB; Hospital Universitari Germans Trias i Pujol-Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona, HGTP-UAB; and, Hospital Universitari Arnau de Vilanova- Universitat de Lleida, HAV-UdL). Students who did not provide written informed consent or who did not complete the questionnaire and provide a blood sample were excluded from the study.

Sample size

This study was part of a broader seroepidemiological project designed to evaluate immunity against several vaccine-preventable diseases among medical students. To ensure adequate accuracy for all the evaluated diseases, the sample size was estimated considering the disease with the most unfavourable expected scenario in terms of seroprevalence (mumps).

The minimum sample size calculated considering that the number of medical students from 3rd to 5th academic years rotating through each participating teaching unit (ranging between 180 and 250 students), a prevalence of IgG against mumps of 76% [20] (worst-case scenario), a precision of 0.05, and a design effect of 1.5, was 329 students.

For measles, considering 94% IgG prevalence [21], the minimum sample size required was estimated at 120 students.

Recruitment and data collection

Students were invited to participate during plenary sessions in which the study objectives, confidentiality measures, and informed consent procedures were clearly explained. Particular emphasis was placed on the need to furnish official vaccination records prior to completing a questionnaire and the need for a blood sample, and also on providing assurances regarding anonymity and data protection.

Students who agreed to participate and provided written informed consent were scheduled by the coordinator of their respective university teaching unit for blood sample collection and questionnaire completion.

Blood samples were collected over two academic periods, from November 2023 to June 2024 and from October 2024 to June 2025. Measles-specific IgG antibodies were quantified using chemiluminescent immunoassays (CLIA) on two analytical platforms: Liaison® XL (Diasorin, Italy), 3 hospitals, and VIRCLIA® platform (Vircell, Spain), 1 hospital. HVdH-UAB, HGTP-UAB, and HAV-UdL determined IgG using Lisaion® XL and results were considered positive for > 16.5 UA/mL, negative for < 13.5 UA/mL, and indeterminate for 13.5–16.5 UA/mL. HC-UB determined IgG using VIRCLIA®, with antibody index results (according to the manufacturers’ instructions) considered reactive for > 1.10, non-reactive for < 0.90, and indeterminate for 0.90- < 1.10 units. Sensitivity and specificity values were 97% and 95% [22], respectively, for Liaison XL and 98% and 100% [23], respectively, for VIRCLIA. Students with an indeterminate result were reanalysed and reclassified according their new status, and those who maintained the indeterminate status were classified as negative [24].

Medical students completed a questionnaire from the European Joint Action on Vaccination (EU-JAV) student survey [25] adapted to reflect the Catalan recommended vaccination schedule and the official immunization calendar of the Interterritorial Council of the Spanish Health System.

Questionnaire clarity and readability were assessed in a pilot test with 10 medical students and HCW. (Supplementary material).

The questionnaire collected information on age, sex, country of birth, intended medical specialty (medical, surgical, other) and measles vaccination history, including type of measles-containing vaccine (MMR or monovalent vaccine), number of doses received, and dates of administration. Attitudes toward vaccination were also assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (strongly agree, tend to agree, do not know, tend to disagree, and strongly disagree), and responses were subsequently recategorized into three groups: “agree” (strongly agree, tend to agree,), “do not know” and “disagree” (tend to disagree, and strongly disagree).

Information on vaccination status was self-reported based on the student’s vaccination record available in La Meva Salut (the official digital health platform and mobile application of the Catalan Department of Health) or was recorded in an official document describing administered vaccines and the corresponding dates.

Vaccination status was categorized as incomplete vaccination, defined as receipt of a single dose, regardless of the vaccine type (MMR or monovalent) or complete vaccination, defined as receipt of two doses of a measles-containing vaccine, regardless of the vaccine type, provided that a minimum interval of 4 weeks between doses was respected [17].

Statistical analysis

Associations between the dependent variables (positive serology and vaccination status) and independent variables (age, sex, country of birth, degree year, future speciality, measles vaccination history, and attitudes to vaccination) were assessed using the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Firth penalized logistic regression was applied to minimize small sample bias due to the small number of outcome events and sparse data in some cells for several covariates. A multivariate analysis to calculate adjusted OR (aOR) values was performed using Firth penalized logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and degree year.

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS® (v.30) and R (v.4.5.1) software’s.

Results

Of the 1,134 medical students invited to participate, 255 were enrolled in the study, corresponding to a response proportion of 22.5% (Fig. 1). Most students were female (202, 79.2%) and aged ≤ 24 years (236, 92.5%), and over half were in 5th-year medical students (153, 60.0%). Most students (224, 94.9%) had received the complete schedule of two doses of measles-containing vaccine (complete vaccination), while 12 (5.1%) had received only one dose of the MMR vaccine (incomplete vaccination). Four students had received a monovalent measles vaccine in combination with MMR vaccine: two students had received one dose of the monovalent vaccine plus two doses of MMR vaccine administered six months apart, one student had received one dose of MMR vaccine followed by one dose of the monovalent vaccine, with an interval of 3.5 years between doses, and another student had received two doses of MMR vaccine plus one dose of the monovalent vaccine, with a 15-year interval between doses.

Discussion

The present study shows that the proportion of students with two doses of measles vaccine was 94.9%. This proportion is very close to the 94.8% reported for healthcare students in a 2019 French study [26]. In contrast, only 59.5% of medical students in an Italian university were found to be fully vaccinated against measles in 2022 [27]. These findings confirm that vaccination coverage in our setting is high and consistent with measles elimination targets.

We have observed that 5th year students were more likely than 4th year students to have completed the measles vaccination schedule, contrasting with results by Baldolli et al. [26], who reported no significant differences between medical students in vaccination coverage by degree year. Kawahara et al. [28] found, for a study of influenza conducted in Japan, that freshmen students were more frequently vaccinated than students in other academic years, possibly explained by the fact that Japanese high school students are explicitly advised to receive the influenza vaccination.

Despite the high vaccination coverage, only 78.7% of our students showed serological evidence of immunity against measles. This discrepancy between documented vaccination and seropositivity is consistent with previous reports regarding highly vaccinated populations [27, 29]. In a 2018 Slovakian serosurvey, Zibolenová et al. [30] found that, after two doses, the seronegative proportion was 5.5% and also that the immunity waning rate was lower than after the first dose, pointing out that IgG antibody negativity does not necessarily mean susceptibility. Enabling a rapid and effective response on exposure is the fact that immunological memory may persist [16], even though vaccine-induced antibody concentrations decrease over time to become undetectable [16, 24]. Waning immunity, therefore, does not appear to play a major role in measles virus transmission [31].

The WHO considers documentation of two doses of measles-containing vaccine as sufficient evidence of immunity, regardless of serology results, and, likewise, the CDC (16, 32). To avoid misclassification of protected individuals and unnecessary interventions, routine post-vaccination serology testing is not recommended in individuals with documented two-dose vaccination. The absence of serological evidence of immunity observed in the present study likely reflects a combination of waning humoral immunity, assay limitations, and the absence of natural boosting, rather than true vaccine failure.

No statistically significant differences were observed according to country of birth. While the point estimate suggested lower seropositivity among non-Spanish-born medical students, this estimate was imprecise due to the small number of foreign-born students. Coppeta et al. [23] reported a positive serology result for 67.8% of Italian medical students compared to 62.6% of medical students from other countries. Ferrari et al. [29] found that, while 64.2% of all medical students had a protective level of IgG antibodies against measles, the proportion was substantially higher for students from European Union/European Economic Area countries (70.5%) than for students from Eastern European countries (34.6%).

The gender distribution of medical students (79.2% female) was similar to that of the participating university teaching units, where women accounted for 71.3% of medical students. Serology in female students was more frequently positive than in male students (80.6% and 71.7%, respectively), although the differences was not statistically significant. Trevisan et al. [19] reported that female students attending an Italian university were more responsive to measles vaccination than males, while Coppeta et al. [23] reported that female students were more frequently immune than male students. In contrast, Santoro et al. [18] in a study of a large population of healthcare students in Italy, and Kia et al. [32] in a study conducted in Iran, observed no difference in antibodies against measles by sex or according to whether the number of vaccine doses administered.

We found no differences in serological protection according to age, unlike Coppeta et al. [25], who reported that students aged < 24 years were more frequently immune than older students, and unlike Santoro et al. [18], who took as reference persons born between 1966 and 1997, when the virus was circulating widely in Italy. A possible explanation for this lack of difference by age might be that, in Catalonia, the oldest students were born in 1993 and so are very likely to have received the two-dose schedule which was introduced for ages 15 months and 11 years. Kim et al. [33], in a study of HCW seroprevalence against measles in 2 hospitals in Seoul, found no differences by age, sex, or time since the second dose, but did observe differences in HCW born after 1985, in both the number of previous vaccinations and the time elapsed since the last vaccination. Several authors report that measles in persons with secondary vaccination failure might be less infectious than in unvaccinated persons [34, 35]. This would suggest that, in outbreak situations, public health management of unvaccinated persons could be prioritized over persons with secondary failure.

Although current international recommendations do not support routine booster vaccination in individuals who have received two documented doses [16, 17, 36], the potential need for a third dose of measles-containing vaccine has been explored, particularly to address waning immunity, among young adults and HCW in high-risk settings [37–39].Other authors indicate that although booster doses may increase antibody titres in the short term, these effects are not sustained over time and do not significantly enhance long-term cellular immunity [40, 41].

Recent measles resurgence in the USA and several European countries has been attributed to decades of false claims of adverse events (including a misleading association with autism), vaccine complacency, hesitancy, and declining childhood vaccination coverage [42, 43].

To interrupt measles transmission a very high level of population immunity is required and management of cases and their contacts is complex because measles infectivity before rash onset. These aspects reinforce the need for complete vaccinated and highly skilled HCW capable of adopting appropriate preventive measures against measles in their professional practice [44].

A limitation of the present study is the potential for selection bias due to the voluntary nature of participation. Although anonymity and confidentiality were assured to encourage participation, the fact that respondents may differ from non-respondents, may undetermined the representativeness of our sample, as students with more positive attitudes toward vaccination may have been more likely to participate. Another limitation is that responses to certain survey items (especially those related to vaccine safety and effectiveness) may have been influenced by social desirability bias.

Additionally, the use of two different commercial serological assays might have introduced some variability. This has been reported for samples with antibody levels close to the cut-off point, as previous studies have demonstrated that inter-assay discrepancies are more frequent in borderline cases, while agreement is high for clearly positive or negative results [45, 46]. In line with other authors [15, 24], we re-analysed indeterminate results and, if persistent, classified them as negative. While this may have slightly influenced seroprevalence estimates, it is unlikely to have substantially altered our main findings. Importantly however, seronegative should be interpreted cautiously and not equated with lack of immunity, as individuals lacking detectable measles IgG may still retain cellular immune responses that provide protection or attenuate disease severity. As for strengths, the study was based on an anonymous and confidential questionnaire, measles vaccination history was confirmed by health documentation to avoid recall error, and opinions on vaccine safety and effectiveness were collected before serology results were known.

In conclusion, our results show that a high proportion of medical students at four university teaching units in Catalonia have received two doses of measles-containing vaccine. However, the fact that a non-negligible proportion shows no serological evidence of immunity, highlights the complex relationship between vaccination, antibody persistence, and immune protection. Our findings should not be interpreted as indicative of widespread susceptibility but rather reinforce the need for careful interpretation of serological data.

The infection risk posed by inadequately immunized students during their future professional activities heightens the risk of nosocomial transmission. In Spain, documented evidence of a complete measles vaccination schedule is not currently mandatory for medical students. In line with public health recommendations, vaccination requirements and preventive policies applicable to HCW should, therefore, be extended to medical students as future HCW, being required to provide documented proof of two doses of measles‑containing vaccine prior to starting clinical medical training.
