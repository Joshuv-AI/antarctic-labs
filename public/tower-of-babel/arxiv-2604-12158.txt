Reinforcement Learning, Optimal Control, and Bayesian Filtering in Data Assimilation
Abed Hammoud

Abstract
We give a finite-horizon variational formulation that places Bayesian filtering and smoothing, variational data assimilation, KL-regularized control, and Kalman-type methods inside one mathematically explicit hierarchy. For a discrete-time hidden Markov model and any admissible one-step candidate law q t q_{t} , we prove 𝒥 t ( q t ) = 𝔼 q t [ − log p ( y t ∣ X t ) ] + KL ( q t ∥ p t f ) = KL ( q t ∥ p t a ) − log p ( y t ∣ y 0 : t − 1 ) \mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[-\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right]+\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{f})=\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{a})-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}) , and, for any admissible path law q q , 𝒥 path ( q ) = 𝔼 q [ − ∑ t = 0 T log p ( y t ∣ X t ) ] + KL ( q ∥ p ( x 0 : T ) ) = KL ( q ∥ p ( x 0 : T ∣ y 0 : T ) ) − log p ( y 0 : T ) \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right]+\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T}))=\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}))-\log p(y_{0:T}) . These identities determine the evidence as the global infimum and make the analysis and smoothing posteriors the unique minimizers whenever those posterior laws belong to the admissible classes. This separates targets that are often conflated: strong- and weak-constraint 4D-Var are MAP estimators under the stated Gaussian assumptions; KL-regularized control recovers the Bayesian posterior only when the passive dynamics, likelihood cost, temperature, and a restrictive representability condition on the policy class are all matched correctly; and the linear-Gaussian specialization yields the Kalman analysis exactly. The ensemble Kalman filter then appears as a Gaussian and finite-ensemble approximation to the forecast-to-analysis map, exact only in the linear-Gaussian infinite-ensemble limit. This framework also clarifies RMSE-based RL data assimilation: such rewards may define effective estimators or pseudo-posteriors, but not exact posterior recovery unless they realize the likelihood-plus-KL objective.

Introduction

Data assimilation combines a dynamical model with partial and noisy observations in order to estimate the state of a system. In geophysical applications the problem is sequential, high dimensional, and often nonlinear. Three mathematical languages dominate the subject. The first is
Bayesian filtering and smoothing
, where the target is a posterior law conditioned on data. The second is
variational data assimilation
, where one minimizes a deterministic mismatch functional over states or trajectories. The third is the
Kalman and ensemble Kalman
language, where one propagates means, covariances, and their ensemble approximations through forecast and analysis updates. These languages are closely related, but they are not interchangeable, and reinforcement-learning terminology can make the distinctions even harder to state precisely.

Historically, variational data assimilation emerged from optimal-control ideas and adjoint equations in meteorology

Our goal is conceptual rather than algorithmic. We do not propose a new approximate filter, prove finite-sample optimality of EnKF-type methods, or claim posterior recovery for generic RL rewards. Instead, we give a mathematically explicit synthesis of the exact finite-horizon statements that survive across these literatures and state explicitly where the usual slogans stop being theorems.

The backbone of the paper is the pair of identities proved in

These identities identify the evidence as the variational infimum and make the posterior the unique minimizer whenever the posterior itself belongs to the admissible family. Restricting that family then recovers familiar approximations: MAP in the point-mass or zero-variance limit, Gaussian variational approximations in Gaussian families, and ensemble approximations when the posterior update is replaced by sample-based Gaussian surrogates. Two distinctions will be used repeatedly.

1.

The
analysis posterior

p_{t}^{a}
is a single-time filtering law. The

p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})
is a law on full trajectories.

2.

A

and a

are point summaries of a posterior law. They need not coincide, and neither summary determines the full posterior distribution in general.

3.

A

is the minimizer of a restricted optimization over Gaussian laws, whereas a

is a random empirical measure built from finitely many particles. Neither object should be called the posterior unless a separate theorem identifies it with the posterior law.

We now turn to variational data assimilation, which fits into this notation as an optimization of a negative log-posterior density under additional structural assumptions.

3
Variational data assimilation and 4D-Var as MAP

Variational data assimilation is often presented as a deterministic optimization method. Under the Gaussian background, model-error, and observation-error assumptions stated below, however, its objective is exactly a negative log-posterior
[
10
,
24
]
. That is the precise sense in which 4D-Var is Bayesian. The resulting minimizer is a MAP estimator, not in general a posterior mean and not the posterior law itself. Outside these assumptions, the same deterministic objective may remain useful, but the MAP interpretation no longer follows automatically.

This section also marks the main exception to the density-based setup from
section

2
. In strong-constraint 4D-Var, the deterministic dynamics place the trajectory law on the model manifold generated by the initial condition, so the optimization is over
x_{0}
or over trajectories constrained to that manifold rather than over Lebesgue densities on the full path space.

3.1
Strong-constraint 4D-Var

Assume that the model is perfect inside the assimilation window:

x_{t+1}=\mathcal{M}_{t}(x_{t}),\qquad t=0,\dots,T-1,

(3.1)

so the full trajectory is determined by the initial state
x_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{0:t}
denote the model composition mapping
x_{0}
to
x_{t}
. Assume further that

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{b},B),

(3.2)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{t}(X_{t})+\eta_{t},\qquad\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_{t}),\qquad t=0,\dots,T,

(3.3)

with
B
and each
R_{t}
symmetric positive definite, and with independent observation errors.

Theorem 3.1

(Strong-constraint 4D-Var objective as negative log-posterior)
.

Under
equations

3.1
,
3.2
, and
3.3
, the posterior density of
x_{0}
given
y_{0:T}
satisfies

p(x_{0}\mid y_{0:T})\propto\exp\left(-J_{\mathrm{sc}}(x_{0})\right),

(3.4)

where

J_{\mathrm{sc}}(x_{0}):=\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{\mathsf{T}}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})\;+\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(y_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}(\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0}))\right)^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}\left(y_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}(\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0}))\right),

(3.5)

Consequently, any minimizer of
J_{\mathrm{sc}}
is a MAP estimator of the initial condition, and the corresponding trajectory
\left\{\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0})\right\}_{t=0}^{T}
is the associated strong-constraint MAP trajectory on the deterministic model manifold.

Proof.

Because the model is deterministic, the likelihood of the observed sequence conditioned on
x_{0}
factorizes as

p(y_{0:T}\mid x_{0})=\prod_{t=0}^{T}p\left(y_{t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0})\right).

Under Gaussian observation errors,

p\left(y_{t}\mid\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0})\right)\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(y_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}(\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0}))\right)^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}\left(y_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}(\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0}))\right)\right).

The Gaussian background density satisfies

p(x_{0})\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{\mathsf{T}}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})\right).

Multiplying prior and likelihood and applying Bayes’ rule yields the claimed posterior density with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since the posterior density is proportional to
\exp(-J_{\mathrm{sc}}(x_{0}))
, maximizing the posterior over the support of that density is equivalent to minimizing
J_{\mathrm{sc}}
. The associated trajectory statement is the induced MAP statement on the deterministic model manifold
\left\{(\mathcal{M}_{0:t}(x_{0}))_{t=0}^{T}:x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}
.
∎

Remark 3.2

(When the minimizer is a MAP estimator)
.

Theorem

3.1
is an exact MAP statement provided the posterior admits the displayed density on the admissible state space and the optimizer is sought over that support. If the posterior density has several maximizers, the MAP estimator is nonunique. If the posterior is multimodal or strongly skewed, the MAP point can be far from the posterior mean.

Proposition 3.3

(Weak-constraint 4D-Var objective as negative log-posterior)
.

Under the weak-constraint model above, the trajectory posterior satisfies

p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\propto\exp\left(-J_{\mathrm{wc}}(x_{0:T})\right),

(3.9)

where

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{wc}}(x_{0:T}):={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(x_{0}-x_{b})^{\mathsf{T}}B^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{b})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(x_{t+1}-\mathcal{M}_{t}(x_{t})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{t}^{-1}\left(x_{t+1}-\mathcal{M}_{t}(x_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left(y_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}(x_{t})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}\left(y_{t}-\mathcal{H}_{t}(x_{t})\right),

The trajectory prior density with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n(T+1)}

p(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0})\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t}),

\displaystyle p(x_{0})

3.3
MAP, posterior mean, and posterior law

Let
\pi
denote any posterior density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then:

1.

A
MAP estimator
is any maximizer of
\pi(x)
.

2.

The
posterior mean
is
\int x\,\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, when the integral exists.

3.

The
posterior law
is the full measure with density
\pi
.

Only in special cases, such as symmetric unimodal Gaussian posteriors, do these three objects align in an obvious way. In nonlinear or non-Gaussian data assimilation they typically do not. The difference is essential for later claims about reinforcement learning: a reward that targets posterior means or trajectory modes is not automatically a reward that targets the posterior law.

4
Posterior-as-variational identities: one-step filtering and path-space forms

We now state the central variational identities. They are direct consequences of Bayes’ rule, but they deserve to be singled out because they put posterior inference, MAP estimation, Gaussian variational approximation, and KL-regularized control under the same umbrella. The key point for the sequel is that the identity itself is unconditional on the admissible family beyond the stated integrability assumptions, whereas the corresponding minimizer statement needs the target posterior law to belong to that family.

The exact infimum statements below all use the same admissible-truncation device.

Lemma 4.1

(Admissible truncations recover the Gibbs infimum)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on a measurable space, let
f\geq 0
be measurable with

0<Z:=\int f\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty,

and define

\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x):=\frac{f(x)\,\mu(\,\mathrm{d}x)}{Z}.

Let
g\geq 0
be measurable and finite
\nu
-almost surely, and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

A_{n}:=\left\{x:\ e^{-n}\leq f(x)\leq e^{n},\ g(x)\leq n\right\},\qquad\nu_{n}(\,\mathrm{d}x):=\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid A_{n}).

Then:

i.

\nu(A_{n})\to 1
and hence
\nu_{n}
is well defined for all sufficiently large
n
;

ii.

\nu_{n}\ll\mu
;

iii.

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n}}\!\left[\left\lvert\log f\right\rvert\right]\leq n
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n}}[g]\leq n
;

iv.

\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|\mu)<\infty
;

v.

\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|\nu)=-\log\nu(A_{n})\to 0
.

Consequently, for any admissible class
\mathcal{A}
that contains all sufficiently large
\nu_{n}
and on which

J(q):=\mathbb{E}_{q}[-\log f]+\operatorname{KL}(q\|\mu)

is well defined, one has

\inf_{q\in\mathcal{A}}J(q)=-\log Z.

Proof.

Because
\nu
has density
f/Z
with respect to
\mu
, it is supported on the set where
0<f<\infty
. Since
g<\infty

\nu
-almost surely, the sets
A_{n}
increase to a
\nu
-full set. This proves
\nu(A_{n})\to 1
.

By construction,
\nu_{n}\ll\nu\ll\mu
, so (ii) holds. On
A_{n}
one has
\left\lvert\log f\right\rvert\leq n
and
g\leq n
, so (iii) follows immediately. Moreover,

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}(x)=\frac{f(x)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}(x)}{Z\,\nu(A_{n})},

hence

\log\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}=\log f-\log Z-\log\nu(A_{n})\qquad\nu_{n}\text{-a.s.}

The right-hand side is
\nu_{n}
-integrable because
\left\lvert\log f\right\rvert\leq n
on
A_{n}
, so (iv) follows. Likewise,

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}}{\,\mathrm{d}\nu}=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}}{\nu(A_{n})},

which gives

\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|\nu)=\int\log\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}}{\,\mathrm{d}\nu}\,\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}=-\log\nu(A_{n})\to 0.

This proves (v).

For any admissible
q\in\mathcal{A}
, the Gibbs identity gives

J(q)=\operatorname{KL}(q\|\nu)-\log Z\geq-\log Z.

Applying that same identity to
\nu_{n}
yields

J(\nu_{n})=\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|\nu)-\log Z=-\log\nu(A_{n})-\log Z\to-\log Z,

so the lower bound is sharp and therefore
\inf_{q\in\mathcal{A}}J(q)=-\log Z
.
∎

4.1
One-step filtering identity

Fix
t\in\left\{0,\dots,T\right\}
and the realized observation
y_{t}
. Let
p_{t}^{f}
be the forecast density and define the analysis density

p_{t}^{a}(x_{t}):=\frac{p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})p_{t}^{f}(x_{t})}{p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1})}.

(4.1)

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{t}
be the collection of densities
q_{t}
such that

i.

q_{t}\ll p_{t}^{f}
,

ii.

\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right\rvert\right]<\infty
,

iii.

\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{f})<\infty
.

For
q_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, define

\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t}):=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[-\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right]\;+\;\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{f}).

(4.2)

Theorem 4.2

(One-step posterior-as-variational identity)
.

Under
2.1
, for every
q_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
,

\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})=\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{a})-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}).

(4.3)

\inf_{q_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}}\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})=-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}).

(4.4)

If, in addition,
p_{t}^{a}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, then
\mathcal{J}_{t}
has the unique minimizer

q_{t}^{\star}=p_{t}^{a}.

(4.5)

Proof.

Because
0<Z_{t}<\infty
by
2.1
and
\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right\rvert
is
q_{t}
-integrable, one has
p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\in(0,\infty)
for
q_{t}
-almost every
X_{t}
. Since
q_{t}\ll p_{t}^{f}
, it follows from
equation

4.1
that
q_{t}\ll p_{t}^{a}
, so every logarithm below is well defined
q_{t}
-almost surely.
By
equation

4.1
,

\log p_{t}^{a}(x_{t})=\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})+\log p_{t}^{f}(x_{t})-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}).

Rearranging,

-\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})+\log\frac{q_{t}(x_{t})}{p_{t}^{f}(x_{t})}=\log\frac{q_{t}(x_{t})}{p_{t}^{a}(x_{t})}-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}).

Integrating with respect to
q_{t}
gives

\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[-\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right]\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\log\frac{q_{t}(X_{t})}{p_{t}^{f}(X_{t})}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\log\frac{q_{t}(X_{t})}{p_{t}^{a}(X_{t})}\right]-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}),

which is precisely
equation

4.3
. Nonnegativity of KL divergence yields

\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})\geq-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}),

so it remains to prove that this lower bound is sharp. Apply
lemma

4.1
with

\mu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{t})=p_{t}^{f}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x_{t},\qquad f(x_{t})=p(y_{t}\mid x_{t}),\qquad g(x_{t})=\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})\right\rvert.

Then
\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{t})=p_{t}^{a}(x_{t})\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}
, and the truncated posteriors
\nu_{n}
from the lemma belong to
\mathcal{Q}_{t}
. Using
equation

4.3
,

\mathcal{J}_{t}(\nu_{n})=\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|p_{t}^{a})-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1})=-\log\nu(A_{n})-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}),

which converges to
-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1})
. This proves
equation

4.4
. If
p_{t}^{a}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, then evaluating
equation

4.3
at
q_{t}=p_{t}^{a}
shows that the lower bound is attained there. Uniqueness follows because
\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{a})=0
if and only if
q_{t}=p_{t}^{a}
almost everywhere.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Posterior admissibility is an extra hypothesis)
.

The normalization condition
0<Z_{t}<\infty
ensures that
p_{t}^{a}
is a well-defined posterior density, but it does not by itself imply
p_{t}^{a}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
. The additional membership requires the posterior expectation of
\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right\rvert
and the relative entropy
\operatorname{KL}(p_{t}^{a}\|p_{t}^{f})
to be finite. We therefore separate the exact identity from the minimizer statement.

Corollary 4.4

(Restricted variational families)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{t}
be any nonempty admissible family. Then

\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{a}).

(4.6)

Thus the two restricted minimization problems are equivalent. In particular, any minimizer of
equation

4.2
over
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is a reverse-KL projection of the analysis posterior onto that family.

Proof.

The additive constant
-\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1})
in
equation

4.3
does not depend on
q_{t}
.
∎

4.2
Path-space identity

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
be the set of densities
q
on
\mathbb{R}^{n(T+1)}
such that

i.

q\ll p(x_{0:T})
,

ii.

\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right\rvert\right]<\infty
,

iii.

\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T}))<\infty
.

For
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
, define

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(q):=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right]\;+\;\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T})).

(4.7)

Theorem 4.5

(Path-space posterior-as-variational identity)
.

Under
2.1
, for every
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
,

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(q)=\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}))-\log p(y_{0:T}).

(4.8)

\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(q)=-\log p(y_{0:T}).

(4.9)

If, in addition,
p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
, then
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}
has the unique minimizer

q^{\star}(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}).

(4.10)

Proof.

Because
0<Z_{0:T}<\infty
by
2.1
and
\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right\rvert
is
q
-integrable, one has
p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\in(0,\infty)
for every
t
,
q
-almost surely. Hence
q\ll p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})
by
equation

2.7
.
By
equation

2.7
,

\log p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})=\log p(x_{0:T})+\sum_{t=0}^{T}\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})-\log p(y_{0:T}).

Therefore

-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})+\log\frac{q(x_{0:T})}{p(x_{0:T})}=\log\frac{q(x_{0:T})}{p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})}-\log p(y_{0:T}).

Integrating with respect to
q
yields
equation

4.8
, and nonnegativity of KL divergence gives the lower bound

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(q)\geq-\log p(y_{0:T}).

To show that this bound is sharp, apply
lemma

4.1
with

\mu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T},\qquad f(x_{0:T})=\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(y_{t}\mid x_{t}),\qquad g(x_{0:T})=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lvert\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})\right\rvert.

Then
\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}
, and the truncated posteriors
\nu_{n}
from the lemma belong to
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
. Using
equation

4.8
,

\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(\nu_{n})=\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}))-\log p(y_{0:T})=-\log\nu(A_{n})-\log p(y_{0:T})\to-\log p(y_{0:T}).

This proves
equation

4.9
. If
p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
, then evaluating
equation

4.8
at that posterior shows that the lower bound is attained there. Since
\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}))=0
if and only if
q=p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})
almost everywhere, the minimizer is unique whenever it exists inside the admissible family.
∎

Remark 4.6

(Path-space posterior admissibility)
.

The same caveat applies on path space: the evidence condition
0<Z_{0:T}<\infty
makes the smoothing posterior well defined, but the minimizer statement additionally needs
p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
.

Corollary 4.7

(Unrestricted, Gaussian, and other restricted families)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
be any nonempty admissible family. Then

\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{path}}(q)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q\in\mathcal{F}}\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})).

(4.11)

In particular:

1.

if
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
and
p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{path}}
, then the unique minimizer is the full smoothing posterior;

2.

if
\mathcal{F}
is a Gaussian family, then any minimizer is a Gaussian variational approximation, equivalently a reverse-KL projection, of the smoothing posterior.

Remark 4.8

(MAP and the “Dirac restriction” heuristic)
.

In a discrete state space, Dirac laws that are supported on prior atoms are admissible, and minimizing
equation

4.7
over Dirac masses literally selects a path maximizing the posterior mass. In continuous state spaces, however, a Dirac law is singular with respect to a smooth prior density, so the KL term in
equation

4.7
is not finite. The mathematically correct continuous-state statement is that MAP is recovered by minimizing the negative log-posterior density directly, as in
section

3
, or as the zero-variance limit of concentrated admissible families. Thus the phrase “restricting
q
to Dirac masses gives MAP” should be read as a shorthand for a limiting point-mass approximation, not as a literal application of
theorem

4.5
with singular measures.

Remark 4.9

(Posterior mean versus variational optimum)
.

The functionals
equations

4.2
and
4.7
optimize over
laws
. Their optimizer is the posterior law, not a point estimator. Posterior means enter only after one chooses a decision rule and a loss function, such as quadratic loss. This distinction will matter again in
sections

6
and
8
.

The rest of the paper keeps four optimization domains separate. First,
theorems

4.2
and
4.5
concern unrestricted optimization over admissible laws. Second, restricting the admissible family produces variational approximations such as Gaussian projections. Third, Section
5
restricts further to
reachable
laws induced by a policy class under fixed passive dynamics, which adds a nontrivial representability constraint. Fourth, Section
7
discusses empirical ensemble approximations, which are random particle or moment surrogates rather than exact optimizers over a law family. Keeping these four levels distinct is what prevents posterior laws, MAP points, Gaussian surrogates, reachable policy laws, and finite ensembles from being conflated.

5
KL-regularized control and control-as-inference

The variational identities of
section

4
can be read as control problems. This section separates two levels that are often merged in the literature: unrestricted optimization over path laws relative to passive dynamics, and optimization over the smaller reachable family induced by a policy class with fixed transition kernels. The distinction is precisely where posterior recovery can fail.

5.1
Posterior as an optimal controlled law

Let
p_{0}(x_{0:T})
be the path density induced by passive dynamics on trajectories, and let
\ell_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable stage costs. Assume the normalizing constant

Z_{\ell}:=\int p_{0}(x_{0:T})\exp\!\left(-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(x_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}

(5.1)

is finite and strictly positive. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
be the set of densities
q
such that

i.

q\ll p_{0}
,

ii.

\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lvert\ell_{t}(X_{t})\right\rvert\right]<\infty
,

iii.

\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_{0})<\infty
.

For any
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, define

\mathcal{J}_{\ell}(q):=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(X_{t})\right]\;+\;\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_{0}).

(5.2)

Theorem 5.1

(Gibbs variational identity)
.

For every
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
,

\mathcal{J}_{\ell}(q)=\operatorname{KL}(q\|q^{\star})-\log Z_{\ell},

(5.3)

where

q^{\star}(x_{0:T}):=\frac{1}{Z_{\ell}}p_{0}(x_{0:T})\exp\!\left(-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(x_{t})\right).

(5.4)

\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}}\mathcal{J}_{\ell}(q)=-\log Z_{\ell}.

(5.5)

If, in addition,
q^{\star}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, then
q^{\star}
is the unique minimizer of
equation

5.2
.

Proof.

Because each
\ell_{t}
is real valued, the density
q^{\star}
from
equation

5.4
is strictly positive wherever
p_{0}
is strictly positive. Hence every
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
q^{\star}
as well.
The density
q^{\star}
defined in
equation

5.4
satisfies

\log q^{\star}(x_{0:T})=\log p_{0}(x_{0:T})-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(x_{t})-\log Z_{\ell}.

Rearranging and integrating against
q
yields
equation

5.3
, and nonnegativity of KL divergence gives the lower bound

\mathcal{J}_{\ell}(q)\geq-\log Z_{\ell}.

To show that this bound is sharp while preserving the admissibility conditions in
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, apply
lemma

4.1
with

\mu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T})=p_{0}(x_{0:T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T},\qquad f(x_{0:T})=\exp\!\left(-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(x_{t})\right),\qquad g(x_{0:T})=\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lvert\ell_{t}(x_{t})\right\rvert.

Then
\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T})=q^{\star}(x_{0:T})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0:T}
, and the truncated Gibbs laws
\nu_{n}
from the lemma belong to
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
. Using
equation

5.3
,

\mathcal{J}_{\ell}(\nu_{n})=\operatorname{KL}(\nu_{n}\|q^{\star})-\log Z_{\ell}=-\log\nu(A_{n})-\log Z_{\ell}\to-\log Z_{\ell}.

This proves
equation

5.5
. If
q^{\star}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, then evaluating
equation

5.3
at
q=q^{\star}
shows that the lower bound is attained there, and uniqueness follows from strict positivity of KL divergence away from the reference law.
∎

Remark 5.2

(Gibbs admissibility is not automatic)
.

The condition
0<Z_{\ell}<\infty
guarantees that
equation

5.4
defines a probability density, but it does not by itself imply
q^{\star}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
. The minimizer statement therefore needs the additional integrability and finite-KL membership of the Gibbs law itself.

Corollary 5.3

(Bayesian posterior as the Gibbs law)
.

If
p_{0}(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T})
is the prior path density from
equation

2.6
and

\ell_{t}(x_{t})=-\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t}),

(5.6)

then
equation

5.4
becomes

q^{\star}(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}).

(5.7)

Consequently,

\inf_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}}\mathcal{J}_{\ell}(q)=-\log p(y_{0:T}),

and if
p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}
, then Bayesian smoothing is exactly the unique minimizer of
equation

5.2
.

5.2
Policies, passive dynamics, and relative-entropy control

To connect
theorem

5.1
to reinforcement learning, introduce actions
A_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and passive state-action dynamics

p_{0}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})=\rho_{0}(x_{0})\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\mu_{t}(a_{t}\mid x_{t})P_{t}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t},a_{t}),

(5.8)

where
\rho_{0}
is the initial density,
\mu_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
is a passive action kernel, and
P_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t},a_{t})
is the state transition density. A Markov policy
\pi=\left\{\pi_{t}\right\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
with
\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid x)\ll\mu_{t}(\cdot\mid x)
induces the state-action path law

q_{\pi}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})=\rho_{0}(x_{0})\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_{t}(a_{t}\mid x_{t})P_{t}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t},a_{t}).

(5.9)

We write

q_{\pi}^{X}(x_{0:T}):=\int q_{\pi}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})\,\mathrm{d}a_{0:T-1}

(5.10)

for the induced state-path marginal, and similarly
p_{0}^{X}
for the state marginal of the passive state-action law
equation

5.8
.

At this point three optimization domains must be kept distinct. One may optimize over all admissible state-action laws
q\ll p_{0}
on the reference space
equation

5.8
; one may optimize only over the reachable subset
\left\{q_{\pi}:\pi\text{ admissible}\right\}
induced by policies with the fixed transition kernel
P_{t}
; and one may look only at the induced state-path marginals
\left\{q_{\pi}^{X}\right\}
.
Theorem

5.1
applies directly to the first domain, while posterior recovery by policies concerns the second and third domains and therefore needs an additional representability argument.

Lemma 5.4

(Path-space KL decomposition)
.

Assume
q_{\pi}\ll p_{0}
. Then

\operatorname{KL}(q_{\pi}\|p_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\log\frac{\pi_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}{\mu_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}\right]=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\!\left[\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi_{t}(\cdot\mid X_{t})\,\|\,\mu_{t}(\cdot\mid X_{t})\right)\right].

(5.11)

Proof.

Dividing
equation

5.9
by
equation

5.8
yields

\frac{q_{\pi}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})}{p_{0}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})}=\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\pi_{t}(a_{t}\mid x_{t})}{\mu_{t}(a_{t}\mid x_{t})}.

Taking logarithms and expectations under
q_{\pi}
proves the first identity. The second follows by conditional expectation with respect to
X_{t}
.
∎

With the stage cost
\ell_{t}(X_{t})
acting on states, define the admissible class on the state-action path space by

\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}:=\left\{q:\ q\ll p_{0},\;\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left\lvert\ell_{t}(X_{t})\right\rvert\right]<\infty,\;\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_{0})<\infty\right\},

(5.12)

where
p_{0}
now denotes the passive state-action law
equation

5.8
. This is the admissible class obtained by applying
theorem

5.1
on the extended measurable space
(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})
with cost depending only on the state coordinates. For any
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}
, define the state-action-law functional

\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}(q):=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(X_{t})\right]\;+\;\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_{0}),

(5.13)

Then
theorems

5.1
and
5.4
show that applying the Gibbs variational identity on this state-action path space makes the policy optimization problem

\inf_{\pi}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(X_{t})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\log\frac{\pi_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}{\mu_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}\right]\right\}

(5.14)

is equivalent to minimizing
\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}(q)
over the reachable family of policy-induced state-action path laws. This is the relative-entropy control or KL-control form underlying control-as-inference formulations
[
20
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
6
,
12
,
9
]
. The unrestricted Gibbs law from
theorem

5.1
is therefore the benchmark on the full state-action path space, while the actual policy problem is its restriction to the reachable subset generated by the fixed kernels
P_{t}
.

Corollary 5.5

(Conditional posterior recovery under representability)
.

Assume the passive state marginal satisfies
p_{0}^{X}(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T})
, where
p(x_{0:T})
is the Bayesian prior path density from
equation

2.6
, and let
\ell_{t}(x_{t})=-\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})
. Assume in addition that the unrestricted Gibbs law
q^{\star}
obtained by applying
theorem

5.1
on the state-action path space belongs to
\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}
, so that it is the unique optimizer of the unrestricted state-action problem. If that law is induced by some admissible policy
\pi^{\star}
, a restrictive representability assumption, then any optimizer of
equation

5.14
induces exactly that law, and its state marginal is the Bayesian smoothing posterior:

q_{\pi^{\star}}^{X}(x_{0:T})=p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T}).

(5.15)

If the policy class is restricted, the optimal induced state-action law is the reverse-KL projection of
q^{\star}
onto the reachable family
\left\{q_{\pi}:\pi\text{ admissible}\right\}
; its state marginal need not be the reverse-KL projection of the smoothing posterior in state space.

Proof.

By
theorem

5.1
applied on the state-action path space and the hypothesis
q^{\star}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}
, the unique optimizer over all admissible state-action laws is

q^{\star}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})\propto p_{0}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(y_{t}\mid x_{t}).

If
q^{\star}=q_{\pi^{\star}}
for some admissible policy
\pi^{\star}
, then
\pi^{\star}
is optimal for
equation

5.14
because that objective is just
\mathcal{J}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{SA}}
restricted to the reachable family of state-action laws. Integrating out the actions gives

q_{\pi^{\star}}^{X}(x_{0:T})\propto p_{0}^{X}(x_{0:T})\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})=p(x_{0:T})\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(y_{t}\mid x_{t}),

which is exactly the smoothing posterior by
equation

2.7
. The restricted-family statement is the corresponding application of
theorem

5.1
on the state-action path space.
∎

Remark 5.6

(Why the representability assumption is nontrivial)
.

The condition in
corollary

5.5
is far from automatic in the present action-KL model. The unrestricted Gibbs law on state-action paths generally twists the effective one-step state evolution after conditioning on future costs, whereas the admissible policy class keeps the transition kernel
P_{t}(\cdot\mid x,a)
fixed and can only reweight the action kernel. Exact posterior recovery by policies therefore requires additional structure beyond matching the likelihood cost and passive state marginal. It can hold in special cases, for example under deterministic dynamics or when the action variable parametrizes the needed transition twist, but not in general.

5.3
A precise one-step RL theorem

We now formulate the filtering version requested in the introduction. At time

as in

\mathcal{R}_{t}(q_{t}):=-\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})=-\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[-\log p(y_{t}\mid X_{t})\right]-\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{f}).

(5.16)

Theorem 5.7

(RL solution equals posterior solution in one-step filtering)
.

Under the assumptions of
theorem

4.2
,

\sup_{q_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}}\mathcal{R}_{t}(q_{t})=\log p(y_{t}\mid y_{0:t-1}).

(5.17)

If, in addition,
p_{t}^{a}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, then the reward functional
equation

5.16
has the unique maximizer

q_{t}^{\star}=p_{t}^{a}.

(5.18)

More generally, if actions are parameterized by a family of transport maps or policies whose induced laws form a set
\mathcal{F}_{t}\subset\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, then any optimal action induces a law solving

\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{a}).

(5.19)

In particular, if
p_{t}^{a}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
, then the optimal induced law is exactly
p_{t}^{a}
.

Proof.

The identity
\mathcal{R}_{t}(q_{t})=-\mathcal{J}_{t}(q_{t})
and
theorem

4.2
give the supremum formula. If
p_{t}^{a}\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}
, the unique-maximizer statement is equivalent to the corresponding minimizer statement for
\mathcal{J}_{t}
. The restricted-family claim follows from
corollary

4.4
.
∎

Remark 5.8

(Why RMSE rewards do not imply posterior recovery)
.

Theorem

5.7
is exact because the reward is the negative of the likelihood-plus-KL objective. If the reward is replaced by a generic error metric, such as Euclidean RMSE, then the optimizer is generally a different Gibbs law or a different point-estimation problem. That distinction will be central in
section

8
.

5.4
Bellman recursion for the action-KL model

For the policy problem
equation

5.14
, let
V_{t}(x)
denote the optimal cost-to-go starting from state
x
at time
t
:

V_{t}(x):=\inf_{\pi_{t:T-1}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{s=t}^{T}\ell_{s}(X_{s})+\sum_{s=t}^{T-1}\log\frac{\pi_{s}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}{\mu_{s}(A_{s}\mid X_{s})}\middle|\,X_{t}=x\right].

(5.20)

The correct dynamic-programming recursion for the present action-KL model is obtained by minimizing over the action kernel at each state.

Proposition 5.9

(Bellman recursion and optimal policy for the action-KL model)
.

Assume
V_{t+1}
is finite and define

G_{t}(x,a):=\int P_{t}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)V_{t+1}(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.

(5.21)

Suppose that, for every
x
and
t=T-1,\dots,0
,

0<\int\mu_{t}(a\mid x)e^{-G_{t}(x,a)}\,\mathrm{d}a<\infty.

(5.22)

Then the optimal value function for
equation

5.14
satisfies

V_{t}(x)=\ell_{t}(x)-\log\int\mu_{t}(a\mid x)\exp\!\left(-\int P_{t}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)V_{t+1}(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right)\,\mathrm{d}a,

(5.23)

with terminal condition
V_{T}(x)=\ell_{T}(x)
. Moreover,

\pi_{t}^{\star}(a\mid x)=\frac{\mu_{t}(a\mid x)\exp\!\left(-\int P_{t}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)V_{t+1}(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right)}{\int\mu_{t}(b\mid x)\exp\!\left(-\int P_{t}(x^{\prime}\mid x,b)V_{t+1}(x^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\right)\,\mathrm{d}b}.

(5.24)

Proof.

The Bellman principle gives

V_{T}(x)=\ell_{T}(x)

and, for
t<T
,

V_{t}(x)=\ell_{t}(x)+\inf_{\nu\ll\mu_{t}(\cdot\mid x)}\left\{\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\mu_{t}(\cdot\mid x))+\int G_{t}(x,a)\,\nu(\,\mathrm{d}a)\right\},

where
\nu
ranges over admissible one-step action kernels at the current state. For fixed
x
, define

\nu_{t}^{\star}(a\mid x):=\frac{\mu_{t}(a\mid x)e^{-G_{t}(x,a)}}{\int\mu_{t}(b\mid x)e^{-G_{t}(x,b)}\,\mathrm{d}b}.

Then the one-step Gibbs identity on the action space yields

\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\mu_{t}(\cdot\mid x))+\int G_{t}(x,a)\,\nu(\,\mathrm{d}a)=\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\nu_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid x))-\log\int\mu_{t}(a\mid x)e^{-G_{t}(x,a)}\,\mathrm{d}a.

Taking the infimum over
\nu
proves
equation

5.23
, and the unique minimizer is precisely
\nu_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid x)
, which is the displayed optimal policy formula.
∎

Remark 5.10

(Relation to desirability formulas)
.

Equation

5.23
is the correct Bellman recursion for the action-KL model defined in
equations

5.8
and
5.9
. It differs from the linear desirability recursion familiar from transition-kernel or linearly solvable control formulations, where the control acts directly on the next-state kernel
[
17
,
19
]
. In the present model the expectation of
V_{t+1}
under
P_{t}(\cdot\mid x,a)
appears inside the exponential. By Jensen’s inequality, replacing it by
\int P_{t}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)e^{-V_{t+1}(x^{\prime})}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}
is generally incorrect except in special cases such as deterministic transitions or constant downstream value.

Remark 5.11

(Temperatures and tempered posteriors)
.

If one replaces
equation

5.14
by

\mathbb{E}_{q_{\pi}}\!\left[\sum_{t}\beta\,\ell_{t}(X_{t})\right]\;+\;\alpha\,\operatorname{KL}(q_{\pi}\|p_{0})

with
\alpha,\beta>0
, then the optimizer becomes

q^{\star}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})\propto p_{0}(x_{0:T},a_{0:T-1})\exp\!\left(-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\ell_{t}(x_{t})\right).

For likelihood costs
\ell_{t}=-\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})
this is a tempered posterior on the relevant path space. Exact Bayesian recovery therefore requires the reward scaling and KL weight to be calibrated correctly, and in the policy formulation it still requires the corresponding Gibbs law to be reachable by the policy class.

6
Linear-Gaussian specialization: Kalman filter

We now show how the previous variational principles reduce to the classical Kalman analysis step
[
5
]
. The key point is that in the linear-Gaussian case the one-step posterior is itself Gaussian, so the unrestricted variational minimizer from
theorem

4.2
already lies inside the Gaussian family.

Assumption 6.1

(Linear-Gaussian forecast and observation model)
.

At time
t
, let

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle\sim p_{t}^{f}=\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{f},P_{t}^{f}),

(6.1)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=H_{t}X_{t}+\eta_{t},\qquad\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_{t}),

(6.2)

where
P_{t}^{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
R_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
are symmetric positive definite and
\eta_{t}
is independent of
X_{t}
.

Theorem 6.2

(Kalman analysis posterior)
.

Under
6.1
, the analysis posterior is Gaussian:

p_{t}^{a}=\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{a},P_{t}^{a}).

(6.3)

Its information-form parameters are

\displaystyle\left(P_{t}^{a}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(P_{t}^{f}\right)^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t},

(6.4)

\displaystyle m_{t}^{a}

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{a}\left(\left(P_{t}^{f}\right)^{-1}m_{t}^{f}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}y_{t}\right).

(6.5)

Equivalently, with Kalman gain

K_{t}:=P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(H_{t}P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)^{-1},

(6.6)

the covariance-form update is

\displaystyle P_{t}^{a}

\displaystyle=\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)P_{t}^{f},

(6.7)

\displaystyle m_{t}^{a}

\displaystyle=m_{t}^{f}+K_{t}\left(y_{t}-H_{t}m_{t}^{f}\right).

(6.8)

Proof.

Bayes’ rule gives

p_{t}^{a}(x_{t})\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x_{t}-m_{t}^{f})^{\mathsf{T}}(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}(x_{t}-m_{t}^{f})-\frac{1}{2}(y_{t}-H_{t}x_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}(y_{t}-H_{t}x_{t})\right).

Expanding the quadratic form in
x_{t}
yields

\displaystyle(x_{t}-m_{t}^{f})^{\mathsf{T}}(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}(x_{t}-m_{t}^{f})+(y_{t}-H_{t}x_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}(y_{t}-H_{t}x_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad=x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}\right)x_{t}-2x_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}m_{t}^{f}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}y_{t}\right)+c(y_{t}),

where
c(y_{t})
does not depend on
x_{t}
. Since
(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}
is symmetric positive definite, completing the square gives
equations

6.4
and
6.5
. Applying the Woodbury identity,

\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}\right)^{-1}=P_{t}^{f}-P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(H_{t}P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)^{-1}H_{t}P_{t}^{f},

yields
equation

6.7
. Substituting this identity into
equation

6.5
gives
equation

6.8
. The details are summarized again in
section

A.3
.
∎

6.1
Kalman update from the central variational theorem

The previous theorem is the direct Bayesian calculation. We now show that the same formulas arise from minimizing the one-step variational functional over Gaussian candidate laws.

Proposition 6.3

(Gaussian minimization of the one-step functional)
.

Under
6.1
, let
q=\mathcal{N}(m,P)
with
P
symmetric positive definite. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{t}(q)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(H_{t}m-y_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}(H_{t}m-y_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}P\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(m-m_{t}^{f})^{\mathsf{T}}(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}(m-m_{t}^{f})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}P\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\log\det P+\frac{1}{2}\log\det P_{t}^{f}-\frac{n}{2}+c_{t},

(6.9)

where
c_{t}
depends on
y_{t}
and
R_{t}
but not on
(m,P)
. The unique minimizer over Gaussian
q
is the posterior Gaussian from
theorem

6.2
; equivalently, the first-order conditions are exactly
equations

6.4
and
6.5
.

Proof.

The expectation of the quadratic observation term under
q
is

\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[(y_{t}-H_{t}X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}(y_{t}-H_{t}X_{t})\right]=(y_{t}-H_{t}m)^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}(y_{t}-H_{t}m)+\operatorname{tr}\left(H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}P\right).

The Gaussian KL divergence satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathcal{N}(m,P)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{f},P_{t}^{f})\right)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}P\right)+\frac{1}{2}(m-m_{t}^{f})^{\mathsf{T}}(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}(m-m_{t}^{f})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log\det P_{t}^{f}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det P.

Substituting these expressions into
equation

4.2
gives
equation

6.9
. Differentiating with respect to
m
and
P
yields the stationarity equations

\displaystyle\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}\right)m

\displaystyle=(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}m_{t}^{f}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}y_{t},

\displaystyle P^{-1}

\displaystyle=(P_{t}^{f})^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}.

These are exactly
equations

6.4
and
6.5
. Strict convexity of
equation

6.9
in
(m,P)
gives uniqueness. A fuller derivation appears in
section

A.1
.
∎

Corollary 6.4

(The Kalman analysis as a variational optimum)
.

In the linear-Gaussian setting, the analysis posterior from
theorem

6.2
is the unique global minimizer of the one-step functional
equation

4.2
. Since the posterior itself is Gaussian, no approximation enters at the variational level.

6.2
Posterior means and deterministic estimators

Proposition 6.5

(Posterior mean under quadratic loss)
.

Let
X
be any square-integrable random vector and let
\pi(\cdot\mid y)
denote a posterior law of
X
given
Y=y
. The unique minimizer of

a\mapsto\int\left\lVert x-a\right\rVert^{2}\,\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid y)

is the posterior mean
\int x\,\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid y)
. In the linear-Gaussian setting of
6.1
, this minimizer is
m_{t}^{a}
.

Proof.

Let
m(y)=\int x\,\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid y)
. Then

\int\left\lVert x-a\right\rVert^{2}\,\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid y)=\int\left\lVert x-m(y)\right\rVert^{2}\,\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid y)\;+\;\left\lVert m(y)-a\right\rVert^{2},

because the cross term

2\int\left\langle x-m(y),m(y)-a\right\rangle\,\pi(\,\mathrm{d}x\mid y)

vanishes by definition of
m(y)
. The second term is uniquely minimized at
a=m(y)
.
∎

Proposition

6.5
will be relevant later: a deterministic RL objective with squared-error loss targets a posterior mean, not the posterior law. In the Gaussian case these coincide with the Kalman analysis mean, but the distinction remains conceptually important.

7
Ensemble Kalman filter as an ensemble approximation

The ensemble Kalman filter is best understood here as a Gaussian and finite-ensemble approximation to the forecast-to-analysis map
p_{t}^{f}\mapsto p_{t}^{a}
in the linear-Gaussian setting, together with a moment closure in more general settings
[
2
,
7
]
. This interpretation is exact only in specific senses; at finite ensemble size it is not the full Bayesian posterior law on state space.

7.1
Gaussian surrogate update

Given a forecast ensemble
\left\{x_{t}^{f,(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
intended to approximate
p_{t}^{f}
, define the sample mean and covariance

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{t}^{f}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{t}^{f,(i)},

(7.1)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{t}^{f}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(x_{t}^{f,(i)}-\hat{m}_{t}^{f}\right)\left(x_{t}^{f,(i)}-\hat{m}_{t}^{f}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}.

(7.2)

If one replaces the true forecast law
p_{t}^{f}
by the Gaussian surrogate
\mathcal{N}(\hat{m}_{t}^{f},\hat{P}_{t}^{f})
, then the exact Gaussian posterior update from
theorem

6.2
yields surrogate analysis parameters

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\hat{P}_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(H_{t}\hat{P}_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)^{-1},

(7.3)

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{t}^{a}

\displaystyle:=\hat{m}_{t}^{f}+\hat{K}_{t}\left(y_{t}-H_{t}\hat{m}_{t}^{f}\right),

(7.4)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{t}^{a}

\displaystyle:=\left(I-\hat{K}_{t}H_{t}\right)\hat{P}_{t}^{f}.

(7.5)

Because
R_{t}\succ 0
, the matrix
H_{t}\hat{P}_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}
is always symmetric positive definite, so the gain
equation

7.3
is well defined even when the sample covariance
\hat{P}_{t}^{f}
is singular. If
\hat{P}_{t}^{f}\succ 0
, then
\mathcal{N}(\hat{m}_{t}^{a},\hat{P}_{t}^{a})
is exactly the Gaussian posterior for the surrogate forecast density
\mathcal{N}(\hat{m}_{t}^{f},\hat{P}_{t}^{f})
, equivalently the minimizer of the one-step functional
equation

4.2
over Gaussian densities built from those surrogate moments. If
\hat{P}_{t}^{f}
is singular, the same mean and covariance formulas still define the EnKF moment update, but the corresponding Gaussian law is degenerate on an affine subspace and therefore lies outside the density-based variational family of
section

4
.

7.2
Perturbed-observation EnKF

The stochastic or perturbed-observation EnKF updates ensemble members by

x_{t}^{a,(i)}=x_{t}^{f,(i)}+\hat{K}_{t}\left(y_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}^{(i)}-H_{t}x_{t}^{f,(i)}\right),\qquad\varepsilon_{t}^{(i)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,R_{t}).

(7.6)

The observation perturbations were introduced precisely so that the empirical covariance of the analysis ensemble is consistent, in expectation, with the Kalman covariance update
[
1
]
. The method was developed and popularized as a practical large-scale sequential estimator built from Gaussian Bayesian update formulas by Evensen
[
2
]
.

Proposition 7.1

(Exact stochastic transport when the gain is exact)
.

Assume the exact linear-Gaussian setting of
6.1
. Let
X_{t}^{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{f},P_{t}^{f})
and
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_{t})
be independent, and define

X_{t}^{a}:=X_{t}^{f}+K_{t}\left(y_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}-H_{t}X_{t}^{f}\right),

(7.7)

where
K_{t}
is the exact Kalman gain
equation

6.6
. Then
X_{t}^{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{a},P_{t}^{a})
, with
(m_{t}^{a},P_{t}^{a})
given by
equations

6.7
and
6.8
.

Proof.

The variable
X_{t}^{a}
is an affine image of a jointly Gaussian vector, so it is Gaussian. Its mean is

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{a}]=m_{t}^{f}+K_{t}(y_{t}-H_{t}m_{t}^{f})=m_{t}^{a}.

Its covariance is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t}^{a})

\displaystyle=\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)P_{t}^{f}\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}+K_{t}R_{t}K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{f}-K_{t}H_{t}P_{t}^{f}-P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+K_{t}\left(H_{t}P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{f}-K_{t}H_{t}P_{t}^{f}=P_{t}^{a},

where the final identities use
equation

6.6
.
∎

Proposition

7.1
explains the origin of the EnKF analysis map. If one started with i.i.d. forecast particles and still used the exact deterministic gain
K_{t}
, the transformed particles would remain i.i.d. draws from the exact analysis posterior. What standard EnKF does instead is replace the exact gain
K_{t}
and exact forecast moments by their ensemble estimates. That produces a practical finite-ensemble algorithm, but it is no longer literally the exact posterior law on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

7.3
Finite ensemble versus infinite ensemble

Several distinct statements must be kept separate.

1.

Exact Kalman posterior.
In the linear-Gaussian model, the posterior law is exactly
\mathcal{N}(m_{t}^{a},P_{t}^{a})
.

2.

Gaussian surrogate update.
If
\hat{P}_{t}^{f}\succ 0
, then
equations

7.3
,
7.4
, and
7.5
are the exact Gaussian posterior formulas for the surrogate forecast density
\mathcal{N}(\hat{m}_{t}^{f},\hat{P}_{t}^{f})
. If
\hat{P}_{t}^{f}
is singular, the same formulas still define a moment update, but not a nondegenerate posterior density on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

3.

Finite-
N
perturbed-observation EnKF.
With the sample gain
\hat{K}_{t}
, the analysis ensemble

\hat{\nu}_{t}^{a,N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{t}^{a,(i)}}

is a random empirical measure. At finite
N
it is generally neither equal to the exact posterior law nor a sample of independent posterior draws.

4.

Finite-
N
deterministic square-root EnKF.
Square-root or transform filters deterministically enforce the surrogate analysis mean and covariance, but the resulting empirical measure is still only an ensemble representation of selected Gaussian moments. Even in the linear-Gaussian setting, this is a finite-ensemble surrogate-law statement, not exact equality with the full posterior law on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

5.

Infinite-ensemble linear-Gaussian limit.
Under linear-Gaussian assumptions, i.i.d. initialization, and standard propagation hypotheses, the standard perturbed-observation EnKF satisfies
\hat{m}_{t}^{f}\to m_{t}^{f}
and
\hat{P}_{t}^{f}\to P_{t}^{f}
, hence converges to the Kalman update as
N\to\infty

[
11
]
. Deterministic square-root variants are designed to realize the same limiting Kalman moment update, but the exact large-ensemble theorem is variant-dependent and is not needed for the present hierarchy. This linear-Gaussian infinite-ensemble statement is the exactness result usually invoked in the EnKF literature.

6.

Nonlinear or non-Gaussian settings.
Even with
N\to\infty
, EnKF-type methods retain a Gaussian or moment-closure update structure and therefore generally do not recover the exact nonlinear non-Gaussian posterior
[
2
,
7
,
21
,
13
]
.

7.4
Deterministic square-root variants

Deterministic square-root or transform filters avoid perturbed observations. Instead, they update the ensemble mean by the Kalman mean formula and apply a deterministic transform to the ensemble anomalies so that the empirical analysis covariance matches the target surrogate covariance
[
22
,
16
]
. From the perspective of
sections

4
and
6
, these methods implement the same Gaussian surrogate posterior update as the perturbed-observation EnKF, but by a deterministic ensemble transport rather than by randomized observation perturbations.

The exactness caveat remains the same. At finite ensemble size, a square-root EnKF matches the prescribed surrogate moments by construction, but that moment-matching property should not be confused with exact posterior recovery. The empirical measure of the transformed ensemble is not the full Bayesian posterior law on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; it is an ensemble representation designed to reproduce selected Gaussian summary statistics, with exact posterior recovery only in the corresponding linear-Gaussian infinite-ensemble limit.

7.5
Relation to controlled particle methods

The control viewpoint extends beyond the EnKF. In feedback particle filtering, particles are driven by a feedback control law chosen so that, at the mean-field level, their conditional distribution matches the true filtering distribution
[
23
]
. In this sense, the filtering problem is recast as an optimal-control problem on interacting particles. Controlled interacting particle systems include both the feedback particle filter and the EnKF as special cases of feedback-based Bayesian update mechanisms
[
14
]
.

This control interpretation provides a natural bridge to the present variational picture. The feedback control law in feedback particle filtering is designed so that the particle law matches the posterior, while the EnKF uses a linear-Gaussian control law determined by first and second moments. Reich’s Schrödinger and transport perspective makes the same point from another angle: sequential data assimilation can be viewed as transporting a forecast measure to an analysis measure, with optimal-transport and Schrödinger-bridge ideas clarifying the relation between exact posterior transport and its Gaussian or ensemble approximations
[
13
]
. In this hierarchy, the EnKF is a particular controlled transport with Gaussian structure, not the general posterior transport itself.

8
RMSE-based RL data assimilation and Hammoud et al.

The preceding sections provide a criterion for when an RL formulation is posterior-exact: the reward and regularization must combine to realize the likelihood-plus-KL objective from
theorems

5.7
and
5.5
. This section uses that criterion to interpret existing RL-based data assimilation work, especially Hammoud et al.
[
4
]
. The claims here are limited to the objective described in the published paper, not to unreported implementation details.

8.1
What the published objective supports

Hammoud et al.
[
4
]
study data assimilation for the Lorenz–63 and Lorenz–96 systems using a stochastic deep RL policy that applies state corrections and generates ensembles of assimilated realizations. As stated in the article abstract, the agent optimizes a geometric return built from terms proportional to the negative root-mean-squared error between observations and corresponding forecast states, and the resulting method performs favorably against the EnKF on the reported test problems.

That is already a meaningful contribution. It shows that RL can learn useful nonlinear correction strategies, can be implemented with stochastic action policies, and can produce Monte Carlo ensembles rather than only single corrected states. It also suggests that RL policies can help in settings where EnKF’s Gaussian update structure is restrictive.

8.2
What RMSE rewards do not prove

An RMSE-style reward does
not
by itself imply that the learned policy samples from the Bayesian analysis posterior. To see why, consider the one-step entropy-regularized objective, interpreted over laws
q_{t}\ll p_{t}^{f}
for which the displayed expectation and relative entropy are finite,

\inf_{q_{t}\ll p_{t}^{f}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{q_{t}}\!\left[\lambda\,r(X_{t};y_{t})\right]+\operatorname{KL}(q_{t}\|p_{t}^{f})\right\},

(8.1)

for some measurable data-fit penalty
r(\cdot;y_{t})
such that

Z_{r}:=\int p_{t}^{f}(x_{t})e^{-\lambda r(x_{t};y_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{t}\in(0,\infty).

Under the same finiteness logic as in
theorem

5.1
, here encoded by
Z_{r}\in(0,\infty)
together with finiteness of the displayed objective, the optimizer is

q_{t}^{\star}(x_{t})\propto p_{t}^{f}(x_{t})\exp\left(-\lambda\,r(x_{t};y_{t})\right).

(8.2)

Therefore:

1.

if
r(x_{t};y_{t})=-\log p(y_{t}\mid x_{t})
, then
q_{t}^{\star}=p_{t}^{a}
, the Bayesian posterior;

2.

if
r
is Euclidean RMSE or any other mismatch metric, then
q_{t}^{\star}
is generally a
different
Gibbs law, associated with a pseudo-likelihood proportional to
\exp(-\lambda r)
.

This observation does not diminish the practical value of the learned policy. It only says that posterior equivalence requires a specific objective, and RMSE alone is not that objective.

Remark 8.1

(Squared Mahalanobis error is the special Bayesian case)
.

If the reward uses the quadratic Mahalanobis misfit

r(x_{t};y_{t})=\frac{1}{2}(y_{t}-H_{t}x_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}(y_{t}-H_{t}x_{t}),

then
\exp(-r(x_{t};y_{t}))
is proportional to the Gaussian likelihood
[
5
,
10
]
. In that case, and only together with the correct KL regularization, the entropy-regularized optimizer coincides with the Bayesian posterior. The issue is therefore not whether an RL reward uses an “error” term, but whether that term is exactly the negative log-likelihood associated with the intended observation model.

8.3
What would be needed for a posterior-equivalence theorem

To obtain a theorem of the form “the RL solution equals the Bayesian analysis posterior,” one needs at least the following ingredients.

1.

A forecast or passive law
p_{t}^{f}
or
p_{0}
relative to which the controlled law is measured.

2.

A reward term equal, up to additive constants, to the observation log-likelihood.

3.

A KL or relative-entropy regularizer with the correct temperature, so that the induced Gibbs law is the desired posterior rather than a tempered or pseudo-posterior.

4.

A policy or transport family rich enough to represent the target posterior, or an explicit statement that one is computing the reverse-KL projection of the posterior onto a restricted family.

These conditions are exactly those of
theorems

5.7
and
5.5
. Without them, a learned policy may still be an excellent estimator, but it is not automatically an exact posterior solver.

8.4
Interpretive hierarchy for RL-based data assimilation

The theory developed here suggests the following hierarchy.

1.

Deterministic squared-error RL.
The target is a point estimator, typically close to a posterior mean under quadratic loss.

2.

Stochastic RL with generic data-fit reward.
The target is a Gibbs law determined by the chosen reward, not necessarily the Bayesian posterior.

3.

KL-regularized RL with exact likelihood reward.

The target is the Bayesian posterior or its reverse-KL projection onto the policy family.

4.

Gaussian or ensemble-restricted KL-regularized RL.
The target is a Gaussian or ensemble approximation of the posterior, yielding Kalman-like or EnKF-like updates.

Hammoud et al. fit naturally into the second category based on the published objective description. Moving to the third category would require the control-as-inference structure to be built into the reward and regularizer explicitly.

9
Conclusion

The manuscript isolates a single exact theorem chain behind several overlapping languages in data assimilation. The analysis posterior
p_{t}^{a}
and smoothing posterior
p(x_{0:T}\mid y_{0:T})
satisfy exact likelihood-plus-KL identities and are the unique minimizers when those posterior laws are admissible; strong- and weak-constraint 4D-Var are MAP procedures under the stated Gaussian assumptions; KL-regularized control recovers the Bayesian posterior only when the passive dynamics, likelihood cost, temperature, and a restrictive representability condition on the policy class are matched correctly; and the Kalman filter and EnKF then appear as the exact linear-Gaussian specialization and its Gaussian or finite-ensemble approximation.

The payoff is precision. It prevents posterior-exact claims from being transferred to objectives that in fact target means, modes, surrogate Gaussians, reachable policy laws, or RMSE-driven pseudo-posteriors. That precision is not restrictive; it gives a clean design principle for new algorithms. Keep the posterior identity explicit at the level of the objective, state the admissibility and representability assumptions needed for exact recovery, and then say exactly which restricted family, transport class, or ensemble approximation is being used to make the problem computationally feasible.

Supplementary proofs and remarks

A.1
Gaussian minimization details

For completeness, we record the derivative calculation used in
proposition

6.3
. Let

\displaystyle F(m,P):={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(Hm-y)^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}(Hm-y)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}HP)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(m-m_{f})^{\mathsf{T}}P_{f}^{-1}(m-m_{f})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(P_{f}^{-1}P)-\frac{1}{2}\log\det P.

Then

\nabla_{m}F=H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}(Hm-y)+P_{f}^{-1}(m-m_{f}),

so the stationary condition
\nabla_{m}F=0
gives

\left(P_{f}^{-1}+H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}H\right)m=P_{f}^{-1}m_{f}+H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}y.

For the covariance variable, standard matrix calculus gives

\nabla_{P}F=\frac{1}{2}H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}H+\frac{1}{2}P_{f}^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}P^{-1},

and the stationary condition is therefore

P^{-1}=P_{f}^{-1}+H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}H.

These are the information-form Kalman equations. Strict convexity in
m
follows from positive definiteness of
P_{f}^{-1}+H^{\mathsf{T}}R^{-1}H
, while strict convexity in
P
on the cone of symmetric positive definite matrices follows from the convexity of
\operatorname{tr}(AP)-\log\det P
when
A
is positive definite.

A.2
MAP as a zero-variance limit

This subsection makes precise the heuristic from
remark

4.8
under conditions that preserve absolute continuity. Let
\pi
be a posterior density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\pi(x)>0
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\log\pi
is continuous with at most quadratic growth:

\left\lvert\log\pi(z)\right\rvert\leq C(1+\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2})\qquad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for some constant
C<\infty
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
, let
q_{x,\varepsilon}=\mathcal{N}(x,\varepsilon I)
. Then

\operatorname{KL}(q_{x,\varepsilon}\|\pi)=-\mathbb{E}_{q_{x,\varepsilon}}[\log\pi(Z)]-H(q_{x,\varepsilon}),

where
H(q_{x,\varepsilon})
denotes the differential entropy of
q_{x,\varepsilon}
. Since

H(q_{x,\varepsilon})=\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi e\varepsilon)

is independent of
x
, minimizing
\operatorname{KL}(q_{x,\varepsilon}\|\pi)
over
x
is equivalent to maximizing
\mathbb{E}_{q_{x,\varepsilon}}[\log\pi(Z)]
over
x
. By continuity of
\log\pi
,

\mathbb{E}_{q_{x,\varepsilon}}[\log\pi(Z)]\to\log\pi(x)\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\downarrow 0.

Indeed, if
\Xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
then
Z=x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\Xi
, so
\log\pi(Z)\to\log\pi(x)
almost surely. The quadratic-growth bound gives

\left\lvert\log\pi(Z)\right\rvert\leq C\left(1+2\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert^{2}\right),

which is integrable for every fixed
x
and
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
. Dominated convergence therefore justifies the displayed limit.

Now define

F_{\varepsilon}(x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\log\pi\left(x+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\Xi\right)\right],

so that minimizing
\operatorname{KL}(q_{x,\varepsilon}\|\pi)
is equivalent to maximizing
F_{\varepsilon}(x)
. Let
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and let
x_{\varepsilon_{k}}
be maximizers such that
x_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to x^{\star}
. Because this subsequence is bounded, there exists
M<\infty
with
\left\lVert x_{\varepsilon_{k}}\right\rVert\leq M
for all
k
. For every
k
with
\varepsilon_{k}\leq 1
,

\left\lvert\log\pi\left(x_{\varepsilon_{k}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}\,\Xi\right)\right\rvert\leq C\left(1+2\left\lVert x_{\varepsilon_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}+2\varepsilon_{k}\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert^{2}\right)\leq C\left(1+2M^{2}+2\left\lVert\Xi\right\rVert^{2}\right),

and the right-hand side is integrable. Since

x_{\varepsilon_{k}}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k}}\,\Xi\to x^{\star}\qquad\text{almost surely},

dominated convergence gives

F_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{\varepsilon_{k}})\to\log\pi(x^{\star}).

For any fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the fixed-center argument above gives

F_{\varepsilon_{k}}(y)\to\log\pi(y).

Because
x_{\varepsilon_{k}}
maximizes
F_{\varepsilon_{k}}
,

F_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{\varepsilon_{k}})\geq F_{\varepsilon_{k}}(y)\qquad\text{for every }k.

Passing to the limit yields
\log\pi(x^{\star})\geq\log\pi(y)
for every
y
, so
x^{\star}
is a MAP point of
\pi
. This is the precise continuous-state version of the common slogan that the point-mass restriction yields MAP. If the posterior has constrained support, the same conclusion requires support-preserving smooth approximants rather than full Gaussians.

A.3
Matrix identities

The covariance-form Kalman update follows from the Woodbury identity

\left(A^{-1}+UC^{-1}V\right)^{-1}=A-AU\left(C+VAU\right)^{-1}VA,

applied with
A=P_{t}^{f}
,
U=H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
C=R_{t}
, and
V=H_{t}
. This gives

P_{t}^{a}=\left((P_{t}^{f})^{-1}+H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}^{-1}H_{t}\right)^{-1}=P_{t}^{f}-P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(H_{t}P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)^{-1}H_{t}P_{t}^{f}.

If
K_{t}
is defined by
equation

6.6
, then

P_{t}^{a}=(I-K_{t}H_{t})P_{t}^{f}.

The equivalent Joseph form,

P_{t}^{a}=\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)P_{t}^{f}\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}+K_{t}R_{t}K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},

is obtained by expanding the right-hand side:

\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)P_{t}^{f}\left(I-K_{t}H_{t}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}+K_{t}R_{t}K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}=P_{t}^{f}-K_{t}H_{t}P_{t}^{f}-P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+K_{t}\left(H_{t}P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}},

and then using

K_{t}\left(H_{t}P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+R_{t}\right)=P_{t}^{f}H_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}.

A.4
Continuous-time remarks

The finite-horizon discrete-time identities in the main text are the cleanest setting for the posterior-as-variational theorem. Continuous-time analogues arise in several ways. In diffusion filtering with continuous observations, feedback particle filters choose a feedback control so that the conditional law of the particles matches the exact posterior in the mean-field limit
[
23
]
. From another viewpoint, Schrödinger bridge formulations and transport couplings interpret data assimilation as an entropy-regularized measure-transport problem between forecast and analysis laws
[
13
]
. These continuous-time theories reinforce the main message of the paper: posterior updates can be expressed as controlled measure transports, and approximate filters differ primarily by the restricted family of transports they permit.
