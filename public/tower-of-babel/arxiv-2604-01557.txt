Sublinear-query relative-error testing of halfspaces
Xi Chen Anindya De Yizhi Huang Columbia University University of Pennsylvania Columbia University xichen@cs.columbia.edu anindyad@cis.upenn.edu yizhi@cs.columbia.edu Shivam Nadimpalli Rocco A. Servedio Tianqi Yang MIT Columbia University Columbia University shivamn@mit.edu rocco@cs.columbia.edu tianqi@cs.columbia.edu

Abstract
The relative-error property testing model was introduced in [ 11 ] to facilitate the study of property testing for “sparse” Boolean-valued functions, i.e. ones for which only a small fraction of all input assignments satisfy the function. In this framework, the distance from the unknown target function f f that is being tested to a function g g is defined as Vol ⁡ ( f ​ △ ​ g ) / Vol ⁡ ( f ) \mathrm{Vol}(f\hskip 1.70709pt\triangle\hskip 1.70709ptg)/\mathrm{Vol}(f) , where the numerator is the fraction of inputs on which f f and g g disagree and the denominator is the fraction of inputs that satisfy f f . Recent work [ 12 ] has shown that over the Boolean domain { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} , any relative-error testing algorithm for the fundamental class of halfspaces (i.e. linear threshold functions) must make Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) oracle calls. In this paper we complement the [ 12 ] lower bound by showing that halfspaces can be relative-error tested over ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} under the standard N ⁡ ( 0 , I n ) N(0,I_{n}) Gaussian distribution using a sublinear number of oracle calls — in particular, substantially fewer than would be required for learning. Our results use a wide range of tools including Hermite analysis, Gaussian isoperimetric inequalities, and geometric results on noise sensitivity and surface area.

Introduction

Over the past three decades Boolean function property testing has become a well-established research topic in theoretical computer science, see e.g. the books and monographs
[
45
,
30
,
3
]
. In this paper we consider an extension of the standard model of property testing, known as
relative-error
testing, which was recently proposed in
[
11
]
.

To describe the relative-error model, we first recall that in the standard model of Boolean function property testing the goal is to distinguish between the two cases that (i)
f\in{\cal C}
, where
{\cal C}
is the class of functions that have the property that is being tested, versus (ii)
f
is
\varepsilon
-far (under the uniform distribution) from every function in
{\cal C}
, meaning that

\mathsf{dist}(f,{\cal C})\geq\varepsilon,\text{~~~where~}\mathsf{dist}(f,{\cal C})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\min_{g\in{\cal C}}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)\text{~~~and~~~}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})\neq g(\bm{x})].

A testing algorithm in the standard model can obtain information about the unknown function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
that is being tested by making black-box oracle calls to
f
.

The impetus for the study of relative-error testing is that the standard model described above is poorly suited for testing
sparse
functions, i.e. functions which have few satisfying assignments. This is because any such function has very small uniform-distribution distance to the constant-0 function, and so the tester can simply ignore the input function
f
and answer according to the constant-0 function. Indeed, for sparse functions it is arguably more natural to ask whether
f
is “close” to having the property of interest (i.e. close to belonging to
{\cal C}
) for a notion of closeness that is
relative
to the small size of
f^{-1}(1)
rather than to the “absolute” scale of all
2^{n}
possible inputs.

With this motivation, the relative-error property testing model which was defined in
[
11
]
changes the standard model in the following ways:

•

The distance between the target function
f
and a function
g
is now measured using
relative distance
, which is the uniform-distribution distance defined above scaled by the sparsity of
f
:

\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\frac{\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})\neq g(\bm{x})]}{\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})=1]}},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)={\frac{\mathsf{dist}(f,g)}{\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})=1]}}.

Relative distance thus captures the distance between
f
and
g
at the scale of
f
, and continues to be meaningful even if
f
is very sparse.

Technical overview

2.1
Our algorithm for halfspace testing using queries and samples:

Overview of
Section

1.1

To explain the main idea of
Section

1.1
, we start by recalling
two fundamental notions in Gaussian analysis. For any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its
Gaussian volume
, denoted by
\mathrm{Vol}(A)
, is
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[\bm{x}\in A]
.
Furthermore, if
A
is sufficiently smooth, we can also define its
Gaussian surface area
, denoted
\mathsf{surf}(A)
, as
\int_{x\in\partial A}\varphi_{n}(x)d\sigma(x)
, where
\varphi_{n}(\cdot)
denotes the standard
n
-dimensional Gaussian density; in other words, we integrate the standard Gaussian density over the surface of
A
. Given these notions of volume and surface area over the Gaussian space, one is naturally led to the “isoperimetric problem:”
for a given (Gaussian) volume, what set minimizes the (Gaussian) surface area?

This question was answered by Borell
[
6
]
and Sudakov-Tsirelson
[
46
]
, who showed that for any
0<p<1
, for any measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{Vol}(A)=p
, it always holds that
\mathsf{surf}(A)\geq\mathsf{surf}(H_{p})
where
H_{p}
is a halfspace with
\mathrm{Vol}(H_{p})=p
. In other words, in the Gaussian space, for any given volume
p
, the halfspace with volume
p
has minimal surface area. Subsequently, Ehrhard
[
26
]
showed that halfspaces are the
unique
minimizers of the Gaussian surface area. In other words, if a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
\mathrm{Vol}(A)=p
and
\mathsf{surf}(A)=\mathsf{surf}(H_{p})
, then the set
A
is a halfspace.

One is now naturally led to the
robustness
question: Suppose a set
A
has
\mathrm{Vol}(A)=p
and
\mathsf{surf}(A)
is
\delta
-close to
\mathsf{surf}(H_{p})
. Then is it the case that
A
is
\delta^{\prime}
-close to a halfspace, where
\delta^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
? Cianchi
et al.

[
16
]
were the first to affirmatively answer this question. Soon thereafter, in a significant breakthrough, Mossel and Neeman
[
40
]
obtained a “dimension free robust isoperimetry result”, i.e. one in which the dependence between
\delta^{\prime}
and
\delta
is independent of the ambient dimension
n
.

This result was quantitatively improved by Eldan
[
27
]
and then by Barchiesi
et al.

[
2
]

(the latter result is the underlying technical ingredient we use in
Section

1.1
).
A natural question at this point is “what is the connection between robust isoperimetry and testing halfspaces?”

The connection arises from a 2014 result of Neeman
[
42
]
, who gave an algorithm for testing surface area over Gaussian space. The precise technical guarantee is somewhat cumbersome to state, but roughly speaking, the algorithm makes
\mathrm{poly}(S,1/\varepsilon)
-queries to an unknown set
A
and (i) accepts with high probability if
\mathsf{surf}(A)\leq S
, but (ii) rejects with high probability if
\mathsf{surf}(B)>(1+\varepsilon)S
for every set
B
which is
\varepsilon
-close to
A
4
4

4

This perturbation of
A
is necessary because one can increase the surface area of a set arbitrarily by modifying on a measure zero set.
. Now, observe that if an unknown set
A
of some given volume
p
is a halfspace, then its surface area is exactly given by
\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))
where
\Phi
is the cdf and
\varphi
is the pdf of the standard Gaussian. So to test whether
A
is a halfspace, we can use Neeman’s algorithm to test whether its surface area is essentially that of a halfspace of volume
p
, and accept if and only if that is the case. Correctness of this simple algorithm is established using the dimension-free robust isoperimetry result of Barchiesi
et al.

[
2
]
that was mentioned above.

2.2
Our algorithm for sample-based halfspace testing: Overview of
Section

1.1

Our starting point for
Section

1.1
is the work of Matulef, O’Donnell, Rubinfeld, and Servedio
[
37
]
which gives an algorithm (the “MORS algorithm”) that tests halfspaces over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
using
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
queries. The MORS algorithm relies on two structural ingredients:

•

First, there is a function
U\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
(see
Definition

25
) which, given as input the volume
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
of a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}

1

\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[f(\bm{x})\cdot\bm{x}_{i}\right]}^{2}

•

Second, if
U(\mathrm{Vol}(f))\approx\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
, then the set
f
is close to some halfspace.

The above facts suggest a natural algorithm: estimate
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
and
\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
, and then compare

to
\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]

The algorithm

\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
in a suitable sense, then
f
is close

to some halfspace. In this sense,

With

5.3
in hand, we apply a test which is similar in spirit to the MORS algorithm: estimate
\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
and see if it is (sufficiently) close to
U(\mathrm{Vol}(f))
.

This leads to a second technical challenge: in order to get a sample-based algorithm we must accurately estimate
\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
using access to samples from
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
alone. (Note that the MORS algorithm relies on query access to the function

, and there are sample-based testers

]

2m
i.i.d. samples
\bm{x}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{x}^{(m)},\bm{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{y}^{(m)}\leftarrow\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
, we compute the statistic

\bm{T}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\bm{x}^{(i)}\cdot\bm{y}^{(j)}.

It is readily verified that
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\bm{T}]=\mathrm{Vol}(f)^{-2}\cdot\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
; since
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
is known, we can estimate
\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]
if we can estimate
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\bm{T}]
. To estimate
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\bm{T}]
, we must establish a bound on the variance of
\bm{T}
; to do this, we employ (consequences of)
hypercontractivity of the Gaussian measure
(see Chapters 9 and 11 of
[
44
]
), drawing inspiration from
recent work
[
19
]
.

2.3
Our algorithm for halfspace testing when the volume is unknown:

Overview of
Section

1.1

We obtain the algorithm of
Section

1.1
, which does not need to have (an estimate of) the value of
p
, by combining the ideas underlying
Section

1.1
and
Section

1.1
, as well as bringing in new ideas. In particular, a crucial structural property of LTFs which we use for
Section

1.1
is the following: not only does the level-
1
Hermite weight of an LTF
f
depend only on the bias of the function
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[f]
as mentioned earlier, but under the Gaussian distribution LTFs are in fact
robustly extremal
, among all
\{0,1\}
-valued functions, in terms of how their level-1 Hermite weight depends on on the bias of the function.
In more detail, writing
h_{p}
to denote a halfspace with Gaussian volume
p
, it is the case that
any
function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
with Gaussian volume
p
must satisfy

\mathbf{W}^{=1}[f]\leq\mathbf{W}^{=1}[h_{p}].

(1)

(As alluded to in the last paragraph of
Section

2.2
, the LHS of the inequality above is precisely
\mathrm{Vol}(f)^{2}\cdot\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim f^{-1}(1)}[\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}].
)
Moreover the inequality can only be an equality if
f
is a halfspace, and it can only be close to an equality if
f
is close to a halfspace. Versions of this structural property are established, in varying degrees of explicitness, in
Theorem 7 of
[
34
]
, Theorem 26 of
[
37
]
, and Theorem 3.3.4 of

[
31
]
; our
Section

6.1
gives a version of this which is — crucially, for our purposes — quantitatively stronger than those earlier results in the small-
p
regime.

With this perspective, we can reinterpret the testing algorithm of
Section

1.1
in the following light: if the algorithm were
not
given
p
, it could compute an estimate
\tau
of
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim f^{-1}(1)}[\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}]
using draws from
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
and solve the equation

\tau={\frac{\mathbf{W}^{=1}[h_{p_{2}}]}{(p_{2})^{2}}}

for the value
p_{2}
. If
f
were an LTF and the value of
\tau
were precisely the value of
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim f^{-1}(1)}[\bm{x}\cdot\bm{y}]
, then this would give us the exact correct value
p_{2}=p
. On the other hand, because of the extremal property (
1
) mentioned above, if
f
is far from every LTF then (up to a small factor due to estimation error) the value of
p_{2}
will be
significantly larger than
the true value
p=\mathrm{Vol}(f).

Now let us return to
Section

1.1
; the key for our analysis is that this result relies on a robust extremal property of LTFs that
goes in the other direction
.
Recall that the
Section

1.1
algorithm essentially works by estimating

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim f^{-1}(1),\bm{y}\sim N_{\lambda}(\bm{x})}[f(\bm{x})\neq f(\bm{y})],\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim f^{-1}(1),\bm{y}\sim N_{\lambda}(\bm{x})}[f(\bm{y})=0]

(where “
\bm{y}\sim N_{\lambda}(\bm{x})
” means that
\bm{y}
is a
\lambda
-correlated random perturbation of
\bm{x}
; see
Section

4.2
for a formal definition),
and checking whether it takes the “right value” that it should take for an 0/1-valued LTF with expectation
p
, which is
{\frac{1}{2p}}\mathrm{Sens}_{\lambda}(LTF_{p})=C\sqrt{\lambda\ln(1/p)}.
In the
Section

1.1
algorithm we use a value of
\lambda
which depends on
p
, but for a
fixed
value of
\lambda
, the function
{\frac{1}{2p}}\mathrm{Sens}_{\lambda}(LTF_{p})
is a
decreasing
function of
p
; as we will see, this will be crucial for us.

Now, known isoperimetric properties of halfspaces can be shown to imply that if
f
is any
\{0,1\}
-valued function
f
with
\mathrm{Vol}(f)=p
, then
f
must (essentially) satisfy

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim f^{-1}(1),\bm{y}\sim N_{\lambda}(\bm{x})}[f(\bm{x})\neq f(\bm{y})]\geq{\frac{1}{2p}}\mathrm{Sens}_{\lambda}(h_{p});

i.e. that halfspaces are
minimizers
of the quantity
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim f^{-1}(1),\bm{y}\sim N_{\lambda}(\bm{x})}[f(\bm{x})\neq f(\bm{y})]
, which the algorithm of
Section

1.1
estimates from samples and queries.
This means that if we run the algorithm of
Section

1.1
with the value
p_{2}
obtained from the modified algorithm of
Section

1.1
as described earlier, there are two possibilities:

•

If
f
is a halfspace, then
p_{2}
will be (approximately) equal to the true value of
p
, and since we are running the algorithm of
Section

1.1
with (essentially) the right value of
p
, it will accept.

•

On the other hand, if
f
is far from every halfspace, then as explained earlier
p_{2}
will be significantly
larger
than the true value of
p
. But since
{\frac{1}{2p}}\mathrm{Sens}_{\lambda}(LTF_{p})
is a decreasing function of
p
, the algorithm of
Section

1.1
can only accept if it is run on a “guessed” value of
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
which is either
equal to or smaller than
the true value of
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
. So in this case, the algorithm will reject.

This concludes the overview of the proof of
Section

1.1
. One point of technical interest is that the actual proof of
Section

1.1
does not use
Section

1.1
as a black-box. In fact, unlike
Section

1.1
which crucially relies on the robust isoperimetry results of
[
2
]
,
Section

1.1
only relies on the classical result of Borell
[
7
]
which states that halfspaces are the minimizers of noise sensitivity. The result of Borell has many proofs by now, including some quite elementary (see
[
18
]
and references therein). So, arguably, the proof of
Section

1.1
is significantly simpler than that of
Section

1.1
(though conceptually,
Section

1.1
is inspired by
Section

1.1
).

Preliminaries for relative-error testing over
N(0,1)^{n}

We use boldfaced letters such as
\bm{x},\bm{f},\bm{A}
, etc. to denote random variables (which may be
real-valued,
vector-valued, function-valued, or set-valued; the intended type of the random variable will be clear from the context).
We write
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
to indicate that the random variable
\bm{x}
is distributed according to probability distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

We will write
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
for the collection of standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given two sets
A
and
B
, we use
A\,\triangle\,B
to denote their symmetric difference, i.e.
A\,\triangle\,B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.
As in
[
44
]
we write
\mathbb{N}
for the set
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

The Gaussian distribution and Gaussian relative-error testing.

We will write
N(0,I_{n})
to denote the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution, and denote its density function by
\varphi_{n}
, i.e., for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}}}\cdot e^{-\|x\|^{2}/2}.

We frequently write
\varphi\equiv\varphi_{1}
to denote the
one-dimensional standard Gaussian density.
We write
\mathrm{Vol}(K)
to denote the Gaussian measure of a (Lebesgue measurable) set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is

\mathrm{Vol}(K)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[\bm{x}\in K].

Given a
0/1
-valued function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, we write
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
to denote
\mathrm{Vol}(f^{-1}(1))
.

Finally, we will write
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
for the c.d.f. of
N(0,1)
, i.e.

\Phi(r)=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{g}\sim N(0,1)}{\left[\bm{g}\leq r\right]}=\int_{-\infty}^{r}\varphi(x)\,dx.

We will frequently use the following standard tail bound on (univariate) Gaussian random variables:

Proposition 5

(Proposition 2.1.2 of
[
47
]
or Exercise 2.2 of
[
48
]
)
.

Suppose
\bm{g}\sim N(0,1)
is a one-dimensional Gaussian random variable. Then for all
r>0
, we have

\left({\frac{1}{r}}-{\frac{1}{r^{3}}}\right)\cdot\varphi(r)\leq\Phi(-r)=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{g}\sim N(0,1)}[\bm{g}\geq r]\leq{\left({\frac{1}{r}}-{\frac{1}{r^{3}}+{\frac{3}{r^{5}}}}\right)}\cdot\varphi(r).

From
Section

3
we get the following estimate on
\Phi(\cdot)
that will be convenient:

Fact 6
.

Let
r
be any positive value bounded away from
0
by an absolute constant, e.g.
r\geq 0.01
.
Then we have

1-\Phi(r)=\Phi(-r)=\Theta(e^{-r^{2}/2}/r).

Gaussian relative-error testing.

The model of relative-error testing under the Gaussian distribution is a natural variant of the model of relative-error testing over
\{0,1\}^{n}
that was described earlier.
Now a call to
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
returns a draw from
N(0,1)^{n}|_{f^{-1}(1)}
, the standard Gaussian distribution conditioned on
f^{-1}(1)
.
Similar to before, a relative-error testing algorithm for
{\cal C}
must output “yes” with high probability (say at least 9/10; this success probability can be easily amplified) if

, and must output “no” with high probability (again, say at least 9/10) if

, where

\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,{\cal C})=\min_{g\in{\cal C}}\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)
and
the relative distance between
f
and
g
is defined as

\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)={\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(f^{-1}(1)\hskip 1.70709pt\triangle\hskip 1.70709ptg^{-1}(1))}{\mathrm{Vol}(f)}}.

.

.

For sample-based testing, we will use the following result due to Harms

.

. This is because if

4.1
Gaussian surface area

We begin by recalling the notion of surface area in the Gaussian space.

Definition 9
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The surface area of
A
(under the standard Gaussian measure
\varphi_{n}
) is given by

\mathsf{surf}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(A_{\delta})-\mathrm{Vol}(A)}{\delta},

where
A_{\delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}d(x,A)\leq\delta\}
. For sets
A
with a smooth boundary,
\mathsf{surf}(A)
is equivalent to

\int_{x\in\partial A}\varphi_{n}(x)d\sigma(x),

where
d\sigma(x)
is the standard surface area element in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\partial A
denotes the boundary of the set
A
.

There is a close connection between the notion of surface area and noise sensitivity, as shown by Ledoux
[
35
]
:

Theorem 10
.

For any
t\geq 0
and any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
{\cal C}^{1}
boundary,
we have

\mathbf{NS}_{t}(A)\leq\frac{2\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\mathsf{surf}(A).

A near-converse of this result was shown by Neeman
[
42
]
:
every set
A
as above is close to a set
B
whose surface area can be bounded in terms of the noise sensitivity of
A
.

Theorem 11

(Theorem 1.2, Theorem 2.1 of
[
42
]
)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set with
{\cal C}^{1}
boundary and let
t,\xi>0
.
Then, there exists a set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

1.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(A\ \triangle\ B)\leq\frac{\mathbf{NS}_{t}(A)}{\xi}
;
and

2.

\displaystyle\mathsf{surf}(B)\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\cdot\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\log(1/\xi)}}\bigg)\bigg)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{e^{2t}-1}}\cdot\mathbf{NS}_{t}(A)
.

We recall (see e.g. Chapter 5 of
[
44
]
) that the function

I(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))

(2)

is sometimes known as the
Gaussian isoperimetric function
(cf.
Section

4.1
below). This function will be used in various ways throughout our arguments, including in the proof of the following simple fact:

Fact 12
.

Let
0<q\leq p\leq 1/2
and
p/q\leq(1+\varepsilon)
. Then,

\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))\geq\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(q))\geq\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)}\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p)).

Proof.

The function
I(\cdot)
is easily seen to be monotonically non-decreasing in the interval
(0,1/2]
, which gives the first inequality.
Moreover, it is known that the function
I(p)
is concave (see Exercise 5.24
[
44
]
). This implies that

I(q)\geq\frac{q}{p}I(p)\geq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}I(p).\qed

We will also need some basic facts about the function
\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))/p
which we state here.

Claim 13
.

The function
R(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))}{p}
is decreasing in the interval
(0,1/2)
.

Proof.

Let
s\in(-\infty,0)
be chosen so that
\int_{-\infty}^{s}\varphi(t)dt=p
. Then, note that

R(p)=\frac{\varphi(s)}{\int_{-\infty}^{s}\varphi(t)dt}.\qed

We also need the following simple fact characterizing the surface area of any halfspace in the Gaussian space in terms of its volume; the proof of the final equality is a calculation using standard bounds on the Gaussian pdf and cdf, in particular
Section

3
.

Fact 14
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be a halfspace and let
\mathrm{Vol}(f^{-1}(1))=p
. Then

\mathsf{surf}(f^{-1}(1))=\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))=\Theta\left(p\sqrt{\ln(1/\min\{p,1-p\})}\right).

4.2
Other basic algorithmic and structural ingredients

Gaussian noise sensitivity.
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
t\geq 0
, we let
N_{t}(x)
denote the distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
e^{-t}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\bm{y}
where
\bm{y}\sim N(0,I_{n})
.
Further, for any function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
, we let
P_{t}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{\bm{y}\sim N_{t}(x)}[f(\bm{y})]
.

Definition 15
.

For any Boolean function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
t\geq 0
, we define

\mathbf{NS}_{t}(f)
(read as the “noise sensitivity” of
f
) as

\mathbf{NS}_{t}(f)=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n}),\bm{y}\sim N_{t}(\bm{x})}[f(\bm{x})\not=f(\bm{y})].

For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\mathbf{NS}_{t}(A)
to be
\mathbf{NS}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{A})
where
\mathbf{1}_{A}(\cdot)
is the indicator function of
A
.

We observe that using calls to
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
and
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
, we can efficiently estimate the (normalized) noise sensitivity of
f
:

Lemma 16

(Estimating the normalized noise sensitivity)
.

For noise parameter
t>0
and error parameter
\kappa>0
, there is an algorithm
Est-Sense
which for any

f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, given query and sample access to
f
,
outputs a
\pm\kappa

accurate additive estimate to
\mathbf{NS}_{t}(f)/\mathrm{Vol}[f]
with confidence
0.99
using
\Theta(\kappa^{-2})
samples from
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
and the same number of queries to
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
.

Proof.

Sample
\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
and
\bm{y}\sim N_{t}(\bm{x})
. Then,
note that

\mathbf{NS}_{t}(f)=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\big[f(\bm{x})\not=f(\bm{y})\big]=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\big[\bm{x}\in f^{-1}(1)\land\bm{y}\not\in f^{-1}(1)\big]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\big[\bm{y}\in f^{-1}(1)\land\bm{x}\not\in f^{-1}(1)\big].

As the distribution of
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
is exchangeable, it follows that

\mathbf{NS}_{t}(f)=2\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\big[\bm{x}\in f^{-1}(1)\land\bm{y}\not\in f^{-1}(1)\big]=2\mathrm{Vol}(f)\cdot\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\big[f(\bm{y})=0\hskip 2.27626pt|\hskip 2.27626ptf(\bm{x})=1\big].

Standard sampling shows that the RHS probabilty can be estimated to error
\pm\kappa/2
with confidence
0.99
using
\Theta(\kappa^{-2})
calls to
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
and the same number of queries to
f
. The lemma follows.
∎

Another key ingredient we require is the following deep result of Barchiesi et al.
[
2
]
which obtains a sharp stability version of the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality. In particular, they establish the following:

Theorem 17

(Main theorem of
[
2
]
and subsequent discussion: sharp stability version of Gaussian isoperimetric inequality)
.

Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
s\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\mathrm{Vol}(E)=\Phi(s)
, i.e., the Gaussian volume of
E
is the same as that of the one-dimensional halfspace
\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\leq s\}
. Define the
Gaussian isoperimetric deficit
(namely, the gap between the two sides of the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality) to be

D(E)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathsf{surf}(E)-\varphi\big(\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{Vol}(E)\big)=\mathsf{surf}(E)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-s^{2}/2}.

Then, there is a halfspace
H=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x\cdot\omega\leq s\}
(for some unit vector
\omega
) such that

\mathrm{Vol}\big(E\triangle H\big)^{2}\leq C^{\prime}(1+s^{2})e^{-s^{2}/2}\cdot D(E),

where
C^{\prime}>0
is a universal constant (which can be taken to be at most
320\sqrt{2}\pi^{2}
).

Remark 18
.

The main theorem in
[
2
]
defines the isoperimetric deficit
D(E)=\mathsf{surf}(E)-e^{-s^{2}/2}
— i.e. vis-a-vis their definition, we have an extra factor of
1/\sqrt{2\pi}
. The reason is just that while our definition of the surface area of a set
A
is
\int_{x\in\partial A}\varphi_{n}(x)d\sigma(x)
, the definition in
[
2
]
is equivalent to defining it as
\smash{\int_{x\in\partial A}\sqrt{2\pi}\varphi_{n}(x)d\sigma(x)}
.

An easy corollary of this is the following:

Corollary 19
.

Let
E
be a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathrm{Vol}(E)=p
with
p\leq 0.1
.
Suppose

D(E)\leq\frac{\beta^{2}p}{{\log^{3/2}(1/p)}}

for some
\beta
. Then there is a halfspace
H
that satisfies
\mathrm{Vol}(H)=p

and
\mathrm{Vol}(E\ \triangle\ H)\leq C\beta p

for some absolute constant
C>0
.
(Recalling
\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))=\Theta(p\sqrt{\log(1/p)})
, an equivalent statement is if

D(E)\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{\log^{2}(1/p)}\cdot\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p)),

then there is a halfspace
H
that satisfies
\mathrm{Vol}(H)=p
and
\mathrm{Vol}(E\ \triangle\ H)\leq C\beta p
.)

Proof.

Let
H=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{1}\leq s\}
be a one-dimensional halfspace such that
\mathrm{Vol}(H)=p
. Note that

\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))={\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}}e^{-s^{2}/2}

so
e^{-s^{2}/2}=\Theta(\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p)))

and using
\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))=\Theta(p\sqrt{\log(1/p)})
,
we have

s^{2}=\Theta(\log(1/p))
.

Now, applying
Section

4.2
, we have that there is a halfspace

H
with
\mathrm{Vol}(H)=p
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(E\triangle H)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\log(1/p)\cdot\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))\cdot\frac{\beta^{2}}{\log^{2}(1/p)}\cdot\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))\right).

This finishes the proof by plugging in

\varphi(\Phi^{-1}(p))=\Theta(p\sqrt{\log(1/p)})
.
∎

5.1
Hermite analysis over

Our notation and terminology follow Chapter 11 of
[
44
]
. We say that an
n

is a tuple

|\alpha|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}.

For

to denote the space of functions

that have finite second moment under the Gaussian distribution, i.e.

\|f\|^{2}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}\left[f(\bm{x})^{2}\right]<\infty.

We view

as an inner product space with

\langle f,g\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}\big[f(\bm{x})g(\bm{x})\big].

We recall the Hermite basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
:

Definition 22

(Hermite basis)
.

The
Hermite polynomials

(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are the univariate polynomials

h_{j}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

For example, we have

h_{0}(x)=1,\quad h_{1}(x)=x,\quad\text{and}\quad h_{2}(x)=\frac{x^{2}-1}{\sqrt{2}}.

The following fact is standard:

Fact 23

(Proposition 11.33 of
[
44
]
)
.

The Hermite polynomials
(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
form a complete, orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

For
n>1
, the collection of
n
-variate polynomials given by
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
where

h_{\alpha}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})

forms a complete, orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we define its
Hermite coefficient on

\alpha
as
\widehat{f}(\alpha)=\langle f,h_{\alpha}\rangle
. It follows that
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be uniquely expressed as

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)h_{\alpha}

with the equality holding in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; we will refer to this expansion as the
Hermite expansion
of
f
. One can check that Parseval’s and Plancharel’s identities hold in this setting:

{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)\widehat{g}(\alpha).

It is also readily verified that
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[f(\bm{x})]=\widehat{f}(0^{n})
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[f(\bm{x})]=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0^{n}}\widehat{f}(\alpha)^{2}
where
{\bm{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}
.

Finally, we write
\smash{\mathbf{W}^{=k}[f]}
for the
Hermite weight of
f
at level-
k
, i.e.

\mathbf{W}^{=k}[f]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\widehat{f}(\alpha)^{2},

with
\mathbf{W}^{\leq k}[f]
defined similarly.

We will require bounds on the level-
1
and
2

\mathbb{R}^{n}
; towards this, we recall the

(see e.g. Proposition 11 of
[

]

[

]

Proposition 24

[
19
]
)

Suppose

We note that

Lemma 28

Suppose

Proof.

Consider the function

since

is decreasing, we have
V^{-1}(x)\leq 0.1
for all
x\in[a,a+\zeta]
and thus,

V^{-1}(a)-V^{-1}(a+\zeta)\leq\zeta\cdot\Theta\left({V^{-1}(a)}\right).

For the other direction, when
c_{0}
is sufficiently small, we have
from the inequality above that

V^{-1}(a)-V^{-1}(a+\zeta)\leq\zeta\cdot\Theta\left({V^{-1}(a)}\right)\leq c\cdot\Theta\left({V^{-1}(a)}\right)\leq 0.1\cdot{V^{-1}(a)}

and thus,
V^{-1}(a+\zeta)\geq 0.9\cdot V^{-1}(a)
.
As a result, we have

V^{-1}(a)-V^{-1}(a+\zeta)\geq\zeta\cdot\Theta\left({V^{-1}(a+\zeta)}\right)\geq\zeta\cdot\Theta\left(V^{-1}(a)\right).

This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We have the following corollary of
Section

6.1
:

Corollary 38
.

Let
0<p,\widehat{p}\leq 0.1
, with
a\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=V(p)
and

\widehat{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=V(\widehat{p})
such that
|a-\widehat{a}|\leq c_{0}
for some sufficiently small constant
c_{0}
. Then there are two positive
constants
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
such that

1.

|\widehat{p}-p|\leq\tau_{1}p|\widehat{a}-a|
; and

2.

If
a-\widehat{a}\geq 0
, then
\widehat{p}-p\geq\tau_{2}p(a-\widehat{a})
.

Proof.

The only thing worth pointing out is that when
a,\widehat{a}
satisfy

for some sufficiently small constant
c_{0}
,
by the same argument above, we have
0.9\widehat{p}\leq p\leq\widehat{p}/0.9
.
∎

Appendix A
A lower bound for sample-based testers

Theorem 40

(Sample-based Gaussian LTF testing lower bound, if we are not given
p
)
.

Let
A
be any algorithm which uses only samples from an unknown measurable
\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
(so in particular,
A
is not given an estimate of
\mathrm{Vol}[f]
, nor does it have black-box oracle access to
f
). If
A
is a relative-error
0.1
-testing algorithm for LTFs over
N(0,I_{n})
, then
A
must make
\Omega(n)
samples from
f
, even under the guarantee that
\mathrm{Vol}(f)
is either
1/2
or
1
.

The proof is an easy consequence of the following lower bound, which is proven in the arXiv version of
[
22
]
:

Claim 41
.

Let
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
be the standard Gaussian CDF.
Let
A^{\prime}
be any algorithm which is given i.i.d. samples drawn from a distribution
{\cal D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is either:

•

Case 1:
{\cal D}
is
N(0,I_{n})
; or

•

Case 2:
{\cal D}
is
N(0,I_{n})|_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Phi^{-1}(1/4)\leq u\cdot x\leq\Phi^{-1}(3/4)\}}
for some unknown unit vector
u

(so in Case 2,
{\cal D}
is the standard Normal distribution conditioned on an unknown origin-centered “slab” of Gaussian volume 1/2). Suppose that with probability at least 99/100, algorithm
A^{\prime}
correctly identifies whether
{\cal D}

A^{\prime}
must use
\Omega(n)
