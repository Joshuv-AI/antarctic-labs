VoidPadding: Let [VOID] Handle Padding in Masked Diffusion Language Models so that [EOS] Can Focus on Semantic Termination
Chunyu Liu, Zhengyang Fan, Kaisen Yang, Alex Lamb

Abstract
MDLMs generate text by denoising a preallocated masked response canvas, making response-length modeling central to instruction tuning. Existing MDLMs often inherit the autoregressive convention of using repeated [EOS] tokens for padding during instruction tuning, giving [EOS] a dual role as both a semantic terminator and a padding token. We show that this dual role is a root cause of [EOS] overflow under large-block decoding. To decouple these roles, we propose VoidPadding, which introduces [VOID] for padding and reserves [EOS] for termination. During inference, the learned [EOS] signal enables early stopping, while the learned [VOID] signal guides adaptive response canvas expansion. On Dream-7B-Instruct, VoidPadding improves the block-size-averaged four-task mean across mathematical reasoning and code generation benchmarks by + 17.84 +17.84 points over the original model and + 6.95 +6.95 points over RainbowPadding, while reducing decoding NFE by 55.7% on average. Code is available at https://github.com/Haru-LCY/VoidPadding .

Introduction

Masked diffusion language models
4
;
47
;
35
;
34
(MDLMs) offer a promising alternative to autoregressive language models
39
(ARLMs). Whereas ARLMs generate tokens sequentially from left to right, MDLMs use bidirectional attention to denoise the entire sequence in parallel
7
. This bidirectional conditioning, however, makes MDLMs sensitive to the padding tokens introduced in instruction tuning.

During instruction tuning
14
, instruction–response pairs are typically processed in batches for efficiency and padded to the
maximum sequence length within each mini-batch.
Following a convention inherited from ARLM training, MDLMs often use repeated
[EOS]
tokens as
padding
38
. In ARLMs, causal
masking
(
46
)
prevents earlier positions from conditioning on

[EOS]
padding, so
[EOS]
primarily serves as a
semantic terminator. In MDLMs, however,
[EOS]
padding is also included in the
training target. Thus,
[EOS]
takes on two
roles: the semantic terminator role and the padding role.

During inference, MDLMs generate on a predefined response canvas whose positions are jointly modeled under bidirectional
attention
12
.
The padding role of
[EOS]
introduced during instruction tuning therefore offers a length signal: high
[EOS]
confidence
near the tail indicates that the current canvas may exceed the desired output length
32
;
53
. However, this signal
becomes fragile under large-block decoding.
Once high-confidence tail
[EOS]
tokens are generated, they become part of the denoising context and can further increase nearby

[EOS]
probabilities, leading to short effective responses and repeated
[EOS]
.
This failure mode is
known as
[EOS]
overflow
(
28
)
.

During inference,
[VOID]

, and the canvas may be expanded based on tail

Based on these insights, we propose

[VOID]

Training sequences are formatted as

[VOID]
padding.
During inference,
[VOID]

[EOS]

, which stops decoding once no masks remain before the
leftmost visible
[EOS]
.
This prevents
[EOS]
overflow and avoids redundant generation after the

Additionally, we propose an optional inference-time

[VOID]

length signal to expand insufficient canvases before denoising.

Our contributions are:

1.

We systematically analyze the mechanism behind
[EOS]

[EOS]

[VOID]

[EOS]

[EOS]

Preliminary

2.1
Masked Diffusion Modeling

MDLMs generate by denoising discrete
sequences
(
44
;
41
)
.
Let
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\ldots,V\}
be the token vocabulary,
\mathsf{M}\notin\mathcal{V}

the mask token, and
\mathbf{z}_{t}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\{\mathsf{M}\})^{L}
the sequence state
at diffusion step
t\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
, where
T
is the total number of denoising steps.
Here,
t=0
denotes clean data and
t=T
the fully masked sequence.
The masked and visible positions are denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{i:z_{t}^{i}=\mathsf{M}\}
and

\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{t}=\{1,\ldots,L\}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{t}
, respectively.

The forward process gradually corrupts a clean sequence
\mathbf{z}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
by independently masking each position over
T
steps.
At each step, tokens are progressively replaced by
\mathsf{M}
according to a fixed schedule, so that
\mathbf{z}_{T}
is fully masked.
During training, a timestep
t
is sampled and the model predicts the original token at each masked position, yielding the denoising cross-entropy objective

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{0},t,\mathbf{z}_{t}}\left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}-\log p_{\theta}(z_{0}^{i}\mid\mathbf{z}_{t})\right].

This objective trains the denoiser on various masking patterns, enabling

p_{\theta}
to predict masked tokens from different visible contexts
(
30
)
.

MDLM decoding procedures include block diffusion
(
3
)
and pure
diffusion
(
56
)
.
Both of them start from a fully masked canvas of length
L
with block size
B
,
where pure diffusion corresponds to
B=L
.
At each step, the model uses bidirectional attention to condition on the whole sequence
and predicts
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z}_{t})
for all
i\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
in parallel,
so one denoising step corresponds to one model forward pass.
Decoding then proceeds in two steps within the current block.
First, it selects commitment positions, commonly using
confidence
9
or negative entropy scores
(
6
)
.
Second, it selects tokens for these positions, typically by argmax under

p_{\theta}
.
Decoding moves to the next block once the current block is fully committed and terminates
when all masks have been filled.

2.2
Instruction-Tuning Data Construction

Instruction tuning (IT) trains pretrained language models on prompt–response pairs,
improving instruction following and zero-shot generalization
14
.
For efficient batching, prompt–response sequences are terminated by

[EOS]
and padded to a common length.
MDLM IT often follows the ARLM convention of using
[EOS]
as the padding token.
Let
B=\{(p_{k},r_{k})\}_{k=1}^{b}
be a mini-batch of size
b
, where
p_{k}
is the prompt
of length
P_{k}
and
r_{k}
is the raw response of length
\ell_{k}
.
Over the IT data distribution, let
\ell
denote the raw response length before the
terminal
[EOS]
, and define its CDF as
F(m)=\Pr(\ell\leq m)
.
After appending one semantic terminator
[EOS]
, each full sequence is padded with

[EOS]
to the maximum full-sequence length in the mini-batch,
S_{B}=\max_{1\leq k\leq b}(P_{k}+\ell_{k}+1)
.
Thus, example
k
has padded response
y_{k}=r_{k}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}^{L_{B,k}-\ell_{k}-1}
of length
L_{B,k}=S_{B}-P_{k}
.
During IT, both noise and loss are applied only to
y_{k}
.

3
Analysis of
[EOS]
Overflow

[EOS]
overflow is a common issue of MDLM inference under large block sizes
(
28
)
.
In a typical case, the model produces a very short answer followed by repeated
[EOS]
tokens, as shown in Figure
3
(a).
However,
[EOS]
overflow is not observed in ARLMs, even though both ARLMs and MDLMs use
[EOS]
as padding tokens during IT.
This motivates us to ask:
Why is
[EOS]
padding safe in ARLMs but fragile in MDLMs?

Observation 1:
[EOS]
overflow is not caused by limited large-block generation capacity.

We first test whether
[EOS]
overflow is caused by insufficient large-block generation capacity.
Specifically, we ban
[EOS]
commitment in LLaDA-8B-Instruct by setting the
[EOS]
logit to
-\infty
throughout decoding, and then evaluate on GSM8K
15
with
L=B=512
.
Although this removes the semantic stopping signal and often causes repeated reasoning traces and answers, it raises the accuracy of the first extracted answer from 28.43% to 70.66%.
Thus, the model is not inherently unable to generate large blocks.

as a learned length signal. Initial

Observation 2: Training with
[EOS]
padding teaches
[EOS]
to act as a length signal.

Next, we examine whether
[EOS]
padding provides an additional signal in MDLM
training beyond semantic termination.
Specifically, we train an
[EOS]
-padding diagnostic model on LLaDA-8B-Base, with
training details provided in Appendix
A
.
Following Section
2.2
, for each mini-batch

B=\{(p_{k},r_{k})\}_{k=1}^{b}
, we construct the padded response

y_{k}=r_{k}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}^{L_{B,k}-\ell_{k}-1},

where
L_{B,k}=S_{B}-P_{k}
and
S_{B}=\max_{1\leq k\leq b}(P_{k}+\ell_{k}+1)
.
We define response-side positions using a 1-indexed coordinate
i\in\{1,\dots,1024\}
.
For the padded data distribution, we record the raw response-length CDF
F(i-1)=\Pr(\ell\leq i-1)
defined in Section
2.2
, along with the

[EOS]
token ratio
p_{i}(e)
at response-side position
i
.
For the model distribution, we use the same batch-padded examples, mask the entire padded
response
y_{k}
while keeping the prompt
p_{k}
visible, and report the initial

[EOS]
probability
q_{i}(e)
from a single forward pass through the diagnostic
model.
As shown in Figure
2
, the three curves closely
align,
F(i-1)\approx p_{i}(e)\approx q_{i}(e)
, indicating that
[EOS]
padding teaches
the model to associate
[EOS]
with desirable response length.

Observation 3: Large–block inference converts the
[EOS]
length signal into overflow.

We analyze a large-block decoding trajectory of LLaDA-8B-Instruct with

L=B=512
. Figure
3
(a) shows a
two-stage
[EOS]
overflow pattern. In Stage 1, on the initial fully
masked canvas, tail positions have very high
[EOS]
probability and

[EOS]
is committed in the initial steps. In Stage 2, on a
canvas with a committed
[EOS]
suffix,
[EOS]
commitments move
leftward: positions before the suffix also take
[EOS]
as their
committed token, producing overflow.
We then formalize this pattern under the idealized version of
Observation
2
.
Let
i
index response-side positions and let
e
denote
[EOS]
.
For a training-time EOS-padded target, let
\bar{Y}_{i}
be the token at
position
i
, and write
p_{i}(e)=\Pr(\bar{Y}_{i}=e)
. During inference, let

q_{i}
be the model distribution at masked canvas position
i
, let

\hat{y}_{i}=\arg\max_{v}q_{i}(v)
be its proposed token, and let
Y_{i}
be the
token committed at canvas position
i
.

For Stage 1, on a fully masked response canvas, we idealize
Observation
2
as

q_{i}(e)=p_{i}(e)=F(i-1).

Since standard MDLM decoding uses argmax token
proposals (Section
2.1
),
q_{i}(e)>1/2

implies
\hat{y}_{i}=e
.
Assume the canvas is long enough that
F(L-1)>1/2
. On the initial fully
masked response canvas, we call the suffix of positions with
q_{i}(e)>1/2
the
high-confidence
[EOS]
tail, and define its beginning as

r=\min\{i\in\{1,\dots,L\}:F(i-1)>1/2\}.

For Stage 2, let
S_{e}=\{Y_{r}=\cdots=Y_{L}=e\}
denote the committed

[EOS]
suffix event during inference, and let

\bar{S}_{e}=\{\bar{Y}_{r}=\cdots=\bar{Y}_{L}=e\}
denote the corresponding
training-time
[EOS]
padding suffix event.
We assume that after
S_{e}
occurs, the model’s
[EOS]
probability for
earlier positions matches the corresponding training-time
[EOS]
-padded
conditional probability: for any
i<r
,

q_{i}(e\mid S_{e})=p_{i}(e\mid\bar{S}_{e})=\Pr(\bar{Y}_{i}=e\mid\bar{S}_{e}).

Theorem 1

(Stage 1: initial
[EOS]
commitments)
.

If the first block reaches the high-confidence
[EOS]
tail,
r\leq B
,
then every selected position
j\in\{r,\dots,B\}
is committed as

[EOS]
, i.e.,
Y_{j}=e
.

Theorem 2

(Stage 2: leftward spread of
[EOS]
commitments)
.

If
S_{e}
has occurred, then for any
i<r
satisfying

2F(i-1)>F(r-1)
, selecting position
i
in a later denoising step yields

Y_{i}=e
.

Proofs of the theorems are given in
Appendix
B
.

We next perform an intervention motivated by
Theorem
2
: after a position is committed as

[EOS]
, we set attention scores to that position to
-\infty
, as
shown in Figure
3
(b).
Initial
[EOS]
commitments remain, but the model avoids

[EOS]
overflow and generates the correct answer. This shows that Stage 2
is the dominant driver of
[EOS]
overflow.

overflow stems from a

[EOS]
is expected to serve only as a semantic terminator. In ARLM IT, causal masking hides
padding
[EOS]
tokens from earlier positions, so
[EOS]
remains
a semantic terminator and overflow does not arise.

4
Method

Motivated by this training–inference role mismatch, we propose

VoidPadding
, which introduces a new
[VOID]
token as the
padding token, so that
[EOS]
serves only as the semantic terminator.
We further design
VoidExpansion
for inference, which uses the

[VOID]
length signal learned during IT to expand an insufficient
canvas before decoding.
Together, this training–inference co-design decouples padding from semantic
termination while preserving the hidden length signal carried by

[VOID]
tokens.
Figure
1
provides an overview of the method, with
details given in the following subsections.

4.1
VoidPadding

Instruction-Tuning Design.
VoidPadding follows the IT data construction
described in Section
2.2
, except that
padding
[EOS]
tokens are replaced by
[VOID]
tokens. Thus, for

B=\{(p_{k},r_{k})\}_{k=1}^{b}
and
L_{B,k}=S_{B}-P_{k}
, the padded response is

y_{k}^{\mathrm{VOID}}=r_{k}\oplus\texttt{[EOS]}\oplus\texttt{[VOID]}^{L_{B,k}-\ell_{k}-1}.

We then follow the standard MDLM training procedure in
Section
2.1
, with noise injection and loss
computation performed on
y_{k}^{\mathrm{VOID}}
.
Notably, we apply standard MDLM masking for non-
[EOS]
positions.
The single
[EOS]
token in each example is always masked, forcing the
model to predict it as a stable semantic terminator.

Inference Design.

During inference,
[VOID]
remains a hidden padding signal and is never
generated: setting its logit to
-\infty
before token selection prevents the
high-confidence
[VOID]
tail from becoming visible output.
We also propose
[EOS]
Termination
, which stops decoding once
all positions before the leftmost visible
[EOS]
are unmasked and then
discards the rest of the canvas.
In contrast,
VanillaStopping
, the standard MDLM stopping rule, stops
only after all masked positions are decoded
(Section
2.1
), causing redundant decoding
after the semantic endpoint.

4.2
VoidExpansion

As discussed in Section
2.1
, MDLM decoding
starts from a pre-defined response canvas with fixed length
L
and block size

B
. When this canvas is too short, generation quality drops sharply
(Appendix Figure
8
).
Motivated by this short-canvas degradation, VoidExpansion uses the learned

[VOID]
length signal to expand the canvas to a sufficient, block-aligned length

\hat{L}=nB
before decoding.

Similar to how IT with
[EOS]
padding induces a length signal in

[EOS]
(Observation
2
), IT with

[VOID]
padding induces a
[VOID]
length signal.
Specifically, sufficient canvases have redundant tails that prefer

[VOID]
, whereas insufficient canvases retain tail positions that favor
non-
[VOID]
tokens.
We therefore quantify this
[VOID]
length signal as follows.
Let
p
denote the prompt tokens,
\mathsf{M}
the mask token, and
w
the
tail-window size.
At canvas length
\ell
, we run one forward pass on

p\oplus\mathsf{M}^{\ell}
and define the tail window

\mathcal{T}(\ell)
as the last
\min(w,\ell)
response-canvas positions,
indexed by
i
:

\displaystyle p_{i}^{\mathrm{void}}

\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(x_{i}=\texttt{[VOID]}\mid p\oplus\mathsf{M}^{\ell}),

\displaystyle p_{i}^{\mathrm{nonvoid}}

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{\texttt{[VOID]}\}}p_{\theta}(x_{i}=a\mid p\oplus\mathsf{M}^{\ell}),

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{nonvoid}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{T}(\ell)|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}(\ell)}p_{i}^{\mathrm{nonvoid}},

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{gap}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{T}(\ell)|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}(\ell)}\left(p_{i}^{\mathrm{void}}-p_{i}^{\mathrm{nonvoid}}\right).

Here
s_{\mathrm{nonvoid}}
measures tail preference for non-
[VOID]

tokens, while
s_{\mathrm{gap}}
measures tail preference for
[VOID]

over the strongest non-
[VOID]
alternative at each position.
Thus, high
s_{\mathrm{nonvoid}}
or low
s_{\mathrm{gap}}
suggests that
the current canvas is insufficient.

VoidExpansion uses this
[VOID]
length signal to expand the insufficient
canvas, as shown in Algorithm
1

It defines a canvas as sufficient when

s_{\mathrm{nonvoid}}(L)\leq\tau_{\mathrm{nonvoid}}\>\text{and}\>s_{\mathrm{gap}}(L)\geq\tau_{\mathrm{gap}}.

Starting from the block-aligned initial canvas length
L=\lceil L_{0}/B\rceil B

L

met, it expands the canvas by one block and repeats until the criterion is met or
the maximum length restriction

Related Work

Diffusion Language Models.

Diffusion language models (DLMs) can be broadly categorized into continuous DLMs
(
17
;
24
;
27
;
43
)
, which operate in embedding spaces, and discrete DLMs
(
22
;
42
;
16
)
, which operate over discrete token spaces.
Masked diffusion language models
(
21
;
19
;
4
)
(MDLMs) are a representative discrete class that iteratively predicts masked tokens.
Recent large-scale MDLMs, including LLaDA series
38
;
57
;
8
;
7
, Dream series
56
;
52
, SDAR
12
, TraDo
47
, and WeDLM
34
, show strong complex reasoning and long-text generation abilities.
Recent work also improves generation quality through better samplers
(
54
)
.

Efficient MDLMs.

Although MDLMs support parallel token prediction, inference remains costly because generation usually requires many denoising iterations with full-sequence bidirectional attention. Work on efficient MDLM inference includes sampling methods that reduce denoising steps or finalize more tokens per step
(
33
;
26
;
49
)
, cache-based methods that reuse computation via approximate caching or KV caching
50
;
25
;
45
;
51
;
36
;
48
, and training-based distillation for fewer-step generation
(
37
;
2
;
11
)
.

Variable-Length Generation.

MDLMs often require a preallocated output canvas, wasting computation on over-allocated slots and limiting usability
(
18
)
. Length allocation methods address this issue
13
. Inference-only methods, including SmartCrop
40
,
\rho
-
[EOS]

53
, and DAEDAL
32
, typically use
[EOS]
as a length signal, while training-based methods include dLLM-Var
(
55
)
, which learns reliable
[EOS]
prediction for block-wise termination, and FlexMDM
(
29
)
, which decouples length prediction from token prediction.

8
Conclusion

We show that a root cause of
[EOS]
overflow in MDLMs is a
training–inference role mismatch:
[EOS]
is trained as both padding
and semantic terminator, but used only for termination at inference.
VoidPadding instead uses
[VOID]
as padding tokens and reserves

[EOS]
for semantic endings, enabling VoidExpansion for canvas
allocation and
[EOS]
Termination for early stopping.
Together, our training–inference co-design improves large-block robustness,
decoding efficiency, and variable-length generation.

Limitations

Many MDLM training and inference conventions are inherited from ARLMs.
Our study focuses on one concrete failure mode of this transfer:

[EOS]
padding, which is largely benign under causal masking, can
become fragile under bidirectional denoising because padding positions can
serve as both context and denoising targets.
Thus,
[EOS]
overflow should be viewed as one symptom of a broader
class of ARLM-inherited assumptions that may fail in MDLMs.
Other choices, including data formatting, special-token semantics, stopping
rules, and batching strategies, may require similarly MDLM-specific design.

Our analysis also suggests that
[EOS]
overflow is not primarily a model
capacity failure. Instead, it emerges from the interaction between a learned
length prior, bidirectional context, and standard large-block sampling rules.
VoidPadding mitigates the padding–termination mismatch, but the design of
effective MDLM samplers remains open.
Future samplers may need to jointly consider token confidence, commitment
timing, canvas allocation, and stopping decisions under bidirectional denoising.
