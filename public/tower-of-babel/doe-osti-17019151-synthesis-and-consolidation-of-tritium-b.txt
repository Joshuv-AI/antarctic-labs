Abstract:

In this study, the mechanical properties of Li 4SiO4 and Li2ZrO3 are evaluated to
determine their feasibilities as a tritium breeder ceramic. Materials were synthesized
using Li2CO3 micropowder as well as SiO2 and ZrO2 nanopowders. Samples were
subjected to ring-on-ring tests to analyze their flexural strengths. Weibull analysis was
performed to predict failure of samples when subjected to 20 MPa of stress. Results
suggest a bright future for Li4SiO4 as several sintered samples met the minimum
threshold for flexural strength as well as density. Alternatively, initial data for Li 2ZrO3
seems to suggest it would be an impractical choice for a tritium breeder ceramic as the
desired mechanical properties were not met.

I. Introduction

 Inertial Fusion Energy (IFE) is dependent on a Deuterium-Tritium (D-T) fusion
reaction. Given that tritium is not readily available in nature and has a short half-life of
only 12.3 years, in situ generation of tritium is required [1]. The D-T reaction produces
14.1 MeV neutrons which can irradiate a breeder material and create tritium as a
byproduct. However, not all neutrons from the D-T reaction are available due to
absorption in the breeder blanket or leakage through gaps [2]. Thus, it is essential to
choose a material that has a high cross section, in order to maximize the number of
neutrons being used to generate tritium.

 In order for a reaction to be self-sustaining, it must be able to produce one
tritium atom for each one used in the reaction. Lithium is of special interest as it has the
highest cross section for producing tritium when interacting with a neutron [2]. Lithium
on its own is too weak, so a lithium compound must be used to maintain the attractive

cross section properties while also obtaining critical mechanical properties that are
needed to survive the harsh environment inside a reactor.

 All compounds must meet 5 primary material constraints shown in figure 2 in
order to be a viable option for a breeder material [2]. For this study, the two elements
selected are Si and Zr which are used to form Li4SiO4 and Li2ZrO3. Li4SiO4 is of interest
because of its high molar lithium concentration which can help achieve high tritium
breeding ratios (TBR). It is already the material of choice for the helium cooled solid
breeder test blanket module in China [3]. Li2ZrO3 on the other hand has a desirable low
tritium release temperature and high TBR [4-5]. Together, these two elements form the
compounds of interest for this study and are evaluated for their mechanical properties.

II. Materials and Methods

 Li4SiO4 and Li2ZrO3 were both synthesized with Li2CO3 micro-powder obtained
from Thermo Fisher Scientific as the lithium source. The SiO2 and ZrO2 nanopowders
(30nm) were obtained from US Research Nanomaterials. In order to account for Li2O
volatilization at elevated temperatures, excess Li 2CO3 was used to ensure a complete
reaction. From previous literature [7], it was found that 10 mol% excess Li2CO3 was
sufficient for a complete reaction of Li2ZrO3, while 20 mol% excess had to be used for

Li4SiO4 due to the increased lithium. The following chemical formulas were used for the

The precursors were wet ball milled using deionized water for 2 hours in a HDPE mill jar
with 10mm polyacrylate milling media. The milled mixture was then placed into a
furnace at 150 °C to dry overnight. Following the drying procedure, the powder is de-
agglomerated using a mortar and pestle before being placed into a furnace again to
synthesize at 700 °C for 8h and 900 °C for 8h for the Li4SiO4 and Li2ZrO3 respectively.

Figure 1: Schematic of powder processing procedure. De-agglomerating occurs in between ball
milling and depositing into an alumina crucible as well as after the powder has been synthesized

 A 25.4mm die was used to create Li4SiO4 and Li2ZrO3 pucks. 1.4g and 0.8g of
Li4SiO4 and Li2ZrO3 powder were used respectively. The respective amounts of each
powder were weighed out and then placed into the die, making sure the powder was
level to minimize uneven compaction. 50 MPa of pressure was applied to the Li4SiO4
powder using a hydraulic press. Previous attempts using 20 MPa were unsuccessful as
the green bodies were not compacted enough to handle and constantly fell apart
during the extraction from the die. 30 MPa of pressure were applied to the Li2ZrO3
powder as previous attempts with lower pressures failed to form a workable green body.
The pucks were then placed into alumina crucibles and subjected to a 2°C/min ramp
rate and 1h hold time at various temperatures. Lithium silicate was synthesized under
vacuum to decompose the excess lithium carbonate and prevent melting of lithium
carbonate which occurs upon reaching 720 °C in air.

III. Results

 1. Li2ZrO3
Limited work was done with Li2ZrO3 due to equipment failure that arose early in the
process. Data was collected of fractured samples ranging from 700 to 1300 °C.
Throughout the various trials, it was found that densification onset for Li2ZrO3 occurs
past 1110 °C as seen in Figure 3. This was very apparent in the data as the relative
density took a big leap from the 50% range to the 60-70% range. The flexural strength
had an abnormally weak outlier at 1100 °C which may be due to bad testing or sintering
conditions during that sample run, but not enough samples at 1100 °C were collected to
safely conclude.

 2. Li4SiO4
 The bulk of the work done in this study was with Li 4SiO4, which had samples
sintered at temperatures ranging from 500-750 °C. As with the Li2ZrO3, it was found that
the pucks did not sinter below 650 °C, as the samples experienced an increase in
diameter instead of the expected decrease. More issues arose as the sintering
temperature increased which included difficulty in removing samples from the alumina
crucible. At 750 °C, the samples began to fuse to the alumina crucible they were placed
in as the Li2CO3 began to melt, making it extremely difficult to remove without breaking.
A powder bed was used to try and prevent the fusion of the sample and crucible,
however, at 750C, the powder bed began to form hard agglomerates on the surface of
the sample which required sanding for over 30 minutes, a task which was impractical.

 Dropping the sintering temperature to 725 °C along with using a pre-sintered
powder bed helped reduce this phenomenon while still maintaining a relatively high
density and flexural strength. Using a pre-sintered powder bed meant using coarser
powder which has less surface area and in turn sinters less. Additionally, the reduction in
temperature to 725 °C is conveniently below the melting point of Li 2CO3 which is ~ 728
°C [9]. These two factors resulted in a dense sample without any of the aforementioned
issues.

 Given that sufficient samples were collected, Weibull analysis was carried out and
it was determined that at the minimum threshold of 20MPa, 12% of samples will
fracture. The characteristic strength was determined to be 30.2367 MPa, which is the
value at which 63.2% of samples will fail.

IV. Discussion

 From the data collected, it can be concluded that the flexural strength and
relative density for Li2ZrO3 are proportional to sintering temperature despite the outlier
at 1100 °C. Although insufficient samples were fractured to make a definitive statement
on the viability of Li2ZrO3 as a tritium breeder ceramic, initial data suggests that it would
not have the minimum mechanical properties of 20 MPa to be handled without
fracturing or falling apart while being handled. This threshold was determined
experimentally as samples that were below 20 MPa exhibited extremely poor mechanical
properties and could not be handled. Despite sintering up to 1300 °C, the highest
flexural strength that was around 18 MPa at ~70% theoretical density which falls below
the threshold. Further testing is needed for a conclusive result.

 Li4SiO4 followed a similar trend to the Li2ZrO3. Both the flexural strength and
relative density are proportional to the sintering temperature. Temperatures below 650
°C resulted in un-sintered samples which exhibited poor flexural strength of 1-3 MPa.
Despite having similar densities from 700 – 750 C, °there was a difference in mechanical
properties as the 700 °C samples had various samples below the threshold while 725 °C
only had one. This can likely be attributed to the differences in the microstructures as
higher temperatures lead to stronger bonds because diffusion rates are increased.
Additionally, lower temperatures usually lead to more irregular shaped cracks when
compared to higher temperatures due to the increased thermal energy that leaves more

 8

spherical pores since the entire diffusion process is much smoother, giving atoms time
to fill any sharp corners that may be present in the system. Although the samples at 750
°C had high density and flexural strength, the removal process issues mentioned in the
results section made them impractical to produce. The flexural strength values for the
700 °C samples are likely higher than what the data suggests because a majority of the
samples are warped, and as seen in Figure 6, the samples fracture along the warpage
making the samples weaker since it is a defect induced fracture. Therefore, even the 700
°C samples that are below the 20 MPa threshold would likely meet the minimum
requirements should the sample be free of warping.

 The current data suggests that Li4SiO4 is a promising candidate for a tritium
breeder ceramic due to its mechanical properties and density. From the 10 samples
collected at 725 °C, a Weibull modulus of 4.9656 was determined from a linear trendline
which had an R2 value of 0.9753 which indicates that our approximation is valid. Analysis
determined that 12% of samples will fail at the designated threshold of 20 MPa. This
failure rate is similar to that of Li2AlO3 and Li2TiO3 which was previously determined
during past experiments. This failure rate is likely higher than the true failure rate, again
due to the warping of the samples which gave a lower flexural strength value than a
warpage free sample would give. Additionally, the theoretical densities hovered around
the 75-80% range which is ideal, as an extremely dense sample would be nearly
impossible to remove the tritium from. Therefore, continuing to work with the silicate
compound and optimizing its properties would yield even greater results than the initial
work has done thus far.

V. Conclusion

 Li2ZrO3 is likely not a viable tritium breeder ceramic given the initial data.
However, a full study is needed to conclusively declare. Despite the flexural strength and
density increasing with temperature, a sample was never reached that met both the
density and flexural strength requirements. Li4SiO4 on the other hand shows promising
results with mechanical properties that could be optimized in future work. Numerous
samples that met both the density and flexural strength requirement were produced,
yielding a 12% failure rate at the threshold stress. The current 12% failure rate is likely
not high enough for fusion applications however, eliminating the warpage issue with the
 9

samples would yield more accurate flexural strength results, which would in turn lower
the failure rate.

 Future work should include more samples than was presented in this current
study, having a minimum of 10 at a specified temperature to perform Weibull analysis
and determine failure rates for the compound. rate. For silicate, more samples at 725 °C
should be collected to improve the accuracy of the Weibull analysis. Time should also be
dedicated to reducing warping. Additionally, performing SEM and XRD on fracture surfaces
should be done in order to track grain size and phase composition, which could help explain the
difference in mechanical properties at comparable densities.
