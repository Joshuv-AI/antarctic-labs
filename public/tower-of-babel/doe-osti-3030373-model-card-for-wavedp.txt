 Model short description

 o WaveDP is a deep learning model designed to reduce noise from seismograms and measure signal arrival
 times simultaneously.

 Model description

 o WaveDP was trained using both earthquake signals and pure noise waveforms from the Stanford
 Earthquake Dataset (STEAD) [1]. The model achieved a median signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
 improvement of 7 dB. Because the model uses seismograms as direct input, the model is four times faster
 than an existing model for signal denoising that uses spectral representations of seismograms as input [2].

 Model type

 o Decoder–encoder (Figure 1)

 1

 Figure 1. A simplified view of the model structure.

 Input and output

 o Time series (ground motion) data were used for this model.

 o Input—This model was trained with a three-channel time series. Each channel contained 5,700 data
 points with a sampling rate of 100 Hz or 57 s of seismogram.

 o Output—The output is a batch of denoised three-channel seismograms and probabilities that are a
 function of time. For each time series in the batch, the output probabilities have three channels (5,700 data
 points each). The output probability channels correspond to P waves, S waves, and noise. These
 probabilities can be used to infer P and S wave arrival times.

 Compute infrastructure

 o Device—This model was trained on a workstation.

 o Hardware—This model was trained on one NVIDIA A100 GPU.

 o Software—This model was trained with Lightning (https://github.com/Lightning-AI/pytorch-lightning)
 and PyTorch (https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch).

 2. INTENDED USES

The WaveDP model is designed to reduce noise from three-component seismic data sampled at 100 Hz and
measure arrival times of P and S waves from seismic waveforms. The model was trained using earthquake data at
local distances (0–350 km) and may not perform well for earthquake data at larger distances or for nonearthquake
sources.

 Intended use

 o The model can be applied to three-component seismic data recorded by seismic stations at local distances
 and can reduce common background noise and measure P and S wave arrival times.

 Primary intended users

 o Data analysts working with seismic data

 Out-of-scope use cases

 o Applying the model to data other than seismic time series, earthquake data at distances larger than 350 km
 from the source, and nonearthquake sources

 4. BIAS, RISKS, AND LIMITATIONS

The model may have a less-than-optimal performance for earthquake data outside of a source–receiver distance
range of 10–110 km and a magnitude range of 0–4.5 because of biases in the training data.

 Known training data biases

 o The source–receiver distance and earthquake magnitude of the training data are not uniformly distributed.
 Most earthquake data have a source–receiver distance between 10 and 110 km and a magnitude between
 0 and 4.5. Transfer learning has been successfully applied to adapt a pretrained model for different scales
 [3]. The project team suggests fine-tuning the WaveDP model for out-of-distribution earthquake data.

 Biased prediction examples

 o NA

 Model limitations

 o The model was trained for a sampling rate of 100 Hz and may not perform ideally for other sampling
 rates.

 5. TRAINING DETAILS

5.1 TRAINING DATA

This study used STEAD to train and evaluate this model because STEAD is among the best benchmark datasets
available for local to regional data. STEAD is a global dataset with more than 1 million seismic waveforms (each
60 s long) that originated from approximately 450,000 earthquakes and background noise captured by more than
2,500 seismic stations. Each waveform in STEAD was attached with metadata such as earthquake locations,
station locations, and signal arrival times when available. This study divided the waveforms into three sets: train,
development, and test. The test set consisted exclusively of seismograms that originated from earthquakes in the
Oklahoma region or were recorded by stations in the same area. The Oklahoma region was selected for the test set
because of the considerable number of earthquake signals available. The remaining seismograms were distributed
between the train and development sets. Earthquake seismograms and noise waveforms were randomly
apportioned so that 95% of waveforms belonged to the train set. The train, development, and test sets comprised
approximately 950,000, 50,000, and 30,000 earthquake seismograms, respectively, as well as 220,000, 11,000,
and 2,000 noise waveforms, respectively.

5.2 TRAINING PROCEDURE

This study focused on training a deep learning model to reduce noise in seismic signals within the time domain
and measure P and S wave arrival times. Pure noise waveforms were combined with earthquake signals to
generate noisy signals. The team followed data augmentation approaches detailed in Chai et al. (2025) [5]5. To
enhance dataset diversity, 21 randomly selected and amplitude-scaled noise waveforms were added to each clean
signal. Additionally, a random time shift was applied to each noisy signal, further enriching data diversity. Pure
noise waveforms were included in the training to improve the model performance for continuous data. The
models processed three-component seismograms that were 57 s long and sampled at a rate of 100 Hz; the models
outputted denoised three-component seismograms and probabilities of P wave, S wave, and noise for each
sampling point in the seismograms. Target P wave and S wave probabilities were constructed using truncated
Gaussian functions with a width of 0.01 s centered at the P and S wave arrival times. The resulting noisy signals,
along with their corresponding clean signals (original seismograms) and target probabilities, served as input-target
pairs for model training. The training process employed a learning rate of 10−6, had a batch size of 256, and
included 50 training epochs using the Adam optimizer.

 6. EVALUATION DETAILS

6.1 EVALUATION DATA

The test set, which was extracted from STEAD [1], is composed solely of seismograms originating from
earthquakes in the Oklahoma region and pure noise waveforms recorded by stations in the same area. The model’s
processing speed is significantly influenced by the hardware used and the efficiency of data loading processes.
The system used for this test took approximately 4 min to evaluate the entire test set, which consists of around
131,000 seismograms (see Section 7 for hardware specifications).

6.2 EVALUATION PROCEDURE

A model prediction consists of denoising output and arrival time picking output. The team evaluated the model
performance on both types of outputs. The denoising performance was quantified using SNR improvements that
were calculated as the difference in SNR between the denoised signals produced by the WaveDP model and their
corresponding noisy inputs. The SNRs were computed following the methodology detailed in Mousavi et al. [1].
WaveDP achieved a median SNR improvement of 7.1 dB and an average SNR improvement of 7.2 ± 5.2 dB.

 5

The performance on arrival time picking was quantified using median absolute error (MAE), accuracy, precision,
recall, and F1 for both P and S waves. The arrival time prediction was considered a true positive if the absolute
difference between the predicted and ground truth arrival times was ≤0.5 s. MAE was calculated as the median of
the absolute differences between the predicted signal arrival times using WaveDP and the ground truth arrival
times for true positives. If the absolute difference was >0.5 s or if an arrival time was predicted for a pure noise
waveform, the model prediction was considered a false positive. The model predictions were considered false
negatives when no arrival times were predicted by the model for waveforms with P and S wave signals. Accuracy
is calculated as the ratio of the number of true positives to the total waveforms. Recall is computed by dividing
the number of true positives by the sum of the number of true positives and false negatives. Precision is calculated
by dividing the number of true positives by the sum of the number of true positives and false positives. The F1
score is the harmonic mean of recall and precision. The model’s performance on arrival time picking is as
follows:

MAE—0.02 s for both P and S waves
Accuracy—0.92 for P wave, 0.91 for S wave
Recall—0.98 for P wave, 0.97 for S wave
Precision—0.99 for both P and S wave
F1—0.98 for both P and S wave
