ABSTRACT
This report evaluates resilient energy system options for the planned Ko‘olauloa Community
Resilience Hub (KCRH) in Hau‘ula, O‘ahu, intended to provide shelter and essential services during
extended grid outages. Working with Hui O Hau‘ula and project partners through DOE’s ETIPP
program, we (1) define critical services and design-basis threats, emphasizing a 30-day outage driven
by hurricane or earthquake hazards; (2) develop EnergyPlus/OpenStudio building energy models to
quantify loads for normal and resilience operations and compare code-compliant versus high-
efficiency designs; (3) perform blue-sky techno-economic optimization using HOMER; and (4)
conduct black-sky, stochastic multi-objective resilience optimization using Sandia’s Microgrid
Design Toolkit. Results show that an all-renewable solution can meet resilience requirements but is
costly and space-intensive. Hybrid solutions using PV, batteries, and propane backup achieve
comparable critical-load availability at substantially lower cost, along with a smaller impact on the
site and building. High-efficiency building design reduces both operating energy needs and required
backup system size.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The island of Oahu in the state of Hawaii is home to almost one million people, constituting
approximately 70% of the state’s population. Approximately 81% of Oahu’s population lives in
greater Honolulu, with another 7.5% living in the urban centers of Kaneohe and Kailua. The
remainder of Oahu’s population is spread in narrow strips of land along the windward and
downwind sides of the north coast, the Laie-Hauula and Haleiwa-Waialua-Pupukea rural urban
clusters, respectively, shown in orange in Figure ES- 1. The population of these rural clusters is
diverse in ethnicity, age, and income. This report focuses on ways to provide resilient energy services
to the Koʻolauloa Community Resilience Hub, that is being planned as resilient infrastructure, to
protect residents of these Northshore communities in the Koʻolauloa District from Kaʻaʻawa to
Waimea Valley.

 Figure ES- 1. Population distribution on the island of Oahu, Hawaii [Urban and Rural Centers in
 the state of Hawaii, 2010 – HSDC 2013-2]

Because of the topography of the island, vehicle access to Hauʻula is limited to the two-lane coastal
highway, Kamehameha Highway. A large portion of the length of Kamehameha Highway is very
close to the ocean and is vulnerable to erosion from waves from the ocean side and damage from
runoff water from the mountain side. The road is often blocked in one or both directions due to
scheduled or emergency repairs and vehicle accidents.
Power is delivered to Hauʻula via overhead distribution lines running along the coast, primarily
along the Kamehameha Highway. These distribution lines are vulnerable to a variety of threats,
including severe weather, earthquakes and damage from vehicle collisions. While outages last only a
few hours, Hauʻula residents are concerned that, in the case of a major disaster, grid power may not

 11

be available for up to several weeks. This concern, in combination with the difficult access which
may also be exacerbated by a disaster, has led the Hauʻula community to consider how to best
support its members, and especially the most vulnerable, during such an event with no grid power
and in isolation from the island’s urban areas.
The Hauʻula community’s exploration of its resilience options was led by the Hui O Hauʻula (HoH)
organization. HoH is a small 501c3 nonprofit charitable organization dedicated to the well-being of
the population of the Koʻolauloa district providing civic and social engagement supporting health
and wellness, emergency preparedness, education, cultural regeneration, social and economic
development, resilient infrastructure development (roads, bridges, power, wastewater treatment,
broadband) and digital technology skills for all through social engagement, volunteerism and
partnerships with local, city, state, and national organizations. HoH partners with local businesses,
individuals, organizations, and many outreach programs. Through long-term engagement with the
community, HoH determined that the optimal way to provide resilience during extreme events such
as hurricanes, earthquakes, tsunamis, flash-flooding and similar disasters would be to build a new
community center located away from a flood zone, strong enough to withstand extreme climate
events, large and well-equipped enough to provide shelter and services, with a power supply that
would enable it to continue operating for several weeks without grid power and fuel supply. The
building should also be consistent with local cultural values including aesthetics, respect for elders,
respect for tradition and environmental sustainability.
Through a combination of serendipity and mutual interest, the architectural firm +Lab, based in
Brooklyn, NY on the East Coast and Santa Monica, CA on the West Coast, eventually became an
integral partner of the project, beginning in 2019. +Lab’s ethos is based on giving a voice and
visibility to communities who have been on the front lines of extreme weather resilience, adaptation,
regeneration and equity. In its projects, +Lab engages cultural wisdom as the guide to a resilient,
sustainable future, respecting uniqueness of place.
The Ko‘olauloa Community Resilience Hub (KCRH), shown in Figure ES- 2, is a 40,000 ft2 building
whose shape mimics an upturned canoe, a traditional method of sheltering from the hostile
elements. Its architecture contains spaces meant to serve the dual purpose of a community center
during blue-sky conditions, including a basketball court, meeting rooms, classrooms, and a
commercial-grade kitchen, and as an emergency shelter that can house up to 1,500 people during
black-sky conditions. Its structure is designed to meet Federal Emergency Management Agency P-
361 community saferoom design criteria.

 12

 Figure ES- 2. Architect’s rendering of the KCRH

A question that remained open in the early phases of the design was how to best provide resilient
power to support the KCRH’s functions during extended grid outages. The opportunity to address
this question was presented by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Transitions Initiative
Partnership Project (ETIPP), a program that matches expertise at the national laboratories with the
need for technical assistance by communities. Following a successful application, HoH was awarded
one of the 11 Cohort 2 projects in the fall of 2022. The HoH application was matched with
technical expertise at Sandia National Laboratories, with the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR) providing project management.
The project began with an evaluation and quantification of the needs of the community, including
the definition of resilience, the discussion and definition of various critical services and associated
energy needs, and the threats against which the KCRH should be designed to operate. Given that
the building was still in the early phases of design, the second phase of the project involved the
development of an energy model of the building to determine heating, cooling, and other electrical
equipment loads, as well as the investigation of the ability of the building to maintain reasonably
comfortable conditions with reduced energy availability. As a part of this process, a high-efficiency
building design (a design goal of the +Lab approach) was compared to a building designed to simply
meet building code. The idea here was to determine how a higher capital expenditure, highly
efficient design would compare over time with a baseline design with higher operational
expenditures. The intent was also to determine the reduction in initial capital costs that a high-
efficiency design would produce in terms of the equipment needed to provide resilient power.
The third phase of the technical assistance involved using a well-known commercial system
optimization tool to assess the optimal composition of the resilient energy system in blue-sky
conditions. In this phase, the possible combinations of energy resources that could be installed on-
site (solar, wind, battery storage) were evaluated in terms of net present costs. This exercise provided
an initial estimate of the long-term costs of various options, ranging from diesel backup to systems
that could provide essentially 100% renewable energy to the building. Not surprisingly, the all-
renewable option is considerably more expensive than the diesel-only backup.
A fourth and final phase of the project involved evaluating trade-offs between the three metrics
deemed important by the community, namely resilience, cost, and environmental performance, using

 13

a Sandia-developed analysis tool that exposes the system to a representative sample of all possible
combinations of design basis threats (DBTs), associated hazards and component fragility to hazards.
For this evaluation, the DBT was ultimately determined to be a 30-day grid outage caused by either a
hurricane or an earthquake. These were determined to be the most substantial threats due to the
damage they could cause to equipment at the KCRH.
The analyses produced several key conclusions:
 1. It is possible to provide the required level of resilience with a renewable-only solution,
 although the cost is high (on the order of $4–5 million) and it could be challenging—but not
 impossible—to install the necessary equipment (solar panels, batteries, and hydrogen energy
 storage) on site.
 2. A solution that combines a moderate amount of renewables and energy storage with an
 equal amount of backup electricity generation using propane can be significantly cheaper to
 install (less than $1 million) and simpler to site in terms of space used to install solar, battery,
 and other equipment.
 3. A high-renewable solution with a relatively small fraction of energy provided by propane
 backup could provide near-net-zero energy production during blue-sky conditions at a cost
 that is still less than half of the all-renewable cost, with moderate site demands for
 installation of the necessary renewable hardware.
 4. While a little bit more expensive and less efficient, propane backup generators are
 recommended as an alternative to diesel backup generators due to the longevity of the fuel
 (propane does not degrade if left in a tank for a long time) and due to the fact that any leaks
 would not contaminate the aquifer.
 5. The additional expense that would be required to design and build a high-efficiency building
 envelope and associated mechanical systems would likely be compensated by lower capital
 costs of the energy equipment and lower operating expenses.
 6. Wind generators of a type that are acceptable to the community would make a relatively
 small contribution to the overall energy production but still improve the overall resilience of
 the system.
While it is up to the community to choose the solution that best fits their needs, it seems that a high-
renewable hybrid solution, with a propane generator and a relatively small propane tank (on the
order of 1,000 gallons) could satisfy the community’s environmental performance expectations, at a
moderate cost, without overwhelming the site with renewable energy generation equipment and
storage equipment.

1. BACKGROUND
Since its inception, the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Transitions Initiative (ETI) has
supported island and remote communities who wish to develop resilient, sustainable and affordable
energy systems, through technical assistance, planning resources, tools, and training. Initiative
activities hinge on local leadership and promote the growth of local institutional capacity. In 2021,
the Energy Transitions Partnership Project (ETIPP) initiative was initiated, with the goal of
accelerating the deployment of resilient energy infrastructure on remote and island communities in
the face of increasing threats from natural disasters. Through funding provided by ETIPP,
communities work with experts from the U.S. Department of Energy’s national laboratories to
determine energy solutions that consider community needs and goals, local financial and natural
resources, and the local workforce, to meet existing and long-term challenges. Each year, a cohort of
projects are selected from island and remote communities that apply through a competitive process.
The 11 communities in the first cohort in 2021 were located in Alaska, Maine, Hawaii, and North
Carolina. These projects have been completed, and lessons learned were transferred to the second
cohort of 12 communities in 2022, located in Alaska, Guam, Hawaii, Maine, and Puerto Rico. The
Koʻolauloa Community Resilience Hub (KCRH) is the object of the Hauʻula project. It is a building
that is intended to serve day-to-day needs of the local community, such as a meeting place for
events, children, youth, families, seniors, people with disabilities and houseless in normal day-to-day
“blue-sky” conditions, and provide basic needs such as water, food, medical services, shelter and
communications under emergency “black-sky” conditions that may arise from a natural disaster or
from a man-made event.

1.1. The Koʻolauloa Community
The Ko‘olauloa district is located on the northeast shore of the island of Oahu, approximately
diametrically opposite to Honolulu. It is home to a diverse population of approximately 20,000,
concentrated in Kahana, Lāʻie, Kahuku, Hau‘ula, Punalu‘u, and Ka‘a‘awa. According to the U.S.
Census1, the median age is 31.2 years, and the median per capita income is $35,047 (about 20%
lower than the median income for Hawaii of $46,361). 11.3% of all residents are below the poverty
line, but 18% of the population over 65 is below the poverty line. 14% of residents are 65 or over.
There are 6,799 housing units in Koʻolauloa, with a median value of $990,400.
The district is connected to the large population centers by a small two-lane road, Kamehameha
Highway. The highway, a typical section of which is shown in Figure 1, is located between steep
mountains and the shore. It is fragile and frequently suffers from damage due to landslides, flooding,
and high surf. Electricity is provided by a distribution loop that runs along the highway and is itself
subject to a variety of threats including wind, landslides and vehicles hitting power poles. Steep
mountains separate the district from Oahu’s population centers. State and city officials estimate that
in the case of a major disaster the district could be isolated from the rest of the island, and without
power, for up to 30 days.
One of the principal threats to the community is inundation from tsunamis. A worst-case scenario
for the state is a magnitude 9+ earthquake in the eastern Aleutian Islands. The tsunami from such an
earthquake, the “Great Aleutian Tsunami,” would produce extensive flooding of lowlands
throughout the entire State of Hawaii. Much of the populated area in the Ko‘olauloa district is in the
Great Aleutian Tsunami inundation zone2. Floods caused by precipitation, storm surge, and high

2. VISION FOR THE KCRH

2.1. The KCRH Concept
In response to growing concerns about challenges related to the increasing frequency of extreme
weather events, the concept for the KCRH was formulated by HoH. The vision for the hub
emerged from the organization's mission to ensure the continuous operation of critical services,
even during emergencies, while creating a thriving, resilient community. The concept was developed
in partnership with the Honolulu City & County Office of Climate Change, Sustainability and
Resiliency, signaling the importance of government collaboration in advancing community resilience
initiatives.4 The resilience hub was designed to serve as a focal point for the community, providing
essential services and acting as a safe space during extreme weather events, and to create a vital
resource for the community. Situated on a state-owned parcel, the KCRH is strategically positioned
in the Ko‘olauloa District, roughly 20 miles east of Hale‘iwa and west of Kāne‘ohe. This location, at
54-256 Hau‘ula Homestead Road, is on a 4.9-acre lot owned by the Department of Land and
Natural Resources. Following a right-of-entry permit granted in 2019, HoH has been conducting site
assessments and maintenance. In 2025, following the completion of the Environmental Review,
HoH secured a long-term lease from the Board of Land and Natural Resources (BLNR). The
KCRH will be constructed on a well-positioned site within Hau‘ula, a community nestled between
the Ko‘olau Mountains and the Pacific Ocean. This central location within the Ko‘olauloa District
makes it an ideal Resilience Hub site for regional emergency and community services. The project
site’s elevation ranges from 26 to 98 feet above mean sea level, strategically chosen to minimize the
risk of flooding and tsunamis. The natural slope of the hillside will be leveraged to reduce the need
for extensive earth-moving during construction, aligning with sustainable building practices. In 2021,
HoH hosted a Blessing of the Land for the site of the KCRH to celebrate and reflect on the
responsibility and importance of the stewardship of the land.

Hawaiian cultural principles with modern resilience strategies. The use of a canoe-inspired roof
design and traditional Hawaiian architectural elements reflects the community’s cultural heritage.

energy-efficient systems, all contributing to the Resilience Hub’s role as a model of holistic,
culturally informed resilience.

3. THREAT ASSESSMENT
Resilience analysis focuses on the ability of a system to withstand and recover from one or more
specific threats. Resilience has many dimensions, many of which relate to supporting the ability of
people to first survive, then recover from losses, and finally return to their normal lives. Examples
of these dimensions are medical services, shelter, food, potable water, sanitation, and services for
emotional well-being. A fundamental aspect of each of these resilience dimensions is access to
energy. Energy is needed to power buildings, to deliver clean water, to power medical devices, to
cook and store food, to name a few applications.
In this report, the focus is on analyzing aspects of energy-centric resilience, or the ability of an
energy system to withstand a threat and/or recover quickly from it. The energy system in question
provides power to a variety of uses in the KCRH. The building’s connection to the island’s
centralized electricity grid can be considered its “anchor” energy system, with all other systems
revolving around it. In normal circumstances, the grid serves as the source of power that is available
on-call, whether other resources are not available, and as the receiver of excess power at certain
times of day. Unfortunately, the bulk grid is occasionally unavailable, because of circumstances such
as high winds damaging transmission lines, an earthquake damaging a power plant, and so on. Such
circumstances are known as the “design basis threat” (DBT). In this analysis, each DBT is associated
with probability of loss of grid power and statistics on the duration of the outage. Each DBT is
associated with a set of hazards, each of which has the potential to also damage or disable local
energy systems designed to provide energy to a facility when a grid outage occurs. For example, if
the DBT is a hurricane, associated hazards may be high winds, flying debris, high precipitation or
flooding. Individual components of a distributed energy system may be susceptible to one or more
of these hazards. A rooftop PV may not be able to withstand winds over a certain intensity, or a
pad-mounted transformer may be susceptible to high water. These circumstances may establish
design criteria for systems intended to improve resilience to specific DBTs, and to determine the
probability of failure of individual components and to improve the accuracy of the resilience
estimates.

3.4. Windstorms
The trade winds that blow through the Pacific Ocean are subject to high intensities from high
pressure areas north of the islands. Windstorms can damage infrastructure and create windborne
debris missiles. Since 2018, there have been 2 declared disasters and 44 windstorm events. Future
climate projections suggest that muggy weather may result in fewer days of northeast trade winds.
Atmospheric temperatures will likely increase due to the change in the trade winds. The overall
probability that a windstorm is declared a disaster is once every 5 years. There is likely to be nine
high wind events per year.

3.5. Drought
Droughts are characterized by periods of abnormally dry weather that can diminish the water flow
of the islands and the moisture content of the ground. Since 2018, there have been 13 declared
disasters and 3 multiyear events associated with drought. All counties are vulnerable to drought. The
trade winds are heavily responsible for rainfall on the islands. An increase in the frequency of
meteorological and agricultural droughts is projected. With increased temperatures, accessibility to
freshwater will be more limited. The state of Hawaii can expect drought conditions on an ongoing
basis.

3.6. Hurricane
Hurricane season in Hawaii typically ranges from June to the end of November. Since 2018, there
have been several instances of hurricanes and tropical storms that have caused significant damage to
the Hawaiian Islands11. They are Lane (2018), Olivia (2018), Walaka (2018), Ema (2019), Douglas
(2020), Linda (2021), and Dora (2023). The state of Hawaii has a 68.5% chance of a hurricane at any
magnitude occurring within sixty nautical miles of land each year. The City and County of Honolulu
assessed the local vulnerability and potential losses to determine that the average annualized loss is
$140 million. The estimated population impacted by a Category 4 hurricane in the county of
Honolulu is expected to be 135,313 or 13.81% of the county’s population. Vulnerable populations
include low-income communities which are isolated from major roads.

3.9. Torrential Rain/Flooding
Hawaii has a wet season between the months of October and April11. Overall, rainfall has been

3.12. Defining DBTs
For the purposes of the modeling that will be performed in this work, it is possible, based on
available data, to characterize grid outages statistically, as linked to individual DBTs, in terms of
probability of occurrence at a specific date and time, and in terms of the mean time to repair.
Hazards associate with each DBT can also characterize statistically in terms of a probability density,
which quantifies the severity of the specific hazard-relative components in the system. For example,
the wind speed associated with a hurricane categorization can be used to estimate the probability of
failure of a PV array based on its construction.

As an example of such a categorization, consider the DBT associated with hurricanes. Even
Category 1 hurricanes can result in widespread damage and outages. For example, when Hurricane
Iwa hit Hawaii in 1982, it took approximately a week for utility power to be restored to major
population centers, but many were left without power for even longer12. The statistics say that
Hawaii can expect to be in the path of a hurricane approximately once every 10 years, with the
hurricane season concentrated between June 1 and November 30, peaking in the month of August.
Given that extended grid outages can result from hurricanes of any intensity, the mere occurrence of
a hurricane can be considered a DBT for the purposes of this analysis. The hazards associated with a
hurricane that can affect the operation of the distributed equipment at the KCRH are wind speed,
precipitation and flooding, and cloud cover (that drastically reduces PV power production). The
probability of maximum wind speed of a given magnitude, given that a hurricane is present, is a
complex matter and the object of ongoing research. For the purposes of this study, the method
presented by Darling13 (1991) can be adapted. Essentially, the probability of a specific windspeed is
represented by a Weibull distribution, whose parameters are characterized by a data fit, as shown in
Figure 6. Similarly, the probability of precipitation can be defined statistically. While the KCRH is
not threatened by storm surge, there is the possibility of flooding due to storm runoff that must be
considered. In this work, flooding is defined by the probability density function of the total
precipitation amount.

3.13. Focus of the Resilience Analyses
The above summary shows that many types of events could produce a DBT. However, for the
purposes of this evaluation, only hurricanes and earthquakes were considered in this analysis,
because the resilience measures that mitigate these DBTs also mitigate hazards from other possible
disasters. Furthermore, based on feedback from the Hauʻula stakeholders, for the purposes of the
resilience analysis, we decided to focus on 30-day outages (the maximum outage duration
communicated by state emergency management to HoH) only, rather than considering a statistically
determined spectrum. This is because the interpretation of the recommended resilience options
would otherwise be made more difficult by the need to distinguish between short-duration and long-
duration outage performance.

5. BUILDING ENERGY SIMULATION

5.1. Building Energy Modeling Software
Building energy models allow a user to estimate energy consumption for a building. The results of
energy modeling can be used for a variety of purposes, including the assessment of a proposed
design, the selection of HVAC components for a design in advanced phases, or even the assessment
of the real-world performance of an existing building. A building energy model can be based on
statistical analysis of energy data (i.e., data-driven), physics-based, or hybrids of data-driven and
physic-based approaches.19 For applications that require years of simulation time into the future,
physics-based modeling is the most effective approach, as historical data may not exist or may not
be suitable to estimate future performance. Physics-based building energy models use software that
solves the convective, conductive, and radiative heat balance equations over time for a model of the
building envelope. Boundary conditions to these equations consist of ambient conditions outside a
building, such as temperature and solar irradiance, and internal thermal sources and sinks resulting
from occupancy, use of appliances or equipment, HVAC equipment. Significant simplifications to
three-dimensional space–time are needed to make simulation of a building’s thermal systems
practical. As a result, one-dimensional networks that connect at nodes in three-dimensional space
are generally used. Thermal and mass balances are computed at these nodes with simplification for
how fluid mixing and radiation scattering and absorption occur. Building energy simulation tools
such as the U.S. Department of Energy’s EnergyPlus® software20 and DOE-221 can be used by
modelers to create building energy models through text inputs. Many software packages leverage
these and other building energy simulation tools to create graphical user interfaces for constructing
building energy models efficiently. For this study, the OpenStudio22 software, which leverages
EnergyPlus, was used to construct a set of building energy models for the KCRH.23

5.4. Climate and Weather Files for Hauʻula
The climate of the Hawaiian Islands is very diverse, ranging from dry desert coast land to rainforest
climate zones. Hauʻula is directly north of Honolulu on the northeast coast of island of Oahu. The
northern coast of Oahu is cooler, cloudier, and rainier than the southern coast. The closest coastal
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration station is the Kaneʻohe Bay Marines base, 40 km
to the southeast of Hauʻula. Wheeler Airfield is closer but has significantly different climate due to
altitude change and being inland. The weather files for Kaneʻohe Bay were therefore adjusted for
temperature, radiation, and ground temperatures to reconstruct a weather file representative of the
local Hauʻula climate. Other important differences, including humidity and precipitation, do not
have sufficient influence on the building energy model to require adjustment.

5.6. Comfort Conditions Provided by the KCRH
While the energy consumption of the KCRH is important in designing and selecting equipment that
can power the building, its primary role is to keep occupants comfortable while providing the
necessary medical, food, and communications services. Because the KCRH is expected to function
for an extended period of time, potentially into the next century, it is important to consider how the
building will cope with future climate. In this analysis, we considered possible future climate
scenarios in which the KCRH may operate. Climate scenarios are generally referred to as shared
socioeconomic pathways (SSPs).
The SSPs considered here represent both the most likely outcomes and worst-case outcomes for
global surface temperature. SSP2–4.5 is a “middle-of-the- road” pathway where trends do not
change from historic ones, SSP3–7.0 emphasizes regional rivalry characterized by “resurgent
nationalism,” and SSP5–8.5 assumes that much of the world’s new development will be fossil fuel-
dependent (O’Neill et al. 2017). The 4.5, 7.0, and 8.5 in the second half of the SSP nomenclature all
represent the expected level of radiative forcing in W/m2 in the year 2100 resulting from greenhouse
gas concentration.

Figure 14. End uses of energy for modes of operation and efficiency levels, averaged through the
 year

The analysis was conducted for the Kaneʻohe Bay weather station, and the resulting model was
applied to the adjusted weather file outlined in the previous section for SSP2–4.5, SSP3–7.0, and
SSP5–8.5. EnergyPlus Weather files for 2020, 2040, 2060, and 2080 were generated. The extreme
values for confidence intervals for heat waves (5% and 95%) were included, neglecting the 50%
case. The aggregate use of these extreme cases in the Monte Carlo study assures the outer extremes
of future heat waves are accurately captured. The resulting set of 7,200 files was run on the two
operations modes of the KCRH (normal and resilience) for a total of 14,400 EnergyPlus runs. The
rationale is that the future is non-deterministic, and we cannot assume, a priori, what combination of
weather and other operating conditions (e.g., the availability of various forms of energy) the KCRH
will be exposed to. The resulting approach is therefore to expose the building to a representative
sampling of all possible weather and operating conditions that it could face over the next several
decades, even though only a small subset will be realized.

 54

The results of the Monte Carlo study, shown in Figure 15, show quantifiable trade-offs that can
inform the conceptual design of the KCRH energy system. There is a clear increase in loss of
thermal resilience (in terms of percentage of hours in a temperature category of concern, with
“Caution” being the most common) with advancing time in the future and with SSP severity, for
both normal (blue-sky) and resilience (black-sky) modes of operation. Cutting space cooling, as
practiced under resilience operations, amplifies the loss of thermal resilience on average by a factor
of five. The sensitivity of thermal resilience outcomes is especially pronounced for the worst-case
scenario, SSP 5–8.5. Conversely, the energy utilization index in resilience operations mode, shown in
Figure 16, shows little sensitivity to future conditions since the cooling is cut for this mode. On the
other hand, the normal operations mode has a clear trend, but the increase is small, at a maximum
0.8% change in energy utilization index. This is further illustrated in Figure 17, which shows that the
spread in thermally dangerous hours increases in later years without an increase in cooling load.
Clearly, most of the change in thermal resilience is in spaces that are not air-conditioned. The small
differences in cooling energy ultimately result from a combination of the mild climate, reduced
sensitivity to climate variation at Hawaii’s latitude, and the fact that the HVAC equipment, sized
according to a constant design day, simply cannot sufficiently cool the facility for extreme events.
On the other hand, the consequence of not having more cooling capacity for extreme events is
increasing the hours of exposure to dangerous conditions with much more sensitivity.
Though the decrease in thermal resilience is not desirable, dropping cooling loads to reduce energy
use for resilience operations is still acceptable. Several factors support this: (1) the primary DBT is a
hurricane, which brings significant cooling, making extreme heat less relevant for a period following
the event. (2) Appropriate contingency planning can overcome thermally hot conditions in the
facilities. There is still air-conditioned space in the KCRH. In worst-case scenarios where dangerous
temperatures exist, staff in charge of the KCRH can prioritize access to this air-conditioned space
for individuals who are heat-stressed. In addition, the walk-in refrigeration capacity is supported for
the resilience operations mode, and chilled drinks and ice can be available on-site for aiding thermal
comfort of the surrounding population that is not in distress. (3) External conditions in Hawaii are
hot and humid but seldom dangerously hot for healthy resting individuals, especially outdoors. Even
the most extreme heat wave events generated in our simulation are survivable. We therefore
conclude that all thermal resilience concerns for the resilience operations mode of the KCRH can be
overcome if: (1) thermally distressed individuals can be given prioritized access to cooled spaces, and
(2) the microgrid-supported, 16-m2 walk-in refrigeration unit includes capacity to cool drinks for up
to 1,500 people for 3 days and for 282 people for 30 days. It is likely that the same conclusion
concerning thermal resilience of buildings can be drawn for other locations of similar climate and
building characteristics. In short, increases in frequency, duration and intensity for heat waves for
the KCRH design do not sufficiently affect outcomes to make building-wide space cooling a
requirement for future survival. Hauʻula’s present and future climates therefore leave room for
gaining the significant benefits of the resilience operations mode as defined in Table 15. This will
result in a smaller microgrid than would be needed for running in normal mode (with full cooling
services) at all times. The cost benefits from needing a smaller microgrid may cover a significant
amount of the additional cost needed to support the high-efficiency design.

6. BLUE-SKY TECHNO-ECONOMIC ANALYSIS

6.1. Goals of the Techno-Economic Analysis
There are many options to power a building with a distributed energy system that can provide a
desired level of resilience. The goal of the techno-economic analysis is to understand how these
options rank over a prolonged period, in terms of levelized cost of energy. Along with the cost of
energy, the techno-economic analysis provides information on other criteria that may be used to
narrow the field of possible solutions, including emissions, energy exported to the grid, and cash
flow. In this case, the preliminary techno-economic analysis will be used to guide the resilience
optimization, by providing insight into which system components that provide resilience also
perform economically.

6.2. Techno-Economic Analysis Software
The tool chosen for this analysis is a version of Hybrid Optimization of Multiple Energy Resources
(HOMER). This software was originally developed by NLR and subsequently acquired and
commercialized by UL. The specific version of the software used is HOMER Pro, designed
specifically for exploring the lowest-cost solutions for remote power systems, microgrids, and
islanded utilities. HOMER is a simulation model that attempts to simulate a viable system for all
possible combinations of technologies specified by a user. Depending on the setup of the problem,
HOMER may simulate hundreds or even thousands of system combinations. HOMER simulates
the operation of a hybrid microgrid for an entire year, in time steps from 1 minute to 1 hour.

6.3. Required Inputs for the Techno-Economic Analysis
The inputs for a HOMER techno-economic analysis can best be summarized by referring to a
typical model, represented schematically in Figure 18. Electric Load 1 represents critical loads, and
Electric Load 2 represents non-critical loads. The latter class of loads is automatically dropped in the
simulation when grid service is not available. The resources that are called upon to meet the loads
are selected by a user from a suite of options. In the case shown in Figure 18, the resources selected
are the grid, a backup generator, wind turbines, PV arrays at two different orientations, and storage
batteries.

  Figure 18. Components of a HOMER model for the KCRH, representing critical and non-critical
 loads and potential resources to meet the loads

 59

Specific inputs that characterize each category of resource are required. For example, for a PV array,
the solar resource is provided in terms of a direct normal irradiance file and a global horizontal
irradiance file, both of which can be obtained from various climate databases. Also needed for a PV
array are technical characteristics of the modules, such as the efficiency, temperature sensitivity,
degradation parameters, and economic characteristics, such as the cost per kW. These parameters
are available from manufacturer datasheets and are also provided in an extensive HOMER built-in
library. Similar data are required for the storage battery and for the wind turbine.
The characteristics of the grid are of particular interest. These include reliability (expressed in terms
of number of outages per year, mean outage duration, and standard deviation of outage duration).
Various rate structures can be input, including time-of-use rates that can vary by time of day, day of
the week, and season. Other grid service parameters include the ability to feed power to the grid, the
ability to sell power to the grid, and how payments for grid exports are processed. Finally, the
average emissions profile for the grid can be input to estimate the effect of emissions resulting from
utilization of distributed resources.

6.4. Inputs and Assumptions
Reasonable inputs and assumptions are fundamental to obtain a technical solution that will satisfy
the needs of the facility owners in real life. Because the HOMER analysis optimizes for cost only,
care must be exercised to impose constraints that satisfy multiple desired outcomes—for example,
high resilience and low emissions. Here, the choice of grid parameters, renewable energy options,
and backup generation options are provided and explained. The loads that need to be served are also
described.

6.4.1. Critical and Non-Critical Electrical Loads
The primary input for the blue-sky simulation is the electrical load. As noted previously, two load
profiles are needed by HOMER, one being the critical load, and the other being everything else. In
the context of this analysis, critical loads are defined as the loads determined when the building
operates in “resilience” operations, and are assigned to “Load 1” in HOMER, while noncritical
loads, defined as the difference between “normal” operations and “resilience” operations, are
assigned to “Load 2” in HOMER. Thus, when grid power is available, the total load, comprising the
sum of Load 1 and Load 2, is equal to the load under normal conditions, while, when the grid is not
available, Load 2 is dropped by HOMER and the total load is the load in resilience operations. The
two loads are shown graphically in Figure 19 to Figure 22, for both BAU and high-efficiency
building and mechanical system design.

6.4.2. Electricity Service Characteristics
For this preliminary analysis, HoH expressed the goal of maintaining the electricity tariff that is
applied to the existing community center, namely HECO Schedule G (General Service non-
demand), 9.6 c/kWh, $63/month customer charge for three-phase service, no net metering, no sale
to grid, < 25 kW, < 5,000 kWh/month. Therefore, this tariff structure was initially used for the
analysis.

HOMER offers the opportunity to characterize grid reliability by providing statistics for the outage
frequency and duration. The input connected to reliability of the grid was chosen to reflect the
worst-case scenario of 30 days with no grid power, that is, one fixed 30-day event. By selecting this
characterization of reliability, HOMER is forced to search for solutions that provide power to
critical loads for this worst-case scenario, even though a 30-day outage on a yearly basis is highly
unlikely.

The ability of the system to sell energy back to the grid is also a mechanism that often allows
renewable systems to be cost-effective. For this preliminary analysis, no grid sales or net metering
mechanisms were allowed.

the actual model of turbine chosen by the community, with characteristics that are more suited to

Battery storage has become the default energy storage option for microgrids of all sizes, at scales
from residential buildings to neighborhoods and campuses. During grid outages, batteries can
partially or entirely displace fossil-fueled generators when renewable generation is not available.
When grid-connected, a microgrid can use batteries to reduce energy and demand costs. Li-ion
batteries are, in turn, the default chemistry today. Li-ion batteries combine good energy and power
density characteristics with a relatively long life and reasonable costs. There are several types of li-ion
batteries, differing primarily in the chemistry of the cathode, while the anode is generally based on
graphite. Details of the electrochemistry of different cathodes is beyond the scope of this report, but
it is important to understand how different chemistries perform for various applications. The spider
plot shown in Figure 23 is useful to compare the different performance dimensions of different
cathode chemistries. For a stationary application, lifetime and cost and safety are more important
than specific energy or power density. The lithium iron phosphate and the lithium titanate oxide
chemistries offer the best lifetime and safety, but lithium iron phosphate has the edge on cost, and
so it is a good candidate for a resilience hub application. Nickel manganese cobalt chemistries are
also widely used for both automotive applications and stationary applications, because of their good
combination of cost, lifetime, safety, and specific energy, meaning a smaller footprint than lithium
iron phosphate for a specific amount of energy storage capacity than an lithium iron phosphate
battery. For this analysis, the LG 3.3-kWh/3.6-kW NMC battery was chosen among the various
options in the HOMER technology menu, because of its combination of proven performance and
cost. The LG battery has an initial cost of $4,811 and a replacement cost of $3,270, and an operation
and maintenance cost of $208/year. This is in line with NLR’s Annual Technology Baseline estimate
of $1,548/kW (capital expenditure) and $37.3/kW/yr (operations and maintenance) in a
conservative scenario (chosen to reflect higher overall costs in an island situation). However, we
note that technology is evolving rapidly and a more in-depth evaluation should be performed in a
full engineering design of the KCRH’s energy system.

The risk of fire is another aspect of li-ion battery storage systems that should be considered,
especially in a facility close to residential neighborhoods and with the potential of very high
occupancy during special events or during emergencies. Battery fires can result in the dispersion of
toxic materials in the atmosphere and potentially in ground water because of firefighting activities.
Moreover, battery fires are difficult to contain by conventional methods because burning batteries
generate chemical species that can react with each other without the contribution of oxygen in the
atmosphere. Battery fires generally start when one or more individual cells overheat, following
mechanical damage, manufacturing defects, poor charge or discharge management, or a combination
thereof. Localized overheating of a small number of cells can result in the evolution of gases from
the electrode materials, that can react with each other, with or without the contribution of
atmospheric oxygen, and release large amounts of heat. If local overheating is not detected and
stopped in a timely way, adjoining cells can overheat and release additional heat in turn, producing a
so-called “thermal runaway” reaction that is very difficult or impossible to stop. The risk of battery
fire can be minimized by careful selection of technology, starting by requiring that the manufacturer
and/or system integrator comply with standards such as UL1973, UL9540, and UL9540A. Similar
precautions should be considered for the installation process, and for continued operation and
maintenance.

While li-ion batteries offer competitive cost and high performance, with a high round-trip efficiency
often in excess of 90%, there are disadvantages. From an environmental viewpoint, there are

 66

concerns with end-of-lifetime disposal of the materials, as well as with the energy embedded in the
manufacturing process. Another disadvantage of li-ion battery storage is that energy and power
characteristics are difficult to decouple.35 Generally, a battery with twice the storage capacity also has
twice the power capacity, even if the power is not required. An alternative storage technology to li-
ion batteries is provided by hydrogen. A typical hydrogen system is composed of an electrolyzer to
produce hydrogen with excess renewable energy or with grid power, a storage system to store the
hydrogen for periods of time that are appropriate for the application, and a fuel cell to convert
hydrogen to electricity. At first glance, a hydrogen system could be used in the same way as a battery,
to accumulate energy when it is cheap and easily available, store it when not needed, and use it when
appropriate. However, hydrogen storage is different from battery storage in two fundamental ways.
Its round-trip efficiency is low, typically 50% or lower (compared to over 90% for a li-ion battery),
meaning that a substantial fraction of energy is lost in the conversion process, making it less useful
for the purposes of reducing demand charges or similar energy arbitrage. The fact that energy
capacity and power capacity can be completely decoupled means that the energy storage can be sized
to allow system operation for extended periods of time (days to weeks) without having to pay for
high-power production capacity or hydrogen production capacity. For example, one can imagine a
system with enough storage to provide 50 kW for 30 days that can replenish its full stored energy
capacity in 3 months. A system like this could be considered as an alternative to a fossil generator
with a 30-day tank, with the main difference being that full storage capacity is replenished slowly,
on-site, with clean resources, rather than on an as-needed basis with a tanker truck coming to refill
the tank.

As with batteries, there are many choices for electrolyzers, fuel cells, and hydrogen storage. There
are three main types of electrolyzer: alkaline, polymer electrolyte membrane, and solid oxide. While
technology is fast evolving, in general, solid oxide electrolyzers are the most efficient (80% range),
alkaline are the least efficient (50%–60%) and proton exchange membrane are somewhere in the
middle. Solid oxide electrolyzers operate at high temperatures, on the order of 700-800°C, more
suited to an industrial environment. Alkaline electrolyzers use highly corrosive materials, also making
them more well-suited to a tightly controlled industrial environment. Proton exchange membrane
electrolyzers, on the other hand, operate at moderate temperatures and do not involve hazardous
temperatures or materials, with high cost being the only downside. Proton exchange membrane fuel
cells are also the most appropriate technology for this application, for similar reasons. As for the
hydrogen storage, there are three main options: compressed storage, metal hydrides, and liquid
hydrogen. Compressed storage requires high-pressure vessels and compressors. Liquid storage
requires cryogenic liquefaction of hydrogen. Metal hydride storage combined relatively low pressures
with high volumetric efficiency, at the expense of weight. Because this is a stationary application,
weight is not an issue of major concern, so this type of storage will be considered in this evaluation.

While electrolyzers, hydrogen tanks/storage devices, and fuel cells have been used in industry for
decades and can be considered mature technologies, integrated systems that can replace a battery are
still relatively rare. An instance of a manufacturer of a hydrogen storage system of this type is GKN,
an Italian company that specializes in containerized metal hydride hydrogen storage solutions, with
an electrolyzer at the front end and a fuel cell at the back end.

6.4.4. HOMER Optimization Features
The algorithm that HOMER uses to optimize a microgrid is proprietary, so details of how the
solution space is searched are not readily available. However, the problem is a classical constrained
optimization problem, in which a function (in this case, net present cost of energy, including capital
cost, operating cost, and residual value) is minimized while a set of constraints have to be met, in the
sense that at any given time loads (electrical and thermal) must be met by a combination of all
available energy resources (grid, PV, batteries, and so on). At the same time, the generation
resources must also meet criteria set by the user (for example, a maximum size of a PV array that
results from space constraints). The search space can be defined by the user as a continuum (i.e., the
size of a component can vary between zero and a maximum size) or as a discrete set of options.
HOMER can optimize up to four continuum resources simultaneously, and an unlimited number of
resources provided as discrete resources. In general, a mix of the two input modes can be used,
potentially in iterative steps. In theory, there is an infinite set of possible solutions, so HOMER
chooses between solutions that optimize a particular subset of possible solutions, for example those
with PV and grid only, those with PV, grid, and battery storage, and those with grid and fossil
generator only. Finally, HOMER presents the chosen feasible solutions, ranked by net present cost.

6.4.6. Discussion of Optimization Results and Implications
The HOMER optimization produces insight into how a distributed energy system can improve the
resilience of the KCRH. The lowest net present cost resilience solution is provided by a diesel

 73

backup generator that can satisfy critical loads during a prolonged outage. Despite being the
cheapest, this solution is not ideal. From the point of view of resilience, the diesel tank must be large
enough to fuel the generator for a long period, 1,530 gallons for the case of the high-efficiency
design and 1,930 for the BAU design. A tank that can last for the entire grid outage in required
because refueling is not guaranteed during emergency situations. The diesel + PV solution allows for
a smaller tank, 1,120 gallons for the high-efficiency design and 1,260 gallons for the BAU design.

The Hauʻula community is concerned about diesel fuel leaks, that could contaminate the local
aquifer, which is also the most likely source of drinking water for the KCRH, so large fuel tanks are
considered a less desirable option. While it is possible to power the KCRH entirely on renewable
energy for the most extreme situation considered, the cost is prohibitive and the PV array required is
very large, requiring several acres of surface area. A good compromise could be a system with high
renewable penetration, provided by a 114-kW PV array that could still fit on the roof of the main
KCRH building for the high-efficiency case, a 227-kWh battery storage system, and a diesel tank
large enough to power the KCRH critical loads for 3 days, approximately 200 gallons. Another
option that will be considered later is to use a backup generator with a different fuel, such as
propane, for which leaks diffuse in the atmosphere rather than contaminating the ground. Propane
generators are slightly less efficient than their diesel counterparts, requiring more fuel.

Ultimately, there are trade-offs to be made between cost, emissions, land use, and risk of
contamination. The community must consider their highest priorities, constraints, resource
availability, and resources that could be used as alternatives to a power system.

7. BLACK-SKY RESILIENCE ANALYSIS

7.1. Goals of the Resilience Analysis
While the blue-sky analysis focuses primarily on the economic value of distributed energy systems
over decades of operation under normal conditions, the resilience analysis focuses on the few weeks
of the system’s life during which there is no grid power and the system is solely responsible to power
the KCRH. As was the case for the techno-economic analysis, there are many options to provide
power during black-sky conditions: some are cheaper than others, some provide more resilience
than others, and some emit more pollutants than others. The “perfect” solution would be extremely
inexpensive, extremely resilient, and might not emit any greenhouse gas, but unfortunately such a
solution does not exist with current technology. The goal of the present resilience analysis is to find
a solution that offers the best compromise: it is sufficiently resilient to guarantee critical services
during a prolonged outage, it is affordable, and it meets low emissions goals that are expected by
many communities today, and are particularly sought after by the Hauʻula commnunity. Rather than
start from a blank slate, the options for resilience are guided by the results of the techno-economic
analysis that was described in the previous chapter.

7.2. Resilience Analysis Software
The Microgrid Design Toolkit37 (MDT), software developed at Sandia beginning in 2016, was
chosen for this analysis. MDT is the most advanced tool for analyzing the resilience of a distributed
energy system, and it was the recipient of an R&D 100 award in 2018. One of the most distinctive
features of MDT is that it uses a stochastic approach to measure resilience. Specifically, events that
cause major outages (known as a DBT) are not predictable, in the sense that, while the frequency of
occurrence may be known statistically, their actual realization is unknown. To account for all
possible occurrences of a DBT, the simulation is performed thousands of times, to obtain
sufficiently reliable resilience statistics. As an example, an earthquake may happen during a long
period of sunny sky, so that a PV generator would provide power at full capacity, or it might happen
during a period of cloudy skies, in which case a PV generator may not be able to cover the full load.

7.3. Resilience Analysis Model
As discussed previously, Hauʻula is subject to several possible threats, including hurricanes,
earthquakes and tsunamis. A direct hit by the eye of a hurricane may be predictable hours in
advance, but not a week in advance. A tsunami may only allow tens of minutes to prepare. There is
little evidence that earthquakes can be predicted at any time scale. So, the KCRH distributed energy
system must be ready to operate for an extended period at any time, with any weather, and other
possible disruptions to regular operations, such as delivery of fuel. In MDT, the statistics of
occurrence of a DBT are provided by a user, along with a statistical characterization of the severity
of the DBT. For example, a hurricane might be characterized by the likelihood of an occurrence at
specific times of year, and it might be associated with hazards such as wind speed, rain intensity and
cloud cover, that are also characterized statistically. Similar statistical characterizations can be
provided to the software so that all possible combinations of threats and hazards are considered.
Weather files are an important input to the simulation, as they determine both the electrical and
thermal loads, and the ability of some of the distributed resources to produce power. Weather
conditions are already built-in to the electrical load produced by the building simulation. For

7.4. Optimization Results
MDT presents results in the form of a Pareto-optimal front. Points on the Pareto front, which,
depending on the number optimization dimensions, may be in the form of a curve, a surface, or a
hyper-surface, represent the best possible value of one of the optimization dimensions when the
other dimensions are held constant. For example, if the optimization dimensions are cost and
resilience, for a fixed cost, the point on the Pareto front represents the technology portfolio with the
highest resilience, among all the possible different technology solutions available in the search space.
The Pareto front for the case considered here is presented in Figure 31. The Pareto front effectively
conveys the trade-offs that must be made in a multi-objective optimization: better performance in
one of the dimensions entails worse performance in the others. For example, seeking the maximum
cost fitness (or the lowest-cost metric group) results in reduced resilience and reduced

 79

environmental performance. The best compromise between the optimization metrics is often found
at the “knee” of the Pareto front, the area of the surface where the gradient of the optimization
metrics is similar in all directions. In areas away from the knee, varying one of the metrics (e.g., cost)
by a small abount may produce an excessive change in another metric (e.g., resilience). These areas
of the Pareto front represent parts of the solution space where there are diminishing returns
(e.g.,where trying to extract an additional small percentage of resilience might incur in costs that are
several-fold).
In what follows, three solution portfolios will be presented: one with an emphasis on environmental
performance, one with an emphasis on balancing the three optimization metrics, and one where low
capital cost is sought.

renewable energy spilled per outage is over 9,000 kWh, indicating that the solar array and wind
generator combination is oversized for the islanded case. However, this excess energy could be fed
into the grid during blue-sky conditions, so its value depends on how much the utility is prepared to
pay for energy exports from the microgrid.
MDT also provides statistics on the utilization of different components of the system. For example,
it is interesting to inspect the statistics on the state of charge of storage devices, because this
indicates whether these storage systems are over-utilized, under-utilized, or utilized well. In this case,

 80

the hydrogen storage spends approximately 95% of the time between 20% and 80% state of charge,
indicating that it is sized correctly. Similarly, the li-ion battery spends 100% of the time with state of
charge almost equally divided between 10% and 90% (with a slight preference for 90% charged,
likely during times of high solar generation), meaning that it is sized appropriately to both serve the
load and to recharge when excess energy from the generators is available.

The microgrid configuration for this solution is shown in Figure 33. In this solution, the largest
storage option is chosen for the li-ion battery, while the smallest hydrogen storage system is selected.
The solar generator has a total nameplate power of 300 kW, which will likely not fit entirely on the
roof area of the KCRH, requiring additional area, perhaps as a solar shading structure or on the roof
one of the auxiliary buildings. As in the zero-emissions solution, a 25-kW wind generator is
specified. The 50-kW propane generator is coupled with a medium-sized 1,000-gallon tank. The total
cost of this solution is approximately $2.19 million.

The resilience performance of the system is high. Critical loads are served with 100% energy

The microgrid configuration for a typical low-cost solution is shown in Figure 34. In this solution,
the smallest storage option is chosen for the Li-ion battery, with no hydrogen storage. The solar
generator has a total nameplate power of 175 kW, and provides the entirety of the renewable

generator is selected. There is no hydrogen storage, and battery storage is an 80-kW/336-kWh li-ion

7.5. Overall Implications of the Resilience Analysis
The three technology portfolios presented are intended to highlight the choices that must be made
by the community as the design of the KCRH progresses from concept to construction. Regulation
that requires backup fossil generation notwithstanding, it is possible to power the system entirely by
renewables during a 30-day outage, with power availability of 100% for critical loads and
approximately 85% for lower-tier loads. But this comes at a cost, monetary and otherwise. Aside

 82

from the $5 million in initial capital cost, a 375-kW PV array also requires a lot of space. The roof of
the KCRH is not large enough to accommodate it, and it will be necessary to build other support
structures such as shaded parking or agrivoltaics to accommodate at least 200 kW of the 375-kW
total.
The storage components needed to support an all-renewable solution are also large, with the li-ion
battery likely housed in a 20’ ISO intermodal shipping container and the hydrogen battery housed in
several (3–4) containers. It should also be noted that the hybrid storage solution must be evaluated
carefully, as it adds complexity and potentially skillsets that are not easily available on-island. The
9,000 kWh of renewable energy spilled during black-sky conditions could be pushed back to the grid
during blue-sky operation, potentially recovering, over time, some of the energy initial capital
expenditure.

 Figure 35. A propane generator and associated tanks of comparable sizes to what could be
 installed at KCRH. For scale, the Kohler generator is approximately 6' in height.

The “compromise” solution involves a fossil backup generator that would be used to provide, on
average, 15% of the energy. A typical propane generator tank setup is shown in Figure 35. Given
that a fossil backup is likely to be required by code, installing a propane generator is also a
reasonable solution, as it allows a reduction in the size of the PV array from 375 kW to 300 kW, and
a considerable reduction in the size of the hydrogen storage needed, from 10 MWh to 2 MWh. The
capital cost of this solution is also considerably smaller, at 44% of the zero-emissions solution. It
should also be noted that this analysis considers only 30-day grid outages (this is an extremely
unlikely event, and the vast majority of outages will be substantially shorter), meaning that stored
hydrogen and battery will likely provide power until grid services are restored. The average
renewable energy spilled per outage in the compromise solution is approximately 6,000 kWh, larger
than the average energy produced by the generator, 4,658 kWh. The reason why renewable energy is
spilled is that storage is either not large enough or cannot charge fast enough to take advantage of
the excess energy. A more sophisticated controller would likely reduce the amount of spilled energy.
In a grid-tied situation, the KCRH would likely operate as a net-zero building.
Finally, the low-cost solution is also viable and should be considered. About 50% of the energy
would still come from renewable resources, with 3,400 kWh per outage spilled due to insufficient
storage resources, meaning that the average energy supplied by renewables in a blue-sky scenario
would be even higher, over 60%. It would likely be possible to site the 175-kW PV array on the

 83

main building roof. Propane generators require relatively little maintenance compared to their diesel
counterparts, and fuel can be stored indefinitely, as it is not subject to degradation. Moreover, a
phased approach to a fully or mostly renewable generation portfolio could be adopted over time.

8. DISCUSSION OF OPTIONS
In this section, we summarize findings of previous sections and highlight issues that are relevant to
stakeholders’ decision-making. We highlight considerations of resilience, performance of the system
with respect to emissions and sustainability, and economics. We will also highlight issues to do with
the footprint of the energy system so that cultural and aesthetic implications may also be evaluated
by the Hauʻula stakeholders.

8.1. Systems With Hybrid Renewable/Fossil Backup
Traditionally, fossil-fueled backup generators in grid-connected buildings provide power to essential
services such as lighting, security, and life safety systems during grid outages. To ensure that backup
generators perform their function, it is customary to start and run the backup generator on a regular
basis (typically monthly) to ensure that they are in good working order. This also ensures that the
fuel is used and that it is replenished regularly. In this analysis, the intent is to augment the backup
generator with distributed renewable systems, so that the utilization of the generator would only
occur during particularly long outages or when renewable energy is not available—for example,
during extended periods of cloudy weather.
The choice of options is large. The analysis focused on resilience for extended outages lasting 30
days, with the assumptions of 3 days of cloudy weather post-hurricane and no refueling. The
question of what is a reasonable amount of energy provided by the backup generator compared to
the energy provided by renewables is still open. To enable framing of the trade-offs that need to be
made, we first examined a system for which the average fraction of energy supplied by the generator
over the course of a large sample of month-long outages is 15%. The characteristics of the system
are as follows:
 • PV: 300 kW
     •   Wind: 25 kW
 • Propane genset: 50 kW, with 1,000-gallon fuel tank
 • Li-ion battery: 160 kW/672 kWh
 • Hydrogen storage: 14 kW/2,000 kWh.
It is also likely that, for the majority of outages that are substantially shorter than 30 days, the
renewable penetration could be much closer to 100%.
The total estimated cost of this solution is approximately $2.2 million. However, there is some
uncertainty in this. While the cost of the PV and the li-ion battery is well known, the real cost of the
hydrogen storage might only be availabe via a vendor quote, and may be higher than the estimate
provided here.
Another issue that is likely of importance is that a 300-kW nameplate system, assuming an efficiency
of 20%, requires a total PV panel area of at least 1,500 m2. While the KCRH rooftop may be able to
accommodate part of this system, other structures (e.g., shaded parking) may be required for the
rest. Also, it is unclear whether the lower section of the parcel of land where the KCRH is to be
sited is outside of the tsunami zone, although elevated parking shading may remain out of the water.
So, while this solution is attractive because of its reasonable (if somewhat uncertain) cost, and
because of its high renewable fraction, other practicalities might make it difficult.

 85

Given that 30-day outages are a worst-case scenario and rare (such an outage has not been recorded
since Hurricane Iniki in 1992), perhaps a lower renewable fraction might be acceptable. A
configuration that provides a renewable energy fraction of 51% is as follows:
 • PV: 175 kW
 • Wind: none
 • Propane genset: 50 kW, with 3,000-gallon fuel tank
 • Li-ion battery: 80 kW/336 kWh
 • Hydrogen storage: none.
The estimated cost of this system is substantially lower, at $738,000. It should also be noted that the
175-kW system, at a capacity factor of 0.25, would provide an average of 35 kW power production,
sufficient to meet the average building load of approximately 25 kW with the ability to export some
power to the HECO grid during blue-sky conditions. This system would likely provide a higher
renewable fraction during shorter outages. Finally, the system is also considerably simpler, has a
smaller footprint, and is less likely to suffer from component failures.
In both cases, the fuel chosen is propane. Propane generators are less efficient than their diesel
counterparts, but have some key advantages that make them particularly suitable for this application.
First, there is no danger of contaminating the acquifer should a tank leakage occur: propane is in the
gas phase at atmospheric pressure, and propane emanating from a leakage would easily disperse in
the atmosphere. A second advantage over diesel is that propane stored in a tank does not degrade.
This is particularly significant in this case because, other than the regular monthly startup, the
generator would rarely be used, so that fuel might remain in the tank for years. While there is a
potential for ignition, that is easily mitigated by location of the equipment (tank and generator) in a
well-ventilated area.

8.2. All-Renewable Systems
The resilience analysis shows that a system that provides all power to the KCRH from renewable
resources is possible. This is thanks in part to Hawaii’s fortunate combination of relatively small
thermal loads and abundant year-round solar resource. The concept of an all-renewable system is
also aligned with the societal goal of a zero-carbon economy in the medium-term future. However,
an all-renewable system also requires some compromises. The configuration of an all-renewable
system is as follow:
 • PV: 375 kW
   •   Wind: 25 kW
 • Propane genset: none
 • Li-ion battery: 160 kW/672 kWh
 • Hydrogen storage: 30 kW/10,000 kWh.
A 375-kW PV array could be difficult to site, as it requires 1,875 m2. Assuming, optimistically, that
900 m2 could be accommodated on the roof of the KCRH, 975 m2 would still need a ground-
mounted structure to accommodate it. A possible configuration could be as a shading structure, for

 86

example for parking/charging. An example of such a structure is shown in Figure 36. The structure
should be designed to survive damage from tsunamis and hurricane-force winds.

Figure 36. Example of a solar canopy used as shading for parking. The large canopy visible in the
 image has an area of approximately 600 m2. The canopy is used to charge electric transit buses
 owned by the Vineyard Transit Authority on Martha’s Vineyard, Massachusetts.

The large PV array is sized to provide sufficient electricity during a sequence of cloudy sky days
following a hurricane, but it is oversized during blue-sky operations. To prevent spillage of energy,
the KCRH would export significant amonts of energy to HECO, potentially recovering some of the
cost of installation over time. The cost of the all-renewable power system is approximately $4.93
million, with the caveat of high uncertainty in the cost of the hydrogen system.
The large hydrogen storage can provide power to the building for approximately 2 weeks. This is not
useful in blue-sky conditions, but is one of the assets that provides resilience during the most
extreme circumstances, when solar production is not sufficient to replenish the li-ion battery on a
daily basis. This asset serves a similar purpose to the propane backup in the hybrid backup.
A detailed life cycle emissions analysis focusing on embedded energy in all the components, as well
as the energy required to decommission the components after their useful lifetime, is beyond the
scope of this report; however, it may be desirable in case the all-renewable option is chosen to
ensure that the benefits of an all-renewable system are realized.

8.3. The Role of Energy Efficiency
Energy efficiency measures in the envelope and mechanical systems of the KCRH deliver
approximately 33% energy savings in blue-sky operations and 25% energy savings in the case of
black-sky operations. Given that the energy system is sized based on resilience operations, it could
be argued that the additional upfront capital cost to power a building built to code, rather than to
maximum efficiency, PassivHaus-like standards would cost approximately 33% more. In the case of
the least-cost solution provided here, the extra cost would be approximately $243,000. For the fossil-
backed solution with 85% renewable penetration, the extra cost would be approximately $726,000,

 87

while the extra cost for the all-renewable solution is approximately $1.63 million. The higher cost of
energy, or lower revenue from selling energy to HECO, should also be considered when evaluating
options.

8.4. Revisited Blue-Sky Analysis
To close the loop between blue-sky techno-economic analysis and black-sky resilience analysis, it is
instructive to combine the two sets of results to obtain an estimate of the long-term costs of options
that are able to satisfy resilience needs. To that end, we used HOMER’s optimization capabilities in
a similar process as presented in Section 6, but limited the choice of distributed power options to
those identified by the MDT resilience analysis. However, the grid outage scenarios were not 30-day
ones, but a random realization of typical outages in recent history, up to a few days’ duration.
Following conversations with HECO, we also changed the electricity rate from “Schedule G” to the
new “Smart DER” schedule, combined with the “Shift and Save Schedule G,” a time-of-use rate
that allows small commercial customers to sell energy to the grid. This schedule, which went into
effect on April 1, 2024, encourages distributed energy resources to export power to assist the grid,
and provides compensation for providing these services, as shown in Figure 37.
