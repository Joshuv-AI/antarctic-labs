Robust Learning with Optimal Error
Guy Blanc

Abstract
We construct algorithms with optimal error for learning with adversarial noise. The overarching theme of this work is that the use of randomized hypotheses can substantially improve upon the best error rates achievable with deterministic hypotheses. ∘ \circ For η \eta -rate malicious noise, we show the optimal error is 1 2 ⋅ η / ( 1 − η ) \frac{1}{2}\cdot\eta/(1-\eta) , improving on the optimal error of deterministic hypotheses by a factor of 1 / 2 1/2 . This answers an open question of Cesa-Bianchi et al. (JACM 1999) who showed randomness can improve error by a factor of 6 / 7 6/7 . ∘ \circ For η \eta -rate nasty noise, we show the optimal error is 3 2 ⋅ η \frac{3}{2}\cdot\eta for distribution-independent learners and η \eta for fixed-distribution learners, both improving upon the optimal 2 ​ η 2\eta error of deterministic hypotheses. This closes a gap first noted by Bshouty et al. (Theoretical Computer Science 2002) when they introduced nasty noise and reiterated in the recent works of Klivans et al. (NeurIPS 2025) and Blanc et al. (SODA 2026). ∘ \circ For η \eta -rate agnostic noise and the closely related nasty classification noise model, we show the optimal error is η \eta , improving upon the optimal 2 ​ η 2\eta error of deterministic hypotheses. All of our learners have sample complexity linear in the VC-dimension of the concept class and polynomial in the inverse excess error. All except for the fixed-distribution nasty noise learner are time efficient given access to an oracle for empirical risk minimization. Our techniques reveal close connections between optimal robustness and algorithmic fairness: We show that optimal error agnostic learning is equivalent to multiaccuracy and that optimal malicious and distribution-independent nasty noise learners can be constructed via a black-box post-processing of a calibrated multiaccurate learner, though with suboptimal sample and oracle complexity. Our techniques for improving these complexities may be of independent interest.

What is the optimal error of robust learners?

Randomized vs. deterministic hypotheses.
It is well-known that for many models of adversarial noise, empirical risk minimization (ERM) achieves optimal error when benchmarking only against
deterministic
hypotheses. For example, consider
\eta
-rate nasty noise, in which the adversary is allowed to arbitrarily corrupt an
\eta
-fraction of the data. In the paper introducing nasty noise, Bshouty, Eiron, and Kushilevitz show that ERM produces a hypothesis with
\approx 2\eta
error and that no algorithm outputting a deterministic hypothesis can achieve better error
[
11
]
.

To prove this lower bound,
[
11
]
first observe that if
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are at distance
2\eta
, the randomized function
f_{\mathrm{mix}}=\frac{f_{0}+f_{1}}{2}
is only at distance
\eta
from each of
f_{0}
or
f_{1}
. Therefore, an
\eta
-rate adversary can create a dataset that has labels sampled from
f_{\mathrm{mix}}
regardless of whether the true target is
f_{0}
or
f_{1}
. The key step is to then force all of the
2\eta
distance between
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
to come from them classifying a single point,
x^{\star}
, differently. Then, any
deterministic
hypothesis must commit to either positively or negatively classifying
x^{\star}
. Regardless of its decision, it will have
2\eta
error on whichever of
f_{0}
or
f_{1}
it disagrees with.

This lower bound is unsatisfactory. First, observe the incongruence between the adversary generating samples from a randomized function,
f_{\mathrm{mix}}
, while the learner is forced to output a deterministic hypothesis. More generally, learning with adversarial noise is, as the naming suggests, learning in an inherently stochastic environment. In stochastic environments, randomized hypotheses, which can equivalently be viewed as outputting probabilities, are far more informative than deterministic hypotheses. For example, in weather prediction, such probabilities are more useful than a deterministic positive or negative prediction. (see also the excellent introduction of Kearns and Schapire
[
35
]
for many more examples in which randomized hypotheses are preferable).

Second, observe that this lower bound against deterministic hypotheses involves a single “heavy” point,
x^{\star}
, that appears multiple times in the dataset. In practical settings, there is little reason to not simply memorize the labels of heavy points. Our work shows that even if one wishes to output a deterministic hypothesis, heavy points are the only obstruction to improved error. This is because randomized hypotheses can easily be converted to deterministic hypotheses with comparable error whenever there are no heavy points (via randomized rounding, see
A.1
). Hence, our results also improve the error rates of deterministic hypotheses whenever there are no heavy points.

Our results

2.1
Malicious noise

We first state our optimal algorithm for learning with
malicious noise
, a model of robust learning first studied by Valiant
[
53
]
only one year after he defined the PAC model. In
\eta
-malicious noise, if the true target is
c^{\star}
and data distribution is
\mathcal{D}
, each point is created as follows:

1.

With probability
1-\eta
, a clean point is sampled
(\bm{x},c^{\star}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}
.

2.

With probability
\eta
, the adversary may choose
any

(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

Hence, approximately
\eta
-fraction of the dataset is chosen by the adversary. It is well-known that the optimal error of any learner forced to output a deterministic hypothesis with
\eta
-malicious noise is
\approx\eta/(1-\eta)
and that this is achieved by ERM
[
36
]
. Cesa-Bianchi et al.
[
14
]
followed this with an investigation of learners allowed to output random hypotheses and showed:

1.

No learner can achieve error better than
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
.

2.

With a massive sample (larger than the domain size), it is possible to achieve error approaching
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
. Such a large sample allows for a memorization-based strategy, though their result still requires a clever probabilistic construction in addition to memorization.

3.

With a sample size linear in the VC dimension of the concept class, it is possible to achieve error approaching
\frac{6}{7}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
. As they remark, their learner requires solving a “challenging combinatorial problem” and does not appear to be efficiently implementable even given an ERM oracle.

They explicitly ask whether it is possible to give a learner with efficient sample complexity and error
\approx\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
and note “it seems [this question is] not easy to answer.” We resolve it.

Theorem 1

(Optimal learning with malicious noise, see
Theorem

8
for the full version)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
with VC dimension
d
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is an algorithm that learns
\mathcal{C}
with
\eta
-malicious noise and error at most
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}+\varepsilon
using
O(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^{2}})
samples. Furthermore, this algorithm is efficiently implementable using
O(1/\varepsilon)
calls to an ERM oracle for
\mathcal{C}
.

This learner improves on that of
[
14
]
both because it achieves optimal error with a reasonable sample size and because it is efficiently implementable using an ERM oracle. The full version,
Theorem

8
, furthermore shows that the sample complexity can be improved to
\tilde{O}(\frac{d}{\varepsilon})
in exchange for an error of
(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha)\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}+\varepsilon
where
\alpha
is an arbitrarily small constant. This mirrors how
[
14
]
state their result.

Nasty noise

In the nasty noise model
[
11

, also called strong contamination
[
17

, first a clean dataset of i.i.d.

\approx\eta

[

(Optimal distribution-independent learning with nasty noise, see

.

The same algorithm from

Our lower bound in

Theorem 3

(Optimal fixed-distribution learning with nasty noise, see
Theorem

11

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
with VC dimension
d
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and
\varepsilon>0

\eta

\eta+\varepsilon

It is an enticing open problem to achieve an error rate of

The relationship between malicious and nasty noise.
Recent work

2.3
Agnostic noise and nasty classification noise

Nasty classification noise
[
11
]
is a restricted variant of nasty noise where the adversary may only corrupt labels; i.e. change the point
(x,y)
to
(x,-y)
. This is closely related to agnostic noise
[
27
,
37
]
in which the adversary chooses some (possibly randomized) function
{\bm{f}}
which is promised to be
\eta
-close to the target
c^{\star}
, and the learner is given i.i.d. examples
(\bm{x},{\bm{f}}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}
. Nasty classification noise can only be harder than agnostic noise so we state
Theorem

4
using this harder model (though the two models were recently shown to be equivalent up to polynomial factors in the sample and time complexity
[
10

). In both cases, the optimal error of learners outputting deterministic hypotheses is
2\eta

[
11
]
.

Theorem 4

(Optimal learning with nasty classification noise, see
Theorem

12
for the formal version)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
with VC dimension
d
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is an algorithm that learns
\mathcal{C}
with
\eta
-nasty classification noise and error at most
\eta+\varepsilon
using
\tilde{O}(\frac{d}{\varepsilon^{4}})
samples. Furthermore, this algorithm is efficiently implementable using
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
calls to an ERM oracle for
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 1

(Two definitions of error for agnostic noise)
.

There are two ways to define the error of a hypothesis
h
with agnostic noise: One is to measure
\mathrm{error}(h,c^{\star})
where
c^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}
is the original uncorrupted function. With this measure, randomness is necessary for optimal error and
Theorem

4
is the first optimal error agnostic learner. A second common approach is to measure
\mathrm{error}(h,{\bm{f}})
where
{\bm{f}}
is the
corrupted
function. In this second case, ERM already achieves the optimal error of
\approx\eta
.

2.4
Broader observations and connections

A close connection to algorithmic fairness.
Our techniques illuminate a connection between definitions from the algorithmic fairness literature and optimal error robust learners. We show that
multiaccuracy

[
39
]
is equivalent to a slight weakening of the learner given in
Theorem

4
, and any calibrated multiaccurate hypothesis
[
25
,
13
]
can be post-processed into one satisfying the requirements of
Theorems

1
and
2
, albeit with suboptimal sample and oracle complexity. We elaborate on these connections in
Section

10
.

The necessity of improper learners.

If we restrict to deterministic hypotheses, ERM is optimal for all of nasty, malicious, and agnostic noise. This implies that optimal error can be achieved by a
proper
learner: One that always outputs a hypothesis in the concept class
\mathcal{C}
. The analogous notion of properness for learners outputting a randomized hypothesis is to require that hypothesis to be a mixture of functions contained within
\mathcal{C}
. However, we show that such learners cannot achieve optimal error.

Claim 2.1

(Improper learners are necessary)
.

None of the error guarantees of
Theorems

1
,
2
,
3
and
4
are achievable by algorithms that are forced to output hypotheses that are mixtures of functions within
\mathcal{C}
, even when the VC dimension of
\mathcal{C}
is
1
, the excess error
\varepsilon
is
\Omega(1)
, and the algorithm receives an unbounded number of samples.

Our learners output hypotheses that are mixtures of functions within
\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C})
, i.e. can be formed by taking the majority of
k
functions within
\mathcal{C}
. The learner of
Theorems

1
and
2
uses a constant
k
(we show
k=7
suffices), whereas those of
Theorems

3
and
4
set
k=O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
.

The necessity of distinct learners.
A further implication of the optimality of ERM is that, when restricting to deterministic hypotheses, there is one algorithm that is optimal for all of nasty, malicious, and agnostic noise.
In contrast, not all of our learners are the same. We show this is necessary.

Claim 2.2

(Distinct learners are necessary)
.

There is no one algorithm achieving the error guarantee of both
Theorem

1
and
Theorem

4
even when the VC dimension of
\mathcal{C}
is
1
, the excess error
\varepsilon
is
\Omega(1)
, and the algorithm is given an unbounded number of samples.

In spite of
2.2
, it is possible to design a single learner that improves upon the optimal deterministic learner for all three noise models. In particular, the learner of
Theorem

1
achieves optimal error for malicious noise and
\frac{3\eta}{2}
error nasty noise (by
Theorem

2
) which translates to
\frac{3\eta}{2}
error in the easier agnostic/nasty classification noise setting. All of these improve upon what is possible with deterministic classifiers.

3
Technical overview

Our learners are designed through a unified framework. We first convert the desired accuracy guarantee to a linear loss function. Then, using the minimax lemma, show there exists a single hypothesis that has good loss for all possible target functions. Finally, for those algorithms efficiently implementable with an ERM oracle, we use a general optimization procedure
Theorem

6
to efficiently search for such a hypothesis.

In this section, we will describe two representative proofs. First, we describe our malicious noise learner (
Theorem

1
) which illustrates these three steps cleanly. Second, we will describe our fixed-distribution nasty noise learner (
Theorem

3
) as that requires a particularly tricky analysis.

3.1
Learning with malicious noise

Step 1: Define a linear loss function.

It will typically be convenient to work with a real-valued predictor,
\bar{h}:X\to[-1,1]
. At the end, we output its randomization
\bm{h}\coloneqq\mathrm{Rad}(\bar{h})
,

\bm{h}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{with probability }\frac{1+\bar{h}(x)}{2}\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Given a dataset
S
, we first define a loss function
\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})
such that if
\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})
is small, then
\bm{h}
will have the desired accuracy in the case where the target function
c^{\star}
is
c
.

In the case of malicious noise, we receive a dataset
\bm{S}=((\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}),...,(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n}))
. If the noise rate were
\eta
, then there is an uncorrupted index set
I\subseteq[n]
of size roughly
(1-\eta)n
, where
\bm{S}_{I}
are i.i.d. samples of the form
(\bm{x},c^{\star}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}
. A standard generalization argument gives that

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\bm{h}}[\bm{h}(\bm{x})\neq c^{\star}(\bm{x})]\approx\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(I),\bm{h}}[\bm{h}(\bm{x}_{\bm{i}})\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{i}}].

(1)

Therefore, our goal is to learn a hypothesis
\bm{h}
satisfying

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(I),\bm{h}}[\bm{h}(\bm{x}_{\bm{i}})\neq\bm{y}_{\bm{i}}]\leq\frac{\eta}{2(1-\eta)}.

(2)

The challenge in establishing the above inequality is that we do not know what the uncorrupted indices
I
are. Still, we show there is a loss that is only small if
\bm{h}
simultaneously has small error on
all

I
that are consistent with some
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

Claim 3.1

(Malicious error can be linearized)
.

For any dataset
S\in(X\times\{\pm 1\})^{n}
, let
\bar{h}:X\to[-1,1]
be any function satisfying, for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})\leq 0\quad\quad\text{where }\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}\left[c(\bm{x})\bm{y}\cdot\big(2-\bar{h}(\bm{x})\bm{y}\big)-\bar{h}(\bm{x})\bm{y}\right].

(3)

Then, the randomized function
\bm{h}=\mathrm{Rad}(\bar{h})
satisfies
Equation

2
simultaneously for all
\eta
and
I
of size
(1-\eta)n
for which there exists some
c\in\mathcal{C}
that is fully consistent with
S_{I}
.

Step 2: Use the minimax lemma to establish the existence of a low complexity hypothesis with small loss.

Next, to ensure that the generalization step of
Equation

1
holds with a small sample size, we want to establish the existence of “low complexity”
\bar{h}
with small loss. In the case of malicious noise, we will always choose a
\bar{h}
that is a convex combination of functions in
\textsc{Maj}_{7}(\mathcal{C})
, each formed by taking the majority of at most
7
functions within
\mathcal{C}
. This class has the same VC dimension as
\mathcal{C}
up to constants (see
4.6
), and so
\approx\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})
samples suffice. To establish the existence of such an
\bar{h}
, we apply the minimax lemma:

\min_{\bar{h}\in\mathrm{Conv}(\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C}))}\Big\{\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})\right\}\Big\}=\max_{\text{distribution }\mu\text{ on }\mathcal{C}}\Big\{\min_{\bar{h}\in\mathrm{Conv}(\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C}))}\Big\{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu}\left[\ell_{S}(\bm{c},\bar{h})\right]\Big\}\Big\}.

Hence, our task becomes to show that for any distribution
\mu
over concepts, there is a way to choose
\bar{h}
so that the expectation of
\ell_{S}(\bm{c},\bar{h})
over
\bm{c}\sim\mu
is nonpositive. On a technical level, this makes our job substantially easier since we can choose
\bar{h}
as a function of
\mu
. Indeed, the following becomes quite easy to show.

Claim 3.2

(A good hypothesis exists for any distribution of concepts)
.

Let
g:[-1,1]\to[-1,1]
be any function satisfying, for all
t\in[-1,1]
,

\frac{2t}{1+t}\leq g(t)\leq\frac{2t}{1-t}.

(4)

Then, for any distribution
\mu
over functions and dataset
S

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu}\left[\ell_{S}(\bm{c},\bar{h})\right]\leq 0\quad\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{h}(x)=g\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu}[\bm{c}(x)]\right).

Finally, we need to pick
g
satisfying the constraints of
Equation

4
so that, for any distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{C}
, the function
g\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu}[\bm{c}(x)]\right)
is in
\mathrm{Conv}(\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C}))
. We show this is possible for
k=7
(see also
Figure

4
for a visualization of these constraints and our specific choice of
g
).

At this point, we have a sample efficient but time inefficient learner: Brute force search for any
\bar{h}\in\mathrm{Conv}(\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C}))
satisfying
\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})\right\}\leq 0
and output
\bm{h}=\mathrm{Rad}(\bar{h})
.

Step 3: Using an ERM oracle to make the learner efficient.

We first observe that for any candidate hypothesis
\bar{h}
, an ERM oracle allows us to easily estimate
\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})
. This is because the
c
with maximal loss is that which minimizes a
weighted
empirical risk minimization problem,

\argmax_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})=\argmin_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}\cdot\mathds{1}[c(x_{i})\neq y_{i}]\quad\quad\text{where }w_{i}=\overline{h}(x_{i})y_{i}-2.

It is quite straightforward to convert a weighted ERM problem to a standard unweighted problem
4.3
. Therefore, we have an efficient procedure to check whether a candidate hypothesis
\bar{h}
already has small loss, and if it does not, return a particular
c\in\mathcal{C}
for which it has large loss.

Given this, we show an extremely simple iterative algorithm (see
Figure

1
) successfully finds a hypothesis with small loss. This algorithm maintains a distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{C}
and hypothesis
\bar{h}=g(\mu)
(where
g
is as in
3.2
). At each step, if there is some
c
for which
\ell_{S}(c,\bar{h})
is large, it updates
\mu
in the direction of
c
.

LearnMal
(S,\mathcal{C},g,\varepsilon):

Input:
A dataset
S
, concept class
\mathcal{C}
,
g
satisfying
Equation

4
, and parameter
\varepsilon>0
.

Output:
A hypothesis
h
satisfying
\ell_{S}(c,h)\leq\varepsilon
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
where
\ell

Equation

3
.

1.

Initialize
\mu_{1}=c
for any
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

2.

Repeat for
t=1,2,\ldots

(i)

Set

(approximately) maximizing
\ell_{S}(c,h_{t})
.

(ii)

If
\ell_{S}(c_{t},h_{t})\leq\varepsilon

. Otherwise, update:

\mu_{t+1}=\mu_{t}+\gamma(c_{t}-\mu_{t})\quad\quad\text{where}\quad\gamma=\frac{2}{t+2}.

A priori, it is not even clear that there should exist a single choice of

\overline{h}\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{\mu}}}[g\circ{\bm{\mu}}]
that has small loss. Yet, the learner in
Figure

1
successfully converges to such a single
\mu^{\star}
.

Theorem 5

(Special case of
Theorem

6
)
.

The algorithm in
Figure

1
converges in
O(1/\varepsilon)
iterations.

Theorem

5
is a special case of a more general theorem, which we also use to prove
Theorem

4
and may be of independent interest. We consider loss functions
\ell_{S}(c,h)
which can be written as

\ell_{S}(c,h)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}[f(c(x),h(x),y)]

for some multilinear function
f
. We show the same simple algorithm converges provided
f
meets two mild constraints: The first is that we need an analogue of
3.2
to hold (roughly speaking, that the minimax lemma can establish the existence of a good hypothesis). Second, we need the loss function “prefer” (i.e. be smaller) when
c
and
h
agree than when they disagree.

3.2
Fixed-distribution nasty noise, overview of
Theorem

3

Our most technically challenging theorem to prove is that it is possible to learn with
\eta
-error given a sample corrupted by the nasty adversary, provided the learner knows the distribution
\mathcal{D}
over unlabeled examples. In this setting, first a clean sample is generated
\bm{S}^{\circ}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}(\bm{x},c^{\star}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}
and then an adversary corrupts approximately
\eta
-fraction of the points to produce
\bm{S}
, which is given to the learner.

The key step is to appropriately define a quantity
\eta(c,S,\mathcal{D})
which estimates how many corruptions the adversary would need to make to corrupt a “typical” clean sample
\bm{S}^{\circ}
into the corrupted sample
\bm{S}=S
. When defining this estimate, there are two criteria to balance.

1.

The estimates should be large enough so that, given a corrupted dataset
\bm{S}
and base distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the learner can find a single hypothesis
\bm{h}
for which

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\bm{h}}[\bm{h}(\bm{x})\neq c(\bm{x})]\leq\eta(c,\bm{S},\mathcal{D})\quad\quad\text{for all }c\in\mathcal{C}.

(5)

2.

The estimates cannot be (much) larger than the number of corruptions the adversary made. In particular, for the true target
c^{\star}
, it should be the case that with high probability over the randomness of the sample and the adversary’s decisions

\eta(c^{\star},\bm{S},\mathcal{D})\leq\eta+\varepsilon.

Our method for constructing these estimates will combine two natural methods for certifying that the adversary needed to make a large number of corruptions to produce a given sample. First, we observe that if
S
disagrees with
c
on
\eta
-fraction of the points, then the adversary would have needed to corrupt
\eta
-fraction of any
\bm{S}^{\circ}
consistent with
c
to form
S
. This means we can take

\eta^{(\mathrm{labels})}(c,S,\mathcal{D})=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}[c(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}].

It turns out the above estimate will not be large enough to ensure
Equation

5
because it does not depend on the distribution
\mathcal{D}
. One way to take advantage of the learner’s knowledge
\mathcal{D}
is to use it to detect
distribution shift
in the unlabeled portion of
S
. In particular, for any “low complexity” detection function
f
, it was likely that the adversary had to change roughly

\eta^{(\mathrm{shift)}}(c,S,\mathcal{D})=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}[f(\bm{x})=1]-\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})=1]

fraction of the points within
\bm{S}^{\circ}
to produce
S
. This method, unlike the first, does take advantage of
\mathcal{D}
, but will still not be large enough to ensure
Equation

5
holds because it does not depend on
c
. Our final approach will combine these two approaches. We fix some detection class
\mathcal{F}
and define

\displaystyle\eta(c,S,\mathcal{D})=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\left\{\eta(c,f,S,\mathcal{D})\right\}\quad\quad\mathrm{where}

\displaystyle\quad\eta(c,f,S,\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}[c(\bm{x})\neq\bm{y}\text{ }\mathrm{or}\text{ }f(\bm{x})=1]-\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})=1].

The proof of
Theorem

3
then follows the same two steps we previously outlined. We first show in
8.1
that, provided the class
\mathcal{F}
is low complexity, these estimates will not be too large (i.e.
\eta(c^{\star},\bm{S},\mathcal{D})\leq\eta+\varepsilon
). The more challenging piece is to instantiate
\mathcal{F}
so that the estimates are large enough, meaning
Equation

5
holds. For this second step, we once again use the minimax lemma to show it suffices, for any distribution
\mu
over concepts, to choose a hypothesis
h_{\mu}
and detection function
f_{c,\mu}
for which

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu}\left[\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[h_{\mu}(\bm{x})\neq\bm{c}(\bm{x})]\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu}\left[\eta(\bm{c},f_{\bm{c},\mu},\bm{S},\mathcal{D})\right].

This is analogous to
3.2
but more complicated. Now rather than only choosing the hypothesis
h_{\mu}
, we must also simultaneously choose detection functions
f_{c,\mu}
for all
c
in the support of
\mu
. We elaborate on how to choose these functions in
Section

8.2
. For now, we just mention that there is a sense in which the definitions of
f_{c,\mu}
and
h_{\mu}
are forced once we set up the above equation properly. This implies that our method for lower bounding corruptions is exactly expressive enough to prove the desired error bound.

Preliminaries and notation

Basic notation.
We use
[n]
as shorthand for
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For
S=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
and indexing set
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\subseteq[n]
, we use
S_{I}
as shorthand for
(S_{i_{1}},\ldots,S_{i_{m}})
. We frequently use the function
\mathrm{sign}:\mathds{R}\to\{\pm 1\}
defined as

\mathrm{sign}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x\geq 0$}\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Probability.
Throughout this paper, we use
bold font
to denote random variables. To denote that
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}
independent and identically distributed according to
\mathcal{D}
, we will interchangeably use either
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}
or
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
. For a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we will use
\mathcal{D}(x)
as shorthand for
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\bm{x}=x]
. For a (multi)set
S
, we use
\mathrm{Unif}(S)
to denote the uniform distribution over
S
.

We will frequently invoke three basic probability distributions. The
Rademacher
distribution with mean
\mu\in[-1,1]
, denoted
\mathrm{Rad}(\mu)
takes on
+1
with probability
\frac{1+\mu}{2}
and
-1
with probability
\frac{1-\mu}{2}
. The
Bernoulli
distribution with parameter
p\in[0,1]
, denoted
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
, takes on
1
with probability
p
and
0
otherwise. The
Binomial
distribution with parameters
n\in\mathds{N}
,
p\in[0,1]
, denoted
\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)
, is the sum of
n
i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
random variables.

We will also use the classic Chernoff bound.

Fact 4.1

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}
be independent random variables each bounded on
[0,1]
. For any parameter
\Delta
and
\bm{Z}\coloneqq\bm{z}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{z}_{n}
,

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}[\bm{Z}\geq(1+\Delta)\mu]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-\Delta^{2}\mu}{2+\Delta}\right)\quad\quad\text{where }\mu\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{Z}].

We’ll often apply it in the following regime.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any
p,\alpha\in[0,1],\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, taking

n\coloneqq O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon(\alpha+\varepsilon)}\right)

suffices to ensure that

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{Z}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)}[\bm{Z}\geq n\cdot((1+\alpha)p+\varepsilon)]\leq\delta.

We will only need to define total variation distance for distributions with finite domain, since it is only used in our lower bound construction (which is finite).

Definition 1

(Total variation distance)
.

For any two distributions
\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2}
over the same finite domain
\Omega
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2})\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\Omega}\max\left\{0,\mathcal{D}_{1}(x)-\mathcal{D}_{2}(x)\right\}\quad\quad\text{where }\mathcal{D}(x)\coloneqq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\bm{x}=x].

Learning models.
We work with standard binary classification and always use
\{\pm 1\}
labels (our results are invariant to any renaming of these labels, e.g. to
\{0,1\}
). We begin by recalling the definition of standard (noise-free) PAC learning.

Definition 2

(Noise-free PAC learning)
.

An
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-distribution-independent PAC learning algorithm

A
for a
concept class

{\mathcal{C}}
(a class of functions from
X
to
\{\pm 1\}
)
that uses
n
examples
is a learning algorithm with the following property:
For any (unknown)
target concept

c^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
and (unknown) distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
X
, if
A
receives as input a dataset
\bm{S}
consisting of
n
i.i.d. labeled examples
(\bm{x},c^{\star}(\bm{x}))_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
the hypothesis
\bm{h}
that
A
returns has
error rate
at most
\varepsilon
, i.e.

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{h},c^{\star})\leq\varepsilon,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{h},c^{\star}):=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D},\bm{h}}[\bm{h}(\bm{x})\neq c^{\star}(\bm{x})].

If a learning algorithm
A
only satisfies the above guarantee for some particular distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
, then we say that
A
is a
fixed-distribution
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC learning algorithm for distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
.

We will default to the more challenging distribution-independent PAC model unless otherwise mentioned. Only
Theorem

3
specializes to the fixed-distribution model.

The many definitions of adversarial noise are all modifications of the above definition in which there is an adversary with some control of the dataset
\bm{S}
the learner receives.

Definition 3

(Malicious noise,
[
53
]
)
.

When learning with
\eta
-malicious noise, the sample
\bm{S}\coloneqq((\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n}))
is generated in a sequential fashion. For each
i\in[n]
,

1.

With probability
1-\eta
, a clean sample
\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}
,
\bm{y}_{i}=c^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})
is sampled independently of all prior points.

2.

With probability
\eta
, the adversary may set
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})
to
any
point
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

The learner then receives
\bm{S}
and is not told which were adversarially chosen.

Nasty noise is similar to malicious noise. The key distinction is that the adversary may choose which points it wishes to corrupt.

Definition 4

(Nasty noise,
[
11
]
)
.

When learning with
\eta
-nasty noise, the input to the learning algorithm is generated via the following process.

1.

A clean sample
\bm{S}^{\circ}=\{(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{y}_{n})\}
is generated where each
\bm{x}_{i}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}
and each
\bm{y}_{i}=c^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})
.

2.

After observing this sample, the adversary chooses some subset
\bm{J}\subseteq[n]
of indices to corrupt with the requirement that the marginal distribution of
|\bm{J}|
is
\mathrm{Bin}(n,\eta)
.

3.

The adversary chooses a corrupted dataset
\bm{S}
by arbitrarily changing
\bm{S}^{\circ}_{j}
for all
j\in\bm{J}
(i.e. the only requirement is that
\bm{S}_{i}=\bm{S}^{\circ}_{i}
for any
i\notin\bm{J}
). The learner then receives
\bm{S}
.

Nasty classification noise is similar to nasty noise except the adversary may only corrupt labels.

Definition 5

(Nasty classification noise
[
11
]
)
.

Learning with
\eta
-nasty classification noise is identical to
\eta
-nasty noise (
Definition

4
) except, for
\bm{S}=((\bm{x}_{1}^{\prime},\bm{y}_{1}^{\prime}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{n}^{\prime},\bm{y}_{n}^{\prime}))
it must be the case that
\bm{x}_{i}^{\prime}=\bm{x}_{i}
for all indices
i
, even those falling within
\bm{J}
.

Agnostic noise is similar to nasty classification noise in that the adversary can only corrupt labels, but the adversary must commit in advance to how it will corrupt those labels (note that we still measure accurate w.r.t.
c^{\star}
, not
{\bm{f}}
, see also
Remark

1
).

Definition 6

(Agnostic noise,
[
27
,
37
]
)
.

In
\eta
-agnostic noise, the adversary chooses a (possibly randomized) function
{\bm{f}}
satisfying that
\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}({\bm{f}},c^{\star})\leq\eta
. The learner then receives i.i.d. examples
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
where
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{y}={\bm{f}}(\bm{x})
.

Any learner for nasty classification noise is automatically a learner for agnostic noise with the exact same parameters. A partial converse was recently shown, with a small increase in the failure probability
\delta
as well as a polynomially worse sample size
[
10
]
.

Many of our learners will be efficient given access to an ERM oracle. For simplicity, we write the below as maximizing the agreement on a dataset. Note this is equivalent to minimizing disagreement on a dataset with all the labels flipped.

Definition 7

(ERM oracle)
.

An
ERM oracle
for concept class
\mathcal{C}
takes as input a dataset
S=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
and returns any
c\in\mathcal{C}
maximizing
\sum_{i\in[n]}c(x_{i})y_{i}
. In the case of ties, the ERM oracle may return an arbitrary maximizer.

We will use a folklore conversion of standard ERM oracles to weighted ERM oracles.

Fact 4.3

(Weighted ERM using unweighted ERM)
.

For any
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, there is an algorithm that takes as input a data set
S=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
and weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\in[-b,b]
, runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(b,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta))
, uses one call to an ERM oracle for
\mathcal{C}
, and with probability at least
1-\delta
, outputs some
c\in\mathcal{C}
satisfying,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}c(x_{i})y_{i}\geq\max_{c^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}c(x_{i})y_{i}\right\}-\varepsilon.

The algorithm in
4.3
generates a synthetic dataset consisting of
m\coloneqq\mathrm{poly}(b,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta))
independent samples from
S
where the point
(x_{i},\mathrm{sign}(w_{i})\cdot y_{i})
is sampled with probability proportional to
|w_{i}|
. It then runs unweighted ERM on this synthetic dataset.

VC dimension and Rademacher complexity.

We will work with the standard notion of
VC dimension
.

Definition 8

(VC dimension)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
of functions mapping
X
to
\{\pm 1\}
,
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})
is defined to be the cardinality of the largest set
S
on which
\mathcal{C}
takes on all
2^{|S|}
labelings.

VC dimension is well known to bound generalization error.
To get the tightest sample complexities, we will use a multiplicative form of the generalization bound.

Fact 4.4

(Generalization error from VC dimension, multiplicative form,
[
42
]
)
.

For any target function
f:X\to\{\pm 1\}
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
, concept class
\mathcal{C}
, and parameters
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, set

n=O\left(\frac{\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})\cdot\log(2+\alpha/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon(\alpha+\varepsilon)}\right).

Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, all
c\in\mathcal{C}
satisfy

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(c,f)\leq(1+\alpha)\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\bm{S})}[f(\bm{x})\neq c(\bm{x})]+\varepsilon.

The above generalization bound can be easily extended to any function that is a mixture over concepts in
\mathcal{C}
by taking an expectation of both sides.

Corollary 4.5

(Generalization bound for randomized hypothesis)
.

Under the conditions of
4.4
, we also have that, with probability
1-\delta
over
\bm{S}
, for any randomized function
\bm{h}
that is a mixture of functions within
\mathcal{C}
,

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{h},f)\leq(1+\alpha)\cdot\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\bm{S}),\bm{h}}[f(\bm{x})\neq\bm{h}(\bm{x})]+\varepsilon.

We will also use two straightforward manipulations of VC dimension. The first is about majority expansion:

Fact 4.6

(The VC dimension of majority expansion,
[
23
]
)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
with VC dimension
d
, the VC dimension of
\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C})
is at most
O(d\cdot k\log k)
, where

\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\mathcal{C})=\{x\mapsto\mathrm{sign}(c_{1}(x)+\cdots+c_{k}(x))\mid c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in\mathcal{C}\}.

The second manipulation concerns the VC dimension of symmetric differences.

Fact 4.7

(The VC dimension of symmetric difference,
[
54
]
)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
, let
\Delta(\mathcal{C})
denote the class containing every function of the form
-c_{1}\cdot c_{2}
for
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
\mathrm{VCdim}(\Delta(\mathcal{C}))\leq 10\cdot\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})
.

We will also work with Rademacher complexity.

Definition 9

(Rademacher complexity)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions mapping
X
to
[-1,1]
,
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over
X
, and
n
be a sample size. The Rademacher complexity is

\mathrm{Rad}_{n,\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}\\
\bm{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\sigma}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})\end{subarray}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\bm{\sigma}_{i}f(\bm{x}_{i})\right|\right].

We use a few standard properties of Rademacher complexity, all of which can be found in or are easy consequences of the results in
[
5
]
. The first is that it controls generalization error.

Fact 4.8

(Generalization error from Rademacher complexity)
.

For any
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{D}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, every
f\in\mathcal{F}
satisfies

\left|\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})]-\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\bm{S})}[f(\bm{x})]\right|\leq O\left(\mathrm{Rad}_{n,\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{F})+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right).

Rademacher complexity can be bounded using VC dimension and satisfies many composition properties. The following is an easy consequence of these.

Fact 4.9

(Rademacher bound from VC dimension)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
and
O(1)
-Lipschitz function
\varphi:[-1,1]\to[-1,1]
,

\mathrm{Rad}_{n,\mathcal{D}}(\varphi\circ\mathrm{Conv}(\mathcal{C}))\leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{C})+1}{n}}\right)\qquad\text{for all $n$ and all distributions }\mathcal{D}\text{ over }X.

The minimax theorem.
We’ll use von Neumann’s minimax theorem stated in a form particularly convenient for us.

Fact 4.10

(The minimax theorem
[
55
]
)
.

For any finite concept classes
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{C}
and
\ell:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathds{R}
,

\inf_{\text{distribution }\mu_{h}\text{ over }\mathcal{H}}\left\{\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{h}\sim\mu_{h}}[\ell(\bm{h},c)]\right\}\right\}=\sup_{\text{distribution }\mu_{c}\text{ over }\mathcal{C}}\left\{\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu_{c}}[\ell(h,\bm{c})]\right\}\right\}.

We do not assume that our concept classes are finite, but they do always have finite VC dimension. Finite VC dimension means we can take a finite covering over the concept classes with arbitrarily low error, giving the following.

Corollary 4.11

(Minimax lemma on finite VC classes)
.

For any concept classes
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{C}
with finite VC dimension and a loss function
\ell(h,c)
that depends on only

1.

The evaluations of
h
and
c
on a finite number of points.

2.

Smoothly (with finite Lipschitz constant) on
\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(\bm{x})\neq c(\bm{x})]
for some distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

Then,

\inf_{\text{distribution }\mu_{h}\text{ over }\mathcal{H}}\left\{\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{h}\sim\mu_{h}}[\ell(\bm{h},c)]\right\}\right\}=\sup_{\text{distribution }\mu_{c}\text{ over }\mathcal{C}}\left\{\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mu_{c}}[\ell(h,\bm{c})]\right\}\right\}.

5
A simple algorithm for optimizing losses

In this section, we give a simple algorithm for finding a hypothesis with low loss induced by some function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{3}\to\mathds{R}
. This procedure will be used in the proof of all our ERM efficient learners.

Throughout this section, we will work with the multilinear extension of
f
, denoted
\tilde{f}:[-1,1]^{3}\to\mathds{R}
,

\tilde{f}(c,h,y)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{c}\sim\mathrm{Rad}(c),\bm{h}\sim\mathrm{Rad}(h),\bm{y}\sim\mathrm{Rad}(y)}[f(\bm{c},\bm{h},\bm{y})].

The goal is to find a (real-valued) hypothesis
h:X\to[-1,1]
with low loss for every
c\in\mathcal{C}
on a dataset
S
where the loss is

\ell_{S}(c,h)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}\left[\tilde{f}(c(\bm{x}),h(\bm{x}),\bm{y})\right].

(6)

We will have two requirements on the function
f
. The first requirement is that the agreement coefficients below must be nonpositive for both choices of
y\in\{\pm 1\}
. This corresponds to the loss being larger when
c
and
h
disagree.

Definition 10

(Agreement coefficients)
.

For any
f:\{\pm 1\}^{3}\to\mathds{R}
, the agreement coefficients of
f
are defined as

a_{y}=f(1,1,y)+f(-1,-1,y)-f(1,-1,y)-f(-1,1,y).

Our algorithm will maintain
\mu\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathcal{C})
. For a fixed function
g
, it will output
h\coloneqq g\circ\mu
. Our second requirement is that there exists a function
g
for which this strategy always has nonpositive loss in expectation over
\bm{c}\sim\mu
(this corresponds to
3.2
for the special case of our malicious noise loss).

Definition 11

(
g
-feasible)
.

We say
f:\{\pm 1\}^{3}\to\mathds{R}
is
g
-feasible if, for any
\mu\in[-1,1]
and
y\in\{\pm 1\}
, its multilinear extension
\tilde{f}
satisfies

\tilde{f}(\mu,g(\mu),y)\leq 0.

Equivalently, for any dataset
S
and function
\mu:X\to[-1,1]
,
\ell_{S}(\mu,g\circ\mu)\leq 0
.

We’re now ready to state the main theorem of this section.

Learn

5.1
Establishing a convex domain and progress measure

Throughout this section, we fix some dataset
S=((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n}))
and a concept class
\mathcal{C}
of functions from
X\to\{\pm 1\}
. For any function
h:X\to[-1,1]
, we use

as shorthand for

h_{S}\coloneqq(h(x_{1}),\ldots,h(x_{n})).

We define
\Omega
to be the convex closure of
\mathcal{C}

\Omega\coloneqq\mathrm{Conv}(c_{S}:c\in\mathcal{C}).

(7)

We first observe that
\Omega
satisfies the following requirements.

Proposition 5.1
.

\Omega
is convex, compact, and has diameter at most
2\sqrt{n}
.

Proof.

Convexity follows immediately from the definition and the diameter bound from
\Omega
being a subset of
[-1,1]^{n}
.

For compactness, there exists a finite sequence
c^{(1)},\ldots,c^{(m)}
which take on all labelings that
\mathcal{C}
achieves on
S
, since there are at most
2^{n}
unique labelings. Then, an equivalent definition of
\Omega
is the mapping
F(\Delta)
where

F(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})=\sum_{i\in[m]}\lambda_{i}c^{(i)}_{S}\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\lambda_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

F
is continuous and
\Delta
is compact. Therefore,
\Omega=F(\Delta)
is compact.
∎

We next give a decomposition of the loss function that will be useful in designing our objective
P
.

Proposition 5.2

(Decomposing the loss function)
.

The loss
\ell_{S}(c,h)
defined in
Equation

6
can be decomposed as

\ell_{S}(c,h)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}\left[c(\bm{x})\left(a_{\bm{y}}h(\bm{x})+b_{\bm{y}}\right)+Q_{\bm{y}}(h(\bm{x}))\right]

where

\displaystyle a_{y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f(1,1,y)+f(-1,-1,y)-f(1,-1,y)-f(-1,1,y),

\displaystyle b_{y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq f(1,1,y)+f(1,-1,y)-f(-1,1,y)-f(-1,-1,y),

\displaystyle Q_{y}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(1+t)\left(f(1,1,y)+f(-1,1,y)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+(1-t)\left(f(1,-1,y)+f(-1,-1,y)\right).

Proof.

A straightforward check of the
8

, that

f(c(x),h(x),y)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(c(x)\cdot\left(a_{y}h(x)+b_{y}\right)+Q_{y}(h(x))\right).

Any two multilinear functions that agree on all end-points must be identical (see e.g. Theorem 1.1 of the textbook
[
46

). Hence, the above implies that for any
y\in\{\pm 1\}
and
c(x),h(x)\in[-1,1]
we also have
\tilde{f}(c(x),h(x),y)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\left(c(x)\cdot\left(a_{y}h(x)+b_{y}\right)+Q_{y}(h(x))\right)
. The desired decomposition then follows by taking an expectation of
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)
.
∎

Using this decomposition, we are ready to define our progress measure. The key is that, in

9
, the term

is independent of

. Therefore, the choice of
c
which minimizes
\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),c_{S}\rangle
is exactly that which maximizes
\ell(c,g\circ\mu)
.

Claim 5.3

(Defining our progress measure)
.

For any

, let
G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}g(t)dt
be its antiderivative and define, for any
\nu\in[-1,1]^{n}
,

P(\nu)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(a_{y_{i}}\cdot G(\nu_{i})+b_{y_{i}}\cdot\nu_{i}\right).

(8)

Then, for any functions
c,\mu:X\to[-1,1]
,

\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),c_{S}\rangle=-\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)+Q(\mu_{S})

(9)

where
\ell_{S}
is as defined in
Equation

6
,
a,b,Q_{y}
are defined in
Proposition

5.2
, and

Q(\nu)\coloneqq\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i\in[n]}Q_{y_{i}}(g(\nu_{i})).

Proof.

Apply
Proposition

5.2
with

:

\ell(c,g\circ\mu)=\frac{1}{4}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}\left[c(\bm{x})\left(a_{\bm{y}}\cdot g\circ\mu(\bm{x})+b_{\bm{y}}\right)+Q_{\bm{y}}(g\circ\mu(\bm{x}))\right].

Rearranging,

\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)-Q(\mu_{S})=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i\in[n]}c(x_{i})\left(a_{y_{i}}\cdot g\circ\mu(x_{i})+b_{y_{i}}\right).

By definition of

and
G^{\prime}=g
, we have that

\frac{\partial P(\nu)}{\partial\nu_{i}}=-\frac{1}{4n}\cdot(a_{y_{i}}\cdot g(\nu_{i})+b_{y_{i}})

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),c_{S}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i\in[n]}c(x_{i})\left(a_{y_{i}}\cdot g\circ\mu(x_{i})+b_{y_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)+Q(\mu_{S}).\qed

We next show the following

Proposition 5.4

(Convexity of
P
)
.

If
g
is nondecreasing and

as defined in
Proposition

5.2
are nonpositive, then

defined in
5.3
is convex.

Proof.

Recall that,

P(\nu)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(a_{y_{i}}\cdot G(\nu_{i})+b_{y_{i}}\nu_{i}\right).

Since linear functions are convex, and the sum of convex functions is convex, it suffices for

to be convex for all

. Whenever

are nonpositive, it then suffices for

to be convex, which follows from

being nondecreasing.
∎

Proposition 5.5

(Lipschitzness of
\nabla P
)
.

Suppose
g
is
L
-Lipschitz on

. Then
P
from
5.3
satisfies

\|\nabla P(\nu)-\nabla P(\nu^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot L\cdot\|\nu-\nu^{\prime}\|_{2}}{n}\qquad\text{for all }\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in[-1,1]^{n}.

Proof.

We bound,

\displaystyle\|\nabla P(\nu)-\nabla P(\nu^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\frac{\partial P(\nu)}{\partial\nu_{i}}-\frac{\partial P(\nu^{\prime})}{\partial\nu^{\prime}_{i}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(-\frac{a_{y_{i}}}{4n}\cdot(g(\nu_{i})-g(\nu^{\prime}_{i}))\right)^{2}

(
Equation

10
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{a_{y_{i}}^{2}}{16n^{2}}\cdot(L(\nu_{i}-\nu^{\prime}_{i}))^{2}

(

Next, since

Taking the square root of both sides give the desired result.
∎

5.2
Applying Jaggi’s bound

The key observation is that the duality gap can be used to bound the loss.

Claim 5.6

(Duality gap bounds loss)
.

For any
\mu:X\to[-1,1]
,

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)\right\}\leq\max_{\omega^{\star}\in\Omega}\left\{\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),\mu_{S}-\omega^{\star}\rangle\right\}.

Proof.

For any
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),\mu_{S}-c_{S}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),\mu_{S}\rangle-\langle\nabla P(\mu_{S}),c_{S}\rangle

(Linearity)

\displaystyle=\big(-\ell_{S}(\mu,g\circ\mu)+Q(\mu_{S})\big)-\big(-\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)+Q(\mu_{S})\big)

(

9
)

\displaystyle=\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)-\ell_{S}(\mu,g\circ\mu)

\displaystyle\geq\ell_{S}(c,g\circ\mu)

(

Proof of

Proof.

We show that for any fixed choice of

This equation is hardest to satisfy when

We will show the above holds by considering three regimes on how large

Small regime:
If

Large regime:

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathrm{sign}(\bar{\mu})\neq\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{{\bm{\mu}}})]=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathrm{Bin}(k,(1-\left|\bar{\mu}\right|)/2)\geq k/2]\leq 2^{k}\cdot\left(\frac{1-\left|\bar{\mu}\right|}{2}\right)^{k/2},

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bar{\mu}\cdot(\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{{\bm{\mu}}})-\mathrm{sign}(\bar{\mu}))]

\displaystyle=4(1-\left|\bar{\mu}\right|)\cdot\left(2(1-\left|\bar{\mu}\right|)\right)^{k/2-1}

10.1

Definition 13

(Multiaccuracy,

Let

39
]
justify multiaccuracy as a fairness condition. For example, suppose we wish to ensure that

is unbiased on various protected groups

the functions

and

5

5

The guarantee becomes weaker for smaller sets. This is necessary if one wants a sample efficient method to construct

\big|\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bar{h}(\bm{x})\mid\bm{x}\in S_{i}]-\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{y}\mid\bm{x}\in S_{i}]\big|\leq\frac{\tau}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{x}\in S_{i}]}.

We show that multiaccuracy is also desirable for its robustness properties.

Claim 10.1

(Multiaccuracy is equivalent to agnostic learning with randomized hypothesis)
.

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
of
\{\pm 1\}

\mathcal{D}_{x,y}
over

1.

The randomized hypothesis

satisfies

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}_{x}}(\bm{h},c)\leq\eta(c)+\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{for all }c\in\mathcal{C},

where
\mathcal{D}_{x}
is the marginal distribution of
\mathcal{D}_{x,y}
over

The hypothesis

Proof.

We first analyze
\eta(c)
. For the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{x,y}
to be formed by the agnostic adversary, it must pick the function
{\bm{f}}(x)
whose output has the distribution of

Therefore, the first requirement can be written as

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x}}[\bm{h}(\bm{x})\neq c(\bm{x})]\leq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x,y}}[\bm{y}\neq c(\bm{x})]+\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{for all $c\in\mathcal{C}$.}

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x}}\left[\frac{1-c(\bm{x})\bar{h}(\bm{x})}{2}\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x,y}}\left[\frac{1-c(\bm{x})\bm{y}}{2}\right]+\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{for all $c\in\mathcal{C}$.}

The above rearranges to exactly the multiaccuracy requirement,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{x,y}}[c(\bm{x})(\bm{y}-\bar{h}(\bm{x}))]\leq 2\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{for all $c\in\mathcal{C}$.}\qed

There are various algorithms for constructing multiaccurate learners (see e.g.
[
