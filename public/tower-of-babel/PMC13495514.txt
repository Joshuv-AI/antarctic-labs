Salvage treatment of traumatic total talar loss: a case report on the eventual application of 3D printing technology
Authors: Siyu Zhou, Jingyi Liang, Jianye Hou, Lihui Xu, Qiushi Liang, Xiyue Chang, Jie Liu, Feng Tian, Wan’an Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraumatic total talar loss is a rare and challenging condition commonly associated with talus extrusion and dislocation. Currently, clear treatment guidelines are lacking for massive talar defects. We report a case of a comminuted, open fracture with a bone defect.Case presentationWe present the case of a 26-year-old woman who was admitted after a car accident. The complexity of the injury and the innovative nature of the treatment method provide new insights for managing traumatic total talar loss and highlight the importance of personalized 3D printing for bone defect cases.

Background

Traumatic total talar loss is a rare injury, typically resulting from high-energy trauma accompanied by concomitant injuries [1–9]. Due to the talus’s tenuous vascular supply and lack of muscular attachments, open injuries frequently result in avascular necrosis and deep infection, ultimately necessitating total talectomy. The management of such massive segmental bone defects remains a significant challenge in foot and ankle surgery, with no universally established clinical guidelines.

Conventional strategies include tibiocalcaneal (TC) arthrodesis, the Ilizarov bone transport technique, and the Masquelet induced membrane technique. While TC arthrodesis can provide stability, the removal of the talus necessitates direct contact between the tibia and calcaneus, inevitably resulting in significant limb shortening and disruption of the normal malleolar-hindfoot relationship. Reconstruction techniques like Ilizarov and Masquelet are often associated with prolonged treatment duration, high burden of care, and complications such as pin-tract infections. Although Total Talar Replacement (TTR) is an option, it is frequently contraindicated in traumatic cases due to insufficient bone stock and compromised soft tissue envelopes.

In recent years, additive manufacturing technology has offered a novel solution for complex skeletal reconstruction. A custom-made 3D-printed porous titanium spacer can theoretically restore limb length and anatomical congruity while providing immediate structural support. In this study, we report the case of a young female patient with a secondary massive talar defect complicated by severe deformity. Following the failure of initial talar reimplantation, limb salvage was successfully achieved using a personalized 3D-printed porous titanium spacer combined with tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) fusion. This study demonstrates that 3D-printed titanium spacers, primarily reported for osteonecrosis, serve as a viable salvage alternative for delayed post-traumatic talar loss.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital ten hours after a car accident. She had multiple fractures and skin contusions all over her body. An open injury was visible on the right ankle, accompanied by soft-tissue defects and gross contamination with mud and grass clippings. Extruded bone fragments were visible, measuring approximately 2 × 4 × 4 cm. The right dorsalis pedis pulse was palpable but weak, accompanied by decreased distal skin temperature. A severe contusion of the right knee was observed, characterized by marked swelling and localized tenderness. The active flexion and extension of the toes were largely preserved with only mild restriction, suggesting that the integrity of the extensor and flexor tendons was maintained. However, direct inspection of the lateral wound revealed disruption of both the peroneus longus and brevis tendons. After a comprehensive physical examination and imaging, the final diagnoses were: (1) Open comminuted fracture of the right talus with extrusion of the talar body (Gustilo-Anderson Type IIIA) (Fig. 1) (2), Dislocation of the right ankle and subtalar joint (3), Avulsion fracture of the right calcaneus, accompanied by fractures of the cuboid, bases of the third and fourth metatarsals, and the lateral malleolus (4), Composite skin and soft-tissue defects of the left foot and ankle, and (5) Fracture of the right tibial plateau and generalized multiple fractures and contusions.

The patient underwent emergent surgical debridement of the right ankle wound, utilizing 3000 mL of normal saline for extensive irrigation and povidone-iodine for disinfection. The non-viable comminuted fragments of the right talar body were excised, and the bone edges were trimmed until healthy bleeding bone was exposed. Bone grafting was applied to the talar body defect, and the fracture fragments were reduced and fixed using Kirschner wires (K-wires) (Fig. 2). Simultaneously, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) was performed for the right tibial plateau fracture. Following the stabilization of the talus, the dislocations of the right ankle and subtalar joints were reduced. The associated fractures, including the avulsion fracture of the right calcaneus, as well as fractures of the cuboid, the bases of the third and fourth metatarsals, and the lateral malleolus, were managed via open reduction and internal fixation to restore anatomical alignment. The skin and soft-tissue defects of the left foot and ankle underwent thorough debridement and primary repair. Postoperatively, the patient was managed according to standard trauma protocols.

After surgery, the wound was managed with negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) for drainage and infection control. Due to residual necrosis observed in the wound bed during the first postoperative week, surgical debridement was performed followed by NPWT exchange. A second debridement and exchange were required in the second postoperative week. By the third postoperative week, the soft tissue showed healthy granulation, with no signs of infection or necrosis. Radiographically, the talus remained stable, showing no evidence of resorption or collapse. A posterior peroneal artery perforator flap was subsequently performed to reconstruct the soft tissue defect in the skin of the ankle. Postoperatively, the flap demonstrated good vascular perfusion with no neurological deficits. Following successful wound closure, the patient was discharged.

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient was initially unable to attend her scheduled follow-up appointments. However, as symptoms progressed, she returned to our hospital three months after the flap surgery (four months post-injury) presenting with significant limitation in ankle mobility, specifically restricted dorsiflexion and forced plantarflexion. Imaging studies revealed a significant defect of the right talar body accompanied by massive bone resorption. Clinically, the patient presented with foot drop, Achilles tendon contracture, ankle stiffness, and an inability to actively or passively dorsiflex the ankle to a functional position. Moreover, there was no considerable improvement following physical therapy. Due to the loss of the talar body, the tibia came into direct contact with the residual talar head, neck, and the calcaneus, resulting in severe wear of the articular surfaces(Fig. 3).

Subsequently, an Ilizarov external fixation frame was applied to correct the deformity, restore anatomical alignment, and distract the joint space for subsequent talar implantation. The treatment lasted two months, during which serial inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) and routine blood tests were performed, confirming no signs of infection. The patient requested to remove the heavy external fixation frame due to intolerance. Given the patient’s refusal of autologous bone grafting and the complexity of the defect, we opted for a custom 3D-printed spacer as the definitive salvage procedure.

The design and fabrication of the patient-specific spacer employed a reverse engineering strategy based on high-resolution computed tomography (CT) data. Initially, the raw DICOM data were imported into Mimics software to reconstruct a precise 3D digital model of the defect site. Rather than simply replicating the contralateral talus, the prosthetic design was biomechanically optimized. Following the design phase, the spacer was manufactured by Beijing AK Medical Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) using Selective Laser Melting (SLM) technology, an additive manufacturing process that fuses Ti-6Al-4 V (TC4) powder layer by layer. This titanium alloy was selected for its exceptional biocompatibility, high strength-to-weight ratio, and corrosion resistance. To address the dual requirements of mechanical load-bearing and biological fixation, the spacer featured a hybrid structure: a solid core to withstand physiological stress and a porous surface interface. The pore size was strictly controlled within the range of 800 ± 200 μm. This specific porosity was chosen based on established evidence that it optimally facilitates osteoblast migration, angiogenesis, and osseointegration, while avoiding the structural weakness associated with overly sparse lattices. Thus, the mesh parameters were standardized to ensure reliable biological integration without compromising the mechanical integrity provided by the solid structural components (Fig. 4).

A lateral transfibular approach was utilized to expose the ankle joint. Following thorough debridement of fibrous scar tissue and preparation of the fusion surfaces on the distal tibia and calcaneus, the sterilized 3D-printed porous titanium spacer was press-fit into the bone defect. To promote osseointegration and fusion, a mixture of autologous cancellous bone harvested from the resected distal fibula and allograft bone was tightly packed at the bone-implant interface. The construct was then rigidly stabilized using a retrograde TTC intramedullary nail, which was inserted through the calcaneus, passed through the spacer, and anchored into the distal tibia. Finally, after fluoroscopic confirmation of proper alignment and spacer positioning, the wound was closed in layers (Fig. 5).

Postoperatively, antibiotics were administered for 48 h, stitches were removed after two weeks, and full weight-bearing walking was initiated at three months. The patient recovered well and returned to work six months after the surgery. Clinical and radiographic outcomes were satisfactory at 1, 3, and 4 years of postoperative follow-up (Fig. 6). The patient maintained equal limb length, with no signs of loosening or infection of the porous titanium spacer, and evidence of osseointegration. At the final four-year follow-up, the patient achieved an AOFAS score of 82. Physical examination showed a stable, painless ankle. Muscle strength was Grade 5/5 in all key groups. Crucially, the patient reported a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score of 0 and demonstrated the ability to ambulate independently on level ground and climb stairs without assistive devices. This pain-free restoration of weight-bearing function confirms the clinical success of the 3D-printed reconstruction. Subjectively, the patient expressed high satisfaction with the outcome, reporting a significant improvement in her quality of life as she returned to daily activities (Fig. 7).

Discussion and conclusion

Currently, there are no guidelines for the treatment of traumatic total talar loss. In the present case, the complexity and progression of the patient’s condition posed a challenge in choosing an appropriate surgical approach to restore limb length and the mechanical axis while ensuring sufficient weight-bearing capacity. Initially, the Ilizarov technique was employed to successfully correct the alignment of the ankle and hindfoot. However, the subsequent challenge was managing the massive talar defect. We carefully evaluated several alternative strategies described in the literature, including the Masquelet technique, femoral head allografts, and porous tantalum spacers, but each presented specific limitations. The Masquelet technique was deemed unfeasible because the patient had a history of multiple fractures that limited available donor sites, and she refused autologous bone harvesting. Furthermore, a local shortage of allografts precluded both the Masquelet second stage and the use of femoral head allografts. Regarding prosthetic replacement, both the patient and the surgical team ruled out conventional TTR or standard total talus spacer primarily due to concerns over aseptic loosening and the high risk of future revision. Tantalum spacers were also excluded due to their inability to perfectly match the patient’s complex talar anatomy. Consequently, to address the defect without relying on limited bone stock or risking spacer loosening, we chose to proceed with a patient-specific 3D-printed porous titanium spacer.

To repair the talar defect, we employed 3D printing technology, selecting titanium alloy as the material because of its biocompatibility, low toxicity, and high corrosion resistance. While emerging bioprinting technology has introduced the concept of depositing biological materials (bio-inks) to replicate native tissue, it remains in its early developmental stages for orthopedic constructs due to suboptimal mechanical properties for load-bearing applications [10]. Therefore, we adopted a bio-hybrid strategy. By fabricating porous titanium alloy to simulate the structure of trabecular bone, we aimed to provide a favorable environment for the growth and integration of new bone while maintaining mechanical strength. We further functionalized the scaffold by incorporating autologous cancellous bone and intramedullary tissue containing bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells into the porous structure [11, 12]. Although distinct from printing with live cells, this approach effectively combines the mechanical stability of 3D printing with the osteogenic potential of autologous tissue, offering a viable solution for patients with massive bone defects.

Previously, 3D-printed porous titanium implants have been utilized as structural spacers to prevent collapse and facilitate ankle arthrodesis in cases of talar osteonecrosis, providing necessary mechanical support against physiological loads. However, the present case presents a fundamentally distinct set of challenges. Unlike osteonecrosis cases where the bony geometry is often preserved and the spacer serves primarily as a fusion cage, this patient presented with a massive traumatic defect (total volumetric loss), necessitating a spacer that could fully restore anatomical continuity. Furthermore, distinct from the healthy soft tissue envelope typically encountered in elective surgery for osteonecrosis, this case involved a hostile environment following flap reconstruction. The successful integration of a large porous scaffold in such a compromised bed distinguishes this traumatic application from standard treatments for avascular necrosis [13, 14]. The promising potential of 3D-printed total talus replacement is further corroborated by emerging large-scale clinical efforts. For instance, the ongoing Talus Replacement Registry (NCT03965143) is currently collecting longitudinal data to establish standardized indications and outcomes for these custom spacers. Moreover, recent literature has begun to substantiate the safety of this technique in broader cohorts; a 2025 study by Kim et al. reported favorable medium-term results in 39 patients utilizing 3D-printed pure titanium spacers for massive talar defects [15]. The versatility of this approach is also expanding beyond trauma, with Galea Wismayer et al. recently demonstrating its efficacy in reconstructing defects following talar tumor resections [16]. Collectively, these studies suggest that patient-specific 3D-printed reconstruction is evolving from a novel concept to a viable clinical option for complex limb salvage.

Drawing upon experiences treating talar osteonecrosis, this is the first case in which a 3D-printed talus spacer was used to reconstruct a talar body defect resulting from an open talus fracture dislocation. In contrast to aseptic elective surgeries for osteonecrosis, open injuries are highly prone to infections. We managed the situation by infection control through debridement and NPWT, repairing the skin and soft tissue defect with a peroneal artery perforator flap. After confirming the absence of infection risks through multiple assessments of infection indicators, including complete blood count, C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, we proceeded to implant a 3D-printed spacer. The personalized talar spacer perfectly filled the talar defect, the porous titanium material facilitated osseointegration, and autologous bone grafting promoted tibiotalocalcaneal fusion. This experience provides valuable insights into the future of treating complex foot and ankle disorders.

Despite the encouraging outcomes observed in this patient, several limitations inherent to this technique warrant careful consideration. First, the high manufacturing costs and extended production cycles limit the widespread accessibility of 3D-printed spacers and preclude their application in the acute phase of trauma; thus, this method is strictly reserved for staged reconstruction rather than emergency management. Second, the risk of periprosthetic infection remains a critical concern, particularly in open fractures with compromised soft tissues. Consequently, this technique is contraindicated in active infection or contaminated fields and is suitable only for delayed settings where the soft tissue envelope has been adequately stabilized. From a biomechanical and materials science perspective, challenges also persist. Although the porous design reduces the elastic modulus of the spacer, a mismatch between the rigid titanium alloy and native bone may still induce stress shielding over time, potentially affecting long-term bone remodeling. Furthermore, current metal 3D-printing technology relies primarily on osteoconductivity rather than osteoinduction. The lack of fully functional bio-inks capable of fabricating vascularized, living bone tissue remains a significant hurdle. Finally, as a single case report, the generalizability of our findings is limited. Although the four-year follow-up is promising, broader patient cohorts and longer-term data are essential to fully evaluate the survivorship and potential complications of these large porous spacers.

In conclusion, this four-year follow-up demonstrates that patient-specific 3D-printed porous titanium spacers offer a viable limb-salvage solution for massive traumatic talar defects, particularly when traditional reconstruction methods are precluded by compromised soft tissues or bone loss. Beyond the foot and ankle, this digital-to-physical workflow holds significant potential for wider applications in orthopedics, such as the reconstruction of complex articular defects following tumor resection or revision arthroplasty. However, the technology is not yet a panacea; its widespread adoption is currently limited by high manufacturing costs, production delays, and the lack of intrinsic biological activity in metal spacers. Future research must therefore focus on validating long-term survivorship through multi-center registries and advancing the development of osteoinductive bio-inks to transition from structural replacement to true biological regeneration.
