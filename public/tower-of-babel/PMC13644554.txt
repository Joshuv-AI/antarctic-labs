Rethinking mitochondrial metabolism: Intraindividual variability meets population constraints
Authors: José L Cabrera-Alarcón, José A Enríquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances integrating high-resolution 3D structures with population genetics and comparative evolutionary analyses indicate that mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) acts as a primary evolutionary engine driving nuclear coadaptation. This process reflects a trade-off between individual heterozygosity and mtDNA-driven variability, which together expand the mitonuclear haplotype pool within species.Keywords: OxPhos, mitonuclear evolution

The mitochondrial DNA paradox: Variability within individuals, constraint across populations

The oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) system has long been viewed as a metabolically essential but structurally invariant cellular machinery. This was the universal view at the end of the last century [1]. OxPhos genes have traditionally been considered a class of housekeeping genes responsible for cellular energy production, assumed to be ubiquitously expressed with low variability across tissues [2]. However, OxPhos is now known to be more flexible than previously thought. Rather than acting as a fixed housekeeping system, OxPhos gene expression and mitochondrial organization change to match the metabolic needs of different cell types, physiological states, and developmental stages [3]. OxPhos complexes are not structurally uniform: some subunits, such as those in cytochrome c oxidase [Complex IV (CIV)], have alternative isoforms that are used in a cell-type- and condition-dependent manner 4, 5. In addition, the respiratory complexes (RCs) can assemble into supercomplexes in different ways, and this organization varies depending on context instead of being constant [6]. These supercomplexes can exist alongside free complexes, and their relative levels differ between species and conditions [7]. This flexibility is further complicated by the fact that the OxPhos system is encoded by two different genomes, which require coordinated evolution, where mismatches between them can lead to functional conflict. Several studies highlight the widespread influence of mitonuclear coevolution as a source of divergence across multiple biological levels. These include the mitochondrial translation system (tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, and the mitoribosomes), where RNA–protein interfaces must remain compatible despite rapid mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) changes [8] and the emergence of lethal hybrid incompatibilities in respiratory Complex I (CI) that promote reproductive isolation [9]. Similar principles are evident in yeast, where mitonuclear mismatches lead to complete sterility [10], and in classic copepod studies, which show that disruption of coadapted mitonuclear interactions causes severe reductions in physiological performance and fitness across hybrid populations [11]. Regarding recent findings, mitonuclear coevolution must face a paradox: the oxidative phosphorylation system displays both individual-level variability and strict population-level constraints. This duality offers a more complex perspective on mitochondrial metabolism and its evolutionary trajectory (Figure 1) [12].

Individual-level variability and population constraint: Two sides of the same coin

Recent population-genomic analyses have uncovered a surprising degree of genetic variability within single individuals, challenging the traditional view of OxPhos as a metabolically uniform system. Structure-informed population genomics now suggests that many individuals could, in principle, encode multiple configurations of OxPhos complexes; one recent analysis of 2504 genomes illustrates the scale of this potential variability. Thus, it revealed that over 90% of individuals possess the genetic potential to assemble at least two distinct versions of CI. This diversity is even greater for CIV and Complex V (CV), largely due to the presence of tissue-specific alternative isoforms. Heterozygosity in nuclear OxPhos subunits appears to be a major source of this intraindividual variability. If individuals routinely harbor multiple OxPhos configurations, this diversity could serve as a form of ‘metabolic insurance’, providing the flexibility needed to adapt to varying physiological demands, stress, disease, or aging. Recognizing this diversity opens new perspectives for understanding why individuals may respond differently to metabolic challenges.

A contrasting pattern emerges, however, when comparing nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. While nuclear genes generate diversity within individuals through heterozygosity, mtDNA shows the opposite trend: minimal variation within a single person but substantial diversity across populations. This duality suggests an evolutionary strategy to resolve the challenges of mitonuclear compatibility. The elevated mutation rate of mtDNA, often considered a genetic liability, may instead function as an adaptive mechanism: by generating diversity across populations, it creates a reservoir of mitochondrial haplotypes that can maintain functional compatibility with the diverse nuclear backgrounds they encounter. In this view, selective sweeps (rapid fixation of advantageous mtDNA variants within populations) ensure that a broad spectrum of mitonuclear combinations exists at the population level to support both individual genetic variation and adaptation to local environments. Thus, rather than a flaw, the high mutation rate of mtDNA may be a selected feature essential for maintaining the delicate balance between two coevolving genomes and adaptation.

Beyond structure: Functional evolutionary patterns

While mitonuclear coevolution is a known concept, integrating structural data with evolutionary patterns offers a refined perspective on the specific constraints and interaction dynamics that govern this process. This integrative strategy has been previously used to distinguish adaptive from compensatory mutations in nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, revealing the molecular basis of convergent evolution, such as the shared residue changes involved in endothermy in birds and mammals [13]. Building on this framework, recent systematic analyses of OxPhos components reveal distinct evolutionary patterns across RCs (Box 1). CI and CIV appear to be primary drivers of this divergence, characterized by relaxed selective pressure on mtDNA-encoded subunits at contact surfaces between mtDNA-encoded and nuclear-encoded subunits (heterologous binding interfaces). This pattern indicates that interaction surfaces may have evolved to accommodate the differing evolutionary dynamics of the two genomes rather than maintaining strict conservation.

Evolutionary strategies of the OxPhos system

The OxPhos system displays distinct evolutionary strategies across its RCs. CI and CIV emerge as major evolutionary drivers but follow opposing trajectories. CI, which includes 7 of the 13 mtDNA-encoded subunits, is strongly shaped by mtDNA dynamics, whereas CIV relies mainly on nuclear-encoded subunits and frequently incorporates interchangeable paralog isoforms as an adaptive mechanism.

Complex III (CIII2) is the least conserved RC among vertebrates and likely represents a major source of lineage-specific divergence. In contrast, Complex II (CII) and CV are the most conserved. CII, the only RC entirely encoded by the nuclear genome, has limited freedom to diverge due to its compact four subunit architecture. CV, although larger, contains numerous repeated subunits whose stoichiometric precision is tightly constrained by strong selective pressures.

Across the entire system, mtDNA-encoded subunits constitute a persistent source of evolutionary change (even within species) while nuclear-encoded partners must continuously adjust to maintain mitonuclear compatibility, particularly at protein–protein interfaces. The elevated mtDNA mutation rate therefore functions as a selected feature rather than a liability, driving OxPhos evolution at complex-specific rates and shaping metabolic diversity across animals.

Expressed individual variability: Mechanisms of constraint

Mitonuclear coevolution may also operate through coordination of gene expression: nuclear-encoded factors regulate mtDNA transcription, RNA processing, and translation, while the mitochondrial functional state feeds back to nuclear transcriptional programs. How this bidirectional tuning constrains or amplifies individual OxPhos configurational diversity remains largely unexplored.

However, allelic imbalance emerges as a critical, yet often overlooked, mechanism that modulates mitonuclear evolution by limiting expressed variability. Evidence indicates that certain autosomal OxPhos genes exhibit biased expression toward specific alleles, effectively reducing functional diversity despite underlying genetic heterozygosity. This regulatory layer suggests that cells may have evolved specific mechanisms to constrain the potential instability of unlimited OxPhos diversity while preserving the metabolic advantages of genetic flexibility [12]. Such fine-tuning likely represents an evolutionary compromise: balancing the need for metabolic adaptability against the risk of excessive mitonuclear incompatibilities that could compromise essential cellular functions. From a clinical point of view, recognizing allelic imbalance as a key modifier of penetrance is essential, as it may significantly influence the phenotypic outcome in mitochondrial-related diseases and warrants consideration in diagnostic interpretations.

Clinical and evolutionary implications

Integrating evolutionary information across diverse lineages provides a powerful framework for improving the clinical interpretation of mitochondrial disease variants. This logic underlies approaches such as ConScore, which use deep phylogenetic conservation to discriminate benign from deleterious variants in OxPhos. Borrowing evolutionary information to improve clinical variant interpretation is a strategy already employed by existing functional predictors, for instance, in combination with structural data [14]. However, mitonuclear coevolution represents a distinct phenomenon with unique constraints, such as asymmetric evolutionary rates, dual-genome inheritance, and interface-specific selective pressures, that may not be fully captured by generic approaches. This suggests that more specialized tools, designed to account for the particular dynamics of mitonuclear coevolution, may offer greater predictive power for OxPhos variants [12]. Beyond its immediate diagnostic value, this evolutionary perspective also sheds light on the dynamics of mitonuclear coevolution. Notably, the reduced conservation of interface residues in mtDNA-encoded subunits compared with noninterface positions aligns with coevolutionary models of protein–protein interactions, where interface residues undergo coordinated amino acid changes rather than strict conservation [15].

Future directions

Mitonuclear coevolution is an open field with relevant research avenues, such as the functional significance of individual OxPhos variability. Do individuals with different complex configurations show measurable differences in metabolic capacity, stress responses, or disease susceptibility? What is the role of allelic imbalance in shaping metabolic phenotypes across different tissues and developmental stages?

Perhaps most intriguingly, the evolutionary framework presented here may extend beyond OxPhos to other systems requiring tight coordination between nuclear and organellar genomes. Similar patterns might emerge in chloroplast-encoded components of photosynthesis, offering opportunities to test the generality of these evolutionary principles.

Concluding perspective

A broader perspective on mitonuclear coevolution is proposed here, considering the coexistence of intraindividual variability and population-level constraints. Rather than representing metabolic uniformity, our cellular powerhouses showcase evolution’s ability to maintain essential function while preserving the genetic flexibility necessary for adaptation and survival.

Modern genomic approaches can reveal hidden complexity in even the most studied biological systems. As human genetic variation continues to be cataloged, similar patterns of constrained diversity likely await discovery in other essential cellular processes. The challenge now lies in understanding how this diversity translates into functional differences and ultimately contributes to the remarkable phenotypic variation we observe across human populations.
