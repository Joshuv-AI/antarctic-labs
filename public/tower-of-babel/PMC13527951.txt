Snapshots of food security status in Western Sydney post-COVID-19 lockdown: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Alexandria Nivelle Mekanna, Kingsley Arua Kalu, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe COVID-19 lockdown had significant effects on daily life, particularly food security among Australian adults. This study examined the longer-term impact of lockdown measures on food security in Western Sydney, a region more socio-economically challenged within the Sydney area.MethodsA total of 548 adults were recruited via convenience sampling at least 6 months post-lockdown (May-August 2022 and January-February 2023). Of these, 523 completed the validated six-item USDA Household Food Security Survey Module and were included in the quantitative analysis. Fifty-four participants took part in 12 focus group discussions (FGDs), including some participants who did not complete the survey. FGDs explored food security perceptions based on its four pillars, both during and after lockdown. Inductive thematic analysis was conducted manually in Excel and verified in NVivo (version 14.23.0).ResultsThe mean age of survey participants (n = 523) was 25.1 ± 9.7 years, and 63% were women. Among participants with available birthplace data (n = 521), 267 were Australian-born and 254 were overseas-born; birthplace information was missing for two participants. Only 6% reported improved food security post-lockdown. Using the USDA tool, females were significantly more likely than males to report meal size reductions post-lockdown (p = 0.0001)> FGDs revealed that food availability, access, utilisation and stability were affected by rising food prices, fear of virus contamination and shifts in shopping habits during and after lockdown.ConclusionWhile the measured food insecurity prevalence was low based on the survey findings, the qualitative findings indicated that some participants continued to experience disruptions or vulnerabilities related to food security at least 6 to 15 months post-lockdown. These findings highlight the need for both crisis-responsive and recovery-oriented food security policies, with particular attention to supporting adult women who appear disproportionately affected.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28086-6.

Introduction

Federal, state and territory governments in Australia imposed strict measures on travel, social interaction and entry to public places, termed as lockdown, to help slowdown the transmission of COVID-19 [1] from March 2020 until October 2021 [2]. In Sydney, more stringent lockdown measures were implemented in several Western Sydney local governmental areas (LGAs) particularly Cumberland, Fairfield, Liverpool, Blacktown and Canterbury-Bankstown due to higher COVID-19 infection rates relative to the rest of New South Wales [3]. These areas faced stricter restrictions including limited movement [3]. Additional measures included the substitution of in-store shopping with online only and the prohibition of commuting to work outside the area of residence, with few exceptions [3].

Previous studies have shown that COVID-19 associated lockdowns were correlated with significant changes in food security status of populations worldwide [4–6]. Food security is commonly conceptualised through four pillars: availability (sufficient quantities of food being consistently available), access (having adequate physical and economic access to food), utilisation (appropriate use of food to meet nutritional needs), and stability (the ability to maintain availability, access and utilisation over time). The demand for food relief in Australia increased from 15% in 2019 before the lockdown to 31% in 2020 during the lockdown [7]. More than a quarter of Australians experienced food insecurity for the first time during the pandemic [7]. In Australia, the COVID-19 lockdown worsened unemployment rate to 7.4%, contributing to a worsened food security situation in year 2020 [8]. A significant financial burden was seen on both the government and individuals [9]. In many countries, as in Australia, food manufacturers decreased production, while the food supply chain was drastically disrupted [5].The lockdown thus impacted food availability by disrupting primary production, transportation and warehousing, wholesale and food processing and retail businesses supply [10]. Certain transitory lockdown measures may also have affected food accessibility by restricting frequencies and distance of travel for almost all purposes including grocery shopping [11]. The lockdown impacted the lives of all Australians, but food security among those who experienced financial hardships before the pandemic were reported to be more severely impacted [7, 12]. During the pandemic, there was an increase in panic shopping and stockpiling especially in earlier stages of the lockdown [7]. This worsened food insecurity in vulnerable populations due to reduced availability of essential food items such as fresh produce, dairy, and non-perishable staples, driven by supply chain disruptions and increased demand during the pandemic [7]. Western Sydney is characterised by a higher proportion of lower income households (19.2%), recent migrants (40.9% of people born overseas) and people with diverse cultural backgrounds compared to other parts of Sydney thus rendering the area with a higher proportion of populations more susceptible to food insecurity [13]. Additionally, higher unemployment rates and financial hardships in this area could have exacerbated food insecurity during the pandemic, making it a crucial region to study in the context of food relief needs and food security [14].

Despite the numerous studies already reviewed [15] for food security changes in Australia during lockdown, little seems to have been reported for the impact on food security status in Western Sydney LGAs where more stringent lockdown rules [16] were applied. Potential gaps in food security policies in these areas have previously been suggested [17]. The longer-term impacts of lockdown on food security in this population also require investigation for intervention planning. While food relief services exist, the demand often exceeds capacity, and the existing policies may not be adequately tailored to the specific needs of the diverse population in Western Sydney [17].

Therefore, the aim of our study was to examine the impacts of COVID-19 lockdown on food security status in residents of those LGAs (high-risk areas) during and more importantly months after lockdowns, to assess the need for remediation for adverse effects and the development of a comprehensive national/regional food policy in preparation for future large-scale disruption to food system in Australia due to natural or human causes.

Subjects and methods

Ethics approval and consent

This study was approved by Human Ethics Committee at Western Sydney University. Ethics approval number: H14800. Written consent was obtained from all study participants prior to their participation in this study. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sampling

Convenience sampling was used, where participants were recruited based on their availability across specific strata, including age groups, gender, country of birth and Local Government Areas (LGAs). Each stratum was then sampled by mixed recruitment strategies including intercept, online and letter-box flyer distribution and door-to-door survey. Age groups 18 to 64 years were stratified out of community members to be included in the sample, following the screening criteria outlined in ‘Recruitment and Screening’. Convenience sampling was used due to the need for timely and accessible data collection in the post-lockdown period when restrictions and resource constraints limited recruitment options. This approach allowed for the inclusion of diverse participants who were readily available, ensuring a practical and efficient method to explore food security experiences [18]. Convenience sampling provided valuable insights into trends and associations relevant to the research context [18].

Study design

A mixed methods (quantitative and qualitative approach) study was carried out in a sample from Western Sydney LGAs including Cumberland, Fairfield, Liverpool, Blacktown and Canterbury-Bankstown. This was comprised of quantitative surveys and qualitative focus groups. Both surveys and focus groups were conducted at least seven months post COVID-19 lockdown from a period of May 2022 to April 2023. Collecting data several months after lockdown ending was an attempt to address the evidence gap for any longer-term effects of the lockdown policy on population lifestyles, potentially critical for long-term risk of chronic diseases. The study used a convergent mixed-methods design, where both quantitative and qualitative data were collected in parallel to give a complete understanding of the study topic. According to Creswell (2014) and Tashakkori & Teddlie (2010), such design enhances the validity and interpretability of the study findings with in-depth perspectives of the topic being studied [19, 20]. Qualitative and quantitative data were integrated in study analysis by means of cross-verification to allow convergence of findings, which allows for the identification of patterns and validates results [21]. This approach facilitated an inclusive interpretation, where numerical trends from surveys were combined with qualitative insights, refining the overall understanding of the study topic [22]. The survey and focus group data were collected either online or face-to-face based on preference specified by the participants. Of the 523 survey participants, 5 completed the survey online and 518 completed it face-to-face. Twelve focus group discussions were conducted, of which one was conducted online and eleven were conducted face-to-face. This flexibility was adopted to boost recruitment, particularly among groups with culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) background as online or face-to-face options help accommodate varying language proficiencies, digital literacy and cultural preferences, thereby improving participation and data representativeness [23]. The proportion of residents with CALD background is generally much higher in Western Sydney compared with the rest of metropolitan Sydney [24]. Both the online focus groups and face-to-face sessions were recorded using Zoom, with face recognition features disabled to ensure participants’ confidentiality. Participants could choose to participate in either the survey, a focus group or both, with face-to-face participants joining Zoom sessions on-site and online participants remotely. Those findings will be reported elsewhere.

Recruitment and screening

Following approval, flyers were distributed across public places in the LGAs to increase the representativeness of the sample in terms of age, gender and LGA. Distribution occurred via several social media accounts such as Facebook and LinkedIn including personal accounts of the researchers and accounts of community organisations. Flyers were also distributed face-to-face in shopping centres, local councils, private shops, community centres, universities, other public facilities (transport sites, schools, parks, libraries), and private residences, in addition to mailbox dropping at private residences. The use of flyer distribution in a diverse range of public places was strategically chosen to engage with a broad and diverse segment of the population [25]. These methods are particularly effective in reaching individuals who may not be accessible through online or intercept-based strategies, ensuring a more representative sample [26]. The study included participants who were able to speak and write English at a minimum grade 5 level. Screening was conducted based on the following eligibility criteria: (1) residence within the five target LGAs (2), willingness to disclose gender as female or male (3), ability to speak and write English at a minimum of grade 5 level (4), absence of self-reported significant health conditions, including mental health challenges or risk of psychological distress (5), no self-reported special dietary requirements for medical reasons and (6) not currently pregnant and no pregnancy within the past two years. The pregnancy criterion was set to minimise potential influence of special dietary needs on associations tested for the broader project [27]. This exclusion ensured the data accurately reflected the general population with minimal confounding related to pregnancy [27]. The English language criterion was to ensure participants’ ability to complete ASA-24 dietary assessment for the whole project. This tool requires reading and understanding detailed instructions and food descriptions [28]. Participants with special dietary requirements or severe medical conditions were excluded because these factors may independently influence food purchasing behaviours, food access and dietary patterns, making it difficult to distinguish their effects from those associated with COVID-19 lockdown measures. Gender analyses were pre-specified to compare male and female participants. The number of participants identifying outside these categories was insufficient for separate statistical analysis and therefore these participants were excluded.

Data collection

Surveys

Cross-sectional quantitative data was collected at two different timepoints from separate cohorts of participants, using a modified version of the validated six-item USDA Household Food Security Survey Module [29] administered through the survey platform Qualtrics (Qualtrics International Inc). The time difference between the two different timepoints was 6 months, and data was grouped in the analysis for the two different timepoints because no major policy changes, economic disruptions or extreme weather events occurred between the two timepoints. Thereby, analysing the data together (before and after 6 months) provides a comprehensive picture of food security trends. Additionally, descriptive comparisons of socio-demographic distributions across the two cohorts were conducted by examining proportional differences in age group, gender, country of birth and LGA. Differences between cohorts were minimal, supporting pooled analysis. Nevertheless, a limitation of this approach is that potential differences between the two cohorts were not formally tested, which may have influenced the findings. It is more practical for studies involving vulnerable groups where respondents may find a shorter survey easier to complete [30, 31]. The survey questions covered the post-lockdown period and contained six questions for food security to examine the key pillars of food security, namely availability, access and utilisation. Availability was assessed by items about buying food that did not last and eating less due to limited availability; not being able to afford balanced meals, cutting down the size of meals and how often the meal size was cut. These items were categorised under availability in this study to reflect instances where participants experienced actual shortfalls in the quantity or consistency of food, regardless of the underlying economic cause. Questions for utilisation inquired about frequencies of feeling hungry. In this study, frequency of feeling hungry was included under the utilisation category to capture the practical consequences of inadequate food intake on daily dietary experiences, serving as a proxy for the household’s ability to consistently consume sufficient food. Further two questions were added to assess stability: “Compared to before the lockdown, you perceive your current food availability, access, and stability as much better, much worse or unvaried”; “Compared to during the lockdown you perceive your current food availability, access and stability as much better, much worse or unvaried.” The focus group protocol is available as a supplementary material. The module content for the food security survey can be found at https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/DataFiles/50764/short2024.pdf (May 2024).

Key sociodemographic variables collected included age, gender, country of birth and LGA. Socioeconomic data such as income and employment were not collected to encourage response rates and minimise participant burden. A limitation of this study is the absence of these socioeconomic indicators, which may influence food security outcomes, as well as potential seasonal effects that could not be fully accounted for.

Focus groups

Qualitative data was collected using focus groups either online via Zoom or face-to-face with a duration of 30 to 45 min. The four pillars of food security were discussed for the periods covering ‘during lockdown’ and ‘post-lockdown’, compared with pre-lockdown and during lockdown, with perceived causes for changes in food security status. Focus groups were audio recorded with pseudonyms. The focus group protocol is available as a supplementary material (Sect. 1).

Data analysis

Surveys

Quantitative data was analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 29.0.2.0 (20)). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were calculated for sociodemographic characteristics and food security outcomes. Participants were categorised based on age (18, 19–30, 31–50, 51–64 years) and gender groups (Male or Female) following the Australian Dietary Guidelines classification [32]. Using these categories ensures consistency with national standards for analysing and interpreting dietary data [33]. Other categories included country of birth (Australia or overseas) and area of residence (Cumberland, Fairfield, Liverpool, Blacktown or Canterbury-Bankstown). Comparison of post-lockdown status with pre-lockdown status was categorised as ‘much better’, ‘much worse’ or ‘unvaried’ (did not change), same for post-lockdown with during lockdown period. Associations between categorical food security variables and sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, country of birth and area of residence) were assessed using Chi-square tests of independence. Each food security item was treated as a categorical variable. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference between groups. Participants’ responses to the six-item USDA Household Food Security Survey Module were scored following USDA guidance, with the prevalence of food insecurity calculated by combining the different categories; any additional questions on stability were analysed separately and did not impact this scoring.

Focus groups

Verbatim transcription of the focus group recording was carried out using Otter.ai software (version Otter Pro 2022) and verified manually. A hybrid thematic analysis [34] approach was employed, merging both inductive and deductive elements to detect and define themes and sub-themes. Key themes related to changes in food security during and post-lockdown when compared to pre-lockdown and during lockdown, as well as the perceived causes of these changes, were identified. NVivo software (version 14.23.0) was used to validate the manual analysis, ensuring consistency and reliability. Coding was conducted by the primary researcher, and the resulting codes and themes were cross-verified against the manual Excel analysis after importing the dataset into NVivo to ensure consistency. The coding framework and theme structure were developed iteratively, with themes and interpretations discussed among the research team to enhance analytical rigour and minimise potential bias. NVivo software (version 14.23.0) was used to verify and organise the coding structure and thematic framework. This approach was deemed suitable for exploring participants’’ experiences and perspectives in depth, particularly in the context of food security post-lockdown [35]. To ensure rigorous reporting, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [36] was used as a framework. The completed COREQ checklist is available as a supplementary material. The focus groups were carried with a clear protocol, including recruitment procedures, participant demographics, and the setting, which ensured inclusivity and diversity. Data saturation was monitored during data collection, and investigator triangulation was applied by involving multiple researchers when reviewing coding and interpretation to minimise bias. This process ensured transparency and credibility in the analysis and reporting of the focus group findings. Data saturation was monitored by comparing emerging codes after each focus group and was considered reached when no new concepts or themes were identified across successive groups, as independently confirmed by the research team.

Twelve focus groups were carried out. As a principle, five participants/group were aimed for during the recruitment of focus groups.

Results

Participant characteristics and perceived changes in food security status

The survey achieved a high response rate of 98.1%. The sample comprised more females than males (67% vs. 37%) and a roughly equal distribution of participants born in Australia (51%) and overseas (49%). Participants were proportionately represented across the five LGAs in Western Sydney, with the largest representation from Cumberland (36.7%) and Blacktown (22.6%). The prevalence of food insecurity in the survey sample was 3.1%. Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants including age, gender, country of birth and area of residence. There was a higher proportion of participants aged 18 years in the sample, likely due to stronger engagement with the recruitment flyers distributed at local universities, which were part of the sampling approach described in Methods. According to recent census data [24] the proportion of overseas-born residents across the five LGAs is relatively high, suggesting that the 49% overseas-born in the sample of this study is broadly consistent with the demographic profile of these areas. Likewise, the age distribution in the sample of this study generally reflects the younger population structure of the region, although the proportion of 18-year-olds is relatively elevated due to strong engagement from universities.

Theme 1: Perceived positive changes during lockdown

Some participants reported positive changes in food security during lockdown. These included increased household food planning, more home cooking and greater awareness of food purchasing and consumption practices. Participants described spending more time preparing meals at home and managing food resources more carefully during lockdown.

Theme 2: Perceived negative changes during lockdown

Subtheme 2.1: Reduced food availability

Participants frequently reported difficulties accessing essential food products during lockdown. Shortages of staple foods and culturally preferred imported products disrupted normal shopping and dietary practices.

Participants also reported reduced availability of imported foods, including Indian and Korean products, which affected their ability to maintain culturally preferred diets.

Subtheme 2.2: Limited food access

Participants described movement restrictions, concerns about exposure to OVID-19, and vaccination-related restrictions as barriers to obtaining food.

“It feels like during lockdown you would not be able to buy everything you need, you can only access half of what you need because of not being able to go out.” (25 years, male)

Subtheme 2.3: Reduced food security stability and utilisation

Participants reported that financial pressures resulting from lockdown restrictions affected their ability to maintain food security. Loss of employment or business income reduced household purchasing power and influenced food choices.

“My husband had a small business that he had to shut down during COVID and this impacted our food security status.” (59 years, female)

Participants indicated that reduced household income affected their ability to purchase preferred foods and maintain usual dietary patterns.

Theme 3: Perceived positive changes post-lockdown

Following the lockdown period, some participants reported improvements in food availability and access. Increased availability of staple food products and reductions in the prices of some items, including vitamins and mineral supplements, were perceived as positive developments. Participants also reported that shopping habits gradually returned to normal as restrictions eased.

Theme 4: Perceived negative changes post-lockdown

Subtheme 4.1: Increased food prices

Despite the easing of restrictions, participants continued to report increased prices for several food products. Commonly mentioned items included dairy products, vegetables, meat, rice, cooking oil, cucumbers, lettuce, peas, tomatoes and eggplant.

Participants perceived these sustained price increases as a major contributor to ongoing food insecurity and financial stress.

Subtheme 4.2: Reduced availability of selected food products

Participants reported continued difficulties obtaining certain foods post-lockdown, particularly fruits, vegetables and rice. Some participants also noted ongoing challenges accessing culturally preferred food products.

Subtheme 4.3: Ongoing behavioural and financial challenges

Participants described ongoing challenges related to food access, changes in shopping behaviours, personal concerns regarding food affordability and fluctuations in household income. These factors were perceived as contributing to persistent food security vulnerabilities despite the end of lockdown restrictions.

Overall, participants described both positive and negative changes in food security following the COVID-19 lockdown. While some improvements in food availability and access were reported, ongoing concerns related to food prices, affordability and access continued to affect food security among some participants.

Discussion

The COVID-19 lockdown imposed worldwide has caused changes in food security status of different populations [38–40]. Based on the quantitative findings of this study in Western Sydney, 7% (n = 37) of the participants reported a worsened food security status post compared with pre-lockdown. The rest recounted no impacts on food security status post vs. pre-lockdown. Approximately 6% (n = 32) of the participants reported a much better food security status after lockdown when compared to during lockdown, 1% much worse and no changes in 93% (n = 489) of participants. Although the survey indicated a low prevalence of food insecurity, focus group participants described ongoing challenges related to food affordability and food access, suggesting that some impacts of the lockdown persisted beyond what was captured by the survey measure. A key strength of this study is its combination of quantitative and qualitative data, which allowed for a nuanced assessment of self-perceived food security among adults in Western Sydney. The prevalence of self-reported food insecurity was 3.1%. The relatively low prevalence of food insecurity may reflect limitations of the survey tool (6-item short form of the U.S. Household Food Security Survey Module) in capturing nuanced or transient experiences of food insecurity, highlighting a positivist epistemological perspective that emphasises measurable outcomes [41]. From a constructivist ontology, participants’ lived experiences revealed through the focus groups suggest that food insecurity may be more context-dependent and subjective, indicating that quantitative measures alone may underrepresent the true extent of food insecurity [42]. A strength of this study was the ability to recruit a sizable sample efficiently using convenience sampling. However, the use of convenience sampling may have limited the inclusion of individuals from lower socioeconomic or marginalised backgrounds, potentially underestimating the true prevalence of food insecurity [43].

According to the Australian Bureau of Statistics National Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey (2023), 13.2% of Australian households experienced food insecurity at some time in the 12 months prior to the interview based on data collected between January 2023 and March 2024, using a multi-item measure of food security status [44]. In comparison with this recent national estimate, the low prevalence of self-reported food insecurity observed in the present Western Sydney sample post-lockdown (3.1%) appears lower than 13.2%, despite the earlier 2011-12 national figure of 3.3% [45] often cited in the literature. This discrepancy may reflect differences in measurement tools, reference periods and survey methodologies, as well as potential sample and regional factors influencing food security. Based on previous findings, the prevalence of food insecurity increased (22.6%) post-lockdown compared to pre-lockdown period in Tasmanian-based Australian adults (n = 1,097) [46]. A scoping review demonstrated that food insecurity had drastically increased (34.5%) in Australia because of the COVID-19 lockdown [47]. Similarly, the findings of this study align with another study (n = 1,005) which has shown that the pandemic has contributed to increased prevalence (19.6%) of household food insecurity in Australia [48]. Also, a similar study conducted among food consumers (n = 115) in Southwest Australia revealed that the pandemic had significantly impacted Australia’s food supply chain [49], which aligns with this present study. The implications of these findings are profound; they signal not only an immediate need for resources but also a call to action for policymakers to develop region-specific strategies to address issues highlighted in this study.

An interesting finding of this study is a ‘higher frequency of meal size cutting’ in females (14% almost every month) than males (0% almost every month). This gender difference in meal-size cutting likely reflects gendered coping strategies in the face of food insecurity, with women more often reducing their own portions to stretch limited food so that everyone in the household can still eat [50]. A similar trend was reported in Food Bank 2019 data which has shown that in Australia, females are 50% more likely to experience food insecurity more than males because of lower incomes or higher rates of single parenthood [51, 52]. A similar trend has been observed in subsequent Food Bank data, which consistently show that women in Australia are more likely to experience food insecurity than men [53]. These quantitative findings are supported by the focus group discussions, which identified increased food prices and financial pressures as ongoing challenges that may have contributed to food-related coping strategies among participants. Further studies are required to confirm whether food security disruptions in segments of the population will remain in Australia following the pandemic. It may also be useful to assess the duration of impacts of the lockdown for planning for future food system disruption.

Another interesting finding was in association analysis between food security status and different age, gender, country of birth and LGA groups. Significant associations were found for ‘buying food that did not last over the past 12 months’ across different age groups, countries of birth and areas of residence. The finding is likely due to differences in income, employment and social support systems across age groups, countries of birth and regions, which can affect food access and affordability [54]. Participants aged 19 to 30 years had the highest prevalence of running out of food, suggesting younger adults were particularly vulnerable. In addition, overseas-born participants reported higher rates of food running out compared with Australian-born participants indicating that migrant populations may face additional economic or access barriers. This finding is supported by the focus group discussions, where participants reported reduced availability and increased prices of culturally preferred imported foods, highlighting additional barriers that impact overseas-born participants. These findings are like previous studies in the literature which have found younger adults in Australia at higher risk of food insecurity, and overseas-born groups also face higher risks due to economic and access barriers [55]. Significant associations were found for ‘not affording to eat balanced meals over the past 12 months’ across different age groups, countries of birth and areas of residence. This finding is likely due to disparities in income, employment and access to resources, which affect the ability to afford nutritious and balanced meals across different age groups, countries of birth and areas of residence [56]. Based on previous literature, specific groups in Australia are more susceptible to food insecurity, especially young people and culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations [57], thereby justifying the significant associations. This implies that these ‘at risk’ groups may require greater attention to nutrition policy, with tailored interventions that address their specific barriers to food access and affordability. Policies should consider culturally appropriate food assistance programs, income support measures and community-based initiatives to improve food security outcomes for vulnerable populations.

The focus group findings helped explain several of the survey findings. While the survey identified a relatively low prevalence of food insecurity, participants described increased food prices, reduced availability of some food products, limited physical access to food, fear of infection and income disruptions. These experiences provide additional context for understanding the food security challenges reported in the quantitative data. The qualitative findings are similar to those reported by another qualitative study which presented the challenges faced by the disadvantaged Australian populations to have access to enough food during the pandemic with increased prices of food products [58]. This research integrates both qualitative and quantitative data, providing a more comprehensive understanding of food insecurity dynamics in Western Sydney. By capturing lived experiences alongside statistical associations, this study offers unique insights into the long-term implications of the pandemic and the region’s structural vulnerabilities, including potential gaps in food policies and emergency response measures. The focus group of this study also presented impacts post-lockdown. The qualitative findings reflect previous literature that attributed the drastic rise in prevalence of food insecurity in Australia during the pandemic to negative changes in food availability, access and stability [47]. It should be noted that most previous studies examined food security status during the lockdown period rather than post-lockdown to examine longer term impacts of the pandemic on food security status in Australia, which was intended in this study. Regarding the sustained increases in the prices of cucumbers, lettuce, peas, rice, tomatoes, eggplant, oil and meat that were also reported post-lockdown by focus group participants, these are similar to previous literature which has shown that post-lockdown, sustained price increases were noted for starchy foods (roots, tubers, plantains), fresh produce (cucumbers, lettuce, peas, tomatoes, eggplant) and animal-source foods (meat, oil) [59, 60]. The focus group results reflect the challenges Australians have faced in the post-lockdown period, marked by inflation and ongoing COVID recovery. This period, when compared to pre-pandemic years, has been difficult for many, creating a complex backdrop for understanding the responses. It is important for researchers and policymakers, both now and in the future, to recognise these contextual struggles when interpreting the findings. The divergence between the quantitative findings and qualitative findings of this study likely reflects limitations in the sensitivity of the 6-item USDA tool to capture short-term or context-specific disruptions, as well as differences in the composition of the survey and focus group samples, meaning the qualitative insights reveal nuanced experiences of hardship that were not fully captured in the quantitative prevalence estimate. While the survey suggested low prevalence of food insecurity (3.1%) and mostly ‘unvaried’ food security post-lockdown, focus group participants described significant challenges in food security, including shortages of staple and culturally preferred foods, movement restrictions, fear of infection and sudden income loss. Notably, overlaps were observed for the access and utilisation pillars, where the survey indicated higher meal size cutting in females and certain age groups, corresponding with participants’ reports of limited access to foods and altered consumption patterns. This cross-verification highlights that qualitative insights provide a nuanced understanding of temporary hardships not fully captured in survey prevalence estimates.

Further studies to confirm the findings of this current study in a longer-term post-pandemic context is warranted. Causes for changes in food security status during and post-lockdown in Western Sydney reported by the focus groups included changes to physical access to foods, behaviour changes, personal feelings and income variations. Changes to physical access to foods included factors such as not being able to go out and stringent lockdown rules on the non-vaccinated. Emotions such as fear of disease transmission were reported to alter shopping behaviours with limited in-store shopping. Behavioural changes included changes in shopping habits. Income variations included decreased income and increased consumers’ concerns about how much money they could spend, especially among overseas-born participants. These reasons are similar to ones reported in previous literature that explained that changes in economic circumstances because of the lockdown negatively impacted food security in Australia, especially for international students [47]. Other reasons stated in previous literature were also similar to the reasons stated in this study including strict social distancing restrictions, income reductions and job losses that affected the financial access to food [46]. Fear of disease transmission was reported in previous literature where 78% of Australian adults reported worsened mental health since the pandemic, out of which 25.9% had a fear of contamination [61]. Qualitative findings from the present study appear to be consistent with those presented in food security reports from Foodbank Australia in the years 2022 and 2023, where food security prevalence in New South Wales was at 65 and 63%, respectively [62]. It thus seems that the lockdown measures impacted food security by disrupting multiple components of the food system (availability and access), the financial and mental health status of at least some groups of people, which further impacted food access. In addition to availability, access and stability, it is important to consider the dimensions of agency and sustainability in understanding food security. Agency reflects the capacity of individuals and households to make choices about what foods they consume and how they obtain them [63], which was evident in participants’ adaptations to shopping behaviours and coping strategies post-lockdown. Sustainability highlights the long-term resilience of food systems, including the ability to maintain access to affordable, nutrition foods [63], which was challenged by prolonged price increases and disruptions in food supply observed in this study. Overall, the focus group findings complemented the survey results by providing insight into the underlying reasons for changes in food security, including rising food prices, reduced food availability, altered shopping behaviours and income-related challenges.

The strengths of this study include a mixed methods design to evaluate the self-reported impacts of COVID-19 lockdown on food security status pre- and post-lockdown. The mixed- methods design has helped collect both quantitative and qualitative data with a meaningful understanding of changes in food security status during and after the lockdown with possible reasons for these changes and the need to further strengthen or reinforce the local food supply chain/regional food policies. Considering the small geographic area and the size of the residing population, the relatively large sample size was a major strength of this study. Previous studies in the literature mainly collected data during the lockdown phase only. However, this study collected data several months after the lockdown. This has allowed for a better understanding of longer-term impacts examination rather than examining only short-term impacts of the lockdown. By capturing a diverse population in areas such as Western Sydney, this study helps to highlight region-specific challenges and solutions that can inform more targeted interventions. The limitations of this study are self-reported data from the surveys which may increase self-selection and reporting biases [64]. Another limitation of this study is that the two additional items in the survey, assessing the difference across three periods (pre-lockdown, lockdown and post-lockdown) may not provide sufficient depth to fully capture the impact of COVID-19 on food security status. Moreover, the focus group setting may have caused social desirability bias, as participants may have felt pressure to report their true experiences when surrounded by other people within the same groups [65]. Focus groups were selected over individual interviews because they enable shared discussion and allow participants to build on each other’s experiences, generating richer and more nuanced insights [66]. Additionally, the group setting encourages collective reflection on common challenges, which is particularly valuable for exploring community-level perceptions and behaviours [67]. An additional limitation of this study is that it relied on chi-square tests and adjusted regression models were not conducted. This limits the ability to control for potential confounders [68]. As a result, the reported associations should be interpreted with caution, as effect sizes and confidence intervals from adjusted models were not available. Moreover, a sensitivity analysis comparing the two different timepoints of data collection was not conducted; therefore, potential temporal variations in food insecurity prevalence between the timepoints cannot be ruled out. In addition, the elimination of individuals with severe medical conditions, special dietary needs and those who were pregnant represents a limitation, as it may have led to underestimation of food insecurity prevalence among higher-risk subgroups. Moreover, the reliance on retrospective self-report to assess food security in this study is a limitation, as recall bias may have affected the accuracy of participants’ responses [69]. Furthermore, the exclusion of key socioeconomic indicators, such as income and employment status represents a limitation, as it restricts the ability to fully account for factors that may influence food insecurity outcomes [70]. In addition, the absence of baseline pre-lockdown data represents a limitation, as it prevents causal inferences about the impact of lockdown on food security and the assessment of temporal changes within the sample. Although a modified version of the USDA six-item module was used, formal assessment of the tool’s internal consistency, sensitivity and specificity was not conducted in this study, which represents a methodological limitation.

In conclusion, this mixed-methods study found that food security in Western Sydney remained impacted post-lockdown (May 2022-April 2023), particularly among adults aged 18 to 64 years. Females experienced a higher frequency of meal size reduction, and key factors influencing food security included rising food prices, reduced availability and limited access. Changes in diet quality were linked to environmental, behavioural and economic factors. Given ongoing food insecurity in certain groups, targeted policies and health promotion programs should focus on vulnerable populations. Strengthening local food supply chains and social protection measures remain crucial, and further research is needed to assess the longer-term impacts of COVID-19 on food security, perhaps at the state level and lower. In preparation for any potential future disruption to national food system to ensure food security as a basic human right [71], the recently published ‘Feeding Australia: National Food Security Strategy’ provides a framework to coordinate actions at national, state and local levels [72].
