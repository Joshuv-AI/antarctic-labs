The epidemiology of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections in 2023 and hypothesis of "Immunity Debt": A single-center correlation analysis of the post-COVID-19
Authors: Daixi Lin, Yulin Xiao, Shasha Cheng, Yuqing He, Hengbiao Sun, Xiaoming Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the epidemiological characteristics of Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) infection in our hospital in 2023 and explore the relationship with underlying “immunity debt” by comparing positive rates before and after COVID-19 prevention and control measures.MethodsA total of 12504 patients with respiratory diseases were included, and age, gender, and month were analyzed for their association with MP infection. The effect of COVID-19 prevention and control was evaluated by comparing positive rates from 2018 to 2023.ResultsIn 2023, the MP-positivity rate peaked in October at 59.18% (390/659) and was predominantly seen in pediatric patients. In addition, comparison with previous years revealed a higher infection rate in children aged 0-3 years in 2023. A significant difference was observed in positive rates before, during, and after COVID-19 prevention and control, with the highest rate post-control; and females had a higher infection rate in all three periods. In a word, the 2023 MP-positive rates surge, the marked increase in susceptibility among children aged 0-3, and the consistently higher infection rate in females.ConclusionThe study suggests a significant increase in MP positive rates, particularly in pediatric patients and females in 2023. The implementation of COVID-19 prevention and control measures may have contributed to the higher infection rate observed after its removal. In conclusion, the 2023 MP epidemic represents a significant shift in post-pandemic infectious disease dynamics. Understanding this surge may require a comprehensive view within a Multifactorial Framework which contain Natural Epidemic Cycle, The "Immunity Debt" Hypothesis and Behavioral and Ecological Factors.

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a small microorganism that can cause infections in both the upper and lower respiratory tract. It is the pathogen responsible for Mycoplasma pneumonia and primarily spreads through droplets, with an incubation period of 2-3 weeks [[1], [2], [3]]. MP is more prevalent during autumn and winter seasons, and regional epidemics occur every 4 to 7 years [4]. The first-line treatment for MP is macrolide antibiotics, and laboratory testing is crucial for diagnosis [5].

In 2023, there was an outbreak of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection following the relaxation of COVID-19 prevention and control measures, leading to speculation that this may be related to "immunity debt." This term refers to a low exposure period to specific viruses and bacteria, which can weaken population immunity and increase susceptibility to infection [6]. This is especially of concern for young children who may delay their first exposure to common pathogens [7].

Non-pharmaceutical interventions, such as wearing masks and regular disinfection, implemented to prevent COVID-19 have also effectively reduced transmission of other respiratory pathogens [8]. However, after the lifting of these measures, there has been an epidemic of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection in China. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the epidemiological characteristics of this outbreak by examining cases from a hospital between 2018 and 2023. By understanding the relationship between "immunity debt" and Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, this study aims to provide evidence for clinical prevention and treatment strategies against MP infection.

Methods

Date collection and treatment of specimens

A retrospective analysis was conducted on outpatient and inpatient respiratory tract infection patients at the Third Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University, Guangzhou, China from January 2018 to November 2023. The diagnosis of Mycoplasma pneumoniae primarily relies on laboratory serological testing, combined with clinical symptoms and other diagnostic examinations. The inclusion criteria for positive cases comprise the following three aspects;

1). Presentation of one or more symptoms such as fever, cough, and sputum production; 2). IgM antibody titer greater than 1:160; 3). Based on imaging examinations, laboratory tests and clinical responses to treatment, the diagnosis was made of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection. It's worthing that imaging findings (when available) should be used as a reference; in the absence of imaging data, diagnosis should primarily rely on laboratory test results and response to macrolide antibiotic treatment.

The patients were divided into different age groups: infants (0-3 years old), pre-school age (4-6 years old), school age (7-9 years old), juvenile age (10-12 years old), and other (>12 years old). To investigate the impact of COVID-19 on the infection rate of Mycoplasma pneumoniae, the study period was further divided into three groups: January 2018-December 2019, January 2020-December 2022, and January 2023-November 2023. The Chi-square test was used to compare the difference in Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection rate among these groups. Written informed consent was obtained from participants, and all procedures were discussed by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University.

The detection of Mycoplasma pneumoniae is mainly based on serological detection. Venous blood of patients is collected and serum is collected by centrifugation for immunoagglutination detection. The antibodies detected include IgM and IgG.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by Excel and SPSS 27.0 software and expressed in the form of "positive rate (number of positive cases/total number of cases)". The positive rate of different ages and years was tested by Chi-square test, and P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Of the 12504 patients included, 6372 were male and 6132 were female; the full distribution is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a common cause of community-acquired pneumonia, especially in preschool-aged children. Approximately 20% of all cases of community-acquired pneumonia in this age group are caused by MP [1]. One of the most commonly used methods to aid in clinical diagnosis and treatment of MP is the detection of IgM/IgG antibodies in serum, and titers of IgM greater than 160 has diagnosticvalue for recent or acute infection.

And unlike Western countries, mainly dealt with influenza and respiratory syncytial virus infections [6]. China saw a surge in MP infections in the winter after the epidemic, with peak incidence in children and young people. It is mostly isolated as sporadic cases, although occasional outbreaks can occur. MP infection typically provokes a slow onset and may progress to pneumonia. The infection is highly prevalent worldwide, and its incidence tends to be sporadic, occurring once every 4-7 years. It can last for up to a year, and the infection rate during epidemic years can be up to several times higher than during non-epidemic years [4]. Our data analysis showed a small peak in MP positive rates in 2019 (with a positive rate of 41.23%). This finding is consistent with the results reported by Weiling Qiu et al. [9]. However, with the outbreak of the novel coronavirus at the end of 2019, strict non-drug intervention measures were implemented across the country to prevent infection. This led to a significant decrease in the positive rates of other respiratory pathogens, including MP. As the coronavirus prevention and control measures were gradually lifted, MP also saw a resurgence. This trend was particularly evident in northern cities like Beijing [10]. Our data from July to November 2023 showed a gradual increase in MP positive rates, reaching a peak of 59.18% in October. This was significantly higher than the data from previous years, indicating a surge in MP infections in recent years.

Interestingly, our data also showed a significant increase in MP infections in the age group of 0-3 years old compared to previous years. The strict non-drug intervention measures implemented during the epidemic drastically maybe reduced the infants and toddlers born during the epidemic which exposure to pathogens, resulting in a delayed immune response. It maybe supports the hypothesis which is known as "immunity debt," which occurs when low or no exposure to a virus or bacteria leads to a lack of immune stimulation, making a high proportion of the population vulnerable to infection. This increases the likelihood of future epidemics, as a growing number of people remain "susceptible" and herd immunity declines. Younger children, in particular, may experience a delay in their first exposure to common pathogens, making them more susceptible to infections like MP [8].

The national lockdowns and other measures taken to slow the spread of the virus possibly limited people's opportunities to build immunity against pathogens, resulting in increased susceptibility to respiratory infections.Our data analysis in all age group also support the above viewpoint.

We divided our data into three groups, with the control group consisting of January 2018 to December 2019, the second group consisting of January 2020 to December 2022, and the observation group consisting of January to November 2023. Our chi-square analysis showed that the positive rates in the observation group were significantly higher than in the control groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, our data showed a higher infection rate in women compared to men, especially in the observation group. These results suggest that the coronavirus prevention and control measures may have made people more susceptible to MP infections.

Our analysis reveals that the COVID-19 pandemic exerted a profound influence on the epidemiological landscape of Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) infections. The implementation of strict non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the pandemic (2020-2022) led to a sustained suppression of MP transmission. However, the subsequent unprecedented surge in 2023 suggests a complex interplay between several ecological and immunological factors. By sorting out and collecting the situations of respiration outbreak infections around the world after the COVID-19 pandemic,We explain this phenomenon within a Multifactorial Framework which contain Natural Epidemic Cycle, The "Immunity Debt" Hypothesis and Behavioral and Ecological Factors.

Cycles Historically, MP exhibits cyclical peaks every 4 to 7 years. It is highly probable that the 2023 outbreak represents a natural epidemic peak that was significantly suppressed and delayed by the restrictive measures of the pandemic era [11]. Because the NPIs likely alter the transmission dynamics of respiratory pathogens. Accumulating evidence indicates that NPIs not only suppressed COVID-19 but also transiently nearly eliminated other common respiratory virus such as influenza, respiratory stncytial virus(RSV),and MP from community circulation [6,8,10].

While NPIs were effective in reducing the immediate clinical burden, they likely interrupted the natural acquisition of herd immunity. This period of low pathogen exposure may have created what is colloquially termed an "immunity debt." Prolonged lack of exposure to MP reduces the frequency of "immune training" within the population, leading to a decline in protective antibody levels and an accumulation of susceptible individuals. This is particularly evident in our data regarding the 0-3 year old cohort—children born during the pandemic who had virtually no prior exposure to MP. The geographical context further supports this hypothesis. Previous studies indicate that MP infection rates in Asia are historically higher than in Western countries [[12], [13], [14], [15]], suggesting a higher baseline of susceptibility or different environmental dynamics. The sudden re-exposure of a "naïve" population to the pathogen after the lifting of NPIs likely acted as a catalyst, intensifying the 2023 epidemic peak beyond historical norms.

The abrupt resumption of pre-pandemic contact patterns (e.g., school opening, social gathering,the use of mask has decreased.) following NPIs withdrawal facilitated rapid transmission. Furthermore, the suppressed circulation of other respiratory viruses (such as influenza and RSV) during the pandemic may have altered ecological niches [8,10], reducing viral interference and creating a appropriate environment for MP resurgence.

Attributing the 2023 surge solely to a single factor would be an oversimplification. Rather, we propose that the outbreak resulted from a synergistic effect: the natural 4-7 years epidemic cycle was delayed and compressed by NPIs, then fueled by a weakened population immunity (immunity debt) and changes in social behavioral patterns. Furthermore, the disruption of the microbial ecological niche where reduced circulation of other respiratory pathogens (e.g., Influenza and RSV) [6] during the pandemic may have altered host susceptibility cannot be ignored.

In conclusion, the 2023 MP epidemic represents a significant shift in post-pandemic infectious disease dynamics. Understanding this surge requires a comprehensive view that integrates population immunity status, pathogen ecology evolution, and the long-term consequences of large-scale social interventions.

Furthermore, our data shows that in the later stage of the epidemic outbreak, the positive rate of infants aged 0-3 years was significantly higher than that of other age groups. Therefore, in clinical treatment, clinicians should pay more attention to the MP infection situation of the 0-3 years old infant population and take more appropriate and efficient treatment measures. The application of targeted sequencing in cases of treatment failure is expected to significantly reduce the incidence of severe infections and treatment-related adverse outcomes.

However, our study is limited as it only included samples from a single hospital and had a limited sample size. Future studies should consider a larger sample size and include data from different regions for more comprehensive and accurate analysis. A technical limitation of this study is the reliance on the Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) IgM result combined with clinical symptoms for diagnosis, rather than the 'gold standard' of paired sera or universal nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT). In a large-scale retrospective outpatient setting, obtaining convalescent sera to demonstrate a four-fold IgG increase is clinically impractical as most patients do not return for follow-up testing after symptom resolution. While NAATs offer high sensitivity, they are limited by the difficulty of obtaining lower respiratory tract specimens in outpatients and the inability to distinguish between active infection and asymptomatic colonization. A single high IgM titer may not reliably distinguish recent from past infection, especially in non-epidemic periods. A single high IgM titer can reliably indicate that a Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection has occurred recently (within weeks to months). However, without paired serology showing dynamic changes or direct pathogen detection by PCR, it cannot confirm that this infection is the cause of the presenting symptoms. This limitation is particularly prominent in non-epidemic years, when respiratory infections caused by other pathogens predominate, and incidental residual IgM from past infection is more likely to be misinterpreted as the etiology of the current illness. Consequently, the reported positivity rate of M. pneumoniae infection is spuriously elevated during non-epidemic years. Because IgM positivity is more likely to be spuriously elevated in non-epidemic years due to residual antibody from prior infections, the true magnitude of the 2023 surge is likely underestimated in our data, which would strengthen rather than weaken the core finding. The "clinical-serological" composite criteria used in this study represent a pragmatic approach necessitated by the realities of a large-scale retrospective outpatient study, aimed at minimize diagnostic bias and ensure that the cases analyzed represented true acute infections represented true acute infections,and maximizing the capture of acute cases, though it may introduce some misclassification bias.

Not only that, we acknowledge that our data used in this study were derived exclusively from hospital-based sources, which capture patterns of healthcare-seeking behavior rather than reflecting true community-level prevalence of infectious diseases. Hence our explanation of an “immunity debt” is subject to limited generalizability, because it may overrepresent cases severe enough to require hospital care and underrepresent asymptomatic, mild, or community-managed illnesses. This distinction is crucial, because trends in hospital presentations do not necessarily equate to population-wide changes in immunity or infection dynamics.

Another limitation of this study lies in the fact that imaging examinations are not universally accessible. This is particularly evident in outpatient cases, where imaging examinations are often conducted based on clinical judgment rather than standardized protocols. As a result, the consistency of case classification may be affected. For instance, respiratory infections with atypical or subclinical imaging manifestations may be misclassified solely based on clinical criteria; meanwhile, some cases that have not been confirmed by imaging may have different diagnostic categories from those confirmed by imaging. The uneven application of imaging examinations in the study population may introduce potential biases in the description of disease characteristics.
