Beatty solutions of almost Golomb equations
Benoît Cloitre

Abstract
The almost Golomb equation of order r r is the implicit functional equation a ⁡ ( ∑ j = 0 r − 1 a ⁡ ( n − j ) ) = n a\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a(n{-}j)\Bigr)=n for nondecreasing sequences of positive integers with a ⁡ ( 1 ) = 1 a(1)=1 . Its earliest solution, the almost Golomb sequence of order r r , is r r -regular in the sense of Allouche and Shallit and has oscillating ratio a ⁡ ( n ) / n a(n)/n . We prove that for every r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 that is not an even perfect square, the equation admits a second monotone solution given by an inhomogeneous Beatty sequence of slope 1 / r 1/\!\sqrt{r} . Composing the equation with a a leads to a triple-nested identity which admits a continuous one-parameter family of inhomogeneous Beatty solutions, parametrised by a shift d d ranging over an explicit interval. We determine these intervals sharply for r = 2 r=2 and r = 3 r=3 , each proved by a local regime analysis combined with equidistribution of an irrational orbit. The endpoints of these intervals sit naturally inside the Pell–Ostrowski framework of Fokkink, and the defect set at the upper endpoint for r = 2 r=2 is characterised as the return-time set of an irrational rotation to an explicit interval.

The continuous equation

In the discrete equation (
1

f(n)=\sum a(n{-}j)
denotes the sliding-window sum
and the identity reads
a\circ f=\mathrm{id}
.
The continuous analogue replaces

\varphi

\varphi\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\varphi(x{-}j)\Bigr)=x.

(6)

A natural candidate is the affine function

\varphi(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{r}}+\frac{\sqrt{r}-1}{2}.

(7)

Theorem 4
.

The function (
7
) is the unique affine
solution of (
6
).
Moreover, every continuous nondecreasing solution
is a homeomorphism of

satisfying
the inverse equation

\varphi^{-1}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\varphi(x{-}j).

(8)

Proof.

Suppose first that
\varphi(x)=cx+d
is affine. Then

\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\varphi(x{-}j)=r(cx+d)-\frac{cr(r{-}1)}{2}

and then

\varphi\Bigl(\sum\varphi(x{-}j)\Bigr)=rc^{2}x+\Bigl(rcd-\frac{rc^{2}(r{-}1)}{2}+d\Bigr).

This equals
x
if and only if
rc^{2}=1

and
d(\sqrt{r}+1)=(r{-}1)/2
.
The unique positive solution is
c=1/\!\sqrt{r}
,

d=(\sqrt{r}-1)/2
.

We now show that every continuous nondecreasing
solution is a homeomorphism satisfying the inverse
equation. Let
\varphi
be any such solution and set

g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\varphi(x{-}j)
.
Then
g
is continuous and nondecreasing, and

\varphi(g(x))=x
for all
x
,
so
\varphi
is surjective and
g
is injective.
Since
g
is continuous, nondecreasing, and injective,
it is strictly increasing.
If
\varphi
had a flat interval
[u,v]
with

\varphi\equiv\lambda
there, then

g(\lambda)\in[u,v]
but
g(x)<u
for
x<\lambda

and
g(x)>v
for

, contradicting
continuity of
g
at
\lambda
.
So
\varphi
is strictly increasing, hence a
homeomorphism, and
g=\varphi^{-1}
.
∎

Whether the inverse equation (

\beta
for
the integer
t
, whose parity determines the
floor
u=\lfloor s\beta\rfloor
and thereby the structure
of the counting bound.

Lemma 11
.

\lfloor s\beta\rfloor=\lfloor(t{-}1)/2\rfloor
.

Proof.

Since
\beta=t/(s+\alpha)
and

s+\alpha\in(2s,2s{+}1)
, we get

\frac{t}{2s{+}1}<\beta<\frac{t}{2s}.

Multiplying by
s
:

\frac{st}{2s{+}1}<s\beta<\frac{t}{2}.

Since

\frac{st}{2s{+}1}-\frac{t{-}1}{2}=\frac{2s{-}t{+}1}{2(2s{+}1)}>0

(as
t\leq 2s
), we obtain

(t{-}1)/2<s\beta<t/2
, whence

\lfloor s\beta\rfloor=\lfloor(t{-}1)/2\rfloor
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

N(r)=(s{+}1)(t{-}1)-2\sum_{k=1}^{s}\lfloor k\beta\rfloor
.

Proof.

For
0\leq m\leq s{-}1
, the number of integers

j\in[1,r{-}1]
with
\lfloor j/\alpha\rfloor=m
and

\{j/\alpha\}<\beta
is

C_{m}=t+\lfloor(m{-}s)\beta\rfloor-\lfloor m\beta\rfloor

(since
m\alpha+\alpha\beta=m\alpha+t-s\beta

and the endpoints are irrational).
For
m=s
:
C_{s}=t{-}1{-}\lfloor s\beta\rfloor
.
Using
\lfloor-m\beta\rfloor=-\lfloor m\beta\rfloor-1
for
m\geq 1

and summing gives the result.
∎

Since
\lfloor k\beta\rfloor
counts integers
m\geq 1

with
m\leq k\beta
, swapping the order of
summation gives

\sum_{k=1}^{s}\lfloor k\beta\rfloor=\sum_{m=1}^{u}\bigl(s{+}1-\lceil m/\beta\rceil\bigr),

(14)

where
u=\lfloor s\beta\rfloor
.

We now prove the non-square part of
Theorem
5
.

Proof of Theorem
5
, non-square case.

By Proposition
9
,
it suffices to show
N(r)<r\beta+s-1
.
By Lemmas
11

and
12
, we split by the
parity of
t
.

Suppose first that
t=2u{+}1
is odd.
Then
\lfloor s\beta\rfloor=u
,
and substituting (
14
)
into Lemma
12
gives

N(r)=2\sum_{m=1}^{u}\lceil m/\beta\rceil
.
Since
\beta
is irrational, each

\lceil m/\beta\rceil<m/\beta+1
, so

N(r)<\frac{u(u{+}1)}{\beta}+2u.

The margin is

r\beta+s-1-\frac{u(u{+}1)}{\beta}-2u=\frac{(1{-}\beta)^{2}(\beta^{2}{+}2\beta{+}4u{+}3)}{4\beta}>0

(the equality is checked by substituting

r=s^{2}+t
and
\beta(2s{+}\beta)=t=2u{+}1
,
then clearing denominators).

Suppose now that
t=2u
is even.
Then
\lfloor s\beta\rfloor=u{-}1
, and the same
substitution gives

N(r)=s{+}1+2\sum_{m=1}^{u-1}\lceil m/\beta\rceil
.
Using
\lceil m/\beta\rceil<m/\beta+1
again:

N(r)<s+1+\frac{u(u{-}1)}{\beta}+2u-2.

The margin is

r\beta+s-1-s-1-\frac{u(u{-}1)}{\beta}-2u+2=\frac{u(1{-}\beta)^{2}}{\beta}+\frac{\beta^{3}}{4}>0

(checked by substituting
\beta(2s{+}\beta)=t=2u

and clearing denominators).

In both cases
N(r)<r\beta+s{-}1

and the theorem follows from
Proposition
9
.
∎

3.4
Perfect squares

When
r=s^{2}
, the parameter
\beta
vanishes
and the counting argument of the previous
subsection degenerates.
The window sum can instead be evaluated exactly
by grouping terms modulo

clean application of Hermite’s identity.

Theorem 13
.

Let

and
a(n)=\lfloor n/s+s/2\rfloor
.
For all

:

1.

if
s
is odd,

and
a(S(n))=n
;

2.

if
s
is even,

S(n)=sn-s(s{-}2)/2

and
a(S(n))=n+1
.

Proof.

Write
q=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
.

Suppose first that
s=2q{+}1
is odd.
Then
d=s/2=q+1/2\notin\mathbb{Z}
, and

a(n)=q+\lfloor(n{+}q)/s\rfloor
.
For
n\geq s^{2}
the full window is active, so

S(n)=s^{2}q+\sum_{j=0}^{s^{2}-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n-j+q}{s}\Bigr\rfloor.

Write

with
0\leq k,u\leq s{-}1
.
Then
\lfloor(n{-}ks{-}u{+}q)/s\rfloor=\lfloor(n{-}u{+}q)/s\rfloor-k
, giving

S(n)=s^{2}q+s\sum_{u=0}^{s-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n{-}u{+}q}{s}\Bigr\rfloor-s\cdot\frac{s(s{-}1)}{2}.

As
u
ranges over
\{0,\ldots,s{-}1\}
,
the values
n{+}q{-}u
hit each residue class
modulo
s
exactly once, so by Hermite’s identity
(
\sum_{v=0}^{n-1}\lfloor x+v/n\rfloor=\lfloor nx\rfloor
for all

)

\sum_{u=0}^{s-1}\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n{+}q{-}u}{s}\Bigr\rfloor=n+q-s+1.

Substituting and using

yields

S(n)=sn-s(s{-}1)/2
.
Finally,

,
since
0\leq q/s=(s{-}1)/(2s)<1
.

Suppose now that
s=2m
is even.
Then

and

.
Applying Hermite’s identity in the same way gives

.
Then
a(S(n))=m+\lfloor n{-}(s{-}2)/2\rfloor=n+1
,
since
(s{-}2)/2=m{-}1\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Corollary 14

The canonical Beatty identity

(

4.1
The continuous equation

For the strong identity
\varphi(g(x))=x

and the triple-nested
\varphi(\varphi(g(x)))=\varphi(x)

(where
g(x)=\sum\varphi(x{-}j)
),
the continuous equation gives the same unique affine
solution (
7
) in both cases:
if
\varphi(g(x))=x
, composing with
\varphi

immediately yields
\varphi(\varphi(g(x)))=\varphi(x)

and conversely, injectivity of

the strong identity from the triple-nested one.

In the discrete setting, the floor breaks this
equivalence. The strong identity pins down a unique

(the left endpoint of the Beatty interval), while
the weaker triple-nested identity tolerates a range
of shifts. The floor in the outer composition

absorbs small errors, opening the
interval

to the right.

4.2
Expansion

Throughout this section,
c=1/\!\sqrt{2}
,

Write

(15)

Applying

(16)

A second application of

4.3
Necessity

Any solution must satisfy
a(1)=\lfloor c+d\rfloor=1
,
requiring
1-c\leq d<2-c
. The failure of (
5
)
outside

is produced by a local argument on the
three residue classes of
\theta_{n}\in[0,1)

combined with density of the orbit

(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
We describe the dynamics once, and then read off each
endpoint as a special case.

Expansion (
16
) together with

gives

(19)

Writing

is
determined by

m(n)=k\iff D-c\Theta_{2}(n)\in\Bigl[k+\tfrac{1}{2},\,k+\tfrac{3}{2}\Bigr).

Two simple implications are immediate. If

,
then

, so survival of the identity

, that is,

n
is a repeat. Symmetrically, if

must be a repeat. These implications are

(N
+
)

m(n)\geq 1\ \Rightarrow\ a(n+1)=a(n)

(N

)

Taken as a global statement over all

) and

that this

The three residue classes of

4.5
The Ostrowski–Pell framework behind the
r=2
triple-nested family

The endpoint formulas, the return-time structure
of the defect set, and the underlying irrational
rotation are naturally expressed in the
Ostrowski–Pell framework attached to the
quadratic irrational
\alpha=1+\sqrt{2}
, organised
by Fokkink
[
4
]
as a continuation of the
Wythoff array of Conway and Ryba.

Shared continued-fraction backbone

The slope
c=1/\!\sqrt{2}
has continued-fraction
expansion

c=[0;1,2,2,2,\ldots],

whose convergents
p_{k}/q_{k}
have denominators

q_{k}=1,3,7,17,41,99,239,577,\ldots

(
A001333
),
the Pell denominators satisfying the recurrence

q_{k+1}=2q_{k}+q_{k-1}
. The characteristic
equation
x^{2}=2x+1
of this recurrence has
dominant root
\alpha=1+\sqrt{2}
, the silver ratio.
Fokkink’s Pell tower is built from the same
recurrence as the
m=2
instance of the family
of bi-infinite sequences

X_{n+1}=m\,X_{n}+X_{n-1},\qquad m\geq 1,

where
m
is a positive-integer parameter of the
tower, unrelated to the shift
d
of our Beatty
ansatz. The case
m=1
recovers the Fibonacci
recurrence and the Wythoff array of Morrison and
Kimberling.

Shared numeration backbone and the wall column

Every positive integer admits a unique

Ostrowski representation

n=\sum_{k\geq 1}e_{k}q_{k}

with digits
e_{1}\in\{0,1\}
,
e_{k}\in\{0,1,2\}

for
k\geq 2
, and
e_{k}=2\Rightarrow e_{k-1}=0
.
In the tower picture, this numeration labels the
rows. Fokkink distinguishes two natural Beatty
sequences attached to the tower. The first
column
A_{1,n}
is the non-homogeneous Beatty
sequence

A_{1,n}=\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n\alpha}{\alpha-1}-\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\Bigr\rfloor=\Bigl\lfloor n\cdot(1{+}c)-\tfrac{1}{2}(2{-}\sqrt{2})\Bigr\rfloor

of slope
\alpha/(\alpha-1)=1+c\approx 1.707

(
[
4
, Corollary 2.4]
). The wall
column
A_{m,0}

5
Internal structure

Throughout this section we consider the case

and write

for the canonical Beatty

5.1
Sturmian words

The difference sequence

w_{\mathrm{B}}(n)=a_{\mathrm{B}}(n{+}1)-a_{\mathrm{B}}(n)\in\{0,1\}
is a Sturmian word of slope

c=1/\!\sqrt{2}
and intercept
d{-}(1{-}c)=3c{-}1=(3-\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{2}
, namely

w_{\mathrm{B}}(n)=\lfloor c(n{+}1)+d\rfloor-\lfloor cn+d\rfloor
.
The word is balanced, and every pair of factors
of the same length differ by at most one in
the number of
1
’s.

The multiplicity

m(n)=\#\{k:a_{\mathrm{B}}(k)=n\}
takes values
in
\{1,2\}
, and
m(n)=2
iff

w_{\mathrm{B}}(n{-}1)=0
, i.e.,

\{cn+d\}<1{-}c
.
This characterises the positions of
repeated values purely in terms of the
Sturmian rotation.

A morphism on
\{0,1\}^{*}
generating this
Sturmian word (via the standard Rauzy construction
[
7
]
)
is

\sigma\colon 0\mapsto 01,\;1\mapsto 011
,
with
\sigma^{k}(0)\to w_{\mathrm{B}}
as

k\to\infty
.
The substitution is associated with the continued
fraction
[0;1,2,2,2,\ldots]
via the classical
correspondence between Sturmian morphisms and
Ostrowski numeration
[
7
, Chapter 6]
.

5.2
Bifurcations

The two solutions
a_{\mathrm{G}}

and
a_{\mathrm{B}}
first diverge at

n=12
(Section
1
), and their
difference
words diverge one step earlier, with

w_{\mathrm{G}}(11)=0
and
w_{\mathrm{B}}(11)=1
.
This is the smallest instance of a broader
phenomenon. For
r=2
, the greedy
a_{\mathrm{G}}
and
the Beatty
a_{\mathrm{B}}
turn out to be only
two members of a large family of monotone
solutions, organised along a binary branching
structure.

More precisely, a companion paper
[
3
]

proves that the set
\mathcal{S}
of all
nondecreasing solutions of (

) with

r=2
admits a canonical bijection with the
Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, in which
the greedy corresponds to
0^{\infty}
, the
“lazy” (anti-greedy) solution to

,
and the ratio

converges
to

with a log-periodic residual
for every
\omega
. The Beatty solution studied
here lies outside this tree, and is characterised
by its Sturmian difference word rather than by
any binary branching pattern. A filtering
theorem then selects, among the continuum of tree
solutions, the countably many that are

-regular (exactly the eventually constant
directing words). We refer the reader
to
[

]
for statements and proofs.

6.1

Theorem 24

For

Consequently:

(b)

Proof.

Using

(33)

Now

,

, and

\theta_{n-1}\leq 1/2
, yielding

\theta_{n-1}\in[1-c,1/2]
.

This interval is non-empty since
1/2>1-c
, with
length
1/2-(1-c)=c-1/2=(\sqrt{2}{-}1)/2
. The
characterisation is established.

For (a), in the effective case

\theta_{n}=\theta_{n-1}+c-1\in[0,c-1/2]
.
Since
c-1/2<1-c
, we have
\theta_{n}<1-c
, so

\theta_{n}+c<1
and

a_{\mathrm{B}}(n{+}1)-a_{\mathrm{B}}(n)=\lfloor\theta_{n}+c\rfloor=0
.

For (b), the sequence

(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is the orbit of the
irrational rotation
\theta\mapsto\{\theta+c\}
,
uniformly equidistributed in
[0,1)
by Weyl’s
theorem. The set of
n
with

\theta_{n-1}\in[1-c,1/2]
therefore has natural
density equal to the length of that interval.
∎

The absorption mechanism now reads explicitly. At a
defect position, the Sturmian address
\theta_{n}

lies in the low interval
[0,c-1/2]
, forcing

a_{\mathrm{B}}(n{+}1)=a_{\mathrm{B}}(n)
. The
identity (
5
) then survives because the
extra
+1
in
a_{\mathrm{B}}(f(n))
is absorbed by
the outer application of
a_{\mathrm{B}}
,

a_{\mathrm{B}}(a_{\mathrm{B}}(f(n)))=a_{\mathrm{B}}(n{+}1)=a_{\mathrm{B}}(n).

6.2
The gap sequence

The characterisation

\mathcal{D}=\{n\geq 2:\theta_{n-1}\in[1-c,1/2]\}

expresses
\mathcal{D}
as the return-time set of
the rotation
\theta\mapsto\{\theta+c\}
to the
interval
[1-c,1/2]
. By the three-distance theorem
of Sós
[
11
]
(see Alessandri and
Berthé
[
10
]
for a modern
exposition within combinatorics on words),
the gaps take at most three values. The
return-time analysis of
Proposition
25
below
identifies these values as
3
,
4
, and
7
, and
computes the corresponding letter frequencies.

The letter
4
is always preceded and followed
by
3
. Every occurrence of
4
in the gap sequence
appears within a block
3,4,3
, verified numerically
for
k\leq 10^{5}
.

Proposition 25
.

The gap sequence of
\mathcal{D}
on the alphabet

\{3,4,7\}
has letter frequencies

f_{3}=\sqrt{2}-1,\quad f_{4}=3-2\sqrt{2},\quad f_{7}=\sqrt{2}-1,

matching Theorem
24
(b), and the
average gap is
2(\sqrt{2}+1)
. The gap sequence
of
\mathcal{D}
is

A395253
.

Proof.

The defect set is

\mathcal{D}=\{n\geq 2:\theta_{n-1}\in J\}
with

J=[1-c,\,1/2]
, so
|J|=c-1/2=(\sqrt{2}-1)/2
.
For each return time
i\geq 1
, let

J_{i}=\{\theta\in J:R_{c}^{i}\theta\in J\text{ and }R_{c}^{k}\theta\notin J\text{ for }1\leq k<i\},

where
R_{c}\colon\theta\mapsto\{\theta+c\}
.
We compute the sub-intervals directly from the
iterates. Using
c\in(1/2,1)
and
2c<3c-1<3c<2
,
the values
kc\bmod 1
for small
k
are

R_{c}\theta=\theta+c-1,\qquad R_{c}^{2}\theta=\theta+2c-1,\qquad R_{c}^{3}\theta=\theta+3c-2

for
\theta\in J
. Neither
R_{c}\theta\in[0,c-1/2]

nor
R_{c}^{2}\theta\in[c,2c-1/2]
meets
J
, so
every return time is at least
3
.

Return time
3
occurs when

\theta+3c-2\in[1-c,1/2]
, equivalently

\theta\in[3-4c,\,5/2-3c]
. Since
1-c>3-4c

and
5/2-3c<1/2
, intersection with
J
gives

J_{3}=[1-c,\,5/2-3c],\qquad|J_{3}|=(5/2-3c)-(1-c)=3/2-2c=\tfrac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{2}.

For
\theta\in J\setminus J_{3}=(5/2-3c,\,1/2]
, the
return time exceeds
3
. The next iterate

R_{c}^{4}\theta=\theta+4c-3
lies in
J
when

\theta\in[4-5c,\,7/2-4c]
. Intersection with

(5/2-3c,\,1/2]
gives

J_{4}=(4-5c,\,1/2],\qquad|J_{4}|=1/2-(4-5c)=5c-7/2=\tfrac{5\sqrt{2}-7}{2}.

The remainder

J_{7}=(5/2-3c,\,4-5c]=J\setminus(J_{3}\sqcup J_{4})

has length

|J_{7}|=(4-5c)-(5/2-3c)=3/2-2c=\tfrac{3-2\sqrt{2}}{2},

and
R_{c}^{7}\theta\in J
for every

\theta\in J_{7}
by direct calculation. The
three-distance theorem
[
11
,
10
]

rules out return times other than
3
,
4
,
7
,
so
J=J_{3}\sqcup J_{4}\sqcup J_{7}
up to the
two measure-zero endpoints.

The orbit
(\theta_{n-1})_{n\geq 2}
equidistributes
in
[0,1)
by Weyl’s theorem, so each
J_{i}
is
visited with density
|J_{i}|
, and the frequency
of gap value
i
among defect positions is

f_{i}=\frac{|J_{i}|}{|J|}.

Substituting the lengths and using

(\sqrt{2}-1)(\sqrt{2}+1)=1
to rationalise yields

f_{3}=f_{7}=\sqrt{2}-1,\qquad f_{4}=3-2\sqrt{2}.

The average gap is

3f_{3}+4f_{4}+7f_{7}=3(\sqrt{2}{-}1)+4(3{-}2\sqrt{2})+7(\sqrt{2}{-}1)=2\sqrt{2}+2=2(\sqrt{2}{+}1),

which equals the reciprocal of the density

\rho(\mathcal{D})=(\sqrt{2}-1)/2
established in
Theorem
24
(b).
∎

Conjecture 26
.

The gap sequence of
\mathcal{D}
belongs to the
minimal subshift generated by the primitive
substitution

\sigma\colon\quad 3\mapsto 3,4,\qquad 4\mapsto 3,7,\qquad 7\mapsto 3,7,7.

(34)

The substitution has incidence matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&2\end{pmatrix},

characteristic polynomial

\lambda^{3}-3\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1=(\lambda-1)(\lambda^{2}-2\lambda-1)
,
and Perron eigenvalue
1+\sqrt{2}
(the silver
ratio). Since
M^{2}
has all positive entries,

\sigma
is primitive of index
2
, and the
Perron eigenvector gives letter frequencies
matching Proposition
25
.

The conjecture is supported by numerical evidence
(verified for
k\leq 10^{5}
) and by the following
natural candidate proof, which we leave for future
work to complete.

Sketch of a Rauzy induction argument. Using the
sub-intervals
J_{3}
,
J_{4}
,
J_{7}
computed in
the proof of Proposition
25
,
the first-return map
R_{c}^{\mathrm{ret}}\colon J\to J

permutes the three pieces. Direct inspection of the
images
R_{c}^{3}(J_{3})
,
R_{c}^{4}(J_{4})
,

R_{c}^{7}(J_{7})
inside
J
suggests the
transitions

J_{3}\to J_{3}J_{4},\qquad J_{4}\to J_{3}J_{7},\qquad J_{7}\to J_{3}J_{7}J_{7},

which transpose to (
34
).
A complete proof would require the full Rauzy
induction machinery on three letters,
see
[
7
, Chapter 6]
.

Remark 27
.

The three gap values
3,4,7
are exactly

q_{2}

q_{3}-q_{2}

q_{3}

6.3
A two-letter coarsening

Grouping the three gap values into two classes
