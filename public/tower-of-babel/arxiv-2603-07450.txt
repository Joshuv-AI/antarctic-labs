Abstract
When the concept of fluid antenna system (FAS) is applied to multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems, this gives rise to MIMO-FAS, a.k.a. fluid MIMO. Under rich scattering, the spatial correlation matrices are governed by the zeroth-order Bessel function J 0 ​ ( ⋅ ) J_{0}(\cdot) through the continuously adjustable antenna positions, creating a highly non-convex landscape for optimization with fluctuating local optima—the Bessel landscape . In this paper, we tackle the joint transmitter (TX) and receiver (RX) antenna position optimization problem in fluid MIMO to maximize the ergodic capacity by shaping this landscape. Using Kronecker channel decomposition, we firstly develop a suite of analytical results that expose the problem’s intrinsic structure: (i) a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) capacity approximation that decomposes the objective into separable log-determinant terms of the TX and RX correlation matrices, 𝐑 T \mathbf{R}_{T} and 𝐑 R \mathbf{R}_{R} , respectively, (ii) a closed-form capacity loss bound linking det ( 𝐑 T ) ​ det ( 𝐑 R ) \det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\det(\mathbf{R}_{R}) to the performance gap relative to the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) ideal MIMO channel, and (iii) the globally optimal inter-element spacing when the number of fluid elements at the TX is N = 2 N=2 at the first zero of J 0 J_{0} . Guided by these insights, we propose two algorithms within an alternating optimization (AO) framework. The first algorithm is AO with particle swarm optimization (PSO) which deploys a particle swarm to explore the Bessel landscape globally without gradient information. Then in the second method, we use successive convex approximation (SCA) to obtain the gradient in closed form via J 1 ​ ( ⋅ ) J_{1}(\cdot) to construct convex surrogates for orders-of-magnitude faster convergence. Both algorithms, AO-PSO and AO-SCA, are provably monotone convergent. Simulation results show that the proposed schemes closely approach the i.i.d. capacity upper bound, outperforming fixed-position antenna (FPA) deployments by over 7 7 bps/Hz at high SNR, while AO-SCA achieves identical performance to AO-PSO with a speedup factor exceeding 10 5 10^{5} .

To overcome these difficulties, we first establish a suite of analytical results that unveils the problem’s intrinsic structure. In particular, a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) approximation of the ergodic capacity (Proposition
1
) shows that the objective admits an additive decomposition into log-determinant terms of the TX and RX correlation matrices,
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})
and
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})
, thereby demonstrating a separable structure that is obscured in the original coupled formulation. Next, a closed-form capacity-loss bound (Corollary
1
) characterizes, in bps/Hz, the exact penalty incurred by spatial correlation relative to the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) MIMO benchmark, explicitly linking the performance gap to
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})
. For the special case of
N=2
, we further derive the globally optimal inter-element spacing in closed form as the first positive zero of
J_{0}
(Proposition
2
), yielding a fundamental design rule: placing two antennas at approximately
0.383\lambda
achieves full decorrelation. Collectively, these insights provide engineering intuition and directly motivate the algorithmic decomposition strategy developed next.

Building upon the separability, an alternating-optimization (AO) framework that decomposes the TX-RX position design into a sequence of single-sided subproblems is developed: at each step, one side’s antenna locations are optimized while the other side is held fixed. For each subproblem, we propose two complementary solvers. The first employs particle swarm optimization (PSO), which maintains a diverse population of candidate position vectors to explore the oscillatory Bessel landscape in parallel without gradient information, thereby offering strong robustness to local optima. The second is based on successive convex approximation (SCA): by exploiting a closed-form gradient of
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R})
expressed through the first-order Bessel function
J_{1}(\cdot)
, we construct a concave quadratic surrogate at each iteration and reduce the subproblem to a sequence of efficiently solvable convex programs. Both AO-PSO and AO-SCA incorporate a projection-based feasibility mechanism to enforce finite-aperture and minimum-spacing constraints, and we establish monotone convergence of the resulting objective sequence. Numerically, AO-SCA matches the capacity attained by AO-PSO while achieving orders-of-magnitude runtime savings, yielding an attractive complexity-performance tradeoff for practical deployment.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We formulate the ergodic capacity maximization problem for fluid MIMO with position-dependent Kronecker-structured spatial correlation under Jakes’ model, subject to aperture and minimum spacing constraints.

•

We derive a high-SNR ergodic capacity approximation that decomposes the capacity into a log-determinant function of the TX and RX correlation matrices (Proposition
1
), establish a closed-form capacity loss bound (Corollary
1
), and obtain the optimal antenna spacing in closed form for the
N\!=\!2
case (Proposition
2
).

•

We propose AO-PSO to solve the non-convex problem, and prove its monotone convergence. The algorithm alternately optimizes the TX and RX antenna positions using PSO with a feasibility projection mechanism.

•

We develop AO-SCA that transforms the position optimization into a sequence of convex subproblems. By deriving the gradient of
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})
in terms of the first-order Bessel function
J_{1}(\cdot)
, we obtain a projected gradient ascent algorithm that is orders of magnitude faster than AO-PSO while achieving the same capacity.

•

Our Monte-Carlo simulations validate the analytical results, showing that both AO-PSO and AO-SCA significantly outperform FPA, single-sided FAS optimization, and random placement schemes, closely approaching the i.i.d. MIMO capacity upper bound.

II
System Model

II-A

Antenna Configuration

We consider a point-to-point narrowband fluid MIMO system where both the TX and RX are equipped with FAS. The TX deploys
N
fluid antenna elements in a linear region of length
A\lambda
, where
\lambda
is the carrier wavelength. The antenna positions of the TX along the array axis are collected in the vector
\mathbf{t}=[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
t_{i}\in[0,A\lambda]
for all
i
. Similarly, the RX deploys
M
fluid antenna elements over a linear space of
B\lambda
, with position vector
\mathbf{r}=[r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{M}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
r_{j}\in[0,B\lambda]
for all
j
. Each fluid antenna is connected to a dedicated RF chain, and the positions
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
are jointly optimized to optimize system performance.

II-B

Channel Model

II-B
1
Multipath Propagation

We adopt a geometry-based stochastic channel model with
L
scattering paths. The physical channel matrix from the TX to RX is expressed as

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{C_{0}d^{-\alpha}}{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}g_{\ell}\,\mathbf{b}(\phi_{\ell},\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{a}^{H}(\theta_{\ell},\mathbf{t}),

(1)

in which
C_{0}
is the reference channel power gain at unit distance,
d
is the link distance,
\alpha
is the path-loss exponent,
g_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
are i.i.d. complex path gains, and the transmit and receive field response vectors (FRVs) are

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}(\theta_{\ell},\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle=\bigl[e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}t_{1}\cos\theta_{\ell}},\ldots,e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}t_{N}\cos\theta_{\ell}}\bigr]^{T}\!\in\mathbb{C}^{N},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}(\phi_{\ell},\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\bigl[e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}r_{1}\cos\phi_{\ell}},\ldots,e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}r_{M}\cos\phi_{\ell}}\bigr]^{T}\!\in\mathbb{C}^{M}.

(3)

Here
\theta_{\ell}
and
\phi_{\ell}
denote the angle of departure (AoD) and angle of arrival (AoA) of the
\ell
-th path, respectively.

II-B
2
Spatial Correlation under Jakes Scattering

Under the Jakes (Clarke) isotropic scattering model
[
87
]
, the AoDs
\{\theta_{\ell}\}
and AoAs
\{\phi_{\ell}\}
are independent and uniformly distributed over
[0,\pi]
. Taking expectations over the random angles and the i.i.d. path gains
\{g_{\ell}\}
, the spatial covariance between two TX elements at positions
t_{i}
and
t_{i^{\prime}}
, and two RX elements at positions
r_{j}
and
r_{j^{\prime}}
, is computed as

\mathbb{E}\!\left[H_{j,i}\,H_{j^{\prime},i^{\prime}}^{*}\right]=C_{0}d^{-\alpha}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(t_{i}-t_{i^{\prime}})\cos\theta}\right]}_{[\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})]_{ii^{\prime}}}\\
\times\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(r_{j}-r_{j^{\prime}})\cos\phi}\right]}_{[\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})]_{jj^{\prime}}},

(4)

where
H_{j,i}
denotes the
(j,i)
-th entry of
\mathbf{H}
. Since
\theta\sim\mathcal{U}[0,\pi]
, the expectation evaluates exactly to the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind
[
34
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta\cos\theta}\right]=J_{0}\!\left(\frac{2\pi\Delta}{\lambda}\right),

(5)

yielding position-dependent transmit and receive spatial correlation matrices

\displaystyle[\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})]_{ii^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=J_{0}\!\left(\frac{2\pi|t_{i}-t_{i^{\prime}}|}{\lambda}\right),~i,i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,N,

(6)

\displaystyle[\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=J_{0}\!\left(\frac{2\pi|r_{j}-r_{j^{\prime}}|}{\lambda}\right),~j,j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,M.

(7)

Both
\mathbf{R}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\mathbf{R}_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
are real symmetric positive semi-definite matrices whose entries are determined by the antenna position vectors
\mathbf{t}
and
\mathbf{r}
, respectively. Note that since
J_{0}(\cdot)
is real-valued, the correlation matrices are real despite originating from a complex channel model.

Remark 1
(Properties of the Bessel Correlation Function)

The zeroth-order Bessel function
J_{0}(x)
governs the spatial correlation in (
6
) and (
7
), and exhibits several properties that are central to the position optimization problem:

(i)

Monotone envelope decay:

|J_{0}(x)|
is bounded by
\sqrt{2/(\pi x)}
for
x>0
, implying that the correlation magnitude decreases as the inter-element spacing increases.

(ii)

Oscillatory zeros:

J_{0}(x)
has countably many positive zeros at
x_{1}\approx 2.405
,
x_{2}\approx 5.520
,
x_{3}\approx 8.654
, …, so that zero correlation is achieved at discrete spacings
d_{k}=x_{k}\lambda/(2\pi)
,
k=1,2,\ldots
. The first zero occurs at
d_{1}\approx 0.383\lambda
, which is notably
smaller
than the commonly used half-wavelength spacing.

(iii)

Non-monotonicity:

J_{0}(x)
takes both positive and negative values, so a larger spacing does not always yield lower correlation, making the optimization multi-modal.

(iv)

Positive semi-definiteness:

For any set of positions
\{t_{i}\}
,
\mathbf{R}_{T}
is positive semi-definite since
J_{0}(\cdot)
is the characteristic function of the uniform distribution on
[0,\pi]

[
34
]
.

II-B
3
Eigenvalue Structure of the Correlation Matrix

Let
\mu_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{N}\geq 0
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R}_{T}
; since
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}_{T})=N
, we have
\sum_{k}\mu_{k}=N
. Two extreme cases are of particular interest:

•

Full rank
(
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
): All
\mu_{k}=1
, corresponding to fully independent spatial channels. This is achieved when all pairwise correlations are zero.

•

Rank deficient
(
\mathbf{R}_{T}\to\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}/N
):
\mu_{1}\to N
,
\mu_{k}\to 0
for
k>1
. This occurs when all antennas are collocated, reducing the MIMO system to a single-antenna link.

The condition number
\kappa(\mathbf{R}_{T})=\mu_{1}/\mu_{N}
quantifies the eigenvalue spread:
\kappa(\mathbf{I}_{N})=1
(optimal), while
\kappa\to\infty
for a rank-deficient matrix. Specifically, the goal of position optimization can be viewed as to drive
\kappa
toward unity.

II-B
4
Kronecker Correlated Channel Model

Based on (
1
)–(
7
), the MIMO channel admits the
Kronecker decomposition
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\;=\;\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{R}_{T}^{1/2}(\mathbf{t}),

(8)

where
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
has i.i.d. entries
\mathcal{CN}(0,C_{0}d^{-\alpha})
, representing the de-correlated (virtual) channel coefficients, and
\mathbf{R}_{T}^{1/2}
,
\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}
are the unique Hermitian positive semi-definite square roots of the spatial correlation matrices.

The Kronecker model (
8
)
[
37
]
assumes that the TX and RX scattering environments are statistically independent, which is valid when the arrays are small relative to the link distance. It separates TX and RX correlation, reducing the channel statistics to the pair
(\mathbf{R}_{T},\mathbf{R}_{R})
.

Remark 2
(Correlation as a Position Function)

Unlike FPA systems where
\mathbf{R}_{T}
and
\mathbf{R}_{R}
are fixed, in FAS they are
explicit optimization variables
through
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
. This additional DoF allows the physical layer to directly shape the eigenvalue spectra of the correlation matrices, driving them toward
\mathbf{I}
(maximum spatial DoF) by optimizing inter-antenna spacings.

II-C

Signal Model and Ergodic Capacity

The received signal vector at the RX is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n},

(9)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
denotes the transmitted signal vector satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{H}]=(P/N)\mathbf{I}_{N}
,
P
is the total transmit power, and
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{M})
is additive white Gaussian noise. Assuming Gaussian signaling and CSI known only at the receiver (CSIR), the instantaneous mutual information is

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r};\mathbf{H})=\log_{2}\!\det\!\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{P}{N\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{H}^{H}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\right).

(10)

Substituting (
8
) into (
10
) and taking expectations over the random channel
\mathbf{G}
, the ergodic capacity is

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{G}}\!\left[\log_{2}\!\det\!\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{P}{N\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\,\mathbf{G}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\right)\right].

(11)

Note that
C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
depends on
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
exclusively through the eigenvalue distributions of
\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})
and
\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})
, making the correlation structure the fundamental quantity to be optimized.

Remark 3
(Monte-Carlo Capacity Estimation)

The expectation in (
11
) does not admit a closed-form expression and we estimate it via Monte-Carlo averaging:

\displaystyle\hat{C}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\log_{2}\!\det\!\left(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{P}{N\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{H}_{s}\mathbf{H}_{s}^{H}\right),

(12)

where
\mathbf{H}_{s}=\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}_{s}\mathbf{R}_{T}^{1/2}
and
\{\mathbf{G}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
are i.i.d. samples. We use
S=200
for optimization and
S=1500
for final evaluation. The
same

\{\mathbf{G}_{s}\}
are shared across all position candidates within each AO iteration for fair comparison.

III
Problem Formulation

We seek the antenna position vectors
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
that maximize the ergodic capacity (
11
) subject to practical constraints on the antenna positions. That is,

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{t},\,\mathbf{r}}\quad

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle t_{i}\in[0,\,A\lambda],\quad\forall\,i=1,\ldots,N,

(13b)

\displaystyle r_{j}\in[0,\,B\lambda],\quad\forall\,j=1,\ldots,M,

(13c)

\displaystyle|t_{i}-t_{i^{\prime}}|\geq d_{\min},\quad\forall\,i\neq i^{\prime},

(13d)

\displaystyle|r_{j}-r_{j^{\prime}}|\geq d_{\min},\quad\forall\,j\neq j^{\prime},

(13e)

where (
13b
) (resp. (
13c
)) limits each TX (resp. RX) antenna to the physical aperture of the array. Constraints (
13d
) and (
13e
) enforce a minimum inter-element spacing
d_{\min}
to prevent mutual coupling and ensure mechanical feasibility.

III-A

Feasibility Analysis

Problem (
13
) is feasible if and only if

\displaystyle A\lambda\geq(N-1)d_{\min}~~\text{and}~~B\lambda\geq(M-1)d_{\min}.

(14)

The feasible set for the TX positions can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\triangleq\bigl\{\mathbf{t}\in[0,A\lambda]^{N}:t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N},\;t_{i+1}-t_{i}\geq d_{\min}\bigr\},

(15)

which is a compact polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, guaranteeing the existence of a global maximizer.

III-B

Properties of the Optimization Problem

Remark 4
(Non-Convexity)

Problem (
13
) is non-convex because (i)
J_{0}(\cdot)
is oscillatory, creating multiple local optima, and (ii) the spacing constraints define a non-convex feasible set (a union of disjoint convex regions, one per antenna ordering).

Remark 5
(Capacity Bounds)

The ergodic capacity
C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
is bounded by

\displaystyle C_{\rm FPA}\leq C^{*}\leq C_{\rm iid},

(16)

where
C^{*}=\max_{(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\in\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{R}}C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
is the globally optimal capacity,
C_{\rm FPA}
is the capacity with fixed uniform
d_{\min}
-spaced arrays (a feasible point of (
13
)), and
C_{\rm iid}=\mathbb{E}[\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{I}_{M}+\frac{P}{N\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{H})]
is the i.i.d. MIMO capacity upper bound achieved when
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{R}_{R}=\mathbf{I}
.

Remark 6
(Decoupled TX/RX Structure)

The objective (
11
) depends on
\mathbf{t}
and
\mathbf{r}
only through
\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})
and
\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})
, respectively. At high SNR, the capacity approaches
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[\log_{2}(1+\frac{P}{N\sigma^{2}}\mu_{k}\nu_{k})]
, where
\{\mu_{k}\}
and
\{\nu_{k}\}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R}_{T}
and
\mathbf{R}_{R}
. This separable structure motivates the AO framework to be developed in Section
V
. When
N=M
and
A=B
, symmetry implies
\mathbf{t}^{*}=\mathbf{r}^{*}
.

IV
Performance Analysis

In this section, we derive analytical expressions that characterize the relationship between the antenna positions and the ergodic capacity, providing both design insights and verifiable benchmarks for the proposed optimization algorithm.

IV-A

High-SNR Capacity Approximation

We first present an asymptotic capacity expression valid in the high-SNR regime, which reveals the explicit dependence of the ergodic capacity on the spatial correlation structure.

Proposition 1
(High-SNR Capacity Approximation)

For the fluid MIMO system with
M=N
and
\mathbf{G}
having i.i.d.
\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
entries, as
\gamma\triangleq P/(N\sigma^{2})\to\infty
, the ergodic capacity admits the approximation

C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\approx N\log_{2}\gamma+\log_{2}\!\det\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})\bigr)\\
+\log_{2}\!\det\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})\bigr)+\kappa_{N},

(17)

where the constant
\kappa_{N}
, independent of
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
, is given by

\displaystyle\kappa_{N}=\frac{1}{\ln 2}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\psi(m),

(18)

and
\psi(\cdot)
denotes the digamma function.

Proof:

At high SNR (
\gamma\gg 1
), the identity matrix in (
11
)
becomes negligible, yielding

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log_{2}\!\det\!\left(\gamma\,\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\,\mathbf{G}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\right)\right].

(19)

Since
M=N
, the determinant of the product factors as

\det\!\left(\gamma\,\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\,\mathbf{G}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\right)\\
=\gamma^{N}\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\left|\det(\mathbf{G})\right|^{2}.

(20)

Taking
\log_{2}(\cdot)
and the expectation, the first three terms are deterministic. The remaining term
\mathbb{E}[\log_{2}|\det(\mathbf{G})|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{H})]
involves a complex Wishart matrix
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{H}\sim\mathcal{CW}_{N}(N,\mathbf{I}_{N})
, for which
[
37
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ln\det(\mathbf{W})]=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\psi(m).

(21)

Combining and converting to
\log_{2}
via the factor
1/\ln 2
yields (
17
) and (
18
), which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
1
reveals that at high SNR, the ergodic capacity is determined by two independent terms:
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})
and
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})
. This motivates the following characterization of the capacity penalty incurred by spatial correlation.

IV-B

Capacity Loss Due to Spatial Correlation

Corollary 1
(Capacity Loss)

Define the
correlation-induced capacity loss
as
\Delta C\triangleq C_{\rm iid}-C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
, where
C_{\rm iid}
denotes the i.i.d. MIMO capacity (
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{R}_{R}=\mathbf{I}
). At high SNR,

\displaystyle\Delta C\approx-\log_{2}\!\det\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})\bigr)-\log_{2}\!\det\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})\bigr)\geq 0,

(22)

with equality if and only if
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{R}_{R}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
.

Proof:

For the i.i.d. case,
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})=\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})=1
; hence,
C_{\rm iid}\approx N\log_{2}\gamma+\kappa_{N}
. Subtracting (
17
) gives (
22
). The non-negativity follows from Hadamard’s inequality: for any positive semi-definite
\mathbf{R}
with unit diagonal,
\det(\mathbf{R})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{N}[\mathbf{R}]_{ii}=1
; thus,
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R})\leq 0
. Equality holds if and only if
\mathbf{R}
is diagonal, i.e.,
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}
.
∎

Remark 7
(Determinant Maximization Interpretation)

Corollary
1
establishes that in the high SNR regime, (
13
) is asymptotically equivalent to

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r}}\;\det\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})\bigr)\det\!\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})\bigr),

(23)

subject to the constraints (
13b
)–(
13e
). Since the determinant of a correlation matrix equals the product of its eigenvalues, the optimal positions should spread the eigenvalue spectrum as uniformly as possible, driving
\mathbf{R}_{T}
and
\mathbf{R}_{R}
toward the identity.

IV-C

Optimal Spacing Analysis for
N=2

For the special case
N=2
, we can derive the optimal inter-element spacing in closed form.

Proposition 2
(Optimal Spacing for
N=2
)

Consider
N=M=2
with aperture
A\lambda\geq d_{\min}
. The TX correlation matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{T}=\begin{bmatrix}1&J_{0}\!\left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\right)\\[3.0pt]
J_{0}\!\left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\right)&1\end{bmatrix},

(24)

where
d=|t_{1}-t_{2}|
is the antenna spacing. Its determinant is

\displaystyle\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})=1-J_{0}^{2}\!\left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\right).

(25)

The determinant is maximized (i.e.,
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})=1
and
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
) at the optimal spacing

\displaystyle d^{*}=\frac{x_{1}\lambda}{2\pi}\approx 0.383\lambda,

(26)

where
x_{1}\approx 2.4048
is the first positive zero of
J_{0}(x)
.

Proof:

We have
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})=1-\rho^{2}(d)
where
\rho(d)=J_{0}(2\pi d/\lambda)
. Since
|\rho(d)|\leq 1
,
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\leq 1
with equality if and only if
\rho(d)=0
, i.e.,
J_{0}(2\pi d/\lambda)=0
. The first positive zero of
J_{0}(x)
occurs at
x_{1}\approx 2.4048
, giving
d^{*}=x_{1}\lambda/(2\pi)\approx 0.383\lambda
. At this spacing,
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
, achieving full decorrelation and eliminating the capacity loss.
∎

Remark 8
(Beyond
N=2
)

For
N>2
, achieving
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
requires
J_{0}(2\pi|t_{i}-t_{i^{\prime}}|/\lambda)=0
for all
i\neq i^{\prime}
. With uniform spacing
d
, the pairwise distances are
d,2d,3d,\ldots,(N-1)d
. While
d^{*}=0.383\lambda
zeroes out
J_{0}
for distance
d
, the Bessel function
J_{0}(2\pi\cdot 2d^{*}/\lambda)=J_{0}(2x_{1})\approx-0.178\neq 0
. Therefore, uniform spacing at
d^{*}
does not achieve
\mathbf{R}_{T}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
. This fundamental limitation motivates the AO-PSO to be developed in the next section, which can optimize non-uniform antenna spacings to maximize
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})
.

IV-D

Low-SNR Regime Analysis

The high-SNR approximation in Proposition
1
becomes inaccurate when
\gamma=P/(N\sigma^{2})
is small. In the low-SNR regime, the capacity exhibits fundamentally different behavior with respect to the antenna positions.

Proposition 3
(Low-SNR Capacity Approximation)

At low SNR (
\gamma\to 0
), the ergodic capacity satisfies

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\approx\frac{\gamma}{\ln 2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})\bigr)\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{R}(\mathbf{r})\bigr)=\frac{NM\gamma}{\ln 2},

(27)

which is
independent
of the antenna positions.

Proof:

Using the first-order Taylor expansion
\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{A})\approx\frac{\gamma}{\ln 2}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})
for
\gamma\to 0
,
and substituting into (
11
) gives

C\approx\frac{\gamma}{\ln 2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\,\mathbf{G}^{H}\mathbf{R}_{R}^{1/2}\bigr)\bigr]\\
=\frac{\gamma}{\ln 2}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{R}\bigr)\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{T}\bigr),

(28)

where the last equality follows from
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{R}_{T}\,\mathbf{G}^{H}]=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}_{T})\mathbf{I}_{M}
for i.i.d.
\mathbf{G}
. Since
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}_{T})=N
and
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}_{R})=M
regardless of the antenna positions, the result follows.
∎

Remark 9
(SNR-Dependent Benefit of FAS)

Proposition
3
reveals a fundamental dichotomy: at low SNR, the capacity is determined solely by the total received power (
NM\gamma
) and is insensitive to the spatial correlation structure, so all placement schemes perform similarly. At high SNR, the multiplexing gain dominates and depends on
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\cdot\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})
(Proposition
1
). Thus, FAS position optimization provides the greatest benefit in the moderate-to-high SNR regime, where the correlation-induced capacity loss
\Delta C
in (
22
) is most significant.

IV-E

Capacity Scaling with Aperture Size

Proposition 4
(Minimum Aperture for Full Decorrelation (
N=2
))

For
N=2
, the minimum aperture for full decorrelation is
A_{\min}\lambda=d^{*}\approx 0.383\lambda
. For
N>2
, full decorrelation is generally unattainable, but
\Delta C
decreases monotonically with increasing aperture since
\mathcal{T}(A_{1})\supset\mathcal{T}(A_{0})
for
A_{1}>A_{0}
, and
|J_{0}(x)|\leq\sqrt{2/(\pi x)}
ensures
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\to 1
as
A\to\infty
. In practice,
A=2\lambda
–
3\lambda
suffices for moderate
N
.

V-C

Convergence

Proposition 5
(Monotone Convergence)

The sequence
\{C(\mathbf{t}^{(k)},\mathbf{r}^{(k)})\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by Algorithm

is monotonically non-decreasing and bounded above by the i.i.d. MIMO capacity
C_{\rm iid}

C^{\infty}\leq C_{\rm iid}
.

Proof:

Consider the TX update step at iteration

. Since the current iterate
\mathbf{t}^{(k)}
is included in the PSO swarm (as a warm-started particle), the PSO global best satisfies

\displaystyle C\bigl(\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)},\mathbf{r}^{(k)}\bigr)\geq C\bigl(\mathbf{t}^{(k)},\mathbf{r}^{(k)}\bigr).

(39)

By the same argument for the RX update:

\displaystyle C\bigl(\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)},\mathbf{r}^{(k+1)}\bigr)\geq C\bigl(\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)},\mathbf{r}^{(k)}\bigr).

(40)

Combining the results, we have
C^{(k+1)}\geq C^{(k)}
. The upper bound follows from Remark
5
:
C(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})\leq C_{\rm iid}
for any
(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{r})
. Since
\{C^{(k)}\}
is non-decreasing and bounded above, it converges by the monotone convergence theorem.
∎

Remark 10
(Convergence to Local Optimum)

Proposition
5
guarantees convergence of the objective sequence, but not to a

optimum. The AO framework converges to a coordinate-wise stationary point. To mitigate the risk of poor local optima, we employ (i) diversified PSO swarm initialization and (ii) warm-starting one particle from the current AO iterate. In practice, near-identical solutions are obtained across different initializations (Section
VII
), suggesting a favorable landscape with few significant local optima.

VI
SCA-Based Alternative

AO-PSO entails significant computational overhead from Monte-Carlo fitness evaluations. In this section, we develop an alternative SCA approach that exploits the high-SNR structure to construct a sequence of tractable convex subproblems.

VI-A

High-SNR Equivalent Reformulation

From Proposition
1
and the determinant maximization interpretation in (
23
), at high SNR, (
13
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{t},\,\mathbf{r}}f(\mathbf{t})+f(\mathbf{r})~~\mathrm{s.t.}~~\mathbf{t}\in\mathcal{T},\;\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{R},

(42a)

where
f(\mathbf{t})\triangleq\log_{2}\!\det(\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t}))
. The objective is separable in
\mathbf{t}
and
\mathbf{r}
, so each can be optimized independently. However,
f(\mathbf{t})
is
non-convex
because the mapping
\mathbf{t}\mapsto\mathbf{R}_{T}(\mathbf{t})
is nonlinear through the oscillatory
J_{0}(\cdot)
.

VI-B

Closed-Form Gradient via Bessel Identities

We derive the gradient of
f(\mathbf{t})
with respect to each position
t_{n}
. By the chain rule for matrix functions,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t_{n}}=\frac{1}{\ln 2}\,\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\mathbf{R}_{T}^{-1}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}_{T}}{\partial t_{n}}\right).

(43)

Using the Bessel derivative identity
J_{0}^{\prime}(x)=-J_{1}(x)
, the off-diagonal Jacobian entries can be found as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial[\mathbf{R}_{T}]_{nj}}{\partial t_{n}}=-\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,J_{1}\!\left(\frac{2\pi|t_{n}-t_{j}|}{\lambda}\right)\operatorname{sgn}(t_{n}-t_{j}),

(44)

for
j\neq n
, with
\partial[\mathbf{R}_{T}]_{nn}/\partial t_{n}=0
. The full gradient component is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t_{n}}=-\frac{4\pi}{\lambda\ln 2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq n\end{subarray}}^{N}[\mathbf{R}_{T}^{-1}]_{nj}\,J_{1}\!\left(\frac{2\pi|t_{n}\!-\!t_{j}|}{\lambda}\right)\operatorname{sgn}(t_{n}\!-\!t_{j}).

(45)

Computing
\nabla_{\mathbf{t}}f\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
requires one
N\times N
matrix inversion and
N(N-1)/2
evaluations of
J_{1}(\cdot)
, costing
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
—with
no Monte-Carlo sampling
.

Remark 11
(Gradient Interpretation)

The term
[\mathbf{R}_{T}^{-1}]_{nj}
captures the sensitivity of
\log\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})
to the
(n,j)
-th correlation entry. Since
J_{1}(x)>0
for small
x
, the gradient pushes closely spaced antennas apart to reduce correlation. At larger spacings, it fine-tunes positions to hit the zeros of
J_{0}
.

VI-C

Projected Gradient Ascent and SCA Framework

SCA constructs a first-order surrogate at iterate
\mathbf{t}^{(k)}
:

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\mathbf{t};\mathbf{t}^{(k)})=f(\mathbf{t}^{(k)})+\nabla f(\mathbf{t}^{(k)})^{T}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{t}^{(k)}),

(46)

which is affine (hence convex) in
\mathbf{t}
. Rather than solving the resulting linear programming on
\mathcal{T}
(which yields extreme-point solutions and may oscillate), we use a
proximal projected gradient ascent
step:

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}\!\left(\mathbf{t}^{(k)}+\eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{t}^{(k)})\right),

(47)

where
\eta>0
is the step size and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
is the projection onto
\mathcal{T}
(bound clipping + spacing enforcement, identical to (
37
) and (
38
)). A backtracking line search on
\eta
ensures
f(\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)})\geq f(\mathbf{t}^{(k)})
. AO-SCA (Algorithm
3
) alternates between TX and RX updates, each solved by
I_{\rm SCA}
projected gradient ascent steps on the log-determinant objective.

VI-D

Convergence and Complexity Comparison

Proposition 6
(AO-SCA Convergence)

With backtracking line search, the AO-SCA algorithm generates a monotonically non-decreasing sequence
\{f(\mathbf{t}^{(k)})+f(\mathbf{r}^{(k)})\}
that converges to a coordinate-wise stationary point of (
42
).

Proof:

Each projected gradient step with backtracking guarantees
f(\mathbf{t}^{(\ell+1)})\geq f(\mathbf{t}^{(\ell)})
. Over the
I_{\rm SCA}
inner iterations,
f(\mathbf{t}^{(k+1)})\geq f(\mathbf{t}^{(k)})
. The same holds for the RX update. Since
f(\mathbf{t})\leq 0
for any
\mathbf{t}
(Hadamard’s inequality), the sequence is bounded above and hence converges.
∎

The per-iteration complexity of AO-SCA is estimated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(I_{\rm SCA}\cdot N^{3}),

(48)

which is
independent of the Monte-Carlo sample size
S
. In contrast, AO-PSO costs
\mathcal{O}(I_{\rm PSO}\cdot Z\cdot S\cdot N^{3})
per AO step. For typical parameters (
I_{\rm PSO}=60
,
Z=20
,
S=200
), AO-SCA is approximately
2.4\times 10^{5}
times faster per AO iteration.

Remark 12
(SCA vs. PSO Trade-off)

AO-SCA is a
local
optimizer: it converges to the nearest stationary point from the given initialization. AO-PSO is a
global
optimizer: the stochastic swarm explores the search space broadly and is less sensitive to initialization. Moreover, AO-SCA optimizes the
high-SNR surrogate

\log_{2}\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})
rather than the exact ergodic capacity, so its solution may be suboptimal at moderate SNR. In practice, as will be shown in Section
VII
, AO-SCA achieves near-identical capacity to AO-PSO at high SNR while being orders of magnitude faster, but AO-PSO is more robust at moderate SNR and with poor initialization.

VIII
Conclusion

This paper studied spatial-correlation optimization for fluid MIMO systems, where both the TX and RX adopt multiple fluid antennas over a linear aperture. Under Jakes’ model and Kronecker channel decomposition, we derived the position-dependent correlation matrices and formulated an ergodic-capacity maximization problem. Our analysis yielded a high-SNR approximation that makes the role of
\det(\mathbf{R}_{T})\det(\mathbf{R}_{R})
explicit, a closed-form capacity-loss bound, and a globally optimal spacing rule for the two-antenna case. For the general case, we developed two solvers: AO-PSO for global exploration and AO-SCA, which leverages the gradient via
J_{0}^{\prime}(x)=-J_{1}(x)
to solve a sequence of convex surrogates. Simulation results showed sizable gains over FPA arrays and optimized fluid MIMO achieves near-i.i.d. capacity performance.
