Final Technical Report

 The Non-Equilibrium Quantum Frontier

Principal Investigator: Vedika Khemani
Institution: Stanford University
Location: Stanford, CA
Award Number: DE-SC0021111
DOE Program Office: Office of Basic Energy Sciences
Research Area: Theoretical Condensed Matter Physics
Award Period: September 1, 2020 – August 31, 2025

Abstract

Modern quantum science is entering a regime in which many particles can be controlled, driven,
and measured while remaining quantum coherent. This creates a fundamental opportunity: we
can now ask how the basic laws of statistical mechanics and phases of matter are modified when
many-body quantum systems are far from thermal equilibrium. These questions are central to
fundamental science because they probe how ordinary thermodynamic behavior emerges from
microscopic quantum laws, and when it can fail. They are also important for discovery because
non-equilibrium systems can host forms of order and information-processing behavior that are
impossible in equilibrium.
This DOE Early Career award supported a theoretical research program on this “non-equilibrium
quantum frontier.” The work studied how isolated quantum many-body systems thermalize, how
they can avoid thermalization, how quantum information spreads or is protected, and what new
nonequilibrium phases of matter can arise in driven, measured, and programmable quantum systems.
The project was motivated both by foundational questions in many-body physics and quantum
information, and by rapid experimental progress in quantum simulators and quantum processors,
which now make it possible to test theoretical ideas in controllable laboratory platforms.
Over the lifetime of the award, the project advanced a broad program in non-equilibrium quantum
many-body physics. The work clarified how isolated quantum systems thermalize, identified
mechanisms by which thermalization can fail or be anomalously slow, developed new diagnostics of
quantum chaos and transport, and explored new nonequilibrium phases made possible by driving,
measurements, and feedback. A unifying thread was the development of new information-theoretic
formulations, using concepts such as entanglement, learnability, scrambling, and error correction, to
diagnose and organize new forms of quantum many-body behavior.

Major Goals of the Project

The project was organized around three major goals.

  1. Understanding when and how quantum systems thermalize.
 A central goal of statistical physics is to explain how macroscopic thermal laws emerge
 from the microscopic dynamics of many interacting particles. For isolated quantum systems,
 this question is especially sharp: microscopic time evolution is reversible and preserves
 quantum information, while thermalization appears to erase memory of microscopic details.
 Understanding this apparent tension is essential for determining when equilibrium statistical
 mechanics applies, and when non-equilibrium forms of order, memory, or slow dynamics can
 persist. A central goal of this project was to new theoretical frameworks for understanding
 how many-body quantum systems thermalize, and for identifying regimes in which this process
 fails, is incomplete, or occurs only on anomalously long time scales.

  2. Developing quantum information theoretic diagnostics for many-body dynamics.
 A major development in modern many-body physics is the emergence of quantum information as
 a conceptual framework for studying complex quantum matter. Questions about thermalization,
 measurement, and robustness can be reformulated in terms of how quantum information is

 distributed, scrambled, revealed, or protected. A central of this project was to develop such
 information-theoretic formulations of quantum dynamics, using tools such as random circuits,
 entanglement, classical shadows, learnability, and error correction to diagnose and organize
 new forms of quantum many-body behavior.

  3. Discovering new phases and universal phenomena outside equilibrium.
 Equilibrium thermodynamics strongly constrains what phases of matter can exist. Once
 systems are driven, measured, or actively controlled, new possibilities open up, including time
 crystals and measurement-induced phases. A key goal of this project was to identify novel
 intrinsically non-equilibrium phenomena, understand their universal properties, and connect
 them to experimentally accessible platforms.

Major Accomplishments and Scientific Highlights

Over the award period, work acknowledging DE-SC0021111 led to 33 publications , including 31
published papers and 2 arXiv preprints (under review). The published output includes 6 papers in
PRL, 3 in PRX, 4 in PRX Quantum, 1 in Nature 1 in Science, and 2 invited reviews in
Annual Review of Condensed Matter Physics. The publications span quantum thermalization,
many-body localization, quantum scars, monitored dynamics, Floquet phases, quantum simulation,
classical shadows, and quantum error correction [1]–[33].
We now discuss some highlights of the research conducted. Reference numbers refer to the cumulative
publication list at the end of this report.

1. New nonequilibrium phases and phase transitions in monitored quantum
dynamics

A major achievement of the award was the development of new theoretical frameworks for quantum
systems whose dynamics combine unitary evolution with controlled measurements. Such “monitored”
systems are natural in present-day quantum devices, where quantum evolution, measurement, and
feedback are all experimentally accessible. Before this work, measurement-induced phases were
largely understood through a competition between scrambling unitary dynamics, which spreads
and hides quantum information, and measurements, which reveal information and tend to destroy
entanglement. The project showed that measurements alone can generate sharp entanglement phase
transitions, establishing measurement-only dynamics as a new setting for nonequilibrium many-body
physics [3]. This was important because it showed that the structure of the measurement ensemble
itself can generate rich dynamics: measurements enriched by symmetry, topology, or frustration can
produce new phases, critical points, and dynamical manipulation of quantum information. This
perspective later fed into broader developments on error correction, such as dynamically generated
qubits, and Floquet-codes.
The project also developed the idea of spacetime-duality for non-unitary circuits. Measurement-
induced phenomena are difficult to observe experimentally because measurements produce random
outcomes, and reproducing the same quantum trajectory normally requires costly postselection.
Spacetime duality provided a way to reduce this overhead and gave a new analytic handle on
non-unitary dynamics [4], [10]. These theoretical developments led to concrete protocols and diag-

nostics for observing measurement-induced entanglement phenomena on noisy quantum hardware,
culminating in a Google Quantum AI experiment that used hybrid quantum-classical methods to
probe measurement-induced entanglement and teleportation on up to 70 qubits [19], representing
one of the largest experimental demonstrations to date of measurement-induced many-body physics
on a quantum processor.
Related work introduced learnability transitions in monitored quantum dynamics, where a measure-
ment record undergoes a sharp transition in how much it reveals about an initial quantum state
[24]. Other work studied absorbing-state transitions in interactive dynamics with feedback [20]
and dynamically generated qubits in measurement-only models [16]. Together, these results helped
establish monitored quantum dynamics as a rich new arena connecting non-equilibrium many-body
physics, quantum information, and near-term quantum processors.

2. Information-theoretic diagnostics of quantum dynamics

The project advanced the understanding of how isolated quantum systems approach thermal
equilibrium, and developed sharp new diagnostics of quantum chaos.
One line of work significantly revised a standard benchmark for quantum chaos in many-body
systems. For decades, random matrix theory and the Page entropy of Haar-random states have
served as canonical reference points for chaotic quantum eigenstates. This work showed that those
benchmarks are incomplete for local Hamiltonians, because physical eigenstates retain structure
imposed by locality, energy conservation, and other constraints, even when conventional diagnostics
such as level statistics look fully chaotic. The project introduced a quantitative information theoretic
metric based on the full microcanonical distribution of eigenstate entanglement, not just its average,
and used it to compare physical eigenstates to appropriately constrained random ensembles [27]. This
provided a more sensitive formulation of many-body quantum chaos and revealed small “maximally
chaotic” pockets in parameter space where deviations from the constrained random benchmark are
minimized. A later related work extended this framework away from infinite temperature, showing
how finite-temperature eigenstates of chaotic local Hamiltonians can also be understood through
constrained random-state ensembles [32].
A second line of work used learnability as a bridge between quantum information and many-body
dynamics. First, we used insights from many-body thermalization to address a practical problem in
quantum information: how many measurements are needed to learn observables of a quantum state.
Classical shadows provide a powerful framework for this task, and the sample complexity is controlled
by a quantity called the shadow norm. We showed that this shadow norm is governed by a familiar
process from quantum dynamics: the Heisenberg evolution and relaxation of operators under the
randomizing circuit [18]. This led to much sharper bounds on the performance of shallow shadows,
including an exponential improvement over purely local randomized measurements for learning
spatially contiguous Pauli observables at an optimal circuit depth. In a complementary direction,
we used classical-shadow ideas to formulate a novel information-theoretic learnability diagnostic for
measurement-induced phase transitions, showing that these transitions can be understood in terms
of what an observer can infer about the initial quantum state from the measurement record [24].
A third line of work developed new diagnostics of transport motivated by the capabilities of
modern quantum simulators. Traditional transport measurements often focus on coarse-grained
quantities such as the average current or diffusion constant. However, quantum gas microscopes and
superconducting-qubit arrays can now access much richer data, including site-resolved snapshots of

 3

the entire many-body state. We showed how this additional information can be organized through
full counting statistics, namely the full probability distribution of charge or magnetization transferred
across a system [21]. This revealed that systems with the same average diffusive transport can
nevertheless belong to distinct dynamical universality classes, distinguished by higher moments and
non-Gaussian fluctuations invisible to conventional hydrodynamic probes. Building on this insight,
we proposed a quantum-turnstile protocol that uses an ancilla as a counter for the time-integrated
current, providing a scalable and robust route to measuring full counting statistics in experiments
and tensor-network simulations [26]. These ideas were realized in a Google Quantum AI experiment
on a 46-qubit superconducting spin chain, which measured the full distribution of magnetization
transferred across the system [23]. The experiment found superdiffusive behavior in the first two
moments, consistent with the long-standing KPZ interpretation of Heisenberg spin transport, but
the third and fourth moments ruled out the standard KPZ prediction. This provided a striking
demonstration that quantum simulators can access higher-order information that is invisible to
conventional transport probes and can qualitatively revise conclusions about dynamical universality.

3. Floquet dynamics, time crystals, and long-lived quantum scars

A central theme of the award was that periodic driving can be used not merely as a control tool,
but as a route to new forms of quantum matter. Floquet systems are intrinsically out of equilibrium
and can realize phases, such as discrete time crystals, that are forbidden in thermal equilibrium.
The project developed this perspective in the setting of near-term quantum processors, providing a
blueprint for programming a discrete time crystal on a digital quantum simulator [6].
The award also advanced the theory of quantum many-body scars, which are atypical nonthermal
eigenstates embedded in otherwise thermalizing spectra. Scars are important because they sharpen
the question of how thermalization can be incomplete: most states behave thermally, but special
initial states can retain coherent memory for anomalously long times. The project developed
algebraic frameworks for scarred dynamics using Lie algebras, q-deformed Lie algebras, and tensor
operators [1], [7]; clarified the structure and entanglement dynamics of scarred Hamiltonians [31];
and helped synthesize the field in a broad review [13].
A key outcome was the discovery that Floquet engineering can protect much more than expected.
We showed that eigenstates of Floquet-engineered Hamiltonians can retain exponentially long-
lived fidelities over a broad range of drive frequencies, despite generic expectations of heating and
hybridization [25]. This result extended Floquet prethermalization from a statement about slow
heating to a stronger statement about the stability of individual many-body eigenstates, with direct
implications for engineering scarred and other structured Hamiltonians.

4. Many-body localization, resonances, and slow thermalization

The project advanced the understanding of many-body localization, a regime in which interacting
quantum systems can fail to thermalize. Many-body localization is important because it provides
a robust exception to conventional statistical mechanics and a possible mechanism for protecting
quantum memory in isolated systems. Work supported by the award studied many-body resonances
and avalanche instabilities, which are central to understanding how localized systems eventually
lose stability and thermalize [8].

 4

The project showed that finite systems can be dominated by broadly distributed many-body
resonances, while rare thermal “avalanches” are expected to control asymptotic stability. This
helped refine the distinction between truly localized regimes and regimes that are very slowly
thermalizing on experimentally and numerically relevant time scales [8], [17]. Additional work
studied many-body localization transitions with correlated disorder [11], prethermal many-body
localized regimes [17], slow thermalization in charge-conserving Floquet random circuits [22], and
thermalization in mesoscopic Floquet systems [15]. These results emphasized the importance of
finite-time and finite-size regimes in studies of thermalization, which are precisely the regimes most
accessible to current experiments and numerical simulations.

5. New connections between quantum error correction, LDPC codes, and phases
of matter

In the later years of the award, the project opened a new direction connecting many-body physics
with quantum error correction, especially low-density parity-check and quantum LDPC codes. A
central challenge in quantum computing is to build ‘good” quantum memories, meaning codes
that can store a finite density of logical qubits while protecting them with a distance that grows
extensively with system size. Conventional spatially local topological codes, such as the toric
code, have a close connection with topological phases of matter: their ability to protect quantum
information is tied to physical notions such as locality, excitations, topological order, and phase
stability. However, such local codes are not ‘good” in the coding-theoretic sense. In fixed spatial
dimension, locality imposes strong tradeoffs between rate, distance, and locality.
Good LDPC codes evade these limitations by keeping the checks sparse while giving up ordinary
spatial locality. Their connectivity is intrinsically nonlocal, often organized by expander-like
graphs or other non-Euclidean geometries. This creates a conceptual challenge: the best modern
candidates for efficient quantum memories do not fit naturally into the standard condensed-matter
framework of phases protected by locality in Euclidean space. The project asked how the code–phase
correspondence should be reformulated for these sparse nonlocal systems, and how ideas from gauge
theory, duality, and phases of matter can be extended beyond spatially local Hamiltonians.
This question is increasingly timely because new quantum simulation platforms, including pro-
grammable atomic, molecular, and optical systems, are beginning to realize flexible and long-range
patterns of connectivity. These platforms make it possible to ask many-body questions beyond
conventional Euclidean geometry, while quantum LDPC codes provide a theoretically rich and
technologically important class of examples.
The project developed a physical theory of good LDPC codes using ideas from gauging, duality,
and phases of matter on expander-like interaction graphs [29]. This work recast modern code
constructions in a language closer to condensed matter physics and clarified how robust quantum
information can be viewed as a form of quantum order in sparse, nonlocal many-body systems. A
related line of work connected perturbative stability of quantum phases to error-correction thresholds
of quantum codes, linking two notions of robustness that are often studied separately: stability of
phases under perturbations and correctability of errors in quantum information [30]. Together, these
works provided new formulations for understanding quantum memories and quantum LDPC codes
using the tools of many-body physics, while also expanding the kinds of geometries and notions of
locality that can be considered in condensed matter theory.

Impact

This award supported a broad program at the non-equilibrium quantum frontier. The project
developed new frameworks for understanding how quantum many-body systems thermalize, fail
to thermalize, respond to driving and measurement, and protect quantum information. A central
impact was to help establish non-equilibrium many-body physics as a setting where new forms of
order, dynamics, and information processing can be sharply formulated and experimentally tested.
One major contribution was the development of new information-theoretic diagnostics for complex
quantum dynamics. These results show how quantum information theory can provide organizing
principles for many-body physics, and help formulate sharper questions about phases and dynamics.
The work on monitored dynamics reformulated measurement-induced phase transitions in terms
of measurement-only dynamics, dynamically generated quantum information, and learnability
from measurement records [3], [16], [24]. The work on full counting statistics showed how site-
resolved snapshots can reveal higher moments and non-Gaussian transport fluctuations invisible
to conventional probes such as diffusion constants [21], [26], [23]. The work on classical shadows
used thermalization and operator relaxation to obtain sharper bounds on the sample complexity
of learning observables from shallow randomized measurements [18]. Together, these examples
show how information-theoretic viewpoints can turn broad questions about quantum dynamics into
precise probes of novel and complex many-body behavior.
The project also shaped the interpretation and design of quantum simulation experiments. A
key example is the discrete time crystal, one of the first striking examples of an intrinsically
non-equilibrium quantum phase of matter. The project helped translate this conceptual idea into
a concrete blueprint for programming a discrete time crystal on a digital quantum processor [6].
Work on spacetime duality and monitored dynamics shaped protocols and diagnostics for observing
measurement-induced entanglement on noisy hardware, culminating in a Google Quantum AI
experiment probing measurement-induced entanglement and teleportation on up to 70 supercon-
ducting qubits [4], [10], [19]. Work on full counting statistics also connected directly to a Google
Quantum AI experiment measuring the full distribution of magnetization transfer, showing that
higher moments can revise conclusions drawn from conventional transport observables [21], [26],
[23]. These examples point to a broader future direction: using programmable quantum devices not
only as computational machines, but as laboratories for discovering and testing new principles of
non-equilibrium many-body physics.
A further impact was the opening of new connections between many-body physics and quantum
error correction. The project formulated good LDPC codes using ideas from gauging, duality, and
phases of matter on sparse nonlocal geometries [29]. This direction is likely to grow in importance
as quantum information science seeks physical principles for robust quantum memories, and as new
platforms make sparse, long-range, and non-Euclidean connectivity increasingly natural settings for
condensed matter physics.
The award also contributed to workforce development in quantum science and engineering. It
supported graduate students working at the interface of condensed matter physics, statistical
mechanics, and quantum information, and produced a substantial body of publications acknowledging
DE-SC0021111. Its long-term impact will extend through these trained researchers, the theoretical
tools developed under the award, and the research directions it opened across non-equilibrium
dynamics, quantum simulation, and quantum information.

Training and Professional Development

The award partially supported and trained graduate students working in quantum many-body
physics and quantum information science. Graduate students supported or partially supported
during the award period included Cheryne Jonay, Nicholas O’Dea, Adithya Sriram, and Kyung-Su
Kim. Jonay, O’Dea and Kim graduated with PhDs and moved on to postdoctoral positions, and
Sriram will be graduating this year.
The research program also involved substantial collaboration with Stanford postdoctoral researchers,
including Matteo Ippoliti, Tibor Rakovszky, Joaquin Rodriguez-Nieva, and Alan Morningstar. Most
of these collaborators subsequently moved into faculty positions.

Dissemination

Results from this award were disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, arXiv preprints,
invited talks, conference presentations, seminars, colloquia, and talks by graduate students. The
work reached communities spanning condensed matter physics, quantum information, statistical
physics, and quantum simulation.

The main products of the award were peer-reviewed publications, preprints, talks, and trained
