EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Feral cattle (Bos taurus) have been documented along the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA)–owned section of the Rio Grande Corridor of White Rock
Canyon (WRC) in Los Alamos County, New Mexico, since the 1970s (NPS 1994). Feral cattle activity is
known to be especially impactful to riparian ecosystems, which are hubs for biodiversity and provide
resources for multiple endangered species including the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher (Empidonax
traillii extimus) and the Yellow-billed Cuckoo (Coccyzus americanus) within Los Alamos National
Laboratory (LANL) property (Poessel et al. 2020, Sanchez 2021).

To gain a preliminary understanding of the status of riparian ecosystems within WRC and the impacts of
feral cattle as of winter 2025, a team within the Triad National Security, LLC, Environmental Protection
and Compliance Division’s Environmental Stewardship Group (EPC-ES) at LANL conducted a month-
long vegetation-monitoring project in September 2025. Riparian vegetation-monitoring protocols and
rapid assessments were used to quantify the intensity and extent of feral cattle impacts on riparian
ecosystems. The data collected are a baseline for comparison following the proposed managed removal of
feral cattle from the canyon.

Within the riparian ecosystem, LANL ecologists noted indicators of current and heavy utilization by feral
cattle, including multiple wallowing areas where soils were completely denuded of vegetation and highly
compacted. Based on the severity and extent of impacts observed in these areas, cattle use appears
consistent and ongoing over multiple years, supporting the need for removal and managed restoration.

 Introduction
Land and natural resources within the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) complex are owned and
managed by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA).
The Laboratory’s eastern boundary is in White Rock Canyon (WRC) along the Rio Grande within
Technical Areas (TAs) 70 and 33, with approximately 4 miles of property along the river (Figure 1). In
1999, the riparian corridor from the mesa tops down to the river was designated as the White Rock
Canyon Reserve by former New Mexico Governor Bill Richardson and is recreationally open to the
public (DOE 1999). This area provides viable foraging and stopover habitat for many species of wildlife,
including the Yellow-billed Cuckoo and the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher, both of which are federally
listed under the Endangered Species Act (ESA).

Riparian areas are some of the most biologically abundant and diverse ecosystems and provide extensive
ecosystem services such as water quality and quantity. They house a wide variety of plant and animal
species and serve as a connection among habitats, especially for migratory species (Poessel et al. 2020).
Unmanaged, long-term cattle grazing has been linked to alterations in landscape composition, loss of
native species, increase in non-native invasive species, soil erosion, and water contamination (Fleischner
2013). These effects ultimately result in habitat loss and unnatural ecosystem succession. Cattle activity
also interferes with natural hydrological processes through degradation of streambanks, alteration of
channel flow, and increased turbidity. Biological contaminants from fecal matter and carcasses also
contribute to water pollution in riparian systems (Belsky 1999).

Section 7(a)(1) of the ESA establishes an obligation for all federal agencies (including DOE/NNSA) to
use their authorities in furtherance of the purposes of the ESA by carrying out programs for the
conservation (i.e., recovery) of listed species. The Rio Grande corridor on DOE property contains areas
with potential habitat for two ESA-listed species: the Yellow-billed Cuckoo and the Southwestern Willow
Flycatcher. Evidence indicates that excessive livestock grazing is detrimental to these riparian obligate
species’ habitats (Kauffman and Krueger 1984, USFWS 2002). Both species need dense, multilayered
woodlands with plenty of foliage for nesting, especially riparian areas with cottonwoods and willows,
alongside nearby water sources to support their need for cover, humidity, and food (Johnson et al. 2016,
Hatten and Paradzick 2003, Wohner et al. 2020, Stanek et al. 2021). These key attributes of Yellow-billed
Cuckoo and Southwestern Willow Flycatcher habitat are among riparian characteristics most affected by
livestock grazing (USFWS 2002). These habitat features in WRC have been so severely degraded by the
presence of cattle that the area is much less suitable for breeding populations of Yellow-billed cuckoos
and Southwestern Willow Flycatchers. Riparian habitats are rehabilitated most effectively and/or
completely through cattle removal (Ohmart 1996), and complete removal of cattle corresponds to
increased abundance of migratory birds (Poessel et al. 2020).

In addition, feral cattle can be unpredictable and aggressive, creating a safety hazard for LANL workers
and the public. Los Alamos County has reported aggressive cattle on public trails throughout the county,
resulting in multiple trail closures (Clark 2021, O’Neill 2021). Cars have also struck feral cattle that have
wandered onto local highways (Clark 2021).

In the last decade, concern has increased for resource protection and human safety within WRC from feral
cattle. Bandelier National Monument (BNM) managers were also concerned about impacts and voiced a
desire to construct fencing or barriers to restrict cattle from entering the monument along the DOE

property line. After meetings among DOE, area stakeholders, and New Mexico Department of
Agriculture livestock inspectors, LANL biologists initiated surveillance with motion-activated wildlife
cameras to quantify the number of cattle in the area and to determine if the numbers fluctuated seasonally.
Since these discussions began in 2018, BNM has repaired the existing fences in an effort to keep the
cattle on DOE property, but conversations in November 2025 confirm that the fences require annual
maintenance and are not currently functional for keeping cattle off of BNM (Personal Communications).

LANL biologists published a report in 2021 that documented feral cattle activity in WRC based on data
from game cameras deployed from 2018 to 2019. They found that the greatest amount of cattle activity
occurred along the northern portion of the TA-70 boundary. Based on camera data, researchers estimated
a population of approximately 20 feral cattle in WRC at that time, likely the maximum carrying capacity
for the overgrazed and degraded environment (Sanchez 2021). Before this research, a 2013 study
documented a maximum herd size of three cattle (Hathcock 2013). In 2023, the Triad National Security,
LLC (Triad), EPC-ES team was directed by NNSA to develop an implementation plan for the removal of
feral cattle on DOE property (Stanek et al. 2024). The plan (LA-CP-23-20452) was finalized in 2023.
Further NNSA interest in chronicling the effects of feral cattle presence arose once more in 2025 by the
Feral Cattle Working Group, resulting in this data collection effort.

 Methods
Team members collected data during five different field days throughout September 2025. The main area
of interest for the study was the corridor of WRC within the boundaries of TA-33 and TA-70—areas that
previously had the most cattle activity (Sanchez 2021). Geographic information system (GIS) mapping
was used to generate a set of random points within this corridor. Once in the field, plots were chosen from
the randomized set based on accessibility and evidence of cattle damage. Twelve plots were established
among three plot types: four plots along the bank of the Rio Grande (“Streambank”), four plots near
spring locations (“Spring”), and four plots upland locations (“Upland”; Figure 1). Upland locations
consisted of areas with no immediate river access, such as benches higher up the side of the canyon.

Plot layouts were adapted from the Assessment, Inventory, and Monitoring (AIM) National Aquatic
Monitoring Framework. Each plot was composed of three staggered, 20-meter transects running parallel
from each other, 20 meters apart, and perpendicular to the Rio Grande (BLM 2024; Figure 2). This layout
created a total plot area of 320 square meters. Photographs were taken at the 0-, 10-, and 20-meter mark
of each of the three transects in all four cardinal directions, for a total of 36 photographs per plot.

Line-point intercept (LPI) data were taken every 1 meter along each of the three transects. Vegetation
class and species were recorded, along with any non-living litter. A canopy gap assessment was
conducted for all shrubs and trees ≤10 feet fall that intersected the transect lines to measure canopy cover.
Characteristics such as species, height, and disturbances (i.e., grazing, trampling) were noted. A
densitometer was used to assess canopy cover at every meter along each transect. The presence or
absence of overhead vegetation was noted along with the species type.

To record evidence of cattle disturbances, the scope and intensity of various disturbances were recorded
within each plot according to “Rapid Assessment of Cattle Impacts in Riparian Exclosures” developed by
the Center for Biological Diversity (Center for Biological Diversity 2018). Surveyors visually assessed
the area and accounted for six different types of cattle impact categories (grazing evidence, browse
pressure, ground cover disturbance intensity and extent, and streambank degradation intensity and extent)
and noted the disturbance on a scale of 1–4 (See Appendix A, Table A1). An estimate of the number of
cow patties in each plot was also recorded. Impact categories were then averaged for each plot to
determine an overall impact level (light, moderate, or significant; see Appendix A, Table A2).

Statistical analyses were conducted in R to determine plant community differences among plots (R Core
Team 2025). A biodiversity analysis to determine differences in plant composition was performed to
evaluate the difference in abundance and diversity of native versus non-native plant species between the
different plot types. We tested for significant results using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallace test by
ranks because the data were non-normal using the dplyr package in R (Wickham et al. 2026).

To further establish patterns of feral cattle impacts leading up to a drone flight scheduled for Spring 2026,
we deployed seven game cameras in October 2025 along WRC (Figure 3). The cameras were set up in
locations where we previously witnessed the most cattle activity (Sanchez 2021).

 Results
Results indicated that native species were more abundant than non-native species in the Spring (p<0.001)
and Upland (p<0.005) plot types (Figure 4). The Spring plots had the least amount of cattle impact
(Figure 1, Table 1) and the lowest abundance of non-native vegetation (Figure 4). Additionally, using the
Shannon Diversity Index, significant differences in plant diversity between native and non-native
communities were found to vary across the different habitat types (p<0.05; Figure 5). The percent cover
of species did not differ significantly between the different plot types but grasses did dominate in the
Streambank and Upland plots.

The vegetative species with the highest percent cover (>15%) as measured by LPI were blue grama
(Bouteloua gracilis), desert olive (Forestiera pubescens), sand dropseed (Sporobolus cryptandrus), and
Russian thistle (Salsola sp.). There were more non-native invasive trees in the plots along the streambank
and significant signs of cattle use near the Rio Grande, including wallows, patties, and vegetation
disturbances (Photo 1; see page 8). The plots closest to springs had the highest count of native trees and
densest canopy coverage with fewer signs of cattle browsing and grazing, but wallows and patties were
present (Photo 2). Upland plots had significant signs of cattle use, including significant browse activity on
cacti (Photo 3). Denuded vegetation was typical in streambank and upland plots (Photo 4).

Of the 12 plots, 8 had significant overall signs of cattle damage (Figure 1, Table 1). This damage was
consistent throughout plots with an abundance of tracks, trails, wallows, and browse pressure on native
woody regeneration (Photo 1 through Photo 4).

During the initial two camera checks in December 2025 and January 2026, we captured images of cattle
on five of the seven cameras (Photo 5 through Photo 7). The deployed cameras contained documented
images of cattle—both untagged (Photo 5) and tagged (Photo 6 and Photo 7)—suggesting that domestic
cattle are free roaming and also contributing to ecological degradation.

 Discussion
The findings from this investigation indicate long-term, significant adverse impacts to the riparian
ecosystems found within WRC by feral cattle. Removal of cattle from this location is the first step toward
ecosystem restoration. In-depth management options and considerations that are specific to WRC can be
found in the White Rock Canyon Feral Cattle Removal Plan (LA-CP-23-20452). In significantly
impacted areas, invasive species removal and native-tree managed restoration through pole planting could
be necessary to provide native seed dispersal, understory regeneration, and soil stabilization.

According to the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS), Ecological Site Descriptions (ESDs),
the plots in WRC are composed of two main ecological sites: shallow sandstone and riparian (USDA
NRCS 2025). Six main species should make up much of the vegetative population in these sites: Rio
Grande cottonwood (Populus fremontii), narrowleaf willow (Salix exigua), western wheatgrass
(Pascopyrum smithii), Nebraska sedge (Carex nebrascensis), blue grama (Bouteloua gracilis), and
sideoats grama (Bouteloua curtipendula). Although surveys documented the presence of some of the
predicted species—mainly narrowleaf willow, blue grama, and sideoats grama—many expected species

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 5

are missing from the population altogether. This difference in species composition suggests a transition
away from the reference community. A reference state is defined as the plant community that was best
adapted to the unique combination of environmental factors associated with a particular ecological site,
deemed to be the most productive and sustainable plant community. The reference state delivers
maximum ecosystem services like superior soil stability, water infiltration, carbon sequestration, and
biodiversity. The shift from the reference community is marked by invasive species that co-dominate the
overstory and a mix of native and non-native grasses and forbs that make up the understory. According to
the state and transition models, site transitions such as these are often the product of unmanaged grazing
(USDA NRCS 2025).

 Future Efforts
We will continue to check cameras monthly, leading up to the drone flight, and then transition to camera
checks every 3 months post-flight. This initial effort to chronicle cattle impacts in WRC provided us with
a preliminary set of data that can be used as a baseline comparison during future monitoring efforts. The
game camera images of untagged and tagged cattle (Photo 5 through Photo 7) add to the complexity of
removing cattle from WRC. We recommend that a long-term monitoring procedure is implemented to
document ecosystem changes following the removal of the cattle.
