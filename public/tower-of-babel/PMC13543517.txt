Biochemical characterization of chitinolytic Bacillus isolates from a hypersaline wetland and their biopriming potential for improving wheat germination under salinity stress
Authors: Sara Abedini, Shohreh Ariaeenejad, Azadeh Lohrasbi-Nejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalinity negatively affects seed germination and crop yields. This study explored chitinolytic bacteria from the Lipar Pink Wetland as potential wheat seed-priming agents under salt stress. Using shrimp shell waste, chitinase-producing bacteria were isolated, and their live cells and chitinase-active supernatants produced on colloidal chitin were tested as wheat seed-priming treatments in normal and saline conditions.ResultsChitin extraction from shrimp shells using a conventional acid–alkali process yielded 53.3–56.7% chitin on a dry-weight basis. FTIR analysis confirmed characteristic functional groups, and FESEM revealed a compact, flake-like morphology. Among 45 bacterial isolates screened on chitin-containing media, three strains (S1, S49, and S80) formed clear hydrolysis halos and were selected for further analysis. Their chitinase-active extracellular fractions showed broad activity across 35–50 °C, with optimum temperatures of 40 °C for Sup49 and 45 °C for Sup1 and Sup80. Sup1 exhibited the highest activity under acidic conditions, whereas Sup49 and Sup80 performed best at near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH. Sup80 also showed superior thermostability at 60 °C and greater tolerance to salinity and EDTA than the other fractions. Partial 16 S rRNA phylogeny placed S49 and S80 within Bacillus cereus sensu lato-related lineages. Seed priming showed that 150 mM sodium chloride reduced wheat performance, decreasing germination in the untreated control from 73.74 ± 7.82% under normal conditions to 34.44 ± 1.92% under salinity. Under normal conditions, shorter priming durations were generally more effective. Hydropriming for 3 and 7 h produced high germination rates, while N-S80.3 h produced the numerically greatest total seedling length. Under salinity, S-Sup1.3 h showed the strongest numerical overall response, increasing germination to 74.67 ± 4.62% and producing the highest shoot length, root length, total seedling length, and seedling vigor index among saline treatments with available growth data. However, its GP was statistically grouped with S-Hydropriming.24 h and S-Sup49.7 h.ConclusionsChitinolytic Bacillus-related isolates and their chitinase-active extracellular supernatants showed preliminary potential for wheat seed priming under salinity. Because the supernatants were not purified chitinase preparations, their effects may reflect combined extracellular metabolites and chitin-derived products. Future research should focus on microbial traits, plant stress physiology, pathogen challenges, biosafety, and field performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09331-x.Keywords: Chitinolytic Bacillus, Extracellular chitinase, Seed biopriming, Salinity stress, Halotolerant bacteria, Germination traits

Introduction

Chitinases (EC 3.2.1.14) catalyze the hydrolysis of chitin, a structural polysaccharide widely found in fungal cell walls and arthropod exoskeletons. In natural environments, chitinolytic microorganisms contribute to organic matter turnover and nutrient cycling, while also influencing microbial community structure through competition and antagonism toward chitin-containing organisms [1]. In agricultural systems, chitinase-producing bacteria have attracted attention because they may suppress fungal pathogens, improve rhizosphere health, and indirectly support early plant establishment [1–3].

Seed priming is a widely used pre-sowing treatment that enhances germination performance and promotes more successful seedling establishment under adverse environmental conditions [4–6]. Controlled hydration before sowing initiates pre-germinative metabolic repair processes, promotes reserve mobilization, and can increase stress tolerance during the early stages of development [7]. Biopriming extends this concept by combining seed hydration with beneficial microorganisms, thereby enabling microbial colonization of the seed surface and delivery of bioactive compounds such as enzymes, phytohormones, and stress-protective metabolites [8]. Among microbial candidates, spore-forming Bacillus species are particularly attractive because of their physiological robustness, shelf stability, and multiple plant-beneficial traits, including biofilm formation, nutrient mobilization, phytohormone modulation, and antagonistic activity against pathogens [9]. Microorganisms originating from saline habitats may offer an additional advantage because halotolerant microbes, and in many cases their extracellular products, are adapted to remain functional under elevated salinity [10, 11].

Salinity is one of the major constraints limiting cereal production worldwide, particularly during germination and early seedling growth. In wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), salt stress reduces water uptake through osmotic effects. It causes ion toxicity, nutrient imbalance, membrane destabilization, and oxidative damage, all of which impair embryo growth and seedling establishment [12]. Because successful stand establishment is a critical determinant of later productivity, developing effective priming strategies for saline conditions remains an important objective in crop improvement and stress management [13, 14].

Hypersaline coastal wetlands may represent promising reservoirs of microorganisms with stress-adapted functional traits. The Lipar Pink Wetland (LPW) on the Makran coast of Iran is a saline-to-hypersaline coastal ecosystem characterized by distinctive physicochemical conditions and strong environmental gradients. Such habitats may harbor bacteria capable of producing extracellular enzymes and metabolites that remain active under elevated salinity [15–17]. In particular, chitinolytic bacteria from hypersaline environments may offer two potentially useful features for agricultural applications: tolerance to salt stress and the secretion of extracellular chitinases with functional stability across challenging environmental conditions [18]. Chitinases are well known for their antifungal and biocontrol roles, and plant chitinases have also been implicated in abiotic stress responses, including salinity stress. Recent studies suggest that some plant chitinase genes contribute to salt tolerance by helping regulate reactive oxygen species homeostasis and stress signaling [19, 20].

To date, direct applications of purified or crude chitinase preparations have been reported primarily for suppressing seed-borne or phytopathogenic fungi and improving seed germination under biotic stress [21, 22]. In contrast, the contribution of chitinase-active bacterial supernatants to seed priming under abiotic salinity stress remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, the present study examined whether chitinase-producing Bacillus isolates from a hypersaline wetland, and their chitinase-active extracellular culture supernatants, could influence wheat germination and early seedling establishment under saline conditions. Therefore, the novelty of this study does not rest on a claim of a chitinase-specific mechanism for salt tolerance, but on the combined biochemical characterization of chitinolytic Bacillus-related isolates from a hypersaline wetland and the comparative evaluation of their live cells and corresponding chitinase-active extracellular culture supernatants as wheat seed-priming treatments under salinity stress. The objectives included: (i) preparing and characterizing chitin from shrimp-shell waste for microbial screening; (ii) isolating and molecularly identifying chitinase-producing bacteria; (iii) characterizing key biochemical properties of their extracellular chitinases, including temperature, pH, salinity tolerance, and responses to chemical modulators; and (iv) comparing the effects of live bacterial biopriming and chitinase-active extracellular supernatant priming on wheat germination and seedling traits under 150 mM NaCl stress, against hydroprimed and non-primed controls.

Materials and methods

Materials

Hydrochloric acid (HCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), absolute ethanol, 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS), Tris-HCl, sodium chloride (NaCl), potassium chloride (KCl), calcium chloride (CaCl₂), cobalt chloride (CoCl₂), manganese chloride (MnCl₂), ferric chloride (FeCl₃), magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄), zinc sulfate (ZnSO₄), copper sulfate (CuSO₄), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), lysozyme, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), proteinase K, chloroform, and isopropanol all from Merck, Co.

Chitin extraction from shrimp shells

Shrimp shells were obtained from a local market and processed for chitin extraction following standard methods [23]. The shells were boiled for 5 min, then washed, dried at 80 °C for 6 h, ground, and stored at -20 °C.

Chitin extraction involved three main steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1. First, 30 g of dried shrimp shell powder were demineralized by stirring it with 270 mL of 5% HCl for 2 h. After washing to achieve a neutral pH, the residue was treated with 270 mL of 10% NaOH solution at 100 °C for 2 h, with continuous stirring, to remove any remaining proteins, a process known as deproteinization. In the third step, for decolorization, the resulting off-white to pale pink powder was treated with 50 mL of 0.315% NaClO solution for 10 min at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 10:1 (w/v) to remove residual pigments. Lastly, the purified chitin powder was dried in an oven at 80 °C, and the extraction yield percentage was measured as follows:

Preparation of colloidal chitin

Colloidal chitin was prepared according to Rangel et al. [24] with some modifications. Powdered chitin (40 g) was homogenized in 600 mL of 37% (w/w) HCl with vigorous stirring for 60 min at 25 °C. Next, 280 mL of cold absolute ethanol was added to the mixture. It was allowed to stand for 5 h to facilitate the formation of colloidal chitin. Afterward, the precipitate was collected using centrifugation (16,000× g, 10 min at 4 °C) and washed several times with distilled water until the solution was neutral. The chitin colloidal suspension produced contained approximately 0.05 g of chitin per mL. The extracted chitin was analyzed using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) to examine surface morphology and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) to identify functional groups.

Sample collection and bacterial isolation

The Lipar Pink Wetland near Chabahar, also known as the Lipar Wetland, is located about 15 km east of Chabahar in the Sistan and Baluchestan province of Iran (25° 15′ 20.88″ N, 60° 49′ 43.22″ E).

Soil samples were collected from the Lipar Pink Wetland at a depth of 20 cm below the saline lake and transported to the laboratory. Serial dilutions of the soil samples were prepared and plated onto LB agar to isolate bacterial strains. To screen for chitinase-producing bacteria, three types of culture media were employed. LBChit, consisting of LB medium supplemented with 1% colloidal chitin, selective ChitMedia, prepared according to Rangel et al. [24], and the synthetic M9 seawater medium as reported by Govindaraj et al. [25]. Chitinase activity was screened by streaking isolated colonies on colloidal chitin agar plates. Clear halos around colonies indicated enzymatic activity.

Chitinase production and activity assessment

Strains forming halos on LBChit medium were selected and inoculated into 50 mL of LB medium containing 1% colloidal chitin. The cultures were grown at 28 °C while shaking at 130 rpm. 1 mL of culture was collected every 24 h after inoculation, and the supernatant, containing the extracellular enzyme, was collected by centrifugation at 7,000 rpm for 10 min. Supernatant and separated bacterial biomass were stored at -40 °C until further analysis. The selected isolates were designated S1, S49, and S80, and these codes are used consistently throughout the manuscript. Their corresponding chitinase-active extracellular culture supernatants were designated Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80, respectively.

Chitinase activity was measured using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method with colloidal chitin as the substrate. The reaction mixture contained 50 µL of crude extracellular supernatant and 50 µL of 0.5% colloidal chitin in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) and was incubated for 40 min at the optimal temperature. After adding 120 µL of DNS reagent, the mixture was boiled for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm [25]. All experiments were conducted in at least triplicate, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Determination of optimum temperature and pH

To determine the optimal temperature of chitinases (Sup49, Sup80, and Sup1), 50 µL of 0.5% chitin colloid was incubated at temperatures ranging from 35 to 60 °C. After that, 50 µL of crude extracellular supernatant was added to the reaction. After 40 min, the reaction was stopped by adding 120 µL of DNS solution and boiling for 5 min. Chitinase activity was measured by monitoring optical density at 540 nm.

To determine the optimal pH for chitinase activity, citrate buffer (10 mM) at pH 5.5 and 6.5, Tris-HCl buffer (10 mM) at pH 7.5 and 8.5 were prepared, along with a 0.5% (w/v) colloidal chitin in each buffer. Then, 50 µL of crude extracellular supernatant was added to 50 µL of the substrate solution at the respective pH levels. The mixtures were incubated for 40 min at their optimal temperatures. After incubation, 120 µL of DNS solution was added, and the mixture was heated to 95 °C for 5 min to stop the reaction. Activity of Sup49, Sup80, and Sup1 was evaluated by measuring the amount of reducing sugars released at 540 nm.

Assessment of chitinase thermostability

To assess chitinase stability, crude extracellular supernatants were incubated at 55 °C and 60 °C. At 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, and 45 min, 50 µL of the sample was withdrawn and cooled on ice for 10 min. After cooling, 50 µL of substrate was added to each sample, and the samples were incubated at the optimal temperature for 40 min. The remaining enzyme activity was then measured to evaluate the effects of heat treatment. Enzyme activity was considered 100% prior to thermal treatment, and the remaining activity was measured.

Chitinase activity under salinity and chemical modulators

Sup49, Sup80, and Sup1 activity was evaluated at various salinity levels and under different additive conditions. We tested salinity tolerance in an enzyme-optimized reaction buffer containing 0, 50, 100, and 200 mM NaCl, providing insights into the enzyme’s ability to adapt to varying salinity conditions.

To study the effects of metal ions and chelation, reaction mixtures were supplemented with CaCl₂, MgSO₄, MnCl₂, FeCl₃, ZnSO₄, CuSO₄, CoCl₂, and KCl at a final concentration of 5 mM. EDTA was tested at final concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2.5, and 5 mM. Enzyme activity was measured under the same assay conditions and expressed as relative activity compared with the control without additives, which was set to 100%.

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Selected bacterial strains, S1, S49, and S80, were cultured in Luria–Bertani broth at 30 °C for 24 h while shaking at 120 rpm. The cells were collected by centrifugation (14,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C) and then resuspended in SET buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 75 mM NaCl, 25 mM EDTA, pH 7.5). Following treatment with lysozyme (1 mg mL⁻¹ at 37 °C for 30–60 min), SDS (final concentration of 1% w/v) and proteinase K (0.5 mg mL⁻¹) were incorporated and incubated at 55 °C for 2 h. The DNA was extracted using chloroform (1 volume of chloroform and 1.3 volume of 5 M NaCl), precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol, pelleted via centrifugation (13,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C), washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and then resuspended in 50 µL of nuclease-free water. The purity and integrity of the DNA were confirmed by measuring the A260/A280 absorbance ratio and performing 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the universal bacterial primer 27 F (5ʹ-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3ʹ) and the 1492R primer variant (5ʹ-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3ʹ), which was the exact reverse primer sequence ordered and used in this study. The PCR reactions (20 µL) comprised 2× Taq Master Mix (from Ampliqon), primers (10 µM), approximately 2 µL of template genomic DNA, and nuclease-free water. The cycling conditions on an Applied Biosystems™ Veriti™ consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 60 s; with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplicons were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel.

PCR products were sequenced using Sanger sequencing at Pishgam Biotech in Tehran. The resulting chromatograms were quality-checked using SnapGene Viewer (v 7.0.2). The partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences of S49 and S80 were deposited in the NCBI GenBank database under accession numbers PZ413528 and PZ413529, respectively. We identified the closest relatives through NCBI BLASTN and retrieved the top homologous sequences for phylogenetic analysis. A neighbor-joining tree was constructed in MEGA7 with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess node support.

Biopriming of wheat seeds

Three chitinase-producing isolates, S1, S49, and S80, were grown in liquid LB medium supplemented with 5% (w/v) colloidal chitin at 28 °C for 24 h. After incubation, cultures were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to separate bacterial cells from the extracellular culture fraction. The bacterial pellets were washed three times with sterile PBS. For each isolate, the bacterial suspension was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard and diluted with sterile PBS to an estimated concentration of 10⁸ CFU/mL for live-cell biopriming. This consistently standardized working suspension was utilized across all priming treatments and biological replicates. The McFarland standardization estimates cell density by turbidity, making the final inoculum an approximate concentration rather than an exact viable plate count. The corresponding culture supernatants, designated Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80, contained measurable extracellular chitinase activity, along with other soluble bacterial products and possible chitin-derived hydrolysis products generated during growth on colloidal chitin, and were considered chitinase-active extracellular culture supernatants rather than purified chitinase preparations. Wheat seeds (Triticum aestivum L.) were surface-disinfected with 70% ethanol for 1 min, followed by 1% sodium hypochlorite for 3 min, and then rinsed thoroughly with sterile distilled water. Seed priming was performed under sterile conditions in a completely randomized design, with treatment combinations defined by salinity condition, priming treatment, and priming duration. The priming treatments included live bacterial suspensions of S1, S49, and S80, their corresponding chitinase-active extracellular culture supernatants (Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80), and hydropriming with sterile distilled water, and a non-primed control.

For each priming treatment, 25 wheat seeds were immersed in 5 mL of the corresponding priming solution, giving a seed-to-solution ratio of 25 seeds:5 mL, equivalent to 0.20 mL seed⁻¹. The same seed-to-solution ratio was used for live bacterial suspensions, extracellular culture supernatants, and hydropriming treatments. Priming was conducted for 3, 7, and 24 h. After priming, seeds were rinsed with sterile water and dried back to approximately their original moisture content. Seeds were then placed in sterile Petri dishes lined with filter paper and incubated under two irrigation conditions: distilled water and 150 mM NaCl.

Each treatment combination consisted of three biological replicates. One Petri dish containing 25 seeds was considered one biological replicate, and individual seeds within each Petri dish were treated as subsamples for calculating dish-level values.

Germination and seedling growth assay

Germination was monitored in darkness at 25 ± 1 °C for 5 days. Seeds with white radicles ≥ 2 mm in length were considered germinated. Germination counts were recorded every 24 h, and cumulative germination percentage was calculated. To evaluate seed germination dynamics, the germination-, vigor-, and uniformity-related parameters listed in Table 1 were calculated using the germinationmetrics package in R [26], following standard germination indices described by Ranal and Santana [27]. Seedling vigor indices VI-I and VI-II were calculated according to Abdul-Baki and Anderson [28]. At the end of the seventh day, seedling growth and biomass traits were measured. All germination, vigor, growth, and biomass parameters were calculated at the Petri dish level and used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis of data

The experiment was arranged as a completely randomized design, and treatment combinations were defined according to salinity condition, priming treatment, and priming duration. The non-primed control was included as the untreated reference within each salinity condition. Therefore, each treatment combination represented one specific salinity × treatment × duration condition, while treatment ranking and Duncan’s grouping letters were interpreted separately within normal and saline conditions.

Each treatment combination consisted of three biological replicates. One Petri dish containing 25 seeds was considered one biological replicate and served as the basic experimental unit. Individual seeds within each Petri dish were treated as subsamples for calculating germination and seedling traits at the dish level and were not considered independent biological replicates. Petri dishes were arranged randomly within the growth conditions to minimize positional effects.

For each germination, vigor, uniformity, growth, and biomass parameter, one-way ANOVA was performed separately within each salinity condition using the treatment × duration combination as the grouping factor. Prior to ANOVA, model residuals were examined to assess the suitability of the data for parametric analysis, including the distribution of residuals and variance homogeneity. Duncan’s multiple-range test was then used for mean separation at p ≤ 0.05 within each salinity condition; therefore, grouping letters should be compared within normal conditions or within salinity conditions, but not between the two conditions. Results are presented as untransformed means ± standard deviations to maintain biological interpretability, because no data transformation was applied before reporting the final tables. The germination indices were computed in R 4.5.1 using the germinationmetrics package [26], and treatment-trait heatmaps were produced using the pheatmap package [29]. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS v.9, and data organization and graph preparation were carried out in Microsoft Excel version 2108.

Results

Chitin extraction and characterization

Chitin was extracted from shrimp shell waste using a conventional acid–alkali method involving demineralization, deproteinization, and decolorization. The extracted chitin powder appeared as a white, visually uniform material (Fig. 1). The extraction process yielded approximately 16–17 g of dried chitin from 30 g of dried shrimp shell powder, corresponding to an apparent recovery of 53.3–56.7% on a dry weight basis.

The FTIR spectrum of the chitin sample extracted from the shrimp shell is consistent with the presence of functional groups indicative of N-acetylated polysaccharides and glycosidic skeleton. The band at 3434 cm⁻¹, shown in Fig. 2a, arises from the overlapping stretching vibrations of O–H and N–H bonds, consistent with hydrogen bonding within the chitin structure. Additionally, the band at 2886 cm⁻¹ is associated with C–H stretching in aliphatic groups (–CH/–CH₂ and –CH₃ from acetyl groups) found in sugars and N-acetyl units [30]. In the amide region, the observation of a peak at 1659 cm⁻¹ (Amide I) assigned to the C = O stretching of the N-acetyl groups, together with a peak at 1562 cm⁻¹ (Amide II) due to N–H bending and C–N stretching, shows the chemical signature of chitin, consistent with the preservation of the N-acetylated nature of the polymer [31]. The FTIR spectrum supported the presence of functional groups consistent with an N-acetylated polysaccharide/chitin-like substrate; however, the degree of acetylation was not directly quantified because the FTIR analysis was performed for functional group identification rather than validated DA determination.

Scale bar = 500 nm

In the fingerprint region, the bands at 1379 cm⁻¹ (CH₃ acetyl group/C–H bending) and 1316 cm⁻¹ (Amide III, C–N and N–H vibrations) indicate the presence of amide groups and chain acetylation [30, 31]. Also, the bands at 1261 and 1205 cm⁻¹ are related to the overlapping vibrations of C–N (amide) and C–O in the polysaccharide framework [30]. The band at 1159 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the asymmetric C–O–C stretching of glycosidic bonds, while the bands at 1116, 1073, and 1028 cm⁻¹ relate to C–O and C–O–C stretching in the sugar ring, consistent with the polysaccharide backbone of chitin. Importantly, the band located at 896 cm⁻¹ is related to the β-(1→4)-glycosidic bond present in chitin [30].

In SEM images (Fig. 2b), the extracted chitin powder appears primarily as flake- or plate-like particles with sharp edges and angular fractures. This morphology is generally consistent with previously reported chitin structures from crustacean shells, which often exhibit compact flake-like or lamellar features. The relatively smooth and uniform surface morphology is in agreement with previous reports describing purified chitin after partial removal of the non-chitinous shell matrix [32].

Isolation and screening of chitinase-producing bacteria

Soil samples collected from the Lipar Pink Wetland were taken to the laboratory, and serial dilutions were prepared and plated onto LB agar to isolate bacterial strains. After purification of morphologically distinct colonies, the isolates were screened for their ability to grow on chitin-containing media (LBChit, ChitMedia, and M9 supplemented with colloidal chitin). A total of 45 bacterial isolates were cultured on three different media. However, hydrolysis halos were observed only on LBChit medium. Among these, S1, S49, and S80 were selected for further study due to their large and consistent hydrolysis halos. The selected strains displayed notable differences in halo diameter on LBChit medium, with chitin hydrolysis ranked in descending order as S80 > S49 > S1 (Fig. 3).

Clear hydrolysis halos around the colonies indicate extracellular chitinolytic activity. a S80, b S49, and (c) S1. Based on halo size, the relative chitinolytic activity was observed in the order S80 > S49 > S1

Temperature and pH profiles of extracellular chitinases

The activities of Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80, produced by three bacterial strains isolated from the Pink Lake in Chabahar, were measured over the temperature range of 35 to 60 °C to determine the optimal temperature. All three chitinases displayed a broad temperature optimum between 35 and 50 °C (Fig. 4a). Within the tested temperatures, Sup49 showed maximal activity at 40 °C, whereas Sup1 and Sup80 reached their highest values at 45 °C. Enzymatic activity decreased across all three enzymes with increasing temperature, so that at 60 °C the relative activity reached about 90.1 ± 0.1% for Sup49, 92.7 ± 0.1% for Sup1, and 94.3 ± 0.2% for Sup80. Overall, these data indicate that the chitinases obtained from Pink Lake isolates exhibit optimal performance at 40–45 °C and retain high relative activity up to 60 °C under the assay conditions tested. The evaluation of extracellular chitinase activity in three strains showed that the enzymatic response to pH was strain-dependent. Sup1 showed maximum relative activity at pH 5.5 (Fig. 4b) and decreased progressively as pH increased (89.3 ± 1.0% at 6.5, 85.6 ± 0.3% at 7.5, and 73.3 ± 0.2% at 8.5). In contrast, Sup49 had lower activity at acidic pH (85.0 ± 0.4% at 5.5), but peaked at pH 6.5–7.5 (97.7–100%) and maintained relatively high activity at pH 8.5 (89.9 ± 0.2%). Sup80 also showed the lowest activity at pH 5.5 (65.6 ± 0.3%) but increased as it approached neutral/slightly alkaline pH, reaching a maximum at pH 7.5 (100%) and slightly decreasing at pH 8.5 (86.7 ± 0.8%). Sup1 exhibited higher activity under acidic conditions, whereas Sup49 and Sup80 showed optimal activity at near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH levels.

Thermal stability of extracellular chitinases

The three extracellular chitinase-active fractions exhibited distinct thermal stability profiles at 55 and 60 °C (Fig. 5). The highest residual activities were observed for Sup1 and Sup80 at a temperature of 55 °C. After 45 min, Sup1 still retained 91.5 ± 1.5% of its activity, and Sup80 retained 88.9 ± 1.5%, while Sup49 decreased to 71.2 ± 1.8%. At 55 °C, Sup49 was the least stable enzyme, experiencing a sharp decline starting around 25–30 min. In contrast, Sup1 and Sup80 exhibit a gradual, slow decline.

Thermal stability of extracellular chitinases (Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80) at 55 °C and 60 °C. Residual activity was measured after different incubation times and expressed relative to the untreated control (0 min = 100%). Sup1 and Sup80 were more stable than Sup49 at 55 °C, while Sup80 showed the highest stability at 60 °C. Data are presented as mean ± SD

At 60 °C, the differences became more pronounced. Sup80 showed the best stability, retaining 83.3 ± 1.7% of its activity after 45 min. However, the activities of Sup1 and Sup49 remained at 69.8 ± 1.9% and 67.7 ± 1.5%, respectively.

Impact of metal ions on chitinase activity

The effect of various salts on chitinase activity varied by metal type and among the enzymes from the three isolates. In all samples (Fig. 6), FeCl3 exerted the most severe inhibitory effect, reducing enzymatic activity to 49.3 ± 1.7%, 44.3 ± 1.9%, and 38.0 ± 1.7% in Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80, respectively. MgSO4 increased enzyme activity for Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80 to 110.0 ± 1.3%, 108.7 ± 1.6%, and 107.0 ± 2.0%, respectively. ZnSO4 and CuSO4 inhibited activity, with CuSO4 having a stronger effect on Sup49. KCl resulted in a slight decrease in the activities of Sup1 and Sup80, while Sup49 showed a slight increase. CaCl2 had little to no effect on the activity of the three enzymes. MnCl2 had a stimulating effect in Sup1 and Sup49, increasing activity to 121.4 ± 1.4% and 105.2 ± 1.7%, respectively, but had little effect on Sup80. CoCl2 increased the activity (111.4 ± 1.8%) in Sup1, but was almost neutral in Sup49 and Sup80. In general, Sup1 showed the strongest positive response to some ions, especially Mn2+ and Co2+.

Enzyme activity was measured in the presence of different salts and expressed relative to the control (100%). FeCl3 showed the strongest inhibitory effect on all three enzymes, whereas MgSO4 generally enhanced activity. Impact of NaCl on chitinase activity

Evaluation of different NaCl concentrations showed that the response of extracellular chitinase activity to salinity was strain-dependent. Under salt-free conditions (0 mM), all three enzymes showed maximum relative activity (100%). Across the tested salinity range of 50–200 mM (Fig. 7a), Sup80 did not show any reduction in activity; instead, its activity remained slightly above the control level (101.5–102.1%), indicating high salt tolerance and possible stabilization under the tested ionic conditions. In contrast, Sup1 showed a mild but consistent decrease in activity in the presence of NaCl, retaining about 92% activity at 50–200 mM, which suggests moderate salt sensitivity. Sup49 showed the greatest inhibition, with activity decreasing to approximately 84.4–85.1% under saline conditions, indicating greater sensitivity to salinity. Overall, Sup80 may be better suited than the other two enzymes for potential biotechnological applications in saline environments.

Impact of EDTA on chitinase activity

The effect of EDTA on extracellular chitinase activity differed among the three bacterial strains, indicating differences in metal dependence and enzyme stability. As shown in Fig. 7b, all three enzymes exhibited maximum activity (100%) in the control samples without EDTA. However, upon addition of EDTA (0.1–5 mM), the activities of Sup1 and Sup49 decreased markedly and remained mainly within the range of 74–79%. Given the chelating properties of EDTA, this pattern may suggest that these enzymes depend, at least in part, on divalent cations for optimal activity or stability. In contrast, Sup80 was not inhibited and instead showed a slight increase in activity (approximately 99–105%) at most EDTA concentrations, indicating low metal dependence or greater resistance to ion chelation. Overall, Sup80 may be a more stable candidate for potential biotechnological applications in the presence of chelators or under conditions of fluctuating metal-ion availability.

Phylogenetic classification of S49 and S80

Phylogenetic analysis of isolates S49 and S80 yielded high-confidence partial 16 S rRNA sequences. The S1 isolate was included in the biochemical and seed-priming assays due to its consistent chitinolytic activity. However, it could not be classified due to the lack of a high-confidence 16 S rRNA sequence. Thus, S1 and Sup1 are regarded as an unclassified chitinolytic isolate and its corresponding chitinase-active culture supernatant, respectively. The Neighbor-Joining phylogenetic tree based on partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences placed S49 and S80 within the genus Bacillus, clearly separated from the outgroup (Staphylococcus aureus). Both isolates were assigned to lineages within the Bacillus cereus sensu lato group (Fig. 8). S49 was found to be near taxa related to B. proteolyticus and B. wiedmannii; however, the bootstrap support for this placement was low, with only about 16% support for the closest subclade and 43% for the broader grouping. Therefore, it is advisable to report S49 conservatively as a Bacillus sp. related to the B. cereus sensu lato group, rather than assigning it to a specific species. In contrast, S80 clustered most closely with Bacillus paramycoides, with a bootstrap support of 68%, indicating a more reliable subclade-level placement. Phylogenetic analysis places both isolates within the Bacillus genus, particularly within the B. cereus group; however, it shows that partial 16 S rRNA sequences have limited ability to distinguish between species in this complex. Therefore, S49 and S80 should be considered potential subjects for further research rather than confirmed agricultural inoculants at this time.

The quantitative results confirmed the inhibitory effect of salinity on seed germination. Germination percentage in the non-primed control decreased from 73.74 ± 7.82% under normal conditions to 34.44 ± 1.92% under 150 mM NaCl, corresponding to an approximately 53.3% reduction. This decline was accompanied by delayed germination and weaker seedling establishment. The highest GP group under normal conditions included N-Hydropriming.3 h (97.22 ± 2.42%), N-Sup1.3 h (94.67 ± 2.31%), N-Hydropriming.7 h (93.33 ± 2.89%), N-S80.7 h (92.00 ± 0.00%), and N-S49.7 h (83.56 ± 0.38%).

Seedling growth traits showed a related but not identical pattern. Under normal conditions, N-S80.3 h produced the numerically highest total seedling length, 28.84 ± 2.93 cm, followed by N-S1.7 h (27.16 ± 0.55 cm), N-S49.7 h (26.49 ± 0.69 cm), N-Hydropriming.7 h (25.85 ± 1.71 cm), and N-Hydropriming.3 h (25.33 ± 2.37 cm). These treatments were statistically grouped among the highest-performing treatments for seedling elongation. Vigor and biomass traits further supported this ranking. The highest VI-I group under normal conditions included N-Hydropriming.3 h (2459.35 ± 183.90), N-Hydropriming.7 h (2412.27 ± 179.09), N-S80.3 h (2364.10 ± 273.10), N-S49.7 h (2213.63 ± 49.20), N-S1.7 h (2121.23 ± 114.12), and N-S80.7 h (1890.15 ± 358.01). Biomass also varied among treatments, with N-S49.7 h showing the numerically highest FW (1.97 ± 0.41) and N-S80.7 h showing the numerically highest DW (0.45 ± 0.00). These results indicate that short-duration priming, particularly 3–7 h, was generally more effective than prolonged priming under non-saline conditions. The chitinase-active extracellular supernatants showed treatment- and duration-dependent effects. Under normal conditions, N-Sup1.3 h produced one of the strongest germination responses, with GP reaching 94.67 ± 2.31%. This treatment was included in the highest Duncan group for GP and also showed a relatively high VI-I value, 1788.45 ± 128.21. However, its seedling length (18.89 ± 1.26 cm) was lower than that of the strongest live bacterial and hydropriming treatments. This observation indicates that germination percentage and seedling elongation did not always respond in parallel. Other supernatant treatments showed more trait-specific responses. For example, N-Sup49.3 h reached a seedling length of 23.29 ± 0.26 cm, while N-Sup80.7 h reached 24.47 ± 0.33 cm. In contrast, several 24 h supernatant treatments showed weaker germination or vigor responses, suggesting that prolonged exposure to supernatant did not necessarily improve seed performance.

Under salinity, treatment responses were more variable, but several treatments improved germination relative to the saline control. S-Sup1.3 h produced the numerically highest GP under salinity, 74.67 ± 4.62%, corresponding to a 116.8% increase relative to the saline control. S-Hydropriming.24 h also improved GP to 69.33 ± 16.65%, while S-Sup49.7 h reached 60.00 ± 4.00%. These treatments were statistically grouped among the highest-performing treatments for GP under salinity. Selected live bacterial treatments also improved germination under salt stress, including S-S1.24 h (60.00 ± 4.00%), S-S49.24 h (60.00 ± 6.93%), and S-S80.24 h (57.24 ± 11.11%). Therefore, the strongest numerical germination response under salinity was observed for the 3 h chitinase-active extracellular supernatant from isolate S1. However, it was statistically grouped with S-Hydropriming.24 h and S-Sup49.7 h according to Duncan’s multiple-range test.

Growth and vigor traits measured under salinity further supported the strong response of S-Sup1.3 h among treatments with available seedling growth data. Among saline treatments for which seedling growth and biomass traits were measured, S-Sup1.3 h produced the numerically highest shoot length, 3.00 ± 0.52 cm, root length, 7.79 ± 0.76 cm, and total seedling length, 10.78 ± 0.59 cm. S-Hydropriming.24 h also produced measurable seedling development, with shoot length of 2.28 ± 0.10 cm, root length of 5.31 ± 0.89 cm, and seedling length of 7.59 ± 0.79 cm, whereas S-S1.24 h showed lower values for these traits. S-Sup1.3 h also produced the highest VI-I under salinity (806.23 ± 84.48), exceeding S-Hydropriming.24 h (534.56 ± 183.45), and S-S1.24 h (326.32 ± 64.38). These results indicate that, among the saline treatments with measured seedling growth and biomass traits, S-Sup1.3 h showed the strongest overall numerical improvement in germination percentage, seedling growth, and VI-I under the present experimental conditions.

Hierarchical clustering of standardized trait values summarized the multivariate relationships among treatments and traits (Fig. 10b). The high-performance cluster included N-Hydropriming.3 h, N-S1.7 h, N-S80.3 h, N-S80.7 h, N-S49.7 h, N-Hydropriming.7 h, and N-Sup1.3 h. These treatments were associated with lower germination timing indices (t50, MGT, and FGT) and higher shoot length, root length, seedling length, VI-I, FW, DW, and VI-II. This clustering pattern was consistent with the quantitative ranking and showed that the strongest overall responses were mainly observed under normal conditions and shorter priming durations. The intermediate cluster featured treatments like S-S1.24 h, S-Sup1.3 h, and S-Hydropriming.24 h, showing partial improvement under salinity but not full recovery. Lower-performance clusters included prolonged 24 h treatments and untreated controls, indicating lower overall performance in these treatment groups.

Discussion

This research focused on chitin from shrimp shells to isolate chitin-degrading bacteria from the Lipar Pink Wetland. It evaluated their impact on wheat seed priming in saline conditions. The isolated bacteria exhibited varying chitinase activity, influencing seed priming differently under normal and saline conditions. Importantly, the extracellular treatments used here were not purified chitinase preparations, but chitinase-active culture supernatants that may contain chitinase, other secreted bacterial products, and chitin-derived hydrolysis products. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a preliminary evaluation of chitinolytic Bacillus isolates and their extracellular culture fractions, rather than as evidence for a direct effect of chitinase alone on wheat germination.

The initial screening revealed visible hydrolysis halos only on LBChit medium, with none in ChitMedia or M9. This observation suggests that detectable extracellular chitin hydrolysis is affected by the medium. The nutrient-rich LBChit likely improved bacterial growth and more detectable chitin degradation around colonies, thereby facilitating halo formation. In contrast, the lower nutrient availability in ChitMedia and M9, along with the ionic composition of the artificial seawater-based M9 medium, may have restricted growth, enzyme activity, enzyme diffusion, or substrate accessibility under the tested conditions. Therefore, the absence of halos does not necessarily indicate a lack of chitinolytic potential but rather reflects the influence of medium composition on the plate-based screening assay.

Isolates S49 and S80 were identified as belonging to the genus Bacillus after examining partial sequences of the 16 S rRNA gene. Both isolates were associated with lineages connected to the B. cereus sensu lato group. In particular, S49 was found to be closely related to species such as B. proteolyticus and B. wiedmannii. The bootstrap support for this positioning was limited, hindering definitive species-level classification. S80 clustered closely with taxa related to B. paramycoides, showing moderate support, and both isolates are classified as Bacillus spp, within the B. cereus sensu lato group. Due to the limited discriminatory power of partial 16 S rRNA sequences within the B. cereus sensu lato group, the taxonomic placement is preliminary. Future multilocus sequence analysis using markers such as gyrB, rpoB, and pycA or whole-genome average nucleotide identity (ANI) is needed for definitive species-level identification.

The chitinase-active fractions displayed distinct biochemical profiles. Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80 displayed the highest activities at 40–45 °C and retained substantial activity up to approximately 50–60 °C under the assay conditions tested. This suggests that the extracellular chitinase-active fractions from these isolates are compatible with moderate-temperature environments, although their stability differed among isolates. Sup1 showed greater activity at acidic pH, while Sup49 and Sup80 were more active at near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH, indicating differing pH preferences among extracellular enzyme fractions, consistent with the variability in Bacillus-derived chitinases. For example, recombinant chitinase rChiA-DP from B. proteolyticus IMH/B-1 showed maximum activity at 40 °C and pH 7.0, indicating a neutral mesophilic profile [33]. Chitinase activity has also been reported in B. wiedmannii strain AzBw1, although detailed optimization data remain limited [34]. Similarly, studies on B. paramycoides have mainly focused on optimizing chitinase production rather than on detailed characterization of enzyme activity under different physicochemical conditions [35].

The effects of metal ions further indicated that chemical modulators differentially influenced the extracellular chitinase activities of Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80. FeCl3 was the most effective inhibitor, suggesting that Fe3+ strongly reduced activity under the conditions tested. Zn2+ and Cu2+ also inhibited all three enzyme fractions, with Sup49 and Sup80 being more affected than Sup1, whereas MgSO4 slightly enhanced activity. This pattern is consistent with previous observations for Bacillus chitinases, where Fe3+, Cu2+, Zn2+, and Mn2+ inhibited recombinant B. proteolyticus chitinase activity, while divalent or monovalent ions such as Mg2+, Ca2+, K+, and Mn2+ enhanced activity in some Bacillus sp. R2 chitinase preparations [33].

Residual activity assays showed significant differences in salt tolerance among chitinase-active fractions. Sup80 maintained higher activity with increased NaCl concentration, peaking at 200 mM, consistent with halotolerant Bacillus chitinases. For example, B. paramycoides B26 showed maximum chitinase production at 2% NaCl, equivalent to approximately 342 mM NaCl, and recombinant B. proteolyticus IMH/B-1 chitinase retained more than 91% residual activity at 2% NaCl [33, 35]. In the present study, Sup80 showed the strongest salt-tolerant enzymatic profile, Sup1 showed an intermediate response, and Sup49 was more sensitive to increasing NaCl concentration.

A mechanistic distinction is necessary when interpreting the seed-priming responses to chitinase-active extracellular culture supernatants. The supernatants used were not pure chitinase and may contain other bacterial products and chitin-derived hydrolysis products. Therefore, the effects of Sup1, Sup49, and Sup80 on wheat seeds should be viewed as responses to these combined supernatants rather than to chitinase alone. This distinction is important for understanding germination data, as improvements under salinity may result from the combined activity of multiple compounds and priming effects, rather than a single enzyme.

The quantitative findings on seed priming showed that salinity significantly reduced wheat germination and seedling growth. In the non-primed control group, germination decreased from 73.74 ± 7.82% under normal conditions to 34.44 ± 1.92% at 150 mM NaCl, corresponding to an approximately 53.3% reduction and confirming the strong inhibitory effect of salinity on wheat seed performance. This reduction is consistent with established effects of salinity during seed germination and early seedling growth, in which osmotic stress restricts water uptake, ionic imbalance disrupts cellular metabolism, and oxidative stress contributes to membrane injury and reduced seedling vigor [12, 14, 36–38]. In the current dataset, certain priming treatments have been shown to reduce this inhibition partially. This partial reduction is in line with previous findings that indicate seed priming and biopriming can enhance early seed performance under abiotic stress by improving hydration, activating pre-germinative metabolism, and supporting seedling establishment [14, 39–41]. However, the response varies significantly based on crop species, priming duration, microbial strain, and stress intensity [39–42].

Under normal conditions, hydropriming, selected live bacterial treatments, and the 3 h chitinase-active extracellular supernatant from isolate S1 were among the best-performing treatments. However, their effects differed among germination, growth, and vigor traits. Hydropriming for 3 h and 7 h produced high germination percentages of 97.22 ± 2.42% and 93.33 ± 2.89%, respectively, and were among the highest-performing treatments for GP under normal conditions. N-Sup1.3 h also showed a high GP value of 94.67 ± 2.31%, while live bacterial treatments such as N-S80.7 h, N-S49.7 h, N-S80.3 h, and N-S1.7 h showed strong germination, seedling growth, or vigor responses. N-S80.3 h produced the numerically highest total seedling length under normal conditions, 28.84 ± 2.93 cm, but it was statistically grouped with several other high-performing treatments, including N-S1.7 h, N-S49.7 h, N-Hydropriming.7 h, and N-Hydropriming.3 h. The highest VI-I group included N-Hydropriming.3 h, N-Hydropriming.7 h, N-S80.3 h, N-S49.7 h, and N-S1.7 h. These findings were consistent with a previous study [39] and suggest that, under normal conditions, short-duration priming can boost early seedling performance by enhancing hydration and activating pre-germinative metabolism. Prolonged priming for 24 h was generally less favorable for several treatments, suggesting excessive exposure may diminish the benefits or cause treatment-specific stress.

Under salinity, the response pattern was clearly treatment-specific. Importantly, the quantitative analysis did not support a uniform superiority of either live bacterial treatments or extracellular supernatant treatments. Among salinity treatments, S-Sup1.3 h showed the strongest overall numerical response, increasing GP to 74.67 ± 4.62% and producing the highest shoot length, root length, total seedling length, and VI-I among saline treatments with available growth data. However, its GP response was statistically grouped with S-Hydropriming.24 h and S-Sup49.7 h, indicating that the germination improvement was treatment- and duration-dependent rather than unique to Sup1.3 h alone. Selected live bacterial treatments also improved germination, but they were placed in a lower Duncan group. Results indicate that salinity response was influenced by isolate identity, treatment type, and priming duration, not just live-cell versus supernatant. The increased root-to-shoot ratio in some saline treatments should be interpreted with caution. Based on the measured growth traits, this pattern may reflect relatively stronger inhibition of shoot elongation than root growth, rather than a confirmed adaptive mechanism. The response of S-Sup1.3 h suggests that this treatment may support early seedling growth under salinity, particularly in terms of root and shoot elongation, but physiological measurements are needed to determine whether this reflects an adaptive stress response, a direct biostimulant effect, or the combined action of multiple extracellular compounds.

The positive response of S-Sup1.3 h likely reflects the composition of the extracellular culture fraction produced by S1 on colloidal chitin; however, because its GP response was statistically grouped with S-Hydropriming.24 h and S-Sup49.7 h, this effect should be interpreted as treatment- and duration-dependent rather than as a uniquely superior effect of Sup1 alone. This fraction may include active chitinase, other secreted bacterial proteins, metabolites, and chitin hydrolysis products. Song et al. found that hydrolysates from colloidal chitin, containing GlcNAc and N, N’-diacetylchitobiose, stimulated rice and wheat seedling growth in a concentration-dependent way [43]. Thus, the response observed for S-Sup1.3 h may be attributed to a mixture of bacterial metabolites, secreted proteins, and chitin-derived hydrolysis products rather than to chitinase alone.

This interpretation is also consistent with studies showing that microbial culture filtrates or cell-free supernatants can influence seed germination and seedling growth under stress, but that their effects are often highly dependent on concentration, exposure time, and biological source [44–46]. For example, a phytohormone-rich culture filtrate from Nocardioides sp. improved salinity responses in wheat seedlings [44]. In contrast, cell-free supernatants from salt-tolerant Bacillus amyloliquefaciens and other Bacillus strains improved germination or seed vigor only at specific concentrations or dilutions [45, 46]. In addition, bio-priming with selected Bacillus isolates enhances germination and seedling growth under fungal pressure, highlighting the context-dependent effects of extracellular bacteria [47]. Future work should incorporate enzyme-specific controls, including purified chitinase, heat-inactivated supernatant, protease-treated supernatant, and defined GlcNAc/chitooligosaccharide controls, as well as pathogen-challenge assays, to assess chitinase-dependent and chitinase-independent mechanisms.

The partial improvement observed for selected live bacterial treatments is also consistent with earlier studies showing that halotolerant or plant-growth-promoting Bacillus strains can improve wheat or cereal seedling performance under salinity. Ayaz et al. reported that salt-tolerant Bacillus strains improved wheat growth traits and phytohormone regulation under salt stress [48]. Similarly, biopriming of durum wheat with endophytic diazotrophic bacteria, including B. subtilis MA17, enhanced growth and salinity tolerance [49]. Studies also found that Bacillus megaterium and Bacillus aryabhattai PM34 enhanced wheat’s salinity tolerance [50–52], highlighting the potential of Bacillus-based biopriming while underscoring the need for strain-specific validation.

Live bacterial treatments may differ from extracellular supernatant treatments because seed biopriming can allow beneficial bacteria to adhere to or enter seeds and acclimatize under the prevailing conditions before sowing [8, 40]. Viable cells can remain on the seed surface or emerging radicle, producing bioactive compounds during germination. In contrast, extracellular culture supernatants are cell-free fractions of bacterial media containing secreted bioactive compounds. Their effects depend on the quantity, composition, and exposure duration of these products, rather than on active colonization or ongoing metabolite production [45, 46]. The observed responses can also be discussed in the context of reported Bacillus-mediated salt-stress mitigation pathways. Previous studies have shown that Bacillus-based seed biopriming or inoculation can improve salinity tolerance by regulating antioxidant activity, adjusting osmotic stress, modulating phytohormone levels, mobilizing nutrients, and enhancing seedling vigor [48–50]. For example, Bacillus sp. MGW9 improved maize seed germination under salt stress by increasing proline, soluble sugars, relative water content, and antioxidant enzyme activities, including SOD, CAT, POD, and APX, while reducing MDA accumulation [53]. In durum wheat, endophytic Bacillus biopriming improved salinity tolerance and was associated with plant-growth-promoting traits, including IAA, siderophore, nitrogen fixation, phosphate solubilization, and hydrolytic enzyme activities [49]. These studies suggest that ROS scavenging, osmolyte accumulation, improved water status, phytohormone-related regulation, and nutrient-mobilization mechanisms are plausible pathways by which live Bacillus treatments may support seedling establishment under salinity [49, 53, 54]. However, bacterial colonization was not measured in this study, and traits like IAA production, ACC-deaminase activity, siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, EPS production, and biofilm formation were not characterized. Thus, viable-cell-associated mechanisms for the LPW isolates are plausible but not demonstrated.

Our results also highlight that extracellular supernatant effects are likely concentration-, composition-, and duration-dependent. Sup1.3 h was beneficial for germination under both normal and saline conditions, and under salinity, it also showed the strongest combined growth and VI-I response among treatments with available seedling growth data, whereas longer supernatant priming durations were generally less favorable. This pattern is consistent with previous reports showing that bacterial culture supernatants can improve germination or seedling growth only within specific concentration or exposure-time windows [45, 46]. Therefore, the extracellular products produced by S1 may have a narrow beneficial window, and excessive exposure or differences in supernatant composition may reduce the positive response. Future work could optimize extracellular supernatant treatment by evaluating dilution series, exposure times, and the effects of active versus inactivated fractions. This would help clarify the roles of enzymatic activity, bacterial metabolites, and chitin-derived products on seed responses. However, the positive effect of S-Sup1.3 h should not be solely attributed to chitinase, as Sup1 may contain active chitinase, other bacterial products, and soluble chitin-derived hydrolysis products.

Previous seed-protection study supports this cautious interpretation: chitinase improved germination mainly under fungal infection, whereas chitinase alone did not affect germination in the absence of fungal challenge [22]. Thus, the present pathogen-free salinity assay does not establish that chitinase directly improves wheat salt tolerance. Instead, it suggests that extracellular products generated by chitinolytic Bacillus during growth on colloidal chitin may influence early seed responses and should be further dissected in future mechanistic assays.

This research has several limitations. Key biochemical indicators in plants, such as ROS levels and antioxidant enzyme activity, were not evaluated, leaving the mechanisms behind improved seedling performance under salinity unresolved. Bacterial identification was based on partial 16 S rRNA sequencing, which does not provide definitive species-level resolution. The live-cell inoculum was standardized by McFarland turbidity to an estimated 10⁸ CFU/mL, but viable plate-count calibration and dose–response testing were not performed; therefore, the optimal inoculum dose remains unknown. In addition, seedling growth and biomass traits were not available for all saline treatment combinations, which limits direct comparison of growth-related responses across all treatments under salinity. The experiments were conducted under controlled conditions in Petri dishes, focusing on germination and seedling traits, so the findings are preliminary. To confirm the agronomic relevance of these treatments, further research involving soil-based, greenhouse, and field experiments, along with long-term growth and yield assessments, is needed.

Conclusion

This study explored shrimp shell waste as a source for isolating chitinolytic bacteria from the Lipar Pink Wetland, and the recovered isolates showed diverse biochemical and seed-priming responses. Among the extracellular fractions, Sup80 showed the strongest salt-tolerant enzymatic profile, whereas Sup1.3 h produced the strongest seedling response under salinity. Seed-priming experiments indicated that salinity inhibited wheat germination and seedling growth, although some treatments partially mitigated these effects. Under normal conditions, hydropriming, selected live bacterial treatments, and N-Sup1.3 h were among the strongest treatments, whereas under salinity S-Sup1.3 h showed the strongest numerical overall response for germination and seedling growth, while being statistically grouped with S-Hydropriming.24 h and S-Sup49.7 h for GP. The beneficial response to Sup1.3 h may reflect the combined effects of its chitinase-active extracellular culture fraction, other secreted bacterial products, and chitin-derived hydrolysis products. These Bacillus isolates and their extracellular culture fractions provide a promising preliminary basis for further research on wheat biopriming under saline conditions, underscoring the need for strain-level genomic identification and biosafety screening, including assessments of virulence-associated genes, toxin-related genes, hemolytic activity, and pathogenicity-related traits, before any agricultural application is considered. Future work should prioritize the optimization of live-cell and extracellular-supernatant formulations and the mechanistic validation of plant stress responses to determine whether these treatments can be translated into practical wheat biopriming strategies for saline agriculture in arid and semi-arid regions.
