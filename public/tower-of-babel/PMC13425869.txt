Estimation of cycloplegic spherical refraction from non-cycloplegic clinical and biometric data in children using machine learning: a retrospective pilot study for screening triage
Authors: Huixia Hua, Daohuan Kang, Caiping Shi, Fan Qi, Andrzej Grzybowski, Wen Sun, Kai Jin, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate non-cycloplegic clinical and biometric models for estimating cycloplegic spherical power (DS), spherical equivalent (SE), and astigmatic power-vector components (J0 and J45) in children, and to assess their utility as a preliminary screening-triage aid rather than a substitute for cycloplegic refraction.MethodsThis retrospective pilot study included 590 children aged 2.4–14.8 years who underwent cycloplegic refraction at the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The children were randomly divided at the patient level into training and held-out test sets at a ratio of 7:3, ensuring that both eyes from the same child remained in the same set. Astigmatism was expressed using J0 and J45. Power-vector components were calculated separately for non-cycloplegic and post-cycloplegic refraction, and post-cycloplegic J0/J45 served as prediction targets. Seven algorithms were screened; DT, BPNN, GPR, and LR were selected for clinically oriented evaluation using regression metrics, error thresholds, screening indices, subgroup analyses, GEE analysis, and Bland-Altman plots.ResultsAge, axial length, corneal astigmatism, corneal power, and UCVA were the main predictors for spherical refraction. After patient-level splitting, DT, BPNN, GPR, and LR showed moderate performance for DS and SE. DT achieved RMSE/R2 values of 0.776 D/0.819 for DS and 0.781 D/0.823 for SE; BPNN achieved 0.752 D/0.802 for DS and 0.759 D/0.798 for SE. GPR achieved 0.921 D/0.806 for DS and 0.941 D/0.800 for SE, while LR achieved 0.876 D/0.824 for DS and 0.949 D/0.797 for SE. The vector-based analysis showed interpretable J0 outputs for several models, including GPR (R2 = 0.671), but J45 was weak across models (R2 approximately − 0.08 to 0.06). For SE-based screening, GPR achieved sensitivity/specificity of 94.09%/86.46% for myopia and 80.00%/98.20% for hyperopia. Only 53.75% of GPR SE estimates were within +/-0.50 D.ConclusionsNon-cycloplegic clinical and biometric variables provided moderate estimates of cycloplegic DS and SE after patient-level data partitioning. The J0/J45 vector analysis indicated partial predictability of J0, whereas J45 prediction was clinically unreliable. This approach should be regarded as an exploratory triage aid for spherical refractive estimation rather than a substitute for cycloplegic refraction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-05082-3.

Introduction

Uncorrected refractive error is one of the most common and treatable causes of visual impairment worldwide, and the increasing prevalence of childhood myopia has intensified the demand for scalable pediatric refractive services [1–4]. Accurate refractive assessment in children is clinically important because undercorrected or delayed correction may affect visual development, school performance, amblyopia risk, and long-term ocular health. In routine pediatric ophthalmology, cycloplegic refraction is the clinical reference standard because accommodation can substantially bias non-cycloplegic measurements, especially in younger children, hyperopic children, and children with high accommodative tone [5–7].

Despite its diagnostic value, cycloplegic refraction is difficult to implement at scale. Drug instillation, waiting time for adequate cycloplegia, transient photophobia, near blur, and parental concerns can reduce workflow efficiency in busy clinics and screening settings. These practical barriers have motivated interest in non-cycloplegic approaches that can help identify children who most need cycloplegic assessment within established pediatric screening frameworks [8]. Such approaches should not be interpreted as replacements for pharmacological cycloplegia. Rather, their appropriate role is preliminary triage: estimating risk, prioritizing referrals, and reducing unnecessary waiting for low-risk children when clinical context permits.

Ocular biometry offers a practical starting point for refractive estimation. Axial length, corneal power, and lens-related changes are central to emmetropization and myopia progression [9–13]. A longer eye generally shifts refraction toward myopia, while corneal and lenticular powers modify the final refractive endpoint. Machine learning can combine these variables with functional screening information, such as uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), but the underlying biology is not mysterious. Because much of the spherical signal is expected to be monotonic and partly linear, a complex model should be compared with an interpretable baseline rather than assumed to be better.

Recent ophthalmic machine-learning studies have shown that non-cycloplegic data can contain useful information for predicting myopia status, future myopia onset, or cycloplegic spherical equivalent [14–16]. The promise is real, but so are the weak points: performance varies across cohorts, external validation is often limited, and clinically important subgroups may be thinly represented. For pediatric screening, the central question is not only whether a model performs well on average. It is whether the model avoids missing children who still need cycloplegic confirmation.

Astigmatism raises additional methodological challenges. Refractive astigmatism is not defined only by cylinder magnitude; it also depends on axis. Therefore, scalar cylinder alone is not an ideal modeling target, and power-vector components J0 and J45 provide a more appropriate framework for statistical analysis [17–19]. Furthermore, clinically useful screening models require more than RMSE and R2. Sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, false-negative rates, error thresholds, subgroup performance, and agreement plots are needed to judge whether model errors are acceptable for real-world triage [8, 20–24].

This retrospective pilot study examined whether routinely available non-cycloplegic clinical and biometric data could be used to estimate post-cycloplegic spherical power (DS), spherical equivalent (SE), and astigmatic vector components (J0 and J45) in children. Beyond conventional regression metrics, we assessed clinically relevant error thresholds, SE-based identification of myopia and hyperopia, refractive-category errors, subgroup performance by age and axial length, GEE-based robustness, and Bland-Altman agreement. Model development used patient-level partitioning to reduce paired-eye leakage. We considered the model as a possible aid for screening triage and referral prioritization, rather than as a tool for diagnosis or spectacle prescription without cycloplegia.

Methods

Study design

The cohort comprised 590 children (1122 eyes with available complete records) aged 2.4–14.8 years who presented to the Ophthalmology Outpatient Clinic at the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, between March and May 2025 and underwent cycloplegic refraction. Inclusion criteria were age within the study range, complete examination records, and absence of organic ocular pathology or systemic disease known to affect refraction. Exclusion criteria included media opacity affecting measurement quality and previous ocular surgery. Because the study was retrospective, the cohort reflected routine hospital practice rather than a population-based screening sample. This design allowed rapid evaluation of the modeling concept but also introduced limitations related to referral patterns, refractive distribution, and subgroup balance. The analysis therefore focused on whether the available non-cycloplegic variables could provide clinically interpretable preliminary estimates.

Clinical and biometric measurements

Baseline variables included age, gender, and UCVA. UCVA was measured using a standard Snellen chart and converted to LogMAR for analysis. We retained UCVA because it is widely available in screening workflows and reflects functional visual status, but we explicitly recognize that it is not a structural biometric variable. UCVA can be influenced by attention, cooperation, chart familiarity, age, and existing refractive error, so models including UCVA should be described as using non-cycloplegic clinical and biometric data rather than purely biometric data.

Ocular biometry was performed under non-cycloplegic conditions using the Lenstar LS 900 (Haag-Streit, Koeniz, Switzerland). Measurements included axial length (AL), flat and steep keratometry (K1 and K2, diopters [D]), corneal astigmatism (AST=|K1-K2|), central corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and central retinal thickness. Because AL and corneal power are well-established correlates of spherical refractive status, they were considered clinically important predictors regardless of their algorithmic ranking [9–12].

Cycloplegic refraction was performed after compound tropicamide was instilled four times at 5-minute intervals, with autorefraction obtained 20 min after full dilation using the AR-1 autorefractor (Nidek Co., Ltd., Gamagori, Japan). Recorded values included spherical power (DS), cylinder (DC), cylinder axis, and spherical equivalent (SE = DS+DC/2). Although this protocol reflects a rapid real-world clinical workflow, residual accommodation may persist compared with stronger cycloplegia such as cyclopentolate or atropine [6, 7]. The outcome should therefore be interpreted as post-tropicamide cycloplegic refraction.

Astigmatism was reparameterized using power-vector notation. Cylinder and axis were transformed into J0=(-C/2) x cos(2alpha) and J45=(-C/2) x sin(2alpha), where C is cylindrical power and alpha is cylinder axis [17]. Because trigonometric functions require angular consistency, axis values recorded in degrees were converted to radians before computation. Non-cycloplegic and post-cycloplegic J0/J45 values were calculated separately; only post-cycloplegic J0 and J45 were used as prediction targets. Predicted and measured vector outputs were exported and inspected, and Bland-Altman differences were calculated as predicted minus measured values against the mean of predicted and measured values.

Feature characterization and model development

The analytical workflow of the study is summarized in Fig. 1. Feature behavior was explored using Morris screening and Sobol’ sensitivity indices [25–27]. A random-forest surrogate model with 500 trees was used for sensitivity estimation through SALib version 1.5.0. We used these analyses to prioritize and interpret candidate features, not to imply that complex feature-selection procedures are necessary for clinical practice.

Candidate predictors included age, gender, UCVA, AL, K1, K2, AST, K1 + K2, central corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, and central retinal thickness. Based on sensitivity results and clinical interpretability, age, AL, AST, K1 + K2, and UCVA were retained in the modeling pipeline. DS and SE were treated as spherical targets, while J0 and J45 were treated as astigmatic vector targets.

Seven algorithms were initially screened: decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), support vector regression (SVR), k-nearest-neighbor regression (KNN), backpropagation neural network (BPNN), gaussian process regression (GPRBN), and linear regression (LR). All computational analyses were performed using MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), utilizing the ‘fitrtree’, ‘TreeBagger’, ‘fitrsvm’, ‘fitrknn’, the Deep Learning Toolbox, ‘fitrgp’, and ‘fitglm’ functions, respectively, from the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox and Deep Learning Toolbox. A fixed random seed of 42 was used for patient-level 7:3 data partitioning and model screening where applicable. Feature standardization was applied to models requiring distance- or kernel-based computations (SVR, KNN, and GPR), using the mean and standard deviation estimated from the training set only. Tree-based models and LR were trained on raw features without standardization.

RF was implemented using 100 trees with out-of-bag error monitoring and default hyper-parameters. SVM used a Gaussian kernel and hyper-parameters were tuned by 5-fold cross-validation within the training set over a grid of 9 Constraint values and three Epsilon values. BPNN was implemented as a single-hidden-layer feedforward network with 10 hidden neurons, Levenberg-Marquardt back propagation, and mean-squared-error loss. Input and output were normalized to the 0–1 range. The model denoted as GPR in this study was implemented using Gaussian process regression for continuous refractive outcomes. Model fitting was performed exclusively within the training set. A squared-exponential kernel was adopted, and the kernel-scale hyperparameter was selected through 5-fold cross-validation within the training set. For each outcome, the final model was retrained on the full training set using the optimal kernel scale. Feature standardization was estimated from the training set only, and the same standardization parameters were then applied to the held-out test set. DT used 5-fold cross-validation within the training set. KNN used a custom Euclidean-distance implementation; the optimal K was chosen by 5-fold cross-validation within the training set by minimizing RMSE, with features standardized. LR was fitted with no regularization or standardization.

DT, BPNN, GPR, and LR were selected for clinically oriented analyses because these models showed comparatively stronger or more interpretable performance after patient-level partitioning. LR was included as a transparent comparator because the relationship between AL, corneal power, and spherical refractive error is expected to be largely monotonic and partly linear.

Training strategy and statistical evaluation

Model development used patient-level partitioning. The 590 children were randomly divided into a training set (70%, n = 413 children) and a held-out test set (30%, n = 177 children) using a fixed random seed. Both eyes from the same child were kept within the same set. This strategy was adopted to reduce optimistic performance estimation caused by highly correlated fellow-eye measurements being distributed across different data splits. All preprocessing, feature standardization, model fitting, hyperparameter tuning, and model selection were confined to the training set, with internal cross-validation used where applicable. The held-out test set was used only for final evaluation.

Model performance was assessed on the held-out test set using RMSE, MAE, and R2. Clinical interpretability was assessed by calculating the proportion of predictions within +/-0.25 D, +/-0.50 D, and +/-1.00 D. SE-based screening performance was evaluated using measured cycloplegic SE as the reference. Myopia was defined as SE <= -0.50 D and hyperopia as SE >= +0.50 D. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and false-negative rate were calculated.

Prediction errors were further stratified by refractive category, age group (< = 6 years and > 6 years), and axial-length strata (< 22 mm, 22–24 mm, and > 24 mm). Because both eyes were retained within partitions when available, supplementary GEE analyses with exchangeable working correlation and robust standard errors were performed to account for residual within-child correlation [28]. Agreement was assessed using Bland-Altman plots and 95% limits of agreement [29].

We interpreted the results in the context of screening triage. An SE error within 1.00 D may be acceptable for broad prioritization, but it is not precise enough for prescribing glasses or for deciding that cycloplegic refraction can be omitted. For this reason, threshold-based and classification-based results were considered clinically important, not merely supplementary. We paid particular attention to false-negative results, because missed hyperopia or amblyopia-risk refractive error may be more harmful than unnecessary referral. These analyses were used to assess internal robustness and clinical interpretability, not external validation.

Results

Cohort and feature characteristics

The cohort included children across a broad developmental age range (2.4–14.8 years). The descriptive refractive profile was myopia-predominant, with 808 myopic eyes (72.0%), 133 emmetropic eyes (11.9%), 181 hyperopic eyes (16.1%), and 135 high-astigmatism eyes (12.0%) (Supplementary Figure S1). This distribution is clinically relevant because average model performance may conceal worse performance in smaller but important subgroups, particularly hyperopic children and children with higher astigmatism.

Sensitivity analyses showed that age, AL, AST, K1 + K2, and UCVA contributed more strongly to DS and SE than to J0 and J45. As illustrated in Fig. 2, Morris screening and Sobol’ indices both suggested that spherical refractive components were better explained by available non-cycloplegic variables, whereas astigmatic vector components had weaker and less stable predictor-response relationships. This pattern supported separate interpretation of spherical estimation and astigmatism prediction.

Clinical screening and agreement analyses

Clinical screening results for the retained models are summarized in Table 2; Fig. 3. For SE-based myopia detection, GPR achieved sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and false-negative rate of 94.09%, 86.46%, 94.49%, 85.57%, and 5.91%, respectively. For hyperopia detection, the corresponding values were 80.00%, 98.20%, 89.80%, 96.13%, and 20.00%. LR showed sensitivity/specificity of 89.45%/84.38% for myopia and 89.09%/96.76% for hyperopia. The confusion matrices show that false-negative results still occurred, particularly for hyperopia. These results suggest useful triage potential in selected settings but still do not justify omitting cycloplegic refraction in children with suspected hyperopia, amblyopia risk, strabismus, or unreliable visual acuity.

Error-threshold analyses are reported in Supplementary Table S1. For SE, 30.03%, 53.75%, and 79.88% of GPR predictions were within +/-0.25 D, +/-0.50 D, and +/-1.00 D, respectively. For LR, the corresponding proportions were 31.53%, 50.15%, and 80.78%. Refractive-category analysis quantified errors in clinically important subgroups: the hyperopia RMSE/MAE was 1.0010/0.8045 D for GPR and 0.8771/0.6728 D for LR, while the corresponding myopia values were 0.9594/0.6406 D and 0.9990/0.6709 D (Supplementary Table S2).

The classification metrics and error thresholds therefore support a cautious interpretation. Patient-level splitting reduced the risk of inflated estimates from paired-eye leakage, and the vector-based analysis produced interpretable GPR J0 behavior. Nevertheless, approximately half of SE predictions still fell outside +/-0.50 D, and J45 was poorly explained. These findings are compatible with triage support but not with refractive prescribing or any decision to omit cycloplegic refraction when clinically indicated.

Subgroup analyses suggested less stable spherical performance in younger children and short-AL eyes, although subgroup estimates were limited by sample size. For GPR, SE RMSE was 1.5552 D in children aged < = 6 years and 0.8151 D in older children; it was 1.2629 D in eyes with AL < 22 mm, compared with 0.6961 D for AL 22–24 mm and 1.1041 D for AL > 24 mm (Supplementary Table S3). GEE analysis confirmed strong associations of AL, K1 + K2, and UCVA with DS and SE after accounting for within-child correlation (all P < 0.001). AL was also associated with J0 (P = 0.0040), whereas no included predictor showed a significant association with J45 (Supplementary Table S4).

Visual diagnostics supported the numerical findings. As shown in Fig. 4, the GPR J0 outputs showed a dispersed prediction pattern rather than near-constant behavior, and the Bland-Altman plot showed a conventional mean-difference distribution. The J0 pattern for GPR and LR differed, consistent with their different model behavior and R2 values. The full Bland-Altman agreement panels are shown in Fig. 5, and full prediction-diagnostic plots are provided in Supplementary Figure S2.

Discussion

Main interpretation

We examined whether routinely available non-cycloplegic clinical and biometric variables could estimate post-cycloplegic refractive outcomes in children. The principal finding is that these variables contain a clinically useful but incomplete signal for spherical refraction. DS and SE were estimated with moderate accuracy, whereas the performance remained insufficient for prescribing or for deciding that cycloplegic refraction can be omitted. This pattern is biologically plausible because AL, corneal power, and visual acuity are closely linked to spherical refractive status [9–13]. It also agrees with recent machine-learning studies showing that pediatric myopia status or refractive outcomes can be partially inferred from non-cycloplegic data [14–16]. The important distinction is that our analysis was framed around triage rather than replacement: average regression performance was interpreted together with error thresholds, false-negative results, subgroup performance, GEE analysis, and Bland-Altman agreement.

The findings should also be read in light of the long-standing evidence that non-cycloplegic refraction is vulnerable to accommodative bias in children. Cycloplegic refraction remains the reference method for pediatric refractive assessment, especially in younger children and hyperopic children [5–8, 30]. Population-based and school-based studies have shown that non-cycloplegic measurements can overestimate myopia and underestimate hyperopia because accommodation is not fully relaxed [31, 32]. Our results are consistent with this literature. Hyperopia detection had a higher false-negative rate than myopia detection, and refractive-category analyses showed non-negligible error in hyperopic eyes. Therefore, the model may help prioritize children for cycloplegic referral, but it cannot safely rule out clinically meaningful hyperopia or amblyopia-risk refractive error.

Compared with previous artificial-intelligence studies in refractive prediction, this study adds several clinically oriented elements. Some recent models have focused on predicting myopia onset, myopia status, or refractive error from non-cycloplegic clinical data or retinal images [14–16, 33]. These studies support the broader concept that ocular and clinical features encode information about refractive status. However, many such studies emphasize aggregate metrics or classification accuracy. In contrast, we examined whether the estimates are usable for a specific workflow question: screening triage before cycloplegia. This is why the present analysis reports absolute-error thresholds, myopia and hyperopia screening indices, category-specific errors, subgroup behavior, and agreement plots. These outputs are more clinically informative than RMSE or R2 alone.

The comparison between more flexible algorithms and LR is also informative. GPR, DT, BPNN, and LR all performed reasonably for DS and SE, and the difference between complex and simpler models was not large enough to justify a strong claim of algorithmic superiority. This is compatible with the known monotonic relationship among AL, corneal power, and spherical refractive status. In a small or moderate-sized medical dataset, a transparent model with similar performance may be preferable for clinical explanation, quality control, and future external validation. The value of machine learning in this setting may therefore lie less in replacing clinical reasoning and more in combining routinely collected variables into a reproducible triage estimate.

Astigmatism modeling

Astigmatism required separate interpretation. Prior refractive prediction studies often focus on spherical equivalent or binary myopia status, but astigmatism is axis-dependent and cannot be adequately represented by cylinder magnitude alone. Power-vector analysis provides a more appropriate statistical framework because J0 and J45 incorporate cylinder magnitude and axis [17–19]. In this study, non-cycloplegic and post-cycloplegic vectors were calculated separately, angular units were handled consistently, and post-cycloplegic J0/J45 were used as prediction targets.

GPR showed plausible J0 prediction behavior, whereas J45 remained weak across models. This result is clinically reasonable. J45 reflects oblique astigmatic structure and is likely influenced by factors not captured by the current feature set, including posterior corneal astigmatism, lenticular astigmatism, eyelid forces, measurement variability in axis, and dynamic accommodation. Thus, the vector analysis indicates that J0 may be partly predictable from the available variables, whereas J45 remains inadequately modeled.

Clinical and methodological implications

Clinically, the data support a cautious role for non-cycloplegic estimation in triage. In high-volume clinics or school-screening programs, a model with high sensitivity for myopia could help identify children who should be prioritized for cycloplegic assessment. However, the false-negative rate for hyperopia and the wide Bland-Altman limits show that safety cannot be assumed. Any practical implementation should therefore use conservative referral thresholds and automatic safeguards for preschool children, suspected amblyopia, strabismus, high hyperopia, abnormal visual behavior, or unreliable UCVA.

Methodologically, the study reinforces several principles for prediction modeling in ophthalmology. First, reporting only average RMSE or R2 is not enough when the intended use is clinical triage. Threshold performance, false-negative rates, calibration, subgroup behavior, and agreement should be reported alongside regression metrics. Second, patient-level partitioning is essential when both eyes are available, because fellow-eye correlation can otherwise lead to optimistic performance estimates. Third, a model that performs well in a hospital cohort may not generalize to community screening, where disease spectrum, cooperation, and referral thresholds differ. These points align with broader recommendations for transparent reporting, interpretability, and bias assessment in medical prediction models [20–24].

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. This was a retrospective single-center study from a hospital cohort, so the findings may not apply directly to community or school-screening settings. Children referred for cycloplegic refraction may differ from screened children in refractive distribution, cooperation, and symptoms; external validation in a community or school-screening cohort is therefore required before screening use. Patient-level splitting reduced leakage by keeping fellow eyes within the same set, but both eyes were still included when available, and residual within-child correlation was addressed through GEE analysis. Subgroup sizes were small, especially for younger children and eyes with short AL. Compound tropicamide may leave residual accommodation, particularly in younger or hyperopic children. Finally, the model did not include posterior corneal astigmatism, lens astigmatism, accommodation measurements, or higher-order optical features, which likely contributed to weak J45 prediction.

Conclusions

Non-cycloplegic clinical and biometric variables provided moderate estimates of cycloplegic DS and SE in this retrospective pilot cohort after patient-level data partitioning. The J0/J45 vector analysis indicated partial predictability of J0 in selected models, whereas J45 was not reliably predicted. The approach should therefore be regarded as an exploratory triage aid for spherical refractive estimation rather than a substitute for cycloplegic refraction. Larger multicenter studies with external validation, prespecified referral thresholds, and stronger astigmatism-related measurements are required.
