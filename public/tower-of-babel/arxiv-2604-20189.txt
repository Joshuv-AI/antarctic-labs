New bounds on Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of monomial curves and application to sumsets
Le Tuan Hoa, Doan Quang Tien

Abstract
A monomial curve C C is defined by a sequence of coprime integers 0 = a 0 < a 1 < ⋯ < a k = : d 0=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}=:d . One gap of this sequence is a i + 1 − a i − 1 a_{i+1}-a_{i}-1 . Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine bound (1983) says that reg ⁡ ( C ) ≤ d − k + 2 \mathrm{reg}(C)\leq d-k+2 , which is equal to the sum of all gaps plus 2. L’vovsky (1996) showed that it is enough to take the sum of two largest gaps plus 2. In this paper, under some specific conditions, we give several new bounds which are better than L’vovsky’s bound. Our method relies on the study of Apéry sets and Frobenius numbers. From this we can give new criteria to check the (arithmetically) Cohen–Macaulay and Buchsbaum property of C C . Algorithms are provided to check these properties as well as to compute reg ⁡ ( C ) \mathrm{reg}(C) and other invariants. We also give an application to study the structure of sumsets. Keywords and phrases. Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity, Monomial curve, Frobenius number, sumset. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 13D45. Secondary 13H10, 14M25, 11B13.

Introduction

A projective monomial curve
C:=C(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\subset{\mathbb{P}}_{K}^{k}
is defined by a sequence of coprime integers
0<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}=:d
. From the general result of
[
11
]
on the Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of curves, we already know that
\mathrm{reg}(C)\leq d-k+2
. Because of the importance of the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity, it is still of great interest to find better bounds for
\mathrm{reg}(C)
. On one side,
C
is so special, that one can hope to get a better bound. A gap of the above sequence is
a_{i+1}-a_{i}-1
for
i=0,1,\ldots,k-1
, where
a_{0}:=0
. Then
d-k
is the sum of all gaps. L’vovsky
[
18
]
in 1996 proved that it is enough to take the largest and second largest gaps
\lambda_{\max}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}
:
\mathrm{reg}(C)\leq\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}+2
. This is a rather good bound. However, under additional assumptions, one can provide much better bounds, or even compute it, see, e.g.,
[
4
,
12
,
17
]
. Our first goal is to provide further bounds for some rather broader classes of monomial curves.

On the other hand, the coordinate ring of
C(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is isomorphic to the semigroup ring
K[S]
, where
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
is generated by elements
(a_{i},d-a_{i})
,
i=0,1,\ldots,k
(
a_{0}:=0
) – a combinatorial subject. It is therefore natural to ask if one can give a combinatorial proof for the above mentioned results? A first combinatorial proof of Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine bound for
C
was provided only in 2014, see
[
20
]
. Recently, it was shown in
[
8
, Section 5]
that this bound can be deduced from a result
[
10
, Theorem 2]
on the so-called sumsets structure; thus we have a second combinatorial proof. For the L’vovsky’s bound, the question remains open.

Our method is purely combinatorial. It is based on the study of Apéry sets and related invariants such as Frobenius numbers and degrees of elements in a numerical semigroup. So it provides a bridge between this problem and some classical aspects in Number Theory. Moreover, our approach leads to new characterization of the (arithmetically) Cohen–Macaulay and Buchsbaum property of
C
. This is the second aim of this paper. Note that this problem is a particular case of a more general problem of determining the Cohen-Macaulay property of the so-called simplicial affine semigroup rings. The latter problem was studied earlier by many authors, see e.g.,
[
9
,
23
,
25
]
. The curve case was studied in
[
3
,
24
,
25
,
14
]
.

Using the Apéry sets and degrees of its elements, we can describe the first local cohomology module
H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])
explicitly, at least from the computational point of view, see Lemma
3.4
and Theorem
4.4
. From that we can give criteria for the Cohen–Macaulay and Buchsbaum property of
C
, see Theorem
3.5
. Using these criteria, we can classify some new classes of monomial curves, see Proposition
3.7
, Corollary
3.8
and Corollary
4.9
. In
[
14
]
, the Cohen–Macaulay property of
C
is studied via Gröbner bases.

Back to the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity, this number is defined by two invariants:
a_{1}(K[S])
and
a_{2}(K[S])
(see the definition at the beginning of Section
4
). Using Apery sets one can give a formula to compute

(see Lemma

) or to bound it in terms of Frobenius numbers (see Lemma

is to bound

of

we can give lower and upper bounds on

. Unfortunately, the obtained bounds are not explicit in terms of

, and therefore we cannot reprove L’vovsky’s bound. However, using this theorem, under some additional assumptions, we can provide explicit bounds on
\mathrm{reg}(C)
, which are better than L’vovsky’s bound, see Proposition
4.11
and Theorem
4.13
. It is interesting to mention that Theorem
4.13
is based on an improvement of a rather old bound on the Frobenius number given in
[
22
]
.

The structure Theorem

2
Frobenius number and Apéry set

In this section we recall the notions of Frobenius number and Apéry set. We also provide a new bound on Frobenius number which will be used in Section
4
to bound the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity.

We denote the set of non-negative integers and the set of integers by
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}
, respectively. If
a
is a real number, we denote by
\lfloor a\rfloor
the largest integer not exceeding
a
and by
\lceil a\rceil
the smallest integer
n
such that
n\geq a
. The notation
[p,q]
means the set
\{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\mid p\leq n\leq q\}
, where
p\leq q
are integers. If
A
is a subset of an additive semigroup
S
and
n
is a positive integer, we denote by
nA
the sum
A+\cdots+A\subseteq S
(
n
times).

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}
be a set of integers
0<\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{k}
with
\mathrm{gcd}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})=1
, where
k\geq 2
. In this paper we always assume that all coefficients
x_{i}
in a sum
\sum_{i}x_{i}\alpha_{i}
are non-negative integers.
We denote by
\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle:=\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\alpha_{i}\mid x_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
the numerical semigroup generated by
{\mathcal{A}}
. For each
0\leq i\leq\alpha_{k}-1
, let

\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i)=\min\{\alpha\in\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle\mid\alpha\equiv i\bmod\alpha_{k}\}.

For convenience, we also set
\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\alpha_{k}):=0
. The set

\mathrm{Ap}_{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\{\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(0),\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(1),\ldots,\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\alpha_{k}-1)\}

is called
Apéry set
of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
The greatest integer number not belonging to
\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
is called
Frobenius number
and is denoted by
F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
or
F({\mathcal{A}})
. Note that
F({\mathcal{A}})+1
is equal to the conductor of the semigroup
\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
. It is clear that

F({\mathcal{A}})=F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})=\max\{\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(1),\ldots,\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\alpha_{k}-1)\}-\alpha_{k}.

More generally, let
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}
. If
t_{0}=0,\ldots,t_{\alpha-1}\in\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
such that
t_{i}\equiv i\bmod\alpha
, then

F({\mathcal{A}})\leq\max\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{\alpha-1}\}-\alpha.

(1)

We often use the following formula

\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i)-\alpha_{k}\leq F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}).

(2)

Note that for a fixed
i
one can use Integer Programming to compute
\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i)
:
\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i)=i+n\alpha_{k}
, where
n
is the value of the following problem:

\begin{array}[]{cc}&n=\min x_{k}\\
{\mathrm{subject\ to}}&\begin{cases}x_{1}\alpha_{1}+\cdots+x_{k-1}\alpha_{k-1}=i+x_{k}\alpha_{k},\\
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}.\end{cases}\end{array}

However, the computation of Frobenius numbers is in general a NP-hard problem. Even in the case
k=3
, various polynomial time algorithms to compute
F({\mathcal{A}})
are known but none lead to an explicit formula. The interested readers can consult the book
[
21
]
for more information and references. In the Appendix we give Algorithm 1 to compute the Apéry set, from which we can also compute
F({\mathcal{A}})
.

For our application, we need good upper bounds on
F({\mathcal{A}})
. A simple bound is known as Schur’s bound proved in
[
2
]

F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\leq(\alpha_{1}-1)(\alpha_{k}-1)-1.

(3)

The equality holds if
k=2
. We now recall a bound obtained by Selmer. The numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are said to be
independent
if no
\alpha_{i},\ 2\leq i\leq k
, can be written as a sum
\sum_{j\leq i-1}x_{j}\alpha_{j}
. Analyzing the proof of
[
6
, Theorem 1]
, Selmer observed that if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are independent, then for each
i\leq\alpha_{1}-1
, there is
t_{i}\in\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
such that
t_{i}\equiv i\bmod\alpha_{1}
,
t_{i}=\sum_{j\leq k}x_{j}\alpha_{j}
and
\sum_{j\leq k}x_{j}\leq 2\lfloor\alpha_{1}/k\rfloor
. From this he got

F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\leq 2\alpha_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k}\right\rfloor-\alpha_{1},

provided that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are independent. In fact, this bound still holds under a weaker assumption as follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
\alpha_{i}\not\equiv\alpha_{j}\bmod\alpha_{1}
for all
i\neq j
. Then

F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\leq 2\alpha_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k}\right\rfloor-\alpha_{1}.

Proof.

For the convenience of the reader, we give here a sketch of proof. Let
m:=\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k}\right\rfloor
and
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\{0,\overline{\alpha}_{2},\ldots,\overline{\alpha}_{k}\}
the set of residues of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
modulo
\alpha_{1}
. We can consider
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
as a subset of the group
{\mathbb{Z}}/\alpha_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
By the assumption, the number of elements
\sharp\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}=k
. Using this notation, it is shown in the proof of
[
6
, Theorem 1]
that
m\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}+m\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
contains a complete residues system modulo
\alpha_{1}
. Then one can choose
t_{i}\in(2m){\mathcal{A}}
such that
t_{i}\equiv i\bmod\alpha_{1}
. Since
t_{i}\leq 2m\alpha_{k}
, and by (
1
),

F({\mathcal{A}})\leq\max\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{\alpha_{1}-1}\}-\alpha_{1}
, the statement follows.
∎

For an example,
\left\{5,7,9,16\right\}
satisfy the assumption of the above lemma, but they are dependent. We can reformulate the above result as the following:

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that the number of residues of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
modulo
\alpha_{1}
is
k^{\prime}
. Then

F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\leq 2\alpha_{k}\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k^{\prime}}\right\rfloor-\alpha_{1}.

Local cohomology modules of a monomial curve

We now see how one can use Apéry sets to study the local cohomology modules of the coordinate ring of a projective monomial curve
C\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{k}_{K}
, which is isomorphic to
K[s^{d},s^{a_{k-1}}t^{d-a_{k-1}},\ldots,s^{a_{1}}t^{d-a_{1}},t^{d}]

a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{k}=:d

a_{0}=0
. In order to explicitly indicate
C

C(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
. We only consider the case
k\geq 3
. Note that
C
is smooth if and only if
a_{1}=d-a_{k-1}=1
. We set
{\mathbf{e}}_{i}:=(i,d-i)

{\mathbb{N}}^{2}

\begin{array}[]{ll}S&:=\langle(a_{i},d-a_{i})\mid i=0,\ldots,k\rangle=\langle{\mathbf{e}}_{h}\mid h=a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\rangle\subset{\mathbb{N}}^{2},\\
S^{\prime}&:=\{{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\mid{\mathbf{u}}+p{\mathbf{e}}_{0}\in S\ \text{and}\ {\mathbf{u}}+p{\mathbf{e}}_{d}\in S\ \text{for some }\ p\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.\end{array}

We also use the following notations:

\begin{array}[]{ll}A_{1}&:=\{0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}=d\};\ S_{(1)}:=\langle A_{1}\rangle,\\
A_{2}&:=\{0,d-a_{k-1},\ldots,d-a_{1},d\};\ S_{(2)}:=\langle A_{2}\rangle,\\
\mathrm{Ap}_{i}&:=\mathrm{Ap}_{A_{i}\setminus\{0\}}:=\{\omega_{i}(0),\ldots,\omega_{i}(d-1)\}\ (i=1,2),\\
H&:={\mathbb{Z}}S=\{{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\mid u_{1}+u_{2}\equiv 0\bmod d\}.\end{array}

Note that

As graded modules, we have

(iii)

Let

{\mathbf{w}}_{j}=(\omega_{1}(j),\omega_{2}(d-j))\ \ (0\leq j\leq d-1).

(4)

With this notation, we can rewrite Lemma

(i)

S^{\prime}=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathbf{w}}_{d}\}+{\mathbb{N}}{\mathbf{e}}_{0}+{\mathbb{N}}{\mathbf{e}}_{d}

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{k}\}
and

Note that

Remark 3.2
.

1)

As shown in
[
3
,
7
]
, it is important to calculate or estimate
\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i))
. It is clear that if
n\in\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
and

, then
\delta(n)\leq\lfloor b/\alpha_{1}\rfloor
. However, in many cases this estimation is too big.

For any number

\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(m+n)\leq\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(m)+\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(n).

However, it could happen that
\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(m)\gg\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(m+n)
. In particular, one could have
\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(n)\gg\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(n+d)
. This causes estimation of
\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i))
complicated even if we know a bound from
\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle

\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(i)
and the degree of that bound.

For an example, let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{1,d-1,d\}
(

\delta_{\mathcal{A}}(2d-2)=2
. Since

\omega_{{\mathcal{A}}}(d-2)=d-2

, although

.

2)

In order to calculate the degree of a given number, one can use Integer Programming: if

\begin{array}[]{cc}&\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(n)=\min x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\\

However, for calculating degrees of a (larger) set of numbers, by

\begin{array}[]{ll}\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(0)&:=0,\\
\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(1)&:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ 1\in{\mathcal{A}},\\

\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(n)&=1+\min\{\delta_{{\mathcal{A}}}(n-\alpha_{i})\mid\alpha_{i}\leq n;\ i=1,2,\ldots,k\}\ \text{for}\ n\geq 2.\end{array}

{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\not\in S

i\in{\mathbb{I}}
, which implies the inclusion
\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}\subseteq S^{\prime}\setminus S
. The last inclusion now follows from (
5
).
∎

Recall that
K[S]
is called a Buchsbaum ring if
{\mathfrak{m}}H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(K[S])=0
. With the above lemma we can give a quite effective characterization for
K[S]
to be a Cohen–Macaulay or Buchsbaum ring. Based on this characterization we can construct algorithms to verify these properties, see Algorithms 2 and 3 in the Appendix.

Theorem 3.5
.

(i)

The ring
K[S]
is Cohen–Macaulay if and only if
{\mathbb{I}}=\emptyset
.

(ii)

Assume that
{\mathbb{I}}\neq\emptyset
.
If the condition

\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+1

(7)

is satisfied for all
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
, then
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(K[S])\cong\bigoplus_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}K{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
as
K
-vector spaces. In particular,
\ell(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(K[S]))=\sharp({\mathbb{I}})\leq d-k
.

(iii)

The ring
K[S]
is non-Cohen–Macaulay Buchsbaum if and only if
{\mathbb{I}}\neq\emptyset
, the condition (
7
) is satisfied for all
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
and there is no pair
\{i,j\}\subseteq{\mathbb{I}}
such that
{\mathbf{w}}_{j}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i}={\mathbf{e}}_{h}
for some
h\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}\}
.

Proof.

(i)

By Lemma
3.1
(ii)
,
K[S]
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring if and only if
S^{\prime}\setminus S=\emptyset
. By the inclusions in Lemma
3.4
, this is equivalent to
{\mathbb{I}}=\emptyset
.

(ii)

Under the assumption (
7
), by Lemma
3.3
, we can immediately see that
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{0}\in S
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{d}\in S
for all
i
. Hence, by Lemma
3.4
,
S^{\prime}\setminus S=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
. By Lemma
3.1
(ii)
, this implies
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(K[S])\cong\bigoplus_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}K{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
as
K
-vector spaces, whence
\ell(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(K[S]))=\sharp({\mathbb{I}})\leq d-k
.

(iii)

Necessity.
Assume that
K[S]
is a non Cohen–Macaulay Buchsbaum ring. By
(i)
,
{\mathbb{I}}\neq\emptyset
. Since
{\mathfrak{m}}H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(K[S])=0
and
\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}\subseteq S^{\prime}\setminus S
, by Lemma
3.1
(ii)
,
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{0}\in S
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{d}\in S
. By the inclusions in Lemma
3.4
we must have

S^{\prime}\setminus S=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
.

Fix
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
. By the definition of
{\mathbb{I}}
,
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\geq\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+1
. Since
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{0}\in S
, by Lemma
3.3
,
1+\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{0})\geq\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{0})=\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})
. Hence
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+1
.

Using the symmetry given in Lemma
3.4
in the description of
{\mathbb{I}}
, we also get
\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+1
. Thus the condition (
7
) holds.

Finally, if there are
i\neq j
from
{\mathbb{I}}
such that
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-{\mathbf{w}}_{j}={\mathbf{e}}_{h}
for some
h\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}\}
, then
{\mathbf{e}}_{h}+{\mathbf{w}}_{j}={\mathbf{w}}_{i}\not\in S
, whence
{\mathfrak{m}}H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])\neq 0
, a contradiction. So, the last condition in
(iii)
holds.

Sufficiency.
Since
\mathbb{I}\neq\emptyset
,
K[S]
is not Cohen–Macaulay ring by
(i)
. Under the condition (
7
), as observed in
(ii)
,
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{0}\in S
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{d}\in S
and
S^{\prime}\setminus S=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
. Hence, if
{\mathbf{e}}_{h}+{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\in S
for all
h\in A_{1}
and
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
, then we
{\mathfrak{m}}H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])=0
. We now assume that
K[S]
is not a Buchsbaum ring. Then there must be
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
and
{\mathbf{e}}_{h},\ h\in A_{1}
such that
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{h}\not\in S
. Since
S^{\prime}\setminus S=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{h}\in S^{\prime}
, this implies
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+{\mathbf{e}}_{h}={\mathbf{w}}_{j}
for some

. In particular

. By the definition of Apéry set, we must have

. So,
{\mathbf{w}}_{j}-{\mathbf{w}}_{i}={\mathbf{e}}_{h}
for some

is a Buchsbaum ring.

∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\epsilon=\max\{a_{1},d-a_{k-1}\}
. Assume that
i,i+1,\ldots,i+\epsilon-1\in A_{1}
for some

. Then

is a Cohen–Macaulay ring if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For all
x\in[0,i-1]\setminus A_{1}
we have
x\not\in\langle A_{1}\rangle
and
x+d\in 2A_{1}
.

(ii)

For all

we have

and
x\in 2A_{1}
.

Proof.

Under the assumption, by (
1
), we have
F_{1}\leq i-1
and
F_{2}\leq d-(i+\epsilon)
. Hence, for

,

{\mathbf{w}}_{x}=\begin{cases}(x,d-x)&\text{if}\quad x\leq i-1\ \text{and}\ x\in\langle A_{1}\rangle,\\
(x+d,d-x)&\text{if}\quad x\leq i-1\ \text{and}\ x\not\in\langle A_{1}\rangle,\\
(x,d-x)&\text{if}\quad x\geq i+\epsilon\ \text{and}\ d-x\in\langle A_{2}\rangle,\\
(x,2d-x)&\text{if}\quad x\geq i+\epsilon\ \text{and}\ d-x\not\in\langle A_{2}\rangle.\end{cases}

Assume that
K[S]
is a Cohen–Macaulay ring.
If
x\leq i-1
and
x\in\langle A_{1}\rangle
, then
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})\geq 2
, while
\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})=1
, so

. Similarly, if

and

. By Theorem

(i)
these two cases cannot happen.

In the other two cases, again by Lemma

,

implies that

and

, respectively. Since

, we must have

and

. In other words,

and
(ii)
hold.

(i)
.

We call
\lambda_{i}:=a_{i}-a_{i-1}-1
the
i
-th gap of

, where

and

. If

, it is called a

(which is real meaning of a gap!). Below is another partial result.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\}=\bigsqcup_{j=0}^{r}[b_{2j},b_{2j+1}]
, where
r\geq 1
,
0=:b_{0}\leq b_{1}<b_{2}\leq b_{3}<\cdots<b_{2r}\leq b_{2r+1}:=d
, and
b_{2j-1}+2\leq b_{2j}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
. Assume that
a_{1}=1
. If there is
1\leq j\leq r
such that
b_{2j+1}-b_{2j-1}\geq\lambda_{k}+2
(
\lambda_{k}
is the last gap, which could be zero), then
K[S]
is not a Cohen–Macaulay ring.

Proof.

Assume that
b_{2j-1}=a_{i-1}
. Then
a_{i}=b_{2j}
. First consider the case
\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{k}
. We have
\lambda_{i}=a_{i}-a_{i-1}-1>0
. Since
a_{1}=1
,
1<i<k
. Let
x:=a_{i}+a_{k-1}-d
. Then
x<a_{i}
and
x=a_{i}-1-\lambda_{k}=a_{i-1}+\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k}>a_{i-1}
. This means that
x\not\in A_{1}
. Since
a_{1}=1\in A_{1}
,
\omega_{1}(i)=i
for all
i<d
. Hence,
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})=\delta_{1}(x)\geq 2
. On the other hand,
d-x=(d-a_{i})+(d-a_{k-1})\in\langle A_{2}\rangle
, whence
{\mathbf{w}}_{x}=(x,d-x)
and
\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})=1<\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})
. By Lemma
3.4
,
x\in{\mathbb{I}}
. Hence, by Theorem
3.5
(i)
,
K[S]
is not a Cohen–Macaulay ring.

We now consider the case
\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{k}
. Then
b_{2j+1}\geq b_{2j}+1
(otherwise
\lambda_{k}+2\leq b_{2j+1}-b_{2j-1}=\lambda_{i}+1
, a contradiction). We have
a_{i-1}+1+d=y+a_{k-1}
, where

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=a_{i-1}+1+d-a_{k-1}

\displaystyle=a_{i-1}+\lambda_{k}+2=\lambda_{k}-(a_{i}-a_{i-1}-1)+a_{i}+1=\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{i}+a_{i}+1>a_{i}=b_{2j}.

By the assumption,

. Therefore
d-(a_{i-1}+1)=(d-y)+(d-a_{k-1})\in\langle A_{2}\rangle
, whence
\omega_{2}(d-x)=d-x
and
{\mathbf{w}}_{x}=(x,d-x)
, where

. Since

,

by Lemma

. Hence, by Theorem
3.5
(i)
,
K[S]
is not Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

4
Bounding Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity

The purpose of this section is to give bounds on the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
K[S]
. If
R
is a
d
-dimensional standard graded
K
-algebra with the maximal irrelevant ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}
, then
for
i\leq d
we set

a_{i}(R):=\sup\{n\mid H^{i}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(R)_{n}\neq 0\}.

The
Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity
of
R
is the number

\mathrm{reg}(R)=\max\{a_{i}(R)+i\mid 0\leq i\leq d\}.

Since
K[S]
is a domain, the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
K[S]
is the number

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=\max\{a_{1}(K[S])+1;\enskip a_{2}(K[S])+2\}.

Note that the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of the corresponding monomial curve
C
is the number
\mathrm{reg}(C):=\mathrm{reg}(K[S])+1
.

If
A_{1}=[0,d]
, it is well-known that
K[S]
is a so-called normal ring, hence a Cohen–Macaulay. The latter property also immediately follows from Lemma
3.1
(ii)
. Moreover, from Lemma
3.1
(iii)
,
H^{2}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])
has no non-negative grading, whence
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=1
. Hence,
from now on we always assume that
k<d
.

Using (
6
) we can compute
a_{2}(K[S])
.

Lemma 4.1
.

a_{2}(K[S])=\max_{0\leq i\leq d-1}\{\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}-2=\max_{0\leq i\leq d-1}\frac{\omega_{1}(i)+\omega_{2}(d-i)}{d}-2.

Denote the Frobenius number of
A_{i}\setminus\{0\}
by
F_{i}
, that is
F_{i}:=F(A_{i}\setminus\{0\})
(
i=1,2)
. By (
2
),
\omega_{i}(j)\leq F_{i}+d
for all
j\leq d-1
. Hence, a direct application of the above lemma gives the following simple upper bound:

Lemma 4.2
.

a_{2}(K[S])\leq\left\lfloor\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}\right\rfloor
.

For explicit bounds in terms of
a_{i}
, we need the following result:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\epsilon=\max\{a_{1},d-a_{k-1}\}
.

\left\lfloor\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}\right\rfloor\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}a_{1}+(d-a_{k-1})-3,\\
\epsilon-2&\text{if}\enskip i,i+1\in A_{1}\ \text{for some}\ 0\leq i\leq d-1,\\
0&\text{if}\enskip i,i+1,\ldots,i+\epsilon-1\in A_{1}\ \text{for some}\ 0\leq i\leq d-\epsilon+1.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

(i)

Using Schur’s bound (
3
), we get

\left\lfloor\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{(a_{1}-1)(d-1)+(d-a_{k-1}-1)(d-1)-2}{d}\right\rfloor=a_{1}+(d-a_{k-1})-3.

(ii)

Note that
F_{1}\leq F(a_{1},i,i+1)
and
F_{2}\leq F(d-a_{k-1},d-(i+1),d-i)
. So, applying Schur’s bound to
F(a_{1},i,i+1)
and
F(d-a_{k-1},d-(i+1),d-i)
, we get

\left\lfloor\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{(a_{1}-1)i+(d-a_{k-1}-1)(d-i-1)-2}{d}\right\rfloor\leq\epsilon-2.

(iii)

Under the assumption, by (
1
) we see that
F_{1}\leq i-1
and
F_{2}\leq d-(i+\epsilon)
. Hence, by Lemma
4.2
and the above discussion, we get

\left\lfloor\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{i+d-(i+\epsilon)-1}{d}\right\rfloor=0.

∎

Thanks to the above lemmas, it is clear that the main task in bounding
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
is to bound
a_{1}(K[S])
.
We can below give a way to compute
a_{1}(K[S])
in terms of Apéry sets and degrees of certain numbers.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
K[S]
is a non-Cohen–Macaulay ring. Then for each
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
,
\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-1\geq 0
, and for each

\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-n_{1}-1\geq 0

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathcal{L}}:=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+(n_{1}d,n_{2}d)\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}};&0\leq n_{1}\leq\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-1,\\
&n_{2}=\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-n_{1}-1\},\\

&0\leq n_{2}\leq\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-n_{1}-1\}.\end{array}

Then
H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])\cong\bigoplus_{{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}K{\mathbf{w}}
as graded
K
-vector spaces. In particular, we have

a_{1}(K[S])=\max_{{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})
and
\ell(H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])=\sharp({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})

By Lemma
3.4
,
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
. Hence, by Lemma
3.3
(iii)

\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-1\geq 0

\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}+n_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{d})=\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\geq n_{1}+\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+1=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}+n_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{d})+1

3.3
(iii)

{\mathbf{w}}_{i}+n_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{d}\not\in S

\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)=\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}+n_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{d})

3.3
(ii)

\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-n_{1}-1\geq 0

is a non-Cohen–Macaulay ring, by Theorem
3.5
(i)
,
{\mathbb{I}}\neq\emptyset
.
By Lemma
3.1
(ii)
,
H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])\cong K[S^{\prime}\setminus S]
. Hence
a_{1}(K[S])=\max_{{\mathbf{u}}\in S^{\prime}\setminus S}\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})
and
\ell(H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])=\sharp(S^{\prime}\setminus S)
. Since
\max_{u\in{\mathcal{L}}}\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})=\max_{u\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})

{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=S^{\prime}\setminus S
.

Let
{\mathbf{u}}\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
. By Lemma
3.4
,
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{w}}_{i}+(n_{1}d,n_{2}d)
for some

and
n_{1},n_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}

{\mathbf{u}}\not\in S

3.3
(ii)

n_{1}+n_{2}+\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})\leq\delta_{1}({\mathbf{u}})-1=\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-1,

whence

n_{2}\leq\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-n_{1}-1.

(8)

Similarly, by Lemma
3.3
(iii)
, we have

\displaystyle n_{1}+n_{2}+\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{2}({\mathbf{u}})-1=\delta_{2}(\omega_{2}(d-i)+n_{2}d)-1

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{2}(\omega_{2}(d-i))+n_{2}-1.

Hence

n_{1}\leq\delta_{2}(\omega_{2}(d-i))-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-1=\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-1.

(9)

Combining (
8
) and (
9
) we get
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
.

Conversely, let
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{w}}_{i}+(n_{1}d,n_{2}d)\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. By (

),
{\mathbf{u}}\in S^{\prime}
. By definition of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
,

\delta_{1}({\mathbf{u}})=\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)\geq\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+n_{1}+n_{2}+1=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})+1.

By Lemma
3.3
(ii)
,
{\mathbf{u}}\not\in S
. Hence
{\mathbf{u}}\in S^{\prime}\setminus S
, as required.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

We always have

\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}|\ i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
.

In the above theorem, besides Apéry sets, we have to calculate the degrees of certain sums
\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d
. This is not an easy task. As mentioned in Remark
3.2
, the larger
n_{1}
is, the more difficult to compute
\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)
. In the following lower and upper bounds, only two Apéry sets are involved. Therefore, these bounds are better to be applied.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{I}}\neq\emptyset
. Then

(i)

a_{1}(K[S])\geq\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}),\ \delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}-1
;

(ii)

a_{1}(K[S])\leq\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}-2
;

(iii)

a_{1}(K[S])\leq\left\lfloor\left(1-\frac{a_{1}}{d}\right)\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}+\frac{a_{k-1}}{d}\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}\right\rfloor-2
.

Proof.

(i)

Let
j\in{\mathbb{I}}
such that

\max\{\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{j}),\ \delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\}=\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i}),\ \delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}.

W.l.o.g. we may assume that
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})\geq\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})
. Let
{\mathbf{w}}:={\mathbf{w}}_{j}+(0,n_{2}d)
, where
n_{2}:=\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})-1
. Then
{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathcal{L}}
(with
n_{1}=0
). Hence, by Theorem
4.4
,
a_{1}(K[S])\geq\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}})=\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})-1
.

(ii)

Let
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{w}}_{i}+(n_{1}d,n_{2}d)\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. Since

n_{1}\leq\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-1
and
n_{2}\leq\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-n_{1}-1
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{u}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+n_{1}+n_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i)+n_{1}d)-1

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i))+n_{1}-1

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})+\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})-2,

which implies
(ii)
by Theorem
4.4
.

(iii)

Using a representation
\omega_{1}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\beta_{j}a_{j}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\beta_{j}=\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i))
, we get

\omega_{1}(i)\geq\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})a_{1}
. Similarly,
\omega_{2}(d-i)\geq\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})(d-a_{k-1})
. Hence

\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\geq\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\frac{a_{1}}{d}+\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\frac{d-a_{k-1}}{d}.

Replacing
\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})
in the inequality in
(ii)
by the right hand quantity above, we then get
(iii)
.

∎

Note that the upper bounds in the above theorem is at most two times of the lower bound. From this point of view, they are quite good. In Section
5
we give an example to show that all bounds of the above theorem are sharp.

Unfortunately, the computation of Apéry sets and degrees of their elements are in general very complicated, so that in general we cannot derive from the above theorem explicit bounds in terms of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
. In particular, we cannot derive even the Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine bound in
[
11
]
for monomial curves, which states that

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\operatorname{deg}(C)-\operatorname{codim}(C)=d-k+1.

Recall that for a sequence of positive integers
{\mathcal{A}}=\{0=:\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}
with
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{k}
its
i
-th gap is the number
\lambda_{i}=\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}-1
, where
1\leq i\leq k
. Note that
d-k=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}
.

If
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
, by abuse of terminology, we also call the set
[a_{i}+1,a_{i+1}-1]
the
i
-th gap.

The largest gap
\lambda_{\max}:=\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\}
. Assume that
\lambda_{\max}=\lambda_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. Then, the second largest gap is
\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}:=\max\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i-1},\lambda_{i+1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\}
.
L’vovsky
[
18
]
provided a much better bound than the one of
[
11
]
:

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}+1.

In the sequel, under some special assumptions, we give better bounds than the above L’vovsky’s bound.

First, we can reprove
[
12
, Theorem 2.7 and Proposition 3.4]
.

Corollary 4.7
.

(i)

Assume that
a_{1}=1
and
a_{k-1}=d-1
. Let
\varepsilon=\max\{i|\ 1,\ldots,i,d-1,\ldots,d-i\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}\}\}
and
\lambda_{\max}
be the largest gap of
A_{1}
. Then

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}-1}{\varepsilon}\right\rfloor+2.

(ii)

Assume that
a_{1}=1,\ldots,a_{p}=p,a_{p+1}\geq p+2
for some
p\geq 1
. Denote the first non-zero gap of
A_{1}
by
\lambda=a_{p+1}-p-1
. Then

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\geq\left\lceil\frac{\lambda}{p}\right\rceil+1=\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda-1}{p}\right\rfloor+2.

In particular, the bound in
(i)
is attained if
\lambda=\lambda_{\max}
and
\varepsilon=p
.

Proof.

(i)

Since
1\in A_{1},A_{2}
,
\omega_{1}(i)=\omega_{2}(i)=i
, whence
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=(i,d-i)
for all
i\leq d
. In particular
\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=1
. By Lemma
4.1
,
a_{2}(K[S])+2=1
. So, in order to prove
(i)
, it is left to estimate
a_{1}(K[S])
under the assumption that
K[S]
is not a Cohen–Macaulay ring. Let
x\in{\mathbb{I}}
. There is
i
such that
a_{i}<x<a_{i+1}
. Since
x=a_{i}+x-a_{i}
, and
1,\ldots,\varepsilon\in A_{1}
, by Remark
3.2
(2),
\delta_{1}(x)\leq 1+\lceil(x-a_{i})/\varepsilon\rceil
. Similarly,
\delta_{2}(d-x)\leq 1+\lceil(a_{i+1}-x)/\varepsilon\rceil
. Hence, by Theorem
4.6
(ii)

\displaystyle a_{1}(K[S])+1

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\frac{x-a_{i}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x-a_{i}}{\varepsilon}+1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{\varepsilon}+1-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\lambda_{\max}-1}{\varepsilon}+2.

Hence,
a_{1}(K[S])+1\leq\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}-1}{\varepsilon}\rfloor+2
, as required.

(ii)

Under the setting, it is clear that
\omega_{1}(i)=i
for all
i\leq d-1
and
\delta_{1}(a_{p+1}-1)\geq 1+\lceil\lambda/p\rceil
. By Theorem
4.6
(i)
,
a_{1}(K[S])+1\geq 1+\lceil\lambda/p\rceil
. It is clear that
\lceil\lambda/p\rceil=\lfloor(\lambda-1)/p\rfloor+1
.

∎

If
K[S]
is a Cohen–Macaulay ring, using the Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.2
(or better Lemma
4.1
), we get a good bound on
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
. For an example, all Cohen–Macaulay rings in Proposition
3.7
have regularity
2
.

If
K[S]
is a non-Cohen–Macaulay Buchsbaum ring, then by Theorem
3.5
, we get
a_{1}(K[S])+1=\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\}+1
. Hence by Lemma
4.1
,
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq a_{2}(K[S])+3.
This is a particular case of a more general result of
[
15
, Corollary 2.8]
, which states that for any Buchsbaum graded
K
-algebra
R
of dimension
d
,
\mathrm{reg}(R)\leq a_{d}(R)+d+1
.

From this observation, using Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
, we immediately get:

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\epsilon=\max\{a_{1},d-a_{k-1}\}
. Assume that
K[S]
is a Buchsbaum ring, then

(i)

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq a_{1}+(d-a_{k-1}).

(ii)

If, moreover, there are
i,i+1\in A_{1}
for some
0\leq i\leq d-1
, then
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\epsilon+1
.

(iii)

If, moreover, there are
i,i+1,\ldots,i+\epsilon-1\in A_{1}
for some
0\leq i\leq d-\epsilon+1
, then
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq 3
.

The main goal of
[
24
]
is to determine when
K[S]
is a Cohen–Macaulay or Buchsbaum ring in terms of
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}=d
. We already discussed the Cohen–Macaulay property in the previous Section
3
. Concerning the Buchsbaum property the problem was completely solved in the case

, see
[
24
, Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.3]
. In a recent paper
[
17
]
, a new characterization in terms the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity was given. In the following consequence, using the above study, we can give a very simple sufficient criterion for

to be a Buchsbaum ring, and quickly derive some results from
[
17
]
. Moreover, we see that the implication
(3)\Rightarrow(1)
in
[
17
, Theorem 2.4 and Theorem 2.7]
holds without the assumptions there.

Corollary 4.9
.

(i)

If
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=2
, then
K[S]
is a Buchsbaum ring.

(ii)

(
[
17
, Theorem 2.3]
) If
a_{1}=1
and
a_{k-1}=d-1
, then
K[S]
is a Buchsbaum ring if and only if
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=2
.

(iii)

Assume that
a_{1}=1
and
a_{k-1}=d-1,\ k<d
. Let
\varepsilon=\max\{i\mid 1,\ldots,i,d-1,\ldots,d-i\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}\}\}
. If
\lambda_{\max}\leq\varepsilon
, then
K[S]
is a Buchsbaum ring.

Proof.

(i)

Assume that
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=2
. W.l.o.g we may assume that
K[S]
is not a Cohen–Macaulay ring. Then
a_{1}(K[S])=1
. From Lemma
3.1
(ii)
and Lemma
3.4
,
S^{\prime}\setminus S=\{{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{I}}\}
whence
\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=1
for all
i\in{\mathbb{I}}
. By Theorem
3.5
(iii)
,
K[S]
is a Buchsbaum ring.

(ii)

If
a_{1}=d-a_{k-1}=1
and
K[S]
is a Buchsbaum ring, then
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=2
follows from Corollary

4.8
(ii)
. The converse follows from
(i)
.

(iii)

Since
k<d
and
a_{1}=d-a_{k-1}=1
,
K[S]
is not Cohen-Macaulay. Under the assumption, by Corollary
4.7
,
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq 2
, whence
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=2
. Hence, the statement follows from
(ii)
.

∎

Rewrite
A_{1}=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\}=\bigsqcup_{j=0}^{r}[b_{2j},b_{2j+1}]
, where
0=:b_{0}\leq b_{1}<b_{2}\leq b_{3}<\cdots<b_{2r}\leq b_{2r+1}:=d
and
b_{2j-1}+2\leq b_{2j}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
. Note that
r
is the number of non-zero gaps of
A_{1}
. Since we already assume that

,
r\geq 1
. The case
r=1
can be solved below.

Corollary 4.10
.

Assume that
A_{1}=[0,p]\cup[q,d]
with
p\geq 1
and
p+2\leq q\leq d
.

(i)

If
q<d
we may further assume that
p\leq d-q
. Then
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=\left\lceil\frac{q-1}{p}\right\rceil
.

(ii)

If
q=d
, then
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\in\{a+1,a+2\}
, where

a=\left\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\right\rfloor-\left\lceil\frac{p\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\rfloor+2}{d}\right\rceil.

Proof.

(i)

follows from Corollary
4.7
(ii)
.

(ii)

By
[
24
, Corollary 3.4]
,
K[S]
is Cohen–Macaulay. Hence we only need to estimate
a_{2}(K[S])+2
. Since
1\in A_{1}
,
F_{1}=-1
and
\omega_{1}(j)=j
. By Brauer’s bound
[
2
, Theorem 7]
(see also Theorem 3.3.1 in
[
21
]
and discussion after that), we have

F_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{d-2}{p}\right\rfloor(d-p)-1
. Hence, by Lemma
4.2
,

\displaystyle a_{2}(K[S])\leq\left\lfloor\frac{F_{2}-1}{d}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\rfloor(d-p)-2}{d}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\left\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\right\rfloor-\frac{p\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\rfloor+2}{d}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\right\rfloor-\left\lceil\frac{p\lfloor\frac{d}{p}\rfloor+2}{d}\right\rceil=a.

On the other hand, assume that
F_{2}\equiv(d-j)\bmod d
for some
1\geq j\leq d-1
. Then
\omega_{2}(d-j)=F_{2}+d
. By Lemma
4.1
,

a_{2}(K[S])\geq\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{j})=\left\lceil\frac{F_{2}-1+d+j}{d}\right\rceil-2\geq a-1,

as required.

∎

Let
r\geq 2
. In
[
17
, Section 3]
, under some very special assumptions, there are some very good bounds established for non-smooth curves. For an example,
[
17
, Theorem 3.4]
states that if
b_{1}=0,b_{2r}<d
and
2b_{2}-1\leq b_{3},\ r\geq 2
, then

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}-1}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right\rfloor+3,

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\min\{b_{3},d-b_{2r}\}
. Using Theorem
4.6
, we can reprove this result. However, the computation is still not simple, so that we do not present it here. Instead, we consider the case of possibly small
b_{3}-b_{2}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
A_{1}=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{k}\}=\bigsqcup_{j=0}^{r}[b_{2j},b_{2j+1}]
, where
0=:b_{0}\leq b_{1}<b_{2}\leq b_{3}<\cdots<b_{2r}\leq b_{2r+1}:=d
and
b_{2j-1}+2\leq b_{2j}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
,
r\geq 2
. If
b_{1}=0,b_{2r}<d
and
b_{5}-b_{4}+1\geq b_{2}
, then

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq 2+\max\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor;\ \left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor\right\}.

Proof.

Let

M:=\max\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor;\ \left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor\right\}\geq 0.

From the assumption we see that
1\in A_{2}
. Hence
F_{2}=-1
and
\omega_{2}(d-x)=d-x
for all
x\leq d
. Since
b_{5}-b_{4}+1\geq b_{2}
,
b_{1}=0
, we have
1\leq F_{1}\leq b_{4}-1
and

\omega_{1}(x)=\begin{cases}x&\text{if}\quad b_{4}\leq x<d\ \text{or}\ x\in\langle[b_{2},b_{3}]\rangle\ \text{and}\ x<b_{4},\\
x+d&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By Lemma
4.2
,
a_{2}(K[S])+2\leq\lfloor\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}\rfloor+2=2
. So, it is left to show that
a_{1}(K[S])\leq M+1
, provided that
{\mathbb{I}}\neq\emptyset
. By Theorem
4.6
(ii)
there is
x\in{\mathbb{I}}
such that

a_{1}(K[S])\leq\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})+\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})-\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})-2=:D(x).

It is enough to show that
D(x)\leq M+1
. Let
\epsilon^{\prime}:=\min\{b_{2},d-b_{2r}\}
. We distinguish three cases.

Case

a.

x>b_{5}
. In this case
{\mathbf{w}}_{x}=(x,d-x)
, whence
\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})=1
. There is
i
such that
b_{5}\leq a_{i}<x<a_{i+1}
. In the proof of Corollary
4.7
(i)
we know that

\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})\leq 1+\left\lceil\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rceil\leq 1+\left\lceil\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil.

(10)

Note that
x=b_{4}+(x-b_{4})
. If
x-b_{4}\in A_{1}
, then
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})=\delta_{1}(x)=2

x-b_{4}\not\in A_{1}

(\text{a}1)

If
x-b_{4}>a_{i}
, then using

From (

) and (

D(x)\leq\left\lceil\frac{a_{i+1}-x}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil+\left\lfloor\frac{x-b_{4}-a_{i}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{i}-3}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil\leq 1+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{i}-4}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rfloor\leq M+1.

(12)

(\text{a}2)

a_{j}<x-b_{4}<a_{j+1}
for some
j<i
, that is
x
and

belong to two different gaps of

From (

\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})\leq 1+\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{i}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rceil
. Hence,

D(x)\leq\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{i}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rceil+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{j}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor\leq 1+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{i}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{j}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor.

Note that if
\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{l}

and

\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{i}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{j}}{b_{2}}\right\rfloor\leq M.
Using this remark, from (

) and (

.

Case

b_{3}<x<b_{4}
.

(\text{b}1)

x\not\in\langle[b_{2},b_{3}]\rangle
. Then

-th gap with

\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{x})\leq 1+\left\lceil\frac{b_{4}-x}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rceil\leq 1+\left\lceil\frac{\lambda_{i}}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rceil\leq 2+\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{i}-1}{d-b_{2r}}\right\rfloor.

(15)

Hence, the argument at the end of Case

On the other hand

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\frac{b_{4}-b_{3}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rceil=\left\lfloor\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\right\rfloor+1\leq M+1.

. Then

and

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\{\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{k}\}\ (k\geq 2)
. Assume that
\alpha_{i}\not\equiv\alpha_{j}\bmod\alpha_{k}
for all
i\neq j
. Then for all
1\leq i\leq d-1
we have

\delta(\omega(i))\leq 1+\left\lfloor\frac{2\alpha_{k}\lfloor\alpha_{1}/k\rfloor+\lambda_{\max}}{\alpha_{1}}\right\rfloor\leq 1+\frac{2\alpha_{k}}{k}+\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\alpha_{1}}.

Proof.

The second inequality immediately follows from the first one. Let
F:=F(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
. There is an unique
1\leq j\leq\alpha_{k}
such that
\omega(i)\equiv F+j\pmod{\alpha_{k}}
. Since
F
is a largest integer not belonging to
\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
,
F+j\in\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
and

\omega(i)=F+j-p\alpha_{k}
for some
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
p\geq 1
, then
\omega(i)\leq F
. Note that for any
n\in\langle{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
,
\delta(n)\leq\frac{n}{\alpha_{1}}
. By Lemma
2.1
,
F\leq 2\alpha_{k}\lfloor\alpha_{1}/k\rfloor
. Hence

\delta(\omega(i))\leq\frac{F}{\alpha_{1}}\leq\frac{2\alpha_{k}\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{1}}{k}\rfloor}{\alpha_{1}}-1.

Next, assume that
\omega(i)=F+j
. Let
l
be the largest index such that
\alpha_{l}\leq j
. Then
j-\alpha_{l}\leq\alpha_{l+1}-1-\alpha_{l}=\lambda_{l}
and

\omega(i)=F+j=F+\varepsilon+1\cdot\alpha_{l}
, where
0\leq\varepsilon\leq\lambda_{l}
. Hence

\delta(\omega(i))\leq 1+\delta(F+\varepsilon)\leq 1+\frac{F+\varepsilon}{\alpha_{1}}\leq 1+\frac{F+\lambda_{l}}{\alpha_{1}}\leq 1+\frac{F+\lambda_{\max}}{\alpha_{1}}.

Using again the above bound on
F
, we get

\delta(\omega(i))\leq 1+\frac{2\alpha_{k}\lfloor\alpha_{1}/k\rfloor+\lambda_{\max}}{\alpha_{1}},

which implies the first inequality.
∎

Theorem 4.13
.

Assume that
a_{i}\not\equiv a_{j}\pmod{a_{1}}
and
a_{i}\not\equiv a_{j}\pmod{d-a_{k-1}}
for all
i\neq j
. Then

a_{1}(K[S])<\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}\left(1+\frac{a_{k-1}-a_{1}}{d}\right),

where
\lambda_{\max}
is the largest gap of the sequence
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},a_{k}=:d
.

As a consequence, we have

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq 1+\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}\left(1+\frac{a_{k-1}-a_{1}}{d}\right).

Proof.

By the assumption, it follows that
a_{1}\geq k
and
d-a_{k-1}\geq k
. Note that the largest gap of the sequence
d-a_{k-1},\ldots,d-a_{1},d
is also the same
\lambda_{\max}
. By Lemma
4.12
, we then get
\delta_{1}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\leq 1+\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}
and
\delta_{2}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\leq 1+\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}
. Hence, by Theorem
4.6
(iii)
, we get

\displaystyle a_{1}(K[S])

\displaystyle\leq\left\lfloor\left(1-\frac{a_{1}}{d}\right)\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{1}(\omega_{1}(i))\}+\frac{a_{k-1}}{d}\max_{i\in{\mathbb{I}}}\{\delta_{2}(\omega_{2}(d-i))\}\right\rfloor-2

\displaystyle\leq\left\lfloor 1+\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}\right\rfloor\left(1-\frac{a_{1}}{d}+\frac{a_{k-1}}{d}\right)-2<\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}\left(1+\frac{a_{k-1}-a_{1}}{d}\right),

as required.

For the bound on
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
, by Lemma
2.1
, we have
F_{1}<2da_{1}/k
and
F_{2}<2d(d-a_{k-1})/k
. Recall that
k\geq 3
. Since

4.2

a_{2}(K[S])+2\leq\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}}{d}+2<2\cdot\frac{d+a_{1}-a_{k-1}}{k}<\frac{2d-2k+4}{k}+2=\frac{2d+4}{k}.

On the other hand, note that under our assumption
a_{1}\geq k
, whence
\lambda_{max}\geq a_{1}-a_{0}-1\geq k-1
. This implies

\displaystyle\frac{2d+\lambda_{\max}}{k}\left(1+\frac{a_{k-1}-a_{1}}{d}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2d}{k}+\frac{\lambda_{\max}+2(a_{k-1}-a_{1})}{k}

\displaystyle>\frac{2d+3k-5}{k}\geq a_{2}(K[S])+2.

Since
\mathrm{reg}([K(S)]=\max\{a_{1}(K[S])+1,\ a_{2}(K[S])+2\}

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
follows from the above inequality and the inequality on
a_{1}(K[S])
.
∎

The above bound is less than
6d/k

We conclude this paper by giving an application to the structure theory of sumsets. Let
A=\{0=:a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}=:d\}
be a set of
k+1\geq 4
non-negative
relatively prime integers. The
h
-fold sumset is the set
hA
. Sumsets play a central role in the Additive Number Theory, see
[
19
]
.

The following result is called the fundamental structure theorem in Additive Number Theory.

Theorem 5.5
.

[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
There are integers
c_{1},c_{2}
and sets
C_{i}\subseteq[0,c_{i}-2]
(
i=1,2
) such that

hA=C_{1}\sqcup[c_{1},hd-c_{2}]\sqcup(hd-C_{2}),

for all
h\geq\max\{1,\ (k-1)(d-1)d\}
.

In order to get information on the smallest possible integer
h
in the above theorem, the following notation was recently introduced.

Definition 5.6
.

[
8
, Definition 1.3]
The least integer
\sigma
such that the decomposition in Theorem
5.5
holds for all
h\geq\sigma
is called the
sumsets regularity
of
A
and denoted by
\sigma(A)
.

It turns out that there is a close relationship between the above invariant and other invariants of the ring
K[S]
of the monomial curve
C(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
, see, e.g.,
[
5
]
. Below we recall a relationship between the sumset regularity and the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
.
Let
H_{K[S]}(n)=\dim_{K}K[S]_{n}
be the Hilbert function of
K[S]
. Then
H_{K[S]}(n)
agrees with
P_{K[S]}(n)
for all
n\gg 0
, where
P_{K[S]}(t)
is the so-called Hilbert polynomial. The
regularity index
(of Hilbert function
H_{K[S]}(n)
) is the number

\operatorname{ri}(S):=\min\{n_{0}\geq 0\mid H_{K[S]}(n)=P_{K[S]}(n)\ \text{for all}\ n\geq n_{0}\}.

Since
K[S]
has positive depth, from the Grothendieck–Serre formula

P_{K[S]}(t)-H_{K[S]}(t)=\ell(H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])_{t}-\ell(H^{2}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}(K[S])_{t}

we always have
\operatorname{ri}(S)\leq\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
with equality when
a_{1}(K[S])>a_{2}(K[S])
. With the remark that
F(A)+1
is the conductor of
\langle A\rangle
, we have

Lemma 5.7
.

(i)

[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
We have

\sigma(A)=\max\left\{\operatorname{ri}(K[S]),\ \left\lceil\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}+2}{d}\right\rceil\right\}.

(ii)

[
8
, Theorem 3.16]

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\sigma(A)+1
. More precisely,

(a)

If
\operatorname{ri}(S)\geq\left\lceil\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}+2}{d}\right\rceil
, then
\sigma(A)\leq\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\sigma(A)+1
.

(b)

If
\operatorname{ri}(S)<\left\lceil\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}+2}{d}\right\rceil
,
then
\left\lceil\frac{\sigma(A)}{2}\right\rceil+1\leq\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\sigma(A)+1
.

In some particular cases, there are more precise relationships between the invariants involved in the above theorem, see Propositions 3.2 and 3.18, and Corollary 3.21 in
[
8
]
. Below is another case.

Corollary 5.8
.

Assume that
a_{1}=1
. Then
\sigma(A)\leq\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\sigma(A)+1
.

Proof.

The upper bound is Lemma
5.7
(ii)
. For the lower bound, note that
F_{1}=-1
, because
a_{1}=1
. Choose an index
0<i\leq d-1
such that
\omega_{2}(d-i)=F_{2}+d
. By Lemma
4.1
,

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\geq a_{2}(K[S])+2\geq\operatorname{deg}({\mathbf{w}}_{i})=\frac{F_{2}+d+\omega_{1}(i)}{d}\geq\frac{F_{2}+1}{d}+1,

whence

\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\geq\left\lceil\frac{F_{2}+1}{d}\right\rceil+1>\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}+2}{d}
. Since
\operatorname{ri}(S)\leq\mathrm{reg}(K[S])
, by Lemma
5.7
(i)
,
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\geq\sigma(A)
, as required.
∎

Examples in
[
8
, Table 1]
show that both cases in the above corollary can happen. Namely,
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=\sigma(A)
for the curve
C(1,3,11,13)
, and
\mathrm{reg}(K[S])=\sigma(A)+1
for the curve
C(1,3,5,6,12)
.

The best bound on
\sigma(A)
until now is given in
[
10
, Theorem 1]
as follows:

\sigma(A)\leq d-k+1=\operatorname{deg}(C)-\operatorname{codim}(C).

This upper bound is exactly the well-known Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine bound for curves. In
[
8
, Section 5]
, Gimenez and González-Sánchez showed that one can deduce the Granville–Walker bound from the one of Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine, and vice versa. In particular,
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
provides another combinatorial proof of the Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine bound. Note that the first combinatorial proof of the Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine bound was given in
[
20
]
.

Below we show that
\sigma(A)
is also bounded by L’vovsky’s bound.

Proposition 5.9
.

L’vovsky’s bound holds for
\sigma(A)
, that is

\sigma(A)\leq\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}+1.

Proof.

By Schur’s bound (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\left\lceil\frac{F_{1}+F_{2}+2}{d}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\frac{(a_{1}-1)(d-1)+(d-a_{k-1}-1)(d-1)}{d}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq(a_{1}-1)+(d-a_{k-1}-1)\leq\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}.

On the other hand, by L’vovsky’s bound,
\operatorname{ri}(S)\leq\mathrm{reg}(K[S])\leq\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sl}}+1
. Hence the statement immediately follows from Lemma
5.7
(i)
.
∎

Note that using the results in Section
4
, under some specific conditions, the proof of the above proposition also gives several better bounds on
\sigma(A)
than L’vovsky’s bound. An open question is if one can find a combinatorial proof for the above proposition (i.e. without using L’vovsky’s result).
