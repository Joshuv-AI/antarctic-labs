Effects of external oblique intercostal plane block on postoperative pain and analgesic requirement in laparoscopic cholecystectomy: a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mazlum Dursun, Hülya Tosun Söner, Selen Topalel, Meral Erdal Erbatur, Osman Uzundere, Sedat Kaya, Erhan Gökçek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is one of the most commonly performed minimally invasive surgical procedures. Postoperative pain may negatively affect patient satisfaction and recovery. External oblique intercostal plane block (EOIB) is proposed as a new regional anaesthesia technique to reduce this pain. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of ultrasound-guided EOIB on postoperative pain scores, postoperative opioid consumption, intraoperative hemodynamic parameters, and patient satisfaction in patients undergoing LC.MethodsAfter approval of the ethics committee, 64 patients aged 18–65 years, in ASA I-II class, who underwent elective LC for cholelithiasis were included in the study. Patients were randomly allocated into two groups: the EOIB group and the control group. Visual analogue scale (VAS), heart rate, systolic-diastolic-mean arterial pressure, analgesic consumption, nausea-vomiting frequency and patient satisfaction parameters were recorded for 24 h postoperatively.ResultsVAS scores were significantly lower in favour of EOIB at all time points (p < 0.001). There was a significant difference over time and between groups. (p < 0.001) In addition, patient satisfaction was significantly higher in the EOIB group at 24 h (p < 0.001).ConclusionsEOIB improves pain control after LC, reduces the need for analgesics and significantly improves patient satisfaction.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06712498; registered 26 November 2024 (retrospectively registered).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04029-x.Keywords: Analgesia, Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Patient satisfaction, Regional anesthesia

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is currently one of the most preferred surgical methods for the treatment of gallbladder diseases, especially cholelithiasis [1]. It is widely used due to its advantages such as less tissue trauma, lower morbidity and mortality rates, shorter hospital stay and faster postoperative recovery compared to open surgery [2]. However, although it is a minimally invasive procedure, moderate to severe pain may occur in patients after laparoscopic surgery [1, 3]. This pain is mainly caused by somatic pain at the incision site, visceral pain due to peritoneal irritation and organ manipulation, and shoulder pain due to diaphragmatic irritation [1, 3, 4].

Postoperative pain management is critical for patient comfort, mobilisation and prevention of postoperative complications. However, since opioids, which are frequently used to provide analgesia, have serious side effects such as sedation, respiratory depression, nausea-vomiting and addiction, multimodal analgesia protocols have been developed to reduce the use of opioids, especially in laparoscopic surgeries [5]. Among these approaches, regional anaesthesia methods are prominent in terms of both reducing systemic side effects and providing more effective pain control [5, 6].

In recent years, ultrasonography (USG)-guided plane blocks have started to take place among the regional methods that provide effective analgesia in abdominal surgeries [5–7]. Techniques such as transversus abdominis plane block (TAP), quadratus lumborum block (QLB), erector spinae plane block (ESP) and paravertebral block have been found to be especially effective in suppressing incisional pain and have shown successful results in reducing the need for opioids and postoperative complications [6, 7]. Among these blocks, the External oblique ıntercostal plane block (EOIB), which has been described more recently, attracts attention due to its ease of application and effective distribution over the thoracoabdominal area [8].

The fact that EOIB, which is based on injecting local anaesthetic into the intercostal nerves and external oblique muscle plane at the thoracic level (T6-T9), can be performed under USG guidance with the patient in the supine position provides an important advantage in patients with obesity, restricted mobility or poor position tolerance [9, 10]. In addition, this technique, which can target somatic components, promises to provide more comprehensive pain control in abdominal surgeries [5, 6]. Although the role of EOIB in laparoscopic surgeries has not yet been sufficiently clarified in the literature, a limited number of studies show that this technique may have positive effects on postoperative analgesia, opioid consumption and patient satisfaction [9–11].

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of ultrasound-guided EOIB on postoperative pain scores, postoperative opioid consumption, postoperative additional analgesic requirement, intraoperative haemodynamic parameters and patient satisfaction in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Materials and methods

The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT06712498 on 26 November 2024. Because of a delay in the administrative registration process, this took place after patient enrollment had already started, so the trial is reported here as retrospectively registered. The eligibility criteria and the primary and secondary outcomes defined at the outset were not altered at any point before or after registration. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964).

The study was conducted between 10.06.2024 and 09.08.2024 among patients who were to undergo elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the General Surgery Clinic of the hospital. Inclusion criteria were determined as being between the ages of 18–65 years, being in American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I-II class and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. Of the 67 patients eligible to participate in the study, 3 refused voluntary participation and the study was completed with a total of 64 patients (Fig. 1).

Individuals under the age of 18 years and over the age of 65 years, those who were allergic to local anaesthetics, those with a history of opioid or alcohol dependence, those who developed acute cholecystitis in the perioperative period, those with contraindications to laparoscopic surgery, those with ASA class III-IV, and those with a body mass index (BMI) of 35 and above were excluded from the study. Patients were also excluded if they had any contraindication to a peripheral nerve block, namely infection at the planned injection site, a known coagulopathy or ongoing anticoagulant therapy, or refusal to undergo the block.

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio. The randomization list was computer-generated by an independent investigator who was not involved in patient recruitment or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Outcome assessments were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation. After the patient was included in the study, the relevant envelope was opened before the procedure, and the group assignment was determined. Patients in the first group underwent bilateral EOIB before surgery after induction of anesthesia. The second group was determined as the control group, and EOIB was not applied to these patients. All randomized patients were analyzed according to their assigned groups. No patients were excluded after randomization, and no crossover or block failure occurred.

General anaesthesia was performed in the supine position, and induction was achieved with intravenous midazolam (0.1–0.4 mg/kg), fentanyl (2 µg/kg), propofol (2–3 mg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) after preoxygenation with 100% oxygen. No additional intraoperative analgesic drugs were used thereafter. After orotracheal intubation, the patients were connected to a mechanical ventilator, and anaesthesia was maintained with desflurane targeted to a minimum alveolar concentration of approximately 1.0 in both groups. The agent was discontinued at the end of surgery in all patients. Surgery time was defined as the interval between surgical incision and skin closure, while anesthesia time was defined as the period from induction of anesthesia to extubation. Since anesthetic management followed the same protocol in the two groups, the longer anesthesia time recorded in the EOIB group is attributable to the additional time required to perform the block before surgery rather than to differences in intraoperative management.

Patients in group 1 underwent bilateral EOIB with 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine injection at the level of the external oblique intercostal muscle with a 22-gauge, 100 mm long block needle under ultrasonographic guidance at the T6-T7 vertebral level before surgery. All EOIB procedures were carried out by the same anesthesiologist, who was experienced in ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia, in order to keep the technique consistent across patients. No block was applied to the patients in group 2. Approximately thirty minutes before the end of the operation, all patients received 50 mg dexketoprofen intravenously for analgesia and 4 mg ondansetron to prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting. After the operation was completed, intravenous atropine (0.01 mg/kg) and neostigmine (0.02 mg/kg) were given to reverse neuromuscular blockade.

Patients were monitored for hemodynamic parameters in the postoperative recovery unit. The primary outcome was the VAS pain score at postoperative hour 0. Secondary outcomes included VAS scores at postoperative 2, 6, 12, and 24 h, postoperative rescue analgesic requirement, total 24-hour tramadol consumption, intraoperative and postoperative hemodynamic parameters, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and patient satisfaction at 24 h. Postoperative VAS scores were assessed at rest. Pulse rate, blood pressure, and VAS scores were recorded at postoperative 0, 2, 6, 12, and 24 h in patients transferred to the ward. Postoperatively, rescue analgesia was administered to patients with a VAS score of 5 or higher, in the form of 100 mg intravenous tramadol.

Statistical analysis

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software. A two-sided sample size calculation was performed based on the primary outcome measure, the VAS score at postoperative hour 0. Assuming a clinically meaningful difference of 1.5 VAS points, a common standard deviation of 2.0, α = 0.05, and 80% power, 29 patients were required per group. Considering possible data loss, 32 patients were included in each group.

Repeated postoperative VAS measurements were analyzed using a Generalized Estimating Equations model with an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust standard errors. The model included group, time, and group × time interaction terms. Between-group differences at each time point were reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals.

Statistical analysis of the data was performed with SPSS 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software. Continuous variables with parametric distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and those with non-parametric distribution were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as n (%). The suitability of normal distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. Differences between groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Kruskal–Wallis test, or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05. There were no missing data for the primary or secondary outcomes; therefore, no imputation was performed.

Results

Operative time did not differ between the groups (59.41 ± 14.74 vs. 53.94 ± 13.02 min; p = 0.121), whereas anesthesia time was significantly longer in the EOIB group (74.34 ± 16.42 vs. 64.88 ± 13.51 min; p = 0.014) (Table 1). We think that block application prolongs the anesthesia time in the block group. Multivariate regression analyses including covariate that anesthesia time was not significantly associated with postoperative VAS scores at any time point (all p > 0.05). In contrast, group assignment remained a significant independent predictor at all time points (0.001 ≤ all p). In terms of mean arterial pressure (MAP), MAP was found to be significantly lower in the EOIB group compared to the control group at 45th minute (p = 0.012), at the first postoperative evaluation (p = 0.007) and at 6th hour (p = 0.037) (Table 2). Peak heart rate (HR) was significantly lower in the EOIB group at the first postoperative assessment (p = 0.019), at 2nd hour (p = 0.028) and at 24th hour (p = 0.006) (Table 2).

In postoperative pain assessment, VAS scores were significantly lower in the EOIB group compared to the control group at all time points (p < 0.05) (Table 3). When repetitive measurement analysis was performed with the GEE model, it was shown that the group effect (Wald χ² = 106.21, p < 0.001), the time effect (Wald χ² = 147.59, df = 4, p < 0.001) and the group×time interaction (Wald χ² = 40.05, df = 4, p < 0.001) were significant. Postoperative VAS scores between both groups differed statistically significantly across all measurement points. The differences in VAS between the groups are presented in Table 3 as the mean difference and 95% confidence interval for each time point (all time points p < 0.001 or p = 0.001). The need for additional analgesics was significantly lower in the EOIB group at 0 and 6th hour postop (p < 0.001). Although the difference did not reach statistical level at postoperative second and 12th hours, it was clinically in favour of EOIB (Fig. 2). (Table 4)

Discussion

In this study, patients who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy were divided into two groups as control and EOIB and we found that pain scores, the need for additional analgesia decreased and patient satisfaction scores increased in EOIB group. To provide intraoperative or postoperative analgesic effects in upper abdominal surgeries such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy, it is necessary to perform an intercostal nerve block between T6 and T10 to block the T6–T10/T11 dermatomes. During the EOIB block application, the local anesthetic spreads along the abdominal side wall, the anterior-midaxillary line of the ribs, and the fascia of the external oblique muscle, blocking the lateral and anterior branches of the intercostal nerves at the T6-T10 levels.

There are limited number of studies investigating the effectiveness of EOIB in the literature. In our study, we compared the pain scores of the patients in group within time, unlike other studies. In a randomised controlled study by Korkusuz et al., it was shown that VAS and NRS pain scores were significantly lower in the postoperative period in patients who underwent EOIB compared to the control group. In addition, patient satisfaction was found to be higher in the EOIB group; significant improvement was achieved in parameters affecting the comfort levels of the patients such as breathing, eating and communicating with their close environment. In the same study, the need for additional analgesia was found to be lower in the EOIB group at each time point in the hourly evaluations performed in a 24-hour period [8]. Similarly, in the study of Doymuş et al. pain scores measured at the 1st, 2nd, 4th, 8th and 12th hours postoperatively were found to be significantly lower in favour of EOIB. In addition, total fentanyl consumption in 24 h was 505.83 ± 178.56 µg in the EOIB group and 880.83 ± 256.78 µg in the control group and this difference was found to be statistically significant [12].

Another study by Kavaklı et al. evaluated the efficacy of EOIB in patients undergoing laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy and found that NRS scores measured at 2, 6 and 12 h were significantly lower in the EOIB group. Although the scores at 24 h were similar between the groups, total 24-hour morphine consumption was found to be 7.5 mg in the EOIB group and 14 mg in the control group, and this difference was statistically significant [13]. In our study, it was observed that VAS scores were significantly lower in the EOIB group at 0, 2, 6, 12 and 24 h. The difference in VAS in the EOIB group compared to the control group was above the minimum clinically significant difference (MCID) threshold of approximately 1.3–1.5 points, indicating that there was not only a statistically significant but also a clinically significant difference. In terms of the need for additional analgesics, significantly less tramadol was used in the EOIB group, especially in the first 6 h. This shows that both pain control was more effective and opioid consumption decreased. In a study comparing EOIB and subcostal tap blocks in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy, both techniques were found to be equally effective in controlling pain and reducing opioid consumption in the first 24 h after surgery. Only within the first 4 h postoperatively was opioid requirement lower in the TAP block group compared to the EOIB group in another study, Shrey and Sinha found similar postoperative pain control between upper abdominal surgery with EOIB and subcostal TAP block.

In terms of the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, a significant decrease was found in the EOIB group. Gangadhar et al. also reported that the incidence of PONV was significantly lower in patients undergoing EOIB [14]. These findings are consistent with the fact that the incidence of nausea and vomiting was lower in the EOIB group compared to the control group in our study.

In the literature, the effects of EOIB on haemodynamics have been examined to a limited extent. In our study, mean arterial pressure was found to be significantly lower in the EOIB group at 45 min of the operation, at the first postoperative evaluation and at 6 h. Peak heart rate was also significantly lower in the EOIB group at 0, 2 and 24th hours postoperatively. These data suggest that block application may be associated with reduced nociceptive response. In addition, in our study, the duration of anesthesia was statistically significantly longer in the EOIB group. However, this difference was not directly related to EOIB application or analgesic requirement. In addition, corrected analysis was performed in our study, and it was observed that anesthesia duration did not significantly affect postoperative pain outcomes.

Patient satisfaction questionnaire results also showed a significant difference in favour of EOIB. Approximately half of the patients who underwent EOIB gave a “very satisfied” evaluation, whereas this rate remained at 6.3% in the control group. This result is in parallel with the superiority of the QoR-15 score reported in previous studies [8, 15]. However, the fact that QoR-15 evaluation was not performed in our study can be considered as a limitation in this field.

Although it is a new technique, it has been reported that EOIB may be feasible, no complications were observed in this cohort and effective when performed under ultrasonography guidance, which is supported by case series and randomised controlled trials [8, 14]. It has the advantages of being performed in a plane with low anatomical vessel density and can be performed in the supine position even in obese patients.

The limitations of our study include its single-centre and small sample size for complications assessment, lack of assessment of pain scores by movement, and exclusion of some parameters (e.g. QoR- 15). For these reasons, it is recommended to test the findings in larger populations and in different surgical groups. Also, the fact that no sham block was applied in the control group and inability to perform dermatomal sensory evaluation are important limitations of the study. We acknowledge that rescue analgesia administered at one assessment point may have lowered the VAS scores recorded at later time points. Rescue analgesic requirement, however, was itself analyzed as a secondary outcome and was significantly lower in the EOIB group. Any such carry-over effect would therefore have acted against our hypothesis by reducing pain scores preferentially in the control group, which received rescue medication more often; the between-group differences we observed in VAS are, if anything, a conservative reflection of the true analgesic benefit of the block.

Conclusions

In conclusion, EOIB was found decreased pain scores, decreased need for additional analgesics and increased patient satisfaction. Based on these results, EOIB can be considered as a valuable option to be included in multimodal analgesia protocols in abdominal surgeries.
