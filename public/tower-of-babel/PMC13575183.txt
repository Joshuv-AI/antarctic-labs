Thermo-CR: real-time physics-based cloud shadow removal via thermodynamic atmospheric modelling and multi-source fusion
Authors: R Sachin, Raghavendra Singh Jagawat, Aasritha Koganti, Vishnu Vardhan Reddy, S Sandhya, S Abhishek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpaceborne optical sensors provide continuous Earth observation, but atmospheric interference still limits their practical reliability. On average, clouds cover 67% of the Earth’s surface. This constant coverage degrades the data continuity needed for precision agriculture, disaster monitoring, and proactive Internet of Things (IoT) systems. Recent deep generative networks produce visually appealing cloud-free images. However, when faced with thick clouds (>50% opacity), these models often hallucinate topologies. They synthesize statistical guesses instead of recovering the actual ground reflectance. For high-stakes telemetry, predictable failure is safer than an undetected hallucination. This paper introduces Thermo-Cloud Removal (Thermo-CR), a real-time cloud removal framework. It integrates Radiative Transfer inversion, weather-driven transmission estimates, geographic priors, and multi-scale fusion to restore optical imagery without requiring Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR). Thermo-CR treats the cloudy atmosphere as a thermodynamic medium. By pulling live meteorological telemetry (Relative Humidity (RH) and Temperature (T)) through the Open-Meteo REST API, the system calculates optical depth and performs a deterministic inversion of the Radiative Transfer Model. Pure inverse models amplify noise under extreme occlusion (). To prevent this, we apply a Global Positioning System (GPS)-anchored multi-scale fusion with clear-sky temporal priors. We evaluated Thermo-CR on a synthetically occluded paired dataset covering varied topologies (Amazon, London, Seattle). The system degrades predictably under 90% cloud cover and avoids structural hallucination. It achieves an average Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) of 0.9925 and a Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) of 55.94 dB in under 13 milliseconds per frame, outperforming standard Dark Channel baselines.Keywords: Atmospheric scattering, Radiative transfer, Image fusion, Deterministic cloud removal, Edge computing, Internet of Things (IoT)Subject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Sensors like the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) and Sentinel-2 deliver high-frequency multi-spectral data used across major global infrastructure projects, including carbon cycle analysis1, agricultural tracking2, and autonomous disaster networking3. However, optical instruments require a clear atmospheric column to function. Meteorological surveys show that clouds obscure roughly 67% of the Earth’s surface at any given time4. Building reliable cloud-removal algorithms is therefore an engineering requirement for deploying continuous IoT sensor networks. If an edge-node cannot “see” through a weather front, the entire telemetry pipeline breaks down.

Historically, cloud removal algorithms split into two camps: traditional physics-based inversions and deep-learning inference models. Physical models, rooted in Koschmieder’s Law2, try to invert the atmospheric scattering process mathematically. Methods like the Dark Channel Prior (DCP) guess the transmission map by looking for the lowest intensity pixel in a local area. While mathematically lightweight, these inversions fall apart when clouds get thick. If regional transmission hits zero, the math guarantees infinite noise amplification. The result is just a chaotic grid of amplified sensor noise.

To avoid this noise, researchers shifted to artificial neural networks. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Diffusion Models, and Vision Transformers learn the statistical map between cloudy inputs and clear-sky targets. They produce great visual results. But these learning paradigms have a dangerous flaw for IoT infrastructure: they hallucinate. A GAN asked to remove an opaque 90% cloud layer does not “see” through it. Instead, it fakes a statistically probable land texture to fill the hole. In precision remote sensing—like measuring soil heat flux2 or forest biomass1—a fake forest or a synthetically erased disaster zone is much worse than a simple data loss error. Mission-critical domains need graceful degradation with known bounds, not convincing fabrications.

Therefore, this paper proposes Thermo-CR: a real-time cloud removal framework combining Radiative Transfer inversion, weather-driven transmission estimates, geographic priors, and multi-scale fusion. We avoid SAR completely. By pulling live weather data to bound the physics engine, and decaying to known spatial priors when occlusion is too heavy, Thermo-CR avoids generative hallucination by explicitly prioritizing deterministic historical substitution over AI-driven topographical fabrication.

Research Gap and Novelty: The primary research gap in contemporary atmospheric correction is the substantial operational latency introduced by multi-source radar dependency or heavy GPU-bound Vision Transformers. While these methods achieve high perceptual quality offline, they are fundamentally incompatible with real-time, low-power edge nodes. The novelty of Thermo-CR lies in completely bypassing active radar cross-referencing. By leveraging instantaneous Open-Meteo REST APIs to dictate the thermodynamic boundary conditions of the Radiative Transfer Equation, we replace multi-day satellite alignment latency with a sub-20ms deterministic inversion.

Forthcoming Future Trends: As the deployment of autonomous drone swarms and real-time border telemetry networks expands, the reliance on server-side GPU processing will become a critical bottleneck. Future trends in remote sensing are rapidly shifting toward “edge-compute atmospheric correction,” where the IoT node itself must autonomously “see” through extreme weather fronts without stalling the telemetry pipeline. Thermo-CR establishes the foundational physics-constrained architecture required for this autonomous edge-computing paradigm.

Related works/literature review

Researchers have tried many different techniques to remove cloud cover from satellite feeds.

Traditional Physics-Based Dehazing: Older methods rely on the simplified Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE)5. The Dark Channel Prior6, for example, guesses the transmission map (t(x)) by assuming non-sky patches have at least one dark color channel. These approaches set the baseline for atmospheric correction. However, their rigid assumptions often break down in practice. Snow, deserts, and bright rooftops lack dark pixels, tricking the algorithm into labeling them as thick clouds1. Also, static physical models cannot change their scattering coefficients dynamically when the weather shifts. This makes them weak during rapid meteorological events like hurricanes.

Deep Generative Models (GANs and CNNs): To bypass the strict math of the RTE, remote sensing shifted to Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). Early models like Pix2Pix7 pushed the field forward. Recently, Anandakrishnan et al.8 introduced a Parallel Attention-Guided GAN (PACR-GAN) to filter thin clouds using Convolutional Block Attention Modules. Other teams built multi-scale fusion networks9 and multi-attention GANs10. We also see Landsat-specific models like AMGAN-CR11, skip-connection autoencoders12, and Bishift Networks13. These generative models work well on thin, even haze because they learn the visual mapping from cloudy to clear domains. Yet, they lack the photometric ability to rebuild densely occluded targets predictably. When tested against unknown terrain with thick clouds, they frequently trigger severe topographical hallucinations12.

Transformers and Diffusion Models: Hoping to improve convolution limitations, researchers recently turned to Transformers and Diffusion models. Transformers like the Hybrid Sparse Transformer (HSFormer)14 and Spatiospectral Guided Transformers15 capture long-range pixel dependencies well. Similarly, Sequence-to-Sequence Diffusion Models (SeqDMs)16,17 reverse Gaussian noise to print clear-sky imagery. While visually impressive, these models are heavily GPU-bound. A massive multi-head attention mechanism can take seconds to process a single frame. This high computational cost violates the low-power, real-time needs of field IoT hardware18.

Multi-Source and SAR-Optical Fusion: Single-source optical cameras cannot physically see through thick clouds. To fix this, researchers fuse optical data with microwave radar. Wang et al.19 built a Spatial-Spectral Residual Network that uses Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) to guide the optical reconstruction. We see similar logic in 3D-2D Deep Fusion Frameworks20, spatiotemporal attention networks21, and Coord-Spectral Priors3. Beyond traditional SAR, recent advances in multi-source meteorological fusion successfully incorporate polarimetric radar variables into physics-constrained deep learning models for complex convective nowcasting22–24. While theoretically sound for post-processing and offline analysis, fusing active radar creates massive latency problems in real-world edge operations. You have to wait for an active radar satellite (like Sentinel-1) to pass over the exact same spot as the optical satellite. This spatial and temporal alignment often takes days. Unlike prior work, Thermo-CR relies purely on explicit atmospheric physics and pre-downloaded GIS maps. We avoid SAR completely to stay real-time.

Materials and methods

To construct a deterministic, physics-guided cloud removal pipeline capable of edge deployment, the Thermo-CR architecture is partitioned into interconnected modules requiring specific hardware, curated datasets, and API algorithms.

Hardware and components

The proposed Thermo-CR system is designed to operate as a lightweight edge-computing node, fundamentally differentiating it from massive GPU-bound architectures required by contemporary Generative Adversarial Networks. Traditional multi-scale deep fusion networks9 often necessitate tens of millions of trainable parameters, rendering them unsuitable for low-latency IoT devices. Conversely, Thermo-CR is optimized to execute on standard consumer-grade Tensor Processing Units (TPUs), embedded NVIDIA Jetson platforms, or high-efficiency ARM architectures such as the Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (4GB RAM) operating strictly under a 5 W power envelope.

The core physical requirement is real-time dual-band connectivity (LTE/5G or high-bandwidth LoRaWAN gateways) to meteorological telemetry arrays rather than profound computational vectorization capabilities. By shifting the computational burden from iterative latent-space inference generation to streamlined fluid dynamic matrix modeling, the system achieves sub-15ms processing latency on localized edge nodes.To explicitly define the “real-time” requirement for our target IoT application, we establish a strict operational latency budget of < 100ms (10 FPS) per frame to support continuous autonomous drone navigation and border telemetry. Because the core physics inversion and Laplacian fusion execute in ∼13ms on localized CPU edge nodes (while the API retrieval executes asynchronously in ∼865ms), Thermo-CR comfortably satisfies this strict threshold. Memory footprint during inference peaks at approximately 250MB, ensuring that parallel sensor daemon processes (e.g., ground-moisture readings or border proximity sensors) remain uninhibited. We explicitly assume a fixed downward-facing sensing geometry typical of low Earth orbit (LEO) observation satellites, where the optical path length through the troposphere can be approximated reliably given the cloud top height retrieved from standard meteorological reporting.

To rigorously quantify the computational efficiency required for edge computing, we formally analyze the architectural time complexity. A standard Vision Transformer processes images with a self-attention mechanism scaling quadratically with the number of pixels N, causing massive latency bottlenecks on high-resolution satellite imagery. Conversely, the proposed Thermo-CR algorithm relies entirely on hardware-optimized pixel-wise arithmetic operations and matrix broadcasting. Consequently, calculating the optical depth, deriving the transmission map, and inverting the Radiative Transfer Equation all operate in strictly linear time . Furthermore, the spatial Laplacian pyramid decomposition operates recursively with logarithmic spatial reductions, maintaining an overall computational complexity bounded strictly by . This deterministic scaling ensures entirely predictable execution times, validating its feasibility for mission-critical IoT microcontrollers without risk of computational buffer overflow.

Algorithms and software used

The foundational algorithm of Thermo-CR eschews the traditional Dark Channel Prior (DCP) spatial estimations. While DCP assumes the existence of dark pixels within a local patch to estimate atmospheric light, this assumption routinely fails across brilliant terrain. Furthermore, estimating the scattering coefficient purely from RGB pixel intensities ignores the fundamental thermodynamic reality of the atmosphere.

Thermo-CR addresses this by ingesting live thermodynamic data. The software backend is programmed entirely in Python 3.10+, utilizing the highly optimized ‘NumPy‘ and ‘OpenCV‘ (cv2) libraries for immediate matrix broadcasting over the image tensors. The system interfaces directly with the Open-Meteo REST API endpoint via asynchronous HTTP ‘GET‘ requests (‘aiohttp‘), retrieving real-time hyper-local Relative Humidity (RH) and Ambient Temperature (T) corresponding to the exact terrestrial GPS bounding box and timestamps of the incoming satellite telemetry.

The software fundamentally skips standard deep-learning libraries (e.g., ‘PyTorch‘, ‘TensorFlow‘), entirely bypassing the need for complex deep-learning feature extraction modules or backward gradient passes utilized in12.

Discussion

Failure Case Analysis and Architectural Limitations: Before analyzing comparative methodologies, several system limitations and strengths must be formally addressed to ensure rigorous scientific transparency. First, Thermo-CR cannot reconstruct fully opaque convective clouds. Under absolute zero-transmission conditions—such as a 99.9% dense convective storm system where optical depth —no optical photons naturally penetrate the layer. It is crucial to examine how the system behaves at this exact 99.9% cloud cover failure point compared to a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN). When faced with 99.9% opacity, a modern GAN will forcibly hallucinate a statistically probable, yet entirely fake, geographical topology (e.g., pasting non-existent roads or fabricating phantom coastlines) to produce a visually pleasing output. Conversely, because Thermo-CR is bound by strict physics, it mathematically recognizes the zero-transmission state and triggers a safe, deterministic fallback. It relies entirely on the pre-verified spatial prior () without synthesizing fake high-frequency structural changes. Therefore, while a GAN provides a beautiful but scientifically corrupted image, Thermo-CR degrades gracefully and honestly, ensuring that hallucinated data is never passed to the IoT telemetry pipeline.

Second, the system remains highly dependent on the strict spatial alignment of the GIS prior; rapid topological changes (e.g., sudden flooding or localized infrastructure destruction) will not be inherently captured if they are completely obscured by extreme opacity, as the prior will aggressively assert historical data over missing high-frequency features. While this temporal mismatch represents a tangible operational limitation (e.g., temporarily masking a newly constructed building during a heavy storm), it was incorporated as a deliberate architectural trade-off. In mission-critical IoT telemetry, injecting a mathematically verified, historically accurate structural baseline is operationally safer than relying on generative AI platforms that hallucinate statistically probable but entirely fabricated geography. Third, the system fundamentally assumes the localized accuracy of the queried weather metadata, making it vulnerable to API outages or sparse sensor distributions in highly remote areas. To strictly quantify this sensitivity, Table 3 introduces an API noise ablation test, confirming that injecting stochastic variance into the relative humidity stream only degrades the PSNR by a marginal 1.7 dB, largely protected by the multi-scale fusion architecture. Furthermore, to strictly prevent localized pipeline collapse during complete network failure or Open-Meteo API timeouts (>2000 ms latency), the system is architected to default to a Temporal Persistence Cache, reading the last known localized weather state from memory. Because meteorological variables (e.g., temperature, humidity) shift slowly over time, this cached fallback maintains deterministic stability without forcing the pipeline to stall or synthesize a predictive weather model. However, despite these operational boundaries, its strengths are profound: it requires absolutely no heavy SAR telemetry coordination, is aggressively optimized for real-time CPU edge processing, and remains strictly physically interpretable while actively preventing the unverified neural hallucination that plagues modern GAN architectures.

Thermodynamic bounding of inverse errors

A critical advantage of the Thermo-CR architecture over purely statistical inverse models is its ability to mathematically veto incorrect transmission guesses using foundational thermodynamic physics. In traditional Dark Channel Prior (DCP) methods, transmission maps are highly susceptible to bright surfaces (e.g., white concrete or snow), causing massive miscalculations in optical depth. However, Thermo-CR utilizes live ambient Temperature (T), Relative Humidity (RH), and Atmospheric Pressure (P) to directly parameterize the Hanel droplet-growth curve. Because relative humidity drives aerosol hydro-growth and temperature dictates the phase state of the scattering medium, the physical size and density of the tropospheric droplets are rigidly bounded. If a pixel mathematically suggests a transmission value that violates the maximum possible physical scattering coefficient derived from the local thermodynamic state, Thermo-CR deterministically overrides the calculation. This physics-based boundary prevents the arbitrary mathematical divergence that traditionally plagues atmospheric inversions.

Conclusion

The pervasive nature of tropospheric cloud cover severely limits the autonomous, real-time applicability of spaceborne optical monitoring systems. While contemporary deep learning frameworks—ranging from Generative Adversarial Networks to Sequence-to-Sequence Diffusion Models—yield high-fidelity perceptual reconstructions, they inherently introduce the critical risk of topological hallucination beneath optically thick cloud layers. In applications demanding strict geographical and photometric accuracy, such as disaster telemetry and IoT coastal border monitoring, deterministic failure is demonstrably safer than a statistically convincing generative hallucination.

To resolve this critical flaw in structural logic, we developed Thermo-CR, a real-time, deterministic atmospheric reconstruction engine optimized exclusively for edge deployment arrays. By executing a direct thermodynamic inversion of the Radiative Transfer Model—driven by live meteorological data ingested synchronously via the Open-Meteo REST API—Thermo-CR calculates the precise scattering medium topology without stochastic or probabilistic guessing parameters. Furthermore, the integration of a responsive confidence-weighted spatial fusion engine mathematically guards against the extreme exponential noise amplification prevalent in traditional inversion methods under 90%+ opacity simulations.

Our empirical evaluations categorically demonstrate that Thermo-CR safely and successfully balances physics-based radiometric recovery alongside high-resolution geometric priors, achieving an average structural fidelity (SSIM 0.9925) and pixel-level accuracy (PSNR 55.94 dB) that is highly competitive with modern architectures.

Operating at sub-15ms edge latencies (specifically, executing the Optical Depth & Transmission estimation in ∼4.0 ms, the direct RTE Inversion in ∼0.5 ms, and the multi-scale Laplacian Fusion in ∼8.5 ms), this framework bridges the operational gap between theoretical atmospheric scattering physics and live IoT deterministic deployment. It ensures that environmental edge-nodes output verifiable, hallucination-resistant telemetry regardless of extreme meteorological occlusion bounds.

Ultimately, we demonstrated that atmospheric physics combined with structural priors enables stable real-time cloud restoration under extreme conditions.

Shortcomings and future directions

Despite achieving high-fidelity deterministic restoration under severe occlusion, Thermo-CR exhibits specific physical shortcomings. The framework remains fundamentally vulnerable to absolute zero-transmission scenarios (99.9% opacity), such as those found directly within the dense core of mature convective supercells. In these localized environments where optical depth approaches infinity, the Radiative Transfer Equation mathematically collapses, forcing the algorithm to rely entirely on “historical substitution” via the static spatial prior rather than true photometric recovery. While this deterministic fallback strictly prevents generative hallucination, it inherently masks rapid topological changes occurring beneath the extreme weather front. Future research trajectories must therefore explore the integration of 3D predictive wind-vector forecasting models into the Thermo-CR pipeline. By dynamically extrapolating cloud movement rather than treating occlusion as a static vertical column, subsequent architectures could accurately predict the temporal drift of extreme convective cells, allowing for highly targeted sensor synchronization and mitigating the zero-transmission fallback entirely.
