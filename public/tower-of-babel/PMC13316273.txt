Systematic identification of genomic nonresponse biomarkers to cancer therapies
Authors: J. Usset, J. de Ligt, S. Roerink, P. Roepman, E. Cuppen, F. Martínez-Jiménez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe costs of cancer therapies are rising rapidly worldwide, with novel therapies such as targeted treatment and immunotherapies being major contributors, but their effectiveness can be low or uncertain due to limited postmarket surveillance. Reliable biomarkers to identify patients highly unlikely to respond to cancer therapies represent an increasingly important clinical and societal need, as they could prevent unnecessary treatments, reduce side effects, and alleviate pressure on health care systems.Materials and MethodsWe developed a robust statistical framework for the identification of nonresponse biomarkers for systemic treatments and applied it to whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing data of cancer patients (N = 2594) with advanced disease.ResultsOur approach identified known and potentially novel genomic and transcriptomic biomarkers of nonresponse, such as immune evasion driver events in skin melanoma patients treated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 checkpoint inhibitors and KRASG12 mutations in metastatic colorectal cancer patients treated with different chemotherapy regimens. Analytical power analysis revealed that for most treatments and/or cancer types, the cohort sizes remain underpowered.ConclusionsSystematic identification of nonresponse signals reveals multiple potential biomarkers that will require larger cohort sizes for prospective clinical implementation.

Introduction

Precision oncology has largely focused on the identification of targetable biomolecules that guide novel treatments, which has primarily revealed biomarkers for targeted therapies and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB).1,2 To our knowledge, however, relatively limited attention has been given to the identification of genomic biomarkers that identify patients unlikely to respond, so-called nonresponder biomarkers, despite their substantial clinical value. The key distinction in evaluating nonresponse biomarkers is the robust evidence required to justify withholding an otherwise beneficial therapy. Clinically, this demands a high degree of confidence that the likelihood of benefit is negligible. Specifically, while a biomarker associated with a 30%-40% response rate may be considered useful for identifying likely responders, a nonresponse biomarker must be linked to a response rate acceptably low to be considered actionable in guiding treatment exclusion. When expected patient benefits are high (survival increased by years instead of weeks) or side effects of the treatment are minimal, the bar will be higher than for treatments with limited effects and/or high risk of severe side effects. By flagging individuals unlikely to benefit from specific treatments, nonresponder biomarkers can spare patients unnecessary toxicity, reduce pressure on health care systems,3,4 and prompt timely consideration of alternative strategies, including clinical trial enrolment.

Analytical studies are increasingly uncovering genomic determinants of treatment resistance across cancer types. For example, alterations in the WNT/β-catenin pathway and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDK4/6) have been implicated in limiting the efficacy of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors,5 while elevated chromosomal instability scores have been associated with resistance to multiple standard chemotherapeutic agents.6 Similarly, a higher cumulative burden of oncogenic driver events or pathway-level alterations has been proposed as a marker of diminished response to later-line therapies.7 Although such biomarkers are valuable for identifying patients at risk of treatment failure, they rarely quantify the probability of nonresponse, i.e. the consequences of withholding a therapy based on the identified biomarker. A notable example comes from the field of checkpoint inhibitors for non—small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), in which a low tumour mutational burden (TMB) is associated with poor response. However, when the patient population is stratified based on a TMB cut-off of 10 mutations per megabase, the group of patients that would not receive treatment would still include 30% of responding patients.8 However, the work of van de Haar et al9 shows that an acceptable stratification can be achieved when the TMB biomarker is combined with STK11, KEAP1, or EGFR alterations, resulting in a subgroup of 20% of all patients for which clinical benefit from ICB is below 3%. Although illustrative, this finding remains anecdotal and underscores a broader gap: the lack of systematic approaches to identify, validate, and evaluate the clinical implantation of nonresponse biomarkers across tumour types and therapeutic contexts.

The rapid implementation of comprehensive genomic profiling approaches for cancer diagnosis across institutions10, 11, 12 coupled with harmonized clinicogenomic guidelines for reporting13 present a unique opportunity to systematically identify nonresponder biomarkers across diverse patient populations.

In this study, we present an analytical framework to systematically identify genomic and transcriptomic biomarkers of nonresponse from real-world clinical and molecular data. We analysed data from 2594 patients with complete annotated clinical outcomes across cancer types and treatment regimens. Our analysis uncovered multiple known and novel biomarkers associated with nonresponse to ICB and other cancer therapies that displayed strong signals in our discovery cohort. However, power analysis revealed that for most treatment–tumour type combinations, the existing cohorts remain underpowered to confidently identify biomarkers associated with extremely low response rates. These findings underscore the necessity for vastly increasing real-world clinicogenomic datasets, for example, by increasing federated data-sharing approaches, to enhance detection capability and translate data-driven insights into patient-centred clinical decisions.

Materials and methods

Data source and study design

We used data from a publicly accessible, access-controlled database (n = 7011). All patients in this database have provided explicit informed consent for data sharing for cancer research purposes. The research described here, including applicable human ethics, has been evaluated by the independent Data Access Board of the data provider and is registered under DR-347. The database includes cancer patient data collected from 2012 to the present and is updated continually.

Within the database, we focused on the subset of 2594 patients with annotated biopsy and clinical response data measurements after treatment. All these available data arose from the following two prospective, multicentre studies designed and performed in The Netherlands:•The CPCT-02 study (NCT01855477, https://www.cpct.nl, N = 2449 patients) was a multicentre study designed to generate whole-genome sequencing (WGS) datasets from patients with metastatic or incurable locally advanced solid tumours to support clinical trial matching and translational biomarker research. Lesions were assessed by imaging whenever possible according to RECIST v1.1 criteria, with clinical examination used only if necessary. Baseline examinations were performed within 4 weeks of treatment start, and follow-up evaluations used the same method used at the baseline. Follow-up examinations were scheduled initially within 8-12 weeks, and then subsequently every 12 weeks for the first year and thereafter at the discretion of the investigator. Clinical data were captured by electronic case report forms (eCRFs) completed by an investigator or local data manager, with additional queries resolved within about 1 month, and reviewed by a central data centre verified through source document checks. Both studies included patients with histologically confirmed unresectable locally advanced or metastatic solid tumours, including both de novo and recurrent metastatic disease, with eligibility based on clinical and radiological evidence rather than formal American Joint Committee on Cancer staging. In total, the two studies gathered data from 41 academic, teaching, and general hospitals across The Netherlands, and the genomic materials for both studies were gathered by standardized protocols.17

Patient population

Eligible patients were adults (aged ≥18 years) with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumours who had a recent tumour biopsy and were able to receive systemic or targeted therapies (CPCT-02, DRUP). For the DRUP study, patients were further required to have progression after standard therapy or no remaining standard treatment options. Many patients had received multiple treatment lines; analyses were performed at the patient level using the most recent treatment lines taken after the tumour biopsy.

Patients were stratified into treatment cohorts based on primary tumour site, molecular subtype defined by immunohistochemistry (e.g. ER-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer), and treatment characteristics after biopsy, using hierarchical levels of annotation including individual drug, mechanism of action, and treatment class. Cohorts were defined both across tumour types (pan-cancer) and within specific cancer types to balance cohort homogeneity with available sample size. For patients receiving multiple lines of therapy, only the most recent treatment administered after the biopsy date was considered. In total, 56 cohorts were defined, each comprising at least 30 patients with a minimum of 15 responders and 15 nonresponders (Supplementary Figure S1a and Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100721). These included 23 drug-specific cohorts, 19 mechanism-of-action cohorts, and 14 treatment class cohorts (e.g. chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and hormonal therapy), with a median cohort size of 62 patients and a median of 36 nonresponders. All included patients had measured whole-genome sequencing and annotation of clinical outcomes with RECIST criteria.

Treatment outcomes

Nonresponders were defined as patients who did not achieve at least a partial response or who had stable disease lasting <6 months based on RECIST v1.1.18

For progression-free survival (PFS) analyses, the hazard event was defined as either RECIST-defined progressive disease or death. Time to event was measured from the treatment start date to earliest occurrence of a hazard event. For censored patients with no hazard event, the follow-up time was calculated from the treatment start date to the last recorded treatment date or response assessment.

Whole-genome and transcriptome sequencing

WGS data were generated from tumour (fresh-frozen) samples and matched normal samples (peripheral blood) using the Illumina platform with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads under standard Illumina sequencing conditions. Normal samples achieved a median sequencing coverage of ∼38×, while tumour samples reached a median coverage of ∼109×.19 Whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS) was performed on RNA extracted from the same tumour specimens used for DNA sequencing, generating an average of 2563 million sequencing reads per tumour sample.

DNA reads were mapped to the reference genome GRCh37/hg19 (GCF_000001405.13) using BWA-MEM2 v2.220 and processed further for comprehensive cancer genomics characterization using the Nextflow pipeline OncoAnalyser v2.0.0,21 which was built on the open-source WiGITS toolset.22 The OncoAnalyser repository contains in-depth details on parameter settings, resources, and software versions.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) reads were mapped to the same reference genome using STAR v2.17.1.b23 using the described parameters and were further processed using Isofox24 as part of the OncoAnalyser 2.0 pipeline.

Derived biomarkers

WGS data were available for all patients (N = 2594), and bulk RNA-seq reads were available for a subset of patients (n = 2115). All genomic materials were gathered by standardized protocols and processed uniformly using the WiGiTS pipeline.17,22

Across clinical, genomic, and transcriptomic domains, we derived 2663 binary features for systematic association testing. These features included gene driver and pathway events, mutational signatures, chromosomal copy number summaries, clinical features, somatic summary metrics, telomere and human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-related features, homologous recombination deficiency metrics, complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3)/V(D)J sequence metrics, RNA-based gene set expression features, and neoepitope burden measures. Features were binarized using clinically relevant or quantile-based thresholds. Full definitions, derivations, and thresholds are provided in Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100721.

For genomic features (i.e. potential biomarkers) included in the analysis, we considered all features that can be derived from the analytical tools implemented in the WiGiTS tumour analysis pipeline.22 These include, among others, driver alterations, gene hotspots, mutational signatures, TMBs, whole-genome doubling status, microsatellite instability and homologous recombination deficiency status, telomere length, immune infiltration inferred from CDR3 sequences, and additional genomic characteristics derived from WGS data. A comprehensive description of these features can be found in the pipeline source code, as well as in prior publications describing the distribution and clinical relevance of these metrics in large cancer cohorts.25

For transcriptomic features, we derived 1560 features from publicly available gene sets, following the approach described in a previous study.26 These gene sets were used to quantify transcriptional programs relevant to tumour biology and the tumour microenvironment.

Neoantigen prediction was performed using the Neo pipeline27 in combination with the HLA class I typing pipeline LILAC,28 as previously described.29 Briefly, patient-specific HLA-I alleles were inferred from germline sequencing data, and their status in the tumour (e.g. mutations or loss of heterozygosity affecting HLA-I) was annotated using LILAC. Only intact (functionally retained) HLA-I alleles were considered for neoantigen prediction.

Statistical analysis

We developed an analytical framework to systematically identify biomarkers associated with nonresponse across cohorts (Figure 1). All derived binary features were evaluated for enrichment in patient subgroups with low response rates (<5%) using two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests, while PFS analyses were performed using univariate Cox proportional hazards (Cox-PH) models, with primary tumour type included as a covariate for pan-cancer cohorts and tumour purity for RNA-based features. To reduce the multiple testing burden, analyses were restricted to features with at least six events per cohort, and features with extreme standard errors due to collinearity were excluded. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method, with adjustments applied separately for two-tailed Fisher’s and Cox-PH tests. Individual features with response rates (<5%) and with an adjusted P value < 0.1 were highlighted. Results

Systematic identification of nonresponse biomarkers

To systematically identify features associated with nonresponse, we screened for biomarkers significantly enriched in patient subgroups with low response rates (<5%) and assessed statistical significance by performing both two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests and univariate Cox-PH analyses across all features within each defined cohort (Figure 1A-C). Applying a stringent multiple-testing corrected nonresponse screen—defined as an observed objective response rate <5% and false discovery rate-adjusted P < 0.10—we identified only two biomarkers that were significantly associated with nonresponse across treatment cohorts using both the Fisher and Cox-PH models (Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100721). First, low-to-moderate predicted neoantigen load was associated with nonresponse in NSCLC patients treated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 therapy (4% response rate; 23 nonresponse events; 1 response event; baseline response rate 49%; odds ratio = 0.048; Fisher adjusted P = 0.048, Cox-PH adjusted P = 0.086). Second, the group of KRASG12 mutations, previously implicated in resistance to trifluridine/tipiracil in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC),31 was associated with nonresponse across multiple chemotherapy regimens in colorectal cancer (5% response rate; 62 nonresponse events; 3 response event; baseline response rate 23%; odds ratio = 0.072; Fisher adjusted P = 0.088, Cox-PH adjusted P = 0.011). One nonresponse biomarker reached statistical significance only in Fisher’s exact test. This feature was elevated complement and coagulation cascade RNA expression in prostate cancer patients undergoing androgen deprivation therapy (0% response rate; 22 nonresponse events; 0 response event; baseline response rate 37%; odds ratio = 0; Fisher adjusted P = 0.068, Cox-PH adjusted P = 0.33).32

We also observed 31 markers that were significantly associated in only the Cox-PH models of PFS. These included immune evasion driver events—primarily biallelic B2M loss—in melanoma patients treated with ICB (0% response rate; 10 nonresponse events; 0 response event; baseline response rate 51%; odds ratio = 0; Fisher adjusted P = 0.13, Cox-PH adjusted P = 0.05).33,34 Importantly, only 16 of the 56 cohorts exhibited at least one nonresponder biomarker association (i.e. two-tailed Fisher’s exact test or Cox-PH) (Supplementary Figure S1b).

Notably, many additionally biologically plausible nonresponder markers were observed with <5% response rates, but with statistical significance less than the strict threshold for multiple testing (Figure 2B, C). For example, elevated RNA expression of members of the TGFβ signalling pathway was linked to never-response in patients receiving androgen deprivation therapy (0% response rate; 18 nonresponse events; 0 response event; baseline response rate 34%; odds ratio = 0; Fisher adjusted P = 0.15, Cox-PH adjusted P = 0.36).35 Importantly, the larger cohorts, especially the pan-cancer ICB cohorts, showed many significant markers enriched for nonresponse but lacked individual significant markers within the estimated chance of response <5% threshold, suggesting a possible lack of true deterministic nonresponse markers.

The full list of significant associations and their systematic curation is comprehensively annotated in Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100721, providing a valuable resource that may guide clinical decision making and prospective validation in dedicated studies.

Independent validation of nonresponse biomarkers

We performed validation of selected biomarkers using publicly available datasets provided through cBioPortal.36 For B2M biallelic loss in metastatic melanoma, three immunogenomic studies of patients treated with checkpoint inhibitors were analysed,37, 38, 39 and all other validations were performed using the MSK-CHORD dataset.40 B2M biallelic loss in metastatic melanoma patients was associated with nonresponse and worse survival relative to responders, in line with previous studies.29,41 In colorectal cancer patients, KRASG12 mutations, including KRASG12D, were associated with modestly worse overall survival outcomes after chemotherapy treatment, consistent with prior literature.42 In prostate cancer patients treated with abiraterone, DNA damage response pathway (DDR) alterations showed a weak association with overall survival, with a stronger effect observed in patients with biallelic deletions of DDR tumour suppressor genes, consistent with published reports linking DDR alterations to worse outcomes in prostate cancer.43 NRF2 pathway alterations did not show an association with survival in patients treated with carboplatin or gemcitabine. Overall, these validation analyses offered partial support for several candidate biomarkers, although the results were constrained by the modest cohort size, the limited availability of treatment response data (i.e. overall survival was the only endpoint available), and the narrow representation of cancer types. This underscores the inherent challenges of performing external validation in heterogeneous publicly available datasets. The complete external validation analyses are available in the Supplementary Note S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100721.

Combined biomarkers to detect nonresponse to checkpoint inhibitors

A key challenge for our systematic nonresponder analysis was the stringent focus on univariate features associated with response rates <5%. Given these strict response rate thresholds, as observed, few features reached statistical significance individually. However, many features showed a strong association with nonresponse across various cohorts, albeit with response rates >5%, suggesting that multivariate combinations of features could be created to better classify nonresponse.

As an example of a multivariate classifier, we focused on the cohort of patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors, of whom 370 had both complete WGS and RNA-seq data available. From the systematic association testing in this immunotherapy cohort, we selected features that were highly statistically significant for nonresponse and PFS (Fisher adjusted and Cox-PH P values < 0.01). The correlation analysis showed that these nonresponse associated features were grouped into four independent clusters with biological meaning: (i) low neoantigen load, low tumour mutation load (TML), TMB per megabase <6, low measured RNA neoantigens; (ii) low T-cell infiltration, 12 gene sets related to reduced immune infiltration; (iii) high expression of a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) gene set for basal cell carcinoma, linked to hedgehog signalling; and (iv) high expression of a KEGG gene set measuring the renin-angiotensin system (Figure 3B).

To build the multivariate classifier, we selected one representative feature from each cluster—low TML, low CD8 effector T-cell expression, high basal cell carcinoma expression, and high renin-angiotensin system expression. For each patient, we then quantified the number of nonresponse associated features present. The subset of patients with all four nonresponse biomarkers showed minimal likelihood of deriving durable clinical benefit and significantly shorter PFS compared with patients with each of the biomarkers individually (0 of 5, 0% response rate, Figure 3C), suggesting that rational combination of nonredundant biomarkers could be a way to further refine subpopulations of patients that will not respond. Furthermore, patients with three of these four nonresponse markers also showed a low response likelihood (2 of 42, 5%) (Figure 3C). Importantly, this analysis, enabled by a large cohort size and strong univariate associations, highlights the potential of well-powered cohorts to develop robust, multivariate markers of primary resistance.

Cohort size to detect robust nonresponse biomarkers

In our systematic study, we highlighted examples of nonresponder signals with observed response rates <5% across treatment cohorts. Statistical significance for these signals was assessed relative to their respective baseline response rates within each cohort (ranging from 18% to 73% in the study cohort), rather than against absolute low response thresholds (e.g. <5% or <2%). To estimate the maximum probability of response when zero responders are observed for a biomarker, we applied the Clopper–Pearson exact method.30 We found that at least 59 nonresponder events are needed to establish a response rate <5% and 149 events for <2%. Required cohort sizes depend on biomarker prevalence, with rarer markers requiring larger cohorts. For example, in skin melanoma patients with biallelic inactivation of immune evasion driver genes (0% responders, 10 nonresponse events, 6% biomarker prevalence, 166 patients, and baseline response rate 51%), the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval is 26% (Figure 4A). Discussion

Biomarkers of nonresponse fundamentally differ from stratification biomarkers in that they aim to identify patients unlikely to benefit from specific therapies. The key challenge in evaluating nonresponse biomarkers is the robust evidence required (e.g. less than 1%-5% of probability of response) to justify withholding an otherwise beneficial therapy. This distinction is clinically significant, as reliable nonresponse biomarkers could help avoid ineffective treatments, reducing unnecessary toxicity and health care costs. However, despite their potential, the discovery and clinical implementation of such biomarkers remain challenging.

This effort enabled a comprehensive and systematic identification of nonresponse biomarkers, which can be further refined as additional data becomes available. Although WGS is rapidly advancing towards clinical implementation, its adoption is not yet universal. Some of the identified biomarkers are univariate (e.g. single-gene or pathway alterations) and could, in principle, be translated into targeted clinical assays or immunohistochemistry-based panels routinely used across many centres. However, several biomarkers rely on multigene features or the assessment of biallelic gene disruptions/deletions, which are more difficult to capture using current clinical sequencing panels.

Robust identification of nonresponse biomarkers requires large, well-annotated cohorts, as demonstrated by our power analysis. In this study, we conducted a systematic, unbiased screen for biomarkers of nonresponse using one of the largest integrated resources combining WGS and WTS with detailed clinical annotation. Although this comprehensive analysis identified several promising candidate biomarkers—many of which are consistent with prior evidence and that were partially validated with external datasets—it also underscored critical challenges for clinical translation. First, the statistical power to confidently define true nonresponder subgroups remains limited in a systematic context. In our analysis, only four univariate biomarkers exceeded the stringent significance thresholds. Importantly, statistical power is not solely a function of sample size; factors such as biomarker prevalence and response heterogeneity substantially influence the ability to detect and validate nonresponse signals. Importantly, the limitation of low biomarker prevalence may be mitigated by aggregating individual driver events into broader molecular pathways—a strategy supported by several of our never-responder signals. Second, many of the identified biomarkers were associated with never-responder phenotypes in relatively small cohorts. Whether these represent true never-responders remains uncertain and requires validation in larger, independent datasets. Third, the scarcity of definitive never-responder biomarkers in larger cohorts—such as those treated with immunotherapy—suggests that combinatorial biomarker strategies may offer improved discriminatory power, as illustrated in our multivariate analysis. Fourth, in this study, we defined durable clinical benefit for stable disease as 6 months; however, shorter time frames (e.g. 4 months) are also used in clinical practice, which may influence the outcomes of the analyses. Finally, it is important to recognize that identifying a biomarker associated with poor response is not equivalent to reliably estimating a sufficiently low probability of benefit—an essential threshold for informing decisions about withholding therapy. Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for expanded, response-annotated real-world datasets and the development of rigorous statistical frameworks to enable the discovery and clinical implementation of nonresponse biomarkers.

To address these limitations, the incorporation of real-world data from systematic diagnostic profiling efforts could substantially enhance the power and generalizability of nonresponse biomarker discovery. Equally important is the development of federated data infrastructures that integrate harmonized sequencing protocols and analytical pipelines across institutions and countries. These coordinated frameworks are essential to support large-scale, reproducible identification of nonresponder subgroups and to accelerate their clinical translation across diverse health care systems.
