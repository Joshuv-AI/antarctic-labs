The Role of Continuous Glucose Monitoring in Managing Hyperglycaemia First Detected in Pregnancy: A Narrative Review
Authors: Emmanuel Cosson, Laura Chabrol Haas, Fleur Levrat‐Guillen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHyperglycaemia first diagnosed in pregnancy (HIP) is the most common medical condition affecting pregnancy, affecting up to 40% of pregnancies in some countries. The global incidence of HIP is increasing owing to rising rates of overweight and obesity, advanced maternal age, and early screening for HIP. Current guidelines recommend self‐monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) via frequent capillary finger‐prick testing, which can be painful and inconvenient, contributing to poor adherence, psychological distress, and suboptimal glycaemic control. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) offers an alternative approach, providing real‐time, automated glucose readings without the need for repeated invasive testing. This review evaluates the current evidence on the clinical benefits of CGM in HIP, including its effects on glycaemic control, maternal and neonatal outcomes, and patients' quality of life. Although existing studies are limited by small sample sizes and methodological variability, findings suggest that CGM might improve adherence, patient satisfaction, glycaemic control, and neonatal birth weight compared with SMBG. However, the lack of large‐scale randomised trials has hindered widespread adoption and reimbursement of CGM for HIP. There is a clear need for more patient‐centred glucose monitoring strategies and further research to confirm the long‐term benefits of CGM in HIP management and to identify the patient populations most likely to benefit from CGM use. Addressing these gaps is essential to optimising care and improving outcomes for the growing population affected by HIP.Keywords: continuous glucose monitoring, gestational diabetes mellitus, hyperglycaemia in pregnancy, overt diabetes in pregnancy, pregnancy outcomes, quality of life

Introduction

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), which has transformed glucose monitoring in type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) [1], is increasingly proving valuable in supporting patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) [2, 3]. CGM enables real‐time tracking of glucose levels, offering a more dynamic and less invasive alternative to traditional self‐monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), which relies on frequent capillary finger‐prick tests. Using a small sensor worn on the body, CGM provides automated readings which support glucose management more effectively than SMBG [1, 2, 3].

Studies of pregnant women with T1D have shown beneficial effects of CGM on maternal glucose control and neonatal outcomes [4]. In particular, in a randomised controlled trial (RCT), CGM use by pregnant women with T1D was associated with significant improvements in maternal glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), time in range (TIR), and time above range (TAR) as well as reductions in the rates of large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) births, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions, and neonatal hypoglycaemia compared with SMBG [5]. Data from this trial [5] and an observational study of pregnant women with T1D using CGM [6] showed that even a 5% increase in TIR was associated with improved neonatal outcomes [7].

In this article, we focus on hyperglycaemia first diagnosed during pregnancy (HIP). HIP is the most common medical condition affecting pregnancy [8], with its incidence rising globally in parallel with increasing maternal age and the obesity epidemic [9]. Its growing burden highlights the need for improved diagnostic and management strategies [10]. We explore how CGM could be useful in the management of HIP.

HIP: Key Facts

HIP is diagnosed based on an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), but there is a lack of international consensus on who should undergo an OGTT, when it should be performed (early in pregnancy to identify possible undiagnosed pregestational T2D and early hyperglycaemia, then usually between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation when insulin resistance increases), and the diagnostic plasma glucose threshold [10, 11]. HIP comprises two entities: gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and overt diabetes in pregnancy (DIP). Glucose thresholds for DIP (which might correspond to unknown T2D before pregnancy) meet the diagnostic criteria for diabetes in non‐pregnant individuals, whereas glucose thresholds for GDM are lower [12, 13]. Globally, GDM is estimated to affect 14% of pregnant women [14], with up to 40% of pregnancies affected in some countries [8].

Emerging evidence suggests that early HIP (detected before 20 weeks of gestation) represents a distinct metabolic state compared with HIP diagnosed later [8, 15, 16]. Some researchers propose that HIP may, in fact, be the first clinical manifestation of an underlying chronic metabolic disorder with implications extending across the lifespan of both mother and child [17]. The concept of early HIP has generated considerable debate, particularly regarding its classification and clinical implications. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO) 2013 criteria [12], it falls under the umbrella of HIP, while the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) urged caution in 2016, citing insufficient evidence to determine long‐term risk [18]. However, the Treatment of Booking GDM (TOBOGM) RCT has since shown that immediate treatment of early GDM (before 20 weeks' gestation) in women with at least one risk factor for hyperglycaemia significantly improves neonatal outcomes compared with no immediate treatment [19]. At a subsequent IADPSG summit based on the TOBOGM RCT findings, most delegates supported testing for early GDM [20].

Increasing maternal glucose concentrations during pregnancy correlate with perinatal complications [10]. The main maternal HIP‐related outcomes are gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, caesarean delivery, and traumatic labour/perineal tears; neonatal complications are preterm birth, LGA birth, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, NICU admission, and respiratory distress syndrome [10, 21]. HIP is also a strong predictor of future cardiometabolic disease in the mother and metabolic disease in the child [8, 22].

Optimising early diagnosis and treatment of HIP is essential to reducing both immediate and future health risks for mothers and their children [10, 22, 23, 24]. Adopting healthy dietary patterns preventing excessive gestational weight gain (GWG), engaging in regular physical activity, and reducing sedentary behaviour are foundational strategies for mitigating the risk of HIP‐related outcomes [10, 25]. SMBG before and after meals guides the introduction of pharmacotherapy [10]. Women are asked to track their glucose levels consistently, which can be difficult to maintain over the course of pregnancy. Failure to reach glucose targets (pre‐prandial ≥ 95 mg/dL [5.3 mmol/L]; post‐prandial ≥ 120 mg/dL [6.7 mmol/L] at 2 h) is usually followed by the introduction of pharmacological treatment, the gold standard being insulin therapy [10].

Altogether, HIP has a psychological impact, especially through the burden of SMBG, nutrition changes, and eventually injection of insulin [26, 27]. In this context, the aim of our review is to investigate whether and how CGM use by women with HIP, as compared with SMBG, could improve glucose control, HIP‐related prognosis, and quality of life. We also explore literature dealing with potential drawbacks and limitations of CGM. Finally, we focus on emerging research directions for CGM in HIP.

The literature was selected through a targeted approach, informed by a prior targeted literature review and these predefined topics of interest. To identify relevant studies, we searched the PubMed database using keywords including “continuous glucose monitoring,” “pregnancy,” “gestational diabetes mellitus,” “hyperglycemia first diagnosed in pregnancy”, “neonatal outcomes,” “glycemic control,” “management,” “accuracy,” and “costs.”

CGM After Diagnosis of HIP

Glucose monitoring after diagnosis of HIP guides treatment decisions to achieve good blood glucose control and thus reduce the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [10, 28]. According to the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), the testing frequency may be up to seven times per day [28]. However, the pain/discomfort, inconvenience, and social stigma associated with capillary finger‐prick SMBG are barriers to its frequent use [29], and poor adherence to SMBG is common among women with HIP [30]. Furthermore, SMBG provides only snapshots of glucose levels and may miss glucose fluctuations occurring between capillary finger‐prick tests. A role for CGM in this setting has been investigated, with a recent systematic review highlighting the ability of CGM to detect both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia events more effectively than SMBG in women with HIP/GDM [31]. For example, 7‐day CGM provided a more comprehensive assessment of nocturnal hypoglycaemia than SMBG in a cohort of 90 women with HIP (mean gestational age: 27 weeks) [32]. In another study of 73 women with HIP (gestational age: 22–34 weeks), CGM detected a higher proportion in need of antihyperglycaemic medication than SMBG [33]. CGM has been shown to detect masked hypoglycaemia in pregnant women with and without HIP, suggesting that low glucose levels are a physiologic adaptation of pregnancy, although the duration of hypoglycaemic episodes was longer in those with HIP [34]. Evidence for CGM targets in women with HIP is limited; current guidelines recommend a glucose target range of 63–140 mg/dL based on data from pregnant women with T1D, but the target for percent TIR in women with HIP is unclear [35, 36]. Glucose Control With CGM Compared With SMBG

By providing more comprehensive glucose monitoring than SMBG, CGM could improve the management of HIP [31]. In an Australian observational study, 55 pregnant women with diabetes (37 with HIP, 10 with T2D, and 8 with T1D) underwent SMBG and CGM, providing 68 72‐h CGM traces for analysis. Treating clinicians reported that 42 of the 68 CGM traces provided additional information (e.g., hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia not detected by SMBG) which altered clinical management decisions [37].

A propensity score matching study of 1398 women with HIP in China found a significant reduction in fasting glucose level before delivery in the CGM group compared with the SMBG group, as well as significant improvements in measures of glycaemic variability from the first 6 days to the last 6 days of using CGM [38]. By contrast, a pilot study conducted among women with HIP in Germany compared a group that was randomly offered intermittently‐scanned CGM (isCGM; n = 37) with a group using SMBG (n = 74) and found no significant difference between the two in HbA1c progression [39]. The isCGM group had slightly higher HbA1c at birth than the SMBG group, but the clinical relevance of this finding is uncertain; it may reflect selection bias, as women in the isCGM group were also found to be diagnosed with HIP earlier in their pregnancies and may therefore have had a more complex metabolic condition than those in the SMBG group [39].

RCTs comparing glucose control between women using CGM and those using SMBG are summarised in Table 1. In the Flash Glucose Monitoring in GDM (FLAMINGO) RCT in Poland (n = 99), women using isCGM demonstrated significantly greater reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose levels during the first four weeks of monitoring than those using SMBG [46]. CGM was associated with higher TIR than SMBG in a US‐based RCT by Valent et al. (n = 111) [51] and in the multinational Real‐time CGM for Reduced Adverse Complications and Events in Women With Gestational Diabetes (GRACE) RCT (n = 345) [49, 50]. Another large RCT (DipGluMo; n = 297) [48] found a lower TIR in the SMBG group compared with the SMBG group at 34–38 weeks of gestation but not directly postpartum; this finding should be interpreted with caution as it is based on masked CGM data from the SMBG group, 43% of whom refused at least one CGM session for personal reasons, resulting in an imbalance of the data. In addition, SMBG was performed six times daily in DipGluMo [48], compared with at least four times daily in GRACE [50] and the study by Valent et al. [51]; this might have contributed to better glycaemic control in the DipGluMo SMBG group. Treatment differences might also have contributed; use of rapid‐acting insulin was much more common in GRACE (CGM, 41.2%; SMBG, 30.3%) [49] than in DipGluMo (CGM, 4.5%; SMBG, 1.4%) [48].

Two RCTs showed significant reductions in the incidence [45] and duration [40] of hypoglycaemia with CGM compared with SMBG (Table 1). A third RCT [43] found a significantly increased incidence of hypoglycaemia overall but not symptomatic hypoglycaemia in the CGM group compared with the SMBG group; this finding likely reflects better detection of asymptomatic hypoglycaemia by CGM compared with SMBG.

Other RCTs in women with HIP/GDM showed no significant difference between CGM and SMBG groups in measures of glycaemic balance and variability (Table 1), but this might reflect excellent care/adherence in SMBG groups in the RCT setting, or it might be a consequence of insufficient sample sizes or study durations to achieve statistical significance in the individual studies. Overall, systematic reviews of RCTs and observational studies have identified evidence for the superiority of CGM over SMBG for the assessment of dysglycaemia [31, 52], and meta‐analyses of RCTs point to a beneficial effect of CGM on HbA1c in women with HIP/GDM (Hedges' g: −0.43 [95% confidence interval (CI): −0.63, −0.22] [53]; mean difference at end of pregnancy: −0.22 [95% CI: −0.42, −0.03] [54]). Collectively, the evidence suggests a role for CGM in improving glucose control compared with SMBG in women with HIP/GDM [52].

How Might CGM Enable Better Glucose Control Than SMBG?

CGM offers more comprehensive information than SMBG while reducing the burden of repeated capillary finger‐prick testing. This might improve glucose control by improving the detection of glycaemic imbalances [31, 52], supporting self‐efficacy, and enabling timely adjustments of health behaviours and medication (Figure 2) [29].

Improved Compliance and Health Behaviours

The reduced burden associated with CGM use (see Section 7) might improve compliance with glucose monitoring. In a RCT in China, compliance with glucose monitoring among women with HIP was significantly greater in the isCGM group (94.5%) than in the SMBG group (74.5%) [45]. The isCGM group also showed improved healthcare behaviours including improved diet control, weight monitoring, appropriate exercise, and attendance at obstetric check‐ups, compared with the SMBG group [45]. CGM might help patients to optimise their exercise routine and diet by enabling them to track the effects of physical activity and food choices on glucose levels [45]. CGM has also been associated with reduced GWG compared with SMBG in multiple studies (see Section 6) [38, 41, 45, 47, 54].

Improved Understanding of HIP

Use of isCGM was associated with a reported increase in the understanding of HIP in the German pilot study described above, along with a slight increase in perceived hypoglycaemia which likely reflects an increased awareness and ability to detect hypoglycaemic events [39]. CGM might therefore be useful for individuals with a history of hypoglycaemia during pregnancy, particularly when taking glucose‐lowering medications, and those concerned about hypoglycaemia as a result of activity or exercise.

Support for Treatment Decisions

An observational study of women with HIP in Australia demonstrated that CGM provides a more comprehensive assessment of nocturnal hyperglycaemia than SMBG, potentially helping improve treatment decisions [32]. In a Polish study, women with GDM choosing to use isCGM had more check‐up visits (including teleconsultations) than those choosing to use SMBG [55], which may have provided more opportunities to optimise treatment.

CGM facilitates the sharing of glycaemic data with healthcare professionals remotely in real time (telemonitoring), without requiring manual documentation by the patient (which might be particularly helpful for patients with poor literacy). Telemedicine (including telemonitoring and remote medical consultation and health education) has been shown to improve glycaemic control compared with standard care in a meta‐analysis of RCTs in women with HIP [56].

Some RCTs in women with HIP have shown significantly greater use of insulin [40, 41] or antihyperglycaemic therapy [33] in CGM groups compared with SMBG groups; one of the studies evaluated hypoglycaemia duration and found a significant decrease with CGM compared with SMBG [40], even though insulin use was increased. CGM has been associated with non‐significant trends toward greater use of insulin [47, 48] or intensive insulin [43] in other RCTs. Majewska et al. [46] showed no significant difference in qualification to insulin therapy between the isCGM and SMBG groups, but the isCGM group more frequently altered their dietary habits than the SMBG group. Among women with HIP who used CGM once for 3–7 days, 52.6% reported responding to correct for hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia, and 48% had their management plan modified based on CGM data [42].

Overall, CGM might enable early identification of the need for medical therapy and support discussions between patients and their healthcare team in which data can be reviewed together for shared decision making. In addition, CGM could increase patient comfort with medical treatment by allowing patients to see the benefit of consistent use of a recommended medication like insulin. However, CGM might also increase the need for escalation of therapy.

Outcomes—Translation to Improved Prognosis?

Measures of glucose control in pregnant women have been associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes in various studies [57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62]. For example, increased plasma glucose levels (less severe than in overt diabetes) obtained on OGTT were associated with increased rates of LGA births, cord‐blood serum C‐peptide level above the 90th percentile, primary caesarean delivery, clinical neonatal hypoglycaemia, premature delivery, shoulder dystocia or birth injury, NICU admission, hyperbilirubinaemia, and preeclampsia in the Hyperglycemia and Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes (HAPO) study [57]. Pregnant women with insulin‐treated diabetes and adverse neonatal outcomes (preterm delivery, macrosomia, LGA births, small for gestational age [SGA] births, neonatal hypoglycaemia, neonatal jaundice, and NICU admission) had significantly higher TAR and lower TIR than those without adverse neonatal outcomes [58]. Women who delivered LGA infants had significantly higher overnight glucose levels than women with non‐LGA infants [59]. Upper centiles of the CGM parameters percent coefficient of variation and lability index were associated with an increased risk of caesarean delivery in Asian pregnant women with overweight or obesity [60]. In women with HIP, TAR, area under the curve, and mean blood glucose were associated with any adverse pregnancy outcome (defined as preterm birth, LGA birth, fetal distress, premature rupture of membranes, and/or NICU admission) [61]. In another study of women with HIP, increased TAR after lunch was associated with an increased risk of macrosomia and LGA births [62].

If CGM is able to improve glucose control compared with SMBG in women with HIP, would this translate into improved maternal and neonatal outcomes? Observational studies have produced mixed results in this regard (Table 2). A comparative study in Germany involving 37 women with HIP using CGM and 74 using SMBG found no significant differences in perinatal outcomes such as caesarean section, GWG, LGA births, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and NICU admissions; this study also showed no significant difference in HbA1c progression but may have been affected by selection bias (as discussed in Section 4) [39]. Larger studies in China [38] and Poland [55] (including 3062 women with HIP and 277 women with GDM, respectively) also found no significant differences between CGM and SMBG groups in the incidence of caesarean section, LGA births/macrosomia, or SGA births, but the Chinese study did find significant reductions in GWG, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and transfers to the neonatal department [38]. Conversely, the Polish study found a significant increase in GWG in women choosing to use CGM compared with those choosing SMBG [55].

Individual RCTs have also yielded mixed findings (Table 2). A RCT that compared periodic use of CGM with SMBG in 340 women with HIP in China showed significant reductions in several adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes with CGM, including preeclampsia, primary caesarean delivery, premature delivery, LGA birth, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, hyperbilirubinaemia, and respiratory distress syndrome [40]. SGA births and NICU admissions showed no significant difference between the CGM and SMBG groups. In a sub‐analysis by insulin use, CGM was associated with significant reductions in primary caesarean delivery, premature delivery, LGA birth, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and hyperbilirubinaemia in women without insulin treatment but not in those with insulin treatment. However, interpretation of this finding is limited by the small number of women receiving insulin treatment in this study (n = 64) [40].

A smaller RCT in China compared periodic use of CGM with SMBG in 106 women with HIP, and found a reduction in GWG (as well as increased prevalence of insulin use) with CGM [41]. Excessive weight gain occurred in 56.4% of SMBG users compared with 33.3% of CGM users, while appropriate weight gain was achieved by 62.7% of CGM users versus only 38.2% of those using SMBG. Inadequate weight gain was slightly less common in the CGM group (3.9%) than in the SMBG group (5.5%). Other outcomes (including caesarean deliveries, LGA and SGA births, macrosomia, and neonatal hypoglycaemia) showed no significant difference between the two groups.

GWG showed a notable difference between periodic CGM and SMBG groups in another RCT in China that included 124 women with GDM [47]. In the SMBG group, 46.8% exceeded the recommended weight gain, compared with 25.8% in the CGM group. Furthermore, 59.7% of the CGM group gained weight within the recommended range, compared with 40.3% of the SMBG group. Birth weight was significantly reduced in the CGM group compared with the SMBG group (3124 ± 370 g vs. 3292 ± 387 g), but the incidence of LGA or SGA births and macrosomia showed no significant difference [47]. A fourth RCT in China (n = 110 women with HIP) also found an increased proportion of patients gaining weight within the recommended range in the group that used isCGM for 2 weeks (90.9%) compared with the SMBG group (70.9%) [45].

The GlucoMOMS RCT was conducted in the Netherlands and Belgium, and included 290 pregnant women with insulin‐treated diabetes (T1D, T2D, or HIP) [63]. Women assigned to periodic use of CGM had a significantly lower incidence of preeclampsia than the SMBG group, both in the overall study population and in the subset with HIP (n = 108). No significant differences in neonatal outcomes were identified [63].

The FLAMINGO RCT in Poland (which showed improvement in some measures of glycaemic balance with isCGM compared with SMBG; see Section 5 above) found that women assigned to isCGM for 4 weeks had a markedly lower incidence of macrosomia than those assigned to SMBG until birth (4.1% vs. 20.0%). However, the incidence of caesarean deliveries, LGA births, and neonatal hypoglycaemia did not differ significantly between the two groups [46].

Two RCTs—the Swiss DipGluMo study (n = 297 women with GDM) [48] and a study in Saudi Arabia (n = 122 women with HIP) [42]—found no significant differences in pregnancy outcomes between CGM and SMBG groups. In the Saudi Arabian study, this may have been due to the short duration of CGM (single use for 3–7 days) [42]. However, CGM was used continuously for a long period (mean 67.3 days) in the DipGluMo study [48]. The lack of effect on outcomes in the DipGluMo study was mirrored by a lack of significant glycaemic benefits compared with SMBG and might be explained by excellent care in the SMBG arm. The GRACE RCT (which had a similar design to DipGluMo but with less frequent monitoring in the SMBG group) showed a significant reduction in the incidence of LGA births with CGM compared with SMBG (3.5% vs. 10.3%) [49, 50].

The different findings across RCTs may reflect differences in multiple factors including sample size, study population, CGM duration, SMBG frequency, and HIP treatment. Variations in study design make comparison across studies challenging. Nevertheless, meta‐analyses have been performed (Table 2). Chang et al. [53] included RCTs in women with T1D, T2D, or HIP/GDM in their meta‐analysis and found small but statistically significant reductions in birth weight (Hedges' g: −0.16 [95% CI: −0.27, −0.04]) and the incidence of caesarean deliveries (Hedges' g: −0.17 [95% CI: −0.33, −0.02]) associated with CGM compared with SMBG [53]. Garcia‐Moreno et al. included only studies of women with HIP/GDM in their RCT meta‐analysis and found that CGM was associated with reductions in GWG (mean difference: −1.17 [95% CI: −2.15, −0.19]) and birth weight (mean difference: −116.26 [95% CI: −224.70, −7.81]) compared with SMBG [54]. Significant associations with other outcomes (preeclampsia, caesarean deliveries, preterm, LGA and SGA births, neonatal hypoglycaemia, NICU admissions, and Apgar scores) were not identified; it should be noted that this meta‐analysis included a small number of RCTs and low number of participants which limited its statistical power [54]. Both meta‐analyses were published in 2022 and did not include key recent RCTs such as DipGluMo and GRACE.

Convenience and Quality of Life

In addition to glycaemic balance and complications, disease burden and quality of life are important considerations for patients with HIP. Nearly 90% of women with HIP report a negative impact on quality of life [64]. HIP stigma may cause women to avoid testing or reporting of blood glucose levels and may also lead to disordered eating and social isolation, while the need for insulin therapy may be associated with feelings of fear and guilt [10]. Diabetes distress is likely in women with HIP and has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [65]. Alleviation of the psychosocial impact of HIP is therefore an important aspect of disease management.

Patients diagnosed with HIP must monitor glucose levels until birth. SMBG requires 4–7 capillary finger‐prick tests every day, which can be painful, inconvenient, and difficult for patients to maintain on top of the already substantial burden of managing exercise, diet, and possibly medication, as well as general pregnancy self‐care. Poor adherence to SMBG has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes; a study of 91 newly diagnosed individuals with HIP in France showed that patients with good adherence to the testing guidelines were less likely to have preeclampsia than those with poor adherence (1.9% vs. 12.2%). In addition, only 35.7% of patients with adequate SMBG timing required insulin treatment, compared with 63.0% of those with poor SMBG timing [30].

Several studies suggest that CGM may offer advantages over traditional SMBG in terms of convenience, patient satisfaction, and quality of life (Table 3). Two prospective, single‐arm studies in the UK and Austria [66] and in Portugal [67] reported favourable ratings for isCGM sensor application, sensor wear and use, and comparisons with SMBG in most participants. A US‐based prospective single‐arm study found that women with HIP rated isCGM more favourably than SMBG in terms of ease of use and freedom in daily life, whereas SMBG caused them more pain and embarrassment [68]. Women with HIP in a German pilot study [39] and a prospective observational study in Korea [64] also reported improved treatment satisfaction, flexibility, and convenience with isCGM. Pregnant women with diabetes (n = 70; 89% with HIP) who underwent simultaneous isCGM and SMBG in a prospective study in India all preferred isCGM over SMBG [69]. In the DipGluMo RCT (which focused on women with GDM), participants in both the CGM and SMBG groups reported that they would have chosen CGM if they had the choice; the CGM group also reported that CGM was easier to use than SMBG [48]. Taken together, these results suggest that CGM may improve quality of life for women with HIP by alleviating the burden associated with SMBG.

Limitations

As noted above, the target for percent TIR in women with HIP is unclear (Section 3), and CGM might increase the need for escalation of therapy (Section 5.3). Other limitations of CGM in HIP are described below.

Accuracy During Pregnancy

While SMBG measures glucose in the bloodstream, CGM assesses glucose in the interstitial fluid. Although there is a 5–10‐min delay in interstitial fluid glucose response to changes in blood glucose, this lag is generally not clinically significant for routine treatment decisions [70, 71].

CGM readings were found to differ from venous blood glucose readings in studies of pregnant women with T1D, T2D, or HIP, with mean absolute relative differences (MARDs) of 9.5% [72] and 10.3% [73] and a mean bias of −0.9 mg/dL [72]. IsCGM readings were also found to be lower than venous blood glucose readings during OGTT in a study of pregnant women with and without HIP (MARD: 12.3%) [74]. Several studies directly comparing isCGM and SMBG found that isCGM readings tended to be lower than SMBG values, both in a mixed population of pregnant women with T1D, T2D, or HIP [75] and in populations solely comprising women with HIP [67, 68, 76]. The overall MARD between the isCGM and SMBG values in these studies ranged from 13.2% [76] to 19% [75]. A MARD < 17% has been considered to indicate acceptable agreement in CGM studies [67]; of the four studies mentioned above, three reported a MARD below this threshold [67, 68, 76]. In the remaining study where the first version of FreeStyle Libre was used [75], the authors highlighted that in 25%–35% of cases requiring therapeutic adjustments for hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia, clinical decisions would have differed depending on whether isCGM or SMBG data were used [75]. However, the study compared interstitial glucose with capillary glucose measurements rather than the reference technique of venous glucose measurement [77]. In addition, the algorithm used by FSL to measure interstitial glucose has since been revised, improving the system's performance; a study of FSL with the revised algorithm in 144 adults with T1D or T2D showed a MARD of 9.2% compared with venous blood glucose [77, 78]. Three other studies in mixed populations of pregnant women with diabetes found good agreement between isCGM and SMBG: Citro et al. [79] and Scott et al. [66] reported MARDs of 9.3% and 11.8%, respectively, and Pikee et al. [69] found no significant difference between corresponding measurements. The limitation of potential underestimation of blood glucose values should be balanced against the ability of CGM to detect hypo‐ and hyperglycaemic events that are missed by single capillary finger‐prick measurements [37, 45]. Many pregnant women with T1D, T2D on insulin, or HIP have used CGM and had treatments adapted according to interstitial glucose values, with overall no safety signal that would create doubts about the accuracy of the sensors in pregnant women.

Time Needed for Patient Education

Training a patient to use CGM effectively—in addition to SMBG which may on occasion be required for confirmation—may be challenging and time consuming for some healthcare professionals (e.g., those who have little familiarity with CGM). There are many aspects of CGM use to be covered in training, for example sensor insertion, setting of targets, understanding the difference between interstitial fluid and capillary blood glucose and the concept of lag time, using features such as alarms and trend arrows, adjustment of alarm thresholds, and interpretation of data [29]. Nevertheless, CGM use (where approved) has become well integrated into the care pathway for patients with HIP.

Stress and Secondary Anxiety

A systematic review has identified a potential association between CGM use and emotional distress in patients with T1D [80]. More recently, adults with T1D in a qualitative study highlighted the mental health aspect of CGM [81]. The amount of information provided by CGM may cause patients to feel overwhelmed [82]. This could hamper successful CGM use: adults with T1D who showed improved glycaemic control with CGM (’responders’) were found to use retrospective pattern analysis rather than just minute‐by‐minute data analysis, while ’non‐responders’ preferred minute‐by‐minute analysis, with some reporting not being able to manage the large amount of retrospective data [83]. Patient education may help to address the potential for anxiety related to information overload in patients with HIP initiating CGM.

Cost

Uptake of CGM for HIP may be limited by the cost of CGM devices. However, if CGM is able to reduce adverse pregnancy outcomes (see Section 6), this would result in cost savings that could outweigh the acquisition costs [84].

Availability and Functionality of Electronic Devices

An additional consideration is the unequal global availability of CGM technology. In many low‐ and middle‐income countries, including parts of Africa, CGM is not routinely available due to cost, infrastructure limitations, and lack of reimbursement. As a result, most published data originate from high‐income settings, limiting the generalisability of findings. Lack of reimbursement may also limit availability of CGM for HIP in these settings. For example, most state Medicaid programs in the USA do not cover CGM for women with HIP [84]. From an ethical perspective, the expanding use of CGM raises concerns regarding equity, digital literacy, and the potential widening of healthcare disparities if access remains restricted to selected populations.

In some cases, practical problems with the CGM sensors may also limit their utility; in a German pilot study, some participants reported sensor defects and others reported difficulties attaching the sensor or accidentally detaching it [39]. This is another area in which patient education may be beneficial.

Emerging Research Directions for CGM in Hip

Defining Glycaemic Targets

Early adoption of CGM was hindered by the lack of standardised glycaemic targets. However, the introduction of TIR metrics has provided a valuable clinical framework. Adapting these targets for pregnancy‐specific needs represents a critical step toward optimising therapeutic decision making [35].

For pregnancies with T1D, current American Diabetes Association guidelines recommend a TIR (63–140 mg/dL) goal of > 70%, with a level 1 time below range (TBR; < 63 mg/dL) of < 4%, a level 2 TBR (< 54 mg/dL) of < 1%, and a TAR (> 140 mg/dL) of < 25% [36]. Even modest improvements in TIR can improve neonatal outcomes [7].

For HIP/GDM (and pregnancies with T2D), the International Consensus on TIR endorses the same glucose ranges as for pregnancies with T1D but has not recommended target amounts of time in each range owing to insufficient data [35, 36]. Castorino et al. suggested a target TIR of > 90% and a target TAR of < 5% for HIP/GDM, with TIR possibly built up in increments from a lower initial target (e.g., > 80%) as an individualised approach to avoid increasing diabetes distress. However, these suggested target thresholds are currently based on expert opinion [29]. Castorino et al. also recommended considering mean glucose and glycaemic variability in patients with HIP [29]. Achieving a mean glucose level of ≤ 7 mmol/L in early pregnancy has been associated with normal fetal growth in pregnant women with T1D [85], but a target threshold in HIP has not been defined. A new international consensus statement on diabetes technology for pregnancy was presented at the 2025 Annual Meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes [86]; however, this statement was developed before the results of the GRACE RCT were reported. Based on the DipGluMo RCT [48], the consensus group recommended SMBG for all women with GDM, and stated that CGM can be offered although evidence for improved outcomes is lacking [86]. This evidence gap has since been addressed by the GRACE RCT which showed increased TIR and reduced incidence of LGA births with CGM compared with SMBG in women with GDM [49, 50].

The US‐based observational Glucose Levels Across Maternity (GLAM) study by Durnwald et al. compared CGM metrics before OGTT in pregnant women with and without GDM [87]; similar analyses after OGTT would help to define CGM targets for women diagnosed with HIP. This evidence gap may be addressed by the ongoing MAGIC study, which is evaluating CGM metrics at intervals throughout pregnancy and postnatally in 500 women, assessing associations with HIP and pregnancy outcomes [88].

Durnwald et al. also compared CGM metrics in 760 women with and without adverse pregnancy outcomes and found higher mean glucose levels and greater percent TAR (> 140 mg/dL) in participants with LGA births and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy than in those without [89]. The women in this study were predominantly healthy (7.2% had GDM); similar analyses in large cohorts of women with HIP are needed. Liang et al. assessed 1302 women with HIP in China and found associations between several CGM metrics and adverse pregnancy outcomes. The authors identified the best thresholds of TAR (2.5%) and daily mean blood glucose (4.8 mmol/L) to distinguish women with and without any adverse pregnancy outcome, but the discriminatory ability of those thresholds remained low (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.56 and 0.57, respectively).

In summary, further large studies are needed to support the development of evidence‐based CGM targets in HIP, by defining (i) CGM metrics in healthy pregnancies compared with HIP and (ii) CGM metric thresholds associated with adverse outcomes in HIP.

The Importance of Timing

Glycaemic targets might be adapted over the day and by gestational age.

Timing Over the Day

The time window of measurement is an important consideration that warrants further research. Marquez Pardo et al. examined TIR and TAR during specific time windows (1 h before breakfast, lunch, and dinner; 2.5 h after breakfast, lunch and dinner, and overnight [midnight to 7 am]) in 77 women with HIP. In this study, the target range was defined as 70–95 mg/dL before meals, 70–140 mg/dL after meals, and 70–120 mg/dL overnight. Macrosomia and LGA births were significantly associated with TAR after lunch, but not with TAR at any other time, TAR overall, or TIR overall [62]. Requirement for pharmacological treatment was associated with TAR overall; effective treatment may therefore explain the lack of association between TAR overall and adverse neonatal outcomes. Of the time windows studied, TAR overnight showed the strongest association with requirement for pharmacological treatment, highlighting the importance of overnight monitoring [62].

One of the key advantages of CGM is its ability to capture glucose fluctuations throughout the entire 24‐h cycle, including overnight periods that are typically missed by SMBG. This continuous data stream allows for a more comprehensive understanding of glycaemic patterns, particularly during sleep when glucose levels may vary significantly.

Research has explored the relationship between nocturnal glucose levels and adverse pregnancy outcomes. In one study, mothers with HIP who gave birth to LGA infants exhibited significantly higher overnight glucose levels than those who did not [59]. Specifically, women in the LGA group experienced elevated glucose levels for more than 6 h during the night—representing over 25% of the day. This prolonged exposure to hyperglycaemia during sleep would likely go undetected with SMBG alone, underscoring a missed opportunity to optimise glucose control during a critical window of fetal development.

Additional evidence comes from a study involving 96 South Asian women between 8 and 20 weeks of gestation, which compared glycaemic patterns in those diagnosed with GDM (n = 38) and those with normoglycaemia (n = 58) based on OGTT [90]. CGM revealed significant differences between the two groups in mean 24‐h glucose levels, as well as in nocturnal and diurnal glucose patterns. These findings highlight the value of CGM in detecting subtle but clinically relevant variations in glucose control that may influence maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Timing in Relation to Gestational Age

In women who remain normoglycaemic during pregnancy, insulin secretion begins to increase in early pregnancy, followed by a rise in insulin resistance starting in mid‐pregnancy; physiological balance is maintained, with only a small decline in fasting glucose in early pregnancy [8]. Therefore, fasting plasma glycaemia usually decreases after ten weeks of gestation, whereas post prandial glycaemia increases after 24 weeks of gestation. The changes in CGM data over pregnancy are not well known at the current time; the MAGIC study will provide longitudinal data [88].

Patient Selection

The American Diabetes Association guidelines for the management of diabetes in pregnancy note that there are insufficient data to support the use of CGM in all patients with HIP, and that the decision to use CGM in such cases should be individualised based on treatment plan, circumstances, preferences, and needs [36]. Castorino et al. suggested that CGM use could be beneficial for patients with HIP and the following characteristics: use of insulin or other antihyperglycaemic medication; prior history of HIP; comorbidities that increase the risk of hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia; insufficient SMBG data or difficulties performing SMBG; shift work or erratic schedule (inconsistent meal timing); jobs with high levels of physical activity; fear of hypoglycaemia; need for additional support to make lifestyle behavioural changes [29]. CGM might also benefit patients who are struggling with the social stigma and inconvenience that can be associated with SMBG.

CGM for use in the diagnosis or prediction of HIP is another area requiring further research. The OGTT, the current standard for diagnosing HIP, provides only a limited snapshot of glucose levels over a few hours following a fixed glucose load. This approach may miss glycaemic fluctuations occurring outside the testing window. The ongoing MAGIC study is investigating the relationship of maternal glucose levels measured by CGM with subsequent HIP diagnosis and fetal growth [88]. The CGM for the Early Detection and Management of HIP (IMAGINE) RCT also aims to determine whether identification of maternal hyperglycaemia using CGM early in pregnancy, with early initiation of dietary and intensive glucose management, results in fewer adverse pregnancy outcomes than standard care [91]. Researchers have raised concerns about current guidelines, which recommend OGTT at 24–28 weeks, despite evidence that excess fetal growth can be detected as early as 20 weeks. The lack of a validated early diagnostic test may explain why many infants are born LGA despite no formal HIP diagnosis [88].

Long‐Term Metabolic Outcomes

Studies have begun to shift the focus of CGM in HIP from short‐term glycaemic control to its potential in predicting and managing long‐term metabolic outcomes.

A study published in 2025 used CGM and wearable devices to investigate postpartum glucose metabolism in women with a history of HIP [92]. The study revealed persistent differences in glucose variability and circadian glucose rhythms between women with prior HIP and those with normoglycaemic pregnancies—even months after delivery. Notably, these differences were observed even among women who were clinically considered normoglycaemic postpartum. The findings suggest that CGM‐derived metrics might serve as early indicators of long‐term cardiometabolic risk and support the development of targeted postpartum interventions.

In line with these findings, another recent study examined glucose intolerance one year postpartum in women with a history of HIP [93]. Researchers found that CGM metrics collected during pregnancy and at 6–8 weeks postpartum were predictive of long‐term glucose intolerance. These results suggest that CGM has potential not only as a monitoring tool during pregnancy but also as a means of identifying women at elevated risk for developing T2D later in life.

Together, these emerging directions underscore the possibility of an evolving role of CGM—not only as a tool for managing glucose during pregnancy but also as a predictive and preventive instrument across the reproductive and postpartum continuum.

Limitations and Strengths of Our Review

This targeted narrative review was not intended to provide a systematic or exhaustive assessment of all evidence relating to glucose monitoring strategies, and the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded. These limitations should be considered when drawing broader conclusions from this review. However, the review also has important strengths, including its coverage of a large amount of data (both positive and negative) across a wide range of topics and its inclusion of results from a new key RCT (GRACE).

Conclusions

Studies consistently show that CGM offers a more comprehensive picture of glucose fluctuations than traditional SMBG, while also improving quality of life and alleviating the burden associated with SMBG in women with HIP. As well as improving compliance with glucose monitoring, CGM has been associated with changes in medical treatment and healthcare behaviours compared with SMBG. Meta‐analyses suggest that this leads to improved glucose control in women with HIP/GDM and reduced GWG and neonatal birth weight [53, 54]. The limitations of CGM—including lag time and possible underestimation of glucose levels (within acceptable limits using the latest algorithms) compared with SMBG, the additional training time needed, the potential for anxiety related to the large volume of data, and device acquisition costs, availability, and functionality—might be outweighed by improvements in convenience, glucose control, and pregnancy outcomes compared with SMBG.

However, much of the existing research is limited by small sample sizes. Looking ahead, larger RCTs are needed to validate the clinical and psychological benefits of CGM compared with SMBG in HIP. Further studies are needed to define CGM targets (including identification of the appropriate metric[s], thresholds, and timing), identify the patient populations who are most likely to benefit from CGM use, and assess the potential role of CGM in addressing long‐term cardiometabolic risk in women with HIP.
