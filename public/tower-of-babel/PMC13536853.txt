Targeting RELA and STAT3 regulates TNFRSF10A-mediated apoptosis in a novel apoptosis-based prognostic model for clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yuyou Deng, Changzhen Hao, Xiaobing Yang, Chengfan Yu, Ming Xia, Tian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common subtype of renal malignancy and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Although advances in surgery, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have improved outcomes for patients, reliable biomarkers for predicting prognosis remain limited. Therefore, robust gene-based prognostic models are urgently needed to improve risk stratification and guide individualized treatment strategies.MethodsWe developed a novel prognostic model integrating apoptosis and immune - related genes (AIRGs) to predict overall survival (OS) in patients with ccRCC.ResultUsing Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) combined with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, we identified 7 key prognostic genes, namely, CCR4, TNFRSF10A, TEK, TGFA, CD14, IFITM1, and SEMA3G, that collectively demonstrated strong predictive performance in TCGA cohort with c-index = 0.711. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that apoptosis, immune regulation, and multiple oncogenic signaling pathways were significantly associated with the risk score, highlighting the critical role of the tumor microenvironment in ccRCC progression. Transcription factor binding analysis based on the JASPAR database suggested that RELA and STAT3 with scores of 0.829 and 0.951, respectively are potential upstream regulators within the prognostic network, particularly influencing TNFRSF10A expression. External validation using the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) dataset confirmed the robustness of the prognostic model with c-index = 0.612 Furthermore, in vitro experiments demonstrated that RELA and STAT3 regulate TNFRSF10A-mediated apoptotic signaling in ccRCC cells, providing mechanistic support for the bioinformatic findings.ConclusionThis study establishes a biologically informed and clinically relevant prognostic framework for ccRCC. Our findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the RELA/STAT3-TNFRSF10A axis and contribute to the advancement of precision medicine in ccRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04462-9.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, Prognostic model, Apoptosis, Death receptor, Transcription factor

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies globally, with an incidence in Asia reaching approximately 8 cases per 100,000 individuals. Clear cell RCC (ccRCC), the predominant subtype, accounts for roughly 80% of all RCC cases [1] and is associated with a concerning overall 5-year mortality rate of up to 63% [2]. Despite advancements in clinical management, the survival outcomes for patients with advanced-stage disease remain poor, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced prognostic tools and therapeutic stratification.

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as antibodies targeting PD-1/PD-L1, have improved overall survival in a subset of patients, primary or acquired resistance is common [3]. The development of robust, biologically informed prognostic models is crucial for optimizing precision medicine strategies in ccRCC [4]. Unfortunately, existing prognostic models often fail to adequately capture the tumor’s biological heterogeneity, particularly the dynamic interplay between tumor-intrinsic pathways and the immune microenvironment. Meanwhile, accumulating evidence indicates that immunotherapy efficacy is closely linked to host immune responses and the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [5]. While numerous immune-related genes (IRGs) have been implicated in ccRCC prognosis, the prognostic relationship between IRGs and apoptosis-related genes (ARGs) remains poorly understood [6]. Apoptosis, particularly the extrinsic pathway induced by TRAIL, serves as a fundamental mechanism regulating tumor progression and immune evasion, and elucidating its interactions with immune regulation may provide important insights for prognostic assessment and therapeutic strategies [7].

To address this knowledge gap, we applied integrated bioinformatic approaches, including Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [8] and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, to systematically evaluate the expression patterns of ARGs and IRGs in ccRCC. This enabled us to quantify pathway-level enrichment and identify key molecular determinants of patient survival. By focusing on the crosstalk between apoptotic signaling and immune regulation, we aimed to construct a novel prognostic model for predicting overall survival (OS) in ccRCC patients. Furthermore, we performed transcription factor (TF) regulatory network analysis to investigate the upstream mechanisms controlling apoptosis-related gene expression, an area often overlooked in previous model-building studies which lacked subsequent mechanistic exploration.

The primary objective of this study was to establish and validate a prognostic gene signature based on the interplay between apoptosis-related and immune-related genes in ccRCC. This integrative model not only aims to improve survival prediction but also to identify potential therapeutic targets, thereby contributing to personalized treatment strategies and a deeper understanding of ccRCC biology.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and normalization

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and corresponding clinical information for patients with ccRCC were obtained from publicly available databases. For the training cohort, transcriptomic profiles and clinical data from 538 ccRCC patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Fragment per kilobase million (TPM) values were applied to facilitate cross-sample comparison, to eliminate batch effects between the TCGA training cohort and the ICGC validation cohort, gene expression values were first log2 (TPM + 1). transformation. Subsequently, Z-score standardization was performed across all samples using the standard deviation derived solely from the TCGA training set. The ICGC validation data were then standardized using these same TCGA-derived parameters before applying the LASSO-COX coefficients to calculate the risk scores. After excluding cases with incomplete clinical information, 489 ccRCC patients were included in subsequent analyses. An independent validation cohort was obtained from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) database (https://docs.icgc-argo.org/), comprising RNA-seq data and corresponding survival information from 136 ccRCC patients. All expression data were log2-transformed and normalized prior to downstream analyses.

Apoptosis-based tumor clustering analysis

The ESTIMATE R package (version 3.6.3) was used to calculate immune scores, stromal scores, and tumor purity based on normalized gene expression matrices [7]. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was applied to quantify apoptosis activity in individual tumor samples. The apoptosis signature gene set included 87 genes (extracted from the c2.cp.kegg.v7.1.symbols.gmt) and contained both pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic/regulatory components; therefore, ssGSEA was used to evaluate overall apoptosis pathway activity rather than only pro-apoptotic gene activity. Apoptosis scores were standardized across samples, and ccRCC patients were subsequently stratified into high-apoptosis and low-apoptosis groups based on the median apoptosis score for downstream comparative analyses.

Differential expression and pathway enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high-apoptosis and low-apoptosis ccRCC groups were identified using the limma R package. Genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed, with multiple testing correction performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. 2,498 immune-related genes were downloaded from the ImmPort database (https://www.immport.niaid.nih.gov), and 87 apoptosis genes from MSigDB (c2.cp.kegg.v7.1.symbols.gmt). TF information was obtained from the Cistrome Cancer database (http://cistrome.org/CistromeCancer/). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was conducted using the GSVA R package to evaluate pathway-level activity differences between the high- and low-apoptosis groups [9], enabling the identification of key biological processes and signaling pathways associated with apoptotic heterogeneity in ccRCC tumors.

Evaluation of prognostic performance and transcription factor binding site analysis

To assess the prognostic value of the gene signature relative to conventional clinicopathological factors, including age, sex, tumor grade, pathological stage, histological subtype, and TNM classification, multivariate COX regression analyses were performed in the TCGA cohort. OS differences between high-risk and low-risk groups were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, c-index values in TCGA and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration plot and DCA curves were conducted to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the prognostic model at 1, 2 and 3 years [10]. The ICGC ccRCC (Europe) cohort was used as an independent external validation dataset to assess the robustness and generalizability of the model. To explore upstream regulatory mechanisms, transcription factor binding motifs (TFBMs) and potential transcription factor binding sites within the promoter regions of prognostic genes were identified using the JASPAR 2024 database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) with a relative score threshold cut-off value 80% and p-value = 0.05 [11].

Cell culture and pharmacological treatments

The human renal cell carcinoma cell lines 786-O and Caki-1 were obtained from Hycyte Bioscience (Hycyte, China; catalog numbers TCH-C107 and TCH-C147, respectively). 786-O cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, while Caki-1 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5 A modified medium (Meilun, China; CAS No. PWL006). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Hycyte, China). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and passaged when reaching approximately 70–80% confluence. For functional assays, cells were treated with the STAT3 inhibitor Stattic, NF-κB p65 inhibitor SC75741, NF-κB pathway activator phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), and recombinant TRAIL-R1 protein for different concentrations and cultivated and observed at different time point. All compounds were purchased from commercial suppliers and diluted to appropriate working concentrations in complete culture medium prior to treatment.

Cell counting and cell viability assays

Cell counting was performed using the Trypan blue exclusion method to distinguish viable from non-viable cells. Briefly, 50 µL of a single-cell suspension was mixed with an equal volume of 0.4% trypan blue solution (Solarbio Life Sciences, China; Cat. No. 72-57-1) and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. A 10 µL aliquot of the mixture was loaded onto a hemocytometer, and cells were counted under an inverted light microscope. Cells excluding trypan blue and appearing bright and translucent were considered viable, whereas blue-stained cells were classified as non-viable. Cells within the four large corner squares (each measuring 1 mm2) were counted, and the total cell concentration was calculated using the following formula: Total Cell Concentration (cells/mL) = (Total number of cells counted in 4 squares / 4) × 10⁴ Cell viability was assessed using the TransDetect Cell Counting Kit (CCK; TransGen Biotech, China; Cat. No. FC101-01). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and subjected to the indicated drug treatments, with wells containing culture medium alone serving as blank controls. At the designated time points, 10 µL of CCK reagent was added to each well, followed by gentle mixing and incubation in the dark for 1–4 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Cell viability was calculated according to the following formula: Cell Viability (%) = [450 nm OD value of targeted wells / 450 nm OD value of mock wells] × 100%. All assays were performed in triplicate, and each experiment was independently repeated at least three times.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cultured cells or tissue samples using RIPA lysis buffer (Labshark, China; Cat. No. 110502028) supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified, and equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer (Servicebio, China; Cat. No. G2075-10) and denatured at 100 °C for 5–10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using 8–12% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.45 μm; Beyotime, China; Cat. No. FFP39) at 100 V for 90–120 min at 4 °C. Successful protein transfer was confirmed by Ponceau S staining. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, China; Cat. No. ST2254) in TBST buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against DR4 (TNFRSF10A)(Cell Signaling Technology, Cat.No. 42533), Bcl-2(Wanleibio, China; Cat.No. WL01556), caspase-3/cleaved caspase-3, caspase-8, XIAP, and β-actin (all diluted 1:1,000 in blocking buffer) (Wanleibio, China; Cat.No. WL01556, WL02117, WL03561 and WL01372). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system (Servicebio, China, scg-w3000 plus). Protein band intensities of western blot assays were quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; NIH, USA), integrated density values were measured. Target protein levels were normalized to β-actin loading control. Data are representative of three independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the associations between AIRGs and TFs expression levels included in the prognostic model. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed independent-samples t tests after normality assumption test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). OS differences between patient groups were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests. DeLong’s test was performed to compare the differences between AUCs of ROC analysis results. For in vitro cell proliferation and viability assays, Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to determine normally distributed. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Statistical significance between treatment groups and corresponding controls was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test, implemented in GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Differential expression of apoptosis and immune related genes according to tumor apoptotic activity

Given that elevated tumor apoptotic activity was associated with improved OS in patients with kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), we next investigated transcriptomic differences between tumors with high and low apoptotic activity. Among 27 immune related genes, 23 were identified as differentially expressed between the two groups. Members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, including TNFRSF14 and TNFRSF25, were significantly downregulated, whereas tumor necrosis factor ligand superfamily genes, such as TNFSF4 and TNFSF15, were markedly upregulated in the high-apoptotic activity group (Fig. 3A). These findings suggest enhanced ligand-mediated immune signaling in tumors with increased apoptotic activity.

Analysis of apoptosis-related genes revealed that 66 of 87 genes were differentially expressed between the two groups. Genes involved in death receptor-mediated apoptotic signaling, including TNFSF10, TNFRSF10A, TNFRSF10B, and TNFRSF10D, were significantly upregulated in tumors with high apoptotic activity (P-values < 0.05). In contrast, TNFRSF10C did not reach statistical significance and was therefore not classified as differentially expressed. Notably, anti-apoptotic genes such as BCL2 and BCL2L1 (encoding BCL-XL) were also upregulated in the high-apoptotic group, whereas pro-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, including BAD and BID, were expressed at lower levels (P-values < 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

To characterize transcriptional regulation associated with tumor apoptotic activity, a total of 318 TFs were retrieved from the hallmark gene set in the MSigDB. Differential expression analysis identified 31 TFs that were significantly altered between tumors with high and low apoptotic activity. GO enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed TFs revealed significant enrichment in biological processes related to renal system development, including key regulators such as WWTR1, PRDM1, CTNNB1, and STAT1 (Fig. 4D). GSEA reveals apoptosis-associated signaling pathway alterations

GSEA was performed to identify biological processes and signaling pathways associated with tumor apoptotic activity. Within the BP category, the top three positively enriched pathways ranked by P value were dendritic cell antigen processing and presentation, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II protein complex assembly, and negative T cell selection (Fig. 5A). When ranked by normalized enrichment score (NES), the top three positively enriched pathways were antigen processing and presentation, antigen processing and presentation of exogenous peptide antigen, and antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen (Fig. 5B). By integrating both P value and NES, antigen processing and presentation of exogenous peptide antigen, antigen processing and presentation of exogenous antigen, and T cell co-stimulation were identified as the most consistently activated pathways in high-apoptotic RCC samples. In contrast, proton transmembrane transport, oxidative phosphorylation, and aerobic respiration were the most significantly suppressed pathways (Fig. 5C), suggesting metabolic reprogramming associated with increased apoptotic activity. Leading-edge gene analysis further revealed that MEF2A, MEF2C, and HDAC6 were enriched among the top five activated biological processes in high-apoptotic RCC samples when ranked by NES (Fig. 5D), indicating their potential involvement in apoptosis-associated immune regulation.

Apoptosis activated multiple classical molecular pathways in RCC

KEGG pathway analysis was conducted to evaluate pathway activation and suppression in high-apoptotic RCC samples compared with low-apoptotic samples. Multiple classical oncogenic and immune-related signaling pathways were significantly activated in the high-apoptotic RCC group (Fig. 6A). In the EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance pathway (normalized enrichment score, NES = 2.11, p-value = 0.005), hallmark genes including EGFR, VEGFR, PDGFR, JAK, STAT, and PI3K were overexpressed in high-apoptotic samples (Fig. 6B), indicating enhanced growth factor receptor and downstream signaling activity. Within both canonical and non-canonical NF-κB pathways (NES = 1.88, p-value = 0.0056), key regulatory genes such as p50 and cIAP1/2 were upregulated, whereas PIDD and GADD45B were downregulated in high-apoptotic samples (Fig. 6C), reflecting complex modulation of inflammatory and apoptotic signaling. Furthermore, activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway was observed, with increased expression of JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, STAT, MCL1, and c-Myc in high-apoptotic RCC samples. Consistently, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway was also activated (NES = 2.81, p-value = 0.017), as key components such as PI3K and AKT were overexpressed (Fig. 6D). In the RCC pathway, activation of the HIF-1 signaling pathway was evident, characterized by increased expression of HIF-1α in high-apoptotic samples(NES = 2.018, p-value = 0.0018). This activation was accompanied by upregulation of downstream VEGF and TGF-β signaling pathways, suggesting enhanced angiogenic and proliferative signaling in response to a hypoxic tumor microenvironment (Fig. 6E).

Construction of a novel apoptosis-related prognostic model using the LASSO-COX method

To identify prognostically relevant AIRGs and construct a prognostic model, a total of 1,784 DEGs were identified between the high- and low-apoptotic activity groups, we retrieved 267 differential expressed AIRGs when intersect 87 ARGs, 2498 IRGs and 1784 DEGs. Univariate COX regression analysis was first performed, identifying 103 survival genes among 267 differentially expressed AIRGs. Subsequently, the LASSO method was applied to reduce dimensionality and select the most informative prognostic AIRGs. Using an optimal penalty parameter log(λ) value = − 3 (Fig. 7A), the LASSO-COX analysis identified 7 candidate prognostic AIRGs: CCR4, TNFRSF10A, TEK, TGFA, CD14, IFITM1, and SEMA3G (Fig. 7B). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a significant difference in OS between the high-risk and low-risk groups stratified by the LASSO-COX based risk score. The 5-year survival rate was 42.0 ± 3.81% (95% CI: 0.351–0.502) in the high-risk group and 83.6 ± 2.82% (95% CI: 0.781–0.894) in the low-risk group. The median survival time of the high-risk group was 4.29 years, while the 10-year survival rate in the low-risk group reached 73.1% (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 7C). Univariate COX analysis identified that TEK, SMEA3G, TGFA, TNFRSF10A, and CCR4 as protective genes for ccRCC prognosis in TCGA training dataset (Fig. 7D). Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed that the AIRGs based risk score was an independent prognostic factor for OS in KIRC patients, with a HR of 2.788 (95% CI: 1.876–4.144) for the high-risk group (Fig. 7E).

Classical TFs potentially regulate TNFRSF10A expression in pan-cancer analysis

TF prediction analysis based on the JASPAR database identified STAT3 and RELA, two key regulators of the NF-κB and JAK-STAT signaling pathways, as potential upstream TFs of TNFRSF10A, owing to the presence of specific binding motifs within the TNFRSF10A gene promoter region (Fig. 8A). Pan-cancer correlation analysis using Pearson’s method across TCGA datasets demonstrated that STAT3 and RELA expression levels were positively correlated with TNFRSF10A expression in KIRC and most other tumor types, with correlation coefficients greater than 0.5 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 8B). These results suggest a conserved regulatory relationship between RELA, STAT3 and TNFRSF10A across multiple cancers. Consistent findings were observed in the GTEx pan-tissue and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) validation dataset, where STAT3 and RELA expression levels were positively correlated with TNFRSF10A expression in kidney tissue and multiple cancer cell lines. (Pearson r > 0 and P < 0.01; Fig. 8C and 8D). Furthermore, analysis of the GETx and CCLE dataset revealed that, in normal kidney tissues and kidney-derived cancer cell lines, STAT3 and RELA expression remained positively correlated with TNFRSF10A expression, with Pearson r value = 0.871 and 0.853 in GTEx kidney tissues, with Pearson r value = 0.395 and 0.407 in CCLE renal cancer cell lines, respectively (both P < 0.05; Fig. 8E and 8F).

RELA and STAT3 regulate TRAIL-mediated apoptosis in vitro

In vitro functional experiments were conducted using the 786-O and Caki-1 human ccRCC cell lines to investigate the regulatory roles of RELA and STAT3 in TRAIL-mediated apoptosis. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that treatment with low doses of the RELA inhibitor SC75741, the STAT3 inhibitor Stattic, or the NF-κB pathway activator PMA alone did not exert significant cytotoxic effects on either 786-O or Caki-1 cells (Fig. 9A). Specifically, treatment with 0.25 µM Stattic resulted in cell viabilities of 96.39 ± 3.98% in 786-O cells and 86.51 ± 4.72% in Caki-1 cells. Cell proliferation assays further confirmed that low-dose Stattic (0.25 µM) or SC75741 (1 µM) did not significantly inhibit cell growth, whereas higher concentrations of these inhibitors led to marked reductions in cell viability and proliferation (Fig. 9B). Treatment with TRAIL alone (8 ng/mL) significantly reduced cell viability in 786-O cells, with viability decreasing to 62.05 ± 2.23%. Pretreatment with low dose of SC75741 (1 µM) or Stattic (0.25 µM) attenuated TRAIL-induced cell death, whereas pretreatment with low-dose PMA (2 µM) potentiated TRAIL-mediated cytotoxicity. Western blot analysis revealed that treatment with low concentrations of SC75741 (1 µM) or Stattic (0.25 µM) downregulated the protein expression of the death receptor DR4, accompanied by reduced activation of caspase-8 and caspase-3, thereby attenuating the apoptotic response in 786-O cells. In contrast, treatment with low-dose PMA increased DR4 protein expression and enhanced activation of the TRAIL-mediated apoptotic signaling cascade. Furthermore, inhibition of RELA or STAT3 reduced the expression levels of anti-apoptotic proteins BCL-2, Bcl-xL, and XIAP, whereas activation of the NF-κB pathway by PMA pretreatment (2 µM) led to upregulation of these anti-apoptosis proteins (Fig. 9C). Western blot analysis also revealed that siRNA-mediated knockdown of RELA or STAT3 gene significantly decreased DR4 protein expression, the supression effect of knockdown STAT3 gene on DR4 protein expression was less effective compared to knockdown of RELA gene. Furthermore, combined knockdown of both RELA and STAT3 using siRNAs resulted in a further reduction of DR4 protein levels, indicating that inhibition of RELA or STAT3 negatively regulates DR4 protein expression (Fig. 9D).

Prediction performance and external validation of the AIRG prognostic model

Time-dependent ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of the AIRGs-based prognostic model. In the TCGA RCC cohort, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for the risk score reached 0.801, 0.730, and 0.767 at 1-, 2- and 3-year survival (1 vs. 2 year P-value = 0.031, 1 vs. 3 year P-value = 0.464, 2 vs. 3 year P-value = 0.026). (Fig. 10A). To facilitate individualized clinical prediction, a nomogram integrating age, sex, pathological stage, T stage, N stage, and the AIRGs-based risk score was constructed to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in TCGA RCC (Fig. 10B). The calibration plot and DCA curves of 1 and 3-year OS illustrated robust predictive ability of our AIRGs model (Fig. 10C and D). Furthermore, the ICGC cohort was used as an independent external validation dataset to assess the robustness of the prognostic model. In the ICGC RCC cohort, the AUC of risk score were 0.699, 0.703, and 0.642 of 1-,2-, and 3-year survival (Fig. 10E), the c-index was 0.611 (0.711 in TCGA). DCA curves exhibited higher net benefits when threshold > 5% and > 10% in 1 year survival and 3 year survival, respectively (Fig. 10F), calibration plots also exhibited good prediction probabilities in 1 and 3-year survival in ICGC testing data (Fig. 10G). A nomogram was made to predict 1 to 3 year survival of ICGC patients based on risk scores calculated by our AIRGs prognostic model (Fig. 10H). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that the 3-year survival rate was 72.46%±5.55% (61.59% − 83.33%) in the high-risk group and 85.02%±4.37% (76.46% − 93.58%) in the low-risk group but with P value = 0.0084 (Figure S1).

Discussion

In this study, we established a significant association between tumor apoptosis activity and prognostic outcomes in patients with RCC. We identified a seven-gene apoptosis-related signature (CCR4, TNFRSF10A, TEK, TGFA, CD14, IFITM1, and SEMA3G) that demonstrated strong and independent prognostic value for overall survival. Our novel 7-gene signature reflects key biological processes within the tumor microenvironment and underscores the critical role of apoptosis in ccRCC progression. The inclusion of immune-related genes further underscores the intricate interplay between apoptotic signaling and immune regulation in ccRCC, a malignancy characterized by its immunogenicity. While the immune microenvironment’s link to clinical outcomes in ccRCC is well-documented [12, 13], our AIRGs model offers a novel perspective by incorporating DR4-mediated extrinsic apoptosis activity.

The application of GSEA and GSVA revealed significant activation of apoptosis- and immune response-related pathways in ccRCC samples, including T-cell co-stimulation, antigen processing and presentation, T-cell migration, as well as canonical oncogenic and inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB and JAK-STAT. The pronounced enrichment of these gene sets underscores their potential utility as biomarkers for predicting disease progression and therapeutic response. Prior evidence indicates that immune checkpoint genes and their interactions with apoptotic signaling pathways critically influence tumor behavior and patient outcomes [14]. Prognostic models integrating immune-related genes with apoptosis-associated genes may thus offer superior predictive power for OS in ccRCC patients. Our findings emphasize the biological and clinical significance of the crosstalk between immune responses and apoptosis, suggesting that targeting pro-apoptotic pathways in tumor cells could enhance therapeutic efficacy and improve survival outcomes.

Importantly, the simultaneous upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes and anti-apoptotic genes (e.g., BCL2 and BCL2L1) in the high-apoptotic activity group should not be interpreted as an unidirectional increase in ongoing apoptotic cell death. The apoptosis gene set used for ssGSEA was designed to capture the overall apoptosis pathway and therefore included pro-apoptotic effectors, death-receptor pathway genes, and anti-apoptotic or regulatory components. Because ssGSEA summarizes coordinated expression of a predefined gene set in each sample rather than directly measuring executioner caspase activity or the fraction of apoptotic cells [15], our high-apoptosis score is better understood as an apoptosis-related transcriptional program or apoptotic potential. Biologically, this pattern is coherent with the balance model of cell death, in which death-receptor/caspase signaling and BCL-2-family survival buffering jointly determine whether a cell crosses the apoptotic threshold [16, 17]. Upregulation of BCL2 and BCL2L1 may therefore represent a compensatory survival brake or mitochondrial priming state in tumors exposed to death-ligand, inflammatory, or immune stress [18]. This interpretation is also consistent with the enrichment of immune checkpoint, parainflammation, antigen presentation, NF-kappaB, and JAK-STAT signatures in the high-score group. Nevertheless, this paradox introduces uncertainty into the interpretation of our stratification: the ssGSEA-based apoptosis score should be considered an apoptosis-associated stress response signature, rather than definitive evidence of active apoptotic cell death in tumor tissue.

Furthermore, our analysis of transcriptional regulation provided crucial insights into the regulatory functions of DR4 in ccRCC. Specifically, we identified RELA and STAT3, key transcription factors in the NF-κB and JAK-STAT signaling pathways, respectively—as potential upstream regulators of TNFRSF10A. This finding underscores the complexity of transcriptional control in apoptosis and implies that modulating RELA- or STAT3-driven signaling could represent a viable strategy to sensitize ccRCC cells to apoptosis and improve treatment outcomes. Beyond TNFRSF10A, other genes in our prognostic model have established roles in ccRCC pathogenesis. For instance, TEK, a critical angiogenesis regulator frequently upregulated in ccRCC, is associated with enhanced tumor vascularization and aggressive behavior, highlighting its potential as a target for anti-angiogenic therapy [19]. TGFA promotes tumor growth by enhancing proliferation and survival, with its aberrant expression linked to poor prognosis in cancers such as osteosarcoma via miR-376c mediation [20]. CD14, a monocyte/macrophage marker upregulated in ccRCC, may reflect tumor-associated inflammation and has been correlated with disease progression and reduced survival, supporting its role as a prognostic biomarker [21]. IFITM1 has been reported to promote migration and invasion in ccRCC, indicating involvement in metastasis [22, 23]. SEMA3G, a neural guidance molecule, has been implicated in regulating proliferation and metastasis in ccRCC, potentially through effects on intercellular signaling and stromal interactions [24, 25]. Together, these molecular insights provide a comprehensive framework linking apoptosis, immune regulation, and transcriptional control in ccRCC.

For model validation, our AIRGs model demonstrated robust predictive performance in the TCGA cohort, with AUC values of 0.801, 0.730, and 0,766 in 1-,2-, and 3-year survival, with c-index = 0.711, and this was corroborated in an independent ICGC validation cohort (AUC = 0.699, 0.703, and 0.642 of 1-,2-, and 3-year survival, c-index = 0.610). This external validation strengthens the reliability and generalizability of our model and underscores its potential clinical utility for risk stratification and individualized treatment planning using the constructed nomogram (Fig. 10). The accurate prediction of outcomes based on integrated AIRGs signatures represents a meaningful advance in ccRCC prognostic research.

Currently, the most widely used clinical models for localized ccRCC include the Leibovich score and the GRANT (Grade, Age, Nodes, and Tumor) score. The Leibovich score, which integrates tumor stage, nodal status, tumor size, nuclear grade, and histologic necrosis, has demonstrated a C-index of approximately 0.657 for predicting overall survival (OS). The GRANT score offers a simplified alternative with fewer missing data requirements but exhibits limited discriminative ability for RFS (C-index 0.643) [26]. Our AIRGs-based molecular model achieves an c-index of 0.711 for OS prediction in the TCGA cohort, which compares favorably with these clinical models. Besides, our AIRGs signature can be assessed from biopsy specimens, enabling potentially guiding neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy decisions.

Regarding the in vitro experiments, we found that pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 and RELA with low-dose small-molecule inhibitors (Stattic and SC75741, respectively) attenuated TRAIL-induced apoptosis in 786-O cells by downregulating key apoptotic proteins. Under specific conditions, NF-κB can directly upregulate the death receptor DR4 (TNFRSF10A) via promoter binding, enhancing sensitivity to extrinsic apoptosis [27, 28]. In our study, RELA activation enhanced TRAIL-mediated apoptosis, likely through increased DR4 expression.

While STAT3 binding sites are not consistently identified in the core DR4 promoter, STAT3 is a key transcriptional activator of survival genes (e.g., MCL1, BCL2, c-MYC) [29]. It establishes an anti-apoptotic program that buffers caspase activation from death receptor signaling by inducing anti-apoptotic BCL-2 members, inhibiting mitochondrial apoptosis amplification. Although not a direct repressor, STAT3 may indirectly modulate DR4 expression in specific contexts via recruitment of chromatin modifiers. This aligns with our findings that STAT3 inhibition attenuated the apoptotic response. Several study limitations warrant consideration. First, the generalizability of our findings may be constrained by the sequencing depth and sample diversity of the TCGA and ICGC datasets. Second, the lack of large-scale prospective clinical validation limits the immediate translational applicability of our prognostic model. Third, potential batch effects from different sequencing platforms and sample processing methods may introduce variability into gene expression analyses, and the LASSO-COX method carries a risk of overfitting. Additionally, our in vitro experiments relied on pharmacological inhibitors/activators, which may introduce bias in the interpretation of the results.

Future studies should incorporate gene disruption approaches and flow cytometry to assess apoptotic phenomena in 786-O cells. Priority should also be given to functionally validating these candidate genes and their interactions, focusing on their roles in tumor biology, immune evasion, and immunotherapy resistance, as well as potential synergistic mechanisms promoting apoptosis during immunotherapy. These factors necessitate cautious interpretation and underscore the need for validation in independent and more heterogeneous cohorts. From a clinical implementation perspective, several considerations merit discussion. First, our model demonstrated strong predictive performance in the TCGA cohort, prospective clinical validation in multi-center trials is essential to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility. Second, a key advantage of our AIRGs model lies in its biological interpretability: the model not only predicts prognosis but also provides mechanistic insights into the apoptotic and immune regulatory pathways driving ccRCC progression. This dual functionality positions our model as a potentially valuable tool for precision medicine, where risk stratification can be coupled with therapeutic target identification. Furthermore, the association between our risk score and immune checkpoint pathway genes expression suggests potential utility in predicting immunotherapy response, which warrants investigation in future studies. We envision that the integration of this molecular signature with existing clinicopathological nomograms could yield a composite model contributing to more informed treatment decision-making in ccRCC management.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed an AIRGs-based prognostic model that provides a novel and biologically informed framework for predicting survival in ccRCC patients. The identification of key regulatory genes underscores the intricate interplay between apoptotic signaling and immune responses within the tumor microenvironment, offering mechanistic insights with therapeutic relevance. Future research should address the noted limitations by incorporating larger, multi-center cohorts and validating these findings through in vivo functional experiments. Ultimately, this model holds promise for advancing personalized treatment strategies and improving clinical outcomes in ccRCC through more precise, mechanism-driven interventions.
