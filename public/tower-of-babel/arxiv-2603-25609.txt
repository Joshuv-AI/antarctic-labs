Extending theorems of Boros and Menzer

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

In the recent papers
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
, Boros and Menzer considered the alternative function equation

f(x)f(y)=0
for generalized polynomials
f
, and the conditional function inequality
f(x)f(y)\geq 0
for generalized monomials of even degree
m\geq 2
, under measure or Baire category constraints on
f
.
In fact, their theorems for alternative equations are formulated in a general form where
f\colon G\to\mathbb{C}
and
G
is a locally compact Abelian group generated by a neighbourhood of zero, with a
\sigma
-finite Haar measure, and in the category case,
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}
. For the conditional inequality, Boros and Menzer assumed simply that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Our main purpose is to extend results of
[
4
]
to the setting where one assumes mixed measure-category constraints on
f
.

Let us recall from
[
4
]
some basic facts concerning generalized monomials and polynomials. See also
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
.
Let
G
be an Abelian topological group with the operation
+
. Given
f\colon G\to\mathbb{C}
and
h,x\in G
, we define
\Delta_{h}f(x):=f(x+h)-f(x)
. If
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
F\colon G^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero symmetric
m
-additive function (that is, a function addtitive in each variable), consider its diagonalization

f(x):=F(\overbrace{x,x,\dots,x}^{m})\quad(x\in G).

Then
f
is called a
generalized monomial
of degree
m
. Additionally, we may call constant functions as generalized monomials of degree zero. Note that
f(rx)=r^{m}f(x)
for all
r\in\mathbb{Q}
and
x\in G
when
f
is of positive degree
m
.
If
F
and
f
are as above, we have

\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\dots\Delta_{h_{m}}f(x)=m!\,F(h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{m})

for all
x,h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{m}\in G
.
Moreover, it follows that

\Delta_{h_{1}}\Delta_{h_{2}}\dots\Delta_{h_{n}}f(x)=0

for all
x,h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{n}\in G
if
n
is an integer such that
n>m
.
Assuming that
G
is uniquely divisible by
(m+1)!
, a
generalized monomial

f\colon G\to\mathbb{C}
of degree
m
can be characterized as a solution of
the functional equation

(1)

\Delta_{y}^{m}f(x)=m!\,f(y)\quad(x,y\in G)

where
\Delta_{y}^{m}
denotes the
m
-th iterate of the operation
\Delta_{y}
.

A finite sum of generalized monomials is called a
generalized polynomial
. More exactly,
a generalized polynomial of degree at most
p\in\mathbb{N}
is a function
f\colon G\to\mathbb{C}
of the
form

(2)

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}f_{k}(x)\quad(x\in G)

where functions
f_{k}
(for
k=1,\dots,p
) are associated with symmetric
k
-additive functions
F_{k}
as follows

f_{k}(x):=F_{k}(\overbrace{x,x,\dots,x}^{k})\quad(x\in G)

and
f_{0}(x):=F_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
for all
x\in G
.
In fact, generalized polynomials of degree at most
p
can be characterized as solutions of the following functional equation, provided that
G
is uniquely divisible by
(p+1)!
:

(3)

\Delta^{p+1}_{y}f(x)=0\quad(x,y\in G).

Through the paper, we will assume that
G=\mathbb{R}
(or
G=\mathbb{R}^{N}
). It is known that, by the Axiom of Choice, there exist generalized discontinuous monomials (polynomials)
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
. Note that a more restrictive notion of a generalized polynomial
f\colon G\to\mathbb{C}
was considered in the recent paper
[
6
]
.

Let us state two pairs of results from
[
4
]
which have a form of an implication. They will be extended in this paper to a new situation in the sense that the conclusions will be the same, but our new assumptions will present completely different cases that cannot be derived from the old ones.
However, an idea of proofs remains the same.

The first theorem is quoted for the particular case where the considered group is
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(See
[
4
, Theorems 3.2 and 3.6]
)
Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a generalized polynomial such that
f(x)f(y)=0
for all
(x,y)\in D
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume that one of the conditions is true:

(I)

D
is a Lebesgue measurable set of positive measure,

(II)

D
is a Baire measurable nonmeager set.

Then
f(x)=0
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Observe that the set
D
in (I) and (II) can be assumed to be Borel. Indeed, it follows from the fact that a Lebesgue measurable set of positive measure clearly contains a closed set of positive measure. In the Baire category case, we note that a Baire measurable set is the union of a meager set and a
G_{\delta}
set (cf.
[
11
, Theorem 4.4]
). So, if
f
is a generalized polynomial and the function
(x,y)\mapsto f(x)f(y)
vanishes on a Borel set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
that is large in the measure or the Baire category sense, then
f
vanishes everywhere.

The next theorem is formulated in a similar manner.

Theorem 1.2
.

(See
[
4
, Theorems 4.3 and 4.6]
)
Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a generalized monomial of even degree
m\geq 2
fulfilling
f(x)f(y)\geq 0
for all

(x,y)\in D
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume that one of the conditions is true:

(I)

D
is a Lebesgue measurable set of positive measure,

(II)

D
is a Baire measurable nonmeager set.

Then
f(x)f(y)\geq 0
for each
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

As before, the set
D
in (I) and (II) can be assumed to be Borel. So, if
f
is a generalized monomial of even degree
m\geq 0
and the function
\widetilde{f}(x,y):=f(x)f(y)
is non-negative on a Borel set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
that is large in the measure or the Baire category sense, then
\widetilde{f}
is non-negative everywhere.

We are going to obtain the analogues of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
where a Borel set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is large in the mixed measure-category sense.

2.
The Fubini products of
\sigma
-ideals

The mixed measure-category largeness that we use in this paper is connected with the Mendez

\sigma
-ideals
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
and

\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
on the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. See
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
1
]
. Here
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
stand for the
\sigma
-ideals of meager sets and Lebesgue null sets in
\mathbb{R}
.

First, let us recall the notion of the Fubini product of two
\sigma
-ideals. Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be given
\sigma
-ideals of subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. For
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we let
B_{x}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}\colon(x,y)\in B\}
.
Then we put

\Phi_{\mathcal{J}}(B):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon B_{x}\notin\mathcal{J}\}.

Define
\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{J}
as the family of all sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that there exists a Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\Phi_{\mathcal{J}}(B)\in\mathcal{I}
. It is easy to check that
\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{J}
is a
\sigma
-ideal. By the Fubini theorem, the Kuratowski-Ulam theorem and their converses (see
[
11
]
) it follows that
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
are, respectively, the
\sigma
-ideals of Lebesgue null sets and meager sets in the plane. If we do not use a Borel cover
B
in the definition of
\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{J}
, some pathological sets can appear in
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
(cf.
[
11
, Theorems 14.4, 15.5]
).

Consider the well-known decomposition
\mathbb{R}=A\cup B
of the line into disjoint Borel sets
A\in\mathcal{M}
and
B\in\mathcal{N}
(see
[
11
]
). Mendez in
[
9
]
observed that there are the following two useful decompositions of the plane

•

(A\times\mathbb{R})\cup(B\times\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}^{2}\;\mbox{ with }\;A\times\mathbb{R}\in\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}\;\mbox{ and }\;B\times\mathbb{R}\in\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M};

•

C\cup H=\mathbb{R}^{2}
where

C:=(A\times B)\cup(B\times A),\;H:=(A\times A)\cup(B\times B)
,

and

C\in(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})\cap(\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}),\;H\in(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N})\cap(\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}).

From the former decomposition it follows that there is no inclusion between
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}

and
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
.
From the latter decomposition it follows that there is no inclusion between any pair of
\sigma
-ideals:

•

\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
;

•

\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
;

•

\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
;

•

\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
.

In our results, large sets are Borel sets that do not belong to
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
or
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
.
The lack of the respective inclusions between
\sigma
-ideals shows that this sort of largeness cannot be deduced from the largeness in the sense of measure or the Baire category in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

3.
A theorem on the alernative equation

In this section we consider an alternative equation

f(x)f(y)=0,

assumimng that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is a generalized polynomial.

The following facts on zeros of generalized polynomials are stated in
[
4
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.4]
in a more general form.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a generalized polynomial.

(i)

If
f
vanishes on a measurable set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of positive measure, then
f
vanishes everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

If
f
vanishes on a Baire nonmeager set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, then
f
vanishes everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
.

Statement (i) of the above theorem was proved by Székelyhidi
[
12
, Theorem 3.3, p. 33]
in a general version for a Haar measure on a locally compact Abelian group. Statement (ii) was proved in
[
4
]
for the space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Below, for the reader’s convenience, we reconstruct a full simple proof of (i) which is analogous to the argument for (ii) presented in
[
4
]
.

Let
\lambda
stand for Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. The following fact and corollary are known (in
[
12
]
this is referred to a result of Kurepa
[
8
]
). It is connected with the classical theorem of Steinhaus.

Fact 3.2
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable set of positive measure, and let
n
be a positive integer.
Then

\lim_{h\to 0}\lambda\left(A\cap\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}(A+kh)\right)=\lambda(A).

Proof.

The assertion follows from the equivalent condition

\lim_{h\to 0}\lambda\left(A\setminus\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}(A+kh)\right)=0

which will be proved below.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Pick a bounded open set
U\supseteq A
and a closed nonempty set
F\subseteq A
such that

•

there exists
\delta>0
such that
F+nh\subseteq U
for all
h\in(-\delta,\delta)
;

•

\lambda(U\setminus F)<\varepsilon/n
.

Now, for each
h\in(-\delta,\delta)
we have

\displaystyle\lambda\left(A\setminus\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}(A+kh)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda\left(A\setminus(A+kh)\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda\left(U\setminus(F+kh)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\lambda(U)-\lambda(F+kh)\right)<n(\varepsilon/n)=\varepsilon,

as desired.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded measurable set of positive measure, and let
n
be a positive integer.
Then there is a neighbourhood
V
of zero such that for every
h\in V
there is
x\in A
such that

x+kh\in A\quad\text{for }\quad k=1,2,\dots,n.

Proof.

Since
\lambda(A)>0
is finite, we can use Fact
3.2
and pick sufficiently small neighbourhood

V=(-\delta,\delta)
of zero such that
\lambda\left(A\cap\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}(A+kh)\right)>0
for all
h\in V
.
Clearly, we may replace
h
by
-h
and pick
x\in A\cap\bigcap_{k=1}^{n}(A-kh)
which yields the assertion.
∎

Note that a similar fact and a corollary are true in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In fact,
Corollary
3.3
is a measure counterpart of
[
4
, Lemma 3.3]
which gave in
[
4
]
condition (ii) of Theorem
3.1
. Below, we will use a similar argument to obtain condition (i) of this theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
(i)
.
Suppose that
f
is not identically zero. Consider its representation (
2
) with
f_{p}
that is not identically zero. By our assumption,
f
vanishes on a measurable set
E
of positive measure. We may assume that
E
is bounded. For
A:=E
and
n:=p
pick a neighbourhood
V
of zero as in Corollary
3.3
.
Then for any
h\in V
we can pick the respective point
x
. Using properties (
1
), (
3
) and
[
7
, Corollary 15.1.2]
, we have

f_{p}(h)=\frac{\Delta_{h}^{p}f_{p}(x)}{p\,!}=\frac{\Delta_{h}^{p}f(x)}{p\,!}=\frac{1}{p\,!}\sum_{k=0}^{p}{p\choose k}(-1)^{p-k}f(x+kh)=0,

where the last equality follows from the property of
V
.
Hence
f_{p}
vanishes on
V
, and by the equality
f_{p}(ru)=r^{p}f_{p}(u)
for all
r\in\mathbb{Q}
and
u\in V
, we infer that
f_{p}
vanishes everywhere. This yields a contradiction.
\Box

Remark 3.4
.

Note that Theorem
1.1
is a strengthening of Theorem
3.1
. Indeed, if
f
vanishes on a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of positive measure, then
f(x)f(y)=0
for all
(x,y)\in E\times\mathbb{R}
and
E\times\mathbb{R}
is
of positive plane measure. So,
f
vanishes everywhere by Theorem
1.1
. Similarly, we proceed in the case of the Baire category.

Now, we are ready to extend the results on the alternate equation presented in Theorem
1.1
to the new cases
where the largeness of
D
means that it is a Borel set which does not belong to
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
or it does not belong to
\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
. Our proof is based on the same idea which was used in
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be a generalized polynomial such that
f(x)f(y)=0
for all
(x,y)\in D
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a Borel set such that either
D\notin\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
or
D\notin\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
. Then
f(x)=0
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Assume that
D\notin\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
. The proof for
D\notin\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
is analogous.
Let
P:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon f(x)=0\}
. We will show that there exists a Borel set
A\subseteq P
such that either
A\notin\mathcal{N}
or
A\notin\mathcal{M}
. Then by Theorem
3.1
we obtain the assertion.

By the assumption, we clearly have

(4)

D\subseteq(P\times\mathbb{R})\cup(\mathbb{R}\times P).

Since
D\notin\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
, we obtain

B:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon D_{x}\notin\mathcal{N}\}\notin\mathcal{M}.

We know that
B
is Borel; see
[
5
, 22.22]
. (If we replace
\mathcal{M}
by
\mathcal{N}
in the definition of
B
, then
B
is again Borel; see
[
5
, 22.25]
.)

Consider two cases.

If
D_{x}\subseteq P
for some
x\in B
, then
A:=D_{x}\notin\mathcal{N}
is the desired set.

If
D_{x}\setminus P\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in B
, fix any
x\in B
. Then for each
y\in D_{x}\setminus P
we have
(x,y)\in D\setminus(\mathbb{R}\times P)
, so from (
4
) it follows that
(x,y)\in P\times\mathbb{R}
. Hence
x\in B
implies
x\in P
. So, we have proved that
B\subseteq P
. Since
B\notin\mathcal{M}
,
A:=B
is the desired set.
∎

Notice that the respective versions of Mendez ideals can be considered in
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
with the analogous properties. So, Theorem
3.5
can be generalized to the case when
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
.

4.
A theorem on the conditional inequality

Here we are going to extend the results of Boros and Menzer from Theorem
1.2
concerning the conditional inequality

f(x)f(y)\geq 0

for a generalized monomial
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.1
.

(See
[
3
, Lemma 3.5]
,
[
4
, Lemma 4.4]
.)
Let
\mathcal{I}:=\mathcal{N}
or
\mathcal{I}:=\mathcal{M}
. Assume that
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}
fulfills the conditions:

(i)

for all
r\in\mathbb{Q}
and
x\in P
we have
rx\in P
;

(ii)

there is a Borel set
A\subseteq P
with
A\notin\mathcal{I}
.

Then
\mathbb{R}\setminus P\in\mathcal{I}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

(See
[
3
, Lemma 4.1]
,
[
4
, Lemma 4.5]
.)
Let
\mathcal{I}:=\mathcal{N}
or
\mathcal{I}:=\mathcal{M}
. Assume that
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a generalized monomial of degree
m\geq 1

and
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a closed set such that
\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon f(x)\notin H\}\in\mathcal{I}
. Then
f(x)\in H
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Below, we will mimic ideas of the proof in
[
4
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a generalized monomial of an even degree
m
, fulfilling
f(x)f(y)\geq 0

for all
(x,y)\in D
where
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a Borel set such that either

D\notin\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
or
D\notin\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
. Then
f(x)f(y)\geq 0
holds for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

If
m=0
, the assertion is clear. So, let
m>0
. Define

P_{f}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon f(x)\geq 0\},\quad N_{f}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon f(x)\leq 0\}.

Since
f(rx)=r^{m}f(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\in\mathbb{Q}
, the sets
P_{f}
and
N_{f}
satisfy condition (i) in Lemma
4.1
. Also, the set
P_{f}\cap N_{f}=\{x\colon f(x)=0\}
satisfies it. We will check condition (ii) of this lemma for the above sets in the respective situations.

Assume that
D\notin\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
. The proof for
D\notin\mathcal{N}\otimes\mathcal{M}
is analogous. By the assumption we have

D\subseteq(P_{f}\times P_{f})\cup(N_{f}\times N_{f}).

Since
D\notin\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
, we obtain

B:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon D_{x}\notin\mathcal{N}\}\notin\mathcal{M}.

Like in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, we observe that the set
B
is Borel. Consider three cases.

If
D_{x}\subseteq P_{f}
for some
x\in B
, we can check that condition (ii) of Lemma
4.1
holds for
P_{f}
with
A:=D_{x}
. Hence by this lemma
\mathbb{R}\setminus P_{f}\in\mathcal{N}
. Then observe that
f
and
P_{f}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
with

H:=[0,\infty)
. Thus
f(x)\geq 0
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
D_{x}\subseteq N_{f}
for some
x\in B
, we can check that condition (ii) of Lemma
4.1
holds for
N_{f}
with
A:=D_{x}
. Hence by this lemma
\mathbb{R}\setminus N_{f}\in\mathcal{N}
. Then observe that
f
and
N_{f}
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
4.2
with

H:=(-\infty,0]
. Thus
f(x)\leq 0
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
D_{x}\setminus P_{f}\neq\emptyset
and
D_{x}\setminus N_{f}\neq\emptyset
for each
x\in B
, for any
x\in B
we can pick

y_{i}\in D_{x}
(
i=1,2
) such that
f(y_{1})<0
and
f(y_{2})>0
. Since
(x,y_{i})\in D
, we have

f(x)f(y_{i})\geq 0
for
i=1,2
. Consequently,
f(x)=0
. Thus we have proved that
B\subseteq P_{f}\cap N_{f}
.
Hence condition (ii) in Lemma
4.1
holds for
Z_{f}:=P_{f}\cap N_{f}
and
A:=B
. Now, by Lemma
4.1
,
we obtain
\mathbb{R}\setminus Z_{f}\in\mathcal{M}
. Finally, we apply Lemma
4.2
to
f
and
Z_{f}
with
H:=\{0\}
.
So,
f(x)=0
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Clearly, the assertion of the theorem holds in each of the above cases.
∎

Like it was mentioned for Theorem
3.5
, also Theorem
4.3
can be generalized to the case when
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
.

5.
Concluding remarks

Although the proofs of Theorems
3.5
and
4.3
follow several nice ideas from
[
4
]
and
[
3
]
, they produce new results. There is also a small additional effect.
Our simple observation that it suffices to consider a

The following problem seems interesting.

Problem 5.3

Let us call a set
