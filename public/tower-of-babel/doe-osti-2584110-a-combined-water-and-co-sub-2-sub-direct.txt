EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The primary objective of the project was to demonstrate the technical and economic
performance of a technology that simultaneously captures CO2 and water from the air ─ a
Hybrid Direct Air Capture system (HDAC). In HDAC, air is passed over water capture section as
well as a CO2 selective sorbent to remove CO2 from the air stream. Combining potable water
generation and CO2 capture in a single device with the unique energy conserving features of the
proposed design enables long-term projected CAPEX under $750/t-CO 2 and levelized cost of
capture (LCOC) of $140/t-CO2.
Project DE-FE-0031970 “A Combined Water and CO2 Direct Air Capture System” ran for four
years and three months from 10/01/2020 to 12/31/2024 for three budget periods and an
extension period. The total project budget at completion was $3,534,408 consisting of a
$2,680,064 federal share and $854,344 cost share. The pilot plant was successfully designed,
engineered, built and commissioned during the initial three budget periods.
The plant demonstrated successful water capture during this period, whereas initial CO 2
capture was well below target values. The project team requested an extension period during
which alternate materials and beds were evaluated. By the end of the project, both moisture
swing adsorption of CO2 and water capture had successfully been demonstrated at target
capture rates making further scale-up of the technology viable.

MAJOR ACCOMPLISHMENTS
The project team successfully constructed a 3000CFM HDAC pilot unit. Upon operation, water
capture performance was consistently within expected operational values, whereas initial CO 2
capture performance was lower than expected. This prompted a reassessment of operational
possibilities for the facility, as well as the successful application for an extension of the project
to include operation of newly developed alternative materials. During the extension period,
these novel materials were installed and successfully operated, showing successful long-term
operation of the plant and materials over multiple months within the performance window
required for future commercial scale-up.

3. PROJECT INTRODUCTION AND OVERVIEW
3.1. Scope of Project
The primary objective of the project was to build a pilot plant for a capture capacity of 30tpa of
CO2, to demonstrate the technical and economic performance of a technology that
simultaneously captures CO2 and water from the air, a Hybrid Direct Air Capture system
(HDAC). A preceding atmospheric water extraction (AWE) section of the unit utilizes a pressure
swing regeneration cycle to capture and remove water from incoming air. Subsequently, the air
is passed over a CO2 selective sorbent to remove CO2 from the air stream. This combines water
generation, and CO2 capture in a single device with the unique energy conserving features of
the proposed design enables a projected CAPEX under $750/t-CO 2 and levelized cost of capture
(LCOC) of $140/t-CO2.

3.3. Technological Introduction
The schematic of the initial HDAC system proposed is shown in Figure 2. The system combines
an atmospheric water extraction (AWE) bed with a CO2 direct air capture (DAC) bed. The AWE
section operates as proposed by MCGRAIL et al., 2020. Humid ambient air flows over a
desiccant bed that removes moisture from the air flow. A paired desiccant bed is isolated from
the air stream and simultaneously regenerated under a partial vacuum. The desiccant beds are
thermally coupled by continuously pumping a heat transfer fluid between the beds. This
provides a highly efficient heat transfer mechanism to “balance” the heat of water vapor
adsorption generated in the active desiccant bed with the endothermic heat of desorption
consumed in the desiccant bed undergoing regeneration. Air exiting the desiccant bed thus
remains near ambient temperature.
The low humidity air stream exiting the desiccant bed enters a second set of sorbent beds
selective for CO2 removal (DAC section). Parasitic water uptake on the CO2 sorbent is minimized
in the low humidity air flow, and the sorbent can maintain optimal CO 2 capture efficiency and
minimize operational issues due to unwanted water adsorption on the CO 2 sorbent. The CO2-
depleted air stream is then exhausted to ambient. Sorbent regeneration is accomplished under
a partial vacuum. A vacuum pump provides suction on the desiccant and CO 2 sorbent beds
during the regeneration cycle. After modest compression, the vapor pressure has been raised
sufficiently to condense water vapor. Remaining CO2 and water vapor are compressed to
atmospheric pressure for discharge.

4.3. Desiccant Selection
Desiccant selection on the project was based on the sorbent materials development for
dehumidifier technology (McGrail et al. 2020) , and AWE in desert climates (Fathieh et al.,
2018). The desiccants selected were the metal-organic framework (MOF) Fe-MIL-100
(Horcajada et al., 2007), which provides effective water removal from air with high to moderate
relative humidity as illustrated by its adsorption isotherm in Figure 4. The isosteric heat of
adsorption of Fe-MIL-100 is 2632 kJ/kgH2O (Rezk et al., 2012). This desiccant has shown excellent
biocompatibility (Chen et al., 2019) and is synthesized from iron salts and trimesic acid, and has
received extensive study (Feng et al., 2018; Zhong et al., 2018). Fe-MIL-100 has shown itself to
be excellent candidate for commercial manufacture.
As indicated by Figure 4, the adsorption performance of Fe-MIL-100 declines below 30%
relative humidity. If further water removal is desired in the HDAC system, implementation of a
second desiccant in the downstream section of the AWE contactor is required. MOF-303 has
been selected as this desiccant due to the extensive data available on its properties (HANIKEL et
al., 2019), and lack of hysteresis in its adsorption behavior (see Figure 5). Its isosteric heat of
adsorption is 2900 kJ/kgH2O. This MOF was also readily available for testing in sufficient
quantities from various suppliers.
A vendor was contracted to manufacture Fe-MIL-100 and MOF303 for the project. Initial lab-
scale production batches were tested by the PNNL to confirm porosity and uptake capacity, as
well as finalize the method for larger scale production and coating for the HDAC unit. Figure 6
shows water uptake isotherms confirming succesful uptake capacity above the target of 50wt%,
as well as the progression of specific surface area of the material during production
development.

4.4. CO2 Capture Sorbent
PNNL had been developing advanced CO2 removal technology based on a novel solid sorbent
known as self-assembled monolayers on mesoporous supports (SAMMS) since 2005. SAMMS
for CO2 capture is an amine-functionalized sorbent on a mesoporous silica support. The chemi-
sorbent functions by forming an ammonium carboxylate upon interaction with CO 2, prompting
its capture even at low CO2 partial pressures (<0.4 mbar). The experience with SAMMS provided
a solid foundation for adapting SAMMS technology into a commercially viable HDAC product.

 Figure 7. CO2 adsorption isotherms for SAMMS sorbent.
The SAMMS sorbent is regenerated via a combined temperature and pressure swing, requiring
the beds to heat-up prior to releasing CO2. A second physi-sorbent was incorporated in the
design to provide CO2 mass flow earlier in the regeneration cycle and improve overall
efficiency. IWVC contracted with the University of North Texas to access their MOF sorbent
materials. The UNT team scaled the batch process for the three MOF candidates: SIFSIX1,
SIFSIX2 and SIFSIX3 (further referred to as UNT-MOF). Samples of the materials were provided
to PNNL for sorption testing. Isotherm data for UNT’s SIFSIX sorbent are shown in Table 2 and
for the UNT-MOF in Figure 8. The SIFSIX sorbents have a hydrophobic pocket that provides
weak H2O binding. Even though relative humidity of the air contacting the SIFSIX is low, the
hydrophobicity helps further inhibit unwanted parasitic water uptake, which degrades overall
capacity in other DAC physi-sorbents (MUKHERJEE et al., 2019).
The decision was made to use only the preferred sorbent UNT-MOF in the manufactured
design. Not only did the UNT-MOF show excellent performance during prior laboratory testing,
the physi-sorbent also aligned well with the project teams objective to avoid a temperature
swing needing external heat to be provided to the DAC contactor. The latter would have been
the case for the SAMMS sorbent material.

IWVC contracted with the University of North Texas to produce UNT-MOF CO 2 sorbent
materials. UNT-MOF is a hydrolytically stable fluorinated MOF with a pore system ideal for
effective and energy-efficient removal of CO2. The material exhibits typical CO2 desorption
behavior as conventional physical adsorbents but has a lower energy input compared to
chemical sorbents. The square-like channels of the MOF afford close-proximity of the fluorine
centers within the framework which enables attainment of CO2 at low concentrations
(Prashant et al., 2016).
Tests were performed by the PNNL to evaluate the performance of UNT-MOF. It was found
that UNT-MOF exhibits cyclic capacities of approximately 1 mmol CO2/gsorbent at CO2
concentrations of 400ppm. The results of CO2 adsorption tests performed by PNNL can be
found below in Figure 9. A coated sorbent coupon has shown similar adsorption, desorption,
and kinetic properties to the base powder sorbent, showing that the sorbent’s performance
was not hindered by the addition of a few percent of a binding agent. The excellent
performance of UNT-MOF in these laboratory tests drove the project team’s decision to use
only this physi-sorbent in the pilot system.

4.5. Contactor Design
Method
A simple analytical modeling approach was used to determine sorbent loading, particle size,
and other size and performance parameters necessary to attain a target efficiency for capturing
water in the AWE section and CO2 in the DAC section. The modeling approach used is applicable
because the sorbent bed system is formed with thin films of the sorbents on a microchannel
heat exchanger (MCHX) radiator-style supports. Microchannel heat exchangers provide
excellent heat transfer characteristics and largely unimpeded mass transfer of the water and
CO2 in the air stream to the sorbents (Lee et al., 2017). Air flows through the channels between
the fins while the microchannel tubes are used to move heat transfer fluid between the
desiccant beds in the AWE section and provide heating and cooling for temperature swing
cycling of the DAC section.
The model uses approaches to determine the required particle size and surface area for the
target uptake rate of water and CO2, a corrugated fin model to determine fin surface area, and
Reynolds numbers and friction factor correlations to determine optimal fin angles and pressure
drop. The set of equations are provided in detail in the “Continuation Application for Budget
Period II”, and are used to determine the overall geometry of the contactors required to
achieve target performance.
Vacuum Process Engineering (VPE) was contracted to fabricate the aluminum contactor panels
to be coated with the AWE and DAC sorbents. Manufacturing issues led to substantial delays in
the completion and delivery of the contactors. The issues were resolved in close collaboration
with the project team, which also greenlit minor design changes in panel geometry, specifically
an increase in the header depth, to allow for improved manufacturability of the panels shown
in Figure 1.

 Figure 11. Set of AWE contactor panels on the factory floor.

 21

The custom design and manufacturing issues resulted in various challenges in the final product.
The length and depth of the fins made these difficult to manufacture, making them susceptible
to damage during fabrication or transport – leading to higher pressure drop and uneven flow
distribution within the beds during operation, or require the corrective repair of leaks. Talks
with commercial suppliers of microchannel heat exchangers indicated that their components
would be more robust and far more cost-effective, using flat fins, shorter fin lengths and lower
individual contactor depth. As of the second generation of contactors employed from the end
of budget period 3 through the extension period, these commercial contactors shown in Figure
13 were installed.

4.6. DAC and AWE Contactor Coating
Initial project milestones outlined plans to coat the contactors by spray coating. However,
spray coating tests were unsuccessful, and the PNNL pivoted efforts to evaluate dip coating.
PNNL conducted a series of tests to evaluate the efficacy of dip coated coupons and the
mechanical stability of coated coupons. Dip coating tests were successful, and the method was
selected for full-scale contactor coating.
Coating tests included both adsorption and desorption performance testing as well as
mechanical stability testing of sorbent and desiccant materials coated onto aluminum coupons
in various thickness and with various coating methodologies. Coated coupons underwent
mechanical durability testing by aggressive agitation via orbital shaker for extended periods of
time. This testing was used to evaluate coating delamination, cracking, and coating mass
change before and after testing. Coatings with loading amounts of less than 0.03 kg/m 2 were
shown to be exceptionally durable when the correct coating procedures were adhered to, as
indicated by Figure 14. AWE coating, such as that in Figure 14, exhibited a loss in mass between
1% and 2.5% after being exposed to agitation on the orbital shaker for an hour.

 Figure 14. Images of coated coupons before and affter mechanical durability testing.
Performance testing was conducted by comparing the adsorption and desorption performance
of differently coated coupons with the performance of the powdered base material. Exemplary
results of performance testing for Fe-MIL-100 are shown in Figure 15. The selected coating
methods with loading of 0.02 to 0.03 kg/m2 show no significant loss in performance to the base
powder for both the desiccant and sorbent materials and coating procedures selected.
The desiccants and CO2 sorbents used in the HDAC system were deposited on aluminum
contactors. Fins were joined to microchannels using vacuum brazing to ensure structural
rigidity. The contactors were leak-tested for quality control and repaired where necessary.
Following fabrication, contactors would undergo a cleaning process consisting of a mild acid

surface cleanliness, surface wettability, coating adhesion, and coating structural integrity.

Condenser and Vacuum Pump
At the initial stages of the project, highest efficiency custom designs for both the condenser and
compressor were considered. The condenser was to be custom-design engineered by the PNNL
according to previous research (Yu et al. (2018), Doan et al. (2020), Dharaiya et al. (2009)). A
condenser geometry was developed that consisted of a plate-type counterflow heat exchanger
with alternating cooling fluid and condensate plates along the stack. In addition, each channel
inside of an individual condensate plate connecting the headers reduces in volume to a
microchannel outlet to accommodate the density change during the condensation of water
vapor. Figure 17 shows the condenser design, with the tapered condensate channels combined
with the tapered headers allowing for more uniform flow distribution and near constant fluid
velocity.

 27

 Figure 17. Condenser Plate Fluid Solution Space for CFD Analysis.
Similarly, a CO2 compressor to the exact process specifications was to be custom-built for
highest operational efficiency. RadMax Technologies had been selected to manufacture the
compressor at the initial stages of manufacturing. At this stage, both the condenser and
compressor posed budgetary and scheduling risks. In addition, components designed for such
specific operational cases would lose their peak operational efficiency when not operating
under those exact circumstances.
Therefore, during the manufacturing phase of the project, the engineering team decided to
forego both design, manufacture and testing of the custom compressor and condenser train.
The project would instead focus efforts on implementation of the key component of the HDAC
system: successful cyclical adsorption and desorption of desiccant and sorbent coated
contactors. Better knowledge of the output of the HDAC system would be required to design
such a highly optimized compressor and condenser equipment.
Instead, a boosted liquid ring vacuum pump paired with a shell-and-tube heat exchanger was
selected for use in the pilot plant. While a liquid ring vacuum pump shows lower efficiency
than the initially proposed design, the pump operated reliably and robustly under all
conditions. This reduced project risks associated with achieving the primary objective of
operation of the HDAC pilot plant.
The pump selected was a frame-mounted 400 CFM Vacuum Booster operating with a 125 CFM
2-Stage Liquid-Ring Vacuum Pump from MD-Kinney. A model of the finalized 2-stage liquid ring
vacuum pump can be seen below in Figure 18. The pump and condenser pairing offer multiple
advantages for operation of the pilot plant, such as fewer issues in condensate removal, high
reliability among competing commercially available vacuum pumps, and a multitude of
capabilities across a wide range of pressures. In addition, the liquid ring vacuum pump would
be used both for evacuation of the chambers as well as for the regeneration of the beds and
removal of water vapor and CO2.

 28

 Figure 18. Model of water vacuum pump and condenser.

Chamber Sealing Mechanism
A sliding door design was chosen for sealing purposes. The unit utilizes a sliding door with a top-
side linear motor and rail to transition the door. A bottom guide rail ensures an outward
movement during transition from one chamber to the other for smoother movement and less
friction with the seal. The door hangs from a hinge, and electrical magnets assist the initial
inward movement towards the seal. The final position and seal are achieved as the chamber is
placed under vacuum, and the door is suctioned towards the beds. The components have been
indicated in Figure 19.
This design, once commissioned, has shown excellent ability to create a seal, but does require
regular maintenance to ensure the seal surfaces are in good condition. This design minimizes
void space within the chamber and reduces the amount entrained air. Other sealing concepts
previously considered are discussed in the Continuation Application for BP2.

Fan and Air Handling
The procured fan has a capable air volume flow 400 – 4000 CFM. The plant can therefore cover
a substantial range of air flow conditions. The fan is located at the exit of the chambers in
induced-draft configuration, pulling air through the system instead of pressurizing it at the front
end. A combination of high accuracy air measurement sensors is installed in the duct to
understand the amount of air and quantity of CO2 moving through the unit.

 29

 Figure 19. HDAC sliding door sealing concept.

Sensors and Controls
The sampling plan for the system is designed to ensure monitoring can be conducted to
demonstrate HDAC performance. The air entering and leaving the system is monitored for CO 2,
temperature, and humidity, and flows are measured for mass-energy balance calculations.
Process equipment is fitted with pressure and temperature sensor where reasonable to
monitor operation of the equipment. The purity of collected CO 2 (%) and flow rate of CO2 as
well as condensate water have been sampled at the back end of the system to capture
production rate and compositions relative to expected system performance. Discrete “grab”
samples of the collected gas and water will be obtained for laboratory analysis for impurity
testing. The various flow rates and pressure, temperature and humidity measurements, will
allow the mass and energy balance around the system to be calculated by the PLC.
The selected CO2 sensor for inlet and outlet air concentration is a PP Systems WMA-5 CO2 gas
analyzer, with an accuracy of 1% of the span over a calibrated range. The product CO 2 sensor is
an Edinburgh Sensors Carbon Dioxide Gas Card with an accuracy of 2% over the entire range
from 0-100%. Various humidity sensors from Vaisala are employed to monitor humidity at the
inlet, in the internals, and outlet of the chambers.

5.5. Generation 2 Integrated AWE and DAC Testing

Cyclical Performance
The project team decided to replace the AWE and DAC beds. The AWE beds were replaced with
coated commercial off-the-shelf contactors with tighter spacing of the parallel flow channels, as
discussed in section 4.5. The desiccant material remained Fe-MIL-100, but the batch had been
produced by another commercial partner. The DAC beds contained a novel material identified
by IWVC and employs a set of packed beds in a tray configuration with flow moving vertically
through each tray. These notably contain a moisture swing adsorbent (MSA) that would capture
and release CO2 as a function of the water vapor concentration it is exposed to. Both the AWE
and DAC beds are shown below in Figure 29. Notably, the MSA beds to not require heat
integration and operate solely on the moisture swing between the process steps. This provides
an immense cost saving over the coated radiator-style contactors needed for the AWE section.

Figure 29. Replacement AWE capture beds (left) and DAC capture trays (right) installed in Q1
 2024.
Replacing the water capture beds immediately impacted water capture performance. This may
have been due to the desiccant material itself, improved air flow during adsorption, or due to
improved heat transfer within the beds during desorption, or a combination of these points.
While the total water uptake capacity of the material remained identical, kinetics of both the
uptake and release of water were now greatly improved, resulting in a fourfold increase in the
cyclical water capture achieved within the target cycle time of the project.
The CO2 capture performance, given by the adsorption curve in Figure 30, showed immediate
improvement. At a throughflow of 2000CFM, the unit captured 80g of CO2 in 15min. This is an
improvement in capacity to the previous generation by a factor 4, but, more importantly, the
time required for the entire cycle was within operationally reasonable parameters. Additionally,
the cyclical process could successfully run over multiple repeated cycles as shown by the CO 2
breakthrough and cumulative CO2 capture shown in Figure 31.

Long-term operation together with improvements in sensor reliability and accuracy allowed for
the identification of characteristics not identifiable in individual cycles. Figure 34 and Figure 35
show the per-cycle cumulative yield of water and CO2 for cycles run through a 24-hour period.

desorption for both water and CO2. In addition, Figure 34 shows the increased removal of water

5.7. Operational Summary
Over the course of the 20-month operational window of the pilot plant during the project, the
project team was able to increase the volumetric productivity of the pilot plant DAC from
negligible values to the original project target of 8.5 kg CO2/m3/h, corresponding to 30tpa of CO2
for 0.4m3 of DAC bed. Progress through individual generations is shown in Figure 40. Key
learnings taken from this project include:

  Hybrid Direct Air Capture with Moisture Swing Adsorbent has successfully been
 demonstrated and operated in a pilot facility.
  AWE and MSA sorbents showed working capacities and performance in the expected
 ranges required to warrant further commercial scale-up of the technology.
  Improvement of the quality of the AWE contactors and cross-sectional uniformity of the
 water capture resulted in the improvement of water production rates by a factor 4.
  The initial MOF material (P/T swing) did not yield satisfactory results due to limitations
 on minimum vacuum pressure achievable.
  Automated operation has resulted in operation of over 5000 cycles, yielding over 500 kg
 of CO2 captured and more than 15000 liters of water captured
  Structured Adsorbers increased the volumetric production rate by a factor of 10
 compared to the project’s packed bed configuration, achieving best-case performance
 of around 9kgCO2/m3/h.
 These results have been instrumental in allowing IWVC to continue the pursuit of advancing
 this technology to commercially relevant scales.
