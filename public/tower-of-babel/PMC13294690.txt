Scanning Electron Microscopy Identification of Microscopic Onyx Fragments in Blood Aspirated from a Distal Access Catheter after Arteriovenous Malformation Embolization: A Case Report
Authors: Satoru Takahashi, Sakyo Hirai, Kyohei Fujita, Shoko Fujii, Hirotaka Sagawa, Mariko Ishikawa, Hikaru Wakabayashi, Kazutaka Sumita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEndovascular embolization with Onyx (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), a non-adhesive liquid embolic agent, is widely used to treat cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs); however, ischemic complications remain a concern. Although aspiration of blood from a distal access catheter (DAC) after microcatheter retrieval is commonly performed in clinical practice, its mechanistic rationale has not been clarified. We report a case in which scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were used to evaluate whether microscopic Onyx fragments could be detected in aspirated blood.Case PresentationA patient with a Spetzler–Martin grade III occipital AVM underwent transarterial embolization using Onyx. Retrieved microcatheters and blood aspirated from the DAC were analyzed using SEM/EDS. Tantalum-containing microscopic aggregates consistent with Onyx were identified on the microcatheter surface and in the aspirated blood. The detected fragments measured approximately 50–90 µm. In addition, a simplified experimental model demonstrated reproducible adhesion of Onyx to microcatheters and frequent detection of Onyx material within the DAC lumen.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that microscopic Onyx fragments can be detected in blood aspirated from a DAC using SEM/EDS. While the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain, our findings suggest that microscopic Onyx-derived material may persist within the catheter system after embolization.

Introduction

Cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are abnormal vascular shunts that can cause seizures, focal neurological deficits, or intracranial hemorrhage. Treatment strategies include microsurgical resection, stereotactic radiosurgery, and endovascular embolization, often used in combination.

Onyx (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), a non-adhesive liquid embolic agent composed of ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH) copolymer, dimethyl sulfoxide, and tantalum powder, allows for controlled injection and deep nidus penetration and has become widely adopted for AVM embolization.1) Despite its advantages, ischemic complications remain a recognized risk, with reported rates ranging from 2.6% to 9.6%.2,3)

Ischemic events following embolization are generally attributed to the unintended occlusion of normal vessels; however, the extent to which fragmented embolic material migrates distally is not fully understood and has not been evaluated.

To date, the presence of embolic material has primarily been evaluated by angiographic findings or macroscopic inspection, both of which lack the resolution required to detect microscopic debris. In particular, minute fragments within the catheter system cannot be reliably identified using conventional light microscopy. Scanning electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) enables high-resolution morphological and elemental analyses at the micrometer scale, allowing for the detection of tantalum-containing Onyx-derived material that would otherwise remain undetectable by conventional light microscopy. In this context, the present report aims to demonstrate the microscopic detection of Onyx-derived material within the catheter system after embolization. This study was carried out in accordance with the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki and its revisions and was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Science Tokyo (approval number: M2020-102).

Case Presentation

Clinical background and embolization procedure

A patient in their 20s presented with a right visual field defect. MRI demonstrated a left occipital AVM, Spetzler–Martin grade III, supplied by branches of the left middle cerebral artery, posterior cerebral artery, occipital artery, and middle meningeal artery (Fig. 1A and 1B). T2*-weighted imaging confirmed an associated hemorrhage. Transarterial embolization using Onyx 18 (Medtronic) was performed under general anesthesia with systemic heparinization. Embolization was carried out via the left occipital artery, middle meningeal artery, and 2 posterior cerebral artery feeders (Fig. 1C–1F). A distal access catheter (DAC; 3.4-Fr Guidepost; Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan) was first positioned to provide stable support and to facilitate selective navigation of the microcatheter to the target feeder. A DeFrictor Nano microcatheter (Medico’s Hirata, Osaka, Japan) was then advanced through the DAC to the intended position. An Onyx plug was formed at the microcatheter tip prior to advancing into the nidus, followed by Onyx injection using a plug-and-push technique. After embolization, the microcatheter was retrieved under negative pressure. The injection duration and reflux length were recorded for each feeder. In the left occipital artery feeder, the injection time was 5 min and 44 s, with 8 pauses of approximately 10 s each, and the maximum angiographically observed reflux length was approximately 6 mm. In the middle meningeal artery feeder, the injection time was 7 min and 8 s, with 15 pauses of approximately 10 s each, and the reflux length was approximately 15 mm. In the 1st posterior cerebral artery feeder, the injection time was 25 min and 17 s, with 3 pauses of approximately 10 s each, and the reflux length was approximately 8.5 mm. In the 2nd posterior cerebral artery feeder, the injection time was 47 min and 50 s, with 53 pauses of approximately 10 s each, and the reflux length was approximately 12 mm. The total volume of Onyx injected was not systematically recorded. After microcatheter removal, 5 mL of blood was aspirated from the DAC after each microcatheter retrieval. Postprocedural angiography confirmed substantial nidus embolization without evidence of distal occlusion, and diffusion-weighted imaging showed no ischemic lesions. The patient’s neurological status remained stable, and staged treatment was planned.

SEM/EDS analysis of retrieved microcatheters

SEM (JSM-7900F; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) examination of the retrieved microcatheters revealed Onyx aggregation at both the tip and the shaft. In the feeder from the left occipital artery, slight reflux was observed angiographically (Fig. 3A), and corresponding Onyx aggregates were identified on the catheter surface (Fig. 3B and 3C). In the posterior cerebral artery feeder, greater reflux obscured the catheter tip (Fig. 3E and 3F), and more extensive aggregation was observed (Fig. 3G). EDS confirmed the presence of a tantalum-containing material that was consistent with Onyx in these regions (Fig. 3D, 3H and Table 1).

SEM/EDS analysis of aspirated blood

Blood aspiration was performed after each microcatheter retrieval, and the blood aspirated from the DAC after Onyx injection through the left occipital artery was analyzed using SEM. The distance between the DAC tip and the microcatheter tip was estimated to be approximately 9 cm based on fluoroscopic markers in the lateral view, and both catheter tips were located within the occipital artery. The aspirated blood samples were centrifuged, and the sediment was resuspended in purified water. The entire resuspended sample was then distributed onto the observation substrate, and SEM imaging was performed over the entire available area. Thus, the analysis was not limited to a selected portion of the sample but was intended to comprehensively evaluate all detectable structures within the prepared specimen. Candidate structures were identified based on morphological similarity to control Onyx material and the presence of tantalum signals on EDS. However, no predefined quantitative criteria, blinded assessment, or reproducibility analysis was applied in this study. A total of 4687 images at ×1000 magnification were acquired semi-automatically and reviewed manually. Platelets and red blood cells were identified based on morphology (Fig. 4A and 4B), and EDS confirmed the absence of tantalum in the native blood components (Fig. 4C). Manual inspection identified several aggregated structures that were morphologically similar to the control Onyx material (Fig. 4D and 4E). These aggregates measured approximately 50 × 60 μm and 50 × 90 μm. EDS of these structures demonstrated tantalum-containing elemental profiles that were consistent with those of Onyx (Fig. 4F and Table 1).

Experimental model results

To further evaluate whether Onyx adhesion to microcatheters and residual embolic material within the DAC could be reproducibly observed under controlled conditions, an experimental model was constructed. A straight tubular structure with a blind distal end was created using a 3D printer to simulate a simplified vascular lumen. The inner surface of the tube was marked at 5-mm intervals to allow for measurement of the Onyx reflux length. A microcatheter was inserted into the tube, and a DAC was positioned and fixed approximately 10 cm proximal to the microcatheter tip. Onyx was injected through the microcatheter until reflux was observed. At that point, the microcatheter was withdrawn, and approximately 3 mL of fluid was aspirated from the DAC lumen. Both the retrieved microcatheter and the aspirated fluid were examined for the presence of Onyx. The injection was performed by board-certified doctors. A total of 8 experimental runs were performed (Fig. 5A). Onyx adhesion to the microcatheter surface was observed in all experiments (8/8, 100%) (Fig. 5B). In addition, Onyx material was identified in the aspirated fluid in 7 of the 8 experiments (87.5%) (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 5C and 5D).

Discussion

This single-case report demonstrates that microscopic Onyx (Medtronic)-derived material can be detected on retrieved microcatheters and in blood aspirated from a DAC after AVM embolization using SEM/EDS. Importantly, this study does not demonstrate that the detected microscopic material results in distal embolization, ischemic lesions, or any clinical consequence, but rather documents the presence of embolic material within the catheter system at a microscopic level. Aspiration of blood from the DAC after microcatheter removal is commonly performed to reduce embolic complications. Tantalum-containing aggregates identified in aspirated blood may further support this procedural step by suggesting that it removes microscopic Onyx-derived material from the catheter system after embolization. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

The formation of an Onyx plug at the microcatheter tip is an inherent component of the embolization technique, and some degree of catheter surface adhesion is therefore expected. The angiographically observed reflux length varied among feeders (6–15 mm). Although catheter surface aggregation was observed in association with plug formation, no quantitative analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between reflux length and the extent of Onyx adhesion. Moreover, the precise origin of the detected fragments—whether derived from the catheter surface during retrieval or from proximal portions of the Onyx cast—could not be determined in this study.

Although experimental studies have shown that emboli measuring 30–100 μm can occlude small penetrating arteries,4–6) this report does not establish a direct causal relationship between the detected fragments and ischemic complications. No ischemic lesions were observed in this case, and the clinical significance of microscopic Onyx fragments remains uncertain.

Some limitations of this report must be acknowledged. This report describes a single case, and procedural variables such as catheter position, aspiration volume and strength, feeder diameter, and injection parameters may have substantially influenced the findings. As this is a single-case observation, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Additionally, the results are qualitative in nature and may not be generalizable to other patients. Although the entire prepared specimen was examined, potential biases in detection and interpretation cannot be completely excluded. While the clinical impact of these microscopic fragments remains unclear, the present findings provide qualitative evidence that embolic material may persist within the catheter system after Onyx injection. Further investigation in larger series or controlled experimental settings is required to determine the frequency, origin, and potential clinical relevance of this phenomenon.

Conclusion

Using SEM/EDS, microscopic Onyx (Medtronic)-derived material was identified on retrieved microcatheters and in blood aspirated from a DAC after AVM embolization. These findings provide qualitative evidence that Onyx-derived material may persist within the catheter system during the procedure. However, the clinical significance of these findings remains uncertain, and no conclusions can be drawn regarding their relationship to embolic complications or procedural outcomes. Further studies are required to clarify the frequency, origin, and potential clinical relevance of this phenomenon.
