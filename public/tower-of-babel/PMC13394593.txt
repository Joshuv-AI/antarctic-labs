Adherence to aspirin use during pregnancy among women at risk of preeclampsia: A cross‐sectional study
Authors: Emma M. Swift, Sigurbjörg A. Guðnadóttir, Eygló H. Haraldsdóttir, Hulda Hjartardóttir, Lilja Ó. Atladóttir, Helga Helgadóttir, Lárus S. Guðmundsson, Rannveig Sigurvinsdóttir, Kristjana H. Ásbjörnsdóttir, Jóhanna Gunnarsdóttir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAspirin prevents severe preeclampsia, but its effectiveness depends on its use. Aspirin is recommended in pregnancy for women at risk of preeclampsia. However, evidence on prophylactic aspirin uptake and adherence is limited. The aim was to examine whether women at risk received recommendations to use aspirin and estimate the prevalence of inadequate adherence.Material and MethodsCross‐sectional internet survey conducted in Iceland. Pregnancies were classified as low, moderate, and high risk for preeclampsia according to guidelines. The Morisky‐Medicine‐Adherence‐Scale© (MMAS‐8) was used to estimate adherence, classified as good (8 points), moderate (6–7.9), and inadequate (<6). Prevalence ratios (PR) for inadequate adherence were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI), with the high‐risk group as reference.ResultsSurvey responses were received from 887 women with gestational age between 12 + 0 and 36+ 6, with 122 at high and 137 at moderate preeclampsia risk. In total, 229 women reported being recommended aspirin: 87 at high risk (71%), 92 at moderate risk (67%), and 50 at low risk (8%). Nonadherence was reported by 13 women (5.7%). The prevalence of inadequate adherence was 25% among high‐risk, 21% among moderate‐risk (PR 0.83; 95% CI: 0.47–1.46), and 27% among low‐risk respondents. No individual risk factors were significantly associated with inadequate adherence.ConclusionsRespondents at risk of preeclampsia commonly did not remember receiving appropriate recommendations for aspirin. This may considerably limit the effectiveness of screening and aspirin prevention in Iceland, and a more systematic approach could be needed. However, adherence to aspirin in our population was comparable to previously reported adherence to medication for chronic illnesses and not related to risk status.

Key message

Approximately one‐third of women classified at risk did not recall receiving an aspirin recommendation, suggesting opportunities for improvement within preventative antenatal care. Adherence did not vary by preeclampsia risk severity. The most frequent reason for nonadherence was forgetfulness.

INTRODUCTION

Preeclampsia is a life‐threatening disorder affecting about 3–5% of pregnancies and a common cause of maternal and fetal deaths worldwide. 1 International guidelines recommend first‐trimester screening for preeclampsia and prevention with aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid). 2 , 3 Aspirin intake to prevent pregnancy complications has been studied for almost half a decade. 4 The effectiveness of aspirin was highlighted in 2017 by the results of the Aspirin for Evidence‐Based Preeclampsia Prevention trial, which showed that aspirin prescribed before 14 weeks of gestation in high‐risk pregnancies decreases preterm births associated with preeclampsia. 5 Very limited evidence exists on aspirin adherence during pregnancy, despite the potential implication for the success of preeclampsia screening and prevention programs. 5 , 6 We did not find any studies on aspirin adherence in pregnancy from the Nordic countries, including Iceland. According to expert opinion in Norway, it has been estimated that aspirin is used in about 50% of pregnancies at high risk of preeclampsia (approximation that combines both prescription and medicine adherence). 7

Guidelines from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) are currently used in Iceland for clinical risk assessment in the first trimester, 8 even though their sensitivity is only 40% when classifying pregnancies as high risk that later will be complicated by preterm preeclampsia. 9 The Fetal Medicine Foundation (FMF) screening model can improve sensitivity up to 70%, 10 , 11 however, this approach is resource demanding and costly. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Aspirin adherence should be studied in every pregnant population before implementing costly screening programs for preeclampsia. Therefore, the first aim of this study was to examine whether aspirin intake in pregnancy was recommended appropriately according to guidelines about preeclampsia screening and prevention in Iceland. The second aim was to estimate the prevalence of inadequate adherence, according to different risk factors indicating the use, and the third aim was to explore reasons for inadequate adherence.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study setting

The study was a cross‐sectional internet survey targeting pregnant women in Iceland, a small nation of 389,450 inhabitants and 4302 births in the year 2024. Antenatal care in Iceland is free of charge and attended by almost all pregnant women. At the first antenatal appointment, midwives take a detailed obstetric and overall health history with a review of the woman's health records. They assess preeclampsia risk according to the NICE guidelines 8 and refer selected women with high‐risk pregnancies to an obstetrician. According to the guidelines, aspirin is recommended in pregnancies with either ≥ one strong risk factor or ≥ two moderate risk factors. The following conditions are considered strong risk factors: Previous history of hypertensive disorder in pregnancy, chronic hypertension, pre‐gestational diabetes mellitus, renal disease or autoimmune disease such as systemic lupus erythematosus, and moderate risk factors are the following: family history of pre‐eclampsia (first degree relative), nulliparity, body mass index (BMI) of 35 kg/m2 or more at the first visit, age more than 40 years, pregnancy interval more than 10 years, or multifetal pregnancy. Women considered to be at high or moderate risk are given recommendation to buy over‐the‐counter aspirin (®Hjartamagnýl) and take 150 mg in the evening, from 12 weeks of pregnancy until 36+6 weeks of pregnancy. As aspirin use is recommended but rarely prescribed during pregnancy in Iceland, no data about electronic dispensing records are available.

Survey development

A questionnaire to assess clinical risk factors and aspirin intake in pregnancy was developed. Participants were asked if aspirin intake had been recommended, if they had bought aspirin, if they had used aspirin, and the frequency of last week's intake. Most of the questions were in multiple‐choice format, but the survey also included open‐ended follow‐up questions aimed to evaluate potential reasons for inadequate adherence: “What prevented you from using aspirin?” (individuals reporting nonadherence) or “What prevented you from using aspirin daily?” (individuals that reported missing one or more doses in the previous week). A pilot questionnaire was tested in a group of 26 women from February 14th to March 16th, 2023. The questions were found to be comprehensible and appropriate by the respondents. Most women in this pilot test (23 of 26) reported daily intake of aspirin during the previous week. Consequently, a single question about the frequency of intake was suspected to have a low sensitivity for inadequate adherence. The Morisky Medicine Adherence Scale© (MMAS‐8), an instrument including 8 items that is protected by United States copyright and trademark laws, was used to measure adherence. 18 , 19 , 20 After obtaining a license agreement (available from MMAR, LLC., https://www.moriskyscale.com), an Icelandic translation of MMAS‐8 was provided by Dr. Donal Morisky. A secure web application (REDCap) was used to administer the internet survey, 21 , 22 which contained two sections, the first about risk factors and aspirin intake and the second about socioeconomic factors. Strong risk factors were captured by: “Did you have any of the following diseases at the beginning of your pregnancy?” requesting a separate response to each of the following problems (yes/no/do not know): “High blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, another autoimmune disease (e.g. rheumatic or inflammatory bowel disease)”. The item about systemic lupus erythematosus was included separately as the Icelandic word for autoimmune disease (“sjálfs‐ónæmi”) sounds very similar to the word for allergy (“ofnæmi”). Participants were asked about age as well as weight and height (continuous variables) and BMI was calculated. The survey also included questions to assess the following risk factors that are not included in NICE guidelines: In vitro fertilization in the current pregnancy and previous history of stillbirth or preterm birth (<37 weeks of gestation). Further information about the survey can be found in Supplement A.

Definitions of outcome and covariates

Adherence was defined by the MMAS‐8 instrument as good (score of 8 points), moderate (6 up to 7.9 points) and inadequate (lower than 6 points) according to recommendations of the authors of this adherence scale. 18 Risk factors were defined according to the NICE guidelines, but as autoimmune disease (yes/no) was suspected to be inaccurately self‐reported, only the item about systemic lupus erythematosus was used to define strong risk, and other autoimmune diseases were classified as moderate risk factors. Data was divided into respondents with strong risk factors (high risk), respondents with two or more moderate risk factors (moderate risk), and respondents with only one moderate or no risk factors defined by the NICE guidelines (the term low risk was used in tables, acknowledging that some of these individuals could have been defined at risk by other screening methods). The following sociodemographic covariates were used to describe the population: Maternal age (<25 years, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39 and ≥ 40 years), BMI (<18.5 underweight, 18.5–24.9 normal weight, 25.0–29.9 overweight, 30.0–34.9 obesity class I, 35.0–39.9 obesity class II, ≥40 obesity class III), parity (nulliparous, parous), and area of residence (Capital area, West and west‐fjords, North, East and South).

Data collection

The criteria for participation in the survey were to be currently pregnant, understand Icelandic, and have attended at least one antenatal appointment during the current pregnancy. Participation was not restricted to a specific gestational age. Initiation of aspirin use is generally recommended from 12 weeks in Iceland, and the treatment usually discontinued before 37 weeks. Therefore, the analysis was restricted to responses from 887 participants with gestational age 12 weeks to 36 weeks and 6 days. The survey was promoted as a quality control of antenatal risk assessment and an investigation of women's views of the use of medication during pregnancy (not specifically mentioning aspirin). Anonymous participants were recruited through social media from 1st of September 2023 to 31st of March 2024. The survey was advertised in Facebook groups that are commonly used by pregnant individuals in Iceland, and on social media accounts of Landspitali University Hospital and Reykjavik Birth Centre, a midwifery‐led birth unit in Reykjavik. Also, a paper advertisement with a QR code linking to the survey was placed at the ultrasound departments in Landspitali and the Hospital in Akureyri, where most pregnant individuals in Iceland attend an optional scan at 12–13 weeks and a routine scan at 20 weeks. To evaluate if respondents were representative of our population, data was obtained from the Icelandic Medical Birth Registry and Statistics Iceland to compare with the survey responses. 23 , 24 , 25

Data analysis

Residency area of 20‐44‐year‐old Icelandic women on the 1st of January 2024 was compared with the survey participants. Distribution of maternal age and BMI of the survey participants were compared with data from the Birth Registry on all births in 2020 and chi‐squared test used to test for differences in proportion (Supplement B). Similarly, risk stratification (high/moderate/low risk) and the presence of individual strong risk factors for survey respondents were compared to all births in 2020, stratified by parity (Table 1). For the Birth Registry data, births 2000 to 2019 were used to define previous history of hypertensive disorder in pregnancy (ICD‐10 codes: O11, O13 – O15) for parous women who delivered in 2020, but information about previous deliveries was lacking in this data for 9% who likely delivered the previous child abroad or before 2000. Diagnostic codes were used for Birth Registry data to find pregnancies with strong risk factors: Chronic hypertension (O10), pre‐gestational diabetes mellitus (O24.0, O24.1, O24.2, O24.3, E10‐E14), renal disease (N00, N01, N03‐N08, N10‐N19) or systemic lupus erythematosus / antiphospholipid syndrome (M32, M68.6) and other autoimmune diseases (M05.9, M06.0, M06.9, M07.3, M07.5). Information about family history of preeclampsia was not available in the Birth Registry data.

The number and percentage of survey respondents who received a recommendation to use aspirin in pregnancy was described for groups at high and moderate risk, for each individual risk factor within each risk group, and those at low risk according to the NICE guidelines. Adherence level (inadequate, moderate, good) according to MMAS‐8 was described as percentages according to risk groups (high, moderate, low risk) and individual risk factors. Prevalence ratios (PR) for inadequate adherence by preeclampsia risk status were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI), using the high‐risk group as reference. Thematic analysis was done on brief text answers describing reasons that prevented adequate adherence using a structured tabular approach. 26

RESULTS

The number of participants who completed the survey was 1066. Gestational age was 12 +0 to 36 + 6 weeks in 887 respondents (Figure 1), of whom 122 were at high risk for preeclampsia, and 137 at moderate risk (Tables 1 and 3). The distribution of the area of residence of the respondents was similar to the total female population of Iceland, although slightly more resided in the capital area, p = 0.004 (Figure S1). Survey respondents included 398 (46%) nulliparous and 476 (54%) multiparous pregnant women (Table 1). Overall, about 30% of survey participants were classified at risk for preeclampsia, whereas 10% of the births in 2020 could be classified as being at risk using data from the Birth Registry (Table 1). In the survey, a higher proportion of women reported chronic hypertension and previous hypertensive disorder in pregnancy, compared with a registered diagnosis of these problems for the births in 2020. The age distribution of survey participants was representative for the pregnant population (p = 0.350) (Figure S2) but survey participants had higher BMI (Figure S3) compared with the births in 2020 (p = 0.002). Participants who remembered being advised to take aspirin were more likely to be older, have higher BMI, and on disability benefits (Table 2). However, they were similar in terms of level of education.

In total, 229 respondents reported having a recommendation to use aspirin, nonadherence was reported by 13 women (5.7%), and one participant chose not to answer (Figure 1). Among 215 respondents who used aspirin, 27 (12.5%) reported missing one or more doses of aspirin during the preceding week, and 13 (6.0%) chose not to answer last week's frequency of intake. Aspirin adherence was further examined using MMAS‐8 score (n = 207). According to the last item of MMAS‐8 that assesses forgetfulness using a 5‐point Likert scale, 90% of responders reported forgetting aspirin never/rarely or once in a while (data not shown). The prevalence of inadequate adherence (score <6) was 24% overall, 25% in the high‐risk group, and 21% in the moderate‐risk group (Table 4). When comparing different risk groups using the high‐risk group as reference, no significant difference could be found in the prevalence of inadequate adherence (Table 5). The prevalence of inadequate adherence was 14% among participants who reported in vitro fertilization treatment, compared with the high‐risk group the PR was 0.56 (95% CI: 0.24 to 1.30). The difference was not statistically significant.

Participants who did not use aspirin despite recommendations or who missed one or more doses during the preceding week were invited to explain this further in open‐text responses. Almost all respondents (n = 36) articulated reasons for abstaining from purchasing or using aspirin or missing doses the previous week. Three themes emerged: Forgetfulness, side effects, and hesitancy. Participants often indicated that forgetting to take aspirin was a reason (n = 18). Some explained further that forgetting to take aspirin for the past week was due to circumstances such as traveling or that they tended to forget to take all medications and vitamins in general. The second theme was side effects, but only three participants mentioned experiencing gastrointestinal discomfort or nausea. The third theme, hesitancy, included 8 participants who were not convinced that they needed the medication or that it would be helpful in lowering the risk of preeclampsia for them specifically. This was described by one participant as “… it was a feeling of not needing it”. Another participant wrote “… I am doing other preventive things instead”.

DISCUSSION

Our study showed that about 70% of women in Iceland at risk of developing preeclampsia recalled having received advice about aspirin use. Nonadherence among those who had received these recommendations was uncommon in our study population. The prevalence of inadequate adherence was 24% according to the MMAS‐8 scale, and interestingly, inadequate adherence was not dependent on which risk factors led to the recommendation. The most prominent self‐reported factor preventing women from using aspirin daily was forgetfulness.

Our results indicate that not all women at risk of preeclampsia were receiving adequate recommendations from health care providers. This may be due to lack of screening for risk factors or inadequate knowledge about the need for recommending aspirin among healthcare providers but may also, in part, be due to women not understanding or remembering the recommendations after the first appointment. Results from a US study comparable to ours showed that 57.7% of high‐risk and only 4.8% of moderate‐risk women recalled having aspirin recommended. 27 In a later report from a quality improvement project in the US, recommendations about aspirin use were correctly given to only 28% of the women at high or moderate risk before the intervention, rising to 79% after the quality improvement initiative to increase coverage. 28 In cost–benefit studies comparing clinical risk assessment to screening with the FMF model, the uptake of aspirin prescription after clinical assessment has been assumed by clinical experts to be in the range of 10% to 50%. 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 In this study, aspirin advice covered 71% of the high‐risk and 69% of the moderate‐risk group, in line with findings reported in a cost–benefit study from the UK, which found that 75% of women at risk of preeclampsia had been prescribed aspirin according to register data. 13 Our results could perhaps be interpreted as acceptable compared with previous reports from the US and UK. However, since most participants reported adherence to aspirin prophylaxis when advised, there is opportunity for improvement. The uptake of aspirin use might be further improved with interventions such as educating midwives about the importance of screening for both common and uncommon risk factors of preeclampsia. In Iceland, healthcare providers follow a checklist to assess risk at the first antenatal appointment and recommend aspirin accordingly, usually without a follow‐up discussion after the initial consultation. 29 Applications that provide women with the opportunity to perform individual risk assessment themselves at the beginning of their pregnancy could provide targeted information about potential preventive treatments like aspirin and help women make health‐related decisions, ultimately increasing the use of aspirin in pregnancy. 30 Pre‐conception consultations for women at high risk of pre‐eclampsia could also improve information and consequently uptake of aspirin adherence. Such consultations sometimes take place in Iceland but are not routinely offered, except to women with pre‐existing diabetes.

Very few individuals in our study who had received recommendations to take aspirin actively decided against using the medication (5%). In a previous study from the US, nonadherence to aspirin in pregnancy (defined as failure to start treatment as advised or premature discontinuation) was reported by 16 of 75 women (22%). 27 Reported reasons were forgetfulness, safety concerns, and being unsure whether aspirin was needed. This is similar to what we found in our study, apart from the safety concerns that did not appear as a theme from open‐text responses. As forgetfulness is a common reason for missed aspirin doses, a mobile app promoting adherence during pregnancy could be beneficial. However, a small, randomized study (n = 126) found no difference in adherence between standard care and an adherence app, possibly due to limited power. 31

In this study, 24% had inadequate aspirin adherence according to the widely used MMAS‐8 instrument. The quality of the measurement properties of different instruments for medicine adherence was evaluated in a recent systematic review that listed MMAS‐8 as a potentially useful instrument, but it should be noted that reports about structural and content validity have been inconsistent. 19 , 20 To our knowledge, aspirin adherence in pregnancy has neither been estimated before with MMAS‐8 nor has the instrument been validated in pregnancy. In one study using the Simplified Medication Adherence Questionnaire (SMAQ) instrument, adherence to aspirin during pregnancy was found inadequate in 46.3% (19 of 41 women). 32 The prevalence of inadequate aspirin adherence in our study was comparable to reported inadequate adherence to medication for systematic autoimmune disorders during pregnancy (26%), measured with MMAS‐8. 33 Similarly, a multinational study found inadequate adherence to medications in pregnancies complicated by chronic illnesses in 32% of pregnancies (range 17% to 55%) using the same instrument. 34 Thus, adherence to preventive aspirin use during pregnancy in Iceland appears similar to adherence to long‐term medication in other countries.

In this study, no significant association was found between risk groups and adherence. Similarly, Shanmugalingam et al. found no association between strong risk factors and adherence except for a previous history of preeclampsia. 6 Their study assessed adherence using repeated measures of platelet function and salicylic acid levels at 4‐week intervals from 16 weeks of pregnancy, which is likely much more sensitive than the MMAS‐8 in detecting inadequate adherence. This may partly explain why they found an association with assisted reproductive treatment and education in a cohort of 140 aspirin users. The uncertainty in our estimates may suggest that we lacked power to detect better adherence to aspirin among women reporting in vitro fertilization. However, both studies indicate that awareness of underlying health risks seems to have minimal impact on adherence among women who conceived without fertility treatment.

To our knowledge, MMAS‐8 has not been used to assess the association between adherence to prophylactic aspirin and pregnancy outcomes, and it is therefore unclear whether the level of inadequate adherence observed in this study affects the risk of preterm preeclampsia. The effect of aspirin adherence on preterm preeclampsia was evaluated in two studies, in the trial by Rolnik et al. which used tablet counts, 5 and the cohort study by Shanmugalingam et al. which repeatedly measured platelet activity through pregnancy. 6 These studies suggest that taking ≥90% of recommended aspirin doses may be required for effective prophylaxis. It is unclear how many women with moderate or inadequate adherence according to MMAS‐8 missed more than 10% of their doses. In this study, 87.5% reported daily aspirin use in the previous week which corresponded well to 90.0% responding having never, rarely, or once in a while forgotten to take aspirin (item within MMAS‐8). Assuming stable adherence over time and truthful reporting, most women in our population likely took >90% of their aspirin doses. Further studies are needed to test if the MMAS‐8 instrument may be used in pregnancy to predict effective prevention of preterm preeclampsia.

Implementation of preeclampsia screening using the FMF model is being discussed within the Nordic countries, and it is currently being tested if implementation of the FMF screening in Denmark will lower the rate of preterm preeclampsia. 35 With the FMF model, women can be more accurately classified as being high‐risk and, as a result, receive recommendations to use aspirin compared with the standard clinical risk assessment. In Denmark, the FMF model correctly classified 66.8% of pregnancies as high‐risk if maternal risk factors, blood pressure, ultrasound, and biomarkers were used; but if only maternal risk factors and blood pressure were included, it correctly classified 53% at risk. 11 The sensitivity of the currently used screening in Iceland is known to be about 40%, 9 and the effectiveness of aspirin to prevent preterm preeclampsia is about 62%. 5 This information can be used with the proportion of aspirin use to very roughly approximate the effectiveness of different types of screening programs (ignoring aspirin adherence for simplicity). Our results showed that 70% of the risk group recalled being recommended aspirin, which indicates that no more than one in every six preterm preeclampsia cases can be effectively prevented by our screening program. If we were to introduce screening with the FMF model, including only maternal risk factors and blood pressure (and assuming similar sensitivity as in Denmark), it is possible that such a systematic approach would improve the use of aspirin to 80%, and we could prevent approximately one in every four preterm preeclampsia cases. Adding ultrasound and biomarkers might improve the effectiveness of the screening program due to improved sensitivity. However, assuming the same proportion of aspirin use, no more than one in three preeclampsia cases would be prevented, at considerably higher cost. Before implementing a new screening program, the resulting costs and savings need consideration for the best possible use of resources. Aspirin adherence was considered in a Norwegian study that estimated the cost–benefit of implementing the FMF screening model. This study demonstrated overall cost savings, but only if aspirin adherence was adequate and prevalence of aspirin use after implementation would be better than before (80% vs. 50%). 7 Interestingly, some cost–benefit studies have assumed a much lower uptake of aspirin use with the standard clinical assessment (10–13%) than reported in this study (70%). 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Further, more studies are needed to understand if information about calculated individual risk provided by FMF screening may be perceived differently by pregnant women than more general information about increased risk from clinical assessment.

Data for this study were collected anonymously, which likely encouraged honest responses. Reporting nonadherence can be sensitive, particularly for pregnant women aware of aspirin's potential benefits for their unborn child. Although cross‐sectional studies have inherent limitations, the online design allowed participation from women across Iceland, which was a strength. The survey targeted pregnant women without referencing aspirin use, enabling estimation of uptake among those at risk.

Potential limitations include selection bias from the convenience sample recruited via social media; although comparisons suggested reasonable representativeness, women with hypertension appeared somewhat overrepresented. This may have inflated reported adherence and recommendation rates as chronic hypertension may be the most logical risk factor for preeclampsia among patients and caregivers. Misclassification of risk status due to self‐reported data is possible, especially regarding autoimmune disease, as the Icelandic terms ónæmi (immunity) and ofnæmi (allergy) may have caused confusion. Some participants may have developed gestational diabetes and mistakenly responded yes to the survey question on pre‐pregnancy diabetes. Also, recall bias concerning aspirin recommendations cannot be excluded. Consequently, the proportion reporting no recommendation may be overestimated. Importantly, information about recommendations for aspiring prophylaxis was assessed only by recall and not by accessing prescription or medical records. Finally, as the survey was only in Icelandic, adherence patterns among migrant women remain unknown.

CONCLUSION

The results of this study suggest that the main challenge of preeclampsia prevention could be to systematically make sure that every woman at risk receives recommendations to use aspirin. Adherence to aspirin was adequate in the majority of women studied, but the results might suggest that this could be improved by reminders. Although the method of preeclampsia screening may affect the prevalence of aspirin recommendation due to a more systematic or centralized approach, this study provides no evidence that the severity of preeclampsia risk will independently affect aspirin adherence. A measure of both aspirin uptake (health‐care dependent) and adherence (patient dependent) should be included in all future studies that estimate the cost–benefit of the FMF screening model.
