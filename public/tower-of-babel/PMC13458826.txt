Considering biomechanical environment in the prevention and treatment of ACDF complications
Authors: Javier Melchor Duart Clemente, María Luisa Gandía-González, Mario Taravilla-Loma, Víctor Rodríguez-Domínguez
Published: 2026

We read with interest the paper by Mundis entitled “Long cervical fusions: are multiple segmental plates less likely to fail than a single long plate?” (1). It is interesting to read how their hypothesis that a long single plate construct retains less stiffness than multiple segmental ones, particularly for rotational stiffness, so long-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusions (ACDFs) face higher risks of mechanical failure and stress concentration at the caudal (lower) segments as the long plate transfers significant load to the end of the construct; conversely, segmentally separated plates (using multiple shorter plates) can distribute this load more evenly, potentially reducing the risk of plate breakage or subsidence.

Certainly, increased distal instability may explain why mechanical complications—and particularly pseudoarthrosis—are higher at the distal level. this increased instability may play an important role, as neutralizing unstableness/increasing stability—usually by means of posterior fusion- may solve the problem (2). Given the fact that biomechanical environment influence on ACDF fusion rates, biomechanical considerations are not only important to explain the problem or the solution for it, but rather—moving further—they may be important trying to avoid it. Anterior cervical plates are used trying to increase fusion rates, and in their evolution dynamic (vs. static) plates have been designed, which can be in turn either rotational (with lower fusion rates and higher hardware problems) (3) or traslational, the latter being supposed to foster fusion thanks to the property of better load transfer (4). This issue on different plate designs and their working patterns could also have been also of interest to deal about in the paper, as load sharing, load transfer and distal stress transfer (as well as lenght of plate, the shorter the better) (5) are all interesting issues which affect biomechanical behaviour and affect fusion and failure rates after multilevel cervical anterior fusions.

Congratulations again to the authors for this interesting paper, which helps better understanding of cervical plates biomechanics.
