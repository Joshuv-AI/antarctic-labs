Orientation Matters: Learning Radiation Patterns of Multi-Rotor UAVs In-Flight to Enhance Communication Availability Modeling
Martin Zoula, Daniel Bonilla Licea, Jan Faigl, Václav Navrátil, Martin Saska

Abstract
The paper presents an approach for learning antenna Radiation Patterns (RPs) of a pair of heterogeneous quadrotor Uncrewed Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) by calibration flight data. RPs are modeled either as a Spherical Harmonics series or as a weighted average over inducing samples. Linear regression of polynomial coefficients simultaneously decouples the two independent UAVs ’ RPs . A joint calibration trajectory exploits available flight time in an obstacle-free anechoic altitude. Evaluation on a real-world dataset demonstrates the feasibility of learning both radiation patterns, achieving 3.6 dB RMS error—the measurement noise level. The proposed RP learning and decoupling can be exploited in rapid recalibration upon payload changes, thereby enabling precise autonomous path planning and swarm control in real-world applications where setup changes are expected.

III
Problem Statement

We are interested in fast and infrastructure-free radiation pattern identification for modular, non-holonomic
UAV
systems.
Formally, given a pair of
UAVs

a
and
b

{\boldsymbol{P_{a}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{P_{b}}}
in
\mathrm{dB}

{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}_{a,b}}}=({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}_{a}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}_{b}}})
of both
UAVs
.
In the notation used, the bold symbols denote matrices whose entries are samples from the respective continuous-time quantities.

We are provided with a dataset
{\boldsymbol{P_{rx}}}={\boldsymbol{P_{tx}}}+\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}_{{tx},{rx}}}})+20\log_{10}\frac{\lambda}{4\pi{\boldsymbol{d}}}
, where
\mathcal{G}
is the joint coupled

of both

and
{tx}\neq{rx};~{tx},{rx}\in\left\{a,b\right\}.

The task is to find
(i) a joint trajectory

to provide useful training samples,
(ii) a learning scheme to decouple
\mathcal{G}({\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}_{{tx},{rx}}}})
into
G_{rx}({\boldsymbol{\alpha^{rx}_{tx}}},{\boldsymbol{\beta^{rx}_{tx}}})+G_{tx}({\boldsymbol{\alpha^{tx}_{rx}}},{\boldsymbol{\beta^{tx}_{rx}}})
, and
(iii) a particular
G
model under the following assumptions.

1.

Wave polarization is neglected.
The
RP
in
Eq.

2
depends only on
\alpha
and
\beta
, disregarding the “roll” component.
We operate with
5
observed variables
\alpha_{a}^{b},\beta_{a}^{b},\alpha_{b}^{a},\beta_{b}^{a}
, and
d
.
Although inclinations
\beta^{a}=-\beta^{b}
are mutually dependent, each is bound with the respective independent

and needs to be kept separate.

2.

The reference frame origin bound with an

is also the origin of the respective
UAV
-with-antenna system.

3.

The radio channel is reciprocal; thus, the transmission from
a
to

.

4.

The channel is fixed and free of interference.

5.

Environment is static and obstacle-free. Radios operate in the far field, i.e.,

.

6.

Signal scattering off the propeller blades
[
15
]
is treated as a component of the measurement noise.

7.

The
UAVs
and their antennas can be different.

Recall the multi-rotor

IV-C

The
Spherical Harmonics (SH)
Model

Let us assume the radiation pattern
G
is a scalar function defined on a sphere over two polar variables
\alpha,\beta
.
Such a function may be expressed in terms of an orthogonal polynomial system, e.g., Legendre polynomials.
The scalar spherical harmonics comprise a series of regularized Legendre polynomials, already proven as a suitable tool in radiation pattern modeling
[
1
]

It reads as

G(\alpha,\beta;{\boldsymbol{p}})=\sum_{l,m}p_{l,m}Y_{l,m},\quad Y_{l,m}=\\
\begin{cases}-1^{m}\sqrt{2\frac{2l+1(l-{|m|})!}{4\pi(l+{|m|})!}}\mathcal{P}^{|m|}_{l}(\cos\alpha)\sin({|m|}\beta),&m<0\\
\sqrt{\frac{2l+1(l-{|m|})!}{4\pi(l+{|m|})!}}\mathcal{P}^{m}_{l}(\cos\alpha),&m=0\\
-1^{m}\sqrt{2\frac{2l+1(l-m)!}{4\pi(l+m)!}}\mathcal{P}^{m}_{l}(\cos\alpha)\sin({|m|}\beta),&m>0.\end{cases}

(5)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the associated Legendre polynomial.
See
Fig.

4
with an illustration of several initial functions of the function system.
We use spherical harmonics to our advantage, as they are a convenient tool compatible with our learning scheme.

IV-D

The
Basis Grid (BG)
Model

We also investigated a “primary-domain” approach, as opposed to “dual-domain” described above in the
SH
method.
(An analogy might be seen in the Fourier time and frequency domains.)
The
BG
samples the azimuth-inclination domain for both
RP
terms;
each such sample represents a single regression parameter, locally contributing to the total gain in the queried direction.
Formally,

G(\alpha,\beta;{\boldsymbol{p}})=\sum_{i,j}p_{i,j}\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,i,j),

(6)

where
\mathcal{K}
is a kernel function over points on the unit sphere

\mathcal{K}(\alpha,\beta,i,j)=\exp\left(\frac{-\|(\alpha,\beta),(i,j)\|^{A}}{2\sigma}\right),

(7)

i\in\left<-\pi,\pi\right>,j\in\left<\frac{-\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right>
,

\sigma
is a length-scale hyperparameter, and
\|\circ\|^{A}
is angular distance between points on sphere defined by angles
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(i,j)
, respectively.
Each parameter
p_{i,j}
represents a point on a sphere defined by azimuth-inclination angles
i,j
.
Note that equidistant sphere sampling is possible only for the 5 Platonic polyhedra;
uniformly sampling
\left<-\pi,\pi\right>\times\left<\frac{-\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right>
over-represents polar regions.
The
BG
method was inspired by preliminary results indicating the
Nearest Neighbor (NN)
method (described in
Section

V-A
) performed the best, but could not decouple
\mathcal{G}
into
G_{a}+G_{b}
.
The parameters
p_{i,j}
were thus intended to capture this significant locality in the radiation pattern.

IV-E

The
Polynomial (P)
Model

For the sake of completeness, we also investigate an unconstrained polynomial model over the azimuth and inclination input variables, although it does not reflect the periodicity of its domain:

G(\alpha,\beta;{\boldsymbol{p}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}p_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\mathcal{B}(\alpha,\beta;{\boldsymbol{k}}),

(8)

where
\mathcal{B}
yields all possible polynomials over
\alpha,\beta
up to requested order as encoded in

.

The measured values might be inconsistent between flights, but should be consistent during a single short flight.
In addition, preliminary tests indicated significant nonreciprocity in the radio system.
Although the antennas are reciprocal in the far field, uncalibrated signal processing electronics introduce slack of up to several
\mathrm{dB}
between
a\rightarrow b
and
b\rightarrow a
channels.
Hence, we always limited the learning and evaluation to a single direction.

VI
Conclusion

The paper explored a novel perspective on
Radiation Pattern
identification and learning.
By treating the
whole

UAV
as a complex directional antenna system, a learning method was proposed to identify the
Radiation Patterns
of
both

UAVs
after executing a specific, synchronized calibration trajectory.
Among different analytical
Radiation Pattern
models, the
Spherical Harmonics
emerged as the most promising, predicting signal quality with
RMS
error as low as
3.6\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
measurement noise level.
We also discussed a potential issue of overfitting in the model.
Compared with the literature we are aware of, the paper presents a new, feasible concept for data-driven antenna
Radiation Pattern
identification, suitable for autonomous modular aerial robotic scenarios.

We list the following among the possible future research directions.
The research should be reproduced using more accurate equipment, accounting for polarization, phasing, and different frequencies.
An extended analysis of overfitting, sampling bias, and sensitivity to localization errors should be conducted.
Further, different learning trajectories with varying
d
might be considered, and the joint trajectory might be optimized as an information-gathering problem instance.
The further perspective
Radiation Pattern
models include principal cuts
[
29
]
and Gaussian processes
[

]
