Remarks on Brunn-Minkowski-type inequalities related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator
Francisco Marín Sola, Francesco Salerno

Abstract
We investigate Brunn-Minkowski-type inequalities for the torsional rigidity T γ T_{\gamma} and the first eigenvalue λ γ \lambda_{\gamma} associated with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator. Counterexamples are provided showing that neither concavity nor convexity properties hold for T γ T_{\gamma} on general bounded convex sets. We also demonstrate that log-concavity and log-convexity properties fail in this setting. In the case of centrally symmetric sets, we answer a question raised by Cordero-Erausquin and Eskenazis by showing that T γ 1 / ( n + 2 ) T_{\gamma}^{1/(n+2)} is neither convex nor concave. On the positive side, we prove that T γ 1 / 3 T_{\gamma}^{1/3} is convex with respect to Minkowski addition when restricted to Euclidean balls centered at the origin. For λ γ \lambda_{\gamma} , we answer negatively a question posed by Colesanti, Francini, Livshyts, and Salani by showing that the inequality λ γ ( Ω t ) − 1 / 2 ≥ ( 1 − t ) λ γ ( Ω 0 ) − 1 / 2 + t λ γ ( Ω 1 ) − 1 / 2 \lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})^{-1/2}\geq(1-t)\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})^{-1/2}+t\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})^{-1/2} does not hold, even for centrally symmetric sets. Keywords: Brunn-Minkowski inequality; Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator; Gaussian torsional rigidity; Gaussian eigenvalue. MSC 2020: Primary 52A40, 35J25; Secondary 35P15.



1
Introduction

Let
K_{0}
,
K_{1}
be convex bodies (compact and convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-empty interior) and
t\in[0,1]
. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality states that

|K_{t}|^{\frac{1}{n}}\geq(1-t)|K_{0}|^{\frac{1}{n}}+t|K_{1}|^{\frac{1}{n}},

(1)

where
K_{t}=(1-t)K_{0}+tK_{1}=\{(1-t)x+ty\,:\,x\in K_{0},\,y\in K_{1}\}
. Here
|\cdot|
stands for the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure or
volume
. Equality holds in (
1
) if and only if
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
are homothetic. For an extensive survey article on this and related inequalities we refer the interested reader to
[
18
]
.

Inequalities of the same type as (
1
), where the volume is replaced by other functionals, arise in many different contexts (see e.g.
[
23
, Section 7]
). In particular, for those coming from Calculus of Variations, a systematic study was given by Colesanti in
[
10
]
. More generally, given a functional

F:\mathcal{K}^{n}\rightarrow(0,+\infty),

where
\mathcal{K}^{n}
is the class of all compact, convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is homogeneous of order
\alpha\not=0
, we say that
F
satisfies a Brunn-Minkowski inequality if

F((1-t)K_{0}+tK_{1})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\geq(1-t)F(K_{0})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+tF(K_{1})^{\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(2)

for all
K_{0},\,K_{1}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and for all
t\in[0,1]
. Due to the homogeneity, to prove (
2
), it suffices to show that
F
is quasi-concave (quasi-convex) if
\alpha>0
(
\alpha<0
), namely

\begin{split}F((1-t)K_{0}+tK_{1})&\geq\min\{F(K_{0}),\,F(K_{1})\}\quad\text{ if }\alpha>0,\\
F((1-t)K_{0}+tK_{1})&\leq\max\{F(K_{0}),\,F(K_{1})\}\quad\text{ if }\alpha<0.\end{split}

(3)

In this paper, we are concerned with some geometric quantities related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator, namely the torsional rigidity
T_{\gamma}
and the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{\gamma}
associated to this operator (see §
2
for the precise definition). Such quantities, usually referred to as the Gaussian torsional rigidity and the Gaussian eigenvalue, represent the counterpart, in Gauss space, of the torsional rigidity and principal frequency for the Laplace operator, whose corresponding Brunn-Minkowski inequalities were proved in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
respectively (see also
[
10
]
).

In a recent work
[
11
]
, a Brunn-Minkowski-type inequality for
\lambda_{\gamma}
is established. Specifically, they prove that the first eigenvalue is convex with respect to the Minkowski addition (see also
[
13
]
for a different proof of this and
[
12
]
for the extension to the nonlinear setting);

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem 1.2,
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
,
\Omega_{1}
be open, bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
t\in[0,1]
. Then

\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})\leq(1-t)\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})+t\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1}),

(4)

where

\Omega_{t}=(1-t)\Omega_{0}+t\Omega_{1}.

In the case of the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian, an inequality of the type (
4
) implies the concavity of the functional
\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\,\cdot\,)
thanks to its homogeneity. In contrast, due to the lack of it, an exponent Brunn-Minkowski inequality does not follow from (
4
). A similar phenomenon happens for the Gaussian measure; although a Brunn-Minkowski-type inequality follows from Borell’s characterization
[
3
]
, namely

\gamma(\Omega_{t})\geq\gamma(\Omega_{0})^{1-t}\gamma(\Omega_{1})^{t},

an exponent version is not immediately deduced. Indeed, it was conjectured in
[
19
]
that an exponent version of the previous inequality, i.e., the inequality for
\gamma^{1/n}
, could be true for convex sets containing the origin. A counterexample to the latter was provided by Nayar and Tkocz in
[
22
]
, where the possibility of such an inequality for centrally symmetric convex sets remained open. This result was then achieved by Eskenazis and Moschidis in
[
16
]
(see also
[
21
]
and the references therein for crucial contributions towards proving the conjecture). So a natural question, also posed in
[
11
]
, is whether a similar improvement holds for (
4
). We answer this question in the negative, at least if it is understood as a generalization of what happens in the Euclidean case. In particular, we show with a counterexample that an inequality of the type

\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\geq(1-t)\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})^{-\frac{1}{2}}+t\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

does not hold even for centrally symmetric sets.

In view of the Euclidean case and Theorem
1.1
, it is also natural to wonder whether a Brunn-Minkowski-type inequality holds true for the Gaussian torsional rigidity. In more detail, the following questions arise:

(Q1)

Do concavity or convexity properties hold for the Gaussian torsional rigidity with respect to the Minkowski sum for bounded open convex sets?

(Q2)

Does the case of centrally symmetric sets allow us to say anything more?

In this paper, we answer these questions as follows. Referring to (Q1), we show with two examples that both concavity and convexity properties cannot be true in general for open, bounded convex sets. Concerning question (Q2), and addressing
[
14
, Question 18]
, we provide a counterexample showing that the functional
T_{\gamma}^{1/(n+2)}
is neither concave nor convex for centrally symmetric convex sets. Specifically, considering
\Omega_{i}=B_{R_{i}}
(an Euclidean ball of radius
R_{i}
centered at the origin),
i=0,1
, we find that, in dimension
n\geq 3
, no concavity or convexity property holds for the Gaussian torsion with exponent
1/(n+2)
. This will be the content of Example
3.3
.

In light of the latter, one may wonder about the smallest exponent
\alpha
such that the functional
T_{\gamma}^{\alpha}
is convex (or concave) for centrally symmetric convex sets. We provide a partial answer; we show that, for Euclidean balls, the functional
T_{\gamma}^{1/3}
is convex with respect to the Minkowski addition. This is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Consider
R_{0},\,R_{1}>0
, then

T_{\gamma}(B_{R_{t}})^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq(1-t)T_{\gamma}(B_{R_{0}})^{\frac{1}{3}}+tT_{\gamma}(B_{R_{1}})^{\frac{1}{3}},

(5)

where
R_{t}=(1-t)R_{0}+tR_{1}
. Equality holds if and only if
R_{0}=R_{1}
. Moreover the exponent is sharp.

Regarding the sharpness of the exponent, one may ask about the smallest dimensional exponent
\alpha_{n}
such that the functional
T_{\gamma}(B_{R})^{\alpha_{n}}
is convex. The answer to this question follows immediately from the proof of Theorem
1.2
. For the sake of completeness, we collect it in the next corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

Consider
R_{0}
,
R_{1}>0
, then

T_{\gamma}(B_{R_{t}})^{\alpha_{n}}\leq(1-t)T_{\gamma}(B_{R_{0}})^{\alpha_{n}}+tT_{\gamma}(B_{R_{1}})^{\alpha_{n}},

where
R_{t}=(1-t)R_{0}+tR_{1}
and

\alpha_{n}=\sup_{R>0}\left\{\frac{R^{2n-2}e^{-R^{2}}}{\left(\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)^{3}}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)^{2}}{t^{n-1}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}}\,dt\left[\frac{n-1-R^{2}}{R^{n}e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}}\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds-2\right]+1\right\}.

We observe in pass that, if
T_{\gamma}^{\alpha}
is convex for some
\alpha>0
, then
T_{\gamma}^{\beta}
is convex for any
\beta>\alpha
. The following relationship between the various exponent discussed above can be inferred from the proof of Theorem
1.2
:

\frac{1}{n+2}\leq\alpha_{n}\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{3}.

Note that when
n=1
the first inequality holds as an equality. Numerical evidence suggest equality when
n=2
, whereas the inequality is strict for
n\geq 3
.

The proof of a more general result seems to require a greater understanding of why convexity fails in the general case and how central symmetry comes into play. In light of Theorem
1.2
and the counterexamples found, it may be reasonable to conjecture the following.

Conjecture 1.4
.

Let
\Omega_{0}
,
\Omega_{1}
be two open, bounded and centrally symmetric subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq(1-t)T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})^{\frac{1}{3}}+tT_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})^{\frac{1}{3}},

where
\Omega_{t}=(1-t)\Omega_{0}+t\Omega_{1}
. Equality holds if and only if
\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{1}
.

Finally, the case of unbounded sets is investigated. In the next result, we show that, for a particular family of unbounded sets, namely half-spaces, the Gaussian torsional rigidity is also convex.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the half-space

H_{s}=\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,x_{1}\geq s\}.

Then, for all
s_{0},\,s_{1}\in\mathbb{R}

T_{\gamma}(H_{s_{t}})\leq(1-t)T_{\gamma}(H_{s_{0}})+tT_{\gamma}(H_{s_{1}}),

where
s_{t}=(1-t)s_{0}+ts_{1}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall some preliminary tools about the Gaussian measure and define the Gaussian eigenvalue and torsional rigidity. In Section
3
we discuss the counterexamples: first for the Gaussian torsional rigidity, answering question
(Q1)
; then for its exponent version, and finally for the exponent version of the Brunn-Minkowski inequality for the Gaussian eigenvalue. Section
4
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
, while that of Proposition
1.5
is deferred to Appendix
A
. We conclude in Appendix
A
by discussing the numerical evidence, when
n=2
, for the Brunn-Minkowski inequality for the torsion functional with exponent
1/(n+2)
.

2
Preliminaries

The Gauss space

Consider the Gaussian measure

\gamma(\Omega)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{\Omega}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}}\,dx,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
, is Lebesgue measurable. The Gauss space is the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the measure
\gamma
. We observe that
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
is a probability space. Note that
\gamma
is not invariant under translation, while it is invariant under rotations about the origin. We mention that, given a measurable set
\Omega
, it is possible to define the Gaussian perimeter of
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality (we refer to
[
17
, §5.7]
for the precise definition of the Gaussian perimeter and for a comprehensive discussion on the inequality) states that, among all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with prescribed Gaussian measure, half-spaces have the least Gaussian perimeter. We will return in the next two subsections to the role of half-spaces as isoperimetric figures. In what follows, given an open, bounded set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
W^{1,2}(\Omega,\gamma)
will denote the weighted Sobolev space of
L^{2}
functions with weak first derivative in
L^{2}
. Then,
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\gamma)
will be the space of
W^{1,2}(\Omega,\gamma)
functions with zero-trace.

The first eigenvalue of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set with Lipschitz boundary. The Gaussian version of the principal frequency can be defined as

\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)=\inf_{w\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,\gamma)\setminus\{0\}}R_{\gamma}(w),

(6)

where

R_{\gamma}(w)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\,d\gamma}{\int_{\Omega}w^{2}\,d\gamma}.

The next result shows that (
6
) is actually a minimum.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set with Lipschitz boundary. Then there exists
v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,\gamma)\setminus\{0\}
such that
\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)=R_{\gamma}(v).

The following result characterizes (
6
) as a solution to a partial differential equation (see e.g.
[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set with Lipschitz boundary and let
v\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,\gamma)\setminus\{0\}
be a minimizer in (
6
). Then
v
satisfies

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{L_{\gamma}}v=\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)v&\text{ in }\Omega\\
v=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(7)

Remark 2.1
.

Since
R_{\gamma}(|v|)=R_{\gamma}(v)
, we can choose a nonnegative minimizer
v\geq 0
. Furthermore, from (
7
) and the maximum principle, we can conclude that
v>0
in
\Omega
and
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

We observe that the solution to (
7
) is unique up to a positive multiplicative constant, that is, the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{\gamma}
is simple. Finally, the definition of the eigenvalue can be extended to unbounded domains. We refer, for instance, to
[
15
]
, where in particular it is proven that half-spaces minimize the first eigenvalue among sets of given Gaussian measure, i. e., for every
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open

\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{\gamma}(H),

where
H
is a half-space such that
\gamma(\Omega)=\gamma(H)
(see also
[
2
]
for more details on the topic,
[
7
]
for a quantitative improvement and
[
8
]
for the extension to Robin boundary condition). This result represents the Gaussian counterpart of the Faber-Krahn inequality for the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian.

The torsional rigidity for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded set with Lipschitz boundary. The Gaussian torsional rigidity can be defined as

T_{\gamma}(\Omega)=\sup_{w\in W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega,\gamma)}\frac{\left(\int_{\Omega}w\,d\gamma\right)^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\,d\gamma}.

As in the case of the first eigenvalue, it is possible to show that this supremum is actually a maximum, attained by the solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}v=1&\text{ in }\Omega\\
v=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(8)

Moreover, it is not difficult to check that

T_{\gamma}(\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}v(x)\,d\gamma(x),

(9)

where
v
is the solution to (
8
).

We observe that the definition of Gaussian torsional rigidity can be extended to unbounded domains. We refer, again, to
[
15
]
, where it is proved that half-spaces maximize the Gaussian torsional rigidity among sets of given Gaussian measure. Specifically, for every
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open

T_{\gamma}(\Omega)\leq T_{\gamma}(H),

where
H
is a half-space such that
\gamma(\Omega)=\gamma(H)
(see also
[
9
]
for the extension to Robin boundary condition). This result represents the Gaussian counterpart of the Saint-Venant inequality for the torsional rigidity of the Laplacian. We mention another isoperimetric result concerning the first eigenvalue and torsional rigidity related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator. In more detail, in
[
20
]
, the authors prove that the half-space minimizes the first eigenvalue among sets with given torsional rigidity, that is

\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)\geq\lambda_{\gamma}(H),

where
H
is a half-space such that
T_{\gamma}(\Omega)=T_{\gamma}(H)
, for every open, bounded subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Lipschitz boundary. This result represents the Gaussian counterpart of the Kohler-Jobin inequality (see
[
6
]
for a complete discussion on the topic).

3
Counterexamples

Torsional rigidity

We start by solving the general
1
-dimensional problem, after which we will exhibit the particular choice of
\Omega_{i}
. Let
\Omega_{i}=[a_{i},b_{i}]
, then the torsion problem (
8
) becomes

\begin{cases}u^{\prime\prime}(x)-xu^{\prime}(x)=-1&a_{i}<x<b_{i}\\
u(a_{i})=u(b_{i})=0.\end{cases}

Multiplying in the equation by
e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}
and integrating twice we find

u(x)=\int_{a_{i}}^{x}e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\left(C-\int_{a_{i}}^{t}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)\,dt.

(10)

From the boundary condition
u(b_{i})=0
the constant is given by

C=\frac{\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\int_{a_{i}}^{t}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\,dt}{\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt}.

Then, from (
10
), we have

T_{\gamma}([a_{i},b_{i}])=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u(x)e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\,dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\left[\int_{a_{i}}^{x}e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\left(C-\int_{a_{i}}^{x}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)\,dt\right]e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\,dx.

(11)

From the statement of Theorem
1.2
, one may expect that the result also holds true for non-centrally symmetric sets. To highlight the role of central symmetry, we will provide a
1
-dimensional (non-centrally symmetric) example in which
T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})>(1-t)T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})+tT_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})
for some
t\in(0,1)
.

Example 3.1
.

Choosing
\Omega_{0}=[-1,1]
,
\Omega_{1}=[-2,0]
and
t=1/2
. Then, from (
11
) we find that

T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})-(1-t)T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})-tT_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})\approx 0.024229258576.

In addition, we will show in the following example that neither log-concavity (which we cannot exclude a priori) nor log-convexity holds in general for centrally symmetric sets:

Example 3.2
.

Let
\Omega_{0}=[-1,1]
,
\Omega_{1}=[-3,3]
and
t=3/4
. Then, from (
11
) we find

T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})-T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})^{1-t}T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})^{t}\approx-0.296068463159.

Moreover, choosing
\Omega_{0}=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
,
\Omega_{1}=[-1,1]
and
t=1/2
. Then, from (
11
) we find

T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{t})-T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{0})^{1-t}T_{\gamma}(\Omega_{1})^{t}\approx 0.0184327921054.

We will now show that neither concavity nor convexity of the functional
T_{\gamma}^{\frac{1}{n+2}}
can be proven, which will be the content of Example
3.3
. In this regard, we first need to introduce a formula for
T_{\gamma}(B_{R})
; let
u
be the solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathcal{L}_{\gamma}u=1&\text{ in }B_{R},\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial B_{R}.\end{cases}

(12)

Since the domain in which we are solving the equation is a ball centered at the origin, we know that the solution is a radial function. Using

\Delta=\partial_{rr}+\frac{n-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}},\quad\left\langle x,\nabla\right\rangle=r\partial_{r},

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
is the
Laplace-Beltrami operator
, the PDE in (
12
) becomes the following ODE

\begin{cases}u^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{n-1}{r}-r\right)u^{\prime}(r)=-1&0<r<R\\
u^{\prime}(0)=u(R)=0.\end{cases}

Then, we find

u(r)=\int_{r}^{R}e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}}t^{-(n-1)}\left(\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)\,dt.

Now, using (
9
), we can see the torsion functional as the following function:

T_{\gamma}(R):=T_{\gamma}(B_{R})=n\omega_{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)^{2}}{t^{n-1}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}}\,dt.

(13)

We need to compute the first and second derivative of (
13
). First, a straightforward computation shows that

T_{\gamma}^{\prime}(R)=n\omega_{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)^{2}}{R^{n-1}e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}}.

(14)

Thus, differentiating again with respect to
R
, we find

T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(R)=n\omega_{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\left(2-\frac{n-1-R^{2}}{R^{n}e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}}\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right).

(15)

Example 3.3
.

Let
G_{\gamma}(R)=T_{\gamma}(R)^{\frac{1}{n+2}}
. A straightforward computation gives

G_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(R)=-\frac{n+1}{(n+2)^{2}}T_{\gamma}(R)^{-\frac{2n+3}{n+2}}T_{\gamma}^{\prime}(R)^{2}+\frac{1}{n+2}T_{\gamma}(R)^{-\frac{n+1}{n+2}}T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(R).

Therefore, the statement that
G_{\gamma}
is convex is equivalent to prove that
G_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(R)>0
and the last, since
T_{\gamma}(R)>0
for all
R>0
, is equivalent to prove

T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(R)-\frac{n+1}{n+2}\frac{T_{\gamma}^{\prime}(R)^{2}}{T_{\gamma}(R)}>0.

Then, from (
13
)-(
14
)-(
15
), the convexity (concavity) of
G_{\gamma}(R)
is equivalent to the positivity (negativity) of the following function:

f(R)=2-\frac{n-1-R^{2}}{R^{n}e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}}\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds-\frac{n+1}{n+2}\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)^{3}}{R^{2n-2}e^{-R^{2}}}\left(\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\left(\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds\right)^{2}}{t^{n-1}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}}\,dt\right)^{-1}.

Considering, for instance,
n=3
, one has
f(1)\approx-0.019
, while
f(2)\approx 0.248
. This phenomenon is not specific to
n=3
but seems to occur for every
n\geq 3
. In the appendix, along with numerical evidence for
n=1,2
, plots for
n\geq 3
are included, and it will be possible to see explicitly what has been stated.

First eigenvalue

Before giving the counterexample, we need an expression of the eigenvalue on a ball centered at the origin. As in the case of the Laplacian, this will be possible by looking at the first eigenvalue as a zero of a special function. Consider the eigenvalue problem (
7
) where
\Omega=B_{R}
. We can write the differential operator as follows

\Delta-\left\langle x,\nabla\right\rangle=\partial_{rr}+\frac{n-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}-r\partial_{r}.

Now we expand
u
in terms of spherical harmonics

u(r,\theta)=\sum_{l\geq 0}R_{l}(r)Y_{l}(\theta),

where
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Y_{l}(\theta)=-l(l+n-2)Y_{l}(\theta)
. Then the equation becomes

R_{l}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(\frac{n-1}{r}-r\right)R_{l}^{\prime}(r)-\frac{l(l+n-2)}{r^{2}}R_{l}(r)=-\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)R_{l}(r)

(16)

for all
l\geq 0
. Introducing the change of variable
r=\sqrt{2s}
, we have

\frac{d}{dr}=\sqrt{2s}\frac{d}{ds},\quad\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}=2s\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}+\frac{d}{ds}.

(17)

Then, if we consider
R_{l}(r)=s^{\frac{l}{2}}w(s)
, applying (
17
) we find

\begin{split}R_{l}^{\prime}(r)&=\frac{l}{\sqrt{2}}s^{\frac{l-1}{2}}w(s)+\sqrt{2}s^{l}w^{\prime}(s),\\
R_{l}^{\prime\prime}(r)&=\frac{l(l-1)}{2}s^{\frac{l-2}{2}}w(s)+(2l+1)s^{\frac{l}{2}}w^{\prime}(s)+2s^{\frac{l+2}{2}}w^{\prime\prime}(s).\end{split}

With this notation, the ODE (

sw^{\prime\prime}(s)+\left(l+\frac{n}{2}-s\right)w^{\prime}(s)-\frac{l-\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)}{2}w(s)=0,

which is in the form of Kummer’s equation

sw^{\prime\prime}(s)+(b-s)w^{\prime}(s)-aw(s)=0

with

and

. A solution

•

Kummer confluent hypergeometric function

M\left(\frac{l-\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)}{2},\,l+\frac{n}{2};\,s\right)=\,_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{l-\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)}{2};\,l+\frac{n}{2};\,s\right)
,

•

Tricomi confluent hypergeometric function
:
U\left(\frac{l-\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)}{2},\,l+\frac{n}{2};\,s\right)
,

see
[
1
, Chapter 13]
for a more comprehensive discussion. Since we are interested in a solution that is regular at

, we have

R_{l}(r)=r^{l}\,_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{l-\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)}{2};\,l+\frac{n}{2};\,\frac{r^{2}}{2}\right)

and from the boundary condition

Thus, the first eigenvalue is given by

_{1}F_{1}\left(-\frac{\lambda_{\gamma}(\Omega)}{2};\,\frac{n}{2};\,\frac{R^{2}}{2}\right)=0.

(18)

4
Main result

The present section deals with the proof of Theorem
1.2
. To make the explanation clearer, we will now present an outline of the proof, which we will formalize in the course of this section.

We want to prove that the function
f(R)=T_{\gamma}(R)^{\alpha}
is convex for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, that is, to prove that
f^{\prime\prime}(R)>0
for all
R>0
, and that the best possible choice of
\alpha
, independent of the dimension, is
\alpha=1/3
. A straightforward computation shows that
f^{\prime\prime}(R)>0
is equivalent to

\alpha\geq 1-\frac{T_{\gamma}(R)T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(R)}{T_{\gamma}^{\prime}(R)^{2}}=:\alpha_{n}(R).

The claim is then

\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{R>0}\alpha_{n}(R)=\frac{1}{3}.

To do this, we will get the following information about
\alpha_{n}(R)
:

(i)

\lim_{R\rightarrow 0^{+}}\alpha_{n}(R)=\frac{1}{n+2}\leq\frac{1}{3}
;

(ii)

\lim_{R\rightarrow+\infty}\alpha_{n}(R)=0
;

(iii)

whenever

\frac{d}{dR}\alpha_{n}(R)\bigg|_{R=R^{*}}=0,

for some
R^{*}>0
, then
\alpha_{n}(R^{*})\leq 1/3
.

In more detail, (i) implies that there exists
\delta>0
such that
\alpha_{n}(R)<1/3
for all
R\in(0,\delta)
. In a similar manner, (ii) implies the existence of
M>0
such that
\alpha_{n}(R)<1/3
for all
R>M
.

Once we know this, if for some
R_{0}>0
and
n\geq 1
we have
\alpha_{n}(R_{0})>1/3
, then from what we have said it must be
R_{0}\in[\delta,M]
. Then, from the Weierstrass theorem, there exists
R^{*}\in[\delta,M]
such that

\alpha_{n}(R^{*})\geq\alpha_{n}(R_{0})>\frac{1}{3},\quad\frac{d}{dR}\alpha_{n}(R)\bigg|_{R=R^{*}}=0,

which is a contradiction to (iii).

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is organized as follows. We first prove points (i)-(ii)-(iii), which will be the content of the next four lemmas. The conclusion is then achieved in the proof of Theorem
1.2
. We start by introducing the following notation:

\begin{split}&c_{n}=n\omega_{n}(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}},\quad I(R)=\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds,\quad J(R)=\int_{0}^{R}\frac{I(t)^{2}}{I^{\prime}(t)}\,dt,\\
&h(R)=\frac{I(R)}{I^{\prime}(R)},\quad v(R)=\frac{J(R)}{h(R)I(R)},\quad p(R)=\frac{n-1}{R}-R.\end{split}

(20)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
v
be defined as in (
20
). Then

\lim_{R\rightarrow 0^{+}}v^{\prime}(R)=\frac{1}{n+2}.

Proof.

The idea is to write
v(R)
for
R\sim 0
. Recalling the definition of
v
from (
20
), we start by expanding
e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}
under the integral sign

I(R)=\int_{0}^{R}s^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{s^{2}}{2}+O(s^{4})\right)\,ds=\frac{R^{n}}{n}-\frac{R^{n+2}}{2(n+2)}+O(R^{n+4}),

and

I^{\prime}(R)=R^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{R^{2}}{2}+O(R^{4})\right).

From these, we can write

h(R)=\frac{I(R)}{I^{\prime}(R)}=\frac{R}{n}+O(R^{3}).

Hence,

h(R)I(R)=\frac{R^{n+1}}{n^{2}}+O(R^{n+3}).

(21)

Regarding
J(R)
, since the integrand satisfies

\frac{I(t)^{2}}{I^{\prime}(t)}=\frac{t^{n+1}}{n^{2}}+O(t^{n+3}),

we get

J(R)=\frac{R^{n+2}}{n^{2}(n+2)}+O(R^{n+4}).

(22)

Then, combining (
21
)-(
22
), we find

v(R)=\frac{J(R)}{h(R)I(R)}=\frac{R}{n+2}+O(R^{3}).

The latter gives the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
v
be defined as in (
20
). Then

\lim_{R\rightarrow+\infty}v^{\prime}(R)=0.

Proof.

First, note that

\begin{split}&I(R)\rightarrow c_{n}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{n-1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\,ds,\\
&I^{\prime}(R)=R^{n-1}e^{-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}\rightarrow 0,\end{split}

as
R\rightarrow+\infty
. Hence,
h(R)=I(R)/I^{\prime}(R)\rightarrow+\infty
and
h(R)\sim c_{n}R^{1-n}e^{\frac{R^{2}}{2}}
. Now, recalling that

p(R)=\frac{n-1}{R}-R=\frac{I^{\prime\prime}(R)}{I^{\prime}(R)},

we find

h^{\prime}(R)=1-\frac{I(R)I^{\prime\prime}(R)}{I^{\prime}(R)^{2}}=1-\frac{I(R)p(R)}{I^{\prime}(R)}=1-h(R)p(R).

(23)

Thus, using (
23
) and the definition of
p(R)
, we get that

\frac{1+h^{\prime}(R)}{Rh(R)}=\frac{2-h(R)p(R)}{Rh(R)}=1+\frac{2}{Rh(R)}-\frac{n-1}{R^{2}}\rightarrow 1,

(24)

as
R\rightarrow+\infty
.

Define now
\varphi(R)=Rv(R)
. Starting from the definition of
v(R)
, a straightforward computation gives

v^{\prime}(R)=1-\frac{2v(R)}{h(R)}+p(R)v(R),

then

\varphi^{\prime}(R)=R+v(R)\left(1+Rp(R)-\frac{2R}{h(R)}\right).

Moreover, we find, from the definition of
p(R)
, that

1+Rp(R)-\frac{2R}{h(R)}=n-R^{2}-\frac{2R}{h(R)},

which, together with
v(R)=\varphi(R)/R
, gives

\varphi^{\prime}(R)=R-R\varphi(R)+\varphi(R)\left(\frac{n}{R}-\frac{2}{h(R)}\right).

Finally, we get

\varphi^{\prime}(R)=R(1-\varphi(R))+E(R)\varphi(R),

(25)

where

E(R)=\frac{n}{R}-\frac{2}{h(R)},

and
E(R)\rightarrow 0
as
R\rightarrow+\infty.

We will now prove that
\varphi
is bounded. In this regard, we first observe that, since
v(R)>0
for all
R>0
, then
\varphi(R)>0
. Furthermore, since
E(R)\rightarrow 0
, consider
R_{0}>0
such that, for instance,
|E(R)|\leq 1/2
for all
R\geq R_{0}
. Now, if for some
R_{1}\geq\max\{R_{0},\,2\}
,
\varphi(R_{1})=2
, then from (
25
) and the boundedness of
E(R)
we have

\varphi^{\prime}(R_{1})=-R_{1}+2E(R_{1})\leq 1-R_{1}<0.

Therefore,
\varphi
is pushed below the value
\varphi(R_{1})=2
, and we can conclude that
0<\varphi(R)\leq M
, for all
R>0
, where

M=\max\left\{2,\sup_{R\in(0,R_{0}]}\varphi(R)\right\}.

We will finish the proof by showing that
\varphi(R)\rightarrow 1
as
R\rightarrow+\infty
, which together with (
24
) will imply the thesis.
In this regard, fix
\delta>0
and consider
R_{1}>1+M
large enough such that

|E(R)|\leq\delta

(26)

for all
R\geq R_{1}
. We first prove that
\varphi
cannot cross
1+\delta
from below. To do this, assume by contradiction that it is false, namely that there exists
R_{2}\geq R_{1}
such that

\varphi(R_{2})=1+\delta\quad\text{ and }\quad\varphi^{\prime}(R_{2})\geq 0.

Then, from (
25
)-(
26
), we find

\varphi^{\prime}(R_{2})=-\delta R_{2}+E(R_{2})(1+\delta)\leq\delta(1+M-R_{2})<0,

where we have used
\delta<M
(which we can always assume, provided that we choose
\delta
sufficiently small) and
R_{2}\geq R_{1}>1+M
. The latter gives
0\leq\varphi^{\prime}(R_{2})<0
, that is a contradiction. Arguing in the same way, it is possible to show that
\varphi
cannot cross
1-\delta
from above, that is, there does not exist
R_{2}\geq R_{1}
such that
\varphi(R_{2})=1-\delta
and
\varphi^{\prime}(R_{2})\leq 0
.

To summarize, what we have shown so far is that, if for a certain
\overline{R}\geq R_{1}
, we have
1-\delta\leq\varphi(\overline{R})\leq 1+\delta
, then
1-\delta\leq\varphi(R)\leq 1+\delta
for all
R\geq\overline{R}
. Assume now that
\varphi(R)>1+\delta
for all
R\geq R_{1}
. Then, from (
25
)-(
26
), we have

\varphi^{\prime}(R)=R(1-\varphi(R))+E(R)\varphi(R)\leq-\delta R+\delta\varphi(R)\leq-\delta R+\delta(1+M)<0,

where we have used again
R\geq R_{1}>1+M
and
\varphi(R)\leq M<1+M
. In particular, if we integrate in the previous inequality on
[R_{1},R]
, we find

\varphi(R)\leq\varphi(R_{1})+(R-R_{1})\left(\delta(1+M)-\frac{\delta}{2}(R+R_{1})\right)\rightarrow-\infty

as
R\rightarrow+\infty
. This is a contradiction since
\varphi(R)>0
. This ensures that there exists
\overline{R}_{1}\geq R_{1}
such that
\varphi(\overline{R}_{1})\leq 1+\delta
and, from what we have proved before,
\varphi(R)\leq 1+\delta
for all
R\geq\overline{R}_{1}
.
Assume now
\varphi(R)<1-\delta
for all
R\geq R_{1}
. Hence, from (
25
), we find

\begin{split}\varphi^{\prime}(R)&=R(1-\varphi(R))+E(R)\varphi(R)>\delta R+E(R)\varphi(R)>\delta R-\delta\varphi(R)\\
&>\delta R-\delta(1-\delta).\end{split}

Integrating again on
[R_{1},R]
the previous inequality, we find

\varphi(R)\geq\varphi(R_{1})+(R-R_{1})\left[\frac{\delta}{2}(R+R_{1})-\delta(1-\delta)\right]\rightarrow+\infty,

which contradicts the fact
\varphi(R)\leq M
. Thus, there exists
\overline{R}_{2}\geq R_{1}
such that
\varphi(\overline{R}_{2})\geq 1-\delta
and, from what we have proved,
\varphi(R)\geq 1-\delta
for all
R\geq\overline{R}_{2}
. Finally, we have found that, for all
R\geq\max\{\overline{R}_{1},\,\overline{R}_{2}\}
, it holds

1-\delta\leq\varphi(R)\leq 1+\delta.

Since
\delta
was arbitrary, we can conclude that
\varphi(R)\rightarrow 1
as
R\rightarrow+\infty
. To finish the proof, observe that the latter and (
24
) imply that

v^{\prime}(R)=1-\frac{1+h^{\prime}(R)}{h(R)}v(R)=1-\frac{1+h^{\prime}(R)}{Rh(R)}\varphi(R)\rightarrow 1-1=0

as
R\rightarrow+\infty
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
h
be defined as in (
20

h^{\prime}(R)>0
.

Proof.

We first recall that, from (
23
), we have
h^{\prime}(R)=1-h(R)p(R)
. We distinguish three cases.

Case 1:

n=1
. In this case we have
p(R)=-R<0
, while
h(R)>0
for all
R>0
, then
h^{\prime}(R)=1-h(R)p(R)>0
for all
R>0
.

Case 2:

n\geq 2
and
R\geq\sqrt{n-1}
. In this case we have

p(R)=\frac{n-1}{R}-R\leq\sqrt{n-1}-R\leq 0,

while
h(R)>0
for all
R>0
, then
h^{\prime}(R)=1-h(R)p(R)>0
for all
R>0
.

Case 3:

n\geq 2
and
0<R<\sqrt{n-1}
. Now we have
p(R),\,h(R)>0
for all
R>0
. We first remark that, looking at the expansion of
h(R)
, for
R\sim 0
, in the proof of the previous lemma, we have

\lim_{R\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{\prime}(R)=\lim_{R\rightarrow 0^{+}}(1-h(R)p(R))=\lim_{R\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(1-\left(\frac{n-1}{R}-R\right)\frac{R}{n}\right)=1-\frac{n-1}{n}>0.

Assume that there exists
R_{0}\in(0,\sqrt{n-1})
such that

h^{\prime}(R)>0\quad\forall\,R\in(0,R_{0})\quad\text{ and }\quad h^{\prime}(R_{0})=0.

Thus
h^{\prime\prime}(R_{0})\leq 0
, and the latter gives us

0\geq h^{\prime\prime}(R_{0})=-h^{\prime}(R_{0})p(R_{0})-h(R_{0})p^{\prime}(R_{0})=-h(R_{0})p^{\prime}(R_{0}),

where in the last equality we have used
h^{\prime}(R_{0})=0
. Then, we find
h(R_{0})p^{\prime}(R_{0})\geq 0
, that is a contradiction since

p^{\prime}(R)=-\frac{n-1}{R^{2}}-1<0,

(27)

and
h(R)>0
.

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that for some
R^{*}>0
, it holds
v^{\prime\prime}(R^{*})=0
, then

v^{\prime}(R^{*})=\frac{2h^{\prime}(R^{*})+p^{\prime}(R^{*})h(R^{*})^{2}}{(1+h^{\prime}(R^{*}))^{2}+2h^{\prime}(R^{*})+p^{\prime}(R^{*})h(R^{*})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Proof.

We start by recalling that

v(R)=\frac{J(R)}{h(R)I(R)}.

Now, differentiating, since
1+h^{\prime}(R)=2-p(R)h(R)
, we find

v^{\prime}(R)=\frac{J^{\prime}(R)}{h(R)I(R)}-\frac{J(R)(h(R)I(R))^{\prime}}{h(R)^{2}I(R)^{2}}=1-\frac{(1+h^{\prime}(R))v(R)}{h(R)}=1-\frac{2v(R)}{h(R)}+p(R)v(R).

(28)

Differentiating again in (
28
) we have

v^{\prime\prime}(R)=v^{\prime}(R)\left(p(R)-\frac{2}{h(R)}\right)+v(R)\left(\frac{2h^{\prime}(R)}{h(R)^{2}}+p^{\prime}(R)\right).

(29)

Assume that there exists
R^{*}>0
such that
v^{\prime\prime}(R^{*})=0
. Taking into account that
p(R)h(R)=1-h^{\prime}(R)
, we get from (
29
) that

v^{\prime}(R^{*})=\frac{v(R^{*})\left(\frac{2h^{\prime}(R^{*})}{h(R^{*})}+p^{\prime}(R^{*})h(R^{*})\right)}{1+h^{\prime}(R^{*})}.

(30)

Setting

w(R)=\frac{v(R)}{h(R)},\quad A(R)=2h^{\prime}(R)+p^{\prime}(R)h(R)^{2},\quad s(R)=1+h^{\prime}(R),

(
30
) becomes

v^{\prime}(R^{*})=\frac{w(R^{*})A(R^{*})}{s(R^{*})}.

Moreover, from (
28
), we have
v^{\prime}(R)=1-s(R)w(R)
. Combining the previous two identities we get

w(R^{*})=\frac{s(R^{*})}{A(R^{*})+s(R^{*})^{2}}.

(31)

Therefore

v^{\prime}(R^{*})=1-s(R^{*})w(R^{*})=\frac{A(R^{*})}{A(R^{*})+s(R^{*})^{2}}.

(32)

By Lemma
4.3
, we have
s(R)=1+h^{\prime}(R)>0
, and

. Since (

, then it must be

and (

and

\displaystyle s(R^{*})^{2}-2A(R^{*})

\displaystyle=(1+h^{\prime}(R^{*}))^{2}-2(2h^{\prime}(R^{*})+p^{\prime}(R^{*})h(R^{*})^{2})

Appendix

We now give the proof of Proposition
1.5
. To do this, we need the following elementary lemma. A proof is included for the sake of completeness.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt\geq\frac{s}{s^{2}+1}e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}.

Proof.

Consider the function

f(t)=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\frac{t}{t^{2}+1}.

Then the first derivative is given by

f^{\prime}(t)=-e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\frac{t^{4}+2t^{2}-1}{(t^{2}+1)}.

Since

\frac{t^{4}+2t^{2}-1}{(t^{2}+1)}=1-\frac{2}{(t^{2}+1)^{2}}\leq 1,

we can infer that

-f^{\prime}(t)=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\frac{t^{4}+2t^{2}-1}{(t^{2}+1)}\leq e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Integrating the latter we get

\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt\geq\int_{s}^{+\infty}-f^{\prime}(t)\,dt=f(s)-\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}f(t).

Since
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}f(t)=0
, the claim is proved.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.5
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the half-space

H_{s}=\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,x_{1}\geq s\}.

We recall that (see
[
20
, §2.3]
) the Gaussian torsional rigidity of
H_{s}
is given by

T_{\gamma}(s):=T_{\gamma}(H_{s})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\left(\int_{t}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}}\,d\tau\right)^{2}\,dt.

We thus want to show that, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(s)\geq 0
. A straightforward computation gives

\begin{split}T_{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\left(\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt\right)^{2},\\
T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(s)&=-\frac{se^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt.\end{split}

Hence,
T_{\gamma}^{\prime\prime}(s)\geq 0
if and only if

2-se^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt\geq 0.

The latter is clearly true when
s\leq 0
. Then, given

f(s)=se^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt,

we need to show that
f(s)\leq 2
. Indeed, we will prove that
f(s)\leq 1
by using the following two facts:

•

f
is monotonically increasing,

•

f(s)\leq\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}f(s)=1.

On the one hand, we get from Lemma
A.1

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(s)&=e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt+s^{2}e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\int_{s}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt-s\\
&\geq\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}+\frac{s^{3}}{1+s^{2}}-s=0.\end{split}

On the other hand, using for instance L’Hôpital’s rule

\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}f(s)=\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{-e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}}{-e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}-s^{2}e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}}}s^{2}e^{s^{2}}=\lim_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{s^{2}}{1+s^{2}}=1.

as we wanted to see.
∎
