Antioxidant and anti-senescence supramolecular complexes for managing dry eye diseases
Authors: Xiaohui Song, Jiayan Zhu, Rouye Wang, Yili Yao, Tinglian Zhou, Wenbin Dai, Xianglin He, Qiao Jin, Ke Yao, Haijie Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDry eye disease (DED) is a common ocular disorder that severely impairs daily life. Oxidative stress has been identified as a key pathogenic factor driving cellular senescence and corneal epithelial damage in DED. Herein, we developed a biocompatible supramolecular complex based on the host-guest interaction of TEMPO-modified β-cyclodextrin (TCD) and puerarin (Pue), designated as P@TCD, for managing DED. The P@TCD could mitigate the ROS accumulation, reduce DNA damage, promote cell cycle progression, and attenuate cellular senescence in the corneal epithelium, thus preventing the further deterioration of DED. In two DED mouse models, P@TCD demonstrated favorable therapeutic efficacy by reversing corneal epithelial defects, alleviating conjunctival damage, and accelerating lacrimal gland recovery, thereby restoring tear film homeostasis, especially improving tear film stability with TBUT values of more than 5.3 s, compared to the DED models of less than 2.8 s, which is even more than the Cycloome® of 3.7 s. These results suggested the considerable potential of this dual ROS scavenging strategy for the effective treatment of DED.Keywords: Dry eye diseases, Reactive oxygen species, Cellular senescence, Supramolecular complexes, Puerarin

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a chronic, multifactorial disorder of the ocular surface with a global prevalence exceeding 30%, which significantly impairs daily life[[1], [2], [3]]. The primary manifestations of DED include tear film instability and ocular surface dysfunction, accompanied by visual disturbances, inflammation, and neurosensory abnormalities [4,5]. The conventional treatments for DED include artificial tears, anti-inflammatory agents, immunosuppressants, and surgical interventions [6]. However, these therapeutic options are associated with delayed onset of action, limited duration of efficacy, and potential adverse effects [7]. Therefore, the development of therapeutic strategies for the safe and effective treatment of DED is urgently needed.

As a prevalent and multifactorial disorder of the ocular surface, DED exhibits a strong age-associated prevalence, with both incidence and severity increasing markedly in the elderly, indicating the potential contribution of senescent cell accumulation on the ocular surface to the pathogenesis of DED [8,9]. Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a key mechanism underlying both cellular senescence and the development of DED [10,11]. Specifically, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can induce DNA damage and elicit cell cycle arrest, leading to cellular senescence, which subsequently exacerbates corneal epithelial dysfunction, reduces conjunctival goblet cell density, disrupts lacrimal gland structure, and damages tear film stability, thereby contributing to the vicious cycle of DED[[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Therefore, scavenging excessive ROS to attenuate cellular senescence may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for ameliorating DED [16].

Recently, studies have demonstrated that extracted components of traditional Chinese medicine exhibit beneficial therapeutic effects in the treatment of oxidative stress-related diseases, such as arthritis, acute lung injury, and DED [17,18]. Among them, Puerarin (Pue), a flavonoid compound extracted from the dried root of Pueraria lobata, exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering effects [19]. Studies have demonstrated that Pue can enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thereby protecting cells against oxidative stress–induced apoptosis [20]. Moreover, Pue exhibited promising therapeutic potential in the treatment of inflammation-related diseases by modulating apoptosis and cellular senescence pathways [21]. These properties make it a promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of DED [22,23]. However, its poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability limit its further clinical application[[24], [25], [26]].

Cyclodextrins (CDs) have been widely used as pharmaceutical excipients in numerous FDA-approved drug products, including Sporanox®, Captisol®, and Cereni® [27]. Their unique molecular structure, characterized by a hydrophilic exterior and a hydrophobic internal cavity, enables the formation of inclusion complexes with a wide range of poorly water-soluble active pharmaceutical ingredients, thereby enhancing the aqueous solubility and bioavailability while reducing toxicity and adverse effects [28]. Among native CDs, β-CD has emerged as an ideal inclusion carrier due to its moderate cavity size, high encapsulation efficiency, and relatively low cost [29]. Furthermore, chemical modification with diverse functional groups can endow CDs with various extra performances, including enhanced antioxidant properties [30].

Herein, we developed a supramolecular complex via the self-assembly of antioxidant TEMPO-functionalized β-CD (TCD) with the hydrophobic drug Pue, formulated as eye drops (P@TCD) for the treatment of DED (Fig. 1). The supramolecular complexes P@TCD exhibit robust and broad-spectrum free radical scavenging activity and favorable biocompatibility. The antioxidant effect of P@TCD was evaluated using H2O2-stimulated human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs). Additionally, the potential mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of P@TCD was investigated using transcriptomics, immunofluorescence staining, and RT-qPCR. Furthermore, its practical feasibility was evaluated in two typical DED mouse models. Taken together, this work demonstrated a supramolecular complex based on P@TCD, which provides a promising strategy for TEMPO-functionalized β-CD in ocular drug delivery and dry eye treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials: β-cyclodextrin-NH2 (β-CD-NH2) was purchased from Zhiyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shandong, China). TEMPO and Puerarin (Pue) were obtained from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. DPPH assay kit and hydroxyl radical assay kit were obtained from Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). MilliQ Water 18.2 (MΩ/cm) was obtained using a Millipore MilliQ Academic Water Purification System. All other solvents and chemicals were used without further purification and of analytical purity.

Synthesis of TCD: TCD was prepared through a simple synthetic route, as shown in Fig S1. Briefly, 10 mL of DMF with TEMPO (0.3 mM) and EDC/NHS (0.33 mM) was stirred for 4 h at room temperature to activate carboxyl groups. Then β-CD-NH2 (0.3 mM) in 7 mL of DMF was added dropwise and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The resultant solution was precipitated in ice acetonitrile three times and dried in a vacuum to obtain TCD (DMSO-d6 for 1H NMR).

Preparation and characterization of supramolecular inclusion complex: P@TCD was prepared via the host-guest interaction between TCD and Pue. Pue (2 mM) was dissolved in 2 mL of H2O. After that, TCD (2 mM) dissolved in 2 mL of H2O was dropped and stirred intensely for 24 h. The resultant solution was dialyzed (MWCO 1000 Da) against water for 48 h to obtain supernatant. The concentration of TEMPO and Pue was determined by UV-vis (UV-2550, Shimadzu).

Preparation of FITC-labelled P@FCD: Briefly, FITC-labelled cyclodextrin was first synthesized via the reaction between FITC-NHS ester and amino-functionalized cyclodextrin (CD-NH2). Subsequently, P@FCD was prepared by co-assembling Pue with a mixture of CD and 1% (molar ratio) FITC-labelled cyclodextrin under the same conditions as P@TCD, ensuring that the labeling process does not affect the supramolecular assembly behavior.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurement: 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) serves to capture the •OH radicals produced through the Fenton reaction. TEMPO and TCD were introduced into a solution containing H2O2, Fe2+, and DMPO, and the mixture was allowed to react for 1 min. The characteristic EPR signal corresponding to •OH was then recorded using an ESR spectrometer (A300, Bruker) [31].

Free radical scavenging evaluation: The free radical scavenging evaluation of P@TCD was detected by the DPPH assay kit and the hydroxyl radical detection kit. Briefly, DPPH working solution containing DPPH in absolute ethanol was prepared and mixed well with 400 μM TEMPO, TCD, Pue, and P@TCD. The reaction was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance of each reaction solution was measured at 517 nm using UV-vis (UV-2550, Shimadzu). Moreover, the •OH working solution, prepared according to the manufacturer's protocols, was thoroughly mixed with the sample using a vortex mixer, then incubated at 37°C for 1 min, and the colour developer was immediately added to stop the reaction. The samples were measured at 550 nm using UV-Vis (UV-2550, Shimadzu) after incubation at room temperature for 20 min. Similarly, the free radical scavenging evaluation of different concentrations (0, 100, 200, 400, 800 μM) of P@TCD was detected following the steps above.

Measurement of intracellular ROS: HCECs were cultivated with 400 μM H2O2 to simulate an oxidative stress state. To evaluate the intracellular ROS scavenging property of P@TCD in an oxidative stress microenvironment, HCECs were cultured at a density of 4 × 104 cells/well in a 24-well plate and treated with P@TCD and H2O2. The fluorescence probe DCFH-DA was then added to the cells and incubated for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. After washing with PBS three times, fluorescence images of each group were captured with an inverted fluorescent microscope.

SA-β-Gal Assay: Detection of SA-β-gal was performed according to the manufacturer's protocols with the Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (C0602, Beyotime, China). Briefly, the cells were fixed with fixation buffer for 15 min at room temperature and then stained with 1 mL of preprepared staining solution at 37°C overnight. Fluorescence images were acquired with a light microscope, and the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was quantified by using ImageJ.

In vivo biosafety assessment: C57BL/6 mice (male, 6–8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center, Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences. The potential ocular or body irritation caused by Pue, TCD, and P@TCD was evaluated by monitoring the eye surface with the slit lamp. Five μL of 10 mM Pue, TCD, and P@TCD were initially instilled in the right eyes of each mouse twice a day for 28 consecutive days. Ocular irritation symptoms, including clinical signs of redness, swelling, discharge, conjunctival congestion, iris abnormalities, and corneal lesions, were recorded at 1, 24, 48, 72 h, and 28 d after the first instillation of Pue, TCD, and P@TCD. The OCT images were acquired by securing anesthetized mice on the OCT instrument (Micron IV, Phoenix Research) and capturing images as the probe scanned the central area of the corneas, enabling observation of changes in the corneal structure and anterior chamber. The long-term safety of P@TCD to the cornea at 28 d was evaluated by H&E staining.

Precorneal retention evaluation: To evaluate the ocular surface retention time of P@TCD, fluorescence intensity differences in mouse eyes were quantified using an IVIS Lumina imaging system at specified time points (1, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 min post-administration) with excitation/emission wavelengths of 488 nm/525 nm. To avoid potential confounding effects of anesthesia, mice were euthanized at predetermined time points (2, 25 min) following topical application without anesthesia, and their eyeballs were immediately enucleated for frozen section preparation. For cryosection imaging, equivalent concentrations of FITC and FITC-labelled P@FCD were applied to the corneal surfaces of mice. Eyeballs were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (4583, SAKURA, Japan) and snap-frozen on dry ice. Thin slices of 10 μm thickness were obtained using a cryostat and then sealed with DAPI. A fluorescence microscope was used to observe the resulting images.

Establishment of dry eye disease models: BAK and SCOP were used to set the DED mouse model. Briefly, each eye of male C57BL/6 mice was administered with 5 μL of 0.2% (w/v) BAK eye drops twice per day for 7 consecutive days, while the other mice were set as the normal group without administration. At the same time, each eye of male C57BL/6 mice received subcutaneous injections of 200 μL of 0.25% (w/v) SCOP four times daily for 14 consecutive days, while the other mice served as the normal group. Subsequently, DED mice in each model were randomly assigned to five treatment groups and received topical instillation of 5 μL of saline, 10 mM TCD, Pue, P@TCD, or Cycloome® three times daily for seven consecutive days in the BAK-induced DED model and twice daily for three days in the SCOP-induced DED model.

Therapeutic efficacy assessment: To evaluate the therapeutic effects of P@TCD against DED in vivo, a series of clinical assessments was performed to monitor tear volume, TBUT, and ocular surface morphology via fluorescein sodium staining. These measurements were conducted at baseline (D 0, after model establishment) and on days 1, 3, and 5 (D1, D3, and D5) during the treatment period. For fluorescein staining, 2 μL of 1% (w/v) fluorescein sodium was instilled into the right eye, followed by gentle eyelid closure 5 times to ensure uniform dye distribution. Stained corneas were imaged using a slit lamp under cobalt blue illumination, and fluorescein scores were quantified for statistical analysis. Corneal fluorescein staining was evaluated using a standardized scoring system (0–4) across four quadrants: 0 (absent), 1 (slightly punctate staining with <30 spots), 2 (punctate staining with >30 spots), 3 (severe diffuse staining), and 4 (presence of fluorescein plaques). Three representative areas were assessed for each eye. Tear secretion was quantified using Schirmer's test, where a standardized cotton thread was placed in the lateral one-third of the lower conjunctival fornix for 15 s. The wetted length (mm) was measured with a precision ruler, and triplicate measurements from the right eyes of each group were averaged for analysis. Tear film stability was assessed by measuring TBUT. Following instillation of 2 μL of 1% fluorescein sodium, the interval (in seconds) until the first appearance of a dark spot in the tear film was recorded under cobalt blue illumination using a slit lamp. Triplicate measurements from the right eyes were averaged for statistical analysis.

Histopathology: After different treatments, C57BL/6 male mice were euthanized, and the eyes, conjunctiva, and lacrimal glands of each mouse were processed into H&E or PAS sections.

Determination of oxidative stress levels, anti-aging, anti-apoptosis, and anti-inflammatory effects of P@TCD: ROS levels in tissues were measured using a dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescent probe. Briefly, after frozen sectioning of the eyes, the sections were stained with DHE (S0064, Beyotime, China) for 30 min, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI. The anti-apoptotic effect was measured using the TUNEL in situ apoptosis kit (E-CK-A322, Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Anti-aging and anti-inflammatory effects were measured by immunofluorescence staining. All stained sections were examined for staining characteristics and imaged using CLSM. The fluorescence intensity in the images was quantified using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis: The results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an independent-sample t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The difference was considered statistically significant at ∗p < 0.05, highly significant at ∗∗p < 0.01, and not significant (ns) where p > 0.05.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of P@TCD

The supramolecular complexes were strategically designed and prepared based on the host-guest interaction of antioxidant TEMPO-functionalized β-CD, TCD, and the hydrophobic drug Pue to achieve robust and broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging functions. In this system, β-CD served as the self-assembly unit to form a nanoscale scaffold, while TEMPO was covalently introduced as a stable radical species for ROS scavenging. The Pue was further embedded to potentiate the antioxidant activity of TEMPO (Fig. 2a andS2). The successful synthesis of TCD was characterized by 1H NMR and mass spectrometry. As shown in Fig. 2b and c, the three peaks at 6.8, 7.2, and 7.3 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum correspond to TEMPO, and the strongest ion peak at 1445.63 in the mass spectrum is in perfect agreement with the theoretical molecular weight, further confirming the effective incorporation and indicating that one TEMPO molecule is grafted onto each CD. Subsequently, to verify the formation of supramolecular assemblies between Pue and TCD, the interactions among different groups within the nanoparticles were investigated through 2D NOESY NMR. As shown in Fig. 2d, a pronounced cross-peak was observed between the aromatic proton of Pue at 7.8 ppm and the proton region corresponding to the CD cavity at 3–6 ppm, suggesting close spatial proximity and host–guest recognition. This non-covalent interaction likely drives the assembly of the hybrid nanosystem. Additionally, the characteristic absorption peak of P@TCD was revealed through UV–Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2e). P@TCD exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 365 nm for TEMPO, indicating that the inclusion complex contains TCD. Meanwhile, Pue exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 305 nm, which redshifted to 312 nm upon host–guest assembly into P@TCD [32]. The particle size and morphology of P@TCD were also characterized by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM). The results indicated that P@TCD forms ultrasmall nanoparticles with an average size of approximately 4 nm (Fig. S3). Moreover, we have performed in vitro drug release experiments to evaluate the release behavior of P@TCD with a drug loading capacity of 22.9%. The results showed a sustained release profile, with a drug release of 39.1% within the first 2 h and approximately 50.2% cumulative release within 24 h (Fig. S4).

Furthermore, the antioxidant capacity of P@TCD was evaluated by the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analysis and free radical scavenging assays. As shown in Fig. 2f, the control group exhibited a characteristic strong EPR signal with a red multiple peak profile, indicating a high concentration of generated •OH radicals with minimal scavenging activity. In contrast, the •OH signal intensity decreased sharply in the presence of P@TCD, indicating the high efficiency of P@TCD for •OH scavenging. The free radical scavenging ability was further assessed using a •DPPH assay kit and •OH assay kit. As shown in Fig. 2g and h, the P@TCD demonstrated a significantly enhanced scavenging capacity of •DPPH and •OH compared to TCD and Pue. A more in-depth analysis demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in scavenging efficiency, with scavenging rates reaching 57.69% for •DPPH and 68.19% for •OH at the highest concentration of 800 μM (Fig. 2i and j). These results collectively demonstrated that P@TCD effectively scavenged various free radicals, suggesting notable antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential.

Cellular anti-oxidation and anti-apoptotic effects of P@TCD

Excellent biocompatibility is a fundamental prerequisite for the application of P@TCD as an eye-drop formulation [33]. Thus, the biocompatibility of P@TCD was conducted using the CCK-8 test and live/dead cell staining. As shown in Fig. S5, the viability of HCECs remained above 90% when treated with P@TCD at concentrations ranging from 0 to 1000 μM, indicating that P@TCD exhibited almost no cytotoxicity toward HCECs and possessed excellent biocompatibility. In addition, the fluorescent imaging showed that HCECs maintained high viability across various concentrations of P@TCD, further supporting the excellent biocompatibility of P@TCD (Fig. S6).

Given the well-established association between DED and a high oxidative stress environment, the ROS scavenging capacity of P@TCD was then assessed using the DCFH-DA assay. As shown in Fig. 3a and b, HCECs exhibited an obvious green fluorescence signal under H2O2 stimulation, indicating a notable increase in ROS levels. In contrast, pretreatment with TEMPO, TCD, Pue, and P@TCD significantly reduced fluorescence intensity, indicating effective scavenging of ROS. Notably, the P@TCD group exhibited the weakest fluorescence signal among all intervention groups, with quantitative fluorescence intensity reduced to approximately 0.97-fold of the normal group, which was significantly lower than that of the H2O2 group (1.43-fold of normal) and demonstrated superior efficacy compared to both TEMPO-alone and Pue-alone treatments. This superior efficacy can be mechanistically attributed to the synergistic antioxidant effects of Pue and TCD. The stable nitroxyl radical TEMPO acted as a potent superoxide dismutase mimetic, catalytically converting O2−• into H2O2 and O2, while the natural flavonoid Pue not only directly scavenges hydroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen through its phenolic hydroxyl groups but also upregulates endogenous antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase [34]. Furthermore, the TCD component enhances cellular uptake and bioavailability, thereby promoting ROS scavenging in the cytoplasm [30].

Subsequently, apoptosis detection in HCECs under H2O2 stimulation was performed using flow cytometry analysis following different treatments (Fig. 3c and d). In comparison with HCECs exposed to H2O2 alone, the P@TCD group exhibited a progressive reduction in the early apoptosis rate (37.9% to the H2O2 group and 31.9% to the P@TCD group) and late apoptosis rate (22.4% to the H2O2 group and 14.0% to the P@TCD group). Moreover, the anti-apoptotic effect of P@TCD on HCECs was further evaluated by TUNEL staining [35]. As shown in Fig. 3e and f, a marked increase in red fluorescence was observed in the H2O2 group, indicating extensive DNA fragmentation and a high level of apoptosis as hallmarks of oxidative damage. In contrast, cells pretreated with P@TCD exhibited a significantly attenuated apoptotic response, with fluorescence intensity reduced by nearly 36.7% compared to the H2O2 group. Meanwhile, the P@TCD group exhibited reduced red fluorescence compared to both the Pue and TCD groups, indicating that the combinatorial formulation of P@TCD exerts a more potent cytoprotective effect. These findings further confirmed that the synergistic effect of Pue and TCD conferred enhanced protection against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in HCECs.

Since the normal function of mitochondria is essential for maintaining cellular physiological functions and internal environmental homeostasis, a decline in mitochondrial membrane potential can disrupt mitochondrial function, thereby initiating the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis [36,37]. The mitochondrial membrane potential was then assessed using the JC-1 assay (Fig. 3g and h). The H2O2 group showed an increased level of JC-1 monomers with green fluorescence and a reduced level of JC-1 aggregates with red fluorescence, indicating ROS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. In contrast, treatment with P@TCD reversed this effect, suggesting functional recovery of mitochondria from ROS-induced damage. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the P@TCD possessed comprehensive antioxidant capabilities, effectively protecting HCECs from ROS-induced oxidative damage and mitochondrial apoptosis, thereby highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy for oxidative stress-associated ocular surface disorders.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

To systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying P@TCD-mediated protection of HCECs against oxidative stress-induced damage, transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on the H2O2-treated and P@TCD-treated groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the cellular secretome showed that replicates clustered distinctly, indicating high intra-group consistency (Fig. 4a). As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 4b), P@TCD treatment led to 715 significantly upregulated and 735 significantly downregulated genes compared with the H2O2 group.

Subsequently, KEGG analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle, cellular senescence, apoptosis, p53 signaling, FoxO signaling, and TNF signaling (Fig. 4c). These results strongly suggested that P@TCD exerts its protective effects through coordinated modulation of multiple signaling pathways, particularly by promoting cell cycle reactivation and inhibiting senescence-associated signaling. Moreover, GO enrichment analysis was performed to further define the biological processes associated with the DEGs (Fig. 4d). The results showed significant enrichment in pathways related to injury and repair, including response to oxidative stress, DNA damage response, p53-mediated signal transduction, cell cycle checkpoint signaling, and cellular senescence.

To visualize the regulatory impact of transcriptomic changes, a heatmap of key genes involved in cell cycle and senescence pathways was generated. As shown in Fig. 4e, the H2O2-treated group displayed a pro-senescence transcriptional profile, characterized by downregulation of cell cycle–promoting genes and upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors. Notably, P@TCD treatment reversed this pattern, restoring a pro-proliferative and anti-senescent gene expression signature. GSEA further supported these findings (Fig. 4f and g), showing significant enrichment of gene sets related to nucleotide metabolism and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton in the P@TCD group, indicating enhanced cellular resilience under oxidative stress.

Overall, transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that P@TCD effectively mitigates oxidative stress-induced cellular damage through comprehensive remodeling of the gene expression profile in HCECs, specifically by suppressing signaling pathways implicated in cellular senescence and reactivating cell cycle-related genes (Fig. 4h).

Anti-cellular senescence effect of P@TCD

Oxidative stress serves as a critical mechanistic link between the pathogenesis of DED and the senescence of HCECs [38]. The DED microenvironment, characterized by hyperosmolarity and chronic inflammation, induces excessive generation of ROS and causes persistent oxidative damage to cellular components, thereby initiating and maintaining cellular senescence [39]. In turn, senescent cells secrete pro-inflammatory factors through the SASP, exacerbating local inflammation and tissue dysfunction and ultimately driving the chronic progression of DED [40,41]. To investigate whether such an oxidative microenvironment, particularly the accumulation of excessive ROS, affects cell fate, we examined established biomarkers of cell cycle and cellular senescence, including p16, CCND1, RBL1, γ-H2AX, and SA-β-gal.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that H2O2-treated cells exhibited a significantly increased fluorescence intensity for p16 with 1.57-fold (Fig. 5a and b) and a decreased fluorescence intensity for CCND1 to 78.08% (Fig. 5c and d) relative to the normal group, consistent with the induction of a senescent phenotype. Besides, γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity was also elevated in the H2O2-treated group (1.82-fold relative to the normal group), indicating DNA damage in HCECs (Fig. 5e and f). These adverse effects were substantially reversed in the TCD and P@TCD treatment groups, with P@TCD demonstrating a more pronounced protective effect. Furthermore, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, a widely used biomarker for identifying senescent cells, was also quantified. As shown in Fig. 5g and h, the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells increased markedly from approximately 8.16% in the normal group to 40.62% in the H2O2-treated group. In contrast, P@TCD treatment significantly reduced this proportion to 9.29%, underscoring its potent anti-senescence efficacy. At the transcriptional level, RT-qPCR results confirmed that p16 and RBL1 mRNA levels were elevated by approximately 5.84-fold and 1.53-fold in the H2O2-treated group compared to the normal controls (Fig. 5i k, Table S1), reinforcing activation of the p16INK4A-Rb senescence pathway. Moreover, CCND1 expression was significantly suppressed under H2O2 stress, with the mRNA level downregulated by approximately 64.88% (Fig. 5j). Notably, P@TCD treatment reduced the p16 and RBL1 mRNA levels and restored the CCND1 mRNA level to near-normal levels, indicating recovery of cell cycle progression. Taken together, these results demonstrated that P@TCD effectively counteracts H2O2-induced cellular senescence by mitigating DNA damage and modulating key regulators of cell cycle arrest.

Biosafety and preocular retention assessment of P@TCD

The development of new ophthalmic formulations requires a comprehensive evaluation of their ocular tolerance and safety profile before assessing therapeutic efficacy [42,43]. Thus, we evaluated the safety of P@TCD as an eye-drop formulation in mice. The status of the cornea and limbus, along with sodium fluorescein staining, was examined using a slit lamp, and corneal thickness was assessed by optical coherence tomography (OCT). As shown in Fig. 6a and S7, no obvious differences in corneal status were found in the Pue, TCD, and P@TCD groups. In addition, sodium fluorescein staining yielded negative results, indicating the absence of ocular surface irritation after P@TCD administration. Besides, OCT imaging revealed no obvious changes in corneal thickness after treatment with P@TCD eye drops (Fig. 6bandS8). Furthermore, the H&E staining images indicated that there were no appreciable differences in cornea morphology between treated and normal mice (Fig. S9).

In ocular drug administration, a considerable amount of the administered drug is rapidly cleared from the precorneal area before exerting its therapeutic effects. Therefore, prolonging residence time on the ocular surface, such as by increasing the formulation concentration, may serve as a key strategy to enhance drug bioavailability. In vivo fluorescence imaging (Fig. 6c) demonstrated that FITC-labelled P@FCD exhibits significantly enhanced ocular retention compared with free FITC solution over time. Specifically, at 25 min post-administration in anesthetized mice, the residual fluorescence intensity of P@FCD remained substantially higher (67.23 ± 2.51%) than that of free FITC (54.9 ± 5.46%) (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, fluorescence images of ocular cryosections also revealed a similar trend (Fig. 6e and f). In conscious mice, fluorescence intensity measurements from ocular cryosections at 25 min after eye drop administration revealed significantly higher values for P@FCD compared with free FITC.

The therapeutic effect of P@TCD in benzalkonium chloride (BAK)-Induced DED mouse models

As shown in Fig. 7a, the DED mouse model was established through topical administration of BAK, which induces excessive corneal epithelial damage, tear film instability, and corneal inflammation [44]. Subsequently, model mice were randomly assigned to five groups and treated with saline, TCD, Pue, P@TCD, and Cycloome®. The recovery of corneal epithelial cells was assessed using cornea fluorescence staining, where the lack of intact epithelium could be stained. After continuously administering BAK for 7 days, the corneas exhibited strong fluorescence intensity, indicating successful establishment of the DED mouse model (Fig. 7b). Meanwhile, corneal defects with green fluorescein staining remained prominent in the saline group. In contrast, mice treated with P@TCD showed the lowest fluorescence staining intensity among all treatment groups. A scoring system was subsequently implemented to quantitatively assess corneal epithelial defects. Corneal staining in DED mice treated with saline was scored at 6.8 ± 0.8, with TCD at 4.2 ± 1.9, Pue at 5.2 ± 1.2, Cycloome® at 2.6 ± 0.9, and P@TCD at 2.2 ± 1.3, representing the lowest corneal staining score among all treatment groups (Fig. 7c andS10). In addition to fluorescein staining, the assessment of DED symptoms included tear-related evaluations such as clinical assessment of tear stability via tear break-up time (TBUT) and tear secretion measurement (Schirmer test). As shown in Fig. 7d and S11, the saline group exhibited the least improvement in TBUT, with a value of 2.23 ± 0.68 s. Pue and TCD demonstrated limited therapeutic effects on tear film stability, yielding TBUT values of 3.70 ± 0.96 s and 4.60 ± 0.63 s, respectively. Cycloome® demonstrated greater efficacy than Pue and TCD, with a TBUT of 5.20 ± 0.79 s. The P@TCD group achieved the highest TBUT value of 5.37 ± 0.67 s, showing significantly better tear film stability compared to the Cycloome® group, indicating the superior therapeutic benefit of P@TCD. We next performed the Schirmer test to evaluate tear secretion. As shown in Fig. 7e and S12, the average tear volume in the normal group was 6.46 ± 1.00 mm, whereas in the saline-treated group, it increased from 0.63 ± 0.41 mm to 3.28 ± 0.57 mm, indicating partial alleviation of dryness in the DED mouse model. Mice treated with Pue and TCD exhibited higher tear volumes of 3.82 ± 0.63 mm and 4.39 ± 0.68 mm, respectively. P@TCD and Cycloome® demonstrated the most pronounced therapeutic effects, with tear volumes of 5.80 ± 1.01 mm and 6.70 ± 0.62 mm, respectively, comparable to those of the normal group.

To further investigate the ability of P@TCD to restore ocular surface morphology, hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining were adopted. As shown in Fig. 7f, the H&E staining revealed a marked reduction in corneal epithelial thickness in the saline group. In contrast, treatment with TCD, Pue, P@TCD, and Cycloome® resulted in restoration of anterior corneal surface morphology. As observed in the saline group, the corneal epithelium was damaged by BAK, resulting in irregular cell arrangement, whereas a more organized multilayered epithelium and increased corneal epithelial thickness were evident in the TCD, Pue, P@TCD, and Cycloome® groups. In addition to corneal epithelial damage, goblet cell loss is also an important histological hallmark of DED. Only a small number of goblet cells were detected in the Saline group. Following topical administration of TCD, Pue, and Cycloome®, the number of goblet cells increased, with the largest goblet cell area observed in the P@TCD group. Based on the above data, we conclude that P@TCD effectively alleviates ocular surface damage in the BAK-induced DED models.

To investigate the underlying mechanism of P@TCD in the treatment of the BAK-induced DED model, immunofluorescence staining was further performed (Fig. 7g–k, S13). In the corneal epithelium of the DED mouse model, a marked increase in ROS was detected, indicating significant oxidative stress that serves as a primary instigator of cellular damage. Therapeutic intervention in the P@TCD group effectively scavenged these excessive ROS, reducing ROS levels by 60.7% relative to the DED group, thereby mitigating this core pathogenic trigger. The expression of p16, a key cell cycle regulator and established biomarker of cellular senescence, was significantly upregulated in the saline group, with levels increased by 1.50-fold compared to the normals. This upregulation was markedly suppressed following P@TCD treatment, showing a 68.7% reduction to 1.03-fold of normal, indicating that P@TCD alleviated oxidative stress-induced cell cycle arrest and attenuated the senescence program. Furthermore, TUNEL staining, which detected DNA fragmentation characteristic of apoptotic cell death, revealed increased fluorescence intensity in the DED group, with a 1.83-fold increase compared to the normal group. The 54.9% reduction of fluorescence intensity in TUNEL-positive cells following P@TCD treatment demonstrates its efficacy in protecting corneal epithelial cells from oxidative stress-driven apoptosis. The expression of DNA-damage biomarker γ-H2AX was increased by 1.26-fold in the saline group and decreased by 69.8% in the treatment of P@TCD. And the expression of cell-cycle-related CCND1 was decreased by 79.8% in the saline group and increased by 1.47-fold with the treatment of P@TCD. Lastly, the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, known to degrade corneal epithelial integrity and exacerbate tissue damage, were significantly overexpressed in the DED model. Downregulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in the treated groups indicated that P@TCD treatment concurrently attenuated the detrimental inflammatory response on the ocular surface. Collectively, these results demonstrated that P@TCD supramolecular inclusion complex comprehensively counteract the pathogenesis of DED by eliminating excessive ROS, attenuating cellular senescence, inhibiting apoptosis, and reducing local inflammation, thus offering a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of DED.

The therapeutic effect of P@TCD in scopolamine (SCOP)-Induced DED mouse models

The SCOP-induced DED mouse models were also established to investigate the therapeutic effect of P@TCD through subcutaneous injection of SCOP (Fig. 8a). Corneal epithelial recovery was assessed by fluorescein staining, which selectively highlights areas lacking intact epithelium due to epithelial defects. As demonstrated in Fig. 8b, the corneas of DED model mice exhibited intense fluorescein staining on day 0 (D 0), confirming successful model establishment. After 3 days of treatment, the saline group still displayed prominent corneal epithelial defects as indicated by green fluorescein-stained areas. In contrast, both the P@TCD and TCD treatment groups showed minimal fluorescence intensity, with P@TCD exhibiting the greatest reduction in staining compared to all other therapeutic interventions. The standardized scoring system of quantitative fluorescein staining analysis revealed significant differences among treatment groups (Fig. 8c andS14): the saline group showed the highest scores of 8.20 ± 1.64 on corneal staining, while Pue with 6.80 ± 1.30, TCD with 6.80 ± 0.55, and Cycloome® with 8.00 ± 1.22, demonstrated moderate improvement. Notably, P@TCD showed the lowest staining scores with 4.00 ± 0.71 among all groups, which exhibited the most substantial therapeutic effects. To further evaluate tear film stability, TBUT was subsequently measured (Fig. 8d andS15). The saline group showed severely impaired tear film stability, with a markedly reduced TBUT of 2.80 ± 0.38 s. In contrast, P@TCD and Cycloome® treatment significantly improved tear film stability, achieving a TBUT of 5.67 ± 0.62 s and 3.73 ± 0.37 s. Notably, P@TCD outperformed both TCD and Cycloome®, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy in restoring tear film integrity and secretion. The Schirmer test results revealed that the normal group had an average tear volume of 6.00 ± 0.33 mm, whereas saline-treated mice exhibited marginal improvement, increasing from 0.73 ± 0.15 mm to 3.07 ± 0.60 mm, indicating the least alleviation of dryness in the DED model (Fig. S16). In contrast, P@TCD and Cycloome® treatment significantly enhanced tear secretion, achieving 5.47 ± 0.18 mm and 4.07 ± 0.55 mm (Fig. 8e), closely resembling the normal physiological range. To further investigate the P@TCD recovery of ocular surface morphology, H&E and PAS staining were performed. As shown in Fig. 8f, after 3 days of treatment, H&E staining confirmed that all experimental groups maintained an intact corneal epithelium without observable defects or pathological alterations. Goblet cell loss is also a histological hallmark of DED. In the saline group, only a few goblet cells were detected. Following topical administration of TCD, Pue, P@TCD, and Cycloome®, the number of goblet cells increased, with the largest goblet cell area observed in the P@TCD group. After DED induction, histological analysis revealed distinct structural disorganization in the lacrimal glands of the saline group, characterized by lobular size variation, tissue atrophy, pathological fusion, intracellular vacuolization, and lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. 8f). Notably, both P@TCD and TCD treatments effectively preserved lacrimal gland architecture and restored histological organization.

As shown in Fig. 8g–k and S17, the corneal epithelium of DED mice exhibited significantly elevated levels of ROS, along with increased expression of the senescence-associated protein p16, the DNA-damaged biomarker γ-H2AX, apoptosis-related TUNEL staining, and the proinflammatory mediators TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, as well as the decreased expression of cell cycle-related biomarker CCND1. Specifically, ROS levels in the DED model group increased by approximately 1.74-fold compared to the normal control, while p16 expression increased by about 1.50-fold, γ-H2AX expression increased by about 1.41-fold, TUNEL-positive cells increased by nearly 1.09-fold, and the proinflammatory mediators TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels were elevated by approximately 1.40-fold, 1.52-fold, and 1.41-fold, respectively. On the contrary, the CCND1 expression was decreased by 72%. These changes reflected the underlying pathogenic mechanisms of DED, including oxidative stress–induced DNA damage, cell cycle arrest mediated by p16 INK4A-Rb pathway activation, mitochondrial apoptosis signaling, and inflammation-driven extracellular matrix degradation. In contrast, treatment with TCD, Pue, P@TCD, and Cycloome® significantly attenuated these pathological abnormalities. The results indicated that P@TCD effectively scavenged excess ROS, attenuated oxidative damage, delayed cellular senescence by modulating p16-related pathways, suppressed apoptosis, and mitigated local inflammatory responses, thereby representing a promising therapeutic strategy for the management of DED.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a supramolecular complex termed P@TCD as an eye-drop formulation through the self-assembly of antioxidant TEMPO-modified β-CD, specifically TCD, and Pue. P@TCD could mitigate the accumulation of intracellular ROS, thereby reducing DNA damage and modulating the p16INK4A-Rb signaling pathway to promote cell cycle progression and attenuate cellular senescence in the corneal epithelium [45]. In both the BAK-induced and the SCOP-induced DED mouse models, P@TCD exhibited favorable therapeutic efficacy, as evidenced by amelioration of corneal damage, enhancement of conjunctival goblet cell density, restoration of lacrimal gland structure, and significant improvements in tear homeostasis, including tear volume and tear film stability. In conclusion, P@TCD represents a promising therapeutic strategy for treating DED by reducing ROS generation, attenuating cellular senescence, alleviating corneal epithelial damage, and thus interrupting the subsequent vicious cycle of DED. Given its favorable biosafety and therapeutic efficacy, P@TCD has established a solid foundation for the development of clinical treatment strategies for DED.
