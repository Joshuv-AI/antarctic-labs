An enhanced multi-strategy educational competition optimizer for numerical optimization
Authors: Zihan Li, Tianmin Zhang, Hongzhen Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study proposes an Enhanced Multi-Strategy Educational Competition Optimizer (EMSECO) to address the inherent limitations of the standard Educational Competition Optimizer, specifically its imbalanced exploration–exploitation dynamics, insufficient diversity preservation, and suboptimal convergence behavior. Three synergistic mechanisms are integrated. Firstly, an undergraduate education stage is embedded within the original cyclical framework, leveraging differential vector guidance to augment spatial coverage and sustain diversity. Secondly, a graduate education stage is introduced as an external intensification module, utilizing elite individual and elite subpopulation guidance to strengthen local exploitation. Thirdly, a random walk restart strategy is incorporated to perturb stagnated individuals, thereby suppressing premature convergence. Comparative experiments against AE, MRFO, SAO, EOSMA, ALSHADE, and EPSCA across multiple dimensionalities demonstrate that EMSECO achieves superior performance, attaining average Friedman rankings of 1.2, 1.4, 1.7, and 2.0 for 10-, 30-, 50-, and 100-dimensional CEC 2017 test functions, respectively. Practical applicability is further validated through seven real-world engineering problems. In summary, the proposed EMSECO algorithm exhibits enhanced global exploration capacity, improved local exploitation efficiency, and superior convergence stability.

Introduction

Optimization refers to the systematic process of identifying the most effective solution from a set of feasible alternatives for a given problem. Three fundamental components govern any optimization endeavor: the objective function (or fitness function), constraints, and decision variables1. The primary aim is to determine optimal or near-optimal values for these variables subject to specified constraints, thereby extremizing the objective function2. The rapid advancement of industrialization and escalating technical demands have substantially amplified the complexity of numerous engineering challenges. Contemporary optimization problems frequently exhibit nonlinear, non-differentiable, multimodal, and discontinuous characteristics, rendering them intractable through conventional analytical approaches3. While both classical optimization techniques and metaheuristic algorithms have been deployed to address such problems, traditional deterministic methods demonstrate considerable limitations: poor adaptability to problem structures, prohibitive computational overhead, pronounced susceptibility to premature convergence, and elevated risk of entrapment in local optima4. These inherent deficiencies have catalyzed growing research interest in metaheuristic methodologies.

Metaheuristic algorithms typically integrate stochastic global sampling mechanisms with intensified local search procedures, harnessing the synergistic interplay between exploration and exploitation to navigate vast, structurally complex search landscapes in pursuit of optimal or satisficing solutions5. Exploration ensures comprehensive coverage of the search domain, safeguarding against premature stagnation in suboptimal regions, whereas exploitation concentrates computational resources on promising neighborhoods to accelerate convergence and refine solution quality. By circumventing dependence on rigorous mathematical formulations, these algorithms accommodate black-box and high-complexity systems, thereby enabling the treatment of high-dimensional, heavily constrained, non-convex problems that defy classical approaches. Although theoretical guarantees of global optimality are generally absent, metaheuristics routinely deliver high-quality solutions within acceptable computational budgets. In recent years, metaheuristic algorithms have achieved widespread adoption across diverse engineering and intelligent system applications—owing to their robust optimization capabilities, remarkable versatility, and straight forward implement ability. Representative domains include mission planning6,7, photovoltaic model identification8,9, coverage optimization in wireless sensor networks10,11, hyperparameter optimization12,13, medical image segmentation14,15, and feature selection16,17, among others.

Metaheuristic algorithms are conventionally taxonomized into three principal categories based on their inspirational origins and operational mechanisms: evolutionary-based, physics-based, and swarm-based algorithms. Evolutionary-based algorithms, epitomized by Genetic Algorithm (GA)18, Differential Evolution (DE)19, draw inspiration from natural evolutionary processes. Subsequent research endeavors have predominantly focused on variant formulations and hybrid extensions of these foundational paradigms. Physics-based algorithms originated with Simulated Annealing (SA)20, which emulates the thermodynamic annealing process. Light Spectrum Optimizer (LSO)21, Stellar Oscillation Optimizer (SOO)22 are also belong to this category. This category has subsequently diversified into chemistry-inspired algorithms (e.g., Chemical Reaction Optimization (CRO)23 and Henry Gas Solubility Optimization (HGSO)24) and mathematics-inspired algorithms (e.g., Sine Cosine Algorithm (SCA)25, Bezier Curve-based Optimization (BCO)26 and Perpendicular Bisector Optimization Algorithm (PBOA)27), reflecting the broadening of metaphorical foundations beyond classical physics. Swarm-based algorithms trace their lineage to Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)28 and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)29. This branch has undergone substantial expansion, ramifying into animals-based algorithms (e.g., Tuna Swarm Optimization (TSO)30, Black-winged Kite Algorithm (BKA)31, Crayfish Optimization Algorithm (COA)32, Fishing Cat Optimizer (FCO)33 and Artificial Meerkat Algorithm (AMA)34), plants-inspired algorithms (e.g., Dandelion Optimizer (DO)35, Moss Growth Optimization (MGO)36 and Ivy Algorithm (IA)37), and human-based algorithms (e.g., Catch Fish Optimization Algorithm (CFOA)38, Rider Optimization Algorithm (ROA)39 and Human Evolutionary Optimization Algorithm (HEOA)40), mirroring the rich behavioral complexity observable across ecological and social systems. Notwithstanding the demonstrated versatility of these algorithms in addressing complex nonlinear and multimodal landscapes, their empirical performance exhibits marked problem-dependent variability. The No Free Lunch (NFL) theorem41 establishes that no single algorithm universally dominates across all problem classes. Indeed, individual algorithms harbor intrinsic limitations—including deficient exploratory capabilities, compromised convergence reliability toward global optima, and parameter sensitivity—which collectively constrain their efficacy. These deficiencies have motivated sustained research efforts directed toward both de novo algorithmic development and systematic enhancement of existing methodologies.

The Educational Competition Optimizer (ECO) was introduced by Lian et al.42. in 2024 as a novel anthropogenic metaheuristic. Drawing inspiration from the competitive academic progression of students through primary to secondary educational stages, ECO distinguishes itself from conventional counterparts through its multi-phase search architecture, which confers enhanced adaptability across diverse optimization landscapes. Since its inception, ECO has been successfully deployed in several engineering applications, including photovoltaic system regulation43, battery parameter identification44, overcurrent relay coordination optimization45, and reservoir production optimization46, demonstrating its practical efficacy in addressing real-world constrained problems. Nevertheless, the canonical ECO formulation exhibits non-negligible deficiencies that constrain its broader applicability. Specifically, three critical challenges persist: rapid population diversity deterioration as iterations progress, pronounced susceptibility to premature convergence, and inherent difficulties in calibrating the exploration–exploitation equilibrium. These limitations collectively impair the algorithm’s capacity to navigate complex multimodal fitness landscapes and maintain sustained search vigor throughout the optimization trajectory. In response to these shortcomings, extensive research efforts have been directed toward algorithmic refinements aimed at bolstering ECO’s global exploration competence and problem-specific adaptability. Tang et al. incorporated a leapfrog strategy to fortify ECO’s global exploration capacity, coupled with an early termination mechanism designed to facilitate escape from local optima entrapment47. Li et al. introduced a Powell local search mechanism during the later evolutionary stages to intensify exploitation, synergistically integrated with a population regeneration protocol to counteract diversity loss48. Hammad et al. proposed a hybrid ECO variant embedding orthogonal learning strategies, specifically targeting the preservation of population diversity in terminal search phases49. Meanwhile, Emam et al. amalgamated a local escape operator with Gaussian perturbation tactics, thereby effectively augmenting both local evasion capabilities and exploitative refinement50. Notwithstanding these multidimensional enhancements to ECO documented in the literature, substantial scope for improvement persists with respect to three critical aspects: the dynamic equilibrium between global exploration and local exploitation, the efficacy of local optimum evasion, and the precision of terminal convergence. To address these residual deficiencies, this study proposes an Enhanced Multi-Strategy Educational Competition Optimizer (EMSECO). Undergraduate education stage

The canonical ECO algorithm concludes its evolutionary trajectory at the high school education stage, which imposes inherent constraints on its optimization capability. Specifically, this three-phase architecture exhibits pronounced susceptibility to premature convergence in complex multimodal landscapes: the intensified exploitation mechanisms characterizing the terminal phase rapidly erode population diversity in the absence of sustained exploratory provisions. Consequently, the algorithm struggles to maintain search diversity throughout prolonged optimization processes, compromising its reliability for high-dimensional or intricately structured problems. To remedy these deficiencies, this study introduces an intermediate undergraduate education stage (UES) following the high school phase. By deploying the current-to-Xwmean/1 mutation strategy for positional updating, the UES realizes controlled directional perturbations through weighted mean-centric difference vectors. This mechanism rejuvenates population diversity via subpopulation-based information exchange while preserving convergence momentum, thereby enhancing basin-hopping capability across rugged fitness landscapes. The resultant four-phase hierarchical framework improves robustness across varying problem complexities and dimensionalities. Graduate education stage

The UES introduced in the preceding section, in conjunction with the original three educational phases, constitutes a comprehensive search mechanism spanning the entire optimization trajectory. While UES effectively remedies the deficiency of population diversity deterioration characteristic of basic ECO in later evolutionary phases, it does not substantively enhance local exploitation capability. Given that the undergraduate phase precedes the terminal graduate education stage, this study proposes a Graduate Education Stage (GES) update strategy explicitly designed for intensified exploitation.

In GES, intensified exploitation is prioritized, rendering elite guidance paramount. However, exclusive reliance on the global best risks premature convergence to local optima. Inspired by the Grey Wolf Optimizer’s hierarchical leadership structure, GES stochastically selects one guiding individual from the top-three ranked candidates per iteration, thereby diversifying elite information sources while maintaining exploitation pressure. It should be noted that the selection probabilities for the three individuals are identical, each being one-third. Furthermore, the elite subpopulation encapsulates the swarm’s evolutionary trajectory. Estimation of Distribution Algorithms leverage probabilistic models to learn high-quality solution distributions, enabling refined local search within promising regions and remedying ECO’s deficient terminal-phase convergence precision. Additionally, model-guided sampling exhibits enhanced directionality, curtailing ineffective search expenditures and accelerating convergence toward optimal basins. Random walk restart strategy

The later evolutionary phases of the basic ECO algorithm, diversity rapidly deteriorates, causing the population to cluster in local optimal regions. Furthermore, when the best individual becomes trapped in local optima, the remaining population struggles to achieve substantive improvements during subsequent updates. Under such circumstances, the basic algorithm lacks effective mechanisms to restore exploratory capabilities. To remedy these deficiencies, this study proposes a Random Walk Restart Strategy (RWS) to address stagnation scenarios that are intractable for canonical ECO. RWS emulates the stochastic wandering behavior observed in natural organisms, incorporating Cauchy mutation to perturb current positional coordinates. The Cauchy distribution exhibits a higher probability of generating large-span jumps compared to Gaussian distributions, effectively “catapulting” stagnated individuals out of local basins. Even when the population has largely converged, Cauchy mutation retains the potential to produce exploratory points distant from current regions, thereby preventing wasted computational effort on futile fine-tuning near suboptimal solutions. On the other hand, Lévy flight predominantly performs small-scale random walks, while occasionally making direct jumps to another local optimum. In contrast, Cauchy mutation exhibits greater stability than Lévy flight and is capable of generating more effective jumps. Moreover, to further enhance overall convergence performance and optimization capability, this study designs a cosine decay factor to dynamically modulate search step sizes, ensuring that the algorithm emphasizes extensive exploration in early iterations and fine-grained exploitation in later phases. Time complexity analysis

Time complexity analysis serves as a pivotal metric for evaluating the computational efficiency of meta-heuristic algorithms. For the basic ECO, let N, D and T denote the population size, the dimensionality of the solution space, and the maximum number of iterations, respectively. During the initialization phase, the generation of N individuals across D dimensions yields a time complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( {N \times D} \right)$$\end{document}. Regarding the proposed EMSECO, the initialization phase similarly involves traversing N individuals across D dimensions, resulting in a complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( {N \times D} \right)$$\end{document}. The undergraduate education stage (UES) is integrated into the original cyclical framework alongside the three canonical educational phases, with all four stages executed in rotational succession. Consequently, each stage maintains identical computational complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( {\frac{N}{4} \times D \times T} \right)$$\end{document}. For the graduate education stage (GES), assuming \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{G}$$\end{document} individuals participate in this phase, the computational complexity is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( {N_{G} \times D \times T} \right)$$\end{document}. For the random walk restart strategy (RWS), assuming \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{R}$$\end{document} individuals fall into stagnation per iteration, the computational complexity is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( {N_{R} \times D \times T} \right)$$\end{document}. Thus, the primary determinants of EMSECO’s computational overhead are the execution frequencies of GES and RWS. To ensure experimental fairness, all competing algorithms employ maximum fitness evaluation count (FEsMax) as the termination criterion. This standardization prevents unfair advantages arising from multiple internal iterations per algorithmic cycle.

Results and analysis of numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct systematic validation of the proposed EMSECO algorithm employing comprehensive benchmark suites. To rigorously appraise its optimization efficacy, comparative experiments were orchestrated against a spectrum of canonical and state-of-the-art metaheuristic variants. Parameter sensitivity analysis

Parameter sensitivity analysis constitutes a critical component in metaheuristic algorithm research, serving to evaluate the impact of parameter perturbations on algorithmic performance and thereby elucidate the degree of dependency on specific parameter configurations. Through systematic sensitivity analysis, the adaptability of proposed algorithms under diverse parameter settings can be validated, mitigating performance reliance on particular parameter values while furnishing rational guidance for parameter selection. Consequently, rigorous analysis of critical parameters not only deepens theoretical comprehension of underlying algorithmic mechanisms but also establishes foundations for reliable deployment in complex real-world optimization scenarios. This subsection presents comprehensive parameter sensitivity analysis for the proposed EMSECO algorithm, providing theoretical and empirical support for parameter calibration. Specifically, parameter G as the control parameter governing the graduate education stage, determining the proportion of individuals participating in this phase. Its value directly modulates the exploration–exploitation equilibrium. When G assumes larger values, a greater fraction of individuals is guided by elite subpopulation and global best information, enabling EMSECO to conduct intensified refinement within promising regions and remedy the deficient convergence precision characteristic of canonical ECO in terminal phases. Conclusions

To enhance the search capability of the basic Educational Competition Optimizer (ECO), this study proposes an Enhanced Multi-Strategy Educational Competition Optimizer (EMSECO). The proposed EMSECO integrates three synergistic improvement mechanisms: the Undergraduate Education Stage (UES), the Graduate Education Stage (GES), and the Random Walk Restart Strategy (RWS). Firstly, UES is embedded into ECO’s inherent search framework, leveraging diverse differential vector guidance to enrich population diversity. Secondly, GES is introduced as an external intensification module, utilizing elite individual and elite subpopulation guidance to curtail ineffective search and strengthen exploitation capability. Thirdly, RWS is imposed on stagnated individuals to facilitate new solution generation and assist the algorithm in escaping local optima.

To validate the efficacy of the proposed methodology, this study employs the CEC 2017 benchmark suite, widely recognized in the academic community. Through Friedman test, Nemenyi post-hoc test, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test, the superiority of EMSECO relative to advanced algorithms is substantiated. Furthermore, six engineering design optimization problems provide additional validation of the practical applicability of the proposed approach.

Notwithstanding the favorable overall performance of EMSECO, certain limitations remain that warrant further investigation. On one hand, the algorithmic validation has predominantly relied on benchmark functions and specific engineering cases, which may not fully mirror the complex and dynamic nature of real-world application scenarios, potentially constraining comprehensive assessment of applicability across diverse practical problems. On the other hand, the incorporation of multiple enhancement strategies, while improving optimization efficacy, introduces additional computational overhead, which may impose constraints on real-time optimization applications requiring rapid response capabilities.

Regarding future research directions, we envision extending the algorithm’s application domain to resource scheduling, investment decision-making, and industrial planning. Simultaneously, a multi-objective variant will be developed to address increasingly intricate real-world optimization challenges. Furthermore, we intend to explore the utilization of large language models for designing more potent search operators, thereby further fortifying the search capability of the proposed methodology.
