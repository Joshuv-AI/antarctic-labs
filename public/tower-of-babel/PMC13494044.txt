Jingling Granules alleviate atherosclerosis through activating autophagy and inhibiting human umbilical vein endothelial cells apoptosis
Authors: Yuhan Wang, Yanzi Liu, Dong Niu, Chang Liu, Qiang Wu, Li Cao, Feng Gao, Shu Lu, Fuming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerosis (AS) remains a leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity worldwide. Strategies that promote autophagy and limit apoptosis hold therapeutic promise. Jingling Granules (JLG), a clinically used formulation, have shown vascular protective effects, but the underlying mechanisms are incompletely understood. Here, we investigated whether JLG attenuates atherosclerosis by modulating autophagy and apoptosis through mTOR signaling. In high-fat diet-fed ApoE−/− mice, JLG improved serum lipid profiles, moderately reduced atherosclerotic lesion area, and suppressed systemic inflammation. JLG enhanced autophagic flux and attenuated endothelial cell apoptosis. Mechanistically, JLG downregulated phosphorylated mTOR (p‑mTOR), indicating mTOR pathway inhibition. Consistently, in palmitic acid‑challenged HUVECs, JLG promoted autophagy and reduced apoptosis; these effects were abolished by the mTOR activator MHY1485. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that JLG decelerates atherosclerosis progression by inhibiting mTOR to enhance autophagy and suppress apoptosis, highlighting its therapeutic potential in early stage atherosclerosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63278-5.Keywords: Traditional Chinese medicine, Vascular endothelial damage, MTOR, Hyperlipemia, ApoE−/− miceSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cardiology, Cell biology, Diseases

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is one major chronic arterial disease that causes much mortality worldwide, formatting of fibrofatty lesions in the arterial wall, which gradually develop into atheroma and characteristic plaques. Unstable atherosclerotic plaque rupture and platelet agglutination can cause local thrombosis, leading to partial or total occlusion of the affected artery1,2. The cardiovascular chain of events suggests that vascular endothelial injury and endothelial dysfunction are key initiating factors in the cardiovascular chain of events and throughout the course of atherosclerotic disease. Endothelial cell damage and dysfunction lead to low-density lipoprotein (LDL) deposition into the vessel wall, which plays a vital role in the initiation of vascular disorders and AS3. Vascular endothelial dysfunction causes sustained pathological injury to the vascular endothelium, which gradually aggravates the dysfunction, thus causing a series of reactions in related vascular walls, and further causing pathological changes in vascular endothelial injury. Therefore, understanding the potential mechanism of vascular endothelial injury and endothelial dysfunction is an important basis for effective treatment of cardiovascular risk events caused by AS4.

Current clinical therapies to prevent or inhibit the progression of AS are limited and carry residual risks. Statins are the first choice to start treatment for AS, however, more than half of patients experience serious side effects such as associated muscle pain, liver and kidney injury and elevated serum creatine kinase (CK)5. Therefore, alternative treatment modalities demand prompt exploration. Notably, apoptosis and autophagy, two types of programmed cell death, intervene in the development and progression of AS by regulating the effects of endothelial cells on lipid metabolism and inflammation6. Evidence suggests that inhibition of excessive endothelial cell apoptosis is an important target for preventing thrombosis and maintaining vascular homeostasis against AS7. Several reports also have demonstrated that activation of autophagy through inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibited plaque endothelial cell death, enhanced endothelial cell stability, and limited the development of AS8,9. However, their intercross links remain unknown. Perhaps the effective regulation of the relationship between autophagy and apoptosis can be a breakthrough in the treatment of endothelial dysfunction.

Nowadays, a growing number of scholars have carried out a series of studies on Chinese herbal medicine protecting vascular endothelium through multiple pathways10. Based on clinical practice, prestigious Chinese physician Prof. Shu-Hua Tang has applied Jingling Granules (JLG) in the treatment of AS for decades. JLG consists of Polygonati Rhizoma, Ganoderma, Gynostemmae Pentaphylli Herba, Curcumaelongae Rhizoma, Polygoni Cuspidati Rhizoma Et Radix, Rhapontici Radix. (The Latin names were obtained from the TCMSP database) We found that JLG can effectively relieve arterial elastic hardness, vascular aging, hyperlipidemia, and other risk factors in advance at the stage of vascular endothelial injury and endothelial dysfunction without plaque formation. The development of AS is inhibited by blocking the initiation stage11. JLG, composed of Turmeric, Ganoderma Lucidum, and Gyno stemma, has been used to protect the blood vessels’ endothelium and treat cardiovascular disease. Among them, Polygonatum kingianum, Polygonum cuspidatum, and Radix Rhapontici were potential drugs to improve fat accumulation and lower blood lipids12–14. Previous studies identified and quantified various active components in JLG, including ganoderic acid A, Quercetin, Emodin, and Resveratrol, using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry15. Some of these active ingredients have been shown to improve lipid metabolism and ameliorate AS16–18. Although several studies have associated the expression of a single gene in JLG with AS, the direct effects of JLG on established AS have not been determined. In this study, the hypothesis was raised that JLG reverses the imbalance between apoptosis and autophagy by regulating mTOR signaling, as well as exhibiting therapeutic effects in endothelial dysfunction. To sum up, our study links the association between autophagy and apoptosis with JLG in treating AS.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), palmitic acid (PA), and rapamycin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Antibodies against c-Caspase 3, LC3A/B, p-mTOR (Ser2448), mTOR, p62/SQSTM1, Beclin-1, Bax, and Bcl-2 were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Goat anti-rabbit IgG polyclonal (ab15007; Abcam, United States) was used as the secondary antibody. Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 1347575) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The mTOR activator MHY1485 was purchased from Med Chem Expression Co. (HY-B0795). Apoptotic rate was determined using Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise stated.

Reparation of Jingling Granules

The elixir granules were composed of no-decoction preparation (Polygonati Rhizoma 15 g, Ganoderma 10 g, Gynostemmae Pentaphylli Herba 30 g, Curcumaelongae Rhizoma 15 g, Polygoni Cuspidati Rhizoma Et Radix 15 g, Rhapontici Radix 15 g purchased from Jiangsu Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Jingling granules are produced in Jiangyin Tianjiang Pharmaceutical. According to the instructions of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 edition, the fourth general principle 0104 granules, the active components were extracted by the boiling method, and the ethanol was recovered after refining with the same amount of ethanol. The ethanol was prolonged properly so that the precipitation was complete and the supernatant was easy to separate. The above six aromas were successively boiled with purified water for 1.5 h each time, combined with the decoction and filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated to a clear paste with a relative density of about 1.08 (90–95 ℃). It was cooled to room temperature, and the same amount of ethanol was added to precipitate and allowed to rest. The supernatant was concentrated to 1.20 (60–65 ℃) with a relative density of 1.20 (60–65 ℃). The same amount of water was added, stirred and left for 8 h, and the supernatant was concentrated to a clear ointment with a relative density of 1.38–1.40 (60–65 ℃). The supernatant was concentrated to 1.30–1.33 (75–80 ℃), 1.25–1.30 (75–82 ℃), 1.7–1.84 (70–78 ℃). Take 1 part of clear ointment, 3 parts of sucrose powder, 1.25 parts of dextrin and an appropriate amount of ethanol, make them into particles, dry them and obtain them. (1) In order to make the finished product pure, the extract was concentrated to a relative density of about 1.08 and precipitated with the same amount of ethanol to remove impurities such as gum and protein and to facilitate granulation. (2) The prepared sugar powder should be dried at 600 C, and the crystal water can be removed with a sieve mesh of less than 10 mesh (No. 1 sieve), which is smaller than that used for wet particles, and the part that cannot pass through the sieve hole is properly broken, and then according to the particle size requirement, pass through 60 mesh or 80 mesh (No. 5 sieve) according to the lower limit of the particle size specification, and take the particles between 10 and 80 mesh. The specification is 18 g per bag. The Jingling granules were dissolved twice with 40 ml of water ultrasonically, and the supernatant was centrifuged and filtered. The part of the decoction-free preparation was melted based on body weight and body surface area to perform human-mouse equivalent dose conversions for dosing concentrations in sterile water19. Prepared JLG was administered by gavage in ApoE−/− mice.

Animal model

All animal experimental procedures were performed following the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine.

Half the number of C57BL/6 mice and ApoE−/− mice of both sexes at 5–6 weeks of age were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of the Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. The mice were housed in an SPF-grade barrier facility and fed with food and water at 23 ± 2 °C. After one week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 8), including the normal control group (NC), hyperlipidemic AS model group (AS model), hyperlipidemic AS model group (AS model + Rapa), hyperlipidemic AS model group (AS model + JLG100) and atherosclerotic hyperlipidemia model group (AS model + JLG200), respectively. C57BL/6 mice were used as normal controls and fed with normal chow. After one week of adaptive feeding, the hyperlipidemic AS model was established using ApoE−/− mice, which were fed with high-fat chow for 11 weeks under the premise of free water intake, and the weight change was measured once a week. After one week of high-fat modeling, the mice were administered by gavage with different doses of drugs for 10 weeks, including low elixir pellets (0.11 g/10 g/d), high elixir pellets (0.22 g/10 g/d), and rapamycin (4 mg/kg/d), respectively, while continuing HFD feeding for a total of 10 weeks. Anesthesia was administered 12 h after final dosing. Blood and aortic samples were then collected for tests. After 12 weeks of treatment, mouse aorta and serum samples were collected individually for further tests.

Serum lipids assay

After an 8-week intervention of JLG, blood from the abdominal aorta was collected. Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) were detected by the automatic biochemical analyzer (AU5800, Beckman Coulter Co., Ltd.).

Histological analysis

Immediately after blood sampling in mice under anesthesia, a portion of the aorta or the whole aorta was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (4 μm thickness) were placed on poly-Lysine coated slides and incubated at 60 °C for 1.5 h. Routine hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) and Oil Red O staining were performed using the Lipid Staining Kit (Oil Red O) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The stained sections were observed under a light microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH). Additional sections of whole aortas were stained with oil red O (ORO) and flat pinned. Pathological changes were evaluated microscopically by measuring the area of atheromatous plaques. Plaque area analysis was carried out using Image Pro Plus software version 5.1 (Media Cybernetics, Inc.). In each group, ORO plaques were mainly found in the aortic arch and its branches. The damage percentage of vascular intimal plaque was calculated using ImageJ.

Western blotting analysis

Whole proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) and then quantified using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Equal proteins (50 µg) were separated via SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against proliferating cell nuclear antigen, LC3A/B, p62/SQSTM1, Beclin-1, Bax, Bcl-2, caspase-3, mTOR, and P-mTOR (Cell Signaling Technology, Germany). Following this, the membranes were further probed for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (ab15007; Abcam, United States). At last, specific protein signals were enhanced using Clarity Max™ Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). All antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).

RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA from the aorta was extracted using Trizol Reagent (Gibco-BRL, Paisley, UK), referring to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted mRNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Equal weight RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA first strand with M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) and random primers (β-actin). Then the SYBR Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) and quantitative PCR primers were employed to detect expressions of TNF-α, Beclin-1, IL-6, and β-actin. β-actin acted as an endogenous control of TNF-α, Beclin-1, and IL-6. 5’-TCTTGGTTCTTGGCCACTCC-3’(reverse);

5’- ATGGAGCCACCGATCCACA-3′(reverse).

Immunofluorescence analysis

The aortic root of frozen sections was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The sections were then blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h and probed with anti-LC3A/B (1:100) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing 3 times with TBST (1% Tween-20 in TBS), the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488/555-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Then, they were washed 3 times with TBST, and DAPI solution was added to stain the cell nuclei. The dishes were analyzed by laser confocal microscopy with excitation at 488 nm and 555 nm. ImageJ software was used for quantitative analysis, and total antibody staining was normalized to DAPI. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) underwent the same steps.

Cell culture

HUVECs were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 50 µg/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Palmitic acid (PA) was used to induce an atherosclerotic cell model. Here, we used PA to induce HUVECs and constructed a high-fat model. Afterward, the optimal proliferative activity concentration of JLG was selected and divided into low (100 µg/mL) and high (200 µg/mL) dose groups before administration. The rapamycin intervention group was used as a control. HUVECs were pre-treated with 800 µM PA for 24 h, followed by JLG, lyophilized drugs, and rapamycin for 24 h.

CCK-8 assay

HUVECs (about 8,000 per well) were seeded into 96-well plates overnight. HUVECs were pre-treated with PA for 24 h, and then incubated with low (100 µg/mL) or high (200 µg/mL) doses of JLG for 24 h, respectively, based on the optimized concentrations from 0 to 600 µg/mL. 10 µL Cell Counting Kit-8 solution (CCK-8, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was then added into each well before further incubation of 0.5 h at 37 °C. Later, the absorbance was determined at the wavelength of 450 nm to assess cell proliferation.

Apoptosis analysis

To verify the anti-modulatory effect of JLG by inhibiting mTOR-induced autophagy, the cell apoptotic percentage of each group after administration was tested using Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) following the protocols of the manufacturer. Briefly, collected cells were re-suspended in 100 µL 1× Binding buffer, followed by the addition of 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL PI staining solution. After mixing, treated cells were incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Then, the apoptotic cell percentages were measured via flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) after adding 400 µL 1× Binding buffer.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.1 (GraphPad Software, USA) was used in the Statistical analysis. Data from at least three independent experiments were expressed as mean ± standard error (x¯± SE). Normally distributed data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed with nonparametric tests. If the P-value was less than 0.05, the statistical differences were considered to be significant (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01).

Results

Compound identification in serum following JLG administration and network pharmacological analysis

To identify the bioactive compounds derived from the JLG in vivo, serum samples were collected from mice following oral administration of JLG. The serum metabolites were subsequently analyzed using Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography coupled with Q-Exactive HFX Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Exactive HFX). Both positive and negative ionization modes were employed to achieve comprehensive metabolite profiling (Fig. 1a and b). The Base Peak Chromatograms (BPC) generated in each mode displayed well-resolved peaks, allowing for the detection of numerous metabolite over the chromatographic time scale. A total of over 30 key bioactive compounds were tentatively identified by comparing their exact molecular masses, retention times, and fragmentation patterns with available reference standards and database entries. These included (Ganoderic acid C120, Scopoletin, Formononetin21, Resveratroloside22, Alismol and Salidroside23, which are consistent with the known chemical profile of the herbal components within JLG (Supplementary Table 1). Notably, these specific compounds have been documented in previous studies to exert therapeutic effects against atherosclerosis. Quantitative analysis revealed that these compounds reached detectable concentrations in the serum, suggesting their potential absorption and bioavailability. Subsequently, to systematically predict the pharmacological targets and pathways associated with these bioactive compounds, a network pharmacology analysis was conducted. Using the chemical structures of these compounds, potential targets of JLG bioactive components and AS-related pathogenic genes were retrieved from public databases such as SwissTargetPrediction and GeneCards (Fig. 1c, d and e). After target collection, protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed and analyzed (Fig. 2a). Functional enrichment analysis (GO) and pathway analysis (KEGG) revealed that the putative targets were significantly enriched in biological processes and pathways related to autophagy activation and apoptosis inhibition—particularly in the context of AS (Figs. 1f and 2b). Key pathways included the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and the AMPK-mTOR autophagy regulation axis (Fig. 2c). These integrated findings demonstrate that multiple bioactive components of JLG are absorbed into the bloodstream and may collaboratively modulate autophagic and anti-apoptotic pathways, providing a pharmacological basis for the cardioprotective effects of JLG.

JLG ameliorated aortic lesions and inhibited atherosclerotic plaque in ApoE-/- mice

To further elucidate the therapeutic effects of JLG, the atherosclerotic plaque formation in ApoE−/− mice was evaluated, in which JLG was found not only to influence the body weight of the ApoE−/− mice (Fig. 3a) but also to inhibite their atherosclerotic plaque growth. As shown in Fig. 3b, photographs of the aorta suggested that a clinical dose of JLG ameliorated aortic lesions and contributed to a moderate reduction in atherosclerotic plaque growth. Histological examinations demonstrated thicker plaque in ApoE−/− mice than in the JLG mice. In Oil Red O staining and H&E staining (Fig. 3c and d), JLG reduced the pathological lesions in a dose-dependent manner. These therapeutic effects of JLG demonstrated above were inferior to those in rapamycin group (Fig. 3e and f).

JLG reduced serum lipids and inflammation in ApoE-/- mice

To determine JLG’s effects on the biochemical basis that leads to atherosclerotic plaque growth, we collected mice serum samples to detect their blood lipid level changes. As shown in Fig. 4a and b, levels of LDL-C, TC, and TG decreased, and the level of HDL-C increased after the treatment of JLG, with LDL-C decreasing significantly than those of TC and TG (Fig. 4c and d). As inflammation plays a critical role in promoting plaque formation and progression, the expressions of cytokines’ mRNA were analyzed by RT-qPCR. Compared to the C57BL/6 controls, we found higher mRNA expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in ApoE−/− mice aortic tissues, and the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly reduced after using JLG (Fig. 4e and f), compared with these two expressions in the rapamycin group. Although rapamycin therapy was more effective than JLG in reducing plaque area and overall lipid accumulation, JLG showed superior effects in attenuating systemic inflammation, lowering LDL-C levels and Total Cholesterol levels indicating a distinct therapeutic profile that may complement plaque-targeted strategies.

JLG activated autophagy through inhibiting mTOR expression in the aorta of ApoE-/- mice

Previous studies have demonstrated that the mTOR pathway is a vital link to the autophagy process24. Given that rapamycin induces cell autophagy by inhibiting mTOR expression, we speculated that JLG inhibited the mTOR signaling pathway to induce autophagy as well. Therefore, Western blotting was applied to detect the aortic tissue from JLG groups in ApoE−/− mice, after which a notably increased ratio of phosphorylated mTOR to mTOR was observed (Fig. 6e and f), thus confirming JLG activated autophagy through mTOR-regulating factors. To further elucidate upstream regulation, we examined Akt phosphorylation. JLG treatment significantly elevated the p-Akt/total Akt ratio compared to the Model group (Fig. 6g and h), indicating Akt pathway activation. This is consistent with the well-known mTORC1-S6K1 negative feedback loop: chronic mTORC1 hyperactivation in the Model group impairs PI3K/Akt signaling, while JLG-mediated inhibition of mTORC1 relieves this feedback suppression, restoring Akt phosphorylation. These findings suggest that JLG activates Akt upstream of mTOR, contributing to the regulation of autophagy.

JLG inhibited mTOR and enhanced autophagy in HUVECs

It is well established that vascular endothelial cells undergo autophagy to protect their structures from inflammation and oxidative stress when stimulated by ox-LDL in AS25. To further validate the autophagic effect of JLG in activating vascular endothelial cells, we first assessed the effects of JLG on the viability of HUVECs by performing a CCK-8 assay. Considering the cell viability after JLG treatment (Fig. 7b), we chose two concentrations, including a low (100 µg/mL) and a high (200 µg/mL) dose for 24 h in the further experiments. After detecting the expression of LC3A/B using confocal microscopy, autophagic flux was determined. As shown in Fig. 7a and c, JLG increased the expression of LC3A/B in a dose-dependent manner. The rapamycin autophagy activator group served as a control to further support our hypothesis. Moreover, the JLG-induced effect was significantly reversed by mTOR activator MHY1485.

The promotion of autophagy by JLG reversed PA-induced HUVECs apoptosis via mTOR

To determine the relationship between JLG-induced autophagy via the mTOR signaling pathway and HUVECs apoptosis, MHY1485, an mTOR agonist, was applied to perform further autophagy inhibition in order to observe its anticipated reverse effect. As expected, Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining and flow cytometry results showed that JLG and rapamycin reduced the cell death rate compared with that in the PA-treated group. mTOR activator MHY1485 significantly restored the reduction in proliferation of HUVECs, and notably, the efficiency of JLG in cells was comparable to that of the mTOR conventional inhibitor rapamycin (Fig. 7d and e). The results demonstrated that JLG activated autophagy via inhibiting mTOR and reversing the apoptosis, suggesting that mTOR played a crucial role in the whole process (Scheme 1).

Discussion

Endothelial dysfunction is one of the primary causes of AS26 and this stage can be triggered independently by risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, and dyslipidemia27, which involves cellular autophagy, apoptosis, and inflammation of responses28,29. Impaired structure and function of endothelial cells will weaken the vascular barrier function, make blood lipids and monocytes more easily deposited in the radiosensitive intima of blood vessels, further promote the formation of foam cells and AS plaques, and accelerate the occurrence of AS. Therefore, vascular endothelial injury and dysfunction are the key early influences in the pathogenesis of AS, and are one of the basic ideas for the prevention and treatment of the occurrence and development of AS. Current evidence suggests that herbal medicine has strong therapeutic potential to improve conditions with cardiovascular endothelial injury30. Some studies have identified that the representative compounds isolated from JLG can control and delay vascular endothelial injury and dysfunction to effectively regulate vascular function and inhibit the occurrence and development of AS31,32.

Our study in vitro and in vivo reveals that JLG, a TCM formula, could alleviate the progression of carotid AS. Particularly, JLG protects against endothelial damage and inhibits atherosclerotic plaque growth by inducing cell autophagy and attenuating apoptosis. Meanwhile, it promotes the survival of HUVECs via inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway. These findings provide insights into the possible mechanism underlying JLG-induced autophagy and suggest that JLG is a potential therapeutic option for AS at its early stages.

Unlike previous trials, our study was designed with a rapamycin group as control. Rapamycin possesses multiple protective effects through stimulating autophagy pathways33,34. In our research, consistent with the lipid-reducing effect in previous reports via Ginsenoside Rb1 induced ApoE−/− mice35 or quercetin-induced protective effect on AS18, both JLG and rapamycin lowered lipid levels and attenuated lipid deposition in arterial roots, and reduced plaque thickness in high-fat diet feeding ApoE−/− mice. It is worth noting that Chinese herbal medicine JLG was inferior to rapamycin in limiting the TG but superior to rapamycin in lowering expressions of other biochemical results and significantly reduced the activation of inflammasomes at optimal doses. The above data showed that JLG is a superior solution to reduce the inflammatory response and relieve AS pathology, and is more effective in maintaining plaque stability.

In recent years, numerous studies have suggested that the regulation of autophagy and apoptosis is often correlated, which has attracted considerable attention9. It is widely believed that autophagy plays a vital protective role, inducing atherosclerotic cells to survive in various stressful conditions36. Proved that resveratrol, an important regulator of autophagy, could inhibit endothelial cell inflammation by inducing autophagy37. LC3 changing from type I to type II is a definite marker of autophagosome formation and is widely monitored as an autophagy-related protein. Beclin-1 and p62/SQSTM1, as autophagy-associated molecules, play important roles in modifying localization related to the development of AS29,36. The present study indicated that JLG treatment accelerated the formation of autophagosomes and increased LC3II and BECN-1 levels and p62/SQSTM1 degradation, thus providing further support for autophagy induction in high-fat diet-induced AS in ApoE−/− mice. Moreover, our in vitro experiments also support the hypothesis that JLG significantly upregulated the LC3 immunofluorescent expression in HUVECs. And its ability to induce autophagy was achieved following the optimization of Jingling Granules doses in HUVECs without affecting cell viability.

Furthermore, JLG may inhibit cell apoptosis. The Bcl-2 family proteins have been reported to be involved in caspase-dependent apoptosis38. In our study, JLG reduced the expression of Bax and the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and decreased the expression of cleaved caspase 3; the flow cytometric assay showed that JLG inhibited HUVECs apoptosis, while rapamycin presented an opposite result to it. These findings were consistent with our speculation. Notably, the cytoprotective protein Bcl-2 has been reported to influence apoptosis and modulate autophagy by regulating BECN1/beclin-139, the precise mechanism of Beclin-1-mediated apoptosis inhibition, however, is not yet clear40. Thus, whether JLG induces autophagy and inhibits apoptosis by affecting the interaction between Beclin-1 and Bcl-2 needs to be further explored.

Of course, there were studies showing seemingly different results from ours, in which, for example, the exact role of autophagy in the pathology of conditioned AS remains controversial24,41, which had demonstrated the complexity of autophagy regulating atherosclerotic disease. Both moderate levels of autophagy and differences in external intervention protocols (ApoE−/− mice, precisely calculated dosage and frequency of PA administration, and purified endothelial cells, e.g.) may lead to relative changes in autophagy and apoptotic protein levels, therefore, a subtle control of autophagy levels in AS deserves further investigation.

It is known that activation of mTOR inhibits autophagy initiation, autophagosome nucleation, and phosphorylates42, on the contrary, inhibition of mTOR defends against the development of AS43. To further explore the molecular mechanism, we investigated the effects of mTOR signaling on the activation of autophagy by JLG in ApoE−/− mice. We found that JLG decreased the levels of p-mTOR/mTOR in ApoE−/− mice. JLG may attenuate PA-induced HUVECs apoptosis by reducing mTOR signaling to promote autophagy. Firstly, the LC3 fluorescence signal was enhanced in JLG-induced HUVECs dose-dependently. Secondly, MHY1485 (autophagy inhibitors) acts through specific inhibition of mTOR phosphorylation44. Nevertheless, co-incubation with MHY1485 increased the effect of PA. Furthermore, JLG exhibited similar effects to those of rapamycin. Meanwhile, JLG alleviated the apoptosis rate in PA-treated HUVECs in a background of flow Cytometry. Notably, MHY1485 counteracted the anti-apoptotic effect of JLG in PA-treated HUVECs. Above all, JLG may halt the progression of AS by inhibiting mTOR to promote autophagy and reduce apoptosis in the HUVECs.

We recognize that the absence of direct histological staining for VCAM-1, ICAM-1, and CD68 limits the spatial resolution of our inflammatory assessment. Future studies incorporating such immunohistochemical analyses would further strengthen the local anti-inflammatory evidence. We also acknowledge that the sample size for our histological astaining analysis (n = 3) is relatively modest, and larger cohorts would enhance statistical robustness. We encourage future investigations with expanded sample sizes to validate our findings.

In conclusion, in vivo experiment, JLG induced autophagy to reduce apoptosis and delay the damage of atherosclerotic plaque in ApoE−/− mice through mTOR pathway, and in vitro experiments confirmed that JLG acted on HUVECs similar to mTOR inhibitors to enhance autophagy flux, improve apoptotic environment, and reduce endothelial damage. To confirm the interaction between mTOR upstream signaling targets, additional studies are needed in the future. Also, JLG may exert its anti-atherosclerotic effect through other mechanisms.
