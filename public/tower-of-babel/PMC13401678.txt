Anaemia Among Women in Lesotho: Findings From 2023 to 2024 Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Addae Boateng Adu‐Gyamfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnaemia remains a severe public health concern worldwide, with more women of reproductive age being affected, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Anaemia affects an estimated 613.2 million women of reproductive age (15–49 years) globally. The burden is disproportionately concentrated in South Asia and sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA), regions that consistently record the highest anaemia prevalence worldwide. The study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with anaemia among reproductive‐age women in Lesotho.MethodsThis study used cross‐sectional data from the 2023–2024 Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey (LDHS) conducted by the Ministry of Health. The analysis included 3,105 women aged 15–49 years with complete haemoglobin measurement. Data were collected from November 27, 2023, to February 29, 2024. Descriptive statistics were used to estimate the prevalence of anaemia. A binary logistic regression model was fitted to identify the factors associated with anaemia, and the results were presented as crude and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe overall prevalence of anaemia among women of reproductive age in Lesotho was 53.7% [95% CI = 51.2–56.2]. The odds of anaemia were significantly highest among those aged 45–49 (AOR = 1.78; 95% CI = 1.17–2.70). Married or cohabiting women had lower odds of anaemia than those never in union (AOR = 0.55; 95% CI = 0.40–0.74). Women who had given birth once had lower odds of anaemia than nulliparous women (AOR = 0.64; 95% CI = 0.46–0.89). Women for whom obtaining money needed for healthcare was not a problem had lower odds of anaemia than those who reported it as a big problem (AOR = 0.73; 95% CI = 0.57–0.93). Women in Quthing (AOR = 0.60; 95% CI = 0.39–0.91) and Qacha's Nek (AOR = 0.61; 95% CI = 0.39–0.95) had lower odds of anaemia than those in Butha‐Buthe.ConclusionAnaemia remains a severe public health problem among women of reproductive age in Lesotho. Age, marital status, parity, financial access to healthcare, and region of residence were associated with anaemia. Public health interventions addressing socioeconomic barriers and improving access to healthcare services may contribute to reducing the burden of anaemia among women in Lesotho.

Background

Anaemia is a major public health condition characterised by reduced haemoglobin concentration in the blood, resulting in reduced oxygen transport and delivery to tissues and various parts of the body [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines anaemia in non‐pregnant women as haemoglobin concentration below 12.0 g/dL, while in pregnant women, it is defined as haemoglobin below 11.0 g/dL [1]. Globally, anaemia affects approximately 1.9 billion people, with women of reproductive age disproportionately affected [2]. It is estimated that more than 500 million women aged 15–49 years are anaemic worldwide [3]. In low‐ and middle‐income countries, especially in sub‐Saharan Africa and South Asia, where nutritional deficiencies, infectious diseases, and socioeconomic challenges contribute significantly to its prevalence, 30% of women of reproductive age are anaemic [1].

Anaemia poses serious health consequences. It can lead to fatigue, impaired mental and physical performance, reduced productivity, and increased risk of morbidity and mortality [4, 5]. Anaemia itself is an independent risk factor for mortality [6] and contributes to the progression and severity of other health conditions such as stunting in children and poor maternal and perinatal health consequences for pregnant women [1]. It is very prevalent in low‐and middle‐income countries and occurs mostly in reproductive age women and children at all stages of life [7]. Among women of reproductive age, anaemia is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including maternal mortality, preterm birth, and low birth weight [8].

Women of reproductive age are vulnerable to anaemia due to several biological and socioeconomic factors. Menstrual blood loss, repeated pregnancies, poor dietary intake of iron and other micronutrients, and limited access to healthcare services increase the risk of anaemia in this population [9]. Lesotho, a lower‐income country in southern Africa, faces several socioeconomic and health challenges that may increase the risk of anaemia among women. As of 2019, Lesotho had an anaemia prevalence of 27.90% among women of reproductive age [10]. The country also has a high prevalence of HIV infection, widespread poverty, and significant food insecurity [11, 12, 13]. Limited dietary diversity and inadequate intake of nutrient‐rich foods contribute to micronutrient deficiencies. Despite these challenges, limited research has examined the burden and determinants of anaemia among women of reproductive age in Lesotho using nationally representative data. Most existing studies in Lesotho have focused on anaemia among children or specific subpopulations.

While the 2023–2024 Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey (LDHS) provides valuable data on anaemia prevalence, it only presents descriptive statistics and does not comprehensively explore the determinants of anaemia among women of reproductive age or how these factors interact to influence anaemia risk within this population. Therefore, this study utilises the nationally representative LDHS dataset to conduct a focused analysis of anaemia among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Lesotho. By examining the prevalence and identifying factors associated with anaemia within this specific population, this study provides deeper analytical insights than those presented in the standard DHS report, thus providing evidence that can inform targeted public health interventions and policies.

Methods

Data Source and Study Design

The 2023–24 LDHS, conducted by the Ministry of Health, is the fourth survey of its kind, following those in 2004, 2009, and 2014. Cross‐sectional data collection took place from November 27, 2023, to February 29, 2024, with technical support from ICF through the DHS Programme, funded by USAID. Additional support was provided by the Lesotho Bureau of Statistics, UNICEF, UNAIDS, and other agencies. The primary objective of the survey is to provide updated demographic and health data. The sampling frame for the 2023–24 LDHS is based on the 2016 Lesotho Population and Housing Census (PHC). It includes all census enumeration areas (EAs), which are geographic units containing roughly 100 households, such as urban blocks or rural villages. Each EA is mapped with boundary details and household counts from the 2016 PHC. Lesotho is divided into 10 districts, further subdivided into constituencies and community councils [14]. Cross‐sectional data from 3192 women extracted from the 2023–2024 LDHS, conducted by the Ministry of Health was used. Data were collected from November 27, 2023, to February 29, 2024. Both descriptive and inferential analyses were performed.

The sample was stratified into urban, peri‐urban, and rural areas, resulting in 29 strata (excluding peri‐urban areas in Butha‐Buthe). In the first stage of sampling, 400 EAs were selected with probability proportional to size, and household listings were created for each selected EA. In the second stage, 25 households per EA were systematically chosen, and women aged 15–49 from these households were eligible for the Woman's Questionnaire. In a subsample of half the households, men aged 15–59 were also surveyed. These subsample households also participated in additional modules on chronic disease, mental health, domestic violence, and child well‐being. A sample of 9976 households was initially selected, with 9853 being occupied. Out of the occupied households, 9810 were successfully interviewed, resulting in a response rate exceeding 99%. Within these interviewed households, 6536 women aged 15–49 were deemed eligible for individual interviews, and 98% of them participated in and completed the interviews. However, in this study, 3105 non‐pregnant women with complete information on anaemia were included. Details of the survey methodology and sampling are provided in the published report [14].

Study Variables

Outcome Variable

Anaemia status was the outcome variable. In the 2023–24 LDHS, anaemia data were collected by Haemoglobin level in grams/decilitre using the WHO [15] new guidelines adjusting for smoking and altitude. Blood samples for anaemia testing were collected from consenting women aged 15 and older. A drop of blood was obtained through a finger prick and collected into a microcuvette. Haemoglobin levels were then measured on‐site using a portable, battery‐operated HemoCue 201+ device. Women were classed as anaemic when their Haemoglobin level in grams was less than < 12 g/dL (=1) and non‐anaemic when their Haemoglobin level in grams was ≥ 12.0 g/dL (=0) [14, 15].

Independent Variables

Several independent variables were considered in the study. These were selected based on their association with anaemia in previous studies, theoretical and practical significance [2, 9, 16, 17, 18]. These were: Age, level of education, marital status, wealth status, working status, parity, sex of household head, exposure to newspaper, television, radio, internet use, National Health Insurance subscription (NHIS), getting permission to visit health facility, money needed for treatment, distance to health facility, residence and region of residence. Data Analyses

Stata version 18.5 was used for data management and analysis. Approval to download the data was obtained from Measuredhs. Both descriptive and inferential analyses were used to analyze the data. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the variables. Bivariate analysis was also performed to examine the prevalence of anaemia across the independent variables. Prior to estimation, the author declared the primary sampling unit (cluster), the sampling stratum, and the individual sampling weight (v005 divided by 1,000,000) using the svyset command in Stata. Bivariate associations between anaemia and each categorical covariate were assessed using the design‐based test produced by svy: tabulate, that is the Rao and Scott second‐order corrected chi‐square statistic converted to an F statistic (Table S1). Since anaemia status was a dichotomous variable, a binomial logistic regression model was fitted to assess the predictors of anaemia status among women in Lesotho. The logistic regression model demonstrated good fit to the data (F(9, 361) = 0.59, p = 0.81), suggesting no evidence of model misfit [19]. Both crude and adjusted results from the binary logistic regression model were presented as crude odds ratios (CORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), respectively. Sample weights and the complex survey design of the data were incorporated into the analysis using the sample weight “wt” and “svy” functions in Stata, respectively. The variance inflation factor (vif) was used to check for collinearity among the variables, and the results showed no strong collinearity.

Results

Demographic Characteristics of Respondents

The demographic characteristics of the respondents are shown in Table 1. It was found that 19.9% of the respondents are aged 15–19 while 9.7% are aged 45–49. The majority (58.8%) have attained a secondary level of education, with only 0.7% having no formal education. With marital status, almost half (48.9%) of the respondents are married or cohabiting. About 53% (53.1%) are not working. Concerning wealth status, a quarter (25.4%) of the respondents are in richer households. Fifty‐six percent (56.1%) are in rural households, 31.8% in Maseru, 18.2% in Leribe and 3.0% in Qacha's Nek.

Crude and Adjusted Odds Ratios for Factors Associated With Anaemia Among Women of Reproductive Age in Lesotho (LDHS 2023–2024)

The results of the multivariable analysis of factors associated with anaemia among women in Lesotho are presented in Table 2. Compared with women aged 15–19 years, the odds of anaemia were significantly higher among those aged 20–24 (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI = 1.01–1.95), 30–34 (AOR = 1.60; 95% CI = 1.08–2.37), 35–39 (AOR = 1.66; 95% CI = 1.11–2.47), and 45–49 years, with the highest odds in the oldest group (AOR = 1.78; 95% CI = 1.17–2.70). With marital status, married or cohabiting women had lower odds of anaemia than those never in union (AOR = 0.55; 95% CI = 0.40–0.74). Women who had given birth once had lower odds of anaemia than nulliparous women (AOR = 0.64; 95% CI = 0.46–0.89). Frequent television viewing (at least once a week) was associated with higher odds of anaemia compared with no viewing (AOR = 1.53; 95% CI = 1.15–2.05). Women for whom obtaining money needed for healthcare was not a problem had lower odds of anaemia than those who reported it as a big problem (AOR = 0.73; 95% CI = 0.57–0.93). By region, women in Quthing (AOR = 0.60; 95% CI = 0.39–0.91) and Qacha's Nek (AOR = 0.61; 95% CI = 0.39–0.95) had lower odds of anaemia than those in Butha‐Buthe.

Discussion

The study assessed the prevalence and factors associated with anaemia among 3105 reproductive‐age women in Lesotho. The overall prevalence of anaemia was 53.7%, indicating a high burden of anaemia among women of reproductive age in Lesotho. The factors associated with anaemia were age, marital status [20, 21], parity, financial access to healthcare, and region of residence. The prevalence recorded in this study is higher than the global prevalence of anaemia reported by the World Health Organization in 2021 (30%) [9]. It is, however, similar to the prevalence reported in other lower‐ and middle‐income countries such as Ethiopia (50.1%) [22] and lower than the 76.7% reported in Pakistan [23] and 63% in Mali [9]. This difference in prevalence may stem from socioeconomic and cultural differences between the populations. Despite global efforts to reduce anaemia, progress has been uneven, with significant regional disparities [24]. While high‐income countries have made substantial gains through improved nutrition, iron fortification programmes, and better healthcare access, many low‐ and middle‐income countries continue to struggle with persistently high anaemia rates. This figure is almost double the prevalence of 27% reported in the 2014 Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey [25]. The differences between this study's estimate and earlier figures may partly reflect methodological differences between data sources, including differences in sampling, haemoglobin measurement, altitude and smoking adjustment, and the anaemia classification applied. This sharp rise in prevalence may reflect a growing burden of chronic conditions such as HIV/AIDS, increasing rates of infectious disease, and other contributing factors such as malnutrition [11, 26]. This mirrors the global trend reported by the WHO, which noted a rise in anaemia prevalence from 30% to 35% between 2011 and 2016 [15]. This trend is alarming and warrants comprehensive investigation and targeted public health interventions to address the multiple determinants of anaemia.

Although adolescents aged 15–19 had the highest prevalence of anaemia in the bivariable analysis, in the multivariable analysis, women aged 45–49 years had significantly higher odds of anaemia compared with those aged 15–19 years. They were at approximately twice the odds of developing anaemia (AOR = 1.78). This is a classic negative confounding (suppression) pattern: the adjusted estimate isolates the effect of age itself, whereas the crude prevalence reflects the combined influence of age and everything correlated with it. This is consistent with the findings of Le [27], who found that anaemia prevalence increased with age, with peak prevalence occurring in the age group 40–49 and over 80 years. A national survey in Ecuador also found that women aged 35–49 years were more likely to experience moderate to severe anaemia compared with younger age groups [28]. This may be attributed to cumulative nutrient depletion and increased prevalence of chronic diseases, which may contribute to anaemia prevalence [29]. Additionally, women within the age bracket of 45–49 may be experiencing perimenopause, which manifests with symptoms such as menorrhagia and increases anaemic tendencies [28].

Married or cohabiting women had significantly lower odds of anaemia (AOR = 0.55; 95% CI = 0.40–0.74). This aligns with the findings of Ismail et al. [30] and contrasts with the findings of prior studies [28, 31]. This could be due to differences in sample sizes and population demographics. Married and cohabiting women may have improved dietary habits, which may affect iron intake and overall nutrition. They may also have improved access to healthcare, such as prenatal counselling, due to shared financial resources. A study in Brazil showed that compared with women who were married or in a relationship, pregnant women without a partner had significantly higher odds of being anaemic, demonstrating the influence of a support system on health outcomes [32].

Women who had given birth once had significantly lower odds of anaemia (AOR = 0.64; 95% CI = 0.46–0.89) compared with those with no births. The odds of anaemia increased slightly for women with two or more births. Women with low parity tended to comply with antenatal care visit schedules [33]. Women experiencing their first pregnancy often took extra care to ensure the best outcomes. They were more likely to attend antenatal check‐ups, take iron supplements, and follow nutritional advice closely. However, with subsequent pregnancies, they may have felt more confident in their experience and, as a result, became less consistent with antenatal care, which could have increased their risk of anaemia. Many studies have also associated multiparity with higher risks of anaemia, likely due to the depletion of iron stores [9, 34, 35]. These insights underscore the importance of sustained antenatal care engagement across all pregnancies to minimise anaemia risk. It is therefore important to manage anaemia and iron deficiency beyond antenatal care settings.

The results also showed that television viewing was associated with anaemia. This association was not present in the bivariate analysis and emerged only after adjustment, which suggests it is a suppressed rather than a robust direct effect. The positive direction is also counterintuitive, since frequent television viewing typically co‐occurs with higher socioeconomic status, which is generally protective against anaemia. This finding is therefore interpreted cautiously, as a probable marker of unmeasured contextual factors (e.g., more urbanised dietary patterns or residual confounding not fully captured by the household wealth index) rather than a causal influence of television exposure on anaemia. This finding therefore warrants confirmation in studies with richer dietary and socioeconomic data.

Strengths and Limitations

The study has several notable strengths. First, the study used nationally representative data to assess the prevalence and factors associated with anaemia among women in Lesotho. The relatively large sample size and nationally representative nature of the data make it possible to generalise the findings to all women of reproductive age in Lesotho. Data on the outcome variable were collected following clinical procedures, taking into consideration the WHO guidelines [15]. However, since the study employed a cross‐sectional design, it did not allow causal interpretations of the findings. As this study relied on secondary data, several clinically relevant variables, including HIV status, inflammatory markers, malaria burden, and other nutritional biomarkers, were not included in the study. The absence of these factors introduces the potential for residual confounding, and associations with socioeconomic determinants should therefore be interpreted with caution, as their effects may be overestimated.

Conclusion

Anaemia remains a severe public health problem among women of reproductive age in Lesotho, affecting more than half of women aged 15–49 years. Age, marital status, parity, financial access to healthcare, and region of residence were associated with anaemia among women of reproductive age in Lesotho. Public health interventions aimed at improving nutrition, reducing socioeconomic barriers to healthcare, and strengthening anaemia prevention programmes may contribute to reducing the burden of anaemia among women in Lesotho. It is also recommended that the Ministry of Health of Lesotho and the Lesotho National Women's Parliamentary Caucus collaborate to allocate more resources to reduce or eradicate anaemia among women in Lesotho.
