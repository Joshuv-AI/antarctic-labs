Dynamic palladium catalysis enables chiral amplification toward acyclic Schiff base atropisomers
Authors: Qi Liu, Jun Gu, Shu-Yun Cui, Linghua Tan, Ying He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchiff bases, despite their structural versatility and broad applications, have remained elusive as acyclic atropisomers due to synthetic challenges. Herein, we report a palladium-catalyzed asymmetric N-allylation of diarylmethanimines with allylic precursors, enabling the construction of acyclic Schiff base atropisomers bearing C–C axes. Crucially, dynamic ligand exchange between the imine substrates and the palladium pre-catalyst drives its evolution from a less active to a more reactive species. This self-optimizing system leads to a unique time-dependent enhancement of enantioselectivity up to 96% ee. Experimental and density functional theory studies reveal that substrate-induced reorganization of the catalytic system optimizes the reaction pathway, leading to high yields (up to 83%) and absolute Z stereoselectivity. The method exhibits remarkable functional group tolerance and scalability, and its utility is demonstrated through diverse transformations. This work provides efficient access to previously inaccessible atropisomeric scaffolds and establishes a paradigm of substrate-guided catalyst evolution for asymmetric catalysis.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Asymmetric catalysis, Asymmetric synthesis

Introduction

Schiff bases (imines) have found extensive applications in diverse fields, including asymmetric catalysis, pharmaceutical design (e.g., antimicrobial/anticancer agents), functional materials (e.g., covalent organic frameworks, fluorescent probes), and analytical chemistry (Fig. 1A) 1–11. Their significance stems from the unique reactivity of the C = N bond, offering several key advantages: (1) These compounds feature straightforward synthesis, as they can be easily prepared under mild reaction conditions, typically via efficient one-pot methods 12; (2) The nitrogen’s lone pair confers exceptional coordination versatility, allowing these compounds to serve as effective ligands for diverse metal ions and enabling functional complex assembly 13; and (3) Their inherent chemical flexibility derives from the reactive C = N bond, which provides access to valuable derivatives including imines, oximes, and various heterocyclic structures 14–16. Despite these advances, the efficient synthesis of acyclic chiral Schiff base derivatives remains underdeveloped. Progress in this field has been mainly limited to desymmetrization and dynamic kinetic resolution for the synthesis of non-atropisomeric molecules 17–26. In contrast, the construction of Schiff base atropisomers remains a significant challenge and unexplored despite their potential applications in various fields27–30.

State of the art for the synthesis of acyclic Schiff base atropisomers.

Over the past decade, synthetic methodologies for axially chiral alkenes have advanced significantly, with numerous innovative strategies being developed 31–34. Leveraging these advances, our group has established an asymmetric allylic substitution-isomerization (AASI) approach for the synthesis of such molecules (Fig. 1B) 35–41. Building upon the structural framework of acyclic axially chiral alkenes, a simple C-to-N atom swap would facilitate the construction of Schiff base atropisomers. To this end, we hypothesize that the atropisomeric Schiff bases herein can be synthesized via functionalization of 1H-imines. While imines are conventionally employed as electrophiles due to their polarized C = N bond, the imine nitrogen retains latent nucleophilicity 42,43. This reactivity can be harnessed through the electrophile trapping under either transition-metal (TM) catalysis or organocatalysis. We envisioned that asymmetric allylic substitution44–48 of 1H-imines with designed allylic precursors might offer a unique route to access atropisomeric Schiff bases (Fig. 1C). Nevertheless, several significant hurdles must be overcome to realize this transformation: (1) The reaction must overcome the inherent difficulty in selectively generating a single geometry (Z- or E-) of Schiff base, as only one configuration leads to the axial chirality. (2) The Z/E isomers of acyclic Schiff base may interconvert during the reaction process, further complicating the stereochemical outcome. (3) Even upon successful formation of the desired products, its low rotational barrier (even lower than that of axially chiral alkenes) imposes stringent demands on reaction conditions to avoid the racemization of Schiff base atropisomers. These competing factors create a delicate balance where the reaction must simultaneously achieve high stereoselectivity while operating under conditions gentle enough to maintain the chiral axis integrity.

In this work, we describe a palladium-catalyzed asymmetric N-allylation that efficiently constructs acyclic Schiff base atropisomers with excellent yields and enantioselectivities (Fig. 1C). In contrast to conventional asymmetric catalysis where stereocontrol relies on preorganized chiral ligands, this work unveils a substrate-guided dynamic catalysis mechanism, wherein the imine substrate actively participates in restructuring the palladium complex. This process orchestrates a self-optimizing catalytic cycle that leads to stereochemical amplification. This unique interplay between substrate and catalyst, where the reactant serves as a ligand to steer enantioselectivity, represents a paradigm shift in asymmetric allylic functionalization, offering a versatile strategy to access challenging acyclic atropisomers with exceptional stereocontrol.

Results and Discussion

Given the efficiency of organocatalyzed asymmetric allylic substitution (AAS) 49–52, we initiated our investigation by screening a series of chiral Lewis bases in the asymmetric allylic substitution of 1H-imines with allylic carbonates (Fig. 2). The reactions provided the products in high yields with excellent Z-selectivity, but systematic variation of chiral Lewis bases, solvents, and substituents could not improve enantioselectivity beyond 60% enantiomeric excess (ee), underscoring the intrinsic challenges of stereocontrol in this transformation. (See Suppl. Information Table S1 for details). Notably, time-course analysis revealed that the ee of the product remained constant throughout the reaction, ruling out the possibility of kinetic resolution or dynamic kinetic resolution under the reaction conditions.

Given the established efficacy of TM-catalyzed AAS, we then pursued Pd-catalyzed asymmetric N-allylation44 to enhance stereocontrol of this transformation. However, we anticipated potential challenges, as the imine’s strong metal chelation may attenuate catalytic activity through inhibitory coordination effects. Gratifyingly, the Pd-catalyzed reaction of 1a with 2a proceeded smoothly at room temperature (rt) by using L1 as the ligand, but yielded product 3a in a racemic form (Table 1, entry 1). The bulkier phosphorus ligand L2 also led to 3a with low enantioselectivities (entry 2). To our delight, the use of L3 resulted in product 3a in good yield (84%) with moderate enantioselectivity (50% ee) (entry 3). Encouraged by this result, we evaluated a series of phosphoramidite ligands (entries 4-8). Among these, L8 proved most effective, delivering 3a in 81% yield with 63% ee (entry 8). Evaluation of solvent effects revealed that ethyl acetate provided superior performance compared to other solvents (entries 9-12). Lowering the reaction temperature dramatically improved the stereocontrol, affording 3a with up to 90% ee while maintaining good yields. (entries 13 and 14). Finally, base screening identified Li2CO3 as optimal for this transformation (entries 15-18). Notably, the reaction proceeded in good yield without base, albeit with marginally reduced enantioselectivity (entry 19).

Having optimized the reaction conditions, we next explored the substrate scope. As shown in Fig. 3, substrates 1 bearing naphthalene ring with C6 substituents reacted smoothly, delivering the corresponding products 3a-3d in good yields (71-80%) with excellent to high enantioselectivities (83–96% ee). The reaction also accommodated naphthalene rings with C7 substituents, yielding product 3e in 73% yield with 87% ee. However, it was incompatible with C3-substituted naphthalene analogues (3f). Notably, the reaction tolerated the 4,5-disubstituted naphthalenes, affording 3g in 75% yield with 83% ee. In addition, the quinolinyl substrate was well-tolerated, affording 3h with 81% yield and 81% ee. However, the replacement of naphthyl with phenyl groups resulted in significantly diminished enantioselectivity (3i and 3j), highlighting the critical role of planar fused-ring systems in effective chiral induction.

Next, we explored the substrate scope by varying the R¹ group in compounds 1. Regardless of whether they bore electron-withdrawing or electron-donating groups, para-substituted phenyl-containing substrates 1 reacted smoothly to give products 3k-3s in 67–83% yields with 93–96% ee. The absolute configuration of 3n was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray analysis (CCDC 2392496), and the configurations of other products were assigned by analogy (See Suppl. Information Table S6 and Fig. S1 for details). Remarkably, the reaction also tolerated benzyl chloride as a reactive group, affording 3t in 70% yield with 92% ee. In addition, substrates 1 bearing phenyl moieties with meta- and ortho-substituents were accommodated, yielding products 3u-3b’ in 67–83% yields with 90–96% ee. The disubstituted phenyl substrates also reacted efficiently, affording products 3c’-3e’ with excellent to high enantioselectivities, though 3e’ was obtained in moderate yield. When R1 was varied to a fused ring, the reaction afforded products 3f’-3h’ with 93–96% ee. The protocol also showed broad tolerance for heterocyclic R¹ substituents, generating products 3i’-3l’ with high enantioselectivities (91–95% ee) and good yields. However, a dramatic decrease in enantioselectivity was observed when R¹ was an alkyl group (3m’). Furthermore, the methoxy group of 1 was readily modified to other alkoxy groups (e.g., OEt, OiPr), affording products 3n’ and 3o’ in excellent yields and with high ee. Replacing methoxy group with a methyl group also proceeded efficiently to give 3p’ in 80% yield, while resulting in a slightly reduced ee of 75%. Screening of the ester group of 2 revealed that the COOiPr group was tolerated under the reaction conditions, affording 3q’ in 68% yield and 84% ee, whereas the methyl and benzyl esters gave lower enantioselectivities despite good yields.

Interestingly, during reaction optimization studies, the Pd-catalyzed asymmetric N-allylation of 1a and 2a at −35 oC showed a remarkable time-dependent enhancement in enantioselectivity, with the ee of product 3a increasing progressively from 50% at 6 h to 90% after 96 h (Fig. 4A). Intrigued by the time-dependent ee increase, we first assessed whether kinetic resolution of 3a could explain this phenomenon through selective consumption of one atropisomer. Given the presence of hydrolytically labile imine and ester moieties in 3a, we hypothesized several potential reactions may occur to generate compounds 4-7. However, our control experiments revealed the remarkable stability of 3a under the reaction conditions, and full mass balance recovery (> 98%) of 3a with no ee erosion ruled out kinetic resolution via selective degradation (Fig. 4B).

We then investigated whether the imine-containing products 3 could potentially participate in the catalytic cycle as chiral Lewis base or ligand53–61 that leads to chiral amplification (Fig. 4C). To test this hypothesis, we conducted a series of control experiments. The potential catalytic role of enantiopure product 3b (96% ee) was initially evaluated in the model reaction of 1a and 2a. As a result, only trace amounts of product 3a with complete absence of enantioselectivity were obtained at room temperature. This result indicated that the imine product 3a cannot function as an effective chiral Lewis base catalyst, thus excluding the product catalysis in the observed chiral amplification. Furthermore, the introduction of enantiopure 3b (with or without rac-L8) into the reaction system failed to induce any enantioselectivity development (Fig. 4C, eqs 2 and 3). Finally, adding enantiopure 3b (96% ee) to the early-stage reaction mixture did not significantly enhance the reaction rate or enantioselectivity (Fig. 4D). These results collectively exclude the possibility of product catalytic pathways or product-ligand displacement mechanisms, instead supporting that the observed enantioselectivity enhancement stems solely from progressive optimization of the original Pd catalytic system over time.

To probe the evolution of enantioselectivity, we performed the asymmetric N-allylation under condition 1. A prominent feature emerged that the ee of 3a rose sharply at low conversion and plateaued after approximately 20% conversion (Fig. 4E). This rapid initial ascent is indicative of an exceptionally fast self-optimization process, whereby a dynamic equilibrium favoring the highly selective catalytic species is established almost instantaneously. This finding implies that the formation and stabilization of the highly selective active catalytic species occur at the very early stage of the reaction process. Together with the phenomenon depicted in Fig. 4A, the observed behavior aligns fully with a dynamic equilibrium model. In this framework, the reaction provides a powerful initial driving force that shifts the catalyst population toward the most selective form, with the subsequent plateau marking the system’s approach to its optimal compositional state.

We then turned our attention to potential substrate effects on the observed enantioselectivity amplification (Fig. 5). First, we investigated the coordination behavior between Pd(OAc)2 and phosphoramidite ligand L8 for the model reaction of 1a with 2a. When employing a 1:1 ratio of L8 to Pd(OAc)2, pre-mixing the catalyst components led to rapid blackening of the solution, suggesting the decomposition of the under-ligated palladium species into inactive palladium black. As a result, only trace amounts of 3a were obtained at −35 oC (Fig. 5A). While the reaction proceeded sluggishly at room temperature, it afforded product 3a with an unexpected inverted enantioselectivity (−34% ee). This phenomenon stands in marked contrast to the high yields and positive enantioselectivities observed at higher ligand-to-metal ratio, suggesting that a Pd(L8)2 active species may be involved in the reaction. This result is consistent with previous studies on Pd-catalyzed AAS involved reactions by using phosphoramidites as chiral ligands 62–65.

To elucidate the coordination behavior of the catalyst, we conducted 31P NMR titration experiments in CDCl₃ with L8/Pd(OAc)2 ratios of both 1:1 and 2:1. Notably, the spectra for both ratios were highly similar, featuring two distinct peaks at 121.0 ppm and 120.1 ppm. This similarity indicates that, regardless of the initial stoichiometry, one palladium center preferentially coordinates with two phosphoramidite ligands to form the thermodynamically stable Pd(L8)2(OAc)2 resting state. The dual signals likely arise from slowly interconverting rotamers caused by the significant steric bulk of the ligand. Furthermore, in the 2:1 system (Fig. 5B), the free ligand resonance (δ 141.2 ppm) completely disappeared, confirming the quantitative formation of this 1:2 complex. Remarkably, the subsequent introduction of substrate 1a to the preformed Pd(L8)2(OAc)2 complex triggered two critical spectral changes: the reappearance of the free ligand resonance at δ 141.2 ppm and the concurrent emergence of a distinct new peak at 126.5 ppm. We assign this new signal to the proposed mono-ligated anionic active species, [L8-Pd-1A]− (where 1A− denotes the anionic form of 1a). These observations provide compelling experimental evidence that imine 1a actively participates in a ligand exchange process, displacing one sterically demanding L8 ligand to induce the structural reorganization necessary for the subsequent catalytic cycle.

Furthermore, in sharp contrast to the system in Fig. 5A, the reaction proceeded smoothly to full conversion when the Pd precatalyst was prepared with an additional equivalent of 1a, affording 3a in 88% ee within 120 h (Fig. 5C). This result underscores the decisive role of imine 1a in the catalyst activation process, demonstrating its participation in the transformation of the palladium complex from a less active precursor into a more reactive and selective species. Notably, under strongly basic conditions, the dicationic [1a-Pd-L8]2+ complex can readily undergo deprotonation to afford the monocationic [1A-Pd-L8]+ species. This key intermediate was unambiguously detected by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) during the reaction, thereby providing direct evidence for its participation in the catalytic system (Fig. 5D). Given that the true catalytic species in Pd-catalyzed AAS is Pd(0), we propose the possible pathways, as depicted in Fig. 5E: the anion of 1a (1A⁻) undergoes partial ligand exchange with Pd(L8)2 to access [1A-Pd-L8]− species, which then reacts with 2a to produce different η3-allylpalladium intermediates.

To unravel the reaction mechanism, we then performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the Pd-catalyzed reaction of 1a with 2a (Fig. 6). The exclusion of Li2CO3 from the computational model was rationally justified by experimental evidence demonstrating that the base-free system still achieved substantial efficiency (68% yield) and excellent stereocontrol (87% ee) for product 3a formation. (Table 1, entry 19). As shown in Fig. 6, Pd0(L*)2 was selected as the reference state. Rapid ligand exchange takes place to generate two possible palladium species: L*Pd(0)-1AM or L*Pd(0)-1AP. Driven by the dynamic transformation of the palladium complex, two distinct Pd-catalyzed asymmetric N-allylation pathways proceed. In the pathway mediated by Pd⁰(L*)2, the reaction initiates through π-activation of 2a by Pd⁰(L*)2, forming the stabilized intermediate Int1 with a relative energy of −13.1 kcal/mol. From Int1, oxidative addition proceeds via TS1 to form Int2, overcoming an overall barrier of 21.6 kcal/mol. Subsequently, Int2 evolves into the more stable intermediate Int3 at –10.2 kcal/mol. Int3 can undergo nucleophilic attack through three possible transition states: TS2(Z,M), TS3(Z,P), and TS4(E). Because TS3(Z,P) is lower in energy than TS2(Z,M) and TS4(E), Int4(Z,P) becomes the major intermediate, ultimately leading to the formation of product 3a with P configuration. In contrast, π-activation via the mono-ligated Pd0(L*) species (generated by L* dissociation) is energetically unfavorable, as evidenced by the high-energy intermediate TS10 at 34.0 kcal/mol.

In the pathway mediated by L*Pd(0)-1AM or L*Pd(0)-1AP, intermediates Int7 and Int12 are initially formed and subsequently convert into two key η3-allylpalladium species Int8 and Int13. Among them, Int8 is identified as the more favored intermediate, as evidenced by the relatively lower energy barrier required for its formation. Subsequently, the nucleophilic attack of 1a to Int8, facilitated by the OtBu anion, can access three competing transition states (TS6–TS8), thereby defining the stereodetermining step of the reaction. Among these, the pathway via TS6 is energetically favored, leading predominantly to Int9(Z,M), which ultimately furnishes the major product 3a with M configuration. Compared to the pathway catalyzed by Pd⁰(L*)₂, which yields the P-configured 3a (green line), the present route (blue line) is energetically more favorable and leads exclusively to the M-configured product 3a.

Based on combined experimental and computational results, as shown in Pathway 1 of Fig. 7, the catalytic cycle initiates through π-activation of 2a by the Pd(L8)2 complex, followed by oxidative addition to form the key η³-allyl-palladium intermediate Int3. The dissociative OtBu anion of Int3 subsequently assisted deprotonation of 1a to generate a nucleophilic species that attacks the Int3 to afford Int5, ultimately completing the cycle via dissociation that regenerates the Pd(L8)2 complex while releasing product ent-3a. Alternatively, the [1A-Pd-L8]⁻ species can be dynamically generated via ligand exchange from Pd(L8)2. As shown in Pathway 2, subsequent coordination of 2a to this intermediate leads to the formation of Int7. Oxidative addition of Int7 then affords the η3-allylpalladium intermediate Int8. Nucleophilic attack of imine 1a, assisted by the OtBu anion, on Int8 eventually yields Int9. The catalytic cycle concludes with product dissociation, releasing the desired product 3a and regenerating the active [1A-Pd-L8]⁻ complex for further turnover. The palladium species in this two catalytic cycle undergoes a dynamic transformation from a less active to a highly active state. In the reaction system, Pd(L8)2 and [1A-Pd-L8]⁻ species compete in the catalytic cycle. At the induction stage of the reaction, the imine substrate facilitates the transformation of the precursor complex into the [1A-Pd-L8]⁻ complex via ligand exchange or coordination stabilization. This species serves as the genuine active catalytic species characterized by superior enantioselectivity. Once the catalytic cycle is established, [1A-Pd-L8]⁻ remains the predominant active species throughout the process, effectively outcompeting the less selective Pd(L8)2 species. Consequently, the ee value of product 3a increases rapidly during the initial phase and subsequently reaches a plateau. This trend reflects the transition to a steady-state catalytic cycle where the high intrinsic enantioselectivity of the actual active species dictates the stereochemical outcome, even as the substrate concentration gradually depletes. This mechanistic model aligns well with the kinetic and enantioselectivity trends observed in Figs. 4A and 4E.

To explore the synthetic potential of the product, we conducted racemization, scale-up, and derivatization experiments (Fig. 8). The racemization of 3b in toluene at 50 °C revealed a rotational barrier of 27.65 kcal/mol and a half-life of 147.48 h (Fig. 8A), thus confirming its sufficiently good configurational stability for subsequent synthetic transformations (See Suppl. Information Table S3 for details). Furthermore, the reaction was successfully scaled up to 1.0 mmol, affording axially chiral Schiff base 3a in 70% yield with 90% ee (Fig. 8B), which demonstrates its practical robustness. Finally, we performed a series of diverse synthetic transformations to further illustrate the product’s utility as a versatile intermediate (Fig. 8C). The ester group in 3a was readily reduced using diisobutylaluminium hydride (DIBAL-H) to yield allylic alcohol 8 (91%, 90% ee). The sequential reduction and bromination of 3a produced 9 in 90% yield with 88% ee. Treatment of 3a with (Z)-N-hydroxybenzimidoyl chloride afforded isoxazole 10 in excellent yield with a 1/1 dr while maintaining high enantioselectivity. A double 1,2-addition yielded 11 (73%, 90% ee), and subsequent dehydration provided 12 in 81% yield with 90% ee. Additionally, hydrolysis of the ester group in 3a generated carboxylic acid 13 in 91% yield with 88% ee. Reaction of 3a with m-chloro-perbenzoic acid (m-CPBA) furnished compound 14 in high yield while maintaining the enantioselectivity.

In conclusion, we have developed a palladium-catalyzed asymmetric N-allylation for constructing acyclic Schiff base atropisomers through a substrate-guided dynamic catalysis mechanism. This work establishes a versatile platform for synthesizing these previously inaccessible chiral architectures with excellent yields (up to 83%) and enantioselectivities (up to 96% ee). The key breakthrough lies in the discovery that the imine substrates actively participate in reorganizing the catalytic system through dynamic ligand exchange. This unique substrate-catalyst interplay leads to progressive optimization of the reaction pathway, manifested in the observed time-dependent enhancement of enantioselectivity.

Beyond its synthetic utility, this study provides fundamental insights into dynamic stereocontrol in asymmetric catalysis. The substrate-guided mechanism represents a paradigm shift from traditional catalyst-dominated approaches, demonstrating how reactant structural features can be harnessed to guide selective transformations. The robustness of this method is highlighted by its broad functional group tolerance, scalability, and versatile down-stream derivatizations. These findings open avenues for designing dynamic catalytic systems that leverage substrate-catalyst cooperativity, accessing other challenging atropisomeric scaffolds, and developing asymmetric transformations where reactants actively participate in stereocontrol. The principles uncovered here may inspire innovative strategies for addressing long-standing challenges in asymmetric synthesis and catalyst design.

Method

General procedure for the synthesis of acyclic Schiff base atropisomers

In a glovebox filled with argon, Pd(OAc)2 (0.01 mmol, 10 mol%), and L8 (25 mol%) were added to a vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar. The vial was then charged with toluene (0.5 mL) and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, Li2CO3 (0.2 mmol) and compound 2 (0.3 mmol) were added. The vial was removed from the glovebox and transferred to a cryogenic reactor pre-cooled to −35 oC. Afterwards, compound 1 (0.1 mmol) was dissolved in toluene (0.5 mL) and then added to the vial. The reaction was carried out under an argon atmosphere at −35 oC, and monitored by thin layer chromatography (TLC). After the reaction was completed, the crude product was purified by column chromatography on silica gel to afford the desired products 3.
