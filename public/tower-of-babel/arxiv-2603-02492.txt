E-variables and tests of randomness for distribution classes
Georgii Potapov, Yuri Kalnishkan

Abstract
E E -variables are a relatively new approach for testing statistical hypotheses that has been experiencing major development during the last several years. In this paper we introduce the method of e e -variable-approximability and use it to develop a general approximation technique allowing us to construct e e -variables for popular distribution classes important for applications. E E -variables were originally based on a concept of Levin’s (average-bounded) randomness tests from Algorithmic Information Theory. We show that our construction of e e -variables can be used to provide an explicit construction for a randomness test with respect to a class of distributions.



1
Introductions

1.1
History

The problem of statistical hypothesis testing, that is, the problem of deciding whether given data can be described by a certain hypothesis, is naturally important in scientific research as well as applications.

Traditionally, hypothesis testing is done using
p
-values. Intuitively, a
p
-value is the probability under the null hypothesis that a chosen statistic on the given data is at least as extreme as its observed value. This defines a derivative statistic that we would call
p
-variable. Despite being widely used,
p
-values lack certain “natural” properties (for example, the value of a
p
-variable on a randomly chosen initial segment of the data, in general, is not a
p
-variable itself), making them easy to accidentally misuse.

E
-variables
are statistical tests that can be viewed as a flexible and versatile alternative to
p
-variables for statistical hypothesis testing. Formally, an
e
-variable is a non-negative statistical test with expected value at most
1
(
[
15
]
).

Their use allows for a flexible experiment design that allows to test against the null for given alternative hypothesis and lacks many disadvantages of the commonly used
p
-values.
E
-variables are easy to combine, which simplifies meta-analysis, and running product of
e
-variables on independent bathces of data is a supermartingale, and thus continues to be an
e
-variable at random stopping time (
[
13
,
4
]
).

In this work we revisit the conceptual origin of
e
-variables —
randomness deficiency
. Randomness deficiency, introduced by Levin (
[
8
]
,
[
7
]
) is a concept from Algorithmic Information Theory, which can be loosely defined as an optimal test of randomness which exponent is an
e
-variable. Informally speaking, randomness deficiency is a function that counts number of regularities of its argument, a random object, and serves as a measure of how implausible an outcome of an experiment is (
[
10
]
).

The papers
[
5
]
and
[
14
]
give different but related definitions of a random
“Bernoulli bit sequence”
in terms of algorithmic randomness. The differences in these definitions correspond to differences between distributions of sequences with i.i.d. bits and exchangeable distributions, and can be quantified using randomness deficiency of number of
1
s in the sequence.

One of the results of papers
[
14
]
and
[
12
]
, mentioned in our paper as Theorem
2
, states that, in terms of randomness deficiency, it is approximately as plausible for an integer
k
between
0
and
n
to be obtained from
some
binomial distribution as it would be plausible for
k
to be obtained from the uniform distribution on integer intervals
[s_{j}:s_{j+1}-1]\ni k
, where
\{s_{1}<s_{2}\ldots<s_{m}\}
is some set that depends only on
n
.

Randomness tests relative to a class of distributions were more generally studied in
[
1
]
, where their existence was proven from a general topological argument for the Cantor space and effectively closed classes of measures.

1.2
Our contribution

One of the goals of our research is to generalise the results of
[
14
]
and
[
1
]
to make them more applicable to practical machine learning.

Regarding algorithmic randomness, our main results are theorems
5
and
6
, which claim lower semi-computability of randomness tests for a class of distribution for several important distribution families, such as uniform distributions on intervals, Poisson distributions, normal (Gaussian) distributions and Cauchy distributions. Furthermore, our results explicitly express randomness with respect to a class in terms of universal uniform tests of randomness.

Compared to the similar statement in
[
1
]
, we work in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
rather than on the Cantor set. Our results are proven for classes of distributions that do not constitute topologically closed sets in the space of probability distributions (for example, the sequence of uniform distributions on
[0:n]
should converge to the measure which is zero on all numbers, which is not a uniform measure on a finite integer segment).

Theorems
5
and
6
follow from theorems
3
and
4
, which can be seen as their non-algorithmic, purely statistical counterparts. We provide a uniform way to turn any set of
e
-variables for simple hypotheses
\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}
into an
e
-variable for the composite hypothesis
\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
. Our construction is not based on either reverse information projection or calibration of
p
-variables, and can use any
e
-variables for simple hypotheses as black boxes to produce a valid
e
-variable for the corresponding complex hypothesis.

Both our statistical and algorithmical results are built on the use of complexity reducing parameter estimation, which can be seen as another theoretical justification for minimum description length principle.

2.2
Computable and semicomputable functions

We use a standard definition of computable and lower (upper) semicomputable functions that follows
[
2
]
and
[

Definition 2.3
.

A function

is
lower semicomputable
(l.s.c.) if its undergraph
\{(x,t):t<f(x)\}
can be presented as a union
\bigcup_{(m,n)\in W}B_{m}\times[-\infty,r_{n})
with
W\subseteq\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
an enumerable set and
(r_{i})_{i}
and

being pre-specified enumerations of
\mathbb{Q}
and of a countable topological base of
\mathbf{D}
, respectfully.

We also say that

is upper semicomputable (u.s.c.) if
-f
is l.s.c., and
f
is computable when it is both l.s.c. and u.s.c.

A more general definition of computability (needed for theorems

and

Definition 2.4
.

In general, we say that a function
f:X\to Y
is computable if, for some prespecified enumerations of countable bases
\mathbf{B}^{X}=\left(B_{1}^{X},B_{2}^{X},\ldots\right)
and
\mathbf{B}^{Y}=\left(B_{1}^{Y},B_{2}^{Y},\ldots\right)
of

and

\bigcup_{k}f^{-1}\left(B_{k}^{Y}\right)\times B_{k}^{Y}=\bigcup_{(m,n)\in W}B_{m}^{X}\times B_{n}^{Y}

for some enumerable set
W
.

Remark
.

Note that lower semicomputability implies lower semicontinuity, upper semicomputability implies upper semicontinuity and computability implies continuity.

In this paper, we consider (semi)computability of functions defined either on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{R})\times\{0,1\}^{*}
or
\mathbb{N}\times\mathfrak{P}(\mathbb{N})\times\{0,1\}^{*}
. Slightly informally, we can think that function
f
is lower semicomputable on such domain if there is an oracle Turing machine that approximates

from below and accesses “infinite” components of its input

through oracles.

Oracle for a real number or a distribution, on a rational input
\varepsilon>0
, returns an
\varepsilon
-appoximation of the object.

An
\varepsilon
-approximation of a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
would be a measure
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
concentrated on a finite number of rational points

and assigning them rational probabilities
p_{i}
, such that
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}
is
\varepsilon
-close to
\mathbb{P}
in Lévy–Prokhorov metric, that is, for all measurable sets

we have

\mathbb{P}(A)<\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}(A^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon,~~~\mathbb{P}(A^{\varepsilon})>\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}(A)-\varepsilon,

where
A^{\varepsilon}
is the
\varepsilon
-neighbourhood of
A
.

2.3
Randomness tests

Definition 2.5

(Randomness test)
.

For a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
, consider a function
f(\cdot|\cdot,\cdot):\mathcal{X}\times\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty]
, which takes as its arguments an object

, a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathcal{X}
and an auxiliary object in
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. We say that

is a
uniform randomness test
(or a test of randomness in a sense of Levin), if it is:

1.

a lower semicomputable function (assuming an appropriate computable structure on
\mathcal{X},\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
\mathcal{Y}
),

2.

for all probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and all strings
y
the function
f(\cdot\mid\mathbb{P},y)
is an
e
-variable for the simple hypothesis
\{\mathbb{P}\}
.

From a practical point of view, lower semi-computability is a very mild restriction that allows us to construct a universal test of randomness (described below). Philosophically, lower semi-computability represents the idea that, if an
e
-variable rejects the null with a chosen significance level, than this must be eventually established from the data measured with high enough but finite precision.

The next theorem-definition is a well known result.

Theorem 1

(existence and definition of a universal uniform test (

From now on we are going to use symbols

The current paper is mainly concerned with studying the following object.

3.2
Interpolating approximation

Discontinuity of

For

Definition 3.2

We say that the family

whenever all

are

Theorem 4

For

The proof illustrates flexibility of

-variable approximability property.

Proof.

Notice that an

-variable
For a given family

in the following way. Let

Then there is some

4
Lower semicomputable approximations of randomness deficiency
\inf
-projection

We say that a family of distributions
\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is computable if the function
(\theta\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{\theta})
is computable.

Lemma 4
.

For a computable family of distributions
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
on a discrete set
\mathcal{X}
that is
e
-variable-approximable from a net
\mathcal{S}
with a computable estimator
\hat{s}
and factor
C
, then there is a randomness test
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty]
such that

f(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\theta},y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y)

for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

Sketch of the proof.

The proof technique is standard for the field.
Lower semi-computability of
\mathbf{t}
together with computability of
\mathbb{P}
allows us to enumerate a list of bounded continuous piece-wise linear functions
0=g_{0}\leq g_{1}\leq\ldots
with finite description, with
g_{n}(x,y)\uparrow\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y)
. For these function we can estimate integrals
\int g_{n}(x,y)\mathbb{P}({\rm d}x)
. Without loss of generality, we say that
C\geq 1
and rational. We can then define

f(x\mid\mathbb{P},y)=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{n}\left\{g_{n}(x,y)\mid\int g_{n}(x,y)\mathbb{P}({\rm d}x)<2C\right\}.

From
e
-variable-approximability it follows that the condition on integral being less than
2C
is always true for
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, and thus
f=^{\times}\mathbf{t}
in this case.
∎

Theorem 5
.

For a computable family
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
that is
e
-variable-approximable with a computable estimator
\hat{s}
, it holds that

\mathbf{t}^{\mathcal{P}}(x\mid y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y).

Proof.

Let
f
be the uniform test from lemma
4
.

For any
\theta

f(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\theta},y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y),

where the right hand term does not depend on
\theta
.

We get then, that for any
\theta

f(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\theta},y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y)\geq\mathbf{t}^{\mathcal{P}}(x\mid y).

From optimality of randomness deficiency we also obtain that

\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\theta},y)\geq^{\times}f(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\theta},y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y).

Since constants under
\geq^{\times},=^{\times}
do not depend on
x,\theta,y
, we can take the infimum over all
\theta
and get

\mathbf{t}^{\mathcal{P}}(x\mid y)\geq^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\hat{s}(x)},y),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5
.

For a computable family of distributions
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
that is continuously
e
-variable-approximable from a uniformely computable net
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
,
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\hat{s}
being l.s.c. uniformely in both arguments, and factor

, then there is a randomness test
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathfrak{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty]
such that

f(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\theta},y)=^{\times}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{s},y)\hat{s}(x,s)

for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
be either
\{\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)\}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
or
\{\operatorname{Cauchy}(\theta,1)\}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
. Let
n
be an integer and
x\in[n,n+1]
. Then

\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{n},y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{n+1},y).

This lemma can be proven using the same technique as lemma
4
after noticing that there is a computable function on distribution that on the elements of
\mathcal{P}
recovers the parameter. An example of such function would be a function that computes
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\arctan(X)
for given
\mathbb{P}
and recovers the would-be parameter by assuming that
\mathbb{P}
is from
\mathcal{P}
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
be either
\{\mathcal{N}(\theta,1)\}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
or
\{\operatorname{Cauchy}(\theta,1)\}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
. Then

\mathbf{t}^{\mathcal{P}}(x\mid y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor},y).

Proof.

Let
f
be the uniform test from lemma
5
and
\hat{s}(x,n)=\mathbb{I}^{0.2}_{[n-0.4,n+0.4]}(x)
in accordance with theorem

. Then, just as in the proof of theorem

\mathbf{t}^{\mathcal{P}}(x\mid y)=^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor},y)\hat{s}(x,\lfloor x\rfloor)+\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor+1},y)\hat{s}(x,\lfloor x\rfloor+1).

Now, from lemma

\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor},y)\hat{s}(x,\lfloor x\rfloor)+\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor+1},y)\hat{s}(x,\lfloor x\rfloor+1)\geq^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor},y),

\mathbf{t}^{\mathcal{P}}(x\mid y)\geq^{\times}\mathbf{t}(x\mid\mathbb{P}_{\lfloor x\rfloor},y)\geq\mathbf{t}(x\mid y).

∎

5
Discussions

5.1
Discussion of definition
3.1

In Section
3
we introduced the notion of
e
-variable approximability.
This notion might not seem very intuitive, so let us consider a few examples when seemingly more intuitive ways to combine
e
-variables fail.

First, we will demonstrate that using a simple
\inf
-projection (
x\mapsto\inf_{\theta}e_{\theta}(x)
) might lead to non-measurable functions.

Example 1
.

Let us consider a family of normal distributions
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}
and some non-measurable set
L\subset\mathbb{R}
. For each
\mu\in L
let
e_{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{I}\{x\neq\mu\}
, and for all
\mu\notin L
let
e_{\mu}\equiv 1
. Then all the functions
e_{\mu}
are measurable with
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}e_{\mu}(X)=1
, but the function
\inf_{\mu}e_{\mu}(x)=\mathbb{I}\{x\notin L\}
is not measurable and thus cannot serve as a statistical test.

Remark
.

Taking an infimum over a countable set would preserve measurability, but it is weaker (up to a multiplicative constant) than our proposed approach and, what is also important for us, does not preserve lower semi-computability.

The next two example shows that for non-trivial families we cannot use maximum likelihood estimator for
\hat{s}

Example 2
.

Let us consider a family of normal distributions
\{\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\}_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}
and an arbitrary constant
C>)
. For each value of
\mu
we define a function
e_{\mu}(x)=C\cdot 10^{100}\mathbb{I}\{x=\mu\}
. Then, obviously, each
e_{\mu}
is measurable and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}e_{\mu}(X)=0\leq 1
, hence all
e_{\mu}
are
e
-variables, but at the same time for all
\mu
we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\frac{1}{C}e_{\operatorname{MLE}(x)}(x)=\frac{1}{C}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}e_{x}(x)=10^{100}>1
.

Example 3
.

Let us consider a family of Poisson distributions
\{\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)\}_{\lambda>0}
, then
\operatorname{MLE}(x)=x
. For each natural number
n
define a function
e_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Pois}(n)(\{n\})}\mathbb{I}\{x=n\}=\exp(n)\frac{n!}{n^{n}}\mathbb{I}\{x=n\}
. Then each
e_{n}
is measurable and
\mathbb{E}_{n}e_{n}(X)=1
, hence all
e_{n}
are
e
-variables, but at the same time for all
\lambda>0
we have
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}e_{\operatorname{MLE}(X)}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}e_{X}(X)=\exp(X)\frac{X!}{X^{X}}>1
.

Moreover, since
\exp(x)\frac{x!}{x^{x}}\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}
, for any
C>0
there will be a large enough
\lambda
for which
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\frac{1}{C}e_{X}(X)>1
.

5.2
Further research

There are several directions which we think are worth investigating further.

In theorem
3
we see that there may be many different nets for the same family of distributions and even many different estimators for the same net. This naturally leads to the question of characterising the nets and finding which nets and estimators allow the optimal normalising factor.

Estimators in our work are based on the idea that the data represents information about the distribution that is distorted by random noise, so we need to “trim” the data in order to reduce the impact of the noise while leaving useful information. This view is closely related to the Minimum Description Length principle, and it might be interesting to explore further how theory behind MDL principle can be used to improve our results.

is convex, the function
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\eta}A
is non-decreasing, so, whenever
\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}\leq\eta_{3}
or
\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}\geq\eta_{3}
, the differences
(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})
and
\left(\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\eta}A\right)(\eta_{2})-\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\eta}A\right)(\eta_{3})\right)
are of the same sign, meaning

\operatorname{kl}(\eta_{3}\|\eta_{1})\geq\operatorname{kl}(\eta_{3}\|\eta_{2})+\operatorname{kl}(\eta_{2}\|\eta_{1}),

which completes the proof.
∎

B.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Proof for uniform distributions.

For both discrete and continuous uniform distribution families the proof can be done directly.

For
2^{n}<x\leq 2^{n+1}
we get
\hat{s}(x)=2^{n+1}
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}e_{\hat{s}(X)}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{N}\frac{1}{N+1}e_{\hat{s}(x)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\sum_{2^{n}<x\leq 2^{n+1}}\frac{1}{N+1}e_{\hat{s}(x)}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n}\frac{2^{n+1}+1}{N+1}\underbrace{\sum_{x\leq 2^{n+1}}\frac{1}{2^{n+1}+1}e_{2^{n+1}}(x)}_{\mathbb{E}_{2^{n+1}}e_{2^{n+1}}(X)\leq 1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n}\frac{2^{n+1}+1}{N+1}\leq 3,

Meaning that
\mathbb{E}_{N}\frac{1}{3}e_{\hat{s}(X)}(X)\leq 1
for all
N
.

The proof for continuous case is essentially the same.
∎

Proof for Poisson distributions.

The density function
p_{\lambda}(n)
of
\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)
is

p_{\lambda}(n)=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{n!}\exp(n\log\lambda-\lambda).

To apply Lemma
3
, we are switching to parametrisation
\eta=\log\lambda
, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}f=\mathbb{E}_{\operatorname{Pois}(\mathrm{e}^{\eta})}f
.

Notice that
n=\mathbb{E}_{\log n}X
, and so by Lemma
3
we have
(p i)
and
(p i’)
for
d=\operatorname{kl}
and
g=\log
.

When parametrising distributions with
\eta
, we have need to work with
\mathcal{S}_{H}=\{2\log t\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}}
. We also have

\operatorname{kl}(\eta_{1}\|\eta_{2})=\mathrm{e}^{\eta}_{1}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})-(\mathrm{e}^{\eta_{1}}-\mathrm{e}^{\eta_{2}})=\lambda_{1}\log\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})=\operatorname{kl}_{\lambda}(\lambda_{1}\|\lambda_{2}).

To prove
(p ii)
, take any
t^{2}
and
n\in\hat{s}^{-1}(t^{2})
. Notice that
|n-t^{2}|\leq t
. We have:

\displaystyle\operatorname{kl}(\log n\|\log t^{2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{kl}_{\lambda}(n\|t^{2})

\displaystyle=n\log\frac{n}{t^{2}}-(n-t^{2})

\displaystyle=n\log\left(1+\frac{n-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)-(n-t^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{n}{t^{2}}-1\right)(n-t^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-t^{2})^{2}}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1.

Property
(p iii)
holds trivially since
\hat{s}
sends a number to one of the nearest complete squares — elements of
\mathcal{S}
.

Property
(p ii’)
is proven similarly to
(p ii)
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{kl}(\log t^{2}\|\log(t+1)^{2})

\displaystyle=t^{2}\log\frac{t^{2}}{(t+1)^{2}}-(t^{2}-(t+1)^{2})

\displaystyle=-2t^{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)+2t+1

\displaystyle\geq-2t^{2}\frac{1}{t}+2t+1

\displaystyle=1.

\displaystyle\operatorname{kl}(\log(t+1)^{2}\|\log t^{2})

\displaystyle=(t+1)^{2}\log\frac{(t+1)^{2}}{t^{2}}-((t+1)^{2}-t^{2})

\displaystyle=2(t+1)^{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{t}\right)-2t-1

\displaystyle\geq 2(t+1)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\right)-2t-1

\displaystyle=\frac{(t+1)^{2}(2t-1)-(2t+1)t^{2}}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2t^{2}-1}{t^{2}}\geq 1.

Since we now have all
(p i)
–
(p iii)
,
(p i’)
,
(p ii’)
,
e
-value approximability of
\{\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)\}_{\lambda>0}
follows from Lemma
2
.
∎

Proof for normal distributions with fixed variance.

Density
p_{\mu}
of
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)^{\otimes n}
is

p_{\mu}(x_{1:n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|x_{1:n}^{2}\|/2}\exp\left(\mu\sum_{k}x_{k}-\frac{n\mu^{2}}{2}\right),

and, since
\sum_{k}x_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\frac{\sum x_{k}}{n}}\sum_{k}X_{k}
from Lemma
3
we get properties
(p i)
and
(p i’)
for
d(\theta_{1}\|\theta_{2})=\operatorname{kl}(\theta_{1}\|\theta_{2})=\frac{n}{2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})^{2}
and
g(x_{1:n})=\frac{\sum_{k}x_{k}}{n}
.

To prove property
(p ii)
, we note that
x_{1:n}\in\hat{s}^{-1}(s)
means that
\left|\frac{\sum_{k}x_{k}}{n}-s\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\sqrt{n}}
, and so

d(g(x_{1:n})\|s)=\frac{n}{2}(g(x_{1:n})-s)^{2}\leq\frac{n}{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4n}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{8}.

Since
\hat{s}(x)=\operatorname{round}_{\mathcal{S}}(g(x))
, the property
(p iii)
is trivial.

The difference between the consecutive elements
s,s^{\prime}
of the net is exactly
\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n}}
, so

d(s\|s^{\prime})=\frac{n}{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{n}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2},

which establishes property
(p ii’)
and finishes the proof.
∎

Normal distribution with zero mean value.

We have

p_{\sigma^{2}}(x_{1:n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x_{1:n}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}-\frac{n}{2}\log\sigma^{2}\right).

Since
\|x_{1:n}\|^{2}=\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}
and all the coordinates are i.i.d., we have
T(x_{1:n})=\mathbb{E}_{g(x_{1:n})}T(X_{1:n})
for
T(x_{1:n})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}
and
g(x_{1:n})=\frac{\|x_{1:n}\|^{2}}{n}
.

As canonical parametrisation
\eta=1/\sigma^{2}
reverses order, we still get the reverse triangle inequality
(p i’)
when defining
d=\operatorname{kl}
in terms of variance, and thus can continue to work in standard parametrisation.

For two variances
\sigma^{2}_{1},\sigma^{2}_{2}
, we have

\operatorname{kl}(\sigma^{2}_{1}\|\sigma^{2}_{2})=\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}_{1}}{\sigma^{2}_{2}}-\log\frac{\sigma^{2}_{1}}{\sigma^{2}_{2}}-1\right).

Since
\hat{s}(x)=\operatorname{round}_{\mathcal{S}}(g(x))
, we immediately get
(p iii)
. We also have that for any
s=\hat{s}(x)
that
g(x)/s\in\left[\frac{\sqrt{n}}{1+\sqrt{n}},\frac{1+\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]
, and for consecutive
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
we have
s/s^{\prime}\in\left\{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{1+\sqrt{n}},\frac{1+\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}
.

Let
h(t)=\frac{n}{2}\left(t-\log t-1\right)
, then
\operatorname{kl}(\sigma^{2}_{1}\|\sigma^{2}_{2})=h\left(\sigma^{2}_{1}/\sigma^{2}_{2}\right)
.

First, we will show
(p ii’)
.

\displaystyle h\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-\underbrace{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)}_{\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{1}{4n}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{4n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8},

\displaystyle h\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{1+\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{n}}-\underbrace{\log\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{n}}\right)}_{\leq-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{n}}-\frac{1}{4\left(1+\sqrt{n}\right)^{2}}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{4\left(1+\sqrt{n}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{32},

so for any two consecutive elements
s,s^{\prime}
of the net we have
d(s\|s^{\prime})\geq 1/32
.

Now, to show
(p ii)
, let
t
be between
-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{n}}
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

\displaystyle h(1+t)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}((1+t)-\underbrace{\log(1+t)}_{\geq t-t^{2}}-1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{2}(t-t+t^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|t|^{2}n}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\frac{n}{2}=\frac{1}{2}

∎

Proof for Cauchy distributions.

For Cauchy distributions, we have
p_{\theta}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+(x-\theta)^{2})}
, and so

\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{p_{s}(x)}=\log\left(1+(x-s)^{2}\right)-\log\left(1+(x-\theta)^{2}\right).

Take
g(x)=x
,
d(\theta_{1}\|\theta_{2})=\log\left(1+(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})^{2}\right)\geq\log 1=0
.

For
\hat{s}=r^{\varepsilon}
, we have that
x\in\hat{s}^{-1}(n)\implies x\in[n-0.5-\varepsilon,n+0.5+\varepsilon]
, so

d(x\|n)\leq\log(1+(0.5+\varepsilon)^{2})\leq\log 2.

Property
(p iii)
for
r^{\varepsilon}
follows from the same observation in a straightforward manner.

Now, assume there are
k
integers between
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. Then
|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\geq k-1
and

d(\theta_{1}\|\theta_{2})=\log\left(1+(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})^{2}\right)\geq 2\log(k-1).

The result now follows from Lemma
1
.
∎

Proof for normal distributions with

.

The proof is mostly identical to the proof for
\operatorname{round}_{\mathcal{S}}
with a slightly weaker bounds.
