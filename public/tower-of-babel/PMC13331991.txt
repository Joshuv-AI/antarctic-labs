Photocontrolled biomimetic PEEK scaffold with black phosphorus-strontium ion cascade release for vascularized bone regeneration
Authors: Fang Guo, BingQian Wang, Xiaoning Su, Xingxing Lv, Shuo Huang, Yayuan Guo, Yang Xue, Changkui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective repair of large bone defects poses a major clinical challenge. Traditional 3D printed polyetheretherketone (PEEK) scaffolds provide customized structures and mechanical adaptability. However, their biological inertness and lack of dynamic bioactive microenvironment limit vascular regeneration and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately limiting overall bone regeneration. This study combined photothermal responsive black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) with strontium ions (Sr2+), and further modified the combination with polydopamine (PDA) to construct ‘PDA@BP-Sr Nanocomposites’; subsequently a photo responsive biomimetic porous PEEK scaffold (PEEK-PDA@BP-Sr, PPBS) was developed. Under near-infrared light-induced controllable mild photothermal conditions (40-42 °C), this porous scaffold enables the time-dependent release of bioactive ions, significantly promoting the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Moreover, it activates the ERK1/2 signaling pathway by upregulating HSP70 expression, effectively regulating the osteogenic differentiation of rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (rBMSCs) in vitro. In a rabbit femoral defect model, in-vivo experiments further confirmed that NIR light irradiation enables the PPBS scaffold to dynamically regulate the vascularization bone formation coupling. This promotes early vascularization and bone regeneration, significantly accelerating bone defect repair. This study successfully developed a novel bone repair scaffold that combines structural biomimicry, mechanical adaptation, and efficient biological activity. Additionally, the study also provides important technical support for bone defect repair under conditions of insufficient blood supply.Keywords: Black phosphorus nanosheets, Sr2+, PEEK scaffolds, Near-infrared, Angiogenesis, Osteogenesis

Introduction

The repair of large-scale bone defects remains a key challenge that requires urgent resolution in craniofacial surgery and orthopedic clinical practice. The defects can occur due trauma, tumor resection, congenital developmental abnormalities, and chronic bone diseases [1,2]. As a highly vascularized tissue, the development and remodeling of bone depend on the precise spatiotemporal synergy between the vascular system and osteoblasts [3]. The vascular system supplies oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste transport to bone tissue and secretes angiogenic factors that can directly regulate osteoblast activity and bone matrix deposition, forming a coupled network of “angiogenesis bone formation” [4]. Insufficient early vascularization at the site of bone defects is the core cause of repair failure, often manifested as fibrosis wrapping around the implant, bone necrosis, and poor bone integration at the interface [5]. Although bone tissue engineering is a promising alternative to autologous or allogeneic transplantation, current biomaterials are limited by bottlenecks such as insufficient initiation of early vascularization and lack of coordinated regulation between osteogenesis and vascularization, restricting clinical translational efficacy.

3D printing technology provides innovative means for constructing biomimetic bone repair scaffolds by precisely controlling the geometric topology, pore connectivity, and mechanical microenvironment of the scaffold [6]. The elastic modulus (3-4 GPa) of polyetheretherketone (PEEK) is close to the higher range of natural trabecular bone and significantly lower than that of metal materials, making it one of the most promising alternatives to metal materials such as titanium alloys in bone repair scenarios where stress shielding effects need to be reduced. [7,8]. However, the biologically inert surface of PEEK can easily cause fiber wrapping around the implant instead of functional bone integration [9,10]. Especially in dynamic load areas such as the temporomandibular joint, complications such as implant loosening and displacement are more likely to occur [11,12]. Although traditional surface modification strategies such as acid-base etching, porous treatment, and active ingredient doping can improve the surface properties of PEEK to a certain extent, major limitations remain. Physical and chemical etching is difficult to achieve uniform modification inside complex shaped porous scaffolds [11]. The introduction of active ingredients such as β-TCP often comes at the cost of sacrificing mechanical strength [13]. More importantly, current technologies lack synergistic regulation of “early angiogenesis initiation” and “osteoinduction”, impeding vascular bone coupled regenerative microenvironment around the implant, and instead promotes fibrous isolation layers limiting osseointegration. The above problems seriously restrict the clinical application of PEEK scaffolds, and there is an urgent need to develop a new material system that combines biomimetic interfaces, mechanical adaptability, and vascular osteogenic synergistic regulation functions.

Black Phosphorus Nanosheets (BPNSs), as an emerging two-dimensional layered material, have shown significant advantages in tissue repair due to their high specific surface area, excellent conductivity, and good biocompatibility [[14], [15], [16]]. However, its surface phosphorus atom lone pair electrons are prone to react with oxygen/water, resulting in insufficient physiological stability [17,18]. Recent studies have shown that metal ions with vacant orbitals can enhance physiological stability and impart additional biological activity by forming coordination bonds with lone pair electrons on BPNS surfaces [19]. Strontium ion (Sr2+) is a metal ion with dual biological effects. Sr2+can upregulate the expression of osteogenic related genes (such as Runx2 and OCN) in mesenchymal stem cells, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation [[20], [21], [22]]. Notably, Sr2+ can also actively participate in the process of angiogenesis by regulating the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells [20,23]. This provides an ideal selection of active ions for achieving synergistic regulation between osteogenesis and angiogenesis. Polydopamine (PDA), inspired by mussel adhesive proteins, exhibits unique interfacial binding properties. Simple immersion in an aqueous dopamine solution enables uniform deposition of a 50 nm thickness coating on both the surface and internal pores of the material. This coating has rich functional groups such as carboxyl, amino, and imino groups. These groups can act as a “bridge” for secondary reactions with other compounds [24], providing an ideal carrier platform for constructing multifunctional synergistic systems.

Based on this, this study innovatively developed a light controlled biomimetic PEEK scaffold (PEEK-PDA@BP-Sr, PPBS), with the following construction strategy: Firstly, a PDA coating was deposited on the surface of the PEEK scaffold through the self-polymerization of dopamine (DA), referred to as PEEK-PDA. Subsequently, by leveraging the abundant catechol and amino active groups on the surface of this coated scaffold as bridging sites, the pre-prepared PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets were efficiently and firmly anchored onto the scaffold surface. This process successfully constructs a bioactive coating that not only enhances the stability of the BPNSs but also achieves functional synergy between Sr2+ and BPNSs. Finally, utilizing the near-infrared (NIR) photothermal effect of BPNSs, the release of Sr2+ and BPNSs were precisely regulated (as shown in Scheme 1). Evaluate the ion release behavior, photothermal performance, and biocompatibility of PPBS scaffolds through in vitro experiments, explore their effects on angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation, and analyze the molecular mechanism of photothermal PPBS scaffolds mediated osteogenic differentiation. At the same time, combining with the rabbit femoral defect model, verify its effect on promoting vascular and bone regeneration around and inside the scaffold. The aim of this study is to provide a novel scaffold material for bone defect repair that combines biomimetic structure, mechanical adaptability, and light controlled vascular osteogenic synergistic function, promoting the clinical translation of bone tissue engineering.

Materials and methods

Materials

Black phosphorus (BP) crystal powder was purchased from Nanjing/Jiangsu XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Strontium chloride (SrCl2), 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP), dopamine hydrochloride (DA), Tris-Hydrochloride Buffer (TrisHCl), acetone, and ethanol were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Fabrication of porous PPBS scaffolds

Synthesis of PDA@BP-Sr

Black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) were prepared by liquid-phase exfoliation method, and the exfoliated BPNSs were dispersed in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) for later use [17], To prepare the BP-Sr complex, BPNSs were dispersed in deionized water (50 mg/mL) and 5 mg of strontium chloride (SrCl2) was added. The reaction was stirred continuously for 3 h under inert gas protection and light avoidance conditions. The BP-Sr precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the BP-Sr precipitate was dispersed in a 10 mM Tris HCl buffer solution (pH 8.5) containing 2 mg/mL dopamine (DA) for polymerization reaction. After centrifugation, washing, and drying again, the target product was finally obtained PDA@BP-Sr. As a control sample, BPNSs were directly added to the above-mentioned dopamine solution using the same method to prepare the PDA@BP. Store at 4 °C in the dark.

Fabrication of PP, PPB, and porous PPBS scaffolds

The PEEK filaments, as the material for 3D printing in this paper, were reprocessed from the PEEK pellets (450G, VICTREX Corp. UK). Porous PEEK scaffold with uniform structural parameters (400 μ m wire diameter, 500 μ m pore size, and 60% porosity) was prepared using fused deposition modeling (FDM) technology. After 2 h of 200 °C heat treatment, a highly crystalline porous PEEK scaffold was obtained. The specific dimensions included standard parts for mechanical testing prepared according to ISO 604:2002 standard (compressive strength: 10 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm, compressive modulus: 50 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm), disc-shaped scaffolds for in vitro experiments (diameter 14 mm, height 2 mm), and cylindrical scaffolds for in vivo experiments (diameter 6 mm, height 10 mm).

The porous PEEK scaffold prepared by FDM was immersed in a 10 mM Tris HCl buffer solution (pH 8.5) containing 10 mg dopamine, and incubated for 24 h in a constant temperature shaker (37 °C, 100 rpm) under dark conditions until the inner and outer surfaces of the scaffold were uniformly coated with PDA. After washing and drying, PDA modified PEEK scaffolds (PP group) were obtained. PP scaffolds were immersed in a dispersion containing 50 μ g/mL PDA@BP or PDA@BP-Sr and allowed to react for 24 h under the same conditions. After washing and drying, PDA@BP-modified PP scaffolds (PPB group) and PDA@BP-Sr-modified PP scaffolds (PPBS group) were obtained. All samples were sterilized by 60Co irradiation and stored at 4 °C in the dark for future use.

Physicochemical characterization of the porous PPBS scaffolds

Characterization of the PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets

The surface morphology and elemental composition of BPNSs were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL, JSM-IT200, Japan) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The particle size and zeta potential of BPNSs and PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Panalytical, UK). The structure and elemental composition of the nanomaterials were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100Plus) combined with EDS (Oxford Xplore30, Oxford, UK). The crystal structure of the material surface was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD; SmartLab SE, Rigaku Corporation). The chemical composition and binding energy were determined using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Shimadzu Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD, Japan).

Characterization of the porous PPBS scaffolds

Microcompeted tomography (Micro CT, SkyScan 1276, Bruker) was performed on the FEM printed PEEK scaffolds to test and analyze pore size, porosity, and pore connectivity. The compression modulus and strength of porous PEEK scaffolds were measured using an electric hydraulic servo mechanical testing system (SANS CMT4304, MTS Corporation, USA) according to ISO 604:2002 (Plastics - Determination of compression properties) standard, at a test speed of 1 mm/min. The morphology and elemental composition of the scaffold surface were observed by SEM and EDS. The surface hydrophilicity and roughness of the four scaffold groups were studied using a contact angle goniometer (SL200 A/B/D, Solon Tech, China) and an atomic force microscope (AFM; Bruker Dimension ICON, Germany). Three parallel samples were tested for each group.

In vitro photothermal effects

To evaluate the photothermal effects of different samples, PDA, BPNSs, and PDA@BP-Sr were dispersed in ultrapure water (25 μg/mL). Each suspension was irradiated with NIR (1.0 W/cm 2, 808 nm) laser (LWIRL808-5W, Beijing, China) for 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 min, with ultrapure water as control. Temperature changes and thermal images were recorded using an infrared thermal imager (Fluke TiS55, USA). Photothermal stability of PDA@BP-Sr were assessed through 5 laser switch cycles (on/off = 10 min/10 min). To investigate the effect of different concentrations of BPNSs on the photothermal properties of PEEK-PDA scaffolds, under irradiation at an intensity of 1.0 W/cm2 (808 nm), BPNSs at varying concentrations (5, 20, 50, 100 μg/mL) were loaded onto PEEK-PDA scaffolds (referred to as PPB 5, PPB 20, PPB 50, and PPB 100) and immersed in 1 mL of liquid for 10 min of irradiation while temperature changes were recorded. The photothermal performance of each experimental group of scaffolds was then tested under the same near-infrared parameters (1.0 W/cm2, 808 nm, 10 min), and temperature change curves were recorded.

In vitro degradation

Given the rapid degradation of bare BPNSs, UV visible spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Beijing, China) was used to monitor the absorbance of BPNSs and PDA@BP-Sr Soak absorption spectra in PBS for different time points to evaluate the PDA@BP-Sr modificationon BPNSs stability. According to the national standard GB/T 16886.12-200, PPBS scaffolds and PPBS + NIR scaffold extraction solutions were prepared [25]. The scaffolds were immersed in PBS solution at a mass volume ratio of 1g: 10 mL for 28 days. Phosphate assay kit (E-BC-K832-M, Elabscience) and inductively coupled plasma emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer, Optima 7000DV, USA) were used to determine the concentrations of PO43− and Sr2+in the extraction solutions at predetermined time points (1 day, 4 days, 7 days, 14 days, 21 days, 28 days), and cumulative release curves were plotted to observe the ion release characteristics of PPBS scaffolds.

In vitro experiments

Coculture of cells and the scaffolds

BMSCs of SD rats were frozen in our laboratory, and human umbilical vein endothelial cell line (HUVEC) was purchased from the cell bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). BMSCs were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin dual antibody (P/S, Beyotime, China) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma Aldrich, USA). The experiment selected 3-5 passages of BMSCs in logarithmic growth phase for in vitro evaluation. HUVECs were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin dual antibody and 10% fetal bovine serum. The cells were incubated in a constant temperature incubator (MCO-15AC, Sanyo, Osaka, Japan) at 37 °C, 5% CO 2, and 95% relative humidity.

The scaffold materials used in in vitro experiments were sterilized by 60Co irradiation, before conducting cell co culture. Two cell scaffold coculture models were employed. one was to directly inoculate cells onto the surface of the scaffold material (direct contact), and the other was to culture cells in the lower chamber well plate with the scaffold placed in the upper Transwell chamber (indirect contact). During the coculture, the culture medium was replaced every 48 h. The PPBS + NIR group underwent its first NIR irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 808 nm) 3 h after cell inoculation, followed by daily irradiation for 10 min each time, with a stable temperature maintained at 40-42 °C.

Cell proliferation

rBMSCs and HUVECs were seeded onto the surface of each group of scaffold materials at a density of 2 × 10 5 cells/mL. After incubation for 1, 3, and 5 days, 10% volume of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; ab228554, Sigma Aldrich, USA) reagent was added to the culture medium. After incubation for 1 h, the absorbance value at 450 nm wavelength (Ab450) was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Thermo 3001,Thermo Scientific,USA). The cell proliferation curves of each group were then plotted.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells

To avoid interference from the transparency of porous PEEK scaffolds on fluorescence detection, this study conducted immunofluorescence staining experiments using an indirect contact co culture system. BMSCs and HUVECs were cocultured with different groups of scaffolds using indirect contact method as described in 2.4.1. After 3 days, cells were double-stained with Calcein AM/PI (Solarbio, China).

After coculturing for 24 h, cytoskeletal staining was performed, including 4% paraformaldehyde fixation (Solarbio, China), vinculin primary antibody (ab129002, 1:200, Abcam) overnight incubation at 4 °C, PBST washing, and labeling of secondary antibody (BMSCs: Alexa Fluor®488, Ab150077. HUVECs:Alexa Fluor®594, Ab150080, 1:500, Abcam) were incubated in the dark for 1 h, finally, F-actin was labeled with FITC-phalloidin (BMSCs: YF®594-Phalloidin YP0052L. HUVECs:YF®488 Pahaloidin, YP0059L, 1:200, Beyotime, China), and nuclei were stained with DAPI(C1006, Beyotime, China).

After co culturing for 72 h, immunofluorescence staining of CD31 (80530-1-RR, 1:800, Proteintech) and EMCN (67854-1-Ig, 1:1000, Proteintech) in HUVECs was also performed according to the above method. After 14 days, immunofluorescence staining expression of COL1A1 (SAB5700733, 1:200, Sigma) and RUNX-2 (PA063, 1:200, AntiProtech) in rBMSCs was detected. Fluorescence images were collected using a fluorescence microscope (CKX-41, Olympus, Japan). The images were then quantitatively analyzed for fluorescence intensity using Image J software.

ALP and Alizarin red staining of rBMSCs cells

To evaluate the regulatory effects of different scaffolds on the osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs through direct and indirect contact, cells were co cultured with each scaffold in osteogenic induction medium (complete medium supplemented with 10 mM β - glycerophosphate sodium, 10 nM dexamethasone, and 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid) for 7 days using the two methods described in 2.4.1. ALP staining was performed using a BCIP/NBT staining kit (C3206, Beyotime); while parallel samples were collected for cell lysis, Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) detection kit (P0321, Beyotime) was employed to measure the absorbance at 405 nm, and standardize the total protein concentration using the BCA method.

After co culturing for 14 days, the calcium nodules were stained with Alizarin Red (C0148S, Beyotime) to observe the deposition of calcified nodules. To quantify calcium deposition, 10% solution of dodecylpyridine chloride was added to each well to quantify the amount of mineralized matrix. The absorbance was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader at 562 nm.

Migration and tubular formation experiments of HUVECs

In the cell scratch experiment, cells were seeded into a 6-well plate (Transwell lower chamber) at a density of 5 × 104 per well, and a scaffold was placed in the upper chamber. When the cell fusion rate reaches 100%, 200 μ L sterile pipette tip was used to scratch the monolayer cells and replace them with serum-free culture medium. After 0 h and 12 h, an inverted microscope was used to capture images of the scratch area, and to measure the average width of the scratch, ImageJ software was used to calculate the scratch healing rate.

In the cell migration experiment, cells were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (pore size, 8 μm; Corning, USA) placed in 24-well plates. Scaffold materials were placed in the lower chamber, followed by the addition of serum-free DMEM medium. After 12 h of cultivation at 37 °C and 5% CO 2, the non-migrating cells in the upper chamber were wiped off. The cells that migrated to the bottom were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS, and stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution (C0121, Beyotime, China) for 10 min. Images were captured usin gan inverted microscope and the number of migrating cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

In the tube formation experiment, HUVECs were directly cocultured with scaffold material for 48 h. Cells were collected and seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/mL onto Matrigel (354234, Corning) coated 24 well plates. After 6 h of cultivation at 37 °C, Calcein AM staining was performed, and images were collected under a fluorescence microscope. The total length of capillaries, number of connecting nodes, and number of branches were analyzed using ImageJ.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

HUVECs cells and rBMSCs cells were cocultured with each group of scaffolds in direct contact according to 2.4.1. After co culturing HUVECs for 72 h, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and reverse transcribed into cDNA. The mRNA expression levels of CD31, EMCN, and VWF were then detected by real-time quantitative PCR, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Meanwhile, after co culturing rBMSCs cells with osteogenic induction medium for 14 days, the same real-time quantitative PCR technique was used to detect the mRNA expression levels of ALP, COL1A1, RUNX2, OCN and Hsp70, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. All data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers for the above-mentioned angiogenic genes and osteogenic genes are listed in Supplementary Materials Table S1 and Table S2, respectively.

Western blotting assay

HUVECs and rBMSCs were cocultured with various scaffold materials in direct contact for 72h and 14 days, respectively. After washing, lysis, centrifugation at 4 °C and 12000 rpm/min for 5 min, the supernatant was collected, and the protein sample concentration was determined by BCA method. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto membranes, which were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibody. After incubation with the secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 2 h, protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence reagent (IC-8001, BioCytoSci) and quantified with ImageJ software. The expression levels of CD31 (80530-1-RR, 1:1000, Proteintech) and EMCN (67854-1-Ig, 1:1000, Proteintech) proteins in HUVECs was detected, with GAPDH (GB15004, 1:10000, Servicebio) as the internal reference. The expression of OCN (DF12303, 1:1000, Affinity), RUNX2 (GB13264, 1:1000, Servicebio), HSP70(GB15242, 1:1000, Servicebio), and P-ERK1/2 (GB11004, 1:1000, Servicebio) proteins in rBMSCs was detected, with β-actin (GB15003, 1:5000, Servicebio) as the internal reference.

Potential molecular mechanism of photothermal responsive PPBS scaffold in regulating osteogenic differentiation

RNA-sequencing was used to evaluate mRNA profiles in rBMSCs. Analysis was conducted on two groups:Control Group (PEEK) and PPBS + NIR group. In short, rBMSCs were directly cocultured with two sets of scaffolds for 7 days, lysed, and collected using 1.5 mL enzyme free EP tubes. After passing the quality test, transcriptome RNA sequencing was commissioned to Wuhan Sevier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified to perform enrichment analysis. The threshold for significant differential expression is set to a corrected P-value less than 0.05 and | logFoldChange |>1. In addition, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were conducted. A corrected P-value less than 0.05 indicates significant enrichment of pathways.

To further investigate the potential molecular mechanism of photothermal responsive PPBS scaffolds in regulating osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, HSP70 inhibitor VER155008 (T7010,TargetMol) and ERK1/2 inhibitor U0126 (T21332, Targeted Mol) were used for intervention validation. Experimental grouping: P, PPBS + NIR, PPBS + NIR/VER155008 and PPBS + NIR/U0126. After cell inoculation, the PPBS + NIR/VER155008 or PPBS + NIR/U0126 groups were intervened with inhibitors at concentrations of VER155008 (5 μ mol/L) or U1206 (10 μ mol/L). Subsequent indicator testing follows the previous method: alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining is the same as 2.4.4. The expression detection of HSP70, ERK1/2, p-ERK1/2, and osteogenic related proteins is the same as in 2.4.7.

In vivo experiments

The construction of bone defects and scaffold implantation

This study selected 45 healthy male New Zealand white rabbits aged 6-8 weeks and weighing 2.5-3.5 kg. All animal experiments were approved by the ethics committee (approval number: XYLS2024120). Propofol intravenous induction (6-8 mg/kg) combined with 2.5% isoflurane inhalation was used to maintain anesthesia in rabbits, prior to surgery. After exposing the femoral condyle through the lateral parapatellar approach, cylindrical bone defects with a diameter of 6 mm and a depth of 10 mm were prepared using a circular drill. The corresponding materials were implanted in each group, hemostasis was achieved and the wounds were closed layer by layer. Prophylactic use of cefotaxime (10 mg/kg, subcutaneous injection, once daily, for 7 consecutive days) after surgery. The experimental animals were randomly divided into three groups: PEEK group, PPBS group, and near-infrared stimulation (PPBS + NIR) group. The PPBS + NIR group received near-infrared laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm 2) every 5 days for 10 min after surgery, and the temperature was dynamically monitored and controlled at 40-42 °C using an infrared thermal imaging device.

Three animals were randomly selected from each group for microvascular perfusion at 2 and 4 weeks after surgery (method reference [26]). After perfusion, femoral specimens were taken, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and decalcified with 10% EDTA solution for 2 months for vascular related Micro-CT and histological detection. The remaining animals were collected at 4, 8, and 12 weeks after surgery and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent osteogenic Micro-CT and histological testing. In addition, to verify the in vivo safety of the PPBS scaffold, rabbit hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were collected for H&E staining analysis 12 weeks after scaffolds implantation. The experimental process strictly follows animal ethical standards.

Micro-CT assessment and histological evaluation

Micro-CT was used to evaluate the vascular and bone regeneration effects after porous scaffolds implantation. The images were reconstructed using AYRecon software and analyzed using VG Studio Max 3.5 software. In terms of bone regeneration assessment, a cylindrical area of 6 mm × 10 mm was set as the region of interest (ROI), with a focus on the growth of new bone inside the scaffold. The vascular assessment is analyzed using a dual region approach. In addition to the cylindrical area inside the 6 mm × 10 mm scaffold mentioned above, an additional 2 mm range around the scaffold was selected as the ROI to comprehensively evaluate the vascular regeneration inside and around the scaffold. The construction of bone 3D images or blood vessel 3D images within each group of scaffolds were analyzed based on a unified threshold (bone>30500, blood vessels>6500). Subsequently the bone volume fraction (BV/TV), average blood vessel diameter, blood vessel surface area, and blood vessel volume within and around the scaffolds were calculated.

After completing the Micro-CT scan, the sample was thoroughly dehydrated using gradient alcohol and xylene, and then encapsulated in a plasticizer containing methyl methacrylate and dibutyl phthalate to obtain a hard tissue embedding block. A hard tissue slicer (EXAKT 312, EXAKT company, Germany) in conjunction with tungsten carbide blades was used to prepare 150 μ m thick slices; which are then polished to 50 μ m using a grinding machine (EXAKT 400CS, EXAKT company, Germany) with 400-2400 grit sandpaper. The vascular perfusion samples were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica M205 FA, Leica company, Germany), and the vessel diameter and density were quantified using Image J software (the yellow area represents the contrast agent in the blood vessels). For non-perfused samples, Van Gieson staining was used to observe the morphology of newly formed bone tissue and the distribution of collagen fibers. The bone implant contact rate (BIC) was calculated as the ratio of scaffold surface length directly apposed to bone to the scaffold surface length, representing the percentage of the implant circumference in direct contact with bone without intervening soft tissue layer. Immunofluorescence staining was used to detect the expression of vascular markers CD31 (Abcam ab24590, 1:200) and EMCN (Santa Cruz sc-65495, 1:100), as well as osteogenic markers Runx2 (CST 12556, 1:500) and OCN (Abcam 72026, 1:1000). All image analysis was performed quantitatively using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

All graphical data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Statistical analyses from at least three biological repeat experiments were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). Statistical analyses primarily employed one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test for intergroup variance assessment. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Characterization of PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets and different groups of scaffolds

The synthesis process of PDA@BP-Sr is shown in Fig. 1A. BPNSs were prepared by liquid-phase exfoliation method, and characterized by SEM (Fig. 1B). The prepared BPNSs exhibited a typical two-dimensional layered structure, with a smooth surface and clear edges. EDS analysis confirmed the uniform distribution of P element. The observation results of TEM (Fig. 1C) further revealed the microstructural characteristics of the material, with a crystal interplanar spacing of 0.256 nm observed in high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images. The particle size test results (Fig. 1D) showed that after modification with SrCl2 and PDA, the particle size of BPNSs increased from 212.8 ± 10.24 nm to 545.3 ± 26.5 nm. Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 1E) showed that the surface potential of BPNSs increased from −20.7 ± 0.8 mV to −11.6 ± 0.9 mV after SrCl2 modification, confirming binding to the negatively charged BPNS surface. Subsequently, the PDA with negative charge (−6 ± 0.4 mV) was coated with BP-Sr, and the PDA@BP-Sr potential decreased to (−17 ± 1.4 mV), indicating the introduction of additional negatively charged functional groups. The pattern of particle size increase and Zeta potential first increasing and then decreasing preliminarily confirms that SrCl2 and PDA have successfully modified the surface of BPNSs. XRD was used to analyze the crystal phase states of BPNSs and PDA@BP-Sr (Fig. 1F). The results showed that the diffraction peaks of BP nanosheets at 2 θ = 16.9°, 26.5°, 34.2°, and 35° were attributed to the (020), (021), (040), and (111) crystal planes of BP, respectively. The absorption peaks of BPNSs and PDA@BP-Sr overlap well with the absorption peak of standard card PDF # 09-0020. This indicated that the prepared PDA@BP-Sr did not alter the crystal properties of BPNSs and exhibited good crystallinity. XPS analysis shows the presence of C, N, O, P, and Sr elements on the surface of PDA@BP-Sr (Fig. 1G). The high-resolution XPS spectra of C1s, P2p, and Sr3d are shown in Fig. 1H. In the high-resolution P2P spectrum, the characteristic double peaks appearing at 130.0 and 130.8 eV corresponded to the 2P3/2 and 2P1/2 energy levels, respectively. A weak phosphorus oxide (PxOy) sub-band peak at 133.9 eV indicated partial BP oxidation, while high-resolution Sr3d spectra showed characteristic doublets at 133.18 and 134.91 eV corresponding to the Sr3d5/2 and Sr3d3/2 energy levels, respectively, exhibiting typical spin orbit splitting characteristics. TEM-EDS characterization (Fig. 1I) demonstrated that PDA@BP-Sr has a typical layered structure similar to the original BPNSs. The element distribution results showed significant enrichment of O, P, N, and Sr elements on the nanosheets. In summary, the characterization results of particle size, Zeta potential, XRD, XPS, and TEM-EDS fully demonstrated the successful synthesis of PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets.

The high crystalline PEEK scaffold prepared by FDM technology has been tested to have a wire diameter of (386 ± 7) μm, a pore size of (472 ± 11) μm, and a porosity of (57 ± 2) %, exhibiting excellent pore connectivity (99.93 ± 0.05) %. Fig. 2B shows the size distribution of the wire diameter in both transverse and longitudinal sections of the PEEK scaffold. The results of the mechanical performance tests (Fig. 2C, Fig. S1A) showed that the compressive strength and the compression modulus of porous PEEK scaffolds were 16.56 ± 0.61 MPa and 397 ± 56 MPa, respectively. In comparison, the compressive strength of the untreated PEEK scaffold was only 7.18 ± 1.18 MPa (Fig. S1B). In this study, the PEEK scaffolds were annealed at 200 °C for 2 h. This treatment induced molecular chain rearrangement, increased crystallinity and relieved residual internal stress generated during forming, which effectively improved and strengthened the mechanical properties of the scaffolds. Further analysis of the surface morphology of the scaffold was conducted using SEM, AFM, and water contact angle. The SEM results (Fig. 2D) showed that the unmodified PEEK scaffold (P group) had a smooth surface, while the surface of the PPBS scaffold exhibited BP-Sr nanosheet structures modified with spherical PDA nanoparticles. EDS showed the uniform distribution of O, P, N, and Sr elements in the PPBS scaffold compared with the unmodified counterpart (Fig. S2), proving the successful loading of PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets on the surface of PEEK scaffold. In addition, compared with the contact angles of P (90.9 ± 9.4°) and PP (68.7 ± 4.8°) scaffolds, the PPBS scaffold (45.0 ± 2°) significantly improved its hydrophilicity (Fig. 2E and F). The AFM results showed that PDA@BP-Sr significantly improved the surface roughness of PEEK scaffold (Fig. 2E and G).

To evaluate the photothermal performance of the material, NIR (1.0 W/cm 2, 808 nm) was used to irradiate PDA, BPNSs, and PDA@BP-Sr dispersions with a concentration of 25ug/mL at different times (2, 4, 6, 8, 10 min), with ultrapure water (UPW) as the control. As shown in Fig. 3A, PDA@BP-Sr exhibited the most significant temperature increase (44 ± 2.3 °C), which was higher than PDA (32 ± 1.3 °C), BPNSs (40 ± 1.6 °C), and the control group (26 ± 1.9 °C). To evaluate the photothermal stability of PDA@BP-Sr, temperature monitoring was measured for five cycles (on/off = 10 min/10 min) under the same NIR laser parameters. The results showed (Fig. 3B) that within 100 min, PDA@BP-Sr did not exhibit significant performance degradation and demonstrated excellent photothermal stability. Considering that black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) serve as the key functional component responsible for the excellent photothermal performance of the PPB scaffold, this study investigated the effect of BPNSs concentration on the photothermal response of the scaffold. The results demonstrated that the heating efficiency of the PPB scaffold significantly improved with increasing BPNSs concentration (Fig. S3). To avoid potential thermal damage to normal tissues and cells caused by excessive heating, a concentration of 50 μg/mL BPNSs and a light irradiation intensity of 1 W/cm2 were selected as the optimized parameters for subsequent experiments [27,28]. Fig. 3C shows the temperature change curves of different scaffolds under the same NIR irradiation. Compared with the other scaffolds, the PPBS scaffold showed significantly enhanced photothermal conversion ability. Overall, the PDA@BP-Sr modified PEEK scaffold exhibited superior photothermal conversion performance and stability.

The UV vis spectroscopic analysis results showed that BPNSs had poor stability in PBS solution, with a significant decrease in absorbance within 48 h and a continuous decrease in absorption intensity with prolonged degradation time (Fig. 3D). The absorption spectrum of PDA@BP-Sr nanosheets only showed slight attenuation, and their characteristic absorption peaks could still be clearly detected after 18 days (Fig. 3E). These results confirmed that the PDA@BP-Sr composite slows BPNSs oxidative degradation through combined physical barriers and chemical modifications. Thereby, markedly enhancing stability in aqueous environments and potential biological systems. The analysis of ion release behavior further clarified the degradation and ion release laws of the material. The results showed that the release of Sr2+ in the PPBS scaffold exhibited a two-stage characteristic, with the first 7 days being a rapid release period and a cumulative release concentration of (7.94 ± 0.53 μg/ml); Subsequently entering the platform phase. Notably, Sr2+ release in the PPBS + NIR group was significantly higher than that in the PPBS group at all time points, with a release concentration of (12.72 ± 0.53 μg/mL) on the 7th day and a slow upward trend until the 28th day (Fig. 3F). The release trend of PO43− generated by the degradation of BPNSs in PPBS scaffolds was consistent with Sr2+. The release concentration of PO43− in the PPBS + NIR group was (11.36 ± 0.45 μg/mL) on the 7th day, and continued to slowly increase until the 28th day (Fig. 3G), indicating that BPNSs in this group were in a continuous degradation state throughout the entire detection cycle.

This study used direct contact coculture to inoculate rBMSCs and HUVECs onto different groups of PEEK scaffolds (P, PP, PPB, PPBS, PPBS + NIR) to evaluate the cytocompatibility of PEEK scaffolds before and after modification. The CCK-8 results showed that there was no significant difference in cell proliferation activity among the groups after 1 day of cultivation. With increasing culture time, all groups showed a time-dependent cell proliferation, following the trend PPBS + NIR > PPBS > PPB > PP > P (Fig. 4A and B). Cell proliferation in the PPBS + NIR group and the PPBS group was significantly higher than in the other groups (P < 0.05).

These results indicate that PPBS scaffolds promote rBMSCs and HUVECs the proliferation, adhesion, and cell spreading, demonstrating good biocompatibility, while NIR irradiation further promotes cell viability.

Effects of different scaffold groups on osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs

To distinguish the effects of the physical microenvironment and ion release from the scaffold surface on osteogenic differentiation, this study adopted both direct and indirect co-culture systems with the scaffolds. Early and late osteogenic markers were evaluated by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. After BMSCs were directly co-cultured with scaffolds for 7 days, ALP staining revealed varying degrees of bluish-purple active substance deposition in all five groups (Fig. 7A). The color of the PPBS group and PPBS + NIR group was significantly higher than that of the P and PPB groups, while the PPBS + NIR group had the deepest blue purple color and the largest area. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that ALP activity in the PPBS + NIR group was significantly higher than that in other groups (Fig. S4A). After 14 days of cultivation, ARS staining further confirmed this trend. The PPBS + NIR group had the most orange red calcium deposits (Fig. 7B), and semi-quantitative analysis at 562 nm wavelength showed that NIR photothermal stimulation significantly enhanced the calcium deposition ability of the PPBS scaffold (Fig. S4B). Indirect co-culture was applied to verify the biological functions of released ions and exclude interference from scaffold morphology. The results showed (Fig. 7C–D, Fig. S4C–D) that PPBS scaffolds significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation, and the PPBS + NIR group retained the highest levels of ALP activity and calcium deposition. As shown in the qPCR results (Fig. 7E), the expression levels of osteogenic related genes (ALP, COL1A1, RUNX2, OCN) in the PPBS group and PPBS + NIR group were significantly higher than those in the P group and PP group, with the highest expression level in the PPBS + NIR group. PPBS scaffold promotes osteogenic differentiation by activating the HSP70/ERK1/2 pathway

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of PDA@BP-Sr complex promoting osteogenic differentiation, RNA seq transcriptome analysis was performed on BMSCs cultured on PEEK scaffolds and PPBS + NIR scaffolds, respectively. The results of principal component analysis (PCA) showed a significant separation in the transcriptome characteristics between the PEEK group and the PPBS + NIR group samples, indicating a statistically significant difference in gene expression between the two groups, which can be used for subsequent analysis (Fig. S6A). The volcano plot (Fig. S6B) and cluster heatmap (Fig. S6C) of differentially expressed genes further confirm the significant gene expression differences between the two groups of samples. The GO enrichment analysis results showed that DEGs in the PPBS + NIR group were significantly enriched in biological processes related to the ERK1/2 signaling cascade and MAPK pathway positive regulation (Fig. 8A); KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed that the MAPK signaling pathway is a key enriched pathway (Fig. 8B), which highly corresponds to the entries such as “positive regulation of ERK1 and ERK2 cascade” and “positive regulation of MAPK cascade” in GO analysis. Based on the significant upregulation of HSP70 protein expression and ERK1/2 phosphorylation level (p-ERK1/2) in the PPBS + NIR group as described in section 3.4, we speculate that the photothermal responsive PPBS scaffold can activate the ERK1/2 signaling pathway by upregulating HSP70 expression, thereby promoting the expression of osteogenic related genes such as RUNX2 and ultimately inducing osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

To verify the above mechanism, this study conducted intervention experiments using HSP70 inhibitor VER155008 and ERK1/2 pathway inhibitor U0126. The experimental groups included P group, PPBS + NIR group, PPBS + NIR/VER155008 group, and PPBS + NIR/U0126 group. The Western blotting results (Fig. 8C and D) showed that compared with the pure PEEK group, the expression of HSP70 protein was significantly upregulated in the PPBS + NIR group, and the phosphorylation level of ERK1/2 (p-ERK1/2) was also significantly enhanced. Consistent with this, the expression of key osteogenic transcription factors RUNX2 and OCN protein significantly increased. After treatment with the HSP70 inhibitor VER155008 (PPBS + NIR/VER155008 group), there was no significant change in the total expression of HSP70 protein induced by photothermal stimulation, but the phosphorylation level of downstream ERK1/2 was significantly inhibited, which further led to a substantial downregulation of RUNX2 and OCN protein expression and and a significant blockade of the osteogenic differentiation process of stem cells. Notably, The unaltered HSP70 expression after inhibitor treatment is consistent with previous related research conclusions. The reason for this may be that the mechanism of action of VER155008 is to target and inhibit the protein function and activity of HSP70, rather than regulate the expression level of HSP70 [32,33]. When using ERK1/2 inhibitor U0126, ERK1/2 phosphorylation can be effectively blocked, and the upregulation of RUNX2 and OCN is also inhibited. The ALP staining results (Fig. 8E) showed that after inhibiting the HSP70 or ERK1/2 pathway, the blue purple positive staining depth and area of the PPBS + NIR/VER155008 group and PPBS + NIR/U0126 group were significantly weaker than those of the PPBS + NIR group. These results suggest that PPBS coating may first induce HSP70 expression under near-infrared light excitation, which in turn activates the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, ultimately driving RUNX2/OCN expression and promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

The procedure and timeline for implantation in the body are shown in Fig. 9A. Previous studies have confirmed that the penetration depth of 700∼800 nm near-infrared light through biological tissues can reach 5-6 mm [34]. Based on the actual measurement results, the near-infrared light can effectively penetrate the soft tissue on the lateral side of the femoral condyle and activate the photothermal material scaffold implanted in the bone to trigger the photothermal effect. When the bone defect site repaired with PPBS is exposed to NIR irradiation, it can reach 40-42 °C in 2 min (Fig. 9B). The Micro-CT results showed that 2 weeks after surgery, the blood vessels around the scaffold in group P were finer and sparsely distributed. Four weeks after surgery, large and mature vascular structures had formed around all three groups of scaffolds, with the PPBS + NIR group having better vascular network integrity and maturity. At the internal level of the scaffold, the distribution of unmodified P group blood vessels is still relatively sparse and has poor continuity. Both the PPBS group and the PPBS + NIR group formed a continuous and relatively mature three-dimensional vascular network, with a trend consistent with the blood vessels in the surrounding area of the scaffold. Therefore, the combination of PPBS scaffold and NIR photothermal stimulation can effectively promote angiogenesis and maturation in the bone repair area, providing sufficient nutritional supply and metabolic support for subsequent bone tissue regeneration.

Discussion

3D printing primarily aims to replicate the physical properties of natural tissues, focusing on mechanics, spatial structure, and porosity. Research has shown that pore sizes larger than 100 μm are necessary for vascular infiltration and bone regeneration [35], while 3D through pores of 300-500 μm support the formation of functional capillary networks and bone tissue growth [36,37]. In this study, highly crystalline porous PEEK scaffolds were successfully prepared using FDM technology, with wire diameter, pore size, porosity, and pore connectivity of (386 ± 7) μm, (472 ± 11) μm, (57 ± 2) %, and (99.93 ± 0.05) %, respectively. The scaffolds served as templates for the construction of BP-Sr complexes by coordinating Sr2+ with black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs), and their stability was significantly improved using polydopamine (PDA) encapsulation strategy, resulting in PDA@BP-Sr Nanocomposites. The nano composite was uniformly loaded onto the surface of PDA modified PEEK scaffold, successfully constructing a PPBS composite scaffold with photothermal effect. The various characterization results such as particle size analysis, Zeta potential testing, XRD, XPS, and TEM-EDS mutually confirm the successful construction of PDA@BP-Sr composite. In addition, SEM-EDS clearly presented the surface microstructure and element distribution characteristics of PPBS scaffolds, further verifying the successful preparation of PPBS composite scaffolds. It is worth noting that the compressive strength of the porous PEEK scaffold is 16.56 ± 0.61 MPa, which is consistent with the measured compression values of human trabecular bone (1.5-40 MPa [38]). The compressive modulus is 397 ± 56 MPa, which is similar to the modulus of cancellous bone (0.1-4.5 GPa [39]). The mechanical properties of different regions of the human skeleton show significant differences with different load-bearing functions. The mechanical properties of the PEEK porous scaffold prepared in this study match well with natural trabecular bone, making it an optional material for repairing bone defects in non load bearing and lightly load bearing areas. It is worth mentioning that the solid PEKK has a compression modulus of 2688 ± 292 MPa and a compression strength of 141.7 ± 6.6 MPa [40]. With the support of 3D printing molding technology, its mechanical properties can be adjusted targetedly by optimizing the wire diameter and aperture parameters of the scaffold to meet the requirements of different implantation sites. AFM and water contact angle analyses showed that the PDA@BP-Sr composite significantly increased scaffold surface roughness and hydrophilicity. These changes are beneficial for cell adhesion and differentiation [41], Thereby, enhancing scaffold cytocompatibility.

The PDA@BP-Sr composite material developed in this study exhibits excellent photothermal response capability due to the efficient photothermal conversion characteristics of BPNSs and the inherent photothermal properties of PDA [42]. It can reach a therapeutic temperature range of 40 -42 °C within 10 min of NIR light irradiation. This temperature range can effectively promote tissue repair and avoid thermal damage [42,43], providing a safe and reliable photothermal basis for its biomedical applications. UV-vis spectroscopy and ion release analyses further demonstrate the advantages of Sr2+ and PDA modified BPNSs. Its interaction enhances the structural stability of BPNSs structural stability and suppresses rapid oxidative degradation under physiological conditions, supporting long-term functionalilty. In addition, the ion release pattern shows that the concentration of Sr2+ released by the PPBS + NIR group is within the suitable range of 8.76-87.60 μ g/ml for promoting osteogenic differentiation [44], indicating that the release of Sr2+ in the PPBS scaffold can be precisely regulated within the effective concentration range through photothermal effect. At the same time, the PO43− produced by BP degradation can combine with Ca2+ in the body to form hydroxyapatite [45], providing a key raw material for bone matrix mineralization. More importantly, the system can be combined with non-invasive and precise regulation of near-infrared (NIR) light, allowing the release amount and rhythm of Sr2+ and PO43− to dynamically adapt to the real-time needs of local microenvironment demands. This approach achieves synergistic “photothermal therapy - biologically active ion regulation”, offering an efficient and feasible solution for the design of intelligent, responsive biomedical materials.

In-vitro evaluation of scaffold cytocompatibility is essential for assessing its potential for in vivo application. The experimental results confirmed that the PPBS scaffold has good cytocompatibility, and its components PDA, BPNSs, and Sr2+ significantly promote the proliferation and adhesion of HUVECs and rBMSCs. This effects arises from the inherent characteristics and synergistic effects of each component of the material. Among them, the PDA coating significantly enhances the hydrophilicity of the scaffold with abundant active functional groups such as hydroxyl (-OH) and amino (-NH2) on the surface, providing a good surface microenvironment for cell adhesion and proliferation [[46], [47], [48]]. This further explains why the modified group (PP, PPB, PPBS, PPBS + NIR) exhibited better cell proliferation and spreading ability than the unmodified P group, and laid the foundation for the formation of vinculin, a key protein in adhesion plaques. The nanoscale structure of BPNSs can promote extracellular matrix anchoring and stimulate cell growth [49], and the degradation of phosphates and phosphonates can assist in early fibrinogen formation, providing support for cell differentiation and proliferation [50]. This is consistent with the superior proliferation performance of PPB group compared to PP group in CCK-8 detection, and also provides structural support for the increase of Vinculin area in PPBS group. In addition, the cell proliferation activity and cytoskeleton spreading in the PPBS group were significantly better than those in the PPB group, which may be attributed to the similar chemical properties of Sr2+ and Ca2+, which can selectively promote the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs by activating downstream signaling pathways of calcium sensitive receptors (CaSR) [51]. Notably, PPBS + NIR group showed superior cell proliferation, spreading, and Vinculin protein expression compared to PPBS group, confirming the synergistic effect of NIR illumination. This is consistent with Choudhery's research findings [52], which have been validated through controlled experiments that low thermal stimulation can significantly enhance cell proliferation rate. In summary, the PPBS scaffold has successfully constructed a three-dimensional microenvironment suitable for cell culture and growth, providing important cellular level support for its subsequent in vivo bone repair applications and the implementation of the “photothermal - ion regulation” synergistic mechanism.

Timely and sufficient angiogenesis is one of the core factors determining the efficiency of bone repair in the process of bone defect repair [29]. The PPBS scaffold constructed in this study significantly enhanced the migration activity of HUVECs and the ability to form tubular structures of endothelial cells by loading PDA@BP-Sr complexes and combining NIR photothermal stimulation. Further testing revealed that the expression levels of H-type vascular endothelial specific markers CD31 and EMCN were significantly upregulated in the PPBS + NIR group. H-type blood vessels, as a special subtype of blood vessels in bone tissue, play a key role in regulating the spatiotemporal coupling of angiogenesis and osteogenesis processes [29,53], This modification strategy can effectively induce H-type angiogenesis. The above-mentioned angiogenic effect may be attributed to the synergistic effect of PDA@BP-Sr nanocomposite system and NIR mild photothermal. On the one hand, the released Sr2+ can directly promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tubular structure formation; On the other hand, black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) have excellent electrochemical and interfacial properties, which can enhance intercellular connections and intercellular signal communication, indirectly improving endothelial cell functional activity [54,55]. Meanwhile, the mild thermal effect of 40-42 °C generated by NIR photothermal can optimize the cellular microenvironment and further positively regulate endothelial cell behavior. Therefore, The combination of PDA@BP-Sr composite functional layer and NIR mild photothermal stimulation can significantly enhance the vascular regeneration and H-type vascular induction ability of porous PEEK scaffolds.

The process of bone formation involves osteoblast differentiation and bone mineralization, among which alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and bone mineralization nodule (BMN) formation are key indicators for evaluating bone regeneration ability [56]. This study constructed direct and indirect co culture systems for PPBS scaffolds, and combined with ALP staining and ARS staining results, confirmed that the surface topology, active ion release, and photothermal effect of the scaffold can effectively promote osteogenic differentiation. PCR, WB experiments, and immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the PPBS + NIR group significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein expression of the key osteogenic transcription factor Runx2 (regulating downstream osteogenic gene expression such as COL1a1, ALP, OCN [57]) and the late osteogenic marker gene OCN (its high secretion marker for extracellular matrix deposition initiation [58,59]). Confirmed that PDA@BP-Sr complex and NIR photothermal stimulation can synergistically promote osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs. In addition, the GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis results suggested that differentially expressed genes are significantly enriched in the MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling pathway, providing bioinformatics evidence for molecular mechanism exploration. Previous studies showed that heat shock protein (HSP) was a stress responsive protein, in which HSP70 participated in regulating the heat tolerance and osteogenic differentiation process of mammalian cells [42]. In recent years, researchers also found that it served as a key node in regulating cell differentiation signals [60]. The results of this study demonstrated that PPBS coating can significantly upregulate the expression of HSP70 and p-ERK1/2 in BMSCs under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. Our intervention experiments confirmed that inhibiting HSP70 (PPBS + NIR/VER155008 group) could markedly reduce PPBS + NIR induced ERK phosphorylation and subsequent osteogenic protein expression by PPBS combined with NIR irradiation. Meanwhile, ERK1/2 inhibitors could effectively block the phosphorylation activation of the ERK1/2 pathway. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the superior osteogenic activity of modified PEEK scaffolds is highly dependent on the HSP70-ERK1/2-RUNX2/OCN signaling cascade. This study linked the photothermal and chemical stimuli on the material surface to the classical osteogenesis-related ERK pathway, which deepened our understanding of the action mechanism of PDA@BP-Sr coatings.

In vivo experiments further validated the repair efficacy of PPBS + NIR scaffolds through Micro CT and histological staining. The Micro-CT test results showed that compared with other groups, the volume, area, and average diameter of new blood vessels around and inside the PPBS + NIR group scaffolds were significantly increased. New bone tissue grew orderly from the defect edge to the center, depositing along the three-dimensional network of the scaffold, demonstrating effective vascular regeneration and bone repair efficiency. Moreover, there was almost no fibrous connective tissue gap between the bone tissue and material interface, indicating that it had better bone integration ability. The immunofluorescence staining results showed that the PPBS + NIR group significantly upregulated H-type vascular endothelial markers CD31 and EMCN expression, while also upregulating key osteogenic protein expression such as Runx2 and OCN. The above results form a clear virtuous cycle, where the early rapidly formed vascular network provides sufficient nutrients and growth factors for bone regeneration, while the newly formed bone tissue provides stable structural support for the vascular network. The two mutually empower and synergistically enhance each other, ultimately achieving an efficient and structurally optimized bone regeneration process. Biological safety evaluation showed that after 12 weeks of stent implantation, no significant tissue damage, inflammatory infiltration, or abnormal pathological changes were observed in major organs such as the rabbit heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys through HE staining. The tissue morphology and structure were intact, indicating that the PPBS composite stent has good biocompatibility and safety in vivo.

This study preliminarily confirmed that PPBS scaffolds can mediate osteogenic differentiation via the HSP70/ERK1/2 pathway. Short-term in vivo implantation demonstrated favorable biocompatibility with no significant pathological damage to major organs, providing fundamental experimental and theoretical support for the development of multifunctional photothermal bone repair scaffolds. Nevertheless, this study still has several limitations. The independent osteogenic regulatory mechanisms underlying the scaffold's photothermal effect and functional ion release remain undistinguished. Furthermore, only short-term biosafety evaluation was performed, and systematic investigations regarding the long-term degradation behavior, metabolism of degradation products, and long-term in vivo safety of PPBS scaffolds are still lacking. Future studies will focus on in-depth mechanistic exploration and long-term biosafety assessment to improve the research framework of PPBS scaffolds and further validate their potential for clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a porous PEEK scaffold (PPBS) with photothermal responsiveness, achieving three key innovative breakthroughs through the synergistic strategy of mussel biomimetic PDA coating modification and BP-Sr nanocomposite loading. First, the stable interface formed by PDA coating and Sr2+ coordination significantly improved the BP nanomaterial stability in physiological environments, while retaining excellent photothermal performance within the treatment window of 40-42 °C, laying the foundation for subsequent biological activity regulation. Second, a dynamic NIR-mediated system was constructed to achieve the spatiotemporally controllable release of BP degradation rate and Sr2+/PO43− bioactive ions, overcoming the limitations of traditional scaffolds' passive release. Third, it was elucidated that the PPBS scaffold can upregulate HSP70 expression and activate the ERK1/2 signaling pathway through NIR photothermal stimulation, revealing its mechanism of promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. In vitro experiments have confirmed that the scaffold can significantly promote the migration ability and tubular formation efficiency of HUVECs, as well as accelerate the osteogenic differentiation process of BMSCs. In vivo experiments further confirm its ability to induce H-type angiogenesis and significantly promote bone regeneration and repair in bone defect areas. This strategy combines Sr2+ - mediated biochemical stimulation with NIR - triggered photothermal stimulation, effectively overcoming the biological inertness of pure PEEK materials and providing an innovative, controllable approach to address the critical bottleneck of blood supply deficiency in clinical bone defect repair.
