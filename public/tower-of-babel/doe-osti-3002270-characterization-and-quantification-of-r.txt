Abstract

Over the operational lifespan of a nuclear reactor, reactor pressure vessel (RPV) steels are
subjected to significant neutron irradiation, resulting in complex microstructural changes and the
consequent degradation of mechanical properties. Various physically motivated correlation
models have been developed to predict neutron irradiation-induced embrittlement of RPVs under
different irradiation conditions. However, the efficient and accurate characterizations and
quantification of radiation-induced clusters in RPVs are still challenging, which will affect the
precision of the predictive models for embrittlement of RPV components. In the DOE Visiting
Faculty Program (VFP) research work at Oak Ridge National Lab (ORNL), I integrate machine
learning to aid Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy – Energy Dispersive X-ray
Spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) analyses, which improve the characterization and quantification of
radiation-induced clusters in RPV steels, thereby enabling more accurate predictions of material
behavior under irradiation. The surveillance base- and welded- RPV steels were annealed at
various temperatures of 340 °C, 450 °C and 500 °C for up to 168 hours, respectively. Afterwards,
I have characterized radiation-induced clusters using advanced STEM-EDS techniques and
subsequently applying machine learning algorithms to analyze and refine STEM-EDS datasets,
enhancing the quantification of clusters compositions and distributions. In the end, an efficient
workflow for integrating STEM-EDS data analysis with machine learning to address challenges
including noise reduction has been developed. The completion of this VFP work will support
bridge critical gaps in the accurate quantification of radiation-induced clusters in RPV steels
using STEM-EDS and support the development of more precise models for predicting RPV
embrittlement in the Light Water Reactor Sustainability program supported by Department of
Energy and enhancing the collaboration between ORNL and Alred University. The outcome of
the VFP project will leverage a few research papers submission to peer-reviewed journals in the
relevant scientific field and a few oral presentations at national and international conferences.

Steels Using STEM-EDS and Machine Learning

1. Introduction

Reactor Pressure Vessel (RPV) steels are vital structural components of nuclear reactors, serving
as the primary containment system for the reactor core. Over the operational lifespan of a reactor,
these steels are subjected to significant neutron irradiation, resulting in complex microstructural
changes. These changes include the formation of radiation-induced defects such as dislocation
loops and voids, phase instability leading to the formation of nanoscale clusters or precipitates
(e.g., Ni-Mn-Si-rich clusters/precipitates), and solute element segregation at grain boundaries.
These phenomena contribute to embrittlement and degradation of mechanical properties (Eason
et al., 2013). Ensuring the structural integrity of RPVs is paramount for nuclear safety,
particularly as reactor lifetimes are extended beyond their original design specifications.

The embrittlement of reactor pressure vessel (RPV) steels under irradiation is primarily
associated with increases in yield stress, a phenomenon commonly referred to as irradiation-
induced hardening. This hardening is attributed to radiation-induced defects, as well as the
clustering and precipitation of solute elements such as Ni, Mn, and Si. Although these clusters
form at the nanoscale, their presence significantly impedes dislocation motion, resulting in
increased yield strength, a higher ductile-to-brittle transition temperature, and reduced fracture
toughness. To support plant life extension and inform safety assessments, it is essential to
develop a mechanistic understanding of how such clusters contribute to hardening and
embrittlement. Various physically motivated correlation models, which provide statistical fits to
existing surveillance data, have been developed to predict neutron irradiation-induced shifts in
the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) of RPVs under different irradiation
conditions. Notable examples include the Eason, Wright, and Odette (EWO) model published in
1998 (E.D. Eason, 1998) and the Eason, Odette, Nanstad, and Yamamoto (EONY) model
initially released as an ORNL report in 2007 (E.D. Eason, 2007). Therefore, understanding the
nature, composition, size, and distribution of these clusters is critical for predicting mechanical
properties degradation and lifetime of RPVs and developing mitigation strategies.

Commonly employed characterization methods, such as Atom Probe Tomography (APT) and
Small-Angle Neutron Scattering (SANS), have been extensively used to study these radiation-
induced precipitates. APT provides atomic-scale resolution and three-dimensional compositional
mapping but is limited by small sampling volumes and trajectory aberration. SANS, on the other
hand, offers valuable statistical information over larger sample areas but lacks spatial resolution
and detailed chemical compositions. Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy combined
with modern Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) detector has been
demonstrated as an effective and efficient technique, enabling chemical mapping with sub-
nanometer spatial resolution over larger areas (Edmondson, Parish, and Nanstad 2017).

However, STEM-EDS is hindered by challenges such as low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR),
overlapping signals, and noise from small sample volumes. Recent advancements in machine
learning (ML) have demonstrated immense potential for overcoming these limitations. For
example, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Independent Component Analysis (ICA), and

Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) have been applied to enhance the SNR and separate
overlapping signals (Chen, Alexander, and Hébert 2024; Kim et al. 2020). PSNMF (Pan-
sharpening NMF) has shown particular promise in mitigating the Poissonian nature of noise in
STEM-EDS data (Chen, Alexander, and Hébert 2024). In this visiting faculty program research
project, ML-based methods have been applied to analyze STEM-EDS data of radiation-induced
clusters, enabling to improve data quality and enhance our understanding of the underlying
microstructural features.

2. Progress

2.1. Scientific Approach

To investigate the evolution of neutron-irradiated clusters after various post-irradiation annealing
processing. The surveillance base- and welded- RPV steels were annealed at various
temperatures of 340 °C, 450 °C and 500 °C for up to 168 hours, respectively. Subsequently,
STEM-EDS mapping was performed to examine the evolution of radiation-induced clusters in
terms of size and number density in both grain interior and GBs, as well as evolution of
elemental segregation behavior at GBs. The TEM instrument used in this work is Thermo Fisher
Scientific (TFS) Talos F200X which is a 200 keV S/TEM, equipped with "SuperX" energy-
dispersive spectroscopy X-ray (EDX) detector system, which consists of four silicon-drift-
detector (SDD) units, arrayed symmetrically around the sample. This instrument, also named
“Chemi-STEM”, will lead to higher spatial resolution in elemental analysis (up to ~1-2nm spatial
resolution) and higher efficiency in data collections.

To overcome the challenges in precisely determining chemical compositions of the noisy data
from low X-ray yields and mixed signal from phases that overlap along the electron beam
trajectory. A novel EDS mapping data processing method, non-negative matrix factorization-
based pan-sharpening (PSNMF) is applied to address these limitations, aiming to precisely count
the size and volume fraction of the radiation-induced clusters in the surveillance RPV steels.

2.2. Scientific Results

The base RPV steel contains a typical Ni content and a high Cu content, whereas the weld RPV
steel exhibits both high Ni and high Cu contents. These compositional differences between the
base and weld RPV steels may result in distinct cluster evolution behaviors during post-
irradiation annealing (PIA).
Surveillance-grade base and weld RPV steels were annealed at 340 °C, 450 °C, and 500 °C for
up to 168 hours. PIA experiments were conducted at multiple time intervals for each annealing
temperature. After each interval, the hardness of the PIA RPV steels was measured. The samples
were then returned to the furnace to continue annealing until the total duration of 168 hours was
reached.
Microstructural characterization of the PIA samples was performed after 168 hours of annealing
at each temperature. The evolution of Ni-Mn-Si-rich and Cu-rich clusters was investigated using
scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. The cluster size and volume fraction were quantified for each
annealing condition.
Figure 3 presents the microstructures of Ni-Mn-Si clusters in the base RPV surveillance steel
after annealing at different temperatures for 168 hours. A high density of Ni-rich clusters
remains within the grain interiors following annealing at 340 °C. In contrast, these clusters are
nearly completely dissolved into the iron matrix after annealing at 450 °C and 500 °C.

Fig. 3. The microstructures of Ni-Mn-Si clusters in base RPV surveillance steel after annealing at
different temperatures for 168 hours.

Figure 4 shows the microstructures of Ni-Mn-Si clusters in as-irradiated weld RPV surveillance
steel and after annealing at various temperatures for 168 hours. A high density of Ni-rich clusters
is observed in the as-irradiated condition. These clusters persist after 340 °C PIA, though with
reduced clarity. At 450 °C, the clusters exhibit significant dissolution and appear blurred within
the matrix. After 500 °C annealing, Ni-rich clusters are almost entirely dissolved into the iron
matrix.

Figure 5 displays the microstructures of Cu-rich clusters in the base RPV surveillance steel after
168 hours of annealing. Following 340 °C PIA, small and densely distributed Cu-rich clusters are

4. Impact on Laboratory or National Missions

This research complements ongoing efforts at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) to
advance data collection on the microstructural evolution and mechanical property degradation of
neutron-irradiated reactor pressure vessels (RPVs), ultimately aiding in the development of
predictive models for RPV degradation in nuclear environments. The U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) has supported the Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) project, which focuses on
research to develop technologies and solutions to improve the economics and reliability,
maintain safety, and extend the operational lifetimes of the nation’s nuclear power plants. At
ORNL, the nuclear materials team is engaged in R&D aimed at establishing a scientific basis for
understanding long-term environmental degradation and predicting material performance in
nuclear power plants (Chen et al. 2023). This includes reducing operating costs by improving
predictive models for component lifetimes. Understanding the radiation effects and
embrittlement mechanisms in RPV steels is thus of paramount importance. Building on these
efforts, this work integrates advanced microscopy and machine learning techniques to enhance
characterization capabilities and to precisely quantify radiation-induced clusters and precipitates
in RPVs. Such advancements would support the development of more accurate predictive
models for embrittlement and the overall service life of RPV components in the light water
nuclear reactors.

5. Conclusions

Through the VFP project support, the microstructural evolution of radiation-induced clusters
inside surveillance RPV steels after various annealing conditions was investigated and quantified
by using STEM-EDS mapping. This project enabled the Faculty PI to access cutting-edge
research and facilities in nuclear materials science at ORNL. The faculty introduced novel
techniques and academic insights (e.g., machine learning on EDS mapping data processing) into
DOE LWRS project. strengthen collaborative ties between Alfred University and ORNL,
supporting future joint research and proposal efforts.
