Expanding the clinical phenotype associated with an ASXL1 pathogenic variant causing a novel neuromuscular disorder with neurodevelopmental features
Authors: Dominic Spicer, Kathryn Friend, Bing Wu, Nicholas Chia, Roula Ghaoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGermline pathogenic variants in the Additional Sex Combs-Like 1 (ASXL1) gene are associated with the neurodevelopmental Bohring-Opitz syndrome and cancers like Wilms’ tumours. Bohring-Opitz syndrome is a phenotypically heterogeneous condition characterised by feeding difficulties, syndromic facial abnormalities, abnormal posturing and developmental delays, and it has only been reported in infant/adolescent patients. There are no reported neuromuscular phenotypes associated with ASXL1.Case presentationA 71-year-old male proband exhibited congenital facial, extraocular, proximal upper limb and generalised/distal lower limb muscle weakness/wasting. This was associated with dysphagia, childhood motor developmental delay, bilateral upper limb tremor and bilateral pes cavus. He demonstrated some features of Bohring-Opitz syndrome including feeding difficulties, microcephaly, micrognathia and anteverted nares but lacked the characteristic posturing. Muscle imaging demonstrated symmetrical lower limb atrophy. His adult age diagnosis is unique among ASXL1-associated conditions. A genetic diagnosis of the ASXL1 variant (c.1210C>T; p.Arg404Ter) was achieved through phenotype-driven genetic neurodevelopmental panel testing given some overlap with neurodevelopmental patients following unsuccessful initial genetic sequencing.ConclusionsThis expands the phenotype of ASXL1 pathogenic variants with a novel and distinct neuromuscular presentation. This case demonstrates the importance of thorough and accurate phenotype for unusual presentations, as this diagnosis was missed through routine genetic testing.

Introduction

Inherited neuromuscular disorders encompass a heterogenous range of genetic pathologies.1 The increasing diversity of neuromuscular phenotypes, including overlapping neurodevelopmental disorders, reflects the underlying variability in genotype-phenotype associations.2 3

The Additional Sex Combs-Like (ASXL) genes comprising ASXL1, ASXL2 and ASXL3 are human homologues of the Drosophila Asx gene that are involved in epigenetic and transcriptional regulation.4 ASXL1 encodes a chromatin-binding protein necessary for normal determination of segment identity in a developing embryo. The protein maintains stable repression of homeotic and other loci via chromatin disruption to regulate gene transcription.5

Pathogenic germline ASXL variants produce clinically distinct but overlapping neurodevelopmental syndromes.6 Bohring-Opitz syndrome (BOPS) was described in 1999 and is characterised by intrauterine growth retardation, developmental delays and facial dysmorphisms.7 Limb abnormalities characteristically have ‘typical BOPS posturing’ involving multiple joint/flexion deformities.8 BOPS has symptomatic diagnostic criteria8 but with significant phenotypic variability. Atypical presentations of BOPS can include seizure activity and organ abnormalities (cardiac, renal and genital).9 In 2011 it was shown to be associated with de novo pathogenic ASXL1 variants and autosomal dominant inheritance, but with some genetic heterogeneity given only 7/13 patients had identifiable ASXL1 variants.10 Currently there are no reported ASXL1 variants with neuromuscular/muscle-specific phenotypes.

Here we present the first neuromuscular phenotype associated with a pathogenic ASXL1 variant, expanding the genetic phenotype.

Case presentation

The proband is a 71-year-old male from non-consanguineous Caucasian Australian parents. His referral described proximal weakness over a 12 month period; however, the assessment revealed various congenital symptoms.

He was born at term with no delivery complications and was not described as a floppy baby. His 6 week morphology scan noted a preterm growth abnormality possibly affecting his neural tube. He denied any maternally transmitted infections. After birth, he had difficulty feeding from impaired sucking, which in combination with dysmorphic facial features, led to a diagnosis of Moebius syndrome (underdevelopment of cranial nerves VI and VII).11 He experienced childhood speech difficulties with pronunciation, receiving speech pathology input until age 12. He was underweight, had pes cavus and low calf muscle bulk. This resulted in motor developmental delays, only crawling at 13 months old. Subjectively he was the slowest at walking and running among peers and has never been able to swim due to weakness. He describes a bilateral upper limb action tremor present since childhood. While the proband did not undergo any formal cognitive testing, he described childhood learning difficulties and left school at age 15, but denied any issues with full time adult employment.

As an adult, he endorsed difficulties with running, requiring orthotics in his 40 s for his pes cavus. This was exacerbated by a critical illness myoneuropathy from deconditioning following a prolonged post-surgical ICU admission in 2021. Subsequently, he was diagnosed with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) based on a mildly elevated CSF protein despite the absence of acquired demyelinating features on nerve conduction studies (NCS). He was unsuccessfully treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) and steroids. Separately, he commenced levodopa for his tremor, which was subjectively beneficial.

Over the preceding year, the proband noted worsening weakness limiting his mobility, specifically being unable to climb stairs or stand from a seated position and prevented him lifting objects above his head. He now uses a walking aid due to poor balance and describes progressive dysphagia affecting all solid foods.

On examination, the proband has dysmorphic facial features, such as microcephaly, micrognathia, anteverted nares and a high arched palate. There was severely restricted bilateral extraocular abduction without nystagmus, as well as bilateral frontalis weakness (Medical Research Council (MRC) scale 0/5), orbicularis oculi (MRC 2/5) and mouth closure (MRC 4/5) (online supplemental figure 1). His speech was normal with no dysarthria or dysphasia. In the upper limbs, there was a mild bilateral action tremor (without ataxia) and bilateral proximal muscle wasting (online supplemental figure 2) causing weakness in shoulder abduction (MRC 4/5) and wrist flexion (MRC 4+/5). In the lower limbs, there was marked bilateral wasting of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles. He had bilateral pes cavus and hammer toes (online supplemental figures 3 and 4). There was bilateral weakness primarily in the distal lower limbs including ankle dorsiflexion (MRC 2/5), ankle plantarflexion (MRC 3/5), toe flexion/extension (MRC 3/5) and hip flexion (MRC 4+/5). He was unable to stand unassisted. He was areflexic in the lower limbs. Vibration sense was present to the ankles bilaterally. His gait was wide based, Romberg’s positive and with bilateral foot drop. His remaining neurological examination was normal.

Investigations demonstrated elevated CK between 62 and 991 (RR<150 U/L). His CSF testing demonstrated mildly elevated protein (0.72, RR 0.15–0.45 g/L) but was otherwise unremarkable. A neuropathy screen, myositis immunoblot and HMG-CoA reductase antibody were negative (online supplemental figure 5). Upper and lower limb NCS demonstrated a severe length-dependent sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic denervation on electromyography (EMG).

MRI in 2023 included brain, cervical spine and lower limb assessment. This demonstrated mild-moderate small vessel disease, evidence of acquired cerebellar atrophy but no cervical spine abnormalities. Muscle MRI (figure 1) showed selective but symmetrical atrophy with fatty replacement of gluteus maximus (mild), semimembranosus (severe), adductor magnus (severe), tibialis anterior (moderate) and gastrocnemius (moderate).

Previous genetic testing for Charcot Marie Tooth disease (CMT1A duplication analysis) was negative. Further testing included a CGH array and next generation sequencing (NGS) panel for neuropathy and myopathy associated genes which all returned negative. Given the patient’s phenotype, a neurodevelopmental genetic panel was completed. This found a previously reported heterozygous nonsense variant in ASXL1 (RefSeqNM_015338.6)(c.1210C>T;p.Arg404Ter), a pathogenic variant with autosomal dominant inheritance.9 10 12 The variant allele fraction was 42%, consistent with a germline variant without mosaicism. Segregation studies could not be completed as both parents were deceased and testing was not undertaken for the proband’s three asymptomatic siblings (online supplemental figure 6).

Discussion

Here, we describe a novel neuromuscular phenotype associated with an ASXL1 variant. While there are similarities to reported cases of BOPS, there are clear distinctions here in the clinical manifestations and natural history. The congenital progressive muscle weakness, symmetrical muscle wasting and bilateral but selective muscle atrophy seen on MRI are all consistent with an inherited neuromuscular cause. The pattern of muscle involvement includes primarily facial and extraocular muscles, proximal upper limbs and distal lower limbs. Moreover, the presence of dysmorphic facial features, congenital limb abnormalities (pes cavus and hammer toes) and motor developmental delays reinforce its underlying genetic/congenital aetiology. This adult age diagnosis is unique among previous ASXL1-associated BOPS with none diagnosed beyond age 24 and the majority occurring in the first months or years of life.13

Classically, BOPS is defined by distinctive facial dysmorphisms, limb flexion deformities, developmental delays and impaired growth.8 14 Overlap with the BOPS phenotype here included dysmorphic facial features (anteverted nares, hypertelorism, high arched palate and microcephaly), infantile feeding difficulties/progressive dysphagia and developmental delays. Severe intellectual disability has been previously reported; however, this proband describes only a mild intellectual impairment given he comfortably managed full time work despite childhood learning challenges. While the proband has a genetic variant associated with BOPS, he does not meet the clinical diagnostic criteria.8 Features seen in BOPS, such as nevus flammeus, synophrys, prominent metopic ridge, exophthalmos/prominent globes, cleft lip/palate, seizures, cardiac abnormalities, obstructive sleep apnoea, myopia (patient has hyperopia), Wilms’ tumours or structural MRI brain abnormalities, were not present; however, limited childhood medical records makes confirming some features difficult. While the proband does have bilateral pes cavus and hammer toes, he lacked the widespread flexion deformities seen with BOPS posturing. While there is significant phenotypic variability among ASXL1 associated conditions,8 9 this case with predominant muscle involvement and older age at diagnosis is unique.

We acknowledge that the adult diagnosis and comorbidities (critical illness neuromyopathy) makes accurate phenotyping more difficult. NCS/EMG indicate an underlying neuropathic process however without previous testing its aetiology is less clear. While there is cerebellar atrophy seen on MRI, this is not clinically apparent and was not present in older imaging, suggesting an acquired aetiology. Additional cases will help define this phenotype, especially if diagnosed in younger patients. However, this may be complicated by a degree of genotype-phenotype heterogeneity as seen in ASXL1 associated BOPS.9

Despite presenting with a primarily neuromuscular phenotype, the overlapping neurodevelopmental symptoms were crucial to achieving a genetic diagnosis via neurodevelopmental panel testing where neuropathy/myopathy panel testing missed it. This reinforces the importance of accurate phenotyping to guide genetic sequencing. Moreover, it confirms with hindsight that his previous diagnoses of Moebius syndrome and CIDP were likely misdiagnosed manifestations of his ASXL1 condition with inappropriate treatment.

Determining variant inheritance was not possible given the proband’s parents were deceased. Almost all cases of ASXL1-associated BOPS are de novo, except one case of inheritance from a maternal germline mosaicism.15 In this case, the absence of other symptomatic family members suggests a de novo variant. The proband had no known fertility complications but independently chose not to have children to avoid transmission of his undiagnosed condition.

In summary, we present this case of a distinct neuromuscular phenotype associated with an ASXL1 variant. This expands on the known neurodevelopmental phenotype of the gene and serves as a basis for further characterisation of this disorder.
