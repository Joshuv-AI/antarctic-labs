Correspondence for the article “Histological architecture of the intersphincteric region of the anal canal: implications for the anatomical basis of anal fistula pathways”
Authors: Nicola Clemente, James C W Khaw
Published: 2026

The recent article by Muro et al., “Histological architecture of the intersphincteric region of the anal canal: implications for the anatomical basis of anal fistula pathways,” is an excellent addition to the scientific literature [1]. The authors have provided an exquisite, high-resolution histological characterisation of the intersphincteric region, rightfully challenging the long-standing oversimplification of this area as a “simple anatomical plane” [1]. As a research team deeply involved in the radiological and surgical management of complex anal fistulas, we believe Muro’s histological findings offer a compelling anatomical framework that is highly consistent with several recent clinical and radiological concepts we have proposed: namely, the Garg fascia, the outer-sphincteric space, and the RIFIL (roof of ischiorectal fossa inferior to levator) fistula [2].

The mosaic longitudinal muscle and the “middle space”

Muro et al. delineated the longitudinal muscle (LM) as a heterogeneous “mosaic” organisation rather than a uniform layer [1]. Their morphometric analysis reveals that the dense component of the LM terminates at approximately 54% of the internal anal sphincter (IAS) length [1]. Below this point, a “spacious compartment” characterised by loose smooth muscle and connective tissue—termed the loose inferior compartment—dominates [1].

This histological finding aligns with the latest clinical observations that have been categorized as the middle space or the true intersphincteric space [2]. While conventional teaching based on the Parks classification often places this space generically “between the sphincters,” Muro et al. clarify that the primary site of an “intersphincteric abscess” corresponds to this specific loose inferior LM compartment [1, 2]. This area represents a natural path of least resistance for cryptoglandular infections to spread, providing a potential histological basis for the initiation and directional spread of anal fistulas [1]. The coexistence of dense and sparse areas within the LM, previously suggested by high-resolution MRI studies, is now strongly supported by Muro's immunohistochemical analysis [1].

Histological basis of the outer-sphincteric space and Garg fascia

A critical anatomical nuance discussed by Muro et al. is the presence of “interbundle gaps” within the external anal sphincter (EAS) [1]. They observed that loose LM fibers traverse these natural gaps to extend toward the subcutaneous tissue and the ischioanal fossa (IAF) [1]. While Muro et al. suggest these fibres project toward the IAF, a detailed analysis of hundreds of MRI scans and their correlation with operative findings has led us to propose the existence of a previously unrecognised restricting layer: the Garg Fascia [2].

In many transsphincteric fistulas, sepsis penetrates the EAS through the very interbundle gaps Muro describes, but it does not immediately enter the ischiorectal fat [2]. It is hypothesized that it is instead contained and restricted by the fascia covering the EAS (Garg Fascia) [2]. While the anatomical status of this fascia remains a subject of ongoing discussion, the potential space created between the EAS and this proposed layer is termed the outer-sphincteric space [2]. Muro’s identification of the branching loose LM fibers (LM-ex) provides a plausible histological “exit route” through which infection could escape the intersphincteric region to reach this space [1, 2]. Without recognizing this restrictive boundary, these extensions are often misinterpreted as early ischiorectal involvement, potentially leading to wider surgical drainage than necessary [2].

Redefining high extensions: RIFIL vs. supralevator pathways

Perhaps the most intriguing intersection of this research lies in the management of high fistula extensions. Muro et al. describe a consistent gap between the two layers of the levator ani (LA): the inner rectal-attached bundle (LA-re) and the outer posterior encircling bundle (LA-p) [1]. They also noted that loose LM extension fibers (LM-ex) attach directly to the LA-re fibers as they enter the intersphincteric region [1].

It is hypothesised that this structural continuity may serve as the anatomical substrate for the RIFIL fistula (fistula at the roof of ischiorectal fossa inferior to the levator muscle) (Fig. 1) [2]. Clinically, RIFIL fistulas spread superiorly within the outer-sphincteric space along the inferior surface of the levator muscle [2]. Muro et al. noted that in their normal specimens, they could not clearly identify a pre-existing anatomical continuity for “supralevator” (Type 5) spread, suggesting it may require “pathological disruption” [1]. We propose that RIFIL fistulas are the missing link in this interpretation [2]. Many fistulas diagnosed as “supralevator” or “high transsphincteric” are actually RIFIL fistulas—remaining entirely below the levator muscle but high enough at the “roof” of the fossa to mimic supralevator involvement on macroscopic imaging [2].

The distinction is of paramount surgical importance. As noted in a controlled study of 419 operated cases, RIFIL fistulas have a significantly higher recurrence rate (30.6%) compared to non-RIFIL fistulas (7.2%, p = 0.0001) [2, 3]. The correct surgical treatment of a RIFIL fistula involves precise drainage or tract ablation strictly within the outer-sphincteric space below the levator ani. Misdiagnosing a RIFIL fistula as a supralevator fistula and attempting transrectal drainage can inadvertently injure the levator muscle, converting it into a true translevator (Type 5) fistula [2, 3].

Conclusion

Muro et al. have provided a highly detailed histological map that complements and supports the clinical and radiological models we have proposed [1, 2]. By integrating their findings of LM heterogeneity and LA compartmentalization with the proposed concepts of the Garg Fascia, the outer-sphincteric space, and RIFIL fistulas, the surgical community can move toward a more precise, anatomy-based classification system [2].
