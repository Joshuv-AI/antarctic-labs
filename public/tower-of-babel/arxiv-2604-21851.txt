Betting on Bets: Anytime-Valid Tests for Stochastic Dominance
Sebastian Arnold, Yo Joong Choe, Marco Scarsini, Ilia Tsetlin

Abstract
How can we monitor, in real time, whether one uncertain prospect has any upside over another? To answer this question, we develop a novel family of sequential, anytime-valid tests for stochastic dominance (SD; also known as stochastic ordering), a classical and popular notion for comparing entire distribution functions. The problem is distinct from the popular problem of testing for dominance in means, which would not capture distributional differences beyond the first moment. We first derive powerful, nonparametric e-processes that quantify evidence against the null hypothesis that one prospect is dominated by another. For first-order SD, these e-processes are constructed as a mixture of asymptotically growth-rate optimal e-variables and yield a test of power one. The approach further generalizes to sequential testing for SD beyond the first order, including any higher-order SD. Empirically, we demonstrate that the resulting sequential tests are competitive with existing non-sequential SD tests in terms of power, while achieving validity under continuous monitoring that existing methods do not. Finally, we sketch the complementary and challenging problem of testing the non-SD null hypothesis, which asks whether a prospect has a definite upside, and describe the conditions under which we can derive a nontrivial anytime-valid test.

Introduction

Stochastic dominance (SD),
also known as
stochastic ordering,
is a classical framework for comparing an arbitrary pair of random variables
(
56
;
73
, see, e.g.,)
.
In its simplest form, first-order stochastic dominance (FSD) states that the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of one random variable lies entirely below that of another random variable; higher-order SD notions generalize this idea to integrals of the CDFs.
Yet, SD is far more complex and nonparametric than dominance in means; in fact, no finite number of moment inequalities implies FSD or higher-order SD
(
7
)
.
SD is a cornerstone in economics and decision theory
(
23
;
45
;
56
;
85
, e.g.,)
, where a decision maker (DM) is tasked with comparing uncertain prospects under general classes of utility functions.

Recent advances in sequential inference, particularly in the theory of
sequential, anytime-valid inference (SAVI)
based on
e-processes

(
70
;
64
;
62
;
26
)
, motivate us to revisit the problem of SD testing.
While most classical SD tests are fixed-sample tests with asymptotic validity
(
53
;
4
;
48
, e.g.,)
, the modern framework of SAVI warrant a new look at SD testing because the framework is particularly well-suited to SD testing.
First, SD testing is popular in applications where the data is inherently sequential—after all, the goal is to compare and order uncertain
prospects
in the future based on past data.
This makes it desirable to use SAVI methods, which provably control the type I error under optional stopping (at adaptively chosen sample sizes).
Second, e-processes are natural candidates for handling the composite and nonparametric nature of SD testing problems; as we shall see in this paper, these methods do not require any parametric assumptions on the distributions being compared.
We will also see, particularly in Section
6
, that SD testing itself poses unique challenges to the existing SAVI stack, as it will require both constructing new e-processes and rethinking the roles of null and alternative hypotheses.

In this paper, we develop anytime-valid tests and e-processes for various types of SD testing problems where the data arrives sequentially over time.
To ground our discussion in real-world applications, we now introduce our first motivating example.

Example 1

(Testing out a new treatment: Is there an upside?)
.

Consider the problem of deploying a new treatment
Y
into the market.
This could be an app piloting a new subscription plan;
an employer testing out a new technology stack;
a sports team manager scouting a new player to trade for;
and so on.
The DM wants to know whether the performance of
Y
, as measured by a metric of interest, has any upside over an existing treatment
X
(e.g., the baseline subscription plan, the most popular tech stack, or the candidate replacement player).
As the new treatment is deployed, the DM wants to monitor the data over time and collect evidence on this upside.
If there is sufficient evidence, the DM can decide to keep or adopt
Y
as an option; otherwise, the DM can decide to drop
Y
and stick with the existing ones.

Setting aside the practical nuances of each application, the core statistical problem common to all scenarios in Example
1
is to test for an “upside” of one uncertain outcome over another.
We may formalize this as testing whether
Y
is stochastically dominated by
X
, that is:

\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathbb{P}_{Y}\preceq\mathbb{P}_{X}\quad\text{vs.}\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mathbb{P}_{Y}\npreceq\mathbb{P}_{X}\,,

(1)

where
\preceq
denotes a SD relation in some order or under some utility class (to be defined later).
Rejecting the null would inform the DM that treatment
Y
has an upside over
X
and they should consider it as a contender.
To effectively address this problem, we will develop e-processes that are

(a)

Anytime-valid:
By definition, the e-process is valid at any adaptively chosen sample sizes, allowing the DM to monitor the data and stop whenever they wish, without compromising validity;

(b)

(Asymptotically) powerful:
The e-process construction is based on a mixture of growth-rate optimal e-variables (exact for FSD & asymptotic for higher-order SD), such that under any alternative, it enjoys exponential growth and yields a sequential test with asymptotic power one;

(c)

Nonparametric:
The above properties are shown without any parametric assumptions on the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
, requiring essentially no assumptions when testing first-order SD and only a one-sided lower bound when testing higher-order SD; and

(d)

Robust to dependence:
These properties do not rely on independence between the two random variables, including when they are anti-monotonic.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows.
After reviewing the background and related work in Section
2
, in Section
3
we begin with the SD testing problem (
1
) when the SD relation
\preceq
corresponds to first-order SD.
In this case, the resulting test is valid without any tail assumptions, and we derive a mixture of growth-rate optimal
(
26
, GRO;)
e-variables that are shown to achieve positive growth rate and an asymptotic power of one under any alternative.
Then, in Section
4
, we generalize these ideas to testing integral stochastic orders, notably including any
k
-th order SD (
k
-SD), increasing convex order SD, and even infinite-order SD, by assuming only that the data has finite one-sided bound.
This is more general than common assumptions in classical, non-anytime-valid tests, such as
4
;
48
.
In Section
5
, we include multiple simulation experiments that demonstrate the validity and power of these methods in sequential experiments.
In particular, the resulting tests are empirically more powerful against the SD null than baseline methods using time-uniform confidence bands for CDFs
(
33
, e.g.,)
, and competitive with the aforementioned classical SD tests that do not come with anytime-valid guarantees.

In Section
6
, and further in Appendix
B
, we briefly but formally consider the reverse problem to SD null testing (
1
) where the goal is to
affirm
that one random variable stochastically dominates another.
In this case, the null and alternative hypotheses are swapped, making the null much “larger” and thus the inference problem far more challenging
(
17
)
.
We will first discuss the difficulty of this problem and then derive conditions under which we can still derive non-trivial anytime-valid tests using existing tools.
Finally, Section
7
concludes with a discussion of extensions and limitations, and the Appendix contains proofs, additional results, and further extensions.

2
Background and Related Work

2.1
Notation and background on statistical hypothesis testing

Throughout the paper, we consider an i.i.d. sequence
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),\ldots
of paired random variables with joint support
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and write
(X,Y)
for a generic pair distributed as
(X_{1},Y_{1})
.
We denote by
\Pi(\mathcal{Z})
the family of probability measures on
\mathcal{Z}
.

We assume that all random quantities are defined on some underlying measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and denote by
\mathfrak{B}
the family of all probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. We call a subset
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
a
statistical hypothesis
. As usual, we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
the
null hypothesis
, and by
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
the
alternative hypothesis
.
For any
{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathfrak{B}
, we let
\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}
be the distribution of
(X,Y)
under
{\mathbb{P}}
, that is the push-forward measure of
{\mathbb{P}}
under
(X,Y)
. Likewise, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
the marginal distributions of
X
and
Y
under
{\mathbb{P}}
, respectively. With a slight abuse of notation, we treat
\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
equivalently as the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of
X
and
Y
under
\mathbb{P}
, respectively. Whenever the dependence on
\mathbb{P}
can be suppressed, we denote the CDFs of
X
and
Y
simply by
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
. Finally, for
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
(X,Y)^{t}=((X_{i},Y_{i}))_{i=1}^{t}
, and define the data-filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma((X,Y)^{t})\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.

2.2
SAVI: E-variables, e-processes, Ville’s inequality, and sequential tests

A non-negative random variable
E
is called
e-variable
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
, if
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{E}\right]\leq 1
for all
{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Likewise, we call a sequence of adapted, non-negative random variables
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}(E_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq 1
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
sequential e-variables
(for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
).
The running product of a sequence of sequential e-variables (for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
) forms a
test (super)martingale
, that is a nonnegative (super)martingale with respect to any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
with starting value at most 1.

Test supermartingales provide a way to formulate statistical testing as a betting game. In this interpretation, the value of the test supermartingale corresponds to the accumulated wealth of a gambler after a sequence of bets placed against the null hypothesis
(
72
)
.
For example, in a coin-toss game with unknown probability of heads equal to
p\in(0,1)
, a player that bets $1 on heads in each round and receives $2 if the coin comes up heads or $0 otherwise, cannot expect to gain money over time under the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}:p\leq 1/2
.
That is, large values of the test supermartingale quantify evidence against the null hypothesis.
This is made mathematically rigorous by Ville’s inequality
(
75
)
, a time-uniform version of Markov’s inequality forming the fundamental building block for anytime-valid inference.

Lemma 1

(Ville’s inequality)
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be a test (super)martingale for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:M_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha,\quad\forall\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}.

(2)

Importantly, there are composite null hypotheses for which it is not possible to construct non-trivial test supermartingales
(
63
;
30
, e.g.,)
.
However, in such situations it may still be possible to find non-trivial e-processes.
An
e-process
for a composite hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a nonnegative adapted stochastic process
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
E_{\tau}
is an e-variable, for any (possibly infinite) stopping time
\tau
, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{E_{\tau}}\right]\leq 1
for all
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Equivalently, an adapted process
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
if it is upper bounded by a test supermartingale, for each
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Hence, any test supermartingale is also an e-process, and Ville’s inequality continues to hold for e-processes
(
62
)
.
By Ville’s inequality, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and e-process
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, by defining
\psi=\indsymb\left(E_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\right),t\in\mathbb{N}
we directly obtain a valid
level-
\alpha
sequential test
, that is. a sequential test satisfying the
time-uniform type-I error guarantee

\mathbb{P}(\exists t\geq 1:\psi_{t}=1)\leq\alpha
, for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
In this paper, we refer to the left-hand side as the
Ville error
of the test
\psi
under the null
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

2.3
Preliminaries on Stochastic Dominance

We begin by defining
first-order SD
, the simplest and canonical SD relation also known as the
usual stochastic order
.

Definition 1
.

Given random variables
X
and
Y
, we say that
X

dominates
Y
in the first order
if

F_{X}(z)\leq F_{Y}(z),\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}\,,

(3)

and write in this case
Y\preceq_{1}X
.

FSD defines a partial order on the space of probability distributions, that is, it is reflexive, anti-symmetric and transitive, but not a total order as two distributions are incomparable if their corresponding CDFs cross.
It is notably distinct from the (complete) order based on means —in particular,
Y\preceq_{1}X
implies
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{Y}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{X}\right]
but not vice versa.
Importantly, the FSD relation at (
3
) is defined only with respect to the marginal distributions of
X
and
Y
and does not consider their joint distribution.
In practice, we do not have access to the distributions functions
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
and we will have to estimate them in order to draw inference on FSD; see Section
3.1
for the exact formulation of our statistical hypothesis.

The following Lemma gives a well-known equivalent characterizations for FSD.
For a proof, see, e.g., Section 1.A of
73
.

Lemma 2

(A utility-based characterization of FSD)
.

For random variables
X
and
Y
, we have
Y\preceq_{1}X
if and only if

\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]\leq\mathbb{E}[u(X)]

for all measurable and increasing functions
u:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the expectations exist.

Lemma
2
characterizes FSD with “utility functions”: under the classical expected utility framework
(
68
)
,
Y\preceq_{1}X
means that any utility-maximizing DM who prefers more to less would prefer
X
over
Y
.
In this view, SD establishes a preference ordering between prospects for
any
DM with a non-decreasing, but otherwise unspecified, utility function.
This equivalent characterization becomes more natural when considering higher-order SD (in Section
4
), which corresponds to dominance under more restrictive classes of utility functions (e.g., increasing and concave functions for SSD).

In addition to the utility-based characterization, there are two other standard equivalent characterizations for FSD: A pointwise ordering of the generalized inverses of the CDFs and the existence of coupled copies

\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}
such that
\hat{Y}\leq\hat{X}
almost surely. Since we do not use these characterizations in the present work, we refer to Section 1.A of
73
for details.

2.4
Related Work on (Testing) Stochastic Dominance

The literature on SD is vast, dating back at least to
29
and
37
who studied the problem of comparing two nonnegative vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In statistics, some classical methods study testing problems
(
50
;
43
)
are related to, but distinct from, fully nonparametric SD testing.
Many modern testing methods for SD comes from the broader economics and decision theory literature.
There, the focus is on the conditions under which decision makers, equipped with a partially specified utility function, would prefer one uncertain prospect (or a risky asset) over another, under the classical expected utility hypothesis
(
76
;
68
;
23
)
.
See, e.g.,
27
;
28
;
45
;
55
;
56
;
73
for general characterizations of SD in this context.

Of particular relevance to our discussion is
53
’s bootstrap-based test for FSD, based on the
one-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic

\hat{D}^{\mathsf{KS}}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\,[\hat{F}_{X}(z)-\hat{F}_{Y}(z)]\,,

(4)

where
\hat{F}_{X}
and
\hat{F}_{Y}
denote the empirical CDFs for
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Under the i.i.d. assumption and additional regularity conditions on the CDFs (e.g., continuity),
53
derives the asymptotic distribution for the one-sided KS statistic.

This KS-based approach has since been improved and generalized to higher-order SD and non-standard dominance testing
(
19
;
38
;
2
;
16
;
1
;
4
;
60
;
20
;
57
;
89
, e.g.,)
.
It is common to make the regularity conditions on the CDFs and boundedness assumptions when testing higher-order SD
(
4
;
20
, e.g.,)
.
The main approach we discuss in Section
3
can be viewed as an anytime-valid (and finite-sample) generalization of the one-sided KS test, whose validity does not rely on independence across time or regularity conditions on the CDFs.
For higher-order SD, we show that the validity depends either on a lower bound on the outcomes, or on a sub-exponential condition on the tails.

Aside from the KS-based approach, a parallel line of work
(
47
;
48
)
has popularized the
one-sided Cramér-von Mises statistic
, a mixture of squared positive differences between the CDFs

\hat{D}^{\mathsf{mix}}_{\psi}=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\psi(z)\left[(\hat{F}_{X}(z)-\hat{F}_{Y}(z))_{+}\right]^{2}dz\,,

where
\psi
is a mixture prior on
\mathcal{Z}
.
Generally, these “mixture-type” methods rely on more specific regularity conditions and validity under resampling (typically, i.i.d. data).
See
85
for a recent review in this direction.
Finally, we note there are methods for testing different types of SD, such as uniform dominance over investment portfolios
(
69
;
74
)
, dominance in prediction accuracy for machine learning models
(
35
)
, and more.

In summary, most existing methods have only asymptotic and fixed-sample validity, and most rely on additional regularity conditions to enable a bootstrap-based procedure.
Notable exceptions are
33
, who were the first to propose a sequential test for first-order SD based on their general construction of time-uniform and value-uniform CDF bands.
In another line of work, building on the theory of reverse submartingales,
51
derive time-uniform lower and upper confidence bounds for the two-sided KS statistic, which can be adapted to its one-sided version (
4
).
However, in both works, SD testing (and only first-order SD testing) arises as a special case of their objectives.
In our simulations, we will see how sequential tests based on time-uniform CDF bands or KS bounds are more conservative than tests specifically designed for SD.

3
Anytime-Valid Tests for First-Order Stochastic Dominance

3.1
Problem Formulation

In this section we are interested in the null that
Y
is dominated by
X
in FSD. Firstly, we properly embed the FSD Definition
1
into a statistical hypothesis testing problem, by defining the
FSD null
as

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{P}_{Y}\preceq_{1}\mathbb{P}_{X}\}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{P}(X\leq z)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\leq z),\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}\}\,.

(5)

The FSD null forms an intersection null, and may be written as
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\bigcap_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
,
for

\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{P}(X\leq z)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\leq z)\},\quad z\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

(6)

Lemma 3
.

The FSD null at (
5
) is convex.

For completeness, we provide a short proof in Appendix
A
, where all other proofs omitted from the main text can also be found.
Lemma
3
has a positive implication in light of the fact that e-variables remain valid under convex combination: Since any e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is readily an e-variable for the convex hull of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, Lemma
3
implies that we can test
\mathcal{H}_{0}
without inadvertently testing a much larger null hypothesis.

Importantly, the FSD null at (
3
) is defined solely in terms of the marginal distributions of
X
and
Y
.
The following example illustrates how dependency may influence the difficulty of our testing problem if we want to test the null after each round.

Example 2

(A toy example with antimonotonicity)
.

For
z_{1}<z_{2}<z_{3}<z_{4}
, consider
\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}
with
\mathbb{P}_{X}=(\delta_{z_{1}}+\delta_{z_{3}})/2
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y}=(\delta_{z_{2}}+\delta_{z_{4}})/2
such that
X\preceq Y
and
\mathbb{P}\notin\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
The given marginal distributions are realized by the
comonotonic coupling

\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}=(\delta_{(z_{1},z_{2})}+\delta_{(z_{3},z_{4})})/2
, as well as by the
antimonotonic coupling

\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)}=(\delta_{(z_{1},z_{4})}+\delta_{(z_{3},z_{2})})/2
.
We expect to reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}
much easier in the former case where
X_{t}\leq Y_{t}
for all
t\geq\mathbb{N}
, than in the second case where
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}<Y_{t})=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}>Y_{t})=1/2
.

3.2
Testing the SD Null via thresholds

We first construct e-variables for testing the FSD null in a single round for a fixed
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
While its construction is straightforward, the e-variables serve as building blocks for constructing powerful e-processes for sequentially testing
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Lemma 4

(The building-block e-variable)
.

For any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,

S(\lambda,z)\coloneqq 1+\lambda[\indsymb\left(X\leq z\right)-\indsymb\left(Y\leq z\right)]

(7)

is an e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
at (
6
), for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

S(\lambda,z)\geq 0
, for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
, and we see directly that
S(\lambda,z)
is an e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
by writing

\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)=\left\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{\indsymb\left(X\leq z\right)-\indsymb\left(Y\leq z\right)}\right]\leq 0\right\}\,.

(8)

∎

E-variables of the form (
7
) appear ubiquitously in the literature on testing the means of bounded random variables.
Their origins go back at least to optimal gambling strategies for coin betting due to
39
, where
\lambda
is the gambler’s choice on how much wealth to place on the coin outcome. See, e.g.,
58
;
83
;
78
; and
3
among others.
Reversely,
11
recently showed that the family
\{S(\lambda,z)\mid\lambda\in[0,1]\}
are the only admissible e-values for the hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
, in the sense that for any e-variable
E
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
, there exists
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that
E\leq S(\lambda,z)
almost surely.
This implies that it suffices to focus on e-variables of the form (
7
), reducing the problem of finding powerful e-variables to choosing suitable betting parameters
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Remark 1

(Betting on bets)
.

The building block e-variable (
7
) has a particularly natural interpretation in the testing-by-betting language
(
71
)
.
Given two uncertain prospects
X
and
Y
(say, the returns of a stock and a bond), suppose a forecaster claims that the probability of
X
falling below a threshold
z
(say, 0%) is smaller than that of
Y
.
He then proposes a double-or-nothing-or-push bet (on what are essentially “bets” themselves) with three possible outcomes: if
X\leq z
but
Y>z
, then the money is doubled; if
X>z
but
Y\leq z
, then the money is lost; if both fall above or below
z
, then nothing happens.
The skeptic, endowed with a dollar to spare on this bet, chooses to invest a
\lambda
-fraction of her money on this bet and keep
(1-\lambda)
in the pocket.
Denoting the three bet outcomes as

D(z)=\indsymb\left(X\leq z\right)-\indsymb\left(Y\leq z\right),

the e-variable (
7
) is precisely the money she ends up with after a single round of this bet:

S(\lambda,z)=1+\lambda D(z)=(1-\lambda)+\lambda[1+D(z)].

Then, over repeated rounds, the amount of money that the skeptic can make off of this game provides evidence against the forecaster’s initial claim, which corresponds to the null hypothesis (
8
).
This interpretation naturally arises from the aforementioned references that treat
S
as a
coin-betting
e-variable.

We will repeatedly use the lemma below, which follows from the fact that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
forms an intersection hypothesis and that e-variables are closed under taking mixtures
(
77
, see, e.g.,)
.

Lemma 5
.

For any
\psi\in\Pi(\mathcal{Z})
, the mixture
\int S(\lambda(z),z)\,\mathrm{d}\psi(z)
is an e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, where for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
S(\lambda(z),z)
is given at (
7
) with betting parameter
\lambda(z)\in[0,1]
.

By Lemma
5
, for testing the global
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, it suffices to come up with good e-variables
S(\lambda(z),z)
at all thresholds, and to average the obtained evidence.
The betting interpretation is that the skeptic now splits the initial capital across all thresholds
z\in\mathcal{Z}
(each representing a bet) according to weights
\psi
.

The next theorem shows how this idea translates directly when data comes in sequentially.
For
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we let

S_{t}(\lambda,z)=1+\lambda[\indsymb\left(X_{t}\leq z\right)-\indsymb\left(Y_{t}\leq z\right)]\,.

(9)

Theorem 1

(An e-process and a sequential test for FSD)
.

Let
(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Pi(\mathcal{Z})
, and
(\lambda_{t}(z))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
,
z\in\mathcal{Z}

S_{t}=\int_{\mathcal{Z}}S_{t}(\lambda_{t}(z),z)\,\mathrm{d}\psi_{t}(z),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

forms a sequence of sequential e-variables for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and its running product

E_{t}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell},\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

(10)

forms a test supermartingale, and thus an e-process, for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. In particular, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}(E_{t}\geq 1/\alpha)\leq\alpha,\quad\forall\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\,.

Proof.

(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are sequential e-variables by Lemma
5
and predictability of
(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
(\lambda_{t}(z))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
,
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Their running product
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
forms thus a test supermartingale satisfying Ville’s inequality (
2
).
∎

Following Remark
1
, we can interpret Theorem

as initiating a protocol for betting games.

Game 1

(Testing FSD via predictable mixtures)
.

The DM agrees to play the following game.

•

DM starts with initial capital
E_{0}=1
.

•

For each round
t=1,2,\dotsc

DM announces a mixture
\psi_{t}\in\Pi({\mathcal{Z}}_{t})
, and the bets
\lambda_{t}(z)\in[0,1]
, for

.

2.

Reality announces the outcomes
(x_{t},y_{t})
.

3.

DM’s wealth updates as

The skeptic now plays a sequence of betting games, starting with initial capital
E_{0}=1
, over a sequence of outcomes.
The skeptic can choose a different mixture and bet at each round, and they can bet any fraction of money earned from the previous rounds.
Over time, the total wealth of the skeptic serves as evidence against the FSD null (
5

Theorem

establishes the “safety”
(

Example 2

3.3
Choosing log-optimal betting strategies

Theorem
1
shows that
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
at (
10
) forms a test supermartingale for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
for any choice of predicable mixtures and betting parameters.
In this section, we discuss sensible choices which allow the DM in Game
1
to reject the null as fast as possible.
We firstly characterize the optimal betting parameter
\lambda^{\star}(z)
with respect to some fixed threshold
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and propose a natural empirical predicable plug-in version of it.
Later, we propose how to (sequentially) average evidence over the thresholds in order to obtain a sequentially valid test with asympotitic power one.

3.3.1
The growth-rate optimal (GRO) betting strategy & an e-process

For a fixed
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we want to choose
\lambda
such that
S(\lambda,z)
grows as fast as possible under the alternative

\mathcal{H}_{1}(z)=\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)^{c}=\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{Q}(X>z)>\mathbb{Q}(Y>z)\}\,.

Assume that the data is generated by some
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(z)
. We choose
\lambda\in[0,1]
to maximize the
e-power
(
70
,
26
,
79
,
41
),

g(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[{\log S(\lambda,z)}\right]=\log(1+\lambda)p(z)+\log(1-\lambda)q(z)\,,\quad\lambda\in[0,1],

(11)

for

p(z)=\mathbb{Q}(X\leq z<Y)=\mathbb{Q}(D(z)=1),\quad q(z)=\mathbb{Q}(Y\leq z<X)=\mathbb{Q}(D(z)=-1)\,,

and
D(z)=\indsymb\left(X\leq z\right)-\indsymb\left(Y\leq z\right)\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

Proposition 1

(GRO e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
vs.
\mathcal{H}_{1}(z)
)
.

For any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(z)
, the parameter

\lambda^{\star}(z)=\frac{p(z)-q(z)}{p(z)+q(z)}\in(0,1]

yields the GRO-optimal e-variable
S(\lambda^{\star}(z),z)
for testing
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
against the simple alternative
\{\mathbb{Q}\}
.

Proof.

\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}(z)
implies
p(z)+q(z)\geq p(z)-q(z)=\mathbb{Q}(X\leq z)-\mathbb{Q}(Y\leq z)>0
. It is straightforward to check that
g(\lambda)
at (
11
) is strictly concave with unique maximizer
\lambda^{\star}(z)\in(0,1]
.
∎

Proposition
1
yields the following natural predictable plug-in estimator for
\lambda^{\star}(z)
,

\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z)=(1-c)\land\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{t-1}(X\leq z<Y)-\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{t-1}(Y\leq z<X)}{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{t-1}(X\leq z<Y)+\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{t-1}(Y\leq z<X)}\right)_{+}\in[0,1-c],

(12)

for
c>0
small,
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{t-1}
the empirical distribution of
(X,Y)^{t-1}
, and
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z)=0
if the denominator equals zero. We impose the upper bound
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z)\leq 1-c
to avoid e-values becoming exactly zero. The fraction in (
12
) allows for the equivalent representation

\frac{\hat{F}_{X}^{t-1}(z)-\hat{F}_{Y}^{t-1}(z)}{\hat{F}_{X}^{t-1}(z)+\hat{F}_{Y}^{t-1}(z)-2\hat{F}_{(X,Y)}^{t-1}(z,z)},

for
\hat{F}_{X}^{t-1}
,
\hat{F}_{Y}^{t-1}
and
\hat{F}_{(X,Y)}^{t-1}
denoting the empirical CDFs of
{X}^{t-1}
,
{Y}^{t-1}
, and
(X,Y)^{t-1}
, respectively.

3.3.2
Asymptotic power-one guarantee for predictable mixtures

Following Theorem
1
and the derivations from the previous section, at
t\geq 1
, for some predictable mixture distribution
\psi_{t}\in\Pi(\mathcal{Z})
, we consider the
\mathcal{Z}
-mixture sequential e-variable

S_{t}=\int S_{t}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z),z)\,\mathrm{d}\psi_{t}(z),

(13)

as our measure of evidence against the global null
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Before we state our general result for this section, we first state the (simpler) result that, for i.i.d. data, the e-process (
10
) of Theorem
1
grows to infinity under any alternative when the mixture distribution is fixed and has full support.

Proposition 2

(GRO e-process with fixed
\mathcal{Z}
-mixture is powerful against the FSD null)
.

Let
\psi_{t}=\psi
fixed, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, for some
\psi\in\Pi(\mathcal{Z})
with full support, and
S_{t}
be the mixture e-variable (
13
).
Then, the e-process
E_{t}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
, is powerful against
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, that is

\mathbb{Q}\left(\liminf_{t\to\infty}E_{t}=\infty\right)=1,\quad\textrm{for all }\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

(14)

In particular, the e-process yields a sequential test of asymptotic power one against
\mathcal{H}_{1}
: For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{t\geq 1:\varphi_{t}=1\}
, for
\varphi_{t}=\indsymb\left(E_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\right)
, we have

\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)=1,\quad\textrm{for all }\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}.

(15)

Following standard terminology
(
15
;
63
)
, we call any e-process satisfying property (
14
)
consistent
, and any sequential test satisfying property (
15
) a
test of (asymptotic) power one
.
In fact, the proof even shows that the e-process
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}

grows at an
exponential rate
, that is
\mathbb{Q}(\liminf_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\log E_{t}>0)=1
, which is strictly stronger than property (
14
).

Proposition
2
is an instance of the
method of mixtures

(
65
;
34
)
: by integrating over the whole range of betting parameters ensures that the mixture process places positive mass near the oracle parameter, we achieve a positive growth rate under the alternative.
Analogously, in parametric models, mixtures are often taken over the unknown model parameter in order to obtain an asymptotic power one guarantee, see e.g.
26
.
As we already provided a solution for choosing the betting parameters in the previous section, our approach more closely resembles the latter.
Conceptually, however, there remains a distinction to that line of work where the mixtures are typically taken over likelihood ratios without the additional need to select a betting parameter.

While the fixed-
\psi
e-process already yields a test of power one, we can achieve stronger power in practice with adaptive choices of
(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
.
This is because the regions of non-dominance (violating the null) may not be given large enough weights by the fixed mixture.
The following theorem shows that, under a mild assumption, the power guarantee further carries over to predictable mixtures.

Theorem 2

(GRO e-process with predictable
\mathcal{Z}
-mixture is powerful against the FSD null)
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
. For
(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\Pi(\mathcal{Z})
predictable, assume that there exists
\varepsilon,\delta>0
small and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that,

\psi_{t}(\{z:\mathbb{Q}_{X}(z)-\mathbb{Q}_{Y}(z)>\varepsilon\})>\delta,\quad\textrm{for all }t\geq N_{0}\,.

(16)

Then, the e-process
E_{t}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}
, for
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
S_{\ell}
defined at (
13
), is consistent and the corresponding sequential test has asymptotic power one.

Because
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and CDFs are right-continuous, there exists an open interval
U\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
such that
\mathbb{Q}_{X}(z)-\mathbb{Q}_{Y}(z)>0
for all
z\in U
. In particular, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, the set
V=\{z:\mathbb{Q}_{X}(z)>\mathbb{Q}_{Y}(z)+\varepsilon\}
has positive Lebesgue measure. Condition (
16
) is therefore rather mild and merely requires that the mass of
\psi_{t}
eventually remains bounded away from zero on
V
, the region where the violation of stochastic dominance is detectable.

Remark 2

(Closed form expression for the mixture e-variable)
.

When using the plug-in GRO bets (
12
), the map
z\mapsto\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z)
is piecewise constant with jumps only at the observations
(X,Y)^{t-1}
. If follows that
z\mapsto S_{t}(\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z),z)
is piecewise constant with potential jumps at
(X,Y)^{t}
.
Further, letting
a_{t}=\min\{(X,Y)^{t}\}
and
b_{t}=\max\{(X,Y)^{t}\}
, we know that
S_{t}(z)\equiv 1
for
z\in\mathcal{Z}\setminus[a_{t},b_{t}]
.
We may thus compute the mixture e-variable (
13
) exactly by the sum

S_{t}=\psi_{t}(\mathbb{R}\setminus[z_{1}^{t},z^{t}_{n_{t}}))+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}-1}S_{t}(z_{j}^{t})\psi_{t}([z_{j}^{t},z_{j+1}^{t}))\,,

for
a_{t}=z_{1}^{t}<z_{2}^{t}<\ldots<z^{t}_{n_{t}}=b_{t}
the ordered observations in
(X,Y)^{t}
.

3.3.3
On predictable weights for aggregating over the testing thresholds

Next, we propose a method based on adaptive weighting from online learning theory to combine evidence across

\mathcal{Z}
, which exhibits strong empirical performance. At
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we fix
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
given by

z_{1}^{t}<z_{2}^{t}<\ldots<z^{t}_{n_{t}-1}<z_{n_{t}}^{t},

(17)

for some
n_{t}\geq 1
, and set the weights based on the empirical CDF difference:

w_{t}(z_{i}^{t})\propto\exp\left\{\eta_{t}(z_{i}^{t})\big[\hat{F}_{X}^{t-1}(z_{i}^{t})-\hat{F}_{Y}^{t-1}(z_{i}^{t})\big]\right\},\quad\quad i=1,\ldots,n_{t}\,,

(18)

for some predictable tuning parameters
\eta_{t}(z_{i}^{t})>0
. We then suggest to use

\psi_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}w_{t}(z_{i}^{t})\delta_{z_{i}^{t}},

(19)

to compute the e-variable
S_{t}
at (
13
) and
E_{t}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}
as discussed in Theorems
1
and
2
.

Our choice of weights is inspired by the exponential weights aggregation (EWA) method
(
80
)
, which achieves minimax-optimal regret in online learning
(
14
;
8
;
59
)
, and has been adapted to analogous sequential testing problems
(
83
;
58
;
84
, e.g.,)
.
The rationale behind the weights in (
18
) is to place larger bets on the region where stochastic dominance is violated—that is, on thresholds for which
F_{X}(z)-F_{Y}(z)
is large—and smaller bets on regions where the null holds or violations are only minor.

We initially experimented with the original EWA scheme, in which the weights are determined explicitly by the past e-values, that is, by the cumulative gains obtained so far in the game associated with threshold
z
.
However, this implementation is computationally considerably more expensive, as at each time
t
it requires computing all past values
S_{\ell}(\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}(z),z)
,
\ell\leq t
, for every
z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
.
Instead, we propose to use exponential weights (
18
) with
standardized
CDF difference, by setting

\eta_{t}(z_{i})=\frac{\eta}{\widehat{\textrm{sd}}[\hat{F}_{X}^{t-1}(z_{i}^{t})-\hat{F}_{Y}^{t-1}(z_{i}^{t})]},

(20)

for a single pre-specified parameter
\eta>0
. The resulting weights involve a self-normalized test statistic, standard in martingale concentration inequalities
(
34
, e.g.)
and also common in the literature on SD
(
17
;
89
, e.g.)
.
In our experiments (Section
5
), we fix the parameter
\eta=1
.
There, we will empirically compare the adaptive approach with exponential weights against other baselines, including the adaptive approach with weights based linearly on bets,
w_{t}(z_{i}^{t})\propto\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z_{i}^{t})
, and the non-adaptive baseline,
w_{t}(z_{i}^{t})\propto 1
.

Remark 3

(On the choice of the testing thresholds
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
)
.

In light of Remark
2
, in order to compute the exact mixture e-variable at (
13
), it suffices to have access to the probabilities

\psi_{t}([z_{j}^{t},z_{j+1}^{t})),\quad j=1,\ldots,n_{t}-1\,.

(21)

for
z_{1}^{t}<z_{2}^{t}<\ldots<z^{t}_{n_{t}}
the ordered observations in
(X,Y)^{t}
.
Importantly,
\psi_{t}
needs to be predictable, and needs thus in particular to be fixed
before
observing
(X_{t},Y_{t})
.
Since, we can always interpret the weights at (
18
) as weights on the whole intervals
[z_{j}^{t},z_{j+1}^{t})
and distribute the mass uniformly over them, any choice of weights on the ordered observations in
(X,Y)^{t-1}
can in principle be used to derive the target probabilities in (
21
), including the interval probabilities with the non-predictable endpoints
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
.
However, as the sample size increases, the impact of the additional interval splitting at
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
becomes negligible, we simply apply the method as described above, taking
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{t}
to be the ordered observations in
(X,Y)^{t-1}
. By subsequently adding more observations from the pooled sample distribution
(F_{X}+F_{Y})/2
and exploiting known optimality properties of the proposed weighting schemes, it is reasonable to expect that the regularity condition (
16
) in Theorem
2
is satisfied. In practice, we therefore expect the procedure to eventually reject with probability one under the alternative.

Remark 4

(Finite known support)
.

The previous considerations simplify considerably when the outcomes have finite and known support, as is the case for ordinal data with finitely many outcomes.
(To give a practical example: in Example
1
, the benefits of technology adoption may be self-reported by employees in levels like low/medium/high.)
In such cases, it is reasonable to split our capital across all thresholds in
\mathcal{Z}
, bet on all of them, and combine the obtained evidence by the predictable weights (
18
).
We do not expect to see much gains from this adaptivity when there are only a small number of known possible outcomes.

4
Testing higher-order SD and general integral stochastic orders

Testing for first-order SD is a useful starting point.
While conceptually simple, however, testing FSD may be too restrictive in some decision making contexts, as rejection simply indicates there is some DM with an increasing utility function who prefers
X
to
Y
.
This motivates us to develop sequential tests for SD under known subclasses of increasing utility functions, making the SD null less restrictive.

4.1
Higher-order SD: Integrated CDFs and expected utility characterizations

We first review the standard characterizations of higher-order SD
(
73
, e.g.,)
.
Mathematically, the
k
-th order SD relation is defined in terms of iterated integrals of CDFs.

Definition 2

(
k
-th order SD)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two random variables with CDFs
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
, respectively.
For
k\geq 1
, we say that
X

stochastically dominates
Y
in k-th order (
k
-SD)
, denoted by
Y\preceq_{k}X
, if

F_{X}^{[k]}(z)\leq F_{Y}^{[k]}(z),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R},

(22)

where
F^{[k]}
denotes the
k
-th iterated integral of the CDF
F
, defined recursively:
F^{[1]}\equiv F
, and for
k\geq 2
,

F^{[k]}(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}F^{[k-1]}(u)du,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}.

The relation is well-defined when the iterated integrals exist and are finite for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

The key to our e-process construction is to leverage the known characterization of these iterated integrals of CDFs in terms of lower partial moments
(
73
, e.g.,)
:

Lemma 6

(Generator characterization of
k
-SD)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two random variables with CDFs
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
, respectively. For each
k\geq 1
, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

Y\preceq_{k}X
in the sense of Definition
2
, that is,
F_{X}^{[k]}(z)\leq F_{Y}^{[k]}(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
;

2.

\mathbb{E}[g_{z}^{[k]}(Y)]\leq\mathbb{E}[g_{z}^{[k]}(X)]
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
, where
g_{z}^{[k]}(x)=-(z-x)_{+}^{k-1}
for
x\in\mathcal{Z}
.

As before, each characterization is well-defined assuming that the associated expectations are finite for each
z\in\mathbb{R}
.
We refer to the functions
g_{z}^{[k]}
as the
(unnormalized) generator functions
of
k
-SD, as they are sufficient for generating the SD relation in the
k
-th order
(
52
)
.
As with FSD, we now have a simple characterization of
k
-SD in terms of a class of functions indexed by the outcome space
\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 5

(On the range of the testing threshold
z
)
.

Comparing with the FSD Definition
1
and the equivalent characterization in Lemma
2
,
one might be tempted to restrict the thresholds
z
for higher-order SD only to the joint support
\mathcal{Z}
rather than requiring the conditions to hold for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. For second-order stochastic dominance this would indeed be sufficient.
However, for
k\geq 3
, pointwise ordering of the iterated CDF integrals does not, in general, imply the corresponding ordering of lower-order moments required for
k
-th order stochastic dominance
(
86
;
24
)
.
For a concrete counterexample, consider, e.g., the case where
P_{X}=(1/2)(\delta_{1/2}+\delta_{1})
and
P_{Y}=(1/5)\delta_{0}+(4/5)\delta_{1}
, for which one can verify that
F_{X}^{[3]}(z)\leq F_{Y}^{[3]}(z)
, for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}=[0,1]
, even though
\mathbb{E}(X)=1/2<4/5=\mathbb{E}(Y)
.
That is, if
\sup\mathcal{Z}=b<\infty
, the iterated CDF integrals also carry moment information to the right of
b
, and one would need to impose the additional lower-moment conditions
F_{X}^{[j]}(b)\leq F_{Y}^{[j]}(b)
, for all
j\leq k-1
, alongside the iterated integral condition (
22
), when restricting attention simply to
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Remark 6

(The expected utility characterization for
k
-SD)
.

There is also a standard expected utility characterization of
k
-th order SD
(
23
;
66
)
:
for
k\geq 1
,
Y\preceq_{k}X
if and only if
\mathbb{E}_{{F_{Y}}}\left[{u(Y)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{F_{X}}}\left[{u(X)}\right]
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}^{[k]}
, which consists of all differentiable functions whose first
k
derivatives alternate in sign:

\mathcal{U}^{[k]}=\{u\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R})\mid(-1)^{j-1}u^{(j)}(z)\geq 0,\textrm{ for all }z\in\mathbb{R},j=1,\ldots,k\}.

For example, when
k=2
(second-order SD or SSD), the utility class
\mathcal{U}^{[2]}
consists of all increasing and concave functions, which is understood as risk aversion in the expected utility framework, popular in economics and decision theory
(
61
;
67
)
.
In particular, given two bets
X
and
Y
with the same expected value, a DM with a concave utility function would prefer the bet with less variability (i.e., less risk).
1
1

1

For example, given a 50-50 bet that pays either
x_{1}=-\$100
or
x_{2}=\$100
, a DM with a linear utility function would be indifferent from taking the bet or not.
Yet, a DM with a concave utility function would prefer to avoid the bet, reflecting risk aversion. Analogously, a DM with a convex utility function would prefer to take the bet, reflecting risk seeking behavior.

Similarly, the
k=3
case corresponds to a “prudent” DM whose marginal utility is further convex, such that the DM prefers to avoid negative downside risk (i.e., negative skew); the
k=4
case corresponds to a “temperate” DM who prefers to avoid heavy tails on either side
(
86
;
24
;
40
)
.
In this view, rejecting the
k
-SD null
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Y\preceq_{k}X
indicates there exists at least one such a DM for whom
Y
has an upside over
X
.

In this section, we will derive general e-testing procedures for
k
-th order SD for any
k\geq 1
, as well as SD in the increasing convex order; for our experiments, we will focus on testing SSD.

4.2
Testing for higher-order FSD with e-variables

We now consider sequentially testing the
k
-SD null hypothesis. By Lemma
6
, we can express the
k
-SD null as another intersection null over
\mathcal{Z}
:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[k]}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}^{[k]}\mid Y\preceq_{k}X\text{ under }\mathbb{P}\}=\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[k]}(z),

where

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[k]}(z)=\left\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}^{[k]}\mid\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{g_{z}^{[k]}(Y)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{g_{z}^{[k]}(X)}\right]\right\},

and
\mathfrak{B}^{[k]}
denotes the family of all probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that the
k
-th moments of
X
and
Y
exist.
Rejecting the 2-SD null, for example, indicates that
Y
has an upside over
X
in the second order (in expected utility view,
Y
is preferred to
X
by at least one risk-averse DM).

Testing
k
-th order SD is only sensible when the
k
-th order moments exist, such as when the support
\mathcal{Z}
is bounded.
In such cases, Definition
2
implies that we have an
increasing
sequence of nulls,

Using Lemma

for each

Assumption 1

Theorem 3

(Testing higher-order SD for bounded random variables)

Suppose that Assumption

For

1.

For any predictable sequence of mixtures

S_{t}^{[k]}=1+\int_{a}^{\infty}\lambda_{t}(z)[{u}_{z}^{[k]}(Y_{t})-{u}_{z}^{[k]}(X_{t})]\psi_{t}(\,\mathrm{d}z),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

General integral stochastic orders (e.g., increasing convex order and infinite-order SD).

More broadly, our e-process construction in Theorem

, also known as
integral stochastic orders

(

;

)
.
Given an arbitrary class of utility functions
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{i}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}:i\in I\}

if and only if
\mathbb{E}\left[{u_{i}(Y)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{u_{i}(X)}\right]
for all

Note that we simply had

for

In Appendix

and the

The only restrictions on

The betting interpretation (Game
1

according to some weights
\psi_{t}
over

.
The approach may not generalize to heavy-tailed data/utilities or to non-integral stochastic orders.

An alternate construction with sub-exponential e-variables for 2-SD/3-SD.

While the assumption of a finite lower bound is common in existing tests for SD
(
4
;
48
, e.g.,)
, it may be restrictive when the data is unbounded on both sides.
In such cases, we show that it is possible to instead impose a tail condition on the data distributions, in particular that they are sub-exponential, and derive a different building-block e-variable based on the theory of
sub-
\psi
processes

(
32
;
34
)
.
Sub-exponential distributions have tails that decay at least as fast as the exponential distribution, and thus include any bounded and sub-Gaussian distributions as special cases.
In Appendix

4.3
Asymptotically optimal bets for general SD testing via universal portfolios

As with FSD, we begin by choosing an optimal betting strategy for the “atomic” hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
of the intersection null
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\bigcap_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
.
Recall that, for FSD, we were able to derive a GRO betting strategy
\lambda^{\star}(z)
for each threshold
z\in\mathcal{Z}
(Proposition
1
) and obtain a log-optimal e-process for each atomic null
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
.
This derivation leveraged the relatively simple form of the building-block e-variables in the FSD case, something we do not have for SD in general utility classes.

Here, instead of focusing on a particular SD relation, we develop a general betting strategy for SD relations w.r.t. arbitrary utility generators (thresholds).
We can do so for all of the aforementioned SD relations (
k
-SD, increasing convex order, and infinite-order SD).
We leverage recent advances that derive universal, portfolio-based betting strategies that
asymptotically
achieve the log-optimal growth rate.

Suppose we sequentially test any intersection null of the form
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\bigcap_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{u_{z}(Y)}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{u_{z}(X)}\right]\}
for a utility generator
u_{z}
at threshold
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Given an adapted sequence of e-variables
(S_{t}(z))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
for each null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, consider any wealth process
(W_{t}(z))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of the form

W_{t}(z)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\left[(1-\lambda_{\ell}(z))+\lambda_{\ell}(z)S_{\ell}(z)\right],

(24)

where
\lambda_{\ell}(z)\in[0,1]
is the skeptic’s fractional bet in the
\ell
th round on the
z
th game.
We can recover our earlier e-process construction by setting
S_{t}(z)=1+[u_{z}(Y_{t})-u_{z}(X_{t})]
.

Prior work establishes that we can construct betting strategies
(\lambda_{t}(z))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
that yield an
asymptotically log-optimal

(
84
;
82
)
wealth process under any alternative
\mathbb{Q}
for which
(S_{t}(z))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are not e-variables.
Essentially, this means that the process achieves the optimal growth rate in large samples, under i.i.d. data.
When compared to using the GRO betting strategy that we derived explicitly for the FSD case, we are sacrificing finite-sample optimality, at each time
t
, in exchange for the flexibility of the strategy across different utility classes without having to explicitly derive the optimal bet.
We defer the (recently known) optimality results to Appendix
F.1
.

84
find that a canonical betting strategy achieves asymptotic log-optimality is the
universal portfolio (UP)
strategy
(
14
)
.
In essence, the UP strategy begins with an (uninformative) prior
\pi
over the fractional bet parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
, such as the
\mathsf{Beta}(1/2,1/2)
distribution, and updates the posterior over
\lambda
at each time
t
by treating the wealth process
W_{t}(z;\lambda)
itself as a likelihood function.
More formally, let
W_{t}(z;\lambda)
denote the wealth process (
24
) at time
t
for index
z\in\mathcal{Z}
where the fractional bet is fixed to
\lambda
across time, and compute the posterior mean for
\lambda_{t}(z)
: for
t\geq 1
,

\lambda_{t}^{\mathsf{UP}}(z)=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\lambda W_{t-1}(z;\lambda)\,d\pi(\lambda)}{\int_{0}^{1}W_{t-1}(z;\lambda)\,d\pi(\lambda)}.

In practice, we can approximate the integrals by discretizing the space of fractional bets and keeping track of unnormalized posterior weights for each candidate bet.
We include a description of the full UP algorithm that we implement, including computational considerations, in Appendix
F.2
.
We remark that it is possible to use other betting strategies that also achieve asymptotic log-optimality via portfolio-based approaches
(
12
;
58
, e.g.,)
.

The UP strategy described here takes place of the GRO strategy for FSD testing in constructing the building-block e-variables (Lemma
4
) for each threshold
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
To aggregate wealth across thresholds, we recommend using the adaptive approach with predictable weights, described in Section
3.3.3
.

The toy example with antimonotonicity

This is a topical problem and anytime-valid methods have found practical use in online A/B testing scenarios for large tech companies as the methods allow for optional stopping and thus preventing an unnecessary deployment of a suboptimal design

.
Unlike Example

7
Summary and Discussion

We presented a comprehensive study of anytime-valid testing procedures for stochastic dominance based on e-processes.
The resulting sequential tests remain valid under optional stopping for first- and higher-order SD as well as for more general integral stochastic orders, such as the increasing convex order and infinite-order SD.
For first-order SD, we derived growth-rate optimal e-processes and established an asymptotic power-one guarantee by predictably mixing over thresholds.
Methodologically, this construction draws on techniques from online learning where we employ adaptive weighting strategies such as exponential weighting allowing to allocate betting capital adaptively across thresholds where violations of SD may occur.
For higher-order SD, we leverage recent advances on universal portfolio-based betting strategies that asymptotically achieve the log-optimal growth rate.
Empirical results from a range of simulation settings illustrate that the resulting procedures perform well in practice, detecting violations of SD efficiently even in challenging scenarios.

A central feature of our approach is that it is entirely distribution-free. In particular, we impose no parametric assumptions on the marginal distributions of
X
and
Y
, nor do we require independence between them. Unlike several classical approaches that rely on strong distributional assumptions, our methods remain valid even under maximal dependence between the samples, as confirmed by our simulation experiments including strongly negatively correlated settings.

We view the present work as a first systematic step toward sequential testing procedures for SD. Several important directions remain open for future research.
In Section
6
, we briefly sketched how our methods extend to the more challenging task of affirming SD, which is particularly important as it more directly guides the decision maker.
However, it was shown that this problem is fundamentally difficult and that tests for affirming SD typically cannot achieve power against the full alternative without additional assumptions.
This difficulty is one of motivations for earlier works on relaxed SD notions, such as
almost
stochastic dominance
(
44
;
5
)
and
restricted
stochastic dominance
(
17
)
.
Developing powerful, anytime-valid procedures for such relaxed notions of SD remains an interesting direction for future work.
Furthermore, while SD admits well-established multivariate generalizations
(
52
;
73
)
, we restricted attention to the univariate case for clarity.

In closing, we refer the reader to Appendix
H
for additional extensions of our methods.
First, we provide a brief sketch of how our methods extend to multivariate stochastic orders and leave a more comprehensive treatment for future work.
We furthermore discuss sensible choices of the SD null for data with time-varying marginal distributions based on the notion of a time-varying CDF, inspired by
54
, and how our methods readily extend to settings when the data are no longer observed in pairs.

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{P}_{X}\npreceq\mathbb{P}_{Y}\}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\exists z\in\mathcal{Z}:\mathbb{P}(X\leq z)<\mathbb{P}(Y\leq z)\}=\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z)\,,

for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z)=\{\mathbb{P}\mid\mathbb{P}(X\leq z)<\mathbb{P}(Y\leq z)\}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z)\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
, for all
z
, where the only difference between
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z)
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
at (
6
) is that the former imposes a strict inequality whereas the latter allows for equality. That is,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z)
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
are equivalent from a statistical perspective, and testing for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
is equivalent to testing for
\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{H}_{0}(z)
.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
is a very large and thus untractable null.
Moreover, and in contrast to Lemma
3
, the non-SD null
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
is
not
convex anymore as it is easily seen by the following example.

Example 4
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\}
for
z_{1}<z_{2}<z_{3}
, and let
\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{2}\in\mathfrak{B}
with

\mathbb{P}^{1}_{X}=\delta_{z_{2}},\quad\mathbb{P}^{2}_{X}=\frac{2}{5}\delta_{z_{1}}+\frac{3}{5}\delta_{z_{3}},\quad\mathbb{P}^{1}_{Y}=\mathbb{P}^{2}_{Y}=\frac{1}{4}\delta_{z_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{z_{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{z_{3}}\,.

Then,
\mathbb{P}^{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
, for
i=1,2
, but
(\mathbb{P}^{1}+\mathbb{P}^{2})/2\notin\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
.

Combined with
88
, the non-convexity of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
implies that we cannot expect to find a nontrivial e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
, as we stated in Corollary
1
.

B.2
Testing the non-SD null under finite support

Next, we propose an e-value–based method for sequentially testing the non-SD null for finite support.

Assumption 2

(Finite support)
.

For
m\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{Z}=\{z_{i}\mid i=1,\ldots,m\}
with
z_{1}<z_{2}<\ldots<z_{m}
.

As
F(z_{m})=1
for any distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{m})=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}=\cup_{i=1}^{m-1}\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i})
. That is, it is sufficient to consider the thresholds
z_{1}<\ldots<z_{m-1}
in the sequel.
Since we are facing a intersection-union test, a natural approach is to base our test decision based on a minimal test statistic as it is done e.g. by
38
and
17
.
It is routine to check that, for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i})
-e-variables
E_{i}
, the minimum
\min_{i}E_{i}
is an e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
.
This mimics the standard procedure to take the maximum of the individual p-values for an intersection-union test.
Although statistically valid, the minimum approach can be very conservative in practice, as it is often possible to design more powerful e-variables by incorporating dependencies between the individual hypotheses.
However, using the growth rate optimal e-variables for the individual hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i})
from Section
3.3.1
, the following Proposition shows that the min-approach already achieves the best possible asymptotic growth rate.

As argued above, feasibility of the testing problem requires a minimal separation between the distributions of
X
and
Y
. For
0<\varepsilon<1
, we let

\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{Q}(Y\leq z_{i})+\varepsilon\leq\mathbb{Q}(X\leq z_{i}),\;\forall i=1,\ldots,m-1\right\}.

Proposition 4

(Anytime-validity and optimality of the minimum e-process under finite support)
.

Suppose that Assumption
2
is satisfied, i.e.,
X
and
Y
have finite support.

1.

For
S_{t}(\lambda,z_{i})
defined at (
9
), and

(\lambda_{t}(z_{i}))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
predictable, for all
i
,

\mathfrak{e}_{t}=\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}(\lambda_{t}(z_{i}),z_{i})\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

(35)

is an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
.

2.

If, for any
i
,
\lambda_{t}(z_{i})
is chosen as the sequential GRO plug-in given in (
12
), then for any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
,

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathfrak{e}_{t}=\infty,\quad\text{$\mathbb{Q}$-a.s.}.

3.

For any other e-process
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
, we have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left(\log{\mathfrak{e}_{t}}-\log{E_{t}}\right)\geq 0,\quad\mathbb{Q}\textrm{-a.s.}

for all
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
with
\mathbb{Q}(Y\leq z_{i}<X)>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,m-1
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i})\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}(z_{i})
, each

E_{t}(z_{i})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}(\lambda_{\ell}(z_{i}),z_{i}),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

is an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i})
by Theorem
1
.
The anytime-validity of the mininum e-process (
35
) follows immediately by Jensen: for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
and any stopping time
\tau
,

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{\mathfrak{e}_{\tau}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{\min_{i\in\{1,\dotsc,m-1\}}E_{\tau}(z_{i})}\right]\leq\min_{i\in\{1,\dotsc,m-1\}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{P}}}\left[{E_{\tau}(z_{i})}\right]\leq 1.

For the second claim, fix any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
. Then,
\mathbb{Q}\in(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i}))^{c}
for all
i
. In the proof of Theorem
2
, it is shown that, for all

,
\hat{\lambda}_{t}(z_{i})\to\lambda^{\star}(z_{i})\land(1-c)
almost surely, and thus, by the SLLN

\frac{\log(E_{t}(z_{i}))}{t}\to g_{z_{i}}(\lambda^{\star}(z_{i})\land(1-c))>0,

for
g_{z_{i}}
and
\lambda^{\star}(z_{i})
defined as in the proof of Theorem
2
. As

is finite, it follows by uniform convergence that, as
t\to\infty

\frac{\log(\mathfrak{e}_{t})}{t}=\min_{i=1,\ldots,m-1}\frac{\log\left(E_{t}(z_{i})\right)}{t}\to\min_{i=1,\ldots,m-1}\{g_{z_{i}}(\lambda^{\star}(z_{i})\land(1-c))\}>0,\quad\mathbb{Q}-\textrm{a.s.}

(36)

Regarding the last claim, let
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
, and let
(E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be any e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. Then,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log E_{t}\leq\sup_{S\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\log(S),\quad\mathbb{Q}-\textrm{a.s.},

for
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H})
denoting the family of all e-variables for some null
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}
, see e.g.
84
.
As
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
, and thus
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i}))\supseteq\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime})

\sup_{S\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\log(S)\leq\sup_{S\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(z_{i}))}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\log(S)=g_{z_{i}}(\lambda^{\star}(z_{i})),

for all

, and thus

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log E_{t}\leq\min_{i=1,\ldots,m-1}\{g_{z_{i}}(\lambda^{\star}(z_{i}))\},\quad\mathbb{Q}-\textrm{a.s.}

(37)

By assumption,
\lambda^{\star}(z_{i})<1
for all
i
. Thus, by choosing

in (

) sufficiently small, it follows by (

) that the minimum process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
achieves the upper bound on the asymptotical growth rate in (
37
).
∎

Part

of Proposition

shows that we obtain an anytime-valid test for testing non-SD by using the minimum process
(\mathfrak{e}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
2
ensures that we will eventually reject in case that the distribution of

and

Part

, we call any process satisfying

asymptotically log-optimal
; also see Appendix

.

B.3
Testing restricted SD via time-uniform & value-uniform CS for CDFs

Given sequentially observed data
X_{1},X_{2},\dotsc\sim F_{X}
, a
(1-\alpha)
-level
time-uniform
and
value-uniform confidence sequence (tvCS)
for the CDF
F_{X}
is a set of intervals
\{(\hat{L}_{t}(z),\hat{U}_{t}(z))\}_{t\geq 1,z\in\mathcal{Z}}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left({\forall t\geq 1,\,\forall z\in\mathcal{Z}:\hat{L}_{t}(z)\leq F_{X}(z)\leq\hat{U}_{t}(z)}\right)\geq 1-\alpha,\quad\forall\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}.

We refer to
\hat{L}_{t}(z)
and
\hat{U}_{t}(z)
as the
lower
and
upper confidence bounds
of the tvCS (
tvLCB
and
tvUCB
), respectively.

33
and
54
are among the first to derive tvCS, while
10
recently derived tighter tvCS based on PAC-Bayes techniques.

One may reasonably suspect that, if we have a tight tvCS for each CDF, then we can affirm SD when the two tvCS do not intersect with each other at all
z
.
However, we quickly run into an issue when operationalizing this idea.
Suppose, for example, that
X
and
Y
are bounded in
[0,1]
.
Then, by definition, there is no difference in the CDF values at the boundaries:
F_{X}(1)-F_{Y}(1)=0
.
For unbounded random variables, say taking values in
\mathbb{R}
, we analogously know that
\lim_{z\to-\infty}[F_{X}(z)-F_{Y}(z)]=\lim_{z\to+\infty}[F_{X}(z)-F_{Y}(z)]=0
.
Even outside these “boundary cases,” whenever
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
touches at an arbitrary point
z
or in a small tail region, then we cannot distinguish the two CDFs; for a concrete example, recall the Simulation example of Section
5.3
.

To make progress, we need to invoke a relaxation of SD in the alternative. Following
17
, we say that
Y

stochastically dominates
X
restricted to

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
, denoted by
X\preceq_{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}Y
, if

F_{Y}(z)\leq F_{X}(z),\quad\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}.

For any
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\subsetneq\mathcal{Z}
, SD under
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is a strict relaxation of
\mathcal{Z}
.
Thus, by restricting ourselves to a smaller support, we make it easier to affirm an SD relation.

Next, we specify the set of alternatives for which affirming SD is possible by generalizing the
\varepsilon
-separated alternative in the previous section for finite support.
For
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, let

\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{Q}(Y\leq z)+\varepsilon\leq\mathbb{Q}(X\leq z),\;\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid X\preceq_{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}Y\text{ under }\mathbb{Q}\right\}.

Here, we remain agnostic about the size of the witness set
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
and the value of
\varepsilon
.
As
17
point out, affirming SD is empirically sensible only over a restricted range of outcomes in many practical applications.
For example, if
X
and
Y
represent the incomes in two economies, then it may be more relevant to affirm SD over the range of returns that are likely to be observed in practice.

Given a sensible alternative, we can construct a powerful test that rejects the non-SD null when the two tvCS’s do not intersect with each other at all
z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Proposition 5

(Affirming restricted SD using tvCS)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
\{\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)\}_{t\in\mathbb{N},z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
be a
(1-\alpha/2)
-level tvLCB for
F_{X}
, and
\{\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)\}_{t\in\mathbb{N},z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}
be a
(1-\alpha/2)
-level tvUCB for
F_{Y}
, on
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Consider the test that rejects
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
at time
t
only when the UCB on
F_{Y}
is entirely below the LCB on
F_{X}
:

\psi_{t}=\indsymb\left(\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}:\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)<\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(38)

Then, the following statements hold.

1.

(Anytime-validity.)
(\psi_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is an anytime-valid test for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}):X\not\preceq_{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}Y
at level
\alpha
.

2.

(Test of power one.) Define the stopping time
\tau_{\alpha}=\inf\{t\geq 1:\psi_{t}=1\}
.
Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)
, suppose that the the tvUCB for
F_{Y}
and the tvLCB for
F_{X}
approach their respective CDFs, uniformly on
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, at large samples: as
t\to\infty
,

\sup_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\left[\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)-F_{Y}(z)\right]\to 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\left[F_{X}(z)-\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)\right]\to 0,\quad\text{$\mathbb{Q}$-a.s.}

(39)

Then,
(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequential test of power one against
\mathbb{Q}
, that is,
\mathbb{Q}\left(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\right)=1
.

Proof.

(a) Define the “good” events
E^{X}
and
E^{Y}
as

\displaystyle E^{X}=\left\{\forall t\geq 1,\,\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}:\hat{L}_{t}^{X}(z)\leq F_{X}(z)\right\},\quad\textrm{and}\quad E^{Y}=\left\{\forall t\geq 1,\,\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}:F_{Y}(z)\leq\hat{U}_{t}^{Y}(z)\right\},

such that, for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}
,
\mathbb{P}\left({E^{X}}\right)\geq 1-\alpha/2
and
\mathbb{P}\left({E^{Y}}\right)\geq 1-\alpha/2
by the definition of tvCS.

To show anytime-validity, we want to control the rejection probability

\mathbb{P}\left({\exists t\geq 1:\psi_{t}=1}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left({\exists t\geq 1,\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}:\hat{U}_{t}^{Y}(z)<\hat{L}_{t}^{X}(z)}\right)

under any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
, for which there exists some
z_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}
where
F_{X}(z_{0})<F_{Y}(z_{0})
.
Suppose that there exists some time
t
such that
\hat{U}_{t}^{Y}(z)<\hat{L}_{t}^{X}(z)
for all
z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Then, on the event
E^{X}\cap E^{Y}
, we have

F_{Y}(z)\leq\hat{U}_{t}^{Y}(z)<\hat{L}_{t}^{X}(z)\leq F_{X}(z),\quad\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},

which contradicts the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}
.
Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left({\exists t\geq 1:\psi_{t}=1}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left({\left(E^{X}\cap E^{Y}\right)^{c}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left({(E^{X})^{c}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left({(E^{Y})^{c}}\right)\leq\alpha.

(b) Let
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)
.
All of the following statements hold
\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely.
Given that the tvLCB and tvUCB uniformly converge to the true CDFs, we can choose a large enough time
N(\varepsilon)
such that

\hat{L}_{t}^{X}(z)>F_{X}(z)-\varepsilon/2\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{U}_{t}^{Y}(z)<F_{Y}(z)+\varepsilon/2,\quad\forall z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\forall t\geq N(\varepsilon).

Then, for any
t\geq N(\varepsilon)
and
z\in\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, we have

\hat{U}_{t}^{Y}(z)-\hat{L}_{t}^{X}(z)<F_{Y}(z)+\varepsilon/2-(F_{X}(z)-\varepsilon/2)=F_{Y}(z)-F_{X}(z)+\varepsilon\leq 0,

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
\mathbb{Q}
is in the
\varepsilon
-witness set
\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)
restricted to
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Thus,
\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)=1
for any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)
.
∎

Note that the condition (
39
) is a mild, one-sided condition that we expect to hold for any reasonable tvCS construction, even not under
\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)
, as long as the data are i.i.d.
One practical choice is the recently developed tvCS by
10
, which is empirically tight for time-uniform CDF estimation and has widths shrinking at an asymptotic rate of
O(\sqrt{T^{-1}\log T})
under “regular” conditions (see their remark on asymptotic rates in Section 4).

Remark 7

(Necessity of
\varepsilon
-separation for power)
.

33
were the first to present an instance of the sequential test (
38
); however, it was not fully clear the exact conditions under which the test achieves asymptotic power one.
The above result, along with the counterexamples discussed earlier, establish that tests of the form (
38
) are valid for the non-SD null and achieves power only when the associated CDFs are
\varepsilon
-separated.
See further discussion in Appendix
C
.

Unlike in our previous constructions, the resulting procedure does not yield an explicit e-process, and thus we no longer have an explicit betting-based measure of evidence against the non-SD null.
Finally, we hypothesize that an explicit bound on the expected rejection time of the test under
\mathcal{Q}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}},\varepsilon)
can be derived assuming a typical rate of convergence for the tvCS; we leave this for future work.

Appendix C
Kolmogorov-Smirnov-type sequential tests for FSD

In the non-anytime-valid regime, the most classical test for FSD is based on the one-sided KS statistic (
4
).
In the sequential, anytime-valid case, some existing methods
(
33
;
51
)
focusing on time-uniform estimation of CDFs can be adapted to obtain simple, KS-type sequential tests for FSD.
In this section, we briefly describe how to obtain such tests, and show how they ultimately fall short in terms of both applicability and power compared to our proposed methods.

C.1
General KS-type sequential tests based on time-uniform CDF bands

The first mention of a sequential test for stochastic dominance can be found in
33
, where they propose a time-uniform bound on the empirical process
(\hat{F}_{t}(\cdot)-F(\cdot))_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
(a “finite law-of-iterated-logarithm (LIL) bound”) that can be adapted to construct a sequential test for FSD.
Here, we extrapolate and consider a general construction of KS-type sequential tests for FSD based on
time-uniform and value-uniform confidence sequences (tvCS)
, also known as time-uniform confidence bands, such as those developed in
33
,
54
and
10
.
We summarize this construction in the following proposition.

Proposition 6

(KS-type sequential test for FSD using tvCS)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Let
\{\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)\}_{t\in\mathbb{N},z\in\mathcal{Z}}
be a time-uniform and value-uniform lower confidence bound (tvLCB) for
F_{X}
and
\{\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)\}_{t\in\mathbb{N},z\in\mathcal{Z}}
be a time-uniform and value-uniform upper confidence bound (tvUCB) for
F_{Y}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left[\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)-F_{X}(z)\right]>0}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\left({\exists t\in\mathbb{N}:\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left[F_{Y}(z)-\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)\right]>0}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Then, the binary process

\psi_{t}=\indsymb\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left[\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)-\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)\right]>0\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

is a valid level-
\alpha
sequential test for
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Y\preceq_{1}X
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}:Y\npreceq_{1}X
.

The test will reject as soon as there is a crossing at some point
z\in\mathcal{Z}
at any time
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
As a concrete example,
33
yields one-sided tvCS defined as
\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)=\hat{F}_{X,t}(z)-\omega_{t}(A,\alpha)
and
\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)=\hat{F}_{Y,t}(z)+\omega_{t}(A,\alpha)
, where
\omega_{t}
is the uniform width function

\omega_{t}(A,\alpha)=A\sqrt{\frac{\log\log(et/t_{\text{min}})+C(A,\alpha)}{t}},

C(A,\alpha)
is a constant that is calculated numerically,
A\geq 1/2
is a tuning parameter (set to
0.85
in their paper), and
t_{\text{min}}
is a user-specified minimum time after which the bound starts to hold.
Note that the width merely depends on the time
t
and not the data points, so the resulting test is not adaptive to the underlying distributions.
The corresponding level-
\alpha
test is simply

\psi_{t}=\indsymb\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left[\hat{F}_{X,t}(z)-\hat{F}_{Y,t}(z)\right]>2\omega_{t}(A,\alpha)\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},

involving the empirical one-sided KS statistic on the left-hand side.

Interestingly, while
33
derive a sequential test for the FSD null, it is in effect a test for the strictly larger
non-FSD
null,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}:X\npreceq_{1}Y
, which we discuss in Section
6
.
As we formalize in that section, we can further derive a sequential test for the non-FSD null using tvCS as

\psi_{t}=\indsymb\left(\inf_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left[\hat{L}_{X,t}(z)-\hat{U}_{Y,t}(z)\right]>0\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{N}

(40)

which is precisely what
33
propose.
This sequential test is valid against not just the SD null
\mathcal{H}_{0}:Y\preceq_{1}X
, but also the (much larger) non-FSD null
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\prime}:X\npreceq_{1}Y
.
As a test for the FSD null, it achieves no power against any non-FSD alternative where neither
X
nor
Y
dominates each other (whenever the two CDFs cross, say
F_{X}\equiv\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
F_{Y}\equiv\mathcal{N}(0,4)
).
In fact, even when
Y
strictly dominates
X
but
F_{Y}
has a small contact region with
F_{X}
(say, when
Y
is strictly larger to
X
most times but equal to
X
at the tails), the infimum difference
\inf_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}[F_{X}(z)-F_{Y}(z)]
will be zero, so the test (
40
) will not retain power (as long as the tvLCB and tvUCB are valid and uniformly approach these CDFs).
This problem persists when we instead treat (
40
) as a test for the non-FSD null: as we demonstrate in Section
6
, it can only achieve power when
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
are sufficiently separated.

While we focused here on
33
’s first-known sequential test for SD (or non-SD), the analogous construction can work with any tvCS for CDFs, such as those developed in
54
and
10
.
A final remark is that it is unclear how these methods generalize to testing for general SD nulls beyond FSD, as mentioned in Section
4.1
.

C.2
A KS-type sequential test based on reverse submartingales

In another line of work, building on the theory of
reverse submartingales
,
51
derive time-uniform lower and upper confidence bounds for the two-sided KS statistic

D^{\textsf{KS,2}}=\lVert F_{X}-F_{Y}\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}|F_{X}(z)-F_{Y}(z)|

based on the empirical CDFs from i.i.d. samples; see Corollary 14 in their paper.
To adapt their result to FSD testing, as discussed in Section
2.4
, we may rewrite the FSD null as
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid D^{\mathsf{KS}}(\mathbb{P})\leq 0\}
with the one-sided KS statistic

D^{\mathsf{KS}}=D^{\mathsf{KS}}(\mathbb{P})=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\,[\mathbb{P}_{X}(z)-\mathbb{P}_{Y}(z)]\,,

Following their exposition, we directly proceed in the more general case of unpaired samples with
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{t}\sim F_{X}
and
Y_{1},\dotsc,Y_{s}\sim F_{Y}
(see Section
H.2
for further discussion).

Looking carefully at their proofs, we can readily extend their results on two-sided KS statistic to obtain a time-uniform upper confidence sequence
(U_{ts})_{t,s\in\mathbb{N}}
at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
for the one-sided process

M_{ts}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\hat{F}_{X}^{t}(z)-\hat{F}_{Y}^{s}(z)\right\}-D^{\mathsf{KS}}(\mathbb{P}),\quad t\in\mathbb{N},s\in\mathbb{N},

where
\hat{F}_{X}^{t}
and
\hat{F}_{Y}^{s}
denote the empirical CDFs of the samples
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{t}
and
(Y_{i})_{i=1}^{s}
, respectively. This upper confidence sequence
(U_{ts})_{t,s\in\mathbb{N}}
for
(M_{ts})_{t,s\in\mathbb{N}}
directly yields the lower confidence sequence

L_{ts}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\hat{F}_{X}^{t}(z)-\hat{F}_{Y}^{s}(z)\right\}-\kappa_{ts},\quad t\in\mathbb{N},s\in\mathbb{N}

with
\kappa_{ts}\geq 0
, for the unknown quantity of interest
D^{\mathsf{KS}}(\mathbb{P})
, satisfying

\mathbb{P}(L_{ts}\leq D^{\mathsf{KS}}(\mathbb{P}),\forall t,s\geq 1)\geq 1-\alpha,\quad\textrm{for all }\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\,.

(41)

The deduced sequential test
\psi_{ts}=\indsymb\left(L_{t,s}>0\right),t,s\in\mathbb{N},
which rejects
\mathcal{H}_{0}
as soon as
L_{ts}
exceeds zero time-uniformly controls type-I error at level
\alpha
over by the coverage guarantee (
41
), that is the probability that we ever reject under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is bounded by
\alpha
.

Although theoretically appealing, this approach is not competitive in terms of power as the confidence sequences are generally very conservative (see Section
G.1
).

Appendix D
Extensions to testing other SD relations

The e-process construction in Section
4.1
further extends to general SD relations for which utility characterizations exist, also known as
integral stochastic orders

(
18
;
22
)
.
Below, we list a few concrete examples.

D.1
Increasing convex orders

By complete analogy to the construction for 2-SD (increasing concave order), we can also construct e-variables and test supermartingales for testing SD in the increasing convex order (for risk-seeking DMs, in expected utility view).
The associated utility generators are

u_{z}^{\mathsf{icx}}(x)=\frac{(x-z)_{+}}{b-z},\quad x\in\mathcal{Z},

where we now require an upper bound
b
on the support
\mathcal{Z}
instead of a lower bound.
Essentially, increasing convex order relies on the survival function
\bar{F}(z)=1-F(z)
in its definition, leading to the above generator; see
56
for a proof.
The rest of the construction and proof is completely analogous as for Theorem
3
in the
k=2
case (even the analogous
k
-th icx order can be handled similarly).

D.2
Infinite-order SD (Laplace transform order)

Another popular integral stochastic order is the
infinite-order SD
, also known as the
Laplace transform order

(
25
)
:

Y\preceq_{\infty}X\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[{e^{-zX}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{e^{-rY}}\right],\,\forall r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

Naturally, this order is generated by exponential utility functions,
u_{r}^{[\infty]}(x)=1-e^{-rx}\in[0,1],\,x\in\mathcal{Z}
, for
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Infinite-order SD represents the limit of
k
-th order SD as
k\to\infty
, in the sense that it generates the order defined by
\mathcal{U}^{[\infty]}=\cap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{U}^{[k]}
, also known as
completely monotone
utility functions
(
87
)
.
This order is of particular interest in both economics and decision theory, popularly known as modeling
constant absolute risk aversion (CARA)
and also the preference of DMs to “combine good lotteries with bad ones”
(
21
, e.g.,)
.

We may define the infinite-order SD null
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[\infty]}=\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}^{[\infty]}\mid Y\preceq_{\infty}X\}
, where
\mathfrak{B}^{[\infty]}
is the set of probability distributions for which all moments of
X
and
Y
exist.
Since the exponential utility functions are already bounded in
[0,1]
, the building-block e-variables for testing the infinite-order SD null
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[\infty]}
are valid without any further distributional assumptions on
X
and
Y
.
It is known that, for any
k\geq 1
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[k]}\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[\infty]}\subset\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[\mu]}

(e.g.,
56
, Corollary 1.6.7)
, explaining its inclusion relation with
k
-SD and mean dominance nulls in (
23
).

The analogous e-process construction is straightforward, although the utility function class is now indexed over
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}=[0,\infty)
.
We would thus employ adaptive strategies over this space instead of
\mathcal{Z}
.
Compared to
k
-SD testing, the one issue is that the index set on which we choose the test “thresholds”, call it
I=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, is now distinct from the data support, such that we cannot choose them from the empirical quantiles of the observed data.
Empirically, however, we find that a reasonable logarithmic range of fixed indices, such as
\tilde{I}=\{0,10^{-2},10^{-1},1,10^{2},10^{3}\}
, would work well.
The exponential weights
18
can still adaptively select which of these indices are the most profitable in terms of evidence.

Appendix E
Testing
k
-SD for unbounded data with sub-exponential tails

In Section
4.1
, to test higher-order SD, we required that the data has a finite lower bound (Assumption
1
) to ensure the validity of the building-block e-variables.
Below, we show there exist alternative building-block e-variables for testing higher-order SD that are valid even for unbounded data, as long as it has sub-exponential tails.

E.1
A mixture e-process for testing 2-SD for sub-exponential distributions

Assumption 3

(Sub-exponential distributions)
.

X
and
Y
are sub-exponential with parameters
\nu>0
and
c>0
, that is:

\mathbb{E}\left[{e^{\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}\left[{X}\right])}}\right]\leq e^{\psi_{E}(\lambda;c)\nu^{2}},\quad\forall\lambda:|\lambda|<\frac{1}{c},

where
\psi_{E}(\lambda;c)=(-\log(1-c\lambda)-c\lambda)/c^{2}
is the cumulant of a centered exponential with scale
c
.

This popular tail condition can be found in
6
, and it is equivalent to another common definition found in
81
, which states
\mathbb{E}\left[{e^{\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}\left[{X}\right])}}\right]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\nu^{2}/2}
for
|\lambda|<1/c
.
See, e.g.,
32
for a proof of the equivalence.
Sub-exponential random variables have tails that decay at least as fast as an exponential distribution; examples of sub-exponential distributions include bounded, (sub-)Gaussian, chi-squared, and exponential distributions.

Given sub-exponential data and a known family of utility generators, in particular for 2-SD and 3-SD, we can construct alternative building-block e-variables via the
sub-
\psi
method

(
32
)
, where the function
\psi
, in this case, denotes the cumulant generating function of the exponential distribution.
Once we have sub-
\psi
utilities, we can leverage the standard sub-
\psi
e-process construction and the method of mixtures
(
15
;
34
)
to arrive at a powerful e-process.
We summarize the resulting e-variables/processes in the following proposition.

Proposition 7

(Gamma-exponential mixture e-processes for testing second-order SD)
.

Suppose that Assumption
3
holds with parameters
(\nu/2,c)
.

1.

For each
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, and each time point
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

S_{t}^{[2]}(\lambda,z)=\exp\left\{\lambda\left((z-X_{t})_{+}-(z-Y_{t})_{+}\right)-\psi_{E}(\lambda;c)\nu^{2}\right\}

is an e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[2]}
, for any
\lambda\in[0,1/c]
.

2.

Furthermore, the
gamma-exponential mixture process
defined as

M^{[2]}_{t}(z)=\int_{0}^{1/c}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}^{[2]}(\lambda,z)\right)f_{\rho}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=m\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\left((z-X_{\ell})_{+}-(z-Y_{\ell})_{+}\right),\nu^{2}t\right),

where
f_{\rho}(\lambda)\propto(1-\lambda)^{\rho-1}e^{-\rho(1-\lambda)}
is a reparametrized Gamma density truncated to
[0,1/c]
, forms an e-process for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[2]}
.
The mixture function
m(\cdot,\cdot)
has a closed form up to Gamma functions.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

Since
X
and
Y
are sub-exponential with parameters
(\nu/2,c)

(z-X)_{+}
and
(z-Y)_{+}
are also sub-exponential with parameters
(\nu/2,c)
, and hence their difference is further sub-exponential with parameters
(\nu,c)
.
This immediately implies that
S_{t}^{[2]}(\lambda,z)
is an e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[2]}
for any
\lambda\in[0,1/c]

The sequential extension are analogous as in Theorem
1
, making each
E_{t}^{[k]}(\lambda,z)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}S_{\ell}^{[k]}(\lambda,z)
a test supermartingale for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[k]}
.
The particular form of the e-variable from item
1
yields a closed-form expression for this product in terms of the sum of utility differences:

E_{t}^{[2]}(\lambda,z)=\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\left((z-X_{\ell})_{+}-(z-Y_{\ell})_{+}\right)-\psi_{E}(\lambda;c)\nu^{2}t\right\}.

Then, given any mixture distribution
f
over
\lambda\in[0,1/c]
, the resulting mixture process
M_{t}^{[k]}(z)=\int_{0}^{1/c}E_{t}^{[k]}(\lambda,z)f(\lambda)d\lambda
is also a test supermartingale for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{[k]}
.
See Section
E.2
for the exact formula for the resulting mixture function
m
when using the Gamma density
f_{\rho}
, which yields an efficiently computable mixture process (exact form is provided in Section
E.2
).
∎

Essentially, item
1
follows from Assumption
3
and the fact that
(z-X)_{+}-(z-Y)_{+}
is also sub-exponential with parameters
(\nu,c)
; item
2
follows from the standard method-of-mixtures construction for sub-
\psi
e-processes
(
15

34
)
.
Compared to
34

\nu^{2}

\nu^{2}

We also note that this construction can be readily adapted to testing 3-SD by replacing the utility generators with
u_{z}^{[3]}(x)=(z-x)_{+}^{2}

U

U^{2}

It would even be possible to construct e-variables/processes for testing

E.2
Computing the gamma-exponential mixture

The method of mixtures construction in Proposition
7
follows that of the sub-exponential process developed in
34
and
9
.
For completeness, we provide the exact formula for the resulting e-process below.
The mixture density is a reparametrized Gamma distribution truncated to the interval
[0,1/c]
, defined as

f_{\rho}(\lambda)=\frac{\rho^{\rho}}{\underline{\gamma}(\rho,\rho)\Gamma(\rho)}(1-\lambda)^{\rho-1}e^{-\rho(1-\lambda)},\quad\lambda\in[0,1/c],

where
\Gamma(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{a-1}e^{-u}du
is the Gamma function and
\underline{\gamma}(a,b)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{b}u^{a-1}e^{-u}du
is the regularized lower incomplete Gamma function.
The resulting mixture function,

m(s,v):=\int\exp\left\{\lambda s-\psi_{E}(\lambda;c)v\right\}f_{\rho}(\lambda)d\lambda,

takes on two forms, each of which can be computed efficiently.
If
\frac{cs+v+\rho}{c^{2}}>0
, then we have the precise formula:

m(s,v)=C\left(\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}\right)\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{v+\rho}{c^{2}}\right)\underline{\gamma}\left(\frac{v+\rho}{c^{2}},\frac{cs+v+\rho}{c^{2}}\right)}{\left(\frac{cs+v+\rho}{c^{2}}\right)^{\frac{v+\rho}{c^{2}}}}\exp\left\{\frac{cs+v}{c^{2}}\right\};

otherwise, if
\frac{cs+v+\rho}{c^{2}}<0
(essentially the null case), then we have a conservative upper bound:

m(s,v)\leq C\left(\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}\right)\frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{\rho}{c^{2}}\right\}}{\frac{v+\rho}{c^{2}}}\leq 1.

In either case, the resulting mixture function can be computed via standard numerical libraries (e.g.,
scipy.special
in Python), as the associated Gamma functions are readily computable there.
See
9
for details.

Appendix F
Portfolio betting strategies for general SD relations

F.1
Asymptotic log-optimality of portfolio-based strategies

Recall the fractional bet wealth process defined in (
24
) (suppressing the dependence on the game index):

W_{t}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\left[(1-\lambda_{\ell})+\lambda_{\ell}S_{\ell}\right].

(42)

Assume that
(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. e-variables for a composite null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a family of alternatives for which each
S_{t}
is
not
an e-variable, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{{Q}}\left[{S_{t}}\right]>1
for
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
.
We say that the wealth process
(W_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is
asymptotically (and
\mathcal{Q}
-universally) log-optimal

(
84
)
if, for any other wealth process
(W_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and for any
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}
,

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{t}\log W_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\log W_{t}^{\prime}\right)\geq 0,\quad\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s.}

For large
t
and i.i.d. observations, observe that wealth processes of the form (
42
) satisfy

\frac{1}{t}\log W_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\log\left((1-\lambda_{\ell})+\lambda_{\ell}S_{\ell}\right)\approx\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[{\log\left(1+\lambda\left[u_{z}(Y)-u_{z}(X)\right]\right)}\right],

so any betting strategy that optimizes the expected growth rate at each
t
will be asymptotically log-optimal.
Thus, to find an asymptotically log-optimal strategy is is to find general betting strategies that are asymptotically equivalent to the pointwise GRO strategy (which may be hard to compute in general).
We remark that this is the characterization that
82
focuses on, but based on
L^{1}
-convergence rather than almost sure convergence.
As
84
note, neither
L^{1}
-convergence nor almost sure convergence imply another.

How do we construct asymptotically log-optimal betting strategies?
A sufficient condition for the sequential betting strategies is to have
sublinear portfolio regret

(
13
;
14
)
.
Given any wealth process of the form (
42
) with a predictable betting strategy
(\lambda_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, we define its
portfolio regret
at time
t
as the gap in log wealth relative to the best fixed fractional bet in hindsight (
\lambda^{\star}
):

R_{t}=\log W_{t}(\lambda^{\star})-\log W_{t}=\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\log\left((1-\lambda)+\lambda S_{\ell}\right)\right]-\log W_{t}.

84
show that any betting strategy that achieves sublinear portfolio regret, that is,
R_{t}=o(t)
, is asymptotically log-optimal; furthermore, for any alternative
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}
, the resulting wealth process has an asymptotic growth rate that matches that of a GRO strategy for
\mathbb{Q}
.
They also show that such betting strategies achieve an optimal expected rejection time under
\mathbb{Q}
.

F.2
Practical implementation of the universal portfolio strategy

Recall that, for a suitable prior
\pi
on
[0,1]
, the UP strategy is defined as

\lambda_{t}^{\mathsf{UP}}=\frac{\int_{0}^{1}\lambda W_{t-1}(\lambda)\,d\pi(\lambda)}{\int_{0}^{1}W_{t-1}(\lambda)\,d\pi(\lambda)},\quad t\in\mathbb{N}\,.

We can approximate the integrals by discretizing the space of fractional bets.
The resulting algorithm is summarized as follows:

1.

Fix a prior
\pi
on
[0,1]
. A default choice is Jeffreys prior
\pi(\lambda)\propto\lambda^{-1/2}(1-\lambda)^{-1/2}
, or the
\mathsf{Beta}(1/2,1/2)
distribution.

2.

Initialize a grid
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{n_{\text{grid}}}\}\subseteq[0,1]
of candidate bets.

3.

Set the initial log-weights as
w_{0}(\lambda_{g})=\log\pi(\lambda_{g})
for
g=1,\dotsc,n_{\text{grid}}
.

4.

For each time
t=1,2,\dotsc
:

(a)

Update the log-weights
w_{t}(\lambda_{g})
for each candidate bet
\lambda_{g}
by treating the wealth process itself
W_{t}(\lambda_{g})
as a likelihood function:

w_{t}(\lambda_{g})=w_{t-1}(\lambda_{g})+\log\left[(1-\lambda_{g})+\lambda_{g}S_{t}\right],\quad g=1,\dotsc,n_{\text{grid}}.

(b)

Compute the posterior mean
\lambda_{t}^{\mathsf{UP}}
by taking the weighted average of the candidate bets:

\lambda_{t}^{\mathsf{UP}}=\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\text{grid}}}\lambda_{g}\exp\left(w_{t-1}(\lambda_{g})\right)}{\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\text{grid}}}\exp\left(w_{t-1}(\lambda_{g})\right)}.

In our experiments, we use a grid of
n_{\text{grid}}=101
candidate bets uniformly spaced in
[c,1-c]
where
c=10^{-4}
(for numerical stability).
When we calculate the wealth process for a new threshold
z
(in the adaptive variant), we recalculate the wealth process for each candidate bet
\lambda_{g}
for that threshold.

When the UP algorithm is used in conjunction with the adaptive threshold selection strategy based on predictable weights, as described in Section
3.3.3
, each new choice of
z^{\prime}
requires recalculating the wealth process for betting on that threshold.
In such cases, we can reduce the computational cost by directly computing the new wealth process
W_{t}(z^{\prime})
for the new threshold
z^{\prime}
directly from all
t
observations.
Letting
w_{t}(\lambda;z^{\prime})=\log W_{t}(\lambda;z^{\prime})
, we have

w_{t}(\lambda_{g};z^{\prime})=\log W_{t}(\lambda_{g};z^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\log\left[(1-\lambda_{g})+\lambda_{g}S_{\ell}(z^{\prime})\right].

(43)

Then, the posterior mean
\lambda_{t}^{\mathsf{UP}}(z^{\prime})
can be computed from these weights as in (
4b
):

\lambda_{t}^{\mathsf{UP}}(z^{\prime})=\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\text{grid}}}\lambda_{g}\exp\left(w_{t}(\lambda_{g};z^{\prime})\right)}{\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\text{grid}}}\exp\left(w_{t}(\lambda_{g};z^{\prime})\right)}.

(44)

Appendix H
Extensions

Here we elaborate on extensions of our methods to non-i.i.d., unpaired, and multivariate data.

H.1
Non-i.i.d. data

First, we continue to assume that the data arrive in pairs
(X_{t},Y_{t}),t\in\mathbb{N}
, but no longer require that both
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
are identically distributed.
Inspired by
54
who construct time-uniform CDF confidence bounds for non-stationary data, we define the
strong FSD-null (for non-stationary data)
as

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\textrm{s}}=\left\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\leq z\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\leq z\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),\forall z\in\mathcal{Z},\forall t\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{0}\,.

(45)

In words, the null states that
X_{t}
stochastically dominates
Y_{t}
at each time step
t
, conditioned on the past.
Importantly, the methods developed in Section
3
readily extend to this setting. In particular, for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and predictable
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
S_{t}(\lambda,z)
defined in (
9
) remains a sequential e-variable for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\textrm{s}}
, and the sequential construction based on predictable mixtures over
\mathcal{Z}
(Theorem
1
) continues to apply.

By conditioning, the inequality in (
45
) is read in almost-sure sense, and for some given data-streams it may happen that
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\textrm{s}}=\emptyset
.
That is, if there is no continuity within each data stream and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y_{t}}
may change arbitrarily over time, inference for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\textrm{s}}
may become meaningless.
Even in this setting, it may still hold that, conditional on the past,
X
stochastically dominates
Y

on average
at each
t
.
This motivates to define
weak FSD-null (for non-stationary data)
as

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\textrm{w}}=\left\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathfrak{B}\mid\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}(X_{\ell}\leq z\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1})\leq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}(Y_{\ell}\leq z\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}),\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{Z},\forall t\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\,.

(46)

The difference between the strong null (
45
) and the weak null (
46
) mirrors that of the strong and weak nulls for comparing sequential forecasters in
31
and
9
, respectively.
(The strong-versus-weak terminology itself originates from the classical distinction between Fisherian and Neymanian tests of individual versus average treatment effects.)
The question of what stochastic dominance exactly means for non-stationary data remains context-dependent.
For the weak hypothesis, the building block e-variables in Section
3
would have to be replaced with e-variables of exponential form, such as empirical-Bernstein e-variables
(
34
)
, since
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\textrm{w}}
only imposes a condition on the averaged conditional mean differences.
Interestingly, unlike in the case of mean comparison, the strong and weak nulls do
not
coincide even when we assume weak stationarity of each data stream.
In other words, the weak null is strictly larger than the strong null even under weak stationarity.

H.2
Unpaired data

If the data streams
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
do not arrive in pairs, a straightforward adaption of our methods would be to compute the building-block e-variables from Section
3
based on whole batches.
Suppose that, at
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we observe
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{t}}
, for
p_{t},q_{t}\geq 1
.
Then, for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and predictable
\lambda\in[0,1]
, the quantity
1+\lambda(\hat{F}_{{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}}}(z)-\hat{F}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}}}(z))
is a sequential e-variable for the SD null (
5
) with respect to the batch filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\ell}),\ell\leq t\})
, and the sequential construction of Theorem
1
directly applies.
Analogously, the batch approach also applies to higher- order SD, where, for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,
\hat{F}_{{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}}}(z)
is replaced by
\bar{u}_{z}^{[k]}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})=\frac{1}{p_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{p_{t}}u^{[k]}_{z}(X_{j,t})
and analogously for
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
.

H.3
Multivariate extensions

Given the variety of notions of multivariate stochastic dominance proposed in the literature, see e.g.
73
, we limit ourselves to a brief overview and a sketch of how our methods could be extended to obtain anytime-valid sequential tests for such multivariate orders.

Let
\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be random vectors with distributions
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
P_{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, respectively. In the spirit of Section
4
, many notions of multivariate stochastic dominance may be characterized in terms of expected utilities, where, for some class
\mathcal{U}
of utility functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\boldsymbol{Y}
is dominated by
\boldsymbol{X}
, for short
\boldsymbol{Y}\preceq_{\mathcal{U}}\boldsymbol{X}
, if

\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{Y}}}}\left[{u(\boldsymbol{Y})}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{X}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}}\left[{u(\boldsymbol{X})}\right],\quad\textrm{for all }u\in\mathcal{U}\,.

For example, the
usual multivariate stochastic order
, where
\boldsymbol{Y}\preceq\boldsymbol{X}
if
P_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(U)\leq P_{\boldsymbol{X}}(U)
for all upper sets
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is obtained for the the utility functions
u(\boldsymbol{x})=\indsymb\left(\boldsymbol{x}\in U\right)
,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
an upper set.
In this case, all utility functions are bounded, and our results readily extend for the building block e-variables
1+\lambda(\indsymb\left(\boldsymbol{Y}\in U\right)-\indsymb\left(\boldsymbol{X}\in U\right))
.
