1. Executive Summary
Atmospheric convection spans a wide range of spatial and temporal scales and involves complex
interactions with the surrounding dynamic and thermodynamic environment, particularly over tropical
continental regions. These processes remain a major source of uncertainty in weather and climate models,
including persistent biases in the diurnal cycle of convective precipitation that directly affect estimates of
climate sensitivity. Addressing these challenges requires the combined use of high-resolution
observations and cloud-resolving modeling frameworks. In this context, the DOE Atmospheric Radiation
Measurement (ARM) program’s Large-Eddy Simulation ARM Symbiotic Simulation and Observation
(LASSO) activity provides a powerful platform that pairs comprehensive observations with numerical
simulations to enable process-level understanding of atmospheric convection.
Within this context, this Research and Development Partnership Pilot (RDPP) project was designed to
initiate and expand DOE ARM/ASR research capacity at minority-serving institutions, while
advancing scientific understanding of convective processes over the Amazon rainforest. Consistent
with the RDPP mission, the project emphasized partnership development, training, and workforce
capacity building alongside exploratory research activities.
On the scientific side, the project produced two peer-reviewed journal articles, and one manuscript
currently under review (see list in section 3.1). Together, these studies combine long-term ARM
observations and cloud-resolving and convection-permitting modeling to investigate the environmental
controls on the shallow-to-deep convective transition during the Amazon wet season. The results
demonstrate the central role of early-day moisture preconditioning and large-scale dynamical forcing in
regulating isolated deep convection, provide mechanistic insight into convective evolution, and establish
physically informed modeling frameworks for future sensitivity experiments. These scientific outcomes
are described in sections 2.1 to 2.3 and were disseminated in 8 conference presentations (see section 3.2)
and 5 invited talks (see section 3.3), reflecting broad engagement with our community.
Equally important, the project achieved its RDPP capacity-building objectives (see section 2.4). A
sustained research partnership was established among the University of Maryland, Baltimore County
(UMBC), Morgan State University (MSU), and Howard University (HU), and extended to include
collaboration with Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL). The project organized multiple multi-
day training events focused on ARM data, LASSO simulations, and quantitative analysis methods,
directly engaging students, postdoctoral researchers, and faculty across institutions. These activities
broadened participation in ASR research and led to independent adoption of LASSO workflows by
students beyond the immediate project team.
Finally, the project successfully positioned the participating institutions to pursue future DOE research.
Preliminary scientific results, coupled with strengthened partnerships and technical capacity, enabled the
submission of follow-on proposals to DOE ASR funding opportunities. In this way, the project fulfilled
the RDPP goal of seeding durable research capacity and laying the foundation for larger-scale, sustained
engagement with DOE ARM and ASR programs.

2. Review of Project Activities and Main Findings
2.1. Observational study
In the observational study by Viscardi et al. (2024), we assessed the environmental control on the
shallow-to-deep convective transition using measurements from the GoAmazon2014/5 ARM deployment.
To contrast the different atmospheric conditions that lead to different convective regimes, we applied a
classification criterion that identified shallow (N=16), congestus (N=27), or deep (N=60) days during the
Amazon wet season (December to April). Next, we built composites for each regime, including local
atmospheric conditions, convective properties, surface water balance, and large-scale wind properties.
As shown in Figure 1, the deep regime shows higher moisture content, especially below 3 km, with the
most remarkable differences around the 2 km level, with Δrv reaching 1.30 and 0.74 g kg−1 for
Deep − ShCu and Deep − Cong, respectively. In contrast, potential temperature differences between the
convective regimes are frequently less than 0.5 K below the 8 km level, suggesting that there is no
relevant relationship between daytime cloud development and the morning temperature profile. These
results suggest that the importance of early morning excess humidity to the STD transition in the Amazon
is primarily limited to the lower levels, a finding supported by Zhuang et al. (2017) and Tian et al. (2021).
However, it differs from Ghate and Kollias (2016), who noted an excess of humidity solely above 2 km
during precipitating days in the dry season. Moreover, these results contrast with studies over tropical
oceans, where free-tropospheric humidity has been shown to play a more significant role in convective
activity, while boundary layer humidity demonstrates minor variability (Bretherton et al., 2004; Holloway
and Neelin, 2009).

Figure 1. Atmospheric conditions. (a) Potential temperature (K) and (b) water vapor mixing ratio (g kg−1) from
08:00 LST radiosonde observations. The corresponding convective regime differences (Deep − ShCu and
Deep − Cong) for potential temperature (Δθ; c) and mixing ratio (Δrv; d) are also included.
Analyzing the water budget at the surface, we estimated the water vapor convergence term as a residual of
the water balance closure, which is shown in Figure 2. First, we notice that the ∂LWP/∂t appears
negligible. The daytime of ShCu and Cong days shows mostly water vapor divergence. This is primarily
due to more significant negative changes in ∂CWV/∂t and low precipitation rates, as evaporation shows
smaller differences among the convective regimes. On the other hand, the Deep regime exhibits relatively
neutral conditions from night to early afternoon. However, the water budget closure requires convergence
in the period from 14:00 to 18:00 LST. This coincides with the STD transition and is thus primarily
attributed to strong water vapor convergence preceding the late afternoon STD transition. At the end of

the day, the ShCu regime is estimated to lose 3.4 mm of water vapor due to divergence, while the Cong
regime loses 0.9 mm. In contrast, the Deep regime gains 5.2 mm of water vapor due to convergence.

Figure 2. Surface water balance for the (a, d) ShCu regime, (b, e) Cong regime, and (c, f) Deep regime. Panels (a–c)
represent the rates of change (mm d−1) for water vapor convergence (CONV), evaporation (EVAP), precipitation
(PREC), and the time derivatives of column water vapor (∂CWV/∂t) and the liquid water path (∂LWP/∂t). Panels (d–
f) display the accumulated water amount for each term in the water budget during the day (mm).

The main findings of our study can be summarized as follows:
 • Isolated deep convection during the Amazon wet season is systematically preceded by enhanced
 low-level moisture, particularly below ~3 km, already evident in the early morning hours. Days
 dominated by ShCu or Cong convection exhibit drier pre-convective environments, characterized
 by large-scale subsidence and low-level water vapor divergence.
 • Surface moisture fluxes alone do not explain convective development: large-scale water vapor
 convergence is weak during the morning and becomes significant only in the early afternoon on
 deep convection days.
 • A pronounced increase in large-scale upward vertical velocity above ~4 km develops in the early
 afternoon on isolated deep convection days, preceding the shallow-to-deep convective transition
 by 1–2 hours.
 • Large-scale vertical velocity is the strongest predictor of afternoon precipitation, with correlations
 nearly twice as large as those associated with moisture or thermodynamic variables.
 • Low-level wind shear shows a moderate positive association with precipitation intensity, while
 upper-level shear plays a minor, slightly negative role.

The results demonstrate that early-day moisture preconditioning combined with afternoon large-scale
ascent is the primary control on isolated deep convection during the wet season, rather than surface
forcing alone. These findings provide observational constraints on the shallow-to-deep convective
transition, directly relevant to improving the representation of tropical convection in weather and climate
models.

2.2. Modeling study with SAM
In the modeling study by Viscardi et al. (2025), we used idealized cloud-resolving simulations to quantify
the relative influence of moisture profiles and vertical wind shear on the shallow-to-deep convective
transition (STD) over the Amazon, building upon our observational insights from analyzing GoAmazon
ARM observations. The central aim was to diagnose how environmental preconditioning, specifically
profiles of humidity and shear, modulate convective development in a controlled modeling framework
and assess their mechanistic roles in initiating and sustaining deep convection.
The simulations systematically varied low- or mid-tropospheric moisture, vertical wind shear magnitudes
and shear depth. Figure 3 shows the results for the low-level moisture experiment. Our findings indicate
that moisture content in the lower troposphere exerts a first-order control on the initiation of deep
convection: simulations with deeper, moister profiles consistently undergo an earlier and more robust
transition to deep convective cores, whereas drier profiles favor prolonged shallow or congestus states.
Moreover, we found that increased low-level moisture reduces the CIN (convective inhibition) and
augments CAPE (convective available potential energy), leading to enhanced updraft strength and earlier
deepening.

Figure 3. Low-level moisture experiment. The composites show the diurnal cycle of domain-averaged anomalies for
(a–d) Cloud liquid water, (e–h) Total ice, and (i–l) Rain mixing ratios. Each column corresponds to a different
decrease in CWV: (a, e, i) 1 mm, (b, f, j) 2 mm, (c, g, k) 3 mm, and (d, h, l) 4 mm. The solid black contour marks the
0.01 g/kg level for each variable in the control runs, while the colored contours denote relative differences of 50%
(dashed) and 90% (dotted).

The role of vertical wind shear is more subtle, as can be seen in Figure 4. Moderate shear enhances
organization and longevity of convective updrafts, likely by suppressing entrainment and mixing that
would otherwise weaken updrafts in homogeneous flow. However, excessive shear can inhibit deepening
by introducing dynamical tilting and detrainment, thereby decoupling lower- and mid-level buoyancy.
The simulations reveal a non-monotonic dependence on shear: an optimal shear range maximizes the
shallow-to-deep transition efficiency, beyond which further shear increases degrade convective growth.

Figure 4. Similar to figure 3, but for the Low-level wind experiment. From left to right, the columns correspond to a
factor of 0.75 (decrease), 0.9, 1.1, 1.24 (increase) applied to the strength of the low-level wind shear.
Interactions between moisture and shear are highlighted: while moisture sets the thermodynamic potential
for deep convection, shear modulates the dynamic execution. In environments with high low-level
moisture, the beneficial effects of moderate shear are amplified; in contrast, when moisture is marginal,
even favorable shear cannot fully compensate. This points to a synergistic control in which moisture
establishes the baseline convective potential and shear shapes the structural evolution of convective
updrafts.
The main findings of our study can be summarized as follows:
 • Lower-tropospheric moisture is the dominant thermodynamic control on the shallow-to-deep
 convective transition: deeper and moister profiles promote earlier and more intense deep
 convection.
 • Vertical wind shear modulates convective organization and longevity, with moderate shear
 enhancing deepening, but excessive shear inhibiting the transition by disrupting updraft
 coherence.
 • Synergistic interactions between moisture and shear determine STD outcomes: high moisture
 environments leverage moderate shear to maximize deep convective efficiency, whereas low
 moisture conditions suppress deepening regardless of shear.
Collectively, these results provide mechanistic insight into the shallow-to-deep convective transition and
identify robust moisture and wind-shear thresholds that are directly relevant for improving the

representation of convective initiation and growth in convection-permitting and climate models. By
isolating the respective roles of thermodynamic preconditioning and dynamical organization, the
modeling framework helps bridge process-level understanding and model formulation. These findings
offer concrete guidance for the development and evaluation of convective parameterizations in land–
atmosphere coupled simulations.

2.3. Modeling study with WRF
While the idealized SAM simulations provided mechanistic insight into the shallow-to-deep convective
transition under controlled environmental conditions, they are limited by doubly periodic boundary
conditions and a simplified representation of land–atmosphere coupling. To bridge the gap between
idealized process studies and realistic regional simulations, we will be performing a complementary
modeling study using the WRF-ARW model. The WRF framework allows for realistic initial and lateral
boundary conditions derived from reanalysis, explicit representation of mesoscale variability, and
systematic exploration of a wide range of physical parameterizations relevant to tropical land–atmosphere
interactions. The first step, given the myriads of physics parametrizations available on WRF, was to
determine which combination would reproduce the GoAmazon observations more closely.
The study of Gurung et al. (2025), currently under evaluation for Atmospsheric Research, employed
convection-permitting WRF simulations at 1-km horizontal resolution over the central Amazon Basin
during the wet season, centered on the GoAmazon2014/15 observational domain. A comprehensive
ensemble of 144 simulations was performed by systematically varying parameterization schemes
associated with land surface processes (LSM), planetary boundary layer (PBL), microphysics (MP), and
cumulus convection (CU, applied only in the parent domain). Model performance was evaluated against
multiple surface and boundary-layer observations using Taylor diagrams and a Taylor Skill Score (TSS),
allowing both variable-specific and aggregated assessments. The primary goal was to quantify the
sensitivity of key convective and land–atmosphere variables to physical parameterizations and to identify
combinations that provide robust performance across variables, rather than optimizing for a single metric.
The evaluation of the full ensemble reveals substantial variability in model performance across both
variables and parameterization choices. As shown in Figure 5, near-surface temperature (T2) and
boundary-layer height (PBLH) are generally well simulated across most configurations, with relatively
high and tightly clustered TSS values. In contrast, near-surface humidity (Q2) and precipitation rate (RR)
exhibit low skill and large spread across configurations, indicating persistent challenges in representing
moisture and rainfall at convection-permitting scales. Surface fluxes show compensating behavior:
configurations that perform well for sensible heat flux (HFX) often perform poorly for latent heat flux
(LH), and vice versa, highlighting sensitivity to land surface parameterizations and the partitioning of
surface energy. Sensitivity analysis further indicates that different variables respond preferentially to
different physical processes: LSM choices dominate surface fluxes, PBL schemes exert the strongest
control on PBLH, and MP schemes are most influential for precipitation. No single configuration emerges
as optimal for all variables when considered independently, reinforcing the need for a multi-metric
evaluation framework.

Figure 5. TSS scores for all evaluated variables across 144 configurations, with each WRF configuration assigned
to a unique number from 1 to 144. The black dashed horizontal line indicates the average TSS for each variable.
Light blue dots represent the configurations with TSS above the average, while orange dots represent configurations
with TSS below the average. The dark blue dot highlights the configuration with the highest TSS (best
configuration) for each variable.
To identify configurations that perform well across variables simultaneously, individual TSS scores were
combined into a weighted aggregate metric that emphasizes variables with lower overall skill. The
resulting optimal configurations consistently favor the Noah land surface model, YSU planetary boundary
layer scheme, and Morrison microphysics, with the Grell-3D cumulus scheme applied in the parent
domain. Figure 6 illustrates that the combined optimal configuration reproduces the observed diurnal
cycles of temperature, fluxes, and boundary-layer evolution reasonably well, while still exhibiting known
biases in humidity and precipitation. Although the configuration optimized for all variables does not
outperform the best variable-specific configurations in every case, it captures the overall diurnal structure
and magnitude more consistently than most ensemble members. The results also demonstrate that the
cumulus scheme used in the coarse parent domain influences the innermost convection-permitting domain
through boundary conditions, underscoring the importance of multi-domain configuration choices even
when convection is explicitly resolved.

Figure 6. Comparison of the diurnal cycle of observation (black), independent best (blue), and combined optimal
(red) configuration for the evaluated variables. The gray lines are the model output for all 144 configurations.
The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows:
 • Model performance at convection-permitting scales over the Amazon is highly sensitive to the
 choice of physical parameterizations, with different variables responding to different physical
 processes.
 • No single configuration optimizes all variables individually, but a robust combination (Noah
 LSM, YSU PBL, Morrison MP, G3 CU) provides the most consistent overall performance across
 surface, boundary-layer, and convective metrics.
 • Persistent biases in near-surface humidity and precipitation highlight structural challenges in
 simulating tropical convection, even at kilometer-scale resolution.
This study represents a preparatory phase aimed at selecting an appropriate WRF configuration for
convection-permitting simulations over the Amazon. By systematically evaluating the performance of
physical parameterizations, we establish a baseline configuration to be used in forthcoming WRF
experiments focused on isolating the environmental controls on the shallow-to-deep convective transition
under realistic forcing.

2.4. Partnership Development, Training, and Capacity Building Outcomes
In addition to the scientific results described before, this project achieved its core RDPP objectives related
to partnership building, training, and capacity development in DOE ARM/ASR research at minority-
serving institutions. Consistent with the intent of the RDPP call, the project emphasized establishing
sustainable collaborations, lowering barriers to entry into ARM-enabled research, and building
institutional capacity rather than focusing exclusively on near-term scientific outcomes.

Regarding Objective #1, the project successfully established an active research partnership among
UMBC, Morgan State University (MSU), and Howard University (HU), institutions that previously had
limited engagement with DOE ARM and ASR activities. This collaboration has expanded to include a
working relationship with PNNL through interactions with Dr. William Gustafson. These partnerships are
now reflected in shared mentoring of students, including the co-advising of a UMBC PhD student by
faculty at MSU, and direct scientific interactions between students and DOE laboratory researchers.
These connections have transitioned from exploratory to operational and are now embedded in ongoing
student research and advising structures.
For Objective #2, the project exceeded its original scope by organizing two multi-day, hybrid training
events that brought together students, postdoctoral researchers, and faculty from the three partner
institutions and beyond. The first was a two-day tutorial on DOE ARM Large-Eddy Simulations
(LASSO), delivered by PNNL in May 2023, which provided hands-on exposure to ARM data–model
integration workflows. The second was a two-day course on quantitative analysis of meteorological data,
delivered by UCAR researchers in August 2023. Together, these events trained nearly 40 participants and
substantially expanded familiarity with ARM data products, LASSO workflows, and quantitative analysis
tools across the participating institutions.
These training activities directly supported Objective #3 by fostering broader and more durable
engagement with ARM and ASR resources. Notably, students outside the immediate project team have
since adopted LASSO for their own research, indicating that the project’s impact extended beyond its
originally funded participants. In parallel, the modeling work conducted under this project helped build
local expertise in convection-permitting modeling, data evaluation against ARM observations, and high-
performance computing workflows.
Importantly, the capacity-building outcomes of this RDPP project have already translated into
downstream proposal activity. Leveraging preliminary results, partnerships, and training outcomes from
this effort, both the PI and the Co-I submitted a full proposals to DOE ASR in 2023 and were encouraged
to submit a full proposal in the 2025 ASR call. These submissions reflect the project’s success in
positioning the team to pursue larger-scale, higher-impact DOE-funded research efforts.
Overall, this project fulfilled the RDPP mission by establishing new DOE ARM/ASR research capacity at
minority-serving institutions, creating sustained partnerships with a DOE national laboratory, training a
cohort of students and early-career researchers, and laying the groundwork for future ARM-enabled
research. These outcomes represent a durable return on investment that extends beyond the specific
scientific results reported here.
