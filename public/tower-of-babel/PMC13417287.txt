Reductive and Oxidative Transformations of Nitrite at Nickel(II): Insights into the Generation, Electronic Structure, and Reactivity of {NiNO}10
Authors: Balakrishnan S. Anju, Neeraja R. Nair, Janavi Rajput, Reshma Shenoy, Indranil Mondal, Jeffery A. Bertke, Bhaskar Mondal, Subrata Kundu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The diverse redox transformations of nitrite (NO2 –) link key signaling molecules like nitric oxide (NO) and enable precise regulation of NO flux in physiology. This report employs structurally and spectroscopically characterized mononuclear [Ni]–NO x complexes to illustrate nitrogen-oxide redox chemistry, including conversions between nitrite, NO, and nitrate. Reduction of [(Me2PzQu)2NiII(nitrite)]­(ClO4) 1-Qu by p-methylthiophenol (MeArSH) leads to a {NiNO}10 species [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(ClO4)] 3-Qu through the intermediacy of HNO2 and MeArSNO. Interestingly, subtle modifications in the ligand properties distinctly influence the thiol–nitrite reactivity and the stability of the resulting {NiNO}10 species. Importantly, the crosstalk between H2S and nitrite at the nickel­(II) site also results in reductive nitrosylation to afford the {NiNO}10 species. Although the detection of transient HNO2 is challenging, this study uniquely employs a phosphine-mediated deoxygenation strategy to demonstrate intermediacy of HNO2 during thiol reactions of [(Me2PzQu)2MII(nitrite)]+ (M = Ni/Zn). In addition, NO and NO– ligand transfer reactivity of the {NiNO}10 core in 3-Qu have been evaluated. Using iodosylbenzene (PhIO) as a sacrificial O-atom donor, both 1-Qu and 3-Qu complexes have been shown to yield [NiII]–nitrate 2-Qu, thereby outlining a stepwise O-atom transfer (OAT) pathway for nitrosyl → nitrite → nitrate transformations. Taken together, these findings reveal reductive nitrosylation via nitrite–thiol/H2S crosstalk and demonstrate a stepwise OAT-dependent route for NO/nitrite oxidation using a common coordination motif.

Introduction

Nitrogen oxides (NO x ) are ubiquitous in the biological realm. , For example, nitrite (NO2 –) and nitrate (NO3 –) anions serve as the storage pools of nitric oxide (NO) under hypoxia, thereby supporting a diverse array of physiological activities such as neurotransmission, vasodilation, and immune response. , Oxidative transformations of the overproduced NO to nitrite/nitrate allow tight regulation of NO flux. Notably, nitroxyl (HNO), a one-electron reduced and protonated form of NO, is a weakly acidic antioxidant and shows efficacy in treating alcoholism and cardiovascular diseases. In these contexts, molecular-level understanding of both the reductive and oxidative transformations of NO x is critical for developing therapeutic approaches based on the bioactivities of NO/HNO. Enzymatic reduction of nitrite at various transition metal-based active sites, such as heme-FeII in myoglobin/hemoglobin/cytochrome c oxidases, Fe/Cu in nitrite reductases (NIR), and even redox-inactive ZnII in carbonic anhydrase, has triggered enormous research interest to emulate nitrite-to-NO transformation utilizing synthetic model complexes. − Electrocatalytic reduction of nitrite to NO has also gained significant interest in recent years. It is noteworthy that the reductive transformations of NO x at nickel are majorly limited to the reaction of [NiII]­(NO x ) (where x = 2,3) complexes with highly oxophilic reagents like CO and Mashima’s reagent. Moreover, examples of NO x deoxygenation pathways using oxophilic metal sites (such as Mo) or exogenous reagents (such as VCl3) have been reported previously. − Interestingly, O-atom transfer (OAT)-based deoxygenation of nitrite coordinated at copper­(II) leading to NO gas has been shown to proceed smoothly under ambient conditions. A closely related reaction of large excess NaNO2 (∼17 equiv) with an equimolar mixture of (Ph3P)2NiBr2 and Ph3P under a reflux condition in tetrahydrofuran has been reported to yield (Ph3P)2Ni­(NO)Br and Ph3PO presumably following an OAT pathway. A recent study outlines that the interaction between thioacetate and nitrite at nickel­(II) results in NO, where the reaction proceeds through (per)­thionitrite (S x NO–; where x = 1, 2) intermediates generated from a nucleophilic attack of nitrite on the electrophilic site of thioacetate. , Thiol–nitrite interaction in the presence of thio/seleno-acetate at the zinc­(II) site has also been illustrated to produce HNO through the intermediacy of transient HSNO/HSeNO. Unlike the well-studied examples of thiol/thiolate-mediated transformations of nitrite at Fe, Cu, and Zn, ,,,,,,, to the best of our knowledge, insights into the thiol–nitrite interaction at nickel remain unexplored. Cysteine (CysSH) has been demonstrated to react with nitrite at a ferriheme site following an O-atom transfer pathway and affords cysteine sulfenic acid CysS­(O)­H. Harrop and co-workers proposed proton-assisted heterolysis of the O–N bond of nitrite using a nonheme ferrous site. On the other hand, the nitrite anion coordinated at an electron-deficient β-diketiminato ligand has been proposed to react with thiols through a proton-transfer-coupled nucleophilic attack of thiol, resulting in S-nitrosothiol (RSNO). Interestingly, the reactions of thiol with nitrite at zinc­(II) have been illustrated to proceed through a proton transfer from a coordinatively polarized RS–H to nitrite, forming nitrous acid (HNO2), which subsequently leads to RSNO. In light of the previously reported diverse reaction trajectories proposed for the thiol–nitrite interactions at Fe, Cu, and Zn sites, we seek to investigate the reactions of thiol with nitrite at Ni in order to gain insights into the reaction pathways and resultant products. Furthermore, current understanding of the reactions of metal–nitrite complexes with hydrogen sulfide, H2S, the biologically relevant and simplest analogue of thiol, remains extremely limited.

We also aim to explore the oxidative reactivity of NO x at a nickel site, as such transformations play crucial roles in the precise regulation of NO flux in physiology and balancing the N-cycle through nitrification. Notably, Mo-based nitrite oxidoreductase (NXR) has been proposed to mediate nitrite-to-nitrate conversion as the terminal step of nitrification, while little is known about the underlying mechanism. The oxidation of nitrite by oxyhemoglobin, leading to the formation of methemoglobin and nitrate, is well known in the context of nitrite poisoning. Examples of metal–NO oxidation by various reactive oxygen species, ROS (e.g., dioxygen, superoxide, peroxide), leading to nitrite and nitrate, are documented in the literature. − These prior reports typically suggest the involvement of a putative peroxynitrite (ONOO–) intermediate in these oxidative transformations. In contrast, 18O-isotope labeling studies on NXR activity clearly show that the new O atom in nitrate originates from water rather than dioxygen. A recent report illustrates dioxygen reactivity of [(L′)­Ni­(NO)­I] (L’ = bis(3,5-diisopropyl-1-pyrazolyl)­methane) affording [[NiII]­(nitrite)]+ with a triiodide (I3 –) counteranion. The oxidation of iodide to triiodide during the monooxygenation of nitrosyl to nitrite perhaps suggests mechanistic complexity. Despite the fact that NO-monooxygenation (NOM) and NO-dioxygenation (NOD) at Cr, Mn, Fe, and Co sites have been developed earlier based on the decay of transient peroxynitrite, − this work aims to demonstrate a possible alternative route for the oxidation of nitrosyl and nitrite at nickel­(II) via sequential O-atom transfer (OAT). It is noteworthy that the sequential O-atom transfers to oxophilic substrates like CO and VCl3 are well established in the context of NO x – (x = 2, 3) reduction; − however, the conceptually reverse oxidative transformations of nitrosyl and nitrite remain poorly understood. Herein, we illustrate the reduction of nitrite in the structurally characterized complexes [L 2NiII(nitrite)]­(ClO4) (L = Me2PzQu/Me2PzPy) (1-Qu/1-Py), with p-methylthiophenol (MeArSH) affording {NiNO}10 species 3-Qu/3-Py (Figure ). Detailed reactivity studies reveal that the {NiNO}10 forms through reductive nitrosylation of nickel­(II) by the in situ-generated RSNO/HSNO in the presence of thiol/H2S. In addition, the NXR-type reactivity of the nitrite complexes 1-Qu/1-Py with an O-atom donor oxidant, namely, iodosylbenzene (PhIO), yields the corresponding nitrate complexes [L 2NiII(nitrate)]­(ClO4) (2-Qu/2-Py). Thus, this report discloses both nitrite-to-NO and nitrosyl/nitrite-to-nitrate transformations at a common nickel­(II) site.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of [NiII]–Nitrite/Nitrate Complexes

[L 2NiII(nitrite)]­(ClO4) (1-Qu/1-Py) were isolated as reported earlier for the corresponding copper­(II)/zinc­(II)–nitrite complexes. , Molecular structures of both the complexes show the presence of octahedral nickel­(II) sites with nitrite coordinated in a κ2 -O,O fashion and two bidentate ligands Me2PzQu/Me2PzPy (Figures S1 and S4). The metrical parameters obtained from the crystal structures of 1-Qu/1-Py are comparable to those of the previously reported analogous copper and zinc complexes (Table S1). , FTIR spectra of 1-Qu and 15N-nitrite sample 1-Qu-15N show the 14/15N-isotope-sensitive bands at 1210 and 1188 cm–1, respectively. The spectroscopic (FTIR and UV–vis) signatures of 1-Qu/1-Py are comparable (Figures S2, S3, S5, and S6).

Reactions of ligand Me2PzQu/Me2PzPy (2.0 equiv) with Ni­(ClO4)2·6H2O (1.0 equiv) and NaNO3 (1.0 equiv) in methanol provide [L 2NiII(nitrate)]­(ClO4) (2-Qu/2-Py) in 97% and 85% yields, respectively. The X-ray crystal structure of 2-Qu renders an octahedral [NiII] site with a κ2 -O,O-nitrate moiety (Figure S7). FTIR spectra of 2-Qu and 2-Qu-15N show 14/15N-isotope-sensitive vibrational features at 1487, 1384, and 1274 cm–1 (for 2-Qu) and 1453, 1351, and 1251 cm–1 (for 2-Qu-15N) (Figure S9). The nitrate vibrational bands of 2-Qu/2-Py are comparable to those of the previously reported metal complexes with κ2 -O,O-nitrate (Table S2, Figures S10–S12). , It is noteworthy that the vibrational bands observed for nitrite and nitrate complexes of these nickel­(II) complexes are distinct, thereby serving as the diagnostic spectroscopic markers for monitoring the transformations of NO x in the present study.

Reactivity of Thiol/H2S with [NiII]–Nitrite

A treatment of 1-Qu with MeArSH (4.0 equiv) in acetonitrile at room temperature results in a color change from light green to dark blue over a period of 12 h (Figures A and S13). Probing the headspace of the reaction mixture using (TPP)­CoII as a NO trap shows the liberation of NO gas in 30% yield (Figure S14). Vapor diffusion of Et2O into a CH2Cl2 solution of the resultant nickel complex provides dark-blue microcrystals of [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(ClO4)] (3-Qu) in 70% yield. The X-ray crystal structure of 3-Qu reveals a tetrahedral nickel site bound to a nearly linear nitrosyl ligand, one κ1 -O-perchlorate, and one bidentate ligand Me2PzQu (Figures B and S15). FTIR analyses depict a 14/15N-isotope-sensitive band assigned as ν­(NO) at 1768 cm–1 (for 3-Qu) and 1734 cm–1 (for 3-Qu-15N) (Figures C, S16, and S17). The observed bond distances (Ni–N, N–O), Ni–N–O angle, and ν­(NO) vibrational feature of 3-Qu are congruent with the earlier tetrahedral {NiNO}10 complexes (Tables S4 and S5). ,, The UV–vis spectrum of 3-Qu in acetonitrile depicts λmax/nm (ε/M–1cm–1) at 605(200), comparable to the previously reported tetrahedral {NiNO}10 cores (Figure S18). Unlike the previously reported routes that typically utilize NO gas, ,,, except the report by Feltham in 1964 utilizing a large excess of NaNO2, the present study establishes transformation of isolated nickel­(II)–nitrite to {NiNO}10. Crystallization of the end product from MeCN/Et2O provides solvent-coordinated {NiNO}10 species [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(MeCN)]­(ClO4) (3-Qu-MeCN) (Figure S20). These crystallization attempts also give a very minor crop of the μ-thiolate-bound bimetallic complex, structurally characterized as [{(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)}2(μ-SArMe)]­(ClO4) (3-Qu-SArMe) (Figure S21). Moreover, an equimolar reaction of 3-Qu and triphenylphosphine in acetonitrile results in [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(PPh3)]­(ClO4) (3-Qu-PPh3) (Figure S22). These results suggest that the labile [Ni]–OClO3 bond in 3-Qu is susceptible to facile ligand substitution.50 1H and 13C NMR spectra of 3-Qu in CD3CN show sharp peaks, thereby suggesting the diamagnetic nature of {NiNO}10 (Figure S23).

Although MeArSH (pK a ∼6.8 in H2O) reduces nitrite in 1-Qu effectively, a relatively less acidic 4-tert-butylbenzylthiol ( t BuBnSH) (pK a ∼9.4 in H2O) does not provide the {NiNO}10 species 3-Qu while generating NO gas in 24% yield only (Figure S14). However, the reaction of 1-Qu with t BuBnSH in the presence of pyridinium tosylate [PyH]­[OTs] results in 48% NO gas along with 3-Qu (Figures S14 and S24). Interestingly, [(Me2PzPy)2Ni­(nitrite)]­(ClO4) (1-Py) neither reacts with MeArSH nor t BuBnSH to provide {NiNO}10 (Figures S25 and S26). In contrast, the corresponding zinc complex [(Me2PzPy)2Zn­(nitrite)]­(ClO4) reacts smoothly with MeArSH to provide NO gas (74%). Nonetheless, 1-Py reacts with MeArSH in the presence of [PyH]­[OTs] to yield the corresponding {NiNO}10 species 3-Py, as evaluated by UV–vis spectroscopic monitoring of the reaction (Figure S27). 3-Py, however, is metastable and readily releases NO gas quantitatively, thereby hindering the structural characterization of 3-Py (Figure S26). Spectroscopic features of 3-Py exhibiting λmax at ∼595 nm (UV–vis) and ν­(NO) at 1766 cm–1 (FTIR) are comparable to those of 3-Qu (Figures S27 and S28). These findings regarding the thiol reactions of nickel­(II)–nitrite 1-Qu/1-Py suggest that proton transfer is the key step in the underlying reaction mechanism (vide infra). Importantly, the unreactive nature of the nickel­(II)–nitrite complexes 1-Qu/1-Py toward the aliphatic thiol t BuBnSH, in contrast to their zinc analogues, suggests that the nickel­(II) centers are less effective than relatively softer zinc­(II) sites at promoting metal­(II)–thiol interactions (vide infra). Furthermore, the markedly lower thiol–nitrite reactivity at the [(Me2PzPy)2Ni]2+ core compared to that at [(Me2PzQu)2Ni]2+ in generating {NiNO}10 and NO gas underscores that the subtle tuning of the ancillary ligand also plays an important role.

Curiously, we turned to the probe reactivity of [(Me2PzQu)2Ni­(nitrite)]­(ClO4) (1-Qu) toward H2S (pK a ∼6.98 in H2O), , which features comparable pK a as MeArSH. Gratifyingly, diffusion of freshly generated H2S gas into an acetonitrile solution of 1-Qu yields the {NiNO}10 species 3′-Qu, as confirmed by UV–vis and FTIR studies (Figure S29). The molecular structure of the {NiNO}10 species obtained from the H2S reaction shows the presence of a tetrahedral {NiNO}10 cation supported by one Me2PzQu bound in a bidentate fashion and another one in a monodentate fashion (Figures and S30). One of the quinoline donor sites of Me2PzQu is found to be noninteractive to provide the tetrahedral nickel site in [(Me2PzQu)2Ni­(NO)]­(ClO4) (3′-Qu), thereby validating the previously proposed hemilability of the quinoline donor. The isolations of various derivatives of the tetrahedral {NiNO}10 species from these thiol/H2S reactions of 1-Qu featuring variable fourth ligands in the [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)]+ core suggest that the fourth site is labile. 3′-Qu is likely formed initially during the thiol reactions of 1-Qu and subsequently converts to 3-Qu upon dissociation of one coligand. These results also hint at the possible interconversion between these tetrahedral {NiNO}10 derivatives, and isolation of the particular species depends on factors like crystallization conditions. Importantly, this study illustrates the first example of reductive nitrosylation arising from the interplay between H2S and nitrite at a nickel­(II) center. Drawing an analogy with H2S–nitrite interactions and the previous literature, , the reaction is proposed to proceed via the transient formation of the simplest S-nitrosothiol HSNO. While the proposed HSNO generated from the reaction of HNO2 with excess H2S is expected to yield HNO, FTIR analysis of the headspace from the reaction between 1-Qu and H2S shows no evidence of N2O formation, the stable end product typically arising from HNO dimerization.

Mechanism of Thiol–Nitrite Interaction at Nickel­(II) and {NiNO}10 Formation

Considering the previously proposed O-atom transfer (OAT) route for the reactions of (heme)­FeIII–nitrite with thiol or phosphine, the present reaction between thiol and 1-Qu/1-Py could proceed via OAT, leading to {NiNO}10 and transient sulfenic acid as shown in Figure A. Such an O-atom transfer in the present case (Figure A, steps i–ii), however, is precluded as 1-Qu does not react with Ph3P under our reaction conditions. 15N NMR spectroscopic monitoring of a reaction mixture consisting of 1-Qu-15N and MeArSH in CD3CN depicts a transient species with a chemical shift at 576.3 ppm (versus liquid NH3) (Figure S31), consistent with the formation of H15NO2. , The UV–vis spectrum of 1-Qu upon addition of MeArSH in acetonitrile at room temperature shows an immediate growth of the 465 nm absorption feature (Figure S32). Notably, the same species could also be generated from the reaction of a structurally characterized nickel­(II)–perchlorate complex [(Me2PzQu)2NiII(ClO4)]­(ClO4) (4-Qu) with MeArSH in the presence of triethylamine, thereby suggesting that the 465 nm feature arises from the in situ generation of a nickel­(II)–thiolate species and consistent with the absorption features of the previously reported nickel­(II)–thiolate complexes (Figure S32). Thus, these spectroscopic studies (15N NMR and UV–vis) suggest that the initial reaction of nickel­(II)–nitrite and thiol leads to the formation of HNO2 and nickel­(II)–thiolate (Figure B, step (iii). This proton-transfer-assisted thiol-nitrite reactivity is similar to that proposed for the analogous reactions of zinc­(II)–nitrite. However, S-nitrosothiol (RSNO) is expected from the reaction between thiol­(ate) and in situ-generated HNO2 (Figure B, step (iv)) and has been observed for the previous reactions of zinc­(II)–nitrite. RSNO, however, could not be detected spectroscopically for the reaction 1-Qu and thiol, perhaps due to the low steady-state concentration of RSNO in the presence of redox-active metals.

UV–vis monitoring of the MeArSH reactions of 1-Qu/1-Py in the presence of [PyH]­[OTs] shows the formation of S-nitrosothiol MeArSNO (λmax ∼570 nm) and subsequent rapid formation of the {NiNO}10 species 3-Qu/3-Py (Figure S27). , Thus, this experiment in the presence of [PyH]+ illustrates the involvement of the in situ generated S-nitrosothiol in {NiNO}10 formation. Reactions of 4-Qu with S-nitrosothiol or NO gas in the presence of MeArSH also afford {NiNO}10 (3-Qu) as analyzed by UV–vis and FTIR spectroscopy (Figures A–C and S36). In contrast, the control experiments in the absence of thiol do not provide 3-Qu (Figure C). These findings support the mechanistic hypothesis for {NiNO}10 formation through the NO-mediated nitrosylation of [NiII]–thiolate (Figure B, step (v)), where NO gas originates from the homolysis of S-nitrosothiol. This is also consistent with the previous literature illustrating the synthesis of {NiNO}10 from the NO-reactivity of the [NiII(SPh)4]2– core.

Alternative to the NO-mediated reductive nitrosylation of the nickel­(II) site in the presence of thiol/thiolate (Figure B, step (v)), formation of {NiNO}10 species could also involve nitrosylation of the nickel­(II) center by HNO/NO– (Figure B, steps vi-vii). In this pathway, the reaction between thiol and S-nitrosothiol is proposed to serve as a source of HNO, as reported elsewhere for the reactions between glutathione (GSH) and corresponding S-nitrosothiol (GSNO). Notably, 15N NMR spectroscopic monitoring of reactions of MeArSNO and MeArSH shows a trace amount of N2O (Figure D). FTIR analysis on the headspace of a reaction of MeArSNO and MeArSH after 12 h does not show the diagnostic vibrational feature for N2O. Thus, these 15N NMR and FTIR results suggest that the HNO/N2O generation for the reaction between aromatic thiol and the corresponding S-nitrosothiol is extremely sluggish. In comparison to the nucleophilic attack of thiol on the S-site of S-nitrosothiol leading to HNO, S-nitrosothiol of an aromatic thiol (MeArSNO) is perhaps more susceptible to provide NO gas through homolysis driven by stabilization of the aryl thiyl radical MeArS•. Guided by the negligible yield of HNO/N2O from the reaction of MeArSNO and MeArSH (Figure B, step vi), the HNO-mediated nitrosylation of the nickel site is considered not a viable pathway for {NiNO}10 generation (Figure B, step (vii)).

With the experimental evidence of proton-transfer-assisted thiol–nitrite interactions (Figure B, step (iii)), we turned to rationalize the differences in reactivity between 1-Qu and 1-Py, as well as between nickel­(II)–nitrite and the previously reported zinc­(II)–nitrite. The comparatively sluggish reactivity of 1-Py relative to 1-Qu is proposed to arise from the inaccessibility of the thiol coordination site at the octahedral nickel­(II) center in 1-Py. The computed binding energy reveals that the binding of MeArSH is more favorable for 1-Qu than for 1-Py by ∼10 kcal/mol and, therefore, can promote greater reactivity toward 1-Qu (Figure S37). Consistent with the crystal structure of 3′-Qu, the hemilabile nature of the quinoline donor in 1-Qu is proposed to allow thiol to interact with the nickel site (Figure C). The distinct reactivity of aliphatic thiols (e.g., t BuBnSH) toward zinc­(II)–nitrite, relative to nickel­(II)–nitrite, is attributed to the greater ability of zinc­(II) to enhance the Brønsted acidity of the thiol. This arises from the comparatively stronger Lewis acidity of zinc­(II) and the more facile metal–thiol interaction (Figure C).

Evidence of HNO2 through Ph3P-Mediated Deoxygenation

As the spectroscopic evidence for the transient intermediacy of HNO2 is often challenging, we sought to explore the possibility of chemically intercepting the in situ-generated HNO2. Considering the well-established O-atom abstraction ability of phosphines from nitroaromatic compounds in the Cadogan–Sundberg indole synthesis, we reasoned that Ph3P could be employed to intercept the transient HNO2 formed during the reactions of nitrite. Notably, Ph3P is known to react with NO gas to afford N2O, with Ph3PO as the sole phosphine-derived product. Although the rate of phosphine-mediated deoxygenation of NO gas is solvent-polarity-dependent and typically slow in common organic solvents, Ph3P reacts with nitroxyl (HNO) extremely rapidly to provide a mixture of phosphine imide Ph3PNH and phosphine oxide Ph3PO (Figure A). , This reaction between HNO and Ph3P presumably proceeds via deoxygenation and trapping of the nitrene species, namely, imidogen (NH), analogous to closely related reactions of nitrosobenzene (PhNO) with N-heterocyclic carbenes. , Nitrite anion does not react with Ph3P. In contrast, an authentic sample of HNO2, generated from the reaction of NO2 – and p-toluenesulfonic acid (PTSA), reacts with Ph3P to provide a mixture of phosphine-derived products (Figures B, S38, and S39). 31P­{1H} NMR studies show the chemical shifts corresponding to Ph3PNH2 + (35.9 ppm), Ph3PO (29.1 ppm), and (Ph3P)2N+ (21.1 ppm). HRMS analysis further confirms the formation of Ph3PNH2 + (m/z 278.1080, calc. m/z 278.1093), [Ph3PO + H+] (m/z 279.0920, calc. m/z 279.0933), and (Ph3P)2N+ (m/z 538.1830, calc. m/z 538.1848) (Figure S39). The formation of Ph3PNH2 + and Ph3PO as phosphine-derived products in the reaction with HNO2 resembles the products observed in the reactions of Ph3P with HNO. Therefore, we propose that the reaction between HNO2 and Ph3P proceeds through initial deoxygenation, leading to the formation of transient HNO, which subsequently reacts with additional equivalents of Ph3P to ultimately produce Ph3PO and Ph3PNH2 + (Figure B). (Ph3P)2N+ presumably forms through a competitive deoxygenation pathway, while the mechanism underlying its formation is not clear presently. The factors governing the relative ratio of Ph3PNH2 +, Ph3PO, and (Ph3P)2N+ are not fully understood. Nevertheless, the qualitative distribution of the phosphine-derived products demonstrates the utility of Ph3P in intercepting HNO2.

31P­{1H} NMR and HRMS studies on the crude sample obtained from the reactions of [(Me2PzQu)2Ni­(nitrite)]+ and MeArSH in the presence of Ph3P (5 equiv) reveal the formation of Ph3PNH2 +, Ph3PO, and (Ph3P)2N+ (Figures C and S38, S40). However, HNO generated from the reaction between in situ-generated S-nitrosothiol RSNO and thiol (Figure B, step (vi)) could, in principle, respond similarly. However, such a possibility is ruled out as the control experiments between MeArSNO and MeArSH do not show a considerable amount of HNO/N2O. Consistent with the 15N NMR evidence of H15NO2, the observation of Ph3PNH2 +, Ph3PO, and (Ph3P)2N+ during the reaction of 1-Qu, MeArSH, and Ph3P illustrates the chemical interception of HNO2 in situ generated during the thiol–nitrite interaction. Moreover, the thiol reaction of [(Me2PzQu)2Zn­(nitrite)]+ in the presence of excess Ph3P shows similar phosphine-derived products (Figures S38 and S41). In contrast, the 31P­{1H} NMR and HRMS analyses of the sample obtained from the analogous reaction of 1-Py, MeArSH, and Ph3P show trace yields of Ph3PNH2 + and (Ph3P)2N+ (Figures S38 and S42). Thus, these analyses not only probe the intermediacy of HNO2 but also shed light on the efficiency of interactions between thiol and nitrite at nickel­(II). Moreover, this is consistent with the proposed relatively weaker interaction between thiol and nitrite at the [(Me2PzPy)2Ni]2+ core compared to that at [(Me2PzQu)2Ni]2+.

Although phosphine-based assays are well established as highly effective and popular in detecting HNO, our results suggest that phosphine can also promote deoxygenation of species such as HNO2, leading to the transient generation of HNO. Therefore, the observation of phosphine imide and oxide during the reactions of nitrogen oxide (NO x ) species in the presence of phosphine does not necessarily imply the presence of HNO in the reaction mixture in the absence of phosphine.

Electronic Structure of {NiNO}10

Though the FTIR and NMR spectroscopic analysis on 3-Qu suggests the presence of a nickel­(II) site antiferromagnetically coupled to an NO– (S = 1) ligand, the facile dissociative release of NO from 3-Py indicates that the ground electronic state is capable of reaching different states under the experimental conditions. Notably, the electronic structure of a previously reported heme {FeNO}6 core with a N-donor axial ligand has been best described to possess a [FeII]–NO+ ground state with an elusive low-spin [FeIII]–NO• state, thereby enabling NO gas releasing reactivity. Prompted by this dual redox behavior of the metal–nitrosyl cores, we turned to investigate the electronic structure of 3-Qu (Figure ). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic (XPS) studies on 1-Qu, 2-Qu, and 3-Qu were carried out (Figure S43). The Ni 2p3/2 transition maxima for the nitrite/nitrate complexes (1-Qu/2-Qu) appear at 855.5 eV, consistent with the nickel­(II) oxidation state. Interestingly, 3-Qu exhibits a shift of the Ni 2p3/2 maxima by −0.7 eV, while the shakeup maxima for all these complexes appear at 861.0 eV. Considering similar satellite features, this red shift of the Ni 2p3/2 binding energy 3-Qu perhaps suggests that the nickel center in {NiNO}10 possesses a lower formal oxidation state than a + II state in 1-Qu/2-Qu. However, a possible contribution from metal–ligand covalency to the observed red shift in binding energy cannot be excluded completely. For gaining deeper insights into the electronic structure of the {NiNO}10 core in 3-Qu, multiconfigurational complete active space self-consistent field (CASSCF) calculations were performed at the def2-TZVPP level of theory on the DFT-optimized geometry. The DFT-optimized geometry of the (Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(ClO4) core depicts comparable metrical parameters as the X-ray crystal structure (Table S7). Furthermore, at the DFT level, calculations of various valence configurations and their relative energies reveal that the BS­(2,2) configuration featuring Ni2+(d8) + NO1–(π*)2 with overall singlet (S total = 0) is the ground electronic state (Figure S44). The configuration with Ni1+(d9) + NO­(π*)1 with S total = 1 is 13.0 kcal/mol higher in energy in comparison to the ground electronic state. Further analysis of the localized DFT orbitals of the lowest-energy structure suggests the antiferromagnetically coupled nickel­(II) (SNi = 1) and high-spin NO– (SNO– = 1) center, which also mirrors the spin populations of Ni + 1.09, N −0.64, and O −0.56 (Figure S45). This electronic formulation is consistent with that of the previously reported well-characterized tetrahedral {NiNO}10 cores supported by tris(pyrazolyl)­borate ligands. , To capture the multiconfigurational nature of the electronic structure, we analyzed the CASSCF-derived wave function of the singlet ground state of 3-Qu using a CASSCF­(4,4) active space (Figure A). This active space includes the key Ni-3d and NO–π bonding and antibonding orbitals and is sufficient to describe the relevant electronic configurations. The CASSCF­(4,4) calculation reveals heavy multiconfiguration character for 3-Qu (Table S8): the ground-state CASSCF­(4,4) wave function consists of a principal electronic configuration of (d xz + π x *)2(d yz + π y *)2(d xz – π x *)0(d yz – π y *)0 (∼63.7%) to the ground-state wave function (Figure A). The two other appreciable configurations constituting the ground state involve (d xz + π x *)2(d yz + π y *)0(d xz – π x *)0(d yz – π y *)2 (∼11%) and (d xz + π x *)0(d yz + π y *)2(d xz – π x *)2(d yz – π y *)0 (∼11%). A fourth configuration involves single excitations between the Ni-dπ and NO-π orbitals, (d xz + π x *)1(d yz + π y *)1(d xz – π x *)1(d yz – π y *)1 (∼9%), to the ground-state wave function. A fifth configuration involving double excitations between the Ni-dπ and NO-π* orbitals, (d xz + π x *)0(d yz + π y *)0(d xz – π x *)2(d yz – π y *)2 (∼6%), also contributes noticeably to the ground state wave function. As such, the ground singlet state exhibits a significant multiconfigurational nature with five major configurations. The multiconfigurational nature of the electronic structure was further verified using a larger active space calculation, which includes all Ni 3d orbitals, NO π and π* orbitals, and correlation due to double d-shells, resulting in an active space of CASSCF­(14,12) (Figure S47 and Table S10). Notably, the CASSCF (14,12) ground-state wave function is very similar to that of CASSCF­(4,4), suggesting the primary electron correlation effect arises from the four key orbitals presented in Figure .

To gain further insight into the electronic structure, the valence orbital picture was obtained through Pipek–Mezey localization on the CASSCF­(4,4)-derived natural orbitals. A similar localization approach was also utilized in the earlier report by Bergman, DeBeer, Toste, Wieghardt, and co-workers to understand the electronic structure of the Ni–NO complex. The localized molecular orbitals (LMOs) can be assigned to two metal-based orbitals, d xz and d yz , and two NO ligand-based orbitals, π* x and π* y (Figure A). This orbital assignment provides a clear basis for understanding the valence orbital picture. The principal configuration, d xz 1d yz 1π* x 1π* y 1, contributes ∼74% to the ground-state wave function (Figure B, Tables S8 and S9). Most importantly, the unpaired electrons exhibit antiferromagnetic coupling, corresponding to a NiII(S = 1)-NO1– (S = 1) oxidation-state assignment consistent with the DFT results. Additionally, two other configurations, d xz 2d yz 0π* x 0π* y 2 and d xz 0d yz 2π* x 2π* y 0, together contribute approximately ∼4% to the ground-state wave function. These configurations correspond to double excitations from a metal d orbital to an NO π* ligand orbital and can be described as NiII(S = 0)-NO1– (S = 0). Other appreciable configurations involve the single excitation from one of the d orbitals to the NO π* orbital d xz 1d yz 2π* x 1π* y 0 (∼8%) and d xz 2d yz 1π* x 0π* y 1(∼9%). Together, these configurations contribute ∼17% to the ground-state wave function and correspond to a NiI(S = 1/2)–NO0 (S = 1/2) oxidation state. This electronic structure analysis clearly reveals a highly multiconfigurational nature of the {NiNO}10 electronic structure. Moreover, the results align with the DFT results for a BS (2,2)-type electronic configuration and are in good agreement with previous reports. ,

NO versus NO– Transfer Reactivity of {NiNO}10

Though the ground electronic state of the {NiNO}10 core in 3-Qu is best described as [NiII] coordinated to a high-spin NO– ligand, 3-Qu exhibits NO transfer reactivity in the presence of (TPP)­CoII (where TPP = tetraphenylporphyrin). An equimolar reaction of 3-Qu with (TPP)­CoII reveals the formation of (TPP)­CoNO in 69% yield (Figures and S48A). On the contrary, a treatment of 3-Qu with an equimolar amount of (TMPP)­MnIIICl (where TMPPH2 = 5,10,15,20-tetrakis-4-methoxyphenylporphyrin) does not show the formation of (TMPP)­MnNO through the transfer of the NO– ligand, as probed by FTIR analysis. Moreover, a reaction of 3-Qu with a proton source, such as p-toluenesulfonic acid PTSA, does not produce N2O, thereby ruling out the formation of HNO through the protonation of the NO– ligand in 3-Qu (Figure S48B). Notably, protonation of some of the previously reported {MnNO}6, {FeNO}7, and {CoNO}8/9 complexes has been illustrated to provide HNO depending on their electronic properties. ,− Interestingly, an equimolar reaction of 2,6-dichloronitrosoarene (Cl2ArNO) with [(Me2PzQu)Ni­(NO)­(ClO4)] (3-Qu) in acetonitrile provides the [NiII]-diazeniumdiolate complex [(Me2PzQu)2NiII(O2N2 Cl2Ar)]­(ClO4) (5-Qu) in 49% yield. X-ray diffraction analysis of 5-Qu confirms a κ2-O,O binding mode of the diazeniumdiolate anion (Cl2ArN2O2 –) at an octahedral nickel­(II) site (Figures , S49, and S50). The formation of the diazeniumdiolate anion (Cl2ArN2O2 –) from the reaction of NO gas with [NiII]­(Cl2ArNO•–) or through the treatment of the {NiNO}10 core with ArNO has been known in the literature. , Nonetheless, the transformation of 3-Qu to 5-Qu in the presence of ArNO is particularly interesting in the context of NO/NO– transfer ability of 3-Qu. Mechanistically, a nucleophilic attack of the NO– ligand on the electrophilic site of Cl2ArNO could conceptually generate Cl2ArN2O2 –. However, the poor nucleophilicity of the coordinated NO– in the {NiNO}10 core renders this pathway unlikely. Alternatively, we propose that the interaction of Cl2ArNO with the [NiII]­(NO–) core in 3-Qu leads to the in situ generation of [NiII]­(Cl2ArNO•–) and NO gas, which rapidly undergoes ligand-centered radical coupling to provide 5-Qu. Thus, these results suggest that the {NiNO}10 core in 3-Qu functions more as a NO donor rather than a NO– donor.

Oxygenation of Nitrosyl and Nitrite at Nickel­(II)

Exposure of an acetonitrile solution containing [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(ClO4)] (3-Qu) and free ligand (1.0 equiv) to dioxygen results in the nitrite complex 1-Qu in 55% yield (Figure ). FTIR analysis on the end product shows a complete disappearance of ν­(NO) at 1768 cm–1 and development of 1213 cm–1 due to the formation of 1-Qu (Figure S51). While a previous report demonstrates the conversion of tetrahedral [(L′)­Ni­(NO)­I] (L’ = bis(3,5-diisopropyl-1-pyrazolyl)­methane) to [(L′)2Ni­(nitrite)]­(I3), it is unclear whether such a reaction of {NiNO}10 with dioxygen involves a peroxynitrite (ONOO–) intermediate. Notably, the overall transformation of {NiNO}10 3-Qu to nickel­(II)–nitrite 1-Qu may proceed via an outer-sphere pathway. In this scenario, the hemilability of the nitrosyl ligand in 3-Qu leads to the initial release of free NO in the reaction medium. The liberated NO can then rapidly react with dioxygen to generate NO2 in situ. Subsequent capture of NO2 at the nickel­(I) center would furnish the nickel­(II)–nitrite 1-Qu (Figure A). Assessing the lability of the nitrosyl ligand in 3-Qu, a diffusion-controlled NO gas trapping experiment with 3-Qu shows 17% yield of free NO gas in the headspace of an acetonitrile solution of 3-Qu after 12 h. The low yield of free NO gas even after 12 h suggests extremely slow release of NO gas from 3-Qu in comparison to the observed rapid dioxygen reactivity of 3-Qu. To probe the possible involvement of NO2/ONOO– during the transformation of 3-Qu to 1-Qu, the reaction of 3-Qu with dioxygen was performed in the presence of 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol (2,4-DTBP), a classic trap for NO2/ONOO– species. − GCMS and NMR studies on the crude reaction mixture do not show the formation of 6-nitro-2,4-di-tert-butylphenol (Figure S52), thereby disfavoring the intermediacy of NO2/ONOO–. Alternatively, in an inner-sphere mechanism, we propose that dioxygen interacts with two equivalents of 3-Qu through a labile [Ni]–OClO3 site prior to the incorporation of one O-atom per {NiNO}10 moiety (Figures A and B). This mechanism is consistent with the previously proposed monooxygenation pathway of {CoNO}8 in the presence of dioxygen. DFT-based computational investigations were performed to explore the possible reaction pathways following the initial interaction of dioxygen with the [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(ClO4)] core (Figure S53). Though dioxygen typically reacts with nickel­(I) complexes to afford peroxo or superoxo species, , we propose that the presence of redox-active nitrosyl ligand in the [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(ClO4)] core may render the nickel­(II) center reactive toward dioxygen, owing to its multiconfigurational electronic structure. It is noteworthy that the dioxygen adducts, namely, the bimetallic adduct [{(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)}2(μ-O2)]2+ (Int-1) and the monometallic adduct [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(NO)­(O2)]+ (Int-1′), are estimated to have Gibbs free energies of 24.4 and 18.0 kcal/mol, respectively (Figures B and S53). Moreover, the possibility of stepwise formation of Int-1 through Int-1′ could not be excluded. Alternatively, the monometallic species Int-1′ could also proceed through the commonly proposed peroxynitrite species [(Me2PzQu)­Ni­(κ2-OONO)]+ (Int-2′). However, we could not intercept such an intermediate using 2,4-DTBP. On the other hand, the bimetallic route is proposed to proceed through a peroxide bond cleavage to form Int-2. Importantly, the formation of Int-2 is 34.1 kcal/mol more favorable over Int-2’. This significant thermodynamic preference supports the pathway through the bimetallic intermediate as being more favorable than the route that solely proceeds through monometallic intermediates (via Int-1′ and Int-2′). Further progression of the bimetallic pathway (via Int-1 and Int-2) involves a change in the nitrite binding mode and coordination of another equivalent of Me2PzQu ligand, leading to the formation of the observed nitrite product 1-Qu, which is stabilized by −54.6 kcal/mol. In contrast, the peroxynitrite intermediate Int-2′ is calculated to produce the corresponding nitrate complex 2-Qu, through isomerization of the peroxynitrite anion and coordination of an additional equivalent of the Me2PzQu ligand, with a stabilization energy of −37.3 kcal/mol (Figure S53). It is noteworthy that the formation of the nitrate complex 2-Qu is not observed for the reaction of dioxygen with 3-Qu. Mechanistically, for the dioxygen-mediated transformation 3-Qu to 1-Qu, though the viability of the outer-sphere mechanism cannot be ruled out completely (Figure A), the incorporation of a single O-atom from O2 in 3-Qu presumably proceeds through the thermodynamically controlled inner-sphere route involving the dinickel intermediate Int-1.

To further support the proposed hypothesis on single O-atom incorporation, we investigated the reactivity of both the {NiNO}10 core in 3-Qu and nickel­(II)–nitrite complexes (1-Qu/1-Py) with a single O atom donor reagent, namely, iodosylbenzene (PhIO). The reactions of PhIO (3.0 equiv) and 3-Qu/3-Qu-15N in the presence of a free ligand (1.0 equiv) lead to a rapid bleaching of the blue color. FTIR analyses on the end products show the evolution of new 14/15N-isotope-sensitive features at 1486, 1382, 1274, and 1210 cm–1 (for 3-Qu + PhIO) and 1451, 1351, 1259, and 1190 cm–1 (for 3-Qu-15N + PhIO) at the expense of the respective ν­(NO) band of {NiNO}10 (Figures S54 and S55). The vibrational bands at 1210/1190 cm–1 indicate the formation of 1-Qu/1-Qu-15N from the reactions of PhIO, and another set of 14/15N-sensitive bands at 1486/1451, 1382/1351, and 1274/1259 cm–1 are attributed to the formation of [NiII]–nitrate complexes 2-Qu/2-Qu-15N. This finding indicates that nickel­(II)–nitrite is also reactive toward PhIO. A treatment of 1-Qu with PhIO in acetonitrile at room temperature indeed affords 2-Qu as evaluated by the distinct changes in the vibrational bands (Figures , S56, and S57). Similarly, 1-Py also reacts with PhIO to provide 2-Py, as analyzed by comparing the vibrational features of the structurally characterized authentic sample of 2-Py. In contrast, a control reaction between NaNO2 and PhIO under the analogous reaction conditions does not provide NaNO3 (Figures S58 and S59). This perhaps implies a possible participation of the high-valent monometallic nickel–oxygen species in the oxidative transformation of NO x in 1-Qu and 3-Qu. It is noteworthy that the distribution of the nitrite/nitrate product from the reactions of 3-Qu with dioxygen versus PhIO is distinct. This difference is attributed to the nature of the proposed oxidants involved. The dioxygen reaction proceeds via a relatively less reactive bimetallic bis-μ-oxo species, whereas the PhIO reaction involves a more reactive monometallic oxidant. These findings represent the first example of sequential NO x oxidation via oxygen-atom-transfer (OAT) at nickel­(II), whereas the O-atom removal-based reduction of nitrate-to-NO has been illustrated earlier. − ,

Conclusion

In summary, this work illustrates 2e– redox transformations of NO x utilizing thoroughly characterized (X-ray, UV–vis, 14N/15N-FTIR) [NiII]–nitrite/nitrate (1-Qu/1-Py, 2-Qu/2-Py) and nickel–nitrosyl {NiNO}10 (3-Qu) complexes. A detailed study on the thiol reactions of the nickel­(II)–nitrite complexes (1-Qu/1-Py) reveals that the subtle variations in the ligand donor, quinoline versus pyridine, significantly influence the proton transfer from thiol to nitrite at nickel­(II). 15N NMR and UV–vis spectroscopic studies provide evidence for the formation of HNO2 and nickel­(II)–thiolate as the transient intermediates. In addition, this study introduces a unique strategy that exploits the deoxygenation capability of Ph3P to intercept the in situ-generated HNO2. These findings highlight the potential of phosphines in probing reactive species such as HNO2 during the various reactions of nitrogen oxides. Subsequent mechanistic investigations, examining the reactions of the nickel­(II)–perchlorate complex (4-Qu) with S-nitrosothiol/NO gas in the presence of thiol leading to the independent generation of 3-Qu, suggest an NO-mediated reductive nitrosylation pathway during the transformation nickel­(II)–nitrite to {NiNO}10 Interestingly, this study outlines that H2S gas also reacts with the nickel­(II)–nitrite complex, analogous to thiol, to afford a similar {NiNO}10 species, thereby revealing that the interplay between H2S and nitrite is linked to the reactivity landscape metal–nitrosyl.

A combination of spectroscopic, X-ray crystallographic, X-ray photoelectron, and computational studies on {NiNO}10 depicts that the ground-state electronic configuration of such a core is best described as an antiferromagnetically coupled nickel­(II) coordinated to high-spin NO–. However, the multiconfigurational nature of the {NiNO}10 core gives rise to an interesting reactivity profile that is more consistent as an NO donor than an NO– donor. In addition, sequential monooxygenations of the nitrosyl ligand in 3-Qu leading to nitrate in the presence of a single oxygen-atom donor reagent PhIO have been outlined. These findings demonstrate that the consecutive O-atom transfer (OAT)-based oxidation of nitrogen oxides can facilitate the stepwise transformation of nitrosyl → nitrite → nitrate at transition metal sites, thereby providing an alternative and complementary route to peroxynitrite-dependent oxidation. More importantly, the OAT-based oxidation of relatively unreactive nitrite resulting in nitrate also validates the previously proposed hypothesis that Mo-based cofactors mediate the interconversion of nitrite and nitrate (NO2 – + O-atom ⇄ NO3 –) during the catalytic cycles of nitrite oxidoreductase (NXR) and nitrate reductase (NAR) activities. Lastly, this study depicts that a common nickel­(II) platform can mediate both reductive and oxidative transformations of nitrite. The insights gained herein not only unfold new frontiers in the redox chemistry of nitrogen oxides relevant to NO-homeostasis but also guide the use of phosphine-based assays in detection of various reactive nitrogen-oxide-based species such as HNO2 and HNO. Ongoing research in our laboratory focuses on developing mechanism-driven strategies for the quantification of reactive nitrogen oxides in the presence of phosphines.
