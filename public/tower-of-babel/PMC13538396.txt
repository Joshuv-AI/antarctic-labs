Physics-guided cross-domain adaptation: a hierarchical hybrid transformer framework with contrastive learning for robust fault diagnosis under variable working conditions
Authors: Lumin Liu, Chen Hao, Lin Xiong, Xiang Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe domain shift problem induced by variable working conditions severely constrains the cross-domain generalization capability of data-driven fault diagnosis models. Existing methods lack collaborative modeling of the multi-scale time-frequency characteristics of vibration signals at the feature extraction level. They also neglect the constraint guidance of fault physical mechanisms on the feature alignment process at the domain adaptation level. To address these deficiencies, this paper proposes a physics-guided cross-domain adaptation framework—a Hierarchical Hybrid Transformer network with Contrastive Learning (PgHHT-CL). The framework comprises three key designs. At the feature encoding level, a hierarchical hybrid Transformer encoder is constructed. It achieves collaborative extraction of transient impulse components and periodic modulation components in vibration signals through gated interactive fusion of local convolutional branches and global self-attention branches across multiple abstraction levels. At the domain adaptation level, a physics-guided cross-domain contrastive learning strategy leverages the order-invariance relationship between fault characteristic frequencies and rotational frequency from bearing dynamics prior knowledge to constrain the construction of cross-domain positive and negative sample pairs. The feature alignment process is thereby required to satisfy physical consistency beyond statistical distribution matching. At the training optimization level, a joint optimization objective integrates classification loss, cross-domain contrastive loss, and physical consistency loss, with a progressive weight adjustment strategy to ensure stable convergence of multi-task learning. Extensive cross-condition transfer experiments on two public bearing datasets from Case Western Reserve University and Paderborn University show that PgHHT-CL achieves average diagnostic accuracies of 94.94 ± 0.32% and 90.26 ± 0.50%, respectively, attaining the highest mean accuracy across all 18 transfer tasks among the representative state-of-the-art baselines selected for comparison. The framework also exhibits notable robustness under large domain shift and strong noise conditions. Ablation experiments and feature visualization analyses further validate the effectiveness and physical interpretability of the physics-guided strategy and hierarchical hybrid architecture.Keywords: Fault diagnosis, Variable working conditions, Physics-guided contrastive learning, Hierarchical hybrid transformer, Cross-domain adaptationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Rotating machinery, as an indispensable power transmission device in modern industrial production, is widely employed in critical domains such as aerospace, rail transportation, and wind power generation. Core components including rolling bearings and gearboxes are prone to developing fatigue cracks, surface spalling, and other faults under the coupled effects of prolonged high-speed operation and alternating loads. Once failure occurs, it threatens the safe operation of the entire unit and may even lead to severe economic losses and casualties. Frequent changes in operating parameters constitute a prominent characteristic of actual industrial scenarios—fluctuations in rotational speed, load switching, and environmental temperature drift often cause the acquired vibration signals to exhibit strong non-stationary characteristics, resulting in significant differences in the time–frequency distribution of the same fault type under different operating conditions. This domain shift problem induced by operating condition drift severely constrains the generalization performance of traditional data-driven diagnostic models and constitutes the core obstacle to the practical engineering application of intelligent fault diagnosis.

In recent years, transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques have provided feasible solution paths for the aforementioned problem. Unsupervised domain adaptation methods based on adversarial training or distribution alignment strategies have been demonstrated to effectively reduce the marginal distribution discrepancy between source and target domains, achieving remarkable accuracy improvements in cross-condition diagnostic tasks1. To address the more stringent industrial constraint where target domain data is completely unavailable during the training phase, domain generalization techniques have emerged, dedicated to extracting domain-invariant features from multi-source domain data with the aim of maintaining robust diagnostic capability when facing previously unseen working conditions2. At the feature extraction architecture level, Transformer, leveraging the advantages of self-attention mechanisms in long-range dependency modeling, has been introduced into the fault diagnosis field, demonstrating stronger global temporal correlation capture capability compared to convolutional neural networks3. Concurrently, the contrastive learning paradigm has attracted widespread attention from researchers—by pulling together same-class samples and pushing apart different-class samples to enhance inter-class separability in the feature space, it can effectively suppress class boundary confusion during cross-domain transfer4,7. Schemes integrating contrastive learning strategies within domain generalization frameworks have further enhanced the discriminability of feature representations on unknown domains5, while style generation and adversarial contrastive networks designed for single-source domain scenarios have also demonstrated commendable generalization potential6. It is noteworthy that hybrid architectures combining Transformer with domain adaptation strategies have been validated to simultaneously leverage the dual advantages of global feature modeling and distribution alignment4,7. Despite significant breakthroughs achieved by existing works in cross-domain diagnostic accuracy, two critical bottlenecks remain: first, existing methods predominantly learn domain-invariant features in a purely data-driven manner, neglecting the inherent physical mechanism constraints underlying vibration signals, resulting in extracted features that lack physical interpretability and are prone to failure under extreme operating condition shift scenarios; second, simple serial or parallel architectures of Transformer and CNN struggle to achieve collaborative modeling of local transient impulse features and global periodic modulation features, and the hierarchical interaction mechanisms for multi-scale feature fusion have not been sufficiently explored.

Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes a physics-guided cross-domain adaptation framework—a Physics-guided Hierarchical Hybrid Transformer with Contrastive Learning (PgHHT-CL)—for robust fault diagnosis under variable working conditions. The overall research framework of this paper is illustrated in Fig. 1. The core innovations of this framework are manifested at three levels: at the feature encoding level, a hierarchical hybrid Transformer structure is designed, which deeply couples local convolutional branches and global self-attention branches in a hierarchical interactive manner, enabling the network to simultaneously capture transient impulse components and periodic modulation components of vibration signals at different abstraction levels; at the domain adaptation level, a physics-guided cross-domain contrastive learning strategy is constructed, utilizing bearing dynamics prior knowledge (such as the associative relationship between fault characteristic frequencies and rotational frequency) as construction constraints for contrastive positive and negative sample pairs, guiding the network to preserve physically consistent fault discriminative features while reducing inter-domain distribution discrepancies; at the training optimization level, a joint loss function is proposed, which co-optimizes physical constraint loss, cross-domain contrastive loss, and classification loss through adaptive weighting, ensuring the model maintains stable feature extraction and fault discrimination capability when facing large-scale operating condition drift.

Related work

Fault diagnosis methods under variable working conditions

Fault diagnosis under variable working conditions requires models to possess the capability of extracting robust fault features from non-stationary vibration signals. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), as the foundational architecture in this field, have demonstrated excellent performance in feature extraction under single working conditions through local receptive fields and parameter sharing mechanisms. Facing the challenges of multiple noise environments and load fluctuations, multi-branch multi-scale CNNs have been designed to extract spatial features at different scales in parallel from wheelset bearing vibration signals, thereby enhancing the model’s adaptability to load variations8. Under the guidance of attention mechanisms, multi-scale residual attention convolutional networks have further strengthened the selective focusing capability on critical frequency band information, maintaining high stability of diagnostic results under variable load conditions9. Lightweight networks embedded with quadratic attention mechanisms have achieved efficient capture of bearing fault features while preserving interpretability10.

Following the introduction of self-attention mechanisms into fault diagnosis, Transformer structures have opened new technical pathways by virtue of their inherent advantages in global dependency modeling. Improved Transformers have extracted local and global features of vibration data through multi-feature parallel fusion encoders, surpassing traditional CNN and RNN methods in both diagnostic accuracy and robustness11. Interpretable neural networks embedded with time–frequency transforms directly accomplish end-to-end mapping from signals to time–frequency representations at the network front end, effectively overcoming the dependence of manual feature engineering on prior knowledge12. Although the aforementioned methods have achieved notable success in variable working condition diagnosis within a single domain, when training data and test data originate from different operating condition domains with substantial distribution discrepancies, the generalization performance of models tends to degrade sharply.

Cross-domain adaptation and domain generalization

The core idea of cross-domain adaptation is to reduce the distribution gap between source and target domains through distribution alignment or adversarial training. Multi-source domain adversarial discrepancy matching networks leverage complementary information from multiple source domains and dynamically calibrate the contribution weights of each source domain through adversarial discrepancy measures, achieving favorable results in multi-condition transfer diagnosis13. Relationship transfer domain generalization networks approach from the perspective of inter-domain relationship modeling, guiding networks to learn cross-condition domain-invariant representations through feature-level relational consistency constraints14. Deep imbalanced domain adaptation frameworks address the class imbalance phenomenon in actual working conditions by adjusting the weight allocation of each class during the domain adaptation process to improve diagnostic performance for minority-class faults15. Under the practical constraint where target domain data is completely unavailable, domain generalization techniques become a more engineering-feasible solution. Generalization networks equipped with multi-domain specific auxiliary classifiers significantly enhance adaptability to unknown working conditions by enforcing disentangled representations of domain-invariant and domain-specific features16. Gradient-aligned domain generalization methods maintain gradient direction consistency during multi-source domain training through a teacher-student mutual teaching strategy, effectively avoiding conflicts between optimization objectives across different domains17. Adversarial mutual information-guided single-source domain generalization networks, under the extreme circumstance where only single working condition data is available, eliminate domain-specific components by minimizing the mutual information between features and domain labels18. Cross-domain augmentation diagnosis strategies construct virtual augmented domains using convex combinations, expanding the coverage of feature distributions in conjunction with adversarial training2. The limitation of these methods lies in: the distribution alignment process primarily focuses on matching global statistics, which tends to induce misclassification near class boundaries.

Applications of contrastive learning in fault diagnosis

Contrastive learning optimizes the geometric structure of the feature space by constructing positive and negative sample pairs, causing same-class samples to cluster together and different-class samples to separate from each other in the embedding space, fundamentally enhancing the clarity of classification boundaries. Graph contrastive learning frameworks designed for semi-supervised scenarios model vibration signals as graph-structured data, capturing the intrinsic topological relationships of equipment operating states during unsupervised contrastive pre-training, and then fine-tuning with a small number of labeled samples, effectively reducing dependence on labeled data19. Contrastive learning methods integrating self-attention mechanisms introduce attention weight redistribution in the feature projection space, enhancing the model’s focus on fault-sensitive frequency bands20. In complex scenarios where variable speed and imbalanced samples coexist, multi-scale dynamic supervised contrastive learning adaptively adjusts the contrastive loss weights at each scale to simultaneously accommodate the multi-granularity completeness of feature representations and inter-class separability21,31. Contrastive learning also plays a critical role in cross-domain diagnosis—open-set domain adaptation schemes based on supervised contrastive learning and dual adversarial networks maintain high recognition rates for known classes even when unknown fault categories exist in the target domain22. Domain adaptation networks incorporating contrastive learning enlarge inter-class feature distances through global contrastive loss, reducing misclassification probability at distribution boundaries4,7. It should be noted that existing contrastive sample pair construction strategies mostly rely on random data augmentation or domain label information, lacking constraint guidance from the physical mechanism level.

Physics-guided deep learning methods

Although purely data-driven deep learning methods perform excellently with abundant labeled data, their "black-box" nature causes diagnostic results to lack physical interpretability, and they are prone to generating predictions that violate physical common sense when training data is insufficient or operating condition shift is severe. Digital twin technology provides an effective pathway for integrating physical knowledge with data-driven approaches—digital twin frameworks based on gear surface degradation physical models can generate simulation data highly consistent with real working conditions, thereby expanding the distribution coverage of training sets under data-scarce conditions23. Causality-inspired multi-source domain generalization methods approach from the perspective of causal reasoning, extracting physically meaningful invariant features by identifying the causal mechanisms of fault occurrence rather than statistical correlations, demonstrating superior generalization capability compared to traditional statistical alignment methods under unknown working conditions24. Transformer diagnostic frameworks based on attention uncertainty embed Bayesian inference into self-attention layers to quantify prediction uncertainty, enabling the model to provide credibility assessments alongside diagnostic conclusions25. Class-aware adversarial multi-wavelet convolutional networks integrate the frequency band decomposition priors of wavelet analysis into the feature extraction process, leveraging the physical properties of multi-resolution analysis to enhance the discrimination capability for fault components at different frequencies26. Fourier transform-enhanced Transformers improve sensitivity to periodic fault patterns by embedding spectral features during the encoding phase, with diagnostic robustness under strong noise environments being notably improved27. Overall, the integration of physical knowledge in fault diagnosis is still in its nascent stage, with most works remaining at the level of physics-model-assisted data generation or incorporating physical features as auxiliary inputs. How to deeply embed physical constraints into the training objectives and feature learning process of networks—particularly in combination with contrastive learning and cross-domain adaptation—remains a research direction urgently requiring breakthroughs.

Methodology

This chapter provides a detailed exposition of the proposed Physics-guided Hierarchical Hybrid Transformer with Contrastive Learning framework (PgHHT-CL). Physics-guided cross-domain contrastive learning strategy

Classical contrastive learning frameworks construct positive sample pairs through random data augmentation. Although this approach is simple and effective, it cannot guarantee that the augmented samples retain the core information of fault physical features21,31. To address this problem, this paper proposes a physics-guided positive and negative sample pair construction mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Schematic diagram of the physics-guided cross-domain contrastive learning strategy.

For each anchor sample in the source domain, the theoretical order feature is calculated based on the rotational speed information of its corresponding working condition. In the target domain, samples with identical order features and consistent fault labels (estimated through pseudo-labels) are selected as positive samples, while samples with different order features or inconsistent pseudo-labels are designated as negative samples. Unlike standard supervised contrastive loss, the positive sample set P(i) in this formulation simultaneously encompasses samples from both the source and target domains, and its construction follows the physical order consistency principle rather than relying solely on domain labels32. This mechanism ensures that the domain-invariant features learned by the network are not only aligned in a statistical sense but also maintain the consistency of fault mechanisms at the physical level.

Notably, the acquisition of the order feature for each target-domain sample is decoupled from any fault label of the target domain, as the target-domain labels are unavailable during the training phase. Concretely, the rotational frequency is read directly from the operating-condition metadata recorded by the speed encoder during data acquisition; this constitutes condition-level information rather than sample-level supervision. The dominant fault-related order is then obtained from the envelope spectrum of the vibration signal via an order-tracking analysis. The envelope spectrum is computed through Hilbert demodulation, after which the most energetic spectral peak in the order domain is identified as the dominant order. The entire procedure operates strictly at the signal-processing level and does not access any target-domain fault label.

Building upon the order features obtained above, this framework adopts a dual-layer judgment criterion for positive sample pair construction rather than relying on order consistency alone. The physical rationale is that different damage severities at the same fault location (e.g., 0.007, 0.014, and 0.021 inch inner race faults) share identical theoretical order features, so order consistency alone would force these severity-distinct categories to be grouped as positive pairs and thereby compromise fine-grained classification. To resolve this issue, the order feature is positioned as a coarse-grained physical anchor that distinguishes fault locations (inner race / outer race / rolling element), while the target-domain pseudo-label generated by the class prototype strategy serves as a fine-grained discriminator distinguishing severity levels. For a candidate target-domain sample to be selected as a positive partner of a source-domain anchor, three conditions must hold simultaneously: (1) order feature consistency, with the order-difference threshold = 0.05 accommodating measurement-induced order drift caused by speed jitter; (2) pseudo-label consistency between the candidate and the anchor; and (3) pseudo-label confidence exceeding a preset threshold = 0.8 to suppress noisy pseudo-labels.

These two layers carry orthogonal rather than redundant discriminative information: order features encode the physical mechanism of fault excitation but remain invariant to damage severity, whereas pseudo-labels encode data-driven severity classification but may suffer from cross-domain noise. Their combination compensates for each individual limitation. The order constraint anchors the contrastive learning process on physically meaningful fault categories during the early training stage, in which pseudo-labels are still noisy; the pseudo-label filter, in turn, ensures that samples sharing the same fault location but differing in severity are not erroneously paired together. The dual-layer design therefore directly eliminates the potential class-confusion risk that would arise from order-only pairing. Representative judgment outcomes are summarized in Table 2.

The generation of target domain pseudo-labels adopts a class prototype-based strategy34—at the end of each training epoch, the current encoder is used to extract features from target domain samples and compute the prototype center of each class, after which pseudo-labels are assigned to target domain samples based on their distances to each prototype center. To suppress the negative effects of noisy pseudo-labels, only high-confidence samples exceeding a preset confidence threshold are retained for participation in contrastive learning. As training progresses, the quality of encoder feature representations continuously improves, and pseudo-label accuracy improves accordingly, forming a virtuous cycle.

During the inference phase, target domain vibration signals are directly input into the trained HHTE encoder and classification head to complete fault category determination, without requiring additional target domain adaptation steps. Experiments and results analysis

Dataset description and experimental setup

This paper selects two publicly available rolling bearing fault datasets to validate the effectiveness of the proposed PgHHT-CL framework. Dataset A is the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) bearing dataset, with a sampling frequency of 12 kHz, covering the normal state and three fault types including inner race fault, outer race fault, and rolling element fault. Each fault type encompasses three damage diameters of 0.007 inches, 0.014 inches, and 0.021 inches, with motor rotational speeds set at four working conditions of 1730 rpm, 1750 rpm, 1772 rpm, and 1797 rpm, totaling 10 health states. Dataset B is the Paderborn University (PU) bearing dataset, which collects vibration signals through a modular test rig under combinations of different rotational speeds (900 rpm, 1200 rpm, 1500 rpm) and different radial forces (400 N, 700 N, 1000 N), containing real fault samples generated by both artificial damage and accelerated lifetime tests, with signal complexity more closely approximating industrial reality. To rigorously prevent any information leakage between the training and testing splits, this paper adopts a signal-level partitioning protocol rather than a sample-level random split. For every combination of working condition and fault category, the original continuous vibration signal is temporally divided into three mutually disjoint segments according to a 7:1:2 ratio for training, validation, and testing, respectively. Sliding-window segmentation is subsequently applied independently within each segment. The window length is set to 1024 samples for the CWRU dataset and 4096 samples for the PU dataset, consistent with the sample lengths reported in Table 3. Within the training segment, a 50% overlap (stride equal to half the window length) is adopted to obtain sufficient training samples, whereas the validation and testing segments use a non-overlapping stride (stride equal to the window length) to ensure mutual independence among evaluation samples. Because the training, validation, and testing segments occupy temporally non-overlapping intervals of the same raw signal, two windows segmented from any identical signal region cannot simultaneously appear in the training and testing sets, which fundamentally eliminates the risk of data leakage. The full preprocessing pipeline, encompassing signal-level partitioning, sliding-window segmentation, amplitude normalization, and order-feature extraction modules, has been encapsulated as a reproducible Python toolkit that will be released alongside publication for community verification.

The experimental platform was built on the PyTorch 1.13 framework, and all models were trained on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. The initial learning rate of the Adam optimizer was set to 1 × 10−4, the batch size was 64, and the total number of training epochs was 150. The temperature hyperparameter τ was set to 0.07, the loss weight linearly increased from 0 to 1.0 during the first 30 epochs, and was fixed at 0.5. Cross-domain diagnostic tasks were constructed as follows: one working condition was selected as the source domain for model training, and the remaining working conditions were used as target domains for testing, respectively. Each experiment was repeated 5 times, and the mean and standard deviation were reported as the final results.

All comparison methods exhibited noticeable performance degradation on tasks such as W0 → W2 that involve both cross-condition and cross-load transfer, while PgHHT-CL still maintained an accuracy level above 88%. To quantify the contribution of each core module to overall performance, this paper designs six groups of ablation experiment variants: (1) removing the physics-guided module and using standard random augmentation to construct contrastive sample pairs (w/o Phy); (2) replacing the hierarchical hybrid Transformer with a standard ViT encoder (w/o HHTE); (3) removing the gated feature interaction unit and using simple feature concatenation instead (w/o GFIU); (4) removing the physical consistency loss (w/o ); (5) removing the cross-domain contrastive loss and retaining only the classification loss and physical consistency loss (w/o ); and (6) retaining only the order consistency criterion while disabling the pseudo-label gating module in the dual-layer positive-pair judgment, so that all target-domain samples with matching order features are treated as positives regardless of their pseudo-label (w/o Pseudo-label gating). This empirical observation directly validates the necessity of the dual-layer judgment mechanism analyzed in “Physics-guided cross-domain contrastive learning strategy” section: when only order consistency is enforced, target-domain samples that share the same fault location but possess different damage severities are erroneously pulled together in the feature space, producing the predicted class-confusion failure mode. The accompanying increase in standard deviation (from 0.32 for the full model to 0.61 for this variant) further indicates that the order-only configuration is markedly less stable across repeated runs, consistent with the noisier feature geometry induced by such confusion.

To evaluate the constraining effect of the physics-guided strategy on the physical consistency of features, this paper statistically analyzes the correspondence between features extracted by each method and theoretical fault characteristic frequencies in the frequency domain. The specific approach is: the high-attention regions of the HHTE encoder in the frequency domain are identified through the Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) method, and the overlap between these regions and theoretical fault order frequencies is computed, as shown in Fig. 10.

Analysis of Grad-CAM frequency domain attention regions aligned with theoretical fault order frequencies.

The implementation of the gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) based frequency-domain interpretability analysis is detailed below to ensure full reproducibility and methodological fairness across compared methods. Step 1 (Target layer and gradient computation): the analysis targets the feature map produced by the deepest HEB convolutional output, denoted as , where C is the channel dimension and T is the temporal length after downsampling. For a given target class c predicted by the classifier with pre-softmax logit , the channel-wise importance weight is computed as the temporal mean of the gradient of with respect to the feature map. Step 2 (Time-domain class activation map): the class activation map in the time domain is obtained by aggregating feature channels weighted by the gradient coefficients and rectifying with ReLU. Step 3 (Frequency-domain projection): the time-domain activation is projected to the frequency domain via a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) using a Hamming window of length 256 samples with 50% overlap, yielding a time–frequency activation H(f,t). Averaging H(f,t) along the time axis produces a one-dimensional frequency-domain attention distribution H(f), which is subsequently normalized to such that the integral over the half-sampling-frequency range equals 1. Step 4 (Order-frequency overlap ratio): the theoretical fault-related characteristic frequencies are derived from bearing dynamics formulas, encompassing the fundamental frequencies (BPFO, BPFI, BSF) together with their second-order harmonics, yielding M = 6 theoretical frequencies per fault category. A frequency band is constructed around each theoretical frequency, with the bandwidth Δ f = 0.02 , i.e., ± 2% of the nominal characteristic frequency. The choice of ± 2% bandwidth is determined by the upper bound of rotational-speed jitter typically observed in bearing test rigs, which causes the actual fault-related spectral peaks to deviate from their nominal positions; this setting is consistent with the order-tracking bandwidth convention adopted in28. The order-frequency overlap ratio (OR) is then defined as the ratio of the integral of over the union of all order bands to the integral over the full half-band, expressed as a percentage. To guarantee a strictly fair comparison, the identical four-step procedure—including the same target feature-map layer (the last representation layer immediately preceding the classification head), the same STFT window length and overlap, the same frequency bandwidth Δ f, and the same set of theoretical fault frequencies derived per category—is applied to all compared methods (DACL, the w/o Phy variant, and PgHHT-CL). For methods whose deepest representation is produced by an attention block rather than a convolutional block (e.g., DACL’s projection layer), the attention output is reshaped into an equivalent tensor form before Step 1, so that the gradient propagation path and the channel-wise weighting operation remain methodologically equivalent across architectures. No method-specific tuning of Δ f, STFT parameters, or target layer is performed.

PgHHT-CL achieves an average order frequency overlap of 87.3% across all fault types, significantly outperforming the w/o Phy variant (71.8%) and DACL (68.5%), confirming that the physics-guided strategy can effectively guide the network to focus on frequency domain components directly related to fault mechanisms.

Discussion and conclusion

Results discussion and method advantage analysis

From the experimental results in Chapter 4, PgHHT-CL achieves average cross-domain diagnostic accuracies of 94.94 ± 0.32% and 90.26 ± 0.50% on the CWRU and PU datasets, respectively, attaining the highest mean accuracy among the representative state-of-the-art baselines across all 18 transfer tasks while simultaneously exhibiting the smallest run-to-run standard deviations. This result validates from an overall perspective that the technical route combining the physics-guided contrastive learning strategy with the hierarchical hybrid Transformer architecture demonstrates consistent advantages over the compared baselines. To further clarify the contribution differences of each core design element under different scenarios, this paper groups the ablation experiment results by domain shift magnitude—tasks with a rotational speed difference of less than 50 rpm between source and target domains are classified as "small shift," while those exceeding 50 rpm are classified as “large shift”—and summarizes the performance gains of each module under both scenarios, as presented in Table 9.

The grouped analysis in Table 9 reveals two complementary patterns. The gain magnitudes of the physics-guided module and HHTE architecture under large shift scenarios are far higher than those under small shift scenarios—the gain of physics-guided contrastive sample construction increases from 1.67% in the small shift scenario to 4.38% in the large shift scenario, and the gain of HHTE increases from 2.54% to 4.63%, which is highly consistent with the experimental observations of large shift tasks such as A → D and C → D in Chapter 4. In contrast, the gain attributed to the pseudo-label gating layer of the dual-layer judgment mechanism remains consistently large across both small (5.80%) and large (7.35%) shift scenarios, with the largest overall average gain (6.44%) among all examined modules. This distinct pattern aligns with the physical rationale outlined in “Physics-guided cross-domain contrastive learning strategy” section: severity-level confusion among samples sharing the same fault location originates from the order-invariance property itself and is therefore present regardless of domain shift magnitude. The pseudo-label gating module compensates for this geometry-induced ambiguity and thus delivers stable gains across all transfer scenarios. The underlying logic of this phenomenon is: when the working condition difference is small, the data distributions of the source and target domains are inherently close, and even without imposing physical constraints, standard statistical alignment methods can achieve acceptable alignment results; however, when the magnitude of operating condition drift increases and the statistical characteristics of signals undergo drastic changes, purely data-driven alignment strategies are prone to misidentifying operating condition-related interference components as fault discriminative features. At this point, the value of the physical order invariance constraint becomes apparent—it anchors the network’s attention on frequency domain components at the fault mechanism level, thereby maintaining the physical consistency of feature representations even when the statistical distribution undergoes large-scale drift. The dual-layer judgment mechanism then refines this physically anchored alignment by introducing a pseudo-label layer that disentangles same-location severity categories, ensuring that the contrastive objective simultaneously preserves coarse-grained physical consistency and fine-grained classification granularity. The noise robustness experiment likewise corroborates this conclusion: under the extreme noise condition of − 6 dB, the accuracy of PgHHT-CL decreases by only 17.54 percentage points, while the DACL method without physics guidance decreases by 26.36 percentage points. The nearly 9 percentage point gap between the two amply demonstrates that physical prior knowledge provides the model with a robust discriminative pathway independent of signal statistical quality.

Limitations and future work

Although PgHHT-CL achieves satisfactory performance on the cross-condition fault diagnosis tasks addressed in this paper, its application boundary is still constrained by several factors that need to be addressed in subsequent research. In terms of physical prior knowledge acquisition, the bearing dynamics characteristic frequency formulas utilized in this paper depend on explicit geometric parameter information (number of rolling elements, pitch diameter, contact angle, etc.). In actual industrial scenarios, these parameters may drift due to wear after prolonged equipment operation, or the fault mechanisms of certain complex transmission systems may be difficult to precisely describe with analytical formulas, in which case the effectiveness of the physics-guided strategy will face challenges. In terms of target domain pseudo-label quality, although the class prototype-based pseudo-label generation strategy suppresses the influence of some noisy labels through the confidence thresholding mechanism, when fault types are highly imbalanced or new fault types not included in the source domain appear in the target domain, the accumulated errors in pseudo-labels may still mislead the contrastive learning process. In terms of computational efficiency, the three-level dual-branch structure of HHTE increases the parameter count by approximately 50% compared to standard CNNs, leaving room for optimization with respect to the real-time requirements of edge device deployment scenarios.

Addressing the aforementioned limitations, future research can be expanded in the following directions: extending the introduction of physical prior knowledge from hard constraints based on analytical formulas to soft constraints based on digital twin simulation data, reducing the dependence on precise geometric parameters through joint training of simulation and measured data; introducing open-set recognition mechanisms and incremental learning frameworks to address the emergence of new fault categories in the target domain, endowing the model with the capability for online continuous evolution; compressing HHTE into a lightweight version suitable for embedded platforms through knowledge distillation or structured pruning techniques, significantly reducing inference latency and storage footprint while preserving core diagnostic performance, thereby advancing this framework from laboratory validation toward practical engineering deployment.
