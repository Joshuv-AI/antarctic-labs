A predictive model for high-quality blastocyst formation using Day 3 morphological scores in women of advanced maternal age
Authors: Tingting Zheng, Ningjing Chen, Xiaofei Wang, Jianqiang Guo, Hong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop and validate a simple predictive model based on Day 3 embryo morphology to guide blastocyst culture strategy and optimize transfer outcomes for women of advanced maternal age (AMA).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed a total of 6840 cleavage-stage embryos from 1102 fresh oocyte retrieval cycles with subsequent blastocyst culture in AMA patients. Key parameters including the number of oocytes retrieved, Day 2 and Day 3 cell numbers, embryo fragmentation, embryo grade, and the number of high-quality Day 3 embryos were assessed. Their associations with the blastocyst formation rate (BR), high-quality blastocyst formation rate (HBR) and clinical outcomes were evaluated.ResultsLogistic regression identified the number of high-quality Day 3 embryos as a pivotal independent predictor. A threshold of ≥ 4 high-quality embryos was established. For cycles meeting this criterion, the risk of having no Day 5 transferable blastocyst was 19.30%. Within this group, Day 5 blastocyst transfer was associated with significantly higher clinical pregnancy and live birth rates, a lower multiple pregnancy rate, and improved neonatal outcomes (higher gestational age and birth weight) compared to Day 3 cleavage-stage transfer. The threshold showed good predictive performance in a temporally separated validation cohort (AUC 0.91).ConclusionIn AMA patients, a threshold of ≥ 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos may serve as a practical, low-cost criterion to guide blastocyst culture and elective single blastocyst transfer. The approach is associated with favorable clinical and neonatal outcomes but requires prospective validation in unselected populations.

Advanced maternal age (AMA) refers to women over 35 years with poor ovarian stimulation response, poor oocyte quality, fewer high-quality embryos, and higher embryo aneuploidy rates [1–3]. Optimizing transfer strategies to lower multiple pregnancy rate and improve maternal-infant health is a key focus in assisted reproductive technology [4, 5]. Identifying implantation-competent embryos is critical for women of AMA [6], yet more evidence is needed to determine which embryos to transfer, freeze or discard in fresh cycles. Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) can detect aneuploidy, increasing pregnancy and live birth rates while reducing miscarriage [7]. Conversely, long-term safety of cell biopsy remains unevaluated [8, 9], and animal studies suggest potential risks to neurological development, epigenetic modifications, and placental abnormalities [10, 11]. PGT-A also raises cycle cancellation rate in women of AMA [12], especially those with diminished ovarian reserve and few embryos, so the decision requires careful risk-benefit assessment [1, 13]. Time-lapse imaging (TLI) is a non-invasive platform for dynamic embryo assessment. Combined with artificial intelligence, TLI may reduce aneuploidy risk and improve implantation. Nevertheless, its application remains limited by potential light exposure [14], high cost, cumbersome labeling and one-step culture media [15]. In young women with good ovarian reserve, TLI offers no clear advantages over conventional morphological assessment for single-embryo transfer [16]. Non-invasive embryo screening via culture medium DNA analysis is still not ready for routine clinical use [17].

Traditionally, two Day 3 high-quality cleavage-stage embryos were typically transferred in AMA patients to ensure at least one transferable embryo while avoiding risks of extended culture [18]. Balancing blastocyst culture success, embryo availability, pregnancy rates, and multiple gestations remains challenging. Whether to culture all Day 3 embryos to blastocyst and then electively transfer a single blastocyst in women of AMA is debated [19]. This retrospective study analyzed 1102 blastocyst culture cycles from AMA women. To facilitate single blastocyst transfer, we developed a predictive model based on Day 3 morphological and embryo quality, assessed the likelihood of blastocyst formation, identified key developmental factors, and evaluated associations with clinical pregnancy outcomes. The goal was to optimize embryo selection and provide evidence-based guidance for individualized transfer strategies in women of AMA.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This retrospective study included patients who underwent fresh oocyte retrieval with continuous blastocyst culture at the reproductive center of Yichang Central People’s Hospital from January 1, 2014 to December 31, 2022. Inclusion criteria: first oocyte retrieval cycle with > 0 oocytes, ≤ 2 Day 3 embryos transferred or frozen, and remaining embryos cultured to blastocyst. Exclusion criteria: PGT cycles, the cycles with 0 oocyte retrieved, and cycles without blastocyst culture. The final dataset included 4164 eligible cycles (3162 conventional in vitro fertilization (IVF), 578 intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) and 424 early recovery ICSI. After excluding 3062 cycles from women aged ≤ 35 years, 1102 cycles from women of AMA (mean age 38.45 ± 2.40 years) with 6840 embryos were included for blastocyst culture. Among these, 242 cycles underwent culture of all Day 3 embryos (2006 embryos). This dataset served as the training set for predictive model construction. Of the 1102 AMA cycles, only 382 (34.7%) proceeded to fresh embryo transfer. The remaining cycles were canceled for clinical reasons: elevated progesterone on trigger day (16.68%), no oocytes or no transferable embryos (7.43%), OHSS prophylaxis (8.77%), ovulation induction protocols incompatible with fresh transfer (PPOS, mild stimulation, luteal-phase stimulation; 23.24%), and endometrial or uterine cavity issues (bleeding, hydrosalpinx, abnormal endometrium, polyps; 9.18%). Cycles with PGT were excluded to avoid confounding from embryo biopsy, genetic analysis, and the mandatory freeze-all strategy, which would introduce heterogeneity unrelated to our morphology-based model.

IVF procedure

Ovarian stimulation used short, long, or super-long protocols. When ≥ 3 follicles reached > 18 mm, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG, Ovitrelle; Merck Serono) was administered to induce final oocyte maturation, followed by transvaginal oocyte retrieval 35–36 h later. Fertilization was performed by conventional IVF or ICSI according to sperm parameters; early-rescue ICSI was applied based on IVF fertilization results. Pronuclei were assessed 18–20 h after insemination. Embryos were cultured in G1-plus medium (Vitrolife, Göteborg, Sweden) for the first three days. On Day 3, embryos were transferred to G2-plus medium (Vitrolife) and cultured at 37 °C, 6% CO₂ until Day 5, 6, or 7.

Embryo evaluation and morphological scoring

Cleavage-stage embryo score

Two experienced embryologists performed all optical assessments using an inverted microscope with Hoffman modulation contrast (200× and 400× magnification). Embryo evaluation was conducted at two time points: Day 2 (44–46 h post-insemination/ICSI), where cell number was recorded as < 4, 4, or > 4; and Day 3 (67–69 h post-insemination/ICSI), where morphological scoring based on blastomere number, cell regularity, fragmentation rate, and presence of multinucleation was performed using the Peter scoring system [20]. According to this system, Grade I embryos have homogeneous, transparent blastomeres with fragmentation < 5%; Grade II have slightly uneven blastomeres with fragmentation 5–20%; Grade III have obviously irregular blastomeres, cytoplasmic granules, and fragmentation 20–50%; Grade IV have obviously irregular blastomeres, cytoplasmic granules, and fragmentation > 50%. High-quality Day 3 embryos were defined as Grade I or II embryos that had reached at least the six-blastomere stage and had fragmentation < 20%.

Blastocyst score

Blastocyst culture was performed in microdroplets (30 µL, one embryo per droplet) after informed consent. Morphological assessment followed Gardner and Schoolcraft [21], based on blastocoel expansion (B1–B6) and quality of the inner cell mass (ICM) and trophectoderm (TE). High-quality blastocysts were defined as those with a score ≥3BB on Day 5 or Day 6 according to policy.

Analytical metrics

This study collected and analyzed the patients’ age, years of infertility, body mass index (BMI), basal FSH level, causes of infertility and types of infertility, HCG daily estradiol (E2) levels, fertilization methods, D1 pronuclear (PN), Day 3 cell number, embryo fragmentation rate, embryo symmetry, embryo grade, and the number of blastocysts formed.

Judgment standard and calculation method

The blastocyst formation rate (BR) was defined as the proportion of blastocysts observed at 116 ± 2 h post-insemination among normally fertilized oocytes. The high-quality blastocyst formation rate (HBR) was defined similarly using high-quality blastocysts (Istanbul consensus criteria). The day 5 high-quality blastocyst transfer rate was defined as the proportion of cycles with at least one high-quality blastocyst on Day 5 among cycles with at least one 2PN oocyte on Day 1, allowing inclusion of cycles where all embryos were cryopreserved [22]. This study focused on Day 5 high-quality blastocyst transfer rate, because Day 6 blastocysts have lower implantation potential [23]. The clinical pregnancy rate (CPR) was calculated as the number of cycles with at least one intrauterine gestational sac confirmed by ultrasound per 100 embryo transfer cycles. The live birth rate (LBR) was defined as the number of deliveries with at least one live-born infant (≥ 24 weeks) per 100 transfer cycles. The multiple pregnancy rate (MPR) was the proportion of multiple gestations among clinical pregnancies, and the miscarriage rate (MR) was the proportion of clinical pregnancies ending in spontaneous loss before 20 weeks of gestation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were analyzed using SPSS 21.0. The following parameters were compared between cycles with and without blastocyst formation or high-quality blastocyst formation: number of oocytes retrieved; Day 2 cell number (< 4, 4,>4); Day 3 cell number (≤ 6, 7–11, ≥ 12); Day 3 fragments (0–10%, 15–20%, ≥ 25%); Day 3 embryo grade (I, II, ≥III); and the number of Day 3 high-quality embryos (0–2, 3–6, ≥ 7). Univariate and multivariate regression analysis were used to assess associations between women of AMA’s morphological indicators and blastocyst outcomes. The multivariate model adjusted for the following covariates: number of oocytes retrieved (categorical: ≤2, 3–4, ≥ 5), Day 2 cell number (< 4, 4, > 4), Day 3 cell number (≤ 6, 7–11, ≥ 12), Day 3 fragmentation rate (0–10%, 15–20%, ≥ 25%), Day 3 embryo grade (I, II, ≥III), and the number of high-quality Day 3 embryos (0–2, 3–6, ≥ 7). Clinical factors such as ovarian stimulation protocol (short, long, or super-long), endometrial thickness and pattern on the day of trigger, serum progesterone level on trigger day, and BMI were not included in the multivariate model due to limited sample size and the retrospective design; these variables were only compared descriptively between groups. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was obtained for the number of Day 3 high-quality embryos to find a discrimination threshold level that could predict the risk of no Day 5 transferable blastocysts in this cycle. The 95% confidence interval for the area under the curve (AUC) was obtained using the Hanley-McNeil approach. Relationship between the number of high-quality Day 3 embryos and the cycle-level outcomes was plotted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall blastocyst culture and clinical outcomes in AMA women

A total of 1102 fresh ovulation retrieval cycles from women of AMA yielded 6840 Day 3 cleavage-stage embryos (including 4526 high-quality embryos) for blastocyst culture and 3726 blastocysts were formed, of which 2236 were high-quality blastocysts, with a BR of 54.47% and a HBR of 32.69%. The BRs were 6.96% for AA, 4.12% for BA, 7.11% for AB, 14.50% for BB, 8.39% for BC, 4.97% for CB, and 7.60% for CC. A total of 382 cycles underwent fresh embryo transfer, with an overall clinical pregnancy rate of 37.43% (143/382) and a live birth rate of 19.37% (74/382). Higher numbers of retrieved oocytes, Day 3 cells, and Day 3 high-quality embryos, along with lower fragmentation and better embryo grade, were significantly associated with increased BR and HBR (all P < 0.01, Table 1).

Logistic regression for univariate and multifactorial analysis of BR and HBR in AMA women

The number of oocytes retrieved, Day 2 cell number, Day 3 cell number, Day 3 embryo fragmentation, Day 3 embryo score, Day 3 high-quality embryos were statistically associated with BR and HBR (all P < 0.05) in single logistic regression and entered into the multivariate analysis. After adjustment in a multivariate logistic model, it was found that the number of Day 3 cells, embryo grade and the number of Day 3 superior embryos were all independent influences on blastocyst formation and high-quality blastocyst formation in AMA women (Table 2). Compared with women having ≥ 7 high-quality Day 3 embryos, those with ≤ 2 (OR 0.67 [0.54–0.83] for BR, P < 0.001; OR 0.63 [0.47–0.83] for HBR, P = 0.001) and those with 3–6 (OR 0.81 [0.72–0.92] for BR, P = 0.001; OR 0.77 [0.68–0.87] for HBR, P < 0.001) had significantly lower BR and HBR.

Threshold for predicting no transferable blastocyst on Day 5

The number of high-quality Day 3 embryos was positively correlated with Day 5 outcomes (Fig 1). A threshold of 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos was identified: at this cutoff, the risk of cycle cancellation due to no transferable blastocyst on Day 5 was 19.30%, with corresponding CPR and LBR of 37.14% and 20.00%, respectively. When six such embryos were available, the cancellation risk dropped below 5%, and CPR/LBR increased to 47.62% and 23.81%. The threshold of 4 Day 3 high-quality embryos was chosen because it is the first level at which the risk of cycle cancellation falls to a clinically acceptable level (19.30% ≤20%), with meaningful improvements in pregnancy rates.

Validation of 4 or fewer Day 3 high-quality embryos using an independent cohort

To assess the generalizability of ≤ 4 Day 3 high-quality embryos to find a discrimination threshold level that could predict the risk of no Day 5 transferable blastocyst, we performed the validation set to verify the accuracy of the predictive model using an independent dataset consisting of 390 fresh oocyte retrieval cycles from women of AMA treated at the same center between January 2024 and May 2026. This validation cohort included 1803 cleavage‑stage embryos, with a mean female age of 38.35 ± 2.93 years. The ROC curve was constructed based on the number of Day 3 high-quality embryos to predict the presence of the risk of no Day 5 transferable blastocysts in the validation dataset (Fig 2). The ROC curve results showed that an AUC of 0.94 (0.92–0.95) in the training dataset and 0.91 (0.90–0.93) in a temporally separated validation cohort from the same center, indicating good predictive performance. The optimal cutoff for predicting no day‑5 transplantable blastocyst was ≤ 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos (Youden index < 4.5). The sensitivity of the cutoff threshold was 100% in both the training and validation sets, with specificities of 84.06% and 82.67%, respectively.

Clinical outcomes stratified by the number of Day 3 high-quality embryos (≥ 4 vs. <4) and transfer strategy in women of advanced maternal age

Using the threshold identified in our analysis of 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos (identified from the data in Fig 1), clinical outcomes were compared between cycles with ≥ 4 vs. <4 embryos and between Day 3 cleavage-stage transfer and Day 5 blastocyst transfer (Table 3). In cycles with ≥ 4 high-quality embryos, Day 5 blastocyst transfer was associated with a higher CPR (44.44% vs. 38.67%), a significantly lower MPR (2.78% vs. 11.33%), and a consequently higher LBR (26.85% vs. 20.00%) compared to Day 3 transfer (P < 0.001). Neonatal outcomes also favored Day 5 transfer, evidenced by a higher mean gestational age at delivery (38.32 ± 1.96 weeks vs. 37.43 ± 2.33 weeks) and a greater mean birth weight (3375.00 ± 215.47 g vs. 2913.04 ± 651.50 g). In cycles with < 4 high-quality embryos, Day 5 transfer also showed numerically higher CPR (28.57% vs. 21.43%) and LBR (23.38% vs. 16.23%). however, the differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.185). Notably, neonatal outcomes were better after Day 5 transfer (gestational age 37.91 ± 2.19 weeks vs. 37.12 ± 2.57 weeks) birth weight 3215.63 ± 455.11 g vs. 2830.77 ± 630.12 g). MR did not differ significantly between any of the compared transfer strategies within or across the embryo-number groups. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that these analyses are based solely on the 382 cycles that proceeded to fresh transfer. Consequently, the observed differences in clinical pregnancy and live birth rates may be influenced by selection bias. Moreover, the decision to proceed with fresh transfer was influenced by multiple clinical factors (e.g., progesterone levels, endometrial status, ovarian stimulation protocol), which may confound the comparison between Day 3 and Day 5 transfer strategies.

Discussion

Our study identified the number of high-quality Day 3 embryos as a pivotal factor influencing subsequent outcomes in AMA patients. We developed a predictive model demonstrating that the possession of ≥ 4 Day 3 high-quality embryos strongly predicts a higher likelihood of forming at least one high-quality blastocyst. Furthermore, this simple criterion effectively stratified clinical outcomes: cycles with ≥ 4 high-quality embryos achieved significantly higher rates of blastocyst formation, clinical pregnancy, and live birth than those with < 4 (Table 3). More importantly, among fresh cycles with ≥ 4 high-quality embryos, Day 5 blastocyst transfer was associated with significantly higher clinical pregnancy and live birth rates, a lower multiple pregnancy rate, and better neonatal outcomes (higher gestational age and birth weight) compared with Day 3 cleavage-stage transfer. Based on our observations, for AMA patients with ≥ 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos, blastocyst culture followed by elective single blastocyst transfer may be considered to optimize healthy singleton births. It must be clearly stated that the clinical outcome analyses are based on a selected subgroup (382/1102 cycles, 34.7%) that proceeded to fresh transfer. The majority of cycles were canceled for clinical reasons (PPOS protocols, OHSS prophylaxis, elevated progesterone, endometrial issues) that are independent of embryo quality. This introduces selection bias, which likely overestimates the benefit of Day 5 transfer, as patients undergoing fresh transfer may have had more favorable ovarian response and endometrial status. Therefore, these results may not be generalizable to the full AMA cohort and require cautious interpretation. Nevertheless, the predictive value of the ≥ 4 threshold is primarily supported by embryological outcomes (BR, HBR, ROC analysis) from the full 1102-cycle cohort, and the core model remains robust.

Morphological scoring with single blastocyst transfer is the primary strategy for AMA women [24, 25], but better methods are needed to avoid cycles with no transferable blastocysts [1, 26]. A key controversial issue is how to minimize multiple pregnancy rates while maintaining satisfactory BR and clinical pregnancy rates [27]. Although transferring more embryos (e.g., double embryo transfer) can improve the clinical pregnancy rate [28], it dramatically increases multiple gestations [29]. Our morphology-based model is not intended to replace PGT-A or time-lapse imaging; rather, it offers a low-cost, widely accessible first-line screening tool. In resource-limited settings, the ≥ 4 threshold can guide blastocyst culture decisions. In higher-resource settings, it may help prioritize embryos for PGT-A or complement morpho kinetic algorithms. Blastocyst formation is influenced by maternal age, number of oocytes obtained, embryo development rate, and Day 3 high-quality of embryos [30–33]. Our results show that these factors are closely associated with BR and HBR in AMA women. AMA is associated with decreased ovarian reserve and oocyte function [34]. It has been suggested that the age of the female partner is one of the factors influencing the number of oocytes obtained and blastocyst formation [35]. We found that the number of oocytes retrieved and blastocyst formation were positively correlated, but HBR remained low even with ≥ 5 oocytes (only 33.20%), suggesting reduced developmental potential in AMA, likely due to higher chromosomal abnormality rates [36].

The number of Day 3 cells was positively correlated with blastocyst formation [37]. This study found that when the number of Day 3 cells was less than 11, the BR and HBR were the highest in 8 cells, suggesting that either too slow or too fast cleavage of embryos can affect blastocyst formation in women of AMA [38, 39]. The HBR was 5.34% when the number of Day 3 cells was 4, indicating that even poor-quality Day 3 embryos retain some developmental potential, possibly via self-repair mechanisms [40, 41]. The Day 2 4-cell → Day 3 8-cell pattern yielded the highest HBR (46.91%) [42].

Embryo fragmentation also affects HBR [43], likely because fragments disrupt cell junctions, release toxic substances that induce apoptosis, and cause loss of cytoplasm and organelles [44]. We found that the HBR was less than 10% in women of AMA with Day 3 embryo fragmentation of more than 25%. Logistic regression analysis confirmed that fragmentation, embryo grade and the number of high-quality embryos were significantly correlated with the outcome of high-quality blastocyst formation. We found that the rate of HBR was 57.47% for grade I embryos in women of AMA, significantly decreased for grade II embryos, and less than 10% of embryos after grade III could develop into high-quality blastocysts. The possible reason is that patients with high overall embryo quality form more high-quality blastocysts.

Although blastocyst culture technology has improved over the years, it does not yet fully simulate the in vivo environment, especially in laboratories where the culture environment is prone to fluctuations, and prolonging embryo culture to the blastocyst stage may further affect embryo developmental potential [45, 46]. In addition, some patients’ embryos may be more sensitive to the external environment, and early freezing and storage of cleavage-stage embryos may preserve the developmental potential of the embryos [47]. In women of AMA, there is a decrease in egg quality and quantity due to age, which leads to a lower chance of obtaining blastocysts [48, 49]. Therefore, in order to prevent blastocyst failure in patients with no available embryos for transfer and to ensure the effectiveness of blastocyst culture, it is necessary to model the relationship between the number of Day 3 high-quality embryos and blastocyst formation to decide the strategy of whether to culture all Day 3 embryos in blastocyst culture [50]. A model to predict embryonic developmental potential is urgently needed to weigh the above factors [51], so that blastocyst culture can be used to screen for more promising embryos and improve embryo utilization in patients, which is of great importance for women of AMA [52]. To facilitate elective single blastocyst transfer and reduce multiple birth rates, our center employs a standardized strategy: we routinely culture all cleavage-stage embryos to the blastocyst stage, or alternatively, selectively transfer or cryopreserve a maximum of 1–2 high-quality cleavage-stage embryos per cycle and culture the remaining embryos to blastocyst to build a dedicated cohort for future single-embryo transfer. This study quantified the relationship between the number of high-quality embryos on Day 3 and subsequent embryological and clinical outcomes. The data demonstrate a clear positive correlation: as the number of Day 3 high-quality embryos increases, rates of Day 5 high-quality blastocyst formation, transferable blastocyst cycles, clinical pregnancy, and live birth all exhibit a corresponding, progressive increase. In cycles with 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos, the high-quality blastocyst formation rate was 23.82%, and the risk of canceling fresh embryo transfer due to no Day 5 transferable blastocysts was 19.30%, while the associated clinical pregnancy and live birth rates were 37.14% and 20.00%, respectively. When 6 high-quality Day 3 embryos were available, these outcomes were further improved, with the cancellation risk dropping to 4.55% and the clinical pregnancy and live birth rates rising to 47.62% and 23.81%, suggesting that 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos may serve as a useful reference threshold for considering blastocyst culture followed by single blastocyst transfer, pending prospective validation. These findings underscore that the cohort size of high-quality cleavage-stage embryos serves as a robust, integrative indicator of both laboratory developmental potential and ultimate clinical success in AMA patients.

Our morphology-based model does not replace PGT-A or time-lapse imaging. Instead, it offers a low-cost, widely accessible first-line screening tool. In resource-limited settings, the ≥ 4 threshold can guide blastocyst culture decisions. In higher-resource settings, it may help prioritize embryos for PGT-A or complement morpho kinetic algorithms. A key strength of this study is the identification of ≥ 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos as a predictive threshold to guide the decision for fresh blastocyst transfer in AMA women, an approach associated with improved pregnancy and neonatal outcomes without requiring expensive instrumentation. For patients meeting this criterion, Day 5 single blastocyst transfer was associated with higher live birth rates, lower multiple pregnancy risk, and better neonatal health compared to Day 3 transfer in the subset of cycles that underwent fresh transfer.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was retrospective and single-center, which increases the risk of overfitting and limits generalizability. Second, only 382 of the 1102 cycles (34.7%) underwent fresh embryo transfer; the remaining cycles were canceled for clinical reasons (elevated progesterone, no transferable embryos, OHSS prophylaxis, stimulation protocols incompatible with fresh transfer, or endometrial/uterine issues). This introduces significant selection bias, as patients who received fresh transfer may have had more favorable baseline characteristics. Third, the limited availability of clinical outcome data (only 382 cycles with fresh transfer) reduces statistical power and prevents extrapolation of pregnancy rates to the broader AMA population. Fourth, the predictive threshold of ≥ 4 high-quality embryos was derived from the same dataset without true external validation from a different center, so its reproducibility in other centers remains unknown. In our validation cohort, the 100% sensitivity means no transferable Day 5 blastocyst is missed, but the ~ 84% specificity implies that 16% of patients with ≥ 4 high-quality embryos will still fail to produce a transferable blastocyst (false positives). Clinicians should counsel that this threshold reduces risk but does not eliminate it. Fifth, residual confounding is possible because important clinical factors (ovarian stimulation protocol, endometrial condition, progesterone level, BMI, lifestyle) were not included in the multivariate model. Sixth, the single-center setting and specific laboratory protocols further limit generalizability to other IVF settings. Collectively, these limitations necessitate cautious interpretation and underscore the need for prospective, multicenter validation.

Concurrently, prospective, multicenter studies should be conducted to develop predictive models using multiple methodologies and validate their efficacy, aiming to establish models with significant clinical utility. Prospective validation of the ≥ 4 threshold, age‑subgroup analyses, and cost‑effectiveness studies are needed. Integration with non‑invasive tools may improve prediction, and long‑term neonatal follow‑up should be included.

Conclusions

This study contributes a practical, easy-to-use threshold of ≥ 4 high-quality Day 3 embryos for guiding blastocyst culture in AMA women. For patients meeting this criterion, blastocyst culture followed by elective single blastocyst transfer may be considered to optimize healthy singleton births, although the clinical outcome analyses were limited by selection bias (only 34.7% of cycles underwent fresh transfer). It provides detailed neonatal outcomes stratified by transfer strategy, together with a temporally separated validation that supports the threshold’s robustness. Prospective validation in unselected AMA populations is required before clinical implementation. Nonetheless, this model provides a practical, low‑cost reference for individualizing blastocyst culture decisions in AMA women.
