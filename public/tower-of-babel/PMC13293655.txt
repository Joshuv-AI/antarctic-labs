CATS—A tool to contextualize cancer hallmarks to specific cancer types
Authors: Mahfuza Sharmin, Gulden Olgun, Piyush Agrawal, Arashdeep Singh, Sridhar Hannenhalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGene signatures, widely used to infer the activity of biological processes based on transcriptomic data, lack tissue/cellular context. For instance, the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signature derived from one cellular context may not be precisely applicable to another context; overlapping yet distinct genesets are likely to mediate EMT in different contexts. Here, we derive cancer type-specific gene signatures for 14 oncogenic hallmark processes across 23 cancer types by integrating the context-agnostic reference gene signatures with the protein interaction network and cancer-specific transcriptomic data. Overall, our inferred context-specific gene signatures exhibit a higher cancer specificity than the corresponding reference genesets with respect to a variety of biological and clinical features, such as activity in malignant cells, genetic vulnerabilities, patient prognosis, and treatment response. We provide the projections for 14 cancer hallmarks across 23 human cancer types, along with the CATS (Cancer Specific Transcriptomic Signature) software tool and the evaluation repository.Subject areas: automation in bioinformatics, cancer, omics

Introduction

A gene signature refers to a set of genes representing an active biological process, cellular identity, or cellular state.1 For example, cancer cells are often dividing, accompanied by the expression of a specific set of genes referred to as the “proliferation signature,” with KI-67 being a prime representative of this signature.2 Gene signatures representing 14 cancer hallmark processes, viz., stemness, invasion, metastasis, proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, apoptosis, cell cycle, differentiation, DNA damage, DNA repair, hypoxia, inflammation, and quiescence, have been previously curated from the literature3 and multiple databases such as Gene Ontology (GO)4,5,6 and the Molecular Signatures Database, MsigDB.7 However, a major limitation of the current signature genesets is that they are broadly utilized in a context-agnostic fashion, collected over independent, unrelated studies; in fact, many of the genes in these signatures exhibit a limited expression breadth across cancer types. For instance, we observed from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data that the gene gremlin 1 (GREM1), which is part of angiogenesis and invasion hallmark processes, is highly expressed in pancreatic and stomach cancers but has a very low expression in kidney and brain cancers; fibronectin gene (FN1), a part of angiogenesis, EMT, invasion, and metastasis hallmarks, is highly variable across cancer types with the log transcript per million (TPM) value ranging from 66 in Kidney Chromophobe (KICH) to 2,837.8 in Thyroid carcinoma (THCA). Finally, a recent study8 has experimentally demonstrated that across 12 conditions (4 cell lines and 3 drug treatments), EMT-associated expression changes are highly context specific, with an average of only 22% of response genes being shared between any two conditions and over half of all response genes being restricted to no more than two of the 12 conditions. These examples suggest that the context-agnostic application of gene signatures can be misleading. At the same time, it is not feasible to experimentally ascertain context-specific manifestations of numerous important processes across a wide array of known and many unknown contexts, motivating our study.

The aforementioned EMT study8 provides a test case to investigate the variability of a biological process across cellular contexts and to understand its relationships with a broader context-agnostic signature of the process, which could then be used to devise a procedure to infer context-specific projection of a gene signature. We discovered that the context-specific EMT genes were (1) preferentially connected to the pan-context genes in a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and (2) co-expressed in the specific context in which they were ascertained. Guided by these observations, we developed a tool, CATS (Cancer Specific Transcriptomic Signature), which, given a hallmark (or any other) context-agnostic reference geneset and a specific cancer context, aims to identify a partially overlapping geneset that is likely to represent the activity of the hallmark process in the specific context. Such a mapping, or contextualization, is based on three premises: (1) preferential connectivity to the reference geneset in the PPI network, (2) preferential co-expression with the reference geneset in the specific context, and additionally (3) differential upregulation of the gene in the particular context. We applied CATS to identify context-specific manifestations of 14 cancer hallmarks across 23 cancer types.

Numerous studies have explored the context specificity of transcriptomic patterns across tissues,9,10 diseases,11,12,13 and species14 using co-expression and the PPI network to explore the contexts in which a particular biological process is likely active. Such studies are broadly based on the premise that the genes involved in a pathway, in any context, are known. More to the point, studies that attempt to identify context-specific manifestation of a pathway that was ascertained in a context-agnostic fashion are limited. A handful of previously published studies have addressed the problem of mapping tissue-agnostic genesets to specific tissues.15,16 Starting from a reference tissue-agnostic geneset, both the previous works assign a tissue-specific weight to each gene where the weight is defined as a combination of its relative and absolute expression in the tissue of interest. In other words, the same genes in the geneset are ranked differently across different tissues based on expression, enabling threshold-based refinements of the original geneset for each tissue. Both works utilize Human Protein Atlas (HPA; Uhlen et al.17) as the resource for tissue-specific expression and refine the tissue-agnostic gene signatures from MsigDB (Liberzon et al.7), enabling downstream tissue-specific pathway analysis. Among the two, Frost et al.15 provide implementation details and we therefore use it as a representative of prior methods and compare its performance to that of our pipeline. A key novelty in our approach is that while the previous approaches rely solely on tissue-specific transcriptomic data to refine the gene signatures, our approach additionally incorporates tissue-specific protein interaction networks and tissue-specific co-expression networks. In doing so, our approach not only refines a geneset but also first expands the reference geneset to include additional genes in each tissue-specific projection, based on their network proximity to the genes in the reference geneset. Furthermore, unlike previous works, we have applied our tool systematically to the TCGA cohort to obtain cancer-specific genesets for all major hallmark genesets and have performed an extensive evaluation.

We demonstrated the functional relevance and clinical utility of the context-specific hallmark genesets through multiple follow-up analyses. (1) In independent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, we show that the context-specific genesets exhibit higher activity in malignant cells than the corresponding reference geneset. (2) Based on the DepMap database, the context-specific genes have relatively higher dependency scores in the corresponding cell line. (3) The context-specific genes show a relatively greater overlap with cancer-specific risk loci. (4) The context-specific genesets are more frequently predictive of patient survival in the specific cancer cohort, and finally, (5) in multiple clinical trial datasets, context-specific genes can better discriminate responders from non-responders from their pre-treatment transcriptome. As an illustrative case study, we further highlight context-specific biological processes that interface with EMT process in cervical and kidney cancers. Overall, our study underscores the limitations of the context-agnostic nature of the currently used cancer hallmark signatures and presents a systematic approach to contextualize the signatures to a specific context, yielding greater predictive power as well as insights into context-specific processes interacting with the hallmark process. CATS is available as an open-source R package, as well as a GitHub repository (https://github.com/hannenhalli-lab/CATS), and the evaluation of CATS is available via a secondary GitHub repository (https://github.com/mahfuzasharmin/CATSeval).

Results

Context-specific EMT genesets are likely to be co-expressed and have higher number of interactions

To investigate the context-specific variation in EMT-associated genes, Cook and Vanderhyden8 experimentally identified 12 context-specific EMT response genesets comprising 4 cell lines (A549 [lung], DU145 [prostate], MCF7 [breast], and OVCA420 [ovarian cell]) and 3 treatments (TGFB1, EGF, and TNF), revealing substantial differences in the EMT response signature across contexts. We aimed to understand the relationships of the context-specific genesets with the context-agnostic reference geneset. From the 12 EMT genesets obtained from Cook and Vanderhyden,8 in each of the 4 cell lines individually, we first determined the common genes across the 3 treatments and considered these as cell-type-specific EMT genesets (4 corner images in Figure 1A; STAR Methods). Next, from these 4 cell-type-specific genesets, we compiled a consensus set as the genes present in at least three of the four cell-type-specific genesets (indicated by “Y” in the central image in Figure 1A); we consider the consensus set as the pan-context (as a proxy for context-agnostic) EMT signature. For downstream analyses, we exclude the pan-context genes from each of the cell line-specific genesets. We first note that pan-context EMT genes obtained earlier have no overlap with the reference EMT geneset from the CancerSEA database,3 underscoring high variability and context dependence of these genesets.

Based on each cell-type-specific transcriptomics separately, we compared the co-expression of gene pairs in the (1) pan-context EMT geneset, (2) context-specific EMT geneset, (3) between a context-specific gene and a pan-context gene, and as control, (4) between a context-specific gene and a randomly selected gene. As shown in Figure 1B, in all four cell lines, not surprisingly, the pan-context EMT genes show the most correlated expression. Importantly, however, the context-specific genes are more correlated with the pan-context genes than with random genes (Wilcoxon p value < 5e−324 in all cases). Based on a co-expression threshold (STAR Methods), we created a co-expression network across all genes and calculated for each context-specific gene g, the fraction (p) of pan-context genes g is connected to, compared to the genome-wide expectation (q), represented by an odds ratio “p/q.” As shown in Figure 1C, in all four cell lines, p/q distribution is substantially shifted above 1, suggesting a preferential connectivity of context-specific genes to the context-agnostic reference geneset. Next, as for the co-expression network analysis in Figure 1C, we obtained the protein interaction network human Protein-Protein Interaction Network (hPPIN)18 and calculated the p/q ratio for each context-specific gene. Again, as for the co-expression network, the physical interaction network analysis also revealed that in all four cell lines, context-specific genes are substantially more connected to pan-context genes than genome-wide expectations (Figure 1D). Overall, these results suggest that the context-specific EMT genes are likely to be co-expressed, and their protein products are likely to physically interact with the core reference EMT genes, significantly more than the random expectation.

CATS identifies context-specific projection of 14 cancer hallmark genesets across 23 cancers

Informed by the two characteristics of context-specific EMT genesets discussed earlier, we developed CATS: a pipeline for “Cancer Specific Transcriptomic Signature” computation (Figure 2A; STAR Methods). Briefly, given a context-agnostic “reference” geneset (for instance, “apoptosis” from the CancerSEA database3), CATS constructs a geneset specific to a target transcriptomic context (for instance, kidney cancer) in three steps: in step 1, expand the reference geneset to a PPI-expanded geneset by including all the other genes in the genome that exhibit substantially greater connectivity to the reference geneset (≥3-fold compared to genome-wide expectation and connected to at least 5% of reference geneset) in a protein interaction network (based on hPPIN18). Note that the resulting PPI-expanded geneset from step 1 is based only on the PPI network and is thus independent of any transcriptomic context. In step 2, expand the PPI-expanded geneset to a PPI-CoExp-expanded geneset by further including all the other genes that exhibit substantially greater connectivity to the PPI-expanded geneset (≥3-fold compared to genome-wide expectation and connected to at least 5% of the PPI-expanded geneset) in a gene co-expression network constructed from the target cohort (for instance, TCGA kidney cancer); note that the expansion step 2 is context dependent. In step 3, finally, the PPI-CoExp-expanded geneset is pruned by retaining only the genes that are differentially expressed (DE) (logFC >2) in the tissue-specific TCGA cohort relative to the corresponding normal tissue cohort in GTEx.19,20

We first assessed the extent to which the CATS pipeline could recover the cell-type-specific EMT genes experimentally ascertained by Cook and Vanderhyden used earlier. Starting with the pan-context EMT consensus geneset described earlier and applying the CATS pipeline (however, without step 3, as that would create an obvious bias), we found that the condition (cell type and treatment pair)-specific EMT genesets to be significantly enriched (p value < 0.05, odds ratio > 1 for 10 out of 12 cases) in the CATS identified PPI-CoExp-expanded genesets (STAR Methods), thus supporting the validity of the CATS pipeline.

Next, we comprehensively applied CATS to 14 hallmark genesets from CancerSEA across 23 cancer types from TCGA (Table S1). The number of genes in the reference hallmark genesets and projected genesets after each step are depicted in Figures 2B and S1. On average, across all the hallmarks and cancer types, relative to the reference geneset, there is a 2-fold increase in the geneset size at step 1, 20-fold after step 2, and down to 5-fold after step 3, with a substantial variation across hallmarks and cancer types; stemness and quiescence show minimum and maximum variation in the final geneset size across cancers. To check specificity across genesets, for each hallmark, we quantified the overlap between various context-specific sets across cancers using Jaccard indices (JIs). The distribution of JIs for various hallmarks (Figure 2C) suggests minimal overlap with an average JI of 0.2, suggesting a largely disjoint context-specific projection of each hallmark.

In the following sections, we assessed the advantage of CATS-identified context-specific genesets relative to multiple controls: (1) since step 3 of the CATS pipeline selects genes that are DE in a specific context, we compared the CATS-identified genesets with cancer-specific DE genes (logFC>2; we refer to them as DE control genesets); (2) we further compared CATS-identified context-specific genes with those identified by Frost et al.15 (referred to as Frost-refined genesets; see STAR Methods); (3) note that, from the initial reference geneset, CATS retains only a subset while discarding the rest. We directly compared these two subsets of the reference geneset (referred to as reference-retained and reference-discarded genesets) to ascertain whether the reference-retained genes exhibit favorable context-specific performance than the reference-discarded genes.

Context-specific hallmark signature reveals greater activity in malignant cells in a specific context

Here, we assessed whether compared to the reference hallmark geneset, the CATS-identified context-specific hallmark genesets reveal greater activity in malignant cells in scRNA-seq data. Toward this, we compiled scRNA-seq datasets for 6 cancer types—Breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and Prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD). In each dataset, we computed the functional activity score in each cell using the hallmark genesets (either the context-agnostic reference or the CATS-identified context-specific) based on the gene set variation analysis tool GSVA21,22 (STAR Methods); we ensured that the functional activity scores are not biased by the geneset size (see STAR Methods). Figure 3A shows the geneset activity in colon cancer cells (COAD from GSE81861) for the reference and COAD-specific quiescence geneset. While the reference geneset shows low activity dispersed across the two cell clusters, the contextualized geneset reveals high activity specifically in one of the clusters. Further analysis of marker genes of the cluster with high quiescence score revealed enrichment for growth factor binding and extracellular matrix constituents, known to be associated with quiescence.23,24

Figure 3B shows the geneset activity in the GBM scRNA-seq dataset (GSE84465) using the reference and the GBM-specific angiogenesis geneset. As aforementioned, the context-specific geneset activities are clearly higher than the reference genesets, specifically in one of cell clusters; interestingly, this cluster of malignant cells expresses myeloid marker genes such as CD45 and HLA-DRB1, suggesting plasticity.25 Furthermore, the same cluster shows higher expression of endoglin (CD105) and PAUR, which have been previously reported as angiogenic markers in glioblastoma cancer, even though they have not been included in the angiogenesis hallmark signature of CancerSEA.26,27

Figure 3C shows that across 6 cancers (columns) and 14 hallmarks (rows), in vast majority of cases, the context-specific geneset exhibits a greater activity in cancer cells (measured by the log2FoldChange of the number of active cells, and a geneset was considered active in a cell if the activity score >0.1; see STAR Methods for the rationale) compared to the context-agnostic reference geneset. We also observed higher activity across the board when we compared the CATS-identified geneset scores against DE control geneset scores (Figure S2A) and Frost-refined geneset scores (Figure S2B). A similar comparison between reference-retained and reference-discarded genesets also showed superiority of reference genes retained by CATS (Figure S2C).

Overall, these results demonstrate that across cancer types and hallmarks, the CATS-identified context-specific genesets show higher activity in malignant cells and can better segregate malignant cells based on hallmark activity.

Context-specific genes exhibit greater cancer-specific dependency and enrichment for cancer-specific risk loci

Oncogenic processes provide a survival and growth advantage to the tumor. While some of the processes, such as angiogenesis, are relevant in the tumor microenvironment, others, such as cell cycle or proliferation, may provide an intrinsic advantage to the tumor cells independent of the microenvironment. Here, we assessed whether CATS-identified context-specific oncogenic hallmark genes exhibit greater dependency scores in the corresponding cancer-derived cell lines, compared to the reference genes. Toward this, we obtained the in vitro CRISPR KO-based gene dependency scores (https://depmap.org/portal/) from the DepMap database (release 2202).28 We performed this analysis for 13 cancer types (covering 96 cell lines) having representative cell lines in the DepMap database. For a cancer type and hallmark, we compared the fraction of genes, either in the reference or the context-specific set, with a dependency score >0.5 on average in the relevant cell lines. As shown in Figures 4A and 4B, the fraction of genes showing such dependency is higher (one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 0.03) for context-specific genesets than for reference genesets across hallmarks and cancer types. Specifically, for four hallmarks, viz., proliferation, EMT, DNA damage, and DNA repair, the context-specific genes exhibit greater dependency across most cancer types. We also compared the fraction of genes from the CATS-identified genesets with Frost-refined genesets (Figure S3A) and from the reference-retained genesets with reference-discarded genesets (Figure S3B). Both the CATS-identified context-specific and the reference-retained genesets show dependency in a higher fraction of cases than controls (one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 0.3, 2.1e−18, and 1.44e−5).

Next, we assessed the extent to which the CATS-identified context-specific hallmark genesets overlap the cancer-specific risk genes. We compiled cancer risk loci for several cancers from a previous study,29 comprising 682 gene loci across 13 cancers—prostate cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, glioma, lung cancer (Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD)), ovarian cancer, melanoma, pancreatic cancer, esophageal cancer, bladder cancer, and thyroid cancer. For each cancer type and hallmark, we then compared the fractional overlap of the CATS-identified context-specific and reference genesets with the risk loci. Due to the small number of risk genes, the fractional overlaps are small. Overall, the difference between CATS-identified context-specific and reference geneset overlaps is modest but statistically significant (Figure 4C; one-sided paired Wilcoxon p value 0.004). Specifically, PRAD and Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM) exhibit the most improvement across hallmarks, and apoptosis and differentiation hallmarks show the most improvement across cancer types. Similar analysis for (1) CATS-identified vs. DE control, (2) CATS-identified vs. Frost-refined genesets, and (3) reference-retained vs. reference-discarded genes is shown in Figures S4A–S4C (one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p values are 1.1e−5, 0.07, and 0.06). Overall, we found that contextualization of hallmarks using CATS reveals genes with higher cancer specificity.

Context-specific genesets contribute toward survival prognostics and therapy response

Here, we assessed the relative advantages of the context-specific genesets in terms of their ability to predict patient survival and therapy response. First, for a geneset, we measured the fraction of genes whose expression is significantly associated with patient survival in a specific TCGA cancer cohort (STAR Methods). The Figure 5A heatmap shows that, for each hallmark and cancer type, the fraction of CATS-identified genes with a significant positive Cox regression hazard ratio (HR) is higher than that of the reference genes (overall, one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 9.0e−03). To ascertain that the observed trend is not simply because the CATS-identified genes are also DE in specific cancers relative to normal tissue, we repeated the analysis comparing the DE control genesets with the reference geneset, and the results are shown in the heatmap in Figure S5A (overall one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 1.07e−6). Furthermore, a similar analysis was done for the reference-retained geneset vs. the reference-discarded genesets (Figure S5B) and the CATS-identified genesets vs. the Frost-refined genesets (Figure S5C). Both analyses indicated that CATS-identified context-specific genesets have a higher fraction of genes with survival association (overall one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 7.15e−08 and 8.12e−08). Figure 5A also shows the row-wise and column-wise distributions demonstrating that, for some of the hallmarks (notably, DNA damage, DNA repair, and EMT) and some of the cancer types (notably, Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), KICH, and Brain Lower Grade Glioma (LGG)), a consistently larger fraction of context-specific genes is associated with significant positive HR. For the three hallmarks and the three cancer types, Figure S6 shows the distribution of the significance (−log10 of p values) of Cox’s proportional hazard model run jointly with the context-specific geneset signature score and the control geneset signature score for computing survival association, where the control geneset was either the reference geneset or the DE control geneset. The observation suggests that the CATS-identified context-specific genesets are associated with worse prognosis, more so than the reference or the DE control genesets.

Our aforementioned results show the variable efficacy of CATS-identified context-specific genesets across cancer types. In particular, the CATS-identified genesets do not perform well in some of the cancer types, notably, BRCA. We wondered whether the lack of efficacy in BRCA may be due to heterogeneity in BRCA and whether contextualizing the hallmarks to individual BRCA subtypes would be more effective. To test this, we applied CATS to the four BRCA cancer subtypes independently (STAR Methods). As shown in Figure S7, while the pan-BRCA CATS-identified context-specific genesets do not outperform the reference genesets, the BRCA subtype-specific genesets show modest improvement in some instances.

Next, we assessed, using the pre-treatment transcriptomes from multiple clinical trial datasets for BRCA and melanoma for various therapies, the extent to which the reference and the context-specific signatures differentiate the responders from the non-responders. To this end, we compiled gene expression datasets for 4 clinical trials from GEO30,31 and ENA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/home), and in each dataset, we divided the samples into responder and non-responder groups based on the available clinical outcome data (Table 1, STAR Methods). For each hallmark geneset, we computed the fraction of genes for which the responder and non-responder samples show significantly different expression (one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value < 0.05) and compared the fractions of the context-specific and the reference genesets. Across 2 BRCA datasets and 2 SKCM datasets, and all hallmarks, in 68% of the cases, the context-specific genesets show higher fractions of genes showing significantly different expressions between responder and non-responder groups (Figure 5B) (overall, one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 6.7e−05). Among the hallmarks, quiescence most consistently exhibited a higher fraction of genes (mean 0.3, max 0.53), distinguishing the responders from the non-responders. Figure 5C illustrates, for trial PRJEB23709 for SKCM patients under anti-PD1 therapy, that the SKCM-specific quiescence geneset separates the responders from the non-responders far better than the reference geneset. Finally, we observed the aforementioned trend when we compared the CATS-identified genesets with Frost-refined genesets (Figure S8A) and the reference-retained genesets with reference-discarded genesets (Figure S8B), emphasizing the efficacy of predicting therapy response by the CATS-identified context-specific genes (overall, one-sided paired Wilcoxon test p value = 3.8e−3, 1.6e−4, and 2.1e−4).

Contextualized signatures reveal potential tissue-specific processes interfacing with core hallmark genes

Starting from a reference hallmark geneset, CATS identifies the genesets that are likely to reflect processes associated with the activation of the hallmark process in the particular context. Here, we aim to explore such context-specific processes. Toward this, for each hallmark, we analyzed the GO terms enriched in various context-specific genesets. Figure 6A shows the significantly enriched processes (adj. p value < 0.05) across cancer types for angiogenesis; results for all hallmarks are provided in Figure S9. While the processes, uniquely enriched in CATS-identified angiogenesis genes, such as fibroblast growth factors,36 and glycosaminoglycan genes,2 are known to be causally linked with angiogenesis, the context specificity of these associations has not yet been investigated. As a case study, we analyzed (Figure 6B) the enriched processes for EMT hallmark in two cancers—cervical (CESC) and kidney (KIRP). We identified GO terms enriched uniquely in the context-specific geneset and further grouped the constituent genes using the Louvain community detection algorithm37 on their hPPIN connectivity (STAR Methods). For example, in the top image of Figure 6B, a Louvain community of genes from the EMT CESC context-specific genes revealed two groups of genes. The first community of genes is enriched for microtubule activity, ATP hydrolysis, serine kinase activity, anaphase-promoting complex binding, and ubiquitin-protein transferase regulator activity, and the second community is enriched for metalloendopeptidase activity and interleukin-1 receptor binding. Similarly, in the bottom image of Figure 6B, a Louvain community of KIRP context-specific EMT genes revealed three communities. The first is enriched for microtubule activity, ATP hydrolysis, and protein serine/threonine/tyrosine kinase activity. The second community of genes is enriched for cell cycle-related terms, and the third community is enriched for vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor binding and chemokine binding. The common terms across two panels are well supported in literature; various cytoskeletal proteins such as microtubule, actin, intermediate filament, etc., facilitate invasion and proliferation38 and have been targeted for therapy.39 ATP hydrolysis mediates migratory phenotype in glioblastoma cells40; serine/threonine kinases and receptor tyrosine kinases are implicated in numerous oncogenic processes, including EMT. On the other hand, in CESC, processes related to matrix metalloprotein (MMP), and in KIRP, processes related to VEGF, are uniquely revealed. Overexpression of matrix metalloproteinases can be a prognostic marker for cervical cancer,41 and VEGF is involved in the regulation of renal microvascular rarefaction and fibrosis via various mechanisms.42 While experimental evidence for a context-specific role of an individual process may not yet exist, this case study underscores the potential for identifying context-specific functional associations via contextualization of the hallmark processes, which are currently treated in a context-agnostic fashion.

Discussion

Much of the interpretation of transcriptome-based studies relies on the knowledge of functional grouping of genes, such as pathways and processes, compiled in various databases, such as GO, KEGG, MSigDB, etc. These gene collections, or “signatures,” are however ascertained from a variety of experimental approaches in a variety of contexts but nevertheless are treated as context agnostic. This lack of associated context is clearly a major limitation, as various biological processes, such as EMT8 and proliferation,43 are likely to be involved in different cellular contexts by a substantially different set of genes and may likewise be associated with distinct processes in each context. While context specificity of individual genes is evident from the context-specific phenotypic manifestation of various genetic mutations (consider the impact of BRCA mutations specifically in breast and ovarian cancers),43 a systematic study of context-specific instantiation of biological processes and the genes involved therein has not been performed due to obvious experimental challenges. Ultimately, the current practice of utilizing a reference, context-agnostic signature for a process in every context can lead to misleading interpretations. This is the challenge we have attempted to address, namely, given a context-agnostic reference signature for a biological process, how can we derive its particular instantiation to a particular context?

The premise underlying CATS is that the context-specific genes representing a process are preferentially connected to each other and to a core reference geneset and that they are co-expressed in the particular context. This premise is well supported by the experimental data on the context-specific EMT signature.8 We additionally require the gene to be preferentially expressed in a particular context. We applied CATS to contextualize 14 cancer hallmark processes from CancerSEA3 on 23 cancer types in TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/tcga). Overall, we see clear advantages of the contextualized hallmark signatures relative to the reference context-agnostic signature in terms of greater activity in malignant single cells (Figure 3), in vitro gene essentiality (Figures 4A and 4B), overlap with cancer risk loci (Figures 4C and 4D) cancer patient survival associations (Figure 5A), and therapy response (Figure 5B). Furthermore, the contextualized genesets can reveal other biological processes in the specific context that interact with the core hallmark process (Figure 6). In the particular case, we have (Figure 6) the hallmark EMT process interfaces with common as well as unique processes in the context of CESC and KIRP cancers; matrix metalloprotein in CESC and VEGF in KIRP are revealed uniquely in the two cancer types and have some experimental support.41,42 However, the mechanisms of context-specific activations of hallmark processes are not known, and further investigations of the contextual processes revealed by CATS are required.

Note that the conventional hallmark (or other) signatures are simply a list of genes whose expression is an indicator of the activity of the hallmark. Categorically, signatures do not provide detailed pathway-level mechanistic information about how the genes are related to each other and precisely how they relate to the specific phenotype represented by the hallmark. They can thus simply be thought of as markers. It follows then that the CATS-identified context-specific signatures should also be interpreted simply as markers of the activity of the specific process in a particular context. While the reference genesets may, in some cases, include genes very directly involved in the process, by contrast, the contextualized signatures are typically further expanded to include additional genes and processes that are associated with the hallmark process in the specific context.

Limitations of the study

CATS has a few limitations. While physical PPIs and co-expression are useful proxies of functional connectivity between genes, additional types of gene-gene relationships44 and additional indicators of context-specific gene functionality45 could be incorporated. As additional experimental evidence of context-specific gene functions accumulates, those could be used to either guide our method or validate our findings. However, the simplicity and general applicability of CATS is an advantage. Another point worth noting is that “gene signatures” conventionally only include genes that are expected to be activated. However, inactivation of specific genes could also indicate activation of certain other processes; for instance, inactivation of the P53 pathway is expected to be associated with activation of the proliferation pathway. CATS could be extended in future to include both activated as well as inactivated genes in the context-specific signature. CATS uses context-specific differential upregulations to prune the geneset, which requires a normal control; in our application, we used GTEx as the control for cancer samples. In the absence of such control, one can potentially use an expression in a context relative to all other available contexts. The pros and cons of these various scenarios are yet to be explored.

Method details

Data collection

We obtained the scRNASeq data conducting the experiment done for identifying the context-specific EMT genes from GSE147405. This dataset has transcriptomics done for three treatments (TGFB1, EGF, and TNF) on four cell lines - A549 (lung), DU145 (prostate), MCF7 (breast), and OVCA420 (ovarian cell) - comprising 12 conditions. We also compiled the 12 context-specific EMT associated genesets from the supplementary data of the same study.8 Next, we collected the reference genesets from the CancerSEA3 database for 14 hallmark processes. For computing the tissue specific weights in these hallmark signatures, we collected the RNA expression for 40 tissues from the Human Atlas Protein Database and the implementation of the tissue specific geneset weight generation algorithm from Frost et al.15 For the CATS algorithm, we collected uniformly processed bulk RNA-Seq gene expression data for normal tissues (GTEx) and cancer samples (TCGA) from Xena browser.46 For GTEx and TCGA expression data, we utilized log2(x+0.001) transformed Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) normalized RNA-Seq. In this study, 23 human cancer types are considered. The actual number of samples in each cancer type are available in Table S1. Breast cancer subsets were collected from.47 We also collected the TCGA clinical Data from PanCanAtlas.54 For the protein interaction network, we used the human Protein-Protein Interaction Network (hPPIN)18 having 17062 nodes and 208759 edges.

To show functional activities using genesets, we collected the scRNA-Seq data for 6 cancer types from the Gene Expression Omnibus - GSE75688 for breast cancer (BRCA),48 GSE81861 for colon cancer (COAD),49 GSE81812 for esophagus cancer (ESCA),50 GSE84465 for brain cancer (GBM),51 GSE99305 for pancreas cancer (PAAD),52 and GSE99795 for prostate cancer (PRAD).53 For therapy response analysis, we collected the gene expression data from the dataset mentioned in Table 1. Among them, we obtained GSE21974, GSE25066, GSE123728 from the GEO and PRJEB23709 from the ENA (European Nucleotide Archive).

Pan-context genesets from experimental EMT data

The experimental context-specific EMT gene identification study 8 presented EMT genesets for 12 conditions - EMT induction via treatment with TGFB1, EGF, and TNF on A549 (lung), DU145 (prostate), MCF7 (breast), and OVCA420 (ovarian cell) lines. For each condition, the authors collected the single cell transcriptomic samples across 8 time points with 6 replicates and identified the differentially expressed (DE) genes using ‘mgcv’ R package for each condition; we considered their differentially upregulated genes (FDR <0.01) as the condition-specific EMT genes. For each cell type, we defined the cell type context-specific EMT geneset by taking the common upregulated DE genes (logFC>2) across the three treatments, yielding four context-specific EMT genesets for the four cell lines. Finally, we defined the pan-context genesets by taking the genes which were common across at least three out of four context-specific EMT genesets.

For any two genes, we identified the UMI counts of the genes for the cells with metadata of TGFB1/EGF/TNF Inhibited to confirm the expression coming from EMT-induced cells. Then the pairwise correlations were computed across the TGFB1/EGF/TNF-inhibited cells for each cell line separately to obtain the context-specific pairwise gene correlations.

CATS: Cancer specific transcriptomic signature

For a given context-agnostic “Reference” geneset X (e.g., EMT), we projected this geneset to a specific cancer context Y (e.g., Colon) to derive a cancer type-specific gene signature. For exposition, in this section, we will use EMT for “Reference” geneset X, and Colon as the cancer context Y. CATS includes three steps. The first step is the context-agnostic expansion of the reference EMT geneset based on the PPI network, the second set is context-specific expansion based on the colon-specific co-expression network, and the third step is context-specific pruning based on differential upregulation in colon cancer relative to normal colon. Details follow.

Step 1: Context-agnostic expansion

Given the reference EMT signature geneset -- EMT-Sig, and the hPPIN18), for each gene g not in EMT-Sig, we calculated the ratio between the fraction p of genes in EMT-Sig that are connected with g and the fraction q of genes genome-wide that are connected with g. If the ratio p/q ≥ 3 and the g is connected to at least 5% of EMT-Sig, we included gene g into a new expanded set called EMT-Sig-PPI-Expanded.

Step 2: Context-specific expansion

We constructed a network based on gene-gene expression correlation. In the TCGA Colon cancer cohort, we identified the correlation cut-off based on the 99th percentile of randomly sampled 1,000,000 gene pairs and constructed a co-expression network based on this threshold. We used the WGCNA R package (Langfelder & Horvath, 2008) for correlation calculation. Similar to the previous step, for each gene g that is not in EMT-Sig-PPI-Expanded, we calculated the ratio between the fraction of genes in EMT-Sig-PPI-Expanded that are connected with g and the fraction of genes genome-wide that are connected with g. If the ratio is ≥ 3 and the g is connected to at least 5% of EMT-Sig-PPI-Expanded, we include gene g into a new expanded set called EMT-Sig-PPI-CoExp-Expanded.

Step 3: Pruning

Finally, we pruned the EMT-Sig-PPI-CoExp-Expanded by retaining only the differentially expressed genes (FDR ≤ 0.05 AND logFC >2) in the TCGA Colon cancer cohort relative to the normal Colon cohort in GTEx. We used the limma package for the DE analysis.59

Application of CATS on experimental EMT genes

We assessed the extent to which CATS can recover the EMT context-specific genes starting from the CancerSEA EMT hallmark geneset. We assessed CATS on each of the four cell lines and each of the three treatments independently - a total of 12 assessments. In each experiment, starting with CancerSEA EMT hallmark geneset, the Context-Agnostic-Expansion was applied using hPPIN and Context-Specific-Expansion was applied on the scRNASeq of relevant treated cells. The last step of CATS was excluded as that would be circular. We assessed whether the resulting expanded context-specific geneset as determined by CATS, is enriched for the experimentally ascertained context-specific genes from Cook et al.8

Tissue specific geneset weights by Frost

The tissue specific genesets were computed using Frost algorithm.15 For each CancerSEA hallmark genesets we generated the tissue-specific weights in 15 tissues corresponding to 15 cancer types (ACC - adrenal gland, BRCA – breast, CESC - cervix, COAD – colon, ESCA – esophagus, KICH – kidney, KIRC – kidney, KIRP – kidney, LIHC – liver, LUAD – lung, LUSC – lung, OV – ovary, PAAD – pancreas, PRAD – prostate, READ – rectum, SKCM – skin, STAD – stomach, THCA - thyroid gland). The Frost-refined genesets were selected based on whether the weights in relevant tissues were greater than a threshold, which is determined by the median values across all weights regardless of hallmark and tissues.

Functional activity scores on scRNASeq data

CancerSEA utilized several scRNASeq data to demonstrate the functional activity of the hallmark signatures using Gene Set Variation Analysis.22 From their list, we obtained 6 datasets, the annotation of cancer cells from the available metadata in each dataset, following the guidelines of the CancerSEA site to select the set of cells. Single cell signature scores were estimated for 563 tumor cells from GSE75688, 293 Epithelial, Fibroblast, and Endothelial cells from GSE81861, 222 esophageal squamous carcinoma cells from GSE81812, 3589 glioblastoma tumor cells from GSE84465, 96 PDAC cells from GSE9930, 5 and 147 prostate cancer cells from GSE99795.

For each scRNASeq dataset, the functional activity scores were computed using both the reference hallmark genesets and the appropriate cancer-specific hallmark genesets separately. If the functional activity score is greater than 0.1, we consider that geneset as active in the cells. To identify a threshold for the activity score, we computed such functional activity of 1000 random genesets with sizes sampled uniformly from 70 to 1200 representing the range for context-specific hallmark genesets. The median of functional activity scores across all cells was recorded for each random geneset and then the maximum of the 1000 median values was used as the threshold for the reference and context-specific geneset scores. We measure the log2FoldChange of the number of active cells in foreground (e.g., context-specific) and background (reference) genesets to show the higher activity.

The GSVA score is normalized and hence is independent of geneset size. However, to assess the potential bias of the functional activity scores on geneset size, we computed the functional activity of two random sets of 1000 genesets. The first 1000 genesets were of size 70 to 200 to mimic the geneset sizes of reference sets and the second 1000 genesets were of size 70 to 1200 to mimic the geneset sizes of context-specific genesets. The median functional activity across cells was recorded for two random genesets, each with 1000 values.

Survival analysis using hallmark genes

We built the Cox proportional hazard model of each gene across 23 TCGA cancer types using their expression in the relevant cancer type, survival info and age from the clinical data. The model implementation was chosen from the lifelines Python package.62 Next, we obtained the age-control hazard ratio (exp(gene expression)) and its pvalue for each gene of interest. The pvalue <0.05 is considered as significant for considering it for downstream analysis.

ssGSEA scores

For each hallmark geneset (reference or context-specific) we computed a sample-specific Gene Set Enrichment Score (ssGSEA) using the GSVA package22 in each TCGA sample. We then used these ssGSEA scores in place of gene expression in the same Cox proportional hazard model above and the p-values and the hazard ratios (HR = exp(ssGSEA score)) obtained from the model were recorded for each cancer type and hallmark. The HR using ssGSEA scores was computed jointly for the two genesets where the age controlled HR was computed using ssGSEA scores from context-specific geneset and the same from reference geneset.

Therapy response using hallmark genes

Among the four datasets used for therapy response, the counts were already available in the GEO datasets. For PRJEB23709, we obtained the counts data from the fastq data after STAR alignment57 and Salmon quantification.58 For all the datasets, we quantile normalized the log2 counts of the protein coding genes (GenCode version v26.GRCh38) using the ‘limma’ R package.59 The responder/non-responder status were identified as follows: for GSE21974 - R denotes the responder and NR denotes the non-responder of the response variable from the series_matrix.txt; for GSE25066 - from the series_matrix file, pathologic_response_pcr_rd (pCR) or RCB classes (RCB-0/I) indicates responder and pathologic_response_pcr_rd (RD) or RCB classes (RCB-II/III) indicates non-responder; for GSE123728 - “recurrence = 0” were taken as responder and “recurrence = 1” as non-responder; for PRJEB23709 - Recist indication of PR and CR denotes responder and PD and Sd denotes non-responder from the Anti-PD1 samples.

Contextualized processes from GO enrichment

We computed the GO enrichments using clusterProfiler package60 on reference and context-specific genesets independently. Next, we identified the context-specific GO terms, from the terms obtained by the context-specific geneset and discarding any terms obtained by the reference geneset. For each cancer, the combined genesets (reference and context-specific genes) were grouped using the Louvian community detection algorithm63 obtained from NetworkX Python package.61 Each Louvian community of genes were annotated by the GO term if its genes are covered by the community (i.e., more than 50% of the genes for the GO term are part of the community).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantification and statistical analysis were done using established R and Python packages, published tools, or in house codes. The details are provided in the individual Methods sections above.
