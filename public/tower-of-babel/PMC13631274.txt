The first telomere-to-telomere genome assembly of a freshwater bivalve: Gibbosula rochechouartii
Authors: Ming Yan, Min Jiang, Mingfei Zhan, Chao Jiang, Haibo Wen, Fengyue Shu, Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGibbosula rochechouartii is a vulnerable freshwater mussel species endemic to China, with severely degraded habitats. To date, there is no high-quality genome available for this species. In this study, we integrated PacBio HiFi, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), and Hi-C approaches to generate the first telomere-to-telomere genome assembly for this species. The completed genome spans 2.99 Gb with 93.41% completeness, anchored to 19 chromosomes. Comparative genomic analyses indicate that among all currently published unionoidea genomes, Margaritifera margaritifera is the closest relative to G. rochechouartii. This high-quality reference genome is expected to significantly advance conservation research efforts for this species.

Background & Summary

Bivalves first appeared in the Early Cambrian period over 500 million years ago, representing one of the ancient bilaterian lineages that have survived multiple mass extinction events1. As important bioindicators of aquatic ecosystem health, freshwater bivalves face the dual pressures of habitat degradation and environmental pollution. This makes freshwater ecosystems a natural laboratory for studying species’ adaptive evolution2–4. Régnier et al. estimated the total number of extinct mollusc species at 1,032, comprising 638 confirmed extinct, 380 potentially extinct, and 14 extinct in the wild. Among these, 46 are freshwater bivalve species from five families, underscoring the urgent need for their conservation5.

China is one of the world’s most biodiverse countries for freshwater bivalves6. However, their wild populations are in severe decline due to multiple anthropogenic pressures, including overfishing, habitat destruction, and water pollution7. Gibbosula rochechouartii (Heude, 1875), a bivalve species endemic to China, is commonly known as the “Pig’s Ear Mussel” because of its laterally flattened, pig ear-shaped shell. This species inhabits the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River, as well as large natural lakes, where it lives semi-buried in sandy or muddy substrates and is a filter feeder8. Although river development and habitat fragmentation have caused the extinction of several related species, G. rochechouartii has persisted in some disturbed habitats within its range. It was listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List in 20199. Nevertheless, research on its conservation biology remains exceedingly scarce, which significantly hampers the development and implementation of effective protective measures.

To date, research on G. rochechouartii has focused primarily on its taxonomic classification, with notable discrepancies persisting between morphological and molecular evidence concerning its phylogenetic position. Early morphological studies, based on characteristics such as shell tubercles, classified it within Lamprotula10. Following advances in molecular systematics, Huang et al. (2018) proposed its reclassification into the Margaritifera (Margaritiferidae), based on concatenated multi-locus analyses8. Subsequently, Lopes-Lima et al. (2018) supported its placement within Gibbosula through a reconstructed phylogeny of the Unionidae, resulting in its current accepted nomenclature, Gibbosula rochechouartii11. This placement has been further consolidated by the phylogenomic study by Gomes-dos-Santos et al. (2023), which integrated 514 Unioverse loci with mitochondrial genomes12. Chen et al. (2025) based on phylogenetic analysis of the COI also support its attribution to Gibbosula13. While previous studies based on multiple molecular markers have established the taxonomic position of G. rochechouartii within Margaritiferidae, a high-quality reference genome for this species, and indeed for Gibbosula, remains unavailable.

The natural population of G. rochechouartii is highly restricted, and a high-quality reference genome is essential for understanding its adaptive evolutionary potential, evaluating population genetic resources, and informing conservation strategies14. However, genomic studies of G. rochechouartii remain confined to mitochondrial data, leaving fundamental aspects such as its genetic diversity, evolutionary history, and the genetic basis of its population decline poorly understood. Recent progress in telomere-to-telomere (T2T) complete genome assembly now offers opportunities to resolve these complex evolutionary questions15. Nevertheless, existing genomic resources are still largely dominated by marine bivalves, with chromosome-level genomes for freshwater species remaining particularly scarce. This gap has constrained detailed investigation into the mechanisms underpinning their environmental adaptation and genetic susceptibility to threats. To date, research on the genetic resources of freshwater bivalves remains limited, with chromosome-level genomes available for only a handful of species, including Dreissena polymorpha16, Sinohyriopsis cumingii17, Sinanodonta woodiana18 and Sinosolenaia oleivora19.

This study employed an integrated sequencing strategy, combining short-read, PacBio long-read and Oxford Nanopore ultra-long-read technologies, to successfully assemble and present the first telomere-to-telomere (T2T) reference genome of G. rochechouartii, thereby filling a critical gap in high-quality genomic resources for freshwater bivalves. While definitive clarification of its taxonomic status will require additional data from related taxa and further validation, this work establishes a crucial foundation for systematically resolving this long-standing phylogenetic uncertainty. Furthermore, the acquisition of this high-contiguity genome not only provides essential data for investigating the adaptive evolutionary history and genetic diversity patterns of G. rochechouartii, but also serves as an important scientific basis for understanding evolutionary mechanisms in freshwater bivalves and developing targeted conservation strategies.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

A specimen of G. rochechouartii was provided by the Wuhu Yangtze River Mollusk Conservation Base of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs. The specimen, which originated from Poyang Lake, was transported to the laboratory of the Freshwater Fisheries Research Centre of the Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences in Wuxi, Jiangsu. For genomic sequencing, the adductor muscle was used, whereas transcriptomic sequencing was performed on tissues from the adductor muscle, visceral mass, ctenidium, mantle, and foot. Following collection, all samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until nucleic acid extraction.

Genome size estimation and heterozygosity analysis

A small-fragment DNA library was constructed using DNA extracted from the adductor muscle of G. rochechouartii, with an insert size of 300–400 bp. The library was sequenced in paired-end mode on the DNBSEQ platform, yielding 195.57 Gb of raw data (Table 1). Following filtering and deduplication, 186.63 Gb of high-quality clean data were retained. Quality assessment with FastQC v0.11.320 indicated a Q20 value of 98.97% and a GC content of 37.10%. A random subset of 10,000 read pairs was aligned via Blastn (v2.11.0+) against the NCBI database, and no significant exogenous contamination was detected. Genome characteristics, including size, heterozygosity, and repetitive sequence proportion, were estimated using GCE v1.0.021 based on K-mer analysis, while ploidy was inferred using Smudgeplot v0.2.3dev22. The estimated genome size of G. rochechouartii was approximately 2,662 Mb, with a corrected estimate of 2,649 Mb. The heterozygosity rate was 0.99% (Figures S3, S4).

Library preparation and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method for PacBio HiFi sequencing and Oxford Nanopore sequencing. DNA quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration was quantified using a Pultton DNA/Protein Analyzer. Following fragmentation with a Megaruptor 3 system and concentration with AMPure PB beads, SMRTbell libraries were constructed. These libraries underwent size selection (20 kb cutoff) via PippinHT, followed by primer annealing and polymerase binding. Sequencing was performed on the Pacific Biosciences Revio platform, generating 96.43 Gb of raw data.

For Oxford Nanopore sequencing, libraries were prepared using the SQK-LSK110 Ligation Kit. The prepared libraries were loaded onto R9.4 flow cells and sequenced on a PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK), yielding 61.04 Gb of raw data.

Hi-C library construction was performed through a series of steps including crosslinking, chromatin solubilisation, and enzymatic digestion. Following ligation of crosslinked fragments and purification of DNA by phenol-chloroform extraction, the Hi-C library construction process involved amplification and circularisation to generate single-stranded circular DNA (ssCir DNA) as the final product. The qualified library was sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform, yielding 199.5 Gb of raw data (Table 1).

RNA samples underwent poly-A enrichment, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis and circular library preparation using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit V3.1 (Cat#1000005276, MGI). The resulting libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform, generating 5.38 Gb of transcriptomic data that were utilised for genome annotation (Table 1).

Draft genome assembly

A draft genome of G. rochechouartii was assembled through the integration of PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long reads, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) ultra-long reads, and high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing data. The initial assembly was constructed from PacBio HiFi reads using HiFiasm v0.19.923. To mitigate potential genome size overestimation resulting from the retention of heterozygous regions in this highly heterozygous genome, the preliminary assembly was subsequently processed with purge_haplotigs24 for haplotype deduplication. The final assembly spanned 3.04 Gb, with the longest contig measuring 333.46 Mb, an average contig length of 39.43 Mb, an N50 contig length of 27.26 Mb, and a GC content of 35.9%.

Based on the principles that intra-chromosomal interactions are significantly more frequent than inter-chromosomal interactions, and that the interaction frequency decreases with increasing linear distance within a chromosome, contigs and scaffolds were partitioned, anchored, ordered, oriented, and merged to generate a chromosome-level genome assembly. The obtained Hi-C sequencing data were preprocessed using HiCUP v0.7.225. An interaction map of the G. rochechouartii genome was then constructed using Juicer v1.626, and then visualized and corrected with JuiceBox v1.11.0820. After aligning the Hi-C data to the reference genome, the proportion of paired-end reads in which both Read1 and Read2 were uniquely mapped and correctly paired was 35.23%. The genome was subsequently corrected and anchored using the filtered alignment results, ultimately assigning 99.77% of the genomic sequences to 19 chromosomes. (Fig. 1a).

Telomere identification and gap closure

To achieve a telomere-to-telomere genome assembly, we aligned all ONT ultra-long reads to the chromosomal sequences using minimap2 v2.2427. Reads uniquely mapped within 100 bp of chromosome ends were collected, with those containing artifactual chimeric sequences subsequently filtered out. The retained reads were analysed for alignment patterns, designating the read showing the median extension length as the reference and the remainder as queries. The medaka_consensus tool was employed to reassemble these telomeric reads into consensus sequences. These consensus sequences were then aligned to both ends of each chromosome using BLASTN28. Telomeric sequences in the chromosomal assembly were finally replaced based on alignment positions where sequence coverage exceeded 90%.

Gaps between contigs were filled using TGS-GapCloser29, which utilises coverage relationships between ONT ultra-long reads and pre-assembled contigs to extend and bridge contig sequences. The extended genome assembly was polished with Pilon v1.2330 using short-read data. The final T2T genome assembly of G. rochechouartii spanned 2.99 Gb (Table 2). Telomeric repeat sequences (TTAGGG/AATCCC) were systematically identified across the genome, with 23 telomeres detected at the ends of the 19 chromosomes (Fig. 1b).

Repetitive sequence annotation

Repetitive sequences were identified and annotated using a combination of de novo and homology-based approaches. An initial de novo prediction was performed with TRF v4.0931. A custom repeat library was then constructed using RepeatModeler open-1.0.1132 and LTR_FINDER_parallel v1.0.733, which was subsequently employed for de novo prediction in conjunction with RepeatMasker open-4.0.934. Finally, homology-based prediction was conducted using RepeatMasker open-4.0.934 and its integrated RepeatProteinMask tool against the RepBase database (http://www.girinst.org/repbase). In the G. rochechouartii genome, repetitive sequences totalling 1.83 Gb were identified, accounting for 61.27% of the entire assembly. The five most abundant categories of repetitive sequences, in descending order of proportion, were: DNA transposons (20.47%), unclassified repeats (19.37%), LTR retrotransposons (13.35%), LINE elements (5.77%), and SINE elements (0.79%) (Fig. 1c, Table 3).

Protein-coding gene prediction and annotation

Functional annotation of protein-coding genes was performed through an integrated approach combining multiple prediction methods. Homology-based prediction was first conducted using protein sequences from four representative bivalve species (Sinanodonta woodiana, Potamilus streckersoni, Mercenaria mercenaria, and Magallana angulata) retrieved from the NCBI database. Ab initio prediction was subsequently performed using Augustus v3.3.235 and Genscan36. For transcript-based prediction, RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the genome assembly using HISAT2 v2.1.037, followed by transcript assembly with StringTie v1.3.538 in ‘–mix’ mode. Full-length transcripts derived from RNA-seq data, and their coding regions were predicted using TransDecoder v5.5.0 (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder). Gene prediction accuracy was assessed and optimised using BUSCO v5.7.139 with Augustus35 in self-training mode against a lineage-specific dataset. These individual predictions were integrated using MAKER2 v2.31.1040 and HiFAP to generate a consolidated gene set. The final annotation comprised 28,808 protein-coding genes, with average lengths of 31.61 kb for mRNAs, 275.08 bp for exons, and 5,493 bp for introns. Functional annotation was performed through sequence similarity searches and domain/motif analyses against five protein databases: TrEMBL, SwissProt, NR, KOG/COG, and KEGG. Overall, 98.23% of the annotated protein-coding genes (representing 97.78% of predicted mRNA sequences) received at least one functional assignment from these databases (Figure S5).

Non-coding RNA prediction and annotation

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) were annotated using methods specific to each RNA type. Transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE v1.3.141. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences were detected by aligning the genome assembly to known rRNA sequences from closely related species through BLASTN. MicroRNA (miRNA) and small nuclear RNA (snRNA) annotations were generated using the INFERNAL software package from Rfam 14.842, employing covariance models from the Rfam database for genome-wide screening. In total, 3,130 ncRNAs were annotated, consisting of 526 rRNAs, 725 miRNAs, 396 snRNAs, and 1,483 tRNAs (Table S6).

Gene prediction and annotation

Genomic data from the following species were retrieved from public databases for comparative analysis: Sinosolenaia oleivora, S. woodiana, Unio delphinus, Corbicula fluminea, Dreissena polymorpha, Margaritifera margaritifera, Megalonaias nervosa, Sinohyriopsis cumingii, P. streckersoni, Pomacea canaliculata, and M. mercenaria (Table S7). Where multiple transcript isoforms were available for a gene, the longest transcript was selected for subsequent analysis. Protein-coding genes encoding products shorter than 30 amino acids or containing premature stop codons were excluded from the analysis. An all-versus-all blastp search (E-value threshold: 1e-5) was conducted to evaluate sequence similarity among all protein sequences. Orthologous groups were identified using OrthoFinder v2.5.543 with an inflation parameter set to 1.5. Of the 28,808 annotated genes in G. rochechouartii, 28,453 (98.8%) were clustered into 15,729 gene families (Fig. 2a,b).

Evolutionary analysis and divergence time prediction

Gene families encoding proteins shorter than 100 amino acids were excluded from subsequent analyses. For each single-copy gene family, protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.52544, and the resulting alignments were back-translated according to the protein alignment into codon-aware nucleotide sequences. These single-copy gene alignments were then concatenated into a supermatrix using three distinct filtering approaches: the full alignment without filtering, and two methods implemented in Gblocks v0.91b (method 1 and method 2). The supermatrix constructed for species phylogenetic analysis in this study utilized data from 12 species, comprising 457,275 sites. This process generated three datasets: the complete supermatrix, phase-1 sites, and fourfold degenerate (4D) sites. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were reconstructed for each dataset using RAxML v8.2.1245. The final species tree topology was determined based on congruence among the resulting nine trees and established taxonomic relationships.

Divergence times were estimated using MCMCTree v4.9 within the PAML package46, incorporating four fossil-based calibration points obtained from the Timetree database (Table S8)11. Based on phylogenetic analysis of single-copy orthologous genes, G. rochechouartii belongs to the Margaritiferidae. Among all currently published Unionoidea genomes, it is most closely related to M. margaritifera, and together they form a monophyletic clade. This result is consistent with Chen et al.14. It is estimated that this clade diverged approximately 172.7 (167.9–177.9) million years ago (Fig. 2c).

Technical Validation

Genome assembly and assessment

The quality of the G. rochechouartii genome assembly was systematically evaluated with respect to sequence consistency and integrity. Illumina short reads, PacBio long reads, and ultra-long ONT reads were mapped back to the assembled genome to assess accuracy, yielding a short-read mapping rate of 99.27%, a long-read genome coverage of 99.97%, and an ultra-long ONT read alignment rate of 99.99%. K-mer-based quality estimation was performed using Merqury v1.3, incorporating both short and long reads, which produced a high quality value (QV). Assessment of genome assembly completeness with BUSCO v5.7.1 (mollusca_odb10) and Compleasm v0.2.6 yielded high completeness scores of 93.41% and 93.99%, respectively (Table 4). The assembly exhibited low homozygosity and strong diagonal interaction signals in the Hi-C contact matrix (Fig. 1a). Collectively, these results confirm the high quality, reliability, and accuracy of the G. rochechouartii genome assembly.

Structural and functional annotation

Gene annotation quality was evaluated using BUSCO v5.7.1 and Compleasm v0.2.6. Among the highly conserved G. rochechouartii core proteins, 4,917 (92.86%) were identified in the annotation set, of which 90.5% were single-copy and 2.36% were duplicated. Of the remaining conserved genes, 50 (0.94%) were fragmented and 328 (6.19%) were missing (Table 4). Structural annotations also exhibited strong conservation compared to closely related species. In summary, the gene annotations demonstrate high accuracy and completeness.
