Metric-Gradient Projection for Stable Multi-Agent Policy Learning
Zuyuan Zhang, Sizhe Tang, Mahdi Imani, Tian Lan

Abstract
General-sum multi-agent learning is often governed by a stacked update field in which each agent’s policy update changes the optimization landscape faced by the others. This coupling can entangle an integrable component of collective improvement with cyclic interaction dynamics, leading to slow or unstable multi-agent learning. Existing approaches, such as regularization, credit assignment, and consensus methods, stabilize MARL through local or algorithmic modifications; HPML complements them by projecting the joint update field onto a metric-gradient component. We introduce HPML ( H odge- P rojected M ulti-agent L earning), which views the joint update field of a multi-agent system as an element of an L 2 L^{2} space of vector fields and computes a Hodge-type projection onto the closest metric-gradient potential flow. HPML follows the projected component as the update direction, yielding the closest metric-gradient field under the chosen metric and sampling measure. The projection is defined variationally, characterized by a Poisson-type equation, and implemented through graph-based and amortized neural realizations that recover projected directions from samples. We show that the projected dynamics admit a Lyapunov potential and yield equilibrium-gap bounds with an explicit additive non-potentiality term. Controlled experiments validate the geometric mechanism, and CTDE benchmarks show improved stability and normalized return when HPML is used as a plug-in projection layer in MARL pipelines.

2.1
Markov Games and Policy Space

We consider a discounted finite Markov game with
n
agents

\mathcal{G}=\big(\mathcal{S},\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},P,\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\gamma\big),\qquad\gamma\in(0,1),

where
\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space,
\mathcal{A}_{i}
is agent
i
’s finite action set,
P(\cdot\mid s,a)
is the transition kernel, and
r_{i}(s,a)
is agent
i
’s per-stage reward.
A stationary policy for agent
i
is a mapping
\pi_{i}(\cdot\mid s)\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i})
, and we write
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})
for a joint policy.
Let

\Pi:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i})

denote the stationary-policy set, viewed through the standard coordinate embedding as a compact convex set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Each agent’s discounted return under
\pi\in\Pi
is

J_{i}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}r_{i}(s_{t},a_{t})\Big].

We write
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a continuous joint field on policy space.
A canonical game-theoretic choice is the negative pseudo-gradient operator

F(\pi):=\big(-\nabla_{\pi_{1}}J_{1}(\pi),\ldots,-\nabla_{\pi_{n}}J_{n}(\pi)\big),

which is the operator used in the VI formulation below.
More generally, HPML can be applied to any continuous joint update field defined on
\mathcal{X}
.
To avoid sign ambiguity, we distinguish the VI operator
F_{\mathrm{VI}}
from an ascent/update field
U
.
For the canonical game operator above,
U=-F_{\mathrm{VI}}=(\nabla_{\pi_{i}}J_{i})_{i}
.
The projection layer is applied to the supplied update field
U
; if one instead starts from a VI operator, the projected update direction is obtained by applying HPML to
-F_{\mathrm{VI}}
.
In general-sum games, such a field need not be the gradient of any single scalar potential on
\mathcal{X}
; this lack of global integrability is the source of the cyclic behavior that HPML targets.
See Remark
B.1
and Remark
B.2
for the distinction between the equilibrium operator and implementation-level parameter updates.

2.2
Variational Inequality and Duality Gap

Many first-order equilibrium notions for differentiable games can be expressed as a variational inequality (VI).
Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be nonempty, closed, and convex, and let
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Unless otherwise stated,
\left\langle u,v\right\rangle:=u^{\top}v
denotes the Euclidean inner product.

Definition 2.1

(Stampacchia VI)
.

The variational inequality
\mathrm{VI}(\mathcal{X},F)
is to find
x^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}
such that

\left\langle F(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}\right\rangle\geq 0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

When
x
parametrizes a joint policy
\pi
and

F(\pi)=(-\nabla_{\pi_{i}}J_{i}(\pi))_{i=1}^{n},

a solution of
\mathrm{VI}(\mathcal{X},F)
satisfies the standard first-order Nash condition for a differentiable game.
It is generally necessary for Nash equilibrium, and it becomes sufficient under additional structure such as playerwise concavity.

Definition 2.2

(Duality gap (Stampacchia gap))
.

For
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, define

\operatorname{Gap}(\bar{x}):=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle F(\bar{x}),\bar{x}-x\right\rangle.

We use
\operatorname{Gap}(\bar{x})
as the equilibrium-quality metric throughout.
By construction
\operatorname{Gap}(\bar{x})\geq 0
, and
\operatorname{Gap}(\bar{x})\leq\varepsilon
is equivalent to

\left\langle F(\bar{x}),x-\bar{x}\right\rangle\geq-\varepsilon,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

Lemma 2.3

(Zero gap implies a VI solution)
.

If
\operatorname{Gap}(\bar{x})=0
, then
\bar{x}
solves
\mathrm{VI}(\mathcal{X},F)
in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

See Remark
B.3
and Remark
B.4
for the relation to the Minty gap and to monotonicity-based merit-function interpretations.

2.3
Jacobian Symmetry and Local Integrability

Assume
F
is differentiable on
\mathrm{ri}(\mathcal{X})
, and let
J(x):=\nabla F(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
We decompose

J(x)=S(x)+A(x),\qquad S(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(J(x)+J(x)^{\top}\big),\qquad A(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(J(x)-J(x)^{\top}\big).

The symmetric part
S(x)
is the locally potential-like component of the linearization, while the antisymmetric part
A(x)
is a local obstruction to integrability and a diagnostic for cyclic interaction dynamics.

Definition 2.4

(Antisymmetric energy)
.

The antisymmetric energy at
x
is

\mathcal{R}(x):=\left\|A(x)\right\|_{F}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

Proposition 2.5

(Gradient fields have symmetric Jacobians; a converse on open sets)
.

(i) If there exists a twice-differentiable potential
\phi
such that
F=\nabla\phi
on an open set containing
\mathcal{X}
, then
J(x)=\nabla^{2}\phi(x)
is symmetric for all
x
in that open set; hence
A(x)\equiv 0
and
\mathcal{R}(x)\equiv 0
on
\mathcal{X}
.

(ii) Conversely, if
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open and simply connected,
F\in C^{1}(U)
, and
A(x)\equiv 0
for all
x\in U
, then there exists a scalar potential
\phi
on
U
such that
F=\nabla\phi
(unique up to an additive constant).

Proposition
2.5
shows that antisymmetry in
J(x)
is a local obstruction to integrability.
HPML will later replace this local diagnostic by a global
L^{2}
projection at the vector-field level.
See Example
B.5
for the canonical bilinear cyclic-interaction example and Remark
B.6
for the local/global distinction.

This section defines HPML as a metric-aware least-squares projection of the joint update field onto the class of potential flows.
The main objects used later are the projected field, its orthogonality property, and the induced non-potential residual.

3.1
Metric-Gradient Projection

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonempty closed convex set.
Throughout Sections
3
–
5
,
F
denotes the supplied update field to be projected.
All continuous projection statements are understood in coordinates on the affine hull of
\mathcal{X}
; for product-simplex policy spaces this can be implemented by dropping redundant simplex coordinates or by working in an unconstrained parameterization such as logits.
Fix a symmetric positive definite matrix
M\succ 0
and a reference probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
.
For vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define

\langle u,v\rangle_{M}:=u^{\top}Mv,\qquad\|u\|_{M}:=\sqrt{u^{\top}Mu}.

For vector fields
U,V\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, define

\langle U,V\rangle_{L^{2}(M,\mu)}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\langle U(x),V(x)\rangle_{M}\big].

For a scalar potential
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the metric gradient is

\nabla_{M}\Phi(x):=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi(x).

Definition 3.1

(Metric-gradient least-squares projection)
.

Let
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be square-integrable under
\mu
.
Define

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}:=\{\nabla_{M}\Phi:\ \Phi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})\},

and define
\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F
as the minimizer-induced field of

\inf_{\Phi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\|F(x)-\nabla_{M}\Phi(x)\|_{M}^{2}\big].

If
\Phi^{\star}
is any minimizer, the projected field is

\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F:=\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star},

which is unique
\mu
-a.e., while
\Phi^{\star}
itself is defined up to an additive constant.

Definition
3.1
is the core object of HPML: it replaces the original joint field by its closest potential-flow approximation under the geometry induced by
M
and the sampling distribution
\mu
.
We assume the continuous variational problem admits a minimizer; otherwise,
\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F
can be interpreted as the projection onto the
L^{2}(M,\mu)
-closure of metric-gradient fields.
The finite graph and neural implementations used later are finite-dimensional approximations of this same objective.
A weak Poisson-type characterization of the same projection is given in Proposition
C.2
.

3.2
Orthogonality and Residual Energy

Let

G^{\star}:=\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F,\qquad R:=F-G^{\star}.

The projected field and residual satisfy the following variational orthogonality property.

Lemma 3.2

(Normal equation / orthogonality)
.

For every test potential
\Phi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
,

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\langle R(x),\nabla_{M}\Phi(x)\rangle_{M}\big]=0.

Equivalently,
R
is
L^{2}(M,\mu)
-orthogonal to all metric-gradient fields.

Corollary 3.3

(Pythagorean identity)
.

With
G^{\star}
and
R
defined above,

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\|F(x)\|_{M}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\|G^{\star}(x)\|_{M}^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\|R(x)\|_{M}^{2}\big].

Corollary
3.3
separates the field into a potential component and an orthogonal non-potential residual.
This is the Hodge-type structure used by HPML: an
L^{2}(M,\mu)
-orthogonal split into a metric-gradient component and a residual. On domains or complexes with richer topology, the residual can be further refined into curl-like and harmonic components; on the sample graph, the same idea appears as a cut-space/cycle-space decomposition of edge flows. HPML uses the coarser projected-gradient/residual split because the update only requires the integrable direction. Both components are relative to the chosen metric
M
and sampling measure
\mu
, so
E
measures non-potentiality over the region represented by the data.

3.3
Non-Potentiality

Definition 3.4

(Non-potentiality)
.

Define the residual field

R(x):=F(x)-\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F(x)

and its
L^{2}(M,\mu)
magnitude

E:=\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\|R(x)\|_{M}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}.

We call
E
the
non-potentiality
of
F
under metric
M
and sampling measure
\mu
.

The quantity
E
is a global, geometry-aware measure of how far the joint update field deviates from a potential flow over the region emphasized by
\mu
.
If
E=0
, then
F
agrees
\mu
-a.e. with a metric-gradient field; if
E>0
, the residual
R
captures the irreducible non-potential component that can sustain cyclic interaction dynamics.
Closed-loop circulation is carried entirely by this residual; see Proposition
C.5
and Remark
C.6
.

3.4
HPML Update

Let
\operatorname{Retr}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{X}
be a feasibility operator.
HPML updates by following only the projected component:

x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Retr}\Big(x_{t}+\eta_{t}\,\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F(x_{t})\Big).

(1)

In practice, the projection is computed approximately from samples by solving the same variational problem on a discrete structure or by fitting an amortized potential model.

4.1
Graph Samples and Edge Flow

Let
\{x^{1},\dots,x^{N}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
be sampled joint-policy points.
We build a weighted undirected
k
-NN graph
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
on these samples, with node set
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\dots,N\}
and symmetric edge weights
w_{ij}>0
.
Choose an orientation for each edge and let
\mathcal{E}^{\rightarrow}
denote the oriented edge set.

We represent scalar potentials as node values
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Let
B\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}^{\rightarrow}|\times N}
be the oriented incidence matrix, so that the discrete gradient is the edge flow
B\phi
.

Given field estimates
F_{i}:=F(x^{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the edge
1
-form

\omega_{e}:=\Big\langle M\,\tfrac{1}{2}(F_{i}+F_{j}),\,x^{j}-x^{i}\Big\rangle,\qquad e=(i\to j)\in\mathcal{E}^{\rightarrow}.

(2)

This is the discrete analogue of the continuous
1
-form
\langle MF,dx\rangle
.
Its metric consistency with potential differences is formalized in Proposition
D.4
.

4.2
Discrete Projection and Non-Potentiality

Let
W=\operatorname{diag}(w_{e})
be the diagonal matrix of edge weights and define
\|u\|_{W}^{2}:=u^{\top}Wu
.

Definition 4.1

(Discrete potential-flow projection)
.

Given an edge flow
\omega
, define

\phi^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\omega-B\phi\|_{W}^{2}.

The projected potential edge flow is

\omega_{\mathrm{pot}}:=B\phi^{\star},

and the residual component is

\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}:=\omega-\omega_{\mathrm{pot}}.

The optimality condition yields the graph Poisson system

L\phi^{\star}=B^{\top}W\omega,\qquad L:=B^{\top}WB,

(3)

with
\phi^{\star}
unique up to an additive constant on each connected component.

Lemma 4.2

(Orthogonality of the discrete residual)
.

Let
\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}:=\omega-B\phi^{\star}
. Then

(B\psi)^{\top}W\,\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}=0,\qquad\forall\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

i.e., the residual is orthogonal to all discrete gradient flows.

Corollary 4.3

(Discrete energy decomposition)
.

With
\omega_{\mathrm{pot}}=B\phi^{\star}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\omega-\omega_{\mathrm{pot}}
,

\|\omega\|_{W}^{2}=\|\omega_{\mathrm{pot}}\|_{W}^{2}+\|\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}\|_{W}^{2}.

The graph interpretation of
\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}
as a cycle-space component is recorded in Remark
D.1
.

4.3
Discrete Non-Potentiality

Definition 4.4

(Discrete non-potentiality proxy)
.

Define

\mathrm{NonPot}(\omega):=\frac{\|\omega_{\mathrm{cyc}}\|_{W}^{2}}{\|\omega\|_{W}^{2}+\varepsilon},\qquad\varepsilon>0.

This quantity measures the fraction of edge-flow energy unexplained by any potential flow, and serves as a computable proxy for the continuous residual energy
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|R(x)\|_{M}^{2}]
.

Lemma 4.5

(Basic properties of
\mathrm{NonPot}
)
.

For any
\omega
and
\varepsilon>0
, one has

0\leq\mathrm{NonPot}(\omega)\leq 1.

Moreover,
\mathrm{NonPot}(\omega)\approx 0
iff
\omega
is approximately potential on the graph, and it increases with the energy of circulation components supported by cycles.

When samples cluster around recurring joint behaviors,
\mathrm{NonPot}(\omega)
can be read as an empirical certificate of convention-level cyclic interaction dynamics; see Remark
D.2
.

5.1
Amortized Potential Projection

To avoid repeated large linear solves, we also use an amortized alternative that parameterizes the scalar potential and recovers the projected direction by automatic differentiation.

Let
\Phi_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a neural potential model.
Its induced metric-gradient field is

g_{\theta}(x):=\nabla_{M}\Phi_{\theta}(x)=M^{-1}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\theta}(x).

Given samples
\{x_{k}\}_{k=1}^{B}
and field estimates
\{\widehat{F}(x_{k})\}
, we fit
\Phi_{\theta}
by minimizing

\min_{\theta}\;\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta):=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\bigl\|\widehat{F}(x_{k})-g_{\theta}(x_{k})\bigr\|_{M}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{g}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\Phi_{\theta}(x_{k})\Bigr)^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{wd}}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}.

(4)

The gauge term removes the additive-constant ambiguity of the potential.
Once trained, the amortized projected update is

x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Retr}\bigl(x_{t}+\eta_{t}\,g_{\theta}(x_{t})\bigr).

The full amortized procedure is given in Algorithm
3
; implementation schedules and metric variants are summarized in Remark
E.1

5.2
CTDE Parameter-Space Realization

Let

\theta:=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

denote the concatenated actor parameters, and let

\widehat{F}(\theta):=\bigl(\widehat{g}_{1}(\theta),\dots,\widehat{g}_{n}(\theta)\bigr)

be the stacked CTDE actor-update field.
A canonical example is a centralized-advantage estimator of the form

\widehat{g}_{i}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta_{i}}(a_{i}\mid o_{i})\,\widehat{A}(s,a)\Bigr].

HPML projects the stacked field before the actor update:

g_{t}\approx\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}\widehat{F}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{i,t+1}=\operatorname{Retr}\bigl(\theta_{i,t}+\eta_{t}[g_{t}]_{i}\bigr),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

This realizes HPML as a plug-in projection layer on top of standard CTDE updates without changing decentralized execution.
The full training loop appears in Algorithm
4
; the relation between policy-space theory and parameter-space implementation is discussed in Remark
E.2
.
In implementation, HPML changes only the actor-update direction. Rollout collection, critic training, observation preprocessing, reward normalization, and decentralized execution remain the same as in the underlying MAPPO-style pipeline. The graph variant periodically builds a local sample graph from recent stacked update-field estimates, solves the graph Poisson system, and lifts node potentials to actor directions. The neural variant amortizes the same projection objective through a scalar potential network. Thus the additional cost comes from projection refresh and graph-lifting or neural inner optimization, while the critic, reward signal, policy architecture, and execution interface remain unchanged.

5.3
CTDE Integrability Interpretation

The decomposition

F=\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F+R

gives a direct CTDE interpretation.
The projected term is the component of the joint update field compatible with ascent of a single scalar potential, and the residual
R
is the non-potential part outside that integrable structure.

In CTDE, let
z
denote the chosen joint coordinates, either policy coordinates or actor-parameter coordinates, and let

F(z)=(g_{1}(z),\dots,g_{n}(z))

denote the stacked actor-update field induced by critic-based local improvement directions.
The next proposition states that the residual is exactly the obstruction to joint integrability. The key distinction below is between
centralized evaluation
and
exact centralized optimization
: a centralized critic or a single shared reward does not by itself eliminate the residual unless the realized joint actor-update field equals the exact gradient of one common scalar objective in the full joint parameter space.

Proposition 5.1

(Residual as an obstruction to joint greedy integrability)
.

Let
U\subset\mathrm{ri}(\mathcal{X})
be a connected open set on which
F
is continuously differentiable.

(i) If
R\equiv 0
on
U
, then there exists a scalar potential
\Phi
on
U
such that

F=\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F=\nabla_{M}\Phi\qquad\text{on }U.

Hence the joint actor-update field is integrable on
U
, and sufficiently small projected updates admit
\Phi
as a Lyapunov function.
(ii) If there exists a closed piecewise-
C^{1}
loop
C\subset U
such that

\oint_{C}\langle MF(x),dx\rangle=\oint_{C}\langle MR(x),dx\rangle\neq 0,

then no scalar potential
\Phi
on
U
can satisfy
F=\nabla_{M}\Phi
on
U
.
Consequently, the simultaneous actor-update field has no exact representation as ascent of a single common scalar objective on
U
.

2.2
.
If
U(x)
is an ascent direction, then
F(x)=-U(x)
; HPML constructs a potential
\Phi
by projecting
U=-F
so that the projected ascent direction is
\nabla\Phi
.
Equivalently, the VI operator decomposes as
F=-\nabla\Phi+R
, where
R
is the residual obstruction to exact potential ascent.

6.1
Assumptions

Assumption 6.1

(Geometry and smoothness)
.

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is nonempty, closed, convex, and compact with diameter

D:=\max_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\|x-y\|.

The potential
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and
L
-smooth:

\|\nabla\Phi(x)-\nabla\Phi(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

By compactness and continuity of
\nabla\Phi
, define

G:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|\nabla\Phi(x)\|<\infty,\qquad\Phi_{\max}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x),\qquad\Phi_{\min}:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Phi(x).

Assumption 6.2

(Residual bound)
.

The VI operator admits a decomposition

F(x)=-\nabla\Phi(x)+R(x),

where the residual satisfies

\|R(x)\|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\forall x\in\mathcal{X}.

An
L^{2}
version is given in Theorem
F.3
.

Assumption 6.3

(Projection accuracy)
.

At each step, HPML uses an ascent direction
g_{t}
such that

\|g_{t}-\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\|\leq\delta_{t},

where
\nabla\Phi
is the exact projected ascent direction.

6.2
Lyapunov Improvement Under Potential Flow

We first isolate the ideal projected dynamics.

Theorem 6.4

(Monotone Lyapunov improvement for projected gradient ascent)
.

Under Assumption
6.1
, consider the exact projected ascent step

x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}\bigl(x_{t}+\eta\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\bigr),\qquad\eta\leq\frac{1}{L}.

Then

\Phi(x_{t+1})\geq\Phi(x_{t})+\Bigl(\frac{1}{\eta}-\frac{L}{2}\Bigr)\|x_{t+1}-x_{t}\|^{2}\geq\Phi(x_{t})+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|x_{t+1}-x_{t}\|^{2}.

Define the projected-step mapping

\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t}):=\frac{1}{\eta}(x_{t+1}-x_{t}),\qquad x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}+\eta\nabla\Phi(x_{t})).

Then
\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x)=0
iff
x
is first-order stationary for the constrained maximization of
\Phi
.
Moreover, Theorem
6.4
implies

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\eta}(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min}),

so the exact projected dynamics cannot sustain a nontrivial cycle.
The projection inequality used in the proof is recorded separately in Lemma
F.1
.

6.3
Gap Bounds with a Residual Non-Potential Component

We now relate the projected dynamics to the VI gap of the original operator

F=-\nabla\Phi+R.

Lemma 6.5

(Residual contribution to the VI gap)
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\operatorname{Gap}(x)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\langle-\nabla\Phi(x)+R(x),x-u\rangle\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x)+D\|R(x)\|,

where

\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x):=\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\langle-\nabla\Phi(x),x-u\rangle=\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\nabla\Phi(x),u-x\rangle.

Lemma 6.6

(Gap bound via projected-step mapping)
.

Under Assumption
6.1
, let

x^{+}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x+\eta\nabla\Phi(x)),\qquad\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x)=\frac{1}{\eta}(x^{+}-x).

Then

\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x)\leq(D+\eta G)\,\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x)\|.

Theorem 6.7

(Gap bound with additive non-potentiality)
.

Under Assumptions
6.1
and
6.2
, run the exact projected ascent

x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}+\eta\nabla\Phi(x_{t})),\qquad\eta\leq\frac{1}{L}.

Let

\hat{t}\in\arg\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|,\qquad\hat{x}_{T}:=x_{\hat{t}}.

Then

\operatorname{Gap}(\hat{x}_{T})\leq(D+\eta G)\sqrt{\frac{2(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\eta}}+D\varepsilon.

An
L^{2}
output-law version of the same statement is given in Theorem
F.3
, and the circulation interpretation of the additive residual term is summarized in Remark
F.2
.

Reading the residual term.

The bound certifies the original VI operator, not only the projected potential flow. The first term is the stationarity error of the projected dynamics, controlled by Lyapunov improvement, while the additive term
D\varepsilon
is the contribution of the non-potential residual in the original field. Thus HPML chooses an integrable update direction, but any certificate for the original game must still account for residual non-potentiality. The
L^{2}
version replaces the uniform residual bound by residual energy under the output-iterate law, matching the non-potentiality measure in Definition
3.4
and the graph proxy in Definition
4.4
.

Inexact projection.

Graph and neural projections are approximate in practice. Appendix
F
extends Theorem
6.7
to directions
g_{t}
satisfying
\|g_{t}-\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\|\leq\bar{\delta}
, adding projection-error terms while preserving the separation between structural non-potentiality and projection approximation error.

We finally quantify the effect of using an approximate projected direction
g_{t}
.
Let

x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}+\eta g_{t}),\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t}):=\frac{1}{\eta}(x_{t+1}-x_{t}).

Theorem 6.8

(Effect of projection error)
.

Under Assumptions
6.1
,
6.2
, and
6.3
,
run

x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}+\eta g_{t}),\qquad\eta\leq\frac{1}{L},

and suppose
\delta_{t}\leq\bar{\delta}
for all
t
.
Let

\hat{t}\in\arg\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|,\hat{x}_{T}:=x_{\hat{t}}.

Then

\operatorname{Gap}(\hat{x}_{T})\leq\bigl(D+\eta(G+\bar{\delta})\bigr)\sqrt{\frac{4(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\eta}+4\bar{\delta}^{2}}+D(\varepsilon+\bar{\delta}).

The auxiliary inexact gap-mapping estimate and a term-by-term interpretation of the bound are recorded in Lemma
F.5
and Remark
F.6
.

8
Conclusion

We introduced Hodge-Projected Multi-Agent Learning (HPML), a geometric projection method that separates a joint multi-agent update field into a metric-gradient potential component and a non-potential residual. The projected component admits Lyapunov-style improvement, and the residual captures cyclic structure that appears explicitly in the VI-gap bound. Graph-based and amortized implementations make HPML usable as a CTDE plug-in, and the experiments show reduced circulation in controlled games and improved stability and normalized return on the evaluated Melting Pot suite. Key limitations are the dependence on the chosen metric, sampling distribution, field-estimation quality, and projection approximation error.

Appendix B
Additional Notes for Section
2

Remark B.1

(Equilibrium operator and algorithmic update field)
.

For equilibrium analysis, the canonical VI operator is the negative pseudo-gradient

F_{\mathrm{VI}}(\pi)=\big(-\nabla_{\pi_{1}}J_{1}(\pi),\ldots,-\nabla_{\pi_{n}}J_{n}(\pi)\big).

The corresponding simultaneous ascent/update field is
U=-F_{\mathrm{VI}}
.
HPML projects the supplied update field
U
; if the available object is a VI operator, one first changes sign and applies the projection to
-F_{\mathrm{VI}}
.
Some sections use
F
as a generic field to avoid excessive notation, but the intended sign is always determined by whether the object is being used for VI gap evaluation or for an actual update.

Remark B.2

(Primal policies and unconstrained parameterizations)
.

The theory is stated on the compact convex feasible set
\mathcal{X}
of stationary policies in primal coordinates.
In implementations, one often optimizes unconstrained parameters such as logits and maps them back to
\mathcal{X}

through a retraction or mirror map such as softmax.
This changes the coordinate representation of the iterate, but not the conceptual role of
F

as a field defined on feasible policies.

Remark B.3

(Stampacchia and Minty gaps)
.

Definition
2.2
uses the Stampacchia duality gap

\operatorname{Gap}(\bar{x})=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle F(\bar{x}),\bar{x}-x\right\rangle,

which evaluates the operator at the reference point
\bar{x}
.
Another common quantity is the Minty gap

\operatorname{Gap}_{\mathrm{Minty}}(\bar{x}):=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle F(x),\bar{x}-x\right\rangle,

which corresponds to the Minty VI formulation.
The present paper uses the Stampacchia gap because it aligns directly with the projected operator analysis developed later.

Remark B.4

(Gap and monotonicity)
.

Lemma
2.3
is purely algebraic and does not require monotonicity.
When
F
is monotone, however, the duality gap becomes a standard merit function that quantitatively certifies closeness to the VI solution set and supports classical convergence arguments.
In general-sum games, the induced operator is typically non-monotone, which is precisely why cycling may persist and why a structural modification of the field is useful.

Example B.5

(Canonical bilinear cyclic-interaction field)
.

Consider the bilinear min–max problem

\min_{u}\max_{v}\;u^{\top}v

with associated field

F(u,v)=(v,-u).

Its Jacobian is

J=\nabla F=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad S=0,\qquad A=J.

Hence the field is purely antisymmetric and has no potential component in the local Jacobian sense.
This is the canonical example showing that simultaneous first-order updates may follow cyclic orbits around the origin rather than converge.

Remark B.6

(Local and global non-potentiality)
.

Proposition
2.5
identifies antisymmetry of the Jacobian as a
local
obstruction to integrability.
This is not yet a global statement: even if a field looks locally potential-like, global or sample-level obstructions may remain.
The role of HPML in the main text is to move from this local Jacobian diagnostic to a global
L^{2}
projection of the full joint field, thereby producing a residual that measures non-potentiality at the vector-field level.

Appendix C
Variational and Geometric Details for HPML

This appendix records the continuous Euler–Lagrange characterization of the projection,
its circulation interpretation, and several geometric remarks that are not needed in the main text
but clarify the structure behind HPML.

Lemma C.1

(Weighted integration by parts)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain or a flat torus.
Let
d\mu(x)=\rho(x)\,dx
with
0<\rho_{\min}\leq\rho(x)\leq\rho_{\max}<\infty
a.e. on
\mathcal{X}
.
Assume boundary conditions that eliminate boundary terms
(e.g., periodic boundary conditions on the torus; or
\rho\,U\cdot n=0
on
\partial\mathcal{X}
).
Then for any
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and any vector field
U
for which
\div_{\mu}U
is well-defined in
H^{-1}(\mathcal{X})
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu.

Equivalently,
\nabla
and
-\div_{\mu}
are adjoint under the
L^{2}(\mu)
pairing.

Proposition C.2

(Poisson-type characterization of the projection)
.

Assume
M\succ 0
is constant and
d\mu(x)=\rho(x)\,dx
with
\rho>0
.
Assume boundary conditions that remove boundary terms in Lemma
C.1
.
Let
\Phi^{\star}
be any minimizer in Definition
3.1
.
Then the projected field is
\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F=\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}
and
\Phi^{\star}
satisfies the weak equation

\mathcal{L}_{M,\mu}\Phi^{\star}=\div_{\mu}F,\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)=\div_{\mu}F.

Moreover,
\Phi^{\star}
is unique up to an additive constant; one may fix a gauge such as
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\Phi^{\star}]=0
.
A natural boundary condition implied by the variational optimality is the zero-flux residual condition

\rho\,\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}-F\big)\cdot n=0\quad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{X},

whenever traces are well-defined.

Remark C.3

(Local Jacobian diagnostic and global projection)
.

The Jacobian split in Section
2
is a local diagnostic:
antisymmetry in
\nabla F
signals local cyclic interaction behavior.
The HPML projection is a global
L^{2}(M,\mu)
construction on the full field.
For a locally linear field
F(x)\approx Bx
, Euclidean metric
M=I
, and isotropic sampling,
the closest gradient field corresponds to the symmetric part of
B
, namely

B\mapsto\tfrac{1}{2}(B+B^{\top})
.

Remark C.4

(Relation to Hodge decomposition)
.

Lemma
3.2
is the structural statement used in the main text.
On domains or complexes with appropriate topology and boundary conditions,
the residual can often be refined into curl-like and harmonic components.
The discrete construction used by HPML mirrors this picture on graphs or sample complexes,
but the main text only requires the projected gradient component and its orthogonal residual.

Convention-level cyclic interaction as circulation.

Cyclic interaction dynamics need not come from explicit symmetry.
In CTDE training, one often observes repeated revisits to a small family of recurring joint behaviors.

For a piecewise

Remark C.6

If one augments the graph to a higher-order simplicial complex, one can further refine the decomposition into gradient, curl, and harmonic components, but the graph-only variant already removes all non-gradient circulation supported by cycles.

Remark E.2

(Parameter-space realization under CTDE)
.

The main theory is stated on a convex feasible set of policy coordinates, whereas practical CTDE implementations often operate directly in actor-parameter space.
HPML is used there as an operator-level projection layer on the stacked update field.
This does not change the conceptual role of the residual: it still measures the non-integrable part of the joint improvement dynamics, but now in the chosen parameterization.

Remark E.3

(Relation to Bellman residuals and the multi-agent source of circulation)
.

The role of Proposition
5.1
is interpretive rather than evaluative.
It does
not
say that single-agent Bellman or TD operators are ill-defined.
The issue appears only after stacking several critic-driven local actor-improvement directions into one joint field.
Each block
g_{i}
may be a valid local greedy direction, yet the collection
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
may fail to be jointly integrable because the cross-agent response terms need not be symmetric.
That is the sense in which there is a “mismatch”: changing agent
j
can alter agent
i
’s improvement direction without a matching reciprocity in the reverse block.

This phenomenon is natural in multi-agent CTDE because the joint control problem is often factorized into simultaneously updated local blocks for scalability. The decisive distinction is again exact centralized optimization versus centralized evaluation alone. A centralized critic and even a single shared reward do not by themselves remove the obstruction if the implemented actor update is still assembled from local or approximate blocks. The obstruction disappears only when all agents are optimized as one super-agent by the exact gradient of a single centralized scalar objective in the full joint parameter space. Sampling other agents’ actions then contributes stochasticity, but not a structural non-potential cyclic component at the level of the exact expected field. Partial observability and approximation error can exacerbate the effect but are not necessary. Mathematically, non-potentiality is not exclusive to multi-agent systems, but multi-agent blockwise updates are a particularly natural mechanism generating it.

So the residual is not a Bellman-evaluation error. It is a structural inconsistency in the joint greedy-improvement dynamics induced by decentralized multi-agent updates.

E.1
CTDE integrability and residual-only cyclic dynamics

Remark E.4

(Origin of the non-potential cyclic component)
.

The nonzero loop integral in Proposition
5.1
is generated by a local asymmetry in the Jacobian of the one-form
MF
. Writing

Y(x):=MF(x),\qquad K(x):=\nabla Y(x)=M\nabla F(x),

joint integrability requires
K(x)=K(x)^{\top}
.
Equivalently, the antisymmetric part

B(x):=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(M\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x)^{\top}M\bigr)

must vanish.
If
B(x)\neq 0
, then for any sufficiently small parallelogram
C_{\varepsilon}(x;u,v)\subset U
,

\oint_{C_{\varepsilon}(x;u,v)}\langle MF(z),dz\rangle=2\varepsilon^{2}\langle B(x)u,v\rangle+o(\varepsilon^{2}),

so the field has a local circulation component.

In the two-agent Euclidean case
M=I
with
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
F=(g_{1},g_{2})
, this becomes

\oint_{C_{\varepsilon}}\langle F(z),dz\rangle=\varepsilon^{2}h^{\top}\!\bigl(\nabla_{x_{2}}g_{1}(x)-\nabla_{x_{1}}g_{2}(x)^{\top}\bigr)k+o(\varepsilon^{2}).

Thus the relevant “mismatch” is not merely that agents prefer different actions. It is the failure of cross-agent response symmetry: changing agent
2
alters agent
1
’s greedy improvement direction in a way that is not matched by the reverse perturbation. Once this occurs, the stacked field cannot be the gradient of a single common potential.

This mismatch is typically created by strategic coupling in the environment together with decentralized or blockwise actor updates. Importantly, the issue is not determined by reward sharing alone. A single shared team reward and a centralized critic remove the non-potential cyclic component only when the realized joint actor-update field is exactly the gradient of the centralized team objective. If the same MARL system is trained through separate local or approximate improvement blocks, then one agent’s update is still formed against the changing behavior of the others, and the induced cross-agent response terms need not be symmetric. Shared rewards, coupled transitions, role-allocation constraints, convention selection, or mixed cooperative/competitive effects can all make one agent’s local improvement direction reshape another agent’s landscape. Partial observability and function-approximation error can strengthen the asymmetry, but they are not required; the effect can already appear in fully observed matrix or Markov games. Hyperparameters that change the update operator itself can also change the size of the residual. By contrast, the numerical step size and the initial condition do not create the residual field; they determine whether training visits a region where the asymmetry is strong and whether the resulting cyclic motion is amplified in discrete time.

This also clarifies the multi-agent aspect. The decisive distinction is exact centralized optimization, not merely centralized evaluation. If all agents were merged into a single super-agent and updated by exact gradient ascent on one centralized scalar return, then the resulting field would be integrable and this non-potential cyclic component would disappear. By contrast, MARL with a single shared reward can still exhibit the same obstruction whenever the implemented actor update is factorized into local or approximate blocks whose cross-agent responses are not jointly consistent. Sampling the others’ actions contributes stochasticity, but by itself does not imply a structural non-potential cyclic component at the level of the exact expected field; the obstruction appears when the realized update rule departs from exact joint-gradient structure. Mathematically, however, the phenomenon is not exclusive to multi-agent systems; any non-potential update rule may contain such a component. Finally, Proposition
C.5
implies that any nonzero loop circulation yields nonzero residual energy, so the obstruction is not only geometric but also quantitatively detectable.

The next proposition isolates the effect of this residual cyclic component by considering the canonical residual-only skew field from Example
B.5
.

Proposition E.5

(Updating along the residual cyclic component alone need not approach the fixed point)
.

Consider the residual-only field on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

R(u,v)=(v,-u),

whose unique fixed point is
(0,0)
.
(i) Under the continuous-time dynamics
(\dot{u},\dot{v})=R(u,v)
, every non-stationary trajectory satisfies

u(t)^{2}+v(t)^{2}=u(0)^{2}+v(0)^{2}

for all
t\geq 0
and therefore never reaches the fixed point. In particular, every circle

\gamma_{r}(s)=r(\cos 2\pi s,\sin 2\pi s),\qquad r>0,

is an invariant orbit.
(ii) Under simultaneous explicit-Euler updates

z_{t+1}=z_{t}+\eta R(z_{t}),\qquad z_{t}=(u_{t},v_{t}),

one has

\|z_{t+1}\|^{2}=(1+\eta^{2})\|z_{t}\|^{2}

for every step size
\eta>0
. Hence every nonzero iterate moves strictly away from the fixed point, and

\|z_{t}\|=(1+\eta^{2})^{t/2}\|z_{0}\|.

Thus a pure residual cyclic component can make the fixed point unreachable in continuous time and repelling in discrete time.

Proposition
E.5
isolates the role of the residual cyclic component: in continuous time it sustains recurrent motion, while in simultaneous discrete-time updates any positive step size turns the same skew field into outward drift.

Appendix G
Broader impacts.

HPML is a general-purpose MARL optimization tool. Its potential positive impact is improved stability and reproducibility in cooperative and mixed-motive multi-agent systems. A potential negative impact is that more stable multi-agent optimization could also improve systems deployed in competitive, surveillance, or resource-allocation settings; responsible use therefore depends on the application context, evaluation protocol, and deployment safeguards.

\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\phi(x)=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\phi(x)
for all
x\in V
, hence
J_{ij}(x)=J_{ji}(x)
for every pair
(i,j)
,
so
J(x)=J(x)^{\top}
on
V
. With the symmetric–antisymmetric split

A(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(J(x)-J(x)^{\top}\big)
(Definition 2.4 in the paper’s notation),
this gives
A(x)\equiv 0
on
V
, and consequently on
\mathcal{X}
we have

\mathcal{R}(x)=\|A(x)\|_{F}^{2}=0.

For (ii), assume
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open and simply connected,
F\in C^{1}(U)
, and
A(x)\equiv 0
on
U
.
Then for every
x\in U
,

0=A(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(J(x)-J(x)^{\top}\big)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad J(x)=J(x)^{\top}.

Writing this entrywise and recalling
J_{ij}(x)=\partial_{j}F_{i}(x)
, we obtain the cross-partial equalities

\partial_{j}F_{i}(x)=\partial_{i}F_{j}(x),\qquad\forall\,x\in U,\ \forall\,i,j\in\{1,\dots,d\}.

Lemma H.1

(Closed-loop integral vanishes under
J=J^{\top}
)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and simply connected and let
F\in C^{1}(U)
satisfy
\partial_{j}F_{i}=\partial_{i}F_{j}

on
U
. Then for every piecewise
C^{1}
closed curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to U
with
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)
,

\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle F(\gamma(t)),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\,dt\;=\;0.

Proof.

Fix a piecewise
C^{1}
closed curve
\gamma
in
U
. Since
U
is simply connected,
\gamma
is homotopic
to the constant loop at
\gamma(0)
through loops in
U
; concretely, there exists a (piecewise)
C^{1}
map

H:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to U
such that
H(0,t)=\gamma(t)
for all
t
,
H(1,t)=\gamma(0)
for all
t
,
and
H(s,0)=H(s,1)=\gamma(0)
for all
s
.
Define, for each
s\in[0,1]
, the loop integral

I(s):=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle F(H(s,t)),\partial_{t}H(s,t)\right\rangle\,dt.

We compute
I^{\prime}(s)
by differentiating under the integral sign. Using the chain rule and that

\nabla F=J
, we have

\frac{d}{ds}\,\left\langle F(H(s,t)),\partial_{t}H(s,t)\right\rangle=\left\langle J(H(s,t))\,\partial_{s}H(s,t),\partial_{t}H(s,t)\right\rangle+\left\langle F(H(s,t)),\partial_{st}H(s,t)\right\rangle.

Integrating this identity over
t\in[0,1]
gives

I^{\prime}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle J(H)\,\partial_{s}H,\partial_{t}H\right\rangle\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle F(H),\partial_{st}H\right\rangle\,dt,

where for readability
H=H(s,t)
inside the integrals.
For the second term, integrate by parts in
t
. Since
F\in C^{1}
and
H
is (piecewise)
C^{1}
in both
variables, the map
t\mapsto\left\langle F(H(s,t)),\partial_{s}H(s,t)\right\rangle
is (piecewise)
C^{1}
and

\partial_{t}\left\langle F(H),\partial_{s}H\right\rangle=\left\langle J(H)\,\partial_{t}H,\partial_{s}H\right\rangle+\left\langle F(H),\partial_{ts}H\right\rangle.

Rearranging yields

\left\langle F(H),\partial_{st}H\right\rangle=\partial_{t}\left\langle F(H),\partial_{s}H\right\rangle-\left\langle J(H)\,\partial_{t}H,\partial_{s}H\right\rangle.

Integrating from
t=0
to
t=1
gives

\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle F(H),\partial_{st}H\right\rangle\,dt=\Big[\left\langle F(H(s,t)),\partial_{s}H(s,t)\right\rangle\Big]_{t=0}^{t=1}-\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle J(H)\,\partial_{t}H,\partial_{s}H\right\rangle\,dt.

Because
H(s,0)=H(s,1)=\gamma(0)
for all
s
, the boundary term vanishes:
both endpoints coincide, and
\partial_{s}H(s,0)=\partial_{s}H(s,1)=0
for the fixed endpoint loop,
so
\big[\left\langle F(H),\partial_{s}H\right\rangle\big]_{0}^{1}=0
. Substituting back into the formula for
I^{\prime}(s)
yields

I^{\prime}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle J(H)\,\partial_{s}H,\partial_{t}H\right\rangle\,dt-\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle J(H)\,\partial_{t}H,\partial_{s}H\right\rangle\,dt.

Using
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\left\langle b,a\right\rangle
and the identity

\left\langle J\,u,v\right\rangle-\left\langle J\,v,u\right\rangle=\left\langle(J-J^{\top})u,v\right\rangle
, we obtain

I^{\prime}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle(J(H)-J(H)^{\top})\,\partial_{s}H,\partial_{t}H\right\rangle\,dt.

Under the hypothesis
\partial_{j}F_{i}=\partial_{i}F_{j}
, we have
J=J^{\top}
everywhere on
U
, hence

J(H)-J(H)^{\top}=0
along the homotopy and therefore
I^{\prime}(s)=0
for all
s
.
Thus
I(s)
is constant in
s
. Evaluating at
s=1
gives
I(1)=0
because
H(1,\cdot)
is the constant loop,
so
I(0)=I(1)=0
. Since
I(0)
is exactly the integral along
\gamma
, the claim follows.
∎

Fix a base point
x_{0}\in U
. For any
x\in U
, choose a piecewise
C^{1}
curve
\gamma_{x_{0}\to x}:[0,1]\to U

with
\gamma_{x_{0}\to x}(0)=x_{0}
and
\gamma_{x_{0}\to x}(1)=x
, and define

\phi(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle F(\gamma_{x_{0}\to x}(t)),\gamma^{\prime}_{x_{0}\to x}(t)\right\rangle\,dt.

We show
\phi
is well-defined (independent of the chosen path). If
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are two such paths
from
x_{0}
to
x
, consider the closed curve obtained by traversing
\gamma_{1}
and then traversing
\gamma_{2}

backwards; denote this closed loop by
\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2}^{-1}
. The integral along
\gamma_{2}^{-1}
is the
negative of the integral along
\gamma_{2}
, so

\int_{\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2}^{-1}}\left\langle F,d\ell\right\rangle=\int_{\gamma_{1}}\left\langle F,d\ell\right\rangle-\int_{\gamma_{2}}\left\langle F,d\ell\right\rangle.

By Lemma
H.1
, the left-hand side is
0
, hence

\int_{\gamma_{1}}\left\langle F,d\ell\right\rangle=\int_{\gamma_{2}}\left\langle F,d\ell\right\rangle
, proving that
\phi(x)
does not depend on the choice
of
\gamma_{x_{0}\to x}
.

It remains to show
\nabla\phi(x)=F(x)
. Fix
x\in U
and a coordinate direction
e_{k}
.
Because
U
is open, there exists
\delta>0
such that
x+he_{k}\in U
for all
|h|<\delta
.
Using path-independence, we may compute
\phi(x+he_{k})-\phi(x)
by choosing for
\phi(x+he_{k})
the path

\gamma_{x_{0}\to x}
followed by the straight segment from
x
to
x+he_{k}
. Parameterizing that segment by

\sigma_{h}(t)=x+te_{k}
for
t\in[0,h]
, we obtain

\phi(x+he_{k})-\phi(x)=\int_{0}^{h}\left\langle F(\sigma_{h}(t)),\sigma_{h}^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{h}\left\langle F(x+te_{k}),e_{k}\right\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{h}F_{k}(x+te_{k})\,dt.

Dividing by
h\neq 0
gives

\frac{\phi(x+he_{k})-\phi(x)}{h}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}F_{k}(x+te_{k})\,dt.

Since
F
is continuous (indeed
C^{1}
) on
U
, the integrand
F_{k}(x+te_{k})
converges to
F_{k}(x)
as
t\to 0
,
and therefore the right-hand side converges to
F_{k}(x)
as
h\to 0
(it is the average value of a continuous
function over an interval shrinking to a point). Hence the partial derivative exists and satisfies

\partial_{k}\phi(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\phi(x+he_{k})-\phi(x)}{h}=F_{k}(x).

Because this holds for every
k
, we conclude
\nabla\phi(x)=F(x)
for all
x\in U
.

For uniqueness up to an additive constant, suppose
\tilde{\phi}
is another scalar potential on
U
with

\nabla\tilde{\phi}=F
. Then
\nabla(\phi-\tilde{\phi})=0
on
U
. For any
x,y\in U
, choose a piecewise
C^{1}
path

\gamma
from
x
to
y
; applying the fundamental theorem of calculus along curves yields

(\phi-\tilde{\phi})(y)-(\phi-\tilde{\phi})(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla(\phi-\tilde{\phi})(\gamma(t)),\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle 0,\gamma^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\,dt=0,

so
\phi-\tilde{\phi}
is constant on the (path-)connected set
U
. This proves uniqueness up to an additive constant.
∎

H.3
Proof of Lemma
C.1

Proof.

We write
d\mu(x)=\rho(x)\,dx
and interpret all expressions in the natural sense in which they are assumed
to be well-defined; in particular,
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
has
\nabla\psi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
, the left-hand side

\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu
is understood as
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho\,U\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx
, and the right-hand side

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu
is understood as the
H^{-1}
–
H^{1}
dual pairing whenever
\div_{\mu}U\notin L^{2}(\mu)
.

Recall the weighted divergence is given (in the distributional sense) by

\div_{\mu}U\;:=\;\frac{1}{\rho}\,\div(\rho U),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\div(\rho U)=\rho\,\div_{\mu}U.

We first justify the identity for smooth
\psi
; the extension to
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
will then follow by density.
Fix
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
(periodic if
\mathcal{X}
is a torus, or smooth up to the boundary if
\mathcal{X}
is a Lipschitz domain),
and set

W(x):=\rho(x)\,U(x).

Then the left-hand side becomes

\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho\,U\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx=\int_{\mathcal{X}}W\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx.

We use the pointwise product rule for the divergence of a scalar times a vector field:
for every
x
where the derivatives make sense,

\div(\psi W)(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}\big(\psi(x)\,W_{i}(x)\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big(\partial_{i}\psi(x)\big)\,W_{i}(x)\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi(x)\,\partial_{i}W_{i}(x)=W(x)\cdot\nabla\psi(x)\;+\;\psi(x)\,\div W(x).

Rearranging this identity gives, pointwise,

W\cdot\nabla\psi=\div(\psi W)-\psi\,\div W.

Integrating over
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
dx
yields

\int_{\mathcal{X}}W\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\div(\psi W)\,dx-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div W\,dx.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, the divergence theorem applies to the vector field
\psi W
and gives

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\div(\psi W)\,dx=\int_{\partial\mathcal{X}}\psi\,W\cdot n\,dS=\int_{\partial\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\rho\,U\cdot n\,dS.

Under the stated boundary condition eliminating boundary terms (e.g.
\rho\,U\cdot n=0
on
\partial\mathcal{X}
),
the boundary integral is
0
, hence

\int_{\mathcal{X}}W\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div W\,dx.

If
\mathcal{X}
is a flat torus, it has no boundary; equivalently, if one represents it by a fundamental periodic cell,
the boundary contributions cancel pairwise due to periodicity, and one again has

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\div(\psi W)\,dx=0
, so the same identity holds:

\int_{\mathcal{X}}W\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div W\,dx.

Substituting back
W=\rho U
gives

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho\,U\cdot\nabla\psi\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div(\rho U)\,dx.

Using
\div(\rho U)=\rho\,\div_{\mu}U
, we rewrite the right-hand side as

-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div(\rho U)\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\rho\,\div_{\mu}U\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu,

and therefore, for smooth
\psi
,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu.

To pass from smooth
\psi
to
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
, take a sequence
\{\psi_{k}\}
of smooth functions (periodic on the torus,
or smooth up to the boundary on a Lipschitz domain) such that
\psi_{k}\to\psi
in
H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
.
For each
k
the identity already proved yields

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho\,U\cdot\nabla\psi_{k}\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{k}\,\rho\,\div_{\mu}U\,dx.

Because
\rho
is essentially bounded above and below,
\nabla\psi_{k}\to\nabla\psi
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X})
implies

\nabla\psi_{k}\to\nabla\psi
in
L^{2}(\mu)
, and the left-hand side converges by Cauchy–Schwarz:

\Big|\int_{\mathcal{X}}U\cdot(\nabla\psi_{k}-\nabla\psi)\,d\mu\Big|\leq\|U\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\,\|\nabla\psi_{k}-\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\longrightarrow 0.

On the right-hand side, the assumption that
\div_{\mu}U
is well-defined in
H^{-1}(\mathcal{X})
means the map

\psi\mapsto\langle\div_{\mu}U,\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}
is continuous with respect to the
H^{1}
norm, so

\langle\div_{\mu}U,\psi_{k}\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}\longrightarrow\langle\div_{\mu}U,\psi\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}.

Interpreting
-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi_{k}\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu
as this dual pairing when needed, we may pass to the limit and obtain

\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu\qquad\text{for all }\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}).

Reading the identity as an
L^{2}(\mu)
(or
H^{-1}
–
H^{1}
) pairing shows precisely that
\nabla
and
-\div_{\mu}

are adjoint under the
L^{2}(\mu)
pairing.
∎

H.4
Proof of Proposition
C.2

Proof.

Write
d\mu(x)=\rho(x)\,dx
and recall
M\succ 0
is constant. Using
\nabla_{M}\Phi=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi
and

\|v\|_{M}^{2}=v^{\top}Mv
, the objective in Definition
3.1
can be written as the quadratic functional

J(\Phi):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\|F(x)-\nabla_{M}\Phi(x)\|_{M}^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F(x)-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi(x)\big)^{\top}M\big(F(x)-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi(x)\big)\,d\mu(x).

Since
M
is constant, expanding the integrand gives

\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)^{\top}M\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)=F^{\top}MF-2\,F^{\top}\nabla\Phi+\nabla\Phi^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla\Phi,

so
J(\Phi)
depends on
\Phi
only through
\nabla\Phi
and is convex (indeed quadratic) in
\nabla\Phi
.

Lemma H.2

(First variation of
J
)
.

For any
\Phi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and any direction
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
, the map
\varepsilon\mapsto J(\Phi+\varepsilon\psi)

is differentiable and

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J(\Phi+\varepsilon\psi)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=2\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi(x)-F(x)\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi(x)\,d\mu(x).

Proof.

Set
\Phi_{\varepsilon}:=\Phi+\varepsilon\psi
, so
\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\nabla\Phi+\varepsilon\nabla\psi
and

F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)-\varepsilon\,M^{-1}\nabla\psi.

Substituting into
J
and using bilinearity yields

\displaystyle J(\Phi_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Big(\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)-\varepsilon\,M^{-1}\nabla\psi\Big)^{\top}M\Big(\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)-\varepsilon\,M^{-1}\nabla\psi\Big)\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)^{\top}M\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)\,d\mu-2\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)^{\top}M\big(M^{-1}\nabla\psi\big)\,d\mu

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla\psi\big)^{\top}M\big(M^{-1}\nabla\psi\big)\,d\mu.

Because
M(M^{-1}\nabla\psi)=\nabla\psi
and
\big(M^{-1}\nabla\psi\big)^{\top}M\big(M^{-1}\nabla\psi\big)=\nabla\psi^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla\psi
, differentiating in
\varepsilon
gives

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J(\Phi_{\varepsilon})=-2\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu+2\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla\psi^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla\psi\,d\mu,

and evaluating at
\varepsilon=0
yields

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J(\Phi+\varepsilon\psi)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=-2\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(F-M^{-1}\nabla\Phi\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=2\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi-F\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu,

as claimed.
∎

Let
\Phi^{\star}
be any minimizer. Since
J
is (Gateaux) differentiable and
\Phi^{\star}
is an unconstrained minimizer
over the affine space
H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
, the first-order optimality condition is

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J(\Phi^{\star}+\varepsilon\psi)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0\qquad\forall\,\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}).

Applying Lemma
H.2
at
\Phi^{\star}
yields the weak orthogonality condition

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}-F\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=0\qquad\forall\,\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}).

Equivalently,

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=\int_{\mathcal{X}}F^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu\qquad\forall\,\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}).

Now apply Lemma
C.1
twice (with
U=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}
and
U=F
): under the stated boundary
assumptions removing boundary terms, for every
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
we have

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)\,d\mu,\qquad\int_{\mathcal{X}}F^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}F\,d\mu.

Substituting these identities into the weak orthogonality equality gives

-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}F\,d\mu\qquad\forall\,\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}),

hence

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\Big(\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)-\div_{\mu}F\Big)\,d\mu=0\qquad\forall\,\psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}).

This is precisely the statement that

\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}\big)=\div_{\mu}F\quad\text{in }H^{-1}(\mathcal{X}),

i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{M,\mu}\Phi^{\star}=\div_{\mu}F
in the weak sense, and the projected field is

\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F=\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}
by Definition
3.1
.

For uniqueness up to an additive constant, let
\Phi_{1}^{\star}
and
\Phi_{2}^{\star}
be two minimizers and set

w:=\Phi_{1}^{\star}-\Phi_{2}^{\star}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
. Subtracting the two weak equations yields

\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla w\big)=0\quad\text{in }H^{-1}(\mathcal{X}).

Testing this equality with
\psi=w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and applying Lemma
C.1
gives

0=\int_{\mathcal{X}}w\,\div_{\mu}\!\big(M^{-1}\nabla w\big)\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(M^{-1}\nabla w\big)^{\top}\nabla w\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla w^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla w\,d\mu.

Therefore

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\nabla w^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla w\,d\mu=0.

Since
M^{-1}\succ 0
,
\nabla w^{\top}M^{-1}\nabla w\geq\lambda_{\min}(M^{-1})\|\nabla w\|_{2}^{2}
pointwise, hence
the integral can vanish only if
\nabla w=0

\mu
-a.e. on
\mathcal{X}
. Consequently,

M^{-1}\nabla\Phi_{1}^{\star}=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi_{2}^{\star}\quad\mu\text{-a.e.},

so the projected field
\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}
is unique
\mu
-a.e., while
\Phi^{\star}
is determined only up to an
additive constant (more precisely, up to constants on each connected component). One may fix a gauge such as

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\Phi^{\star}]=0
to select a representative.

Finally, the stated boundary condition arises as the natural boundary condition of the variational problem when one
does not impose boundary constraints on
\Phi
. Writing
U:=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}-F
and assuming sufficient trace
regularity so that classical integration by parts applies, one has

\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\div_{\mu}U\,d\mu+\int_{\partial\mathcal{X}}\psi\,\rho\,U\cdot n\,dS.

At optimality the first variation gives
\int_{\mathcal{X}}U^{\top}\nabla\psi\,d\mu=0
for all
\psi
, while the interior Euler–Lagrange
equation gives
\div_{\mu}U=0
in
\mathcal{X}
; therefore the boundary term must vanish for arbitrary boundary traces of
\psi
,
forcing

\rho\,U\cdot n=\rho\,(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}-F)\cdot n=0\quad\text{on }\partial\mathcal{X},

which is exactly the zero-flux residual condition.
∎

H.5
Proof of Lemma
3.2

Proof.

By Definition
3.1
, pick a minimizer
\Phi^{\star}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and set

G^{\star}:=\Pi_{\mathrm{grad}}^{M}F=\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star},\qquad R:=F-G^{\star}=F-\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}.

Consider the least-squares functional on potentials

J(\Psi):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\big[\|F(x)-\nabla_{M}\Psi(x)\|_{M}^{2}\big],\qquad\Psi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X}).

Fix an arbitrary test potential
\Phi\in H^{1}(\mathcal{X})
and form the one-dimensional perturbation

\Psi_{\varepsilon}:=\Phi^{\star}+\varepsilon\Phi,\qquad\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}.

Using linearity of
\nabla_{M}
, we have

\nabla_{M}\Psi_{\varepsilon}=\nabla_{M}(\Phi^{\star}+\varepsilon\Phi)=\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}+\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi=G^{\star}+\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi.

Therefore the residual under this perturbation is

F-\nabla_{M}\Psi_{\varepsilon}=F-(G^{\star}+\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi)=(F-G^{\star})-\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi=R-\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi.

Now expand the squared
M
-norm using
\|v\|_{M}^{2}=\langle v,v\rangle_{M}
and bilinearity of the
M
-inner product:

\displaystyle\|R-\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi\|_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle R-\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi,\;R-\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}

\displaystyle=\langle R,R\rangle_{M}-\varepsilon\langle R,\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}-\varepsilon\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi,R\rangle_{M}+\varepsilon^{2}\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi,\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}.

Because
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{M}
is symmetric (since
M\succ 0
), we have

\langle R,\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}=\langle\nabla_{M}\Phi,R\rangle_{M}
, hence

\|R-\varepsilon\nabla_{M}\Phi\|_{M}^{2}=\|R\|_{M}^{2}-2\varepsilon\,\langle R,\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}+\varepsilon^{2}\,\|\nabla_{M}\Phi\|_{M}^{2}.

Taking expectation with respect to

J(\Psi_{\varepsilon})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|R\|_{M}^{2}]-2\varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\langle R,\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}]+\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla_{M}\Phi\|_{M}^{2}].

Differentiate this scalar quadratic function of

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J(\Psi_{\varepsilon})=-2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\langle R,\nabla_{M}\Phi\rangle_{M}]+2\varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\|\nabla_{M}\Phi\|_{M}^{2}].

Since

is a minimizer of

This is exactly the stated

-orthogonality of the residual

to every metric-gradient field

.
∎

H.7
Proof of Proposition
C.5

Proof.

Because
G^{\star}=\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}
, by definition of the metric-gradient we have

\nabla_{M}\Phi^{\star}(x)=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}(x),\qquad\text{so in particular}\qquad M\,G^{\star}(x)=\nabla\Phi^{\star}(x)\ \ \text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

By the definition of circulation and the
M
-inner product
\langle u,v\rangle_{M}=u^{\top}Mv
, for any piecewise
C^{1}
closed curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{X}
we can write

\mathbin{\circ}_{G^{\star}}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\langle G^{\star}(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle_{M}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}G^{\star}(\gamma(t))^{\top}M\,\dot{\gamma}(t)\,dt.

Substituting
G^{\star}(\gamma(t))=M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))
into the integrand yields

G^{\star}(\gamma(t))^{\top}M\,\dot{\gamma}(t)=\big(M^{-1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))\big)^{\top}M\,\dot{\gamma}(t)=\nabla\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))^{\top}\dot{\gamma}(t),

where we used that
(M^{-1})^{\top}=M^{-1}
and
M^{-1}M=I
because
M
is symmetric positive definite. Hence

\mathbin{\circ}_{G^{\star}}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))^{\top}\dot{\gamma}(t)\,dt.

Since
\gamma
is piecewise
C^{1}
and
\Phi^{\star}
is differentiable, the chain rule applies on each smooth sub-interval of
[0,1]
and gives

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))=\nabla\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))^{\top}\dot{\gamma}(t)\quad\text{for all }t\text{ where }\gamma\text{ is differentiable}.

Therefore, integrating over
[0,1]
(equivalently, summing over the finitely many smooth pieces) and applying the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

\mathbin{\circ}_{G^{\star}}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(t))\,dt=\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(1))-\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(0)).

Because
\gamma
is closed,
\gamma(1)=\gamma(0)
, so
\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(1))-\Phi^{\star}(\gamma(0))=0
and thus
\mathbin{\circ}_{G^{\star}}(\gamma)=0
. Now using
F=G^{\star}+R
and the linearity of both the inner product and the integral, we have

\mathbin{\circ}_{F}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\langle F(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle_{M}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\langle G^{\star}(\gamma(t))+R(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle_{M}\,dt=\mathbin{\circ}_{G^{\star}}(\gamma)+\mathbin{\circ}_{R}(\gamma)=\mathbin{\circ}_{R}(\gamma),

which proves
\mathbin{\circ}_{F}(\gamma)=\mathbin{\circ}_{R}(\gamma)
.

For the quantitative bound, start from the identity just shown and write

|\mathbin{\circ}_{F}(\gamma)|=|\mathbin{\circ}_{R}(\gamma)|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\langle R(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\rangle_{M}\,dt\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}R(\gamma(t))^{\top}M\,\dot{\gamma}(t)\,dt\right|.

Let
M^{1/2}
denote the (symmetric) principal square root of
M
, so that
M^{1/2}M^{1/2}=M
and
(M^{1/2})^{\top}=M^{1/2}
. Then for each
t
where
\gamma
is differentiable,

R(\gamma(t))^{\top}M\,\dot{\gamma}(t)=R(\gamma(t))^{\top}M^{1/2}M^{1/2}\dot{\gamma}(t)=\big(M^{1/2}R(\gamma(t))\big)^{\top}\big(M^{1/2}\dot{\gamma}(t)\big).

Applying the Euclidean Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the last inner product gives the pointwise bound

\big|R(\gamma(t))^{\top}M\,\dot{\gamma}(t)\big|\leq\|M^{1/2}R(\gamma(t))\|_{2}\,\|M^{1/2}\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{2}=\|R(\gamma(t))\|_{M}\,\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{M},

where we used
\|x\|_{M}^{2}=x^{\top}Mx=\|M^{1/2}x\|_{2}^{2}
. Using this bound inside the integral and then applying Cauchy–Schwarz in
L^{2}([0,1])
yields

|\mathbin{\circ}_{F}(\gamma)|\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|R(\gamma(t))\|_{M}\,\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{M}\,dt\leq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|R(\gamma(t))\|_{M}^{2}\,dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{M}^{2}\,dt\right)^{1/2}.

Finally, because
t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[g(t)]=\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\,dt
for any integrable
g
, we can rewrite the last display as

|\mathbin{\circ}_{F}(\gamma)|\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\|R(\gamma(t))\|_{M}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\cdot\Big(\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{M}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

In the Euclidean-metric case
M=I
(so
M^{-1}=I
), the second factor satisfies
\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{M}=\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{M^{-1}}
, which matches the stated form.
∎

, so

\omega_{ij}=\Big\langle\frac{\nabla\Phi(x^{i})+\nabla\Phi(x^{j})}{2},\,\Delta_{ij}\Big\rangle.

On the other hand, by the fundamental theorem of calculus along the straight segment

\gamma(t):=x^{i}+t\Delta_{ij}
, we have

\Phi(x^{j})-\Phi(x^{i})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(\gamma(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\Phi(\gamma(t)),\,\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rangle\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{i}+t\Delta_{ij}),\,\Delta_{ij}\rangle\,dt.

To compare this integral to the endpoint average, use the integral representation of the gradient.
For each
t\in[0,1]
, the map
s\mapsto\nabla\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})
is
C^{1}
, and

\nabla\Phi(x^{i}+t\Delta_{ij})=\nabla\Phi(x^{i})+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds.

Substituting into the line integral yields

\displaystyle\Phi(x^{j})-\Phi(x^{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Big\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{i})+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds,\ \Delta_{ij}\Big\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{i}),\Delta_{ij}\rangle\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{i}),\Delta_{ij}\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\,\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds,

where the last line uses Fubini’s theorem to swap the order of integration:

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}(\cdot)\,ds\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{s}^{1}(\cdot)\,dt\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)(\cdot)\,ds
.
Similarly, using the same identity at
t=1
gives

\nabla\Phi(x^{j})=\nabla\Phi(x^{i}+\Delta_{ij})=\nabla\Phi(x^{i})+\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds,

hence

\Big\langle\frac{\nabla\Phi(x^{i})+\nabla\Phi(x^{j})}{2},\,\Delta_{ij}\Big\rangle=\langle\nabla\Phi(x^{i}),\Delta_{ij}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds.

Subtracting the two expressions yields

\displaystyle\omega_{ij}-\big(\Phi(x^{j})-\Phi(x^{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-(1-s)\Big)\,\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(s-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\,\Delta_{ij}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\,\Delta_{ij}\,ds.

Let
H_{ij}:=\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\|\nabla^{2}\Phi(x^{i}+s\Delta_{ij})\|_{\mathrm{op}}<\infty
. Then

\big|\omega_{ij}-\big(\Phi(x^{j})-\Phi(x^{i})\big)\big|\leq\int_{0}^{1}\Big|s-\frac{1}{2}\Big|\,H_{ij}\,\|\Delta_{ij}\|^{2}\,ds=H_{ij}\,\|\Delta_{ij}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\Big|s-\frac{1}{2}\Big|\,ds=\frac{H_{ij}}{4}\,\|\Delta_{ij}\|^{2}.

Thus

\omega_{ij}=\Phi(x^{j})-\Phi(x^{i})+O(\|x^{j}-x^{i}\|^{2}),

which is the claimed second-order (metric-consistent) approximation of potential differences.
∎

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

Fix
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and write
p:=P(y)\in\mathcal{X}
. By definition of the Euclidean projection onto a nonempty closed convex set,

p
is the unique minimizer of the strongly convex problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\;f(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

Let
z\in\mathcal{X}
be arbitrary and consider the line segment

x(t):=p+t(z-p),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Because
\mathcal{X}
is convex,
x(t)\in\mathcal{X}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Since
p
minimizes
f
over
\mathcal{X}
, the one-variable function

t\mapsto f(x(t))
attains its minimum at
t=0
on
[0,1]
, hence its right derivative at
t=0
is nonnegative:

\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t))\Big|_{t=0^{+}}\geq 0.

We compute this derivative explicitly. First,

f(x(t))=\frac{1}{2}\|x(t)-y\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|p+t(z-p)-y\|^{2}.

Differentiate using
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|^{2}=\langle u(t),u^{\prime}(t)\rangle
with

u(t)=p+t(z-p)-y
and
u^{\prime}(t)=z-p
:

\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t))=\big\langle p+t(z-p)-y,\;z-p\big\rangle.

Evaluating at
t=0
gives

\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t))\Big|_{t=0}=\langle p-y,\;z-p\rangle.

Combining with the necessary optimality condition yields

\langle p-y,\;z-p\rangle\geq 0.

Multiplying by
-1
and recalling
p=P(y)
gives the projection variational inequality

\langle y-P(y),\;z-P(y)\rangle\leq 0,\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{X}.

For firm nonexpansiveness, take arbitrary
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and denote

p:=P(y),\qquad q:=P(y^{\prime}).

Apply the projection variational inequality twice. First, with
(y,z)=(y,q)
(note
q\in\mathcal{X}
), we obtain

\langle y-p,\;q-p\rangle\leq 0.

Second, with
(y^{\prime},z)=(y^{\prime},p)
(note
p\in\mathcal{X}
), we obtain

\langle y^{\prime}-q,\;p-q\rangle\leq 0.

Rewrite the second inequality by factoring out a minus sign from
(p-q)=-(q-p)
:

\langle y^{\prime}-q,\;-(q-p)\rangle\leq 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\langle y^{\prime}-q,\;q-p\rangle\geq 0.

Now add this to the first inequality:

\langle y-p,\;q-p\rangle+\langle y^{\prime}-q,\;q-p\rangle\leq 0.

Combine the inner products:

\langle(y-p)+(y^{\prime}-q),\;q-p\rangle\leq 0.

Group terms as
(y-y^{\prime})-(p-q)
by noting
(y-p)+(y^{\prime}-q)=(y-y^{\prime})-(p-q)
and
q-p=-(p-q)
:

(y-p)+(y^{\prime}-q)=y+y^{\prime}-(p+q)=(y-y^{\prime})-(p-q),\qquad q-p=-(p-q).

Substitute these identities:

\langle(y-y^{\prime})-(p-q),\;-(p-q)\rangle\leq 0.

Use bilinearity to expand the left-hand side:

\langle(y-y^{\prime})-(p-q),\;-(p-q)\rangle=-\langle y-y^{\prime},\;p-q\rangle+\langle p-q,\;p-q\rangle.

Therefore the inequality becomes

-\langle y-y^{\prime},\;p-q\rangle+\|p-q\|^{2}\leq 0,

which rearranges to the firm nonexpansiveness inequality

\|P(y)-P(y^{\prime})\|^{2}=\|p-q\|^{2}\leq\langle p-q,\;y-y^{\prime}\rangle=\langle P(y)-P(y^{\prime}),\;y-y^{\prime}\rangle.

Finally, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\|p-q\|^{2}\leq\langle p-q,\;y-y^{\prime}\rangle\leq\|p-q\|\,\|y-y^{\prime}\|.

If
p=q
then
\|p-q\|=0
and the desired nonexpansiveness
\|p-q\|\leq\|y-y^{\prime}\|
is trivial.
If
p\neq q
, divide both sides by the positive quantity
\|p-q\|
to obtain

\|P(y)-P(y^{\prime})\|=\|p-q\|\leq\|y-y^{\prime}\|.

∎

\operatorname{Gap}(x)\leq(D+\eta G)\,\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x)\|+D\,\|R(x)\|.

Now specialize to the iterates generated by the algorithm and the random output
\tilde{x}_{T}:=x_{\tilde{t}}
,
where
\tilde{t}
is uniform on
\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}
and independent of the iterates. Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Gap}(\tilde{x}_{T})]\leq(D+\eta G)\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]+D\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|R(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big].

We bound the residual term by Cauchy–Schwarz:

\mathbb{E}\big[\|R(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|R(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}=E_{T}.

Next bound
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]
using Jensen/Cauchy–Schwarz:

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}.

Because
\tilde{t}
is uniform and independent, conditioning on the iterates yields

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\ \big|\ x_{0},\dots,x_{T-1}\big]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2},

and taking expectation over the iterates gives

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\big].

For the present (exact, deterministic) projected ascent dynamics, the iterates are deterministic given
x_{0}
,
so the expectations may be dropped; the same bound below holds verbatim even if randomness is present.

By Theorem
6.4
, for each
t
,

\Phi(x_{t+1})\geq\Phi(x_{t})+\frac{\eta}{2}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2},

equivalently,

\frac{\eta}{2}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\Phi(x_{t+1})-\Phi(x_{t}).

Summing from
t=0
to
T-1
telescopes:

\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big(\Phi(x_{t+1})-\Phi(x_{t})\big)=\Phi(x_{T})-\Phi(x_{0}).

Since all iterates lie in
\mathcal{X}
, we have
\Phi(x_{T})\leq\Phi_{\max}
and
\Phi(x_{0})\geq\Phi_{\min}
, hence

\Phi(x_{T})-\Phi(x_{0})\leq\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min}.

Therefore

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{\eta},\qquad\text{and thus}\qquad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\,\eta}.

Using
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{2(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\,\eta},

and hence

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\,\eta}}.

Substituting the two expectation bounds back into the gap inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Gap}(\tilde{x}_{T})]

\displaystyle\leq(D+\eta G)\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathcal{G}_{\eta}(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]+D\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|R(\tilde{x}_{T})\|\big]

\displaystyle\leq(D+\eta G)\sqrt{\frac{2(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\,\eta}}+D\,E_{T},

which is the desired bound.
∎

Lemma H.4

(Potential-gap control by an inexact projected-step mapping)
.

Fix any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and any direction
g\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define

x^{+}:=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x+\eta g),\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x):=\frac{1}{\eta}(x^{+}-x).

Then, for every
u\in\mathcal{X}
,

\langle g,\,u-x\rangle\;\leq\;(D+\eta\|g\|)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|,

and consequently

\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\nabla\Phi(x),\,u-x\rangle\;\leq\;(D+\eta\|g\|)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|+D\,\|\nabla\Phi(x)-g\|.

Proof.

Fix
u\in\mathcal{X}
and write

\langle g,\,u-x\rangle=\langle g,\,u-x^{+}\rangle+\langle g,\,x^{+}-x\rangle.

We bound the two terms separately.

Because
x^{+}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x+\eta g)
, Lemma
F.1
(the VI inequality)
with
y=x+\eta g
and
z=u\in\mathcal{X}
gives

\langle(x+\eta g)-x^{+},\,u-x^{+}\rangle\leq 0.

Expanding and rearranging,

\eta\,\langle g,\,u-x^{+}\rangle\leq\langle x^{+}-x,\,u-x^{+}\rangle.

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz and the diameter bound
\|u-x^{+}\|\leq D
:

\langle g,\,u-x^{+}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\|x^{+}-x\|\,\|u-x^{+}\|\leq\frac{D}{\eta}\|x^{+}-x\|=D\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|.

For the second term,

\langle g,\,x^{+}-x\rangle\leq\|g\|\,\|x^{+}-x\|=\eta\|g\|\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|.

Adding the two bounds yields

\langle g,\,u-x\rangle\leq\big(D+\eta\|g\|\big)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|.

Taking
\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}
on the left gives

\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\langle g,\,u-x\rangle\leq(D+\eta\|g\|)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|.

Now decompose
\nabla\Phi(x)=g+\big(\nabla\Phi(x)-g\big)
and use
\|u-x\|\leq D
:

\langle\nabla\Phi(x),\,u-x\rangle=\langle g,\,u-x\rangle+\langle\nabla\Phi(x)-g,\,u-x\rangle\leq\langle g,\,u-x\rangle+\|\nabla\Phi(x)-g\|\,\|u-x\|\leq\langle g,\,u-x\rangle+D\|\nabla\Phi(x)-g\|.

Taking
\max_{u\in\mathcal{X}}
and substituting the previous bound yields the claimed inequality for
\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x)
.
∎

Lemma H.5

(One-step Lyapunov lower bound under direction error)
.

Under Assumption
6.1
, let
x^{+}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x+\eta g)
with
\eta\leq 1/L
.
Assume
\|g-\nabla\Phi(x)\|\leq\delta
.
Then

\Phi(x^{+})\;\geq\;\Phi(x)+\frac{1}{4\eta}\,\|x^{+}-x\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2}.

Equivalently, with
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)=\frac{1}{\eta}(x^{+}-x)
,

\Phi(x^{+})\;\geq\;\Phi(x)+\frac{\eta}{4}\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\Delta:=x^{+}-x
.

L
-smoothness of
\Phi
implies the standard lower bound

\Phi(x^{+})\geq\Phi(x)+\langle\nabla\Phi(x),\,\Delta\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\|\Delta\|^{2}.

Write
\nabla\Phi(x)=g-(g-\nabla\Phi(x))
and denote the direction error
e:=g-\nabla\Phi(x)
,
so
\|e\|\leq\delta
and

\langle\nabla\Phi(x),\,\Delta\rangle=\langle g,\,\Delta\rangle-\langle e,\,\Delta\rangle.

Because
x^{+}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x+\eta g)
, Lemma
F.1
with
y=x+\eta g
and
z=x\in\mathcal{X}
gives

\langle(x+\eta g)-x^{+},\,x-x^{+}\rangle\leq 0.

Since
x-x^{+}=-\Delta
, this becomes

\langle\eta g-\Delta,\,-\Delta\rangle\leq 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\eta\,\langle g,\,\Delta\rangle\geq\|\Delta\|^{2}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\langle g,\,\Delta\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}.

For the error term, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
|\langle e,\Delta\rangle|\leq\|e\|\,\|\Delta\|\leq\delta\|\Delta\|
.
Apply Young’s inequality in the form

\delta\|\Delta\|\leq\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}+\eta\,\delta^{2},

which holds because
2ab\leq\alpha a^{2}+\alpha^{-1}b^{2}
with

a=\|\Delta\|
,
b=\delta
, and
\alpha=\frac{1}{2\eta}
.
Therefore,

-\langle e,\,\Delta\rangle\geq-\delta\|\Delta\|\geq-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2}.

Combine the bounds:

\langle\nabla\Phi(x),\,\Delta\rangle=\langle g,\,\Delta\rangle-\langle e,\,\Delta\rangle\geq\frac{1}{\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2}=\frac{3}{4\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2}.

Substitute into the smoothness lower bound:

\Phi(x^{+})\geq\Phi(x)+\Big(\frac{3}{4\eta}-\frac{L}{2}\Big)\|\Delta\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2}.

Using
\eta\leq 1/L
implies
\frac{3}{4\eta}-\frac{L}{2}\geq\frac{3}{4\eta}-\frac{1}{2\eta}=\frac{1}{4\eta}
,
hence

\Phi(x^{+})\geq\Phi(x)+\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\Delta\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta^{2},

which is the claim. Replacing
\Delta=\eta\,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x)
yields the equivalent form.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.8
.

Let the iterates satisfy
x_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{X}}(x_{t}+\eta g_{t})
and define

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t}):=\frac{1}{\eta}(x_{t+1}-x_{t}).

Fix any
t
and apply Lemma
6.5
:

\operatorname{Gap}(x_{t})\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x_{t})+D\|R(x_{t})\|.

By Assumption
6.2
,
\|R(x_{t})\|\leq\varepsilon
, hence

\operatorname{Gap}(x_{t})\leq\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x_{t})+D\varepsilon.

Now apply Lemma
F.5
with
x=x_{t}
and
g=g_{t}
:

\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x_{t})\leq\big(D+\eta\|g_{t}\|\big)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|+D\,\|\nabla\Phi(x_{t})-g_{t}\|.

By Assumption
6.3
,
\|\nabla\Phi(x_{t})-g_{t}\|\leq\delta_{t}\leq\bar{\delta}
.
Also
\|g_{t}\|\leq\|\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\|+\|g_{t}-\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\|\leq G+\bar{\delta}
by Assumption
6.1
.
Therefore,

\operatorname{Gap}_{\Phi}(x_{t})\leq\big(D+\eta(G+\bar{\delta})\big)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|+D\,\bar{\delta},

and hence

\operatorname{Gap}(x_{t})\leq\big(D+\eta(G+\bar{\delta})\big)\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|+D(\varepsilon+\bar{\delta}).

Now choose
\hat{t}\in\arg\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|
and set
\hat{x}_{T}=x_{\hat{t}}
.
Then

\operatorname{Gap}(\hat{x}_{T})\leq\big(D+\eta(G+\bar{\delta})\big)\,\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|+D(\varepsilon+\bar{\delta}).

It remains to bound the best-iterate stationarity measure.
Apply Lemma
H.5
to each step with
x=x_{t}
,
x^{+}=x_{t+1}
, and
\delta=\delta_{t}
:

\Phi(x_{t+1})\geq\Phi(x_{t})+\frac{\eta}{4}\,\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}-\eta\,\delta_{t}^{2}.

Rearrange and sum from
t=0
to
T-1
:

\frac{\eta}{4}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\Phi(x_{T})-\Phi(x_{0})+\eta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\delta_{t}^{2}.

Use
\Phi(x_{T})-\Phi(x_{0})\leq\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min}
and
\delta_{t}\leq\bar{\delta}
:

\frac{\eta}{4}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})+\eta\,T\,\bar{\delta}^{2}.

Divide by
T
and use
\min_{t}a_{t}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}a_{t}^{2}
:

\min_{0\leq t\leq T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta}(x_{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{4(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\,\eta}+4\,\bar{\delta}^{2}.

Taking square-roots and substituting into the earlier gap bound yields the explicit estimate

\operatorname{Gap}(\hat{x}_{T})\leq\big(D+\eta(G+\bar{\delta})\big)\,\sqrt{\frac{4(\Phi_{\max}-\Phi_{\min})}{T\,\eta}+4\bar{\delta}^{2}}+D(\varepsilon+\bar{\delta}).

Note on matching the
\eta\bar{\delta}^{2}
form.

If one strengthens Assumption
6.3

\|g_{t}-\nabla\Phi(x_{t})\|\leq\sqrt{\eta}\,\bar{\delta}
(or, equivalently, controls the projection error

\sqrt{\eta}
), then the same derivation replaces
4\bar{\delta}^{2}

by
4\eta\bar{\delta}^{2}

\sqrt{\frac{1}{T\eta}+\eta\bar{\delta}^{2}}
scaling
(up to universal constants).
∎
