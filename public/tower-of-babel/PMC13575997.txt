FICA and HOPE in Clinical Care: A Narrative Review Comparing 2 Spiritual Screening Tools Used by Physicians
Authors: Huma Manjra, Jacob Cho, Andrea Yeung, Luke Okamuro, Christine Liu, Marlaine Figueroa Gray, January Bethany Turner, Andre Cipta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review aims to compare the FICA (faith, importance/influence, community, address in care) and HOPE (sources of hope, organized religion, personal spirituality/practices, effects on care) spiritual screening tools across multiple domains, including clinical utility, implementation feasibility, and psychometric properties, in order to synthesize evidence and provide guidance to health care practitioners and educators regarding the relative strengths and limitations of the 2 tools. This review of published literature involved a structured search in PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, and PsycINFO, from which 2 reviewers independently screened, extracted, and synthesized data using predefined criteria. The studies included in the review assessed the use of the FICA and/or HOPE tools with adult patient populations and health care professionals in multiple countries, primarily within academic and hospital-based settings. FICA was more frequently used in clinical and educational settings and was noted for its structured format and integration into serious illness care. Meanwhile, HOPE was noted for its open-ended format and greater flexibility in exploring personal spirituality, particularly among patients identifying as spiritual but not religious. Although there does not seem to be research validating or using psychometric resources to analyze these tools, qualitative studies indicated their perceived value in enhancing holistic care. Both FICA and HOPE are valuable frameworks for initiating spiritual screenings in clinical practice. FICA may be more suitable in fast-paced environments because of its concise structure, whereas HOPE offers greater depth and personalization. Further research should focus on developing validated outcome measures and examining the impact of spiritual screenings on patient care.

Introduction

Spirituality is defined as “the aspect of humanity that refers to the way individuals seek and express meaning and purpose and the way they experience their connectedness to the moment, to self, to others, to nature, and to the significant or sacred”. 1 Spirituality is an important aspect of health and well-being 2,3 and is associated with better health care outcomes. 4–6 Integrated spiritual care interventions have been associated with reduced use of pain medications, lower anxiety, and an improved overall sense of well-being. 7 Inquiring about one’s spirituality also offers an opportunity for the clinician to provide person-centered care and develop a deeper practitioner-patient relationship. 8,9

Many patients, especially those who are hospitalized or terminally ill, want their health care providers to include spirituality in discussions about their health care, 10,11 yet studies show this rarely takes place 11,12 because of the barriers clinicians face to incorporating spiritual care into clinical practice, such as a lack of experience with discussing spirituality. 13,14

Two of the most well-known spiritual screening tools that may be used by health care practitioners are the FICA (faith, importance/influence, community, address in care) Spiritual History Tool and the HOPE (sources of hope, organized religion, personal spirituality/practices, effects on care) questions. 15,16 The FICA Spirituality Tool was created to offer a step-by-step guide for clinicians to gather information about a patient’s spiritual history and preferences. 15 Empirical studies have demonstrated the feasibility and clinical utility of the FICA tool in diverse settings, such as oncology and palliative care, where it has been shown to correlate with quality-of-life measures and to facilitate the integration of spiritual needs into patient care. 17,18

The HOPE questions were developed as a teaching tool to incorporate a spiritual assessment into the regular patient interview. 16 The goal of the HOPE questions is to generate an open-ended conversation to explore an individual’s spirituality and its effects on their life. In inquiring about a patient’s source of hope, the tool aims to be more sensitive to those who do not identify as religious/spiritual, those with diverse religious backgrounds, and those who have been hurt by religion. This discussion can then guide the clinician to incorporate spirituality into the patient’s care or modify their treatment plan based on their improved understanding of the patient. The literature describes the HOPE tool as a practical instrument for integrating spiritual history into the patient’s overall assessment and indicates that this tool has contributed to understanding the impact of spirituality in diverse clinical settings, especially in primary care and palliative care. 19–21

Whereas studies refer to FICA and HOPE as assessment tools, it is international best practice to recognize them as initial screening tools for interdisciplinary clinicians to take a brief history and identify referral sources for spiritual care support. 22 Meanwhile, a full spiritual assessment is a detailed, in-depth investigation performed by a specialist to inform a comprehensive care plan. 23,24 Thus, this review refers to FICA and HOPE as screening tools, in accord with best practices.

Although FICA and HOPE are the most commonly used spiritual screening tools in clinical settings, there remains a lack of comparative reviews that examine their distinct dimensions, such as clinical utility, implementation feasibility, psychometric properties, and overall strengths and limitations. Most existing studies on spirituality screening tools are either general or limited in scope. These reviews are often limited to specific specialties (eg, oncology, palliative care) or geographic regions, 15,16 offering minimal comparative insight. As a result, health care practitioners and medical educators are left without clear, evidence-informed guidance on which tools are best suited for clinical workflows.

This review aims to address this gap by directly comparing the FICA and HOPE tools, with a focus on their clinical utility, psychometric properties, and feasibility across diverse clinical settings. It seeks to identify practical applications for health care practitioners; highlight current evidence gaps, particularly in validation and implementation; and provide actionable recommendations for integrating spiritual screening into medical education and clinical care.

Although this review centers on screening tools that health care practitioners can use to initiate spiritual inquiry, it is also important to acknowledge the role of chaplain-led spiritual assessment instruments, such as the 7 × 7 Spiritual Assessment Model and the Assessment-Intervention-Meaning model, in follow-up care and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Methods

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, and PsycINFO from April through June 2025 with librarian support. These databases were selected to capture biomedical, nursing, psychological, international, and interdisciplinary literature on spiritual screening tools and the integration of spirituality into patient care. The search was not restricted by publication date and was intentionally broad and inclusive. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Kaiser Permanente Bernard J Tyson School of Medicine. Interviews were conducted in accordance with established ethical standards, and all identifying information was removed to ensure confidentiality. Particular care was taken given the sensitive nature of infertility and religious decision-making.

Title and abstract screening were independently performed by 2 student authors (HM and JC), who also completed full-text review of eligible studies. Because the reviewers were affiliated with different institutions, their database access differed slightly; accordingly, each reviewer screened the databases available through their institution. Inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed articles published in English with full-text availability. Research focused on the FICA spirituality tool and/or the HOPE spiritual assessment was included. In terms of study designs, this review included randomized controlled trials, intervention studies, observational studies, review articles, and qualitative research. The following types of studies were excluded: studies that used tools not designed for clinical use or intended solely for chaplains; studies that focused only on measuring patient spirituality or spiritual distress; non-English publications; and editorials, letters, and conference abstracts without full data. The included studies were conducted across a range of countries and regions, including the United States, Belgium, Jerusalem, the United Kingdom, Australia, Italy, Pakistan, and Brazil, suggesting broad international interest in spirituality within education and clinical training for health professions. Methodologically, the literature was diverse and included qualitative studies, cross-sectional survey designs, and narrative reviews.

Search terms included combinations of keywords such as “FICA spirituality tool,” “HOPE spiritual assessment,” “spiritual screening,” “spiritual history taking,” “electronic health record” or “EHR,” “implementation,” “clinical utility,” “psychometric,” “validation,” “hospital setting,” and “medical education.” In PubMed, additional terms related to clinical use, implementation, training, and validation were not included, because of a lack of results. However, versions of these terms were incorporated in the CINAHL, Embase, and PsycINFO searches to retrieve more relevant results.

A structured Microsoft Excel workbook was used to maintain an audit trail and manage study selection and data extraction. One worksheet tracked retrieved citations, screening decisions, and exclusions, whereas a second worksheet captured standardized data from the included studies. The extracted variables included study characteristics, population, setting, identity of the spiritual assessment tool evaluated, and outcomes relevant to the review aims. Both the student reviewers and the senior authors independently reviewed the Excel workbook, including the article-tracking and data-extraction sheets, to confirm study inclusion decisions and verify the accuracy and validity of the extracted data from the included articles. Results

An initial search identified 230 articles, including conference abstracts, of which 224 remained after the removal of duplicates (Figure 2). Titles and abstracts were screened, and 173 records were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria. A total of 51 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 24 studies were included in the final analysis.

The included studies varied substantially in design, clinical setting, participant population, and intended application of the spiritual screening tools. Of the 24 included studies, 12 evaluated the FICA tool alone, 7 examined only the HOPE tool, and 5 assessed both, either individually or in combination with other instruments. Studies were conducted across diverse settings, including oncology, palliative care, heart failure, primary care, nursing education, and undergraduate and graduate medical education. Nearly half of the studies were conducted in settings involving serious illness, oncology, or end-of-life care.

Four major thematic purposes emerged: 1) educational applications, 2) evaluation of clinical utility, 3) reported psychometric evidence, and 4) feasibility of implementation.

EDUCATIONAL APPLICATION

Several studies evaluated FICA and HOPE as teaching instruments in undergraduate medical education, graduate medical education, and nursing education. 25–28 Across these studies, curricular interventions incorporating FICA and/or HOPE were associated with improved learner confidence in discussing or providing spiritual care, increased recognition of patients’ spiritual concerns, and greater perceived preparedness for addressing spirituality in clinical settings. 25–28 Among psychiatric mental health nursing students, incorporating the HOPE questionnaire demonstrated improved confidence in providing spiritual care and greater awareness of the role of spirituality in psychiatric nursing. 26 Among first-year medical students, postcurriculum assessments showed improved recognition of patients’ spiritual concerns and increased willingness to accommodate patients’ religious and cultural beliefs in medical decision-making. 27 A FICA-based curriculum among health care residents in Brazil was associated with increased awareness of spirituality/religiosity; improved confidence discussing spirituality with patients; and reductions in stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. 25 A pilot curriculum incorporating both FICA and HOPE among preclinical medical students and internal medicine residents demonstrated increased agreement regarding the appropriateness of spiritual inquiry and improved self-perceived competence in conducting spiritual histories. 28

CLINICAL UTILITY

Clinical utility was a commonly discussed theme. FICA was frequently described as supporting patient care and clinical communication by providing a structured approach to spiritual history-taking; helping clinicians explore patients’ sources of meaning, coping, hope, and spiritual strengths; and identifying spiritual concerns relevant to care planning. 17,29–34 In the context of serious illnesses, including cancer, heart failure, and end-of-life care, FICA was used to examine how spiritual concerns relate to well-being, adherence to lifestyle interventions, and medical decision-making, including code status discussions. 17,29–34

HOPE was frequently discussed in palliative care contexts, where studies highlighted its role in supporting comprehensive spiritual care, therapeutic relationships, and care planning aligned with patients’ spiritual needs. 16,20,35,36 It was also described as a helpful framework for addressing spiritual concerns in an unbiased manner. 36

Studies assessing both FICA and HOPE suggested that these tools could help clinicians document patient preferences, clarify spiritual influences on health, and support treatment planning. 35,37 These studies also highlighted their potential in helping nonchaplain practitioners recognize spiritual distress and guide spiritual conversations in routine care. 35,37

Overall, both tools were described as useful tools for facilitating spiritual history-taking, supporting patient-centered care, and helping clinicians integrate spiritual concerns into clinical decision-making.

REPORTED PSYCHOMETRIC EVIDENCE

Reported psychometric evidence for both tools was notably limited across the literature. Most studies did not report any formal psychometric testing or provide indicators related to sensitivity, specificity, validity, or reliability. 3,14,16,17,19,20,25–30,32,35,36,38,39 Only 3 of the 24 included studies reported some form of validation-related evidence. 31,37,40 One study that examined both FICA and HOPE referenced limited psychometric support, 40 and 2 studies assessing FICA alone noted prior work reporting correlations with spiritual quality of life and Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.77 to 0.89. 31,37 However, none of the studies conducted comprehensive psychometric assessment.

FEASIBILITY OF IMPLEMENTATION

Implementation feasibility emerged as a recurring theme across the included studies. FICA was generally characterized as practical, brief, and adaptable for clinical use across clinical settings. 17,40 In patients with advanced heart failure, the framework was described as easily integrated into routine history-taking and goals-of-care conversations, particularly in contexts where existential distress and meaning-making are highly prevalent. The authors also noted that use of the tool does not require extensive theological expertise, reducing a potential barrier to implementation. 33 In oncology and palliative care settings, FICA was similarly described as usable by clinicians and feasible for integration into routine cancer care workflows. 21,39 However, implementation concerns were also identified. In one qualitative study, general practitioners described the tool’s survey-like structure as potentially limiting patient comfort or conversational flow during spiritual discussions. 32

HOPE was also described as clinically feasible, particularly in palliative care settings, where studies emphasized its conversational nature and ease of use. 16,35,36 However, implementation barriers were also reported. One study found that insufficient training and concern about being reported to regulatory bodies contributed to a reluctance to discuss spirituality in clinical care. 19

Across studies evaluating both tools, their mnemonic structures made them easy to remember, time-efficient, and adaptable when chaplains were unavailable. 20,35,40

Overall, both FICA and HOPE appeared to be feasible as practical spiritual screening frameworks, although implementation barriers included limited training, clinician comfort with spiritual inquiry, and challenges integrating spiritual history-taking into routine clinical workflows.

Discussion

This review comparatively evaluated the FICA and HOPE tools, the 2 most commonly used spiritual assessment instruments by health care practitioners, based on clinical utility, psychometric validation, and implementation feasibility. The aim of this work was to explore practical applications for health care practitioners in order to 1) identify the tool that best aligns with their clinical context and patient population; 2) be mindful of current evidence gaps, particularly around implementation and psychometrics; and 3) offer recommendations for integration into medical education and clinical practice.

A major finding of this literature search is that one tool cannot be identified as superior to the other; rather, each offers distinct strengths and limitations depending on both clinical context and intended use.

The HOPE tool is frequently described as more concise, comprehensive, and conversational than FICA, 16 as well as more flexible and inclusive of patients with diverse or nonreligious worldviews. 35,36 Its focus on meaning-making and sources of hope allows for a patient-centered approach that can be easily adapted across cultures and spiritual identities. HOPE’s flexibility, however, can be a limitation in fast-paced clinical settings, where time constraints and a lack of formal training may hinder its use. Clinicians have reported discomfort using HOPE without adequate preparation. 16 Nevertheless, in settings where time and training are available, HOPE may be better suited for individualized care and settings requiring adaptability, such as palliative care and outpatient consultation.

In contrast, this literature search reveals a pattern whereby the FICA tool offers a more structured, standardized, and consistent approach. 35 It is better represented in the literature and is more commonly used in settings involving serious illness, such as oncology, heart failure, and end-of-life care (see the results section). FICA facilitates documentation, promotes consistency in spiritual history-taking, and has even been used to encourage clinician self-reflection, 39 an attribute not observed in HOPE. However, its rigidity may reduce cultural adaptability and lead to perceptions of being overly scripted or impersonal. Additionally, FICA may require more training and time to implement effectively, particularly in settings without institutional support. 34

Both tools were widely regarded across this literature review as accessible and implementable, thanks in part to their mnemonic structures, brevity, and respectful approach to spiritual history-taking. 40 These tools were also shown to provide a structure for documenting patient care preferences, improving therapeutic alliance and exploring factors in medical decision-making.

Both tools also share common implementation challenges, including time constraints, inconsistent training, and lack of routine integration into clinical workflows. 28,35 The literature suggests that training, even brief workshops, substantially improves clinician comfort and uptake of both tools, including among medical students. 28,38 Guided practice with chaplains has also shown promise in improving integration. However, this review found a notable lack of psychometric research evaluating the effectiveness of such training, particularly over the long term.

Educational applications represented another important use case for both FICA and HOPE. However, most educational outcomes were limited to short-term, self-reported measures, with limited studies exploring long-term retention, behavioral change, or patient-level outcomes. Future educational research should evaluate whether training with FICA and HOPE leads to sustained changes in clinician behavior and improves spiritual care delivery in real clinical encounters.

Most studies in this review used either FICA or HOPE in isolation rather than in direct comparison. The only direct comparative study was the 2013 systematic review of Lucchetti et al, which examined 25 tools across 16 criteria. Although their work offered a broad overview of spiritual assessment instruments, it did not provide focused guidance on how to choose between FICA and HOPE in clinical settings. In contrast, this review aims to support health care practitioners in selecting between these 2 widely used tools by synthesizing existing evidence on their practical strengths and limitations.

Health care practitioners benefit from understanding spiritual assessment tools not only for direct clinical use but also to support referrals and interprofessional collaboration. The authors acknowledge that the adoption of FICA and HOPE face some challenges specific to the times during which this literature review was conducted. First, both tools were developed and published at the end of the 20th century, whereas this study was conducted in the 21st century. Although some time has passed since their publication, FICA and HOPE remain 2 of the most prominent and therefore relevant contemporary spiritual screening tools. 15,16 For example, FICA received an update as recently as 2025. In addition, at the time this review was conducted, divisions exist throughout society and politics that transform spirituality into a contentious topic that some practitioners may feel hesitant to broach so as to avoid any political discussions. FICA and HOPE were designed to help practitioners navigate these complicated issues, 15,16 and this literature review was designed to help practitioners navigate between the 2 screening tools.

In educational settings, the FICA tool may be more appropriate in medical school so that students can learn to initiate spiritual screening in a structured and targeted fashion. 27,28 On the other hand, HOPE may be better suited for residency and fellowship training, where health care practitioners are honing their interview skills and building longitudinal relationships with patients. 28 For clinical care, the findings of this review suggest that FICA may be more suitable for structured use in high-acuity or fast-paced settings because of its easy-to-remember structure and standardized documentation, whereas HOPE may be better aligned with patient-centered dialogue in settings where more time is available and with patients from diverse spiritual or cultural backgrounds. However, more research is needed to compare the use of these tools in diverse educational and clinical settings.

Future studies should prioritize more rigorous psychometric evaluation of both FICA and HOPE, including reproducibility, test–retest reliability, responsiveness to change, and applicability across diverse spiritual and cultural contexts. Research should also move beyond palliative and end-of-life care to examine these tools in general medical settings and those involving less serious illnesses. Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate sustained adoption in clinical workflows and to determine whether use of spiritual screening influences patient outcomes, decision-making, and care planning. Future work should also aim for broader global inclusion across cultures and languages to enhance generalizability of findings.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the scope of the tools analyzed was intentionally narrow. Although numerous spiritual screening tools exist, this study focused on only FICA and HOPE because of their widespread clinical use and practicality.

Another limitation was the scarcity of literature evaluating certain aspects of FICA and HOPE. Notably, there is a lack of robust psychometric evaluation, with few studies assessing the reproducibility, reliability, or validity of either tool. Existing studies have concentrated on palliative care, serious illness, and end-of-life settings, with fewer studies in the contexts of primary care, acute care, and less serious illnesses. Evidence regarding sustained, long-term implementation of these tools was also found to be limited.

Limitations also arose in the review process. Although PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, and PsycINFO were searched, the study team did not have access to Ovid MEDLINE. Because PubMed indexes MEDLINE, this limitation was partially mitigated, but some relevant studies may have been missed. Excluding non-English articles also limited the applicability of the findings to non-English-speaking populations.

Finally, an updated version of the FICA Spiritual History Tool was published in 2025; however, because all studies included in this review examined the prior version, the findings and comparisons are based on the original tool.

Conclusion

In sum, this study highlights both the promise and the current limitations of FICA and HOPE as spiritual screening tools. Although each tool offers distinct advantages, the evidence base remains limited, with little robust validation or long-term evaluation. Addressing these gaps through rigorous, contextually diverse, longitudinal research will be essential in efforts to strengthen the role of spiritual screening in advancing whole-person-centered care.
