Reinforcing Human Behavior Simulation via Verbal Feedback
Weiwei Sun, Xuhui Zhou, Jiarui Liu, Weihua Du, Haojia Sun, Yiqing Xie, Qianou Ma, Sihao Chen, Mengting Wan, Longqi Yang, Pei Zhou, Sherry Wu, Sean Welleck, Graham Neubig, Yiming Yang, Maarten Sap, [3pt] Carnegie Mellon University

Abstract
Humans learn social norms and behaviors from verbal feedback (e.g., a parent saying “that was rude” or a friend explaining “here’s why that hurt”). Yet, learning from feedback for LLMs has largely focused on domains like code and math where RL rewards are directly verifiable and condensed into scalar values. As LLMs are increasingly also used for simulating human behavior, e.g., standing in for users, patients, students, and other personas, there is a pressing need to make them more human-like, which requires embracing a fundamentally different kind of signal: feedback that is verbal, subjective, and multi-faceted. We present Ditto , a model trained by treating verbal feedback as a first-class signal in reinforcement learning. After each rollout, Ditto receives verbal feedback and generates a feedback-conditioned improved rollout; both outputs are jointly optimized with GRPO, distilling verbal guidance into the base policy without requiring feedback at test time. We also introduce Soul (Simulation gym Of hUman-Like behavior), a unified benchmark and training data suite spanning 10 tasks across six categories: Theory of Mind, character role play, social skill, learner simulation, user simulation, and persona simulation. Ditto achieves an average 36% improvement over the base model and exceeds GPT-5.4 on 6 of 10 Soul benchmarks, demonstrating that RL with verbal feedback is a promising direction for training LLMs to simulate human behavior.

From the Turing test to modern user simulators, the drive to faithfully model how people think, speak, and act has evolved into a practical necessity for training and evaluating collaborative AI systems.
Today, LLMs are increasingly called upon to serve as these vital proxies—standing in for users in dialogue systems

Reinforcement learning for LLMs, whether RLHF

A score of 0.6 conveys almost nothing about

Humans themselves learn social behavior not from scores but from verbal feedback, through explanations, corrections, and dialogue
(
Tomasello 2019

Related Work

2.1
Incorporating Verbal Feedback in RL Training

Standard RL for language models—whether RLHF
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
, or GRPO
(
DeepSeek-AI 2025
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
—reduces all feedback to scalar rewards or binary preferences.
To handle complex feedback signals, another line of work decomposes the scalar reward into finer-grained or multi-dimensional signals while remaining in the scalar regime
(
Wu et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
.
These methods enrich the reward structure but still compress each dimension into a scalar, losing rich nature of verbal feedback.

To incorporate verbal feedback into improving LLM agents, prior work has explored supervised fine-tuning
(
Scheurer et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
or inference-time self-reflection
(
Shinn et al. 2023
;
Madaan et al. 2023
)
, but these approaches lack the exploration capacity of RL or do not update model weights from experience.
A recent line of work instead incorporates verbal feedback directly into RL optimization.
Self-Rewarding Language Models
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
generate verbal self-judgments to construct preference pairs for iterative DPO.
Experiential RL
(
Shi et al. 2026
)
introduces an experience-reflection-consolidation loop mixed RL and supervised fine-tuning.
Concurrent to our approach, RLTF
(
Song et al. 2026
)
formalizes reinforcement learning from text feedback and proposes self-distillation (RLTF-SD), which trains the single-turn policy to match its own feedback-conditioned second-turn generations.
However, these works focus extensively on domains with verifiable rewards, such as mathematics and reasoning puzzles.
Human behavior simulation poses fundamentally different challenges: interactions are multi-turn, rewards are subjective and multi-dimensional.

LLM-based human simulation spans a broad landscape of tasks.
User simulation has evolved from rule-based and statistical approaches for dialogue management

, information-seeking
(

)
, and recommendation
(

.
Beyond user simulation, LLMs are increasingly used for social interaction
(

;

)
, character role-play
(
Wang et al. 2026
)
, persona simulation
(

)
, and learner simulation
(

.

However, recent efforts have revealed systematic

3
Method

3.1
Problem Formulation

We study the problem of training a language model for human simulation tasks. Given an input
x
(e.g., an instruction, scenario, dialogue history, or user profile), the model generates an output trajectory

y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),

where
\pi_{\theta}
is the current policy.

Each sampled trajectory is evaluated by a task-specific judge or simulator, which returns supervision for learning. In the standard RL setting, this supervision is reduced to a scalar reward

r(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}.

Our goal is to train the policy
\pi_{\theta}
to maximize task performance in these human simulation tasks, while allowing training to exploit richer judge supervision when available.

3.2
Verbal Feedback

In many human simulation tasks, the judge provides not only a scalar reward but also rich verbal feedback, such as dimension-wise scores, textual critiques, and task-specific suggestions. Formally, for a rollout
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
, the judge returns

(r,h)=\mathcal{J}(x,y),

(1)

where
r
is a scalar reward and
h
is structured feedback describing what was done well, what went wrong, and how the behavior can be improved.

This feedback is often highly informative. For example, in human behavior simulation, it may reflect dimensions such as believability, relationship maintenance, knowledge consistency, goal achievement, and adherence to social norms. Standard RL methods typically compress all such information into a single scalar reward, which can lose useful learning signal.

To ensure robustness of learning, we cast
h
as privileged information
(
Vapnik & Vashist 2009
;
Vapnik & Izmailov 2015
)
: it is available during training but not at test time. Our goal is therefore to use this verbal feedback to improve the base policy
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
, while keeping inference-time generation unchanged.

3.3
Learning from Verbal Feedback

We aim to optimize the base policy
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
using reward and feedback during training, so the policy internalizes feedback-driven improvements without requiring feedback at inference time.

To achieve this, for each sampled rollout

y_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),

(2)

we ask the judge to provide reward and feedback

(r_{0},h)=\mathcal{J}(x,y_{0}),

(3)

and then sample a second rollout conditioned on the same feedback:

y_{1}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,h),\qquad r_{1}=R(x,y_{1}).

(4)

Here,
y_{0}
is the original rollout from the base policy, which we refer to as the
draft
rollout, while
y_{1}
is the feedback-conditioned rollout, which we refer to as the
refined
rollout. Both rollouts are generated by the same policy with shared model weights; they differ only in their prompting context, where the refined rollout additionally uses explicit verbal feedback to guide improvement.

We optimize both behaviors during training. First, we train the base policy to learn from feedback-induced improvements without conditioning on feedback at inference. Second, we include an additional GRPO objective on feedback-conditioned rollouts themselves, so the model also becomes better at generating improved responses from feedback during training.

Concretely, for each prompt
x
, we first sample
G
original rollouts
\{y_{i,0}\}_{i=1}^{G}
from the base policy. For each
y_{i,0}
, we obtain judge feedback
h_{i}
and generate a corresponding feedback-conditioned rollout
y_{i,1}
. This gives the training group

\mathcal{G}(x)=\{y_{i,0},y_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{G},

(5)

which contains both the original rollouts and their feedback-conditioned counterparts.

We compute group-relative advantages over samples in
\mathcal{G}(x)
:
We compute group-relative advantages over all samples in
\mathcal{G}(x)
:

A_{i,j}=\frac{r_{i,j}-\mu_{r}}{\sigma_{r}},\qquad\mu_{r}=\frac{1}{2G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{j=0}^{1}r_{i,j},\qquad\sigma_{r}=\mathrm{std}(\{r_{i,j}\}_{i=1,j=0}^{G,1}).

(6)

We then apply the standard clipped GRPO objective to this group:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{group}}=-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i,j}|}\min\!\Big(\rho_{i,j,t}A_{i,j},\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{i,j,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{i,j}\Big)\right],

(7)

where

\rho_{i,j,t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,j,t}\mid x,y_{i,j,<t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,j,t}\mid x,y_{i,j,<t})}.

(8)

In addition, we apply an extra GRPO update on the feedback-conditioned rollouts
\{y_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{G}
alone, treating them as a separate GRPO group:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fb}}=-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i,1}|}\min\!\Big(\hat{\rho}_{i,t}\hat{A}_{i,1},\mathrm{clip}(\hat{\rho}_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i,1}\Big)\right],

(9)

where
\hat{A}_{i,1}
is computed by normalizing rewards within the feedback-conditioned group
\{r_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{G}
. The final training loss is

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{group}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fb}}.

(10)

In this way, feedback serves two roles during training: it produces improved rollouts that help the base policy learn better behavior, and it also supports a direct RL signal for improving the model’s ability to respond to feedback itself.

3.4
Connections to Other RL Approaches

Our approach is inspired by, or developed concurrently with, several recent works on RL with textual feedback. We discuss the most relevant connections below.

Connection to RLTF

Our method is related to RL from Text Feedback (RLTF;
Song et al. 2026
), which also uses training-time text feedback to improve single-turn performance. Both methods first generate a student output, then a feedback-conditioned teacher output, and train the policy to absorb the teacher’s improvement. Our method differs in three ways: (1) we use GRPO over the joint group
{y_{0},y_{1}}
rather than AWR (advantage-weighted regression,
(
Peng et al. 2019
)
); (2) our teacher conditions on a judge-generated reflection
h
rather than raw feedback; and (3) unlike RLTF-SD, which targets tasks with
verifiable rewards
, we focus on social simulation, where feedback is noisy, subjective, and multi-dimensional.
We compare against an RLTF-SD variant in our ablation study (
section

6.2
).

Connection to ERL

Experiential RL (ERL)
(
Shi et al. 2026
)
instead uses supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on reject-sampled teacher rollouts to internalize feedback into the student model. In contrast, we train on all teacher rollouts with an RL objective, allowing us to differentiate teacher rollout quality in continuous-reward settings and improve sample efficiency. We also compare against an ERL variant in our ablation study.

Connection to SDPO

Several works propose self-distillation policy optimization, distilling textual feedback into the student with a reverse KL objective
(
Vapnik & Vashist 2009
;
Vapnik & Izmailov 2015
;
Shenfeld et al. 2026
;
Ye et al. 2026
;
Hubotter et al. 2026
)
. In contrast, our approach uses a forward alternative: sampling from the teacher rather than the student, and optimizing with group-normalized advantage. In addition, we optimize the feedback-conditioned model via
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fb}}
rather than using an EMA model. We also compare against SDPO-like variants in our ablation study.

Theory of Mind.

We include three tasks probing different aspects of mental-state reasoning.

FanToM

(

HiToM

ToMi

Character Role Play.

We include two character role-play tasks.

CoSER

(
Wang et al. 2026
)
evaluates literary character simulation from 771 books, where given a scenario, character profile, and motivations, the model conducts a multi-turn dialogue (up to 20 turns) scored by an LLM judge on Storyline Consistency, Anthropomorphism, Character Fidelity, and Storyline Quality. We evaluate 200 conversations (100 ID, 100 OOD) and use the 26.5k-dialogue training set.

LifeChoices

(
Xu et al. 2024a
)
evaluates persona-driven decision making with 1,462 decision points from 388 novels, where the model selects the option most consistent with a character’s original decision given context, scenario, and identity, emphasizing behavior-level fidelity and reasoning over characters’ knowledge, experiences, and evolving motivations.

Social Skill.

Sotopia

(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
evaluates social skills through dyadic agent simulation.
Given a scenario and private social goals for two agents, the agents interact for up to 20 turns to achieve their goals.
An LLM judge evaluates the interaction across seven dimensions, including believability, relationship, knowledge, secret-keeping, financial outcomes, and goal achievement.
Following
Zhou et al. 2026a
, we evaluate on the Sotopia-hard split of 100 scenarios.
For training, we use the 2,310 scenarios from Sotopia-
\pi

(
Yu et al. 2025
)

Learner Simulation.

Mistakes

(
Ross & Andreas 2025
)
evaluates whether models can faithfully simulate
incorrect

Given a multiple-choice math problem and a natural-language description of a misconception, the model must select the wrong answer a student with that misconception would choose.
We evaluate on 600 instances using accuracy.
For training, we use the Eedi dataset from the NeurIPS 2024 Kaggle competition ‘‘Mining Misconceptions in Mathematics’’

User Simulation.

MirrorBench

(
Hathidara et al. 2026
)
evaluates multi-turn human-like user utterance generation: given a synthesized user goal, the model generates all user turns while an LLM replays assistant responses, and the conversation is compared to the original human dialogue using lexical diversity metrics (MATTR, HD-D, Yule’s K) and LLM-judge realism scores. We evaluate 795 episodes across four domains and train on about 3,400 resampled conversations from the same corpora.

UserLLM

(
Naous et al. 2025
)
evaluates single-turn user utterance generation: given context and user intent, the model generates one user message. We evaluate CSQA (role adherence), NQ (intent adherence), and PRISM (diversity, intent decomposition, termination prediction, AI-detection; 1,800 test instances), and train on datasets constructed from PRISM (intent consistency), NaturalQuestions (user persistence), and CommonsenseQA (role fidelity).

Persona Simulation.

TwinVoice

(
Du et al. 2025
)
evaluates persona simulation via a discriminative task: given a user’s conversation history and a new anchor post, the model selects the response that best matches the user’s communication style. The 5,687 evaluation instances span social, interpersonal, and narrative persona, testing opinion consistency, memory recall, and lexical fidelity, and we use accuracy as the metric. For training, we construct 1,335 instances from three external corpora (Bluesky posts, Pushshift Telegram messages, and Project Gutenberg novels); persona histories are generated from each source, GPT-5.4-mini drafts candidate items, and GPT-5.4 filters them into the final multiple-choice training set.

Conclusion

We introduce
Ditto
, a reinforcement learning approach that treats verbal feedback as a first-class training signal for human behavior simulation. By generating feedback-conditioned improved rollouts and jointly optimizing them with the original outputs,
Ditto
enables the base policy to internalize rich, actionable guidance without requiring feedback at test time. To support this setting, we build
Soul
, a unified training and evaluation suite spanning 10 tasks across six categories of human simulation. Experiments show that verbal feedback substantially improves performance over standard GRPO, especially on generative and multi-turn tasks, while also improving training efficiency and safety-related behaviors. Together, our results suggest that moving beyond scalar rewards toward richer feedback is a promising direction for training more human-like simulators.

Appendix A
Appendix

Limitations.

Our approach has several limitations.
First, we rely on LLM judges as proxies for human judgment; while jury averaging reduces variance, it does not eliminate systematic biases shared across all judges.
Second, our evaluation is limited to English-language benchmarks and may not generalize to other languages or cultural contexts.
Third, the training pipeline requires running
K
judges on
N
candidates per context, which incurs significant computational cost.
Finally, our theoretical variance-reduction guarantees assume uncorrelated judge errors, which is an idealization—in practice, LLM judges may share correlated failure modes.

Future Work.

Promising directions include (1) incorporating human feedback to correct shared judge biases, (2) extending to multilingual and cross-cultural social simulation, (3) developing more efficient jury mechanisms (e.g., adaptive judge selection), and (4) exploring whether the social reasoning capabilities learned through our approach transfer to downstream agent tasks.

C.1
Theory of Mind

FanToM

(
Kim et al. 2023
)
stress-tests Theory of Mind in information-asymmetric multi-party conversations. Characters join and leave conversations at different points, creating natural knowledge gaps. The benchmark poses 1,086 questions across three categories—belief, answerability, and information accessibility—in both multiple-choice and free-response formats.

HiToM

(
He et al. 2024
)
evaluates higher-order Theory of Mind reasoning, requiring models to reason about nested beliefs (e.g., “Alice thinks that Bob thinks the object is in location X”). Scenarios include deception and varying communication patterns (private vs. public).

ToMi

(
Nematzadeh et al. 2018
)
is one of the most classic tests of Theory of Mind. We use the more challenging version by
Sclar et al. 2023
, which extends the paradigm through diverse paraphrasing of ToMi narratives. By varying surface forms of identical scenarios, it tests whether models exhibit robust Theory of Mind reasoning rather than exploiting shallow textual cues. We evaluate on 600 questions covering reality, memory, and first/second-order belief.

C.2
Character Role Play

CoSER

(
Wang et al. 2026
)
is a literary character role-playing benchmark built from 771 books containing 17,966 characters. Using a “given-circumstance acting” methodology inspired by theater, models must simulate multi-turn dialogues as literary characters in specified narrative settings. Evaluation uses LLM judges that identify specific flaws across four dimensions—Storyline Consistency, Anthropomorphism, Character Fidelity, and Storyline Quality—with severity ratings from 1 to 5. We evaluate on both in-distribution (ID, 100 conversations from seen books) and out-of-distribution (OOD, 100 conversations from unseen books) splits (see
Section

C.7
for the full generation protocol, scoring formula, and examples).

LifeChoices

(
Xu et al. 2024a
)
evaluates persona-driven decision making for literary characters. Given a character biography, scenario, and candidate options, the model must predict the decision that best matches the character’s original choice in the narrative. The dataset contains 1,462 decision points from 388 novels; we evaluate on 600 tasks using accuracy (see
Section

C.8
for task format, context length statistics, and examples).

C.3
Social Skill

Sotopia

(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
evaluates social interaction through two-agent simulations in realistic scenarios. Each agent receives a character background, social goals, and relationship context. Performance is measured across seven dimensions—believability, relationship, knowledge, secret-keeping, social rules, financial outcomes, and goal achievement—scored by LLM judges.

C.4
Learner Simulation

Mistakes

(
Ross & Andreas 2025
)
evaluates whether LLMs can simulate
incorrect
student reasoning in K-12 mathematics. Given a multiple-choice math problem and a misconception description, models must select the wrong answer that corresponds to that specific misconception. This tests a key aspect of user simulation: faithfully reproducing systematic human errors.

C.5
User Simulation

MirrorBench

(
Hathidara et al. 2026
)
evaluates user-proxy agents on producing human-like utterances, decoupled from downstream task success. Given an extracted user goal, the model iteratively generates all user turns in a conversation while assistant responses are replayed from the original data. Each generated turn is compared against the real human utterance. Evaluation uses both lexical diversity metrics (MATTR, Yule’s K, HD-D) and LLM-judge metrics, calibrated against human-human and proxy-proxy baselines. We evaluate on four subsets: ChatbotArena, ClariQ, OASST1, and QuLAC (see
Section

C.9
for detailed dataset statistics and examples).

UserLLM

(
Naous et al. 2025
)
evaluates single-turn user utterance generation. Given a conversation context and a user intent, the model generates one user turn. We evaluate on three test sets, each targeting different metrics (see
Section

C.10
for detailed metric definitions and examples):

•

CSQA (Role Adherence):
The model plays a user who asks a commonsense question. Role adherence is measured by a
substring-matching
rule: the answer-choice texts are normalized and checked against the generated turn. If the turn contains one or two (but not all) of the answer choices, it scores 0—the model is leaking specific answers rather than staying in the questioner role. If it mentions none, it scores 1.

•

NQ (Intent Adherence):
The model plays a user asking a factoid question. Intent adherence is measured by an
LLM judge
that classifies whether the generated turn refuses to answer the question (as a real user would) or inappropriately provides the answer itself.

•

PRISM (Multi-metric):
The model generates first turns from high-level intents. Four metrics are computed:
first-turn diversity
(pairwise 1-gram Jaccard distance across generations),
intent decomposition
(1-gram overlap between intent keywords and the generated turn),
termination F1
(whether the model correctly predicts conversation ending), and
AI detector score
(whether the text passes an AI-detection classifier as human-written).

C.6
Persona Simulation

TwinVoice

(
Du et al. 2025
)
is a multi-dimensional benchmark for persona simulation spanning Social Persona, Interpersonal Persona, and Narrative Persona. Given a persona history and a new stimulus problem, the model must identify the response that best matches the target persona in a discriminative 4-way multiple-choice task. The benchmark contains 5,687 test instances in total and reports accuracy, while also organizing examples around six fine-grained capabilities, including opinion consistency, memory recall, logical reasoning, lexical fidelity, persona tone, and syntactic style.

C.7
CoSER

CoSER
(
Wang et al. 2026
)
is a literary character role-playing benchmark built from 771 books containing 17,966 characters. Using a “given-circumstance acting” (GCA) methodology inspired by theater, models must simulate multi-turn dialogues as literary characters in specified narrative settings. We describe the generation protocol, evaluation metrics, and dataset details below.

Generation protocol.

Each task (called a
circumstance
) specifies a scene from a book, including: (1) a
scenario
describing the setting, atmosphere, and dramatic tension; (2) a
plot summary
providing narrative context; (3) detailed
character profiles
for each speaking character; and (4)
character motivations
(inner thoughts and goals). The model must simulate a multi-character conversation in this setting.

The
actor model under evaluation
plays
all
characters in the conversation—the same LLM sequentially portrays each character
c\in\mathcal{C}
in the given setting. Two auxiliary agents support the simulation:

•

Character agents
(actor model): One instantiation per speaking character. Each receives the scenario, its own profile and motivation, and the profiles of other characters. It generates dialogue in the format
[inner_thought] speech (action)
.

•

Environment agent
(auxiliary): Describes environmental changes and minor character reactions based on the ongoing dialogue (e.g., crowd murmurs, weather shifts).

•

Next-speaker predictor (NSP)
(auxiliary): After each turn, predicts which character speaks next (or outputs
<END CHAT>
to terminate). Termination requires a minimum of 5 rounds.

Conversations run for up to 20 rounds. In practice, the NSP terminates most conversations earlier (mean: 14.2 character turns, range: 6–20).

The generated conversation is then compared against the
ground-truth dialogue
from the original book, which serves as the reference for evaluation.

Evaluation protocol.

Evaluation combines LLM-based flaw detection with automatic lexical metrics. For each of four dimensions, an LLM judge (acting as a “literary critic”) receives the full context—book title, plot summary, scenario, character profiles, the reference conversation, and the dimension-specific evaluation criteria—and identifies specific
flaws
in the simulated conversation. Each flaw is assigned a severity from 1 (minor) to 5 (severe).

The per-dimension score is computed as:

\text{score}=\max\!\Big(0,\;\min\!\big(100-(\text{total\_severity}-0.3\times N_{\text{turns}})\times 5,\;100\big)\Big)

where
N_{\text{turns}}
is the number of character turns in the simulation. The
0.3\times N_{\text{turns}}
term provides a length-based tolerance: longer conversations are expected to accumulate more minor flaws. The overall reward is the mean of four dimension scores, normalized to
[0,1]
.

Additionally,
BLEU
and
ROUGE-L
are computed against the reference dialogue, though these are typically low (BLEU
\approx 0.02
) since valid simulations can differ substantially in wording from the original.

Evaluation dimensions.

The four dimensions capture different facets of role-playing quality:

•

Storyline Consistency
: Whether characters’ reactions (emotions, attitudes, behaviors) align with those in the reference conversation. Detects deviations from the original dramatic arc.

•

Anthropomorphism
: How human-like and natural characters behave. Checks for: self-identity (goals, decisions, preferences vs. “helpful AI assistant” behavior), emotional depth (psychological complexity, use of subtext), persona coherence (consistent personality), and social interaction (understanding of others, contextual reactions).

•

Character Fidelity
: How well characters match their established profiles from the book. Evaluated only for major characters. Checks: appropriate language/vocabulary for the character’s background, character-specific knowledge, personality-consistent emotions and behaviors, and appropriate social dynamics given relationships and status.

•

Storyline Quality
: Logical consistency and narrative quality. Checks: natural flow and progression (no verbosity, redundancy, or repetition), and factual consistency between statements.

Human validation.

The original paper validates the LLM-based evaluation against human judgments. Three annotators scored 7 representative LLMs on 60 samples using a 1–10 scale. Human rankings generally align with LLM-judged scores: CoSER-70B ranks highest under both human (6.78 avg, 86.9% win rate) and LLM evaluation. The full GCA evaluation protocol achieves 68.6% alignment with human judges (GPT-4o as critic); DeepSeek-R1 as critic achieves 77.5%. Ablation analysis shows all GCA components contribute: removing reference dialogues, scoring rubrics, length correction, or dimension separation each reduces alignment. Interestingly, simple n-gram metrics (BLEU: 75.3%, ROUGE-L: 72.0%) achieve competitive alignment with LLM judges, though they cannot capture the nuanced flaw-level feedback that GCA provides.

(

Task format.

MirrorBench

MirrorBench
(
Hathidara et al. 2026
)
evaluates user-proxy agents on producing human-like utterances, decoupled from downstream task success. We describe the generation protocol, evaluation metrics, our adaptation, and dataset details below.

Generation protocol.

Given a reference dialogue
d_{j}^{\text{ref}}=[(u_{j,1}^{\text{ref}},a_{j,1}^{\text{ref}}),\ldots,(u_{j,L}^{\text{ref}},a_{j,L}^{\text{ref}})]
and a synthesized user goal
g_{j}
summarizing the user’s intent and communication style, MirrorBench generates a synthetic rollout
\hat{d}_{j}=[(\hat{u}_{j,1},\hat{a}_{j,1}),\ldots,(\hat{u}_{j,L},\hat{a}_{j,L})]
as follows.
At each turn
t
, the user-proxy LLM
\theta_{u}
receives the accumulated dialogue history
[(\hat{u}_{j,1},\hat{a}_{j,1}),\ldots,(\hat{u}_{j,t-1},\hat{a}_{j,t-1})]
plus the goal
g_{j}
as a system prompt, and generates the next user turn
\hat{u}_{j,t}
.
The assistant LLM
\theta_{a}
then produces a contextual reply
\hat{a}_{j,t}
, conditioned on both the ongoing synthetic dialogue and the original reference conversation
d_{j}^{\text{ref}}
, so that the rollout trajectory stays close to the original.
Critically,
all
user turns are generated by the proxy and
all
assistant turns are generated by an LLM—there is no verbatim replay of original turns.
In the original paper, the assistant is fixed to GPT-4o for all primary experiments; the authors report that “LLM hallucinations can occasionally induce drift” in assistant responses but find divergence in
<
1% of samples.

Evaluation granularity.

All metrics operate at the
full-conversation level
, not turn-by-turn.
The judge sees the entire proxy conversation
\hat{d}_{j}
(or its user-side transcript) compared against the entire reference
d_{j}^{\text{ref}}
, and produces a single score per episode.
For lexical metrics, all proxy user turns are concatenated into one token sequence
\hat{\mathbf{t}}_{i}
and compared against the concatenated human user turns
\mathbf{t}_{i}^{\text{ref}}
.

Metrics.

MirrorBench combines two metric families: three
lexical diversity
metrics and three
judge-based realism
metrics, capturing complementary facets of human-likeness.

Lexical diversity metrics

measure vocabulary richness and repetition patterns of the proxy’s user turns, z-score normalized against the human distribution on the same dataset:

•

MATTR
(Moving-Average Type-Token Ratio): vocabulary diversity over a sliding window (
w\!=\!50
).

•

HD-D
(Hypergeometric Distribution Diversity): vocabulary diversity robust to sequence length.

•

Yule’s K
: repetitiveness from the token-frequency spectrum (higher
K
= more repetition).

Judge-based realism metrics

use LLM judges to assess whether the proxy’s user turns
sound
like a real human. The three metrics differ in what information the judge receives and what question it answers:

•

GTEval
(reference-comparative): The judge sees
both
the proxy conversation and the human reference side by side, and scores their similarity on
[0,1]
. This measures how closely the proxy reproduces the specific human behavior.

•

Pairwise Indistinguishability (PI)
(reference-discriminative): The judge sees two anonymized conversations—one proxy, one human—in random order (labeled A/B) and picks which user sounds more human. This is a blind A/B test; the score is the proxy’s win rate. A score near 0.5 means the proxy is indistinguishable from the human.

•

Rubric-and-Reason (RNR)
(reference-free): The judge sees
only
the proxy conversation and rates whether the user sounds human-like based on a rubric covering style, behavior, and tone. Returns YES/NO. This judges realism in absolute terms without any human reference.

Each judge metric is executed with a self-consistency parameter
c\geq 1
(repeated judgments under the same prompt). Scores are calibrated via Human–Human (HH) and Proxy–Proxy (PP) controls: HH compares the human conversation to itself (expected ceiling
\approx 1.0
for GTEval,
\approx 0.5
for PI), and PP compares the proxy to itself (expected baseline).

Our adaptation.

In our evaluation framework, we run MirrorBench in
single-turn assistant mode
: the user-proxy LLM generates all user turns iteratively, but assistant responses are replayed verbatim from the original dataset rather than generated by a separate LLM.
This simplification is inconsequential for conversations with a single user turn (most of Arena, all of QuLAC), since there is no subsequent turn where assistant coherence matters.
For multi-turn conversations (all of ClariQ, most of OASST1), replayed assistant responses may become contextually misaligned if the proxy’s user turn diverges from the original, potentially causing cascading drift.
We use GPT-5.4 as the judge model for all metrics.

UserLLM

UserLLM
(
Naous et al. 2025
)
evaluates single-turn user utterance generation: given a conversation context and a user intent, the model generates one user turn. We describe the generation protocol, evaluation metrics, and dataset details below.

Generation protocol.

Each task provides an
intent
(a natural-language description of the user’s goal) and a
conversation history
(prior user–assistant turns). The model receives a system prompt instructing it to act as a realistic human user—including occasional typos, imperfect punctuation, and information splitting across turns—and generates the next user message. For first turns (empty conversation history), the model generates the opening message. For sequential turns, the model also receives a termination instruction: it may append a special token
<|endconversation|>
to signal that the conversation should end.

Evaluation granularity.

Each task is scored independently. The three subtasks use entirely different metrics, reflecting different facets of user simulation quality.

Metrics.

UserLLM defines three subtasks, each with its own metric:

CSQA — Role Adherence

tests whether the model stays in the
questioner
role rather than leaking answers. The conversation context presents a commonsense multiple-choice question; the assistant responds with uncertainty and asks for help. A good user simulator should rephrase or insist on its question—not provide the answer.

•

The generated turn and all answer-choice texts are normalized (lowercased, punctuation removed).

•

If the turn contains 1–2 of the choice texts as substrings:
role_adherence = 0
(the model is answering rather than asking).

•

If it mentions none (or all, indicating question repetition):
role_adherence = 1
(the model stays in the user role).

NQ — Intent Adherence

tests whether the model insists on its original question when the assistant deflects. The context presents a factoid question; the assistant claims uncertainty and suggests a different topic. A good user simulator should refuse the redirection and re-ask.

•

An LLM judge classifies the generated turn as either
refused
(user re-asked their question) or
accepted
(user followed the assistant’s redirection).

•

intent_adherence = 1
if
refused
,
0
if
accepted
.

PRISM — Multi-Metric

evaluates diverse facets of user simulation on open-ended conversations from the PRISM alignment dataset. Four metrics are computed:

•

First-turn diversity
: Pairwise 1-gram Jaccard distance across all first-turn generations. Higher = more diverse opening messages across different intents.

•

Intent decomposition
: 1-gram overlap (stopword-filtered) between the intent description and the generated turn,
|\text{intent}\cap\text{output}|/|\text{output}|
. Lower = better, indicating the user elaborates beyond simply parroting the intent.

•

Termination F1
: Whether the model correctly predicts conversation endings via the
<|endconversation|>
token, measured as F1 over predicted vs. ground-truth last turns.

•

AI detector score
: Fraction of generated text classified as AI-written by an external detector (Pangram API). Lower = more human-like.

Appendix D
Detailed Comparison of Verbal Feedback Methods

Table

14
provides a systematic comparison of approaches that go beyond single scalar rewards for language model training.
We categorize methods along three axes: how feedback is represented (scalar, multi-scalar, or verbal), at what granularity it is applied (episode, sequence, segment, or token level), and the training paradigm used (SFT, inference-only, DPO, AWR, or GRPO).

Among the RL-based methods that use verbal feedback, RLTF-SD and our approach share the same self-distillation structure—both generate a teacher rollout conditioned on feedback and train the base policy to internalize the teacher’s improvements.
The key algorithmic distinction is the optimization method: RLTF-SD uses advantage-weighted regression (AWR), which performs weighted supervised learning on teacher outputs without importance correction, while our method uses GRPO over the joint student-teacher group with trust-region clipping.
Experiential RL also follows a reflect-and-revise pattern but consolidates improvements through supervised fine-tuning on successful second attempts rather than through on-policy RL.

All prior verbal-feedback RL methods have been evaluated exclusively on tasks with verifiable rewards (code, math, reasoning puzzles, tool use).
Our work is the first to apply verbal-feedback self-distillation to social simulation, where rewards are noisy, subjective, and multi-dimensional.

Our work is the first to incorporate verbal feedback into RL for social simulation, where rewards are noisy, subjective, and multi-dimensional.
