Protecting Heisenberg scaling in quantum metrology via engineered dressed states
Wojciech Górecki, Christiane P. Koch

Abstract
Quantum metrology promises precision beyond classical limits but environmental noise, unless properly controlled, reduces the quantum advantage to at most a constant improvement. A key challenge is therefore to design quantum control strategies that suppress noise while preserving sensitivity to the targeted signal. Here, we suggest to use dressed states generated by static fields to achieve this goal and show that success of this strategy depends on the spectral properties of the environment. For low-temperature noise, we show that Heisenberg scaling can be achieved if and only if the signal generator lies outside the linear span of the system–environment coupling operators. This implies that the proper dressed states may enable Heisenberg scaling even in cases where the well-known Hamiltonian-not-in-Lindblad-span criterion, evaluated without dressing, would forbid it. We illustrate dressed state metrology for the example of NV-center thermometry under magnetic-field fluctuations, with the framework readily applicable to other platforms.

Model—
Consider the system coupled to the environment (”bath”) with Hamiltonian

H=H_{S}\otimes\openone_{B}+\openone_{S}\otimes H_{B}+H_{I},

(1)

where the interaction part may be written as

H_{I}=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha}

(2)

with Hermitian operators
A_{\alpha}
acting on the system and
B_{\alpha}
on the environment.
We assume the standard Born–Markov approximation—weak coupling and the correlation time of the bath much shorter than the characteristic timescale of the system dynamics—together with the rotating-wave approximation (RWA), under which the reduced system dynamics is well described by the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
14
:

\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{S}(t)=-i[H_{S}+H_{LS},\rho_{S}(t)]+\mathcal{D}[\rho_{S}(t)].

(3)

H_{LS}
is the Lamb shift Hamiltonian and
\mathcal{D}[\cdot]
describes the dissipative part of the evolution:

\mathcal{D}[\rho_{S}]=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}(\nu)\left(L_{\beta}^{\nu}\rho_{S}{L_{\alpha}^{\nu}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{{L_{\alpha}^{\nu}}^{\dagger}L_{\beta}^{\nu},\rho_{S}\}\right),

(4)

where
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nu)
is bath spectral density and

L_{\alpha}^{\nu}
is the Lindblad jump operator corresponding to the energy gap
\nu
:

L_{\alpha}^{\nu}\equiv\sum_{\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon=\nu}\Pi_{\epsilon}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\epsilon^{\prime}}.

(5)

Here,
\Pi_{\epsilon}
is the projection onto the eigensubspace of the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
with energy
\epsilon
.
We assume that the matrix
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nu)
is of full rank, i.e., the noises generated by different
A_{\alpha}
are not fully correlated or anticorrelated.
Recall that
\nu=0
corresponds to dephasing noise,

\nu>0
to relaxation or decay, and
\nu<0

to thermal excitation.
For a low-temperature environment, we have
\gamma_{\alpha,\beta}(\nu)\approx 0

\forall{\nu<0}
, i.e., thermal excitation is negligible.

Assuming that the free Hamiltonian of the system depends on a parameter
\omega
,
the goal is to estimate its small deviation from the known value
\omega_{0}
,
\omega=\omega_{0}+\delta\omega
. The free system Hamiltonian may be approximated by
H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega)\approx H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega_{0})+G\delta\omega
, where
G=\frac{dH_{S}}{d\omega}
is the signal generator. We assume control over the system by adding a constant term
H_{C}
to the Hamiltonian, so the total system Hamiltonian reads

H_{S}=H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega_{0})+\delta\omega G+H_{C}.

(6)

Crucially, the choice of
H_{C}
modifies the eigenbasis of
H_{S}
and thus also changes the structure of the Lindblad operators in
Eq. (
5
)
. This opens the possibility of creating decoherence-free dressed subspaces—i.e., subspaces spanned by a subset of eigenstates of the modified system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
that remain invariant under the non-unitary part of the evolution—even when no decoherence-free subspace is present without control. Our goal is
to identify the conditions under which a control Hamiltonian can be found such that, for the resulting master equation, either a decoherence-free subspace sensitive to variations in
\omega
exists (thus immediately enabling HS), or HS can be recovered through the application of QEC.

The dependence of the Lindblad operators on the eigenstates of
H_{S}
follows from the weak-coupling limit and RWA, where rapid oscillations suppress non-resonant terms, leaving only transitions between energy eigenstates.
The opposite situation occurs in the singular-coupling limit (white-noise regime)
14
, where the jump operators are instead determined solely by the interaction operators
A_{\alpha}
irrespective of the system eigenstates.
Then the dressed states cannot be used to effectively mitigate noise, but rather
QEC-based methods are required, as in the
example of perpendicular noise

91
. Our approach is therefore complementary, exploring noise mitigation within the weak-coupling limit.

Conditions for achievability of HS—
We first focus on identifying decoherence-free dressed subspaces sensitive to changes in
\delta\omega
.
Let
\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}
be eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
Eq. (
6
)
with eigenenergies
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}
well separated from the remaining eigenstates. We define the code space as
\mathcal{C}:=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}\}
. For
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}|\gg\delta\omega
, the effective generator acting within
\mathcal{C}
is

G_{\textrm{eff}}\approx\braket{\psi_{0}|G|\psi_{0}}\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}+\braket{\psi_{1}|G|\psi_{1}}\ket{\psi_{1}}\bra{\psi_{1}}
,
since the off-diagonal elements are suppressed under the secular approximation.
Further, we consider the optimization of the metrological protocol over the choice of
\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}
. In an abstract setting, these states can always be engineered as the lowest eigenstates of
H_{S}
by choosing the control Hamiltonian as
H_{C}=-H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega_{0})-\lambda_{0}\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}-\lambda_{1}\ket{\psi_{1}}\bra{\psi_{1}}
(the impact of
\delta\omega G
on the eigenstates of
H_{S}
is negligible; see End Matter
.1
). In many practical cases, much simpler control suffices, as we discuss below in
Application
.

In the absence of thermal excitation,
the problem of maximizing the signal while preserving unitary evolution may then be simplified to the following optimization problem:

\begin{split}&\max_{\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}}\braket{\psi_{1}|G|\psi_{1}}-\braket{\psi_{0}|G|\psi_{0}},\quad\textrm{with}\\
&\braket{\psi_{0}|\psi_{0}}=\braket{\psi_{1}|\psi_{1}}=1,\quad\braket{\psi_{0}|\psi_{1}}=0\\
&\textrm{(dephasing)}\quad\forall_{\alpha}\braket{\psi_{0}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{0}}=\braket{\psi_{1}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{1}},\\
&\textrm{(relaxation)}\quad\forall_{\alpha}\braket{\psi_{0}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{1}}=0,\end{split}

(7)

where the type of noise addressed by each condition is specified in parentheses. The last two conditions imply that all operators
A_{\alpha}
act proportionally to the identity when projected onto the code space, i.e.,
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
. Protection against thermal excitation additionally requires

\textrm{(excitation)}\quad\forall_{\alpha}\forall_{i\neq 0,1}\braket{\psi_{i}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{0/1}}=0.

(8)

We first focus on
Eq. (
7
)
, leaving
Eq. (
8
)
for later. From the dephasing condition alone, it follows that a necessary condition for the existence of a decoherence-free dressed subspace relevant for sensing is

G\notin\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\openone,A_{\alpha}\}
.
If the bath spectral function is peaked around zero, such that dephasing noise dominates, a large gap
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}|
suppresses all jump-induced effects within the code space in the RWA. If instead the bath has significant support at all positive frequencies, protection against relaxation additionally requires
\braket{\psi_{0}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{1}}=0

and the size of the gap
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}|
becomes irrelevant.

In general, there is no guarantee that vectors satisfying these constraints exist, in particular when the number of noise operators
A_{\alpha}
greatly exceeds the system dimension. This limitation can be overcome by introducing a noiseless auxiliary system (ancilla) and assuming full control over both the system and the ancilla. Formally, the system Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
is extended to
\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
, with generator and all coupling operators acting trivially on the ancilla (i.e.,
G
and
A_{\alpha}
should be understood as
G\otimes\openone
,
A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone
), while the control Hamiltonian
H_{C}
acts jointly on
\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}

Theorem 1
.

In the presence of dephasing and relaxation noise, and assuming full control over both the system and a noiseless ancilla, a decoherence-free subspace useful for sensing
\delta\omega
can be generated by properly dressing the Hamiltonian if and only if

G\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\openone,A_{\alpha}\,\,\textrm{for all}\,\,\alpha\}.

(9)

Equivalently, under the above condition, there always exists a pair of vectors
\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
satisfying
Eq. (
7
)
with
\braket{\psi_{1}|G|\psi_{1}}-\braket{\psi_{0}|G|\psi_{0}}>0
. Moreover, the maximization in
Eq. (
7
)

can be formulated as a semidefinite program (SDP).

Once an appropriate control Hamiltonian
H_{C}
is applied, such that
\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}
are energy-separated eigenstates of
H_{C}
, the metrological protocol consists of preparing the input state
\ket{\psi_{\textrm{in}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{\psi_{0}}+\ket{\psi_{1}})
, letting it evolve freely for a time
t
, and performing a projective measurement. In the limit of many repetitions
k
, the variance of the optimal estimator saturates the Cramér–Rao bound
25
,

\Delta^{2}\tilde{\omega}\approx\frac{1}{kF_{Q}}=\frac{1}{kt^{2}\Delta^{2}G_{\textrm{eff}}},

(10)

where the quantum Fisher information
F_{Q}
equals the interrogation time squared multiplied by the variance of the effective signal generator
\Delta^{2}G_{\textrm{eff}}
, yielding Heisenberg scaling in time.

In contrast to the low-temperature case discussed above, where coupling to a noiseless auxiliary system can suffice to engineer a decoherence-free dressed subspace, the situation is markedly different for thermal noise that additionally admits excitation (i.e., a bath spectral function with nonzero support at all frequencies). In this regime, constructing a decoherence-free dressed subspace is generally not possible, as the additional requirement
Eq. (
8
)
makes the corresponding conditions more restrictive. It is therefore natural to relax the requirement of complete noise suppression and instead seek a dressed subspace in which errors may occur, but remain correctable via quantum error correction.

The possibility of achieving Heisenberg scaling using QEC for
fixed
Lindblad operators has been fully characterized in terms of the Hamiltonian-not-in-Lindblad-space (HNLS) theorem

;
91

G\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\openone,L_{i},L^{\dagger}_{i},L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{i}\}
(here the single index

covers both
\nu,\alpha
in
Eq. (
4
)
). This theorem relies on the assumption that the applied control (including QEC) does not modify the Lindblad operators. The assumption is met when QEC
is implemented via strong, effectively instantaneous control pulses separated by time intervals longer than the coarse-graining timescale used to derive the master equation.
We extend this theorem by considering constant control in the weak-coupling regime, where the effective Lindblad operators are determined by both the (uncontrollable) coupling operators
A_{\alpha}
and the eigenbasis of the (controllable) dressed Hamiltonian.

Theorem 2
.

For fully general noise (including thermal excitation), and assuming full control over both the system and the ancilla, a correctable subspace—i.e., one for which an effective unitary evolution can be obtained after properly applying quantum error correction—useful for sensing
\delta\omega
can be generated by appropriately dressing the Hamiltonian if and only if

G\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\openone,A_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\,\,\textrm{for all}\,\,\alpha,\beta\}.

(11)

Namely, this condition is equivalent to the existence of a control Hamiltonian
H_{C}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
, for which

G\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\openone,L_{i},L^{\dagger}_{i},L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{i}\}

with
the modified Lindblad operators given by
Eq. (

)

The appearance of the quadratic term in the HNLS condition (and, consequently, also in

) can be intuitively understood by inspecting the Knill–Laflamme conditions for quantum error correction used in the derivation

While the condition

Relation of
Theorem 1
to HNLS—

The key observation is that the requirements for achieving HS in the presence of dephasing or relaxation can be significantly less restrictive than the standard HNLS condition. For a clear comparison, consider the case in which the interaction Hamiltonian contains only a single operator
A
, which commutes with the system Hamiltonian, i.e.,
[H_{S},A]=0
. In this case, the Lindblad operator is simply
L=A
, and the HNLS condition reduces to
G\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\openone,A,A^{2}\}
, which is more restrictive than
Eq. (
9
)
due to the presence of the additional quadratic term.

The distinction can be understood by noting that the standard
HNLS was derived under the assumption that the QEC
operates on timescales longer than the noise-averaging timescale. Consequently, it addresses the conditions required to correct errors that have already occurred. In contrast, our result pertains to a regime in which errors are continuously prevented from arising in the first place, by exploiting the underlying unitary system–environment dynamics prior to coarse-graining. This is a less demanding task, aptly captured by the Latin maxim
morbum evitare quam curare facilius est
(“it is easier to prevent than to cure”). Only in the presence of thermal excitations does error prevention become impossible, in which case the more restrictive conditions
Eq. (
11
)

can no longer be avoided.

Conclusions—
We have determined
the conditions under which Heisenberg scaling can be achieved in noisy quantum metrology using dressed states generated by static fields, for all types of bath spectral functions. For dephasing and relaxation noise in the absence of thermal excitation, we show that HS is achievable if and only if the signal generator lies outside the linear span of the system–environment coupling operators. This shows that a carefully engineered dressed basis can recover HS even when the conventional HNLS condition—if evaluated for the undressed system—would rule it out.
This weakening of the requirements stems from the ability to exploit the coherent nature of the system–environment interaction prior to coarse-graining. It is in line with recent advances emphasizing the role of noise correlations in shaping fundamental metrological limits
4
;
56
;
47
. We have illustrated our results using the example of thermometry with an NV-center subject to magnetic-field fluctuations but our findings are universal, since they are based on algebraic relation between operators.
They can therefore be directly applied to a wide range of experimental platforms, including trapped ions, Rydberg atoms, cold atomic gases, and related systems.

Our results offer a clear recipe for tailoring control Hamiltonians to achieve desired metrological properties, while also serving as a foundation for future time-dependent sensing protocols. These could include adiabatic approaches for slow control
3
, non-adiabatic master equations for fast driving
23
, as well as periodic driving (Floquet) regimes where the drive frequency explicitly shifts the sampled bath spectrum
(
14
, 8.4)
—all of which constitute natural directions for future research.

.1
Impact of
\delta\omega
on eigenstates of
H_{S}

If the generator
G
does not commute with the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}=H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega_{0})+\delta\omega G+H_{C}
at
\delta\omega=0
, i.e.,
[G,H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega_{0})+H_{C}]\neq 0
, the value of
\delta\omega
affects the eigenstates of
H_{S}
. Since the Lindblad operators depend on these eigenstates, this implies that the signal
\delta\omega
is also encoded in the dissipative part of the evolution equation. We now show that this contribution is negligible compared to the original signal in the Hamiltonian term
\delta\omega G
, at the level of the generator of dynamics, provided that the eigenenergies of
H_{S}
are well separated.

According to first-order perturbation theory, the
n^{\textrm{th}}
eigenstate of the full Hamiltonian is

\ket{\psi_{n}}=\ket{\psi_{n}^{(0)}}+\delta\omega\ket{\psi_{n}^{(1)}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\omega^{2}),

(14)

where
\ket{\psi_{n}^{(0)}}
is the eigenstate at
\delta\omega=0
, and

\ket{\psi_{n}^{(1)}}=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\braket{\psi_{m}^{(0)}|G|\psi_{n}^{(0)}}}{\mu_{n}^{(0)}-\mu_{m}^{(0)}}\ket{\psi_{m}^{(0)}},

(15)

with
\mu_{n}^{(0)},\mu_{m}^{(0)}
being eigenvalues of
H^{\textrm{free}}_{S}(\omega_{0})+H_{C}
. The differences
\mu_{n}^{(0)}-\mu_{m}^{(0)}
correspond to the transition frequencies
\nu
appearing in
Eq. (
4
)
, so the correction to the eigenstates scales as
\delta\omega/\nu
.

Since the Lindblad operators are constructed from transition operators between the eigenstates, substituting
Eq. (
14
)
into their definition, and subsequently into the master equation, yields corrections of order
\delta\omega\cdot\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nu)/\nu
. Assuming operator norm
\|G\|\sim 1
, the component of the signal appearing in the dissipative part of the equation of motion is therefore suppressed by a factor
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nu)/\nu
. At the same time,
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nu)/\nu
sets the scale of the terms already neglected in the secular and coarse-graining approximations underlying the master equation. Therefore, as long as the master equation remains valid, it is consistent to neglect the
\delta\omega
-dependence of the Lindblad operators, also from the perspective of designing metrological protocols.

With the same reasoning, the Lamb shift Hamiltonian

H_{LS}=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}S_{\alpha\beta}(\nu){L_{\alpha}^{\nu}}^{\dagger}L_{\beta}^{\nu}

(16)

can be treated as independent of
\delta\omega
up to corrections of order
\delta\omega\cdot\gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nu)/\nu
, and therefore does not significantly affect the metrological protocol. Including more subtle effects would require going beyond the master-equation description and is beyond the scope of this work.

.2
Proof of
Theorem 1

This section is based on the methods applied in
91
for a similar problem with a different operator space.

(1) Necessity.

If
G=g_{1}\openone+\sum_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}
, then for any
\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}
satisfying
\forall_{\alpha}\braket{\psi_{0}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{0}}=\braket{\psi_{1}|A_{\alpha}|\psi_{1}}
automatically
\braket{\psi_{1}|G|\psi_{1}}-\braket{\psi_{0}|G|\psi_{0}}=0
, so any dedecoherence-free subspace is automatically non-sensitive for parameter changes.

(2) Sufficiency.

Let us define the inner product of two Hermitian matrices
A
,
B
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{S}
as the trace of their product,
\mathrm{Tr}(AB)
. Then any
G
satisfying

G\notin\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\openone,A_{\alpha}\}

(17)

has a unique decomposition
G=G_{\parallel}+G_{\perp}
, where
G_{\parallel}\in\textrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\openone,A_{\alpha}\}
and
\forall_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}(G_{\perp}A_{\alpha})=\mathrm{Tr}(G_{\perp})=0
. Next,
G_{\perp}
may be always written as
G_{\perp}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{Tr}|G_{\perp}|)(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})
where both
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}
are mutually orthogonal matrices with
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_{0/1})=1
and
|G_{\perp}|:=\sqrt{G_{\perp}^{2}}
. Let
\ket{\psi_{0}},\ket{\psi_{1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
be the purifications of
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}
(i.e.
\forall_{i=0,1}\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(\ket{\psi_{i}}\bra{\psi_{i}})=\rho_{i}
) with orthogonal support on the ancilla
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. From
\forall_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}(G_{\perp}A_{\alpha})=0
, such constructed states satisfy

\forall_{\alpha}\braket{\psi_{0}|(A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone)|\psi_{0}}=\braket{\psi_{1}|(A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone)|\psi_{1}}\,.

(18)

Since they have orthogonal support on the ancilla, they satisfy

\braket{\psi_{0}|(A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone)|\psi_{1}}=0.

(19)

Finally, the sensitivity for the signal generated by
G
is

\begin{split}&\braket{\psi_{1}|(G\otimes\openone)|\psi_{1}}-\braket{\psi_{0}|(G\otimes\openone)|\psi_{0}}\\
=&\braket{\psi_{1}|(G_{\perp}\otimes\openone)|\psi_{1}}-\braket{\psi_{0}|(G_{\perp}\otimes\openone)|\psi_{0}}\\
=&\mathrm{Tr}(G_{\perp}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0}))=\mathrm{Tr}\left(G_{\perp}\tfrac{G_{\perp}}{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}|G_{\perp}|}\right)\\
=&\frac{2\mathrm{Tr}(G_{\perp}^{2})}{\mathrm{Tr}|G_{\perp}|}.\end{split}

(20)

(3) Optimization as SDP.

Let
\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{0}},\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{1}}\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
be a pair of orthogonal states. We define:

\tilde{\rho}_{0}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{0}}\bra{\tilde{\psi}_{0}}),\quad\tilde{\rho}_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{1}}\bra{\tilde{\psi}_{1}}),

(21)

and
\tilde{G}=\tilde{\rho}_{0}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}
. Since both states are normalized,
\mathrm{Tr}|\tilde{G}|\leq 2
and
\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{G})=0
.
Condition
\braket{\tilde{\psi}_{0}|(A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone)|\tilde{\psi}_{0}}=\braket{\tilde{\psi}_{1}|(A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone)|\tilde{\psi}_{1}}
is equivalent to
\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\alpha}\tilde{G})=0
.

Note also that the support of
\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{0/1}}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
has no impact on sensitivity
\braket{\tilde{\psi}_{1}|(G\otimes\openone)|\tilde{\psi}_{1}}-\braket{\tilde{\psi}_{0}|(G\otimes\openone)|\tilde{\psi}_{0}}
. Moreover, if these supports are mutually orthogonal, the condition
\braket{\psi_{0}|A_{\alpha}\otimes\openone|\psi_{1}}
is satisfied automatically.
Therefore, looking for an optimal choice of
\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{0}},\ket{\tilde{\psi}_{1}}
, without loss of generality we may restrict to the ones with orthogonal support on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
.

Therefore, the whole problem may be formulated as optimization over a hermitian matrix
\tilde{G}
satisfying:

\begin{split}&\max_{\tilde{G}}\mathrm{Tr}(G\tilde{G})\\
&\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{G})=0,\quad\forall_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\alpha}\tilde{G})=0,\quad\mathrm{Tr}|\tilde{G}|\leq 2.\end{split}

(22)

The constraint
\mathrm{Tr}|\tilde{G}|\leq 2
may be written as linear constraints by introducing as an additional variable a hermitian matrix
X
:

\mathrm{Tr}|\tilde{G}|\leq 2\Leftrightarrow\exists_{X\succeq 0}:-X\preceq\tilde{G}\preceq X,\mathrm{Tr}(X)\leq 2,

(23)

so finally we obtain SDP problem:

\begin{split}&\max_{\tilde{G},X}\mathrm{Tr}(G\tilde{G})\\
&\mathrm{Tr}(\tilde{G})=0,\quad\forall_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}(A_{\alpha}\tilde{G})=0,\\
&\tilde{G}-X\succeq 0,\quad X-\tilde{G}\succeq 0.\end{split}

(24)

Alternatively, the problem may by formulated as a minimization of the Lagrange dual problem (also solvable by SDP)
91
.

.3
Proof of
Theorem 2

Condition
G\notin\mathrm{span}\{\openone,L_{k},L_{k}^{\dagger},L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{j}\}
is equivalent to the existence of a two-dimensional code space
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
satisfying the Knill-Laflamme conditions for the Lindblad operators
91
:

\begin{split}\forall_{\alpha,\nu}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L_{\alpha}(\nu)\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}},\\
\forall_{\alpha,\nu,\beta,\nu^{\prime}}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\nu)L_{\beta}(\nu^{\prime})\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}},\end{split}

(25)

with
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}G\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\not\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Similarly, the condition
G\notin\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\openone,A_{\alpha},A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\}
is equivalent to the existence of a code space
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}
such that

\begin{split}\forall_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}},\\
\forall_{\alpha,\beta}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}},\end{split}

(26)

with
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}G\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\not\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
.

We show that, for a fixed
\mathcal{C}
,
Eq. (
25
)
implies
Eq. (
26
)
, and that
Eq. (
26
)
implies the existence of a control Hamiltonian
H_{C}
whose eigenspace is
\mathcal{C}
and for which
Eq. (
25
)
holds.

(1)
Eq. (
25
)

\Rightarrow

Eq. (
26
)

Recall that the Lindblad operator labeled by
(\alpha,\nu)
is related to
A_{\alpha}
via

L_{\alpha}(\nu)\equiv\sum_{\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon=\nu}\Pi_{\epsilon}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\epsilon^{\prime}}.

(27)

Therefore,
A_{\alpha}
decomposes as

A_{\alpha}=\sum_{\nu}L_{\alpha}(\nu).

(28)

Summing
Eq. (
25
)
over
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
immediately gives
Eq. (
26
)
.

(2)
Eq. (
26
)

\Rightarrow

\exists\,H_{C}
such that
Eq. (
25
)
holds

We choose
H_{C}
such that the system Hamiltonian has only two eigenspaces, namely
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, separated by an energy gap
\nu_{0}
. The Lindblad operators are then

\begin{split}L_{\alpha}(0)&=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}+\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}},\\
L_{\alpha}(-\nu_{0})&=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}},\\
L_{\alpha}(+\nu_{0})&=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}},\end{split}

(29)

for all
\alpha
. By construction,
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L_{\alpha}(\pm\nu_{0})\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}=0
, while
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
implies

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L_{\alpha}(0)\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Next, after projection onto
\mathcal{C}
, the only potentially nonzero quadratic terms are

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(0)L_{\beta}(0)\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(-\nu_{0})L_{\beta}(-\nu_{0})\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}.

For the first term, from
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
one has

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(0)L_{\beta}(0)\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\beta}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}.

(30)

To analyze the second term, we expand the condition

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}A_{\beta}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
as

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}+A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}})(\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\beta}+\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}A_{\beta})\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}.

(31)

Using
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}=0
and
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
, this implies

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}A_{\alpha}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}A_{\beta}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}.

(32)

which means
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(-\nu_{0})L_{\beta}(-\nu_{0})\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\propto\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
.
This proves
Eq. (
25
)
for the chosen
H_{C}
.

.5
No two-dimensional trivial subspace for spin-1

Let
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in\{x,y,z\}}
be the irreducible spin-
1
representation on a three-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
. Then there exists no two-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{H}_{S}
satisfying

\forall_{i\in\{x,y,z\}}\ \Pi_{\mathcal{C}}S_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}=\lambda_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}.

(34)

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction. We use the fact that each
S_{i}
has eigenvalues
-1,0,+1
, as well as the commutation relations.

First, we show that for any direction
i\in\{x,y,z\}
the equality
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}S_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}=\lambda_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}
implies
\lambda_{i}=0
. We start with the
z
direction. Since any two two-dimensional subspaces of the three-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
have a non-empty intersection, we may choose a vector
\ket{u}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{+1}\}
. Since
\ket{u}\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
\braket{u|S_{z}|u}=\lambda_{z}
, and since
\ket{u}\in\mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{+1}\}
, it follows that
\braket{u|S_{z}|u}\geq 0
, which together implies
\lambda_{z}\geq 0
. Similarly, there exists another vector
\ket{v}\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathrm{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{-1}\}
, which implies
\lambda_{z}\leq 0
. Therefore
\lambda_{z}=0
. The same reasoning applies for
x
and
y
.

Since
S_{i}
is traceless,
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}S_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}=0
implies also
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}S_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}=0
(where
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}
denotes the projection onto the subspace orthogonal to
\mathcal{C}
). Therefore,
Eq. (
34
)
implies

\forall_{i\in\{x,y,z\}}\quad S_{i}=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}S_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}+\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}S_{i}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}.

(35)

Since
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}=0
, this leads to

[S_{x},S_{y}]=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}[S_{x},S_{y}]\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}+\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}[S_{x},S_{y}]\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}.

(36)

Since
S_{z}=-i[S_{x},S_{y}]
, it must simultaneously have the forms
Eq. (
35
)
and
Eq. (
36
)
, which implies
S_{z}\equiv 0
. This contradicts the fact that
S_{z}
has eigenvalues
-1,0,+1
. Therefore, there exists no two-diemnsional subspace
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{H}_{S}
satisfies
Eq. (
34
)
.
