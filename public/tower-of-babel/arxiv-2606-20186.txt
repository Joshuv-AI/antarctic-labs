Quasi-random graphs, subgraph counts and graph limits, again Thanks: Supported by the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation and the Swedish Research Council
Svante Janson

Abstract
. We study properties of graphs (or rather graph sequences) saying that some restricted count of subgraphs is approximatively what is expected in a random graph. It has been shown by several authors that many such properties characterize quasi-random graphs, but there are also some exceptions. We continue here the line of investigation in Janson and Sós (2013), and introduce some new versions of these properties, in order to better understand why many of these properties are quasi-random, and to understand the structure of the exceptions that are not. A new feature in the proofs is a simple decomposition of the subspace of symmetric functions in L 2 ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] m ) L^{2}([0,1]^{m}) into subspaces that are irreducible for the action of measure-preserving transformations of [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] ; this simplifies some arguments and gives structure to others.



1.
Introduction

My only paper with Vera T. Sós
[
10
]
was about
quasi-random graphs and graph limits, and more precisely
about some properties of subgraph counts that were shown to characterize
quasi-random graphs. We left also a number of open problems and conjectures.
Some of these were answered by
Hatami, Hatami and Li 2014
.
Here we consider the same type of properties again, and obtain further results
extending and complementing results in
[
10
]
and
[
7
]
.
There are still cases left open and new open problems and conjectures;
we hope that the method and point of view in the present paper can contribute
to the understanding of this type of properties.

We assume that the reader has some familiarity with quasi-random graphs, but we
repeat some basic facts.
Recall that
quasi-random
is an asymptotic property, so it really is a property
of sequences
(G_{n})_{n}
of graphs (with
|G_{n}|\to\infty
as
{n\to\infty}
).

Thomason 1987
;
Thomason 1987
and
Chung, Graham and Wilson 1989
showed
that a number of different “random-like” properties of
a graph sequence
(G_{n})

are equivalent, and we say that
(G_{n})
is
quasi-random
, or more precisely

p
-quasi-random
, if
it satisfies these properties. (Here
p\in[0,1]
is a
parameter.)
We further say that a property of
sequences
(G_{n})_{n}
of graphs (with
|G_{n}|\to\infty
) is a

quasi-random property
(or a
p
-quasi-random property
) if it
characterizes quasi-random (or
p
-quasi-random) sequences of graphs.
Many quasi-random properties of very different types
have been added by various authors.

When the theory of graph limits was introduced by

Lovász and Szegedy 2006
and
Borgs, Chayes, Lovász, Sós and Vesztergombi 2008
;
Borgs, Chayes, Lovász, Sós and Vesztergombi 2012
,
it became clear that
a sequence
(G_{n})_{n}
of graphs is
p
-quasi-random if and only if
G_{n}\to p

in the sense of graph limits, where
p
denotes the graphon that is constant

p

[
12
]
.
Both the present paper and
[
10
]
are based on this relation.

In the present paper we consider quasi-random properties
that are stated in terms of subgraph counts
(typically with some restrictions).
Properties of this type correspond in great generality to corresponding
properties of graphons, such that the question whether a given property
of graph sequences is quasi-random
can be translated to the question whether the corresponding property of
graphons characterizes constant graphons.
This has been a fundamental idea in
[
9
]
,

[
10
]
, and
[
7
]
.
In the present paper we study some further properties of subgraph counts
and the corresponding properties of graphons.

It was noted in
[
10
, Remark 9.6]
that
one ingredient in several proofs there was the study of some
subspaces of
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})

that are invariant under all measure-preserving bijections of
[0,1]
onto itself,
and in particular some such subspaces consisting of symmetric functions only.
It was conjectured that all such subspaces
are direct sums of some set of certain explicit spaces.
The main purpose of the present paper is to prove this conjecture from

[
10
]
(Theorem
4.4
below), and to combine it with tools from previous works
to obtain new results on quasi-random properties.

The proof of Theorem
4.4
uses group representation theory, and in particular some
representations of the finite symmetric groups on finite-dimensional subspaces
of
L^{2}([0,1])^{m}
, which intuitively
may be regarded as approximations of the
infinite-dimensional representation of the infinite group
\mathfrak{G}

of measure-preserving bijections.
(See Appendix
A
for a summary of relevant representation theory.)

Theorem
4.4
helps us to analyze our main problem,
i.e., whether certain subgraph count properties are quasi-random, by allowing us to
consider these irreducible spaces of functions only.
In particular, this makes it possible to split the problem whether the
properties studied here are quasi-random into two parts that can be studied
separately, see Remark
5.1
.
Nevertheless, as seen in
[
10
]
and
[
7
]
,
while this in many cases leads to a simple proof that a certain property is
quasi-random, there are also cases where
this only leads to a reformulation as a non-trivial algebraic question;
this question was solved in some cases in
[
10
]
,
but remains open in other cases.
We do not solve this algebraic problem here; on the contrary, our analysis
below adds in some cases new versions of it that are open.
(See Section
6
.)
Another purpose of the present paper is to draw attention to these problems,
and to stimulate research on them and on what they say about the
general structure of the type of quasi-random properties studied here.

Some further comments and open problems are given in Section
7
.

2.
Notation and some background

2.1.
General notation

We let
p\in(0,1)
be a fixed parameter, usually omitted from the notation.
We denote the number of elements of a finite set

.

All graphs in this paper are finite, undirected and simple.
The vertex and edge sets of a graph

are denoted by

and

. We write
|G|:=|V(G)|
for the
number of vertices of
G
, and

for the
number of edges.

We let
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\dots\}
and,
as usual,
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For any finite set
S
,
we let
\binom{S}{m}
denote the set of all
\binom{|S|}{m}
subsets of
S
of
size
m
.

For a vector
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})

and a subset
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq[m]
,
we write

\displaystyle x_{I}:=(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}}).

(2.1)

We let

x\land y:=\min(x,y)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{E}\}
denotes the indicator function of an event
\mathcal{E}
, and if
A

is a set, we also write
\boldsymbol{1}_{A}(x):=\boldsymbol{1}\{x\in A\}
.

We will consider
(complex-valued)
functions on
[0,1]^{k}
and subsets of
[0,1]^{k}
for
k\geqslant 0
;
all such functions and subsets are tacitly assumed to be (Lebesgue) measurable.
(
[0,1]^{0}
is a one-element set.)
As usual, functions that are equal a.e. are identified.
(We some times write “a.e.” for emphasis, but this is often omitted.)
Integrals are with respect to Lebesgue measure (in one or several
dimensions), and
we usually omit “
\,\mathrm{d}x
”. The Lebegue measure of a set
A
is denoted by
|A|
.
(There should be no risk of confusion with the notation for finite sets.)

For
f
and

in
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
(or in a subspace thereof), we denote their
inner product by
\langle f,g\rangle:=\int f\overline{g}
, and the
L^{2}
-norm by

\lVert f\rVert_{2}:=\langle f,f\rangle^{1/2}
.
We let
f_{n}\overset{L^{2}}{\longrightarrow}f
denote convergence in
L^{2}
.

\mathfrak{S}_{N}
is the symmetric group of all
N!
permutations of
\{1,\dots,N\}
.

\mathfrak{G}
is the (infinite) group of all measure-preserving bijections

\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
.

2.2.
Notation for functional analysis and representation theory

All our Hilbert spaces will be complex.
Let
H
be a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle f,g\rangle
.
Then
f\perp g
means that
f
and
g
are orthogonal, i.e.,
\langle f,g\rangle=0
.

If
L
is a (closed) subspace of a Hilbert space
H
, then

\displaystyle L^{\perp}:=\{f\in H:f\perp g\;\forall g\in L\}.

(2.2)

If furthermore
M\subseteq L
is another subspace, then

\displaystyle L\ominus M:=L\cap M^{\perp}=\{f\in L:f\perp g\;\forall g\in\mathcal{M}\}.

(2.3)

Two subspaces
L,M\subseteq H
are orthogonal, denoted by
L\perp M
,
if
f\perp g
for all
f\in L
and
g\in\mathcal{M}
.
In this case, we denote their sum by
L\oplus M:=\{f+g:f\in L,\,g\in\mathcal{M}\}
.

A unitary operator
T
on
H
is a bijective linear map
T:H\to H
such
that
\lVert Tf\rVert=\lVert f\rVert
for every
f\in H
, which is equivalent to

\langle Tf,Tg\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle
for all
f,g\in H
.

A unitary representation of a group
G
on
H
is a group
homomorphism
g\mapsto\rho_{g}
where each
\rho_{g}
is a unitary operator on

H
,
see Appendix
A
.

Let
\rho
be a unitary representation of
G
on
H
.
A closed subspace
W\subseteq H
is
invariant
if
\rho_{g}(W)\subseteq W

for every
g\in G
.
Then the restriction of
\rho_{g}
to
W
defines a representation

G
on
W
, which we denote by
\rho|{W}
. (Sometimes we simply use

\rho
also for the retriction.)

Given a representation
\rho
of
G
on
H
, and an element
f\in H
,
we define

\displaystyle[\rho(f)]:=\text{the closed linear hull of }\{\rho_{g}(f):g\in G\}\subseteq H.

(2.4)

This is an invariant subspace of
H
; in fact,
[\rho(f)]

is the smallest closed invariant subspace that contains
f
.

A representation
\rho
on
H
is
irreducible
if the only closed
invariant
subspaces are the trivial
\{0\}
and
H
.

2.3.
Subgraph counts

Throughout the paper,
F
is a fixed labelled graph, and
m:=|F|
.
We assume (without loss of generality) that

V(F)=[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}
.
For another labelled graph
G
, we define the following subgraph counts.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{m}
be subsets of
V(G)
, where (as said above)
m=|F|
.

(i)

N(F,G;U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{m})
is the number of
labelled copies of
F
in
G
with the
i
th vertex
in
U_{i}
; equivalently,
N(F,G;U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{m})
is the number of
injective graph homomorphisms

\varphi:F\to G
such that
\varphi(i)\in U_{i}
for every

i\in V(F)=[m]
.

(ii)

\widetilde{N}(F,G;U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{m})
is the symmetrized version obtained
by taking the average over all labellings of
F
; equivalently,

\widetilde{N}(F,G;U_{1}\times\dots\times U_{m}):=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}N(F,G;U_{\sigma(1)}\times\dots\times U_{\sigma(m)}),

(2.5)

summing over all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,m\}
.

Note that for the
version in
(i)
,
the labelling of
F
and the ordering of
U_{1},\dots,U_{m}
are important,
but for the
symmetrized version in
(ii)
these do not matter,
and therefore (
2.5
) is defined also for
unlabelled
F
, although we for conveníence will regard
F
as labelled
there too.
Note also that if
F
is the complete graph
\mathsf{K}_{m}
, we have

\widetilde{N}(\mathsf{K}_{m},\dots)=N(\mathsf{K}_{m},\dots)
.

In the present paper, we will mainly study the
subgraph count

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}(F,G;U_{1}^{m_{1}}\times\dots\times U_{r}^{m_{r}})

(2.6)

where we are given
r
disjoint subsets
U_{1},\dots,U_{r}
and
the subset
U_{i}
is repeated
m_{i}
times; here
1\leqslant r\leqslant m=|F|
and

m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}=m
are given.
In this case,

\widetilde{N}(F,G;U_{1}^{m_{1}},\dots,U_{r}^{m_{r}})

equals,
up to the unimportant symmetry factor
\prod_{i}m_{i}!/m!
,
the number of unlabelled
copies of
F
in
G
that have exactly
m_{i}
vertices in
U_{i}
.
(For each such copy of
F
, there are
\prod_{i}m_{i}!
labellings of

F
for which it is counted, and the total number of labellings of
F
is

m!
.)

We make the following standing assumptions (which we may repeat for clarity).

Assumptions 2.2
.

F
is a (labelled) graph with
e(F)>0
;

m={|F|}
;

1\leqslant r\leqslant m
;

({m_{1},\dots,m_{r}})
is a vector of positive integers with
\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}=m
;

(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
is a vector of positive real
numbers with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\leqslant 1
;

p\in(0,1)
.

Note that, to avoid some trivial exceptions later, we have excluded the cases

e(F)=0
,
p=0
, and
p=1
.
The quantities in Assumptions
2.2
are regarded as fixed,
and may be omitted from the notation.
(In particular, we usually omit
p
, since all results are the same for
every
p\in(0,1)
.)

Definition 2.3
.

Assume Assumptions
2.2
.
We define the following properties
of a graph sequence
(G_{n})
.

(i)

\mathcal{P}_{{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
is the property
that

N\bigl(F,G_{n};U_{1}^{m_{1}}\times\dots\times U_{r}^{m_{r}}\bigr)=p^{e(F)}\prod_{i=1}^{r}|U_{i}|^{m_{i}}+o\bigl(|G_{n}|^{m}\bigr).

(2.7)

holds for all disjoint
subsets
U_{1},\dots,U_{r}
of
V(G_{n})
with

\displaystyle|U_{i}|=\lfloor\alpha_{i}|G_{n}|\rfloor,\qquad i=1,\dots,r.

(2.8)

(ii)

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
is the property that

\widetilde{N}\bigl(F,G_{n};U_{1}^{m_{1}}\times\dots\times U_{r}^{m_{r}}\bigr)=p^{e(F)}\prod_{i=1}^{r}|U_{i}|^{m_{i}}+o\bigl(|G_{n}|^{m}\bigr).

(2.9)

holds for all
U_{1},\dots,U_{r}
as in
(i)
.

Here
U_{1},\dots,U_{r}
depend on
n
, although we omit this from the notation,
and the error terms
o(|G_{n}|^{m})
in (
2.7
) and (
2.9
) are supposed
to hold uniformly for all permitted choices of
U_{i}=U_{i,n}
.

Remark 2.4
.

The relation between the properties of the types

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
is not

.
See the comments in
[

In the present paper, we mainly study
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
.

\triangle

) (or (

Another case studied by several authors is

3.
Transfer to graphons

We assume that the reader is familiar with the theory of graphons,
see e.g.
[
1
;
2
;
11
]
.
We use the standard version of graphons, defined as symmetric functions

W:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
.

If
F
is a labelled graph and
W
a graphon, we define

\Psi_{F,W}(x_{1},\dots,x_{|F|}):=\prod_{ij\in E(F)}W(x_{i},x_{j}).

(3.1)

and its symmetrization

\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=\frac{1}{{|F|}!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{{|F|}}}\Psi_{F,W}\bigl(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma({|F|})}\bigr).

(3.2)

We can now define a graphon analogue of Definition
2.3
.
(We deliberately use the same notation, justified by Lemma
3.3
below.)

Definition 3.1
.

Assume Assumptions
2.2
.
We define the following properties
of a graphon
W
.

(i)

\mathcal{P}_{{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
is the property
that

\int_{{A_{1}^{m_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{r}^{m_{r}}}}\Psi_{F,W}(x_{1},\dots,x_{|F|})=p^{e(F)}\prod_{i=1}^{r}|A_{i}|^{m_{i}}

(3.3)

holds
for all disjoint subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
of
[0,1]
with

\displaystyle|A_{i}|=\alpha_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,r.

(3.4)

(ii)

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
is the property that

\int_{A_{1}^{m_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{r}^{m_{r}}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=p^{e(F)}\prod_{i=1}^{r}|A_{i}|^{m_{i}}

(3.5)

for all
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
as in
(i)
.

Definition 3.2
.

A property of graphons
W
is
quasi-random
if every graphon
W
that satisfies
it is a.e. equal to a constant.
Furthermore, the property is
p
-quasi-random
if it is satisfied only by graphons

W
that are a.e. equal to
p
.

We can now use standard arguments to translate our problem from graph
sequences to graphons.

Lemma 3.3
.

.
Then

Proof.

This follows by standard arguments as in the special case

4.
Representations of the group of measure-preserving bijections

In the present paper, we attack the problem of whether the properties in
Definitions
2.3
and
3.1
are quasi-random by finding certain irreducible subspaces of

L^{2}([0,1]^{m})

for the natural representation of
the group of measure-preserving bijections of
[0,1]
onto itself.
The idea behind this is that graphons are defined up to measure-preserving bijections,
so properties of them are invariant under such bijections; hence, we are
interested in invariant subspaces, so it seems natural to study such
subspaces in general.
In the present section we state the
result on representations
that we will use;
the proof is given
in Section
8
.

We are really interested in the functions
\Psi_{F,W}

and
\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}
on
[0,1]^{m}
defined in (
3.1
),
which are bounded and real-valued (in fact, with values in
[0,1]
).
However, to have access to the general theory of group representations in a
(complex) Hilbert space, we will more generally consider functions in

L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
, and we allow the functions to be complex-valued.

Our main result in this section (Theorem
4.4
) is for symmetric functions only.
Therefore in the sequel we will mainly consider only the symmetric function

\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}
and the corresponding property
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
.

4.1.
Some spaces of functions

We study functions in

L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
, the Hilbert space of complex-valued
square integrable functions on
[0,1]^{m}
, for some integer
m\geqslant 0
.
(For
m=0
, this is by definition the one-dimensional space
\mathbb{C}
of
constants.)
We use the following notation for this space and certain subspaces,
where
m\geqslant 0
and
-1\leqslant k\leqslant m
.
We may sometimes omit the argument
[0,1]^{m}
.
Recall the notation
x_{I}
from (
2.1
).

•

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m}):=
the subspace of symmetric functions in
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
, i.e. functions
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})

such that
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=f(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(m)})
for every

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
.

•

L^{2}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m}):=
the closed subspace of
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
spanned by functions
f(x)

that depend only on a
subset
x_{I}
of the coordinates for some
I\subseteq[m]
with
|I|\leqslant k
.
(Recall the notation (
2.1
).)
For
k=-1
this means that
L^{2}_{\leqslant-1}([0,1]^{m})=\{0\}
.
Note also that
L^{2}_{\leqslant m}([0,1]^{m})=L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.

•

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m}):=L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})\cap L^{2}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
, the subspace of symmetric functions in
L^{2}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
.

•

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m}):=L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})\ominus L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k-1}([0,1]^{m})

(for
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
only).

Note that we have

\displaystyle\{0\}

\displaystyle=L^{2}_{\leqslant-1}\subseteq L^{2}_{\leqslant 0}\subseteq\dots\subseteq L^{2}_{\leqslant m-1}\subseteq L^{2}_{\leqslant m}=L^{2}([0,1]^{m}),

(4.1)

\displaystyle\{0\}

\displaystyle=L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant-1}\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant 0}\subseteq\dots\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m}=L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m}).

(4.2)

Hence,
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
,
k=0,\dots,m
, are pairwise orthogonal subspaces of

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
, and

\displaystyle L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant\ell}([0,1]^{m}):=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\ell}L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m}).\qquad 0\leqslant\ell\leqslant m.

(4.3)

Note also that

\displaystyle L^{2}_{0}([0,1]^{m})=L^{2}_{\leqslant 0}([0,1]^{m})=L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant 0}([0,1]^{m})=L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{0}([0,1]^{m})=\mathbb{C},

(4.4)

the constant functions on
[0,1]^{m}
.

We can characterize
L^{2}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
and
L^{2}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
as follows using
Fourier transforms.
(This is nothing special for the Fourier transform; the lemma holds for
expansions using any orthogonal basis
(\chi_{i})_{i}
of
L^{2}([0,1])
with

\chi_{0}=1
, but we have chosen to be concrete.)
We denote the Fourier coefficients of
f
by

\widehat{f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})
, where
(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

(i)

L^{2}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
equals the space of functions
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})=0\quad\text{unless }|\{i\in[m]:n_{i}\neq 0\}|\leqslant k.

(4.5)

(ii)

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
equals the space of functions
f\in L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
such that
(
4.5
) holds.

(iii)

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
equals the space of functions
f\in L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})=0\quad\text{unless }|\{i\in[m]:n_{i}\neq 0\}|=k.

(4.6)

Proof.

(i)
:
If
f(x)=f_{I}(x_{I})
for some
I\subseteq[m]
with
|I|\leqslant k
and some

f_{I}\in L^{2}([0,1]^{k})
, then
\widehat{f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})=0
whenever
n_{i}\neq 0
for
some
i\notin I
; hence (
4.5
) holds.
By the definition, such functions
f_{I}(x_{I})
span
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
, and thus
(
4.5
) holds for all
f\in L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
.

Conversely, if (
4.5
) holds, then the Fourier expansion shows that

f
is a limit of linear combinations of functions

\prod_{j\in J}e^{2\pi n_{j}\mathrm{i}x_{j}}
for sets
J\subseteq[m]
with
|J|\leqslant k
,
and these products belong to
L^{2}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
by the definition.

(ii)
:
By
(i)
and the definition of
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
.

(iii)
:
A consequence of
(ii)
and the definition of
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
,
using Parseval’s relation (i.e., the orthogonality of the Fourier series).
∎

For
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
,
define a linear map

\Phi_{m,k}:L^{2}([0,1]^{k})\to L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
by,
for
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle(\Phi_{m,k}f)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=\sum_{I\in\binom{[m]}{k}}f(x_{I}),

(4.7)

where we
sum over the
\binom{m}{k}
subsets of
[m]
of size
k
.
It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\text{If $f\in L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{k})$, then $\Phi_{m,k}f\in L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})$}.

(4.8)

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
.
Then
\Phi_{m,k}
is an isomorphism
of
L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{k})
onto
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})
,
and of the subspace

L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})
onto
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})
and consider the Fourier coefficient

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi_{m,k}f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})=\sum_{I\in\binom{[m]}{k}}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{j}n_{j}x_{j}}f(x_{I})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dotsm\,\mathrm{d}x_{m}.

(4.9)

The integral in (
4.9
) vanishes if
n_{j}\neq 0
for some
j\notin I

(by integrating first over
x_{j}
).
Hence we may assume that
J:=\{j:n_{j}\neq 0\}\subseteq I
.
On the other hand, if
J\subsetneq I
, then

e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{j}n_{j}x_{j}}
depends only on at most
k-1
variables
x_{j}
,
and thus

e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{j}n_{j}x_{j}}\in L^{2}_{\leqslant k-1}([0,1]^{m})

is orthogonal to
f
and again the integral is 0.
Hence, the Fourier coefficient in (
4.9
) vanishes unless
J=I
for some

I\in\binom{[m]}{k}
, i.e., if
|J|=k
, which shows that
\Phi_{m,k}f\in L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{k})

by Lemma
4.1
(iii)
and (
4.8
).
Furthermore, in this case, for any
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\neq 0
, we thus have

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi_{m,k}f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{k},0,\dots,0)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{m}}e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{j}n_{j}x_{j}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dotsm\,\mathrm{d}x_{m}

\displaystyle=\widehat{f}(n_{1},\dots,n_{k}).

(4.10)

The Fourier coefficient

\widehat{\Phi_{m,k}F}(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})
is a symmetric function of

n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
for any
F\in L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
, and thus it follows
from (
4.8
), (
4.1
), and Lemma
4.1
(iii)
that

\Phi_{m,k}
is an isomorphism
L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})\to L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
, and in fact an
isometry up to a constant factor
\binom{m}{k}^{1/2}
(the exact value is not
important for us).

Finally, this implies that

\Phi_{m,k}
also is an isomorphism
L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{k})\to L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}([0,1]^{m})

by the decomposition (
4.3
) applied to both sides (with
\ell=k
).
∎

For two functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
on
[0,1]
, we define

\displaystyle h_{1}\otimes h_{2}(x,y):=h_{1}(x)h_{2}(y),\qquad(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2},

(4.11)

and similarly for more than two factors.
We write also
h^{\otimes k}:=h\otimes\dotsm\otimes h
with
k\geqslant 1
factors.

Let
L^{2}_{1}([0,1]):=L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{1}([0,1])
, and note that,
by the definition or by Lemma
4.1
,

\displaystyle L^{2}_{1}([0,1])=\Bigl\{f\in L^{2}([0,1]):\int_{0}^{1}f=0\Bigr\}.

(4.12)

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
1\leqslant k\leqslant m
. Then

L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
is the closed linear span of
the set
\{\Phi_{m,k}(h^{\otimes k}):h\in L^{2}_{1}([0,1])\}
.

Proof.

Let
L\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{k})
.
be the closed linear span of
\{(h^{\otimes k}:h\in L^{2}_{1}([0,1])\}
.
By Lemma
4.2
, it suffices to show that
L=L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})
.

First, we have, for
h\in L^{2}_{1}([0,1])
,

\displaystyle\widehat{h^{\otimes k}}(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\widehat{h}(n_{i}),

(4.13)

which vanishes if some
n_{i}=0
since
\widehat{h}(0)=\int_{0}^{1}h=0
.
Hence,
h^{\otimes k}\in L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})
by Lemma
4.1
, and thus

L\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})
.
For the converse, we note that if
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in L^{2}_{1}([0,1])
,
then the symmetric function

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}h_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes h_{\sigma(k)}\in L
,
since by standard polarization it can be written as a linear combination of
some tensor powers, e.g. as
(see e.g.
[
13
, (22)]
and
[
22
, Appendix]
)

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}h_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes h_{\sigma(k)}=\sum_{J\subseteq[k]}(-1)^{k-|J|}\Bigl(\sum_{j\in J}h_{j}\Bigr)^{\otimes k}.

(4.14)

Taking
h_{i}(x)=e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n_{i}x}
with
n_{i}=0
and using Lemma
8.4
(iii)

shows that the Fourier expansion of any
f\in L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})
can be written as
a norm convergent sum of functions in
L
; hence

L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{k})\subseteq L
.
∎

4.2.
The decomposition into irreducible representations

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be the group of all measure-preserving bijections
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
.
There is a natural unitary representation
\rho
of
\mathfrak{G}
on
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\varphi}(f)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=f(\varphi^{-1}(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi^{-1}(x_{m})).

(4.15)

(This representation depends on the parameter
m
, but
m
will be clear
from the context and we usually omit it from the notation.)

It is obvious that the subspaces of
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
defined in Section
4.1

all are invariant
subspaces of
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
for the representation
\rho
, and we can thus consider
the representation of
\mathfrak{G}
on any of them given by the restriction of

\rho
.
The basis of our results in the present paper is the following
description of the (closed) invariant subspaces of
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The representation (
4.15
) of
\mathfrak{G}
on
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
is irreducible for
every
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
, and these
m+1
representations are non-equivalent.
Furthermore, every closed subspace
M

of
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})

that is invariant under the representation
\rho

is of the form

\displaystyle M=\bigoplus_{k\in K}L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})

(4.16)

for some set
K\subseteq\{0,\dots,m\}
.
In particular, if there exists a non-constant function

f\in M
, then there
exists
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})\subseteq M
.

This theorem might be known in other contexts,
but we have been unable to find a reference
so we give a complete (and lengthy) proof in
Section
8
.

5.
Applications to subgraph count properties

Assume Assumptions
2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}({m_{1},\dots,m_{r}};\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})

be the subset of
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
consisting of all functions
f\in L^{2,\mathsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
such that

\int_{A_{1}^{m_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{r}^{m_{r}}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0

(5.1)

for all disjoint subsets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
of
[0,1]
with
|A_{i}|=\alpha_{i}
,

.
Then
\mathcal{M}
is evidently a closed subspace of

that is invariant under
the action of
\mathfrak{G}
, and
the property
\widetilde{P}_{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})

is precisely, by comparing (

) and (

),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}-p^{e(F)}\in\mathcal{M}.

(5.2)

Hence we can try to analyse the property

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}}(F;\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
in two steps; we first find
\mathcal{M}
, and then try to show that
if (
5.2
) holds, then necessarily

a.e.

We use Theorem
4.4
and the orthogonal decomposition (
4.16
) of
\mathcal{M}
; let

\mathcal{K}\subseteq\{0,\dots,m\}
be the set
K
in (
4.16
) for this subspace

.
We say that an integer

is

.
Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathcal{K}}L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m}),

taking the direct sum over all bad

.

Remark 5.1
.

Note that
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{K}
, and “bad” depend on
{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
, but not
on the graph

.
On the other hand, the property (

), and whether it implies

(by (

)), but not on

{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}
, or

except through

.
We have thus split the problem into two, connected only through the set

.

\triangle

It is shown in
[

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}-p^{e(F)}=0\text{ a.e.\ on }[0,1]^{m}\implies W=p\text{ a.e.\ on }[0,1]^{2}.

(5.4)

This yields a simple sufficient criterion:

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that

\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
, i.e., there is no bad

.
(This is by (

) equivalent to

defined by (

)

.)

In this case, (

) implies

by (

).
∎

In the sequel,
Theorem
5.2
should be remembered as soon as we find

(and thus

); we will
sometimes explicitly repeat the conclusion of Theorem

, but sometimes this
is left to the reader.
As we will see, the case

is

]

[

]

More generally, we have the following.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be the set of bad
k
, for the given
{m_{1},\dots,m_{r}}
and

.
Then

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}-p^{e(F)}\in\bigoplus_{k\in\mathcal{K}}L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})

(5.6)

Proof.

As said above,
the property

),

) yields the result with (

).

, then by Lemma

\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}-p^{e(F)}\perp L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{0}([0,1]^{m})=\mathbb{C}
and thus

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\bigl(\widetilde{\Psi}_{F,W}-p^{e(F)}\bigr)=0.

If

a.e.  and it follows from (

) that

.
Hence, (

) is equivalent to (

).
∎

In the remainder of this section we study the set
\mathcal{K}
, with the hope of
showing that
\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
(so that Theorem

applies)

as much as possible.

Lemma 5.4
.

0
is never bad.
Hence
\mathcal{K}\subseteq[m]
.

Proof.

If 0 were bad,
then we would have
\mathbb{C}=L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, but (

) does not hold
for

.
∎

We can convert the analytic condition (
5.1
) into an algebraic condition
by introducing the elementary symmetric polynomials

\displaystyle e_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{m}):=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leqslant m}z_{i_{1}}\dotsm z_{i_{k}}.

(5.8)

We define further

\displaystyle e^{(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})}_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{r}):=e_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{2},\dots,z_{r},\dots,z_{r})

(5.9)

with

repeated

times.
Explicitly, by elementary combinatorics,

\displaystyle e^{(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})}_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{r}):=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\dots+\ell_{r}=k}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\binom{m_{j}}{\ell_{j}}z_{j}^{\ell_{j}}.

(5.10)

We then have the following result.
(We explicitly state several closely related versions, partly for later
convenience.)

Theorem 5.5
.

(i)

If
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}<1
, then
\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
and no
k
is bad.

(ii)

If

7.1.

8.
Proof of Theorem
4.4

The proof of Theorem
4.4

is based on an approximation with step functions
and an analogue of the theorem for the symmetric
group
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
acting on a space of step functions (Theorem
8.8
).

8.1.
Step functions

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in[N]
,
let
I_{N,i}:=[(i-1)/N,i/N)
; thus
(I_{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
is a partition of

[0,1)
.

For an
m
-tuple
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in[N]^{m}
, let

\displaystyle Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{m}I_{N,i_{j}}.

(8.1)

Thus,
\{Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}:\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{m}\}
is
a partition of
[0,1)^{m}
into
N^{m}
subcubes.
We define the projection
P_{N}
in
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
as the conditional expectation with
respect to this partition, i.e.,

\displaystyle P_{N}f(x):=N^{m}\int_{Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\text{for }x\in Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}.

(8.2)

P_{N}
is an orthogonal projection of
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
onto the subspace
of functions constant on each cube
Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}
.
(Such functions will be called
step functions
.)
This subspace can be identified with
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
,
the
N^{m}
-dimensional complex vector space of
complex-valued functions on
[N]^{m}
, where
[N]^{m}
is equipped with
normalized times counting measure.
We therefore denote this space of step functions by

\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle P_{N}:L^{2}([0,1]^{m})\to\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{m}),

(8.3)

and there is a natural isometry
\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})\cong\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
.

Remark 8.1
.

Readers familiar with the subject will note that

\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
may be regarded as the tensor power

(\mathbb{C}^{N})^{\otimes m}
; this is implicit in some arguments below.
Similarly,
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}([N]^{m})
defined below equals the symmetric tensor power.

\triangle

For each permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, let
\widetilde{\sigma}
be the map

[0,1)\to[0,1)
that maps each interval
I_{N,i}
to
I_{N,\sigma(i)}
by a
translation.
These maps
\widetilde{\sigma}
are measure-preserving bijections of
[0,1)

onto itself, and thus they form a subgroup
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
of
\mathfrak{G}
.
Obviously,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}\cong\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, and we may identify
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}

and
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
.
Hence, we may regard
\rho
in (
4.15
)
(restricted to
\varphi\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
)
also as a representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
on
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.
(We may extend
\widetilde{\sigma}
to
[0,1]
by
\widetilde{\sigma}(1):=1
, but this is obviously
unimportant, and when convenient we may consider
[0,1)

only; this makes no difference since
functions that are a.e. equal are identified in
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
,
and thus
L^{2}([0,1)^{m})=L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.)

Remark 8.2
.

We have
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{nN}
for every
N,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and
it follows that

\mathfrak{G}_{0}:=\bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty}\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
is a (countable) subgroup of
\mathfrak{G}
.
The only measure-preserving bijections that we will use
are of the type
\widetilde{\sigma}
, and thus the proof of Theorem
4.4
shows that
\rho
is
irreducible
on each
L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
also as a representation of
\mathfrak{G}_{0}
.

\triangle

The group
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
acts (by definition) on
[N]
, and this induces an action
on
[N]^{m}
by
\sigma(i_{1},\dots,i_{m}):=(\sigma(i_{1}),\dots,\sigma(i_{m}))
, which in turn
defines a unitary representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}

on
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
by the formula (
4.15
), now letting

x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in[N]

(and replacing
\varphi
by
\sigma
).
(This is a tensor power of the standard representation on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, see
Remark
8.1
.)
Furthermore,
it is clear that the space of step functions

\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})
is an invariant subspace of
the representation
\rho
in (
4.15
) restricted to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
;
furthermore, the representation of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
on
\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})

obtained by restricting

\rho
obviously agrees with the natural representation of

\mathfrak{S}_{N}
on
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})

given by the formula (
4.15
),
now with
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in[N]
and
\varphi\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
.
Hence, the identifications of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
with
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
,
and
\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})
with
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
cause no problem with the
representations.
We will therefore use the same letter
\rho

for these representations
of
\mathfrak{G}
,
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{N}

on
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
and
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
or various subspaces of them.
(The representation depends on
N
and
m
,
and we may write
\rho^{(m)}
for clarity,
but usually

and

are
clear from
the context and not shown explicitly.)

Recall the notation
[\rho(f)]
in (
2.4
).

Lemma 8.3
.

For every
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
P_{N}f\in[\rho(f)]
.

We prove first the case
N=1
, in the following more precise form.
Note that
P_{1}
is the projection
f\mapsto\int_{[0,1]^{m}}f
onto the constant
functions.

Lemma 8.4
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\displaystyle T_{n}:=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}\rho_{\sigma}.

(8.4)

Then, for every
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
, we have
as
{n\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle T_{n}(f)\overset{L^{2}}{\longrightarrow}P_{1}(f).

(8.5)

Proof.

T_{n}
is an operator of norm 1 on
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.
(Actually, it is the projection onto the subspace of functions invariant for
the action of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
, but we do not need this.)

Since (
8.5
) trivially is true when
f
is a constant, it suffices to
prove (
8.5
) for functions
f
with
P_{1}f=\int_{[0,1)^{m}}f=0
.
Furthermore, since every
T_{n}
has norm 1, it suffices to prove (
8.5
)
for
f
in a dense subspace. We may therefore assume,
in addition to
P_{1}f=0
, that
f
is continuous, and that
for some
\delta>0
, we have
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0
whenever
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\leqslant\delta

for some pair of distinct
i,j\in[m]
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
f
is continuous, there exists
\eta>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon

when
x,y\in[0,1)^{m}
with

|x-y|<\eta
.
It follows that if
n
is large enough, then

|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon
whenever
x
and
y
belong to the same cube

Q_{n,\mathbf{i}}
, and as a consequence, using (
8.2
),
|P_{n}f(x)-f(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon

for every
x\in[0,1)^{m}
. In particular, for large
n
,

\displaystyle\lVert P_{n}f-f\rVert_{2}\leqslant\varepsilon.

(8.6)

Assume also that
n>\delta^{-1}
. Then, if
\mathbf{i}\in D_{n,m}

and
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in Q_{n,\mathbf{i}}
, we have
i_{j}=i_{k}
for some pair

j<k
and thus
|x_{j}-x_{k}|<1/n<\delta
; hence, by our assumption,

f(x)=0
on
Q_{n,\mathbf{i}}
.
Consequently,
f=0
on every diagonal cube,
and thus also
P_{n}f=0
there.

Consider now
T_{n}(P_{n}f)
, and note first that this function,
as
P_{n}f
, is constant on every cube
Q_{n,\mathbf{i}}
.
Since every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
permutes the diagonal cubes

Q_{n,\mathbf{i}}
, it follows from (
8.4
) that
T_{n}(P_{n}f)=0
on every diagonal cube.
Furthermore, also the off-diagonal cubes are permuted; moreover,
when
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on the set of indices
[n]^{m}
,
then the number of permutations

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
mapping a given off-diagonal index onto another is the constant

n!/m!
. Consequently, it follows from (
8.4
) that
the value of
T_{n}(P_{n}f)
on an
off-diagonal cube is the average of
P_{n}f
over all off-diagonal cubes.
Since
P_{n}f
has average 0 and is 0 on the diagonal cubes, this yields

T_{n}(P_{n}f)=0
on every off-diagonal cube.
Consequently,
T_{n}(P_{n}f)=0
, provided
n
is large enough.

Hence, for large
n
,

\displaystyle\lVert T_{n}(f)-P_{1}f\rVert_{2}=\lVert T_{n}(f)\rVert_{2}=\lVert T_{n}(f)-T_{n}(P_{n}f)\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert f-P_{n}f\rVert_{2}\leqslant\varepsilon.

(8.7)

This proves that
T_{n}f\to P_{1}f
for
f
of the special type considered in
the proof, and thus as said above for all
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
; this verifies (
8.5
)
and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
8.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider the subgroup
G_{N,n}
of
\mathfrak{S}_{nN}
that
preserves the
N
subsets
\{1,\dots,n\},\dots,\{(N-1)n+1,\dots,Nn\}

of
[nN]
.
This group is obviously and naturally isomorphic to
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{N}
.
Furthermore, the corresponding subgroup
\widetilde{G}_{N,n}
of
\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{nN}

preserves every interval
I_{N,i}
of length
1/N
.
We now define, in analogy with (
8.4
),

\displaystyle T_{N,n}:=\frac{1}{n!^{N}}\sum_{\sigma\in\widetilde{G}_{N,n}}\rho_{\sigma}.

(8.8)

Again, this is an operator on
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
of norm 1.
We claim that, for every
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
,

\displaystyle T_{N,n}(f)\overset{L^{2}}{\longrightarrow}P_{N}(f),\qquad\text{as ${n\to\infty}$}.

(8.9)

To see this, note first that
each
\sigma\in\widetilde{G}_{N,n}
preserves each cube
Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}
,
so we can study these cubes individually.
If we map
Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}
onto the entire cube
[0,1)
by the obvious linear map,
then the action of
T_{N,n}
on
L^{2}(Q_{N,\mathbf{i}})
corresponds to the operator

\displaystyle T_{N,n,\mathbf{i}}\,g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\frac{1}{n!^{N}}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{N}\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}g(\sigma_{i_{1}}(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma_{i_{m}}(x_{m}))

(8.10)

acting on
g\in L^{2}([0,1)^{m})=L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.

This action on
the cube
Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}
depends on the index
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})
;
more precisely, it depends
on which equalities
i_{j}=i_{k}
there are among the indices.
Consider a case where there are
\ell
different values among

\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}
; we may without loss of generality assume that these
are
1,\dots,\ell
. Let
J_{k}:=\{j:i_{j}=k\}
for
k\in[\ell]
.
Suppose that
g
is of the special form

\displaystyle g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=g_{1}(x_{J_{1}})\dotsm g_{\ell}(x_{J_{\ell}}),

(8.11)

where
g_{k}\in L^{2}([0,1]^{|J_{k}|})
for
k\in[\ell]
.
(Recall the notation (
2.1
), and note that this is a more general
version of (
4.11
).)
Then (
8.10
) yields, using the notation (
8.4
),

\displaystyle T_{N,n,\mathbf{i}}\,g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=T_{n}g_{1}(x_{J_{1}})\dotsm T_{n}g_{\ell}(x_{J_{\ell}}).

(8.12)

Lemma
8.4
shows that, as
n\to\infty
,
each
T_{n}g_{k}\overset{L^{2}}{\longrightarrow}P_{1}g_{k}
, and thus if follows from (
8.12
) that

\displaystyle T_{N,n,\mathbf{i}}\,g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\overset{L^{2}}{\longrightarrow}P_{1}g_{1}(x_{J_{1}})\dotsm P_{1}g_{\ell}(x_{J_{\ell}})=\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\int_{[0,1]^{|J_{k}|}}g_{k}=\int_{[0,1]^{m}}g,

(8.13)

for every
g\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
of the special type (
8.11
).
Linear combinations of such functions are dense in
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
, as is seen, for
example, by the Fourier series expansion. Since
T_{N,n,\mathbf{i}}
has norm 1, it
follows that

\displaystyle T_{N,n,\mathbf{i}}\,g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\overset{L^{2}}{\longrightarrow}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}g

(8.14)

for every
g\in L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.

Returning to the cube
Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}
, (
8.14
) shows that

T_{N,n}f\to N^{m}\int_{Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}}f=P_{N}f
in
L^{2}(Q_{N,\mathbf{i}})
,
for every
\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{m}
, which yields (
8.9
).
This completes the proof, since evidently
T_{N,n}(f)\in[\rho(f)]
.
∎

8.2.
Spaces of step functions

Let
N\geqslant 1
and
m\geqslant 0
.
We only need the results for large
N
;
we ignore complications that occur for small
N
by making explicit assumptions
on
N
when needed. (We invite the curious reader to investigate the
exceptional cases with small
N
.)
Recall that we may identify the

N^{m}
-dimensional complex vector space
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
of
complex-valued functions on
[N]^{m}

and the corresponding space
\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{m})

of step functions constant on every cube
Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}
.
We define some subspaces of
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
that will be important;
these can be identified with the corresponding subspace of

\widetilde{\ell}^{2}([N]^{m})\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.

First, we define the set of
diagonal
indices

\displaystyle D_{N,m}:=\bigl\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in[N]^{m}:i_{k}=i_{\ell}\text{ for some pair $k<\ell$}\bigr\},

(8.15)

its complement,
the
off-diagonal
indices,

\displaystyle D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}:=[N]^{m}\setminus D_{N,m}=\bigl\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in[N]^{m}:i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\text{ are distinct}\bigr\}.

(8.16)

We will later also use (for functions in
\widetilde{\ell}([N]^{m})
)
the corresponding subsets of
[0,1)^{m}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{N,m}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in D_{N,m}}Q_{N,\mathbf{i}},

(8.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}

\displaystyle:=\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\notin D_{N,m}}Q_{N,\mathbf{i}}=[0,1)^{m}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{N},

(8.18)

We will mainly be interested in
symmetric
functions in
\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
that vanish on the
set
D_{N,m}
of diagonal indices defined in (
8.15
).
We assume in the sequel
N\geqslant m
(so that
D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}\neq\emptyset
).
We use the natural identification

\displaystyle\ell^{2}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{f\in\ell^{2}([N]^{m}):f(\mathbf{i})=0\text{ for }\mathbf{i}\in D_{N,m}\bigr\}.

(8.19)

We further define

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}([N]^{m})

\displaystyle:=\text{the subspace of symmetric functions in }\ell^{2}([N]^{m}),

(8.20)

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})

\displaystyle:=\ell^{2}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\cap\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}([N]^{m}).

(8.21)

Recall that

\binom{[N]}{m}
denotes the set of all
\binom{N}{m}
subsets of
{N}
of size
m
.
We can identify the space of symmetric functions on
D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}

with the space of functions on
\binom{[N]}{m}
; thus

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})=\ell^{2}\bigl(\tbinom{[N]}{m}\bigr),

(8.22)

where we
normalize the counting measure on
\binom{[N]}{m}
such that
(
8.22
) is an isometry.
In particular,

\displaystyle\dim(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}))=\tbinom{N}{m}.

(8.23)

Let
\nu_{N,m}
denote the counting measure on
\binom{[N]}{m}

with our normalization used in (
8.22
);
then its total mass is

\displaystyle\nu_{N,m}\bigl(\tbinom{[N]}{m}\bigr)=N^{-m}|D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}|=\frac{N!/(N-m)!}{N^{m}}.

(8.24)

Note that for a fixed
m
, this increases to 1 as
{N\to\infty}
, and that for
every
N\geqslant m
we have

\displaystyle m!/m^{m}\leqslant\nu_{N,m}\bigl(\tbinom{[N]}{m}\bigr)<1.

(8.25)

For
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
,
define a linear map

\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}:\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})\to\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})

by,
for distinct
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in[N]
,

\displaystyle(\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=\sum_{I\in\binom{[m]}{k}}f(x_{I}),

(8.26)

where we
recall the notation (
2.1
), and
sum over the
\binom{m}{k}
subsets of
[m]
of size
k
.
Equivalently, we can regard
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
as a linear map

\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[N]}{k}\bigr)\to\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[N]}{m}\bigr)

defined by, for
I\in\binom{[N]}{m}
,

\displaystyle(\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f)(I):=\sum_{J\subseteq I:|J|=k}f(J).

(8.27)

Denote the range of
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
by

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}):=\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k}))\subseteq\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}).

(8.28)

If
0\leqslant k\leqslant\ell\leqslant m
and
f\in\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})
, then,
for
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(N)}_{m,\ell}\Phi^{(N)}_{\ell,k}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\sum_{I\in\binom{[m]}{\ell}}\Phi^{(N)}_{\ell,k}f(x_{I})=\sum_{I\in\binom{[m]}{\ell},\;J\in\binom{I}{k}}f(x_{J})

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{J\in\binom{[m]}{k}}\binom{m-k}{\ell-k}f(x_{J})=\binom{m-k}{\ell-k}\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}).

(8.29)

Thus,

\Phi^{(N)}_{m,\ell}\Phi^{(N)}_{\ell,k}=\binom{m-k}{\ell-k}\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
,
and it follows that, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant\ell\leqslant m
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(N)}_{m,\ell}\bigl(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,\ell})\bigr)=\Phi^{(N)}_{m,\ell}\Phi^{(N)}_{\ell,k}\bigl(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})\bigr)=\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}\bigl(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})\bigr)=\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}).

(8.30)

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
and assume
N\geqslant m+k
. Then the map

\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}:\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})\to\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
is injective.
Hence,
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
is a bijection

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})\to\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
.
Furthermore, there is a constant
C_{m,k}
(not depending on
N
) such that
for every
f\in\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})
,

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{2}\leqslant C_{m,k}\lVert\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f\rVert_{2}.

(8.31)

It follows from (
8.23
) that
when
N<m+k
,

\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
is
not
injective
(and thus (
8.31
) does not hold)
except in the trivial cases
k=m
or
N<k
.

Proof.

In this proof it is convenient to regard
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
as the map

\ell^{2}(\binom{[N]}{k})\to\ell^{2}(\binom{[N]}{m})
given by (
8.27
).

Let
f\in\ell^{2}(\binom{[N]}{k})
.
Fix two disjoint sets
A,B\subseteq[N]
with
|A|=k
and
|B|=m
.
For
j=0,\dots,k
,
let

\displaystyle y_{j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{I\in\binom{A}{j},\,J\in\binom{B}{k-j}}f(I\cup J),

(8.32)

\displaystyle z_{j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{I\in\binom{A}{j},\,J\in\binom{B}{m-j}}\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f(I\cup J).

(8.33)

Then, by (
8.27
) and interchanging the order of summation,

\displaystyle z_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in\binom{A}{j},\,J\in\binom{B}{m-j}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\sum_{I^{\prime}\in\binom{I}{i},\,J^{\prime}\in\binom{J}{k-i}}f(I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\sum_{I^{\prime}\in\binom{A}{i},\,J^{\prime}\in\binom{B}{k-i}}\binom{k-i}{j-i}\binom{m-k+i}{m-j-k+i}f(I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\binom{k-i}{j-i}\binom{m-k+i}{m-j-k+i}y_{i}.

(8.34)

Now suppose that
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f=0
.
Then all
z_{j}=0
, and (
8.2
) yields a triangular
system of equations for
y_{0},\dots,y_{k}
, with strictly positive
coefficients on the diagonal.
Hence this system has the unique solution
y_{0}=\dots=y_{k}=0
.
Moreover, (
8.32
) yields
y_{k}=f(A)
, and thus
f(A)=0
.
Since
A
is arbitrary, we see that
f=0
, and thus
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
is injective.
Hence,

\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
is a bijection of
\ell^{2}(\binom{[N]}{k})
onto its image,
which by the identification (
8.22
) and
(
8.28
) means that
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
is a bijection of

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})
onto
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
.

In particular, taking
N=m+k
,
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}
is a bijection of

\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[m+k]}{k}\bigr)
onto a subset of

\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[m+k]}{m}\bigr)
.
Since these spaces are
finite-dimensional, it follows that there is a constant
C_{m,k}
such that,
for all
f\in\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[m+k]}{k}\bigr)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{m+k}{k}}\sum_{I\in\binom{[m+k]}{k}}|f(I)|^{2}=\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant C_{m,k}^{2}\lVert\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f\rVert_{2}=\frac{C_{m,k}^{2}}{\binom{m+k}{m}}\sum_{J\in\binom{[m+k]}{m}}|\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f(J)|^{2}.

(8.35)

Now, let
N\geqslant m+k
be arbitrary, and take a set
X\in\binom{[N]}{m+k}
.
It follows from the definition (
8.27
) that the restriction
of
\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f
to
\binom{X}{m}
depends only on the restriction of
f
to

\binom{X}{k}
; furthermore, this restriction defines a map

\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{X}{k}\bigr)\to\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{X}{m}\bigr)
which up to notational
differences is the same as

\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}:\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[m+k]}{k}\bigr)\to\ell^{2}\bigl(\binom{[m+k]}{m}\bigr)
.
Hence, (
8.35
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{m+k}{k}}\sum_{I\in\binom{X}{k}}|f(I)|^{2}\leqslant C_{m,k}^{2}\frac{1}{\binom{m+k}{m}}\sum_{J\in\binom{X}{m}}|\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f(J)|^{2}.

(8.36)

The left-hand side is the average of
|f(I)|^{2}
over all
I\in\binom{X}{k}
, and
similarly for the right-hand side.
Taking the average of (
8.36
) over all
X\in\binom{[N]}{m+k}
yields the
averages over all
I\in\binom{[N]}{k}
and
J\in\binom{[N]}{m}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\binom{N}{k}}\sum_{I\in\binom{[N]}{k}}|f(I)|^{2}\leqslant C_{m,k}^{2}\frac{1}{\binom{N}{m}}\sum_{J\in\binom{[N]}{m}}|\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f(J)|^{2}.

(8.37)

This is (
8.31
) if we define the norms using counting measures
normalized to have total mass 1. We use instead the normalization (
8.24
),
but (
8.25
) shows
that this changes the norms by at most a constant factor,
not depending on
N
. Hence, changing
C_{m,k}
,
(
8.31
) is valid also for our norms.
∎

It follows from (
8.30
) and (
8.28
) that if
0\leqslant k\leqslant\ell
, then

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})=\Phi^{(N)}_{m,\ell}\bigl(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,\ell})\bigr)\subseteq\Phi^{(N)}_{m,\ell}\bigl(\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,\ell})\bigr)=\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant\ell}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}).

(8.38)

Hence,
defining also
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant-1}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}):=\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\{0\}=\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant-1}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\subseteq\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant 0}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\subseteq\dotsm\subseteq\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\subseteq\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})=\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}).

(8.39)

We define, analoguously to Section
4.1
, the orthogonal complemets

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}):=\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\ominus\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k-1}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}).

(8.40)

Then

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
,
k=0,\dots,m
, are pairwise orthogonal subspaces of

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
, and

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant\ell}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\ell}\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}),\qquad 0\leqslant\ell\leqslant m.

(8.41)

8.3.
Some representations of the symmetric group

In the present subsection, we consider
\rho
as a representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}

on various spaces.
We use the notation

\rho|{V}
when we want to emphasize that we consider

\rho
as a representation on a subspace
V\subseteq\ell^{2}([N]^{m})
.

We will use some basic facts from the theory of finite-dimensional
representation of finite groups; see Appendix
A

or, for example,
[
5
]
and
[
15
]
.

Remark 8.6
.

There is a large theory describing the representations of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, see for
example
[
5
, Lecture 4]
for some parts of it.
We avoid using any deep results here, and will only use basic representation
theory and some simple combinatorial calculations with characters.
The results below are presumably all known, but we do not
know any references and we give full proofs for completeness.

\triangle

The subspaces
\ell^{2}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
and
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})

defined in (
8.19
) and (
8.21
)
are clearly
invariant under

.

on
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k})
and
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})

\displaystyle\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}(\rho^{(k)}_{\sigma}f)=\rho^{(m)}_{\sigma}(\Phi^{(N)}_{m,k}f),\qquad f\in\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,k}),\;\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}.

It follows that also

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
and

\rho
also yields representations of

on these four spaces.

We denote the characters of the representation

on some spaces by,

\displaystyle\chi_{m}:=\chi_{\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}},\qquad\chi_{m,\leqslant k}:=\chi_{\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}},\qquad\chi_{m,k}:=\chi_{\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}}.

.

Let

Proof.

This follows from Lemma

It follows from (

\displaystyle\chi_{m,k}=\chi_{k}-\chi_{k-1}=:\widehat{\chi}_{k},\qquad 0\leqslant k\leqslant m,\;N\geqslant k+m.

(8.45)

Theorem 8.8
.

Assume
N\geqslant 2m
. Then the representation
\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}

of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}

is irreducible
for
k=0,\dots,m
, and these representations are distinct
(i.e., non-isomorphic). Hence, (
8.41
) yields the (unique) decomposition of

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{\leqslant k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
into its irreducible components.

Proof.

Consider first
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
.
As said in (
8.22
), we can identify
\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})
with the
space
\ell^{2}(\binom{[N]}{m})
of all functions
\binom{[N]}{m}\to\mathbb{C}
, and since a
permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}

acts as a permutation on
\binom{[N]}{m}
in the obvious way, it follows
(see Example
A.5
)
that
\chi_{m}(\sigma)
equals
the number of subsets
A\subseteq N
of size
m
that are fixed by
\sigma
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\chi_{m}(\sigma)=\sum_{A\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(A)=A\}.

(8.46)

Hence, for any
k
and
m\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\langle\chi_{k},\chi_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\chi_{k}(\sigma)\overline{\chi_{m}(\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sum_{A\in\binom{[N]}{k},\,B\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(A)=A\}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(B)=B\}.

(8.47)

We interchange the order of summation, and note that the product of
indicator functions is 1 if and only if
\sigma
maps the four disjoint sets

A\cap B
,
A\setminus B
,
B\setminus A
,
[N]\setminus(A\cup B)
into
themselves. Hence, if
|A\cap B|=j\leqslant k\land m
,
then the number of such
\sigma
is

j!\,(k-j)!\,(m-j)!\,(N-m-k+j)!
. Furthermore, for every
j\geqslant 0
with

j\leqslant k
,
j\leqslant m
, and
m+k-j\leqslant N
, the number of such pairs
(A,B)
of
sets is
\binom{N}{j,k-j,m-j,N-m-k+j}
.
Consequently, if we assume
k\leqslant m
,
then, since
N\geqslant 2m\geqslant m+k
,

\displaystyle\langle\chi_{k},\chi_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{N}{j,k-j,m-j,N-m-k+j}j!\,(k-j)!\,(m-j)!\,(N-m-k+j)!

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}1=k+1.

(8.48)

It follows from (
8.3
) and (
8.45
) that if
k,\ell\leqslant m
,
then

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\chi}_{k},\widehat{\chi}_{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{k},\chi_{\ell}\rangle-\langle\chi_{k},\chi_{\ell-1}\rangle-\langle\chi_{k-1},\chi_{\ell}\rangle+\langle\chi_{k-1},\chi_{\ell-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=(k+1)\land(\ell+1)-(k+1)\land\ell-k\land(\ell+1)+k\land\ell

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&k\neq\ell,\\
1,&k=\ell.\end{cases}

(8.49)

Hence, the characters
\widehat{\chi}_{0},\dots,\widehat{\chi}_{m}
are orthonormal, which shows
(using (
8.45
) again and (
8.43
))
that the corresponding representations

\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{0}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})},\dots,\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}

are irreducible and distinct.
(See Appendix
A
.)

Theorem 8.10
.

Assume
N\geqslant 2m
. Then the irreducible representation

\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}

of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}

appears exactly once as a component of
\rho|{\ell^{2}([N]^{m})}
, and it
does not appear at all as a component of
\rho|{\ell^{2}([N]^{k})}
for any
k<m
.

Proof.

Let us first compute the character
\chi_{\rho|{\ell^{2}([N]^{k})}}
.
For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
, let
\tau(\sigma)
be the number of fixed points of
\sigma
.
Since
\ell^{2}([N]^{k})
is the space of all functions on
[N]^{k}
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{N}
acts by
permuting the elements of
[N]^{k}
, the character is
(see again Example
A.5
)
given by the number of
fixed points of this action, which is
\tau(\sigma)^{k}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho|{\ell^{2}([N]^{k})}}(\sigma)=\tau(\sigma)^{k},\qquad\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}.

(8.50)

We compute the mean of this character, which we denote by

M_{k}:=\langle\tau^{k},1\rangle
.
It is simpler to consider the descending factorials, denoted by

(a)_{\ell}:=a(a-1)\dotsm(a-\ell+1)
. Then,
for
\ell\leqslant N
,
with
\sumx
denoting the sum over
distinct variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}
only and
by interchanging the order of summation,

\displaystyle\langle(\tau)_{\ell},1\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}(\tau(\sigma))_{\ell}=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sumx_{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\in[N]}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(x_{i})=x_{i}\;\forall i\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sumx_{x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell}\in[N]}(N-\ell)!=1.

(8.51)

(See also Remark
8.11
below.)
If
\ell>N
, we instead trivially have
(\tau)_{\ell}=0
. Hence we conclude

\displaystyle\langle(\tau)_{\ell},1\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\ell\leqslant N,\\
0,&\ell>N.\end{cases}

(8.52)

We have
x^{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}(x)_{\ell}
,
where
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{\ell}
are the
Stirling numbers of the second kind,
see e.g.
[
6
, (6.10)]
or
[
21
, 1.(24d)]
.
Hence, (
8.52
) yields

\displaystyle M_{k}:=\langle\tau^{k},1\rangle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\tau(\sigma)^{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}\langle(\tau)_{\ell},1\rangle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k\land N}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}.

(8.53)

In particular, for
k\leqslant N
,

\displaystyle M_{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}=:B_{k},\qquad 0\leqslant k\leqslant N,

(8.54)

where
B_{k}
is the
k
:th Bell number

[
6
, Exercise 7.15]
,
see again Remark
8.11
.
(We can take (
8.54
) as a definition
of
B_{k}
and do not need to know its combinatorial meaning.)

Furthermore, for the case
k=N+1
,
(
8.53
) yields

\displaystyle M_{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}=B_{k}-\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{k}=B_{k}-1,\qquad k=N+1.

(8.55)

Next, we consider
\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{m}\rangle
. We have, by (
8.46
),

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{m}\rangle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}}\sum_{A\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(A)=A\}\tau(\sigma)^{k}

(8.56)

For a given
A
, the permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
that satisfy
\sigma(A)=A

consist of one permutation of
A
and another of
[N]\setminus A
.
Hence (
8.56
) yields, by interchanging the order of summation,

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{A\in\binom{[N]}{m}}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\sum_{\sigma_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{N-m}}(\tau(\sigma_{1})+\tau(\sigma_{2}))^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N!}\binom{N}{m}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\sum_{\sigma_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{N-m}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\tau(\sigma_{1})^{j}\tau(\sigma_{2})^{k-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}\tau(\sigma_{1})^{j}\frac{1}{(N-m)!}\sum_{\sigma_{2}\in\mathfrak{S}_{N-m}}\tau(\sigma_{2})^{k-j}.

(8.57)

If
k\leqslant m
and
k\leqslant N-m
, then
the two normalized inner sums on the last line of (
8.3
)
are evaluated by (
8.53
)–(
8.54
) to
B_{j}
and
B_{k-j}
, respectively.
Hence, (
8.3
) yields

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}B_{j}B_{k-j},\qquad 0\leqslant k\leqslant m,\;N\geqslant k+m.

(8.58)

Note that the right-hand side does not depend on
m
, subject to
k\leqslant m\leqslant N-k
.
Consequently, if
0\leqslant k<m
and
N\geqslant 2m
,
so that (
8.58
) applies both for
m
and for
m-1
,
then (
8.45
) and (
8.58
)
yield

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{k},\widehat{\chi}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{m}\rangle-\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{m-1}\rangle=0.

(8.59)

This yields the claim for
k<m
, since
\tau^{k}
is the character of

\rho|{\ell^{2}([N]^{k})}

and
\widehat{\chi}_{m}
is the character of
\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}
, which is irreducible by
Theorem
8.8
.

If
k=m+1
and
N\geqslant k+m=2k-1
, we obtain similarly from (
8.3
),
(
8.54
), and (
8.55
) (the latter for the case

using also

(see (
8.54

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{k},\chi_{k-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}B_{j}B_{k-j}+\binom{k}{k}(B_{k}-1)B_{0}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}B_{j}B_{k-j}-1.

(8.60)

Consequently, assuming

F_{N}=F_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m-1}}
,

\displaystyle P_{N}f\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}}=\Phi^{(N)}_{m,m-1}F_{N}=\Phi^{(N)}_{m,m-1}(F_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m-1}})=\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}}.

(8.70)

Furthermore, by Lemma
4.2
,

\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\in L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{m-1}([0,1]^{m})
, and thus
\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\perp f_{m}
.
Consequently, using (
8.70
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle f_{m},\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rangle=\langle f_{m},\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}}\cdot\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rangle+\langle f_{m},\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f_{m},\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m}}\cdot P_{N}f\rangle+\langle f_{m},\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f_{m},P_{N}f\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot f_{m},P_{N}f\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot f_{m},\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rangle.

(8.71)

It is well-known that as
{N\to\infty}
, we have
P_{N}f\to f
in
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\langle f_{m},P_{N}f\rangle\to\langle f_{m},f\rangle=\langle f_{m},f_{m}\rangle=\lVert f_{m}\rVert_{2}^{2}>0.

(8.72)

Furthermore,
|\mathcal{D}_{N,m}|\leqslant\binom{m}{2}N^{-1}\to 0
as
{N\to\infty}
, and thus

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot f_{m}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\int_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}|f_{m}|^{2}\to 0.

(8.73)

The operator
P_{N}
is an orthogonal projection and has norm 1, and

\Phi_{m,m-1}
in (
4.7
) is clearly bounded; thus
(
8.69
) implies

\displaystyle\lVert-P_{N}f+\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert f\rVert_{2}+C\lVert F_{N}\rVert_{2}\leqslant C\lVert f\rVert_{2}.

(8.74)

Consequently, we obtain by (
8.73
), (
8.74
), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
as
{N\to\infty}
,

\displaystyle-\langle\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot f_{m},P_{N}f\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{N,m}}\cdot f_{m},\Phi_{m,m-1}F_{N}\rangle\to 0.

(8.75)

We have shown in (
8.72
) and (
8.75
)
that as
{N\to\infty}
, the right-hand side of (
8.4
) tends to

\lVert f_{m}\rVert_{2}^{2}>0
.
This means that if
N
is large enough, then (
8.4
) cannot hold.
This contradiction means that the assumption
R_{N}P_{N}f=0
then cannot hold;
in other words, if
N
is large enough, then we must have
R_{N}P_{N}f\neq 0

so Case 1 holds, and as shown above, then (
8.64
) holds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

We prove first that every closed invariant subspace

M
has a decomposition (
4.16
).
We use induction on
m
. The base case
m=0
is trivial, so we may assume

m\geqslant 1
.
Assume that
M
is a closed
\rho
-invariant subspace of
L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
.
We consider two cases.

Case 1:
M\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}([0,1]^{m})

By Lemma
4.2
,
\Phi_{m,m-1}
is an isomorphism

L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m-1})\to L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}([0,1]^{m})
, and it follows from the definition
(
4.7
) that
\Phi_{m,m-1}
intertwines the action
\rho
of
\mathfrak{G}
on

L^{2}([0,1]^{m-1})
and
L^{2}([0,1]^{m})
.
Consequently,
\Phi_{m,m-1}^{-1}M
is a closed
\rho
-invariant subspace of

L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m-1})
, and by the induction hypothesis we have a decomposition

\displaystyle\Phi_{m,m-1}^{-1}M=\bigoplus_{k\in K}L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}\bigl([0,1]^{m-1}\bigr)

(8.76)

for some set
K\subseteq\{0,\dots,m-1\}
.
We then obtain the desired decomposition (
4.16
) by applying

\Phi_{m,m-1}
and using Lemma
4.2
again.

Case 2:
M\not\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}([0,1]^{m})

In this case there exists
f\in M\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
such that

f\notin L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}([0,1]^{m})
.
Since
M
is invariant, we have
[\rho(f)]\subseteq M
, and thus Lemma
8.14

yields, for every sufficiently large
N
.

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\subseteq[\rho(f)]\subseteq M.

(8.77)

Consider now the orthogonal complement
M^{\perp}:=L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})\ominus M
.
This is also a closed
\rho
-invariant subspace of
L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
, since
M
is.
We have the same two cases for
M^{\perp}
.
If Case 1 holds, i.e.,
M^{\perp}\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{\leqslant m-1}([0,1]^{m})
,
then, as shown above, (
4.16
) holds for
M^{\perp}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle M^{\perp}=\bigoplus_{k\in K^{\perp}}L^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})

(8.78)

for some
K^{\perp}\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,m\}
.
This and (
4.3
) (with
\ell=m
) imply that
(
4.16
) holds witk
K:=\{0,\dots,m\}\setminus K^{\perp}
.

Finally, suppose that Case 2 applies to both
M
and
M^{\perp}
.
Then, for every large
N
, (
8.77
) holds (for some
f
), and also similarly

\displaystyle\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\subseteq M^{\perp}.

(8.79)

But this is absurd, since (
8.77
) and (
8.79
) imply that

\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{m}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})\subseteq M\cap M^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
Hence this case cannot occur, which completes the proof of (
4.16
).

Let
V_{k}:=L^{2,\textsf{s}}_{k}([0,1]^{m})
, and
suppose that
W
is a closed invariant subspace of
V_{k}

Then
W
has a decomposition (
4.16
), and since
W\subseteq V_{k}
it is
obvious that
K=\emptyset
or
\{k\}
, and thus
W=\{0\}
or
W=V_{k}
, which
shows that
\rho|{V}_{k}
is irreducible.

Finally, suppose that two of these representations are
equivalent, say
\rho|{V}_{i}
and
\rho|{V}_{j}
with
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant m
.
Then there exists an invertible bounded linear operator
T:V_{i}\to V_{j}
such
that

T\rho_{\varphi}=\rho_{\varphi}T
on
V_{i}
for every
\varphi\in\mathfrak{G}
.
This implies that
W:=\{f+Tf:f\in V_{i}\}
is a closed invariant subspace of

V_{i}\oplus V_{j}\subseteq L^{2,\textsf{s}}([0,1]^{m})
. But
W\cap V_{k}=\{0\}
for every
k
,
and thus
W
cannot have a decomposition (
4.16
). This contradiction
shows that the representations are non-equivalent.
∎

Appendix A
Group representations

In this appendix we collect for easy reference
some results on group representations that are used above.
The results are known,
and can be found in many textbooks, see for example

[
5
]
and
[
15
]
.

A
representation
of a group
G
on a vector space
V

is a group homomorphism
\rho:g\mapsto\rho_{g}

from
G
to
\mathsf{GL}(V)
, the group of linear bijections (isomorphisms)
V\to V
.
In this paper,
all representations are
unitary
,
which means that
V
is a complex Hilbert space and

\rho_{g}
is a unitary operator in
V
for every
g\in G
.
Furthermore,
the vector spaces
V
that we consider are
always subspaces of
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}},\mu)
for some measure space
({\mathcal{X}},\mu)
.
(Recall that
L^{2}({\mathcal{X}},\mu)
is the Hilbert space of square-integrable
complex-valued functions defined on
X
,
with functions that are a.e. equal identified.)
Note that we consider both finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional Hilbert
spaces
V
.

Let
\rho
be a unitary representation of
G
on
V
.
We recall some notation from Section
2.2
.

A closed subspace
W\subseteq V
is
invariant
if
\rho_{g}(W)\subseteq W

for every
g\in G
. (Then, in fact,
\rho_{g}(W)=W
.)
This means that the restriction of
\rho_{g}
to
W
defines a representation

\rho|{W}
of
G
on
W
.

Given a representation
\rho
of
G
on
V
, and an element
f\in V
,
define

\displaystyle[\rho(f)]:=\text{the closed linear hull of }\{\rho_{g}(f):g\in G\}\subseteq V.

(A.1)

It is easily seen that
[\rho(f)]
is an invariant subspace of
V
; in fact, it
is the smallest invariant subspace that contains
f
.

A representation
\rho
is
irreducible
if the only closed invariant
subspaces are the trivial
\{0\}
and
V
.
We record a well-known fact.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\rho
be a unitary representation of a group
G
on a Hilbert space
V
.
Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\rho
is irreducible.

(ii)

[\rho(f)]=V
for every
0\neq f\in V
.

Proof.

As said above,

[\rho(f)]
is a closed invariant subspace for every

f\in V
.
Hence, if
\rho
is irreducible and
f\neq 0
, then
[\rho(f)]=V
.

Conversely, if

W\neq\{0\}
is a closed invariant subspace,
let
0\neq f\in W
. Since
W
is invariant and closed,
it follows that
[\rho(f)]\subseteq W
.
Consequently,
(ii)
implies
W=V
and thus
\rho
is irreducible.
∎

A.1.
Finite-dimensional representations of finite groups

In this subsection, we assume that
\rho
is a finite-dimensional
representations of a finite group
G
.
(In this case, there is always an inner product on
V
that makes the
representation unitary, so this can be assumed without loss of generality.
In our applications the representations are already unitary for the given inner
products.)

If
\rho
is a representation of a finite group
G

on a finite-dimensional space
V
,
its
character

\chi_{\rho}
is defined as the function
G\to\mathbb{C}
given by the trace
of
\rho
:

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}(g):=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{g}),\qquad g\in G.

(A.2)

Two representations are isomorphic if and only if they have the
same character.

We regard the characters as elements of
L^{2}(G)
, where
G
is equipped with
the normalized uniform (Haar) measure (i.e.,
1/|G|
times counting measure).
Hence, for two characters
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, their inner product is

\displaystyle\langle\chi_{\rho_{1}},\chi_{\rho_{2}}\rangle:=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\chi_{\rho_{1}}(g)\overline{\chi_{\rho_{2}}(g)}.

(A.3)

Proposition A.2

(
[
15
, Théorèmes 3 and 5]
)
.

A finite-dimensional representation of a finite group
G

is irreducible if and only if its character has
norm
1
, and different (i.e., non-isomorphic)
irreducible representations of
G
have orthogonal characters.

Proposition A.3

(
[
15
, Théorème 2]
;
[
5
, Corollary 1.6]
)
.

Let
\rho
be a representation of a finite group
G
on a
finite-dimensional space
V
.
Then there exists a decomposition
V=U_{1}\oplus\dotsm\oplus U_{m}

of
V
as a direct sum of invariant subspaces such
that every
\rho|{U_{i}}
is irreducible.

Proposition A.4

(
[
15
, Théorème 8]
;
[
5
, (2.31) p 23]
)
.

Let
\rho
be a representation of a finite group
G
on a
finite-dimensional space
V
. Suppose that
V=U_{1}\oplus\dotsm\oplus U_{m}

is a decomposition of
V
as a direct sum of invariant subspaces such
that every
\rho|{U_{i}}
is irreducible.
Let
{\rho^{\ast}}
be an irreducible finite-dimensional representation of
G

on a vector space
W
and let
{\chi^{\ast}}
be its character.
Then the linear operator

\displaystyle R_{{\chi^{\ast}}}:=\frac{\dim(W)}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{{\chi^{\ast}}(g)}\rho_{g}

(A.4)

is the projection of
V
onto the subspace
consisting of the sum of all
U_{i}
such that
\rho|{U_{i}}\cong{\rho^{\ast}}
.
If the representation
\rho
is unitary (as we assume above), then the projection

R_{\chi^{\ast}}
is orthogonal.

Example A.5
.

Let a group
G
act on a finite set
S
by permutations
\sigma_{g}
,

g\in G
. This defines a representation
\rho
of
G
on
\ell^{2}(S)
by

\rho_{g}(f)(x):=f(\sigma_{g}^{-1}(x))
(cf. (
4.15
)).
By considering the standard basis in
\ell^{2}(S)
, we see that the character
of this representation is given by

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}(g)=|\{x\in S:\sigma_{g}(x)=x\}|,

(A.5)

the number of fixed points of
\rho_{g}
.

\triangle

Example A.6
.

We give a simple proof that, as claimed in Remark
8.9
,
if
0\leqslant k\leqslant m
and
N\geqslant m+k
, then
the representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{N}

given by
\rho|{\ell^{2,\mathsf{s}}_{k}(D^{\mathsf{c}}_{N,m})}

is the irreducible representation
corresponding to the Young diagram (or the partition)

(N-k,k)
.
(This gives an alternative proof of Theorem
8.8
.)

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{N}
and suppose that
\sigma
has
n_{j}(\sigma)
cycles of length
j
.
Thus
\sum_{j=1}^{N}jn_{j}(\sigma)=N
.
A subset of
[N]
is fixed by
\sigma
if and only if it is a union of cycles
of
\sigma
; in other words, for every cycle we have the choice of either
including it or not.
Hence, the generating function for the sizes of the fixed sets is

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subseteq[N]}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(A)=A\}x^{|A|}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1+x^{j})^{n_{j}(\sigma)}.

(A.6)

Let
[x^{m}]G(x)
denote the coefficient of
x^{m}
in a polynomial
G(x)
,
Then, by (
8.46
) and (
A.6
),

\displaystyle\chi_{k}(\sigma)=[x^{k}]\sum_{A\subseteq[N]}\boldsymbol{1}\{\sigma(A)=A\}x^{|A|}=[x^{k}]\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1+x^{j})^{n_{j}(\sigma)},

(A.7)

and thus by (
8.45
),

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}_{k}(\sigma)=\chi_{k}(\sigma)-\chi_{k-1}(\sigma)=[x^{k}]\biggl((1-x)\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1+x^{j})^{n_{j}(\sigma)}\biggr).

(A.8)

We rewrite (
A.8
) using homogeneous polynomials as

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}_{k}(\sigma)=[x_{1}^{N-k+1}x_{2}^{k}]\biggl((x_{1}-x_{2})\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x_{1}^{j}+x_{2}^{j})^{n_{j}(\sigma)}\biggr),

(A.9)

which agrees with
the Frobenius character formula
[
5
, 4.10 p. 49]

for the character defined by the Young diagram
(N-k,k)

\triangle
