Inverse Reinforcement Learning for Interpretable Keystroke Biomarkers in Parkinson’s Disease
Navin Bondade

Abstract
Keystroke dynamics have been explored extensively as a passive digital biomarker for Parkinson’s disease (PD), typically by extracting summary statistics from typing timing and training a classifier to discriminate PD from healthy controls. We instead apply inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) to keystroke data, modeling each keystroke as a discrete choice over typing speed and recovering, per subject, an interpretable reward function that explains their observed timing behavior. To our knowledge this is the first application of IRL to keystroke dynamics. On the public neuroQWERTY MIT-CSXPD dataset (85 subjects, 42 with PD), an initial four-parameter reward decomposition (speed, effort, smoothness, hand-alternation cost) was found to suffer severe feature collinearity between two terms ( r = 1.000 r=1.000 in typical contexts); we diagnose and correct this, yielding an identifiable three-parameter model. The recovered speed-preference weight correlates with UPDRS-III severity at r = − 0.607 r=-0.607 ( p < 0.001 p<0.001 , n = 42 n=42 ), replicates independently across two sub-cohorts, is stable across nine sensitivity configurations, and retains a statistically significant contribution beyond raw typing speed alone (incremental R 2 R^{2} from 0.194 to 0.338, p = 0.006 p=0.006 ). Two other recovered weights (consistency, hand-alternation) did not survive confound checks and are reported as negative results. We document two implementation bugs found during adversarial code review (session-boundary contamination, a rolling-window data leakage) and show the headline result is materially unchanged after fixing both. We discuss this result in the context of a literature where reported accuracies vary widely between studies (pooled AUC 0.85 0.85 , I 2 = 94 % I^{2}=94\% in a 2022 meta-analysis), and argue that the validation process itself, not only the correlation coefficient, is part of the contribution.



1
Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is typically diagnosed after visible motor symptoms emerge, by which point substantial dopaminergic neuron loss has already occurred
(
Dorsey et al. 2018
)
. This has motivated interest in passive digital biomarkers that could support earlier detection or longitudinal monitoring without requiring a clinic visit. Keystroke dynamics, the timing of key presses and releases during ordinary computer use, is one such candidate, since PD-related bradykinesia and motor rigidity plausibly manifest in fine-grained typing timing
(
Giancardo et al. 2016
)
.

The existing literature on keystroke-based PD detection is large but methodologically uniform: nearly every study extracts a fixed set of summary statistics (means, variances, higher moments) from hold time, flight time, or related signals, and trains a supervised classifier to discriminate PD from control
(
Adams 2017
;
Iakovakis et al. 2018
;
Tripathi et al. 2022
)
. This approach answers
whether
someone’s typing resembles a PD patient’s, but not
why
, the model provides no account of what, behaviorally, has changed.

We take a different approach. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) infers the latent reward function that best explains an agent’s observed sequential decisions, originally developed for behavior modeling in robotics and apprenticeship learning
(
Ng & Russell 2000
;
Ziebart et al. 2008
)
. We treat each keystroke transition as a discrete choice over typing speed, and recover, per subject, the weights of an interpretable reward function (a preference for speed, for rhythmic consistency, and for hand-alternation efficiency) via maximum-entropy IRL. To our knowledge, IRL has not previously been applied to keystroke dynamics. Reinforcement learning has been applied to PD before, but in the reverse direction, to control adaptive deep brain stimulation
(
SEA-DBS 2025
)
, not to infer a behavioral biomarker from passively observed typing.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we formulate keystroke timing as an IRL problem and show an initial natural reward decomposition is statistically unidentifiable, two of four reward terms are correlated at
r=1.000
in typical contexts, and we correct this. Second, we show the recovered speed-preference weight correlates with clinical PD severity (UPDRS-III) robustly across cohorts, hyperparameters, and three independent severity measures, and retains explanatory power beyond raw typing speed. Third, we subject our own pipeline to an adversarial audit, finding and fixing two genuine implementation bugs, and show the headline result is materially unchanged by either fix, evidence against the result being an artifact of either error.

2
Related Work

Giancardo et al. 2016
introduced the neuroQWERTY MIT-CSXPD dataset used in this work, reporting an ensemble support vector regression approach achieving AUC
=0.79
in a clinic-controlled setting.
Adams 2017
reported substantially higher performance (AUC
=0.98
, 96% sensitivity, 97% specificity) on the Tappy dataset using 27 hand-engineered features (including skewness and kurtosis) across separate “Hold” and “Latency” ensemble classifiers, after restricting to participants with at least 2000 keystrokes (reducing their usable cohort to 53 of 103 participants). Subsequent attempts to validate hold-time-based models in more realistic, longer-duration, less controlled settings found substantially lower performance (AUC
=0.77
)
(
Arroyo-Gallego et al. 2017
)
. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis spanning 41 studies and 3,791 PD patients found a pooled AUC of
0.85
(95% CI 0.83–0.88) but with
I^{2}=94\%
, indicating very high between-study heterogeneity
(
Alfalahi et al. 2022
)
, consistent with substantial small-sample optimism in the published literature.

A 2025 cross-dataset benchmarking study
(
Francesconi et al. 2025
)
evaluated eight deep learning architectures across four public keystroke-PD datasets (TyPD, neuroQWERTY MIT-CSXPD, Tappy, and Online English) and found Tappy was consistently the most difficult to model, achieving only 46–71% AUC even with sophisticated windowed CNN/LSTM/transformer architectures, attributed to its uncontrolled, free-text, multi-month home-collection protocol. This is directly consistent with our own preliminary finding (Section
3
) that simple statistical features showed no usable signal on Tappy, motivating our use of the cleaner, clinic-collected neuroQWERTY dataset instead.

None of the prior work surveyed applies inverse reinforcement learning, or any decision-theoretic/preference-recovery framework, to keystroke dynamics. Existing approaches uniformly treat the problem as supervised classification or regression on hand-engineered or learned features.

3
An Initial Negative Result: The Tappy Dataset

Before adopting neuroQWERTY, we conducted an identical descriptive-statistics analysis (four proxy features: speed, effort, smoothness, hand-alternation cost; see Section
5.1
) on the Tappy dataset
(
Goldberger et al. 2000
)
, an 80-subject balanced sample (40 PD, 40 control) drawn from a pool of 227 subjects with usable data. No feature showed a significant group difference (all
p>0.5
), and a logistic regression classifier achieved leave-one-out AUC
=0.377
, below chance. A baseline classifier using conventional hold/flight/latency statistics (mirroring
Adams 2017
’s feature style) achieved AUC
=0.45
–
0.46
, also near chance. We report this as a genuine negative result: it is consistent with Tappy’s documented difficulty in the literature (Section
2
) rather than indicating a flaw in our pipeline, and it directly motivated the dataset switch reported in this paper.

4
Dataset

We use the public neuroQWERTY MIT-CSXPD dataset
(
Giancardo et al. 2016
)
, available via PhysioNet
(
Goldberger et al. 2000
)
. The dataset comprises two sub-cohorts collected at different times: CS1 (
n=31
, two recording sessions per subject) and CS2 (
n=54
, one session per subject), totaling 85 subjects (42 PD, 43 healthy controls), collected in a clinic setting during a free-text typing task. Raw key press and release timestamps are provided (not pre-aggregated statistics). Each PD subject has a same-day UPDRS-III motor severity score, alternating finger-tapping (
afTap
) and single-key tapping (
sTap
) scores, and the original study’s own derived
nqScore
.

Data cleaning followed the dataset’s own reference loader (
nqDataLoader.py
): rows with non-positive or non-monotonic timestamps, or hold times outside
[0,5)
seconds, were removed (3.1% of rows). We additionally removed flight-time outliers exceeding 3 seconds (2.33% of remaining rows), a threshold matching prior published preprocessing of comparable datasets
(
Francesconi et al. 2025
)
.

5
Methods

5.1
Descriptive Proxy Features

As an initial exploratory step, four per-subject descriptive statistics were computed:
speed
(mean flight time),
effort
(mean absolute deviation from a 10-keystroke rolling local average),
smoothness
(standard deviation of consecutive flight-time differences), and
hand-alternation cost
(mean flight-time difference between cross-hand and same-hand transitions, using a standard QWERTY touch-typing hand assignment, since this dataset provides raw key characters rather than pre-labeled hand fields). None of these features separated PD from control as a binary classification, but several correlated significantly with continuous UPDRS-III severity within the PD group (speed:
r=0.585
,
p<0.001
; effort:
r=0.543
,
p<0.001
; smoothness:
r=0.508
,
p=0.001
), motivating the more structured IRL formulation below.

5.2
Inverse Reinforcement Learning Formulation

We model each keystroke transition as a discrete choice over flight time, discretized into
K=5
bins via quintiles of the pooled flight-time distribution across all subjects, with each bin’s representative value taken as the within-bin empirical mean (not the edge-midpoint, which badly overstates typical values under the heavy right-skew of flight time, skewness
\approx 2.7
).

For transition
t
with local context (a 10-keystroke rolling mean and the immediately preceding flight time, both computed using only
prior
keystrokes within the same recording session) and candidate bin
a
with representative value
v_{a}
:

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{speed}}(a)

\displaystyle=-v_{a}

(1)

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{consistency}}(t,a)

\displaystyle=-|v_{a}-c_{t}|,\quad c_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{rolling\_mean}_{t}+\text{prev\_flight}_{t})

(2)

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{hand}}(t,a)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{1}[\text{cross-hand}_{t}]\cdot v_{a}

(3)

The reward is
R(s,a)=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s,a)
and the policy is the maximum-entropy softmax
P(a\mid s)\propto\exp(R(s,a))
. Per-subject weights
\mathbf{w}
are fit by maximizing the log-likelihood of observed bin choices against the unchosen alternatives at each transition, equivalent to the standard MaxEnt IRL gradient (empirical minus expected feature counts), via L-BFGS-B. Gradient correctness was verified against finite-difference numerical gradients (maximum absolute discrepancy
=0
), and convergence was confirmed invariant to initialization across multiple random seeds.

5.3
An Identifiability Problem, Found and Corrected

An initial four-parameter model (separate
\phi_{\text{effort}}
and
\phi_{\text{smoothness}}
terms, following the original conceptual decomposition) was found to have severe feature collinearity: across the five candidate bins in a typical context,
\phi_{\text{effort}}
and
\phi_{\text{smoothness}}
were correlated at
r=1.000
(i.e., numerically identical), because the rolling mean and immediately preceding flight time are usually close to the same value. The model could not distinguish these two reward components; weight was distributed near-arbitrarily between them while their sum remained meaningful (most subjects showed a sign-inconsistent
\phi_{\text{speed}}
weight,
17.6\%
positive, contrary to the expectation that most people prefer faster typing all else equal). We merged the two terms into the single consistency feature in Equation (
2
), yielding the model above, which produces sign-consistent weights for
94
–
95\%
of subjects and resolves the perfect collinearity (residual collinearity between
\phi_{\text{speed}}
and
\phi_{\text{consistency}}
remains at
r=0.946
in typical contexts, discussed in Section
7
).

Negative Results

6.4
Implementation Bugs Found via Adversarial Review

Two genuine bugs were identified through deliberate post-hoc code audit, after an initial validation pass had already concluded the result was robust. (1) Rolling-window calculations did not reset at the session boundary for the 31 two-session CS1 subjects, contaminating context features at the transition between sessions; fixing this left
r
unchanged at
-0.613
. (2) The rolling-mean context feature, computed via
pandas.rolling().mean()
, included the current observation by default, a data leakage error in which the value being modeled partially constituted its own predictive context; fixing this (shifting the series before applying the rolling window) changed
r
from
-0.613
to
-0.607
. We report both bugs and their quantified, materially negligible impact as evidence that the result is not an artifact of either error.

7
Discussion and Limitations

The headline correlation, while robust by the criteria above, should be interpreted as a partial, not complete, contribution beyond simple typing speed: raw typing speed alone explains 19.4% of UPDRS variance, and the IRL-recovered weight raises this to 33.8%, a meaningful but not dominant addition. Residual feature collinearity between the speed and consistency reward terms (
r=0.946
in typical contexts) means the three-parameter model remains imperfectly, though not entirely, identified; the fact that the two weights diverge substantially in their relationship to severity (
r=-0.607
versus
r=0.164
) indicates genuine separable signal survives this collinearity, but full independence between reward components is not claimed. The five action bins were constructed from data pooled across all 85 subjects, including each subject’s own minor contribution to their own bin boundaries (an estimated
\sim
1% influence per subject); this is a mild, disclosed form of data leakage rather than a corrected one. The dataset provides no age or gender fields, precluding direct demographic confound adjustment. Finally, all results are validated within a single dataset (
n=42
PD subjects with severity labels); external replication on an independent cohort is the natural next step and has not yet been performed.

8
Conclusion

We present, to our knowledge, the first application of inverse reinforcement learning to keystroke dynamics, recovering an interpretable per-subject preference for typing speed that correlates robustly with Parkinson’s disease severity across cohorts, modeling choices, and independent clinical instruments, while retaining a statistically significant contribution beyond raw typing speed alone. We document a genuine reward-identifiability failure in our initial formulation and its principled correction, two implementation bugs found via deliberate adversarial code review and their quantified negligible impact, and two reward components that did not survive confound scrutiny. We submit that this validation process, not solely the reported correlation coefficient, constitutes part of the contribution, particularly given a published literature in this area characterized by substantial unexplained between-study heterogeneity (
I^{2}=94\%
) and prior high-profile results that did not replicate at originally reported magnitude.
