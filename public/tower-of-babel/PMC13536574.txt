A three-dimensional reconstruction method for seedlings based on improved DIFIX3D+
Authors: Zibo Yin, Tingting Yang, Suyin Zhou, Xinmei Wu, Junhua Ye, Aijun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of seedlings commonly suffers from under-constrained issues in areas with severe occlusion and insufficient observation, leading to false geometry and rendering artifacts. The existing methods primarily rely on pixel-level consistency constraints, resulting in poor stability when reconstructing seedlings with slender branches and complex structures. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a 3D reconstruction method for seedlings which is based on improved DIFIX3D+. By integrating the Stable Diffusion-VAE module into the DIFIX3D+ framework, this approach enhances the reconstruction constraint mechanism at the representation level. Firstly, high-precision segmentation of seedlings was achieved by eliminating background interference using the Florence-2 semantic priors and the SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2) instance segmentation model. Then, by jointly mapping DIFIX3D+ restored images and their corresponding rendered images into the latent space of Stable Diffusion-VAE, consistency supervision was established within the latent representation domain. It worked in tandem with traditional pixel-level constraints, thereby suppressing the propagation of pixel noise caused by lighting variations, specular reflections, and texture repetitions at the representation level. Experimental results demonstrated that on the self-built dataset, compared to the original 3DGS (3D Gaussian splatting) and DIFIX3D+, the proposed method achieved the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) improvement of 28.99% and 24.25%, respectively, and the structural similarity index measure (SSIM) improvement of 3.16% and 3.74%, while reducing the learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) by 9.24% and 41.94%. The average PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS values reached 31.54 dB, 0.9759, and 0.054, respectively, demonstrating significantly superior reconstruction accuracy compared to mainstream models such as Nerfacto, Plenoxels, and Mip-Splatting. This method combines high visual fidelity with excellent geometric structure representation capabilities, providing a technical reference for high-quality, low-cost 3D reconstruction of seedlings in complex scenes.Keywords: Seedlings, 3D reconstruction, 3DGS (3D Gaussian splatting), Image segmentation

Background

Seedling is the fundamental unit of forest cultivation, landscaping, and fruit tree production, its growth status and structural traits determine forest quality and production benefits. Therefore, achieving high-precision digital representation of seedlings holds significant practical importance. 3D reconstruction of seedlings, as a key technology bridging physical plants and digital space, has become central to high-throughput phenotyping and biomass estimation [1, 2]. Compared to manual measurements that yield only discrete metrics, 3D reconstruction systematically captures seedling branch-leaf distribution, spatial topology, and dynamic growth characteristics—better aligning with modern breeding and precision cultivation demands for scalable phenotypic data [3–5].

Driven by computer vision and 3D perception technologies, methods for acquiring the 3D structure of plants—including seedlings—have undergone continuous evolution from active sensing to passive visual modeling. Active visual reconstruction methods achieve direct perception of spatial distances and 3D structures by projecting controlled light sources onto target scenes and measuring their return signals. Typical implementations include Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) and RGB-D cameras [6]. In contrast, passive visual methods, centered on Structure from Motion (SfM) [7] and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) [8], achieve sparse-to-dense 3D reconstruction through feature matching and camera pose estimation across multi-view images. This approach typically requires only RGB cameras for data acquisition.

For active reconstruction methods, early research primarily relied on TLS to acquire high-density 3D point clouds. Due to its millimeter-level ranging accuracy and strong canopy penetration capability, TLS is widely regarded as the “gold standard” for extracting forest structural parameters [9], enabling efficient and automated acquisition of detailed structural information from individual trees to entire stands [10]. However, TLS is susceptible to factors like wind disturbance and registration errors. It also suffers from high equipment costs, reliance on expert knowledge for processing, time-consuming individual tree segmentation, and the absence of a unified point cloud quality evaluation system [11]. With advances in consumer electronics, RGB-D cameras based on structured light or time-of-flight (e.g., RealSense, Azure Kinect) have emerged as valuable tools for capturing near-field phenotypes in controlled environments due to their portability and multi-modal synchronous capabilities. Kok et al. achieved 3D segmentation and reconstruction of obscured branches using RealSense D435i, reporting a cumulative reconstruction accuracy of 0.72, defined as the ratio of the total number of correctly recovered branches to the total number of actual branches across all samples [12]. Yang et al. performed 3D reconstruction of seedlings using Kinect and a precision turntable, achieving a height measurement R² of 0.991 [13]. However, RGB-D cameras remain constrained by physical limitations in outdoor applications. Their depth measurement accuracy significantly degrades under strong light conditions, and the effective detection range is limited [14].

Compared to active reconstruction methods, passive visual approaches have garnered significant attention in plant 3D reconstruction due to their low equipment costs and high deployment flexibility. Wang et al. achieved 3D reconstruction of holly seedlings using images captured by a stereo camera combined with SfM-MVS technology, achieving R² exceeding 0.9 for both plant height and crown width [15]. Kochi et al. developed an enhanced SfM-MVS system supporting multi-plant and multi-scale modeling [16]. However, the complex topology, low-texture fine details, and significant occlusion by branches and leaves of plants pose challenges for such passive visual methods in achieving precise geometric reconstruction, cross-view consistency, and overall morphological modeling.

To overcome the inherent limitations of traditional multi-view stereo vision in geometric representation and cross-view consistency, implicit neural representation techniques—represented by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [17]—have reshaped 3D vision research in recent years. NeRF models scenes as continuous volumetric density and radiance fields through multi-layer perceptrons, enabling photo-realistic new viewpoint synthesis via volumetric rendering. This effectively mitigates the degradation of SfM-MVS in weakly textured regions. Qiao et al. combined Nerfacto with lightweight segmentation networks to achieve organ-level reconstruction of maize plants [18]; Wu et al. proposed OB-NeRF, reducing single-plant citrus seedling reconstruction time from 10 h to 30 s while outperforming COLMAP and Kinect in accuracy [19]. However, NeRF training and inference remain relatively slow, limiting high-throughput applications [20]. It also exhibits sensitivity to unbounded backgrounds, prone to floating artifacts when foreground masks are absent [2].

To enhance rendering efficiency and detail representation, Kerbl et al. proposed the 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) reconstruction method, which explicitly represents and models scenes through differentiable anisotropic 3D Gaussian distributions [21]. Wang et al. proposed P3DFusion for high-precision cross-scene plant reconstruction, achieving R² values exceeding 0.93 for measured phenotypic parameters like plant height [22]. Ojo’s Splanting method enhanced modeling capabilities for slender plant structures [23]. Hao achieved high-quality 3D reconstruction of small seedlings based on 3DGS, outperforming NeRF [24]. Although these studies validate the potential of 3DGS for plant 3D modeling, its core still relies on pixel-level consistency constraints under limited viewpoints, exhibiting significant under-constraint issues in obscured and under-observed regions. Therefore, when applied to seedlings—objects characterized by complex structures, slender branches and leaves, and dense occlusion, the original 3DGS is prone to false geometry, local missing parts, blurred textures, and rendering artifacts, while struggling to effectively eliminate background interference.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes a 3D reconstruction method for seedlings based on improved DIFIX3D+ [25]. DIFIX3D+ incorporates a single-step diffusion model to apply large-scale 2D generative priors for artifact repair. Through a progressive optimization workflow of “render-repair-distill”, it feeds back into the 3D representation. This approach effectively mitigates false geometry and rendering artifacts in occluded and under-observed regions of seedlings without altering the explicit modeling and efficient rendering mechanisms of 3DGS. However, this model’s constraints primarily operate in the pixel space, relying on pixel-level consistency to indirectly regulate the 3D representation. For reconstructing seedlings with slender branches and leaves and complex topologies, it remains challenging to effectively constrain the stability of the 3D structure. Therefore, this paper introduces the Stable Diffusion-VAE [26] module into the DIFIX3D+ framework, mapping multi-view images from high-dimensional pixel space to a latent space constrained by probabilistic distributions for modeling. Compared to pixel-level constraints, latent space representations leverage the global distribution prior learned by generative models to holistically constrain the reconstruction outcomes at the feature representation level. This effectively mitigates noise amplification caused by factors such as lighting variations, specular reflections, and texture repetitions in the pixel space. Consequently, 3D reconstruction evolves from isolated pixel-by-pixel matching to a synergistic optimization of pixel constraints and latent space statistical constraints. This approach stabilizes the geometric optimization process for reconstructing seedlings with slender branches, dense occlusion, and insufficient observation, significantly enhancing cross-view structural consistency.

Methods

Data acquisition

We selected eight common seedling species with significant morphological differences as research subjects. They encompassed diverse leaf shape characteristics, branch scales, and canopy complexity, with heights ranging from 40 cm to 70 cm. The species include Yulania sinostellata, Litsea pungens, Phoebe bournei, Daphne odora, Camellia japonica, Phoebe chekiangensis, Cinnamomum japonicum and Lagerstroemia indica. Ten specimens were selected from each species, totaling 80 specimens.

To comprehensively capture the structural characteristics of seedlings and ensure the parallax and angular variations required for multi-view reconstruction, the rear camera of an iPhone 14 was used to perform omnidirectional video recording of each seedling from multiple angles. Specifically, the camera completed 360° surround capture around the seedlings at approximately 15°, 45°, and 75° depression angles, ensuring the acquisition of complete structural information from low, medium, to high viewing angles. The data were collected under natural illumination conditions with minimal wind to reduce plant motion. The data acquisition method is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Diagram of data acquisition. a Yulania sinostellata, b Litsea pungens, c Phoebe bournei, d Daphne odora, e Camellia japonica, f Phoebe chekiangensis, g Cinnamomum japonicum, and h Lagerstroemia indica

After data acquisition, FFmpeg (Fast Forward Moving Picture Experts Group) was used to extract frames from each sample’s three video segments. Frame extraction intervals were controlled based on frame rate and video duration, ultimately yielding approximately 100 static images per seedling. Each perspective contributed about 30 images, ensuring balanced data distribution and sufficient coverage of parallax information. The selection of 100 images per seedling as reconstruction input is not an arbitrary choice. It is based on findings by Jiang [27] in their cotton plant 3D reconstruction experiments, which showed that the optimal number of training images for a single plant is closely related to its geometric scale. For typical plant sizes, exceeding 100 images yields limited improvements in reconstruction quality while significantly increasing computational time. Consequently, we similarly employ approximately 100 images as input to balance data quality and computational efficiency.

Data preprocessing

To obtain high-quality foreground masks suitable for 3D reconstruction of seedlings, we developed an automated instance segmentation method based on the collaborative operation of visual language models and promptable segmenters (Fig. 2). The entire approach employs a two-stage strategy. First, Florence-2 [28] detects seedling regions within images by using the manually defined prompt words “plant” and “pot” to distinguish the seedling from the container, thereby generating the corresponding seedling prompt boxes. These prompt boxes are then fed into the SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2) segmentation model [29], leveraging its promptable segmentation capability to generate pixel-level instance masks. Florence-2 and SAM2 utilize their official pre-trained weights (Florence-2-large and sam2_hiera_large, respectively), both operating in zero-shot mode without requiring additional annotation or training. By leveraging the cross-category semantic understanding capabilities of visual language models, this method yields segmentation regions with closed structures, continuous boundaries, and high stability, thereby providing reliable masks for subsequent seedling reconstruction.

Overall data preprocessing workflow

After seedling detection and instance segmentation, we employed COLMAP [30] to perform sparse reconstruction and camera pose estimation on multi-view images, thereby further restoring their true 3D structure. COLMAP’s SfM algorithm automatically solves for camera extrinsic parameters and generates a preliminary point cloud from multi-view images without requiring depth sensors or prior extrinsic parameter. This provides stable and reliable structural initialization and cross-view transformation parameter support for subsequent 3D Gaussian representation.

Three-dimensional reconstruction of seedlings

Method overview and DIFIX3D+ backbone

Seedlings typically exhibit structural characteristics such as slender branches, dense foliage, and severe occlusion. Under limited-viewpoint acquisition conditions, traditional multi-view consistency-based 3D reconstruction methods suffer from significant under-constraint issues in occluded and under-observed regions, often resulting in false geometry, structural discontinuities, and rendering artifacts. To mitigate these issues, DIFIX3D+ introduces a single-step diffusion model and leverages large-scale 2D generation priors for artifact repair in rendering results. Nevertheless, DIFIX3D+ constraints primarily operate in the pixel space, relying on pixel-level consistency to regularize the 3D representation. For seedlings with slender branches, complex topologies, and dense occlusions, this pixel-level constraint mechanism is susceptible to interference from lighting variations, specular reflections, and texture repetitions, making it challenging to provide stable and sufficient structural constraints for 3D geometry optimization. To address these issues, we construct a 3D reconstruction method that integrates Stable Diffusion-VAE with DIFIX3D+. Its core innovation lies in preserving the original pixel-space rendering supervision while introducing the latent space encoding capability into the DIFIX3D+ reconstruction framework. This enables synergistic interaction between pixel-level constraints and latent space regularization. Leveraging the statistical structural constraints provided by the latent space, the model demonstrates enhanced stability when handling detailed textures of seedlings, cross-view lighting variations, and local high-frequency changes, thereby improving the overall reconstructability of 3D geometry and texture consistency. The overall pipeline of the proposed method is illustrated in Fig. 3, which consists of a DIFIX3D+-based reconstruction backbone, a latent-space enhancement module using sd-vae-ft-mse, and a joint optimization strategy integrating pixel- and latent-level constraints.

However, the constraints of DIFIX3D+ primarily operate in the pixel space, relying on local appearance consistency to indirectly regulate the 3D representation. When it is applied to reconstructing seedlings with slender branches, complex topology, and dense occlusions, pixel-level errors are significantly disrupted by lighting variations, specular reflections, and texture repetitions. This leads to gradient fluctuations and weakens the constraint on true geometric structures, thereby limiting the stability of 3D geometric optimization for complex seedling structures.

Latent space enhancement with sd-vae-ft-mse

To address this issue, we introduce Stable Diffusion-VAE encoder (sd-vae-ft-mse), incorporating latent space encoding process into rendering supervision. This allows reconstruction optimization to be further constrained by the regularization of the latent variable space, which possesses a more stable statistical structure, building upon pixel-space supervision. The AutoencoderKL architecture employed by Stable Diffusion is fundamentally a variational autoencoder with Gaussian prior. Its internal encoder maps input images into a parameterized form of latent variable distributions. By learning the posterior distribution and imposing KL regularization constraints using the standard normal distribution as the prior, it forms a smooth, continuous, and distribution-consistent representation structure in the latent space.

The model sd-vae-ft-mse is a publicly available fine-tuned variant of the original kl-f8 VAE from Stable Diffusion. It is obtained by fine-tuning the decoder component on a mixed dataset consisting of LAION-Aesthetics and LAION-Humans (1:1 ratio), while keeping the encoder fixed. The training objective adopts a reconstruction loss combining mean squared error (MSE) and LPIPS (MSE + 0.1*LPIPS), with an emphasis on improving pixel-level fidelity and perceptual consistency. Compared to the original kl-f8 VAE, this variant produces smoother and more stable reconstructions, effectively suppressing high-frequency noise in the latent space and enhancing the consistency of latent representations. In our framework, these properties facilitate more reliable alignment of multi-view inputs in latent space, thereby providing a stable representation for subsequent 3D Gaussian-based reconstruction.

From a reconstruction perspective, emphasizing MSE in the VAE objective is more suitable for 3D optimization tasks. Unlike perceptual losses (e.g., LPIPS), which focus on high-level feature similarity and may tolerate local structural inconsistencies, pixel-wise MSE enforces stricter spatial alignment and intensity consistency. This leads to more deterministic and geometrically faithful reconstructions, which are crucial for multi-view settings where small inconsistencies can accumulate and destabilize camera pose estimation and 3D Gaussian optimization. Therefore, the smoother and more regular latent space induced by MSE-dominant training provides more reliable supervision signals for cross-view consistency, ultimately improving the stability and accuracy of 3D reconstruction.

The sd-vae-ft-mse adopts an encoder-decoder architecture, consisting of four DownEncoderBlock2D and four UpDecoderBlock2D stacked sequentially, with its network structure shown in Fig. 4. Input images enter the encoder at a resolution of 3 × 256 × 256. Spatial scales are progressively compressed by a factor of 2 within each downsampling block, while the number of channels sequentially expands to 128, 256, 512, and 512 to enhance modeling capabilities for high-level semantic and textural structures. Each encoding block incorporates two layers of 3 × 3 convolutions, 32 GroupNorms, and SiLU activation function. This design stabilizes feature distribution across scenes and small batches while maintaining smooth gradient propagation in complex texture regions such as fine branches and thin leaves.

Results

Experimental environment

All experiments were conducted on an Ubuntu 25.04 system environment. The computational platform utilized an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 Ti graphics card (24 GB) accelerated by CUDA 12.9. The experimental framework was built using Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.7.1, providing stable support for the entire workflow encompassing model training, inference, and 3D reconstruction.

Comparison of reconstruction quality across different models

To validate the performance advantages of the proposed 3D reconstruction model of seedlings, we selected the original images as a reference and introduced several representative reconstruction methods—Nerfacto [31], Plenoxels [32], 3DGS, Mip-Splatting [33], and DIFIX3D + as comparative baselines. We systematically evaluated the reconstruction performance of each model under identical data conditions. The results are shown in Fig. 6. In Fig. 6, the red boxes highlight areas for detailed comparison; the blue boxes indicate poor reconstruction cases, such as excessively sharp leaf tips, reconstruction artifacts, and blurred confusion at branch-leaf overlaps; and the yellow boxes denote omissions.

Comparison of reconstruction results from different models. a Yulania sinostellata, bb Litsea pungens, c Phoebe bournei, d Daphne odora, e Camellia japonica, f Phoebe chekiangensis, g Cinnamomum japonicum, and h Lagerstroemia indica

As shown in Fig. 6, Nerfacto and Plenoxels exhibit common issues such as blurred details, structural loss, and local incompleteness in complex seedlings featuring overlapping branches and leaves, specular reflections, and intricate textures. These problems are particularly pronounced in the overlapping foliage regions of morphologically complex seedlings like Phoebe bournei, Camellia japonica, and Lagerstroemia indica. The core reason lies in the inherent limitations of these NeRF-based implicit volume rendering methods, which rely on continuous field representations and voxel networks. They struggle to accurately capture small-scale texture variations and subtle geometric structures, making it difficult to accurately reconstruct the intricate topologies of fine branches, leaf edges, and occluded regions. In contrast, 3DGS employs explicit, differentiable 3D Gaussian modeling, demonstrating significant advantages in overall structural clarity, fine-branch geometric restoration, and rendering efficiency. However, this method remains prone to excessive smoothing in highly reflective areas and fine textures. It may also suffer from background residue interference in complex or layered scenes, leading to local texture distortion or blending with the background. To address these limitations, our model demonstrates significant advantages in seedling reconstruction by incorporating DIFIX’s one-step diffusion artifact repair mechanism and VAE’s prior correction in the latent space. Regarding detail and appearance fidelity, using Yulania sinostellata as an example, high-frequency textures like leaf insect spots appear blurred or smoothed to varying degrees in most methods, whereas our approach preserves fine structures more completely. Additionally, in highly reflective scenes under natural lighting (e.g., Cinnamomum japonicum and Camellia japonica), our method demonstrates more stable recovery of both brightness and morphology in leaf specular highlights. It avoids the weight imbalance in highlight regions observed in 3DGS and the insufficient modeling of specular reflections in Mip-Splatting, resulting in more consistent texture continuity and realism.

The complexity of seedling structures further validates this method’s geometric reconstruction capabilities. The Phoebe bournei and Lagerstroemia indica exhibit highly overlapping and densely layered branch-leaf structures, prone to background residue and leaf adhesion. The original 3DGS and Mip-Splatting methods exhibit structural disorganization and leaf intersection issues in such scenarios, primarily due to their reliance on color consistency optimization, which tends to converge to erroneous Gaussian positions in overlapping regions. Implicit representations like NeRF methods also produce blurred boundaries at complex edges, struggling to provide clear geometric demarcations. In contrast, our model leverages DIFIX’s 2D generative prior to enhance rendering quality in overlapping foliage regions. Combining with VAE latent space constraints to improve cross-view consistency, this stabilizes Gaussian distributions, enabling clearer topological separation and geometric detail in complex foliage areas.

Additionally, we observed typical defects in certain models under specific conditions. For instance, the leaf tips of the Litsea pungens exhibited abnormally sharp “spike” artifacts in 3DGS, reflecting the unstable generation of Gaussian voxels during the late convergence phase. Both Mip-Splatting and 3DGS showed significant omissions in soil regions, indicating unreliable reconstruction in low-texture areas. The thin leaves of the Phoebe chekiangensis appear blurred in most methods, further indicating that both implicit rendering and explicit Gaussian-based approaches struggle to maintain sufficient detail clarity when handling thin-leaf structures. In contrast, our model consistently delivers stable performance across these scenarios, further demonstrating its robust advantages in handling complex boundaries and low-texture regions.

Conclusions

This paper proposes a 3D reconstruction method for seedlings based on an improved DIFIX3D+ framework, establishing a complete workflow from data processing to high-fidelity modeling. It specifically addresses key challenges in seedling reconstruction—such as leaf margin noise, false geometry, and blurring artifacts—that arise from their dense textures, slender branches, and complex occlusion hierarchies. By incorporating the 2D generative prior mechanism from DIFIX3D+, this method effectively mitigates the under-constrained issues in shaded and under-observed regions during seedling reconstruction. Further integration of Stable Diffusion-VAE maps multi-view images into a latent space constrained by a probability distribution, enabling joint supervision with pixel-space constraints. This approach enhances the holistic regularization capability of the 3D optimization process at the representation level.

In the seedling segmentation process, the zero-shot segmentation model constructed in this paper combines the semantic prior of Florence-2 with the instance-level segmentation capability of SAM2. This approach achieves stable and high-precision foreground masks across eight seedling categories without requiring manual annotation. Segmentation accuracy exceeded 0.999 for most categories, with the remaining categories maintaining values above 0.95. This approach reduced the time required for manual annotation from hours to minutes, significantly shortening segmentation preparation time and providing a technical foundation for rapid, high-quality 3D reconstruction of seedlings.

In the 3D reconstruction phase, our method significantly outperforms common approaches such as Nerfacto, Plenoxels, Mip-Splatting, as well as the original 3DGS and DIFIX3D + in key metrics including PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. The average PSNR across eight data categories reached 31.54 dB, markedly exceeding the typical sub-25 dB performance of traditional NeRF-based models. The average SSIM achieved 0.9759, demonstrating superior structural consistency. The average LPIPS decreased to 0.054, indicating perceptual quality closer to real images. Compared to the original 3DGS and DIFIX3D+, the average PSNR improved by 28.99% and 24.25%, respectively; the average SSIM increased by 3.16% and 3.74%, respectively; and the average LPIPS decreased by 9.24% and 41.94%, respectively. The average reconstruction time was approximately 6 min. Leveraging improved VAE feature constraints and optimized Gaussian field representation, our method demonstrates excellent geometric continuity and stability in reconstructing leaf edges, fine branch structures, and occluded regions, effectively overcoming the degradation issues of traditional methods in environments with weak textures and high occlusion.

Despite these advantages, the applicability of the proposed method has certain boundaries that should be clarified. The effectiveness of the VAE-based latent-space consistency constraint relies on sufficiently consistent multi-view observations. For larger trees or specimens with significantly denser canopies, increased self-occlusion and structural complexity may lead to incomplete cross-view correspondences, thereby weakening the reliability of latent-space supervision and potentially introducing local geometric inconsistencies. In such cases, although the current framework may require more structured acquisition strategies, its modular design also allows for future extensions, such as multi-resolution acquisition and hierarchical reconstruction schemes, to better handle complex plant structures.

In addition, the proposed method exhibits higher VRAM consumption (18.45 GB) compared to 3DGS and Mip-Splatting, primarily due to the VAE-based latent-space processing of multi-view images during optimization. This increased memory demand may limit its direct applicability on low-end GPUs or large-scale high-throughput phenotyping platforms. To address this issue, several lightweight optimization strategies can be considered, including reducing input image resolution, adopting sparse-view or patch-based processing, applying mixed-precision computation, and pruning the VAE decoder. These approaches can help balance reconstruction accuracy and computational efficiency while preserving the benefits of latent-space regularization.
