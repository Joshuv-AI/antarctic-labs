Piezo1: Molecular mechanisms and therapeutic potential in ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and lumbar spinal stenosis
Authors: Xue Chen, Shuhan Yang, Chen Zhu, Xiaojun Yu, Yingguang Wang, Ye Tian, Tuanjiang Liu, Shanxi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLigamentum flavum hypertrophy (LFH) is a major cause of lumbar spinal stenosis, which can lead to progressive lower back pain and impaired nerve function, resulting in a significant global disease burden. At present, the pathological mechanism of LFH has not been fully elucidated, and effective treatment methods are lacking. Piezo1 is a channel activated by mechanical stress. It is activated under mechanical stress and participates in various physiological and pathological processes related to oxidative stress, including inflammation, fibrosis, ferroptosis, vascular remodeling and pyrolysis. However, its role in the development of LFH is still unclear. In this review, we examined the role of Piezo1 in oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, ferroptosis, vascular remodeling, cell pyroptosis, as well as its potential connection with LFH. These findings provide new ideas for the treatment of the LFH.Keywords: Ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, Lumbar spinal stenosis, Piezo1, Mechanosensitive ion channel, Oxidative stress, Ferroptosis

Introduction

Low back pain is a complex and pervasive health problem that is increasingly becoming a global disease burden [1]. Most people experience low back pain once or more in their lifetime, and these people make up about 70% of the global population [2]. As leading cause of low back pain, spinal canal stenosis has been paid more and more attention [3]. Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is characterized by degenerative changes of structures including the ligamentum flavum (LF), intervertebral discs, joint capsules, and joint osteophytes, leading to a reduction in the volume of the spinal canal and compression of nerve tissue, resulting in dysfunction [2]. Mild cases may be asymptomatic, and severe cases may cause a series of uncomfortable symptoms such as gait change, nerve root pain, and neurogenic claudication [4]. Among many factors, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy (LFH) is highly correlated with the pathogenesis of LSS. The main characteristic of LFH is the change of fiber arrangement, which increases the remodeling of extracellular matrix (ECM), and finally leads to the obvious increase of LF tissue thickness and ligament fibrosis [5].

Mechanosensitive ion channels are capable of transducing physical stimuli from the cell's surrounding environment, achieving the transformation of mechanical signals to biological signals, and the activation of intracellular signaling pathways, leading to a series of cellular responses [6]. Piezo1 has been shown to be inherently mechanically sensitive, responding directly to physical forces when it recombines into membrane vesicles or lipid bilayer [7], thus delivering mechanical stimuli in countless different physiological processes [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. It mainly involves lymphatic vessels, bones, immune cells, and cardiovascular system [17]. Compared with other mechanosensitive ion channels, such as TRPV4 and other members of the TRP family, Piezo1 acts upstream of signaling cascades. It is the primary sensor that directly detects alterations in mechanical stress and subsequently activates downstream signaling pathways. Activation of the TRPV4 and TRP family partially relies on Piezo1 stimulation [18,19]. In recent years, Piezo1 has gained widespread attention as a potential therapeutic target for a number of diseases, including the musculoskeletal system [20].

Although the research on LFH has been involved in recent years, the potential mechanism of LFH is not perfect at present, and the treatment methods are still limited, so it is of great significance to explore new mechanisms and treatment methods. Mechanical stress overload induces various pathological processes by stimulating the expression of Piezo1 [21], and the occurrence of LFH is closely related to mechanical stress [[22], [23], [24]]. Yoshida et al. found that mechanical stress increases the synthesis of type II collagen in the dorsal fibrocartilage of the LF, thereby inducing LFH [23]. Okada et al. demonstrated that mechanical stress elevates the number of fibrocartilage cells in the LF, exacerbating the severity of LFH and even leading to ossification of the LF [25]. In addition, Fukuyama et al. reported that mechanical stress promotes the accumulation of total collagen in the LF, which serves as a pivotal contributor to LFH [24]. In vitro cellular experiments have shown that mechanical stress promotes the synthesis and secretion of TGF-β1 in LF cells, thereby enhancing collagen production in these cells [26,27]. In vivo animal experiments also confirmed that mechanical stress acts as an independent factor leading to LFH [22,28]. Although these studies have shown that mechanical stress can induce LFH, and mechanosensitive ion channels may play an a crucial role in the development of LFH, thus the specific mechanism is still elucidated. In the following sections, we will focus on studying the role of the Piezo1 signaling pathway in various pathological processes and its potential connection with LFH. In addition, we will discuss modulating Piezo1 signaling pathway as a potential therapeutic strategy associated with LFH.

Piezo1

To our knowledge, the piezoelectric channel family currently includes Piezo1 and Piezo2 [29]. The activation of Piezo1 channels depends on mechanical forces that deform the cell membrane, which are converted into electrical signals, and then the signaling is emitted through the influx of calcium ions, triggering a chemical signal within the cell [20,30]. The Piezo1 protein as a mechanoreceptor is widely expressed in a variety of organs and tissues [31]. In the skeletal muscle tissue, Piezo1 is responsible for maintaining muscle strength, regulating cell apoptosis and aging, and sensing stretching [32]. Furthermore, studies have shown that it is expressed in almost all organs, including the urinary, muscular-skeletal, cardiovascular, nervous, and respiratory systems. The role of Piezo1 in these tissues is mainly to sense mechanical stimuli, maintain homeostasis, promote cell migration, differentiation, aging, apoptosis, and proliferation [32]. In addition, Piezo1 also plays a significant role in some pathological processes related to oxidative stress, including inflammations, fibrosis, ferroptosis, vascular remodeling, and pyroptosis [[33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Given that these oxidative stress-related pathological processes are also involved in the development of LFH, we hypothesize that there is a potential correlation between the Piezo1 and LFH. Piezo1 may have therapeutic potential in the treatment of LFH.

Potential connection between Piezo1 and LFH

Mechanical stress, as one of the main factors of LFH, causes microdamage, leading to oxidative stress-mediated chronic inflammation and eventually fibrosis [40,41]. Previous research have confirmed at multiple levels, including cellular and animal models, that mechanical stress is an independent factor for LFH [22,24,[26], [27], [28]]. Finite element analysis also confirms a significant positive correlation between the increase of mechanical stress and the occurrence of LFH [42].

Piezo1 play a key role in a variety of biological processes regulated by mechanoconductive signaling pathways. Mechanical stress can upregulates the expression of Piezo1, and the upregulated expression of Piezo1 can further increase the expression of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and trigger various oxidative stress responses involved in the complex fibrotic process, including inflammation, fibrosis, ferroptosis, and vascular remodeling [[36], [37], [38],43,44]. There is growing evidence have confirmed that Piezo1 is closely related to liver fibrosis [45], lung fibrosis [46], myocardial fibrosis [47], and kidney fibrosis and other diseases [48]. At the same time, Tong et al. found that the expression of Piezo1 and calcium ion concentration in human-derived LF cells was significantly increased under mechanical stress, indicating that Piezo1 may play a key regulatory role in the development of LFH [49]. Given these findings, in this review, for the first time, we investigate the potential therapeutic effect of Piezo1 on LFH (Fig. 1).

The potential role of Piezo1 in ligamentum flavum hypertrophy (LFH). Mechanical stress induces Piezo1 activation, leading to a series of pathological changes in the ligamentum flavum, such as inflammation, fibrosis, ferroptosis, vascular remodeling, and pyrolysis, resulting in LFH, which in turn leads to lumbar spinal stenosis.

Piezo1 is involved in various pathological progression

Piezo1 is involved in oxidative stress

The imbalance between the cell antioxidant capacity and ROS will lead to molecular damage and/or disruption of redox signal transduction, which is known as “oxidative stress” [50]. Many research indicated that oxidative stress is related to physiological aging and the pathogenesis of various fibrosis related diseases, including pulmonary fibrosis, liver fibrosis, myocardial fibrosis, renal fibrosis, and so on [43,[51], [52], [53], [54]]. The development of LFH is also associated with the oxidative stress-related fibrosis, previous literature showed that a significant increase in ROS was observed in the enlarged ligament cells, while the levels of antioxidants such as glutathione (GSH) were significantly reduced [[55], [56], [57]]. Moreover, it was confirmed that under the influence of oxidative stress, the expression of fibrosis and inflammation markers significantly increased [58]. During the progression of LFH, the activation of signaling pathways caused by oxidative stress and the elevated expression of inflammatory and fibrotic-related molecules can be counteracted by the antioxidant NAC. This indicates that inhibiting oxidative stress may improve LFH [59].

Piezo1 acts as a core mechanosensitive ion channel and plays a crucial regulatory role in oxidative stress responses, which is prominently activated by excessive mechanical loading and inflammatory stimuli, resulting in robust extracellular Ca2+ influx and subsequent cytoplasmic Ca2+ overload [60]. The elevated intracellular Ca2+ then acts as a critical upstream trigger for ROS production by stimulating NADPH oxidase (NOX) activity, and then lead to the generation of superoxide anion radicals (O2•-), which are rapidly converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) via superoxide dismutase (SOD) [[61], [62], [63]]. H2O2 further participates in Fe2+-dependent Fenton reactions to generate hydroperoxyl free radicals (HO2•) and hydroxyl free radicals (HO•). These ROS production can induce lipid peroxidation (LPO), ultimately establishing severe oxidative stress [64,65]. In addition, the sustained elevation of intracellular Ca2+ subsequently activates downstream calcium-dependent signaling molecules, including CaMKII and calcineurin, which, on one hand, collectively promote mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) through the cAMP/PKA pathway and, on the other hand, triggers DRP1-mediated mitochondrial division via the ERK1/2 pathway, resulting in impaired respiratory chain integrity and substantial electron leakage, ultimately driving the massive generation of ROS production and causing cell oxidative stress [[66], [67], [68], [69], [70]]. Xu et al. observed that the oxidative stress levels in mice lacking the specific Piezo1 protein were suppressed [33,34]. Another research conducted by Luo et al. has confirmed that lithospermic acid (LA) prevented oxidative stress and alleviated liver fibrosis in mice by inhibiting Piezo1 and its downstream pathways [71]. In the study by Peng et al., inhibiting the expression of Piezo1 can reduce the level of oxidative stress, thereby influencing the progression of intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) [43]. Furthermore, the ferroptosis mediated by Piezo1 also to some extent exacerbates oxidative stress [72]. Wang et al. also found that the inhibition of Piezo1 can effectively alleviate oxidative stress, thereby reducing the aging and apoptosis of nucleus pulposus cells [73]. Therefore, inhibiting Piezo1 is likely to achieve the goal of treating LFH by alleviating oxidative stress and improving the LFH (Fig. 2).

Schematic diagram of Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx triggers and promotes ROS production. On one hand, the Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx stimulate NADPH oxidase (NOX) activity, and then lead to the generation of ROS. On the other hand, the Piezo1 promote mitochondrial fission and oxidative phosphorylation through Piezo1-Ca2+-CaMKII/calcineurin-ERK1/2-DRP1 and Piezo1-Ca2+-cAMP/PKA pathways, leading to the production of ROS. These ROS ultimately lead to oxidative stress damage such as inflammation, fibrosis, ferroptosis, vascular remodeling, and pyroptosis in cells.

Inhibiting Piezo1 can alleviate the inflammatory response

Inflammatory response plays an important role in the progression of LFH [[74], [75], [76]]. Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) derived from macrophages stimulated the production of type I collagen and type III collagen, greatly promoting LF fibrosis [77]. Interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and IL-6 can effectively induce LFH through the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway [78]. Piezo1 is highly expressed in macrophages and controls macrophage activity through mechanical cues, thereby promoting the expression of inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-6, IL-8, and IL-1β [79,80]. Oxidative stress is closely related to inflammation. The mechanism by which oxidative stress leads to inflammation has been well documented. Under oxidative stress, the elevated ROS can activate various transcription factors, including NF-κB, STAT3, AP-1, and others, thereby triggering an inflammatory response (Table 1) [[81], [82], [83], [84], [85], [86], [87], [88], [89]]. The loss of Piezo1 also led to reduced polarization of macrophages towards M1, which is seen as a pro-inflammatory phenotype [90]. Macrophages and monocytes enhance proinflammatory gene expression in response to the mechanical stress of Piezo1 [91,92]. Macrophages lacking Piezo1 were observed to have a reduced inflammatory response and stronger wound healing response [93].

Piezo1 promotes inflammation in endothelial cells through the assembly of integrins, and specifically knocking out Piezo1 reduces the activity of both inflammatory cytokines and integrins [35]. The study showed that Piezo1 promotes endothelial inflammatory response through blood flow patterns and integrin activation. Sensing the fluid shear stress generated by disturbed blood flow, Piezo1 induces the activation of integrin in a Gq/G11-dependent manner, which in turn activates NF-κB signaling pathway and promotes inflammatory response [35].Wang et al. revealed a mechanism of pulmonary fibrosis. Under mechanical stress and shear stress, Piezo1 is significantly activated in pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAEC), leading to an increase in intracellular calcium influx and upregulation of North ligand Jagged-1 (Jag-1) expression, which ultimately activates the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. And the binding of Jag-1 and North realizes the transformation of endothelial cells into mesenchymal and the transformation of PAEC into myofibroblasts [94]. In the absence of Piezo1, loss of expression of chemokines and inflammatory factors was also observed [79]. Aykut et al. confirmed this viewpoint in a mouse experiment, in which the absence of Piezo1 alleviates the inflammatory damage associated with pulmonary fibrosis [95]. An experimental models conducted by He et al. confirmed that the loss of Piezo1 inhibits epithelial interstitial transformation and renal fibrosis by inhibiting macrophage inflammation. The mechanism is that Piezo1 promotes inflammation through calcium-dependent cysteine protease, and inhibition of calpain apparently blocks the pro-inflammatory effects of Piezo1. At the same time, the loss of Piezo1 reduces the aggregation of macrophages and prevents fibrosis through the Notch signaling pathway and the CCL2-CCR2 signaling pathway [96]. Moreover, in Luo et al. 's study, Piezo1 activation increases Cathepsin S (CTSS) activity and expression via Ca2+ inflow in a mouse model of liver fibrosis, thereby promoting inflammation dependent on the Piezo1/calpain/LAMP axis [90].

However, a study of the role of Piezo1 in fat cells showed that TNF-α and monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1) decreased expression when Piezo1 was activated, and that blocking of Piezo1 increased the expression of MCP-1, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [44]. This suggests that Piezo1 acts as a potential hub, and that its anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory effects depend on different signaling pathways and cell types [95]. How different mechanical patterns guide Piezo1's pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory mechanisms is not yet clear. Learning more about the inflammatory signaling pathway that Piezo1 induces is important for alleviating LFH (Fig. 3).

Possible molecular mechanisms of inflammatory effect of Piezo1 in the progression of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. Upon the activation of the mechanical stress, Piezo1 promotes Ca2+ inflow and ROS generation, thereby up-regulating the expression of inflammatory factors by STAT, AP-1, NF-κB, p38-MAPK, Akt/mTOR and CCL2-CCR2 signaling pathway, ultimately leading to the occurrence of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy.

Inhibiting Piezo1 can alleviate fibrosis

The occurrence of fibrosis is mainly based on the result of previous inflammation. As one of the key pathological mechanisms of LFH [40,97], fibrosis is manifested as a large amount of fibrous connective tissue deposition, replacing the original normal tissue [98]. The evidence that oxidative stress and Piezo1 is involved in human fibrotic diseases is very clear and abundant [51,71,99]. Luo et al. believe that oxidative stress plays an indispensable role in promoting fibrosis development, and they demonstrated the potential of antioxidant stress in treating pulmonary fibrosis by showing that SLC15A3 deficiency confers resistance to oxidative stress [100]. Guan et al. demonstrated that ROS derived from damaged mitochondrial respiratory chains regulates TGF-β1 expression at the transcriptional level, thereby inducing cellular fibrosis [101]. With respect to ROS, the activation of TGF-β1 seems to be direct. ROS can induce the conformational changes of LAP-β1 through oxidative modification, leading to the release of TGF-β1 and cell fibrosis, and OH• play a key role in this process [102]. Meanwhile, a growing body of evidence indicates that Piezo1 plays a crucial regulatory role in a variety of fibrotic diseases, including hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, renal fibrosis, bladder fibrosis, etc [45,96,103,104]. Based on the above evidence, we have reason to believe that the Piezo1/ROS/TGF-β1 pathway may play a key regulatory role in LFH.

Additionally, Fang et al. reported that the mechanism by which Piezo1 promotes pulmonary fibrosis in addition to mediating Ca2+ inflow also promotes the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which acts as a danger signal to promote inflammatory damage and trigger Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), resulting in fibrosis. The knockout or pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 alleviated the EMT process and pulmonary fibrosis [36]. It is worth mentioning that ROS increases the ATP release from cells, thereby exacerbating this process [105,106]. In addition to TGF-β1 and ATP release, Piezo1/ROS/Wnt/β-catenin may also be involved in the regulation of LFH. Previous literature reported that Piezo1 can cause an increase in Ca2+ influx, which in turn upregulates CCL24 expression through the Wnt2/β-catenin and Wnt11/JNK pathways, ultimately stimulating collagen expression inducing cell fibrosis [107,108]. Meanwhile, multiple studies have shown that ROS, especially H2O2, can activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which further demonstrates the possibility of Piezo1/ROS/Wnt/β-catenin pathway regulating LFH [109,110]. Therefore, Piezo1 is closely associated with mechanical stress-induced fibrosis. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms involved may help in alleviating fibrosis and LFH caused by mechanical overload (Fig. 4).

Possible molecular mechanisms of the fibrosis effect of Piezo1 in the progression of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. Upon the activation of the mechanical stress, Piezo1 promotes Ca2+ inflow and ROS generation, thereby induces tissue fibrosis by triggering Epithelial-mesenchymal transition and up-regulating the expression of CCL24 and TGF-β1, ultimately leading to the occurrence of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy.

Inhibition of Piezo1 inhibits ferroptosis

Recent studies have shown that there is a connection between fibrosis and ferroptosis. By inhibiting ferroptosis, fibrosis can be alleviated [46]. Ferroptosis, as a new cell death mode, has been widely concerned in recent years. It destroys cell membrane and causes cell death through iron-dependent lipid peroxidation. In the study of Pei et al., iron death is involved in Bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, and the bleomycin-induced fibrosis can be fully alleviated with ferroptosis inhibitors [46]. Inhibiting ferroptosis can alleviate the fibrosis caused by ferroptosis, Wu et al. reported that the overexpression of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) improves fibrosis and liver injury caused by iron overload [111]. FGF21 up-regulates the expression of SLC7A11 and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and improving the synthesis and antioxidant capacity of GSH [112,113]. In addition, the mechanism of FGF21 inhibiting ferroptosis to inhibit fibrosis has also been reported in cardiac and pulmonary fibrosis [114,115]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates a tight linkage among ferroptosis, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress: impaired and injured mitochondria trigger oxidative stress, consequently initiating ferroptosis [116]. The close relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis has been extensively studied in various diseases. Su et al. suggested that SIRT3-mediated myocardial fibrosis is partially dependent on the iron death of myoblasts caused by p53 acetylation, and they also confirmed that ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) can reduce the levels of TGF-β1 and ROS, weaken lipid peroxidation, and improve myocardial fibrosis [117]. By establishing a mouse model of iron overload, Wu et al. interestingly found that iron overload can lead to liver fibrosis and liver damage, which is completed depending on the lipid peroxidation and imbalance of redox caused by iron overload [111,118]. Iron overload can lead to oxidative stress, causing chondrocyte degeneration [119]. Zhu et al. demonstrated the potential therapeutic target of USP11 for IVDD by alleviating oxidative stress and mitigating ferroptosis [120]. The main pathological mechanism of liver cirrhosis and liver fibrosis is that iron overload induces ROS production, and then autophagy and apoptosis occur [118,121].

Piezo1 plays an essential role in cell ferroptosis induced by oxidative stress. He et al. found that Piezo1-induced Ca2+ influx leads to mitochondrial fission and increased ROS production, ultimately resulting in cellular ferroptosis [68]. Mechanically, on one hand, Piezo1 activation induces Ca2+ influx and subsequent mitochondrial damage, followed by excessive ROS accumulation. The elevated ROS further activates the MAPK signaling pathway, thereby suppressing the Nrf2/HO-1 axis and downregulating GPX4 and SLC7A11. The resultant accumulation of lipid peroxides ultimately drives ferroptosis [122,123]. On the other hand, the activation of Piezo1 also can promote intracellular Fe2+ accumulation, which inhibits the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and downregulates GPX4 and SLC7A11 [[123], [124], [125], [126]]. Since GPX4 and SLC7A11 can effectively reduce lipid peroxides, and downregulates of GPX4 and SLC7A11 will increases the levels of ROS, and malondialdehyde, ultimately inducing redox balance broken, resulting in ferroptosis [123,124]. The accumulation of lipid peroxides in plasma membrane greatly increases the tension of plasma membrane via the oxidation of surface membrane lipids, and Piezo1 channels are activated after sensing the tension. Piezo1 activation triggers Na+ influx and K+ efflux, ultimately leading to cell rupture associated with ferroptosis. During this process, the TRP channels work as a synergistic effect [37]. In Xiang et al. 's study, the pharmacological inactivation of Piezo1, a key iron transporter activated only by mechanical stress, reduced iron accumulation, alleviate ROS, and effectively inhibited iron changes. The knockout of Piezo1 significantly alleviates intervertebral disc degeneration caused by mechanical damage. This suggests that the Piezo1-iron-ferroptosis axis could offer new therapeutic potential for diseases caused by mechanical stress, such as LFH [127] (Fig. 5).

Schematic diagram of the interaction Piezo1 and ferroptosis in the progression of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. Upon the activation of the mechanical stress, Piezo1 promotes inflow of Fe3+, Ca2+, Na+, while the efflux of K+, thereby leading to redox balance broke, ultimately inducing lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis.

Inhibiting Piezo1 can prevent vascular remodeling

Ferroptosis can stimulate the inflammatory cell network, thereby triggering vascular remodeling mediated by inflammatory cells [128]. Vascular remodeling is a complex process in which a variety of cells interact to form new blood vessels on the existing vascular bed [129,130]. It has been confirmed that vascular remodeling is a key mechanism of fibrosis-related LFH [26,131]. During the process of vascular remodeling, Piezo1-induced oxidative stress plays a crucial role. When Piezo1 channel is activated, the increased influx of Ca2+ activates NADPH oxidase, leading to oxidative stress in the cell. Subsequently, overexpressed ROS production, especially H2O2, upregulates the expression of hypoxia inducible factor-1 α (HIF-1α), thereby promoting the migration and proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and ultimately facilitating vascular remodeling [89,132]. Mechanically, ROS can activate the PI3–K/PKB pathway and regulate the translation of HIF-1α by modulating its downstream molecules HDM2, GSK3β, FOXO4, or mTOR [65]. Moreover, O2•- can induce the expression of HIF-1α and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels by activating the ERK1/2, and the elevated expression of MKK3 and MKK6, two upstream regulators of p38, was shown to promote HIF-1α accumulation in both normoxia and hypoxia [133,134]. Beyond this, O2•- and its metabolite H2O2, causing by Piezo1 activation, also can leads to increasing expression of VEGF, vascular cellular adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1), monocyte chemoattractic protein 1 (MCP1), and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) though activating NF-κB,AP-1,p53, and HIF-1α,thereby participating in the process of vascular remodeling [135].

At the same time, due to its widespread distribution in vascular endothelial cells, Piezo1 also can be activated by membrane stretching and blood flow shear forces [8], stimulating downstream calpain-2 activation and promoting endothelial cell rearrangement and new blood vessel formation [35]. Retailleau et al. explained the mechanoconduction mechanism of Piezo1 in vascular remodeling, and found that Piezo1, in addition to promoting calcium influx, also increases the activity of transglutaminase, which is necessary for arterial remodeling [10]. In addition, Li et al. demonstrated that Piezo1 is up-regulated by positive feedback from the stiff matrix/intergron β1/miR-625-5p/Piezo1 and COL1/stiff matrix pathways, and the up-regulated Piezo1 drives a new mechanism that promotes angiogenesis, namely integrin β1/Piezo1 activation/Ca2+ inflow/HIF-1α ubiquitination/VEGF, insulin-like growth factor binding protein 2 (IGFBP2), and CXCL16 signaling pathways [38]. These findings indicate that inhabiting the activation of Piezo1 may prevent the occurrence of vascular remodeling, thereby slowing down the progression of LFH (Fig. 6).

Possible molecular mechanisms of the vascular remodeling effect of Piezo1 in the progression of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. Upon the activation of the mechanical stress, Piezo1 promotes Ca2+ inflow and ROS generation, thereby induces vascular remodeling by Gq/G11-mediated signaling, calpain-2 activation and HIF-1α ubiquitination.

Inhibition of Piezo1 hinders oxidative stress-mediated cell pyroptosis

During the process of fibrosis, vascular remodeling and cell pyroptosis occur simultaneously and influence each other [136]. Pyroptosis is a new programmed death mode activated by inflammasome, which relies on gasdermin D (GSDMD) and inflammatory caspases to release cellular contents and trigger violent inflammatory response [137]. More and more studies have confirmed that pyroptosis is involved in the process of fibrosis, which provides a new idea for anti-fibrosis therapy [138,139]. Therefore, the progression of LFH characterized by fibrosis also involves pyroptosis [140]. During this process of cell pyroptosis, Piezo1-induced oxidative stress is involved [139]. Yue et al. verified that Piezo1 drives mitochondrial ROS accumulation and cytoplasmic translocation of mitochondrial DNA, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation in endothelial cells [141]. Yao et al. also reported that Piezo1/HIF-1α/ROS/NLRP3 cascade is involved in the development of renal fibrosis in chronic kidney disease [142]. ROS production, including O2•-, H2O2 and 1O2, can activates NF-κB through the IKK-dependent pathway, thereby inducing the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome, and the phosphorylation of IkBα is key to this process [84]. Collectively, ROS not only upregulates the expression of NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, and pro-IL-1β, but also facilitates the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [143]. At the same time, it is reported that Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 mediates PANoptosis, and the use of Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 can significantly reduce myocardial fibrosis [144]. Additionally, Pan et al. reported that the Guizhitongluo tablets inhibit Piezo1 to reduce Ca2+ influx, thereby blocking pyroptosis induced by NLRP3 inflammasome and the fibrosis process caused by inflammation [39]. The specific loss of Piezo1 may inhibit and alleviate fibrosis, at least in part by preventing the inflammatory response triggered by pyroptosis [145]. Therefore, inhibiting Piezo1 could alleviate pyroptosis, potentially providing new treatments for LFH(Fig. 7).

Inhibiting Piezo1 stretch-activated channels may improve LFH

Currently, Piezo1 is showing promising results in the treatment of diseases such as kidney fibrosis [48], pulmonary fibrosis [36], hepatic fibrosis [90], steoarthritis [146], tumour [147], and cicatricial hyperplasia [148]. Apparently, using Piezo1 inhibitors could improve outcomes for some diseases. It shows potential in alleviating LFH, but this has not yet been confirmed. At present, Piezo1 inhibitors have limited research and few reports. To our knowledge, Piezo1 inhibitors that have been reported in literature include GsMTx4, Dooku1, Isoquercitrin, Zinc finger BED-type containing 6 (ZBED6), salvianolic acid B (SalB), OB-1, Guizhitongluo Tablet, and ruthenium red [36,38,39,48,144,146,[149], [150], [151], [152], [153], [154], [155]] (Table 2). Among them, GsMTx4 is the most commonly used inhibitor in Piezo1 research, with over 90% of studies using GsMTx4 as an inhibitor of Piezo1. GsMTx4 ameliorates neuronal damage in mice by inhibiting inflammatory response [149]. In renal fibrosis, reversing the elevation of type 1 collagen and fibronectin inhibits ECM deposition to improve fibrosis [48]. GsMTx4 also inhibit the differentiation and proliferation of myofibroblasts to treat scar [150]. Therefore, targeted suppression of Piezo1 may inhibit the progression of LFH.

Conclusion

LFH is a complex pathological process mediated by many factors such as heredity, degeneration, mechanical stress, inflammation and metabolism. We have sufficient necessity to explore new treatments for LFH. Taken together, the Piezo1 inhibitors inhibit inflammation, block fibrosis, inhibit ferroptosis, prevent vascular remodeling, and reduce pyroptosis, which is helpful in alleviating the progression of LFH. However, in the absence of supporting evidence of Piezo1 modulation from LFH patient tissue, preclinical animal models, or in vitro experiments in ligamentum flavum fibroblasts or myofibroblasts, the proposed connection between Piezo1 and LFH remains quite speculative. In the future, we need to address the following research questions: First, demonstrate in vivo animal models and in vitro cellular experiments that Piezo1 inhibitors can mitigate the progression of LFH. In addition, the regulatory mechanisms that inhibit Piezo1 are not yet adequate, and that will need to be explored. The targeted inhibition of Piezo1 offers new directions and hope for the treatment of LFH.
