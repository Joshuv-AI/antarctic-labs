Treatment strategies and survival outcomes of vestibular schwannoma: a comparative study based on Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database
Authors: Long Chen, Hai-Yan Zheng, Tao Hou, Yi Ren, Qiao Huang, Lin Shen, Li-Ping Peng, Dun-Chang Mo, Shi-Hua Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment strategies for vestibular schwannoma (VS) vary widely, and selecting the optimal approach based on individual patient characteristics remains challenging. This study systematically evaluated the impact of different treatment modalities on survival outcomes and treatment-related risks using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, and analyzed temporal trends and tumor size influences on treatment selection.MethodsA total of 10,081 patients diagnosed with VS between 2004 and 2021 were identified from the SEER database. Patients were categorized into four treatment groups: no treatment, surgery alone, radiotherapy (RT) alone, and combined surgery plus RT. To minimize baseline confounding, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed. Comparative analyses of overall survival (OS) and treatment-related mortality risks were conducted across the four groups using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and cumulative incidence functions (CIFs). Additionally, temporal trends and tumor size influences on treatment groups were examined to provide clinical insights into individualized therapeutic strategies.ResultsThe surgery group showed the highest OS, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 94%, compared to 78% in the no-treatment group (log-rank P<0.001). Combined therapy and RT alone had intermediate 5-year survival rates of about 89% and 85%, respectively. These differences remained significant after IPTW adjustment. In the competing risks analysis (36 treatment-related deaths), the surgery alone group was not associated with a statistically significant increase in treatment-related mortality [subdistribution hazard ratio (sub-HR) =0.60; P=0.22], nor was RT alone (sub-HR =1.02; P=0.95); the combined group had zero events, leading to an unstable estimate. Treatment choices evolved over time and were strongly influenced by tumor size.ConclusionsIn this large, retrospective SEER-based analysis, surgery was associated with favorable OS, but the findings are subject to substantial selection bias due to unmeasured confounders (e.g., comorbidities, functional status). Treatment decisions should be individualized based on patient age, tumor size, and clinical symptoms. Future research integrating multicenter clinical data is warranted to advance precision therapy and functional preservation, ultimately improving patient quality of life.Keywords: Vestibular schwannoma (VS), treatment strategies, surgery, radiotherapy (RT), survival

Highlight box

Key findings

• Surgery associated with highest overall survival (~94% vs 78%), but subject to selection bias.

• After adjustment, no treatment modality significantly increased treatment‑related mortality.

What is known and what is new?

• Vestibular schwannoma is a benign, slow-growing tumor with various treatment options including observation, surgery, and radiotherapy (RT). Treatment selection is influenced by tumor size, patient age, symptoms, and institutional expertise, but optimal individualized strategies remain debated.

• This large Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results-based study (n=10,081) with inverse probability of treatment weighting adjustment shows that surgery is associated with the highest overall survival (OS) (5-year rate ~94%), but this finding is subject to substantial selection bias due to unmeasured comorbidities. No treatment modality (surgery or RT) was associated with a statistically significant increase in treatment‑related mortality after adjusting for age and tumor size. Temporal trends demonstrate a shift toward observation (from 35.9% to 51.4%) over the past decade, aligning with contemporary conservative management.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• OS differences across treatment groups largely reflect baseline patient health rather than treatment efficacy; causal inferences cannot be made.

• Treatment decisions should be individualized based on patient age, tumor size, symptoms, and overall health status, rather than assuming superiority of any single approach.

Introduction

Vestibular schwannoma (VS), also known as acoustic neuroma, is a benign, slow-growing tumor originating from the Schwann cells of the vestibulocochlear nerve complex (cranial nerve VIII), which comprises the cochlear nerve and the superior and inferior vestibular nerves (1). Although histologically benign, VS can cause significant morbidity due to its proximity to critical neurovascular structures, leading to symptoms such as hearing loss, tinnitus, balance disturbances, and, in advanced cases, brainstem compression (2,3). The annual incidence of VS is approximately 1.5 per 100,000 person-years, and is increasing due to the widespread use of magnetic resonance imaging and improved clinical recognition (4).

Currently, management options for sporadic VS include observation (no active treatment), microsurgical resection, radiotherapy (RT), or a combination of surgery and RT (5). Observation is typically reserved for small, asymptomatic tumors or patients with significant comorbidities, aiming to minimize treatment-related morbidity (6). Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment for large or symptomatic tumors, offering immediate tumor removal but carrying risks such as cranial nerve injury and other complications (7). RT, including stereotactic radiosurgery, provides a less invasive alternative with favorable tumor control rates, though it may involve delayed adverse effects (8). Combined surgery and RT are generally considered for selected cases, such as residual or recurrent tumors (8,9). Despite these options, no universally accepted treatment algorithm exists; clinical decisions are often individualized based on tumor size, patient age, symptoms, and institutional expertise (4,10).

Previous studies have reported variable survival outcomes and treatment-related risks associated with different modalities, but many suffer from limitations including small sample sizes, single-center designs, or short follow-up periods (11,12). Furthermore, the impact of temporal trends and tumor characteristics on treatment selection has not been comprehensively assessed in large, population-based cohorts. Understanding these patterns is essential for optimizing patient outcomes and guiding evidence-based practice (13).

In this context, the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database provides a valuable resource for analyzing large-scale, real-world data on VS patients across diverse demographics and clinical settings. Utilizing this database, our study aims to systematically compare overall survival (OS) and treatment-related mortality among VS patients receiving no treatment, surgery alone, RT alone, or combined surgery plus RT. Additionally, we explore how treatment choices have evolved over time and vary according to tumor size. These insights contribute to refining therapeutic strategies and promoting personalized management of VS. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0818/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study utilized data from the SEER 18 registries cohort of the National Cancer Institute. Patient records were retrieved through SEER*Stat software version 8.4.4, identifying individuals diagnosed with VS between 2004 and 2021. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Cases were selected based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) code 9560/0 for benign schwannoma, coupled with the topography code C72.4, which specifies the auditory and vestibular nerves. Patients with alternative coding or those reported exclusively from laboratory sources, physician offices, nursing or convalescent homes, hospice, autopsy, or death certificates were excluded. Tumor size was stratified into three categories according to the dataset: less than 1.5 cm, 1.5 to 3 cm, and greater than 3 cm. Treatment modalities were classified based on SEER’s site-specific surgery, RT, and radiation-surgery sequence variables. Microsurgery encompassed both gross total and subtotal resections, whereas “radiation alone” referred to patients who did not undergo microsurgery but received RT documented as administered in hospital inpatient radiation treatment centers or medical oncology facilities.

Data processing and variable categorization

In this study, multiple clinical and demographic variables were extracted and systematically processed for analysis. Records with invalid or unknown survival times were excluded to maintain data quality. Tumor size data were carefully cleaned by removing entries marked as “unknown”, as well as those with implausible values—specifically, tumor sizes ranging from 401 to 989 mm—and the code 990, which denotes microscopic focus. Age was categorized into two groups based on actual patient age: under 65 years and 65 years or older. Race was classified into three categories: White, Black, and other, with all races other than White and Black grouped under other. The year of diagnosis was stratified into two intervals, 2004–2012 and 2013–2021, using the median year as the cutoff. Tumor laterality was categorized as left, right, bilateral, or other/unclear. RT was dichotomized into yes or no, irrespective of the specific treatment modality or sequence. Surgery is classified as either “no” or “yes”. The SEER database classifies chemotherapy into “no” and “yes”. It is important to note that no data were collected regarding pathological diagnostic subtypes. The variable “COD to site rec” (cause of death to site record) was used to classify death causes as follows: (I) deaths due to treatment-related accidents and adverse effects; (II) patients alive at last follow-up; and (III) deaths from other causes unrelated to the primary tumor. This classification enabled a more precise assessment of cause-specific event probabilities.

The primary outcome of the study was OS, defined as the length of time from the date of first diagnosis until the date of death from any cause, or the last follow-up if the patient was still alive at the end of the study period.

Statistical analysis

Patients were stratified into four treatment groups (no treatment, surgery alone, RT alone, and combined surgery plus RT) with the factor levels explicitly ordered to maintain consistent coding and visualization. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics among these groups were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival outcomes, including survival time and status, were analyzed using complete-case data.

Univariate survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank tests to compare survival distributions across treatment groups. Competing risk analyses were performed by estimating cumulative incidence functions (CIFs), and Fine-Gray proportional subdistribution hazard models were employed to assess the impact of covariates on cause-specific hazards.

Subgroup analyses examined the relationships between diagnosis period, tumor size, and treatment selection. To mitigate confounding and selection bias, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied, and weighted analyses were subsequently performed.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance defined as P<0.05. Analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.0).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 10,081 patients diagnosed with VS were identified from the SEER database. These patients were categorized into four treatment groups based on therapeutic modality: no treatment (n=4,517), surgery alone (n=3,796), RT alone (n=1,615), and combined surgery plus RT (n=153) (Figure 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences among groups were observed for age, year of diagnosis, race, laterality, tumor size, and chemotherapy administration (all P<0.05). Notably, younger patients (<65 years) predominated in the surgery group (86.6%) compared to the no treatment group (55.1%). Tumor size also varied markedly, with smaller tumors (<1.5 cm) more frequent in the no treatment group (75.0%), while larger tumors (>3 cm) were more common in the combined surgery plus RT group (67.3%).

Overall, 89.5% of patients were alive at last follow-up, with the highest survival rate in the surgery group (93.4%). These findings provide a comprehensive overview of the study population and confirm the robustness of the IPTW approach in balancing confounders.

Survival analyses

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare OS among the four treatment groups: no treatment, surgery alone, RT alone, and combined surgery plus RT.

Before IPTW adjustment (Figure 2A), significant differences in survival were observed across groups (log-rank P<0.0001). The surgery group demonstrated the highest survival probability over time, followed by the combined surgery plus RT group, RT alone, and no treatment group. At 5 years, survival rates were approximately 94% for surgery, 89% for surgery plus RT, 85% for RT alone, and 78% for no treatment. These results suggest a survival benefit associated with surgical intervention.

After IPTW adjustment (Figure 2B), the survival differences among groups remained statistically significant (log-rank P<0.0001), indicating that the observed survival advantage of surgical groups persists after balancing measured confounders. The overall median follow-up time was 82 months (IQR, 38–135 months), with the longest follow-up in the RT alone group (median 104 months) and the shortest in the no treatment group (median 68 months). However, residual confounding inherent to retrospective data, particularly from unmeasured variables such as comorbidities and functional status, cannot be excluded.

The Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model (Table 4) further quantified these effects. Patients aged ≥65 years had a significantly higher risk of treatment-related mortality [subdistribution hazard ratio (sub-HR) =2.75; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.35–5.59; P=0.005]. Compared with the No treatment group, none of the treatment groups showed a statistically significant increase in treatment-related mortality after adjusting for age and tumor size: surgery alone (sub-HR =0.60; P=0.22), RT alone (sub-HR =1.02; P=0.95). The combined surgery plus RT group had zero treatment-related deaths (0/153), leading to an unstable estimate (sub-HR <0.001, P<0.001). Tumor size categories (1.5–3 cm and >3 cm) were not significantly associated with treatment-related mortality (P=0.37 and P=0.38, respectively).

Together with the CIF results, these findings suggest that active treatment does not confer a significantly higher short-term mortality risk beyond that of observation when accounting for age and tumor burden. However, the low number of events (n=36) limits the precision of these estimates, and the absence of events in the combined group warrants caution in interpretation.

Treatment selection trends by diagnosis year and tumor size

Figure 4 illustrates the distribution of treatment modalities stratified by diagnosis year (Figure 4A) and tumor size (Figure 4B).

Distribution of treatment modalities stratified by (A) diagnosis year groups and (B) tumor size categories. Treatment groups include no treatment, surgery alone, RT alone, and combined surgery plus RT. RT, radiotherapy.

As depicted in Figure 4A, the proportion of patients managed with observation (no treatment) increased over time, from 35.9% in 2004–2012 to 51.4% in 2013–2021, while the proportion undergoing surgery alone decreased from 41.9% to 34.5% over the same period. The use of RT alone declined from 20.5% to 12.7%, and the combined surgery plus RT remained a small minority (1.7% and 1.4%, respectively). These trends reflect a growing clinical preference for conservative management in the later calendar period, consistent with contemporary practice patterns.

Figure 4B shows treatment patterns according to tumor size. Patients with smaller tumors (<1.5 cm) were more likely to receive no treatment or RT alone, whereas those with larger tumors (>3 cm) predominantly underwent surgery alone or combined surgery plus RT. Patients with intermediate tumor sizes (1.5–3 cm) exhibited a more varied distribution of treatment modalities. This pattern reflects clinical decision-making tailored to tumor burden, with more aggressive treatments favored for larger tumors.

Collectively, these findings highlight evolving treatment preferences influenced by both temporal factors and tumor characteristics, emphasizing the importance of individualized therapeutic strategies in VS management.

Discussion

This study utilized the large-scale SEER database to systematically evaluate the survival outcomes and treatment-related mortality risks among patients with VS receiving different treatment modalities, as well as to analyze temporal trends and tumor size influences on treatment selection. Our findings not only highlight the significant survival benefit associated with surgical intervention but also reflect dynamic adjustments in clinical practice, providing valuable insights for personalized management of VS.

From a clinical perspective, the choice of treatment strategy for VS must be individualized, taking into account patient age, tumor size and location, as well as current symptomatology (13,14). These factors critically influence both the feasibility and expected outcomes of different therapeutic approaches. For example, younger patients with larger or symptomatic tumors may benefit more from surgical resection, whereas older patients or those with small, asymptomatic tumors might be better candidates for observation or RT.

For patients requiring surgery, particular attention must be paid to preserving facial nerve function to avoid postoperative facial paralysis, which significantly impacts quality of life (15). Microsurgical resection via the translabyrinthine approach under the operating microscope has proven effective in achieving minimally invasive tumor removal while maximizing facial nerve preservation. However, the suitability of this approach depends heavily on preoperative imaging assessments to evaluate tumor extension and anatomical considerations. This tailored surgical planning is essential to optimize outcomes and minimize complications (16,17).

It is important to note that the translabyrinthine approach necessitates removal of the labyrinth, resulting in complete ipsilateral hearing loss postoperatively (18). To address this, some scholars have proposed simultaneous cochlear implantation during the same surgical session, aiming to restore auditory function and improve postoperative quality of life (19). This combined strategy represents a promising direction for comprehensive management of VS patients undergoing translabyrinthine surgery.

Our survival analysis demonstrated that patients undergoing surgery had the highest OS rates, which remained significant after IPTW adjustment. This suggests an association between surgical treatment and higher OS, although causal interpretation is limited by selection bias from unmeasured confounders (e.g., comorbidities, functional status) (20,21). Although the combined surgery plus RT group showed slightly lower survival than the surgery-alone group, it still outperformed the RT-alone and no-treatment groups. This may reflect selection bias, as combined therapy is often reserved for larger or recurrent tumors. The RT-alone group exhibited better survival than the no-treatment group, supporting RT as an effective option for patients who are not surgical candidates (22). Consistent with selection bias, the surgery group had larger tumors but younger age, while the RT group had smaller tumors but older age, reflecting that chronological age and comorbidity burden are major drivers of treatment allocation independent of tumor size.

In our competing risk analysis, neither surgery alone (sub-HR =0.60; P=0.22] nor RT alone (sub-HR =1.02; P=0.95) was associated with a statistically significant increase in treatment-related mortality after adjusting for age and tumor size. The combined group had zero events, leading to an unstable estimate. This underscores the balance between early treatment risks and long-term gains, emphasizing that clinical decisions should integrate patient age, tumor size, and overall health status (23). Notably, patients aged ≥65 years and males had lower treatment-related mortality risks, potentially due to selection factors or physiological differences, warranting further investigation.

Regarding temporal trends, the proportion of patients managed with observation increased over time (from 35.9% in 2004–2012 to 51.4% in 2013–2021), while surgery alone decreased from 41.9% to 34.5% and RT alone declined from 20.5% to 12.7%. These trends align with contemporary practice patterns favoring conservative management for smaller or asymptomatic tumors, likely driven by increased detection and a focus on quality of life (24). RT usage remained relatively stable, and combined therapy was consistently a minority choice, indicating a clinical preference for monotherapy. Tumor size significantly influenced treatment decisions: smaller tumors were more often managed with observation or RT, whereas larger tumors favored surgery or combined treatment, aligning with clinical considerations of tumor burden and treatment risk (25,26).

The strengths of this study include the extensive coverage and large sample size of the SEER database, combined with IPTW to effectively control baseline confounders, enhancing the reliability and generalizability of the results. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the SEER database lacks critical clinical variables such as comorbidity indices, functional status, symptom severity, tumor growth rate, Koos grade, detailed audiometric data, extent of resection (gross total vs. subtotal), and specifics of RT (e.g., stereotactic radiosurgery vs. fractionated RT, dose, fractionation, technology platform). These omissions preclude a more nuanced analysis of functional outcomes and tumor control, which are arguably more relevant than OS for this benign disease. Second, the use of OS as the primary endpoint is problematic because VS has extremely low disease-specific mortality; differences in OS largely reflect baseline patient characteristics rather than treatment efficacy. Third, the classification of treatment-related mortality relied on the SEER “cause of death to site” variable mapped to “accidents and adverse effects”, which may misclassify some deaths, and the very low number of events (n=36) limits the precision of the competing risks analysis. Fourth, SEER groups all RT modalities into a single category, combining stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife, CyberKnife, LINAC-based), fractionated RT, and proton therapy, which have evolved substantially from 2004 to 2021 and differ in toxicity and tumor control profiles. Fifth, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (frailty, socioeconomic status, patient preference, institutional expertise) likely remains despite IPTW. Sixth, the combined surgery-plus-RT group had zero treatment-related deaths, leading to an extremely wide CI and unreliable sub-HR estimate.

In summary, in this large, retrospective SEER-based analysis, surgery was associated with favorable OS, but the findings are subject to substantial selection bias due to unmeasured confounders. No treatment modality was associated with a statistically significant increase in treatment-related mortality after adjusting for age and tumor size. Temporal trends show a shift toward observation, consistent with contemporary practice. Treatment decisions should be individualized based on patient age, tumor size, symptoms, and overall health status, rather than assuming superiority of any single approach. Future research should integrate multicenter clinical data with detailed functional outcomes (hearing, facial nerve, quality of life) and progression-free survival to better inform personalized management of VS.

Conclusions

This large SEER-based study found that surgery was associated with favorable OS in VS, but this finding is subject to substantial selection bias due to unmeasured confounders (e.g., comorbidities). No treatment modality was associated with a statistically significant increase in treatment-related mortality after adjusting for age and tumor size. Temporal trends showed a shift toward observation, consistent with contemporary practice. Given the retrospective design and the limitations of OS as an endpoint for a benign tumor, causal inferences cannot be made. Treatment decisions should be individualized based on patient age, tumor size, symptoms, and overall health status.

Future research should integrate functional outcomes and progression-free survival to advance personalized management.
