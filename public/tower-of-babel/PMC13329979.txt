Inexpensive Hydrogen Storage: Propylene to Propane using Plasmonic Photocatalysis
Authors: Alison Carroll, Parmeet Dhindsa, Alexander Ahrens, Matheus Laureano Nunes Barbosa, Aliyu A. Ahmad, Nolan Craft, Shan Deneen, Peter Nordlander, Naomi J. Halas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemistry-based hydrogen storage media, such as liquid organic hydrogen carriers, offer an attractive alternative to physical hydrogen storage solutions. Here, we investigate propane as a possible hydrogen storage medium, attractive for its low cost and ease of availability. We report the ambient temperature and pressure hydrogenation of propylene using an antenna-reactor Al@TiO2–Pt single-atom plasmonic photocatalyst. Illumination at two distinct wavelengths, 450 and 800 nm, corresponds to high reactivity toward propane production. Theoretical insight into wavelength-dependent hot-carrier generation reveals nonequilibrium carriers with sufficient energies to activate both steps of propylene hydrogenation at either wavelength, the dissociation of H2 and its incorporation into the propylene carbon–carbon double bond. Paired with light-driven propane dehydrogenation, this study demonstrates that photocatalytic cycling of propylene - propane for hydrogen storage and release can be performed under mild conditions.

Hydrogen (H2) has outstanding potential as an alternative energy source due to its carbon neutrality. However, hydrogen faces fundamental storage and transport challenges due to its low volumetric energy density, embrittlement effects, and diffusion through materials due to its small size. Among the most promising strategies for hydrogen storage is in chemical bonds, where molecular carriers could potentially store and release it reversibly through hydrogenation and dehydrogenation reactions. Most common among these carriers are liquid organic hydrogen carriers (LOHCs) derived from aromatic molecules. Despite their advantages in material cost and low-pressure storage, dehydrogenation and hydrogenation of LOHCs often require high temperatures and pressures using traditional thermal activation. , Such harsh conditions also lead to catalyst deactivation via coke formation or sintering integration.

Recently we reported the selective plasmonic photocatalytic dehydrogenation of propane (C3H8 ⇌ H2 + C3H6) using visible-light excitation of aluminum nanocrystals (AlNCs) with TiO2 surface layers and Pt single-atom sites, with no external heating. In this antenna-reactor catalyst, the plasmonic core–shell Al@TiO2 serves as an antenna, capturing the incident light and enhancing the reactivity of the platinum reactor sites on the nanoparticle surface. Illumination of this catalyst at 550 nm enables efficient dehydrogenation of propane at ambient temperature and pressure conditions, reducing the overall energy requirements of this reaction.

This recent study motivates us to assess whether propane, a widely available and inexpensive feedstock, could also be a practical hydrogen storage medium. To evaluate its potential in a simple manner, we compare the hydrogen storage capacity of propane to one of the most highly developed LOHCs, perhydro-dibenzyltoluene (H18-DBT), by cost. With a molecular weight of 290 g/mol, H18-DBT stores 9 molecules of H2 per molecule of LOHC. At its current wholesale price of $5/kg, it has a molar storage capacity cost of $0.14/mol H2. Propylene has a molecular weight of 42 g/mol but can store only one H2 molecule per propane molecule. However, at the current domestic wholesale price of propane at $0.44/kg (in standard liquid form), propane has a molar storage capacity cost of $0.02/mol H2. This rudimentary analysis bypasses many other important system factors, such as the high energy cost of thermally activated hydrogenation and dehydrogenation, thermocatalyst regeneration or replacement, and the high transport cost of DBT. However, it establishes that the propane – propylene couple may have potential for low-cost hydrogen storage, provided that the hydrogenation of propylene can also be accomplished under similarly mild conditions, which include ambient pressure and no external heating.

Here, we report the highly efficient light-driven hydrogenation of propylene (H2 + C3H6 ⇌ C3H8) using the same plasmonic photocatalyst as was used in our propane dehydrogenation study, AlNC@TiO2–Pt, under mild conditions. In this system, propylene hydrogenation proceeds with maximum reactivity at two distinct wavelengths, 450 and 700 nm. With this photocatalytic system, a maximum external quantum efficiency of ∼ 35% under 33 W cm–2 illumination can be achieved. Nonequilibrium carriers generated by plasmon decay contribute to both reaction steps: hydrogen dissociation, and hydrogen addition at the carbon–carbon double bond of propylene. The observation that hydrogenation can proceed with essentially the same plasmonic photocatalyst as dehydrogenation but with a distinctly different wavelength dependence suggests the possibility of integrating both processes into a light-controlled hydrogen storage and release system for this inexpensive and widely abundant molecular hydrogen carrier.

Using a previously reported protocol, AlNC@TiO­(OH)2 core–shell particles were synthesized by growing an ∼ 10 nm amorphous TiO­(OH)2 shell on aluminum cuboctahedra (100–120 nm) (Figure S1). Deposition of small platinum clusters and single-atom sites was performed on the core–shell particles using a modified strong-electrostatic adsorption technique (Figure S2). , Calcination at 450 °C led to the formation of a crystalline anatase TiO2 shell layer with a direct interface that improves the lifetime and transfer efficiency of hot carriers (HC) generated in the Al plasmonic core upon illumination. , Elemental composition was determined to be 75.8% aluminum, ∼ 19.2% titanium, and 5.0% platinum by weight using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (Table ST1).

Prior to photocatalysis studies, the AlNC@TiO2–Pt plasmonic photocatalyst particles underwent wet impregnation with a γ-Al2O3 catalyst support (SI, Methods) and were annealed in a mild reductive H2 atmosphere. This was to elicit partial platinum encapsulation by TiO x (x ≤ 2) overlayers through strong metal–support interactions (SMSIs). , This resulted in a predominant single-atom distribution of Pt sites on the surface by preferentially forming overlayers on sites with higher surface energy (the Pt clusters). The increased reactivity of single-atom Pt sites is thought to arise from the decreased coordination environment and increased surface free energy of single-atom Pt sites relative to Pt clusters. A high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image of an AlNC@TiO2–Pt particle following reductive annealing is shown (Figure A). Here, Pt sites on the surface brightly contrast the darker surrounding TiO2 shell. Elemental maps of this particle edge highlight the morphology of the catalysts with an Al core, TiO2 shell, and Pt sites distributed on the surface (Figures S2, S3). The measured and calculated diffuse reflectance spectrum of solid AlNC@TiO2–Pt mixed with γ-Al2O3 in air is shown (Figure B). Here, the optical properties of the sample are described using the Kubelka–Munk function, revealing two distinct regions of reflectance representing higher order plasmon modes (∼300 nm) and the Al interband transition (∼800 nm) since dipolar modes are not visible in the diffuse reflectance of plasmonic nanoparticles. The calculated spectrum agrees very well with the measured spectrum. Mie theory calculations of the absorption and scattering spectra of the photocatalyst particles in the γ-Al2O3 support are shown (Figure C) based on experimentally obtained catalyst size distributions (Figure S1). Scattering features representing the dipolar and quadrupolar resonances (∼580 nm, ∼ 300 nm) agree with the experimental absorbance spectrum (Figure S4), where the presence of the catalyst support red-shifts the photocatalyst absorbance. Absorbance calculations highlight the Al interband transition at nominally 800 nm.

An analysis of the binding characteristics of CO molecules on reactive Pt sites was performed using CO probe diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (CO–DRIFTS), (Figure D) (further details see SI). The binding sites of AlNC@TiO2–Pt were measured following annealing in either oxidative (O2) or reductive (H2) atmospheres, revealing morphological differences induced by the different annealing environments. Binding sites of the O2-annealed sample (black) were characterized by two dominant peaks: 2096 cm–1 and 2076 cm–1. The former signature corresponds to the linear binding of CO to single atomic sites based on the weak donation of electrons from the Pt d-orbital to the π* orbital of CO. Such weak donation can be attributed to the slightly positive charge present on single atom Pt sites, whose higher energy requirement for binding is reflected through its higher stretching frequency. Conversely, the latter signature corresponds to CO bound to neutral Pt clusters on the surface formed during the reduction of Pt x O y via CO adsorption. The lower binding energy is reflective of the easier donation of electrons from metallic Pt to CO, enabling a stronger bond. Binding traces at higher frequency features may arise from CO bound to unreduced Pt x O y clusters on the photocatalyst surface. Upon annealing in a reductive atmosphere, higher energy binding signatures corresponding to metallic Pt and unreduced Pt x O y sites are suppressed, revealing single-atom sites at 2086 cm–1.

Initial photocatalytic reactivity of AlNC@TiO2–Pt was evaluated using a continuous gas-flow system and white light from a supercontinuum laser source (Fianium) (SI, Methods). Under white light illumination, AlNC@TiO2–Pt displayed highly selective reactivity toward propane at varying power densities and stability over multiple hours (Figure S6). Since propylene hydrogenation consists of two primary chemical events, hydrogen dissociation and hydrogen addition at the carbon–carbon double bond of the adsorbed propylene molecule, wavelength-dependent studies probing both events were performed using 50 nm bandwidth bandpass filters (Figure A, B). The surface temperature of the photocatalyst was recorded using an infrared camera (Optris). The H2 dissociation rate was measured using the isotope exchange reaction (H2 + D2 → 2HD). Within the visible wavelength range of the light source (450–775 nm ± 25 nm) at a constant power density of ∼ 30 W cm–2, the AlNC@TiO2–Pt photocatalyst exhibited a maximum for HD production at 700 nm (Figure A). This wavelength maximum is consistent with previous reports of Al-based photocatalysts for this reaction. In contrast, the photocatalyst AlNC@TiO2–Pt displayed local maxima for propylene hydrogenation at both 450 and 700 nm under a constant power density of nominally ∼ 54 W cm–2 (80 mW, 0.0015 cm2) illumination, with an increase in temperature toward longer wavelength excitation (Figure B).

As is characteristic of plasmonic photocatalysis studies, the measured wavelength dependencies of the reaction rates show no correlation with the optical absorption spectra. While a photothermal mechanism is directly linked to optical absorption, and such heating could be exploited in thermocatalysis, chemical reactions are most efficiently induced by electron transfer reactions which can significantly lower reaction barriers. The wavelength dependence of the reaction rates thus depends on the availability of hot carriers (HCs) of the right energy to be able to transfer into reactant orbitals. For accurate modeling of HC generation in a metal, it is crucial to include the electronic band structure.

To understand the wavelength dependent reaction rates, we extend a previous model originally developed for plasmonic ammonia decomposition. , In this model the reaction rate is assumed to be proportional to HC injection rates, which are proportional to the number of plasmon-induced HCs with energies overlapping an antibonding reactant orbital, modeled as a Lorentzian function. The width of the resonance is fixed at 0.5 eV (and does not significantly influence the results). The energy of the resonance is used as a fitting parameter and is varied to yield the best agreement with the measured wavelength dependence of the relevant reaction rate (SI, Hot carrier injection model).

In the present reaction, there are two processes involved: the dissociation of molecular hydrogen and the activation of the carbon–carbon double bond in the propylene molecule. To determine the energies of these two resonances, we focus first on the hydrogen dissociation rates (Figure A), then determine the energy of the resonance corresponding to the carbon double bond activation from the data in Figure B.

The calculated initial HC distributions in the illuminated photocatalyst as a function of excitation wavelength are shown in Figure A (More details in the SI, Theoretical Methods). The simulations were performed using density functional theory for an AlNC particle of the same average size (110 nm diameter) as those used in the experiments. The HC distributions are shown relative to the Al Fermi level ϵ F (eV < 0 for hot holes, eV > 0 for hot electrons). Narrow distributions of low energy carriers are observed for longer wavelength excitation, while broader distributions are observed for shorter wavelengths. The distributions are almost rectangular in shape and show only small energy variations characteristic of simple metals with few interband transitions. The distributions depend on the photon wavelength λ Inc. which corresponds to a photon energy εInc=cλInc . For each wavelength, the lowest energy HC is a hot hole at an energy ε F – hε Inc. , created by exciting an electron to just above ε F . The highest energy HC corresponds to the excitation of an electron at ε F to ε F + hε Inc. . Figure B illustrates the basic assumptions of our model: The initial hot electron distributions following excitation at 450 nm (blue) and 700 nm (orange) and the energies of the two relevant molecular orbitals that can serve as acceptors. The energy of the carbon double bond breaking propylene resonance (CC*) is denoted ε 0 and the energy of the antibonding H2 resonance ε 1. These orbital energies were empirically derived based on the experimental wavelength dependence of the production rate. These energies are difficult to calculate quantitatively with precision, even with the most accurate first-principles calculations, and also depend on the exact configuration of Pt atom coordination and nearby oxygen vacancies on the TiO2 surface.

The best fit calculated HC injection rates as I(CC*,λ) and I(H–H,λ) for ε 0 = 1.7 eV and ε 1 = 1.2 eV, respectively, are shown in Figure C. Propylene hydrogenation involves the transformation of two different bonds, H–H dissociation and CC to C–C bond transformation via hydrogen addition. To connect the individual injection rates to overall propylene hydrogenation reaction rates, a detailed modeling of the reaction dynamics would be needed. In addition, electron transfer and tunneling matrix element effects will almost certainly influence the eventual activation of the H–H and CC bonds differently. Such calculations are beyond the scope of the present paper. As a simple illustration of such effects, in Figure D, we show some simplified approaches. If the hydrogen dissociation and bond activation occur simultaneously as a two-electron transfer process, one would expect the overall reaction rate to be proportional to the product of the injection rates R(C 3 H 6,λ) ∼ I(CC*,λ) × I(H–H,λ). If the lifetimes of the activated bonds are long, one would expect that the overall reaction rate would be proportional to the sum of the injection rates weighted by different coefficients α reflecting how efficient a carrier injection is in activating the respective bond, R(C 3 H 6,λ) ∼ α0 I(CC*,λ) + α1 I(H–H,λ). In Figure D we also include the results for (α0,α1) = (1,1), (1,5), and (5,1). The best fit is obtained for (1,5), suggesting that rate of propane production is dependent significantly on activation of the H–H bond.

Laser excitation at 450 and 800 nm was used to determine the reaction orders of both hydrogen and propylene reactants for the hydrogenation reaction (shifted to 800 nm from 700 nm for better correspondence with the Al interband transition of the photocatalyst). In each experiment, the partial pressures of one reagent were varied (between 7 and 43%) while the other reagent was held constant, with the total pressure kept constant with a balance of helium. Reactivities toward propane under constant illumination were plotted against the partial pressure percentage of the variable reagent, where the slope represents the reaction order with respect to the variable reagent. Reaction orders derived from variable propylene and hydrogen partial pressures are shown in Figure A and B, respectively. Under 450 nm (blue) and 800 nm (blue) illumination, reaction orders with respect to propylene were 0.34 and 0.72, respectively. Rate orders with respect to varying hydrogen under 450 nm gave a rate order of 1.16 and 800 nm gave 1.25. Apparent activation barriers for propylene hydrogenation under 450 and 800 nm are 0.43 and 0.41 eV, respectively (Figure S7). These barriers suggest that despite hydrogen dissociation occurring more readily at 800 nm, there is not an appreciable improvement in propane production. Thus, the rate-limiting step is confirmed to be the addition of hydrogen to the adsorbed propylene molecule. In the case of thermocatalysis, the catalyst was found to be unstable and unselective under surface temperatures identical to photocatalysis. The hydrogenation rate decreased with increasing temperature; thus, the activation barrier was not estimated (Figure S8).

Propane production as a function of power density at 450 nm was measured between 5 and 33 W cm–2 (Figure C). Within this range, propane production showed a near-linear dependence on power density with >99.7% selectivity toward propane over other byproducts. An analysis of the catalyst efficiency was performed using external quantum efficiency, a measure which probes the production of the desired product as a function of input illuminated energy (Figure D): External quantum efficiency(%)=2×reaction rate(molC3H8s)×NAoptical powerJsεInc(J)×100% where NA is Avogadro’s number and the incident photon energy ε Inc is dependent on the wavelength of excitation. The equation is multiplied by 2 to reflect the 2 electrons required for propylene hydrogenation assuming a Horiuti-Polyani mechanism. , The external quantum efficiency was found to exceed 35% under 33 W cm–2 illumination. These efficiency percentages quantify the reactivity of the photocatalyst as a function of input energy. With a total illumination area of only 0.004 cm2, substantial gains in quantum efficiency may be possible through optimization of light delivery to the photocatalyst surface. While photothermal heating might also contribute to the reactivity, the distinct wavelength dependence, positive correlation between photon flux and external quantum efficiency and wavelength dependent reaction orders suggests a primarily hot-carrier driven mechanism. Furthermore, superior photocatalytic stability and selectivity toward propane in comparison to thermocatalysis demonstrates a facile plasmon-mediated desorption of products.

In conclusion, storage within organic hydrogen carriers requires facile uptake and release. Efficient hydrogenation of propylene, combined with the previous findings on propane dehydrogenation, demonstrates that the AlNC@TiO2–Pt plasmonic photocatalyst works well for both reactions. Further experimentation optimizing conditions such as reagent partial pressure, illumination wavelength, light source (laser to LED), power density, and external temperature may provide additional insight into the conditions required for practical, scaled-up uptake and delivery of hydrogen using propylene-propane as a light-driven hydrogen storage system.
