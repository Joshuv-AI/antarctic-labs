EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This project supports the U.S. Department of Energy's (DOE) mission to reduce the environmental
and climate impacts of fossil fuels and industrial processes, contributing to the goal of achieving net-
zero emissions across the U.S. economy. The primary objective is to conduct a feasibility study for a
Regional Direct Air Capture (DAC) Hub in the Southern Colorado region, northeast of Pueblo. The
geographic construct of this hub is based on the Denver-Julesburg Basin – a geological area where a
significant number of geological storage studies have been conducted (See Figure 1). The project will
leverage the work of Project Eos, a CarbonSAFE Phase III study led by the Colorado School of Mines
and CarbonAmerica.

 The DAC Hub aims to capture, store, and/or utilize at least
 1,000,000 tonnes of CO2 from the atmosphere annually. To
 achieve this, the project team is designing a system with an initial
 capacity of 100,000 tonnes per year. This feasibility-stage project
 will formulate the Regional DAC Hub concept and team to
 conduct the relevant analysis, networking and community
 stakeholder engagement necessary to advance the project to the
 design stage.

 The consortium members will include DAC technology developers, renewable energy providers,
and an off-taker of the captured CO2. The off=taker, Carbon America, will geologically store the CO2
in the basin outlined in Figure 1.

 It is important to properly select the lead organization for the Hub because of the broad range of
responsibilities of that organization. Challenges to the Hub include:

 • multiple DAC providers and the possibility of adding more in the future. These
 competitors will need to work in a “coopetition” environment;
 • the need for a lead organization that is comfortable managing power utilities, negotiate
 real estate acquisitions, negotiate well agreements, and address community issues and
 outreach;
 • an ability to handle confidential materials and prevent confidential information on
 technologies, etc. being shared with competitors who are also in the Hub; and
 • a financial model that is feasible and has the ability to achieve financial targets that would
 be attractive to all the participants.

 Page 3 of 16

 The lead organization for the hub will need to be financially stable and have a reputation of being
neutral. It should also be strong at outreach to the community and be viewed as a trusted source.
Discussions with the members indicated that they would be comfortable with the lead organization
being a university.

 When discussing the business arrangement of the Hub, members(for the most part) indicated that
they wanted to own and maintain their own business, equipment, assets and technologies. A small
number preferred to license their technology to another entity and have that entity own and operate
the equipment. In no case was there any interest in having the Hub consortium own and/or operate
equipment. The members did feel that it would be important for the consortium to contribute to the
maintenance of common OSBL elements. This would be funded through consortium membership
fees. Critical questions that would need to be worked out further included: (1) management and
distribution of equity to participating companies, (2) ownership transfer of the captured CO2 as it
transferred through the supply chain. The consortium structure provided a facile mechanism for these
two factors.

 The important position of the lead organization becomes even more apparent when one
considers cash flow through the consortium. Financial investments would come in through the lead
organization and be properly dispersed. In turn, the investors would be awarded relative equity stake
in the consortium. The selection and engagement of organization to provide Measuring, Monitoring,
and Verification (MMV) will also be the responsibility of the lead organization. MMV is important since
45Q tax credits are expected to be a major portion of the revenue stream to the consortium. Voluntary
Carbon Credits on the Voluntary Credit Market (VCM) are also expected to be a source of revenue.

 The business model explored will provide a management fee for the lead organization along with
an equity position. This compensation is appropriate because in addition to the roles listed above, the
lead organization will need to:

 • provide senior management direction for the HUB. This includes coordinating location of
 DAC units and construction activities;
 • negotiate equity positions and revenue distributions among HUB members;
 • oversee operations and maintenance of shared infrastructure within the Hub (i.e., the
 Outside Battery Limits (OSBL) components that are shared among all the members;
 • maintain confidentiality and abiding by Justice Department guidelines;
 • provide business functions; and
 • develop a methodology to incorporate new members and remove non-performing
 members.

 The duties of a lead organization in a consortium are comprehensive and crucial for the
consortium’s success. These duties ensure that the consortium operates efficiently and effectively,
fostering collaboration and achieving its goals. Some key responsibilities for the lead organization are:

 • Administrative Support: Provide administrative support to the consortium, ensuring
 smooth operations and coordination among members.
 • Onboarding: Manage and support the onboarding of new consortium members, helping
 them integrate into the consortium’s activities and objectives.

 • Communication: Develop and maintain communication and engagement resources,
 ensuring all members are informed and engaged.
 • Documentation: Maintain both technical and non-technical documentation for the
 consortium, ensuring all processes and decisions are well-documented.
 • Community Support: Provide central community support for all consortium members,
 addressing their needs and facilitating collaboration.
 • Growth and Strategy: Actively grow the consortium in alignment with its objectives and

 • Liaison Role: Serve as a bridge between the consortium and external stakeholders, such

Renewable Energy Availability
 Many states located in the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC), have abundant
renewable resources that are needed for large scale Direct Air Carbon Capture projects. As such, many
Investor-Owned Utilities (IOUs) and cooperatives have invested in renewables, reducing the region's
emissions profile. Colorado is no exception with 39% of its in-state generation coming from
renewables and is among the top states for installed wind and solar capacity as of 2023. The pace of
renewable integration is not slowing either. Some of these counties have developed proposals to
proactively integrate renewable energy projects into their long-term economic development plans.
Pueblo County developed a Regional Comprehensive Plan which was published in September 2022
and has a stated goal of providing 100% renewable energy use for electricity consumption by 2035.
Additionally, the county has taken a proactive approach by providing a map to renewable energy
developers where the county would prefer renewable facilities be located to avoid disturbing
residents and prime agricultural land. Pueblo County, like many counties across the country, has
identified the economic value renewable energy projects provide to communities which include
sustained tax revenues, construction jobs, and stable employment opportunities for technicians.
Colorado has one of the country's best renewable resources, favorable regulatory environment,
buildout of transmission, and support from local communities for new economic development driven
by renewable energy projects. Colorado will be a leader in deploying renewable energy technologies.

DAC TECHNOLOGIES & DATA TABLES
Anchor DAC technologies
 The primary anchor DAC technologies in this Colorado DAC hub are GE Vernova and Sustaera.
During the first development phase of this DAC hub, GE Vernova plans to build a 50,000 net CO2
tonnes per annum DAC plant, and Sustaera plans to build a 50,000 net CO2 tonnes per annum DAC
plant. These two DAC plants will be co-located together in a specific site, with sharing some common
balance of plant equipment’s.

GE Vernova Advanced Research DAC Technology

 GE Vernova Advanced Research (GEVAR) is developing sorbent-based direct air capture (DAC)
materials, binder systems and sorbent-integrated contactors to reduce the cost and improve the
efficiency of DAC. Sorbent based capture materials are chemical structures with high surface area and

                                           Page 10 of 16

high porosity that have an affinity for CO2. Additionally, these chemical structures can be modified
with amines to increase the loading (capture) of the CO2 per mass of sorbent. The sorbent must be
paired with a binder (for example, a polymer) that integrates with a contactor or support, enabling
the sorbent to be in direct contact with the air.

 GEVAR is a diversified industrial research laboratory with core competencies including advanced
materials design, process engineering and scale-up, that leverage decades of materials
commercialization, systems modeling and integration, thermal management, and prototype
manufacturing.

Sustaera
 Sustaera utilizes a simple and modularized process consisting of air contactors, CO2 processing
equipment. The core technology is a stable solid structured sorbent integrated with electric resistive
heating on a low pressure drop substrate.

INITIAL DESIGN OF THE BALANCE OF PLANT (BOP)
 The HUB will have an initial CO2 capture capacity of 100,000 MT/y with two plants, one using GE

sharing common services and infrastructure, to take advantage of economies of scale and synergies.

SAFETY, SECURITY, AND REGULATORY REQUIREMENTS
Site Security
 Site security measures will follow the established protocols of each DAC technology provider,
which meet the overall criteria set by the Hub owner. This will involve limited access to industrial
facilities to authorized personnel. Access control at a minimum will utilize physical security measures
such as fences, security cameras, controlled entry and exit points, and employee ID checks.

Cybersecurity
 A cybersecurity plan was developed during the negotiation process of the award. This plan follows
NIST 800-53 standards for security and privacy controls of information systems. Since being awarded,
there have been no changes to the cybersecurity protocols that were established.

Permitting Workflow Overview
 The primary permitting agency involved in this project is the Colorado Department of Public
Health & Environment (CDPHE) and its sub entities. The CDPHE is a state cabinet-level agency within
the Colorado State government. The CDPHE mission is to serve as the state's lead agency for
environmental management and stewardship, focusing on the protection of air, water, and land
resources.

Air Permitting

 The Clean Air Act requires the United States Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA) to set
National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for pollutants that cause or contribute to air
pollution which may reasonably be anticipated to endanger public health and welfare. The U.S. EPA
has set NAAQS for the six criteria pollutants: carbon monoxide (CO), lead (Pb), nitrogen dioxide (NO2),
ozone (O3), particulate matter (PM), and sulfur dioxide (SO2). Current expectations of emissions from
DAC Hub will result in a net-decrease in overall emissions, however the calculations for the Potential
To Emit (PTE) will be required. Depending on the result of PTE calculations the current expectation is

Water Permitting

 The project owner will need to apply for a permit from the State of Colorado for additional water
uptake and wastewater discharge. The DAC facility's treatment system is intended to provide both
the raw water supply and wastewater treatment for this project.

Stormwater Permitting

 CDPHE National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) stormwater program regulates
stormwater discharges, and discharges associated with industrial and construction activities. This
program covers both industrial and construction activities.
 Operators of industrial facilities that meet the criteria for coverage under the program must
obtain a permit and implement a stormwater pollution prevention plan. In certain instances,
regulated operators may be eligible for an exclusion from the requirement to obtain a permit.
 The CDPHE NPDES Stormwater Program regulates discharge of stormwater to surface waters or
to a municipal separate storm sewer system from construction activities that disturb more than one
acre or are part of certain larger projects that disturb more than one acre. Operators of construction
activities that meet the criteria for coverage must obtain an NPDES stormwater permit and implement
a stormwater pollution prevention plan.
Sequestration Permitting

 Wells injecting CO2 for geologic sequestration are classified as EPA Underground Injection Control
(UIC) Class VI wells. The EPA UIC program regulates all aspects of the injection wells of a sequestration
project including project siting, well construction, injection operations, testing and monitoring,
emergency response, financial responsibility, and eventual plugging and closure of the wells and
injection sites. The UIC program implements the regulations through permitting, site inspections,
required reporting, and compliance reviews to ensure that well owners/operators comply with the
regulations. Regulations for Class VI wells are the most rigorous of the UIC program.

COMMUNITY BENEFITS PLAN (All work ceased January 28, 2025)
 The CBPDP reports on the potential incremental economic development in the affected region
and the projected regional job creation levels, with a focus on specific communities that have
historically been economically distressed or impacted by the adverse environmental effects of heavy
industry. The development proposal details how this project can significantly benefit surrounding
disadvantaged communities by increasing local investments, job growth in other sectors and the local
total tax revenue. Thus, wide-scale DAC deployment can play an essential role in the Pueblo region by
minimizing the local economic impact and community dislocation anticipated to meet state and
national climate goals. These benefits are demonstrated through key applicable benefits that have
been preliminarily quantified, and ongoing monitoring will be established to ensure the DAC Hub
continues to bring benefits to the local communities. The CBPDP offers four contractually obligated
sections of this project’s CBP including Community and Labor Engagement, Investing in the American
Workforce (IAW), Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility (DEIA), and Justice40 Initiative.

 The Community and Labor Engagement section reports the identification and engagement of key
stakeholders that will be affected by the DAC Hub. These key stakeholders that have been identified
include Community and Faith-Based organizations, Agricultural & Landowners, Educational,
Environmental, Labor and workforce Training, Local and regional governance group, and Native
American Tribes. A discussion on potential project or technical aspects to be developed is detailed in
the CBPDP that offers clarity in work processes and maximizes local benefits in this project.
Furthermore, the strategy for conducting Community and Labor Engagement is outlined within the
CBPDP by detailing a communication feedback loop, utilization of valuable resources, and distribution
of tasks.

                                            Page 15 of 16

 The Investing in the American Workforce (IAW) section offers technical details on the strategy for
developing the IAW assessment, engaging potential Labor Partners, as well as a model for Center of
Excellence. The preliminary IAW assessment reviewed workforce needs, impacts, and opportunities
related to the DAC facilities to align technological and environmental goals with positive outcomes for
the local workforce and community. In engaging with potential labor partners, the IAW section of the
CBP will detail engagement strategy, career development, and workforce enhancement plan,
alongside considerations for fair labor practices. Furthermore, as a key component of the CBP, the
project team has begun to design a community-centric Center of Excellence (CoE) model focused on
training and policy best practices for workforce preparedness and stakeholder engagement and data
analysis of resident sentiment toward the DAC and similar technologies to supplement this
information.

 As contractually obligated, the DEIA section of the CBPDP details current DEIA Practices of Project
Partners, Diverse Partner/Supplier Engagement, and Strategies, Milestones, and Timelines, which
include implementation strategies and accountability measures. Finally, the contractually obligated
Justice40 section details Preliminary Energy and Environmental Justice (EJ) Assessment that includes
an analysis of the environmental issues of areas surrounding the Pueblo region. The Justice40 section
also describes the potential benefits and potential impacts to the communities and the plan to ensure
that local communities are fully informed and actively engaged in the opportunities provided.

 The CBPDP is contractually obligated. The full plan is submitted as a separate file rather than
included in the continuation packet. All CBPDP work was completed before January 28, 2025.
