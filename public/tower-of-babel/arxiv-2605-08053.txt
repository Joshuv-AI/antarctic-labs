Reinforcement Learning for Exponential Utility: Algorithms and Convergence in Discounted MDPs
Gugan Thoppe, L.A. Prashanth, Ankur Naskar, Sanjay Bhat

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) for exponential-utility optimization in discounted Markov decision processes (MDPs) lacks principled value-based algorithms. We address this gap in the fixed risk-aversion setting. Building on the Bellman-type equation for exponential utility studied in 26 , we derive two Q-value-style extensions and show that the associated operators are contractions in the L ∞ L_{\infty} and sup-log/Thompson metrics, respectively. We characterize their fixed points and prove that the induced greedy stationary policy is optimal for the exponential-utility objective among stationary policies. These structural results lead to two model-free algorithms: a two-timescale Q-learning–style algorithm, for which we establish almost-sure convergence and provide finite-time convergence rates via timescale separation, and a one-timescale algorithm governed by a sublinear power-law operator. Since the latter does not admit a global contraction in standard metrics, we prove its convergence using delicate arguments based on local Lipschitzness, monotonicity, homogeneity, and Dini derivatives, and provide a scalar finite-time analysis that highlights the challenges in obtaining convergence rates in the vector case. Our work provides a foundation for value-based RL under exponential-utility objectives.



1
Introduction

The traditional objective in a Reinforcement Learning (RL) setting
(
4
;
31
)

is to optimize the value function, which is based on the expected value. Such an objective does not incorporate risk, which is important in several application domains such as finance, healthcare, and robotics. Risk-sensitive RL
(
20
)
, which has garnered increased research attention over the last decade, aims to fill this gap.
In risk-sensitive RL, there are broadly two formulations: constrained and unconstrained. In the constrained setting, one maximizes the usual expected value, while constraining some notion of risk that is usually based on the tail behavior, e.g., variance
(
22
;
32
;
28
)
, quantile
(
16
)
, conditional value at risk (CVaR)
(
27
)
, distortion risk
(
21
)
, coherent risk
(
33
)
, etc.
In the unconstrained setting, one employs an objective that explicitly incorporates risk. Exponential utility formulation is an example of such an objective as it considers all moments of the return distribution
(
2
)
.

While a variety of risk measures, such as variance, value at risk (VaR), CVaR and risk measures based on cumulative prospect theory, have been considered in the literature, exponential utility as a risk measure has not been studied adequately in a discounted MDP context. On the other hand, this risk measure has been investigated more thoroughly in average reward MDPs
(
8
;
23
)
.
Our work aims to fill this gap.

We consider the problem of optimizing the exponential utility in a discounted MDP — a problem considered in a planning context in

26
;
9
.
We shall eschew a detailed discussion of the attitude towards risk achieved by exponential utility, and refer the reader to the classic references
26
;
9
;
14
;
15
. Our focus is algorithmic: we study whether the Bellman-type formulation of

26
can be turned into model-free RL algorithms with
provable guarantees.

The formulation of
26
imposes an inter-stage
consistency requirement, which leads to a Bellman-type equation for exponential
utility. This is distinct from formulations
that directly optimize the exponentiated discounted return, which can lead to
time-inconsistent behavior and non-stationary optimal policies that are harder to
compute or learn. In this work, we adopt the Porteus formulation, corresponding
to a constant risk-aversion setting
(
29
)
, and ask the following
question:

Is it possible to design a (tabular and model-free) RL algorithm that finds the optimal stationary policy for the expected exponential utility?

We answer this question in the affirmative. The central idea is to expose the
fixed-point structure hidden in the Porteus formulation and use it as the basis
for model-free value-based RL.

Our
key contributions
are summarized below.

•

Bellman-type operators:

We introduce two Q-value extensions of the exponential-utility Bellman equation from
26
and show that the associated operators are contractions in the
L_{\infty}
and sup-log/Thompson metrics, respectively. These results provide the structural foundation for a principled value-based treatment of exponential-utility RL.

•

Fixed-point characterization:

We prove that a greedy stationary policy induced by these fixed points is optimal for the exponential-utility objective among stationary policies.

•

Q-learning–style algorithms:

We develop the first Q-learning–style algorithms for exponential-utility optimization in discounted MDPs, bridging a gap between classical risk-sensitive control and model-free RL. Our methods include a two-timescale and a one-timescale variant.

•

Two-timescale analysis:

For the proposed two-timescale algorithm, we establish almost-sure convergence and finite-time convergence rates via timescale separation. These results extend classical Q-learning-style guarantees to the exponential-utility setting.

•

One-timescale analysis:

For the proposed one-timescale algorithm, whose dynamics are governed by a sublinear power-law operator, we establish almost-sure convergence using a novel analysis based on local Lipschitzness, monotonicity, homogeneity, and Dini derivatives. We also provide scalar finite-time analysis that isolates the power-law drift and highlights the challenges caused by the lack of a directly usable global contraction.

Related work.

For exponential utility optimization in an average reward MDP, the reader is referred to
6
;
5
;
23
;
24
, see also
8

In comparison to the average reward case, the research on incorporating exponential utility criterion in the context of a discounted MDP is relatively limited. Some notable works in this direction include
9
;
26
;
15
. Additional references may be found in
19
and the survey
3
. To the best of our knowledge, for an exponential utility formulation, a dynamic programming approach in conjunction with stochastic approximation has not been explored in a discounted RL context. On the other hand, the policy gradient approach has been studied with exponential costs in
12

25
and more recently, in
17

To the best of our knowledge, there is no previous work that employs the dynamic programming approach to find the best stationary policy for exponential utility, and more importantly, we are not aware of any tabular RL algorithms that finds such an optimal policy. The aforementioned works predominantly operate using the policy gradient approach, in particular, to search over a class of smoothly parameterized policies, and do not possess the guarantees for convergence to the optimal stationary policy for exponential utility.

2
Discounted reward MDP with exponential utility

We consider the MDP
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,\gamma)
having a finite state space
\mathcal{S}
with cardinality
S=|\mathcal{S}|
and a finite action space
\mathcal{A}
with cardinality
A=|\mathcal{A}|
. Further,
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition kernel, where
\Delta(\mathcal{U})
denotes the set of distributions over a finite set
\mathcal{U};
in particular,
\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
specifies the probability that the state of the MDP changes from
s
to
s^{\prime}
under action
a.
Finally,
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
the discount factor. Our work is focused on the expected exponential utility of the infinite-horizon discounted sum of rewards generated by the MDP under a stationary policy. In particular, we are interested in policies that optimize (over the class of stationary policies) the aforementioned expected exponential utility, and our goal is to present model-free RL algorithms that learn such optimal stationary policies.

To make this precise, we denote the set of all stationary Markovian policies (including randomized ones) for the MDP
\mathcal{M}
by
\Pi_{\rm st}
, the positive orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{SA}
by
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}:=(0,\infty)^{SA}
and consider the map
X_{(\cdot)}:\Pi_{\rm st}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
defined by
X_{\pi}(s_{0},a_{0})=\mathbb{E}[\exp\{-\hat{\theta}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{j}r(s_{j},a_{j})\}]
, where
\hat{\theta}>0
is the risk sensitivity parameter and
\{(s_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
is a state-action trajectory of the MDP
\mathcal{M}
obtained by starting at the state
s_{0}
at time
t=0
, applying action
a_{0}
at the state
s_{0}
and applying the policy
\pi
from time
1
onwards. Thus, for any given
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
,
X_{\pi}(s,a)
is the expected exponential utility of the discounted sum of rewards obtained when one uses the action
a
at the state
s
and thereafter applies the stationary policy
\pi
.

We are interested in a stationary policy
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\rm st}
such that
X_{\pi^{*}}\preceq X_{\pi}
for all
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
, where
x_{1}\preceq x_{2}
(equivalently,
x_{2}\succeq x_{1}
) denotes that
x_{1}(s,a)\leq x_{2}(s,a)
for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
. To this end, we associate with each
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
the operator
F_{\pi}:\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}\to\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
defined by

\displaystyle F_{\pi}(x)(s,a)=\exp\left(-\hat{\theta}r(s,a)\right)\sum_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})\left[x(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]^{\gamma}.

(1)

As we show below, for each
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
,
X_{\pi}
is the unique fixed point of
F_{\pi}
. Motivated by ideas from dynamic programming in the context of standard risk-neutral discounted MDPs, we pass to the minimum over
\pi
on the right hand side of (
1
). Note that the minimum on the right hand side of (
1
) is achieved if the distribution
\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})
puts all its mass on
\argmin_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}x(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
. These considerations lead us to introduce the optimality operator
F:\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}\to\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
defined by

\displaystyle F(x)(s,a)=\exp\left(-\hat{\theta}r(s,a)\right)\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\left[\min_{a^{\prime}}x(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]^{\gamma}.

(2)

Unlike the classical Bellman operator, the operator
F
acts through the nonlinear power-law term
x^{\gamma}
, leading to a fundamentally different geometry.

To capture this structure, we consider the sup-log or Thompson metric
d(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}\times\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
on
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
defined by

d(x_{1},x_{2}):=\|\ln x_{1}-\ln x_{2}\|_{\infty},\qquad x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}.

(3)

It is not difficult to see that
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
is a complete metric space under the sup-log metric
d
. Our first result shows that, under this geometry, the operators
F
and
F_{\pi}
satisfy the structural properties that drive the rest of our analysis.

Proposition 2.1

(
Structural properties of
F
and
F_{\pi}
)
.

The following statements are true.

1.

F
is a
\gamma
-contraction in
d(\cdot,\cdot)
, that is,
\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
we have

d(F(x_{1}),F(x_{2}))\leq\gamma d(x_{1},x_{2}).

2.

For each
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
,
F_{\pi}
is a
\gamma
-contraction in
d(\cdot,\cdot)
.

3.

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
. Then
F
and
F_{\pi}
are monotone, that is, for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
such that
x_{1}\preceq x_{2}
, we have
F(x_{1})\preceq F(x_{2})
and
F_{\pi}(x_{1})\preceq F_{\pi}(x_{2})
.

4.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
, we have
F(x)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}}F_{\pi}(x)
, where the minimum is taken element-wise.

5.

F
is homogeneous, that is, for each
c>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
,
F(cx)=c^{\gamma}F(x)
.

Proposition
2.1
identifies the geometry under
which
F
and
F_{\pi}
,
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
, become contractive. Since
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
is complete under
d
, Banach’s fixed-point theorem implies that
F
and each
F_{\pi}
admit unique fixed points, denoted by
x^{*}
and
\bar{x}_{\pi}
, respectively. Let
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\rm st}
be a stationary deterministic policy that greedily selects an action in
\argmin_{a}x^{*}(s,a)
at each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Our next main result shows that this greedy policy is optimal for the exponential-utility objective among all stationary policies.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\rm st}
, and let
x^{*}
and
\pi^{*}
be as defined above. Then, we have

x^{*}=X_{\pi^{*}}\preceq X_{\pi}=\bar{x}_{\pi}.

(4)

The result above also identifies the fixed points of
F
and
F_{\pi}
with the optimal and policy-specific expected exponential utilities, respectively. It also
provides motivation for the rest of the paper where we present two model-free RL algorithms for approximating the fixed point
x^{*}
of the operator
F
. One of these two algorithms makes use of a related operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{SA}\to\mathbb{R}^{SA}
originally examined by
26
. In order to align our subsequent treatment to that of
26
;
9
, we henceforth set the risk sensitivity parameter
\hat{\theta}
to
\frac{\theta}{\gamma},
with
\theta>0
. With this substitution, the operator
T
is given by

\displaystyle T(Q)(s,a)={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln\left[\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}[r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right]

(5)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle r(s,a)-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln\left[\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\exp\left(-\theta\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\right].

(6)

As mentioned above, the operators
F
and
T
are closely related. Direct substitution shows that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
, we have

F(x)=\exp\left[-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}T\left(\frac{-\gamma}{\theta}\ln x\right)\right],\ T(Q)=-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\,\ln\Bigl[F\Bigl(\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\,Q\Bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr],

(7)

where the operations are understood to be applied element-wise. The relations (
7
) also imply that
Q^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
is a fixed point of
T
if and only if
Q^{*}=-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln x^{*}
. This immediately shows that the operator
T
possesses a unique fixed point
Q^{*}
which can be obtained from the unique fixed point
x^{*}
as mentioned above. In fact,
the operator
T
is a
\gamma
-contraction in
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, that is,
\forall Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{SA},
we have

\|T(Q_{1})-T(Q_{2})\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|_{\infty}.

The reader is referred to Appendix
A.1
for a proof.

2.1
Illustrative Example: Risk-averse behavior induced by
T

The following example shows the qualitative difference between a policy
obtained from the classical risk-neutral Bellman operator and one obtained from
the risk-sensitive formulation represented by
T
.

Consider a discounted MDP with state space
\mathcal{S}=\{s,s_{\textnormal{bad}}\}
, discount factor
\gamma=0.9
, and risk parameter
\theta=0.1
. The state
s_{\textnormal{bad}}
is absorbing and yields a reward of
-10
, i.e., there is a single action
a
such that

r(s_{\textnormal{bad}},a)=-10,\qquad\mathcal{P}(s_{\textnormal{bad}}\mid s_{\textnormal{bad}},a)=1.

At state
s
, there are two available actions,
\mathrm{safe}
and
\mathrm{risk}
. The
\mathrm{safe}
action yields zero reward and leaves the state unchanged:

r(s,\mathrm{safe})=0,\qquad\mathcal{P}(s\mid s,\mathrm{safe})=1.

In contrast, the
\mathrm{risk}
action yields a reward of
1
and induces the following transition probabilities:

r(s,\mathrm{risk})=1,\qquad\mathcal{P}(s_{\textnormal{bad}}\mid s,\mathrm{risk})=0.01,\qquad\mathcal{P}(s\mid s,\mathrm{risk})=0.99.

Solving the classical discounted risk-neutral Bellman equations shows that the optimal
Q
-values satisfy

Q^{*}_{\mathrm{rn}}(s_{\textnormal{bad}},a)=-100,\qquad Q^{*}_{\mathrm{rn}}(s,\mathrm{safe})\approx 0.826,\qquad Q^{*}_{\mathrm{rn}}(s,\mathrm{risk})\approx 0.917,

where
\mathrm{rn}
denotes risk neutral. Thus, the optimal risk-neutral policy selects the
\mathrm{risk}
action at state
s
.

In contrast, the fixed point
Q^{*}
of our operator
T
satisfies

Q^{*}(s_{\textnormal{bad}},a)=-100,\qquad Q^{*}(s,\mathrm{safe})=0,\qquad Q^{*}(s,\mathrm{risk})\approx-47.59.

Thus, in this case, the optimal policy selects the
\mathrm{safe}
action at state
s
.

In summary, while the risk-neutral formulation favors the
\mathrm{risk}
action due to its higher expected return, the operator
T
assigns it a substantially lower value by penalizing the possibility of rare but severe negative outcomes.

3
Model-free algorithms for optimizing exponential utility

The previous section motivated the fixed points of the
T
and
F
operators.
In this section, we introduce two model-free algorithms for finding them.

Observe that, by treating
s^{\prime}
as a random variable, the summations inside the definitions (
5
) and (
2
) of
T
and
F
,
respectively, can be viewed as conditional expectations. The key difference is
where this expectation appears. In the definition of
T
, it appears inside the

\log
function; hence, unlike classical Q-learning, a single transition
sample does not directly yield a stochastic estimate of the full operator value.
In contrast, in the definition of
F
, the expectation appears linearly at the
outermost level, so a single transition sample gives a natural stochastic
estimate of
F(x)
. Keeping this distinction in mind, we now propose two
model-free algorithms to find the fixed points of
T
and

F
.

Let
\{(s_{n},a_{n})\}_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of state-action pairs obtained using a (possibly time-varying) behavior policy. Specifically, let
(s_{0},a_{0})
be an arbitrary state-action pair and, for
n\geq 0,
suppose
s_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{n},a_{n})
and
a_{n+1}\sim\pi_{n}(\cdot|s_{n+1}),
where
\pi_{n}
is the behavior policy at time instance
n.

3.1
Two-timescale algorithm for estimating
Q^{*}

The first algorithm is motivated by classical Q-learning. Since
Q^{*}
is the
unique fixed point of
T
, a natural approach is to perform a stochastic
fixed-point iteration for
T
. The main difference from the risk-neutral case is
that the conditional expectation in
T
lies inside a logarithm. To handle this,
we introduce an auxiliary iterate
g_{n}
that tracks this inner expectation on a
faster timescale. This leads to

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}

\displaystyle=Q_{n}+\alpha_{n}\left[-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln g_{n}-Q_{n}\right],

(8)

\displaystyle g_{n+1}

\displaystyle=g_{n}+\beta_{n}e_{s_{n},a_{n}}[\hat{G}_{n}-g_{n}(s_{n},a_{n})],

where
\hat{G}_{n}=G(Q_{n},s_{n},a_{n},s_{n+1})
and

G(Q,s,a,s^{\prime})=\exp\left[-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)-\theta\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right].

(9)

Here,
e_{s,a}
denotes the standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{SA}
whose

(s,a)
-th entry is
1
. The faster recursion for
g_{n}
estimates the
conditional expectation inside
T(Q_{n})
, while the slower recursion for
Q_{n}

uses
\log g_{n}
as a plug-in estimate and moves toward
Q^{*}
.

3.2
One-timescale algorithm for estimating
x^{*}

The second algorithm is motivated by the
F
-operator. Since
x^{*}
is the
unique fixed point of
F
, and since the conditional expectation in
F
appears
linearly at the outermost level, a single transition sample provides a natural
stochastic estimate of
F(x_{n})(s_{n},a_{n})
. This suggests the direct
one-timescale recursion

x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}e_{s_{n},a_{n}}[\hat{F}_{n}-x_{n}(s_{n},a_{n})],

(10)

where

\hat{F}_{n}=\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s_{n},a_{n})\right)\left[\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{n}(s_{n+1},a^{\prime})\right]^{\gamma}.

(11)

This update should be contrasted with the sample-based multiplicative update
suggested by the sup-log contraction of
F
:

x_{n+1}=x_{n}^{1-\alpha_{n}}\hat{F}_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},

where powers and products are understood componentwise. Although this update
is natural from the sup-log viewpoint, it is not a stochastic approximation to
the corresponding logarithmic fixed-point iteration. Indeed, in logarithmic
coordinates it involves
\log\hat{F}_{n}
, whereas the desired drift contains

\log F(x_{n})
; in general,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\hat{F}_{n}\mid s_{n},a_{n}\right]\neq\log F(x_{n})(s_{n},a_{n}).

Thus, exploiting the sup-log contraction of
F
in a model-free manner would
again require estimating the conditional expectation inside
F
before taking
the logarithm, leading naturally to an additional tracking recursion. The
additive update (
10
) avoids this issue by working
directly in the original
x
-coordinates, yielding a genuine one-timescale
algorithm. Once
x_{n}
is close to
x^{*}
, an estimate of
Q^{*}
is
obtained through the transformation

Q^{*}=-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln x^{*}
. The price for this simplicity is
analytical: convergence can no longer be obtained by directly invoking the
sup-log contraction property of
F
.

4
Almost-sure Convergence and Finite-time Convergence Rates

We now discuss the convergence properties of the two proposed algorithms.
The two-timescale recursion inherits a global contraction structure from
the
T
-operator, enabling a relatively standard stochastic approximation
analysis based on timescale separation. In contrast, the one-timescale recursion is driven by the power-law operator
F
. Although

F
is contractive in the log-sup metric, this contraction is not directly
aligned with the additive update in the original
x
-coordinates. Consequently,
standard global contraction arguments no longer apply, making both the
asymptotic and finite-time analysis substantially more delicate.

4.1
Analysis of our two-timescale fixed-point method

Consider
(Q_{n},g_{n})_{n\geq 0}
generated by
(
8
). Let

C_{\max}:=\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}}{1-\gamma},

(12)

where

\|r\|_{\infty}:=\max_{s,a}|r(s,a)|
.

Assumption 1

(
Controlled Markovian sampling
)
.

Fix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}
with
\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\max}
and suppose the iterates
are frozen, i.e.,
Q_{n}\equiv Q
for all
n\geq 0
. Then the resulting

(s_{n},a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
sequence forms a Markov chain on

\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
with transition kernel
K_{Q}
and unique stationary distribution

\pi_{Q}
. Moreover:

1.

(
Uniform ergodicity
)
There exist constants
C<\infty
and
\rho\in(0,1)
such that

\sup_{y\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\|K_{Q}^{\ell}(\cdot|y)-\pi_{Q}(\cdot)\|_{\rm TV}\leq C\rho^{\ell},\qquad\forall\ell\geq 0,

uniformly over all
Q
satisfying
\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\max}
.

2.

(
Continuity
)
If
Q_{n}\to Q
with

\|Q_{n}\|_{\infty},\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\max}
, then

K_{Q_{n}}(y,y^{\prime})\to K_{Q}(y,y^{\prime})

uniformly over
(y,y^{\prime})\in(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{2}
.

The next result establishes almost-sure convergence of the two-timescale
iterates. The proof combines timescale separation with the global

\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-contraction of the
T
-operator.

Theorem 4.1

(
Two-timescale asymptotic convergence
)
.

Let

\max\left\{\|Q_{0}\|_{\infty},\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\|\ln g_{0}\|_{\infty}\right\}\leq C_{\max},

and suppose

\sum_{n}\alpha_{n}=\sum_{n}\beta_{n}=\infty,\qquad\sum_{n}(\alpha_{n}^{2}+\beta_{n}^{2})<\infty,\qquad\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}}\to 0.

Further suppose Assumption
1
holds. Then

Q_{n}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}Q^{*},\qquad\left\|-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln g_{n}-TQ_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}0.

We next establish finite-time convergence rates in the generative-model
setting, where
(s_{n},a_{n})
is sampled i.i.d. from a distribution

\nu
satisfying
\lambda:=\min_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\nu(s,a)>0
.
This allows us to focus on the impact of the nonlinear
T
-operator and the
two-timescale coupling, while avoiding additional technicalities arising from
Markovian noise, which can typically be handled using standard techniques

(
30
;
34
)
.

Theorem 4.2

(
Two-timescale finite-time rates
)
.

In the generative-model setting described above, let

\alpha_{n}=(n+1)^{-\alpha}
and
\beta_{n}=(n+1)^{-\beta}
, where

\frac{1}{2}<\beta<\alpha<1
. Then there exist constants

C_{g},C_{Q}^{(1)},C_{Q}^{(2)}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left\|g_{n}-e^{-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}TQ_{n}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq C_{g}\sqrt{\beta_{n}},

and

\mathbb{E}\|Q_{n}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{Q}^{(1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}n^{1-\alpha}\right)+C_{Q}^{(2)}\sqrt{\beta_{n}}=O(n^{-\beta/2}).

The precise constant definitions can be found in the appendix.

By choosing
\beta

5
Outline of the Proofs

Here we discuss the main ideas underlying our structural and convergence analysis. We first establish the contraction, monotonicity, and homogeneity properties of the operators
F
and
T
, and use them to characterize an optimal stationary policy through their fixed points. We then discuss the convergence analysis of our proposed algorithms. Note that the two-timescale method leverages the global
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
contraction of
T
, enabling a relatively standard stochastic approximation analysis via timescale separation. In contrast, the one-timescale recursion is driven by the nonlinear power-law operator
F
, whose contraction holds only in the sup-log metric and is not directly compatible with additive updates in the original coordinates.

5.1
Structural properties and optimality characterization

The proof first establishes that the operators
F
and
F_{\pi}
are
\gamma
-contractions in the sup-log metric by converting multiplicative perturbations into additive logarithmic bounds. The key observation is that if two vectors are close in logarithmic scale, then their componentwise ratios are uniformly controlled, and this control is preserved under the minimum and power-law operations defining the operators. Monotonicity follows directly from the construction, while homogeneity follows from the power-law form of
F
. Next, the proof characterizes the fixed point
\bar{x}_{\pi}
of
F_{\pi}
as the expected exponential-utility value
X_{\pi}
associated with policy
\pi
. This is achieved by combining Jensen’s inequality with repeated applications of the Bellman-type recursion and the monotonicity of
F_{\pi}
. Finally, using the fact that the greedy policy
\pi^{*}
satisfies
F_{\pi^{*}}(x^{*})=F(x^{*})
, we obtain
X_{\pi^{*}}=\bar{x}_{\pi^{*}}=x^{*}
. Monotonicity of
F_{\pi}
then shows that
x^{*}\preceq X_{\pi}
for every stationary policy
\pi
, proving the optimality of
\pi^{*}
.

5.2
Two-timescale analysis

The analysis of the two-timescale method is comparatively more standard due to the global
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
contraction of the operator
T
. The key idea is to exploit the separation between the slower
(Q_{n})
recursion and the faster
(g_{n})
recursion. Since

\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\beta_{n}}\to 0,

the slower iterate
Q_{n}
appears nearly static from the perspective of the faster recursion. Consequently, conditioned on a quasi-static
Q_{n}
, the faster iterate
g_{n}
tracks the equilibrium

g^{*}(Q_{n})=\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}TQ_{n}\right).

To formalize this intuition, we define the tracking error

e_{n}:=g_{n}-g^{*}(Q_{n}),

and analyze its dynamics on the faster timescale. Using the linear structure of the
g
-update together with standard martingale arguments, we show that

\|e_{n}\|_{\infty}\to 0\qquad\text{a.s.}

Substituting this asymptotic relation into the slower recursion yields

Q_{n+1}=Q_{n}+\alpha_{n}[TQ_{n}-Q_{n}+\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}],

where
\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}
is a logarithmic transformation of
e_{n}
. Since
\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}\to 0
, the limiting ODE associated with
(Q_{n})
is

\dot{Q}(t)=TQ(t)-Q(t).

Since
T
is a
\gamma
-contraction in the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
norm, the above ODE admits a unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
Q^{*}
. Standard stochastic approximation arguments
(
7
)
therefore imply

Q_{n}\to Q^{*}\qquad\text{a.s.}

For the finite-time analysis, we again exploit timescale separation. In particular, we first derive a tracking bound for the faster recursion of the form

\mathbb{E}\|g_{n}-g^{*}(Q_{n})\|_{\infty}=O(\sqrt{\beta_{n}}),

and then propagate this error into the slower recursion. Combining the resulting perturbation bounds with the global contraction of
T
yields the finite-time convergence rates for
(Q_{n})
.

5.3
One-timescale analysis

The one-timescale recursion is significantly more delicate to analyze.
Unlike the two-timescale method, the update is driven by the nonlinear power-law operator
F
, whose contraction holds only in the sup-log metric and is therefore not directly compatible with additive stochastic approximation updates in the original coordinates. Moreover, the map
x\mapsto x^{\gamma}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, is not globally Lipschitz near the boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{SA}_{++}
, preventing a direct application of standard global contraction arguments.

We first establish stability of the iterates by showing that the recursion remains within a compact subset

\mathcal{K}:=[C_{\ell},C_{u}]^{SA}\subset\mathbb{R}^{SA}_{++},

bounded away from the origin. This allows us to show that
F
is Lipschitz on a suitable neighborhood of
\mathcal{K}
and that the limiting ODE

\dot{x}(t)=D_{\pi_{x(t)}}[F(x(t))-x(t)]

is well posed. We then show that
\mathcal{K}
is positively invariant under the above ODE.

Next, we establish global asymptotic stability of
x^{*}
for the above ODE. Since standard quadratic Lyapunov arguments are unavailable, we instead exploit the monotonicity and homogeneity properties of
F
from Proposition
2.1
. In particular, homogeneity implies

F(cx)=c^{\gamma}F(x),

while monotonicity gives

x\preceq y\implies F(x)\preceq F(y).

Using these properties, we compare the trajectory
x(t)
with scaled versions of the fixed point
x^{*}
. Defining

m(t):=\min_{i}\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{i}^{*}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad M(t):=\max_{i}\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{i}^{*}},

we obtain

m(t)x^{*}\preceq x(t)\preceq M(t)x^{*}.

Applying monotonicity and homogeneity to the left inequality yields

F(x(t))\succeq F(m(t)x^{*})=m(t)^{\gamma}F(x^{*})=m(t)^{\gamma}x^{*}.

Using this relation together with the ODE dynamics and Danskin’s theorem, we derive the lower right Dini-derivative inequality

D^{+}m(t):=\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{m(t+h)-m(t)}{h}\geq\ell_{\mathcal{K}}\big(m(t)^{\gamma}-m(t)\big),

for some constant
\ell_{\mathcal{K}}>0
. A symmetric argument yields

D^{+}M(t)\leq u_{\mathcal{K}}\big(M(t)^{\gamma}-M(t)\big),

for some
u_{\mathcal{K}}>0
.

We then compare these inequalities with the scalar dynamics

\dot{z}(t)=z(t)^{\gamma}-z(t),

whose unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium is
1
. The comparison argument implies

m(t)\to 1,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad M(t)\to 1,

which in turn yields

x(t)\to x^{*}.

Standard stochastic approximation arguments
(
7
)
now allow us to show
x_{n}\to x^{*}
a.s.

We also provide a scalar finite-time analysis that isolates the effect of the power-law drift and highlights the main obstacle in extending standard stochastic approximation rate arguments to the full recursion. In particular, unlike the two-timescale method, the one-timescale recursion does not admit a directly usable global contraction inequality. Nevertheless, by carefully exploiting the structure of the scalar drift, we establish an
\tilde{O}(n^{-1/2})
convergence rate.

6
Conclusions

Inspired by
26
, we derived two Bellman-type equations catering to an exponential utility formulation. These equations are amenable for the design of RL algorithms and, more importantly, we showed that one can infer an optimal stationary policy for exponential utility using the fixed points of the aforementioned Bellman-type equations. Next, we proposed two tabular RL algorithms for finding these fixed points. The first algorithm operates on two timescales, while the second uses a single timescale. Both algorithms were shown to asymptotically converge to the respective unique fixed points. Further, we establish a rate result for the two-timescale algorithm and a partial rate result for the single timescale variant. The rate results require a generative model and the single-timescale rate is derived only for the scalar case. Nevertheless, the proofs of these results involved significant deviations from standard analysis, especially for the single-timescale algorithm, and the foundations we lay through the novel proof techniques should inspire future research to overcome these limitations.

(27)

and it satisfies

a.s. Hence,
(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
forms a Martingale-difference sequence with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n}).
Also, since

from Lemma
B.1
, we have from (
25
) and (

This relation also shows that
(M_{n})
is square-integrable and satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|M_{n+1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\leq 4\exp\left[\frac{2\theta}{\gamma}C_{\max}\right]\leq 4\exp\left[\frac{2\theta}{\gamma}C_{\max}\right]\bigl(1+\|z_{n}\|_{\infty}^{2}\bigr)\quad\text{a.s.}

Finally, since
z_{n}\in\mathcal{K}
from Lemma
B.1
for each
n\geq 0
and since
\alpha_{n}/\beta_{n}\to 0
,
we have
\epsilon_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}0
.

Now define the averaged drift
\tilde{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{2SA}\to\mathbb{R}^{2SA}
by

\tilde{\phi}(z):=\sum_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\pi_{Q}(s,a)\,\bar{\phi}(z,s,a)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
D_{\pi_{Q}}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\theta}{\gamma}\,TQ\right)-g\right]\end{bmatrix},

where
\pi_{Q},
recall, denotes the stationary distribution of the Markov chain with
transition kernel
K_{Q}
, and
D_{\pi_{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{SA\times SA}
is the diagonal
matrix whose
(s,a)
-th diagonal entry equals
\pi_{Q}(s,a)
. It then follows from
[
7
, Corollary 8.1 and Remark 3, Section 2.2]

that the sequence
(z_{n})
converges almost surely to a connected, internally
chain transitive invariant set of the ODE

\dot{z}(t)=\tilde{\phi}\bigl(z(t)\bigr).

The above ODE admits

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}Q\\[2.84526pt]
\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{\theta}{\gamma}\,TQ\right]\end{bmatrix}:\;Q\in\mathbb{R}^{SA}\right\}

as its unique global attractor. Indeed, since
\dot{Q}(t)=0
, the
Q
-component
remains constant along trajectories. For a fixed
Q
, the dynamics of the

g
-component reduces to

\dot{g}(t)=D_{\pi_{Q}}\Bigl(\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{\theta}{\gamma}\,TQ\right]-g(t)\Bigr),

which is a linear ODE with

\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{\theta}{\gamma}\,TQ\right]
as its globally asymptotically
stable equilibrium. Therefore, we conclude that
z_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathcal{A}
, or
equivalently,

\left\|g_{n}-\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{\theta}{\gamma}\,TQ_{n}\right]\right\|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}0,

(28)

as claimed.

We now discuss
(Q_{n})
’s behavior from the
(\alpha_{n})
-stepsize perspective. The update rule for
Q_{n}
can be rewritten as

Q_{n+1}=Q_{n}+\alpha_{n}[TQ_{n}-Q_{n}+\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}],

where

\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}=\left[-\frac{\gamma}{\theta}\ln g_{n}-TQ_{n}\right].

Now, from (
28
), we have
\epsilon^{\prime}_{n}\overset{\textnormal{a.s.}\ }{\longrightarrow}0.
Hence, from
[
7
, Corollary 8.1 and Remark 3 of Section 2.2 ]
, we have that
(Q_{n})
converges to a connected, internally chain transitive invariant set of the ODE

\dot{Q}(t)=TQ(t)-Q(t).

(29)

To complete the proof, it remains to show that
Q^{*}
is the unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of the above ODE.

For
t\geq 0,
define
z(t):=Q(t)-Q^{*}
and
u(t):=\|z(t)\|_{\infty}.
Since
TQ^{*}=Q^{*},
we have

\dot{z}_{i}(t)=(TQ(t))_{i}-(TQ^{*})_{i}-z_{i}(t),\quad i\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

(30)

Since
Q(\cdot)
is absolutely continuous and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is Lipchitz continuous, it follows that
u(\cdot)
is absolutely continuous and hence differential for a.e.
t\geq 0.
Fix such a
t,
and let
i_{t}
be any index satisfying

|z_{i_{t}}(t)|=\|z(t)\|_{\infty}=u(t).

Then,

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}u(t)=\textnormal{sign}(z_{i_{t}}(t))\dot{z}_{i_{t}}(t).

Substituting (
30
) with
i=i_{t},
and using the
\gamma
-contraction property of
T
, we obtain

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}u(t)=\textnormal{sign}(z_{i_{t}}(t))\bigg((TQ(t))_{i_{t}}-(TQ^{*})_{i_{t}}\bigg)-u(t)\leq-(1-\gamma)u(t).

(31)

Since this is true for a.e.
t\geq 0,
applying the differential form of Gronwall’s inequality yields

\|Q(t)-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{-(1-\gamma)t}\|Q(0)-Q^{*}\|_{\infty},\quad t\geq 0.

Thus, every solution trajectory of (
29
) converges exponentially fast to
Q^{*}.
In particular,
Q^{*}
is the unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of (
29
), as desired.
∎

C^{(1)}_{Q}:=2C_{\max}e^{\frac{(1-\gamma)}{(1-\alpha)}},\quad C^{(2)}_{Q}:=\frac{\gamma}{\theta}e^{\frac{\theta C_{\max}}{\gamma}}C_{g}C_{\Gamma,\alpha}^{0,1/2}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

We prove the three statements one by one.

Statement 1:

Recall that for
0\leq k<N,

\Gamma^{(\beta)}_{k:N}:=\prod_{n=k}^{N-1}\left(1-2\lambda\beta_{n}+2\beta^{2}_{n}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{k:N}:=\prod_{n=k}^{N-1}\left(1-(1-\gamma)\alpha_{n}\right)

Using the fact that
1-x\leq e^{-x},
we have

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(\beta)}_{k:N}\leq\prod_{n=k}^{N-1}e^{-2\lambda\beta_{n}+2\beta^{2}_{n}}=\left(e^{-2\lambda\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}}\right)\cdot\left(e^{2\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta^{2}_{n}}\right)

(44)

and

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{k:N}:=\prod_{n=k}^{N-1}\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\alpha_{n}\right)=\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\right).

(45)

Taking
\beta_{n}=(n+1)^{-\beta},
with
\beta\in(1/2,1)
gives

\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta^{2}_{n}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2\beta}}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2\beta}}\\
\leq 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{n}^{n+1}\frac{1}{x^{2\beta}}dx=1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{2\beta}}dx=1+\frac{1}{2\beta-1}=\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1},

(46)

Hence, setting
C_{\Gamma,\beta}:=e^{\left(\frac{4\beta}{2\beta-1}\right)}
gives

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(\beta)}_{k:N}\leq C_{\Gamma,\beta}e^{-2\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{k:N}\leq e^{-(1-\gamma)\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}}.

(47)

This completes the proof of
Statement 1
. Now, we focus on the next set of bounds.

Statement 2:

Note that, for
k\leq n<N,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}f_{n}\cdot e^{-2\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\leq\bigg[\max_{k\leq n<N}f_{n}\ e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\bigg]\bigg(\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}\ e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\bigg).

(48)

We bound these factors one by one. First, we define
t_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\beta_{m}.
Then, for
0\leq k<N,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-\lambda[t_{N-1}-t_{n}]}\cdot[t_{n}-t_{n-1}]

(49)

Treating the RHS as a Riemann sum, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{-\lambda[t_{N-1}-t_{n}]}\cdot[t_{n}-t_{n-1}]\leq\int_{0}^{t_{N-1}}e^{\lambda(t-t_{N-1})}dt=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(1-e^{(t_{0}-t_{N-1})}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}.

(50)

Now, recall that
f_{n}=\alpha_{n}^{a}\ \beta_{n}^{b}=(n+1)^{-(a\alpha+b\beta)},
and define

h_{n}:=f_{n}\ e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}},\quad\text{for }k\leq n<N.

(51)

For each
k\leq n<N,
consider two cases.

Case 1: When
n\geq\lceil N/2\rceil.

We have
n+1\geq N/2+1>(N+1)/2.
Thus,

\frac{h_{n}}{f_{N}}=\left(\frac{f_{n}}{f_{N}}\right)\cdot e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{f_{n}}{f_{N}}=\left(\frac{N+1}{n+1}\right)^{a\alpha+b\beta}<2^{-(a\alpha+b\beta)},

(52)

where
(a)
follows since
e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\leq 1.

Case 2: When
n\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor.

Here, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}\geq\sum_{m=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N-1}\beta_{m}=\sum_{m=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N-1}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{\beta}}\geq\sum_{m=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N-1}\frac{1}{N^{\beta}}>\frac{N}{2N^{\beta}}.

(53)

Using the fact that
f_{n}\leq 1,
we have

\frac{h_{n}}{f_{N}}=\frac{f_{n}}{f_{N}}\cdot e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{1}{f_{N}}\cdot e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}=(N+1)^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\\
\overset{(b)}{\leq}(N+1)^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}N^{1-\beta}}\overset{(c)}{\leq}{(2N)}^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}N^{1-\beta}},

(54)

where
(a)
follows since
f_{n}\leq 1,

(b)
follows from (
53
), and
(c)
follows since
N\geq 1.
Finally, we can show using calculus that for
p>0,
the function
x\mapsto(2x)^{p}\cdot\exp(-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{1-\beta})
attains maximum at
x=\left(\frac{2p}{\lambda(1-\beta)}\right)^{1/(1-\beta)}
to get

\displaystyle\frac{h_{n}}{f_{N}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left[(2x)^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{1-\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=(2x)^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{1-\beta}}\bigg|_{x=\left(\frac{2p}{\lambda(1-\beta)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}}=\left[\frac{4(a\alpha+b\beta)}{2^{\beta}\lambda e(1-\beta)}\right]^{\frac{a\alpha+b\beta}{1-\beta}}.

(55)

Hence, for all
k\leq n<N,
we have

h_{n}\leq C_{a,b}f_{N},\quad\text{where }\quad C_{a,b}:=\max\left\{2^{-(a\alpha+b\beta)},\left[\frac{4(a\alpha+b\beta)}{2^{\beta}\lambda e(1-\beta)}\right]^{\frac{a\alpha+b\beta}{1-\beta}}\right\}.

(56)

Finally, define
C_{\Gamma,\beta}^{a,b}:=C_{a,b}C_{\Gamma,\beta}.
Now, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}f_{n}\ \Gamma^{(\beta)}_{n+1:N}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}C_{\Gamma,\beta}\bigg[\max_{k\leq n<N}f_{n}\ e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\bigg]\bigg(\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\beta_{n}\ e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}C_{\Gamma,\beta}\bigg[\max_{k\leq n<N}f_{n}\ e^{-\lambda\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\beta_{m}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}C_{\Gamma,\beta}\cdot\left[\max_{k\leq n<N}C_{a,b}f_{N}\right]=C_{\Gamma,\beta}^{a,b}f_{N},

(57)

where
(a)
is obtained by combining from (
47
) and (
48
),
(b)
follows by combining (
49
) and (
50
), and
(c)
follows from (
56
).

To finish the proof of
Statement 2
, we write

\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\cdot e^{-(1-\gamma)\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\alpha_{m}}\leq\bigg[\max_{k\leq n<N}f_{n}\ e^{-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\alpha_{m}}\bigg]\\
\times\bigg(\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ e^{-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\alpha_{m}})\bigg).

(58)

Treating the second factor in the above expression gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}\ e^{-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\alpha_{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}e^{-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}\left[t_{N-1}-t_{n}\right]}\cdot\left[t_{n}-t_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N-1}}e^{\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}[t-t_{N-1}]}\ dt\leq\frac{2}{1-\gamma},

(59)

where we let
t_{n}:=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\alpha_{m}.

Now, for the second factor, we define
\bar{h}_{n}:=f_{n}\ e^{-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}\sum_{m=n+1}^{N-1}\alpha_{m}},
and claim that there exists a constant
C_{\Gamma,\alpha}^{a,b}>0,
such that for all
k\leq n<N,

\bar{h}_{n}\leq C_{\Gamma,\alpha}^{a,b}\ f_{N}.

Using a similar analysis as earlier, we can show that

\frac{\bar{h}_{n}}{f_{N}}\leq\begin{cases}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left[(2x)^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}x^{1-\alpha}}\right]&,\text{when }n\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor\\
2^{-(a\alpha+b\beta)}&,\text{when }n\geq\lceil N/2\rceil\end{cases}.

(60)

Since the function
x\mapsto(2x)^{a\alpha+b\beta}\cdot e^{\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}x^{1-\alpha}}
attains global maximum at
x=\left[\frac{2(a\alpha+b\beta)}{(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},
we conclude that

\displaystyle\max_{k\leq n\leq N}\bar{h}_{n}\leq f_{N}\cdot\max\left\{2^{-(a\alpha+b\beta)},\left[\frac{4(a\alpha+b\beta)}{2^{\alpha}e(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)}\right]^{\left(\frac{a\alpha+b\beta}{1-\alpha}\right)}\right\}.

(61)

Thus, combining (
47
), (
58
), (
B.3
), and (
61
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n=k}^{N-1}\alpha_{n}f_{n}\ \Gamma^{(\alpha)}_{n+1:N}\leq C_{\Gamma,\alpha}^{a,b}\ f_{N},

(62)

where

C_{\Gamma,\alpha}^{a,b}:=\frac{2}{1-\gamma}\cdot\max\left\{2^{-(a\alpha+b\beta)},\left[\frac{4(a\alpha+b\beta)}{2^{\alpha}e(1-\gamma)(1-\alpha)}\right]^{\left(\frac{a\alpha+b\beta}{1-\alpha}\right)}\right\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Analysis for the One-Timescale Fixed-Point Method

We discuss the stability, asymptotic convergence, and the convergence rates of the one-timescale fixed-point method in (
10
).

C.1
Stability

We first show that the sequence
(x_{n})
remains within the compact set

\mathcal{K}:=[C_{\ell},C_{u}]^{SA},

(63)

where
C_{\ell}
and
C_{u}
are as in (
13
). Since
C_{\ell}>0
,
\mathcal{K}
lies in the interior of the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathcal{K}.
Then,
x_{n}\in\mathcal{K}
and
\hat{F}_{n}\in[C_{\ell},C_{u}]
for all
n\geq 0.

Proof.

We use induction to first prove that
x_{n}(s,a)\geq C_{\ell}
for all
s,a
and all
n\geq 0.
It trivially follows that
x_{0}(s,a)\geq C_{\ell}
for all
s,a.
Now, for some
n\geq 0,
suppose that
x_{n}(s,a)\geq C_{\ell}
for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.
Then, for any
s,a,
and
s^{\prime},

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)+\gamma\min_{a^{\prime}}\ln x_{n}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\overset{\textnormal{(a)}}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\|r\|_{\infty}+\gamma\ln C_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(b)}}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle C_{\ell},

(64)

where (a) follows since
r(s,a)\leq\|r\|_{\infty}
and
x_{n}(s,a)\geq C_{\ell}
for all
s,a,
while (b) holds since the definition of
C_{\ell}
implies
(1-\gamma)\ln C_{\ell}\geq-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\|r\|_{\infty}.
Using this last inequality, it follows from the update rule in (
10
) that
x_{n+1}(s,a)\geq C_{\ell}
for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},
completing the induction proof. The same argument also show that
\hat{F}_{n}\geq C_{\ell}
for all
n\geq 0.

A similar induction argument shows that
x_{n}(s,a)\leq C_{u}
for all
s,a
and
\hat{F}_{n}\leq C_{u}
for all
n\geq 0.
The overall claim now follows.
∎

Building on the above result, it is easy to see that
F
maps
\mathcal{K}
to itself.

Corollary C.2
.

Let
K
be defined as in (
63
). Then,
x\in\mathcal{K}\implies F(x)\in\mathcal{K}.

Proof.

The definition of
F
given in (
2
) shows that

F(x)(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s,a)}\bigg[\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)+\gamma\min_{a^{\prime}}\ln x_{n}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right)\bigg].

Hence, by arguing as in the proof of Lemma
C.1
, the desired result is easy to see.
∎

C.2
Asymptotic Convergence

We now discuss (
10
)’s asymptotic behavior. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the filtration of
\sigma
-fields, where

\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(x_{0},s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,s_{n},a_{n}).

The update in (
10
) can then be rewritten as

x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}\left[e_{s_{n},a_{n}}\bigg((F(x_{n}))_{s_{n},a_{n}}-x_{n}(s_{n},a_{n})\bigg)+M_{n+1}\right],

(65)

where

M_{n+1}=e_{s_{n},a_{n}}\bigg(\hat{F}_{n}-(F(x_{n}))_{s_{n},a_{n}}\bigg).

(66)

Then, it is straightforward to see that the associated limiting ODE is given by

\dot{x}(t)=D_{\pi_{x(t)}}[F(x(t))-x(t)],

(67)

where
D_{\pi_{x}}
is the diagonal matrix made up the stationary distribution
\pi_{x}
(defined below (
13
)). Let

\ell_{\mathcal{K}}:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\min_{i\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}D_{\pi_{x}}(i,i)\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{\mathcal{K}}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{K}}\max_{i\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}D_{\pi_{x}}(i,i).

(68)

Since
\mathcal{K}
is compact and bounded away from
0
, it follows that

0<\ell_{\mathcal{K}}\leq u_{\mathcal{K}}<\infty.

(69)

For (
67
) to be well-posed—i.e., to ensure existence and uniqueness of solutions on
[0,\infty)
, along with continuous dependence on initial conditions—standard results typically require either global Lipschitz continuity of the driving function or, more generally, local Lipschitz continuity together with the property that solution trajectories remain within a compact set.

In our setting,
F
involves terms of the form
[\min_{s,a}x(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})]^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in[0,1)
, and hence is not globally Lipschitz; moreover, it fails to be locally Lipschitz near the boundary of
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
. We therefore adopt the second approach to show (
67
)’s well-posedness: we show that
F
is locally Lipschitz on a neighbourhood of
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
and that
\mathcal{K}
is positively invariant under the induced dynamics.

Lemma C.3
.

The function
F
is Lipschitz continuous on an open neighbourhood of
\mathcal{K}
in
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}.

Proof.

Let
\delta:=C_{\ell}/2>0
; the inequality holds since
C_{\ell}>0
. Then,

\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\delta}:=(C_{\ell}-\delta,\,C_{u}+\delta)^{SA}\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}.

It suffices to show that
F
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{K}^{\delta}
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
h(z)=z^{\gamma}.
Then, for
z_{1},z_{2}\geq C_{\ell}/2>0,
there exists some
c
between
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
such that

\displaystyle|h(z_{1})-h(z_{2})|\overset{\textnormal{(a)}}{=}{}

\displaystyle|h^{\prime}(c)||z_{1}-z_{2}|,

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(b)}}{=}{}

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{c^{1-\gamma}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-\gamma}\gamma}{C_{\ell}^{1-\gamma}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|,

(70)

where (a) follows from the mean value theorem, while (b) follows since
c\geq\min\{z_{1},z_{2}\}\geq C_{\ell}/2.

Therefore, for any
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

\displaystyle|(F(x_{1})(s,a)

\displaystyle-F(x_{2})(s,a)|

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(a)}}{\leq}{}

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)\right)\bigg|\Big[\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big]^{\gamma}-\Big[\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{2}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big]^{\gamma}\bigg|

(71)

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(b)}}{\leq}{}

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-\gamma}\gamma}{C_{\ell}^{1-\gamma}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)\right)\bigg|\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{2}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\bigg|

(72)

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(c)}}{\leq}{}

\displaystyle\frac{2^{1-\gamma}\gamma}{C_{\ell}^{1-\gamma}}\exp\left(\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\|r\|_{\infty}\right)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{\infty},

(73)

where (a) follows from (
2
), (b) follows from (
70
), while (c) holds from since
r(s,a)\geq-\|r\|_{\infty}
and since a standard argument shows

\bigg|\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\min_{a^{\prime}}x_{2}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\bigg|\leq\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{\infty}

Taking the supremum over
(s,a)
yields

\|F(x_{1})-F(x_{2})\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma}{C_{\ell}^{1-\gamma}}\exp\left(\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\|r\|_{\infty}\right)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{\infty},

(74)

which proves that
F
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{K}^{\delta},
as desired.
∎

Our next result establishes that
\mathcal{K}
is positively invariant for the limiting ODE in (
67
) and that
x^{*}
is globally asymptotically stable relative to
\mathcal{K}
.

Theorem C.4
.

The set
\mathcal{K}
defined in (
63
) contains the fixed point
x^{*}
of
F
and is positively invariant under the ODE in (
67
). Moreover,
x^{*}
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of (
67
) relative to
\mathcal{K}
; that is, for every initial condition
x(0)\in\mathcal{K}
, the corresponding trajectory satisfies

x(t)\to x^{*}\quad\text{as }\quad t\to\infty.

Proof.

We first show that
x^{*}\in\mathcal{K}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{K}
is a non-empty convex compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}.
Moreover,
F
is continuous and Lemma
C.2
implies that
F(\mathcal{K})\subseteq\mathcal{K}
. It thus follows from Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem that
F
has a fixed point in
\mathcal{K}
. Since
x^{*}
is the unique fixed point of
F
on
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
, it follows that
x^{*}\in\mathcal{K}
, as desired.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{K}
is positively invariant under the ODE in (
67
). Let
x\in\mathcal{K}
lie on the boundary. If
x(i)=C_{\ell}
for some
i=(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, then

D_{\pi_{x}}(i,i)\,[F(x)(i)-x(i)]=D_{\pi_{x}}(i,i)\,[F(x)(i)-C_{\ell}]\geq 0,

since
D_{\pi_{x}}(i,i)\geq 0
and, by Corollary
C.2
,
F(x)(i)\geq C_{\ell}
. Similarly, if
x(i)=C_{u}
, then

D_{\pi_{x}}(i,i)\,[F(x)(i)-x(i)]\leq 0.

Thus, the vector field under (
67
) points inward on the boundary of
\mathcal{K}
. By Nagumo’s invariance theorem
[
1
, Theorem 11.2.3]
,
\mathcal{K}
is positively invariant under the ODE in (
67
).

We finally show that
x^{*}
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium of (
67
) relative to
\mathcal{K}.
As a first step, we show that the function
F
satisfies the following properties on
\mathbb{R}_{++}^{SA}
:

1.

F
is homogeneous of degree
\gamma,
i.e.,

F(cx)=c^{\gamma}F(x)\;\text{ for any }c>0.

2.

F
is monotone, i.e.,

x\leq y\implies F(x)\leq F(y).

Homogeneity holds since, for any
c>0,

\displaystyle F(cx)(s,a)={}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)\right)\left[\min_{a^{\prime}}cx(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]^{\gamma}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle c^{\gamma}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\exp\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r(s,a)\right)\left[\min_{a^{\prime}}x(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right]^{\gamma}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle c^{\gamma}F(x)(s,a).

On the other hand, monotonicity holds since the power function
z^{\gamma}
is monotone and since
x\leq y
implies

\min_{a^{\prime}}x(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\leq\min_{a}^{\prime}y(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

Now we show that any solution trajectory of (
67
), starting in
\mathcal{K},
converges to
x^{*}.
Let
x
be one such trajectory. Since
x^{*}\in\mathcal{K},
the quantities

m(t):=\min_{i\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x^{*}_{i}}\text{ and }M(t):=\max_{i\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x^{*}_{i}}

are well defined for any
t\geq 0
. These definitions imply

m(t)x^{*}\leq x(t)\leq M(t)x^{*}.

(75)

To show
\lim_{t\to\infty}x(t)=x^{*},
it then suffices to show that

1\leq\liminf_{t\to\infty}m(t)\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}M(t)\leq 1.

(76)

We first show that
\liminf_{t\to\infty}m(t)\geq 1.
We have

m(t)^{\gamma}F(x^{*})=F(m(t)x^{*})\leq F(x(t)),

(77)

where the equality follows from the homogeneity of
F
and inequality from (
75
) and the monotonicity of
F.
Let
D_{+}m(t)
be the lower right Dini derivative of
m(t),
i.e., let

D_{+}m(t):=\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{m(t+h)-m(t)}{h}.

Also let
I(t):=\arg\min_{i\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x^{*}_{i}}.

Then,

\displaystyle D_{+}m(t)\overset{\textnormal{(a)}}{=}{}

\displaystyle\min_{i\in I(t)}\frac{\dot{x}_{i}(t)}{x^{*}_{i}}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(b)}}{=}{}

\displaystyle\min_{i\in I(t)}\frac{D_{\pi_{x(t)}}(i,i)[F_{i}(x(t))-x_{i}(t)]}{x^{*}_{i}}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(c)}}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathcal{K}}\min_{i\in I(t)}\frac{[F_{i}(x(t))-x_{i}(t)]}{x^{*}_{i}}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(d)}}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathcal{K}}\min_{i\in I(t)}\frac{[m(t)^{\gamma}F_{i}(x^{*})-x_{i}(t)]}{x^{*}_{i}}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(e)}}{=}{}

\displaystyle\overset{\textnormal{(f)}}{=}{}

C.3
Convergence Rates: Scalar Case

Here, we analyze the convergence rate of a scalar stochastic
approximation model associated with the one-timescale update in
(
10
). The aim is to isolate the challenges posed by
the power-law structure.

We begin by considering the one-state, one-action reduction of the function
F
defined in (
2
). Let the corresponding reward be denoted by
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
F:\mathbb{R}_{++}\to\mathbb{R}_{++}
takes the form

F(x)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma}r\right)x^{\gamma}.

Solving
F(x^{*})=x^{*}
yields

x^{*}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\theta}{\gamma(1-\gamma)}r\right),

so that

F(x)=\left(\frac{x}{x^{*}}\right)^{\gamma}x^{*}.

(78)

In the literal single-state, single-action model with deterministic rewards, the update in (
65
) becomes deterministic. To study the effect of stochastic approximation noise while preserving the same power-law drift, we consider the following scalar recursion related to
10
:

x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\alpha_{n}\left[F(x_{n})-x_{n}+\zeta_{n+1}\right],

(79)

where
(\zeta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a martingale-difference sequence with respect to the
filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, defined by

\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma(x_{0},\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}).

(80)

For this new scalar update, we presume that there exists constants
C_{\ell}
and
C_{u},
similar to (
13
)
such that
0<C_{\ell}\leq C_{u}
and
x^{*}\in\mathcal{K}:=[C_{\ell},C_{u}].
As in Lemma
C.1
, we assume that
x_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
implies
x_{n}\in\mathcal{K}
for all
n\geq 0.
We also assume that the martingale differences satisfy

|\zeta_{n}|\leq C_{u}-C_{\ell},\qquad n\geq 0.

(81)

For the convergence and convergence-rate analysis of
(
8
), the global contraction of
T
plays a
crucial role. In particular, it allows us to use

\|Q(t)-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}
as a global Lyapunov function in (
31
) for the limiting
ODE associated with the
Q_{n}
-update in (
8
).
At the discrete-time level, the same contraction property yields the
strict shrinkage factor in (
43
), which
provides a negative drift toward
Q^{*}
and thereby enables the
convergence-rate analysis.

In contrast, the power-law form of
F
in (
78
)
implies that
F^{\prime}(x)\to\infty
as
x\downarrow 0
. Thus,
F
is
contractive only on the subset of
\mathbb{R}_{++}
that is bounded away from the origin. Indeed, for
any
\epsilon>0
such that
\gamma+\epsilon<1
, there exists

\delta>0
such that

|F(x)-F(x^{\prime})|\leq(\gamma+\epsilon)|x-x^{\prime}|,\qquad x,x^{\prime}\geq x^{*}-\delta.

This lack of global contractivity is the main obstacle in obtaining
convergence rates for our one-timescale method. We now explain how this can be overcome in the scalar case.

Theorem C.5
.

Let

\underline{y}:=\frac{C_{\ell}}{x^{*}},\qquad\overline{y}:=\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}.

Define

C_{1}:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\underline{y}-\underline{y}^{\gamma}}{\underline{y}-1},&\underline{y}\neq 1,\\[8.61108pt]
1-\gamma,&\underline{y}=1.\end{cases}

Let

\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{2C_{1}(n+1)}.

Then

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{x_{n}}{x^{*}}-1\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\widetilde{C}_{2}(1+\ln n)}{n}}=\tilde{O}(n^{-1/2}),

where

\widetilde{C}_{2}:=\frac{1}{4C_{1}^{2}}\left[\max\left\{\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2\gamma},\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}\right\}+\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}\right],

and
\tilde{O}
hides logarithmic terms.

Proof.

Let
y_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}}{x^{*}}.
Since
0<C_{\ell}\leq x^{*}\leq C_{u}<\infty,
we have

0<\underline{y}\leq y_{n}\leq\overline{y}<\infty,\qquad n\geq 0.

(82)

Dividing (
79
) by
x^{*}
and using
(
78
), we obtain

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y_{n}+\alpha_{n}\left[\frac{F(x_{n})}{x^{*}}-y_{n}+\frac{\zeta_{n+1}}{x^{*}}\right]

\displaystyle=y_{n}+\alpha_{n}\left[y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n}+\frac{\zeta_{n+1}}{x^{*}}\right].

(83)

Let

z_{n}:=y_{n}-1.

Then

z_{n+1}=z_{n}+\alpha_{n}(y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n})+\alpha_{n}\frac{\zeta_{n+1}}{x^{*}}.

Squaring both sides, taking conditional expectation with respect to

\mathcal{F}_{n}
, and using
\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[z_{n+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]

\displaystyle=z_{n}^{2}+2\alpha_{n}z_{n}(y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n})+\alpha_{n}^{2}(y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n})^{2}+\alpha_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\zeta_{n+1}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right].

(84)

From (
81
), we have

\left(\frac{\zeta_{n+1}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{C_{u}-C_{\ell}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}.

Also, using (
82
),

|y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n}|\leq\max\{y_{n}^{\gamma},y_{n}\}\leq\max\left\{\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{\gamma},\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right\}.

Therefore, with

C_{2}:=\max\left\{\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2\gamma},\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2}\right\}+\left(\frac{C_{u}}{x^{*}}\right)^{2},

we obtain

\mathbb{E}[z_{n+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]\leq z_{n}^{2}+2\alpha_{n}z_{n}(y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n})+C_{2}\alpha_{n}^{2}.

(85)

We now establish a uniform drift bound. Define

h(y):=\frac{y-y^{\gamma}}{y-1},\qquad y>0,\ y\neq 1,

with the continuous extension
h(1):=1-\gamma
. We first show that
h
is
nondecreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{++}.
For
y>0
and
y\neq 1
,

(y-1)^{2}h^{\prime}(y)=(1-\gamma)y^{\gamma}+\gamma y^{\gamma-1}-1.

Multiplying by
y^{1-\gamma}>0
, define

g(y):=y^{1-\gamma}(y-1)^{2}h^{\prime}(y)=(1-\gamma)y+\gamma-y^{1-\gamma}.

Then

g^{\prime}(y)=(1-\gamma)-(1-\gamma)y^{-\gamma},\qquad g^{\prime\prime}(y)=\gamma(1-\gamma)y^{-(1+\gamma)}\geq 0.

Thus
g
is convex on
(0,\infty)
. Since

g(1)=0,\qquad g^{\prime}(1)=0,

it follows that
g
attains its global minimum at
1
, and hence

g(y)\geq 0
for all
y>0
. Therefore,
h^{\prime}(y)\geq 0
for all

y>0
,
y\neq 1
, and so
h
is nondecreasing on
(0,\infty)
.

Since
h
is nondecreasing and
y_{n}\geq\underline{y}
, we have

h(y_{n})\geq C_{1}
. Moreover,

y_{n}-y_{n}^{\gamma}=h(y_{n})(y_{n}-1).

Hence

(y_{n}-1)(y_{n}^{\gamma}-y_{n})=-h(y_{n})(y_{n}-1)^{2}\leq-C_{1}(y_{n}-1)^{2}=-C_{1}z_{n}^{2}.

(86)

Substituting (
86
) into
(
85
) gives

\mathbb{E}[z_{n+1}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]\leq z_{n}^{2}-2C_{1}\alpha_{n}z_{n}^{2}+C_{2}\alpha_{n}^{2}.

Taking expectation on both sides yields

\mathbb{E}z_{n+1}^{2}\leq(1-2C_{1}\alpha_{n})\mathbb{E}z_{n}^{2}+C_{2}\alpha_{n}^{2}.

Recalling that

\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{2C_{1}(n+1)},

we get

\mathbb{E}z_{n+1}^{2}\leq\frac{n}{n+1}\mathbb{E}z_{n}^{2}+\frac{\widetilde{C}_{2}}{(n+1)^{2}}.

(87)

Multiplying both sides of (
87
)
by
n+1
gives

(n+1)\mathbb{E}z_{n+1}^{2}\leq n\mathbb{E}z_{n}^{2}+\frac{\widetilde{C}_{2}}{n+1}.

Iterating the above inequality from
0
to
n-1
, and using that the
initial term is killed by the factor
1-2C_{1}\alpha_{0}=0
, gives

n\mathbb{E}z_{n}^{2}\leq\widetilde{C}_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}z_{n}^{2}\leq\widetilde{C}_{2}\frac{1+\ln n}{n}.

Finally, by Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{x_{n}}{x^{*}}-1\right|=\mathbb{E}|z_{n}|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}z_{n}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\widetilde{C}_{2}\frac{1+\ln n}{n}}.

This completes the proof.
∎
