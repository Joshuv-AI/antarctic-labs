Impact of a free medication policy on blood pressure among older patients with hypertension: an interrupted time series analysis
Authors: Jie Pan, Biyun Zheng, Shilin Jiao, Ming Wang, Zhiying Yin, Zhijuan Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the intensification of aging in China, limited medication access and financial burden hinder effective blood pressure management in older adults. In July 2021, Quzhou introduced a policy providing free antihypertensive medications to individuals aged ≥ 65 years. This study aimed to evaluate its impact on long-term blood pressure outcomes.MethodsAn interrupted time series analysis was performed using 28 quarters of longitudinal blood pressure data from 1,310 older patients with primary hypertension (aged ≥ 65 years) in Quzhou. Background

Hypertension is the major risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and premature mortality globally [1–4]. With the rapid increase of population aging, China is facing severe challenges in hypertension prevention and control [5]. According to the latest population surveillance data, the prevalence of hypertension among Chinese adults reached 31.6%, while that among those aged 65 years and older exceeded 55% [6, 7]. Although the national government continues to promote equal access to basic public health services, heavy medication burden and poor adherence, due to the frequent coexistence of multiple diseases and the need for long-term or even lifelong medication among older patients, remain the core obstacles to achieving optimal blood pressure control in this population [8].

To improve the quality of chronic disease management and alleviate patients’ financial burden, governments at all levels in China have explored various intervention measures [8–12]. Among these, improving the accessibility and affordability of antihypertensive medications is considered the most direct approach to enhancing patient outcomes. Since July 2021, Quzhou in Zhejiang Province has implemented a policy providing free outpatient access to essential antihypertensive medications for patients aged 65 and above throughout the city [13]. This “fully free” model represents a significant breakthrough in the scope and intensity of the policy. Although preliminary evaluations have shown that the policy effectively increases medication coverage among the older patients, there remains a lack of systematic quantitative assessments of the policy’s health outcomes based on long-term monitoring data to determine whether the increased adherence rates have truly translated into substantial improvements in population blood pressure levels.

In public health policy evaluations, the traditional cross-sectional study or simple before-and-after comparison are often difficult to eliminate confounding factors [14, 15]. This is particularly true for evaluations related to blood pressure, mainly because blood pressure exhibits obvious seasonal fluctuations, usually peaking in winter and dropping in summer [16]. Ignoring this pattern could lead to serious misunderstanding of the policy effect [17–19]. Furthermore, blood pressure naturally changes with age over the long-term. If this background trend is not effectively controlled, the net effect of the policy remains unclear.

Interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) is one of the most robust quasi-experimental designs [12]. It utilizes continuous data from multiple time points to identify immediate level shifts and slope changes [20–22]. Crucially, it achieves this while controlling for long-term trends and seasonality. Therefore, this study used 28 quarters of follow-up monitoring data from Quzhou, spanning from the first quarter of 2018 to the fourth quarter of 2024, and employed an ITS model with seasonally adjusted variables to assess the real impact of the free medication policy on the long-term trajectories of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) in older hypertensive patients. This study aimed to provide empirical evidence for evaluating the “Quzhou Model” and inform the optimization and scale-up of this policy for chronic disease prevention and control in the context of an aging society.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study were derived from a specialized survey titled “Evaluation of the Free Medication Policy for Hypertensive and Type 2 Diabetes Patients Aged 65 and above in Quzhou [23].” The study participants consisted of patients in Quzhou aged 65 and above who had been diagnosed with primary hypertension for at least 6 months. The survey covered all counties (cities and districts) in Quzhou and employed a multi-stage cluster random sampling method stratified by urban/rural areas, counties and blocks. In detail, three towns or subdistricts were randomly selected from each of the six counties (cities and districts), yielding 18 sites in total. Subsequently, three residential communities or administrative villages were chosen randomly within each selected town or subdistrict, with 54 clusters included ultimately. The survey was conducted from July to September 2024, and face-to-face electronic questionnaire surveys were administered using tablets. The survey content primarily included socio-demographic characteristics, illness and medication status, behavioral lifestyles, medication adherence, utilization of free medication, and policy satisfaction and awareness. The self-designed questionnaire for this policy evaluation survey was presented in Supplementary File 1. Blood pressure values were obtained from hypertension follow-up records between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2024, extracted from the electronic health record system of primary healthcare institutions. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Quzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Approval No. 2024-018-01), and all participants signed informed consent forms.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for study participants

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 65 years at the 2024 survey; (2) confirmed diagnosis of primary hypertension by a medical institution before January 1, 2018; and (3) established a specialized hypertension management archive in a primary healthcare institution in Quzhou, with at least one hypertension follow-up record between the first quarter of 2018 and the pre-policy implementation period (the second quarter of 2021). For participants with multiple blood pressure measurements in a single quarter, the within-individual mean value was first calculated to represent the participant’s quarterly blood pressure. The quarterly population average blood pressure was then derived by averaging these individual mean values across all participants.

A total of 2,324 hypertensive patients aged 65 and above initially completed the survey. In accordance with the study requirements, 807 patients who were newly diagnosed with hypertension after January 1, 2018, and 207 patients who had no hypertension follow-up records between the first quarter of 2018 and the pre-policy implementation period (second quarter of 2021). Ultimately, 1,310 hypertensive patients were included as the analysis participants for this study. The selection process for the study participants is shown in Fig. 1.

Interrupted time series model

This study adopted an interrupted time series (ITS) design. The ITS analysis was performed using aggregated quarterly mean blood pressure values across 28 equally spaced time points. Statistical analysis

Time series data often exhibit autocorrelation. In this study, multiple methods were employed for autocorrelation diagnosis [20, 22, 25]: (1) The Durbin-Watson (DW) test was used to detect first-order autocorrelation, where a test statistic close to 2 indicates no autocorrelation, and a significant deviation from 2 suggests the presence of first-order autocorrelation; (2) The Breusch-Godfrey (BG) test was applied to test for higher-order autocorrelation (such as at lags 2 and 4) by constructing an auxiliary regression to determine whether the residual sequence exhibited an autocorrelation structure; (3) Autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) plots were utilized; the ACF plot displays autocorrelation coefficients at different lag orders, while the PACF plot controls for the influence of intermediate lag terms. The combination of both allows for the intuitive identification of the order and pattern of autocorrelation, thereby providing a solid basis for model modification.

To quantify the actual impact of the policy, this study performed counterfactual prediction. Based on the estimated optimal model, the intervention variables interventiont and time_aftert were both set to 0, while keeping other variables (time and quarterly dummy variables) constant. This allowed for the prediction of the expected blood pressure values if there had been no policy intervention (i.e., the counterfactual values). Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 1,310 participants were finally included in this study, with a mean age of 74.6 ± 5.9 years. Among them, 522 (39.8%) were male and 788 (60.2%) were female. Regarding occupation, the largest group was workers in agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, fishery, and water conservancy (n = 753, 57.5%), followed by unemployed, job-seeking, or homemakers (n = 385, 29.4%). The education level was predominantly low: 599 (45.7%) participants were illiterate or semi-literate, and 506 (38.6%) had primary school education. The mean duration of hypertension was 13.3 ± 5.5 years. In terms of antihypertensive medication use, the majority of participants (n = 963, 73.5%) used only free medication, 11.4% (n = 149) used both free and self‑paid medication, 9.9%(n = 130) used only self‑paid medication, and 5.2% (n = 68) reported no medication. Hypertension complications were present in 80.7% (n = 1,057) of the participants (Table S1).

Blood pressure trends over time and seasonal distribution

Figure 2 illustrated the temporal trends of the average SBP and DBP among the study population from the first quarter of 2018 to the fourth quarter of 2024. Overall, prior to the policy implementation (2018 Q1-2021 Q2), SBP exhibited a slow upward trend, while DBP showed a steady decline. Following the implementation of the policy (2021 Q3-2024 Q4), the upward trend of SBP gradually leveled off and shifted toward a downward trend, while DBP continued to exhibit a slow decline. Concurrently, both SBP and DBP demonstrated distinct seasonal fluctuations: blood pressure levels were relatively higher in the first quarter of each year and lower in the third quarter, suggesting that seasonal factors may exert a significant influence on blood pressure variations.

To further validate these seasonal differences, box plots were employed to display the distribution of blood pressure across different quarters (Fig. 3). Results from the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) indicated that the differences across quarters for both DBP (F = 6.11, P = 0.003) and SBP (F = 7.00, P = 0.002) were statistically significant. Following multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method, it was found that DBP in Q3 was significantly lower than in Q1 (P = 0.002) and Q2 (P = 0.033). Similarly, SBP in Q3 was significantly lower than in Q1 (P = 0.003), Q2 (P = 0.021), and Q4 (P = 0.005). These results demonstrate that the seasonal fluctuations in blood pressure are primarily characterized by significantly lower levels during the summer (Q3) compared to other seasons, which is consistent with the patterns observed in the time series plots.

Construction of the basic interrupted time series model (OLS_Basic)

To preliminarily evaluate the impact of the free medication policy on blood pressure, a basic interrupted time series model without seasonal control was first constructed. The results (Table 1) for the DBP model showed that the intercept (β0 = 79.521, P < 0.001) represents the baseline DBP level; the pre-intervention trend (β1=-0.045, P = 0.331) suggests a slight downward trend in DBP prior to policy implementation, though it was not statistically significant; the immediate policy effect (β2=-0.164, P = 0.754) was non-significant, indicating that the policy implementation did not cause an instantaneous change in DBP; furthermore, the post-intervention trend change (β3=-0.057, P = 0.377) lacked statistical significance, implying the policy did not significantly alter the long-term trajectory of DBP. In the SBP model, the intercept (β0 = 135.321, P < 0.001) represents the baseline SBP level; the pre-intervention trend (β1 = 0.287, P = 0.006) indicates a significant upward trend in SBP prior to policy implementation, with an average quarterly increase of 0.287 mmHg; the immediate policy effect (β2=-0.399, P = 0.718) was not significant; however, the post-intervention trend change (β3=-0.303, P = 0.035) was statistically significant, suggesting that the rate of increase in SBP significantly slowed down after policy implementation, with an additional quarterly reduction of 0.303 mmHg.

In terms of the goodness-of-fit of the basic interrupted time series model (OLS_Basic), the DBP model yielded an R² of 0.544 (Adjusted R²=0.488) and the SBP model an R² of 0.387 (Adjusted R²=0.311), indicating limited explanatory power. Construction of the full interrupted time series model with seasonal control (OLS_Seasonal Control)

Given the significant high-order autocorrelation in the basic models, which suggested that seasonal fluctuations were not adequately controlled, this study incorporated quarterly dummy variables to construct a full interrupted time series model (OLS_Seasonal Control). The results (Table 2) for the DPB full model showed that the intercept (β0 = 79.889, P < 0.001) represents the baseline DBP level; the pre-intervention trend (β1=-0.048, P = 0.017) indicates a significant downward trend in DBP prior to policy implementation, with an average quarterly decrease of 0.048 mmHg; the immediate policy effect (β2=-0.005, P = 0.981) remained non-significant; however, the post-intervention trend change (β3=-0.062, P = 0.029) was statistically significant, suggesting that the rate of DBP decline further accelerated after policy implementation, with an additional quarterly reduction of 0.062 mmHg. In the SBP full model, the intercept (β0 = 135.929, P < 0.001) represents the baseline SBP level; the pre-intervention trend (β1 = 0.275, P < 0.001) indicates a significant upward trend in SBP prior to policy implementation, with a quarterly increase of 0.275 mmHg; the immediate policy effect (β2 = 0.025, P = 0.961) was not significant; but the post-intervention trend change (β3=-0.310, P < 0.001) was highly significant, indicating that the upward trend of SBP was effectively reversed after policy implementation, with an additional quarterly reduction of 0.310 mmHg. Newey-West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent standard errors were further calculated to test result robustness (Table S2). Point estimates of all parameters stayed unchanged, and statistical significance of core post-intervention slope effects remained consistent, which corroborates the reliability of primary model findings.

Counterfactual analysis and policy effect evaluation

A counterfactual scenario was constructed based on the parameter estimates from the full model. This scenario projected the blood pressure trajectory assuming no policy implementation. Net policy effects were quantified by comparing these projections with actual observations.

The counterfactual analysis (Fig. 6) revealed that prior to policy implementation (2018 Q1-2021 Q2), DBP was already on a slow downward trajectory, decreasing by an average of 0.048 mmHg per quarter, while SBP exhibited a significant upward trend, increasing by an average of 0.275 mmHg per quarter. Following the implementation of the policy (2021 Q3-2024 Q4), the actual rate of decline in DBP accelerated significantly to 0.110 mmHg per quarter, representing an additional reduction of 0.062 mmHg per quarter compared to the pre-intervention trend. The actual trajectory of SBP underwent a fundamental reversal, shifting from the pre-intervention upward trend to a downward trend of 0.035 mmHg per quarter, resulting in a net change of -0.310 mmHg per quarter relative to the pre-intervention trend. These findings indicate that the impact of the policy differed between the two blood pressure indicators: it accelerated the existing natural decline in DBP, whereas it effectively reversed the prior upward trend in SBP.

Comparison between actual observed values and counterfactual predicted values for diastolic blood pressure (A) and systolic blood pressure (B)

The policy effect, defined as the difference between the actual observed value and the counterfactual predicted value at a specific time point, was illustrated in Fig. 7. The effect on DBP expanded from near zero at the onset of implementation to -0.8 mmHg by the end of the study period. A more pronounced cumulative trend was observed for SBP, with the effect increasing from near zero to -4.4 mmHg. From the start of the policy (2021 Q3) to the end of the study (2024 Q4), the policy resulted in a cumulative reduction of 6.6 (95%CI: -1.2, 14.3) mmHg for DBP and 32.2 (95%CI: 13.6, 50.8) mmHg for SBP. The instantaneous effect at the end of the study (2024 Q4) indicated that actual values were approximately 0.8 mmHg lower for DBP and 4.4 mmHg lower for SBP compared to the counterfactual scenario.

Sensitivity analysis

The median number of blood pressure measurements per participant was 0 (0, 0), with most participants (N = 1068) completing all 28 quarterly scheduled measurements(Table S3). In addition, the proportion of participants with valid BP measurements exceeded 85% of the total participants in each quarter throughout follow-up (Table S4). To test the robustness of the primary findings and exclude the potential influence of incomplete follow-up and time-varying cohort composition, a sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting the analytical sample to participants with valid blood pressure measurements in all quarters across the entire study period. A total of 1068 individuals with complete longitudinal records were included in the sensitivity analysis, compared with 1310 participants in the original full sample. The interrupted time series analysis based on the complete-case sample yielded highly consistent policy effect estimates with the main analysis. The cumulative policy effect on SBP was 34.4 (95%CI: 14.2, 54.7) mmHg in the sensitivity analysis, compared with 32.2 (95%CI: 13.6, 50.8) mmHg in the primary analysis, while the cumulative DBP policy effect slightly changed from 6.6 (95%CI: -1.2, 14.3) mmHg to 6.4 (95%CI: -1.8, 14.5) mmHg (Fig. 8). The negligible differences in effect sizes indicated that variations in quarterly sample size and missing blood pressure data did not substantially alter the estimated policy impacts, demonstrating the robustness and reliability of our core conclusions regarding the beneficial effects of the free medication policy on blood pressure control.

Discussion

This study employed an interrupted time series analysis design to evaluate the long-term impact of a free medication policy on blood pressure control among older hypertensive patients in Quzhou. By introducing quarterly dummy variables, seasonal fluctuations were effectively controlled. The findings demonstrate that this intervention significantly altered the temporal trajectories of both SBP and DBP in the older population.

Characteristics of baseline trends and seasonal fluctuations

Prior to policy implementation, DBP showed a slow downward trend, while SBP exhibited a significant and continuous upward trajectory. These observations align with a 43-year blood pressure monitoring cohort study in the United States [18]. Such phenomena are highly consistent with the pathophysiological patterns of blood pressure evolution across the life course. With advancing age, arterial stiffness in the aorta reduces vascular compliance, leading to increased systolic pressure. Simultaneously, the weakened elastic recoil during diastole results in a gradual decline in diastolic pressure, ultimately causing a significant increase in pulse pressure [26–28]. Furthermore, large-scale studies indicate that blood pressure in winter is, on average, approximately 4 mmHg higher than in summer [16]. The present study observed distinct seasonal patterns, where blood pressure levels in the third quarter (summer) were significantly lower than in other quarters.

From a statistical and methodological perspective, the indispensable role of seasonal adjustment variables in the ITS model was strongly confirmed. The BG tests for the basic model (OLS_Basic) showed significant autocorrelation at lag 4. This indicated a failure to capture quarterly seasonal effects. After incorporating seasonal dummy variables, the goodness-of-fit of the full model improved significantly (R² increased from 0.544/0.387 to 0.932/0.884). Both AIC and BIC values decreased, and the residuals satisfied regression assumptions. This finding serves as a warning for future real-world studies: when utilizing long-term clinical monitoring data to evaluate public health policies, environmental confounders such as seasonality must be strictly controlled to obtain unbiased estimates of the net intervention effect [20–22, 25].

Reshaping effects of the free medication policy and clinical significance

The core finding of this study was that the free medication policy transcended the superficial level of increasing medication coverage and substantially altered long-term blood pressure trajectories. Counterfactual analysis indicated that the policy accelerated the natural decline of DBP by an additional 0.062 mmHg per quarter. More critically, a fundamental reversal was achieved for SBP, shifting it from a “continuous rise” to a “steady decline” (a net change of -0.310 mmHg per quarter). By the final quarter of 2024, SBP was approximately 4.4 mmHg lower than the counterfactual scenario without intervention.

A blood pressure reduction of this magnitude holds immense preventive value in clinical and public health fields. According to a meta-analysis of the large prospective studies globally (incorporating over 1 million adults), the relationship between SBP levels and cardiovascular mortality risk is strictly log-linear. For every 2 mmHg reduction in SBP, the risk of stroke mortality decreases by approximately 10%, and the risk of ischemic heart disease mortality decreases by approximately 7% [3]. Consequently, drawing on evidence from prior epidemiological research, the additional 4.4 mmHg SBP reduction associated with the Quzhou policy is likely to translate into fewer acute cardiovascular events and reduce the corresponding medical and economic burden among the local older population.

Mechanisms and comparisons with similar policies

Compared to other chronic disease medication insurance policies in China, the “fully free” model in Quzhou demonstrated a more powerful and thorough intervention efficacy. The National Essential Public Health Service Package (NEPHSP), implemented since 2009, provides free follow-up and health interventions but generally excludes free medications [8, 29]. A recent large scale longitudinal study in China showed that while NEPHSP improves blood pressure control, SBP improvements often plateau in the later stages of intervention due to patients’ out-of-pocket costs. This is particularly evident among older patients and those with cardiovascular comorbidities, where marginal gains in control rates are limited [11]. In contrast, the Quzhou model intervened directly through “fully free.” This not only accelerated the natural decline of DBP but also reversed the upward trajectory of SBP. This suggests that supplementing routine follow-up with free medication creates a strong synergistic effect. In addition, many regions in China have reduced drug costs by eliminating pharmaceutical markups or increasing outpatient reimbursement ratios. A quasi-experimental study on removing co-payments for essential chronic disease medications in rural China showed a 44.1% increase in primary care visits. However, because absolute out-of-pocket costs remained, many low-income older patients still failed to achieve standardized medication use [10]. Another ITS study evaluating the zero-markup policy showed that the net effect on SBP typically ranged between only − 1.0 and − 1.5 mmHg [9]. In this study, Quzhou reduced out-of-pocket costs to absolute zero, resulting in a net SBP reduction of 4.4 mmHg and a cumulative reduction of 32.2 mmHg·quarter. This data gap provides strong evidence that for price-sensitive older populations, the “total elimination” of economic burdens can cross a threshold, leading to a qualitative leap in blood pressure control.

The reshaping effect of this policy likely stems from two synergistic mechanisms. At the individual level, the “fully free” model directly dismantled economic barriers for low-income older patients. This intervention effectively eliminated the phenomenon of medication reduction or discontinuation driven by financial hardship [30, 31]. Beyond purely financial relief, the policy leveraged a structural “anchoring effect” within the healthcare system. Because accessing free medications necessitates regular follow-up prescriptions at primary healthcare facilities, the frequency of patient-clinician interactions increased significantly. This mechanism inadvertently increases the frequency of patient visits with general practitioners, thereby facilitating more rigorous and effective routine blood pressure monitoring, medication dose adjustments, and lifestyle counseling [32].

Limitations

Several limitations exist in this study: (1) Participant inclusion relied on survival and participation in the 2024 survey, while individuals who died or were lost to follow-up were excluded. This may introduce survivor bias, potentially overestimating the blood pressure benefits of the policy at the population level; (2) Differential follow-up completeness may exist between free and self-paid users, which could affect data integrity and trend estimation; (3) Policy implementation overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-related factors, such as reduced outdoor activities and altered healthcare access, might interfere with blood pressure outcomes and could not be fully separated from policy effects [33]; (4) This study only assessed the beneficial impacts of the policy on blood pressure control and failed to evaluate potential adverse events, including excessive medication and hypotension [34]; Additionally, inevitable measurement and entry errors exist in primary healthcare electronic health record data. Unmeasured confounding from concurrent public health policies and a lack of dynamic individual lifestyle data may also influence the results, and further prospective studies are needed for in-depth mechanism exploration [35].

Conclusions

This study, utilizing interrupted time series analysis, demonstrated that the free medication policy for older hypertensive patients in Quzhou serves not only as a vital social welfare initiative but also as a public health intervention with substantial clinical significance. The policy had significantly altered the longitudinal blood pressure trajectories of the older population, accelerating the natural decline of diastolic blood pressure while achieving a pivotal reversal in systolic blood pressure trends. Furthermore, methodologically, this research underscored the imperative of strictly controlling for seasonal fluctuations when evaluating long-sequence medical data to ensure the robustness and reliability of the findings.

Based on these conclusions, several policy recommendations are proposed. First, given the persistent upward trend of SBP in the older and its significant impact on cardiovascular outcomes, primary care management should prioritize precise SBP target achievement. Second, due to the strong seasonal dependence of blood pressure, it is recommended to significantly increase follow-up frequency during the first and fourth quarters and guide patients in timely medication escalation. Finally, considering the remarkable success of direct medication cost waivers in reversing deteriorating blood pressure trajectories, the “Quzhou Model” is strongly recommended for further promotion in regions with sufficient fiscal capacity. This serves as an effective public health barrier against the challenges of population aging and the burden of chronic diseases.
