Protocol for connector-enabled multigene pathway assembly using Golden Gate Assembly and yeast recombination
Authors: Min-Jun Seong, Jonghyeok Shin, Dae-Hee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for multigene pathway construction in Saccharomyces cerevisiae using a connector-enabled workflow that integrates Golden Gate Assembly with yeast homologous recombination. We provide procedures for assembling Level 1 transcriptional unit (TU) fragments using standardized connectors and directly co-transforming them into S. cerevisiae to form Level 2 multigene plasmids. Compared to conventional approaches, this protocol significantly reduces procedural complexity, minimizes dependence on operator expertise, and substantially accelerates workflow timelines.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Seong et al.1Subject areas: Molecular Biology, Systems biology, Biotechnology and bioengineering

Before you begin

Using the EffiModular platform,1 researchers can efficiently construct complex multigene metabolic pathways in S. cerevisiae. This protocol primarily focuses on the assembly of multigene pathways on episomal plasmids in S. cerevisiae using in vivo homologous recombination. While EffiModular can be adapted for genomic integration using CRISPR-Cas9, the standard workflow described here is optimized for constructing Level 2 expression plasmids.

The central component of the EffiModular platform is the connector, which mediates seamless in vivo assembly through homologous recombination in yeast. The connector system was designed based on the yeast MoClo toolkit and the Multiplex MoClo toolkit.2,3 In this protocol, we first describe the detailed construction of connectors and then present a complete workflow for the rapid assembly of multiple TUs, using the β-carotene biosynthetic pathway as a model system.

The overall assembly process consists of two main steps. First, the designed connectors are combined with Level 0 part plasmids—comprising a promoter, coding DNA sequence (CDS), and terminator—via Golden Gate Assembly to generate level 1 TU fragments. The resulting Level 1 TU fragments are directly co-transformed into yeast cells with a linearized destination vector, without any intermediate purification steps. Homologous recombination then occurs in vivo using the homologous sequences provided by the connectors, enabling direct assembly of the final expression vector, joining multiple transcriptional units in a predefined order within the yeast cell.

Innovation

A key advantage of EffiModular over existing hierarchical systems, such as standardized Modular Cloning (MoClo), is the complete elimination of intermediate steps—specifically Level 1 TU plasmid construction, bacterial transformation, and plasmid purification—by using connectors.2,3,4,5,6

EffiModular uses standardized orthogonal connector sequences to guide the direct assembly of Level 1 TU fragments into Level 2 multigene plasmids, thereby overcoming the throughput bottlenecks inherent to traditional synthetic biology workflows. Furthermore, unlike conventional yeast-based assembly methods that require generating new PCR-based homology regions for each new construct,7,8 EffiModular operates in a PCR-independent manner once the initial connector part library is constructed. This approach minimizes the risk of sequence mutations introduced by PCR errors and alleviates the logistical burden associated with repeated primer design and template preparation.

Preparation of a spacer plasmid (modified pYTK048)

Timing: 5 days

The spacer plasmid serves as a reusable scaffold for generating connector fragments and defines the orientation of the connector assembly. In this system, the connector is a short DNA fragment composed of a homology arm-spacer-homology arm architecture that links transcriptional units in yeast via homologous recombination. Because the spacer’s orientation determines the directionality of connector generation, precise control of restriction-site orientation is essential.

To ensure correct orientation during Level 1 TU fragment assembly, the pYTK048 spacer was engineered by introducing reversed BsaI recognition sites. This modification enables direct, orientation-controlled generation of connectors and allows the same spacer plasmid to be reused for subsequent preparations without further modification.2

Preparation of connectors for assembling multiple transcriptional units

Timing: 3 days

Connectors are functional DNA fragments derived from the spacer plasmid to physically link multiple transcriptional units through homologous recombination in yeast. Each connector contains predefined homology arms that determine the relative order and orientation of adjacent transcriptional units. Once constructed and sequence-verified, connectors can be PCR-amplified and reused across all downstream assemblies.

This procedure details connector construction using the modified pYTK048 spacer plasmid in combination with standardized yeast MoClo parts.

Timing: 6 days

For this purpose, a NotI restriction site was introduced into pYTK047, generating pYTK047_NotI. This linearizable pYTK_NotI backbone served as the destination vector throughout the study.

Expected outcomes

Upon successful completion of this protocol, researchers will obtain Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains harboring accurately assembled multigene expression cassettes generated through the EffiModular workflow. This protocol enables the direct assembly of multiple TUs into a single destination vector in yeast, eliminating intermediate Level 1 plasmid construction, bacterial cloning, or PCR amplification of TU fragments. This substantially reduces hands-on time, accelerating the construction of multigene metabolic pathways.

Depending on pathway design and complexity, this protocol supports the assembly of three to eight TUs in a single yeast transformation.1 Typically, three-TU assemblies achieve ∼90% efficiency, whereas efficiencies decrease to ∼60% for five TUs and ∼50% for eight TUs due to increased combinatorial complexity. Notably, when the yeast selection marker originally encoded on the destination vector is instead incorporated into one of the TU fragments, assembly efficiency for eight-TU constructs can be restored to approximately 80%.

Following yeast transformation, several hundred colonies typically appear on selective plates under standard transformation conditions. Correctly assembled colonies display uniform phenotypes consistent with the intended pathway function. For the β-carotene biosynthetic pathway, uniform colony pigmentation serves as a functional indicator of successful assembly. Sequencing randomly selected colonies further confirms the structural accuracy and fidelity of these constructs.

Limitations

A primary limitation of the EffiModular platform stems from its reliance on yeast homologous recombination, which restricts the reuse of identical DNA sequences within a single assembly. Reusing the same promoters or terminators across multiple TUs increases the risk of unintended recombination events, resulting in rearranged or incomplete assemblies. To mitigate this, the current workflow requires using orthogonal promoters or terminators for each transcriptional unit.

Although assemblies exceeding eight TUs have not been explicitly tested in this protocol, a continued decline in efficiency is anticipated as the number of fragments increases, likely due to the reduced probability that all TUs co-enter a single cell and recombine correctly. To address this issue, increasing selection stringency may help reduce false-positive colonies and improve recovery of correctly assembled constructs. As described above, one possible strategy is to position the yeast selection marker as an independent TU fragment at a strategic location (e.g., a central position) between other expression cassettes. In addition, although not tested in this protocol, additional marker configurations could be explored in future work.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low or no colony formation after yeast transformation (related to Step 4).

Potential solution

Confirm that yeast cells are harvested at an OD600 of approximately 0.8 and are handled gently during LiAc treatment.

Problem 2

High background of empty-vector colonies on selective plates (related to Step 4).

Potential solution

Increase the duration or enzyme concentration of NotI digestion to remove undigested plasmid.

Problem 3

Incorrect assembly order or missing transcriptional units in recovered colonies (related to Step 1).

Potential solution

Ensure that all connectors and TU fragments are designed with correct homology orientation. Adjust the molar ratio of TU fragments to destination vector (e.g., increasing TU fragment input). Avoid reuse of identical promoters or terminators across multiple transcriptional units.

Problem 4

Low efficiency in Golden Gate Assembly of TU fragments (related to Step 1).

Potential solution

Confirm the activity of BsaI-HFv2 and T4 DNA ligase using a control reaction. Optimize cycling conditions or increase the number of GGA cycles if necessary.
