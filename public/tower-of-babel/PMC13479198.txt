Optimising doctor–administrator leadership dyads in healthcare organisations
Authors: Vilert Loving
Published: 2026

Healthcare is complicated. Patients’ clinical presentations and expectations are unpredictable, healthcare settings are variable, unintended complications occur and third party political, regulatory and financial stakeholders influence clinical decisions. This milieu of factors results in a complex system that constantly challenges healthcare organisations to improve their provision of care. Effective leadership is essential for guiding healthcare organisations through this complex system.

Compared with non-clinical administrative leaders (ALs), physician leaders foster higher care quality, care delivery efficiency and physician job satisfaction due to their extensive clinical training and experience.1 2 Unfortunately, with the increasing complexity of healthcare, it is difficult for any leader to possess the subject matter expertise to effectively lead alone. Doctors often lack formal training in business, operations and finance. In contrast, non-clinical managers lack in-depth clinical knowledge. Thus, many healthcare organisations are turning to a dyad leadership model. In a dyad model, a doctor leader (DL) is paired with an AL, and they co-lead the organisation and/or business units within the organisation. This partnership marries the DL’s clinical expertise and the AL’s business knowledge. The dyad model is increasingly popular, with 77% of healthcare organisations using dyad leadership,3 and this trend is true globally.4 5 6 However, such cross-functional teams introduce challenges, with one report estimating that 75% are dysfunctional.7 This article will discuss the benefits and challenges of the dyad leadership model, and it will offer actionable suggestions for organisations to support dyad partnerships and for leaders within a dyad partnership to increase their effectiveness.

Benefits

Historically, healthcare organisational leadership has been the domain of administrators, while doctors autonomously perform their clinical work within the confines of the organisation. This setup engenders a cultural conflict: administrators may believe that doctors prioritise patient care at the expense of organisational performance, whereas doctors may believe that administrators prioritise organisational performance at the expense of clinical outcomes.8 Dyads inherently include doctors into organisational leadership conversations, thereby increasing physician engagement with operational processes.5 9 10 Increased physician engagement correlates with improved clinical quality, patient satisfaction, safety, finances and staff retention.11 12

Dyad partnerships have the potential to improve decision accuracy compared to solo leaders. Research on teams has repeatedly shown that diverse teams composed of individuals with different backgrounds produce higher quality decisions compared with individual experts and teams of individuals with similar backgrounds.13 14 This advantage results from the increased diversity of proposed solutions to a problem, increasing the odds that optimal solutions are achieved. However, this advantage deteriorates with large groups in complex scenarios because information sharing between team members results in convergence of ideas around key opinion leaders.15 Thus, fewer individuals formulate independent opinions. In complex settings, small groups maintain the diversity advantage over large groups.15 Dyad partnerships qualify as small groups with individuals of differing backgrounds operating within a complex environment. This synergistic partnership offers organisational decisions that may be superior to those of individual leaders functioning independently.

In addition to decision accuracy, dyads accelerate operationalisation of new policies and procedures. Since dyads consist of ALs and DLs, this partnership intrinsically encourages communication between doctors and administrators. This cross-cohort communication is important because it prompts faster adoption of change.16 When the organisation appoints respected DLs within dyads and empowers these dyads to make key decisions, front-line doctors are more likely to believe that their opinions were taken into account, expediting their acceptance.11 16

Challenges

Dyad partnerships may be challenged with doctors’ lack of formal training in collaboration skills. Conventionally, doctors function as solo leaders of hierarchical clinical services, with nurses, assistants and administrative staff playing supportive roles. Co-leadership may not develop naturally for doctors.17 Ineffective collaborative team leadership results in less efficient team operations and increased workplace stress.18 As a corollary, because of healthcare organisations’ traditionally hierarchical culture, DLs within dyads are sometimes perceived as more important partners than ALs, and this inequity may hinder ALs’ ability to influence staff.8

Another challenge is that healthcare organisations may not explicitly define dyad leaders’ primary role, decision-making authority and/or reporting structure, especially for newly minted dyad teams.8 19 In any leadership position, lack of role clarity will prevent effective strategy development since team goals will be vague. Further, dyads without explicitly delegated authority may be disengaged since the dyad partners could feel that they are simply delivering decisions from senior leadership rather than making decisions themselves.19 Regarding reporting structure, if each dyad partner has an independent reporting structure, it may result in the dyad partners working in siloes with little shared accountability for projects.10 Independent reporting structures may also slow the resolution of decision impasses because it may be uncertain which supervisor adjudicates.

Finally, time constraints impede dyad partnerships.9 19 In particular, DLs often maintain their clinical practice, and patient care competes with administrative duties. Thus, for time-critical dyad leadership decisions, the DL may not be readily available for the AL. When DLs have insufficient protected time to address administrative duties, they may abdicate much of their responsibilities, thereby removing their clinical expertise from the dyad partnership.20

Factors for success

Organisational-level factors

At the organisational level, there are four factors that senior leadership should address to set up the dyad for success. First, the organisation should clearly define the dyad’s roles and responsibilities, and senior leadership must explicitly delegate the authority to oversee those responsibilities.8 The dyad should be assigned specific and difficult goals that stimulate innovation and group performance, as opposed to non-specific and overly easy goals that demotivate teams and decrease creativity.21 These goals should explicitly detail why the dyad exists and their metrics for success.

Second, dyad leaders should be carefully selected, favouring personable individuals with high emotional intelligence. While technical subject matter expertise is important, interpersonal skills are the most critical attribute of dyad leaders.22 Goleman found that emotional intelligence is twice as important as intelligence and technical skills for effective team leaders.23 Additionally, strongly opposing personalities may inhibit decision-making and potentially lead to one dyad partner dominating the other.24

Third, the organisation should invest resources to train the selected individuals in collaborative leadership skills. For example, National Health Service Trusts have developed paired learning programmes that successfully train DLs and ALs to better understand each other’s viewpoints and priorities, facilitating future collaboration.6 Kim et al reported that organisational leadership training for DLs and ALs increased effectiveness of their partnership.9

Fourth, the organisation must provide protected time for the dyad, particularly for DLs due to their competing patient care priorities. Studies have estimated that 10%–20% of protected administrative time may be sufficient for DLs to be effective dyad leaders,5 9 though this estimate varies based on the scope of the dyad’s responsibilities.

Dyad-level factors

In addition to senior leadership implementing organisational-level success factors, dyad partners have three factors that they can readily implement to increase their leadership effectiveness. First, regular and open communication is critical to building trust. Communication and technical skills are fundamental requirements for workplace trust.25 Due to barriers to entry, skill is largely assumed in the healthcare industry, leaving communication as the primary driver of dyadic trust. Importantly, trust is facilitated when people share similar backgrounds.26 Because of their shared training and roles, doctors often innately trust other doctors and administrators trust other administrators. Dyad leaders must work harder to establish trust given their lack of background commonalities. Therefore, it is critical for dyads to openly share information, resources and thoughts at the outset to initiate a strong foundation of trust.26 Regular formal and informal dyad meetings help to establish this information sharing precedent.

Second, dyad partners should agree to a shared vision and goals, with mutual accountability.8 Although ALs generally focus on operational metrics and DLs focus on clinical metrics, dyad partners should view those metrics as individual components to broader shared goals that align their priorities.19 For example, if a dyad agrees to the shared goal of improving patient access to specialists, the AL may address scheduling workflow optimisation while the DL streamlines the clinical criteria for appointment appropriateness. To cultivate collaboration, both partners should be accountable and judged on the shared goal’s outcome.

Third, the dyad partners should constantly project a joint leadership image to all stakeholders.19 All implemented policies and procedures should be issued by the dyad partnership, not the individual AL or DL. Moreover, any significant decisions that are within the dyad’s scope should be jointly decided. For example, if a staff doctor asks the DL to change an important policy, the DL should state that this decision will be discussed with the AL for a mutual decision. This intentional messaging will highlight the dyad’s shared vision and reinforce their foundation of open communication.

Conclusions

Healthcare organisations are increasingly implementing dyad leadership teams consisting of ALs partnered with DLs. These teams benefit from synergising the operational and clinical expertise, respectively, of these leaders. However, these leaders’ differing professional training, siloed priorities and competing time commitments may hinder their effectiveness. Organisational-level and dyad-level factors can be applied to counteract these hindrances and improve these dyadic partnerships’ dynamics.

IN PRACTICE

The surgery department at Local Hospital is led by a surgeon, Dr John Doe, and the operating room manager, Mary Smith. Local Hospital’s senior leadership chose John and Mary due to their ability to negotiate disagreements among colleagues, indicating their high emotional intelligence, and because their opinions and talent are respected by clinicians and staff. The Local Hospital identified increasing postoperative infections in its recent quality metrics, and senior leadership tasked the dyad partners to decrease these infections by 25% over the next 12 months. John and Mary mutually agree to include this metric as a component of their incentive compensation bonuses for the year. John focuses on surgeons’ and nurses’ hand hygiene, intraoperative catheterisation criteria and technique, and postoperative incentive spirometry utilisation. Mary focuses on equipment sterilisation procedures and implementing mandatory operating room staff education programmes. John and Mary meet biweekly to discuss their progress, and they jointly lead operating room doctor and staff quarterly town halls to openly discuss new protocols and receive feedback. John and Mary deliberate at their biweekly meetings before issuing any key decisions on behalf of the dyad leadership.

EXPRESS CHECKOUT

Dyad partnerships are increasingly the preferred leadership structure for healthcare organisations. These partnerships combine the complementary expertise of an administrative leader and a doctor leader. The dyad leadership structure potentially improves physician engagement, healthcare quality, decision accuracy and change adoption. However, dyads may be challenged by ineffective team collaboration, unclear role delineation and doctors’ competing clinical obligations. Organisations can facilitate success by defining dyadic responsibilities, choosing emotionally intelligent leaders, sponsoring collaborative skills training and protecting doctors’ administrative time. Dyad partners can further these efforts by communicating openly, creating mutually accountable goals and continuously projecting a unified dyadic image.
