Diversity of molecular karyotypes of 234 pediatric patients with autism spectrum disorder in chromosomal microarray analysis
Authors: Gabriela Ręka, Katarzyna Wojciechowska, Borys Styka, Mariola Janiszewska, Monika Lejman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

In 25–35% of ASD cases, the genetic etiology (high-penetrance variants and copy number variations – CNVs) can be identified through whole-exome sequencing or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a group of complex neurodevelopmental disorders that include deficits in social communication and maintaining relationships, and repetitive and atypical sensory-motor behaviors. ASD is currently viewed as a spectrum with a varied set of characteristics that manifest in unique ways in individuals, many of whom require lifelong support of some kind [1, 2]. According to The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), ASD patients might require mild, moderate, or very substantial support. It is estimated that the incidence of these disorders ranges from 1% to 2% in the general population, with a predominance of men over women 4–5:1 [2–5]. The issue of ASD and the search for its etiology is becoming an increasingly popular research topic. The etiology of ASD includes genetic and epigenetic factors, biological conditions of the structure and functioning of the brain, environmental factors in the perinatal period, and immunological disorders [1, 4, 6]. Current evidence emphasizes the role of complex interplay between gene-environment interactions influenced by epigenetic modifications [1, 2]. Over the past 40–50 years, there has been a tremendous increase in research into the genetics of ASD and a growing number of discoveries [1, 7]. Thanks to large-scale studies using whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), hundreds of high-confidence genes already have robust statistical evidence, for example, in the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) database [8]. Current research focuses on the functional convergence of these genes in synaptic signaling pathways and chromatin regulation. High heritability (70–90% in twin studies) encompasses the total genetic contribution, including common polygenic variants that are not yet captured by routine clinical testing [1]. It has been estimated that in approximately 25–35% of cases, the genetic etiology of ASD (high-penetrance variants and copy number variations – CNVs) can be identified through whole-exome sequencing or chromosomal microarray [1, 4, 9]. The unidentified 65–75% of ASD cases likely represent a complex polygenic architecture rather than a lack of genetic influence. ASD can present as an isolated condition (idiopathic/non-syndromic – with no associated signs or symptoms) or as part of a broader clinical picture (syndromic ASD – associated with morphological signs/symptoms useful in the diagnosis of specific genetic disorders) [2, 10]. Clinical symptoms suggestive of ASD may be caused by genetic diseases, such as fragile X syndrome, Rett syndrome, Angelman syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, Smith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [4, 5]. ASD associated phenotypes (intellectual disability, dysmorphisms, congenital malformations) might determine the success of genomic diagnosis. Stratification based on endophenotypes or on the presence of clinical comorbidities is what allows patients to be subdivided into groups with higher diagnostic yield [11]. The overall risk of recurrence of the disorder among siblings of the affected person is rated to be between 6.1% and 24.7% [12]. Epigenetic modification of gene expression includes deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or histone modification, DNA methylation, regulation of chromatin remodeling, or nucleosome assembly [4]. The multiscale genomic architecture of ASD includes inversions, translocations, rare single nucleotide variants (SNVs), common variants (the significant contribution of the polygenic background captured by Polygenic Risk Scores), structural variations (CNVs – large-scale deletions and duplications that can occur de novo due to non-allelic homologous recombination or be inherited), and regulatory/non-coding variation (de novo events in enhancers and promoters that impact gene expression levels, for example as detectable via ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing) [2, 4, 5, 13]. Rare gene variants contributing to an increased risk of ASD may be inherited, arise from germline de novo mutations, or be a result of postzygotic mosaicism [7]. Inheritance patterns and phenotypic expressivity might be varied: heterogeneity of ASD is increased by incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, recessive inheritance, and X-linked variants, variants inherited from unaffected parents, and subclinical traits in family members suggest a complex inheritance of common variants. In the literature, the following example CNV locus are mentioned: 1p36.22, 1q21.1, 1q24.2, 2p13.1, 2p16.3, 2q13, 2q37.3, 3p26.2, 3q29, 4q34.2, 6q24.3, 6q27, 7q35, 8q24.22, 9p21.3, 9q31.2, 12p13.31, 13q13.2-q22, 15q11-q13, 15q22, 16p11.2, 16p13.2, 16q23, 17p11.2, 7q11.23, 18q21.1, 22q11, 22q13, and Xp22 [1, 2, 4, 8, 11, 13]. Among genomic alterations associated with ASD, variants have been distributed in different genes across the genome encoding neuronal signaling proteins, transcription factors, and proteins involved in synaptic architecture [14]. Such variants can result in syndromic phenotypes (specific syndrome of which ASD is only a phenotypic component) or contribute to polygenic vulnerability in non-syndromic autism [4, 5]. These findings highlight the complex genetic architecture of ASD. Cheroni et al. indicated that even from 913 to 1,379 human genes are associated in various ways with ASD [15].

Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) is a method designed for the assessment of molecular karyotypes and detecting submicroscopic genomic imbalances. It reveals CNVs (deletions, duplications) and unbalanced aberrations of the number and structure of chromosomes. Detection of LOH and uniparental disomy (UPD) is exclusive to single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays or hybrid arrays: comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) + SNP. Conventional array CGH platforms detect only CNVs, failing to identify regions of homozygosity without a copy-number change (copy-neutral LOH). CMA, although superior to conventional karyotyping, still has resolution limitations compared with WGS for detecting very small microdeletions or SNVs.

The study aims to analyze molecular karyotypes and compare genetic abnormalities in CMA in children with ASD in an independent clinical cohort and assess the potential relationships between CNV and phenotypic features of ASD in comparison to literature data. Secondary objectives included evaluating clinical and genetic differences across ASD subtypes and identifying distinct genomic subgroups, thereby facilitating the delineation of risk profiles and guiding tailored clinical management strategies in ASD.

Methods

The Independent Laboratory of Genetic Diagnostics of the Department of Pediatrics of the Medical University of Lublin provided single nucleotide polymorphism and microarray-based comparative genomic hybridization results from pediatric patients diagnosed between 2012 and 2024. Genetic tests were ordered according to clinical indications by a geneticist or neurologist from the Genetic Outpatient Clinic or the Department of Pediatric Neurology at Prof. Antoni Gębala Children’s Hospital of Lublin. Overall, 234 individuals who met diagnostic criteria for ASD according to DSM-5 or International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) were included in the study. All patients showed significant deficits in areas necessary for the diagnosis of ASD: social communication, maintaining relationships, and repetitive, atypical sensory-motor behaviors. Inclusion criteria were age ≤ 18, diagnosis of ASD, and being tested in CMA analysis in the past. Exclusion criteria were age > 18, no confirmation of ASD, and lack of being tested with CMA. It was a group of patients with ASD diagnosis from the Central and Eastern Europe macroregion – from the Lublin voivodeship and the immediate vicinity (230 – Polish origin, 4 – Ukrainian origin). The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Medical University of Lublin, Poland (Committee’s reference number: KE-0254/228/11/2022). CMA was performed using CytoScan 750 K and XON platforms, Thermo Fisher Scientific. GRCh37 (hg19) reference genome build was used for CNV coordinates. Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) Software versions 3.3 and 4.3 were used for analysis (v.3.3 for earlies data and v.4.3 for newer results, a potential source of technical variability). International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN) standards were used to report CNV results. Retrospective and prospective cross-sectional analysis of patients’ medical documentation was performed. The following databases were evaluated to assess the pathogenicity of gene variants: DECIPHER, ClinVar, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), Franklin, VarSome, ClinGen, and Database of Genomic Variants [16–22]. Detected CNV were allocated into 1 of 5 grades (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain/unknown significance – VUS, likely benign, benign variants) according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines. Likely benign and benign variants were not reported in the results. We considered all results of parental CMA testing (the abovementioned CMA methodology) if they were available. Lack of parental testing resulted, for example, from the absence of clinical indications for the inheritance testing, inability to test biological parents due to adoption, long waiting time for the next doctor’s appointment, or performing genetic tests at the moment of collecting data, and lack of parents’ consent to the inheritance testing.

Statistical analyses were conducted with an a priori alpha level of α = 0.05 for determining significance, utilizing two-sided p-values. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize demographic, clinical, and genetic variables. Continuous variables were characterized by mean (standard deviation) [minimum, maximum] with 95% confidence intervals (CI) derived via the t-distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson score method. Comparative analyses stratified the cohort by CMA clinical interpretation (normal, VUS, pathogenic) and presence of Asperger syndrome. For continuous outcomes involving three independent groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, incorporating Welch’s correction for unequal variances. Significant ANOVA results prompted post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the Games-Howell test, with group differences denoted via compact letter display (CLD). Group differences from post-hoc pairwise comparisons are reported using CLD notation, wherein groups sharing a common superscript letter do not differ significantly at the adjusted α = 0.05 threshold, whereas groups bearing different letters exhibit statistically significant differences [23]. To address multiple comparisons and control the family-wise error rate, the Holm-Bonferroni adjustment was applied in post-hoc pairwise tests. Categorical outcomes were assessed with Fisher’s exact test for small expected cell frequencies, or Pearson’s chi-squared test otherwise, followed by post-hoc pairwise chi-squared tests and CLD (for 3 groups), and the false discovery rate (FDR) method was used for adjusting for multiple comparisons. Comparisons of continuous outcomes and categorical outcomes constitute two structurally distinct hypothesis families. The continuous family comprises Games–Howell pairwise tests, which already incorporate simultaneous family-wise error rate (FWER) control via the studentised range distribution; the Holm–Bonferroni adjustment provides an additional conservative layer [24, 25]. The categorical family comprises pairwise chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, typically encompassing a larger number of comparisons with individually lower statistical power; the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method is applied here to limit the expected proportion of false discoveries while preserving adequate sensitivity [26]. This family-level differentiation follows the principle that the choice of error rate should reflect the inferential structure and multiplicity burden of each hypothesis family, rather than being imposed uniformly irrespective of family characteristics [27–29]. Effect sizes for ANOVA were quantified using eta-squared (η²), categorized as small (0.01–0.06), medium (0.06–0.14), or large (> 0.14). For the exploratory cluster analysis, patient-level aggregated data were derived from CNV-level observations. Eight continuous variables entered the analysis: number of duplications, number of deletions, mean CNV size (kilobase pairs – kbp, megabase pairs – Mbp), mean total gene count per CNV, proportion of pathogenic variants, proportion of VUS variants, proportion of p-arm involvement, and proportion of q-arm involvement. No raw categorical variables were included; binary indicators at the CNV level were aggregated into continuous proportions at the patient level. Records with missing values on any key variable were excluded via listwise deletion, reducing the cohort from 234 to 92 patients. Z-score standardization (mean = 0, SD = 1) was applied to the complete feature matrix prior to principal component analysis (PCA). Two compositional pairs (VUS + pathogenic = 1; p-arm + q-arm = 1) introduced perfect collinearity (r = − 1.000); PCA resolved this by assigning zero variance to the redundant components (PC7 and PC8), ensuring no double-weighting in subsequent clustering. A sensitivity analysis excluding one variable from each pair confirmed stable results (see Results). Hierarchical clustering on principal components (HCPC) was applied via the FactoMineR package [30] using Ward’s linkage with the D2 (squared Euclidean) metric. The optimal number of clusters was determined by FactoMineR’s internal criterion, which identifies the partition corresponding to the largest relative drop in within-cluster inertia across successive dendrogram cuts [31], evaluating partitions from k = 2 to k = 5. Supplementary validation indices confirmed the three-cluster solution: silhouette width (peak at k = 3, average width = 0.42), Calinski–Harabasz index (maximum at k = 3, value = 48.4), Davies–Bouldin index (minimum at k = 3, value = 0.943), and multi-index validation via NbClust (endorsing k = 3 in 18/30 metrics) [32]. Three clusters were chosen following this criterion. Cluster profiles reported v-tests for deviations from the overall mean, along with means (standard deviations) and p-values, emphasizing parameters with significant differences (p < 0.05); non-significant variables conformed to cohort-wide distributions. Analyses were conducted using R statistical software on Windows 11 Pro 64 bit (build 26100), using the packages rio (v1.2.1), factoextra (v1.0.7), FactoMineR (v2.11), sjPlot (v2.8.15), report (v0.5.8), rcompanion (v2.4.36), gtsummary (v2.2.0), ggplot2 (v3.5.0), dplyr (v1.1.4), and psych (v2.4.6.26) [30, 33–42].

Results

The research group exhibited a marked male predominance; it consisted of 182 males (77.78%, 95% CI 72, 83) and 52 females (22.22%). The mean age in the time of drawing blood for CMA was 6 years 10 months (median: 6 years 2 months, standard deviation (SD): 3 years 7 months, minimal: 8 months, maximal: 16 years 11 months). There were 7 sibling pairs (in this 1 dizygotic twin pair) and 1 three siblings (three non-twin brothers). The siblings of 9.83% of children in the research group were diagnosed with ASD. Patients with early childhood autism represented 43.16% of the research group (95% CI 37, 50), patients with Asperger syndrome – 11.11%, while patients with atypical autism – 2.56%, in others, ASD was not specified. In pregnancy interviews, mothers of 8.97% of patients were diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, obesity, hypothyroidism, or were exposed to harmful chemical factors.

Characteristics of the research group according to comorbidities

In the research group, 58.97% of patients with ASD presented with a normal molecular karyotype. In the abnormal CMA results, the most common were single duplications (14.53%), followed – single deletions (11.97%). In 3 patients, copy number state was 4 (genomic triplication). Multiple CNVs occurred in 12.82%, while other genomic variants (monosomy X, additional Y, LOH, mosaicism) appeared in 1.71%. The diagnostic yield for pathogenic and likely pathogenic CNVs was 8.55% (calculated as a percentage of patients with pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants relative to the total number of patients in the cohort). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were described in 20.83% of patients with abnormal CMA results, 17.71% of them concerned autistic features. VUS were found in 79.17% of patients with abnormal CMA results. Duplication of the 15q13.3 region was noted 4 times (1.71% of patients) and includes exon 1 of the OTUD7A gene and exons 1–4 of CHRNA7. Duplications of the following regions: 1p36.32 (ACTRT2, PRDM16), 3q22.1, 5q14.1, 9p24.1, 9p24.3, 11q23.3 (KMT2A, TMEM25), 12q24.13q24.21 (RBM19), 12q24.33 (TMEM132D), 15q11.2 (NIPA2, NIPA1, CYFIP1, TUBGCP5), 22q11.21 (RIMBP3C, RIMBP3B, HIC2), and 7q33, 22q12.3, Yq11.23 deletions (DAZ3, DAZ2) were noted 2 times each. Deletions and duplications were identified within the same genomic regions in the following locations: 1q21.1 (NBPF20, NBPF19, NBPF10, LINC01719, RNVU1-6, HJV, TXNIP, POLR3GL, ANKRD34A, LIX1L, LIX1L-AS1, RBM8A, GNRHR2, PEX11B, ITGA10, ANKRD35, PIAS3, MIR6736, NUDT17, POLR3C, RNF115, CD160, PDZK1), 1q21.2, 1q32.2, 2p16.3 (NRXN1), 2q33.1, 3q22.1, 3q29, 9p24.2, 9p24.3 (DOCK8), 15q13.3 (CHRNA7), 16p13.2, 22q11.22 (TOP3B), and Yq11.23 (DAZ3, DAZ2). Deletions were not found on chromosomes 11, 14, 20, 21, while duplications – on 14, 19, 20, which is attributable to limited cohort size. Taking into account the individuals whose parents were examined for CNV carriage, most variants were inherited from fathers regardless of the CNV type (deletions in 56.25%, duplications in 47.06%), then from mothers (deletions in 25.0%, duplications in 35.29%), and the least common were de novo variants (deletions in 18.75%, duplications in 17.65%). Genomic variants manifested in roughly one-quarter of the cohort, with duplications and deletions each averaging 0.3 and 0.2 per patient, respectively. Upon stratification according to CMA outcomes – normal in 138 cases (59.0%, 95% CI: 52, 65), VUS in 76 (32.5%, 95% CI: 27, 39), and pathogenic in 20 (8.6%, 95% CI: 5.4, 13) – the subgroups displayed largely aligned demographic attributes, encompassing sex ratios, ages, and ASD subtype distributions. The normal subgroup closely paralleled the broader cohort’s comorbidity profile, featuring elevated incidences of dysmorphic features (80.3%) and psychomotor delay (92.3%), yet with scant genetic deviations. Conversely, the VUS category mirrored clinical presentations but revealed notable CNVs, including duplications in 64.5% and deletions in 48.7%. Although smaller in size, the pathogenic subgroup exhibited heightened comorbidities in specific areas, such as universal psychomotor delay (100.0%) and gastrointestinal issues (25.0%), accompanied by substantial genetic perturbations – duplications in 50.0%, deletions in 65.0% – and an increased syndromic prevalence (20.0%).

Statistically significant divergences surfaced across selected clinical and genetic domains. Perinatal complications showed variation (p = 0.043), with post-hoc analyses indicating reduced occurrence in the VUS group (9.2%) compared to normal (22.5%) and pathogenic (20.0%) strata. Established genetic disorders also varied substantially (p = 0.013), clustering predominantly within the pathogenic subset (20.0%) versus normal (2.9%) and VUS (2.6%). Maternal gestational conditions echoed this trend (p = 0.020), rising in the pathogenic group (25.0%) relative to normal (5.8%) and VUS (10.5%). Genetic indicators displayed the starkest contrasts, with duplication and deletion counts and prevalences (both p < 0.001) negligible in normal cases but markedly amplified in VUS and pathogenic categories (Table 9). It is highly probable that fetal genomic alterations altered the gestational environment and might cause complicated pregnancies.

In an exploratory analysis, stratification based on the presence of Asperger syndrome revealed two subgroups: patients exhibiting the syndrome (n = 26) and those without it (patients with early childhood autism, atypical autism, and not specified) (n = 208). Individuals with Asperger syndrome presented a comparatively milder profile in terms of cognitive and developmental aspects, marked by intellectual disability in 11.5% (95% CI: 3.0-30.5) and psychomotor developmental delay in 37.5% (95% CI: 10.3–74.0) of evaluated instances, which reflect diagnostic criteria. Dysmorphic features appeared in 81.0% (95% CI: 59.1–93.2). Discussion

Submicroscopic chromosomal abnormalities are one of the most common genetic etiologies of intellectual disability, delayed psychomotor development, ASD, dysmorphic features, and congenital defects [46–48]. CMA has historically been the first-line test in ASD patients with a diagnostic yield ranging from 7% to 9%, and remains a valuable first-tier option, especially when phenotype does not indicate the occurrence of a known syndrome of single-gene etiology and in resource-limited settings, but comprehensive sequencing (WES/WGS) is increasingly recognized as the preferred first-line approach for neurodevelopmental disorders [11, 46, 47]. Other studies reported even better diagnostic yields of CMA, ranging between 9.3% and 29% of ASD patients, significantly higher compared to conventional karyotyping [9]. Knowing the genetic etiology of ASD in a child brings comfort to their parents, allows for the prediction of the clinical evolution of the disorder, facilitates making an appropriate rehabilitation plan, improves the efficiency and quality of clinical management, and helps in genetic counseling with establishing recurrence risk in offspring [46, 47]. In our study, the diagnostic yield for pathogenic and likely pathogenic CNVs was 8.55%, which is quite comparable with previous studies and shows the usefulness of CMA in diagnosing the genetic etiology of ASD.

ASD is a heterogeneous disorder influenced by chromosome aberrations, identified syndromes, and a complex interplay between inheritance, environmental factors, and epigenetics [49]. ASD might be a phenotypic feature of many genetic diseases. In the research group, the following diseases with possible ASD occurrence were diagnosed: fragile X syndrome, Jacobs syndrome, Turner syndrome, KBG syndrome (del 16q24.3), Smith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome, and Cohen syndrome [49–52]. Fragile X syndrome is identified as the most common cause of familial intellectual disability and/or ASD, primarily in men [49]. In the study by Joseph et al., in the group of patients with XYY syndrome, rates of intellectual disability, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and ASD were increased relative to the general population. The rate of ASD was 14% and these patients exhibited ASD symptom profiles resembling those observed in ASD without XYY [50]. The constitutional translocation t(11;22)(q23;q11.2) is the most common recurrent non-Robertsonian translocation in humans, usually without clinical symptoms, because the rearrangement of functional genes is not interrupted [52]. In the literature, there was no information about the connection between translocation 46,Y, der(X)t(X; Y)(p22.23;q11.22) and ASD.

According to the literature, ASD susceptibility has been associated with microdeletions or duplications in various independent chromosomal regions, including: 1q21.1, 1q24.2, 1p36.22, 2p13.1, 2q13, 2q37.3, 3p26.2, 4q34.2, 6q24.3, 6q27, 7q11, 7q35, 8q24.22, 9p21.3, 9q31.2, 12p13.31, 13q13.2-q22, 15q11-q13, 15q13.2-q13.3, 15q22, 16p11.2, 16p13.2, 17p11.2, 18q21.1, 22q11, 22q13, and Xp22 [49, 53]. This data, together with our study, confirms the visible genetic diversity possible to detect in CMA in children with ASD. Locus and the size of the CNV, the number of duplicated or deleted genes, inheritance, and pathogenicity of detected variants are highly variable. Our study contributed a cohort-level characterization of CNV in the independent clinical sample of ASD children. Our data confirms the recurrent locus of CNVs described in the literature in broader ASD and neurodevelopmental disorder cohorts.

Duplication of the 15q13.3 region encompasses exons 1–4 of the CHRNA7 gene. CHRNA7 encodes neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunit alpha-7, which is a member of a superfamily of ligand-gated ion channels that mediate fast signal transmission at synapses [18]. In the cohort of Spanish ASD patients in the study by Sandoval-Talamantes et al. CHRNA7 was one of the genes frequently observed in CNV. However, duplications of this gene were classified as VUS in 5 individuals, because CHRNA7 duplications were noted both in patients with and without ASD [54]. The prevalence of 15q13.3 microduplications among control populations has been previously reported from 0.55% to 0.62%. In our research, the prevalence is 1.71% (4/234). Gillentine et al. concluded that patients with 15q13.3 duplication have a higher prevalence of ASD (35%) relative to the general population; however, neither was it significantly higher than that of deletion probands (31%) [55]. Deletions of the 15q13.3 region, including CHRNA7, have been established as pathogenic [55]. In our results, all four 15q13.3 duplications and one 15q13.3 deletion, which encompassed the CHRNA7 gene, were classified as VUS. Biologically, duplications generally cause a milder phenotype than deletions [46]. However, based on the study results, it would be appropriate to conduct research with a control group on a larger population.

The second gene encompassed by 15q13.3 duplication is the OTUD7A gene. OTUD7A encodes a protease, which is a member of a family of deubiquitinating enzymes. It specifically cleaves ubiquitin linkages from proteins targeted for degradation. Localization of OTUD7A to the postsynaptic density in the brain suggests a function in the development and maturation of synapses. The OTUD7A gene is probably involved in brain-specific synaptic signaling [18]. Budisteanu et al. presented 5 cases with 15q13.3 duplication, which included the CHRNA7 and OTUD7A genes. Autistic behavior was revealed in examinations in all 5 patients, according to DSM-5 criteria [56]. Duplications of the 15q13.3 region result in highly variable penetrance, with some patients having no clinical manifestation, while 15q13.3 deletions usually cause severe clinical phenotypes [57]. Lowther et al. assessed 246 individuals with deletions within 15q13.3. ASD was present in 10.9% of cases [58]. Unlike 15q13.3 deletions, which carry established pathogenicity, duplications in this region show limited evidence, low triplosensitivity, and are substantially represented in population controls, where they are frequently classified as VUS or low-penetrance risk alleles. Duplications of the 15q13.3 region in our cohort likely represent VUS or low-penetrance findings.

Deletion 1q21.1q21.2 is in the location of a region of known microdeletion syndrome 1q21.1, in which ASD and developmental delay are clinical manifestations. Among individuals with CNVs at 1q21, ASD is more frequent in those with duplications than in those with deletions [18, 59, 60].

The phenotype of deletion 2p16.3 covering the NRXN1 gene includes ASD. NRXN1 encodes neurexin-1, which belongs to highly polymorphic presynaptic cell-adhesion molecules. Neurexins establish and maintain synaptic connections. In the study by Al Shehhi et al., 60% of individuals with NRXN1 deletion presented with ASD [61].

Deletion 10q26.3, including the EBF3 gene, is a known 10q26 deletion syndrome with possible developmental delay and moderate intellectual disability as clinical manifestations. EBF3 encodes one of a family of highly homologous transcription factors [18]. Minhas et al. described two brothers with ASD who carried an unbalanced translocation involving loss of 10q26.2; however, ASD is generally not considered as a part of the 10q deletion syndrome phenotype [62].

Another known syndrome that is associated with ASD is 16p11.2 microdeletion syndrome, reported in 1 patient from the study group (0.43%). According to the literature, in the general population, the occurrence of 16p11.2 microdeletions has been diagnosed at a frequency ranging between 0.028% and 0.043% [63]. 16p11.2 microdeletions affect nearly 1 in 200 patients with ASD and approximately 1 in 1000 patients with neuropsychiatric or language disorders. Studies indicated that the 16p11.2 microdeletion is strongly linked to ASD and intellectual disability. Cognitive and motor impairments are associated features, with ASD being one of the most prevalent phenotype characteristics for this deletion [63].

There is no proven relationship between 1p36.32 duplication and ASD in the literature. However, duplications in the possibly pathogenic CNV region 1p36 were diagnosed in a few individuals with ASD and delayed speech development [48].

15q11-q13 duplication syndrome is a well-known syndrome associated with ASD [48]. Gene expression patterns and correlation with features of ASD were studied in patients with chromosome 15 disorders (Prader-Willi, Angelman, and chromosome 15 duplication syndromes), including the UBE3A gene encoding ubiquitin protein ligase E3A and SNORD116, involved in RNA processing. Findings suggested the presence of interactions between the expression of UBE3A and SNORD116, with brain-specific processes underlying language and motor impairments in ASD [49]. This duplication syndrome was diagnosed in one patient from the research group (dup 15q11.1q13.1). In the second child with duplication 15q11.2 of smaller size, it was classified as VUS.

Deletions and duplications located in 9p24.3 that included DOCK8 variants are correlated with ASD. We identified two patients (one with a deletion and one with a duplication of 9p24). Sandoval-Talamantes et al. in the cohort of 212 individuals with ASD found one patient with 9p24 duplication and one with 9p24 deletion [54].

22q11.2 microduplication has many overlapping clinical features with the well-known 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, like dysmorphic features, speech delay, intellectual deficits, and behavioral/psychiatric problems [64]. Individuals with 22q11.2 duplication have elevated rates of diagnoses of ASD. The rate of ASD in the cohort of patients with 22q11.2 duplication in the study by Wenger et al. was 14–25%, with an additional third of individuals presenting some features of ASD or other neuropsychiatric concerns [65].

Functions of the assessed genes in the research group are mainly concerned with neurodevelopment, formation, differentiation, and functioning of synapses, metabolism, immune system, RNA, transcription, translation, cell cycle regulation, cell signaling and transport, apoptosis, detoxification, mitochondrial and cytoskeletal functions, or were pseudogenes. This observation confirms the high clinical heterogeneity of the genetic picture of ASD patients, including the basis related to the functioning of the nervous system based on genomic variants. However, most patients carried variants of unknown clinical significance or, in some cases, known to cause only intellectual disability.

Analyses based on the presence of Asperger syndrome had an exploratory character due to the small sample and diagnostic heterogeneity across the study period (ICD-10 and DSM-5 diagnostic frameworks). Intellectual disability and psychomotor delay occurred less frequently among those with Asperger syndrome, which reflects diagnostic criteria rather than biological variation and highlights phenotypic diversity and the need for personalized management strategies of patients along the ASD spectrum. Mean CNV sizes also diverged notably (p = 0.015), proving smaller in Asperger cases, although the practical magnitude of this difference is modest (Cohen’s d = 0.33) and may reflect sampling variability inherent to the small Asperger subgroup (n = 26), warranting replication in a larger, independently ascertained cohort before definitive conclusions are drawn. Yet, no notable variations emerged regarding dysmorphic characteristics (p = 1.000), duplication or deletion rates (p = 0.770 and p = 0.198, respectively), CMA categorizations (p = 1.000), or inheritance patterns (p = 1.000 for each), thereby affirming ASD genetic variability.

Exploratory hierarchical clustering on principal components partitioned the genetic profiles of 92 patients with ASD into three data-driven subgroups. These clusters are descriptive partitions of the observed variation and should not be interpreted as biologically or clinically validated subtypes; independent replication in a larger cohort is required before any clinical inferences can be drawn. Cluster 1 manifested a profile of genomic stability with predominant p-arm involvement (v-test 7.76, mean 0.98 vs. overall 0.29, SD 0.08 vs. 0.40, p < 0.001) and exclusive reliance on VUS (v-test 2.28, mean 1.00 vs. 0.80, SD 0.00 vs. 0.39, p = 0.023), alongside absent pathogenic variants (v-test − 2.28, mean 0.00 vs. 0.20, SD 0.00 vs. 0.39, p = 0.023) and minimal q-arm engagement (v-test − 7.76, mean 0.02 vs. 0.71, SD 0.08 vs. 0.40, p < 0.001). The prominence of p-arm involvement in Cluster 1 likely reflects a known genomic artifact rather than a clinical phenotype. The p-arms of acrocentric chromosomes are highly repetitive heterochromatic regions that frequently yield VUS or benign structural variants in microarray data. VUS cannot be used for prognostic interpretation due to insufficient evidence.

Cluster 2 exhibited an intermediate phenotype marked by high proportions of VUS (v-test 5.70, mean 0.98 vs. 0.80, SD 0.09 vs. 0.39, p < 0.001) and q-arm predominance (v-test 5.59, mean 0.89 vs. 0.71, SD 0.20 vs. 0.40, p < 0.001), coupled with reduced standardized CNV size (v-test − 3.39, mean − 0.27 vs. 0.00, SD 0.19 vs. 0.99, p = 0.001) and total genes (v-test − 3.41, mean − 0.27 vs. 0.00, SD 0.14 vs. 0.99, p = 0.001), low p-arm involvement (v-test − 5.59, mean 0.11 vs. 0.29, SD 0.20 vs. 0.40, p < 0.001), and sparse pathogenic fractions (v-test − 5.70, mean 0.02 vs. 0.20, SD 0.09 vs. 0.39, p < 0.001).

Cluster 3 portrayed the least favorable genomic architecture with absolute pathogenic dominance (v-test 9.37, mean 1.00 vs. 0.20, SD 0.00 vs. 0.39, p < 0.001), amplified standardized CNV size (v-test 5.10, mean 1.12 vs. 0.00, SD 1.88 vs. 0.99, p < 0.001) and total genes (v-test 4.58, mean 1.00 vs. 0.00, SD 1.79 vs. 0.99, p < 0.001), and complete absence of VUS (v-test − 9.37, mean 0.00 vs. 0.80, SD 0.00 vs. 0.39, p < 0.001). Data from cluster analysis alone cannot predict long-term neurodevelopmental evolution.

The limitation of the study is the diagnostic method used for genetic testing. The disadvantage of this method is the lack of ability to detect balanced translocations, low-grade inversions, mosaicism, or point mutations. CNVs smaller than 300 kbp of unknown clinical significance are not included in the results. CMA findings were not validated using secondary, orthogonal molecular techniques. The limitation is also a small number of patients in the research group without dysmorphic features or other diseases. The limitation is also the lack of standardized clinical mapping and control population with CMA results, and analysis of the carrier status in not all variants. The absence of trio analysis restricts clinical interpretation. In the ICD-10 classification, Asperger syndrome functions as a separate diagnostic entity, while the DSM-5 introduces a single category of ASD, which creates a risk of omitting some patients with Asperger syndrome in our analysis. Furthermore, with 92 patients and 6 effective independent dimensions entering the clustering analysis, the sample-to-variable ratio of approximately 15:1 and the smallest cluster size of n = 13 expose the analysis to overfitting risk. Although PCA dimensionality reduction and the convergence of multiple independent validation criteria (silhouette, Calinski–Harabasz, Davies–Bouldin, NbClust) partially mitigate this concern, the clustering solution must be regarded as hypothesis-generating and warrants replication in an independent, adequately powered cohort [66].

Conclusions

The study indicated the prominent heterogeneity of molecular karyotypes of pediatric patients with ASD according to clinical features, the type and size of CNV, and functions of duplicated and deleted genes. Even though genetics does not fully explain the etiology of ASD, the availability of new diagnostic methods, such as CMA testing, has increased the percentage of patients in whom the genetic etiology of ASD has been established. The diagnostic yield for pathogenic and likely pathogenic CNVs in our study was 8.55%, which shows the usefulness of CMA in diagnosing the genetic etiology of ASD. Both deletion and duplication loci were repeated several times within one CNV type (high frequency of 15q13.3 duplications). Both types of CNV were repeated within the same locus in several cases. Limitations suggest the need to perform statistical analyses and cluster analysis on larger groups of patients and include a control group/standardized clinical mapping with trio analyses. There is a need for further analysis of point mutations in next-generation sequencing and investigation of epigenetic changes in a broader research group.
