Melvyn B. Nathanson

Abstract
. This is a survey of the diversity of problems in additive number theory. Equity requires the consideration of less currently popular problems, and suggests their inclusion in the additive canon. Of particular interest are problems about the sizes of sumsets of finite sets of integers and problems about the arithmetical structure of intersections of sumsets.

11B13, 11B05, 11B75, 11P70, 22D99

1.
Canonical additive problems

Here are three of the most important (and most popular) areas of research in additive number theory:

(1)

Partitions and the associated algebraic, analytic, and combinatorial methods,

(2)

Waring’s problem on sums of
k
th powers and the circle method,

(3)

Additive prime number theory - Goldbach’s conjecture and sieve methods.

These are the canonical additive problems. (Waring and Goldbach
were discussed in my book

Additive Number Theory: The Classical Bases

[
12
]
.)
I shall ignore these topics and, instead, focus on other less popular and more “diverse”
questions that have not yet been or have not always been included in the additive canon.
Fairness, or equity, suggests that less well-known and not obviously “important”
topics must always be considered candidates for inclusion, because
only time and not today’s “experts” decide what is really important.

2.
Sum-product: A problem no one cared about

Paul Erdős
[
4
]
, in the book
Number Theory Day, Proceedings of the Conference Held at Rockefeller University, New York 1976
, wrote the following:

Section 7. A new extremal problem

Let
1\leq a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
be a sequence of integers.
Denote by
f_{r}(n)
the minimal number of distinct integers which are the sum or
product of exactly
r
of the
a
’s.

I conjectured that for every
r
and
\varepsilon>0
if
n>n(\varepsilon,r)
,

f_{r}(n)>n^{r-\varepsilon}
.

If true this seems extremely difficult and seems to require new ideas (unless of course an obvious point is being overlooked).

Szemerédi and I observed that it follows from deep results of Freiman that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{2}(n)/n=\infty

but even the proof of
f_{2}(n)>n^{1+\varepsilon}
seems to present great difficulties.

Several years later, in 1983, Erdős and Szemerédi
[
5
]

proved that there exists an
\varepsilon>0

such that

f_{2}(n)>n^{1+\varepsilon}.

An important result, but did anyone pay attention?
In 1991, Erdös wrote that “our paper with Szemerédi has nearly been forgotten.”

In 1997, Nathanson
[
15
]
computed the first explicit value for
\varepsilon
:

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{31}\qqand f_{2}(n)>cn^{32/31}

where
c=0.00028\ldots
.
The explicit exponent
\varepsilon
was quickly improved.

Ford
[
7
]
proved

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{15}-o(1).

Elekes
[
3
]
proved

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4}-o(1)

Solymosi
[
25
]
proved

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{3}-o(1)

Bloom
[
2
]
has the current best result:

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{5}{9813}-o(1)

The “sum-product” problem
has become big business in what is now called “additive combinatorics.”

3.
Freiman’s theorem

In the 1976 Number Theory Day conference at Rockefeller University, Erdős wrote,

“Szemerédi and I observed that it follows from deep results of Freiman that ….”

Almost no one had paid any attention to Grigorii Freiman’s remarkable work on inverse problems in
additive number theory, even though he had published a book in Russian in 1966
and the American Mathematical Society had published an English translation in 1973.
(Of course, in Freiman’s book and papers, the proof of his big theorem was hard to understand.)

Much later, various Hungarians worked on the theorem, and Ruzsa found a beautiful proof
in 1994, which was exposed in the Springer Graduate Text

Additive Number Theory: Inverse Theorems and the
Geometry of Sumsets
(Nathanson
[
13
]
).

Tim Gowers used this to obtain explicit estimates for Szemerédi’s theorem on

k
-term arithmetic progressions
in dense sets of integers, and Freiman’s theorem and its consequences became another
big business in additive number theory.

4.
A noncanonical problem: Sizes of sumsets

In their 1983 paper on the sum-product conjecture, Erdős and Szemerédi wrote:

Let
2k-1\leq t\leq\frac{k^{2}+k}{2}
. It is easy to see that one can find a sequence
of integers
a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}
so that there should be exactly
t
distinct integers
in the sequence
a_{i}+a_{j},1\leq i\leq j\leq k
.’

The lower bound comes from
k
-term arithmetic progressions.
Every integer in the interval of integers
\{0,1,2,\ldots,2k-2\}
is a sum of two integers in the
arithmetic progression
A=\{0,1,2,\ldots,k-1\}
.
Moreover, the set of 2-fold sums of a set of
k
integers
has size
2k-1
if and only if the set is an arithmetic progression.

The upper bound is combinatorial: If
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
is a set of size
k

whose
\binom{k}{2}
sums
a_{i}+a_{j}
with
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
and

k
sums
a_{i}+a_{i}
are distinct, then the number of 2-fold sums is

\binom{k}{2}+k=\binom{k+1}{2}=\frac{k^{2}+k}{2}.

For example: The set
\{1,3,3^{2},\ldots,3^{k-1}\}
has all 2-fold sums distinct.

For every set
A
of integers, let

2A=\{a+a^{\prime}:a,a^{\prime}\in A\}.

For all positive integers
k
, we define the
sumset size set

\mathcal{R}(2,k)=\left\{\left|2A\right|:A\subseteq\mathbf{Z}\text{ and }|A|=k\right\}.

Theorem 1
.

For all positive integers
k
,

\mathcal{R}(2,k)=\left[2k-1,\frac{k^{2}+k}{2}\right].

This is the Erdős-Szemerédi statement.

Problem 1
.

For all
t\in\mathcal{R}(2,k)
, compute (construct) a set
A

such that
|A|=k
and
\left|2A\right|=t
.

Is there an algorithm?

Theorem 2
.

For all
t\in\mathcal{R}(2,k)
, there exists a set
A\subseteq\left[0,2^{k}-1\right]

such that
|A|=k
and
\left|2A\right|=t
, and
A
can be computed in exponential time.

Problem 2
.

If there a subexponential or polynomial bound on the set
A
?
Do there exist integers
n
and
c

such that, for all
k
and
for all
t\in\mathcal{R}(2,k)
, there is a set
A\subseteq\left[0,ck^{n}\right]

with
|A|=k
and
\left|2A\right|=t
?

For every set
A
of integers, let

\widehat{2A}=\{a+a^{\prime}:a,a^{\prime}\in A\text{ and }a\neq a^{\prime}\}.

For all positive integers
k
, we define

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(2,k)=\left\{\left|\widehat{2A}\right|:A\subseteq\mathbf{Z}\text{ and }|A|=k\right\}.

Theorem 3
.

For all integers
k\geq 2
,

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(2,k)=\left[2k-3,\frac{k^{2}-k}{2}\right].

Moreover, for all
t\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(2,k)
, there exists a set

A\subseteq\left[0,2^{k-2}\right]

such that
|A|=k
and
\left|\widehat{2A}\right|=t
.

Problem 3
.

If there a subexponential or polynomial bound on the set
A
?
Does there exist an integer
n
and
c>0

such that, for all
k
and
for all
t\in\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(2,k)
, there is a set
A\subseteq\left[0,ck^{n}\right]

with
|A|=k
and
\left|\widehat{2A}\right|=t
?

Problem 4
.

“Most” sets
A
of size
k
are Sidon sets, that is,
|2A|=\binom{k+1}{2}
.

What is the distribution of sumset sizes
|2A|
?

What are the “popular” sizes, and why are they popular?

Problem 5
.

What is the distribution of sumset sizes
\left|\widehat{2A}\right|
?

What are the “popular” sizes, and why are they popular?

5.
Range of sumset sizes

The
h
-fold
sumset
of a set
A
of integers is the set of all sums of
h

not necessarily distinct elements of
A
:

hA=\underbrace{A+\cdots+A}_{\text{$h$ summands}}=\left\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}:a_{i}\in A\text{ for all }i\in[1,h]\right\}.

The
h
-fold
restricted sumset
of
A

is the set of all sums of
h
distinct elements of
A
:

\displaystyle\widehat{hA}

\displaystyle=\left\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}:a_{i}\in A\text{ for all }i\in[1,h]\text{ and }a_{i}\neq a_{j}\text{ for all }i\neq j\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{s\in S}s:S\subseteq A\text{ and }|S|=h\right\}

where
|S|
denotes the size (or cardinality) of the set
S
.

The sumset
hA
and the restricted sumset
\widehat{hA}
are finite if
A
is finite.

For all positive integers
h
and
k
, we define the
sumset size set

\mathcal{R}(h,k)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{Z}}(h,k)=\left\{\left|hA\right|:A\subseteq\mathbf{Z}\text{ and }|A|=k\right\}.

There is the elementary estimate:

(1)

\mathcal{R}(h,k)\subseteq\left[h(k-1)+1,\binom{h+k-1}{h}\right].

The lower bound comes from
k
-term arithmetic progressions and the upper bound from

B_{h}
-sets of size
k
.

In general, we consider the range of sumset sizes of finite subsets
of an arbitrary additive abelian group or semigroup
X
:

\mathcal{R}_{X}(h,k)=\left\{\left|hA\right|:A\subseteq X\text{ and }|A|=k\right\}.

\mathcal{R}(3,3)\subseteq\{7,8,9,10\}.

\{7,9,10\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}(3,3).

\mathcal{R}(3,3)=\{7,9,10\}

For all

Thus,

is

an interval of consecutive integers for

.

Problem 7
.

What are other “missing” sumset sizes?

For all

Let

Let
h\geq 2
and

. Because the sets

For fixed

8.
Sumset races

Here is another class of sumset size problems in additive number theory.

Let
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, …,
A_{n}
be
n
sets of
k
integers.
For every positive integer
h
, consider the
n
-tuple of
h
-fold sumset sizes

(|hA_{1}|,|hA_{2}|,\ldots,|hA_{n}|).

If the sumset sizes are pairwise distinct, we can associate to this
n
-tuple
a permutation of the integers
1,2,\ldots,n
which describes the relative order
of the sequence of sumset sizes.

If the sumset sizes are not pairwise distinct, we can still associate to this
n
-tuple
an
n
-tuple of positive integers that describes the relative order
of the sequence of sumset sizes.

Problem 13
.

Given
H
not necessarily distinct
n
-tuples of positive integers

\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{H}
, do there exist finite sets

A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}
such that, for all
h=1,2,\ldots,H
,
the relative of the order of the sumset size
n
-tuple

(|hA_{1}|,|hA_{2}|,\ldots,|hA_{n}|).

is the same as the relative order of the
h
-th integer
n
-tuple
\tau_{h}
?

One can ask the same question about sumsets in arbitrary additive abelian groups.

By theorems of Nathanson (for integers) and Khovanskii (for groups),
one cannot prescribe infinite sequences of
n
-tuples in sumset races.

After preliminary work by Paul Péringuey and Anne de Roton
[
22
]
,
Noah Kravitrz proved the following;

Theorem 10

(Kravitz
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{H},\tau_{\infty}
be
n-
tuples of positive integers.
There exist finite sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
such that the sumset size
n
-tuple

(|hA_{1}|,|hA_{2}|,\ldots,|hA_{n}|).

has the same relative order as the
n
-tuple
\tau_{h}
for all
h=1,\ldots,H
,
and the sumset size
n
-tuple has the same relative order
as the
n
-tuple
\tau_{\infty}
for all
h>H
.

Corollary 1
.

If
\varepsilon_{h}=1,0,
or
-1
for all
h=1,\ldots,H
, then
there exist finite sets
A
and
B
such that

|hA|-|hB|

has the same sign as
\varepsilon_{h}
for all
h=1,\ldots,H
.

There is a continuous analogue of Theorem
10
.

Theorem 11

(Nathanson
[
20
]
)
.

Let
G
be a locally compact abelian group
that is not a torsion group and let

\mu
be a Haar measure on
G
.
For integers
n\geq 2
and
H\geq 2
and for
any
n
-tuples
\mathbf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{H}\in\mathbf{N}^{n}
,
there exist measurable subsets

A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
of

such that

\tau\left(\mu(hA_{1}),\ldots,\mu(hA_{n})\right)=\tau(\mathbf{u}_{h})

for all
h=1,\ldots,H
.

There is the following refinement by Jacob Fox, Noah Kravitz, and Shengtong Zhang.

Theorem 12

(Fox, Kravitz, Zhang
[
8

For every sequence

of integers,

A

|hA|-|hB|=t_{h}

(Nathanson)
.

For integers
n\geq 2
and
H\geq 2
,

m_{i,h}\in\mathbf{Z}

10.
Sumset intersection limits

Let
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
be the set of positive integers.
A decreasing sequence of sets
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
is

strictly decreasing
if
A_{q}\neq A_{q+1}
for all
q\in\mathbf{N}

and
asymptotically strictly decreasing
if
A_{q}\neq A_{q+1}

for infinitely many
q\in\mathbf{N}
.
A decreasing sequence of sets
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
that is not asymptotically
strictly decreasing is eventually constant.

Let
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
be a decreasing sequence of nonempty subsets
of an additive abelian semigroup
X
. We define

A=\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}A_{q}=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}A_{q}.

Let
hA
be the
h
-fold sumset of
A
.
For all
q\in\mathbf{N}
, we have
A\subseteq A_{q}
and so
hA\subseteq hA_{q}
and

hA\subseteq\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}hA_{q}.

Equivalently,

h\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}A_{q}\subseteq\lim_{q\rightarrow\infty}hA_{q}

for every sequence
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
.

For
x\in X
, the
representation function

r_{A,h}(x)
counts
the number of
h
-tuples

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{h})\in A\times\cdots\times A
such that

x=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}
. The representation function determines the sumset:

hA=\{x\in X:r_{A,h}(x)>0\}
.

In the additive semigroup
\mathbf{N}
, we have
r_{\mathbf{N},h}(x)<\infty
for all
x\in\mathbf{Z}
and
h\geq 2
.

In the additive semigroup
\mathbf{Z}
, we have
r_{\mathbf{Z},h}(x)=\infty
for all
x\in\mathbf{Z}
and
h\geq 2
.

Theorem 14

(Nathanson
[
19
]
)
.

Let
h\in\mathbf{N}
. Let
X
be an additive abelian semigroup such that
r_{X,h}(x)<\infty

for all
x\in X
. Let
A
be a subset of
X

and let
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
be an asymptotically strictly decreasing sequence
of subsets of
X
such that
A=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}A_{q}
.
Then

hA=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}hA_{q}

and
h\in\mathcal{H}(A_{q})
.
If
r_{X,h}(x)<\infty
for all
x\in X
and
h\geq 2
, then

hA=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}hA_{q}

for all
h\in\mathbf{N}
and
\mathcal{H}(A_{q})=\mathbf{N}
.

Theorem 15

(Nathanson
[
19
]
)
.

If
A
is a set of nonnegative integers and if
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
is a
strictly decreasing sequence
of sets of nonnegative integers such that
A=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}A_{q}
, then

hA=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}hA_{q}

for all
h=1,2,3,\ldots
, that is,

\mathcal{H}(A_{q})=\mathbf{N}.

In general, it is not true that

hA=\bigcap_{q=1}^{\infty}hA_{q}

for all asymptotically decreasing sequences in an additive abelian semigroup.
It is not even true in the integers. Diego Marques and Nathanson proved the following:

Theorem 16

(Marques-Nathanson
[
10
]
)
.

For every
h_{0}\geq 2

there exist sequences
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
such that

\{1,\ldots,h_{0}-1\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(A_{q})
but
h_{0}\notin\mathcal{H}(A_{q})

and also that there exist sequences
(A_{q})_{q=1}^{\infty}
such that

\{1,h_{0}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(A_{q})
but
\{2,3,\ldots,h_{0}-1\}\cap\mathcal{H}(A_{q})=\emptyset
.

11.
The moral of the story

The problems in this paper may interest only a minority of number
theorists and, perhaps, only a minority of additive number theorists
and additive combinatorialists, but, as the history of the sum-product conjecture
and Freiman’s theorem suggests, inclusion of a diversity of problems into the
additive number theory discussion may be not only equitable but even beneficial
to the larger mathematical community.

It suggests one of Aesop’s fables.
Aesop lived in ancient Greece between 620 and 564 BCE.
Herodotus, in the fifth century BCE, mentioned him

Aesop’s fables were transmitted orally and not collected and written down until
three centuries after Aesop’s death.
Stories have been added continuously and are still being added,
so it is not clear which of Aesop’s fables were recited by Aesop.

One of the oldest and most famous of Aesop’s fables is variously known as
“The Old Man and His Sons”
or “The Bundle of Sticks”. It is numbered 53 in the Perry Index.

An old man had many sons who were always quarreling with one another. He had often begged them to live in harmony, but without success. One day he called them together, and producing a bundle of sticks, he asked them each to try and break it. Each tried with all his strength, but the bundle resisted their efforts. Then, cutting the cord that bound the sticks together, the old man told his sons to break them separately. This was done with the greatest ease. “See, my sons,” he exclaimed, “the power of unity! Bound together by brotherly love, you can defy almost every mortal ill; divided, you will fall a prey to your enemies.”

The moral of the fable is: In unity there is strength.

This is a good way to think about the diversity of problems in additive number theory.
