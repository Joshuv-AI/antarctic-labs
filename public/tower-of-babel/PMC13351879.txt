Single-incision uniportal endoscopic bilateral decompression for two adjacent levels of lumbar spinal stenosis: a technical report
Authors: Sen Lin, Shane Zaw, A Aravin Kumar, Tamara Lee Ting Soh, Lester Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic lumbar decompression has become an important minimally invasive technique for the treatment of lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS). Decompression of adjacent levels often requires multiple skin incisions or portals. Strategies to reduce surgical footprint while maintaining effective neural decompression remain an important goal in minimally invasive spine surgery. To our knowledge, single-incision uniportal bilateral decompression at two adjacent lumbar levels has not been formally described. We describe a single-incision uniportal endoscopic technique that allows bilateral decompression of two adjacent lumbar levels through one paraspinal incision, presented through an illustrative clinical case. Laminotomy, medial facetectomy, and flavectomy were performed using an over-the-top contralateral decompression technique with a standardized operative workflow. Adequate bilateral decompression at L3–4 and L4–5 was achieved through a single 0.7-cm incision. Operative time was 115 minutes with six brief fluoroscopy exposures. The patient experienced immediate postoperative symptom relief and ambulated within 24 hours. At 2- and 6-month follow-up, the visual analogue scale (VAS) score improved from 8 to 0. Postoperative clinical assessment confirmed satisfactory bilateral decompression at both levels. Single-incision uniportal bilateral decompression for two adjacent lumbar levels is technically feasible and may further reduce surgical invasiveness compared with conventional multi-portal techniques. While the individual components of this technique are well-established, their integration into a standardized single-incision workflow represents a meaningful technical refinement. Further studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up are required to validate long-term clinical outcomes.Keywords: Endoscopic spine surgery, lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), uniportal endoscopy, multilevel decompression

Key findings

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?

• Uniportal access, over-the-top bilateral decompression, and cannula redirection are individually established endoscopic techniques; two-level decompression conventionally requires two separate incisions.

• These components are integrated into a standardized single-incision workflow, with key refinements including dynamic table arm fixation, modified hand-grip technique, and systematic junctional zone management. A structured decision-making framework with defined patient selection criteria, safety boundaries, and abort criteria is introduced to guide reproducible implementation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Single-incision two-level decompression may further reduce surgical invasiveness and soft-tissue disruption compared with conventional multi-incision techniques.

• The standardized workflow, safety checklist, and structured pearls and pitfalls provide a reproducible framework for surgeons to safely expand their endoscopic practice.

• This technique should be reserved for experienced endoscopic surgeons; proficiency in single-level uniportal bilateral decompression is an absolute prerequisite.

• Prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are required to validate outcomes and define the full learning curve.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common degenerative condition and a leading cause of neurogenic claudication and disability in the elderly population. When conservative treatment fails, surgical decompression remains the most effective treatment to relieve neural compression and improve patient outcomes (1,2).

Conventional open laminectomy provides reliable decompression but is associated with paraspinal muscle injury, postoperative pain, and prolonged recovery. Minimally invasive spine surgery techniques have therefore been developed to reduce surgical morbidity while maintaining adequate neural decompression (3).

Endoscopic spine surgery has evolved rapidly over the past two decades. Percutaneous endoscopic techniques were initially developed for lumbar disc herniation and later expanded to treat LSS (4). Subsequent studies have demonstrated that full-endoscopic interlaminar decompression can achieve outcomes comparable to microsurgical decompression with reduced soft-tissue trauma and faster recovery (5,6).

Recent advances in biportal endoscopic spine surgery have expanded the range of endoscopic procedures, enabling treatment of complex spinal pathologies including multilevel stenosis and lumbar fusion (7). The sliding biportal endoscopic technique (S-UBE) has been described for two-level adjacent lumbar stenosis, achieving favourable outcomes with 95.1% excellent-to-good results and 84–88% facet joint preservation (8). However, multilevel decompression typically requires multiple working portals and separate skin incisions (8,9).

Previous studies have explored strategies to address multilevel pathology while maintaining minimally invasive access. Whilst single-incision percutaneous endoscopic decompression for two-level lumbar disc herniation has been described (10), bilateral decompression at two adjacent levels has not been reported. Comparative meta-analyses suggest that uniportal endoscopic decompression may achieve outcomes comparable to biportal techniques, with some studies demonstrating shorter hospital stays and reduced soft-tissue disruption with the uniportal approach (11,12).

While the fundamental techniques of single-level, uniportal access, over-the-top bilateral decompression, and cannula redirection are well-established in endoscopic spine surgery, their integration into a standardized single-incision approach for two-level bilateral decompression has not been formally described. The primary contribution of this report is therefore not the description of a novel technique, but rather the description and standardization of an operative workflow that combines these established elements into a reproducible approach for single-incision two-level bilateral decompression. In this technical report, we present the operative strategy, decision-making framework, safety boundaries, and an illustrative clinical case to serve as a practical guide for surgeons seeking to safely expand their endoscopic capabilities. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0074/rc).

Endoscopic system and instruments

The procedure was performed using an iLESSYS uniportal endoscopic system (Joimax Pro) with a 7.1-mm outer diameter working sleeve and a 15-degree viewing angle endoscope for initial exposure. For ipsilateral and contralateral decompression, a 4.3-mm working channel endoscope with 15-degree viewing angle was employed to improve manoeuvrability beneath the drilled spinous process base. Instruments include a high-speed endoscopic burr (4.0 mm diameter), 2- and 3-mm Kerrison rongeurs, endoscopic dissectors, pituitary forceps, and a radiofrequency probe.

Cannula trajectory planning

Preoperative planning includes careful review of sagittal and axial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to assess interlaminar space height, degree of ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, and facet joint anatomy. The incision point is selected at the midpoint between two adjacent disc spaces (e.g., the L4 pedicle for L3–4 and L4–5 decompression) at a paraspinal position approximately 0.5–1.0 cm from the midline. The trajectory is planned using the sagittal MRI to ensure that a single incision and cannula angulation can access both interlaminar windows. Ideally, the trajectory should follow a line that intersects both interlaminar spaces with minimal angular adjustment between levels.

Patient selection criteria and anatomical limitations

Proper patient selection is essential for the safe application of this technique. Ideal candidates include patients with: (I) central and lateral recess stenosis at two adjacent lumbar levels (L1–2 through L4–5); (II) adequate interlaminar space height greater than 5 mm; (III) facet joint anatomy permitting medial facetectomy while preserving at least 50% of the joint bilaterally; (IV) absence of significant degenerative scoliosis (Cobb angle <15 degrees); (V) no previous decompressive surgery at the index levels; and (VI) no radiographic evidence of segmental instability. This technique is applicable throughout the lumbar spine; however, at more cranial levels (L1–2), the facet joints are oriented more medially, necessitating a larger facetectomy. Extension to three or more levels through a single incision is not currently recommended. For patients with degenerative scoliosis, we recommend approaching from the convex side, which provides a more direct trajectory to the contralateral side, reduces the extent of spinous process drilling, and facilitates visualization of the concave side where the ligamentum flavum and facet hypertrophy are typically more pronounced. Marked hyperlordosis (>60 degrees) increases the angulation required between levels and may preclude single-incision access. Kyphotic deformity at the target segment is a relative contraindication, as it may position the dural sac directly beneath the lamina, increasing the risk of injury during entry.

Cannula angulation and starting level selection

Proper cannula angulation is essential to safely access both levels through a single incision. We recommend starting at the cranial level (L3–4 when treating L3–4 and L4–5) in most cases, as caudal angulation to reach the second level is more ergonomic than cranial angulation if starting caudally. However, if one level is significantly more symptomatic or technically demanding, that level should be addressed first. Excessive caudal angulation may lead to docking at an incorrect interlaminar window. Under-angulation carries equally significant risk: the drill may contact the top of the lamina, where no ligamentum flavum exists, potentially penetrating directly into the thecal sac. Positioning the cannula too laterally results in excessive facet removal, which may compromise spinal stability.

Dynamic table arm fixation strategy

The dynamic table arm is used only during initial docking and localisation with intraoperative fluoroscopy, obviating the need for the surgeon to wear a lead coat throughout the operation. Once the disc space level is confirmed, the table arm is released to allow dynamic adjustment of the working angle, enabling the surgeon to pivot the working sleeve for optimal visualization of different anatomical areas.

Patient positioning and localisation

The patient is positioned prone on a Jackson table with pelvic support to facilitate lumbar lordosis and allow access to both levels through a single incision. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a paraspinal point is marked at the L4 pedicle (midpoint between the two-disc spaces), and a No. 11 blade is used to make a stab incision (Figure 1). Sequential dilators are introduced to establish a working corridor adjacent to the spinous process, directed towards the cranial disc space. A working sleeve is inserted and initially docked at the L3–4 interlaminar window. The dynamic table arm is secured to hold the cannula firmly in place during initial docking and localisation (Figure 2), obviating the need for a heavy lead gown throughout the procedure. Once the disc space level is confirmed, the table arm is released to allow dynamic adjustment of the working angle, enabling the surgeon to pivot the working sleeve for optimal visualization of different anatomical areas while maintaining instrument control.

Skin incision demonstrating a 0.7-cm paramedian microincision.

Endoscopic setup and fluoroscopic localization at the L3–4 level.

Endoscopic exposure

Subperiosteal dissection is performed to expose the lamina while preserving the supraspinous and interspinous ligaments. A laminotomy is performed using the high-speed burr with continuous saline irrigation, removing the inferior portion of the L3 lamina and the superior portion of the L4 lamina to expose the ligamentum flavum. The ligamentum flavum is maintained as a protective barrier over the dural sac until bony decompression is complete.

Ipsilateral decompression at L3–4

The ligamentum flavum attachment is carefully exposed and the flavum subsequently removed using Kerrison rongeurs under direct endoscopic visualization to decompress the lateral recess and expose the traversing nerve root. Medial facetectomy is performed as needed, with the goal of preserving at least 50% of the medial facet joint to maintain segmental stability. Adequate ipsilateral decompression is confirmed endoscopically by visualization of a freely mobile nerve root and pulsatile dural sac (Figure 3).

(B) Contralateral L3/4 decompressed with space in the lateral recess (shown with black arrow). (C) Ipsilateral L4/5 decompressed with space in the lateral recess (shown with black arrow). (D) Contralateral L4/5 decompressed with space in the lateral recess (shown with black arrow).

Contralateral decompression at L3–4

The base of the spinous process is partially drilled to allow the endoscope to cross the midline (Figure 4). The working sleeve is angulated toward the contralateral side, and the 4.3-mm channel endoscope with 15-degree lens is introduced to improve visualization beneath the drilled spinous process base. Using the over-the-top technique, the contralateral ligamentum flavum is removed and the contralateral lateral recess is decompressed (Figure 3). During this phase, the modified hand-grip technique provides fine control of sleeve orientation. Key safety measures include maintaining the ligamentum flavum as a protective barrier until bony work is complete, using the burr with gentle pressure and constant irrigation, and always preserving a visible rim of bone over the dura during drilling.

Fluoroscopy demonstrating the endoscope crossing the midline for contralateral decompression at L3–4 using the over-the-top technique.

Second-level decompression at L4–5

After completing decompression at L3–4, the cannula is redirected caudally towards the L4–5 interlaminar window through the same incision. The table arm is then fixed to hold the cannula firmly in place. The level of disc space is confirmed with intraoperative fluoroscopy (Figure 5). Sequential dilators are not reinserted as the initial dilation has established the working pathway; however, significant resistance during redirection warrants re-evaluation of trajectory or re-dilation as needed.

Repositioning of the working cannula to access the L4–5 level.

Once the disc level is confirmed, the table arm is released and the decompression sequence is repeated at L4–5: first ipsilateral decompression followed by positioning the endoscope to cross the midline (Figure 6) for contralateral decompression. Adequate bilateral decompression is confirmed endoscopically (Figure 3). During transition between levels, fluoroscopy is used to confirm the correct interlaminar window and prevent docking at an incorrect level.

Fluoroscopy demonstrating the endoscope crossing the midline for contralateral decompression at L4–5 using the over-the-top technique.

Junctional zone management

After completing the primary decompression at each level, the endoscope is systematically angled cranially and caudally to inspect the junctional zones between levels. Specifically, the inferior edge of the L4 lamina is carefully undercut using the Kerrison rongeur to ensure no residual bone compresses the thecal sac at this junctional zone. The ligamentum flavum attachment at this junction is thoroughly released and removed. Adequacy of junctional decompression is confirmed by: (I) direct visualization of dural sac pulsation across the full width of the canal including junctional areas; and (II) free movement of a dissector probe across the lateral recess. If any residual compression is identified, the endoscope is repositioned and additional decompression performed.

Haemostasis and closure

Haemostasis is achieved using a radiofrequency probe. The working sleeve is gradually withdrawn while inspecting the surgical tract for bleeding. The fascia is closed using 3-0 Vicryl followed by subcuticular closure with Monocryl. Dermabond and Prineo dressing are applied.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Postoperative protocol

Patients are mobilized within 24 hours postoperatively. Oral analgesia with acetaminophen and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs is typically sufficient for pain control; no rigid bracing is required. Patients are discharged when ambulating independently with adequate pain control, typically on postoperative day 1. Follow-up includes clinical assessment at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively, with MRI reserved for patients with persistent or recurrent symptoms.

Illustrative case

A 60-year-old male presented with bilateral lower limb pain and neurogenic claudication. Preoperative evaluation included anteroposterior (AP) and lateral lumbar radiographs (Figure 7), which demonstrated preserved spinal alignment without significant scoliosis, adequate interlaminar space height at both L3–4 and L4–5, and mild degenerative changes. MRI of the lumbar spine demonstrated severe spinal canal stenosis at L3–4 and L4–5 (Figure 8) with hypertrophic facet joints and ligamentum flavum thickening. The patient was selected for the single-incision uniportal endoscopic decompression technique based on the following criteria: two adjacent levels of central and lateral recess stenosis, adequate interlaminar space height (>5 mm), facet joint anatomy permitting medial facetectomy while preserving at least 50% of the joint, absence of significant degenerative scoliosis, and no previous surgery at the index levels.

The procedure was performed as described above. Total operative time was 115 minutes, with six brief fluoroscopy exposures for initial localization and level confirmation during transition. Adequate bilateral decompression was achieved at both levels through the single 0.5-cm incision.

Postoperatively, the patient experienced immediate symptom relief and ambulated within 24 hours. He was discharged on postoperative day 1. At 2- and 6-month follow-up, the visual analogue scale (VAS) score improved from 8 to 0, and he had returned to normal daily activities without restriction.

Tips and pearls

Safety considerations and complication management

Dural injury prevention is paramount during contralateral decompression. Key strategies include: maintaining the ligamentum flavum as a protective barrier until bony decompression is complete; using the high-speed burr with constant saline irrigation and gentle pressure; preserving a visible margin of bone over the dura during drilling; and recognizing that risk is highest at the junction of the lamina and spinous process base, where the dura may lie directly beneath thinned bone. At least 50% of the medial facet joint should be preserved bilaterally; if greater facetectomy is required for adequate decompression, concurrent fusion or staged stabilization should be considered.

Indications for conversion to a multi-incision or open approach include: inability to achieve adequate visualization due to anatomical constraints; significant dural injury requiring repair beyond endoscopic capability; or inadequate decompression despite maximal bony removal. Small dural tears can be managed with fibrin sealant and a short period of lumbar drainage (24–48 hours); larger injuries or nerve root lacerations may require microsurgical repair. Surgical pearls

Strategic level selection and workflow

When addressing two adjacent levels through a single incision, the cranial level is best approached first to maintain optimal visualization and avoid working against gravitational collapse of soft tissues. Sequential workflow (cranial to caudal) allows controlled expansion of the working corridor and reduces repeated docking adjustments. This approach also ensures that the more technically demanding contralateral decompression at the cranial level is completed before transitioning to the second level.

Cannula trajectory and docking precision

The success of this technique is highly dependent on initial cannula trajectory, which should be planned to allow access to both interlaminar windows without excessive redirection. The ideal docking point is slightly medialized at the interlaminar window, facilitating both ipsilateral and contralateral decompression while preserving facet integrity. Preoperative sagittal MRI is essential to confirm that a single trajectory line can intersect both target interlaminar spaces with minimal angular adjustment.

Maintaining orientation during level transition

Transitioning between levels through the same incision can lead to loss of anatomical orientation. Frequent fluoroscopic confirmation and maintaining a consistent reference to the spinolaminar junction are essential to avoid wrong-level or off-target decompression. The endoscope should remain within the working sleeve during redirection to provide continuous visualization of the tissue plane and immediate identification of any anatomical deviation.

Over-the-top decompression technique

Adequate drilling and removal of the spinous process base is critical to adequately access the contralateral side. A progressive drilling technique should be used to create a safe working corridor before advancing instruments across the midline. The spinous process should be undercut progressively rather than aggressively, with frequent endoscopic inspection to confirm adequate bone removal without dural exposure. The ligamentum flavum should be maintained as a protective layer until the bony corridor is fully established.

Instrument control and endoscope stability

The modified hand-grip technique is valuable (Figure 9); however, surgeons should emphasize two-point stabilization (hand plus working sleeve) to prevent inadvertent advancement toward the thecal sac. Use of smaller diameter endoscopes improves manoeuvrability, especially when accessing the second level and when working beneath the undercut spinous process base for contralateral decompression.

Pitfalls and how to avoid them

Inadequate cannula angulation

Incorrect angulation may result in docking outside the interlaminar window or direct exposure of dura without protective ligamentum flavum. The solution is to confirm trajectory with biplanar fluoroscopy before initiating drilling. A lateral fluoroscopic view is particularly valuable to confirm that the trajectory is parallel to the interlaminar plane rather than angled toward the spinal canal.

Excessive facet resection

Lateral docking or aggressive decompression risks facet destabilization, particularly when addressing two levels where cumulative facet resection may compound instability. The solution is to maintain a medial trajectory with docking at the medial edge of the interlaminar window, and to limit resection to less than 50% of the facet joint. Intraoperative estimation of facet preservation should be performed at each level, and cumulative resection across two levels should be considered in stability assessment.

Loss of orientation during level shift

Redirection of the working sleeve may lead to disorientation and incomplete decompression, particularly if the surgeon fails to recognize that the sleeve has drifted toward the adjacent lamina rather than the intended interlaminar window. The solution is to reconfirm anatomical landmarks and level using fluoroscopy prior to initiating the second-level decompression. The characteristic morphology of each interlaminar window, including the relationship between the inferior and superior articular processes, should be verified endoscopically before beginning decompression.

Risk of dural injury

The absence of ligamentum flavum during certain stages increases the risk of direct dural violation, especially during contralateral decompression when instruments are advanced across the midline beneath the undercut spinous process. The solution is to maintain a thin protective layer of flavum until bony decompression is complete. Even a residual membrane of flavum provides tactile feedback and visual warning before instrument contact with the dura. If the flavum is inadvertently breached early, the surgeon should proceed with extreme caution and consider switching to finer instruments.

Overextension of indications

This technique may not be suitable in cases of severe facet hypertrophy that limits instrument manoeuvrability, high-grade spondylolisthesis that alters anatomical relationships, significant anatomical distortion from previous surgery or congenital anomalies, or marked obesity that prevents adequate fluoroscopic visualization. The solution is careful patient selection with thorough preoperative imaging assessment; surgeons should consider staged or multi-portal approaches when anatomical constraints suggest limited likelihood of successful single-incision completion.

When to abort the single-incision strategy

Conversion to a second incision or alternative approach should not be delayed when any of the following are encountered: inability to safely access the second level due to limited working corridor or excessive soft tissue resistance; excessive bleeding obscuring endoscopic visualization despite standard haemostatic measures; inadequate decompression confirmed intraoperatively despite maximal bony removal; significant dural injury requiring repair beyond endoscopic capability; or realization that the angle between adjacent interlaminar spaces precludes safe single-incision access. In such cases, conversion should be viewed as appropriate surgical judgment rather than failure—patient safety and adequate decompression must always take precedence over technique preservation.

Discussion

Endoscopic spine surgery continues to evolve as surgeons seek to minimize surgical invasiveness while maintaining effective neural decompression (13). Several studies have demonstrated favourable outcomes of endoscopic decompression for LSS compared with conventional open or microscopic techniques (1,4-6). Biportal endoscopic spine surgery has further expanded the indications of endoscopic techniques, allowing treatment of more complex spinal pathologies including multilevel stenosis and lumbar fusion procedures (7). However, treatment of adjacent-level lumbar stenosis remains technically challenging, as most techniques require separate working portals or additional skin incisions.

Comparison with existing techniques

The technique described in this report integrates established endoscopic principles into a standardized workflow for single-incision two-level bilateral decompression. It is important to contextualize this approach within the broader evolution of endoscopic spine surgery.

❖ Comparison with biportal endoscopy: the sliding biportal technique (S-UBE) described by Xu et al. (2025) (8) for two-level adjacent LSS uses two skin incisions with a sliding approach between levels, achieving a 95.1% excellent-to-good outcome rate with 84–88% facet joint preservation. While biportal techniques offer excellent visualization and instrument versatility due to the separate observation and working portals, the uniportal single-incision approach further reduces soft tissue disruption by eliminating the second incision and preserves the musculofascial continuity between the approach corridor and adjacent segments. Recent meta-analyses comparing uniportal and biportal approaches found comparable clinical outcomes, with some studies demonstrating shorter hospital stays and reduced postoperative pain with the uniportal approach (11,12,14,15). The choice between approaches depends on surgeon familiarity, patient anatomy, and the complexity of the pathology.

The single-incision approach is best suited for patients requiring bilateral decompression at two adjacent levels with relatively preserved anatomy. Biportal may be preferred for cases requiring more complex procedures (e.g., discectomy with interbody fusion) or when instrument triangulation is critical. Standard multi-incision uniportal may be more appropriate when the interlaminar spaces at adjacent levels are oriented at a steep angle to each other, or when the surgeon is early in their endoscopic learning curve.

Learning curve considerations

This technique should be attempted only by surgeons with established proficiency in single-level uniportal bilateral decompression. We recommend that surgeons have performed at least 20–30 single-level uniportal bilateral decompression procedures before attempting two-level decompression through a single incision. The additional learning curve for cannula redirection and second-level decompression through the same incision is estimated at 5–10 cases. We recommend starting with more cranial levels (L2–4) where the interlaminar spaces are wider and the angle between levels is less acute. Mastery of the over-the-top contralateral decompression technique at a single level is an absolute prerequisite, as this represents the most technically demanding component of the procedure. The pearls and pitfalls outlined in this report are intended to accelerate the learning curve by providing structured guidance on the most common technical challenges.

Limitations

This report has several important limitations. First, the current report is based on a single illustrative case with a 6-month follow-up. While this demonstrates technical feasibility, longer-term outcomes from a prospective series are required to fully evaluate the durability of clinical improvement, the incidence of delayed complications, and the risk of adjacent segment degeneration. Second, the generalizability of this technique to other anatomical variations (e.g., higher-grade stenosis, different spinal alignments, more caudal levels) requires further validation. Third, we acknowledge the absence of CT and postoperative MRI scans. Fourth, the absence of a control group or comparative data limits our ability to demonstrate superiority over existing techniques. We have initiated a prospective case series and will report longer-term outcomes (minimum 12-month follow-up) in a subsequent publication.

Conclusions

Single-incision uniportal endoscopic bilateral decompression for two adjacent lumbar levels is technically feasible and may represent a further refinement in minimally invasive endoscopic spine surgery. While the individual components of this technique—uniportal access, over-the-top bilateral decompression, and cannula redirection—are well-established, their integration into a standardized single-incision workflow for two-level pathology provides a reproducible approach that may reduce surgical invasiveness compared with conventional multi-portal techniques. The standardized operative workflow, decision-making criteria, safety boundaries, and practical pearls and pitfalls described in this report are intended to serve as a practical guide for surgeons seeking to safely expand their endoscopic capabilities. Additional prospective studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up are required to validate long-term clinical outcomes, define the full spectrum of applicable anatomy, and determine the learning curve characteristics of this approach.
