Association of combined healthy lifestyle factors with the risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2016–2021
Authors: Na-Noom Jeong, Hyun Joung Lim, Won-Ho Kim, Min-Ju Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLifestyle factors strongly affect the prevention and improvement of liver-related outcomes. However, the relationship between combined lifestyle factors and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has not been comprehensively evaluated. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between a combination of healthy lifestyle factors and MASLD in Korean adults.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 23,111 participants aged 19–80 years who were enrolled in the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2016–2021). A healthy lifestyle was defined based on four factors including diet quality, physical activity, smoking, and sleep duration. Participants were divided into three groups based on healthy lifestyle score ranging from 0 to 4. MASLD was defined as having both hepatic steatosis index ≥ 36 and cardiometabolic risk factor ≥ 1. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the associations between healthy lifestyles and the risk of MASLD. Additional analyses were conducted based on the combination of lifestyle factors.ResultsThe weighted prevalence of MASLD was 24.1%, with the highest rates observed in participants with an unfavorable lifestyle. The protective effect against the risk of MASLD increased with the number of lifestyle factors. Compared to individuals in the unfavorable lifestyle group, the multivariate-adjusted odds ratio for MASLD was 0.825 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.728–0.935) for the intermediate lifestyle group and 0.714 (95% CI, 0.621–0.820) for the favorable lifestyle group. A significant association was found among individuals with combinations of healthy lifestyle factors, and the lowest risk was observed in those with combined diet, physical activity, and smoking, with an odds ratio of 0.680 (95% CI, 0.544–0.848).ConclusionsIn this study, healthy lifestyle factors were inversely associated with MASLD risk. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and future longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these associations. From a public health perspective, our findings support the importance of promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors, particularly smoking cessation and regular physical activity, as potential strategies for MASLD prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28291-3.Keywords: Lifestyle factors, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES)

Background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a chronic liver disease with a global prevalence of approximately 30% and an incidence rate of 5.4% in Korea [1, 2]. Recently, liver multi-society proposed the term “MASLD” to replace non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [3]. MASLD is characterized by an abnormal accumulation of triglycerides (TG) in hepatocytes, occurring in individuals with alcohol consumption of less than 140 g/day for women and 210 g/day for men, along with at least one of the five cardiometabolic risk factors. MASLD can progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis and fibrosis through complex pathophysiological processes and is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4–6].

Lifestyle factors play a key role in the improvement of NAFLD [7]. The European guidelines recommend lifestyle modifications, including improving diet quality and physical activity, and avoiding smoking and alcohol consumption, for managing MASLD [8]. In addition, a recent review suggested updated strategies for preventing NAFLD, including sleep health as a new component [9]. According to Life’s Essential 8 of the American Heart Association, four health behaviors, including diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep health, are major determinants of cardiovascular health (CVH) [10].

Several studies have examined the relationship between a healthy diet and steatotic liver disease (SLD), identifying an inverse association of MASLD with the Healthy Eating Index (HEI), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension, and Mediterranean Diet [11–14]. Furthermore, engaging in physical activity at levels ≥ 600 metabolic equivalent of task (MET) minutes per week reduces the risk of NAFLD by up to 29% [13]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study of the National Health Survey showed that former and never smokers were related with a reduced risk of NAFLD [15]. Inadequate sleep has also been identified as a risk factor for SLD, and both short and long sleep durations have been associated with a higher risk of NAFLD and MASLD compared with moderate sleep duration [16–18].

There is increasing evidence that individuals with unfavorable lifestyle factors are at a higher risk of MASLD; however, previous studies have mostly focused on the correlation of individual lifestyle factors rather than the combination and synergistic effects of various lifestyle factors [19–21]. Moreover, the effects of combined healthy lifestyle factors on MASLD have not been thoroughly evaluated.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between a combination of healthy lifestyle factors and the risk of MASLD in the Korean population.

Methods

Study participants

The Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), is a nationally representative data. A total of 46,828 individuals participated in the KNHANES between 2016 and 2021. Additional details regarding the KNHANES have been described previously [22, 23]. Among 46,828 participants, we excluded 23,717 participants who met at least one of following criteria: (1) aged < 19 years (n = 8,748); (2) history of liver cirrhosis or hepatitis B or C virus infection (n = 585); (3) positivity for serologic markers for hepatitis B or C virus (n = 1,073); (4) alcohol intake of > 210 g/week for men or > 140 g/week for women (n = 5,399); (5) missing data on the hepatic steatosis index (HSI) (n = 2,193); (6) missing data on cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs) (n = 154); (7) missing data on any of the healthy lifestyle factors (including diet, smoking status, physical activity, and sleep duration) (n = 5,155); (8) daily total energy intake of < 500 kcal or > 5,000 kcal (n = 313); and (9) missing data on other variables (n = 97). Finally, 23,111 participants, including 9,068 men and 14,043 women, were included in this study (Fig. 1).

The KNHANES was conducted following approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of KDCA (IRB number: 2018-01-03-P-A, 2018-01-03-C-A, 2018-01-03–2 C-A, 2018-01-03–5 C-A) and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of healthy lifestyle factors

Four healthy lifestyle factors, including diet, physical activity, smoking, and sleep health, were assessed according to the Life’s Essential 8 of the American Heart Association. The overall healthy lifestyle score was calculated based on the number of lifestyle factors, ranging from 0 to 4. The lifestyle was categorized as unfavorable (0–1 points), intermediate (2 points), and favorable (3–4 points) based on the total score. To identify the association of combined lifestyle factors with MASLD, participants were categorized into six groups based on various combinations of healthy lifestyle factors [24].

The Korean Healthy Eating Index (KHEI), derived from a dietary habit survey and a 1-day, 24-hour dietary recall, was used to assess the overall diet quality [25]. The KHEI consists of 14 components, including eight adequacy, three moderation components, and three energy balance components. The component scores ranged from 0 to 5 or 0 to 10 points. The total KHEI score ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better diet quality. Participants were subdivided into tertiles according to the KHEI scores: low (< 57.3), intermediate (57.3–69.4), and high (≥ 69.5). An ideal diet quality was defined by a KHEI score ≥ 69.5, while low and intermediate tertiles were classified as non-ideal diet quality.

Overall physical activity measurement was calculated using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire. Participants were categorized into two groups according to the number of minutes of aerobic physical activity per week. The sufficient physical activity group included those with moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for more than 150 min per week. (1 min of vigorous-intensity exercise is equivalent to 2 min of moderate-intensity exercise.). The insufficient physical activity group included those with physical activity less than 150 min per week. Sufficient physical activity was classified as the ideal level of physical activity.

Smoking status was assessed with the question, “Do you currently smoke?” Participants who answered “yes” were categorized as current smokers, while those who answered “no” were considered to have ideal smoking status.

Sleep health was assessed using self-reported questionnaires. Sleep duration was calculated as the average of weekday and weekend sleep durations as follows: (weekday sleep duration×5 + weekend sleep duration×2)/7. Participants were subdivided into three groups according to sleep duration: ≤6 h, 7–9 h, and ≥ 9 h. Adequate sleep duration (7–9 h) was defined as ideal sleep health [10], whereas sleep duration ≤ 6 h or ≥ 9 h was classified as non-ideal sleep health.

Definition of MASLD

SLD was defined as having an HSI ≥ 36. The HSI, which has been validated as a predictor of hepatic steatosis in the Korean population [26], was calculated from descriptive statistical analysis using the equation: 8 × aspartate aminotransferase (AST) /alanine aminotransferase (ALT) ratio + body mass index (BMI) (+ 2, if diabetes; +2, if women).

MASLD was defined as the presence of SLD and at least one of the five CMRFs: (1) overweight (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2) or abdominal obesity (waist circumference [WC] ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women), (2) fasting blood glucose(FBG) level ≥ 100 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7%, taking anti-diabetic medication, undergoing insulin therapy, or diagnosis for type 2 diabetes, (3) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg,, or anti-hypertensive drug treatment, (4) TG level ≥ 150 mg/dL or lipid-lowering treatment, and (5) high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol level ≤ 40 mg/dL for men and ≤ 50 mg/dL for women or lipid-lowering treatment, based on the presence of SLD.

Other variables

Anthropometric data, socioeconomic status, health-related factors, and nutrition data were examined for all participants. Health-related factors including smoking and drinking were obtained from self-reported questionnaires. Socioeconomic status, medical information, and nutrition data were conducted from face-to-face interviews by trained staff. The demographic characteristics included age (continuous in years), sex (male and female), marital status (married or single), occupation (white-collar, blue-collar, and unemployed), educational level (elementary school, middle school, high school, and college), and household income (lowest, lower middle, upper middle, and highest). All participants fasted for at least 8 h prior to blood sample collection. Blood samples were refrigerated and analyzed at the Seegen Medical Foundation, Korea. Hypertension was defined as having a blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg, or the use of anti-hypertensive medication. Dyslipidemia was defined as having total cholesterol (TC) levels ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG levels ≥ 200 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol levels ≤ 40, or taking a lipid-lowering drug. Diabetes was defined as having a FBG level ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5, taking anti-diabetic medication, or undergoing insulin therapy, or diagnosis for type 2 diabetes.

Statistical analysis

The KNHANES data was extracted using two-stage stratified cluster sampling, and analyzed considering strata, cluster, and weight to reflect complex sampling. The characteristics of participants were described according to MASLD status and lifestyle categories. Statistical differences between groups were tested using SURVEYREG for continuous variables and SURVEYFREQ for categorical variables. Continuous variables are reported as weighted means with standard errors (SEs), while categorical variables are expressed as numbers with weighted percentages. Weighted logistic regression models (SURVEYLOGISTIC) were used to estimate the association between lifestyle factors and the risk of MASLD, expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The weighted multivariable logistic regression models were adjusted for the following potential confounders: age, sex, marital status, occupation, education level, household income, alcohol intake, obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. The total daily energy intake was further adjusted to analyze the association between diet quality and MASLD risk. The effect of individual lifestyle factors was analyzed after adjustment for other lifestyle factors. To identify specific combinations of healthy lifestyle factors associated with MASLD risk, weighted multivariable logistic regression was further conducted according to six combination groups with frequency ≥ 5% [24]. Lifestyle combinations with lower frequencies were excluded to ensure an adequate sample size within each subgroup and to maintain statistical stability and reliable estimation of effect sizes [27]. A combination of two or more healthy lifestyle factors was constructed. In addition, stratified analysis and interaction testing were performed to evaluate the relationship between a healthy lifestyle and MASLD according to age, sex, marital status, occupation, education level, household income, alcohol intake, obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of study participants

The demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. The average age of the study population was 47.6 (SE 0.19) years, and 9,068 (46.6%) were men. Of the 23,111 participants, 5,680 (25.1%), 9,009 (39.3%), and 8,422 (35.6%) had an unfavorable, intermediate, and a favorable lifestyle, respectively. Participants in the favorable lifestyle group were more likely to be women, and have a higher level of education and household income and were less likely to have obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or diabetes. There were significant differences in WC; levels of fasting blood glucose, TC, TG, AST, ALT, HDL; SBP; DBP; and HbA1c between different lifestyle groups.

Healthy lifestyle factors according to MASLD status

The prevalence of healthy lifestyle factors according to MASLD status is shown in Table 2. A total of 5,425 participants (24.1%) had MASLD. The proportion of participants with ideal smoking status and sleep health was 84.3% and 53.4%, respectively, while 46.6% had ideal physical activity and only 29.6% had an ideal diet. Compared to those without MASLD, participants with MASLD were more likely to have non-ideal levels of diet quality, physical activity, smoking status, and sleep health. The proportion of participants having two or more healthy lifestyle factors was 76.3% and 70.4% in the non-MASLD and MASLD groups, respectively.

Combination of healthy lifestyle factors and risk of MASLD

Table 4 presents the association of combined healthy lifestyles with the risk of MASLD. Compared to the unfavorable lifestyle group, the multivariable-adjusted ORs (95% CIs) of MASLD were 0.825 (95% CI: 0.728–0.935) and 0.714 (95% CI: 0.621–0.820) in the intermediate and favorable lifestyle groups, respectively. To evaluate the effect of a specific combination of healthy lifestyle factors on MASLD, six specific combination groups were used in the analysis. Compared to participants with unfavorable lifestyle factors (0 or 1 healthy lifestyle factor), those with two (physical activity and smoking status) and three (diet quality, physical activity, and smoking status/physical activity, smoking status, and sleep health) healthy lifestyle factors had a decreased risk of MASLD, with ORs of 0.755 (95% CI: 0.635–0.897), 0.680 (95% CI: 0.544–0.848), and 0.711 (95% CI: 0.599–0.844), respectively. The stratified analysis showed significant interactions between healthy lifestyle category and education level (P = 0.008) on the risk of MASLD (Additional file 1), suggesting that education level partially mediates the effect of a healthy lifestyle on MASLD risk.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we examined the association between healthy lifestyle factors and MASLD risk in a nationally representative sample of Korean adults. Our results showed that healthy lifestyle factors, including physical activity, smoking status, and sleep duration, were associated with MASLD risk. Moreover, intermediate and favorable lifestyles were associated with a decreased risk of MASLD after adjustment for potential confounders. Interestingly, inverse associations between combined healthy lifestyle factors and MASLD risk were consistently observed among participants with ideal levels of physical activity and smoking status, with the strongest inverse association observed when ideal diet quality was additionally present.

A systematic literature review provided evidence regarding healthy lifestyle factors and MASLD risk [28–30]. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate the relationship between combined healthy lifestyle factors and the risk of MASLD in Korean adults. We found that healthier behaviors were associated with a reduced risk of MASLD; however, a significant association was not observed with diet quality after adjustment for potential confounders. This finding may be partly explained by methodological limitations related to dietary assessment. Specifically, diet quality was evaluated using a one day, 24-hour recall survey, which is subject to recall bias and may not adequately capture usual dietary intake or intra-individual variability in daily consumption. In addition, the KHEI, while useful for assessing overall diet quality, may have limited ability to fully capture dietary components particularly relevant to liver health, such as processed food consumption. Although a non-significant association is consistent with findings from a cohort study in China [20], other studies have indicated that diet quality may be more strongly associated with a lower risk of MASLD [14, 31]. A prospective study of 13,303 Chinese participants reported that more healthy lifestyle factors, such as smoking, alcohol, physical activity, sedentary time, diet, sleep behavior, and BMI, were associated with a lower MASLD risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.73–0.89 for three healthy lifestyle factors, HR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.61–0.75 for four healthy lifestyle factors, and HR = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.49–0.62 for 5–7 healthy lifestyle factors), compared with that in individuals with 0–2 healthy lifestyle factors [20]. Similarly, a prospective cohort study reported that adherence to a favorable healthy lifestyle—defined by non-smoking, non-obesity, sufficient physical activity, and a healthy dietary pattern—was associated with a lower risk of new-onset MASLD (OR = 0.42, 95% CI, 0.19–0.90) for individuals with 3–4 healthy lifestyle components, compared with those with 0–2 components [32].

Similarly, a cross-sectional study of 2,928 Korean adult participants indicated that ideal CVH, including BMI, smoking, physical activity, diet, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and TC, was associated with a reduced risk of NAFLD (OR = 0.13, 95% CI = 0.07–0.023), compared with that in the intermediate CVH group [33]. Furthermore, another cross-sectional study of 3,893 US adults showed that a healthy lifestyle, defined by diet, BMI, physical activity, cigarette smoking, and sleep duration, was associated with a decreased risk of NAFLD (OR = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.58–0.89 for a healthy lifestyle score of 2–3 and OR = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.19–0.33 for a healthy lifestyle score of 4–5) [34]. These results are consistent with our findings, which indicate a protective effect of healthy lifestyle factors on the risk of MASLD. In addition, it is recommended to consider the combination of more than two healthy factors, rather than focusing on a single factor.

More importantly, recent studies have reported an association between specific combinations of healthy lifestyle factors and the risk of NAFLD [13]. Our study also found that, compared to participants with an unfavorable lifestyle, those with two (physical activity and smoking status) and three (diet quality, physical activity, and smoking status/physical activity, smoking status, and sleep health) healthy lifestyle factors had a decreased risk of MASLD, indicating a stronger impact of physical activity and smoking status on MASLD risk. Similarly, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey of the United States 1988–1994 with a follow-up period of 21.6 years showed that current smoking, obesity, and physical inactivity were associated with an increased risk of all-cause, CVD-, and cancer-specific mortality compared to that with never smoking, normal weight, and physical activity (rate advancement periods [RAP] = 13.0, 95% CI = 10.4–15.6 for all-cause mortality, RAP = 12.12, 95% CI = 8.4–15.9 for CVD-specific mortality, and RAP = 18.9, 95% CI = 12.9–25.0 for cancer-specific mortality) [35]. These findings align with those from a prospective cohort study in England, which indicated that current smoking with low physical activity was associated with higher risks of fair/poor self-related health, coronary heart disease, stroke, cancer, and chronic lung disease than was never smoking with high physical activity (relative risk [RR] = 1.92, 95% CI = 1.40–2.65 for fair/poor self-related health, RR = 2.64,95% CI = 1.59–4.37 for coronary heart disease; RR = 3.12, 95% CI = 1.88–5.18 for stroke; RR = 1.89, 95% CI = 1.20–2.98 for cancer; and RR = 14.00, 95% CI = 7.68–25.53 for chronic lung disease) [36]. Although the interaction between smoking and physical activity remains unclear, the combined effect of smoking and lack of exercise is associated with higher risks of all-cause and CVD mortality in patients with hypertension [37], while persistent smokers with a consistently inactive status are associated with increased levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) [38]. Elevated hs-CRP is an independent predictor of CVD and mortality [39], whereas CPR levels increase in response to acute infections and inflammatory conditions [40]. Although our results show no evidence of synergistic effects of physical activity and smoking status, they highlight the importance of encouraging both nonsmoking and sufficient physical activity.

The association between a healthy lifestyle and MASLD is complex and may be explained by several mechanisms. Obesity, insulin resistance, hypertension, and hypertriglyceridemia are risk factors for MASLD. Unhealthy lifestyles, such as insufficient physical activity and excessive calorie intake, lead to obesity, insulin resistance, and the accumulation of TGs in hepatocytes [4]. In particular, smoking and physical inactivity may influence MASLD through shared metabolic pathways involving key lipogenic regulators. Experimental evidence indicates that tobacco smoke exposure can inactivate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) via dephosphorylation, thereby enhancing activation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1), a central transcription factor driving hepatic de novo lipogenesis and triglyceride synthesis, ultimately increasing hepatic triglyceride accumulation [41]. Consistently, a large Korean cohort study reported that current smoking and cumulative smoking exposure were associated with a higher risk of incident NAFLD [42]. Conversely, regular physical activity may exert protective effects on MASLD through the same AMPK–SREBP-1 axis. Exercise improves systemic insulin sensitivity, which may reduce free fatty acid flux to the liver from adipose tissue and limit substrate availability for hepatic de novo lipogenesis [43]. Exercise-induced AMPK activation inhibits SREBP signaling, providing a plausible molecular link between exercise and reduced hepatic lipogenesis [44, 45]. In addition, non-ideal sleep duration may influence MASLD risk through neuroendocrine and metabolic pathways. Short sleep has been associated with increased ghrelin and decreased leptin levels, reduced insulin sensitivity and impaired glucose metabolism [46–48]. While long sleep duration has also been consistently associated with an increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases and elevated inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, suggesting chronic low-grade inflammation that may promote insulin resistance and hepatic lipid accumulation [49, 50]. Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, further studies are required to better understand these associations.

The strength of our study was its evaluation of the relationship between lifestyle factors and MASLD risk in a large and representative sample of the Korean population. However, this study has some limitations. First, as this was a cross-sectional study, establishing a causal relationship between a healthy lifestyle and MASLD was difficult. Therefore, longitudinal prospective studies are required to confirm these associations and to clarify the causal effects of combined healthy lifestyle factors on the incidence of MASLD. Second, we used HSI as a noninvasive test to diagnose MASLD. Although the gold standard diagnosis of MASLD involves liver biopsy and abdominal ultrasound, their application is limited in large-scale population-based studies. In the Korean population, a HSI cut-off value of ≥ 36 has been demonstrated high sensitivity with acceptable specificity, along with good overall diagnostic accuracy, supporting its utility as a practical noninvasive screening tool in epidemiological research [51]. Nevertheless, the use of HSI may be subject to misclassification, and additional studies are needed to further validate these associations. Third, a 1-day, 24-hour recall survey was used to calculate the HEI for diet quality, which may not accurately reflect the daily intake of diet as it relies on the memory of participants for dietary intake from the previous date. In addition, both sleep duration and physical activity were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, without information on sleep quality or sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea, which have been shown to be relevant to MASLD. This approach was consistent with the Life’s Essential 8 framework, which emphasizes sleep duration and physical activity as core components of cardiovascular health [10]. However, self-reported measures may be subject to measurement error, and the validity of these questionnaires in KNHANES has not been fully evaluated against objective assessments such as wearable device–based measurements. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution, and future studies incorporating objective measures of sleep quality, sleep disorders, and physical activity are warranted. Although our study adjusted for various confounders, it may not have considered all potential confounders.

Conclusions

In this study, healthy lifestyle factors were inversely associated with MASLD risk, suggesting that a combined healthy lifestyle may be beneficial for metabolic and cardiovascular risk management. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm the temporal and causal effects of lifestyle behaviors on MASLD development. From a public health perspective, our findings highlight the potential importance of promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors, particularly smoking cessation and regular physical activity, as part of strategies to prevent MASLD. Clinicians and public health practitioners should consider integrating lifestyle counseling, smoking cessation programs, and physical activity promotion into routine preventive care to reduce the burden of MASLD.
