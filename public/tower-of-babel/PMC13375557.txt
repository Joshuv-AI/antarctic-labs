Early Holocene vigorous Black Sea outflow and the onset of sluggish Aegean deep-water convection
Authors: Dimitris Evangelinos, Sara Campderrós, Sergio Trias-Navarro, Cristina Garcia Briña, Eduardo Paredes Paredes, Leopoldo D Pena, Jaime Frigola, Thomas Hasiotis, Grigoris Rousakis, Dimitris Sakellariou, Isabel Cacho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Eastern Mediterranean Sea underwent profound hydrographic changes from the late glacial to the Early Holocene, culminating in deep-water stagnation and the deposition of the most recent organic-rich sediment layer (sapropel S1). While this event has traditionally been attributed to riverine discharge from North Africa and enhanced precipitation over the northern Mediterranean borderlands, the role of Black Sea outflow remains overlooked. Here, we present high-resolution multi-proxy records from a central Aegean sediment core spanning the past 42,500 years. Combining grain size, X-ray fluorescence, radiogenic and stable isotope records, we show that a shift in sedimentation ~ 11,000 years ago marked the onset of silt-rich detrital input predominantly sourced from the Dardanelles-Marmara region, accompanied by surface freshening and an enhanced influx of isotopically light dissolved inorganic carbon. We propose that vigorous Black Sea outflow enhanced stratification and suppressed Aegean deep-water convection, playing a more critical role than previously recognised in sapropel S1 formation.Subject terms: Palaeoclimate, Palaeoceanography

Introduction

The Aegean Sea, located in the Eastern Mediterranean Sea (EMS), is highly sensitive to sea-level variations linked to global ice-volume changes1–4, high-latitude climate variability5,6 and African monsoon dynamics7,8. Moreover, Aegean deep-water formation is exceptionally sensitive to climate forcing and freshwater intrusions from the Black Sea, driving profound hydrographic changes that subsequently propagate across the EMS and beyond9–11. Due to its small volume, the Aegean Sea lacks the inertia of larger ocean basins, making it an ideal natural laboratory for investigating direct responses to major climatic and oceanographic perturbations in the past5. Marine sediments from the Aegean Sea thus offer critical insights into past climate and hydrographic changes, providing unique information for understanding and assessing future climate variability (Fig. 1).

The EMS experienced major hydrological changes during the transition from the late glacial to the Early Holocene, culminating in the deposition of the most recent, dark, organic-rich layer (sapropel S1) between ~10.8 and 6.1 kyr cal. BP2,12,13. The formation of sapropel S1 has been attributed to (1) reduced deep-water ventilation (i.e., deep-water stagnation), and/or (2) enhanced organic carbon accumulation linked to increased primary productivity2,4. Both mechanisms were primarily driven by freshwater inputs into the EMS. This freshwater influx likely reduced salinity and increased surface buoyancy, thereby suppressing deep-water formation and ventilation. The resulting increase in the water column stratification, coupled with the decline in deep-water oxygenation, ultimately triggered the widespread development of suboxic to anoxic conditions and the deposition of sapropel S114–17.

For decades, this freshwater influx has been associated with the intensified African monsoon activity, which led to enhanced river discharge from north Africa, including the Nile2,4,7,12,18,19. Additional freshwater sources have been proposed, such as enhanced runoff driven by intensified precipitation along the northern Mediterranean borderlands20–22, including the Northern Borderlands of the Aegean Sea (NBAS)6,23, the inflow of meltwater from regional and remote glaciers24, and low-salinity Black Sea outflow into the Aegean Sea17,25–29. Sea-level rise and associated hydrographic changes may also have played an important role in the formation of sapropel S12. Model simulations and proxy data suggest that a preconditioning period during the deglaciation, linked to the melting of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets, was essential for the development of bottom-water anoxia30,31. A massive influx of low-salinity Atlantic water from Heinrich Stadial 1 (HS1, 18–15.5 ka), entering through the Strait of Gibraltar, has been proposed to have substantially reduced deep-water ventilation, promoting the development of suboxic/anoxic conditions in the EMS2,31. However, a growing body of evidence suggests an alternative route of freshwater input during the deglaciation and Early Holocene, via the Black Sea outflow into the northern Aegean Sea. This influx, driven by the melting of the Fennoscandian Ice Sheet and outburst floods from glacial lakes in central Asia, may also have played a key role in suppressing deep-water convection28,29,32,33.

In light of the ambiguous evidence for the role of Black Sea outflow in suppressing deep-water formation and ultimately contributing to the deposition of sapropel S1, we analysed a well-dated sedimentary record collected from the Chios Basin in the central Aegean Sea (38°14.60’ N, 25°06.25’ E, 652 m water depth) during the OTE-2019 (HCMR RV Aegaeo, 2019), spanning the last ~42.5 ka (Fig. 1b; Methods). We present a high-resolution, multi-proxy dataset combining grain size and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) core-scanning data, complemented by strontium (Sr) and neodymium (Nd) isotope measurements of terrigenous sediment fraction, as well as stable carbon and oxygen (δ13C and δ18O) isotope records from planktonic foraminifera (Globigerina bulloides). The strategic location of core KC-13 in the central Aegean Sea, which is sensitive to hydroclimatic variability from both the northern and southern borders of the EMS, and proximal to the main deep-water formation region in the northern Aegean (Supplementary Note 1), combined with our multi-proxy approach provides robust reconstructions of terrigenous sediment provenance and transport pathways, as well as hydrographic variability from the late glacial to the Holocene. This integrated dataset allows us to disentangle the influence of Black Sea outflow from other mechanisms and better constrain its role in suppressing deep-water formation leading to sapropel S1 deposition.

Results

Sedimentological and geochemical analyses

Sediments at core KC-13 consist predominantly of moderately bioturbated hemipelagic muds (mean grain size: 8 μm) containing variable amounts of calcareous biogenic material (Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Note 2 for detailed descriptions). Sapropel S1 is defined by its characteristic greenish-brown to very dark greenish-brown colour and by elevated Br/Ti and Ba/Al ratios, indicating enhanced organic matter preservation/accumulation34–36. A distinct tephra layer containing abundant volcanic glass shards is also present within the sequence (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The principal component analysis (PCA) of the XRF core-scanner data provides key insights into the main processes controlling sedimentation at KC-13. PCA identifies two main components, PC1 and PC2, which together explain 91.5% of the total variance (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 3). PC1 (75.4%) shows positive loadings for detrital elements (e.g., Ti, Al, K, Fe and Zr), representing aluminosilicate inputs, and strong negative loadings for Ca and Sr, reflecting biogenic carbonate contributions. PC1 therefore captures the dominant processes driving sedimentation at KC-13, reflecting shifts between marine carbonate productivity and terrigenous sediment supply35 (Fig. 2b). Consistently, Ti/Ca ratios exhibit the same variability as PC1, further supporting the interpretation of alternating detrital versus carbonate productivity inputs (Supplementary Fig. 1). PC2 (16.1% of the total variance) shows a strong anticorrelation between elements associated with organic matter preservation/accumulation (Br, Ba) and those linked to aluminosilicates (e.g., Ti, Al, Zr and Rb). Br/Ti ratios therefore track variations in organic matter preservation/accumulation, reaching their highest values during sapropel S1 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

By combining grain size data with Ti/Al ratios and the PC1 scores, we gain further insights into the terrigenous sediment supply (Fig. 2a–d). Between ~42 and 17 ka, sediments show high PC1 values, high Ti/Al ratios, high silt content (~75%) and low clay/silt ratios, indicating enhanced delivery of silt-rich terrigenous material (clayey silt to silt). From ~17 to 11 ka, PC1 values, Ti/Al ratios, and silt content progressively decrease, while clay/silt ratios increase, reflecting reduced terrigenous input and a shift toward finer-grained, clay-rich material, accompanied by higher biogenic calcareous productivity. After ~11 ka, PC1 values, Ti/Al ratios, and silt content increase again (~75%) and remain elevated until the end of sapropel S1, together with low clay/silt ratios, marking a return to enhanced delivery of silt-rich terrigenous sediments. Following the termination of sapropel S1, PC1 values, Ti/Al ratios and silt content decrease and clay/silt ratios rise, indicating a shift back to reduced terrigenous input and deposition of clay-rich sediments.

Neodymium and strontium isotopes

The detrital fraction of core KC-13 (<63 µm fraction) displays average εNd values of −8.4 ± 0.8 (2σ), with the notable exception of a sample corresponding to the Y5 tephra layer, which exhibits substantially more radiogenic values (εNd = −3.3 ± 0.3) (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 3). Slightly more radiogenic εNd values are observed in samples associated with sapropel S1 and at ~18.2 ka (εNd = −7.9 ± 0.3), compared to the rest of samples (εNd = −8.4 to −8.9). Similarly, the average ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr isotopic composition is 0.71504 ± 0.0003, except for the Y5 tephra layer, which shows a distinctly less radiogenic signature (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr = 0.70920 ± 0.00002, 2σ). Samples from sapropel S1 and at ~18.2 ka also exhibit less radiogenic values (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr = 0.71487 and 0.71387, respectively).

Stable oxygen and carbon isotopes

In this study, we present the oxygen (δ18O) from Globigerina bulloides and Globigerinoides ruber, and carbon (δ13C) isotopic records from G. bulloides (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 5 and Fig. 4f, j). The δ18O records from both species show a general transition from heavier to lighter values, corresponding to the last glacial period, the deglaciation and the Holocene1. During the glacial period, δ18Obulloides averages 3.6‰, while δ18Oruber values are slightly lower (average 2.6 ‰) and more variable. During the Bølling-Allerød (B/A) and Younger Dryas (YD), δ18O values remain relatively stable at ~3.0‰ for G. bulloides and ~2.1 ‰ for G. ruber. From the Early Holocene to the onset of sapropel S1, both records show a similar trend towards lighter values, decreasing from 2.5‰ to 1.4‰ (G. bulloides) and from 1.6‰ to 0.5‰ (G. ruber). During sapropel S1, light δ18O values are recorded, reaching 1.3‰ for G. bulloides and 0.4‰ for G. ruber. After sapropel S1, δ18Obulloides averages 1.4 ‰ and δ18Oruber averages 0.5 ‰.

Discussion

Variations in sedimentation at core KC-13 reflect changing processes and sources of sediment delivery to the central Aegean Sea during the late glacial-Holocene. Hemipelagic sedimentation at core KC-13 was characterised by two contrasting depositional regimes. A silt-rich mode, associated with reduced calcareous marine productivity, marks intervals of enhanced terrigenous sediment supply (Fig. 2a–c). In contrast, a clay-rich mode, associated with increased calcareous marine productivity, reflects periods of reduced detrital input and a greater contribution of biogenic material (Fig. 2a–c). This alternating depositional pattern, recorded in both sedimentological and geochemical data, reflects a complex interplay of processes controlling detrital supply to the distal Chios Basin. These may include sea-level fluctuations, variability in river discharge, changes in ocean circulation and bottom-current dynamics, and variations in the flux of aeolian African dust8,23,37–40.

To decipher the source and transport processes governing terrigenous supply in the central Aegean Sea during the late glacial to Holocene times, we combine silt content (%), titanium/aluminium (Ti/Al) ratios and neodymium (εNd) and strontium (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr) isotopic compositions measured on the detrital fine fraction (<63 μm) of core KC-13 (Fig. 2a–d, f, g). These geochemical data are further compared with coeval clay mineral records from core GeoTü SL143, located in the central Aegean Sea (Figs. 1b and 2e), adjacent to KC-138, providing robust constraints on the provenance of terrigenous sediments. At the core KC-13, Ti/Al variations closely track changes in detrital silt content (Fig. 2c, d). High Ti/Al ratios reflect the presence and/or preferential accumulation of heavy minerals (e.g., ilmenite, rutile and zircon) relative to aluminosilicates, and have been associated with intensified bottom currents35, enhanced African dust inputs, or increased fluvial discharge36,41,42.

To assess whether these variations were driven by changes in bottom-current intensity, we calculated the mean sortable silt (SS¯) of terrigenous sediment fractions (see Methods), a robust proxy for reconstructing bottom flow speed43. The weak correlation between sortable silt percentage and SS¯ (r = 0.15, n = 257) indicates that processes other than bottom-current activity predominantly controlled sediment deposition in the Chios Basin43 (Supplementary Fig. 1; Methods). Clay mineralogical evidence shows that terrigenous supply in the central Aegean was dominated by fluvial inputs, with dust contributing only a secondary component, both today and throughout the last glacial cycle8,44. Dust inputs from the northern and eastern Mediterranean borderlands are considered negligible44. Moreover, the poor correspondence between our silt % and Ti/Al records and the kaolinite/chlorite ratios at core GeoTü SL143, a well-established proxy for Saharan dust influx in the central Aegean Sea8 (Fig. 2h), indicates that African dust input did not control the observed variability in silt content and Ti/Al ratios at KC-13.

We thus propose that silt content in the Chios Basin primarily reflects changes in the delivery of river-derived suspended sediments, with a secondary contribution from African dust. This interpretation is supported by our εNd and ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr records, which yield average values of −8.4 ± 0.4 and 0.7154 ± 0.00056, respectively (Fig. 3). These values, which reflect a mixture of riverine and dust-dominated signatures, are close to modern εNd and ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr compositions of detrital surface sediments from the northern Aegean Sea (εNd = −7.9 and ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶S = 0.712545)45,46. These sediments are predominantly supplied by suspended material delivered by rivers from the surrounding continental borderlands, including the NBAS, western Turkey, the Dardanelles-Marmara region, and the north and central Aegean islands, with an additional contribution from African dust25,44,45. Consistently, intervals of high silt content and elevated Ti/Al ratios broadly coincide with more radiogenic εNd and less radiogenic ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr values, indicating a greater contribution of detrital sediment sourced from river systems draining the northwestern Aegean, western Turkey, and the Aegean islands (Fig. 2f, g). The endmembers of these riverine provinces exhibit overlapping Sr-Nd isotopic signatures, making it difficult to distinguish between them based on radiogenic isotopes alone (Fig. 3). However, the close correspondence between the silt content and Ti/Al ratios of KC-13 and the smectite/kaolinite ratios of core GeoTü SL143 (Fig. 2c–e), which reflect terrigenous sediment input from western Turkey and the Dardanelles-Marmara region8,24,44 (Supplementary Notes 3), indicates that the silt-sized terrigenous fraction at KC-13 was primarily controlled by variations in river discharge from western Turkey and by river-derived suspended material transported through the Dardanelles Strait.

The drivers and mechanisms governing sediment delivery and hydrographic conditions in the Aegean Sea changed substantially across the last glacial-Holocene transition. During the last glacial period, enhanced terrigenous input, reflected in high PC1 values (Fig. 2b) and increased deposition of coarse-grained (silt-sized) sediments in the Chios Basin (Fig. 2a, c) was primarily sourced from western Turkey and/or the Dardanelles-Marmara Sea region (Fig. 2e), broadly following sea-level fluctuations (Fig. 4b). The expansion of large continental ice sheets in both hemispheres caused global sea-level to fall by ~120 m below present during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM)1,47. Paleo-coastline reconstructions for the LGM indicate that large areas of the northern Aegean and western Turkish continental shelves were subaerially exposed, with shorelines extending tens of kilometres seaward compared to today48. This exposure likely led to increased river incision, particularly during wetter periods, enhancing terrigenous supply and facilitating the transport of coarser (prodelta) material into the deeper basins of the northern and central Aegean Sea via turbidite channels, wave reworking or current winnowing of shelf sediments (Fig. 5a)24,37,39. However, low sea levels during this period isolated the Black Sea and Marmara Sea from the Aegean Sea, due to closure of the Dardanelles sill (LGM sill depth: −75 m) (Fig. 4b)8,33,49. Hence, we infer that increased terrigenous sediment supply and silt-sized sediment delivery to the Chios Basin during the late glacial were largely controlled by sea-level fluctuations and associated circulation changes, with western Turkey acting as the dominant sediment source.

With the onset of deglacial warming following the LGM, the melting of continental ice sheets led to sea-level rise47. This transgression resulted in the progressive submergence of continental shelf areas, which increasingly acted as sediment traps for coarser fluvial inputs37. As a result, only finer-grained sediments were able to reach distal basins via suspended sediment plumes associated with high river discharge. This interpretation is supported by multiple sediment cores from the northern and central Aegean Sea24,25,39. Our data are consistent with this trend, showing a gradual decline in terrigenous input (Fig. 2b), increased accumulation of finer-grained, clay-rich sediments (Fig. 2a, c), and a concurrent rise in calcareous marine productivity between Heinrich event 1 (H1, 17.5 ka) and the end of the Younger Dryas (YD; ~12 ka). Together, these lines of evidence indicate a transition to a more distal depositional environment, primarily driven by sea-level rise during the last deglaciation (Fig. 5b). This interpretation is further supported by provenance data from the northern and central Aegean. Lower smectite content indicates a gradual decline in sediment supply from western Turkey and/or the Dardanelles-Marmara Sea region (Fig. 2e, k), alongside increasing sediment delivery from the NBAS8,24, driven by enhanced runoff associated with the melting of the Rhodope mountain ice masses24.

Strikingly, a shift in sedimentation is recorded in the Early Holocene (~11 ka), marked by a resurgence of silt-rich deposition that persisted into sapropel S1. During this interval, silt content reached levels comparable to those of the last glacial period, indicating a major change in sediment dynamics (Fig. 2c). This event coincides with a provenance shift, as reflected by clay mineralogy (Fig. 2e) and Sr-Nd isotopic data (Fig. 2f–g), suggesting increased delivery of detrital material to the Chios Basin from western Turkey and/or the Dardanelles-Marmara Sea region. Given the continued sea-level rise during the Early Holocene1,47, such high silt input into a distal basin is unexpected, as ongoing transgression would typically favour the deposition of finer-grained, clay-rich sediments sourced from the NBAS and western Turkey. Indeed, sedimentological records from the northern Aegean document increased clay content during the Early Holocene and sapropel S1 (Fig. 2j), attributed to sea-level transgression and enhanced precipitation (Fig. 2i) and riverine runoff from the NBAS6,22,23,50. Taken together, these observations suggest that increased runoff from the NBAS and western Turkey cannot account for the elevated silt input observed in the Chios Basin during the Early Holocene. We instead propose that the resurgence of silt-rich deposition at ~11 ka was predominantly sourced from the Dardanelles-Marmara Sea region and was largely controlled by Black Sea outflow through the Dardanelles Strait.

The seaway reconnection between the Aegean and Marmara seas was established at ~14.7 ka51,52 or 13.8 ka33,53, allowing the first incursion of Mediterranean waters into the Marmara Sea, once sea-level had risen above the depth of the Dardanelles sill (−75 to −80 m)33,54. A comprehensive synthesis of available seismic and sediment core evidence from the Black Sea, Marmara Sea and Aegean Sea indicates a shift in flow direction and the onset of a persistent Black Sea outflow into the Aegean from the Early Holocene (~11.5–11.1 ka) and throughout the Holocene33 (Fig. 4a). This outflow was initially driven by the melting of the Fennoscandian Ice Sheet and outburst floods from glacial lakes in central Asia33, and was subsequently sustained and strengthened by enhanced precipitation over the extensive Black Sea catchment during the Early Holocene, culminating during sapropel S122. Together, these processes raised Black Sea water levels above the Bosporus sill (~35 m), causing the Black Sea to stand higher than the Marmara and Aegean seas and maintaining a persistent one-way outflow of Black Sea waters into the Aegean Sea28,29,33.

During this one-way flow regime, increased export of silt-rich suspended material to the Aegean Sea was driven by enhanced winter precipitation22 and riverine runoff along the southern margin of the Marmara Sea, which delivered large quantities of silt-rich terrigenous material into the Marmara Sea33, increasing the suspended load available for downstream transport. In addition, the strong, confined outflow through the narrow Dardanelles Strait may also have contributed to silt-rich sediment export through erosion and remobilisation of fine-grained sediment within the Strait. After sapropel S1, reduced precipitation and river discharge decreased the delivery of terrigenous suspended material to the Marmara Sea, contributing to the decline in silt-rich sediment export. The one-way Black Sea outflow into the Aegean Sea persisted until ~8–7.5 ka, when rising Mediterranean Sea level led to the establishment of the modern two-way flow in both the Bosporus and Dardanelles straits, thereby reducing the export of Black Sea water into the Aegean Sea (Fig. 4a)33. Our interpretation is consistent with sedimentological (Fig. 2c), geochemical (Fig. 2d) and mineralogical data (Fig. 2e), which suggest a reduced input of silt-rich sediment derived from the Marmara-Dardanelles region after ~8–7.5 ka.

The striking correlation in timing, amplitude, and pattern between the silt-sized material at core KC-13, sourced from the Dardanelles-Marmara Sea region (Fig. 4c) and the reconstructed sea-surface salinities28 (Fig. 4d) and freshwater input estimates17 (Fig. 4e) at core SL152, located within the main path of the modern Black Sea outflow (Fig. 1b), provides strong evidence that the observed shift in sediment delivery at core KC-13 was linked to intensified Black Sea outflow into the northern Aegean Sea during the Early Holocene. This interpretation is further supported by increased delivery of land-derived material in the northern and central Aegean, inferred from palynological evidence and high accumulation of terrestrial biomarkers (n-alkanols, n-alkanes), which has been attributed to enhanced Black Sea outflow and increased precipitation over the NBAS6,23,26,27,34,37. Our Br/Ti record (Fig. 4g) closely matches total organic carbon (Fig. 4i) and terrigenous biomarker accumulation rates (Fig. 4h) from nearby cores26,34, indicating that enhanced organic matter production and preservation, including terrestrial inputs, were primarily driven by intensified Black Sea outflow. Based on the above evidence, we attribute the decline in the δ13Cbulloides record at core KC-13 from ~11 ka, culminating during the two sapropel S1 phases (S1a and S1b) to the input of isotopically light dissolved inorganic carbon associated with the intensified flux of terrestrial source carbon through freshwater export from the Black Sea-Marmara system via the Dardanelles Strait (Fig. 4j). This interpretation is further supported by the concurrent δ18Obulloides depletion at KC-13, indicating enhanced regional freshening during winter/autumn seasons (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Note 4), and by the δ¹⁵N record from nearby core MNB-3 (Fig. 4k), which mirrors the δ13Cbulloides signal and points to strong near-surface salinity stratification34. Consistent with model and proxy evidence for intensified winter storm tracks and increased precipitation along the northern Mediterranean borderlands during sapropel S1 formation20,22,50,55, we propose that enhanced winter rainfall across the large Black Sea drainage basin intensified freshwater export to the Aegean Sea through the Dardanelles Strait.

We further propose that the onset of low-salinity Black Sea outflow since the Early Holocene triggered a large-scale hydrographic reorganisation in the northern and central Aegean Sea, suppressing deep-water formation and ultimately leading to the deposition of sapropel S1. Micropaleontological and geochemical evidence along a north-south transect of the Aegean Sea documents higher freshwater input sourced from northern Aegean, during sapropel S117,33,37, consistent with the persistent low-salinity Black Sea outflow into the Aegean Sea since the Early Holocene28,29,33. This influx of low-salinity water led to strong stratification in the Aegean Sea, resulting in the progressive isolation of intermediate/bottom waters from the surface system after ~11 ka, as evidenced by major shifts in micropaleontological and stable isotope records14,31. This, in turn, drove a progressive weakening of deep convection and reduced ventilation at depth, shown in the decline of the composite δ13Cepibenthic record from the EMS, which reflects the release of 12C during remineralization of settling organic matter14–16,31,56 (Fig. 4l). This sluggish overturning circulation limited oxygen renewal at depth, as indicated by benthic markers for very poor bottom-water oxygenation (dysoxic conditions)14,56, thereby allowing the formation of sapropel S1.

Sapropel S1 has traditionally been linked to monsoon intensification during the last African Humid Period (AHP; 14-8 ka), which led to increased river discharge from northern Africa, including the Nile, to the EMS4,7,19. However, neither the onset nor the termination of enhanced Nile outflow aligns precisely with the deposition of sapropel S12,7. While we do not discount the role of regional precipitation/runoff and sea-level rise in preconditioning the basin2,4,22,31, our findings provide compelling evidence that enhanced Black Sea outflow in the Early Holocene, driven by meltwater inputs and enhanced precipitation, played a much more important role. This strengthened outflow promoted widespread water column stratification, suppressed deep convection in the northern Aegean Sea, and ultimately facilitated the formation of sapropel S1. Present-day hydrographic observations support this mechanism, showing that variations in the low-salinity Black Sea outflow can strongly modulate dense-water formation in the northern Aegean Sea10.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that the hydrographic reorganisation of the Eastern Mediterranean during the Early Holocene, culminating in the stagnation of the deep thermohaline circulation and the widespread deposition of sapropel S1, was not solely driven by freshwater input from north African rivers and enhanced regional precipitation. High-resolution multi-proxy evidence from the central Aegean, integrating grain-size distributions, X-ray fluorescence data, radiogenic (εNd and ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr) and stable isotope (δ18O and δ13C) records, reveals a major shift in sedimentation beginning in the Early Holocene and intensifying during sapropel S1. This interval is characterised by the accumulation of silt-rich detritus predominantly sourced from the Dardanelles-Marmara Sea region, accompanied by surface freshening and an increased influx of isotopically light dissolved inorganic carbon. Together, these findings suggest that Black Sea outflow played a more critical role than previously recognised in enhancing surface stratification, suppressing deep-water convection, and ultimately contributing to the formation of sapropel S1 in the Aegean Sea. Our results provide new constraints on the role of Black Sea outflow in the development of sapropel S1, and underscore the need for future Eastern Mediterranean circulation models to incorporate low-salinity Black Sea input throughout the Early Holocene and during the onset and deposition of sapropel S1.

Methods

Core description, core imaging and X-ray fluorescence core-scanning

A detailed visual sediment description of core KC-13 was conducted, focusing on sedimentary structures and textures such as bioturbation, laminations, sediment colour and bed contacts. High-resolution digital images were obtained using a Nikon 60 mm camera lens mounted on an Avaatech X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) Scanner at the CORELAB (Universitat de Barcelona, Spain). Major and minor elemental compositions of the sediments were determined using an Avaatech XRF core scanner at the CORELAB (Universitat de Barcelona, Spain). High-resolution XRF core-scanning measurements were performed at 10, 30 and 50 kV every 1 cm. The surface of the core was carefully cleaned and covered by a 4 μm thick ultralene plastic film to avoid contamination while scanning. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to XRF core-scanner elemental data to explore the main patterns of geochemical variability in the sediment record. The tephra layer was excluded from the PCA because it exhibits a markedly different geochemical signature that is unrelated to paleoclimatic or paleoceanographic changes. Prior to PCA, the elemental data were transformed using a centred log ratio (CLR) transformation following57. The CLR-transformed dataset was subsequently used as input for PCA, which was performed using the Palaeontological Statistics Software Package (PAST), version 4.0358. For the PCA, we selected elements with a robust signal quality Al, Si, K, Ca, Ti, Fe, Br, Rb, Sr, Zr and Ba.

Grain size analysis

High-resolution grain-size measurements were performed every 1 cm on core KC-13 (265 samples) using Beckman Coulter Laser Sizer (LS230; 0.004– 2000 μm) at the Laboratory of Sedimentology (Universitat de Barcelona, Spain). The detrital fraction was isolated by treating the bulk sediment with 1 M hydrochloric acid solution (HCl) to eliminate carbonates, followed by hydrogen peroxide (10% H₂O₂) to remove organic matter. To disperse aggregates and prevent re-agglomeration, sodium pyrophosphate solution was added to the samples prior to the grain-size measurements. To assess whether the sediments were influenced by bottom current sorting, we calculated the mean sortable silt (SS¯), a widely used proxy for reconstructing variations in near-bottom-current velocity in deep-sea sediments. This proxy is based on the principle that coarser mean grain sizes within the sortable silt fraction reflect stronger near-bottom currents due to selective deposition and winnowing43. The mean of the sortable silt (SS¯, defined as the geometric mean of the 10–63 μm silt fraction) and the sortable silt percentage (SS%, defined as the 10–63 μm fraction relative to the <63 μm fraction) were calculated after43. The relationship between SS̅ and SS% was evaluated to assess the suitability of the sortable silt data for bottom-current reconstructions43.

Stable oxygen and carbon isotope analyses

A total of 164 samples were selected for δ18O and δ13C isotopic analyses. Each sample contained 10–15 individual specimens of the planktonic foraminifera Globigerinoides ruber (76 samples) and Globigerina bulloides (88 samples), which were handpicked from the >250 μm sediment fraction. The samples were crushed and cleaned with methanol (ultrasonicated for 10 s) to remove any attached clay and dried under a laminar flow hood. Oxygen and carbon isotopes were measured using a Finnigan MAT 252 mass spectrometer at the Centres Científics i Tecnològics de la Universitat de Barcelona (CCiTUB). Analytical precision for δ18O and δ13C was better than 0.08‰. δ18O data were calibrated to Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB) following NBS-19 standards59.

Neodymium and strontium isotopes

The radiogenic isotope composition of neodymium (Nd) and strontium (Sr) were measured on the siliciclastic fraction (<63 μm) of the sediments to characterise the main source areas of terrigenous material. A total of ten detrital sediment samples were analysed for Sr (87Sr/86Sr) and Nd (143Nd/144Nd expressed as εNd) isotopes. Sample preparation was performed at the Laboratori d’Isòtops Radiògenics i Ambientals (LIRA, Universitat de Barcelona), an ISO 7 clean laboratory equipped with an ISO 5 workstation. Samples were first disaggregated and wet-sieved to isolate the <63 μm fraction, which was then dried at 60 °C. About 100 mg of the dried sediment sample was homogenised. Samples were then subjected to a three-step leaching procedure. First, organic matter was removed using H2O2 (10% v/v). This was followed by biogenic carbonate elimination using buffered acetic acid. Finally, authigenic Fe-Mn oxyhydroxide phases were removed, using a mixture of 0.05 M hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH-HCl), 15% acetic acid and 0.03 M EDTA at a pH of 4. After leaching, the residual detrital fraction was digested on a hotplate in a mixture of ultrapure (double-distilled) concentrated HCl (2 ml), HNO3 (2 ml) and HF (1 ml) acids60. Nd and Sr were purified in a single-step chemistry using a set of 2-ml-bed volume cartridges packed with DGA resin (50–100 µm) mounted on a vacuum box system regulated with an electro-valve (Triskem International)61. Because the samples contained high Rb levels, Sr underwent an additional purification step using 1-ml-bed columns packed with Sr-Spec resin (Triskem International), with the same electro-valve vacuum system.

Nd and Sr isotopes were measured at CCiTUB using a Plasma 3 Multi Collector Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometer (Ametek Nu Instruments). For the Nd isotope measurements, interference of 144Sm on 144Nd was corrected. Instrumental mass bias was corrected using an exponential model and a 146Nd/144Nd ratio of 0.7219. The results were normalised to the JNdi-1 reference standard (143Nd/144Nd = 0.512115 ± 0.000007)62. Systematic measurements of the in-house AMES II standard (143Nd/144Nd = 0.511973 ± 0.000012, 2σ, n = 173) were consistent with previously published results46,63. For 87Sr/86Sr measurements, 87Rb and 86Kr isobaric interferences were corrected following64,65. Instrumental mass bias was corrected using an exponential model66 and a 86Sr/88Sr ratio of 0.1194. The results were then normalised to a 87Sr/86Sr value of 0.710249 for the NIST SRM 987 standard reference material67. Procedural blanks for Sr and Nd were <3 ng and <0.3 ng, respectively, corresponding to <1 and <0.5% of the Sr and Nd amounts in the samples, and therefore had a negligible effect on the results. Accuracy of the results was further confirmed through measurements of the BCR-2 reference material, yielding 143Nd/144Nd of 0.51264 ± 0.00002 (2σ, n = 23) and 87Sr/86Sr ratios of 0.70502 ± 0.00004 (2σ, n = 15), both within the range of previously reported values68. The uncertainties of εNd and 87Sr/86Sr values correspond to the external reproducibility (2σ) of n repeat analyses (n > 12) of the JNdi-1 or the SRM 987 standards from each analytical session and were typically 0.3 and 0.00003, respectively.

Age model

The age model of core KC-13 is based on the integration of ten accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS radiocarbon 14C dates and the high-resolution δ18O records of the planktonic foraminifera Globigerinoides ruber and Globigerina bulloides. These records were correlated with the δ18O record of LC211, indicating that sediments from core KC-13 cover the last glacial cycle, spanning the past ~42.5 ka (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). Radiocarbon dates (14C) analysed in the Laboratory for Ion Beam Physics of ETH (Zürich, Switzerland) on monospecific planktic foraminiferal samples (Globigerinoides ruber, white variety). The samples were handpicked from the 212–355 μm fraction and rinsed in methanol. The age-depth model was constructed using BChorn software and the calibration curve IntCal 2020 after applying a constant reservoir age correction of 400 years69,70. Additionally, we identified a distinct tephra layer between 225 and 239 cm (14 cm thick) in core KC-13, which is bracketed by two AMS radiocarbon ages at 235 cm (~37.69 ka) and 260 cm (~41.54 ka). This tephra horizon closely matches the timing of the Campanian Ignimbrite eruption (marine tephra marker Y5) from Campi Flegrei, Italy, dated to 39.85 ± 0.14 ka71. The Y5 tephra has been documented in numerous sedimentary archives across Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean, up to 3000 km from the source53,72, including sediment cores from the central and northern Aegean Sea at similar stratigraphic depths to KC-138,38,73. Therefore, we interpret the tephra layer as the Y5 tephra and use the well-constrained age of the Campanian Ignimbrite eruption (39.85 ± 0.14 ka) as an additional stratigraphic tie point in our age model. Based on our robust age model, the mean sedimentation rate for KC-13 is estimated at approximately 8 cm/kyr. Sedimentation rates were higher during the Holocene (10.8 cm/kyr) and the last deglaciation (6.9 cm/kyr), while lower rates (6.5 cm/kyr) characterise the last glacial period.
