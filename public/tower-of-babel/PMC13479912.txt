Whole-body vibration-based interventions and dynamic balance in older patients with stroke: a systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis
Authors: Jiajun Lan, Zhikai Qin, Yuming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis evaluated the effects of whole-body vibration (WBV) interventions on dynamic balance among older patients after stroke and examined potential moderators and dose-response relationships.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across several databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Library, and the CNKI International Version, from inception to December 26, 2025. The inclusion criteria focused on randomized controlled trials involving patients who had experienced a stroke, with a mean age of at least 60 years at either the sample or arm level. Standardized mean differences (SMDs), adjusted using Hedges’ g correction, were computed from between-group changes from baseline to the immediate post-intervention period. A three-level random-effects model was employed, with additional sensitivity analyses, robust variance estimation, subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and exploratory dose–response modeling.ResultsTwelve randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 486 participants and reporting 26 effect sizes were included in this analysis. WBV-based interventions were associated with improved dynamic balance in older patients after stroke, with an SMD of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.27 to 1.11, P = 0.002), despite substantial heterogeneity (I² = 72.6%). The pooled effect remained directionally consistent across sensitivity analyses and small-sample-corrected robust variance estimation, although the overall certainty of the evidence was rated as low. Subgroup analyses indicated positive effects for both WBV alone and WBV combined with other rehabilitation components, with no significant differences between the two intervention modes. Additionally, exploratory dose–response modeling suggested a possible intervention range of approximately six sessions per week, each lasting 20–22 min, over 5–6 weeks, yielding a cumulative exposure of approximately 7.6–10.6 h.ConclusionLow-certainty evidence suggests that WBV interventions may improve dynamic balance in older stroke patients. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given significant heterogeneity, concerns about risk of bias, and the limited number of trials. Furthermore, subgroup analyses and dose–response findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive clinical recommendations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07783-2.Keywords: Whole-body vibration, Older stroke patients, Dynamic balance, Multilevel meta-analysis, Dose–response relationship, Rehabilitation

Introduction

Amid a profound shift in the global population structure, population aging has become one of the most serious public health challenges worldwide. Epidemiological data indicate that stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability and the second-leading cause of death globally. Its incidence, prevalence, and disability rates increase exponentially with age, and older adults constitute the primary population affected by stroke [1, 2]. Compared with younger patients, older patients face not only the direct neurological deficits caused by stroke but also the multisystem physiological decline associated with aging. This combined “disease–aging” effect significantly complicates rehabilitation. Additionally, age-related physiological changes—such as sarcopenia, osteoporosis, and declines in sensory function—further diminish compensatory capacity and functional reserve, leading to slower functional recovery and greater susceptibility to complications after a stroke among older patients [3].

Among older patients who have suffered a stroke, balance dysfunction is one of the most prevalent sequelae and poses a significant health risk. Balance control, a complex motor skill, relies on the central nervous system’s effective integration and processing of visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive information, as well as the musculoskeletal system’s precise execution of postural adjustment commands [4]. Previous research has characterized postural equilibrium as an active process essential for maintaining bodily stability during both self-initiated movements and externally triggered disturbances [5]. Consequently, dynamic balance after a stroke is typically assessed not by a single test but by a set of standardized clinical tests and postural-control scales. These assessments evaluate various dimensions, including functional balance, sensory integration, voluntary weight-shifting, trunk control, and mobility-related balance. Although these measures do not fully capture the same constructs, they encompass clinically relevant aspects of post-stroke balance control and are therefore suitable outcomes for this review. This approach is consistent with previous meta-analyses on stroke rehabilitation. For instance, Yang and Butler [6] conceptualized body balance post-stroke as a construct evaluated using the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), the Mini-Balance Evaluation Systems Test, and the Sensory Organization Test (SOT) in their meta-analysis of controlled whole-body vibration training. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis on balance training in stroke patients regarded the BBS and the Trunk Impairment Scale (TIS) as balance-related outcomes, reporting distinct pooled effects for these measures [7]. These studies suggest that although balance-related scales assess various components of postural control, they have been utilized within the same evidence synthesis framework when addressing research questions related to balance recovery after stroke. Among older adults with a high prevalence of osteoporosis, falls often result in catastrophic consequences, such as hip fractures. These injuries not only lead to rapid deterioration in physical function but, in severe cases, can also pose a direct threat to life [8]. The loss of balance control not only limits patients’ mobility and activities of daily living but also directly increases the risk of falls. Studies indicate that the incidence of falls can range from 50% to 73% within one year after a stroke onset [9, 10]. The fear following a fall further compels older patients to voluntarily restrict their daily activities, thereby exacerbating disuse syndrome and creating a vicious cycle of “fall–fear–reduced activity–functional decline–recurrent falls” [11]. These findings indicate that balance dysfunction has become a significant challenge in the rehabilitation of stroke patients. Therefore, improving balance function and reducing fall risk are not only core goals of rehabilitation for older stroke patients but also essential prerequisites for their reintegration into family and social life.

Currently, rehabilitation treatments for balance disorders primarily include traditional neurodevelopmental therapies, such as Bobath therapy, task-oriented training, body-weight-supported gait training, and core stability training. While these approaches have alleviated symptoms in patients to some extent, their use among older stroke patients is often limited [12–14]. WBV, a passive neuromuscular stimulation technique, has been widely used to improve postural control, muscle strength, and work capacity among individuals with various health conditions [15]. WBV imposes relatively low cardiopulmonary demands, is easy to operate, and provides strong sensory input; thus, it is particularly suitable for the rehabilitation of older, frail stroke patients with limited exercise capacity. The underlying mechanism primarily involves facilitation of muscle spindle Ia afferent fibers through high-frequency stretch stimulation. Subsequently, Ia fibers transmit high-frequency impulses to the anterior horn of the spinal cord, monosynaptically activating α-motor neurons and inducing sustained reflexive contractions in the stimulated muscles. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have indicated that WBV may improve spasticity, motor function, balance, and certain fall-related risk factors in stroke patients; however, the evidence regarding its effects on activities of daily living and quality of life remains inconsistent [6, 16]. Meta-analyses published over the past five years suggest that WBV improves balance among stroke patients [17]. However, existing studies have largely focused on assessing whether WBV is ‘effective’ or ‘ineffective’ and have lacked quantitative dose-response analyses of critical physical parameters such as vibration frequency, amplitude, and intervention mode. Regarding the optimal intervention dosage, including the most suitable session duration and treatment course length, no unified clinical guidelines or expert consensus have yet been established. Concurrently, several new randomized controlled trials have been published in recent years, but their findings have not yet been systematically integrated. Thus, this study conducts a systematic meta-analysis of the available randomized controlled trials to address this knowledge gap. The study not only evaluates the overall effect of WBV on balance in older stroke patients but also examines potential moderators of the intervention effect and investigates potential dose–response patterns.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18]. It presents a systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis of RCTs. The review record was retrospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261366562 on April 11, 2026. The formal literature search and screening were completed before the PROSPERO registration; therefore, the registration was not prospective. This timing is recognized as a limitation of the protocol registration process. At the time of registration, data extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, and quantitative data synthesis had not yet been completed.

Study inclusion criteria

The literature included in this review was screened using specific criteria. First, the study design had to be an RCT, and the full text had to be available in either Chinese or English. Second, eligible studies included patients who had experienced a stroke, with a sample mean age of 60 years or older, or with a mean age of 60 years or older reported separately for each trial arm. Because individual participant data were unavailable in most included trials, eligibility was determined at the study level rather than requiring confirmation that every participant was 60 years or older. Studies that included some participants younger than 60 years but reported a mean age of 60 years or older were retained, and this was taken into account when interpreting the applicability of the findings to older stroke populations. This review focused on the impact of structured whole-body vibration training on balance ability, while the control group could receive standard care, pharmacological intervention, or no intervention. To address potential clinical heterogeneity in intervention composition, WBV-based interventions were classified as either WBV alone or WBV combined with other active rehabilitation components (WBV+). WBV alone refers to trials in which whole-body vibration training was the primary intervention, whereas WBV+ denotes trials that incorporated WBV alongside another active rehabilitation component. This classification enabled subgroup analysis to assess how the composition of the intervention affected the pooled effect. Outcome measures were required to be quantified using recognized standardized balance or postural-control tests and scales. Eligible outcomes included measures assessing functional balance, sensory integration, voluntary weight-shifting, trunk control, and mobility-related balance. These measures capture related yet distinct aspects of post-stroke balance control; therefore, all outcomes were standardized using SMDs. To minimize the risk of inappropriate construct pooling, the scale type was further analyzed in subgroup analysis and meta-regression. Additionally, the scale type was examined as a potential source of heterogeneity. Excluded from the review were non-clinical studies, non-original research, unpublished gray literature, and secondary literature, such as review articles. Additionally, single-session intervention studies with loosely designed protocols were excluded. To ensure a comprehensive literature search, although review articles were excluded from the analysis, their reference lists were manually screened to minimize the risk of omitting relevant studies.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted through December 26, 2025, in PubMed, Web of Science, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Library, and CNKI International Version to comprehensively identify RCTs assessing the effects of WBV on balance function in patients with stroke. The search strategy was developed in line with the PICOS framework. The population (P) comprised patients who had experienced a stroke. The intervention (I) was structured, multi-session WBV training. The comparison (C) included usual care, sham intervention, active rehabilitation control, pharmacological intervention, or no intervention. The outcomes (O) focused on changes in balance ability or postural control, assessed with standardized scales. The study design (S) was limited to RCTs. Search terms integrated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) with free-text terms, such as: (“Whole Body Vibration” OR WBV OR “Vibration Training”) AND (“Stroke” OR “Cerebrovascular Accident” OR “Hemiplegia”) AND (“Balance” OR “Postural Control” OR “Equilibrium”) AND (RCT OR “Randomized Controlled Trial”). The complete Boolean search strings, field tags, date limits, language restrictions, and database-specific filters are provided in the Supplementary Document.

Study selection process

This study strictly adhered to the PRISMA statement throughout the literature screening process. Initially, all retrieved records were imported into Zotero 7.0 for automated duplicate detection and removal. The screening process was conducted in two independent stages. In the first stage, two researchers independently reviewed titles and abstracts under blinded conditions and excluded studies that were clearly irrelevant. In the second stage, the full texts of studies that passed the initial screening were retrieved and assessed using the PICOS criteria. In cases of disagreement at any stage, a third senior researcher was invited to resolve the issue through discussion and arbitration, thereby minimizing selection bias. During data extraction, a predesigned standardized form was used, and two researchers independently extracted key information, including sample characteristics and intervention details, in a back-to-back manner, followed by cross-checking. Any discrepancies identified during this process were resolved through discussion among the three reviewers, who determined the final results.

Data synthesis

All analyses in this meta-analysis were conducted in R 4.3.3, using the primary packages meta, metafor, and ggplot2. Because the included studies used varying scales to assess balance-related outcomes, the SMD was consistently used as the effect size in the primary analysis, with Hedges’ g applied to correct for small-sample bias [19]. When the standard deviation of the change score was not reported, it was calculated from the baseline and post-intervention standard deviations using the Cochrane-recommended formula, which includes an estimated within-participant correlation coefficient. Additionally, when possible, missing standard deviations were estimated from standard errors, confidence intervals, t-values, or p-values. If the necessary variance information could not be obtained or reasonably estimated, the corresponding effect size was excluded from the quantitative synthesis. In studies reporting multiple eligible balance-related outcomes at the same assessment point, each outcome was extracted as a separate effect size, and the dependency among these effects was addressed using a three-level random-effects model. However, repeated measurements across different follow-up time points were not pooled in the primary model because they may introduce additional time-dependent dependence. Therefore, when multiple post-intervention or follow-up assessments were reported, only the immediate post-intervention assessment was included in the primary analysis. If necessary, missing standard deviations were calculated from standard errors, confidence intervals, t-values, or p-values following the Cochrane Handbook [20]. When only numerical data were available in figures, values were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer [21]. To address potential dependencies among multiple effect sizes from the same study, a three-level random-effects model was adopted and estimated with the `rma.mv` function. The three levels of the model represent sampling, within-study, and between-study variances [22]. To further assess the robustness of the results under small-sample conditions and with correlated effect sizes, robust variance estimation with a CR2 adjustment was implemented using the clubSandwich package. Statistical inference was conducted using the Satterthwaite approximation for degrees of freedom [23, 24]. In addition, publication bias was assessed using both visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression test [25]. The trim-and-fill method was also applied to potentially asymmetric data to assess sensitivity [26]. To ensure the robustness of the statistical conclusions, rigorous outlier diagnostics were conducted in this study. Outliers were defined as effect sizes with an absolute standardized residual |Z| > 2.5 or a Cook’s distance greater than three times the mean [27]. The sensitivity and heterogeneity analyses primarily encompassed three key aspects: (1) a leave-one-out analysis was conducted using the metainf algorithm to assess the impact of each study on the overall results; (2) a meta-regression model based on Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation was developed to investigate linear relationships between effect sizes and covariates, such as intervention duration and population characteristics, with bubble plots used for visualization [28]; and (3) subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity further. For regions with sparse data or small sample sizes, a Bayesian hierarchical dose–response model was introduced and fitted with restricted cubic splines. To improve the accuracy and stability of parameter estimation, weakly informative prior distributions and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were also used [29, 30].

Risk of bias (quality) assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two researchers using the most recent version of the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0, 2019) [31]. This tool assessed RCTs across five core domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from the intended interventions; (3) bias due to missing outcome data; (4) bias in outcome measurement; and (5) bias in the selection of reported results. For each domain, the risk level was evaluated using the algorithm and classified as ‘low risk,’ ‘some concerns,’ or ‘high risk.’ In cases of disagreement during the assessment, the research team first attempted to resolve the issues through discussion. If consensus could not be reached, the matter was referred to a third senior researcher for final adjudication.

Results

Study selection

The literature screening process is shown in Fig. 1. In the initial search, 172 records were identified across five target databases. Of these, 62 duplicate records were removed through a combination of software-assisted screening and manual verification. The remaining 110 records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. During this process, 78 records were excluded because they did not align with the research topic or meet the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, 32 articles advanced to the full-text review stage, and all full texts were successfully retrieved without omission. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, twenty full-text articles were excluded. The primary reasons for exclusion are summarized in Fig. 1, and a detailed list of the excluded full-text articles, including their specific reasons, is provided in the Supplementary Document. After a stepwise screening process, 12 randomized controlled trials were ultimately included in the systematic review and incorporated into the final meta-analysis [32–43].

Risk of bias of included studies

Figures 2 and 3 present a detailed summary of the risk of bias and an evidence map of the included studies. Two reviewers independently evaluated the 12 randomized controlled trials using the five domains of the RoB 2.0 tool: D1 (bias arising from the randomization process), D2 (bias due to deviations from intended interventions), D3 (bias due to missing outcome data), D4 (bias in outcome measurement), and D5 (bias in the selection of the reported result). Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudicated by a third reviewer. The inter-rater agreement results for each domain were as follows: for D1 (randomization process), the simple agreement rate was 92.3%, and both Cohen’s κ and weighted κ were 0.755, indicating “Good” agreement; for D2 (deviations from intended interventions), the simple agreement rate was 61.5%, and both Cohen’s κ and weighted κ were 0.000, indicating “Slight” agreement; for D3 (missing outcome data), the simple agreement rate was 92.3%, and both Cohen’s κ and weighted κ were 0.847, indicating “almost perfect” agreement; for D4 (outcome measurement), the simple agreement rate was 76.9%, and both Cohen’s κ and weighted κ were 0.552, indicating “Moderate” agreement; and for D5 (selection of the reported result), complete agreement was 100.0%, with both Cohen’s κ and weighted κ equal to 1.000, indicating complete agreement. Overall, the risk of bias across the 12 included studies’ RoB 2 domains was predominantly rated as “some concerns,” with no study classified as having a clearly high risk of bias. The domains related to the randomization process (D1) and deviations from intended interventions (D2) were primarily rated as “some concerns,” indicating that reporting on random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding procedures, and adherence management was inadequate in several studies. For missing outcome data (D3), ratings were relatively balanced between “low risk” and “some concerns,” suggesting that overall follow-up completeness was satisfactory; however, the management of missing data was not explicitly described in some studies. Outcome measurement (D4) was also rated primarily as “some concerns,” reflecting variability in measurement methods and assessor blinding across studies. Both the selection of reported results (D5) and the overall risk of bias were rated as “some concerns,” reflecting ongoing uncertainty regarding protocol transparency and the completeness of outcome reporting. Consequently, the pooled results, along with the subgroup and dose–response analyses, should be interpreted cautiously.

Study characteristics

As shown in Table 1, this study synthesized data from 12 RCTs [32–43]. The trials included post-stroke samples with reported mean ages ranging from 60.08 to 71.02 years, thereby meeting the age criterion for older stroke populations at both the study and arm levels. All trials compared the effects of WBV with standard care or active control interventions. Intervention protocols varied in dosage: training duration ranged from 2 to 12 weeks, and each session lasted 5 to 30 min, with high-frequency training schedules (approximately 5 sessions per week) the most prevalent. Geographically, the included studies were conducted in the Netherlands, Sweden, Spain, Belgium, South Korea, Japan, and China. Balance ability was primarily assessed using the BBS, SOT, TIS, Limits of Stability (LOS), and Community Balance and Mobility Scale (CB&M).

Meta-analysis

This study included 12 RCTs, yielding 26 effect sizes and a combined sample of 486 participants. Because outcome measurement tools varied across studies, the SMD was used to aggregate the effect sizes. The heterogeneity test revealed significant heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 72.6%, P < 0.001). In the three-level meta-analysis, variance decomposition indicated that 27.4% of the total variance was attributable to sampling variance, 0.0% to within-study variance, and 72.6% to between-study variance. This suggests that the observed heterogeneity primarily stems from differences between studies rather than from multiple effect sizes within the same study. In light of this significant heterogeneity, publication bias and influential studies were further examined in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook v6.3. To assess the potential impact of substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%), Egger’s regression test was conducted to evaluate publication bias [25]. Egger’s regression test did not provide clear evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (t = 0.646, P = 0.524; see Fig. 4). However, given the limited number of studies and the statistical dependence among several effect sizes, the test was likely underpowered. It should not be considered conclusive evidence against publication bias or small-study effects. Influential studies were identified using standardized residuals (|Z| > 2.5) and Cook’s distance (greater than three times the mean) [27]. The influence diagnostics indicated that Wang [43] study may be a potentially influential observation. This study exceeded the predefined Cook’s distance threshold and showed a relatively large standardized residual (Fig. 5). However, upon re-evaluating the extracted data, eligibility criteria, and methodological characteristics of the study, no errors in data extraction, eligibility violations, or clear methodological grounds for exclusion were identified. Consequently, to prevent post hoc selective exclusion of an eligible randomized controlled trial, this study was included in the primary effect analysis.

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated that excluding Wang [43] reduced the pooled effect estimate; however, it did not change the effect’s direction or statistical significance (SMD = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.18–0.97). In the primary analysis, which included all eligible studies, the overall effect remained statistically significant (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.24–1.14, P = 0.006). Furthermore, the trim-and-fill analysis did not indicate the need to impute any additional studies [26]. Collectively, these findings indicate that although Wang [43] may have affected the magnitude of the pooled effect estimate, the primary conclusion is not solely reliant on this single study. Detailed results of the diagnostic and sensitivity analyses are provided in the Supplementary Document.

The final pooled analysis found that whole-body vibration significantly improved dynamic balance in older stroke patients (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.27–1.11, P = 0.002; see Fig. 6). To address heterogeneity across studies and potential dependencies among multiple effect sizes within the same study, robust variance estimation with a small-sample correction (CR2) was implemented using the clubSandwich package. The results were consistent with those of the primary analysis (SMD = 0.69, SE = 0.203, t(10.80) = 3.41, P < 0.001), supporting the directional consistency of the pooled effect under robust variance estimation (see the Supplementary Document for details). According to the GRADE assessment, the current evidence has low certainty. This suggests that WBV-based interventions may improve dynamic balance in older patients after a stroke; however, confidence in the effect estimate remains limited. No clear evidence of funnel plot asymmetry was detected; however, given the small number of included studies and the statistical dependence among several effect sizes, publication bias and small-study effects cannot be ruled out. However, most included studies were rated as having ‘some concerns’ in the ROB 2 evaluation, primarily due to insufficient reporting of the randomization process, challenges in implementing blinding, and the absence of pre-specified analysis plans. The certainty of the evidence was downgraded by one level due to a risk of bias, as most of the included studies were rated as having “some concerns” in the Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) assessment. It was also downgraded by another level for inconsistency, given the substantial heterogeneity observed (I² = 72.6%). Nevertheless, after verification using multiple approaches—including Egger’s test, outlier identification and exclusion, robust variance estimation (CR2), sensitivity analysis, and the trim-and-fill method—the main pooled effect remained directionally stable across several sensitivity analyses, although the certainty of the evidence remained low and heterogeneity substantial.

Subgroup analysis

To further clarify potential sources of heterogeneity, detailed subgroup analyses were conducted based on intervention frequency, duration, session length, patient age, stroke stage, outcome measurement tools, vibration amplitude, and vibration frequency (Fig. 7).

The subgroup analyses indicated significant differences in intervention parameters across intervention frequencies (P-int < 0.001). The subgroup receiving interventions six times per week had the highest point estimate (SMD = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.28–2.02, P < 0.001). In contrast, the subgroup receiving interventions five times per week had a smaller effect size (SMD = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.26–0.83, P < 0.001), while the subgroup receiving interventions three times per week or less also showed a positive pooled estimate (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.18–0.85, P = 0.002). Furthermore, intervention durations longer than six weeks (SMD = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.21–1.76, P = 0.013) and session lengths of at least 15 min (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.31–1.02, P < 0.001) were associated with better intervention outcomes. The pooled estimates for participant characteristics indicated positive outcomes in patients aged 65 years or older (SMD = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.41–1.16, P < 0.001) and in those in the subacute phase of stroke (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.31–0.98, P < 0.001). Analysis of vibration parameters revealed positive pooled estimates when amplitudes were below 3 mm (SMD = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.49–1.44, P < 0.001) and when vibration frequencies were at least 30 Hz (SMD = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.21–0.94, P = 0.002). Regarding intervention mode, no significant difference was observed between the combined intervention (WBV+) and WBV alone (P-int = 0.937). The intervention effect of WBV alone (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.27–1.04, P < 0.001) was slightly greater than that of WBV+ (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.33–0.95, P < 0.001). Among outcome measurement tools, the BBS demonstrated greater sensitivity to detecting improvements in outcomes (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.31–1.07, P < 0.001). However, no statistically significant differences were observed across the measurement tools (P-int = 0.468). Due to the limited number of effect sizes in several subgroups and the inability to rule out potential confounding factors related to intervention parameters, the findings from these subgroups should be considered exploratory.

For continuous moderators, no statistically significant linear associations with the effect size were found. This includes motion frequency (β = 0.136, 95% CI: −0.065 to 0.337, P = 0.186), intervention time (β = 0.064, 95% CI: −0.039 to 0.167, P = 0.224), exercise time (β = −0.011, 95% CI: −0.035 to 0.013, P = 0.365), age (β = −0.005, 95% CI: −0.067 to 0.057, P = 0.882), amplitude (β = 0.025, 95% CI: −0.440 to 0.490, P = 0.916), and vibration frequency (β = −0.008, 95% CI: −0.030 to 0.013, P = 0.445).

For categorical moderators, Subacute was set as the reference category for subject type, and BBS as the reference category for scale type. The acute versus subacute contrast was statistically significant (β = 1.499, SE = 0.679, 95% CI: 0.168 to 2.830, P = 0.027). In contrast, the chronic versus subacute comparison did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.117, SE = 0.241, 95% CI: −0.590 to 0.355, P = 0.627). However, the omnibus test for subject type did not reach conventional statistical significance (QM = 5.557, df = 2, P = 0.062), and the acute category was represented by a single effect size from one study. Additionally, the overall scale type effect did not show statistical significance (QM = 4.684, df = 4, P = 0.321), and none of the contrasts comparing scale type with BBS reached statistical significance. Consequently, the findings of the meta-regression should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Nonlinear dose–response modeling

To determine an optimal WBV regimen for balance rehabilitation after stroke, this study used a nonlinear dose–response model with restricted cubic splines (see Fig. 9). The curve-fitting results indicated that the optimal intervention frequency is approximately six sessions per week, with a recommended duration of 5 to 6 weeks and each session lasting about 20 to 22 min. Age-related estimates should be interpreted cautiously, as they were derived from aggregated study-level data rather than individual participant data. From a cumulative effects perspective, the overall Bayesian dose–response analysis (Fig. 10) indicated that the observed total-dose range was 1 to 10 h, with predictions generated from 600 equally spaced values across this range. The model-derived point estimate of the optimal total intervention dose was 9.098 h, with an estimated SMD of 0.784 at that dose. In the posterior optimal-dose distribution, the posterior mean, median, and mode were 7.710, 8.332, and 9.989 h, respectively. However, the 95% credible interval for the posterior optimal dose was wide, spanning the entire observed dose range from 1.000 to 10.000 h, indicating substantial uncertainty. Overall, the exploratory recommended total-dose range was approximately 7.6 to 10.6 h. By reconciling the model-derived estimates with clinical feasibility, the findings suggest an exploratory intervention of approximately six sessions per week over 5 to 6 weeks, with each session lasting 20 to 22 min, yielding a cumulative exposure of 7.6 to 10.6 h. These values should be viewed as hypothesis-generating estimates rather than as precise or clinically validated prescriptions. Although the Bayesian model’s detailed settings, Markov chain Monte Carlo diagnostics, parameter-level diagnostic analyses, posterior predictive checks, and posterior-derived dose estimates are reported in the Supplementary Document, these findings should still be interpreted with caution. Consequently, the cumulative dose range of approximately 7.6 to 10.6 h should be considered an exploratory, hypothesis-generating finding rather than a validated clinical prescription.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that WBV may enhance dynamic balance in older stroke patients, as evidenced by a moderate-to-large pooled effect size (SMD = 0.69). This finding may be partially attributable to neuromuscular mechanisms, although these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the included trials. One possible mechanism is that mechanical vibration increases muscle spindle afferent input and spinal reflex excitability while lowering the recruitment threshold of high-threshold motor units [44]. Previous systematic reviews have shown that WBV may improve lower-limb muscle strength, functional performance, and balance in older adults. Furthermore, improvements in dynamic balance may be associated with coordinated control of the ankle, knee, and hip joints, as well as with increased output capacity of the antigravity muscle groups [45]. For frail or mobility-limited older adults, WBV requires relatively low active metabolic demand while providing repeated sensory input and postural stimulation, which may make it more feasible in rehabilitation settings [46]. However, due to the low certainty of the evidence and substantial heterogeneity across studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously in clinical practice.

The subgroup analyses further indicated that higher training frequency, longer intervention duration, longer session length, the subacute stage of stroke, older age, and vibration parameters with lower amplitude and higher frequency may all be associated with greater improvements in balance. However, the results of the linear meta-regression should be interpreted with caution. Most continuous moderators were not statistically significant. Although the acute versus subacute contrast was statistically significant, the estimate was based on a single effect size from an acute study, and the omnibus test for subject type did not reach conventional levels of significance. Additionally, conducting meta-regression with a small number of studies may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of spurious or unstable findings [47]. Moreover, because the present analyses used aggregated study-level data, the moderator effects may be influenced by ecological bias. They should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level [48]. Consequently, the findings from the meta-regression should be treated as exploratory signals rather than definitive evidence of stable, independent moderators. Furthermore, although the BBS subgroup appeared relatively sensitive in this study, the interaction test across measurement tools did not show a significant difference. This finding suggests that variations among scales likely reflect differences in ‘detection sensitivity’ rather than disparities in the ‘direction of effect’. This interpretation aligns with the current consensus that outcome measurement for post-stroke balance should be standardized as much as possible while still allowing different tools to collectively capture changes in balance and mobility [49]. The finding that a vibration amplitude of less than 3 mm is superior to larger amplitudes may be attributed to older stroke patients’ lower tolerance for mechanical loading. Compared with larger amplitudes, smaller amplitudes can provide adequate sensory input while minimizing excessive mechanical stress transmitted through the lower limbs and trunk. Consequently, smaller amplitudes are more favorable for both patient safety and adherence [50]. Moreover, the effects of vibration parameters on neuromuscular responses are inherently condition-dependent. Existing reviews indicate that these effects are jointly moderated by multiple factors, including vibration frequency, amplitude, training posture, and individual physical condition [51]. The nonsignificant difference between WBV alone and WBV+ does not provide evidence that the pooled effect varies by intervention composition. However, given the limited number of studies within each subgroup and the heterogeneity of co-interventions, the primary pooled estimate should still be interpreted as reflecting the effect of WBV-based rehabilitation rather than the isolated effect of WBV alone. Previous studies have similarly indicated that WBV does not consistently confer additional neuromotor benefits beyond those achieved through comparable exercise or sham vibration. However, it is noteworthy that WBV, when combined with task-specific gait training, may improve walking outcomes [52, 53].

At the dose level, the nonlinear model in this study showed that intervention frequency, intervention duration, session length, and age had distinct inflection points. Notably, the most favorable effects were observed with 6 sessions per week, an intervention period of approximately 5.1 weeks, a session duration of about 21.1 minutes, and a total dose of around 9.1 hours, with the recommended cumulative dose ranging from 7.6 to 10.6 hours. These findings align with subgroup results showing that ‘6 sessions per week’ and ‘at least 15 minutes per session’ were associated with improved outcomes; however, they appear to conflict with the finding that ‘an intervention period of > 6 weeks’ was more beneficial. We attribute this discrepancy to differences between the two analytical frameworks: subgroup analysis grouped categories using arbitrary cutoffs, making it more susceptible to uneven sample distributions within groups, whereas nonlinear regression modeled continuous variables, more closely reflecting the trend that intervention benefits gradually increased before plateauing. Consequently, the period around 5–6 weeks likely marks a transitional stage in which the therapeutic effect gradually accumulates and approaches a plateau, rather than contradicting the conclusion that an intervention period of > 6 weeks is more favorable. Previous randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews in older populations have also indicated that the combination of WBV training frequency and exposure duration affects functional benefits: insufficient stimulation may result. In contrast, excessively prolonged exposure does not necessarily yield additional gains in a linear manner [54–56]. Meta-analyses of very old and frail older adults have shown that WBV can improve balance, mobility, and physical function. However, in clinical practice, it is essential to balance stimulation intensity with patient tolerance [57]. Based on the findings of this study, we propose a regimen of six weekly sessions, each lasting approximately 20–22 min, over 5 to 6 weeks. This protocol should keep the total dose within 7.6 to 10.6 h, prioritizing a vibration amplitude of less than 3 mm and a frequency of at least 30 Hz. We consider this an exploratory recommended WBV protocol for older patients who have had a stroke. This regimen aligns with the optimal intervention range suggested by our study and is physiologically plausible. Specifically, it provides sufficiently frequent sensorimotor stimulation to facilitate the reconstruction of postural control while mitigating the risk of fatigue accumulation and safety hazards associated with excessive mechanical loading in older stroke patients. However, it is crucial to emphasize that this prescription should be viewed as a hypothesis-generating recommendation based on the current evidence. Further validation through larger randomized controlled trials with comprehensive reporting of parameters is needed.

Conclusion

This systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis suggest that WBV-based interventions may improve dynamic balance in older patients after stroke. However, the evidence is uncertain, and substantial heterogeneity is present. The effect appears greater in patients at the subacute stage; however, this finding requires cautious interpretation given the exploratory nature of the subgroup and meta-regression analyses, which were based on a limited number of trials. Exploratory nonlinear dose–response modeling indicates that future studies may consider protocols with approximately six sessions per week, each lasting 20 to 22 min, over 5 to 6 weeks, resulting in a cumulative exposure of approximately 7.6 to 10.6 h. Nevertheless, this regimen should be considered a hypothesis-generating estimate rather than an established clinical guideline. Further high-quality randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes, clearer reporting of WBV parameters, and longer follow-up periods are needed to validate these findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the number of included studies and the overall sample size were relatively small, which may have limited the stability of the pooled results given substantial heterogeneity. Second, most included studies were rated as having ‘some concerns’ in the risk-of-bias assessment, primarily due to insufficient reporting on randomization, blinding, intervention adherence, and pre-specified analysis plans. Third, the included trials varied in vibration parameters, intervention duration, outcome measures, and intervention composition; notably, some studies examined WBV alone, while others investigated WBV alongside additional rehabilitation components. Fourth, the age criterion was applied at the study level using the reported mean age rather than individual participant data, so that some participants may have been younger than 60 years. Fifth, potential language and publication biases cannot be ruled out, as only English- and Chinese-language articles were included, grey literature was excluded, and Egger’s test was underpowered due to the small number of studies and the dependence of effect sizes on the number of studies. The PROSPERO registration was retrospective because the corrected record was submitted on April 23, 2026, after the formal literature search and screening were completed. Consequently, this non-prospective registration timing should be regarded as a limitation of the protocol. Finally, subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and dose–response modeling were exploratory and based on aggregate study-level data; therefore, the model-derived dose range should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as a validated clinical prescription.
