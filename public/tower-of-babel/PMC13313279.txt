MicroRNA-6833-3p drives prostate cancer progression and stemness by targeting the NUMB-mediated NOTCH signaling pathway
Authors: Kai Wang, Gang Wang, Jia Shao, Changjiu Li, Sihan Wang, Hongshen Wu, Huadong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding molecular pathways in prostate cancer (PCa) is essential. This study demonstrates that miR-6833-3p plays a key role in prostate tumorigenesis via multi-omics integration and functional validation. Expression levels of miR-6833-3p, NUMB, and NOTCH1 were measured in PCa cell lines. The direct regulation of NUMB by miR-6833-3p was confirmed via dual-luciferase reporter assays with mutagenesis. Functional effects were examined using NUMB plasmids or miR-6833-3p mimics. Cell function assays and xenograft models in nude mice were employed, with tissues analyzed via HE staining, qRT-PCR, and Western blot. miR-6833-3p and NOTCH1 were upregulated in PCa, while NUMB was downregulated. miR-6833-3p mimics promoted proliferation, migration, and stemness but inhibited apoptosis and the NUMB/NOTCH1 pathway. NUMB overexpression reversed these effects. In vivo, miR-6833-3p accelerated tumor growth and suppressed NUMB/NOTCH1. miR-6833-3p promotes PCa progression and stemness by inhibiting the NUMB-NOTCH pathway, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for detection and targeted therapy.KEYWORDS: MicroRNA-6833-3p, prostate cancer, NUMB-NOTCH pathway, stemness

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common form of malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men worldwide [1]. In 2020, PCa accounted for 1.41 million new cases worldwide [2]. Advanced PCa often progresses to castration-resistant phenotype despite initial responses to androgen deprivation [3,4], leading to metastasis and poor survival outcomes [5]. The heterogeneity of PCa, driven by interactions among genetic predisposition, aging, lifestyle, and environmental factors [6], contributes to treatment resistance and therapeutic challenges. Deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying PCa initiation, progression, and castration resistance is essential for developing precision therapies to improve early detection and patient outcomes.

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) significantly contribute to PCa progression by driving disease recurrence and metastatic dissemination, primarily through their dual capacity for unlimited self-regeneration and evasion of chemotherapeutic interventions [7]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a class of evolutionarily conserved non-coding RNAs, regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by interacting predominantly with the 3’-UTR of target mRNAs, although binding sites can also occur in other regions such as the coding sequence and 5’-UTR [8]. Emerging evidence suggests that miRNAs maintain CSC stemness by modulating Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways in PCa [9,10]. For example, miR-1290 promotes PCa stemness and invasiveness by targeting RORA [11], a nuclear receptor involved in cell differentiation, metabolism, and circadian rhythm regulation. In addition, miR-6833-3p has been implicated in colon cancer metastasis through SMC4, a core subunit of the condensin complex that participates in chromosome condensation and segregation [12], its role in PCa remains unexplored.

NUMB, a conserved tumor suppressor, is frequently downregulated in PCa and correlates with aggressive phenotypes and poor prognosis [13–15]. As a negative regulator of NOTCH signaling, NUMB suppresses NOTCH-induced stemness and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in PCa [16]. NOTCH signaling, activated in 30–50% of advanced PCa cases, promotes castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) progression by maintaining CSC stemness and inducing androgen receptor activity [17,18]. Emerging evidence indicates that microRNAs, including miR-524-5p and miR-146a-5p, modulate the NUMB-NOTCH signaling pathway in various cancer types [19,20]. Although the NUMB-NOTCH pathway is a promising therapeutic target in PCa [21], the miRNA network regulating this pathway remains poorly understood. Through computational prediction in silico, NUMB was recognized as a putative binding partner of miR-6833-3p. Experimental validation via dual-luciferase reporter gene assays confirmed their direct interaction. Mechanistically, our findings reveal that miR-6833-3p drives prostate carcinogenesis and enhances tumor-initiating cell properties through suppression of the NUMB-mediated NOTCH signaling axis. Our findings provide a theoretical foundation for targeting miR-6833-3p as a diagnostic marker and therapeutic strategy for PCa.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human prostate cell models included the non-tumorigenic RWPE-1 epithelium (CRL-11609) and malignant variants PC-3 (CRL-1435), DU-145 (HTB-81), 22RV1 (CRL-2505), and LNCaP (CRL-1740), all acquired through ATCC with complete STR profiling certification and mycoplasma-free validation. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (SNM-001B, Sunncell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, AW-FBS-001, AnWei-sci, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (V900929, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to experimentation. For experiments, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured at 37°C under 5% CO2 using a Thermo Fisher Scientific incubator (51032874, Waltham, MA, USA).

Cell transfection

Lentiviral particles carrying miR-6833-3p-NC (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’) or miR-6833-3p mimics (5’-UUUCUCUCUCCACUUCCUCAG-3’) were generated in HEK293T cells. Subsequently, PC-3 and DU-145 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were plated in 6-well plates and incubated with 50 μL lentiviral supernatant per well in RPMI-1640 medium. After 48 hours, cells were lentivirally transduced with either scramble sequence (NC) or miR-6833-3p mimic lentiviruses and selected with 5 μg/mL puromycin. For transient transfection, NUMB overexpression plasmids (RIBOBIO Biotech Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) were introduced into cells (1 × 104 cells/well, 70–90% confluency) employing Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001) from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd.

Dual luciferase reporter gene assay

Potential miR-6833-3p binding sites in the 3’-UTR of NUMB were predicted using JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/). The 3’-UTR sequences of NUMB in wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) forms (UCUCUU→UCUCAA) were subcloned into pmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter plasmids (Promega). PC-3 and DU-145, cultured in 24-well plates until reaching 70% confluence, were subsequently transfected with either WT or MUT constructs combined with miR-6833-3p-NC control or mimics, using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent. Following 48 h post-transfection, sequential luminescence measurements were performed with firefly luciferase substrate (20 μL, 10s integration) followed by Renilla substrate (20 μL, 2s delay), using detector gain optimization (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with background subtraction (PBS controls).

Methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) assay

PC-3 and DU-145 cells, with cell density of 2 × 104 cells/well in 96-well format, were transfected and incubated for 24–72 h. MTT reagent (10 μL/well; M1020, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added and incubated for 4 hours at 37°C. Formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL solubilization buffer. Cell proliferation dynamics were spectrophotometrically quantified at λ=490 nm (reference λ=630 nm) using a Modular Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SkanIt 5.0 software) in endpoint mode, with pathlength correction applied for 200 μl reaction volumes.

Colony formation assay

Clonogenic survival analysis was performed under low-density seeding conditions (500 cells/well in Corning® 6-well TC-treated plates), maintaining strict media replenishment protocols (every 72 h with 37°C pre-warmed RPMI-1640) until macroscopic colony emergence (Day 14 post-seeding). Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (abs9179, Absin, Shanghai, China) for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by two washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Fixed colonies were then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (C0121, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 minutes at room temperature. Excess stain was removed by thorough rinsing with distilled water, and plates were air-dried overnight. Colonies containing > 50 cells were counted manually under an optical microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ts2, Tokyo, Japan), and images were captured using a digital camera (Nikon DS-Fi3).

Cell migration assay

Cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were stratified into serum-free medium in 8 μm-pore transwell inserts (CLS3422, Corning, NY, USA), while the lower chambers contained 600 μL medium with 10–20% FBS. After 24 hours of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 minutes and stained with 0.1% Giemsa solution (abs47047618, Absin) for 10 minutes. Stained cells were imaged in five random fields per insert using an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti-S, 10× objective), and cell numbers were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.52s, NIH, USA).

Cell apoptosis assay

Transfected cells were resuspended in PBS and stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (5 μL each; Solarbio). Apoptotic cells were analyzed using a Beckman DxFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Western blotting

Protein extraction was performed with RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (P1261, Solarbio). Following thermal denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, protein quantification was conducted using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) detection system (MBC, MBP0041E) according to manufacturer specifications. Protein lysates (20 μg normalized by BCA) were resolved on discontinuous gradient gels (NuPAGE™ 4–12% Bis-Tris, MES buffer pH 6.4) using XCell SureLock™ Mini-Cell at 20 0 V for 45 min, followed by semi-dry transfer (25 V, 7 min) to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Immobilon® FL) pre-activated with methanol. Post-blocking (5% nonfat milk +0.1% Tween-20 in TBS, 2 h RT), membranes were probed with primary antibodies (validated by knockout lysate controls) in antibody incubation chambers (22–25°C, 8 h; 4°C, 16 h) with humidity control (1:1000 dilution): NUMB (ab220362), NOTCH (ab52627), OCT4 (ab181557), SOX2 (ab92494), NANOG (ab109250), and β-actin (ab8226; all from Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Post-primary antibody incubation, membranes were exposed to secondary antibodies (1:10,000; ab6721 and ab205719, Abcam) for 1 hour. Signals were detected using BeyoECL Plus (P0018S, Beyotime) and imaged on a Tanon 5200 system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (v1.52s, NIH, USA) with β-actin normalization.

Animals

Male BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–25 g; Shanghai Bicai Kewei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 PC-3 cells (control, miR-6833-3p-NC, or miR-6833-3p-mimics; n = 6/group). Tumor volume was measured every 3 days (V = 0.5 × length × width2). After 30 days, tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for histology. All animal experimentation were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Affiliated Hangzhou First People’s Hospital. The approved protocol specified a maximum allowable tumor volume (burden) of 2,000 mm3 as the humane endpoint. No animals in this study exceeded this predefined tumor size/burden limit. The procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E)

Tissue specimens underwent immersion fixation (4% PFA in 0.1 M PBS, pH 7.4, 24 h at 4°C) followed by automated tissue processing (Leica ASP6025): graded ethanol dehydration (70%-100%), xylene clearing, and paraffin impregnation (Paraplast Plus, 56–58°C) with vacuum infiltration. Microtome sections (4 μm, RM2255 Rotary) were mounted on charged slides (Superfrost Plus) for subsequent staining by HE staining kit (G1120, Solarbio).

Immunohistochemical staining

For immunohistochemical staining, primary antibody incubation was carried out using Ki-67 antibody (SIHCZ-0001, Solarbio) at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (31460, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 hour at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB substrate kit (B1072, Applygen, Beijing, China), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. All images were acquired with an upright light microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100e).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was purified through TRIzol™ Plus-chloroform phase separation (4°C centrifugation 12,000 g × 15 min), followed by isopropanol precipitation (−20°C×2 h) and 75% ethanol washes. RNA integrity was validated via 2100 Bioanalyzer RNA Nano Chip (RIN ≥ 8.0). Reverse transcription reactions (20 μl) contained 1 μg RNA, 5× VILO Reaction Mix (Biotechrabbit BR0400403), and 10 U RNaseOUT™ (Thermo 10,777,019), incubated in Veriti 96-Well Thermal Cycler with thermal profile: 25°C×10 min (primer annealing) → 50°C×120 min (cDNA synthesis) → 85°C×5 min (enzyme inactivation). qRT-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 7 Pro system (A43165, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Fast SYBR™ Green Master Mix (4385612, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Thermal cycling parameters were set as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 20 seconds, followed by 40 amplification cycles consisting of 3-second denaturation at 95°C and 30-second annealing/extension at 60°C. Gene expression levels were quantitatively analyzed using the comparative threshold cycle (2^-ΔΔCt) approach with normalization to endogenous controls GAPDH and U6. The primer sequences, GenBank accession numbers, amplicon sizes, and annealing temperatures are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data from three independent experiments are expressed as mean ± SD. First, the assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Second, the homogeneity of variances (homoscedasticity) was evaluated using Brown-Forsythe test. For comparisons between two groups, if data passed both normality and homoscedasticity checks, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. If the data violated the assumption of normality, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, if data passed both assumptions, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Dysregulation of miR-6833-3p/NUMB/NOTCH1 pathway in PCa cells

We analyzed the expression profile of the miR-6833-3p/NUMB/NOTCH1 pathway in multiple PCa cell lines. Compared with the RWPE-1 (normal prostate epithelial cell line), PC-3, DU-145, 22RV1, and LNCaP (PCa cell lines) exhibited significantly elevated expressions of miR-6833-3p and NOTCH1, accompanied by reduced NUMB expression (p < 0.01) (Figure 1(A–C)). This expression pattern aligns with the known antagonistic relationship between NUMB and NOTCH1 reported previously [21].

Expression patterns of miR-6833-3p, NUMB, and NOTCH1 in prostate cancer cell lines. (A) miR-6833-3p expression levels in normal prostate epithelial cells (RWPE-1) and prostate cancer (PCa) cell lines (PC-3, DU-145, 22RV1, LNCaP) were analyzed by qRT-PCR using U6 for normalization. (B-C) mRNA expression levels of NUMB and NOTCH1 were measured by qRT-PCR with GAPDH as internal control. (D-E) Transfection efficiency verification through miR-6833-3p expression analysis in PC-3 and DU-145 cells following transfection with miR-6833-3p-mimics or negative control (NC), normalized to U6. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). **p < 0.01.

Pro-oncogenic effects of miR-6833-3p mimics in PCa cells

To investigate the functional role of miR-6833-3p in PCa, we selected PC-3 and DU-145 cells as representative prostate cancer cell models with elevated endogenous miR-6833-3p expression compared with RWPE-1 cells. These cells were transfected with miR-6833-3p mimics, and transfection efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR (p < 0.01) (Figure 1(D,E)). Cell viability assays conducted at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-transfection revealed time-dependent enhancement of viability in both cell lines (Figure 2(A,B)). Compared with the miR-6833-3p-NC group, mimic-treated cells showed statistically significant increases in viability (p < 0.01). Functional assessments demonstrated that miR-6833-3p mimics substantially enhanced colony formation and migratory capacities in PC-3 (p < 0.01) (Figure 2(C,D)) and DU-145 cells (p < 0.01) (Figure 2(E,F)). Conversely, flow cytometry analysis revealed marked suppression of apoptosis in mimic-transfected cells compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Figure 2(G,H)). These findings collectively establish miR-6833-3p’s role in promoting proliferation/migration while inhibiting apoptosis in PCa cells.

Functional consequences of miR-6833-3p overexpression in PCa cells. PC-3 and DU-145 cells transfected with miR-6833-3p-mimics or NC were subjected to: (A-B) cell viability assessment by MTT assay. (C, E) Colony formation analysis with crystal violet staining. (D, F) Migration capability evaluation using transwell chambers. (G-H) apoptosis quantification via flow cytometry. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). **p < 0.01. For panels A-B, statistical comparisons were performed at each time point, and the differences between the miR-6833-3p-mimics and miR-6833-3p-NC groups were significant at 48 h and 72 h.

Regulation of NUMB/NOTCH1 pathway and stemness markers by miR-6833-3p

qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that miR-6833-3p mimics significantly downregulated NUMB mRNA expression while upregulating NOTCH1 in both cell lines (p < 0.01) (Figure 3(A–G)). Concomitant increases in stemness biomarkers (OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG) were observed at mRNA level in PC-3 and DU-145 cells (p < 0.01) (Figure 3(C–J)). Western blot analysis confirmed these trends at protein level, showing coordinated downregulation of NUMB with concurrent upregulation of NOTCH1 and stemness markers (p < 0.05) (Figure 4).

GAPDH served as endogenous control. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). **p < 0.01.

Protein-level validation of NUMB/NOTCH1 pathway modulation and stemness marker regulation. Western blot analysis of (A-F) PC-3 and (G-L) DU-145 cells transfected with miR-6833-3p-mimics or NC: (A, G) representative blots; (B-C, H-I) NUMB and NOTCH1 expression; (D-F, J-L) stemness-related proteins (OCT4, SOX2, NANOG). Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

NUMB as direct target mediating miR-6833-3p effects

Computational prediction utilizing the JASPAR platform revealed a potential miR-6833-3p recognition motif within the 3’-untranslated region of the NUMB transcript (Figure 5(A)). Dual-luciferase assays validated this interaction, showing significant reduction in luciferase activity when mimics were co-transfected with wild-type NUMB plasmid (Figure 5(B,C)). Rescue experiments revealed that NUMB overexpression completely abrogated the pro-proliferative (at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h) and pro-migratory effects of miR-6833-3p mimics (p < 0.01) (Figure 5(D–I)), while restoring apoptotic capacity (p < 0.05) (Figure 5(J,K)).

NUMB overexpression rescues miR-6833-3p-mediated phenotypic effects. (A) Predicted miR-6833-3p binding site in NUMB 3’UTR (JASPAR database). (B-C) Luciferase reporter assay validation using WT/MUT NUMB constructs. (D-K) functional assays following co-transfection with NUMB overexpression plasmid and miR-6833-3p-mimics: (D-E) MTT viability; (F, H) colony formation; (G, I) transwell migration; (J-K) apoptosis analysis. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs respective controls. For panels D-E, statistical comparisons were performed at each time point, and significant differences were observed at 48 h and 72 h between the indicated groups.

NUMB overexpression reverses pathway alterations

qRT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed that NUMB overexpression effectively neutralized miR-6833-3p-mediated suppression of NUMB and subsequent NOTCH1/stemness marker upregulation in both cell lines (p < 0.05) (Figure 6 and 7). These results mechanistically link miR-6833-3p’s oncogenic effects to NUMB targeting and NOTCH1/stemness pathway activation.

Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs respective controls.

In vivo validation of miR-6833-3p effects

Xenograft experiments demonstrated accelerated tumor growth in mice injected with miR-6833-3p mimic-transfected PC-3 cells, evidenced by significantly increased tumor volume/weight compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Figure 8(A,B)). Consistent with in vitro findings, tumors from mimic-treated group showed reduced NUMB and elevated NOTCH1 expression (p < 0.01) (Figure 8(C,D)). Histopathological examination of H&E-stained xenograft sections showed increased tumor cell density in the PC-3+miR-6833-3p-mimics group compared with the control groups (Figure 8(E)). In addition, Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining showed that the Ki-67-positive rate was significantly higher in the PC-3+miR-6833-3p-mimics group than in the PC-3+miR-6833-3p-NC group (p < 0.001), further supporting enhanced proliferative activity in vivo (Figure 8(F)).

(C-D) NUMB and NOTCH1 mRNA levels in excised tumors. (E) Representative H&E staining images of xenograft tumor sections. (F) Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining and quantitative analysis of Ki-67 positivity in excised tumors. Data are presented as the mean ± SD (n = 3 independent replicates). **p < 0.01 vs control group.

Discussion

PCa is prone to treatment resistance and poor prognoses including metastasis and recurrence, all of which are closely associated with tumor stemness. Targeting PCa stem cells has emerged as a promising strategy for managing refractory PCa [22,23]. Our findings demonstrate that miR-6833-3p is significantly upregulated in PCa, while its target gene NUMB shows reduced expression. This regulatory relationship leads to Notch pathway activation, ultimately promoting PCa progression and enhancing tumor stemness. We have identified a novel oncogenic axis mediated by miR-6833-3p, suggesting that targeted inhibition of this microRNA could represent a potential therapeutic strategy for PCa through stemness reduction.

The critical role of microRNAs in cancer pathogenesis has gained increasing recognition in recent years. These small non-coding RNAs regulate various physiological and pathological processes through post-transcriptional gene silencing, exhibiting dual roles in cancer progression – either suppressing tumorigenesis by inhibiting oncogenes or promoting malignancy through activation of cancer-associated pathways [24]. In PCa stemness regulation, Prior research has demonstrated that elevated expression of miR-628 effectively restricts multiple oncogenic behaviors in cellular systems, including proliferative activity, motile capacity, invasive potential, and three-dimensional spheroid development by targeting Jagged-1, thereby reducing cellular stemness [25]. Similarly, Michelle et al. reported that microRNA-1205 acts as a tumor suppressor in PCa through FRYL inhibition [26]. While prior research in colorectal cancer identified miR-6833-3p as a tumor suppressor through LINC-RoR-mediated regulation, our study paradoxically reveals its oncogenic role in PCa. We observed significant miR-6833-3p upregulation in PCa cells and tissues, with functional experiments demonstrating its capacity to enhance cancer cell proliferation, migration, stemness, and in vivo tumor growth. This cancer-promoting effect aligns with the reported role of microRNA-1290 in PCa progression [11], though our study specifically establishes miR-6833-3p’s involvement in stemness regulation.

Through integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we confirmed NUMB as a novel target by miR-6833-3p. Importantly, NUMB has been extensively validated as a therapeutic target in PCa, with multiple microRNAs reported to promote malignancy through its suppression. A study by Wang and colleagues revealed that the miR-543/NUMB signaling pathway promotes proliferative activity, metastatic potential, and stem-like properties in prostate cancer cells [27], while miR-9-5p enhances CD44+ PCa stem cell proliferation and metastasis via NUMB inhibition [28]. Our rescue experiments showed that NUMB overexpression reverses the miR-6833-3p-mediated enhancement of PCa cell proliferation, migration, and stemness, confirming that miR-6833-3p exerts its oncogenic effects primarily through NUMB suppression.

Notably, NUMB exhibits context-dependent tumor suppressive functions, with its Notch pathway antagonism being particularly relevant to PCa pathogenesis. The Notch pathway’s critical role in prostate tumorigenesis has gained increasing attention, particularly regarding its regulation of cell fate determination and stem cell maintenance [17,29]. Notch activation has been associated with anti-androgen therapy resistance and cytotoxic drug insensitivity [30], with elevated pathway activity observed in PCa patients with higher BMI [31]. Our findings demonstrate that miR-6833-3p-mediated NUMB suppression leads to Notch pathway activation, creating a permissive environment for tumor progression and stemness enhancement. We acknowledge the potential complexity of miR-6833-3p/NUMB/Notch interactions, as Notch1 activation may exert feedback regulation on NUMB expression. Furthermore, NUMB’s multifaceted regulatory roles extend beyond Notch signaling, suggesting additional downstream effectors may contribute to the observed phenotypes.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the mechanistic insight is primarily focused on NUMB as the direct and key downstream target. While our rescue experiments strongly support its central role, microRNAs typically regulate a network of genes. The potential contribution of other unidentified targets of miR-6833-3p to the observed phenotypes cannot be entirely ruled out. This potential bias toward a single-gene mechanism might lead to an overestimation of NUMB’s exclusive contribution to the overall oncogenic effect. Future studies employing transcriptome-wide analyses (e.g. RNA-seq following miR-6833-3p perturbation) would be necessary to paint a more complete picture of its regulatory network. Second, the in vivo validation relied on a subcutaneous xenograft model using an established cell line (PC-3). Although this model is valuable for assessing tumor growth, it lacks the authentic tumor microenvironment and the full spectrum of human PCa progression, particularly the transition to castration resistance. This limitation may affect the magnitude of the observed effect and its direct translational relevance to human disease. The use of patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models or genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) in future work would provide a more physiologically relevant context to confirm these findings. Third, our study utilized a panel of PCa cell lines but did not include primary human PCa tissues to correlate miR-6833-3p levels with clinical parameters such as Gleason score, metastasis, and survival. The absence of this clinical correlation means that the prognostic value of miR-6833-3p remains speculative. A potential direction of bias here is that the functional prominence we observed in cell lines might not fully translate to clinical aggressiveness. The spatial distribution and tissue-level co-localization of miR-6833-3p, NUMB, and NOTCH1 in human prostate cancer remain to be further validated. Future studies should incorporate well-annotated human PCa specimens and combine in situ hybridization with immunohistochemical co-localization after appropriate ethical approval and informed consent to more directly establish the anatomical and cellular context of this regulatory axis. Fourth, although Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining was added to evaluate proliferative activity in xenograft tumors, we did not perform a standardized quantitative assessment of mitotic figures per high-power field. This was mainly due to resource limitations and the lack of a prespecified mitotic-counting protocol in the original histopathological analysis. Therefore, the histological findings should be interpreted descriptively, and future studies should incorporate blinded pathological evaluation of mitotic figures across multiple high-power fields to more rigorously characterize tumor proliferative activity. Despite these limitations, our data provide a solid foundation for the functional role of miR-6833-3p and firmly establish the NUMB/NOTCH pathway as a critical mediator of its effects in PCa models. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the comprehensive regulatory network downstream of miR-6833-3p/NUMB and characterizing the dynamic interplay within the NUMB-Notch feedback loop in PCa progression.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identifies miR-6833-3p as a novel regulator of PCa progression and stemness through NUMB-mediated Notch pathway activation. These findings provide the first evidence of miR-6833-3p’s role in PCa pathogenesis and stemness regulation. This underscores its significance not only as a potential diagnostic biomarker but also as a promising therapeutic target.
