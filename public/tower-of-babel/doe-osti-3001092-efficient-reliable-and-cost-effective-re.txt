 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This report summarizes the work performed by University of California San Diego (UCSD) – OxEon
Energy LLC (OxEon) for the U. S. Department of Energy/National Energy Technology Laboratory
(DOE/NETL) under Cooperative Agreement DE-FE0031940 entitled “Efficient, Reliable and Cost-
Effective Reversible Solid Oxide Cell Technology for Hydrogen and Electricity Production”. This
reversible solid oxide cell (RSOC) technology has two main novel elements: (i) a compact and low-cost
stack architecture that consists of multi-cell cell modules in electrical parallel and series connections and
(ii) high performance and fuel-flexible reversible cells with electrodes or all components made by
sputtering deposition process for efficient operation in both fuel cell (power generation) and electrolysis
(hydrogen production) modes.

 The main objectives of the project were
 (1) to validate the design, materials and manufacturing processes of the RSOC technology for both
hydrogen and electricity production,
 (2) to demonstrate operation of the technology at relevant conditions with improved performance,
reliability, and endurance compared to the current state-of-the-art, and
 (3) to confirm the cost effectiveness of the proposed technology via a comprehensive techno-economic
analysis of a selected application.

 To support these objectives, the project work focused on the following activities:
 (a) Application selection and system design and analysis: This activity involved identification and
assessment of a specific application for small-scale distributed hydrogen production systems, comparative
assessment of the technology for hydrogen production, and system design and analysis to develop and
optimize the configuration of the RSOC system for hydrogen and electricity production to meet the
specifications of its intended application.
 (b) Techno-economic assessment: This activity focused on estimating the cost of hydrogen (CoH) for
hydrogen production from the system designed for the selected application.
 (c) RSOC cell development: This effort involved various activities to evaluate sputtering for
fabricating cell components and lanthanum nickel cobalt perovskite (LNC) based oxygen electrode,
develop a process to metallize oxide substrates for all-sputtered cells, and demonstrate high-performance
RSOC cells incorporating sputtered components especially sputtered oxygen electrodes and characterize
fabricated cells and evaluate cell performance in reversible operation.
 (d) RSOC stack development: This effort involved activities to develop cell module components,
define stack assembly specifications and evaluate performance of stacks of cell modules in reversible
operation for hydrogen and electricity production.
 (e) Stack operation demonstration: This task focuses on building a subscale stack unit incorporating
multiple cell modules to demonstrate its operation for hydrogen production from steam and also
electricity from hydrogen and natural gas.

 The project was completed with major accomplishments in the various activities highlighted below.

Application selection and system design and analysis
 o For application selection, the focus was on selecting applications for small-scale distributed
systems especially in terms of hydrogen production. Based on the assessment of requirements and needs
of fueling stations for fuel cell vehicles, the applications selected for the RSOC in this project include
hydrogen fueling stations and distributed hydrogen/power systems for small communities, especially
communities in remote areas. RSOFC systems for these applications are capable of producing 1,500 kg
H2/day and can operate on hydrogen or natural gas to generate electricity
 o A comparative assessment of the solid oxide electrolysis (RSOC in electrolysis mode) for hydrogen
production was for cases of distributed on-site hydrogen production systems (≤ 1500 kg H2/day) in terms

 5

of technological readiness, efficiency entitlement, lifetime, feedstock, hydrogen product (purity, pressure
and storage), operation flexibility and CO2 emissions (vs. polymer exchange membrane electrolysis,
alkaline electrolysis, and methane steam reforming). In terms of reliability, the solid oxide electrolysis has
not been proved with the long lifetime of stacks and systems. However, considering potential advantages
including high electrical efficiency, low materials costs, and versatile operation (fuel cell, electrolysis,
and co-electrolysis modes), the solid oxide electrolysis could become the most efficient electrolysis
technology for hydrogen production in the future with further research and development on reliability and
scale-up.
 o The design of the RSOC system for hydrogen and power generation to meet the specifications of
the selected application was developed and optimized. Stack and system efficiencies were estimated as a
function of fuel utilization at different operating temperature range between 600oC and 700oC. The stack
efficiency in both fuel cell and electrolysis cases increase with fuel/steam utilization ratio. The system
showed the highest efficiency (e.g., about 73% in electrolysis mode and about 65% in fuel cell mode at
700oC) at the utilization of 70%.

Techno-economic assessment
 o The techno-economic assessment focused on estimating the cost of hydrogen (CoH) for hydrogen
production based on the system designed for the selected application comprising distributed systems that
produce 1,500 kg hydrogen/ day. This assessment included several assumptions, for example, : the
number of the RSOC plant was assumed to be 10% of the number of the total gas stations in the U.S, i.e.,
replacing 10% of gas stations in the U.S.; the CoH was estimated for a RSOC system with balance of
plant (BOP) components for hydrogen production mode only; the RSOC technology was based on a
compact and low-cost stack architecture that incorporated high-performance reversible thin-film cells
fabricated on anodized aluminum oxide (AAO) substrate in-plant by sputtering.
 o Based on the assumptions, the three key cost elements in the CoH (capital cost, feedstock cost, and
O&M (Operation & Maintenance) cost) were estimated. The stack cost, the main capital cost calculated in
this assessment, was estimated to be $188.09/kW. The stack cost analysis shows that the cell material cost
accounted for about 80% of the stack capital cost with the AAO cost being the major contributor (90% of
the total material cost).
 o The total CoH was calculated using the DOE H2A analysis tool to be $1.95/kg H2. Among the
items contributing to the CoH, the feedstock and electricity cost contributes 57% of the hydrogen cost.
Improvements in stack performance and/or electricity consumption of the BOPs can reduce
feedstock/electricity cost, thus CoH.

RSOC cell development
 o This RSOC cell effort focused on the development of hydrogen electrode (HE) supported cells of
the configuration consisting of conventional nickel (Ni)-yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) hydrogen
electrode/YSZ electrolyte half cell with sputtered lanthanum nickel cobalt perovskite (LNC)-gadolinium
doped ceria (GDC) oxygen electrode and all sputtered thin-film RSOC cells made by sputtering on AAO
substrates. The HE supported cell was down-selected for cell module and stack development.
 o The baseline specifications of the sputtering process for fabricating all cell components had been
established. The activities in this project focused on defining, optimizing and scaling up the process for
the LNC-GDC oxygen electrode and GDC interlayer. Process parameters such as target size, power,
pressure and target-to-substrate distance were investigated, defined and optimized for these components.
Scaleup of sputtering to deposit LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes with consistent thickness on electrolyte and
half cell substrates of up to 15cmx15cm size LNC-GDC electrodes was demonstrated.
 o Sputtered LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes showed excellent electrochemical performance.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) studies of symmetrical cells and complete cells
incorporating LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes with and without GDC interlayers were conducted at 600oC-
750oC. The results indicate that at each temperature, the polarization resistance was smaller for cells

 6

without the GDC interlayer as compared to the case with the GDC interlayer and confirm that the GDC
interlayer is not needed for LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes.
 o For HE supported cells, a process based on tape casting and screen printing was developed to
fabricate half cells (consisting of Ni-YSZ support, Ni-YSZ active layer and YSZ electrolyte). A number
of modifications were applied to the process to address identified issues such as microstructure
uniformity, electrolyte defect. HE supported half cells were fabricated in 2cmx2cm, 5cmx5cm,
10cmx10cm, and 15cmx15cm sizes.
 o HE supported RSOC cells with sputtered LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes demonstrated significant
performance improvements over the state-of-the-art at reduced temperatures (<800oC), showing, e.g.,
0.79W/cm2 in fuel cell mode with 97%H2-3%H2O at 650oC and about 0.68A/cm2 at 1.3 V in electrolysis
mode with 50%H2-50%H2O at 650oC. Cell performance mapping with HE-supported cells was done with
different operating temperatures and different steam ratio in the fuel gas. Higher operating temperature
gave higher current density at each operating mode (fuel cell and electrolysis). The cell in electrolysis
mode showed current density increase as the steam content went up. HE supported cells also showed low
degradation rates; in one test, the degradation rate of the cell was estimated to be about 0.37%/kh.
 o For all sputtered thin-film RSOC cells, a four-step electroless nickel plating process was carried out
to metallize the AAO substrate. Several variations to the process were attempted to improve coating depth
within the high aspect-ratio pores and address certain issues such as residual salt left on the surface. The
homogeneity and conformality of the coating both on the surface and in the pores of the AAO were
achieved. With some modifications of sputtering parameters, thin-film RSOC cells were successfully
deposited on the Ni-plated AAO and demonstrated excellent performance, showing, e.g., open circuit
voltage (OCV) of > 1.0V, electrolysis current densities at 1.3 V of 810 mA/cm2 with 90% H2-10%H2O at
600oC, fuel cell power density of 700mW/cm2 with 97%H2O-3%H2O at 600oC.
 o Sputtered thin-film RSOC cells with LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes exhibited superior performance
at reduced temperatures. An extraordinarily high peak power density of about 4W/cm2 in fuel cell mode
with hydrogen fuel and a current density of about 3A/cm2 at 1.3V in electrolysis were obtained at 700oC
with 50%H2 – 50%H2O. Cell performance maps for steam electrolysis mode and fuel cell mode was done
for AAO-supported thin-film RSOC cells with different temperatures (550, 600, 650, 700°C) and
different steam content (3%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%). SOFC operation of the AAO-supported thin film
cells was also carried out with 3% wet methane at three different temperatures. At 600°C, the peak power
density reached 0.82 W/cm2, approximately 43.4% of the peak power density at 600°C with 50%H2 –
50%H2O fuel. At 550oC and 650°C, 0.72 and 1.16W/cm2 power densities were achieved.

RSOC stack development
 o The specification of the cell module design was developed. The design was analyzed for
mechanical stress (which indicated mechanical safety factor of greater than 1.0 for the module), flow
distribution (which indicated uniform fuel flow distribution to allow ) and temperature distribution (which
indicated maximum thermally-induced stress of the module smaller than the fracture strength of the
electrolyte to prevent cell cracking)
 o A concept for gas manifolding was developed for cell modules and stacks of cell modules. The
concept can be designed for cross-flow, co-flow or counter-flow configuration.
 o A sealant composition consisting of ceramic (zirconia or magnesium oxide) paste and glass was
developed for cell sealing.
 o A number of cell modules and stacks of cell modules were tested to demonstrate performance and
operational characteristics. These modules/stacks contained from one to sixteen cells with cell sizes
ranging from 2.5cmx2.5cm to 15cmx15cm. These tests demonstrated operation in both fuel cell and
electrolysis mode including reversibility and cyclic operation at different temperatures (650oC-750oC) and
gas compositions (3% to 75% H2O). Several tests were also conducted in fuel cell mode with methane.
Minimal cell-to cell variations in OCVs, peak power densities (fuel cell mode) and current densities at
1.3V (electrolysis mode) were observed. Electrical parallel and series connections of cells and cell
modules were also demonstrated in these tests.

 7

 o Durability of one cell module was evaluated. The evaluation was conducted for operation in fuel
cell mode (potentiostatic, 0.8V with 97%H2- 3%H2O at 650oC and 700oC) and electrolysis mode (with
50% H2- 50% H2O under potentiostatic 1.3V at 700oC and galvanostatic 0.3A/cm2 at 650oC). The test
time ranged from 250 h to 800 h. Cell current density (potentiostatic) or voltage (galvanostatic) was
measured as a function of time; Current-voltage curves and EIS plots were recorded every 10 h. For the
current density-time plots in fuel cell mode, three distinct periods were identified: activation period (from
0 to 10-30 h), degradation period (from 10-30 h to 200 h) and stabilization period (from 200 h). The
current density-time plot in potentiostatic electrolysis mode also showed three periods: activation (from 0
to 80 h), degradation (from 80 h to 300 h) and stabilization (from 200 h). On the other hand, the
galvanostatic electrolysis test exhibited only two periods: degradation (from 9 to 500 h) and stabilization
(from 500 h). Analysis of EIS and deconvolution of the data by distribution of relaxation times (DRT)
indicated that the hydrogen electrode was the primary cause of degradation.
 o Based on the data of the durability tests, the estimated degradation rates assuming 40,000 h
operation were 0.1%/kh (potentiostatic fuel cell, 0.8V, with 97%H2- 3%H2O at 700oC, 0.11%/kh
(potentiostatic fuel cell, 0.8V, with 97%H2- 3%H2O at 650oC), 0.32%/kh (potentiostatic electrolysis, 1.3
V, 50%H2- 50%H2O at 700oC), and 0.88%/kh (galvanostatic electrolysis, 0.3 A/cm2 at 650oC). The
significant difference in the degradation rate in electrolysis mode was probably due to different test
conditions (potentiostatic vs galvanostatic).

Stack operation demonstration
 o The design was defined for the demonstration module/stack unit incorporating 15cnx15cm HE
supported RSOC cells and the laboratory setup was prepared for the test.
 o A test plan which included the specifications of the stack demonstration unit and description of the
test sequence (including test procedures, test parameters, test success criteria and post-test analysis) was
prepared.
 o In a single cell module test, the cell showed full OCV and demonstrated operation with 50%H2-
50%H2O at 650oC – 750oC (achieving, e.g., current density of 0.8A/cm2 in electrolysis at 700oC) and
cyclic operation. This cell was only operated for several hours before it failed due to gas leak.
 o A test with a stack of two cell modules was conducted for about 500 h (400 h in fuel cell and 100 h
in electrolysis) before it failed; post-test analysis indicated the probable cause as gas leakage. The 500h
test showed operation in both fuel cell and electrolysis (with 97%H2- 3%H2O and 50%H2- 50%H2O) and
cyclic operation and demonstrated electrical parallel and series connections. The stack exhibited excellent
performance: OCVs of about 1V with cell-to-cell variations of ± 1 to 2%, fuel cell power density of 224
mW/cm2 at 0.9V (700oC, 97%H2-3%H2O) and electrolysis current density of 0.75A/cm2 at 1.3V (700oC,
50%H2 - 50%H2O).

1. INTRODUCTION

 This report summarizes the work performed by University of California San Diego (UCSD) – OxEon
Energy LLC (OxEon) for the U. S. Department of Energy/National Energy Technology Laboratory
(DOE/NETL) under Cooperative Agreement DE-FE0031940 entitled “Efficient, Reliable and Cost-
Effective Reversible Solid Oxide Cell Technology for Hydrogen and Electricity Production”. This novel
RSOC technology is based on a compact and low-cost stack architecture that incorporates high-
performance and fuel-flexible reversible cells for efficient operation in both fuel cell (power generation)
and electrolysis (hydrogen production) modes. The main objectives of the project were (i) to validate the
design, materials and manufacturing processes of the proposed technology for both hydrogen and
electricity production, (ii) to demonstrate operation of the technology at relevant conditions with
improved performance, reliability, and endurance compared to the current state-of-the-art, and (iii) to
confirm the cost effectiveness of the proposed technology via a comprehensive techno-economic analysis
of a selected application. The project work involved identifying an application for small-scale distributed
systems, conducting design and thermodynamic analysis of the selected system, estimating the cost of
hydrogen (CoH) for hydrogen production from steam, developing RSOC cells and stacks and
demonstrating operation of a demonstration stack unit. The project culminated in the demonstration of a
RSOC stack having the design features with required performance and reliability along with projected
cost suitable for practical applications. The results of the project moved the technology readiness level
(TRL) of this RSOC technology from 3 to 4 and formed the basis for further development to advance the
technology for commercialization.

2. OVERVIEW

 A reversible solid oxide cell (RSOC) is a device based on an oxide electrochemical cell that operates
efficiently in both power generation and hydrogen production modes. In power generation mode, the
RSOC acts as a solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) and generates electricity by electrochemically combining
fuel and oxidant. In reverse (electrolysis) mode when power is applied to the cell, the RSOC acts as an
electrolyzer (in this case, referred to as a solid oxide electrolysis cell, SOEC or SOE) and produces
chemicals such as hydrogen through steam electrolysis. Since the SOFC has the capability for internal
reforming and direct utilization of hydrocarbons, e.g., natural gas, the RSOC can be made fuel-flexible in
fuel cell operating mode. Fuel-flexible RSOCs eliminate the need for an external reformer, thus
simplifying the system and reducing system costs. For the SOEC based on an oxygen ion conducting
electrolyte, it is capable of electrolyzing mixtures of steam and carbon dioxide to produce syngas
(mixtures of hydrogen and carbon monoxide) as a source of fuels and a feedstock for chemical synthesis.

 The RSOC is both the SOFC and SOEC incorporated in a single unit. Since the SOEC is the SOFC
operated in reverse mode and traditionally derived from the more technologically advanced SOFC, the
development of the RSOC will thus depend on and leverage progress in SOFC technology. The SOFC has
been considered and developed for a broad spectrum of power generation applications ranging from watt-
sized devices to multi-kilowatt power plants. To date, many of the applications for the SOFC have
progressed to hardware demonstration and prototype/pre-commercial/early commercial stages with
systems of power up to 200 kW while several applications, especially those with large power outputs, are
at the conceptual/design stage. Significant advancements have been made in the past few years in several
technological areas critical to the development of the SOFC. However, further improvements are needed
in several areas for cells and stacks, relating to the key drivers (efficiency, reliability and cost) to enable
commercialization. These areas include power density (increase), material quantity (decrease), cell size
(increase), reproducibility (increase), life (increase), and degradation (decrease). Improvements in these
areas will require significant changes and novel approaches in materials, design and engineering of
current technology. In addition to the above areas, improvements specific to the RSOC include the
reversibility of the electrodes and the stability of the oxygen electrode in electrolysis mode.

 9

 A novel RSOC technology that addresses the challenges and needs discussed above was proposed and
evaluated in this project. The proposed technology has two key elements: (i) a compact, versatile and low-
cost stack design and (ii) superior-performance, fuel-flexible reversible cells. The combination of these
elements leads to several important improvements for the proposed technology including increased
performance/efficiency, improved reliability/durability and reduced cost.
 (a) Stack Architecture: The stack architecture of this technology is based on a design concept that
consists of arrays of cell modules in electrical parallel and series connections.
 (b) Reversible cell: The RSOC cell for this technology can be based on either electrolyte supported
configuration, hydrogen electrode (HE)-supported configuration or thin-film structure. The baseline
materials for the hydrogen electrode, electrolyte and interlayer are Ni-yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ),
YSZ, gadolinium doped ceria (GDC), respectively of the RSOC cell. The material LNC
(La0.97Ni0.5Co0.5O3-δ) is selected for the oxygen electrode in this project (instead of common materials such
as lanthanum strontium cobaltite (LSC), lanthanum strontium cobalt ferrite (LSCF), and lanthanum
strontium manganite (LSM) perovskites), because LCN has been shown to be suitable for reversible
operation and contains no strontium (Sr). A LNC-based oxygen electrode is thus expected to be more
stable, durable, and chromium poisoning resistant since there is no strontium, thus no Sr segregation and
no interactions with Cr volatile species coming from the stainless steel interconnect (to form strontium
chromium oxides). The electrodes for electrolyte supported cells, cathode for hydrogen-electrode
supported cells, and all components for thin-film cells are fabricated by sputtering. RSOC cells with
sputtered components are expected to have high performance in the fuel cell, electrolysis and dual modes.
Previous work at UCSD has demonstrated that sputtered thin-film cells exhibit exceptionally high power
densities on hydrogen, methane, and other fuels in power generation mode at reduced temperatures
(500°C-700°C). This high performance is mainly due the unique fibrous/columnar electrode
microstructure obtained from the sputtering process developed by UCSD.
 The project work was conducted in relevant UCSD laboratories and OxEon Energy facilities and
laboratories. The project consisted of five technical tasks:
 (a) Application Selection and System Design and Analysis - involving identification and
 assessment of a specific application for small-scale distributed hydrogen production systems,
 comparative assessment of the technology for hydrogen production and system design and
 analysis to develop and optimize the configuration of the RSOC system for hydrogen and
 electricity production to meet the specifications of its intended application.
 (b) Techno-Economic Assessment - focusing on estimating the cost of hydrogen (CoH) for
 hydrogen production from the system designed for the specific application.
 (c) RSOC Cell Development - involving activities to evaluate sputtering for fabricating cell
 components and LNC based oxygen electrode, develop a metallizing process for oxide
 substrates for all sputtered cells and develop high-performance RSOC cells incorporating
 sputtered components especially sputtered oxygen electrodes and characterize fabricated cells
 and evaluate cell performance in reversible operation.
 (d) RSOC Stack Development - involving activities to develop stack components, define stack
 assembly specifications and evaluate performance of stacks of cell modules in reversible
 operation for hydrogen and electricity production.
 (e) Stack Operation Demonstration - focusing on building a subscale stack unit incorporating
 multiple cell modules to demonstrate its operation for hydrogen production from steam and
 also electricity from hydrogen and natural gas that meets testing criteria and metrics.

3. APPLICATION SELECTION AND SYSTEM DESIGN AND ANALYSIS

3.1 Application Selection
 According to California Air Resources Board 2020 Report, over 5 million fuel cell cars are expected
to be on the road by 20301. As the number of fuel cell cars increases, demand for hydrogen fueling station
is also expected to increase. Besides, increasing the number of fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs) is a
worldwide trend. As shown in Table 1, U.S., China, Europe, Japan, and South Korea are planning to
increase the number of fuel-cell cars, buses, trucks, and forklifts by 20302,3. Also, they plan to install
more refueling stations across the countries. Especially in the case of the U.S., more than 7,100 refueling
stations will be needed to provide sufficient infrastructure for the future FCEV market.

Table 1. Current and potential market for hydrogen fueling stations2,3

 Moving our transportation sector from petroleum fuels to hydrogen can provide many societal
benefits, including a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, zero point-of-use criteria air pollutant
emissions, and a reduction in the amount of imported petroleum from politically sensitive areas.
However, there are a number of technical and economic challenges that must be overcome before
hydrogen can be widely used as a transportation fuel. One of the most important is the current lack of
hydrogen infrastructure. Hydrogen fuel is not widely available to consumers today and the current cost of
high-pressure hydrogen at a station is several times that of gasoline. A key component of the hydrogen
fuel cost is the hydrogen delivery cost due to hydrogen is a gas with very low volumetric energy density
at standard temperatures and pressures (over three orders of magnitude less than gasoline). Therefore, the
on-site hydrogen production system could solve this problem. Furthermore, the type of hydrogen fueling
station can be categorized by hydrogen production method for the station: centralized or on-site hydrogen
production. A large, centrally located hydrogen production facility can produce H2 fuel at a lower cost,
but a longer trip to final delivery can eliminate this cost savings. Local or on-site production facilities will
typically reduce delivery costs while raising production costs. Besides that, the centralized hydrogen
production system can be effectively utilized for the state with large population density such as

 11

California, Massachusetts; however, over 76% of the incorporated places in the U.S.A. had fewer than
5,000 people as shown in Figure 14. This means that the centralized mass hydrogen production is not
appropriate for small towns. Therefore, the development of an on-site hydrogen production system could
be needed for the remote area or the place with low population density. Since the on-site hydrogen
production requires only water, the delivery cost is much lower compared to the centralized production
system.

That is enough to fill 300 passenger FCEVs or 25-50 bus/truck FCEVs completely each day. Assuming
each pump serves three vehicles an hour (for 20 minutes each) over 24 hours a day, the maximum
throughput is more than 72 vehicles in a 24-hour day. A H2-fueling station with 4 pumps in four single or
two double ranks, can serve as many as 4 vehicles simultaneously.

3.2 Technology Comparative Analysis for Selected Applications

 Several technologies other than the RSOC that can meet the requirements for small distributed on-site
hydrogen production systems (≤ 1500 kg H2/day) are also suitable for the selected applications. These
technologies include low-temperature water electrolysis (alkaline electrolysis or AE and polymer
electrolyte membrane electrolysis or PEME)6,7 and steam methane reforming (SMR). The basic operating
principles of AE, PEME and SMR ‒ along with solid oxide electrolysis (SOE) (i.e., RSOC in electrolysis
mode) ‒ are schematically shown in Figure 5 and are briefly discussed below.

  Alkaline electrolysis: AE uses an aqueous solution of KOH or NaOH (20-40%) as the electrolyte
operating at low temperatures ranging from 60○C to 80○C. Typically, nickel based metals are used as the
electrodes for alkaline water electrolysis. The electrodes are separated by a diaphragm, separating the
product gases and transporting the hydroxide ions from one electrode to the other. Applying direct
current to the electrodes splits water into hydrogen and oxygen according to the equations shown in
Figure 5(B). At the hydrogen electrode, two molecules of water are reduced to one hydrogen molecule
and two hydroxyl ions are produced. The hydroxyl ions are transferred to the oxygen electrode through
the diaphragm. These two hydroxyl ions are oxidized to one water molecule and 1/2 molecule of oxygen.
The alkaline electrolyzer typically operates at cell voltage of 1.8-2.4 V and current density of 0.2-0.4
A/cm2. AE is a well-established water electrolysis technology with commercial megawatt scale.
  Polymer electrolyte membrane electrolysis: PEME uses a solid polymer membrane as a electrolyte
operating low temperatures ranging from 50○C to 100℃. Solid polysulfonated membranes (Nafion) are
frequently used as a proton conducting electrolyte due to the advantages of high proton conductivity, low
thickness (less than 300 µm), and low gas permeability. The working principle of PEME is the PEM fuel
cell operated in reverse mode. As H2O is supplied to oxygen electrode, one water molecule is spilt into

 15

1/2 molecule of oxygen and 2 protons. These protons are transferred to the hydrogen electrode through
the membrane. At the hydrogen electrode, two molecules of protons are reduced to one hydrogen
molecule as shown in Figure 5(C). The common electrode materials for the PEME are Pt or Pt-Pd for the
hydrogen electrode and IrO2 or RuO2 for the oxygen electrode. The PEM electrolyzer typically operates at
cell voltage of 1.75-2.20 V, current density of 0.6-2.0 A/cm2, and pressure of <30 bar. The largest PEM
electrolyzer operated to date is a 20 MW system.
  Steam methane reforming: Today 95% of the hydrogen produced in the US is made by natural gas
(methane) reforming. SMR is a method of producing hydrogen by reaction of methane with high-
temperature (700○C-1000○C) steam. Methane reacts with steam under high pressure in the presence of a
nickel catalyst to produce hydrogen and carbon monoxide (and a small amount of carbon dioxide). The
carbon monoxide and steam are reacted in the water-gas shift reaction to produce carbon dioxide and
more hydrogen (Figure 5(D)). Carbon monoxide and other impurities are removed from the gas stream by
pressure-swing adsorption, leaving mainly pure hydrogen. Industrial steam reforming plants operate at
pressures between 14 and 40 bar and outlet temperatures in the range of 815○C to 925○C.

  Figure 5. Schematic illustration of (A) Solid oxide electrolysis, (B) Alkaline electrolysis, (C) Polymer
 electrolyte membrane electrolysis, and (D) Steam methane reforming

 A technological comparative analysis was conducted to assess the RSOC in electrolysis mode (SOE)
vs. AE, PEME and SMR technologies for hydrogen production for the selected applications. The analysis
compared the technologies in terms of technological readiness, efficiency entitlement, lifetime, feedstock,
hydrogen product (purity, pressure and storage), operation flexibility and CO2 emissions. Table 3
summarizes key information of component materials, efficiency, feedstock, properties of operational
flexibility and hydrogen product, lifetime and technology readiness level (TRL) for each technology.
Several key differences distinguishing the technologies are summarized below.

 o PEME is the only technology that requires a noble metal-based catalyst for operation. Even though
 tremendous research effort has been dedicated to find alternative catalyst materials or reduce the
 amount of catalyst usage, the cost of catalyst materials for PEME is still considered as the largest
 disadvantage for commercialization.
 o Compared to SMR, electrolysis requires only water (and electricity) for feedstock and is capable
 of producing high-purity hydrogen (average purity >99.5%).
 o It is noteworthy that operation flexibility of AE is lower than that of SOE and PEME 2. Although
 the operating temperature range of PEME is similar to that of AE, PEME can be operated at high
 pressure eliminating the need for an external compression system of hydrogen product. While the
 operating temperature of PEME and AE is limited to under 100○C, SOE can be operated from

 500○C to 1000○C because of the high chemical-mechanical stability of the solid ion-conducting
 phase.
 o Stack lifetime also represents the reliability of each electrolysis technology. In terms of reliability,
 the SOE has not been proved with the long lifetime of the stack. However, considering potential
 advantages including high electrical efficiency, low materials costs, and versatile operation (fuel
 cell, electrolysis, and co-electrolysis modes), the SOE could become the most efficient electrolysis
 technology for hydrogen production in the future with further research and development on
 reliability and scale-up.

 a. Efficiency: The efficiency of water electrolysis highly relates to the operating temperature. As the
temperature goes up, both thermodynamics and kinetics of the electrolysis become more favorable. For
instance, when the temperature is changed from 25○C to 800℃, the theoretical voltage for splitting water
is reduced by over 20%. This fact explains why the efficiency of SOE is far higher than AE or PEME. In
addition, at the high operating temperature of the SOE, the electrical energy required for electrolysis is
reduced and the resulting Joule heat is used in the splitting process. Thus, the SOE can be operated in the
thermal neutral condition. i.e., the condition at which the electricity exactly matches the total energy
demand of the electrolysis reaction, no heat supply or removal is required. On the other hand, the key
factor affecting the efficiency of SMR is steam-to-carbon ratio, reforming temperature and pressure.

 b. Feedstock: Feedstock for SOE, AE and PEME technologies is water and electricity. Electricity can
come from the grid or renewable energy sources such as solar, wind. Feedstock for SMR is methane
(from natural gas, biogas) and steam.
 c. H2 product: Water electrolysis is capable of producing high-purity hydrogen (average purity
>99.5%). For SMR, in order to obtain a pure hydrogen stream, several steps are necessary such as the
reduction of carbon monoxide content in the reformate by water-gas shift reactors, pressure swing
adsorption, and further hydrogen separation/purification devices.
 d. Operation flexibility: At system level, operation flexibility of technology could be a critical
parameter. For example, if the operating temperature of PEME controlled by thermometer is
malfunctioned and rise up to 200○C for a moment, the stack of the system could be severely damaged due
to chemical/mechanical failure in the membrane. This kind of situation is closely related to the operation
flexibility of the system. Several operating parameters could be utilized to estimate the flexibility such as
operating temperature range, pressure range, or contamination sensitivity. In general, in terms of
operation flexibility, the SOE shows flexibility in operating temperature and the PEME has flexibility in
operating pressure. On the other hand, the AE has the issues of part load, low current density and low
pressure operation and the SMR suffers from several operating disadvantages such as mass and heat
transfer issues, coke deposition and sulfur poisoning of the catalyst during the reaction.
 e. Safety: As all hydrogen technologies have some safety issues, water electrolysis and steam
reforming technologies have hazards associated with hydrogen handling. When hydrogen is produced and
stored in compressed tank, the pressure of the tank could be up to 700 bar, for example, for charging high
pressure tank in fuel cell vehicles. System design for securing hydrogen tank in on-site hydrogen fueling
station should be essential.
 f. CO2 emissions: As shown in Figure 6(B), carbon dioxide emissions per hydrogen produced is used
to estimate and compare the emission level for each technology. Assumption included in this graph is that
electrolysis technologies are powered by renewable energy sources such as solar or wind power. Since the
feedstock for electrolysis is water (steam) and electricity, the products are pure hydrogen and oxygen
only. On the other hand, SMR produces significant amount of carbon dioxide during the hydrogen
production process.
 g. Lifetime demonstrated: For water electrolysis, lifetime of the stack is the one of the most important
part of R&D before commercialization. PEME and AE have already demonstrated long term operation,
e.g., 90,000 hours for AE. SOE stacks have shown long-term operation up to 10,000 hours. SMR is a
mature technology and SMR systems have shown lifetime up to 65,000 hours.
 h. Technology readiness: SMR is already commercialized with a capacity of up to 500 tons H2/day.
SMR technology is at highest level of TRL and the lowest cost and most used technology to produce H 2.
However, this technology suffers two major disadvantages: use of fossil fuel resources and significant
greenhouse gas emissions with low efficiency (as shown in Figure 6). Also, development of efficient and
low-cost small-scale SMR units is required for the selected applications. For AE and PEME, industrial
companies are currently aiming to supply 10MW with AE and 20MW with PEME stacks to the market in
the near future. Therefore, these technologies have high level of TRL. However, these technologies have
some disadvantages such as low efficiency and high cost of hydrogen. Although SOE technology is
currently at TRL of 5-6 due to this technology is recently developed, this technology is essentially
developed based on existing SOFC technology, so there is a potential to improve the TRL of this
technology by using knowledge and techniques from SOFC technology3. Due to high efficiency and
system flexibility, SOE technology offers a method to produce hydrogen at a cost comparable to that of
SMR.

electrolysis technology and steam reforming have advantages and disadvantages in terms of important
factors for hydrogen production. The SMR has the disadvantages of relatively low efficiency, poor
operation flexibility and higher CO2 emissions. Among the three water electrolysis technologies
considered in this analysis, the AE is the most well-established technology. High reliability and low

industrial companies are currently developing commercial PEME stacks. For instance, Cummins Inc. is

current density, high purity of hydrogen product and flexible operation are considered as the advantages

 Figure 7 is a schematic of a conceptual RSOC system. In electrolysis mode, city water is through a
filter and a heater to become steam. Steam enters the system through a compressor and is heated through
the two heat exchangers (a hydrogen heat exchanger and an oxygen heat exchanger). The steam is then
heated up to the operating temperature through a heater before entering the solid oxide stack
(electrolyzer). During operation, H2 is produced on the same side as the steam while O2 is produced on
the opposite side of the cell. O2 is exhausted through the O2 heat exchanger. A portion of the H2 and
stream is recycled back into the stack inlet while the rest enters the hot side of the H 2 heat exchanger
before moving on to separation. Both heat exchangers provide recuperation of the heat energy of the
exhaust gases. H2 is separated from H2O by condensing the steam with the use of cooling water (not
shown in the diagram). H2 at this point is considered the product. In fuel cell mode, the fuel (natural gas)
enters a preheat heat exchanger before entering the hydrogen electrode. Air enters the system through an
air blower and then is heated through the recuperator. The air enters the O2 electrode side of the stack,
providing the reactant O2 and cooling the cell/stack. The fuel and air react inside the stack and DC current
is produced. This exhaust air is then split; some of it is re-circulated around to the oxygen electrode inlet
via a recycle blower while the rest enters a burner to burn the excess fuel coming from the stack.

5.1 Sputtering Process

5.1.1 Process Parameters

 The process developed for the fabrication of thin-film components and thin-film RSOCs is based on
magnetron sputtering using an AJA Orion system (AJA International). This system provides various
functions to vary several critical parameters that affect the quality and microstructure of film deposition.
To well-control the quality of deposited films and fabricate desired microstructures, it is important to
precisely manipulate these parameters in the operation of the system. The following experiment setup and
process parameters have been selected and evaluated for the deposition of thin-films:
  Chamber pressure: the chamber pressure is well-known to have the greatest effect on the density of
 the deposit; a denser film can be obtained with a higher vacuum pressure.
  Target-to-substrate distance: the distance between the target and the substrate is another important
 parameter for sputter deposition. As the distance decreases, ions travel shorter distance to form
 denser thin films.

 27

  Power: the magnitude of the power applied to the target relates to number of particles emitted from
 the target per unit time. As a lower power is applied, the number of particles emitted from the
 target is reduced resulting in preventing growth of particle into larger ones and form a denser film.
  Atmosphere: Ar and O2 can be used as processing gases in this system. Sputtering rate is much
 faster with Ar gas than O2 gas because Ar ions are much heavier than oxygen ions.

5.1.2 Process Parameter Optimization

 Substrate temperature
 The grain size of the electrolyte is one of the key factors that affect ionic conductivity since the ion’s
hopping from grain to grain is more sluggish compared to conduction inside of grains. Therefore,
fabricating the electrolyte composed of the larger size of grains with reduced grain boundary density is
preferable for better ion conductivity. During the sputtering, high substrate temperature provides the
energy to sputtered atoms to diffuse on the substrate which leads to grain growth. Furthermore, additional
heat energy induces better bonding between the substrate and the thin film as well as the mechanical
strength of the film. Thus, the electrolyte was deposited at different temperatures, 300°C, 400°C, and
500°C to investigate the morphology and deposition rate of each film. The temperature was the only
condition that was changed and other conditions such as pressure (3mTorr), time (2h), and target to
substrate distance (70 mm) stayed the same (Table 8). Figure 24 is the result of the SEM characterization
of each electrolyte thin film deposited on the Si wafer. All of the films were fully dense without any
defects or delamination. Even though the size of the grains on the surface was barely distinguishable in
the SEM images regardless of the temperature, the film growth rate noticeably decreased with the
temperature increase which indicates well energy provision to the sputtered film based on the fact that
high substrate temperature can cause re-evaporation of sputtered atoms that are not fully stabilized on the
substrate. Considering not only reliability and performance, but also bonding strength with the anode, the
high-temperature sputtering process was selected for electrolyte deposition.

Figure 29. Cross section of LNC-GdCe thin film after 30 minutes of deposition using 3” GdCe target and

 There are many factors affecting the uniformity of sputtered films. Among many of the parameteres,
target to substrate distance (TSD) and pressure of the deposition chamber are the major parameters
significantly affecting the uniformity of sputtered film. As shown in Figure 30(a), edge to center(E/C)
ratio representing the uniformity of sputtered film increases as sputtering TSD increases. Although E/C
ratio varies depending on the materials, increasing tendency is evident for different kinds of materials. In
addition, Figures 30 (b) and (c) show that E/C ratio also significantly changes with chamber pressure. As
chamber pressure increases, E/C ratio of various materials drops sharply from about 0.8 to 0.2. This
phenomenon is highly related to thermalization of sputtered atoms from the target. When ionized argon
gas is bombared on the target, sputtered atoms have certain level of kinetic and thermal energy. As the
sputtered atoms travel to the substrate, the atoms collides with neutral argon gas inside of the chamber.
When sputtered atom collides with argon gas molecule, the atom looses energy and eventually becomes
thermodynamic equilibrium with the gas medium, which is known as thermalization. If the chamber
pressure is too high, travel distance of the sputtered atoms significantly affects the deposition rate, which
could lead to low E/C ratio. Based on this sputtering physics, it is hypothesize that uniformity of LNC-
GDC electrode can be enhanced by controlling TSD and pressure. The sputtering recipe for LNC-GDC
was developed for 1x1 cm2 cell size. Therefore, 45 mm TSD and 30 mTorr chamber pressure needed to
be modified to achieve high uniform structure over 10x10 cm2. In the experiments in this project, TSD
was controlled from 45 to 100 mm and analyzed the E/C ratio with porosity of the nanostructure
accordingly.

 Stainless steel 316 physical sputtering mask (Figure 38) was developed for carefully controlling the
deposition area. In previous cell fabrication process experiments, aluminum foil was used for masking.
However, using aluminum foil as a physical masking for sputtering could result in inaccurate
measurement of active area because of insufficent contact between the foil and the substrate. If there was
a space bewteen the mask and the substrate, sputtered atoms could be deposited inside of the mask. This
was possible because of the characteristic of the sputtering process. During the sputtering process,
vaporized sputtered atoms are flowing inside of the chamber. These atoms have certain level of kinetic
energy resulting in flowing in some direction. This behavior is similar to the gas flowing. Therefore,
wherever the sputtered atoms can flow into, film can be formed.
 The well-contacted sputtering mask could be the key to fabricating the well-controlled sputtered
electrode area. The scale-up sputtering physical mask developed consisted of three part: mask top, mask,
and mask bottom (Figure 39). The mask top was 1.5 mm thick, the mask bottom was 1 mm, and the mask
was 0.1 mm. The mask top and bottom needed to be thicker (more than 1mm) for holding the cell and
pressing the mask during the sputtering deposition process without bending or deformation by the heat.
On the other hand, the mask should be as thin as possibel so that the mask does not block the way of
sputtered atoms. If the mask is too thick, then the deposition area could be smaller than the size of the
mask opening due to the shadowing effect of the sputtering.

5.2.1 Literature Review

 Material selection
 The baseline oxygen electode material selected for the RSOC in this project is La 0.97Ni0.5Co0.5O3-δ.
Conventionally, perovskite oxides such as LnFeO3, LnCoO3, LnNiO3, LnMoO3, and LnCrO3 (Ln is a
lanthanide) have been studied as SOC oxygen electrodes with high activity for oxygen reduction reaction
(ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER). To induce oxygen vacancy into the perovskite lattice, a key
parameter in the ORR mechanism, alkaline earth metal ions with an oxidation state of 2+ are osed in
partial substitution of the lanthanide. Therefore, the state-of-the-art perovskite oxides such as
La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3 (LSCF), La0.75Sr0.25Cr0.5Mn0.5O3, SrTi1-xFexO3, and La1-xSrxCoO3 are essentially
composed of an A-site of Ln3+ with substituted alkaline earth metal ions. However, if the perovskite
oxides have alkaline earth metals as A-site, they have the drawbacks which is the tendency for enrichment
and segregation to the surface of the alkaline earth element. This results in the reaction with other

 45

contaminants (e.g., chromium from interconnecting materials, boron from sealants, as well as carbon
dioxide, water vapor, and other minor contaminants from ambient air), which can lead to degradation of
performance. Therefore, in recent years, considerable research has been focused to develop new electrode
materials. In particular, the development of new material without alkaline earth metal elements has
received much attention. Alkaline earth metal-element-free oxygen electrode is based on perovskite-type
parent oxides such as LnFeO, LnCoO3, LnNiO3, LnMoO3, and LnCrO3.

 Thermal expansion coefficient (TEC)
 Among many candidates, Co is a good cantidate for partial subsitituion on the site because Co oxides
have very good activity for ORR, and thus Co at the B-site site of perovskite can increase ORR activity.
However, high Co content such as LaCoO3 increases TEC to about 21.5 × 10-6 K-1, which may cause
compatibility problems with classical electrolyte materials, e.g., YSZ (10 ~ 12 × 10 -6 K-1).23,24 The
combination of LaCoO3 and LaNiO3 oxides has been suggested to minimize their disadvantages.23-25 The
value of 11.9 × 10-6 K-1 was obtained for LaNi0.4Co0.6O3, which is much lower than those of conventional
oxygen electrode materials such as La0.5Sr0.5CoO3 (LSC) and La0.6Sr0.4Co0.2Fe0.8O3 (LSCF).23

 Chemical compatibility with YSZ/GDC
 When the LaNi0.6Co0.4O3 is fired at 1200 oC with YSZ for 6 hours, it is reported that the undesirable
La2Zr2O7 compound is found.23 However, this is the case of reaction at high temperature, and it is known
that the reaction rate is generally lower.23 When comparing the reactivity of LSCF and YSZ, it was
reported that the tendency toward the reaction is further reduced.26 After co-sintering the LSCF/8YSZ
mixture at 1100 °C, the secondary phase of SrZrO forms in almost the same quantity as the remaining
LSCF. In contrast, when LNC is co-sintered with 8YSZ at the same temperature, the produced secondary
phase of La2Zr2O7 is only a very minor component of the XRD pattern. Even at 1150 °C, the reacted
La2Zr2O7 is still a minor secondary phase. The thin-film SOFC process does not apply heat above the
measuring temperature (< 750 oC), but it is necessary to verify the reactivity at the measuring
temperature. In the compatibility test of the LaNiO3 (LNO) sample with GDC, there was a difference in
phase stability based on the synthesis method, but no identifiable phases assigned to a reaction product
between LNO and GDC were found.27

 Cr resistance
 In experiments reported in the literature, to check the resistance against chrome, cathode materials
were thoroughly mixed with Cr2O3 powder in an agate mortar. The cathode material to Cr2O3 weight ratio
in the powder mixture was 5:5. The mixture was heated at 1073 K in air for 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800
and 1000 h to check the reactivity with Cr species. From the XRD pattern of the LSC-Cr2O3 and LSCF-
Cr2O3 mixture after heating, a cubic perovskite phase, SrCrO4, and a spinel phase were identified in
addition to the raw materials (Figures 46a and 46b). The amount of LaNixCoyFe1−x−yO3 (LNCF) in LNCF-
Cr2O3 heated at 1073K for 1000 h remained almost unchanged compared with the initial amount of LNCF
and no new phases were identified (Figure 46c). These results suggest that each LNCF is relatively
tolerant to Cr2O3 compared with LSC and LSCF, and it appears that its strontium-free nature contributes
to the stability.28

 Electrochemical performance
 At 800°C, symmetric cells of LNC97 (infiltrated with ~5 wt% gadolinium doped ceria or CGO) on

5.2.2 Sputtered LNC Oxygen Electrode

 The oxygen electrode in this project is a composite of LNC and GDC made by sputtering. Data on
microstructure and surmophology of sputtered LNC-GDC electrodes are given and discussed in Section
5.1. Performance of single cells incorporating sputtered LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes is discussed in
Section 6.

 As an alkaline earth metal-element-free oxygen electrode, LNC does have several advantages with low
reactivity with other contaminants (e.g., chromium from interconnecting materials, boron from sealants,
as well as carbon dioxide, water vapor, and other minor contaminants from ambient air), a lower thermal
expansion coefficient (TEC) than other conventional cathodes1,2 (e.g., LSC and LSCF) (e.g., 11.9 × 10-6
K-1 for LaNi0.4Co0.6O3), and excellent chemical compatibility with YSZ or GDC. The electrochemical
activity of LNC for oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) was studied
and compared that to the conventional oxygen electrode such as LSC. The effect of GDC interlayer on the
performance of LNC oxygen electrode was investigated.
 In order to evaluate the effect of the GDC interlayer, impedance spectra analysis of the LNC-
GDC|GDC|YSZ|GDC|LNC-GDC (i.e., with GDC interlayers) and LNC-GDC|YSZ|LNC-GDC (i.e.,
without GDC interlayers) symmetrical cells was performed. The cells were fabricated by depositing the
GDC interlayer (for cells with interlayers) and the LNC oxygen electrode on both sides of the YSZ
electrolyte. The impedance spectra were measured with air at 600, 650, 700, and 750 oC. As shown in
Figure 49, the impedance spectra of cells with and without GDC interlayer were compared. It can be seen
in the figure that the polarization resistance decreases with increasing temperature. At each temperature,
the polarization resistance was smaller for cells without the GDC interlayer as compared to the case with
the GDC interlayer.

 One of the most prominent disadvantages of LSC as oxygen electrode is that Sr reacts with Zr to form
SrZrO3, an insulating layer. Thus, when LSC is implemented on oxygen electrodes, a GDC interlayer
must be placed in between LSC and YSZ. However, as LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes have been
developed through this project, GDC interlayer may be no longer needed, since the LNC oxygen
electrode has no Sr. To evaluate this fact, experiments were carried out with YSZ electrolyte support as
substrate, and LNC-GDC was deposited on top of the support by sputtering process. In between the YSZ
and LNC-GDC, GDC interlayer was deposited also by sputtering process, for “with GDC” samples. On
the other side of the YSZ, Ni-YSZ was screen-printed. This experiment was conducted to see the
potential of eliminating GDC interlayer (in fuel cell mode at 600°C). Interestingly, the “without GDC”
sample showed better electrochemical performance than the “with GDC” sample, about 10% in terms of
the peak power density (Figure 50(a)). This difference was mainly due to the faradaic impedance in the
oxygen electrode area. As shown in Figures 50(b) and (c), faradaic impedances at lower frequency region
had the largest difference in the plot for the samples with and without GDC, while ohmic resistances had
slight gap. This result along with that of symmetrical cells confirms that GDC interlayer is not needed for
LNC-GDC oxygen electrodes.

 Durability
 A number of durability tests were conducted for HE supported cells in this project. Several tests had
certain issues such as cell cracked during test, rapid cell degradation, experimental setup problems, etc.
The tests provided meaningful results are discussed below.

 In one durability test, a galvanostatic durability test in SOEC mode at 700°C was done for 89 hours.
The electrolysis current was fixed at 0.3A/cm2, and 50:50 H2:H2O fuel was supplied to the cell. The
oxygen electrode was exposed to ambient air. Figure 65 shows the galvanostatic test results and time
evolution of the cell IV curves and EIS Nyquist plots at 1.3V. In Figure 65(b), despite the power density
curve decreasing as time went by, the IV curve in SOEC mode improved as the operation continued up to
50 hours. This could mean that it took up to 50 hours for the cells to be activated. After 50 hours, the
current densities in SOEC mode decreased as can be seen with the 89 hours IV curve. This phenomenon
was reflected in the galvanostatic test result, Figure 65(a). From 50 hours to 89 hours, the voltage
measured at 0.3 A/cm2 gradually increased, indicating a degradation of the cell over time. Although it
seemed the galvanostatic curve had fluctuations due to a connection issue, the responsive voltage
improved when comparing the voltage at the beginning and at about 50 hours. This was also supported by
the EIS Nyquist plot result. In Figure 65(c), ohmic resistance increased with increasing operating time
without exception. However, faradaic resistance of 50 hours was rather smaller than that of 0 hour, which
meant that the electrodes were activated up to 50 hours. The degradation rate of the cell was about 0.04V

 The galvanostatic measurements depicted in Figure 68(a) revealed three distinct trend lines, with

curve exhibited degradation at constant rates within each zone. This initial activation is also evident in the

temporal trend of peak power density mirrored that of the galvanostatic curve, featuring rapid degradation
during the first 100 hours, a reduced deterioration rate up to 170 hours, and an almost stable performance
in the final 200 hours. These changes in power density over time are also illustrated in Figure 68(c).

 To investigate the cause of the degradation, EIS analysis was performed at 0.8 V every 10 or 20 hours
during the galvanostatic test (Figure 68(b)). The EIS plots indicate that the ohmic resistance decreased
over time, while the polarization resistance increased. The reduction in ohmic resistance was attributed to
nickel agglomeration. Under the operating conditions (3% wet hydrogen supply at 700 °C), reduced
nickel oxides in the HE-support underwent agglomeration. Initially, the nickel particles were
homogeneously dispersed and discrete to achieve higher porosity and extended triple-phase boundaries
(TPBs), enhancing the hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) reactivity. However, as smaller particles
migrated and formed larger grains, agglomeration occurred. This process improved ohmic resistance
because discrete small nickel particles were less favorable for electron conduction. Conversely, from the
TPB perspective, a greater number of smaller nickel particles create more TPBs, facilitating hydrogen
oxidation reaction (HOR) reactivity. Therefore, while nickel agglomeration over the 400-hour test
improved ohmic resistance, it simultaneously led to a loss of TPBs, resulting in increased polarization
resistance.

 Nickel electroless plating process

 For the metallization of the AAO substrate, a four-step process was carried out to achieve surface
cleaning, activation, sensitization, and electroless plating of nickel. The first step is to remove unwanted
surface impurities. The second step creates nucleation sites of nanocrystalline Pd to catalyze the nickel
plating. The third step achieves the nickel coating within the pores on the Pd catalytic sites.
 For the first step, the AAO substrates are ultrasonicated in acetone, ethanol, and finally deionized
water for five minutes each. The substrates are then left to dry overnight in a drying oven. For the
sensitization and activation steps, solutions of 1g of SnCl2 and 0.05g of PdCl2 are prepared in 100 ml of
DI water. The pH is lowered with 0.5 mL HCl, and the final solution is ultrasonicated for 30 minutes. 33
The dried substrates are placed first in the SnCl2 solution, where Sn2+ ions migrate into the AAO pores.
The substrates are then placed in DI water for 5 minutes, and finally in the drying oven for an hour. This
process is then repeated for the PdCl2 solution, causing spontaneous oxidation of the Sn2+ to Sn4+, which
reduces the Pd2+ ions to a zero-valence state. These two steps are repeated five times to complete the
sensitization/activation step and are dried overnight before the final plating step.
 For the third electroless nickel plating step, a solution is prepared consisting of 150 mL DI water,
3.00g NiCl2, for the nickel source, 4.00 g NaH2PO2•H2O as the reducing agent, 6.75g sodium citrate as
complexing agent34, 4.50g ammonium chloride to lower pH, 2.40g succinic acid as a buffer and
complexing agent, and 1.8g NaHCO3 as a basic buffer. The complexing agents retain the metal ions in
solution allowing for a more controlled deposition over the plating time. This solution should be around
6-7 pH, although upon mixture the pH is usually around 4.5. The components are mixed into the DI water
with magnetic stirring. Once the activated AAO substrates are placed in a mesh bag and in the stirring
solution, the temperature is raised to 45°C and monitored with a temperature probe for 100 minutes. The
substrates are then rinsed in DI water for five minutes and dried overnight.

 This standard procedure was replicated and several variations were attempted to improve coating
depth within the high aspect-ratio pores. The first variation on the nickel-plating step was to heat the final
nickel bath to the standard 45°C, then place the beaker in a vacuum jar to degas the AAO pores. After 10
minutes, the solution was returned to the stir/hot plate to return to 45°C. This process was repeated for a
total of 100 minutes. The second variation was to include a pre-heating soak step in the final step, where
the substrates soaked for 30 minutes at room temperature to increase diffusion time before plating. SEM
and EDS data have been taken for the standard procedure and the variation with the vacuum jar. Analog
conductivity tests on the substrates that were plated with the pre-soaking step appeared to be highly
conductive, with the resistivity in the through-channel direction nearly at 0 ohms. This will need to be
replicated with a digital multimeter so a resistance value can be obtained, however, this observation is a
promising indication that the nickel plating is continuous throughout the pores.

 Figure 71 exhibits morphology of pristine and Ni-coated AAO substrates on surface and cross-section.
All the SEM images were taken from back scattered electrons, in which contrast of images are reflected
by Z number difference. Since Ni has higher Z number than that for Al, Ni compounds are supposed to

  Although through-channel conductivity was achieved, surface morphology analysis revealed
unwanted buildup that blocked the AAO pores and which would prevent conformal deposition with
sputtering. The surface contamination on an electrolessly plated surface may be due to the chlorine atoms
that remain after palladium activation35. In order to test this hypothesis, two methods were explored. First,
all samples prepared by this method were cleaned using the new cleaning procedure mentioned in the
previous report, which involved a hot water bath (95°C) after the palladium bath in order to hydrolyze
excess chlorine. Second, an alternate version of the alternating tin sensitization/palladium activation was
done, where samples underwent the tin sensitization process 1-5 times, with a single palladium activation
step at the end to avoid significant surface contamination. This would analyze the mechanism of tin
buildup, to determine if subsequent tin activation steps with a final palladium activation step was a
suitable replacement in order to avoid contamination. In general, the results indicate that both the thin
sensitization/palladium activation approaches showed a general increase in wall thickness over the five
repetitions. The coating on samples with tin buildup plus one palladium activation step was less uniform
in comparison to the alternating tin and palladium baths. Thus, as significant cleaning of the surface to
avoid pore blocking was achieved via this new cleaning method, the alternating tin/palladium bath
method has been demonstrated to fully coat long-aspect ratio pores without compromising pore
accessibility.

but 3D deposition of Ni on the AAO surface. An even, flat surface is necessary for sputtering thin-film
cells on the surface of AAO, which cannot be achieved through cleaning methods alone.

6. RSOC STACK DEVELOPMENT

6.1 Design and Analysis

 (i) Stack components

 Stainless steel 441 was selected as the baseline material for stack components. This material has a
coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) (11.0x10-6 K-1) closely matching that of YSZ (10-12x10-6 K-1) and
shows adequate oxidation resistance under SOFC operating conditions. In addition, this stainless steel
has been evaluated in planar SOFCs and has shown desired performance at 800 oC or below. The design
of RSOC components considers the following factors: (a) low pressure drop and flow uniformity to
achieve high stack performance: gas flow must be uniform from tube to tube and uniform within the tube
flow field; (b) low contact resistance: the contact area to single cells must be designed in such a way that
the tube will efficiently collect the current; (c) reliability: minimize thermal stress in the stack; (d)
formability: the stack flattened tube must also be capable of being formed with thin sheet metal for
lightweight and low cost without failure.
 The CAD design of the stack flattened tube was performed using commercially AutoCAD software.
The developed concept served as a preliminary design for further numerical and analytical calculations
altogether as a primary design for enquires to manufacturing engineers regarding its manufacturability.
This design is its versatility in incorporating different types of planar cell configuration (HE electrode-
supported cells or substrate-supported thin-film cells).

Design analysis
 Mechanical loading investigation: The stress evaluation for metal components was conducted by
Finite Element Analysis (FEA) model in the commercial ANSYS software. Stress evaluation was
performed from the consideration that the tube located at the bottom of an assembled stack would sustain
the weight of 100 cell repeat units mounted onto it at 750oC. The study evaluated the distributions of the
safety factor (safety factor = maximum stress/yield strength of the material at a certain operating
temperature) of the bottom tube, which is assumed to be made by stainless steel SUS441. The performed
evaluation demonstrates that the safety factors appearing at the tube is of a minimum of the safety factor
of 3.04 at 750oC. These safety factors are greater than 1.0, therefore stack components are strong enough
to work at operating temperature of 750oC.
 Modeling of gas flow distribution: Preliminary analysis was conducted for gas distribution. The results
show that fuel flow distribution is uniform, which allows fuel uniform delivery to the cell for the required
fuel utilization.
 Temperature distribution: Stack metal components were evaluated for temperature distribution and
temperature gradient, and thermal stress in the proposed RSOC concept. Figure 98 provides the
distributions of the temperature inside the cell and thermally-induced stress (first principal stress) in the
electrolyte, respectively, for the direct operation of methane. Under the open-circuit condition, a
considerably large temperature difference of 79oC (Figure 98(A)) and maximum thermally-induced stress
of 110 MPa (Figure 98(B)) occurred within the front half of the cell along the fuel flow direction. The
maximum stress of 110 MPa is smaller than the fracture strength of conventional electrolyte materials
(about 236-375 MPa).8,9

 (ii) Gas manifolding

 One design concept for the stack metal component incorporating gas manifolds was developed. This
concept is based on gas cross-flow configuration. The inlet and outlet of the tubes are extended further to
outside the stack hot-zone, it is possible to seal the extended tubes with gas manifolding by room
temperature or reduced temperature sealing materials. Although the fabrication complexity of the
flattened tube may be slightly increased, but it is considered to be most advantageous in terms of sealing
and simple gas manifolding. Gas manifolds based on counter-flow and co-flow configurations are also
possible when the stack metal components with extended tube are rotation by 90 degrees.

 For better understanding the degradation mechanism, this period was divided into 2 regimes (30-130 h

and electrode/electrolyte interface resistance, also increased. These changes suggest several contributing
factors. First, fuel diffusion limitations might lead to concentration polarization, which in turn increased
gas conversion resistance (P1). This was possibly due to initial Ni agglomeration, which also contributed
to elevated electrode/electrolyte interfacial resistance (P4). The rise in P2 implies that the oxygen
electrode might be undergoing a slow degradation process. In contrast, the fuel electrode reaction (P3) did
not change, suggesting that the Ni-YSZ reaction interface remained stable, with preserved triple-phase
boundary (TPB) connectivity and intact reaction kinetics. Based on these findings, the oxygen electrode-
related reactions appeared to be the primary contributors to cell degradation in this period.
 From 140 to 200 hours, a different degradation behavior was observed. P1 and P3 showed increases,
while P2 stabilized and P4 continued to rise. The stabilization of P2 may indicate that the LNC-GDC
oxygen electrode reached a steady reactive state and was no longer degrading significantly. However,
severe Ni agglomeration likely worsened gas diffusion, reducing the efficiency of hydrogen transport at
the anode. This degradation mechanism was reflected in the increases in P1 and P3, which correspond to
gas conversion and fuel electrode reaction resistances, respectively. Continued Ni agglomeration also led
to greater interface resistance, as shown by the increase in P4. Collectively, these trends suggest that fuel
electrode-related reactions became the dominant degradation mechanism during this later period. In
summary, degradation in the 30–130 hour range was primarily driven by changes in the oxygen electrode,
whereas from 140 to 200 hours, the fuel electrode emerged as the key contributor to performance decline.

electrode/electrolyte interface, showed resistance increases only in the early stage, potentially due to

7.2 Test plan

 This plan includes the specifications of the stack demonstration unit and description of the test
sequence (including test procedures, test parameters, test success criteria and post-test analysis).
 The stack will be assembled at UCSD and OxEon following the assembly procedure established for
this demonstration. In this procedure, hydrogen electrode (HE) current collectors and oxygen electrode
(OE) current collectors are first attached the electrodes of the cell. Cells with electrode current collectors
are then attached and sealed to the stack metal component. Finally, additional OE current collectors with
wiring are attached to current collectors of the OE side to complete the repeat cell module unit.
 The testing of the demonstration stack will follow the test sequence shown graphically in Figure 156.

 Figure 156. Test Sequence

 (i) Heating Up: The stack demonstration unit will be installed in the test station and heated up to
750oC for reduction. The heating rate will be 60 oC/h. During the heat-up, air will flow to the OE side of
the stack and nitrogen on the HE side. At 425oC, hydrogen will be added to the nitrogen at a ratio of
90:10 (N2:H2) to begin reduction. Once the stack temperature reaches the test temperature of 750 oC and
fully OCV obtains, at which point the HE side gas will be set to the desired hydrogen flow rates for
testing. The stack temperature and OCV will be monitored during heating. Both the temperature and
OCV must be stable for at least 1 hour before the testing starts.
 (ii) OCV Evaluation: The test starts with the monitoring of the OCV of each cell in the stack with
hydrogen fuel/air oxidant for up to 20 hours. The objective is to demonstrate full OCV without
degradation for the stack, indicating proper assembly and functionality of the cell and other components
and no gas cross leakage. The OCV evaluation will be conducted at the beginning and the end of the test
as shown in Figure 156.
 (iii) Stack Conditioning: A current of about 50mA/cm2 will be applied for about 40 hours to
condition the stack before the initial stack performance evaluation as shown in Figure 156.

 123

 (iv) Cell Performance: The initial performance of the stack in both fuel cell and electrolysis modes
will be measured with the mixture of hydrogen and steam (fuel cell and electrolysis modes) and methane
(fuel cell mode only) by determining stack IV curve at the beginning of the operation demonstration
(shown after stack conditioning in Figure 156). There will be fuel cell performance testing at the
beginning and end of the fuel cell durability test as indicated in Figure 156. The performance of the stack
in electrolysis mode with 50% steam-50% H2 will be measured at the beginning and end of the
electrolysis durability test (Figure 156).
 (v) Durability Test: The durability of the stack will be tested by monitoring of stack current density
at 0.8V with fuel cell mode (with the mixture of 97% hydrogen and 3% steam) and 1.3 V with electrolysis

degradation rate will be determined.
 (vi) Cyclic Test: For reversible operation demonstration, the stack will be exposed to potentiostatic
cyclic testing between fuel cell and electrolysis modes, and the current response is monitored during the

constant voltage of 0.8 V. The second period is operation in electrolysis mode, where the cell is operated
at a constant voltage of 1.3 V. The gas composition is 50% H2–50% H2O. The test will be conducted with
