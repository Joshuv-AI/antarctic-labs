Reciprocals of Partition Polynomials
Evan Chen, Ken Ono and Jujian Zhang

Abstract
. Ballantine–Beck–Feigon–Maurischat introduced the subsum polynomial sp ⁡ ( λ , x ) := ∏ i ( 1 + x λ i ) \operatorname{sp}(\lambda,x):=\prod_{i}(1+x^{\lambda_{i}}) attached to an integer partition λ \lambda , and studied rational functions obtained by summing reciprocals of these polynomials over natural classes of partitions. They posed ten conjectures which naturally divide into coprimality and divisibility questions, special-value and recurrence formulas, and coefficient-shape problems. We prove all of the conjectures in the first two families: the ordinary and binary coprimality/divisibility conjectures, and the odd and ternary special-value/recurrence conjectures. AxiomProver autonomously produced Lean/mathlib formalizations and machine-checkable proofs of these six conjectures, and also discovered a counterexample to the statement as printed; the corrected form remains open.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The study of integer partitions is a classical subject in number theory and
combinatorics; we follow the standard notation and terminology described, for
example, in Andrews’ book
[
1
]
. A
partition
of a nonnegative
integer
n
is a finite nonincreasing sequence

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})

of positive integers whose sum is
n
. We write
\lambda\vdash n
. The
empty sequence is the unique partition of
0
. The length of
\lambda
is

\ell(\lambda)\coloneq k
, and
m_{\lambda}(i)
denotes the multiplicity of the part

i
in
\lambda
. In particular, we have

\sum_{i\geq 1}i\,m_{\lambda}(i)=n\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\ell(\lambda)=\sum_{i\geq 1}m_{\lambda}(i).

Ballantine–Beck–Feigon–Maurischat recently introduced
[
2
]
a family of interesting rational functions attached
to partitions. We now recall their construction in the notation used
throughout this paper. For a partition
\lambda
, its
subsum polynomial

is

\spol(\lambda,x)\coloneq\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1+x^{\lambda_{j}}).

Equivalently, we have

\spol(\lambda,x)=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{m_{\lambda}(i)}.

This polynomial is the generating function for submultisets of the multiset of
parts of
\lambda
. The reciprocal sum over ordinary partitions is

(1)

\sr(n,x)\coloneq\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{1}{\spol(\lambda,x)}.

1.1.1.
The numerator and denominator of
\sr(n,x)

A common denominator for (
1
)
is

\den^{*}(n,x)\coloneq\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor},

and the corresponding numerator is

\num^{*}(n,x)\coloneq\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}h_{\lambda}(x),

where

h_{\lambda}(x)\coloneq\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i)}.

(The products are finite in effect,
since
\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor=0
for
i>n
.
The exponents of
h_{\lambda}(x)
are nonnegative
because a partition of
n
can contain at most
\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor
parts equal to
i
.)
In other words,

(2)

\sr(n,x)=\frac{\num^{*}(n,x)}{\den^{*}(n,x)}.

However the quotient (
2
) need not be in lowest terms.
The first cancellation considered by Ballantine–Beck–Feigon–Maurischat removes the common divisor of the
individual summands.
Define

G(n,x)\coloneq\gcd\{h_{\lambda}(x):\lambda\vdash n\},

and then define

(3)

\num(n,x)\coloneq\frac{\num^{*}(n,x)}{G(n,x)}\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\den(n,x)\coloneq\frac{\den^{*}(n,x)}{G(n,x)}.

Therefore, we have

(4)

\sr(n,x)=\frac{\num(n,x)}{\den(n,x)}.

We use the empty-partition conventions

G(0,x)=1
,
h_{\emptyset}(x)=1
,
\num(0,x)=1
.

We stress that
G(n,x)
is
not
defined to be

\gcd(\num^{*}(n,x),\den^{*}(n,x))
.
It is the common factor of the summands
h_{\lambda}(x)
.
Therefore, it is conceivable
a priori
that

\num(n,x)
and
\den(n,x)
are not relatively prime as polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

1.1.2.
Example for
n=4

Here we write out the example
n=4
in full.
The five partitions of
4
are

(4),\qquad(3,1),\qquad(2,2),\qquad(2,1,1),\qquad(1,1,1,1),

so their subsum polynomials are

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(4)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\spol(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=1+x^{4}

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(3,1)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\spol(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=(1+x^{3})(1+x)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(2,2)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\spol(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=(1+x^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(2,1,1)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\spol(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=(1+x^{2})(1+x)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(1,1,1,1)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\spol(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{4}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sr(4,x)=\frac{1}{1+x^{4}}+\frac{1}{(1+x^{3})(1+x)}+\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})(1+x)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1+x)^{4}}.

The unreduced common denominator is

\den^{*}(4,x)=(1+x)^{4}(1+x^{2})^{2}(1+x^{3})(1+x^{4}).

The five terms
h_{\lambda}(x)=\den^{*}(4,x)/\spol(\lambda,x)
are

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(4)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{4}(1+x^{2})^{2}(1+x^{3})

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(3,1)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{3}(1+x^{2})^{2}\phantom{(1+x^{3})}(1+x^{4})

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(2,2)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{4}\phantom{(1+x^{2})^{2}}(1+x^{3})(1+x^{4})

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(2,1,1)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=(1+x)^{2}(1+x^{2})\phantom{{}^{2}}(1+x^{3})(1+x^{4})

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=(1,1,1,1)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\phantom{(1+x)^{4}}(1+x^{2})^{2}(1+x^{3})(1+x^{4})

and
\num^{*}(4,x)=\sum_{h\vdash 4}h_{\lambda}(x)
.
Since
1+x
divides
1+x^{3}
, each of these polynomials is divisible by
1+x
,
and in fact this is the greatest common factor:

G(4,x)=\gcd\left\{h_{\lambda}(x)\ :\lambda\vdash 4\right\}=1+x.

After dividing by this common factor, we obtain

\den(4,x)=\frac{\den^{*}(4,x)}{1+x}=(1+x)^{3}(1+x^{2})^{2}(1+x^{3})(1+x^{4}),

and

\displaystyle\num(4,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\num^{*}(4,x)}{1+x}

\displaystyle=5x^{10}+8x^{9}+15x^{8}+14x^{7}+24x^{6}+20x^{5}+24x^{4}+14x^{3}+15x^{2}+8x+5.

In this case
\sr(4,x)=\frac{\num(4,x)}{\den(4,x)}
is already in lowest terms.

1.1.3.
Restricted classes of partitions

We refer to the partitions of
n
as
ordinary partitions
.
We also use the following special classes:

\mathcal{O}(n)
is the set of odd partitions of
n
, meaning
partitions all of whose parts are odd;
\mathcal{B}(n)
is the set of binary
partitions of
n
, meaning partitions all of whose parts are powers of
2
;
and
\mathcal{T}(n)
is the set of ternary partitions of
n
, meaning partitions all
of whose parts are powers of
3
.

The same notation will be used for the restricted classes of partitions. If

\mathcal{C}
is one of
\mathcal{O},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{T}
, let
A_{\mathcal{C}}
be its set of
allowed parts:

A_{\mathcal{O}}\coloneq\{1,3,5,\ldots\},\qquad A_{\mathcal{B}}\coloneq\{1,2,4,8,\ldots\},\qquad A_{\mathcal{T}}\coloneq\{1,3,9,27,\ldots\}.

For
\lambda\in\mathcal{C}(n)
, define

\displaystyle h_{\mathcal{C},\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\prod_{i\in A_{\mathcal{C}}}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i)}

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{C}}(n,x)

\displaystyle\coloneq\gcd_{\lambda\in\mathcal{C}(n)}h_{\mathcal{C},\lambda}(x),

and

\num_{\mathcal{C}}(n,x)\coloneq\frac{1}{G_{\mathcal{C}}(n,x)}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{C}(n)}h_{\mathcal{C},\lambda}(x)\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\den_{\mathcal{C}}(n,x)\coloneq\frac{\prod_{i\in A_{\mathcal{C}}}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor}}{G_{\mathcal{C}}(n,x)}.

1.2.
Ballantine–Beck–Feigon–Maurischat’s Ten Conjectures

In the recent paper
[
2
]
,
Ballantine–Beck–Feigon–Maurischat make ten explicit conjectures about these reciprocals
of partition polynomials. We list them here in the notation fixed above, so
that the statements proved later can be compared directly with the original
conjectures.

1.2.1.
Ordinary partitions

The first two conjectures concern the ordinary numerator and
ask whether it is irreducible, and then whether (
4
) is already
in lowest terms.

Conjecture 1
.

For
n\geq 1
, the polynomial
\num(n,x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Conjecture 2
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have
\gcd(\num(n,x),\den(n,x))=1
.

The following two conjectures concern coefficient-shape properties of the
ordinary numerator and denominator.
Recall that a finite sequence is
unimodal
if it weakly increases up to some point
and then weakly decreases;
and a finite sequence
(a_{i})
of nonnegative numbers is

log-concave
if
a_{i}^{2}\geq a_{i-1}a_{i+1}
for every internal index
i
.

Conjecture 3
.

Write
\num(n,x)=\num_{0}(n,x)+\num_{1}(n,x)
, where
\num_{0}
contains the even
powers of
x
and
\num_{1}
contains the odd powers of
x
. Then

\num_{0}(n,x)
is unimodal.

Conjecture 4
.

The sequence of coefficients of
\den(n,x)
is log-concave except for

n=3,5,6,7
.

1.2.2.
Binary partitions

The next three conjectures concern binary partitions.

Conjecture 5
.

For the binary numerator and denominator, we have

\gcd(\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x),\den_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x))=1\qquad(n\geq 1).

Conjecture 6
.

For
n>5
, the binary numerator
\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)
is unimodal.

Remark
.

6
in
[
2
]
originally
asserted that
\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)
is log-concave for
n\geq 2.

This fails for
n=4
, where

\num_{\mathcal{B}}(4,x)=4+10x+18x^{2}+18x^{3}+20x^{4}+18x^{5}+18x^{6}+10x^{7}+4x^{8}.

The coefficient sequence is unimodal, but it is not log-concave, since
18^{2}<18\cdot 20
. The authors of [2] have informed us that their intended
log-concavity conjecture excludes the small cases
n=4,5
, and remains open for

n>5
.

Conjecture 7
.

For
n\geq 2
and every
2^{s}\leq n
, we have

(1+x^{2^{s}})\nmid\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x).

Remark
.

It is noted in
[
2
]
that
5
and
7
are equivalent.

1.3.
Results

In this note we prove six of the ten conjectures
using elementary number theory and facts about cyclotomic polynomials.
The precise results are as follows.

Theorem 1

(
2
)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, we have

\gcd(\num(n,x),\den(n,x))=1.

Theorem 2

(
5
and
7
)
.

For every
n\geq 2
and every
2^{s}\leq n
, we have

1+x^{2^{s}}\nmid\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x).

Consequently, for every
n\geq 1
, we have

\gcd(\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x),\den_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x))=1.

Theorem 3

(
8
)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, we have

\num_{\mathcal{O}}(n,-1)=o(n!),

where
o(M)
denotes the largest odd divisor of
M
.

Theorem 4

(
9
)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, we have

\num_{\mathcal{T}}(n,-1)=3^{v_{3}(n!)}.

In particular, for every
n\geq 1
, we have

\num_{\mathcal{T}}(3n,-1)=\num_{\mathcal{T}}(3n+1,-1)=\num_{\mathcal{T}}(3n+2,-1)=3^{v_{3}((3n)!)}.

Theorem 5

(
10
)
.

Let
t(n)\coloneq\num_{\mathcal{T}}(n,1)
and put
t(0)\coloneq 1
.
Then

\displaystyle t(3n)=t(3n+1)

\displaystyle=t(3n+2)\text{ for }n\geq 0,

\displaystyle t(3n)-t(3n-2)

\displaystyle=2^{2n}t(n)\text{ for }n\geq 1.

The results above resolve the conjectures of Ballantine–Beck–Feigon–Maurischat that concern
coprimality, divisibility, and special values. For clarity, we summarize below the status of all
ten conjectures in their paper. The remaining unresolved cases are the conjectures concerning
irreducibility and coefficient-shape phenomena; in the binary case, the log-concavity assertion is
false as stated.

We note that several of the reductions used below build directly on the
structural analysis in
[
2
]
. In particular, the exponent calculation
for the common divisor, the reduction of evaluations at roots of unity to
a remainder problem, and the corresponding binary reduction were already
developed in
[
2
, Lemmas 3.1 and 3.14]
and indicated in

[
2
, Section 4]
. Our contribution in Sections 3 and 4 is to combine
these reductions with a same-ray noncancellation argument, which rules out
the remaining possible cyclotomic factors and proves the corresponding
coprimality and divisibility conjectures.

2.
Elementary cyclotomic facts

Let
\Phi_{m}
denote the
m
th
cyclotomic polynomial, irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}
.
We record two elementary facts about
\Phi_{m}
.
They are included to make the proof accessible to readers who have seen cyclotomic polynomials
but may not have used them in this way.
The first lemma identifies exactly which factors
1+x^{i}
contain a given cyclotomic divisor.

Lemma 1
.

Let
d,i\geq 1
.
Then
\Phi_{2d}(x)
divides
1+x^{i}
if and only if
i=dj
for some odd integer
j\geq 1
.
When this occurs,
\Phi_{2d}(x)
divides
1+x^{i}
exactly once.

Proof.

Let
\zeta_{2d}\coloneq e^{\pi\mathrm{i}/d}
. Then
\zeta_{2d}
is a primitive

2d
-th root of unity and
\zeta_{2d}^{d}=-1
. The polynomial

\Phi_{2d}
divides
1+x^{i}
exactly when
\zeta_{2d}^{i}=-1
. This happens
exactly when

i\equiv d\pmod{2d},

which is the same as saying
i=dj
for an odd integer
j\geq 1
.
The second part follows by noting
1+x^{i}
has no double roots.
∎

The second lemma records the values at
1
and
-1
that will be needed in
the odd and ternary specializations.

Lemma 2
.

For
m>1
, we have

\Phi_{m}(1)=\begin{cases}p,&m=p^{a}\text{ is a power of a prime }p,\\
1,&m\text{ is not a prime power}.\end{cases}

If
m>1
is odd, then
\Phi_{2m}(-1)=\Phi_{m}(1)
. In particular, for

a\geq 1
, we have

\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(1)=1,\qquad\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(-1)=3.

Proof.

Let
\mu
denote the classical Möbius function. The standard product
formula

\Phi_{m}(x)=\prod_{e\mid m}(x^{e}-1)^{\mu(m/e)}

implies, after taking the limit as
x\to 1
, that

\Phi_{m}(1)=\prod_{e\mid m}e^{\mu(m/e)}.

For a fixed prime
p
, let
v_{p}
denote the usual
p
-adic valuation.
The exponent of
p
in this product is

\sum_{e\mid m}\mu(m/e)v_{p}(e).

Writing
e=m/f
, this becomes

\sum_{f\mid m}\mu(f)(v_{p}(m)-v_{p}(f)).

Since
m>1
,
\sum_{f\mid m}\mu(f)=0
. Therefore, the exponent is

-\sum_{f\mid m}\mu(f)v_{p}(f)
. This is
1
if
m
is a power of
p
, and
is
0
otherwise. This proves the formula for
\Phi_{m}(1)
.

If
m
is odd, then
\Phi_{2m}(x)=\Phi_{m}(-x)
. Therefore

\Phi_{2m}(-1)=\Phi_{m}(1)
. Finally,

\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(x)=\Phi_{3^{a}}(-x)
, so

\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(-1)=\Phi_{3^{a}}(1)=3
, while

\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(1)=\Phi_{3^{a}}(-1)=1.\qed

3.
Ordinary partitions: proof of
2

Throughout this section,

m_{\lambda}(i)
,
h_{\lambda}
, and
G(n,x)

have the meanings fixed in the introduction.
We do not need a closed formula for the reduced denominator.
In what follows it will be useful to write

\num(n,x)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}q_{\lambda}(x),

where

q_{\lambda}(x)\coloneq\frac{h_{\lambda}(x)}{G(n,x)}.

We now prove
Theorem

1
.
Since
\den(n,x)=\den^{*}(n,x)/G(n,x)
divides
\den^{*}(n,x)
, every irreducible
factor of
\den(n,x)
is a cyclotomic factor of some
1+x^{i}
with

1\leq i\leq n
. Therefore, every irreducible factor of
\den(n,x)
has
the form
\Phi_{2d}(x)
for some
1\leq d\leq n
. It is enough to prove
that
\num(n,x)
does not vanish at a primitive
2d
-th root of unity for
any such
d
. The first lemma computes how much of each possible
cyclotomic factor is removed by the common divisor
G(n,x)
. The following lemma is a cyclotomic reformulation of the exponent
calculation in
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
; the corresponding characterization
of divisibility by
\Phi_{2d}
is implicit in
[
2
, Lemma 3.14(a,b)]
.
We include the short proof to keep the notation of the present paper
self-contained.

Lemma 3
.

Fix
n,d\geq 1
, and put
a\coloneq\left\lfloor n/d\right\rfloor
. The exponent of
\Phi_{2d}(x)
in

G(n,x)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j>1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{dj}\right\rfloor.

Consequently the exponent of
\Phi_{2d}(x)
in

q_{\lambda}(x)=h_{\lambda}(x)/G(n,x)
is

a-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m_{\lambda}(dj).

Proof.

This is the same minimization argument as in
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]
, with

(1+x^{d})
-divisibility translated to
\Phi_{2d}
-divisibility using
Lemma 1.
By
Lemma

1
, the exponent of
\Phi_{2d}(x)
in

h_{\lambda}(x)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{dj}\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(dj)\right).

The exponent in
G(n,x)
is the minimum of this quantity over all partitions

\lambda\vdash n
.

The multiplicity sum

\sum_{j\geq 1,\,j\text{ odd}}m_{\lambda}(dj)
is at most
\left\lfloor n/d\right\rfloor
, because
the counted parts have total size at least

d\sum_{j\geq 1,\,j\text{ odd}}m_{\lambda}(dj)
. This upper bound is attained by a
partition containing exactly
a=\left\lfloor n/d\right\rfloor
parts equal to
d
, with the
remaining
n-ad
filled by ones. Hence the minimum exponent is obtained by
subtracting
a
from the full floor sum:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{dj}\right\rfloor-a=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j>1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{dj}\right\rfloor.

Subtracting this minimum from the exponent in
h_{\lambda}
gives the
exponent in
q_{\lambda}
.
∎

The next lemma identifies the only summands that can survive after evaluating
at a primitive
2d
-th root of unity. It reduces the problem from
n
to
the remainder of
n
modulo
d
. This remainder reduction is essentially
[
2
, Lemma 3.14(c,d)]
.
The constant appearing below is denoted there in terms of
f(n,d,x)
, and
an explicit product formula for it is given in
[
2
, Proposition 3.17]
.
For our purposes, only its nonvanishing is needed.

Lemma 4

(Remainder reduction)
.

Let
n,d\geq 1
, and write

n=ad+r,\qquad a\coloneq\left\lfloor n/d\right\rfloor,\qquad 0\leq r<d.

Then we have

\num(n,\zeta_{2d})=C_{n,d}\,\num(r,\zeta_{2d})

for some nonzero complex number
C_{n,d}
. In particular, we have

\num(n,\zeta_{2d})\neq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\num(r,\zeta_{2d})\neq 0.

Proof.

We first start by analyzing which terms of the sum

\num(n,\zeta_{2d})=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}q_{\lambda}(\zeta_{2d})

are nonzero.
By
Lemma

3
, the exponent of
\Phi_{2d}
in the reduced
summand
q_{\lambda}
is

a-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m_{\lambda}(dj).

At
x=\zeta_{2d}
, a summand survives if and only if this exponent is zero, i.e.,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m_{\lambda}(dj)=a.

If any counted part
dj
has
j>1
, then one of the
a
counted parts has size at least
3d
,
so the counted parts alone have total size at least

(a-1)d+3d=(a+2)d>ad+r=n
. This is impossible.
We conclude the surviving partitions are exactly

\lambda=(d^{a},\nu),\qquad\nu\vdash r,

with the empty partition allowed when
r=0
.
In other words,

(5)

\num(n,\zeta_{2d})=\sum_{\nu\vdash r}q_{(d^{a},\nu)}(\zeta_{2d}).

Next we rewrite
q_{(d^{a},\nu)}
in terms of
q_{\nu}
for
\nu\vdash r
.
Comparison of exponents gives the identity, in the field
of rational functions,

q_{(d^{a},\nu)}(x)=F_{n,d}(x)q_{\nu}(x),

where

F_{n,d}(x)\coloneq\frac{G(r,x)}{G(n,x)}\prod_{i\neq d}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor r/i\right\rfloor}.

This factor is independent of
\nu
, so (
5
) implies

\num(n,\zeta_{2d})=C_{n,d}\,\num(r,\zeta_{2d}),

where
C_{n,d}\coloneq F_{n,d}(\zeta_{2d}).
In the notation of
[
2
, Lemma 3.14]
, this constant is

f(n,d,\zeta_{2d})
; see also
[
2
, Proposition 3.17]
for an
explicit formula.
It remains only to verify that
C_{n,d}\neq 0
.

A factor
1+x^{i}
can vanish at

\zeta_{2d}
only through the cyclotomic factor
\Phi_{2d}
. Since

r<d
, no factor occurring in
\den^{*}(r,x)
, and hence no factor of

G(r,x)
, is divisible by
\Phi_{2d}
. Thus
G(r,\zeta_{2d})\neq 0
.
The product

\prod_{i\neq d}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor r/i\right\rfloor}

has
\Phi_{2d}
-exponent

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j>1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{dj}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{r}{dj}\right\rfloor\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j>1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{dj}\right\rfloor,

because
r<d
.
By
Lemma

3
, this is exactly the
\Phi_{2d}
-exponent of
G(n,x)
.
After cancelling these equal powers of
\Phi_{2d}
,
every remaining numerator and denominator factor is nonzero at
\zeta_{2d}
.
∎

The reduction leaves the case
0\leq r<d
. The following same-ray argument
shows that the remaining terms cannot cancel.

Lemma 5
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
0\leq r<d
. Then

\num(r,\zeta_{2d})\neq 0.

Proof.

If
r=0
, then
\num(0,x)=1
. Assume
1\leq r<d
. For
1\leq i\leq r
,

1+\zeta_{2d}^{\,i}=1+e^{\pi\mathrm{i}i/d}=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi i}{2d}\right)e^{\pi\mathrm{i}i/(2d)}.

The cosine is strictly positive because
0<i<d
. Thus each factor

1+\zeta_{2d}^{\,i}
has positive length and argument
\pi i/(2d)
.

For a partition
\nu\vdash r
, the value

h_{\nu}(\zeta_{2d})
has argument

\frac{\pi}{2d}\sum_{i=1}^{r}i\bigl(\left\lfloor r/i\right\rfloor-m_{\nu}(i)\bigr).

But
\sum_{i}im_{\nu}(i)=r
, so this argument equals

\frac{\pi}{2d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}i\left\lfloor r/i\right\rfloor-r\right),

which is independent of
\nu
. Also, every factor involved is nonzero.
Therefore all numbers
h_{\nu}(\zeta_{2d})
lie on a single open ray
from the origin.

Since
r<d
, no factor
1+x^{i}
with
1\leq i\leq r
is divisible by

\Phi_{2d}
. Thus
G(r,\zeta_{2d})\neq 0
. Dividing by this fixed nonzero
number only rotates and rescales the ray. Hence the reduced summands

q_{\nu}(\zeta_{2d})
also lie on a single open ray. Their sum cannot
be zero.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Every irreducible factor of
\den(n,x)
is some
\Phi_{2d}(x)
with

1\leq d\leq n
. Fix such a
d
. By
Lemma

4
and
Lemma

5
,

\num(n,\zeta_{2d})\neq 0.

Hence
\Phi_{2d}\nmid\num(n,x)
. Therefore no nonconstant irreducible factor
of
\den(n,x)
divides
\num(n,x)
. Since
\den(n,x)
has constant term

1
, no rational integer prime divides all its coefficients. Thus

\gcd(\num(n,x),\den(n,x))=1
in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.
∎

4.
Binary partitions: proof of
5
and
7

We next adapt the same root-of-unity idea to binary partitions. Here the
allowed parts are
1,2,4,\ldots
, and the factors
1+x^{2^{k}}
are already
pairwise coprime cyclotomic polynomials. By the definitions in the
introduction,

h_{\mathcal{B},\lambda}(x)\coloneq\prod_{k\geq 0}\left(1+x^{2^{k}}\right)^{\left\lfloor n/2^{k}\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(2^{k})}.

The following observation is
[
2
, Remark 6]
; we recall the brief
argument for completeness.

Lemma 6
.

We always have
G_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)=1
, for every
n
.
Hence,

\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{B}(n)}h_{\mathcal{B},\lambda}(x)\qquad{\text{\rm and}}\qquad\den_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)=\prod_{k\geq 0}(1+x^{2^{k}})^{\left\lfloor n/2^{k}\right\rfloor}.

Proof.

Since
1+x^{2^{k}}=\Phi_{2^{k+1}}(x)
, these factors are pairwise coprime
irreducibles. For a fixed
k
, put
D\coloneq 2^{k}
. The binary partition with

\left\lfloor n/D\right\rfloor
parts equal to
D
, together with any binary partition of
the remainder
n-D\left\lfloor n/D\right\rfloor
, makes the exponent of
1+x^{D}
equal to zero.
Thus, no
1+x^{2^{k}}
divides all binary summands.
∎

The same pairwise coprimality shows that
5
is
equivalent to
7
: the binary numerator is coprime to
the binary denominator exactly when no factor
1+x^{2^{s}}
divides

\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)
.

We use the binary remainder reduction indicated in

[
2
, Section 4, final paragraph]
, which is the binary analogue of

[
2
, Lemma 3.14]
. The additional point below is the same-ray
noncancellation argument for the remaining sum.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Fix
D\coloneq 2^{s}\leq n
, and let

\zeta\coloneq e^{\pi\mathrm{i}/D}.

Then
1+x^{D}
is the minimal polynomial of
\zeta
, since
D
is a power of

2
. It is enough to show that
\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,\zeta)\neq 0
.

Write
n=aD+r
, with
a=\left\lfloor n/D\right\rfloor
and
0\leq r<D
. In a binary
summand
h_{\mathcal{B},\lambda}(x)
, the only factor
1+x^{2^{k}}
that vanishes
at
x=\zeta
is
1+x^{D}
. Hence a summand can be nonzero only if

m_{\lambda}(D)=a.

The surviving partitions are exactly

\lambda=(D^{a},\rho),\qquad\rho\in\mathcal{B}(r).

For such
\lambda
, comparison of the exponents gives the exact identity

h_{\mathcal{B},(D^{a},\rho)}(x)=C_{n,D}(x)h_{\mathcal{B},\rho}(x),

where

C_{n,D}(x)\coloneq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 0\\
2^{k}\neq D\end{subarray}}(1+x^{2^{k}})^{\left\lfloor n/2^{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor r/2^{k}\right\rfloor}.

This product is independent of
\rho
. Since
1+\zeta^{2^{k}}\neq 0
for

2^{k}\neq D
, we have
C_{n,D}(\zeta)\neq 0
. Therefore, we have

\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,\zeta)=C_{n,D}(\zeta)\num_{\mathcal{B}}(r,\zeta).

This is the binary analogue of the reduction used in

[
2
, Lemma 3.14(c,d)]
and noted after
[
2
, Theorem 4.10]
.

It remains to prove
\num_{\mathcal{B}}(r,\zeta)\neq 0
. If
r=0
, this is immediate.
Assume
1\leq r<D
. For every binary part
2^{k}\leq r
,

1+\zeta^{2^{k}}=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi 2^{k}}{2D}\right)e^{\pi\mathrm{i}2^{k}/(2D)},

and the cosine is positive. Thus each such factor has positive length and
argument
\pi 2^{k}/(2D)
. For
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(r)
, the argument of

h_{\mathcal{B},\rho}(\zeta)
is

\frac{\pi}{2D}\sum_{2^{k}\leq r}2^{k}\cdot\left(\left\lfloor r/2^{k}\right\rfloor-m_{\rho}(2^{k})\right).

Since
\sum_{k}2^{k}m_{\rho}(2^{k})=r
, this argument is independent of
\rho
. All
surviving terms are nonzero and lie on a single open ray. Their sum is nonzero.
Therefore
\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,\zeta)\neq 0
, so
1+x^{D}\nmid\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)
.
This proves
7
, and the coprimality statement follows from the equivalence above.
∎

5.
Odd partitions: proof of
8

We now specialize the restricted numerator for odd partitions at
x=-1
.
The definitions from the introduction give

h_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}(x)\coloneq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\geq 1\\
i\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}(1+x^{i})^{\left\lfloor n/i\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i)},

with common divisor

G_{\mathcal{O}}(n,x)\coloneq\gcd_{\lambda\in\mathcal{O}(n)}h_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}(x)

and reduced numerator

\num_{\mathcal{O}}(n,x)\coloneq\frac{1}{G_{\mathcal{O}}(n,x)}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{O}(n)}h_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}(x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{O}(n)}q_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}(x)

where the reduced summands
q_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}(x)\coloneq\frac{h_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}(x)}{G_{\mathcal{O}}(n,x)}

are defined in the obvious way.
The proof shows that, after this cancellation, only the all-ones partition
survives at
x=-1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

For an odd positive integer
e
, the factor
\Phi_{2e}
divides
1+x^{i}
,
with
i
odd, exactly when
i=ej
for an odd integer
j
. Therefore the
exponent of
\Phi_{2e}
in
h_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{ej}\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(ej)\right).

As before, this is minimized by maximizing the number of parts of the form
ej
with
j
odd.
In symbols, we have
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m_{\lambda}(ej)\leq\left\lfloor n/e\right\rfloor

with equality when
\lambda
has
\left\lfloor n/e\right\rfloor
parts equal to
e
.
Thus the exponent of
\Phi_{2e}
in
G_{\mathcal{O}}(n,x)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j>1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{ej}\right\rfloor.

Consequently, in
q_{\mathcal{O},\lambda}
, the exponent of
\Phi_{2e}
is

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{e}\right\rfloor-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}m_{\lambda}(ej).

We now specialize to
x=-1
.
The only cyclotomic factor that vanishes is
\Phi_{2}(x)=x+1
, which corresponds to
e=1
.
The exponent of
\Phi_{2}
in the reduced summand
is

n-\ell(\lambda),

where
\ell(\lambda)
is the number of parts of
\lambda
.
Among odd partitions of
n
, the maximum possible length is
n
,
attained uniquely by

(1^{n})
. Hence all reduced summands vanish at
-1
except the one
corresponding to
(1^{n})
, that is to say,

\num_{\mathcal{O}}(n,-1)=q_{\mathcal{O},(1^{n})}(-1)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e>1\\
e\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{2e}(-1)^{\left\lfloor n/e\right\rfloor}.

By
Lemma

2
,
\Phi_{2e}(-1)=p
if

e=p^{a}
is a power of an odd prime
p
, and
\Phi_{2e}(-1)=1
otherwise.
Therefore

\num_{\mathcal{O}}(n,-1)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ odd prime}\end{subarray}}\prod_{a\geq 1}p^{\left\lfloor n/p^{a}\right\rfloor}=o(n!),

by Legendre’s formula with the prime
2
omitted.
∎

6.
Ternary partitions: proofs of
9
and
10

Finally, we treat ternary partitions. The same cancellation analysis gives
both the evaluation at
x=-1
and the generating function needed at
x=1
.
We use

h_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}(x)\coloneq\prod_{k\geq 0}(1+x^{3^{k}})^{\left\lfloor n/3^{k}\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(3^{k})},

G_{\mathcal{T}}(n,x)\coloneq\gcd_{\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(n)}h_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}(x),\qquad\num_{\mathcal{T}}(n,x)\coloneq\frac{1}{G_{\mathcal{T}}(n,x)}\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(n)}h_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}(x)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(n)}q_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}(x).

For every
k\geq 0
, we have

1+x^{3^{k}}=\prod_{a=0}^{k}\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(x).

Therefore, the exponent of
\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}
in
h_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}
is

\sum_{k\geq a}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3^{k}}\right\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(3^{k})\right).

As in the previous sections, this is minimized by using

\left\lfloor n/3^{a}\right\rfloor
parts equal to
3^{a}
and filling the remainder with ones.
Hence, the exponent of
\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}
in
G_{\mathcal{T}}(n,x)
is

\sum_{k>a}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3^{k}}\right\rfloor,

and the exponent in a reduced summand
q_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}
is

\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3^{a}}\right\rfloor-\sum_{k\geq a}m_{\lambda}(3^{k}).

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

At
x=-1
, the only vanishing factor is
\Phi_{2}=x+1
, corresponding to
a=0
.
Its exponent in the reduced summand
q_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}
is

n-\ell(\lambda).

The unique ternary partition of
n
with
n
parts is
(1^{n})
. Therefore
only the reduced summand
q_{\mathcal{T},(1^{n})}
survives at
-1
.

For this summand, the reduced exponent of
\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}
is
0
for

a=0
, and is
\left\lfloor n/3^{a}\right\rfloor
for
a\geq 1
. Therefore, we have

\num_{\mathcal{T}}(n,-1)=q_{\mathcal{T},(1^{n})}(-1)=\prod_{a\geq 1}\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(-1)^{\left\lfloor n/3^{a}\right\rfloor}.

By
Lemma

2
,
\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(-1)=3
. Therefore, we have

\num_{\mathcal{T}}(n,-1)=3^{\sum_{a\geq 1}\left\lfloor n/3^{a}\right\rfloor}=3^{v_{3}(n!)}.

In view of

v_{3}((3n)!)=v_{3}((3n+1)!)=v_{3}((3n+2)!),

the block form in
9
follows.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

At
x=1
,
Lemma

2
gives

\Phi_{2\cdot 3^{a}}(1)=1
for
a\geq 1
. Thus the value of
G_{\mathcal{T}}(n,x)
at

x=1
is determined only by the factor
\Phi_{2}(1)=2
. Its exponent in

G_{\mathcal{T}}(n,x)
is

\sum_{k\geq 1}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{3^{k}}\right\rfloor.

For a ternary partition
\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(n)
, we have

h_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}(1)=2^{\sum_{k\geq 0}\left\lfloor n/3^{k}\right\rfloor-\ell(\lambda)}.

After division by
G_{\mathcal{T}}(n,1)=2^{\sum_{k\geq 1}\left\lfloor n/3^{k}\right\rfloor}
, the reduced summand has value

q_{\mathcal{T},\lambda}(1)=2^{n-\ell(\lambda)}.

Therefore, we obtain

t(n)=\num_{\mathcal{T}}(n,1)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{T}(n)}2^{n-\ell(\lambda)}.

This has the ordinary generating function

T(q)\coloneq\sum_{n\geq 0}t(n)q^{n}=\prod_{j\geq 0}\left(1-2^{3^{j}-1}q^{3^{j}}\right)^{-1}.

The factor with
j=0
is
(1-q)^{-1}
. Reindexing the remaining product,
we find

\prod_{j\geq 1}\left(1-2^{3^{j}-1}q^{3^{j}}\right)^{-1}=\prod_{j\geq 0}\left(1-2^{3^{j}-1}(4q^{3})^{3^{j}}\right)^{-1}=T(4q^{3}).

Thus, we find that

T(q)=\frac{1}{1-q}T(4q^{3}),\qquad\text{or}\qquad(1-q)T(q)=T(4q^{3}).

Comparing coefficients of
q^{m}
, with the convention
t(-1)=0
, gives

t(m)-t(m-1)=0

unless
m
is divisible by
3
. This proves

t(3n)=t(3n+1)=t(3n+2)\qquad(n\geq 0).

Comparing coefficients of
q^{3n}
gives

t(3n)-t(3n-1)=4^{n}t(n).

By block constancy,
t(3n-1)=t(3n-2)
. Hence

t(3n)-t(3n-2)=4^{n}t(n)=2^{2n}t(n),

which is the claimed finite-difference formula.
∎

Appendix: Autonomous proof production and formalization

At Axiom Math, we are developing AxiomProver,
an AI system for mathematical research based on autoformalization.
As a test case, we gave AxiomProver
ten tasks: to autonomously formalize and prove Conjectures
1
-
10
,
offering examples of AI assistance as well as limitations in mathematical research.
This appendix is separate from the rest of the manuscript.

7.1.
Division of labor

The prose exposition of this paper, including this appendix, was written without the use of AI.
AxiomProver runs Lean,
an interactive theorem prover and a functional programming language built on dependent type theory,
designed to provide a rigorous computational framework for validating mathematical proofs
[
4
]
.
This appendix is included not only to record that formal proofs were produced,
but also to clarify the division of labor between the human-written
mathematics and the AI-generated formalizations. The
mathematical statements supplied to AxiomProver were written in natural
language and can be found in
[
2
]
.

The role of AxiomProver was to convert the non-formal statements
into Lean and to construct machine-checkable formal proofs. Thus, in the tasks
described below, AxiomProver was neither being asked to make any of the conjectures,
nor to decide the final organization of the paper; it was being asked to
produce complete formal proofs of the supplied statements.

Autoformalization involves automatically and autonomously converting
natural-language mathematics into machine-verifiable formal language.
Lean files are created to pass type checkers,
while the natural language papers aim to communicate ideas to readers.

7.2.
Task specification and results

Given problems in natural or formal language,
AxiomProver attempts to generate a complete formal proof.
When it succeeds, the system outputs two files:

•

problem.lean
, which formalizes the problem statement;

•

solution.lean
, which represents a complete proof in a formal language.

The ten tasks share a uniform layout — for
i\in\{1,\dots,10\}
,
we gave the following files as input for the
i
-th task:

•

Conj_
i
_Statement.tex

•

task.md
contains a single line

Please read ‘
Conj_
i
_Statement.tex
’ and formalize
Conjecture
\ref{conj:conj_
i
}
.

•

.environment
specifies the Lean version used is 4.28.0.

Out of the
10
tasks, AxiomProver was able to autonomously formalize and prove
Conjecture
j
for
j\in\{2,5,7,8,9,10\}
.
It also found the counterexample
n=4
to the part of
[
2
, Conjecture 6]

asserting that
\num_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)
has log-concave coefficients
(see the remark at the end of
Section

).
Here “autonomously” means that, after receiving the
specified input files, AxiomProver generated the corresponding

and

mathematical intervention in the proof script. The authors did not hand-write

7.3.
Artifacts

The relevant files are posted in the following repository:

https://github.com/AxiomMath/PartitionPolynomial

The version of Lean used is 4.28.0.
Compatibility with other versions is not guaranteed due to the evolving
nature of the Lean 4 compiler and its core libraries.

At first glance, the proofs generated by AxiomProver do not resemble the narrative outlined in this paper.
Converting a Lean file into a proof understandable by humans is challenging
because Lean is designed as code for a type-checker, not as a reader-friendly explanation.
It makes all the “obvious” bookkeeping explicit, such as rewrite steps, coercions, side conditions,
and case splits, and tends to follow the most convenient lemmas and tactics for the library,
rather than the most clear conceptual route.
A mathematician can usually condense this significantly by relying on shared historical context,
standard arguments, and informal identifications that Lean cannot assume.
As a result, writing a paper from Lean files is not just about reformatting.
The authors must understand the formal script, reconstruct the underlying ideas,
and then translate the code into a narrative that emphasizes the key insights
while safely omitting routine details.
