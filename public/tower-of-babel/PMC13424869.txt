Prognostic and Predictive Value of Lymph Node Ratio in Stage III Colon Cancer in the Era of Molecular Biomarkers
Authors: Zhenxin Rao, Kozo Kataoka, Ayako Imada, Kazuma Ito, Yuko Fukumoto, Kei Kimura, Jihyung Song, Yuki Horio, Ryuichi Kuwahara, Motoi Uchino, Wim Ceelen, Masataka Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The prognostic and predictive value of the lymph node ratio (LNR; metastatic/harvested nodes) in stage III colon cancer (CC) remains unclear, particularly in combination with molecular profiles such as RAS, BRAF, and microsatellite instability (MSI) status. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed stage III CC patients who underwent curative resection with D3 lymph node dissection (2013-2023). Optimal LNR cutoffs were determined using maximally selected rank statistics. Cox models assessed the prognostic impact of LNR adjusted for clinicopathological and molecular factors. The effect of adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) on recurrence-free (RFS) and overall survival (OS) was evaluated by LNR subgroup. Results: Among 361 patients, optimal LNR cutoffs for RFS and OS were 0.16 and 0.21, respectively. Based on the cutoffs of 0.2, the RFS and OS were longer in patients with low LNR than those with high LNR. Multivariate analysis identified RAS and BRAF mutation, venous invasion, LNR and ACT as independent factors for RFS, while RAS mutation, lymphatic invasion, ACT and LNR were significant for OS. In patients with low LNR (≤0.2), ACT improved RFS (HR 0.57 (95% CI 0.33-0.99)) and OS (HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.28-0.85)) and similar tendency was observed in those with high LNR (>0.2) (RFS: HR 0.56 (95% CI: 0.29-1.09), OS: HR 0.51 (95%CI 0.23-1.13)). Conclusion: After adjustment for multiple biomarkers, LNR remained an independent predictor of OS in stage III CC. ACT was associated with improved survival regardless of LNR, with a consistent trend observed even in patients with high LNR.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide[1]. Stage III colon cancer (CC) is characterized by the presence of lymph node (LN) metastasis; thus, postoperative management is critical in this patient population. Adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) is the standard of care for most patients with stage III disease. However, prognosis remains highly heterogeneous even after curative resection, with reported 5-year overall survival (OS) ranging from approximately 45% to 80%[2]. This heterogeneity underscores the need for more precise prognostic and predictive markers to refine risk stratification and guide treatment intensity.

The lymph node ratio (LNR)―defined as the number of metastatic LNs divided by the number of harvested LNs―has been proposed as a more refined indicator of tumor burden than conventional TNM staging. Several retrospective studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of LNR in stage III CC[3-12]. According to evidence, LNR may outperform conventional pathological N classification in stratifying survival outcomes[13-17]. Nevertheless, important gaps in knowledge regarding the usefulness of LNR remain. Most previous analyses have evaluated LNR without adjusting for established molecular biomarkers such as RAS and BRAF mutations or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status, which influence prognosis and treatment response[18,19]. Furthermore, limited evidence is available regarding the potential role of LNR as a predictive marker for the benefit of ACT. Therefore, the aims of the present study were to reassess the prognostic significance of LNR adjusting for molecular profiles, and explore its potential utility in predicting chemotherapy benefit in patients with pathological stage III CC.

Methods

This retrospective analysis included patients with pathological stage III CC and available data on RAS, BRAF, and MSI status, who were treated with curative lymphadenectomy (Japanese D3 dissection) at Hyogo Medical University (Nishinomiya, Japan) between 2013 and 2023. Patients with rectal cancer and those treated with neoadjuvant therapy were excluded. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hyogo Medical University, Japan (approval number: 4708). Informed consent was obtained via an opt-out process. Open or laparoscopic colonic resection with Japanese D3 lymphadenectomy was performed in all patients; this surgical approach is oncologically equivalent to complete mesocolic excision with central venous ligation[20]. Adjuvant therapy (i.e., 5-fluorouracil or oxaliplatin-based modified FOLFOX6 [leucovorin, 5-fluorouracil, and oxaliplatin] or CapOX [capecitabine and oxaliplatin]) was administered according to guidelines of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum[21].

Lymph Node Ratio (LNR) was defined as the number of metastatic lymph nodes divided by the total number of dissected lymph nodes. The optimal cut-off value for LNR was determined using maximally selected rank statistics from the ‘maxstat’ R package. The independent association between recurrence-free survival (RFS) or OS and a number of established prognostic factors in CC was evaluated by univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses. The co-variables included age, sex, pathological T category, pathological lymphatic and venous invasion, RAS, BRAF and MSI status, tumor size, ACT (yes/no), and LNR. According to guidelines of the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum, pathological lymphatic invasion and venous invasion were divided into four categories: 0, no invasion; 1, mild invasion; 2, moderate invasion; and 3, severe invasion[22]. Pathological lymphatic and venous invasion were analyzed as categorical co-variables.

Survival benefit of ACT was assessed in subgroups based on the calculated optimal cutoffs of LNR; in this analysis, we adjusted for patient age. RFS was defined as the period of time from the date of surgery until recurrence or death from any cause. OS was defined as the period of time from the date of surgery to the date of death from any cause. Both were censored at the most recent time when the patient was known to be alive. Cox proportional hazards models were built with and without interaction terms of LNR with molecular features (BRAF and RAS mutation status). Two-tailed p-values <0.05 indicate statistically significant differences. All analyses were performed with R studio, version 2024.09.1.

Results

Identification of the optimal cutoff values for LNR and its prognostic significance

This retrospective analysis included 361 patients with stage III CC and available data on their molecular profile (i.e., RAS, BRAF, and MSI). Clinical and pathological variables are shown in Table 1. During a median follow-up of 46.6months, a total of 135 recurrences and 103 deaths were observed. Using maximally selected rank statistics, the optimal LNR cutoffs for RFS and OS were calculated as 0.16 and 0.209, respectively. Based on these cutoff values, the RFS and OS was longer in patients with low LNR than those with high LNR (Figures 1a and b). Considering the LNR values (i.e., 0.16 and 0.209 for RFS and OS, respectively), the optimal cutoff value was calculated as 0.2 in this study. As a sensitivity analysis, OS were evaluated using additional LNR cutoffs of 0.15 and 0.25 (Supplementary Figure 1a and 1b). Overall, the prognostic discrimination was directionally consistent across these thresholds, although the separation between survival curves appeared most pronounced at the cutoff value of 0.20. Patient characteristics based on the LNR cutoff values are shown in Table 2. In the high LNR group, 106 recurrences and 78 deaths were observed, while in the low LNR group 29 recurences and 25 deaths were observed. BRAF-mutant status (p=0.038) and higher preoperative and postoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels were more frequently observed in the high-LNR group compared with the low-LNR group. Patients in the low-LNR group were less likely to receive oxaliplatin plus 5-FU (mFOLFOX6 or CAPOX) than those in the high-LNR group. The results of the multivariate analyses for RFS and OS are shown in Table 3. RAS and BRAF mutation, pathological venous invasion, LNR and ACT were identified as independent prognostic factors for RFS. For OS, prognostic factors included RAS mutation, pathological lymphatic invasion, ACT and LNR. Tests for interaction showed that the prognostic effect of the LNR is stronger for BRAF mutant tumors for OS and RFS (Table 4).

Predictive value of LNR for ACT

Overall, ACT was associated with improved survival. In the low-LNR subgroup, ACT was associated with significantly improved RFS (HR 0.57 (95% CI 0.33-0.99)) and OS (HR 0.48 (95% CI 0.28-0.85)). In patients with high LNR, a trend toward improved survival with ACT was observed; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (RFS: HR 0.56 (95% CI: 0.29-1.09), OS: HR 0.51 (95%CI 0.23-1.13)) (Figures 2a and 2b).

Discussion

Over the past two decades, the LNR has been extensively investigated as a prognostic indicator in CC, particularly in stage III disease. Several large retrospective studies and population-based analyses have suggested that LNR provides more refined risk stratification than conventional pN classification[4-12]. In these settings, LNR has been reported to reduce stage migration and better discriminate survival outcomes within the same pN category. Conversely, its clinical utility remains controversial due to heterogeneity in reported cutoff values and its inherent dependence on total number of harvested LNs, which may reflect surgical and pathological variability rather than tumor biology alone. Therefore, although LNR has been proposed as a complementary or alternative nodal metric, its incremental value beyond established staging systems has not been fully clarified. Most previous studies utilizing data from nationwide databases lacked information on molecular biomarkers, which could have confounded their results[4-12,23]. In our study, LNR demonstrated prognostic utility even after adjustment for key molecular biomarkers (i.e., RAS, BRAF, and MSI) in stage III CC. Furthermore, in stage III disease, ACT seemed to be effective irrespective of LNR. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively assess the prognostic and predictive significance of LNR, while taking into account the importance of molecular biomarkers.

In this study, the LNR appeared to be a stronger prognostic factor than any other biomarker. Ferri et al. reported that increased LNR was associated with worse survival, whereas MSI-H only showed a trend toward worse outcomes[24]. This finding is supported by analysis of data from a Japanese nationwide database, which indicated that incorporating LNR into the conventional TNM classification improved stratification[25]. Conversely, several research groups reported that LNR might not superior to pathological N staging[26-28]. Although a systematic review of 16 studies involving >30,000 patients indicated superior performance of LNR over N staging in terms of prognostic stratification[23], this observation may be attributable to differences in cutoff values. As previously mentioned, various studies have explored optimal LNR cutoff values, leading to variability in reported thresholds. In the present study, we used a cutoff value of 0.2, consistent with some prior studies. Notably, in a meta-analysis including 90,000 patients from 14 eligible studies, Pyo et al. identified 0.2 as the optimal cutoff value[29]. However, the optimal cutoff value may vary according to tumor sidedness (right versus left)[30]. Adittionaly, because the cutoff value was determined using maximally selected rank statistics, it is inherently data-driven and may be susceptible to overfitting in this small dataset. A standardized cutoff value for LNR, potentially incorporating tumor sidedness and biomarkers, may be necessary to establish its role as a reliable prognostic marker in clinical practice.

For more than two decades, a low number of harvested LNs, particularly fewer than 12, has been regarded as a poor prognostic factor in stage II and III CC[31,32]. Because the denominator of the LNR is the number of harvested LNs, a lower LN yield can automatically increase the LNR[33]. In our cohort, 22% of patients in the high-LNR group had fewer than 12 harvested LNs, compared with only 8% in the low-LNR group. Whether LNR maintains its prognostic significance in cases with a low number of harvested LNs remains controversial.

Some studies have reported that LNR loses its prognostic value when fewer than 12 LNs are harvested, whereas Rosenberg[34] et al. found that LNR was a prognostic factor regardless of LN yield. A low number of harvested LNs may be attributed to the quality of surgery[35] or pathological assessment[36]. This study was conducted in a single specialized center for colorectal cancer, thereby ensuring surgical and pathological quality and minimizing this potential bias. Further investigation is warranted to clarify the prognostic importance of LNR in this specific subset of patients.

In our analysis, an obvious benefit of ACT was observed especially in patients with low LNR. The impact of LNR on the effectiveness of ACT has also been investigated in gastric cancer[37,38]. In addition, Moug et al. evaluated the association between LNR and ACT benefit in colorectal cancer. The investigators suggested that only patients with a low LNR (range: 0.05-0.18) derived a survival benefit[39]. In our cohort, poor prognostic features such as BRAF mutation and elevated CEA levels were more frequently observed in the high LNR group. Although a statistically significant survival benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) was not demonstrated in this subgroup, a numerically favorable trend was observed. These findings suggest that conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy may still have activity in patients with high LNR; however, the magnitude of benefit appeared more pronounced in those with low LNR. While one possible explanation is that the higher prevalence of biologically aggressive features in the high-LNR subgroup may attenuate the relative impact of chemotherapy, this remains speculative and warrants further investigation with a larger sample size and incorporating additional molecular biomarkers, such as circulating tumor DNA. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a definitive biological mechanism.

This study has three main limitations. Firstly, its retrospective design with a limited sample size may have introduced residual confounding factors, potentially reducing the statistical power of the findings. Secondly, as briefly mentioned earlier, LNR has an inherent limitation in that it can be influenced by the total number of harvested LNs, which in turn may reflect surgical technique and pathological processing quality rather than tumor burden alone. Therefore, variability in lymph node yield may have partially affected the LNR values observed in this study. However, as this was a single-center study, surgical procedures and pathological assessments were relatively standardized, which may have mitigated this variability to some extent. Thirdly, the duration and regimen of ACT were not standardized. It would be valuable to perform a similar analysis using prospective clinical trial data, in which the quality of surgery and ACT are well defined according to protocol. Despite these limitations, our findings highlight the need to further explore the association between LNR and molecular profiles, as well as the potential predictive role of LNR in stratifying ACT.

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that an LNR cutoff value of 0.2 remains a valid prognostic factor in pathological stage III CC, despite the availability of multiple molecular biomarkers. ACT was associated with improved survival across LNR subgroups, with the benefit being more evident in patients with low LNR. Reassessment in larger, protocol-defined cohorts incorporating additional relevant biomarkers is needed to confirm these findings.
