Primary prevention statin eligibility for U.S. adults under the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline
Authors: Mohammed Essa, Devesh Malik, Sukrit Narula, Huanhuan Yang, Yuan Lu, Erica S Spatz, Kamil F Faridi
Published: 2026

Introduction

Lowering low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is central to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) prevention, and guidelines recommend statins based on predicted ASCVD risk. Unlike older guidelines, [1] the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline for primary prevention uses the PREVENT equations, expands eligibility to adults aged 30-79 years, and incorporates 30-year ASCVD risk estimates [2,3]. However, the population-level impact of these updates, including which criteria most significantly impact statin eligibility and current utilization of lipid-lowering therapy (LLT), remains unknown. Therefore, we used nationally representative data to evaluate statin eligibility under the 2026 guideline compared with prior recommendations in the U.S. primary prevention population.

Methods

We used cross-sectional data from the 2011–2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to estimate the national prevalence of statin eligibility under the U.S. 2018 and 2026 blood cholesterol/dyslipidemia guidelines for all adults aged 30-79 years without ASCVD. Eligibility was determined for the entire primary prevention population and for adults without diabetes who had LDL-C <190 mg/dL. We also assessed the proportion of adults taking LLT based on the 2026 guideline recommendations. Prevalence estimates were survey-weighted and age-standardized to the 2020 U.S. Census. This study followed STROBE guidelines and was exempt from review by the Yale Institutional Review Board because the NHANES data are deidentified and publicly available.

Results

Among 8972 participants representing 152.6 million U.S. adults aged 30–79 without ASCVD (mean [SD] age, 51 [12] years; 51.7% women), age-standardized statin eligibility increased from 42.7% (95% CI, 41.5–43.9%) under the 2018 guideline to 62.9% (95% CI, 61.8–64.0%) under the 2026 guideline, corresponding to 31.3 million (95% CI, 28.9–33.8 million) newly eligible adults. Among these eligible adults who were not previously indicated for therapy under the 2018 guideline, 11.4% (95% CI, 9.3–13.8%) had a 2026 Class 1 indication (2.8 million) and 88.6% (95% CI, 86.2–90.7%) had a Class 2a indication only (8.3 million with PREVENT 10-year ASCVD risk 3-<5% and LDL-C 70–189 mg/dL; 6.4 million with 10-year risk <3% and LDL-C 160–189 mg/dL; and 13.8 million with 10-year risk <3% LDL-C 70–159 mg/dL and PREVENT 30-year ASCVD risk ≥10%). Among newly eligible adults aged 30–59 years (25.1 million), 97.8% (95% CI, 96.8–98.6%) had a Class 2a with no Class 1 indication, and 57.6% (95% CI, 53.8–61.3%) had low 10-year PREVENT ASCVD risk (<3%) but met eligibility with 30-year risk ≥10%. Only 2 of 3268 untreated participants were eligible under the 2018 but not the 2026 guideline.

Both men and women had increases in statin eligibility, with men having higher overall eligibility under both guidelines (71.9% [95% CI, 70.8–73.1%] under 2026 and 50.8% [95% CI, 49.3–52.3%] under 2018) compared with women (54.2% [95% CI, 52.6–55.8%] under 2026 and 35.4% [95% CI, 33.7–37.1%] under 2018). Eligibility among adults with diabetes (24.7 million) increased from 79.2% (95% CI, 76.9–81.2%) to 90.4% (95% CI, 87.7–92.5%), representing 1.2 million newly eligible adults. All adults with LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL (4.5 million) were eligible under both guidelines.

In the total population eligible under the 2026 guideline and not already on LLT, 42.0% (95% CI, 39.7 – 44.3%) met a 2026 Class 1 indication (29.0 million) and 57.9% (95% CI, 55.7–60.3%) met a Class 2a indication (12.8 million with PREVENT 10-year ASCVD risk 3-<5% and LDL-C 70–189 mg/dL, 6.5 million with 10-year risk <3% and LDL-C 160–189 mg/dL, and 14.2 million with 10-year risk <3%, LDL-C 70–159 mg/dL, and PREVENT 30-year ASCVD risk ≥10%). Only 29.7% (95% CI, 27.7–31.8%) of 2026-eligible adults were currently on LLT, including 41.2% (95% CI, 37.7–44.8%) of those with diabetes and 25.9% (95% CI, 23.6–28.5%) without diabetes.

In analyses for adults without diabetes and with LDL-C <190 mg/dL (n = 6862 participants representing 124.1 million adults), statin eligibility prevalence increased from 33.7% (95% CI, 32.5–34.8%) under the 2018 guideline to 58.3% (95% CI, 57.2–59.4%) under the 2026 guideline, corresponding to 30.1 million newly eligible adults. Among these newly eligible adults not already on LLT, 10.5% (95% CI, 8.5–12.9%) met a 2026 Class 1 and 89.5% (95% CI, 87.1-91.5%) met a Class 2a indication without meeting Class 1 criteria. Statin eligibility in this population increased with age (Table), with the largest absolute increase among adults aged 50–59 years (from 32.7% to 79.4%; 13.0 million newly eligible). The highest prevalence of statin eligibility was among adults aged 70-79, with 99.1% (95% CI, 98.1-99.6%) eligible under the 2026 guidelines. Men had higher eligibility than women, and increases in eligibility were observed across all race/ethnicity groups (Table). Among untreated adults with pooled cohort equation 10-year ASCVD risk ≥7.5%, 11.7% (95% CI, 8.9-15.2%) had low PREVENT 10-year ASCVD risk <3%, 37.3% (95% CI, 32.1-42.9%) had risk 3–<5%, 44.0% (95% CI, 39.6-48.5%) had risk 5-<10%, and 6.9% (95% CI, 5.4–8.7%) had risk ≥10%. Only 26.6% (95% CI, 24.0–29.4%) of 2026-eligible adults without diabetes and with LDL-C <190 mg/dL used LLT, with variability by age (Table).

Discussion

In this nationally representative study, we found that over 60% of U.S. adults in the primary prevention population aged 30–79 years have an indication for statin therapy under the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline. The new guideline substantially expands statin eligibility compared with the 2018 AHA/ACC cholesterol guidelines, driven almost entirely by new Class 2a recommendations for adults with borderline or low 10-year ASCVD risks. Only 27% of eligible adults in the recent era were taking LLT based on updated recommendations for adults without diabetes who had LDL-C <190 mg/dL.

Our study further demonstrates how statin eligibility and class of recommendation vary substantially by age. Inclusion of 30-year risk thresholds as a Class 2a recommendation for treatment specifically in adults younger than 60 years resulted in the greatest total expansion of eligibility. Importantly, due to use of lower risk thresholds, nearly all U.S. adults eligible under the 2018 guideline were also eligible under the 2026 guideline. We additionally found that statin eligibility was nearly universal at older ages (e.g., >95% of men ≥50 years and ∼99% of all adults 70–79 years met Class 1 or 2a recommendations). Since fewer than a third of eligible adults were on LLT and the vast majority of the newly eligible population qualified for statins based on Class 2a recommendations, broad adoption of shared decision-making, along with consideration of risk-enhancing factors and coronary artery calcium (CAC) assessment, will be needed to ensure successful guideline implementation.

This study has several limitations. Given that the data were cross-sectional, we could not assess how changes in risk factors for individuals may have impacted risk and statin eligibility. Though we accounted for non-response in survey-weighted estimates, our findings may not be generalizable to non-participants. We also did not have data for many risk-enhancing factors (e.g., lipoprotein[a]) or CAC scores, which would have impacted statin eligibility.

In conclusion, more than three in five U.S. adults aged 30–79 without ASCVD have a statin indication for primary prevention under the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline. The new guideline greatly expands statin eligibility through new Class 2a recommendations which apply to younger adults and those with low or borderline 10-year ASCVD risk. More than two-thirds of adults with a primary prevention statin indication based on the 2026 guideline were not on LLT, highlighting the need to implement these new recommendations into current practice.
