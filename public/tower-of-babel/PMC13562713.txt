Strain transfer efficiency model and finite element simulation experiment for end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors
Authors: Xianhuan Luo, Baowu Zhang, Kai Chen, Lu Peng, Bo Tang, Bing Chai, Jianjun Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
FBG sensors are widely used in damage monitoring of wind turbine blades. Due to the adhesive layer, strain transfer loss occurs when blade strain is transmitted to the fiber Bragg grating. This paper proposes a strain transfer efficiency model for the end-bonded method of substrate-type FBG sensors, and conducts numerical analysis on the elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and thickness of the bonding adhesive layer, as well as the gauge length of the FBG sensors. Finally, finite element simulation is used to verify the accuracy of the derivation. The results of numerical analysis and finite element simulation show that the proposed model can describe the law of strain transfer in the end-bonded structure of substrate-type FBG sensors and reveal the influence mechanism of each key parameter on the transfer efficiency.

Introduction

It is reported that the total installed capacity of wind energy will reach 414 GW by 20301. As the core components for converting wind energy into electrical energy, wind turbine blades directly affect power generation efficiency and operational stability. Therefore, effective condition monitoring of wind turbine blades is essential for ensuring the safe and reliable operation of wind turbines.

Current monitoring methods for wind turbine blades mainly include noise analysis2–4, vibration analysis5,6, acoustic emission techniques7,8, and image recognition methods9–11. Due to their advantages of miniaturization, light weight, and immunity to electromagnetic interference12,13, fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors have been increasingly applied in structural damage monitoring of wind turbine blades.

In recent years, several studies have explored the application of FBG sensors in this field. Wang et al.14 attached multiple FBG sensors to wind turbine blades to identify impact locations. Yan et al.15 employed FBG sensors for crack detection, while Wu et al.16 proposed a Z-shaped FBG sensor capable of simultaneous strain and temperature measurement. However, when FBG sensors are surface-bonded to blades, the presence of an intermediate adhesive layer affects measurement accuracy. Liu et al.17 demonstrated that the length and thickness of the adhesive layer significantly influence strain transfer efficiency. Zhu et al.18 experimentally investigated strain response under various loading conditions and found that both the size and material of the adhesive layer affect strain transfer, although no analytical model was established.

In addition, studies on strain transfer mechanisms of bonded optical fiber sensors have been conducted. Chen et al.19 derived strain transfer models for substrate-free and small-diameter sensors and analyzed the effects of adhesive properties and structural parameters. Falcetelli et al.20 investigated the influence of bonding length and loading conditions on strain transfer efficiency in surface-bonded optical fiber sensors. Nevertheless, existing models are not directly applicable to substrate-type packaged FBG sensors. Beyond linear elastic models, the viscoelastic behavior of adhesives has also been considered in strain transfer analysis21–23. Furthermore, environmental factors such as hygrothermal aging have been shown to affect adhesive joint performance and FBG strain transfer accuracy24,25. In the context of composite structures, FBG sensors have been increasingly applied for damage detection in composite wind turbine blades26,27.

Nevertheless, most existing strain transfer models—including the multilayer model proposed by Wang et al.28. for surface-bonded FBG sensors—assume a continuous bonding configuration, i.e., the adhesive layer is present along the entire length of the sensor. In practical calibration and measurement scenarios, however, substrate-type FBG sensors are often end-bonded: the sensor is attached to the host structure only at its two ends, leaving the central grating region free from direct adhesive contact. This end-bonded configuration fundamentally changes the shear stress distribution and boundary conditions compared to continuous bonding. As a result, existing continuous-bonding models are not directly applicable to end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors; applying them would lead to inaccurate predictions of strain transfer efficiency. This end-bonded mounting method is particularly relevant for calibration of substrate-type FBG sensors, where the sensor is typically mounted onto a reference substrate using only two end adhesive joints to enable repeatable and traceable strain transfer.

To address this limitation, this paper proposes a strain transfer efficiency model for end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors, which better reflects their structural characteristics. First, the strain transfer model is derived analytically. Then, numerical analysis is performed to evaluate the effects of adhesive layer material properties and thickness, as well as sensor gauge length, on strain transfer efficiency. Finite element simulations are subsequently conducted to validate the proposed model. Finally, based on the combined results of numerical analysis and simulation, recommended parameter ranges for the adhesive layer and sensor gauge length are provided.

For the convenience of analysis, the following assumptions are made17,19,20: (1) All bonding interfaces are continuous without relative slip; (2) All adhesive materials are linearly elastic and isotropic; (3) Since the thickness of each layer is relatively small, the axial stress distribution across the layer cross-section can be considered uniform; (4) The strain distribution of the base body remains unchanged after bonding; (5) The strain variation gradient of each layer is consistent.

The above assumptions follow common practices in strain transfer analysis. Assumption 1 holds under small strains and well-bonded conditions; excessive loads or aging may cause local debonding. Assumption 2 is adequate for epoxy and metal within elastic limits but fails under viscoelastic or large-strain conditions. Assumption 3 is justified by the small thickness-to-length ratio (< 0.01 in this study), though end effects exist within about one thickness distance. Assumption 4 requires that the sensor/adhesive stiffness be negligible (< 5% of host), which is satisfied here; for flexible substrates, stiffening effects should be considered. Assumption 5 follows from deformation compatibility; large modulus mismatch (e.g., FBG vs. adhesive) may enhance shear lag. Overall, the model is applicable to small-strain, linear-elastic conditions.

Based on the numerical results presented in Figs. 3, 4 and 5, the relative importance of the four parameters on strain transfer efficiency can be ranked as follows. Within the typical ranges examined, the elastic modulus of the bonding adhesive layer exhibits the most pronounced effect: increasing it from 0.5 GPa to 2 GPa raises the efficiency from approximately 85% to 93%, after which the gain diminishes. The adhesive layer thickness shows a nearly linear inverse relationship: a 0.24 mm increase (from 0.10 mm to 0.34 mm) reduces efficiency by about 4%. Therefore, for design optimization of end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors, priority should be given to selecting an adhesive with sufficiently high elastic modulus (≥ 2 GPa) and controlling the adhesive layer thickness within 0.15–0.25 mm. Gauge length can be adjusted as a secondary factor, while Poisson’s ratio can be treated as of minor concern within the studied range.

At both ends, the strain exhibits sharp variations due to bonding boundary effects and other factors, which do not accurately reflect the strain of the main structure. A longer gauge length increases the absolute length of the uniform strain region (the effective sensing zone), thereby reducing the relative contribution of the edge transition zones and improving the accuracy of strain averaging. Conversely, if the gauge length is too short, the entire FBG may lie within the boundary‑affected regions, leading to significant underestimation of the true substrate strain.

Figure 10 shows the effect of bonding adhesive layer thickness on the strain transfer efficiency of FBG sensors, along with the relative error between simulation and theoretical results. The analysis indicates that when the adhesive layer thickness is approximately 0.15 to 0.25 mm, the relative error remains within ± 2%. This range represents the optimal thickness for the bonding layer, providing both reasonable strain transfer efficiency and high agreement between finite element simulations and theoretical predictions. As the adhesive layer thickens, the assumptions of uniform axial stress across the layer cross-section and negligible shear deformation within the adhesive become less accurate. The theoretical model, which simplifies the adhesive as an ideal shear-lag medium, underestimates the actual strain gradient and shear lag effect that become more pronounced in thicker bonds. In contrast, the finite element simulation captures the full three-dimensional stress distribution, including edge effects and non-uniform shear strain through the adhesive thickness. Consequently, the divergence between the two methods grows when the thickness exceeds approximately 0.30 mm. These results offer guidance for the optimal design of bonding adhesive layer thickness in FBG sensor applications for wind turbine blade damage monitoring, enhancing the accuracy and reliability of strain measurements.

Figure 11 illustrates the effect of FBG sensor gauge length on strain transfer efficiency, along with the relative error between finite element simulation and numerical analysis results. Compared with the influence of the bonding adhesive layer, the effect of gauge length on strain transfer efficiency is smaller, with the relative error remaining within ± 0.5% across the analyzed range. When the gauge length is approximately 16 to 20 mm, the relative error is around ± 0.2%. This range represents the optimal selection for FBG sensors, ensuring reasonable strain transfer efficiency and high consistency between simulation and theoretical results. The recommended gauge length range of 16–20 mm is well aligned with commercially available FBG products, where 8 mm, 12 mm, 18 mm, and 22 mm are standard lengths30–33. Custom lengths are generally available upon request. Thus, the recommended range is both theoretically validated and practically feasible.

Basis for optimal parameter ranges: The recommended adhesive thickness of 0.15–0.25 mm is selected because (i) within this range, the relative error between theoretical and FEM results is ≤ ± 2% (Fig. 10), which is acceptable for typical blade strain measurements; (ii) thicknesses below 0.15 mm are difficult to achieve reliably in practice due to surface roughness and clamping tolerances, while thicknesses above 0.25 mm lead to rapidly increasing error ( > ± 5% at 0.34 mm). The recommended gauge length of 16–20 mm is selected because (i) within this range, the relative error is ≤ ± 0.2% (Fig. 11), indicating excellent model accuracy; (ii) shorter lengths (< 16 mm) suffer from edge effect contamination, while longer lengths (> 20 mm) offer diminishing accuracy gains and may exceed practical packaging constraints. These ranges thus balance accuracy, manufacturability, and geometric compatibility.

These findings provide a reference for optimizing gauge length to achieve more accurate strain monitoring in FBG sensor applications for wind turbine blade damage detection. The parametric analysis above treats each factor individually. In practice, the influences of adhesive layer thickness, elastic modulus, and FBG gauge length on strain transfer efficiency are not completely independent but may interact with each other. As can be inferred from the trends in Figs. 3, 4 and 5 and a thicker adhesive layer reduces transfer efficiency, while a higher elastic modulus or a longer gauge length improves it. It is reasonable to hypothesize that when two parameters vary simultaneously, their combined effect might be approximately additive, given that the strain transfer efficiency expression in Eq. However, a rigorous demonstration of this superposition would require additional systematic simulations or experiments, which are beyond the scope of the present work. For instance, increasing the adhesive layer thickness from 0.15 mm to 0.25 mm causes a certain loss in efficiency, but this loss can be partially compensated by selecting an adhesive with a higher elastic modulus (e.g., from 2 GPa to 4 GPa) or by moderately increasing the gauge length (e.g., from 16 mm to 20 mm). Conversely, if the adhesive layer is too thin (below 0.10 mm), even a high elastic modulus may lead to excessive shear stress concentration at the end bonds, which is not captured by the isolated parameter study. Therefore, the optimal parameter ranges recommended in Table 3 serve as a practical starting point, and future work should investigate potential parameter interactions in more detail.

Conclusion

This paper presents an in-depth investigation of the strain transfer behavior of substrate-type FBG sensors for wind turbine blade damage monitoring, yielding the following key findings. A strain transfer efficiency model for end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors was established, and numerical analysis was conducted on critical parameters including the elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and thickness of the bonding adhesive layer, as well as the FBG sensor gauge length. Finite element simulations were performed to verify the results, revealing the influence of each parameter on strain transfer efficiency. Specifically, increasing the elastic modulus of the adhesive layer improves strain transfer efficiency, Poisson’s ratio has a minor effect, and increasing the adhesive layer thickness exacerbates strain transfer loss. For the FBG sensor gauge length, an optimal range exists that minimizes the relative error between simulation and numerical analysis results.

Practical contribution: The study provides a theoretical basis and practical design guidelines for optimizing end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors. The recommended parameter ranges (Table 3) can assist engineers in selecting appropriate adhesive materials, controlling adhesive layer thickness, and choosing suitable sensor gauge length to reduce strain transfer losses and improve measurement accuracy in structural health monitoring of wind turbine blades. To implement these recommendations in practice: control adhesive thickness to 0.15–0.25 mm using shims or precision dispensing; select a commercially available FBG gauge length of 16–20 mm; ensure clean, abraded bonding surfaces and proper alignment; and follow recommended curing protocols. Under these conditions, the end-bonded configuration yields a predictable strain transfer efficiency with ± 3–5% uncertainty, suitable for sensor calibration and damage monitoring. In the broader context of bonded composite systems and structural health monitoring, the understanding of strain transfer mechanisms is essential for reliable damage detection and durability assessment34,35.

Literature on bonded composite interfaces has shown that environmental aging and fatigue loading significantly degrade joint performance and alter interfacial stress distributions36–38. Consequently, the predicted strain transfer efficiency should be interpreted as a reference for design optimization under controlled or quasi-static laboratory conditions, rather than an absolute value for all field scenarios.

Expected influence of environmental aging, fatigue, and viscoelasticity: For wind turbine blade applications, these factors can notably affect strain transfer accuracy. Hygrothermal aging reduces adhesive stiffness and strength, potentially lowering strain transfer efficiency by an estimated 5–15% under severe conditions. Viscoelastic creep under sustained static load may cause a drift in FBG strain readings of approximately 4–7%, depending on bonding length. Fatigue/cyclic loading leads to progressive stiffness degradation and reduced transfer reliability compared to static conditions, with the damage initiation life typically occupying a substantial portion of the total fatigue life. These estimates are illustrative ranges derived from adhesive joint and bonded FBG sensor studies; a precise quantification for the end-bonded configuration requires dedicated future work.

Consequently, the predicted strain transfer efficiency should be interpreted as a reference for design optimization under controlled or quasi-static laboratory conditions, rather than an absolute value for all field scenarios.

To support practical applicability, we provide the following quantitative error estimates when some of the idealized assumptions are partially violated :

Perfect bonding: For a 10% end-debonding, strain transfer efficiency drops by 8–12%; under well-bonded conditions, error ≤ ± 2%.

Linear elastic adhesive: Within typical blade strains (< 2000 µε), error < 1% relative to viscoelastic model; above 5000 µε or > 50 °C, error may reach 5–10%.

Uniform axial stress across thickness: For adhesive thickness hj = 0.15–0.25 mm, theoretical vs. FEM error ≤ ± 2% (Fig. 10); for hj > 0.30 mm, error ≤ ± 5–8%.

Negligible sensor stiffening: For steel substrates, error < 0.5%; for composites, error 2–5%.

Under recommended conditions, the overall uncertainty in predicted strain transfer efficiency from these idealized assumptions is estimated as ± 3–5%, supporting the model‘s utility for design optimization and calibration of end-bonded substrate-type FBG sensors.

Future research: Future work should extend the model to account for dynamic and fatigue loading, incorporate the effects of temperature and humidity, and investigate the influence of anisotropic substrates. Experimental validation under realistic service conditions, including long-term aging and cyclic loading, is also needed. Furthermore, the impact of bonding quality—such as curing protocols, residual stresses, and alignment errors—should be systematically studied to bridge the gap between idealized models and practical sensor installations.
