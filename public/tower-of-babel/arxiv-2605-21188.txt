A Terrain-Adaptive ϵ \epsilon -Constraint MPC for Uneven Terrain Kinodynamic Planning
Otobong Jerome, Geesara Kalathunga, Tiago Nascimento

Abstract
Kinodynamic planning for car-like vehicles on uneven terrain requires simultaneously optimizing competing objectives such as path efficiency and pose stability. This work presents an adaptive ϵ \epsilon -constraint method integrated into a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework, where the ϵ \epsilon bounds are dynamically adjusted based on terrain descriptors to explore the Pareto front in real time. To capture vehicle-terrain dynamics, we develop a semi-parametric model combining analytical vehicle dynamics with a Sparse Gaussian Process (SGP) trained on the same terrain descriptors. The proposed ϵ \epsilon -MPC is evaluated against MPPI and GAKD baselines, achieving a 94 % 94\% navigation success rate while reducing maximum orientation deviation by 24 % 24\% and improving multi-objective trade-off quality by 23 % 23\% .

Various methods have been proposed for generating triangular meshes from sensor data

However, navigating through complex terrains still poses significant challenges for autonomous robots, especially in unstructured environments such as outdoor construction sites, disaster zones, or planetary exploration. Robust navigation in these settings requires planners that not only compute collision-free paths but also respect the kinodynamic constraints imposed by the robot’s dynamics, including velocity, acceleration limits however accurate vehicle-terrain modelling is challenging due to the effects of the terrain.

Traditional mesh planners, such as the continuous vector planner (CVP)

Despite their success in producing terrain-aware geometric paths, they do not explicitly incorporate kinodynamic constraints, limiting their applicability to scenarios where dynamic feasibility is critical.

Kinodynamic planning addresses motion planning problems under both kinematic and dynamic constraints.
The first polynomial-time, provably good approximation to the solution was presented in

Gradient-free methods like Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control are favoured in the former for managing complex dynamics without explicit gradients. Data-driven methods use models such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to learn system dynamics and optimize planning in high-dimensional or partially observed settings.

Recent advances in DRL have demonstrated potential for learning complex navigation policies in dynamic environments
[
9
,
13
]
, yet several critical limitations persist. Most existing approaches are confined to 2D planning and sparse reward functions, with poor generalization across diverse start-goal configurations. Furthermore, current DRL-based planners lack integration with continuous global guidance structures and do not account for kinodynamic constraints, particularly on uneven terrain. Standard RL reward formulations also fail to capture the multi-objective nature of kinodynamic planning, which can be solved using an
\epsilon
-constraint methods, where path optimality and physical stability must be jointly considered. Although,

A solution approach based on terrain-adaptive epsilon-constraint optimization within a receding horizon control framework for generating non-dominated trajectories.

The
\epsilon

A scaled continuous vector field is leveraged to provide global guidance, enabling consistent performance across diverse mesh environments. The proposed terrain-adaptive epsilon-constraint method systematically explore the trade-off frontier between competing objectives. The proposed semi-parametric vehicle-terrain dynamics model, enables offline development and generalization for online deployment.

III
Methodology

The uneven terrain planning is formulated as a receding horizon kinodynamic problem, seeking dynamically feasible trajectories that optimize multiple objectives while using the FMM vector field for global guidance.
The problem is solved as an epsilon-constraint optimization: at each planning iteration, forward trajectories are simulated, and the sequence minimizing a progress objective is selected subject to a hard constraint whose tolerance is dynamically adjusted to local terrain characteristics. Candidate sequences are produced through warm-starting and Fast Marching Method (FMM)-biased sampling, by which exploration is guided toward promising regions of the environment. Along each trajectory, terrain descriptors are extracted and orientation deviations from a terrain-aligned reference frame are computed, yielding a physically meaningful stability risk measure. The resulting receding-horizon controller adapts both its constraint threshold and sampling strategy to the terrain being traversed, ensuring less computation on benign terrain while conservative behaviour is automatically enforced in challenging conditions.

III-A

Problem Formulation

The system is described by:
\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathfrak{d}_{i},\Delta t)
with
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{\text{start}}
and goal
\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}
. At each planning iteration
k
, a multi-objective optimization is solved over horizon
H
from the current state
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, producing an optimized trajectory:
\mathcal{T}_{k}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k})\mid i=0,\ldots,H-1\},\quad\mathbf{x}_{0}^{k}=\mathbf{x}_{k}.
The first control action is then executed, the state is advanced to
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{M}^{k}
, and the plan is recomputed. This process is repeated until
\|\mathbf{p}_{k}-\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}\|<\delta_{\text{term}}
.
Two competing objectives are defined to characterize the quality of a trajectory within each planning horizon:

Objective 1: Path Length.
Path length is minimized by penalizing cumulative directional misalignment with the FMM vector field over the planning horizon:

f_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{H}\left(1-\frac{\mathbf{d}_{i}^{k}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{P,i}^{k}}{\|\mathbf{d}_{i}^{k}\|\,\|\mathbf{d}_{P,i}^{k}\|}\right)s(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}),

where
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{k}=\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}-\mathbf{p}_{i-1}^{k}
is the displacement vector between consecutive states,
\mathbf{d}_{P,i}^{k}
is the ideal direction from the FMM field interpolated at position
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}
, and
s(\mathbf{p})
is a goal-proximity scaling factor:

s(\mathbf{p})=1-\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}\|}{d_{\text{max}}}\right)^{\mathfrak{s}},

where
d_{\text{max}}
is the maximum finite distance from vertices on the mesh to the goal and
\mathfrak{s}>1
controls the rate of intensification near the goal as illustrated in
3
.

Objective 2: Pose Stability.
Cumulative orientation violations relative to the local terrain are minimized to prevent tip-over. For each state, the terrain-aligned orientation is computed from the local terrain normal
\mathbf{n}_{i}^{k}=[n_{x},n_{y},n_{z}]^{T}
extracted from the neighbourhood descriptor
\mathfrak{d}_{i}^{k}
:

\theta_{\text{terrain},i}^{k}=\arctan 2(n_{y},n_{z}),\quad\phi_{\text{terrain},i}^{k}=\arctan 2(-n_{x},n_{z}),

defining the orientation perpendicular to the terrain at
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}
. The deviation from terrain-aligned orientation is:

\Delta\theta_{i}^{k}=\theta_{i}^{k}-\theta_{\text{terrain},i}^{k},\quad\Delta\phi_{i}^{k}=\phi_{i}^{k}-\phi_{\text{terrain},i}^{k}.

The stability objective penalizes excessive deviations:

f_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{k})=\sum_{i=0}^{H}\left[v_{\text{tilt}}(\Delta\theta_{i}^{k})+v_{\text{tilt}}(\Delta\phi_{i}^{k})\right],

where the tilt violation function is:

v_{\text{tilt}}(\Delta\alpha)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }|\Delta\alpha|\leq\alpha_{\text{safe}}\\
\kappa\left(|\Delta\alpha|-\alpha_{\text{safe}}\right)^{2},&\text{if }\alpha_{\text{safe}}<|\Delta\alpha|<\alpha_{\text{max}}\\
+\infty,&\text{if }|\Delta\alpha|\geq\alpha_{\text{max}}\end{cases},

with
\kappa>0
a penalty coefficient,
\alpha_{\text{safe}}
the safe deviation angle from terrain-normal, and
\alpha_{\text{max}}
the maximum allowable deviation before tip-over.

III-B

Vehicle-Terrain Modelling

Control inputs consist of a linear velocity
u_{0}
along the vehicle’s forward
x
-axis and angular velocity
u_{1}
about the vertical
z
-axis, applied for duration
\Delta t
. Since exact dynamics on uneven terrain are difficult to estimate, a residual learning approach is adopted to correct errors between an approximate model and the actual system, balancing model fidelity with the fast computation required for real-time MPC.

Let:

\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}\\
\boldsymbol{\Theta}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\\
\phi\\
\theta\\
\psi\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{0}\\
u_{1}\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathbf{p}=\begin{bmatrix}x&y&z\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is the position, and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi&\theta&\psi\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
represents roll, pitch, and yaw angles. The vehicle dynamics on uneven terrain, modelled in discrete time with step size
\Delta t
, are given by:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}+u_{0,k}\cos\theta_{k}\cos\psi_{k}\Delta t\\
y_{k}+u_{0,k}\cos\theta_{k}\sin\psi_{k}\Delta t\\
h(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\\
\phi_{k+1}\\
\theta_{k+1}\\
\psi_{k}+u_{1,k}\Delta t\end{bmatrix}

where the roll and pitch angles at the next timestep,
\phi_{k+1}
and
\theta_{k+1}
, are computed from the terrain gradient using the function:

\phi_{k+1}=\operatorname{atan2}\left(-\delta_{y}h(x_{k+1},y_{k+1}),\sqrt{1+\left[\delta_{x}h(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\right]^{2}}\right)

\theta_{k+1}=\operatorname{atan2}\left(\delta_{x}h(x_{k+1},y_{k+1}),\sqrt{1+\left[\delta_{y}h(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})\right]^{2}}\right)

\delta_{x}h(x,y)\approx\frac{h(x+\mathfrak{e},y)-h(x-\mathfrak{e},y)}{2\mathfrak{e}}

\delta_{y}h(x,y)\approx\frac{h(x,y+\mathfrak{e})-h(x,y-\mathfrak{e})}{2\mathfrak{e}}

Here,
h(x,y)
is the terrain height function, and
\mathfrak{e}
is a small positive constant.

III-C

Terrain Height Function
h(x,y)
:

To determine the terrain height at a given horizontal coordinate
(x,y)
, we project the query point vertically onto the terrain mesh
\mathcal{M}
. Let the query point be
\mathbf{q}=(x,y,z_{0})
, where
z_{0}
is an arbitrary height above the terrain. Among all faces
F_{j}\in\mathcal{M}
, we select the closest face
F^{*}
in terms of vertical (signed) distance to the plane, defined by its known normal
\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)
and center
\mathbf{p}_{\text{center}}
. The corresponding plane equation is:

ax+by+cz+d=0,\quad\text{with}\quad d=-\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{\text{center}}.

We compute the signed distance
\gamma
from the query point to the face plane:

\gamma=\frac{ax+by+cz_{0}+d}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}}}.

The projection of the query point onto the face plane is then:

\mathbf{p}_{\text{proj}}=\mathbf{q}-\gamma\mathbf{n}.

The terrain height is defined as the
z
-component of the projected point:

h(x,y)=\left[\mathbf{p}_{\text{proj}}\right]_{z}.

This process ensures that
h(x,y)
accounts for arbitrary terrain orientation by selecting the face whose plane is closest to the vertical projection of the query point.

Local Terrain Descriptor

To improve the accuracy of the proposed analytic model, we define a local terrain descriptor
\mathfrak{d}=[n_{x},n_{y},n_{z},\sigma_{z},K(\mathbf{q}),H(\mathbf{q}),A_{\text{total}}]
that encodes geometric features of the terrain around the vehicle’s footprint. The first three components are derived from the area- and distance-weighted average normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{\text{avg}}={\sum_{i}w_{i}\mathbf{n}_{i}}/{\left\|\sum_{i}w_{i}\mathbf{n}_{i}\right\|}
, with weights
w_{i}={A_{i}}/{(d_{i}+\mathfrak{e}_{s})}
, where
\mathbf{n}_{i}
,
A_{i}
, and
d_{i}
are the normal, area, and centroid distance of neighbouring triangle
T_{i}
, and
\mathfrak{e}_{s}
is a small stabilising constant. Surface roughness is captured by the height variation
\sigma_{z}=\sqrt{{1}/{N}\sum_{i}(z_{i}-\bar{z})^{2}}
, where
z_{i}
are centroid elevations within the local neighbourhood. For curvature, we extend the discrete differential geometry operators of
[
15
]
to arbitrary query points through distance-weighted interpolation
\omega_{v}={1}/({\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}_{v}\|+\mathfrak{e}_{s}})
over vertices
v\in\mathfrak{N}(\mathbf{q})
within a specified radius. The discrete Gaussian curvature at each vertex is computed via the angle defect formula
K_{v}=({2\pi-\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{f}}\theta_{j}})/{A_{\text{Mixed}}^{v}}
, where
\theta_{j}
are face angles at vertex
v
and
A_{\text{Mixed}}^{v}
is the mixed Voronoi area
[
15
]
. The discrete mean curvature is obtained from the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{p}_{v})=\frac{1}{2A_{\text{Mixed}}^{v}}\sum_{u\in\mathfrak{N}(v)}(\cot\alpha_{vu}+\cot\beta_{vu})(\mathbf{p}_{v}-\mathbf{p}_{u})
, yielding
H_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{p}_{v})\|
, where
\alpha_{vu}
and
\beta_{vu}
are the angles opposite edge
(v,u)
in adjacent triangles. The mixed area
A_{\text{Mixed}}^{v}
ensures robust computation: for non-obtuse triangles the Voronoi region area is used, while for obtuse triangles the appropriate fraction of the triangle area is substituted. The interpolated curvatures
K(\mathbf{q})
and
H(\mathbf{q})
are then computed as the weighted averages over neighbouring vertices, enabling continuous curvature fields queryable at arbitrary locations. The final component,
A_{\text{total}}=\sum_{i}A_{i}
, captures the neighbourhood extent as the total area of triangles within the local radius.

III-E

Learning a Residual Model with Sparse Gaussian Process

While the nominal model provides a baseline prediction, unmodelled effects of the terrain can cause deviations. We model this deviation as a learned residual using a Sparse Gaussian Process (SGP) regressor.Let the predicted state from the nominal model be
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}
, and the true observed state be
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{\text{true}}
. The residual is:
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{x}_{k+1}^{\text{true}}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}
.We model the residual as:

\Delta\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{f}_{\text{res}}(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathfrak{d}_{k})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},
, where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariance
\Sigma
. The SGP output is the 6D residual:
\Delta\mathbf{x}_{k}=[\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z,\Delta\phi,\Delta\theta,\Delta\psi]^{\top}
. The corrected next state is obtained as:
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}+\Delta\mathbf{x}_{k}
. This residual correction improves prediction accuracy by capturing non-linear terrain-induced effects learned from data as shown in Figure

At each planning iteration
k
, we employ the terrain-adaptive epsilon-constraint method to transform the bi-objective problem into a constrained single-objective optimization over the horizon
H
,
\epsilon_{\text{stab}}
is dynamically computed based on the current terrain characteristics at the robot’s position. This adaptation makes the planner more conservative in challenging terrain while permitting greater exploration in benign conditions.
At each planning iteration
k
, we solve the problem:

\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}\quad&f_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\\
\text{subject to}\quad&\mathbf{x}_{i+1}^{k}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k},\mathfrak{d}_{i},\Delta t),\quad i=0,\ldots,H-1,\\
&f_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\leq\epsilon_{\text{stab}}(\mathfrak{d}_{k}),\\
&\mathbf{x}_{0}^{k}=\mathbf{x}_{k},\\
&\mathbf{p}_{i}^{k}\in\mathcal{F},\quad i=0,\ldots,H,\\
&\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}\in\mathcal{U},\quad i=0,\ldots,H-1,\end{aligned}

where
\mathcal{F}
represents the collision-free navigation space on the mesh,
\mathcal{U}
represents the admissible control set, and
\epsilon_{\text{stab}}(\mathfrak{d}_{k})
is the terrain-adaptive maximum allowable cumulative stability violation over the planning horizon, computed based on the current terrain descriptor
\mathfrak{d}_{k}
at the robot’s position. The stability constraint threshold
\epsilon_{\text{stab}}
adapts to local terrain difficulty through the following formulation:
\epsilon_{\text{stab}}(\mathfrak{d})=\epsilon_{\text{base}}\cdot\min(f_{r}(\mathfrak{d}),f_{s}(\mathfrak{d})),
where the reduction factors based on terrain roughness and slope are computed as:
\begin{aligned} f_{r}(\mathfrak{d})&=1.0-\gamma_{r}\cdot\min\left(1.0,\frac{\mathfrak{d}.\text{roughness}}{\mathfrak{d}_{\max}.\text{roughness}}\right),\\
f_{s}(\mathfrak{d})&=1.0-\gamma_{s}\cdot\min\left(1.0,\frac{\mathfrak{d}.\text{inclination}}{\pi/2}\right),\end{aligned}
where
\gamma_{r}
and
\gamma_{s}
are adaptation strength parameters controlling the sensitivity to roughness and slope respectively. The combined factor is lower-bounded at 0.3 to prevent overly restrictive planning. More challenging terrain (higher roughness or steeper slopes) yields smaller reduction factors, thereby tightening the stability budget and enforcing more conservative trajectory selection. We solve the constrained optimization problem using a sampling-based approach that generates
N_{\text{cand}}
candidate control sequences and selects the best feasible solution. To leverage the global path guidance from the FMM vector field, we employ a hybrid sampling strategy that combines warm-starting from previous solutions with FMM-biased exploration.

Candidate Generation:
Control sequences are generated using two complementary strategies:

Strategy 1: Warm Start.
When a previous solution is available, half of the candidates are generated by shifting the previous best control sequence forward by one time step and adding Gaussian noise. This provides temporal continuity and accelerates convergence:
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbf{u}_{i+1}^{\text{prev}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i},\quad\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{noise}}),
where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{noise}}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{v}^{2},\sigma_{\omega}^{2})
controls the exploration around the previous solution.

Strategy 2: FMM-Biased Random Sampling.
The remaining candidates are generated by blending FMM-suggested controls with random exploration. For each candidate trajectory, we simulate forward and at each state
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, query the FMM vector field
\mathbf{d}_{\text{FMM}}(\mathbf{p}_{i})
to obtain a suggested control. The desired heading is:
\psi_{\text{des},i}=\arctan 2(d_{\text{FMM},y},d_{\text{FMM},x}),
from which we compute an FMM-suggested control using a proportional controller:
\mathbf{u}_{\text{FMM},i}=\begin{bmatrix}\beta v_{\max}\\
k_{p}(\psi_{\text{des},i}-\psi_{i})\end{bmatrix},
where
\beta\in(0,1]
is a conservative velocity scaling factor and
k_{p}
is a proportional gain. The actual control is computed by blending the FMM-suggested control with a uniformly sampled random control
\mathbf{u}_{\text{rand},i}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{U})
:

\mathbf{u}_{i}^{(j)}=\alpha_{\text{FMM}}\mathbf{u}_{\text{FMM},i}+(1-\alpha_{\text{FMM}})\mathbf{u}_{\text{rand},i},

where
\alpha_{\text{FMM}}\in[0,1]
controls the bias strength. Setting
\alpha_{\text{FMM}}=0
recovers pure random sampling, while
\alpha_{\text{FMM}}=1
strictly follows the FMM field.

The terrain-adaptive pose stability formulation inherently adapts to local geometric conditions: orientations that would be unstable on flat terrain become necessary on moderate slopes, while excessively steep regions are marked as lethal obstacles by the FMM computation, generating vector fields that point the vehicle away. The terrain-adaptive epsilon constraint further enhances this adaptability by automatically tightening stability requirements in challenging terrain while allowing more aggressive exploration in benign conditions.

V
Evaluation

We evaluate the proposed terrain-adaptive kinodynamic planning approach through comprehensive simulation experiments designed to assess navigation performance across diverse terrain conditions. The proposed evaluation focuses on three key research questions: (1) Does the FMM-biased sampling strategy improve trajectory quality compared to pure random sampling? (2) How does the terrain-adaptive epsilon constraint affect stability and path efficiency across varying terrain difficulties? (3) Can the planner handle dynamic receding horizon replanning in real-time while maintaining safety?

V-E

Real-World Experiments

To validate the simulation findings, we deployed the proposed planner on a physical AgileX Scout Mini robot in an outdoor environment.
The Scout Mini was equipped with a Velodyne VLP-16 LiDAR for terrain perception, and connected to an ASUS ROG Strix G614JU mobile workstation equipped with a 13th Gen Intel Core i7 processor, 32 GB DDR5 RAM (5600 MT/s), NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 GPU, and a 512 GB NVMe SSD, running Ubuntu Linux.
The real-world result is close to simulation trends: success rates due to unmodeled physical effects like wheel slip and sensor noise, while path deviations increase 22% as the planner selects more conservative routes under uncertainty. Real-world planning times are 15% slower but still meet the 100 ms real-time requirement. The proposed method’s performance advantages over baselines persist in physical deployment, validating that terrain-adaptive constraints scale appropriately to real-world conditions.

V-F

Failure Analysis

Failed trials exhibited three modes: infeasible scenarios where goals lie in FMM-unreachable regions, local minima trapping in narrow corridors (mitigable by increasing
N_{\text{cand}}
to 100), and stability constraint violations at terrain discontinuities where descriptor extraction incompletely captures local geometry. We also explored NSGA-II multi-objective optimization to jointly optimize path efficiency and stability, but its population-based evolutionary search incurred planning times exceeding 2000 ms per iteration, over an order of magnitude slower than MPPI (29 ms) and GAKD (35 ms), while yielding comparable or worse success rates, confirming that for real-time receding horizon planning, simpler sampling-based methods with direct FMM guidance offer superior efficiency without sacrificing solution quality.

V-G

Conclusion and Future Work

The results demonstrate that while weighted scalarization methods can optimize individual objectives effectively, the proposed terrain-adaptive
\epsilon
-constraint approach more effectively balances competing objectives by dynamically adjusting stability constraints based on local terrain features, enabling safer navigation without excessive path deviation. The modest computational overhead is justified by substantial improvements in feasibility and completion rate, particularly critical for autonomous systems in unstructured outdoor environments.
The presented approach provides a principled framework for terrain-adaptive kinodynamic planning that balances global optimality, local safety, and computational tractability, with several promising directions for future work. Extending the framework to dynamic environments represents a natural next step, where integrating temporal prediction into FMM computation and stability assessment would enable robust navigation alongside moving obstacles. Finally, expanding the terrain descriptor with additional semantic properties such as surface softness offers an exciting opportunity to refine traversability assessment and broaden the range of deployable environments.
