ABSTRACT
Critical infrastructure systems such as the electric grid are increasingly cyber-physical; yet, despite the
cyber-physical characteristics of critical infrastructure systems, the physical process system and
communication/control network system are traditionally analyzed in siloes. As these systems
become more cyber-physical, it is crucial that models and methods are available to assess the cyber-
physical system (CPS) interdependencies, characteristics, and event propagation for improved
planning, operation, and response. Thus, we proposed an integrated structural and temporal CPS
interdependency analysis framework, InterGraph-CPS, that provides insight into the CPS function
during normal operation as well as disturbances. This integrated structural and temporal
interdependency framework is uniquely designed for assessing CPSs by account for the challenges of
analyzing cyber and physical data streams together due to data availability, data type, and time scale
differences. By leveraging both structural (e.g., graph analysis) and temporal (e.g., data analytics)
techniques, different CPS behaviors and configurations can be accounted for.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The electric grid is becoming increasingly cyber-physical, rapidly evolving with smart grid
technologies, wide-area monitoring capabilities, and advanced automation. However, these
modernization efforts also broaden the grid’s vulnerability surface and increase cyber-physical
mutuality. Multi-hazard events such as cyber attacks and climate change-driven extreme weather can
cause detrimental, cascading impact. The cyber-physical nature of the grid does not limit cyber
disturbances to the cyber system and physical disturbances to the physical system – disturbances can
propagate between systems. Similarly, operational system changes can affect both cyber and physical
domains; observability is needed for both normal operation and during disturbances.
Obtaining cross-domain observability into the cyber-physical system (CPS), including connected
critical infrastructure, is an important requirement to informing grid operation and response. It no
longer suffices to only monitor the physical system to achieve full observability of the grid.
Conversely, network monitors that only process cyber data are not enough to thwart adversaries
and/or detect communication failures. Therefore, cyber-physical observability (CPO), the ability to
determine both cyber and physical system states with input and output measurements in finite time,
is crucial for characterizing the grid’s intricate cyber-physical interactions and enabling detailed
feature extraction.
Prior work developed an approach to define CPO by combining physical observability algorithms
with graph-theoretic network observability methods. However, the interdependencies between cyber
and physical graph-nodes and their formal definition were not investigated. The CPO graph-
theoretic model, an initial step to leverage for this proposal, must be revised and added to with
techniques such as multi-order dependency analysis, network decomposition, principal component
analysis, and/or coupled oscillators to gain observability into the CPS interdependencies and assess
controllability needs.
We hypothesize that a graph-theoretic modeling approach using these numerical analysis and
decomposition techniques combined with control-theoretic observability/controllability concepts
can be developed to assess cyber-physical interdependencies and dynamics. This InterGraph-CPS
approach can then be used to study the alignment of terms and quantities in a mixed system model
and how those information flows change across system boundaries.
It is crucial to assess how seemingly disparate cyber and physical data sets can be combined in a
rigorous and useful manner for improving grid operation and response; this CPO would help
identify weak points that require hardening and controllability analyses will improve mitigation
strategies to increase grid resilience. Controllability-informed mitigations will be studied with partner
TAMU and leverage prior sensitivity-based control characterization work InterGraph-CPS will be
developed with a unique set of uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods to assess the critical CPS
features and how they can inform operation/response decision-making (utilizing partner SwRI’s UQ
expertise). For data collection and disturbance/change modeling, a high-fidelity cyber-physical
emulation will be leveraged from existing RES MC HARMONIE-SPS LDRD environment with
RTDS and SCEPTRE™; this will enable real-time cyber-physical data extraction and InterGraph-
CPS validation using our novel UQ approach. The second stage of InterGraph-CPS validation will
include emulating a real system, proposal partner Emera Technologies Kirtland Airforce Base

1. INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM STATEMENT

1.1. Introduction and Problem Statement
Cyber-physical systems (CPSs) can be defined as systems that combine sensing, computation,
control, and networking into physics-based processes, connecting them to the internet and to each
other. Critical infrastructure systems such as the electric grid are increasingly cyber-physical due to
recent modernization and expansion efforts. Autonomous and adaptive control as well as cloud-
based analytics and data storage are just two examples of capabilities that strengthen the cyber-
physical connection. More and more processes require distributed analysis and data storage that
leverage internet connections to facilitate these needs. However, despite the cyber-physical
characteristics of critical infrastructure systems, the physical process system and
communication/control network system are traditionally analyzed in silos. For example, the
information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT) systems are often monitored
separately with more focus on the IT system at the enterprise system level. Although there is
movement towards IT/OT convergence [1] for industrial control systems (ICSs), the physics-based
processes and analysis are not considered within the ``converged" situational awareness. Instead, the
physics-based system employs state estimation, stability analyses, and other analytics to assess system
health and operation [2].

As critical infrastructure systems become more cyber-physical, it is crucial that models and methods
are available to assess the cyber-physical system (CPS) interdependencies, characteristics, and event
propagation for improved planning, operation, and response. This includes alerting on changes or
detected anomalies in the CPSs, whether through logs or visualization dashboards, in a coordinated
fashion to relevant stakeholders such as system operators and cyber defenders [3]. However, to
streamline this coordination, it is important to first establish consistent definitions of CPSs and
methods for analysis to comprehensively understand the CPS operation and important
interdependencies. Comprehensive CPS interdependency results can help not only operational needs
such as event detection and response but also planning efforts through identifying key cyber-
physical relationships that may require additional redundancy or security.

In this project, we focus on developing an integrated structural and temporal CPS interdependency
analysis framework that provides insight into the CPS function during normal operation as well as
disturbances. This structural and temporal analysis real-time technique (START) framework is
uniquely designed for assessing CPSs by account for the challenges of analyzing cyber and physical
data streams together due to data availability, data type, and time scale differences. By leveraging
both structural (e.g., graph analysis) and temporal (e.g., data analytics) techniques, different CPS
behaviors and configurations can be accounted for. For example, in the electric grid domain,
different power system types such as transmission, distribution, and distributed energy resources as
well as communication networks and protocols can be analyzed together using graph techniques that
focus on structural connections and incorporate temporal data (e.g., sensor data, IP-based
communication packets) where available. Thus, the START framework provides much needed
flexibility to analyze CPSs of varying characteristics and sensing capabilities. Additionally, each
technique within the START framework can provide cross-correlation of detected changes or events
to increase confidence in system characterization and alerting. This includes detection of individual
cyber and physical events as well as when those events are connected, correlated.

2. BACKGROUND
Interdependency analysis is key for analysis of physics-based systems where variations and changes
of physical components can influence overall system operation and health immediately. The level of
influence or impact depends on the specific physical component, protection and safety mechanisms
in place, and overall system conditions. Similarly, in cyber systems (e.g., IT/OT), interdependency
analysis can be leveraged to identify network traffic patterns and congestion bottlenecks. In this
section, we present existing interdependency analysis approaches and their application, prior work in
cyber-physical interdependency analysis, and overall gaps for cyber-physical situational awareness
(CPSA).

2.1. Interdependency Analysis for Resilience Assessment

Studying interdependence between different system components and their uni- or bi-directional
linkages is an effective approach for assessing system resiliency. Pierre et al. developed an analytical
framework for identifying electric grid failure trends and critical components by leveraging a
cascading outage model and transient dynamic simulations; this effort only focused on the physics-
based system layer and to inform grid planning and response [4].

Falahati et al. proposed a framework that accounted for direct and indirect cyber-physical
interdependencies which enables more comprehensive assessment of resilience impact on grid
disturbances and communication failures [5]. Probabilities of the cyber-physical system states,
mappings between cyber-physical states to predict impact, and reliability metrics (e.g., loss of load
probability and expected energy not served) are used to assess power system reliability in the
presence of cyber or physical failures.

Resilience analysis across interdependent critical infrastructure systems is also an important research
area but does not always consider the communication network inclusion or impact across the
physics-based layers of the interconnected critical infrastructure systems. For example, Wang et al.
present a deep learning framework for studying topological interdependencies across power and gas
systems to help identify different failure scenarios and coupling modes [6]. With these results, the
authors propose different recovery strategies to inform an optimal resilience improvement strategy
across the critical infrastructure systems both from structural and functional perspectives.

Lastly, Petit and Verner also highlight critical infrastructure interdependence but categorize
dependencies as physical, cyber, geographic, or logical [7]. The authors emphasize the complexity of
studying such systems and the need for systems of systems analysis approach as well as bottom-
up/top-down perspectives for more comprehensive results.

2.2. Interdependency Analysis for Cyber-Physical Situational Awareness

The interdependency between communications systems and physics-based systems (e.g., power
system) is crucial to the reliable and resilient function of critical infrastructure systems. For example,
for the electric grid, fiber optic networks and/or cellular networks are often used as a wide-area
network (WAN) to provide monitoring, control, and protection across a transmission system.
Additionally, for distribution systems, Ethernet and/or wireless mesh networks are leveraged to
enable automated and adaptive operations such as demand response and response actions. These
communication systems can be categorized as local area networks (LAN) and can be subdivided into
field-areas, home-areas, etc. [8].

 Liu et al. performed a review of different interdependency analysis techniques for cyber-physical
systems and found the applications to span across resilience analysis (failure analysis, vulnerability
analysis, failure mitigation, failure recovery) and simulation/modeling efforts. Their overall findings
indicated that many of the approaches focused on impact in one direction (i.e., cyber system to
power system), challenges in accurately modeling the CPS, and lack of mitigation/recovery methods
for the integrated CPS. Additionally, the authors highlighted that there is a tradeoff between realism
and tractability across modeling techniques (e.g., graph-based models vs. equation-based models vs.
executable models) and that more study was needed for assessing interdependent relationships.

Pacific Northwest National Laboratory [9] also highlighted the interdependence of information and
communication technology and the power grid in 2015. However, their next steps and future
directions focused on designing a new reference architecture for control systems, ensuring
communication network standalone resilience, and developing new grid management tools to adapt
to faster and more adaptive operation paradigms. Thus, this worked focused more on any power
system disturbance and impact from the cyber domain.

The Department of Energy (DOE) Office of Electricity (OE) also emphasized the importance of
communication infrastructure and power grid interdependencies in their report “Power Grid and

 18

Communication Interdependencies: Key Challenges for Reliable, Resilient Operations.” The report
focused on communication security, prioritization of networking infrastructure for critical services,
and coordinated recovery efforts for both communication infrastructure and the power grid for
more comprehensive resilience [10].

2.3. Cyber-Physical Interdependency Analysis Gaps and Challenges

Cyber-physical modeling and analysis is an ongoing and rapidly evolving research domain for
improving critical infrastructure cybersecurity, as evidenced by the prior work described in the last
section. However, there exists gaps for cyber-physical interdependency analysis for comprehensive
situational awareness, whether from the cyber or physical perspective. These gaps can be
summarized as:

 o Liu et al. highlighted that there is a gap for assessing interdependent relationships
 and quantitative, motivating the need for both structural (graph) and temporal
 techniques to more accurately model CPSs. By incorporating temporal techniques,
 quantitative metrics can be achieved to differentiate CPS interdependencies types
 and intensities.
 o DOE OE emphasized the need for coordinated response across CPSs. Essentially,
 how do we provide operators and defenders the necessary tools and analytics to
 effectively make those decisions that affect both cyber and physical parts of a
 system?
 o Interdependency analysis is not only important for resilience analysis, as presented
 by Wang et al. and Fahalati et al., but for more efficient planning and operation.
 Thus, there is a need to understand bi-directional impact and improvements to the
 comprehensive CPS.
 o In this project, structural techniques leveraging graph theory and network analyses
 were explored to understand interdependencies between cyber-physical components
 and connections. To dive deeper into behavior and dynamics of the components and
 connections, temporal analysis leveraging time-series data from the CPS is needed to
 achieve a more accurate interdependence model for increased depth of CPSA
 insights for system operators and defenders to leverage.

The summarized gaps highlight the crucial need for more comprehensive and integrated structural
and temporal technique for analyzing CPSs. Though, it is important to note the challenges of
integrating temporal and structural techniques for CPSs with different time-scales, different sensor
data availability, and different operational needs across cyber and physical domains. In this report,
we propose an integrated structural and temporal analysis real-time technique (START) framework
that seeks to mitigate these challenges to provide comprehensive CPSA for planning, operation, and
response.

3. CYBER-PHYSICAL EMULATION MODELS

3.1. Emulation
For analysis of cyber-physical systems, emulation is an incredibly powerful tool. In emulation,
components and devices in the system are virtualized for testing and analysis. This may be paired
with simulation models running power flow calculations for the physical dynamical system
connected to these virtual components. In this project, all of this is done using Sandia developed
tools Minimega [11] and SCEPTRE [12], [13].
Minimega is an emulation tool developed at Sandia National Laboratories for fast and scalable
Virtual Machine (VM) management, either locally or on server clusters. SCEPTRE is a cyber-
physical emulation capability that connects minimega virtual environments to simulation providers
for underlying process dynamics. It contains an orchestration tool called Phenix to help run
experiments, and native Industrial Control System (ICS) protocol support for Modbus, DNP3, and
other ICS protocols as well as common Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)
software for use in experimentation and analysis. Both of these fall into a set of capabilities we at
Sandia call Emulytics [14], which contain emulation and analysis toolsets to assist in experimentation
and analysis for security and resilience of cyber-physical systems.

3.2. Western System Coordinating Council (WSCC) 9-bus power system
For initial testing of START, a cyber-physical 9-bus power system dataset with integrated network
traffic monitoring was leveraged. The 9-bus system is a simple approximation of the Western System
Coordinating Council (WSCC) to an equivalent system with 9 buses and 3 generators, as shown on
the left side of Figure 2. For the cyber-side, a corresponding synthetic cyber communication and
control network was created for the WSCC 9-bus system, as described in [15], and is shown on the
right side of Figure 2. The power system one-line diagram will be referred to as the physical model
and the network diagram will be referred to as the cyber model. Together, they comprise the cyber-
physical model of this power system, encompassing the integrated power system operational physics,
sensing, computation, and communication to and control of power system components.

 3.2.1. WSCC 9-bus scenario runs
While the Emera emulation was being developed early in the project, the existing WSCC 9-bus
power system from HARMONIE-SPS LDRD was leveraged [16]. The existing disruption scenarios
from that project were re-used, including baseline, cyber, physical, and cyber-physical. These
scenarios were re-run with additional experimental parameters added for to suit the purposes of the
InterGraph-CPS project goals. Specifically, a variable packet loss was added to the network links of
the target virtual RTUs, in addition to the bandwidth restrictions. This was to simulate a degraded
connection quality, e.g. due to faulty equipment or an unreliable remote connection.
The packet loss was applied in increments of 10%, going from 0% loss to 100% loss. The same loss
was applied for the total duration of a run. There were 4 distinct scenarios (baseline, cyber, physical,
and cyber-physical) with 11 permutations of packet loss per scenario (0% loss to 100% loss in
increments of 10%), and 3 runs of each permutation to account for noise between runs. This
resulted in 132 total runs.

 3.3.1. Initial Emera emulation
The initial implementation of the Emera emulation aimed to replicate the Emera network with the
highest possible fidelity. This effort utilized information from multiple sources, including network
diagrams, inventories, system reports, and interviews with engineers. However, the available data did
not include software or configuration details for the devices within the Emera network, such as the
Heila controllers. This limitation arose from the proprietary nature of the devices and restricted
access to the network.

The Heila nanogrid controllers consist of proprietary software operating Ubuntu Linux with an
ARM CPU. These controllers employ a custom economic dispatch algorithm to optimize power
injection and withdrawal within the MG. Each nanogrid incorporates a single embedded Heila
controller, which communicates with other controllers and a coordinator Heila within the same
subnet via a network switch. The subnet is protected by a firewall that also serves as a gateway to the
Internet.

In addition to the networked devices, the Emera system includes several non-networked
components connected via serial interfaces such as CAN bus, RS-485, RS-422, and fiber-optic links.
These devices perform various functions related to the photovoltaic (PV) and battery systems,
including inverters, controllers, sensors, and protection mechanisms. The system also integrates a
fast charger and data aggregation devices.

 3.3.3. OPAL-RT integration with SCEPTRE
The Emera microgrid (MG) simulation was developed in Simulink and executed on an OPAL-RT
dynamic simulator. To integrate this simulation into the emulation framework, a SCEPTRE
Provider specific to the OPAL-RT simulator was created. This Provider operated similarly to the
existing RTDS Provider but employed distinct communication protocols to accommodate
differences in functionality between the RTDS and OPAL-RT simulators. The communication
protocol used for the OPAL-RT simulator was Modbus/TCP, which supported both read
operations (polling values) and write operations (e.g., modifying power setpoints or toggling the
ATS switch).
The interconnection between the emulated control system and the simulated power system is
facilitated by a SCEPTRE Provider. Bennu virtual field devices, functioning as controllers,
communicate with the Provider using a text-based UDP multicast protocol implemented via

"RTU" in the accompanying image (Figure 8), which subsequently relay these values to the OPC
server using the DNP3 protocol. Similarly, control commands—such as toggling the automatic
transfer switch (ATS) or modifying power setpoints—are transmitted in the reverse direction. The
ZeroMQ channels serve as the equivalent of real-world grid measurements being fed into the field

 3.3.4. Disruption Scenarios for the Emera emulation
Two disruption scenarios, simulating cyber attacks, were designed for the Emera emulation, in
addition to a baseline scenario characterized by the absence of disruptions. The disruption scenarios
consisted of a packet flood Denial of Service (DoS) attack and a Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) data
manipulation attack, implemented via an Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) spoofing technique.
These scenarios were designed to test the robustness and effectiveness of the analysis techniques
developed for InterGraph-CPS.

3.3.4.1. Baseline scenario
The baseline scenario consists of the Emera MG simulation operating without any cyber attacks,
thereby generating a dataset representative of normal operational conditions. This dataset serves as a
reference point for evaluating the efficacy of analysis techniques and facilitating comparisons with
datasets that include disruptions. The baseline scenario was executed in three separate 3-hour runs,
during which all data was collected but no cyber attacks were executed.

3.3.4.2. Denial of Service scenario
The first disruption scenario developed was a Denial of Service (DoS) attack designed to interrupt
communications between the Heila controllers and the OPC server. This attack involved a packet

 27

flood that saturated the communication bandwidth of the targeted field devices. The packet flood
was implemented using the hping3 tool [20], executed on an "attacker" virtual machine (VM)
running Kali Linux [21]. The targets of the attack were three nanogrid (NG) controllers—NG1,
NG4, and NG7—with IPv4 addresses of 10.1.32.45, 10.1.32.100, and 10.1.32.99, respectively.
To ensure the attack was effective, an artificial bandwidth limitation of 500 kbps was applied to the
network links associated with the three target controllers. This restriction was implemented using the
Quality of Service (QoS) functionality provided by minimega. The bandwidth limitation ensured that
the packet flood could reliably overwhelm the communication capacity, thereby disrupting DNP3
communication between the target controller VMs and the OPC server. These artificial constraints
were necessary due to the high efficiency and bandwidth capabilities of the software-defined
networking technology used for VM communication, as well as the ample CPU resources available
to the target VMs. In real-world systems, physical devices typically operate with limited hardware
resources and constrained network bandwidth.
The DoS disruption scenario was executed in three separate runs, each lasting exactly three hours
(10,800 seconds). Each run consisted of three distinct phases: the first 10 minutes involved normal
operations, followed by a 30-minute packet flood disruption, and concluding with 140 minutes of
normal operations after the disruption.

3.3.4.3. Man-in-the-Middle scenario
The Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) disruption scenario caused a loss of communication by dropping
DNP3 packets exchanged between the target nanogrids and the OPC server. This was achieved
through an Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) poisoning attack, which redirected traffic between
the targets and the OPC server through the attacker VM. The attacker VM, running Kali Linux,
utilized a custom Python script built with the advanced Scapy [22] packet manipulation library to
inspect each packet and determine the appropriate action. Possible actions included forwarding the
packet unmodified, dropping the packet (not forwarding), or manipulating the packet (e.g., false data
injection). In this scenario, the attacker exclusively dropped DNP3 protocol packets between the
targets and the OPC server.
As with the baseline and DoS scenarios, the MitM disruption scenario was executed in three separate
runs, each lasting exactly three hours (10,800 seconds). Each run consisted of three distinct phases:
an initial 10 minutes of normal operations, followed by a 2.5-hour (9,000-second) MitM attack
dropping DNP3 packets, and concluding with 20 minutes of normal operations after the disruption.
Future scenarios could leverage the attack script's functionality to perform more sophisticated
attacks, such as false data injection. For example, the attacker could display an incorrect battery state
of charge or selectively block specific types of DNP3 packets, such as control command packets
(e.g., ATS switch actuation), while allowing other packets to pass unmodified.

3.3.4.4.   Limitations
The OPAL-RT simulation was not restarted between runs within the same scenario due to the
absence of automation for starting and stopping cases on the OPAL-RT platform, as well as time
constraints. This limitation was deemed inconsequential for the current experiments, as the physical
system was not impacted. However, it is expected to become significant in future scenarios where
disruptions directly affect the microgrid (MG).
Another challenge was the delay introduced by the Modbus connection between the SCEPTRE
Provider and the OPAL-RT simulator. Polling all 198 data points over Modbus/TCP from the

 28

OPAL-RT required several hundred milliseconds per cycle, and this cumulative delay became
significant over the course of a 3-hour experiment run.
To address this issue, a potential solution is to increase the polling rate of the Provider to multiple
times per second. During post-processing, the collected data could be down-sampled to the desired
rate of one data point per second. This approach would preserve the required temporal resolution
while minimizing the impact of cumulative delays.
The iperf3 tool [23] was initially employed to collect detailed network quality measurements for the
WSCC 9-bus system. However, the packet flood DoS attack disrupted the iperf3 control connection,
resulting in errors during data collection. Additionally, several technical challenges arose when
integrating iperf3 into the SCORCH orchestration framework, leading to reliability issues.
Consequently, the decision was made to exclude iperf3 data collection from the Emera MG
scenarios.
Future work will focus on resolving these technical challenges to enable the inclusion of iperf3 data
in subsequent experiments. One potential solution involves running the iperf3 control connection
over an alternate network interface, such as the existing management interface used by SCEPTRE
for VM management.

3.4. Experiment Data Collection

 3.4.1. Scenario Orchestration

Scenario Orchestration (SCORCH) is a capability for conducting complex real-time emulated
experiments, originally developed under the Science and Engineering of Cybersecurity by
Uncertainty quantification and Rigorous Experimentation (SECURE) Grand Challenge LDRD [24].
SCORCH was utilized to automate key processes, including initiating and terminating data collection
and disruption scenarios, cleaning up virtual machines (VMs) between experiment runs (e.g., deleting
files and resetting network interfaces), and performing basic validation of results to ensure successful
execution. Validation steps included confirming that all expected data parameters were collected,
verifying their formats and correctness, and ensuring both the disruption scenario and power
simulation executed successfully. For the WSCC 9-bus experiments, SCORCH also automated the
starting and stopping of the RTDS simulator, ensuring no timestep overruns occurred.

 Figure 9: Screenshot of a Scorch run for the WSCC 9-bus system with the RTDS simulator

The automation provided by SCORCH was critical to the success of the experimentation due to the
large number of runs required. Manual execution, resetting, and validation of runs would have been
excessively time-consuming, error-prone, and could have introduced inconsistencies in command
timestamps, thereby increasing noise in the data. For instance, the WSCC 9-bus experiments, which
involved variable packet loss, required 132 total runs, while the Emera scenarios consisted of 9 runs
with a cumulative runtime of 27 hours.

 3.4.2. Data post-processing with Harmonize
A substantial amount of data was collected from diverse sources, as outlined above. Manual
processing of this data would have been both time-consuming and prone to errors. To address this
challenge, the Harmonize tool was developed to automate data post-processing, including validation.
The tool aggregated outputs from various collection tools and Scorch components, such as network
packet capture files (PCAP), power system measurements from Elasticsearch, VM resource
utilization metrics, metadata from attack scripts (e.g., start/stop times, targets, parameters),
experiment start/stop times, and experiment metadata (e.g., SCEPTRE topology, scenario details, IP
addresses, hostnames, and versions of Phenix and Minimega). The collected data was collated,
organized, and key values were extracted into three primary outputs: a consolidated data file in
Comma-Separated Values (CSV) format (experiment_results.csv), experiment metadata in a
machine-readable JSON file (experiment_results.json), and a human-readable report in HTML and
Markdown formats.

 31

 Figure 11: Screenshot of a portion of the human-readable report in HTML format
The primary output of the post-processing workflow was a CSV file named experiment_results.csv.
This file consolidated network packet capture data with power system measurements, providing a
unified dataset for testing and evaluating InterGraph-CPS analysis techniques. Each row in the CSV
file represented a single time step in the power system. For the WSCC 9-bus experiments, the
dataset contained 30 rows per second, while the Emera MG data included 1 row per second. To
generate this combined dataset, Pandas [25] DataFrames were created from the network and power
system data. Network-related values included Round Trip Time (RTT) measurements for initial and

4. STRUCTURAL AND TEMPORAL ANALYSIS REAL-TIME
 TECHNIQUES FRAMEWORK
Various structural and temporal analysis real-time techniques (START) were explored to assess
cyber-physical interdependencies. These techniques and their results are presented next. In Section
5, we discuss the integration of the different START approaches and the comprehensive insight they
provide for cyber-physical systems.

4.1. Background
When starting to look at analysis for structural and temporal properties of cyber-physical systems, a
good place to start is by examining graph structure. This can be done by constructing a Cyber-
Physical Graph (CPG) of the system, where each node of the graph is a component or device in
either the communications / control network (Field Devices, Routers, etc...) or in the physical
system (Generator, Load, etc...). The control network and the physical system network can then be
connected through a parallel composition of the two graphs, where the added links are from
sensors, such as Phasor Measurement Units (PMUs), and actuators in the system. These are directed
edges (this as a directed graph) that connect information flow from the physical system into the
communications and control network via sensor devices (PMUs, AMI, etc.) and the actuators
(generators, motors, pumps, etc.).
This type of structure is more fully described in [26].

4.2. Clustering Techniques
Before examining additional ways to study graph structure for important nodes and edges, we can
start by utilizing well known metrics for graph connectivity and structure from graph theory, as well
as additional clustering techniques to characterize groups of nodes in the system. These methods
were examined over the entire CPG to help assess graph structure and isolate important elements of
the system. The methods examined were:
 (1) Graph Components and Condensation Graphs
 (2) K-Shell Clusters
 (3) Graph Embedding Techniques
 (4) Bipartite Graphs
Graph embedding techniques will be fully discussed in Section 4.3, while Bipartite graphs and their
relation to graph clustering are discussed in Section 4.4.2. In this section, we will briefly discuss
graph components and K-Shell decomposition. This section covers material from [27].
Graph components are subgraphs of a graph that are strongly connected. The graph itself is only
strongly connected if it has one component, and if there is more than one component the graph is
not strongly connected. A strongly connected graph means that from any node in the system you
can traverse the graph to any other node in the system. A weakly connected graph is a graph that
would be strongly connected if you convert all directed edges into undirected edges. In a weakly
connected graph, there may be graph components that are sinks or sources, where there is an edge
that only leads in or out of that component. A condensation graph is a graph of the components
and the connections between them, which is helpful for visualizing those important edges. The
following figure shows a CPG of the WSCC 9-bus system with a control network, as described in
this report, as well as the corresponding condensation graph.

In the above figure, we see that the WSCC 9-bus system in its configuration as described in Section
3.2. In this system, there is a central main graph component that is strongly connected, with the 3
generators acting as sources that are only weakly connected. This is because this network does not
incorporate communications and control back to the generators, but mainly incorporates sensor
connections for situational awareness, while the generator controls are separate. The additional or
removal of graph nodes or edges, such as in planning for new power system additions or in the case
of N-1 contingencies, can drastically change this system structure. The use of examining graph
components and condensation graphs helps to see how specific lines and components can become
critical for the connectivity of the graph. More complete discussion and further examples can be
found in [27].
K-Shell Decomposition [28] examines and decomposes a graph by looking the node degree for each
node in the system and separating them based on that metric. Node degree is a value that counts the
number of edges coming into (in-degree) or out (out-degree) of a node, if the number of edges
coming in and out are the same, then that is the degree of the node. K-shell decomposition separates
nodes into separate cores based on their degree. An example figure is shown below.

The above figure color codes nodes in the CPG by their K-shell core, with red for 1-shell, orange
for 2-shell, and grey for 3+. These values represent the degree of these nodes in an undirected
version of the graph (as we are not separating out in-degree versus out-degree). The K-shell for each
node helps to identify isolated nodes (1-shell) or nodes that are more central to the graph (higher
shell value). For 1-shell nodes, the loss of a single link may disconnect the graph, while a more
central component (higher shell) may be more critical for overall data flow and more access to the
information flowing through the system (such as a router). More complete discussion on this topic
can be found in [27].
As can be seen in this section, analyzing graph structure can assist in identifying critical nodes and
edges in the system and isolate where individual could cause disconnected components of the
network and loss of connectivity. This is important as a base for analyzing interdependencies in CPS
and can be extended through further analysis as we go into throughout the rest of this report.

4.3. Graph Embedding Technique
 4.3.1. Introduction of Graph Embedding Technique
Graph embedding techniques are applied in numerous application fields including natural language
processing (NLP) [29], biology [30], and financial fraud detection [31]. The technique extracts the
graph structural information while reducing the high-dimensional graphic data, which could extend
the graph theoretic capabilities to machine-learning approaches. The recent application of graph
embedding to power systems reflects the increasingly data-driven approach to outage prediction
[32], network anomaly detection [33], and node clustering [34], [35]. For example, Xiao et al.
demonstrated how graph embedding techniques can facilitate anomaly detection by extracting low-
dimensional information from high-dimensional graphs without significant information loss [33].

The Node2Vec method, from [29], stands out for second-order biased random walks, which equips

Node2Vec-based Risk Analysis
Risk is typically defined as the likelihood of a given threat, evaluated by multiplying the potential
vulnerability by the impact of the resulting incidents, as outlined by NIST [37]:
Risk = Likelihood × Impact.
To accurately quantify the likelihood of a specific threat, it is imperative to conduct a comprehensive
analysis of the vulnerabilities associated with each system component. Meanwhile, the impact scores
linked to adversarial events are considered to evaluate potential consequences.
The Node2Vec approach is a graph embedding technique that extracts the structure and relational
information of the nodes in a continuous vector space [29]. As described in [29], the technique is
capable of learning node representations based on their network roles or the communities they are
part of. One of the key advantages of Node2Vec is its ability to balance between different searching
strategies in its biased random walk process and be perfectly applied to a weighted bi-directional graph
[29]. The success inspires the potential use in CPS. Applying this to cyber-physical power systems, the
Node2Vec technique can similarly unravel complex interdependencies between various system
components. It starts with the creation of random walks, simulating graph exploration. In this method,
we mirrored the random walks as the access paths within the graph. The skip-gram model is then
applied to predict neighboring nodes for a target node within these walks. The skip-gram technique is
especially important in representative learning [29], which could generate latent variables for
uncovering hidden structures in data and for representing abstract concepts that are not directly
measurable. It enables researchers and data scientists to make inferences about unseen or underlying
processes that affect observable phenomena.
The utilization of random walks in the Node2Vec technique serves exploration of potential access
pathways that an adversary might exploit or intrude between nodal communication. In the previous
section, we focused on employing the Deepwalk-based method to cluster nodes based on their risk
level [34]. Deepwalk technique operated under the assumption of homogeneous linkage between each
node, treating each path with equal possibility regardless of its characteristics. Such representations
are particularly beneficial for capturing the structural similarities and interdependencies between the
nodes.
Assigning exploitability scores to edges can effectively model complexity of an intruder moving from
one node to another. An intruder’s potential ease or difficulty in utilizing a vulnerability to travel from
one place in the network to another can be efficiently measured by assigning the exploitability scores
to the edges in a network graph. Exploitability-weighted graphs can be used in more advanced
analytics like access simulation, where potential access pathways are simulated to understand the most
likely routes an adversary might take. In this paper, we referenced the Common Vulnerability Scoring
System (CVSS) and assigned the Access Complexity (AC) value as the exploitability scores to the edges
within the network [38]. AC represents the degrees of difficulty an adversary could encounter to
exploit a vulnerability in the system [38]. A lower AC score indicates that it is more challenging for
adversaries to exploit the component. According to [38], the edge weight values in the network graph
are clarified based on the AC classes of Hard, Moderate, and Easy, along with their associated AC
values:
• Hard: These threats are highly complex, often requiring elevated privileges and social engineering.
They are less effective against informed individuals, depend on rare configurations, and have limited
opportunities due to narrow race condition windows.

 41

• Moderate: These threats require specialized access conditions and target specific groups of
authorized users or systems. They also require prior information gathering and exploit rare non-default
configurations.
• Easy: These threats are broadly applicable and require minimal expertise or specific conditions. The
adversaries often target common or default configurations and wide user ranges, with easily exploitable
conditions.

likely to be traversed and therefore, are more critical and vulnerable. Using random walk simulations

consequences and inform defense strategies.
Suppose we initiate a random walk on each node thousands of times. This process generates a large
set of access paths. Node embeddings are formulated to symbolize the graph structure and represent
the biased random walks by optimizing the embeddings to preserve neighborhood similarities. The
feature learning approach extends the skip-gram model used in the Deepwalk approach, to predict the
neighborhood context based on the central node [29]. The skip-gram method is a novel approach for
calculating vector representations from large datasets [29], [39]. This adaptation, as utilized in
approaches like Deepwalk and Node2Vec, effectively treats nodes and their sequences generated from
random walks as analogous to words and sentences in text.
Since all nodes are represented by the latent variables, the likelihood of entities behaving similarly or
being exposed to similar risks can be accessed by examining the "proximity" of nodes in the
embedding space through cosine similarity. Cosine similarity could effectively measure the similarity
between two non-zero vectors and provide insights into likelihood calculation. For any two nodes u
and v in the graph, their cosine similarity is calculated using their vector embeddings 𝑧u and 𝑧v , and
the likelihood is estimated by:

The likelihood computation yields a similarity score between -1 and 1. Adversaries often exploit
vulnerabilities in one part of the network to move laterally to other regions. Nodes that are similar in
terms of vector embedding may be part of connected or related subsystems in the CPS, making it
easier for threats to propagate from one subsystem to another. When a node’s score is close to 1, it
suggests their vector embeddings in the latent space are closely aligned, indicating that they possess
similar attributes or are highly involved within the network. A number close to -1 denotes significantly
divergent or opposing embeddings, whereas a score close to 0 denotes no discernible resemblance.
Assessing the impact score of components in CPS is another fundamental aspect of risk analysis. This
process involves assessing the potential consequence of a device being compromised. In this paper,
we propose a comprehensive metric to determine the impact score, considering the multiple integral
roles of each device. These dimensions are Operational Importance (OI), Security and Confidentiality
(SC), Maintenance and Reliability (MR), Network Influence (NI), and Exploitation Complexity (EC)
level. ( IS′ = OI · SC · MR · NI · EC )The product of these dimensions represents the combined
impact of these factors on the system’s overall risk or importance. Using multiplication can be seen as
a way to amplify the risk assessment, where each component’s risk level exponentially increases the
overall risk if all factors are high. Besides, a low score in any single aspect affects the total score. This
is ideal in the CPS environment where a single point of failure is unacceptable.
Equation normalizes the preliminary Impact Score (IS’) to produce the final Impact Score (IS) by
applying min-max normalization. The min-max normalization technique ensures that the impact score
is a proportion of the maximum possible score, keeping it within a normalized range [40]. The
normalization is scaled relative to the maximum possible score, keeping the result within (0, 1), and
facilitating a standardized assessment across different components and systems.

consequence of abundance matching [48], [51], [54], [55]. This abundance matching can be directly

4.4.2.2.1.2.   Bipartite graphs
Mutualistic networks in nature, such as plant-pollinator or plant-herbivore models, have been found
to be resilient to unexpected disturbances [49], [52]. Ecologists have attributed this to their unique
hierarchical network structure, which also supports highly specialized actors [86]. Ecological analyses
of mutualistic networks use bipartite graphs and clustering analyses to model and quantify
interaction structures as related to network function and to allocate conservation efforts amongst
species [52]. A bipartite model groups actors into two sets and highlights interactions between the
two groups. A modularity analysis is then used to identify generalist and specialist species,
interaction clusters or modules between species, and general interaction distribution patterns across
all species. These modules may be thought of as like the clusters identified in the other techniques
investigated here. Modularity (Qb) quantifies the level to which components are clustered into
modules based on interactions. The Newman algorithm [76], [87], produces a value from zero to
one for Qb (4.1) where eii is the percentage of edges in module i, and ai is the percentage of edges
with at least one end in module i, with zero indicating no clustering or modules based on interaction
patterns and one indicating clearly defined clusters with no interactions crossing clusters. A
modularity analysis also produces a visual depiction of the clusters, using colors to highlight within
module interactions. The level of modularity that can be achieved is controlled somewhat by the
overall connectance (4.2) of the interface, or the ratio of actual interactions or edges (L) to total
possible edges in the graph. A higher connectance (closer to one) limits the level of modularity that
can be achieved while a lower level (closer to zero) makes modularity more likely but not
guaranteed. A bipartite network graph is relevant in cyber-physical systems as it uniquely focuses on
the interface between the cyber and physical components, helping to identify any patterns that are
impacting performance.

4.4.2.3.1.    Introduction
Current and future power grids are created with physical and cyber components creating unique
interactions and interdependencies [88], [89]. Interdependencies can be assessed and made a possible
target point by adversaries [67]. Recent attacks in Ukraine (2015, 2019, 2023) and in medical facilities
have prompted concerns regarding secure cyber-physical connections [68], [90], [91], [92].
Additionally, new concerns are emerging regarding functionality under natural and man-made
disasters prompting research and engagement on resilience and defense [93], [94]. A methodology to
characterize the Cyber-Physical Interdependencies (CPI) can assist in modeling various error and
attack propagation techniques in Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) [95].

Literature has heavily characterized diverse techniques available to adversaries for CPS [96], [97],
[98], [99]. Some works go as far as to address specific improvements in supervisory control and data
acquisition (SCADA) systems, and others break down popular attack patterns. Recent and
formidable attack patterns include denial of service (DoS) attacks, deception attacks, and replay
attacks. Mahmoud et al. gives insight into these specific attacks, highlights the need for an advanced
control system, and detail various sequential methodologies for modeling CPS. This includes
triggering strategies, game theory, and stochastic secure control approaches [100]. Such analysis and
methods provide valuable insights into the propagation and likely flow of information involved with
CPS; however, this does not necessarily capture network functionality and interdependence across
close devices that were not directly at risk. A method to develop CPI while modeling the entire
architecture in retrospect to specific cyber-physical connections can provide further insight into
reliance on specific devices, and preferable reliance on cyber or physical systems under disturbance
scenarios.

4.4.2.3.5.3.   Ecology Metrics
Ecologists have created various metrics to standardize the quantitative meaning of resilient and
robust ecological systems. Specific metrics related to community identification such as modularity
and nestedness are applied. Earlier research in bio-inspired design with ENA focused on graph
visualization and matrix-based illustrations to represent interactions among participants in a network
[93]. The N x N structural matrix, consisting of N actors or nodes inside the system boundaries,
extracts quantitative features from the network. This model employs graph-theory-driven techniques
to quantify the system’s attributes. By utilizing partite network representations, modularity analysis
becomes possible, allowing us to comprehend the interaction modules within the network. The
modularity level of a bipartite network is constrained by the total potential interactions compared to
those actualized within a network. Consequently, connectance (C, (4.8)) plays a crucial role in a
modularity analysis and assesses a system’s complexity. Generally, a lower connectance forces a
higher modularity. While connectance alone does not solely determine network stability, it aids in
understanding the network’s modularity since it regulates the achievable level of modularity.

4.4.2.3.7.   Conclusion
In this work, the identification and interactions of the WSCC 9-bus and ACTIV 200-bus were
characterized by metrics commonly used in herbivore, carnivore, and plant relationships in ecology.
These metrics were utilized during the design of bipartite and tripartite graph networks. Tripartite
networks applied in this study advance knowledge on indirect interactions between physical, cyber-
physical, and cyber devices. Beyond quick visualization, they provide insight into how disturbances
influence the interdependencies of critical network actors. This study contributes unconventional
techniques to investigate network changes and adaptations under duress from different denial of

 76

service outages. In summary, the information tripartite networks provide produce higher order
insights into specific outage types and nodal interdependencies.
Some limitations of this work include constructing specific scaled disturbance scenarios to emulate
WSCC 9-bus disturbances from prior work. In the future multi-element multi-stage complex
contingency scenarios can be studied to provide more insight regarding network strength and
resilience. Additionally, other threat types such as Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) should be modeled for
comparison to the work done in this study.

Some future applications include expanding modeling techniques to other verified power grid case
studies with the hope of standardization in the application of partite modeling to electrical
infrastructure. CPS networks have become an underlying foundation and structure for various
industries. Any network with CPS components at risk could benefit from a BN, TN analysis to test
interdependence. For more information on the unseen higher-order interactions, multi-layer
networks could be expanded into k-partite modeling. Overall, the techniques detailed in this study
seek to enhance a fundamental understanding of cyber-physical interdependencies in power grid
networks.

4.4.2.4.1.    Introduction
Modern technologies have made power systems more intelligent yet more vulnerable to attacks. The
adoption of new technologies into preexisting grid components offers more opportunities for cyber-
attacks, such as a Denial-of-Service (DoS) [67], to occur. Dealing with such threats requires treating
cyber-physical systems (CPS) comprehensively to understand the role that system interdependencies
play during disturbances. This prompts an investigation to develop methods that further define
these interconnected systems and their components. Categorizing cyber-physical network interfaces
and identifying underlying structures can assist in network integration and design for resilience.

A goal for future power grid system designs is to improve network resilience and robustness to
unexpected contingencies. Bio-inspired network design is one unconventional method that strives
toward achieving these resilience goals. Beyond power grids, bio-inspired design has been applied to
engineering makerspaces [74], water distribution networks [133], a system of systems design [134],
building and the design of the built environment [135], [136], [137], and industrial resource networks
[138]. Bio-inspired optimization has specifically been applied to redesign power grids to add physical
line connections to maximize the system’s robustness [102]. Previous works have also utilized a
specific graph type common in ecological network analysis which allows for species and device type
comparisons [34], [60], [66]. Bipartite network (BN) and most recently Tripartite network (TN)
graphs utilized modularity and nestedness metrics to understand the role of cyber-physical
interdependencies in the resilience of complex CPS [60]. A modularity analysis supports
investigations into network partitioning since modularity identifies actor groupings based on their
interactions. Examples of bipartite include plant-pollinator networks, due to their resistance to
disturbances and mutually beneficial interactions. Modularity, in conjunction with nestedness, helps
to model the interactions between the cyber and physical components of a power system and reveals
trends between a power system's resilience (based on contingency analyses) and its interconnectivity.

 77

The motivation for this work is to use bio-inspired design methods to improve the resilience of
cyber-physical power systems.

4.4.2.4.2.4. Remote Relay Backups and Sensing
A newer solution to mitigating threats and breaches on relay equipment specifically is altering the
network to create local or remote backups. These relay backups are useful because they can provide
alternative secure and redundant communication channels [142]. In terms of physical security, they
provide additional connection points for fault minimization and duration while supplying
information on locations and types of system failures. Relay protection should encompass sensitivity
to small loads, selectivity in their communication and reporting structure, speed, and reliability to
ensure proper system functionality [142]. Reliability can be further clarified by dependability and
security: dependability is the degree of certainty in which a relay operates correctly under faults;
security is the degree of certainty that the relay will not operate incorrectly for any fault within its
assigned zone [143].

Focusing on remote relay backups in this study prompts redesigning the 9-bus power system with
these redundant relay connections. Specifically, power experts when redesigning prefers a layered
separation between primary and secondary information backup devices. This separation ensures that
the backup system remains secure and unaffected in the event of a cyberattack on the primary
system, allowing the backup system to take over operations seamlessly. They may also employ multi-
level redundancy in their relay backup designs, as seen in Figure 38. This means that a failure in a
primary component triggers an intermediate backup system, followed by a tertiary system if needed.
For example, the protection relays and control centers are organized hierarchically to manage
failures at both local and regional levels.

4.4.2.4.2.5.   Optimization
Bipartite robustness may be useful for understanding attack propagation and pathways in networks
and for guiding designers toward resilience improvements. Payne et.al. established in [34], [60], [66]
that bipartite representations of the WSCC 9-bus system consistently display Qb values closer to 1
and NODF values close to 0. This indicates a highly segregated network with minimal interactions
between core groups or modules. While such a network of independent group clusters can be
beneficial, it better suits distribution-style power systems such as microgrids or nano grids. This
study investigates the benefit of increasing nestedness specifically in transmission power systems.
The network is optimized to maximize nestedness, mimicking the highly nested structures found in
mutualistic networks in nature. Prior work using this approach on industrial water networks found
that designs with a nestedness close to 1 had more effective use of resources, minimizing raw
material inputs and waste outputs, and responded the best to disturbances as compared to networks
with lower nestedness values (closer to 0) [138].

Based on the ideas of remote relay backup implementation, this study adds constraints to the
optimization, including row and column maximums for new line-to-line connections to optimize
specific sections of the 9-bus case study. Performed in MATLAB utilizing the bipartite package [77],
the optimizer takes in the bipartite matrix and iteratively adds connections to optimize nestedness.

 82

Unlike the location-constrained connections in the traditional remote relay backup redesign, the
optimization framework instead considers relays located at all local buses as viable locations for line
additions. The optimization constraints ensure that each device (relay, generator, etc.) has realistic
new connections that can be made: 7 for relays, 7 for buses, 2 for generators, and 3 for new load
connections. This approach allows for refining the bipartite networks, ensuring that the network
maintains high functionality while adhering to the power-specific structural constraints. The iterative
method is useful for studying complex ecological or technological networks for connectivity patterns
in the system's overall performance and robustness.

4.4.2.4.3. Results and Discussion
Understanding the modularity and nestedness of the cyber-physical system concerning disturbance
analysis results provides critical information about the design of cyber-physical interconnections and
the grid's resilience. Table 13 shows the number of network interactions added beyond the original
WSCC 9-bus model and the resulting nestedness (NODF), and bipartite robustness (BR). The
modularity and nestedness of the original WSCC 9-Bus network are consistent with previous results,
finding that the network is highly modular with no nested structures and has low bipartite
robustness (below 30%) [60]. The remote relay scenario increases the number of connections
between relays with 20 new interactions between the cyber and physical subsystems in the network.
These new interactions slightly increase both the nestedness and BR compared to the original WSCC
9-Bus design. The remote relay protection redesign experiences the lowest drop in resilience, as
defined by BR, after the cyber-only disturbance (DS2), suggesting that due to its design, DS2 had the
smallest impact on the starting resilience of the network. The bio-inspired redesign adds more than
double the interactions added by the remote relay redesign, at 50 total new interactions. The
resultant network is significantly more nested (NODF 0.43), confirming the effectiveness of the
optimization to maximize NODF. The bio-inspired redesign increases network resilience the most,
as defined by BR. This suggests that the optimization algorithm selected to add relay connections
that increased NODF and ones that provide a higher operational resilience. The bio-inspired
redesign starts of the most resilient of the 3 network designs and remains the most resilient through
the 2 disturbances tested. This is important because it is not unlikely that one disturbance/issue
follows another, so the post-disturbance BR value is an important indicator of the network’s
vulnerability to a secondary attack. Overall, the results here suggest that compared to the remote
relay protection scheme, the bio-inspired redesign contributes more holistically to system health.
The approach seems to place relay-to-relay interactions in the best possible locations for the overall
system rather than focusing only on a local relay area, ultimately leading to a design with better
overall network area coverage.

4.4.2.4.4.   Conclusion
This work has demonstrated the applicability of bio-inspired design to effectively optimize new
connections for relay protection schemes in the WSCC 9-bus power system case study. By
constructing traditional and bio-inspired relay protection schemes in a CPS network overall network
resilience was increased even under DoS threats. Utilizing an optimization framework that
maximizes bipartite network nestedness assisted in constructing systems with higher levels of
bipartite robustness. It was also found that nestedness maximization led to a trade-off decrease in
modularity creating a more balanced network better suited to the design of transmission power
systems. Although successful in utilizing the 9-bus case study, future work will explore larger
transmission-style case studies to establish a pattern for redesign. Additionally, microgrid and
distribution style systems could produce diverse results from those presented here as a microgrid is a

power distribution-based system. Finally, increasing the number and types of disturbances could lead
to a more concrete interpretation of the results by providing patterns of resilience loss among the
cyber, physical, and CPS system networks.

4.4.3.2.1.1.    Introduction
Modern technologies are revolutionizing power systems, improving their control and making them
more interconnected but also leaving them increasingly susceptible to cyber-attacks. The rapid
adoption of emerging technologies is creating a fertile ground for cyber threats, such as Denial-of-
Service (DoS), Man-in-the-Middle (MiTM) [67], and malware attacks [89]. A prime example of this
growing vulnerability is the report concerning the cyber attack to the Ukrainian power grid in
December 2015 [68]. They detail the repeated targeting of their energy infrastructure monitoring
systems, showcasing the unexpected potential of cyber disruptions to influence physical components
and ultimately lead to costly blackouts. Addressing these significant challenges necessitates a holistic
approach to cyber-physical systems by first focusing on the intricate interdependencies at play
during security breaches and disturbances. and disturbances.

Traditional methods of handling power system emergencies rely on grid operators and hazard
operation plans. Operators are trained to deal with emergencies to prevent blackouts [147] yet their
operation departments often have minimal cooperation and collaboration with cyber departments
[148]. A structural gap exists between power systems’ Operational Technology (OT) and
Information Technology (IT). Current strategies for handling emergencies in cyber-physical systems
(CPS) are evolving but still in their infancy, highlighting the need for integrated and well-conceived

 91

plans that bridge this OT/IT divide [149]. Such plans are essential for developing more resilient and
reliable power systems and preventing previous major US incidents.

Biological ecosystems on the other hand have demonstrated remarkable resilience in the face of
disturbances, a quality captured by ecologists studying network architecture [45] and applied to
human networks by engineering researchers. Ecological Network Analysis (ENA), which features
quantitative metrics relating network characteristics to their functioning, has been applied to human
networks such as supply chains [150], systems of systems [134], water distribution networks [104],
and industrial resource networks [138]. These works however have primarily concentrated on
physical networks, including power systems [73], [102], [151], [152], aiming to emulate ecosystem
resilience. These studies have used several ENA metrics, including Ecological Robustness (RECO),
Average Mutual Information (AMI), Cyclicity (λmax), and Degree of System Order (DoSO), to
evaluate and design physical power systems network structures. Following the successful
characterization of cyber-physical power systems utilizing DeepWalk and bipartite network
modeling methods [34], the cyber component connections of power systems are investigated to
understand the strengths and vulnerabilities of the cyber network layer separately. Cyber network
topologies here are modeled in a similar fashion to ecological food webs and evaluated using ENA
[58] to assess the resilience, risks, and vulnerabilities related to the overall topology of cyber-physical
systems.

Prior work has elaborated on the development of preliminary topologies for a cyber network [151],
examining 2 distinct scenarios (in a 3-substation and an 8-substation cyber-physical system) and
emphasizing the potential of an ENA based approach. That work also stated a need to further
enhance cyber topology modeling by adding more content to the overall system, including firewalls,
substations, and relays, to enable advanced disturbance patterns to be observed and provide insight
into the inner workings of network resilience, robustness, and security. This work advances these
previous studies by computing disturbances specifically applicable to communication connections,
described in [34]. Packet delivery information and changes in topological structure are analyzed,
providing new insight into the possible openings available to adversaries and their impact on the
overall network’s ability to function under duress. The main contributions of this paper are as
follows:
 1. A bio-inspired model for assessing the strengths and weaknesses of communication system
 interactions.
 2. Evaluation of the WSCC 9-bus power system case study using ENA and related network
 methods.
 3. Highlighting the most resilient network structures and the most robust and reliable network
 designs and data routing approaches using ENA.

4.4.3.2.1.2. Background and Problem Formulation
A Denial of Service (DoS) attack is a particularly critical threat that targets communication networks
by flooding the network with an overwhelming volume of traffic using protocols. A DoS attack
overloads the processing of messages and effectively disrupts the flow of necessary operational data.
This disruption can lead to a cascade of negative outcomes, from operational delays to a complete
shutdown of system monitoring and control functions. The consequences of such an attack can be
far-reaching, potentially resulting in power outages, compromised system security, and even safety
hazards.

 92

This study uses an augmented Western System Coordinating Council (WSCC) 9-bus System [72] as

intermediaries between networks, Human Machine Interfaces (HMI) provide system operators with

Evenness, also known as Species Evenness (E, (4.32)), is a vital aspect of biodiversity and is
mathematically assessed through various indices and metrics, with the well-known Shannon
Evenness Index, also referred to as Pielou’s Evenness Index [157], [158]. 𝐻𝑚𝑎𝑥 in (4.32) denotes the
maximum achievable value of the Shannon Index when all species have an equal abundance. An E
value near 1 signifies a community exhibiting evenly distributed species without dominance.
Inversely, a value substantially below 1 implies that certain species dominate, leading to an uneven
network. Greater E has been found to correlate with increased stability and resilience in ecosystems,
due to no single species exerting excessive influence and improved adaptability to alterations or
disruptions. Low E on the other hand may render ecosystems susceptible to disturbances since any
impact on a dominant species can significantly affect the entire community [157], [159], [160], [161].

(DoSO closer to 1). This may correspond to the reporting mechanism enacted from the Relay-to-

4.4.3.2.2.1.   Introduction
Technology-driven integration has created an exponential increase in the number of cyber-physical
systems and the number of targets for cyber-based adversary exploitation. Cyber disturbances often
propagate to physical systems and cause many issues. Man-in-the-middle and false data injection
attacks have been found to instigate cascading failures that can cause entire substation and multi-
device outages [162]. Research has largely focused on attack replication, post-attack analysis, risk
analysis, and preventative attack resources to understand and mitigate negative impacts [34], [66],
[67], [163]. In [162], the Common Open Research Emulator (CORE) was used as a network
emulator to help in forensic cyber analysis via a set of monitoring metrics that can alert the presence
of adversaries. Additional real-time system-level health metrics are still very much needed to address
the increasing frequency, potential severity, and broadening targets of cyber-attacks worldwide.

Modeling the cyber-physical interdependencies of a cyber-physical system (CPS) using static and
temporal graphs provides a systems perspective that can help fill this gap. Static graphs provide
insight into the structural nature of the network as snapshots in time. Temporal graphs capture
dynamic network properties. Both graphs can help quantitatively establish patterns based on
common interactions. Graph models of power network topologies and physical system behaviors
can then use graph and information theory to study the impacts of attacks [164]. Graph-based
models are quick, simple to construct, and endlessly customizable.

Graphs of CPSs result in layers that can highlight power flows on one level, cyber flows on another,
and the cyber-physical interactions between them (Figure 49) (Parandehgheibi, Modiano et al. ,
Soltan, Yannakakis et al. 2016, Wang, Lin et al. 2016). Flow-based edge information in each layer
captures device level, class level, and any larger systems level interactions. Currently, finite state
modeling is a popular method to analyze dynamic behavior, but poor repeatability creates challenges

 100

in post-scenario analysis [68], [165]. There is a need for quantitative metrics that can reliably capture
changes in temporal information while preserving the structure of the network.

 Figure 49: An example of the WSCC 9-bus power system cyber topology.

Ecological Network Analysis (ENA) is a technique employed by ecologists to evaluate food web
models of biological ecosystems to quantitatively connect network structure to function. One
particularly interesting avenue for engineers has been the resultant system-level understanding of
ecosystem resilience and robustness in the face of unexpected disruptions [44], [45], [155].
Engineering investigations of these biological system characteristics have found applicability to
diverse human systems, including but not limited to supply chains [150], systems of systems [134],
and water distribution networks [104]. Past work specific to power grids has explored the design of
the physical power grid, finding that the characteristics of ecosystems are valuable for the adaptation
of current power systems and the design of future power systems to improve N-X contingencies
[71], [73], [102], [152]. Current work specific to cyber-physical power systems has begun evaluating
them from a static perspective, which contributes information on vulnerable devices, device groups
or types, and the risks of attacks on devices [34], [60], [66], [119]. This article builds upon this past
research, applying the ENA metric Degree of System Order (DoSO) to evaluate a Denial-of-Service
(DoS) attack propagating over time from an unknown location within the Western System
Coordinating Council’s (WSCC) 9-bus power system case study [70]. The metric shows promise for
establishing a holistic system-health analysis for understanding temporal cyber interdependencies in
CPSs.

4.4.3.2.2.2.   Methodology

4.4.3.2.2.2.1. WSCC 9-Bus Power System Case Study and Disturbances
Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks are relatively common in CPSs. They use traffic protocols to drown
a network in both/either excess or incorrect operational data [166], with the goal of overloading
system processing units to negatively impact physical and/or cyber devices. Most DoS attacks
propagate from the cyber network causing spikes in system communications that are often too fast
for operators to evaluate the situation accurately. It is also difficult to understand where the DoS
attack originated. This study uses the Western System Coordinating Council (WSCC) 9-Bus System
[72] case study (Figure 50). Substations in the system act as central hubs for the cyber

 101

communication network to communicate with lower-level devices, such as relays, and transmitting
devices, such as substations, to human-machine interfaces located in the control centers. Devices
such as buses, relays, and firewalls are embedded within the substations in this model, thus
supporting a focus on data capture at the primary transport points (i.e., substations).

 Figure 50: The Western System Coordinating Council (WSCC) 9-Bus power system case study.
 Figure modified from [60].

The control center is the location of human influence in the system: it is the main communication
juncture that oversees power synchronization and coordinates system actions. The substations are
centralized locations that represent sections of the CPS system. Typically, substations are modeled as
collection points within the cyber system. Instructions for system actions are then carried in data
information signals or packets and can include physical system reports e.g., power generation status
and commands influencing the state of power devices. The case study information contains packet
round trip times (RTT) and packet information at all three substations over 1700 milliseconds. RTT
is the time it takes to send a packet of cyber information and receive confirmation that it was
delivered to the designated device. Each device in the 9-bus system seen in Figure 50 contains a
maximum packet capacity of 9000, which are packets available for receiving during communications.
Additionally, due to the nature of it being an imperfect network, up to 100 packets could be
dropped on random RTT rows due to various normal communication issues. The three substations
in the WSCC 9-Bus System interact with the control center, providing an optimal opportunity for an
adversary to perform a DoS. Normal and DoS emulation setups are both analyzed.

4.4.3.2.2.2.2. Ecological Network Analysis
 Ecological Network Analysis (ENA) was developed by ecologists to study food webs using
directional graphs. As such, the approach is flexible and can be applied to almost any weighted or
unweighted network directional graph describing components that exchange and transform things

 102

like materials and energy. ENA then uses matrix-based depictions of the directional graph to
calculate quantitative metrics describing various network characteristics [45], [104]. These matrix
depictions consider node-level interactions as well as the magnitude of these interactions. Figure 51

Flow magnitude-based (weighted graphs) ecosystem analysis approaches have been particularly
successfully utilized to design and analyze engineering systems to adopt ecologically similar system-
level characteristics [44], [46], [58], [155]. Degree of System Order (DoSO, (4.38)) is one such metric
that shows potential in prior works to quantitatively assess a network’s structure to predict its
performance during a disturbance. The metric is used by ecologists to quantify the organization,
structure, and complexity of ecological networks and finds that food webs reside in a specific range
of values, from 0.21-0.59, suggesting an ecologically superior system performance occurs when the
system falls in that range [167]. DoSO for a system can have important implications for its stability,
resilience, and ability to respond to disturbances [58]. DoSO is calculated as the ratio of Average
Mutual Information (AMI, (4.37)) and the Shannon’s Index (H, (4.36)) and thus represents the
complexity of the pathways within a network as measured from 0 to 1. AMI represents cumulative
uncertainty regarding a flow's entry into the system and subsequent direction through the system.
Here, Tij represents the magnitude of a flow from any node i to any node j within or crossing the
system boundaries. Total System Throughput (TSTp) represents the probability of the events
occurring at nodes i and j and is the total sum of all flows passing through the network (4.35).
Shannon’s Index (H) represents the maximum potential for systems-level organization and diversity
within a flow network [151]. Shannon originally labeled this term as the “surprisal” that occurs
within a network due to no prior knowledge of an event [168]. As AMI approaches H, the pathways
in the system become more constrained. TSTp is the known event in the network that can thereby
impose a quantifying organization-based constraint.

4.4.3.2.2.3. Results and Discussion
Analyzing the 3400 rows of normal and disturbance CPS data via ENA resulted in the creation of

to correctly identify DoS attacks over time in a power system. Total system throughput had a
maximum difference between normal and disturbance scenarios of 21,000 for all substations and
24,000 for the control center, in sending packets. The origination of the disturbance was determined
to be at t = 232 milliseconds from these values. This was confirmed to be the initial insertion point
of a DoS propagation into the power system network. At this t, the RTT values for the network also
increase from 0.03 milliseconds to the dataset maximum of 3.5 milliseconds. Considering that most
RTT times linger between 0.01 and 0.030 milliseconds for this dataset, a time of 3.5 milliseconds
indicates a temporarily non-responsive device in the network.

4.4.3.2.2.4.    Conclusion
This work searched for systems level methods to monitor and evaluate Substation and Control
Center interactions in a cyber-physical power system. New knowledge for cyber-physical power
system design configurations is presented by modeling packet traffic flow through the system under
various operating conditions. Ecological Network Analysis (ENA) was then shown as able to detect
a DoS attack in a cyber-physical power system. Utilizing round trip time data corresponding with
substation packet information, Degree of System Order (DoSO) specifically was found to be useful
as a system level metric for CPS. The representation of the cyber system level from an ecological
perspective provides new knowledge on system health and communication complexity. DoSO was
found to have unique ranges for each substation depending on whether the information was sent by
the control center or by the substations. Additionally, network analysis over time was able to
pinpoint the origination of the cyber-attack at Substation 2 and displayed the new range of system
level changes to all substations affected. The ENA approach is seen to be effective in characterizing
the state of the system in both normal and disturbance scenarios, especially where the origin of the
disturbance may be unknown to operators. The size of the case study is a limitation of this work. In
addition to processing larger case studies, future efforts will seek to capture more data at the relay
and backup devices to get more detailed packet information and additional insight into large-scale
power distribution systems.

 Understanding Stability of Eigenvalues
In the context of DMD, eigenvalues represent the temporal behavior of modes of the system. They
indicate how modes grow or decay over time. The location of eigenvalues in the complex plane
helps to identify stability: If the real part of an eigenvalue is negative, it indicates a decaying mode,
meaning it is stable. If the real part is positive, it indicates a growing mode, which is unstable. If the
real part is zero, the mode is neutral and oscillatory, which might still be a concern for power
systems depending on the magnitude. For a system to be stable in discrete time the eigenvalues must
lie inside the unit circle. In Figure 56, some of the windows of eigenvalues are plot on top of each
other.

4.6. Long Short-Term Memory Autoencoder Neural Networks
Detection of abnormal events, whether from malicious or failure origins, is a fundamental task in
ensuring the security and resilience of the grid. For cyber-physical systems such as the electric grid,
such detection must process both cyber and physical data streams to accurately characterize and
detect cyber-physical system anomalies [171]. Yet challenges exist around input space
dimensionality, generalizability, multimodality, and etc. that accompany diverse data sets with
varying temporal and spatial characteristics.
Autoencoders (AEs) are one of the most fundamental data fusion techniques that can learn complex
non-linear patterns in the data [172]. They are artificial neural networks (ANN) whose structure is
symmetrical consisting of an encoder and a decoder, shown in Figure 58, and fall in the category of
unsupervised learning focusing on learning the optimal encoding-decoding scheme from data.

 112

Further details on their implementation can be found here [171]. Ultimately, a cyber-physical LSTM
AE was developed that is uniquely suitable for processing time-series data from a CPS.

 Figure 58: Autoencoder encoding-decoding scheme.

The LSTM-based AE model is trained offline only on the normal operation normalized cyber-
physical data, trying to understand its inherent non-linear patterns and temporal dynamics and
minimize the reconstruction error (RE) between the encoder’s input and decoder’s output. Through
this process, it learns how to perform cyber-physical data fusion and how to distill the salient
temporal patterns into a low-dimensional information-rich representation. The key idea of the cyber-
physical threat detection lies on the exploitation of the RE metric, which quantifies the dissimilarity
between the original and reconstructed data. Specifically, the higher the RE, the more pronounced
the deviation from the normal data patterns; therefore, signifying a potential threat/anomaly in the
electric grid. Thus, only after the model is optimized and fully trained, we analyze the reconstruction
errors of the normal data points and find a threshold that indicates when a data point is normal or
abnormal. Overall, the LSTM AE enables classification and detection of normal operation, cyber
events, physical events, and combined cyber-physical events. Results of the LSTM AE with the
previously presented WSCC 9-bus (“HARMONIE) emulation environment are shared in [171];
next, we will provide results of the LSTM AE for the Emera microgrid environment.

 4.6.1. LSTM AE Classification of Emera Microgrid Disturbances
The LSTM Autoencoder was trained only on normal cyber-physical data from the Emera Microgrid
with 18% compression level of the input (i.e., the latent space is ~18% smaller than the input space).
The pre-processing of features was broken into physical features and cyber features and aggregated
using a windowing approach. For physical features, all physical features recording integer or
floating-point values were included in their raw form using the last value in the given window. This

to this approach would be down selecting the number of physical field/features included based on

prior work in a different project. They included ‘single window’ fields such as the number of unique
IP’s and average packet size and then ‘rolling window’ fields which take into account multiple
adjacent windows, such as the standard deviation of packet sizes. There are a larger number of

5. CYBER-PHYSICAL UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION
In this section, an uncertainty quantification (UQ) methodology was developed specific to cyber-
physical systems and tested upon a variety of the InterGraph-CPS techniques.

5.1. Developing Initial UQ Methodology Plan
 A direct outcome of the team engagement and project activities is SwRI’s increased understanding
of each of the four cyber-physical interdependency modeling approaches currently being pursued by
the Sandia and Texas A&M University (TAMU) researchers along with the CPS emulation
environment that links SCEPTRETM and Real-Time Digital Simulation (RTDS) of power systems.
These approaches will comprise the InterGraph-CPS architecture – K-shell analysis, ecological
network analysis (ENA), dynamic mode decomposition (DMD), and synchronous motor (SM)
modeling. This integration is shown in Figure 90.

Figure 90: SCEPTRE-RTDS CPS emulation environment and four InterGraph-CPS modeling
approaches to capture cyber-physical interdependencies.

 Initially, SwRI began drafting Verification, Validation, and Uncertainty Quantification (VVUQ)
documents for each of the InterGraph-CPS modeling approaches. However, through iteration, it was
determined that a UQ plan for the SCEPTER-RTDS emulation environment was more suitable at
this stage of the program to further guide development of InterGraph-CPS. For example, if the key
outputs are understood and the key sensitivities of cyber-physical interactions are quantified,
InterGraph-CPS can be guided towards approaches that adequately capture the same phenomena.
Thus, the goal of the UQ plan is to quantify the sensitivities of both cyber and physical nodal outputs

in the 9-bus grid architecture (Figure 91) under different disturbance scenarios as simulated in the CPS
emulation environments.

First, the LDRD team analyzed four different scenarios – a baseline (S0), a denial of service (DoS)

mitigated DoS (S3). Each scenario was executed 3 different times only, differing by the instantiation

5.2.1.1. Defining and Propagating Uncertainties
 Two methods of defining uncertainties around the 56 flow values were investigated. First, a change
to the nominal value was chosen randomly from a uniform distribution bounded by -1 and 1, denoted
as U (-1,1). This was chosen so that the flow values of 1 would not be negative when modified by the
additive U (-1,1). The other scheme was adding a 10% coefficient of variation (CoV) to each flow
value such that the change to the nominal value was chosen randomly from a normal distribution with
zero mean and 10% of the nominal value, denoted as N (0, 10% CoV). This was done to reflect that
nodes with higher flow values may vary more while still ensuring a low probability of being less than
zero. Both sampling schemes produced similar results in terms of uncertainty propagation, however,
they differ in subsequent sensitivity studies which will be discussed.
 Figure 95 depicts the distributions of computed DoSO and Reco values for the nominal system after
propagating 10,000 Monte Carlo samples of the 21-packet flow through the model to the network
health metrics. Figure 96 contextualizes this variation relative to the window of vitality, in which the
normal operating scenario exhibits robustness to variability by staying within the window of vitality
despite variation in the system parameters. Note that, in the nominal system, uncertainty doesn’t seem
important in assessing DoSO. However, for off-nominal system cases like TAMU’s “Control to Relay,
DS2” case, the considered uncertainty will give us a finite probability of being in or out of the window
of vitality.

 5.2.2. UQ Studies around Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)
SwRI and SNL have additionally discussed applying UQ methods around the DMD modeling
approach used for event detection and impact prediction. In a DMD analysis, modal decomposition
is performed on scenario output results over a window of prescribed number of time-steps (e.g. 100
time-steps). The 32 outputs considered thus far are the voltages and frequencies of 8 Phasor
Measurement Units (PMUs) through time over approximately 150 seconds (i.e. 9000 timesteps at 60
Hz) of a cyber-physical disruption scenario without mitigation as depicted in Figure 99.

 140

 Figure 99: PMU frequencies (top row) and voltages (bottom 3 rows) for the 8 PMUs in the 9-bus
 system for a cyber-physical disruption scenario without mitigation.
The normalized eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the outputs computed over a given window indicate
whether the dynamic system is stable or unstable if the eigenvalues fall inside or outside of a unit
circle, respectively. Figure 100 depicts two eigenvalues λ on the real number line Re{λ} unit circle
where the eigenvalue of a stable system falls within the unit circle (green point) and the eigenvalue of
an unstable system falls outside of the unit circle in the unstable region (red point). Further, even if
the system is stable, there are limit states that are to be upheld for system safety (i.e. voltage is never
less than 95% of the max voltage).

  Figure 100: Assessment of system stability using eigenvector values relative to the unit circle.

 A probabilistic approach was conceptualized in which the eigenvalues of a standard scenario (i.e.,
no cyber-physical attack) could be used to compare against new data. This conceptual approach is
depicted in the progression from Figure 101 to Figure 102. In Figure 101, 10 Mode 1 and Mode 2
eigenvalues from a trusted, stable scenario (red) are shown against a new scenario (yellow). In this
case, the yellow Mode 1 and Mode 2 eigenvalues are stable, however, they are unlike the eigenvalues
observed before. Therefore, we would like to evaluate the probability that the current scenario is part
of the set of trusted operations.
 Figure 102 demonstrates a 2-dimensional probabilistic model trained on the existing data. The
probabilistic model is a kernel density estimate (KDE) in which, say a Gaussian kernel density is placed
around each data point with a fixed bandwidth. Then the probability values in 2D space around each
data point are summed and normalized to create a 2D probability estimate across the entire space.
Therefore, KDEs are sensitive to both the bandwidth parameter and the number of data points
considered. In Figure 102, the bandwidth is fixed at 0.2 and the resulting KDE is comprised of clusters
of high probabilities in between areas with near-zero probability. In Figure 103, however, the
bandwidth is increased to 0.5 making the KDE more diffuse across space. Further, there may be a
non-zero probability outside the unit circle which would suggest the utility of a hierarchical model –
first assessing stability with the unit circle, then assessing probability with a KDE.

 USING THE START FRAMEWORK

To explore the integrated benefits and insights gained from leveraging the diverse InterGraph-CPS

technique for the Emera Microgrid use-case and attack scenarios (MITM and DoS) described in
Section 3.3.4.3. The results for the individual techniques can be summarized as follows:

ENA and Emera Microgrid
ENA provides insight into Emera microgrid structure during normal operation: low network
redundancy with very specialized connections (each nanogrid box with each of the generation and
loads) but with a single back-up power option as well as interconnections between nanogrids. This
implies complex structural cycling due to this shared energy and interconnections and overall strong
cyclicity yet high nodal vulnerability. These ENA results indicate that the Emera microgrid has
resilient structural design with nodal failure points but many back-up interconnections for power
delivery.

LSTM AE and Emera Microgrid

physical or combined cyber-physical). The AE is robust to data outliers and we can use the normal

6.1. Overall START Integration Takeaways
To effectively analyze and increase understanding of cyber-physical systems, the START framework
was developed with a diverse set of graph and temporal techniques. This included graph/structural
techniques such as ecological network analysis (ENA), bipartite/tripartite analysis, Node2Vec,
DeepWalk, and other graph-based analysis techniques and temporal analysis techniques such as
dynamic mode decomposition (DMD), synchronous motor models (PRISM), long short-term
memory autoencoder (LSTM AE) neural networks. In this paper, we focused on a subset of these
START techniques, specifically ENA, DeepWalk, Node2Vec, and LSTM AE to explore the
integration between their results and analyze insights gained.

This report presented results for each of these START techniques for two different use-cases:
WSCC 9-Bus and Emera Microgrid cyber-physical emulation environments where a variety of cyber-
physical disturbances were implemented. For each use-case, we analyzed the individual results and
discussed the integrated insights gained into detection the cyber-physical anomaly and predicting
consequences. Specifically, we found that the LSTM AE classification and detection of an anomaly
could be cross-correlated to increase confidence in detection while both ENA and
DeepWalk/Node2Vec provided insight on structural fragility and changes and predict which nodes
could be attacked next and their risk levels. These multi-faceted perspectives on the same event
analysis provide unique understanding on how the cyber-physical system is being impacted, how
individual cyber-physical nodes are linked and impacted, and how impact could propagate through
the CPS. This is demonstrated with presented results where:

 150

 1. ENA provides overall characterization of the CPS and its strength/fragility,
 2. AE detects and classifies CPS anomalies,
 3. ENA confirms the AE detection with changes in strength/fragility metrics,
 4. DeepWalk and Node2Vec predicts what nodes could be impacted next and their risk levels,
 5. AE detection can confirm predicted node compromise,
 6. ENA and DeepWalk/Node2Vec structural and risk analysis updates,
 7. and START analysis continues.

Overall, this continuous START analysis provides both structural and temporal analysis to not only
understand changes in the CPS interdependencies but also the physical system dynamics.
Additionally, START enabled comparison of unique resilience and data flow characteristics between
transmission power systems and microgrid power systems. In future work, we will fully integrate
START techniques such as DMD to assess the fragility of dynamic physical system and predict
dynamic consequences and PRISM which provides dynamic system characterization. Larger power
systems with diverse configuration and autonomous control (e.g., energy management systems) will
be explored in future START studies, in addition to application to different critical infrastructure
domains. This comprehensive framework, with structural and temporal (both cyber-physical and
purely physical) analysis provides situational awareness across the CPS and flexible granularity for
different planning, operation, and response activities.

7. CONCLUSIONS AND NEXT STEPS
To effectively analyze and increase understanding of cyber-physical systems, the InterGraph-CPS
framework was developed with a diverse set of graph and temporal techniques. This included
graph/structural techniques such as ecological network analysis, bipartite/tripartite analysis,
Node2Vec, DeepWalk, and other graph-based analysis techniques and temporal analysis techniques
such as dynamic mode decomposition, synchronous motor models (PRISM), long short-term
memory autoencoder neural networks.
Each of these techniques were tested with different real-time cyber-physical emulation environments
to validate individual and integrated cyber-physical interdependency insight and detection of
anomalies. This included the development of a new emulation capability based on a real microgrid
system where both the communication and control network and microgrid power system design was
based on the real-world system. A variety of cyber-physical disturbance events were implemented in
these emulation environments and the InterGraph-CPS technique results were used to understand
the system impact, cyber-physical interdependency impact, and predicted impact pathways.
Lastly, a cyber-physical UQ analysis approach was developed to assess each of the interdependency
results to help decision-makers (e.g., cyber defenders and system operators) understand detection
results and take mitigation action, if needed.
Overall, InterGraph-CPS demonstrated successful integrated cyber-physical interdependency
analysis within relevant emulation environments. For next steps, the team would like to explore
automating the InterGraph-CPS technique integrated analysis and testing with real field data or field
sites with key industry partners.
