Bioactive carbon dots from peony seed meal for nanomedicine via circular economy
Authors: Zhi-Fang Liu, Qi Wang, Jian-Hua Xiao, Jiang-Hui Wang, Jian Li, Chen-Jing Han, Seid Mahdi Jafari, Zhi-Yuan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe valorization of agricultural waste into high-value nanomaterials is key to a sustainable circular economy. Here, we transform underutilized peony seed meal into nitrogen/oxygen-doped carbon dots (PSM-CDs) via a simple hydrothermal process. These PSM-CDs exhibit potent superoxide dismutase-mimetic activity, governed by surface carbonyl groups. In a hemolytic anemia model, they act as a dual-pathway therapeutic by scavenging reactive oxygen species and suppressing the NF-κB/NLRP3 axis, while promoting erythropoiesis via ROS-HIF-1 signaling, with excellent biosafety. This work establishes a scalable “waste-to-therapeutics” paradigm, bridging sustainable agriculture and nanomedicine development.Subject areas: applied sciences, biological waste treatment, biosafety, nanomaterials

Introduction

The transition toward a circular bioeconomy necessitates the development of sustainable technologies that can convert agricultural waste streams into value-added products, thereby addressing environmental concerns and fostering resource efficiency.1,2 In this context, the processing of oilseeds generates substantial quantities of protein-rich seed meal, which often remains underutilized despite its potential.3 Peony (Paeonia ostii) seed meal (PSM) is a prime example of such a byproduct.4 With the rapid expansion of the oil peony industry in China, annual PSM production has surged, representing a significant waste management challenge.5 While traditionally used as fertilizer or low-value feed, the high-value conversion of PSM remains largely unexplored.6 Interestingly, PSM is known to contain a diverse array of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids and stilbenes,7,8 suggesting its potential as a premium feedstock for the synthesis of functional nanomaterials, rather than being relegated as waste.

Concurrently, the field of nanomedicine is increasingly embracing the principles of green chemistry, seeking sustainable, and biocompatible nanomaterials for therapeutic applications.9,10 Carbon dots (CDs) have emerged as promising nanotherapeutic agents for managing complex clinical conditions, owing to their unique combination of nanoscale dimensions (<10 nm), negligible toxicity, exceptional biocompatibility, and ease of functionalization.11 The biological versatility of CDs may stem from their tunable surface chemistry, which is dictated by the precursor materials used in synthesis.12 This precursor-dependent variability enables precise engineering of surface functional groups (-COOH, -NH2, and -OH), thereby modulating their bio-interfacial interactions and therapeutic outcomes.13 This has led to a growing interest in using renewable biomass, particularly phytochemical-rich agricultural byproducts, as sustainable precursors.14 Such biomass-derived CDs not only circumvent the use of toxic chemicals but often incorporate surface functional groups (e.g., -COOH, -OH, and -NH2) derived from the precursor’s chemical motifs, potentially contributing to their observed bioactivities.15,16,17,18

Hemolytic anemia is a clinically significant hematological disorder characterized by accelerated erythrocyte destruction and inadequate bone marrow compensation, affecting 1–5 individuals per 100,000 annually worldwide.19 Pathologically, hemolytic anemia stems from diverse etiologies including inherited abnormalities (erythrocyte membrane defects, enzymatic deficiencies, and hemoglobinopathies)20,21 and acquired conditions (autoimmune disorders, infections, and toxin exposure).22,23 Current treatment options, including immunosuppressants, splenectomy, and transfusions,24,25,26 remain suboptimal due to substantial side effects, high relapse rates (30%–50% within 2 years), and incomplete therapeutic responses. Emerging research highlights the critical role of oxidative-inflammatory pathways in disease progression, where ROS not only directly damage erythrocyte membranes but also create a vicious cycle by amplifying inflammation and promoting extravascular hemolysis.19,27 This dual pathophysiology underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that can simultaneously target both oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways to break this destructive cycle and improve clinical outcomes.

Inspired by the convergence of these needs and opportunities, we hypothesized that the specific phytochemical profile of PSM (e.g., flavonoids, stilbenes) could be leveraged not merely as a carbon source, but as a source of heteroatoms and reactive functional groups for engineering bioactive CDs. Herein, we propose a precursor-directed synthesis approach, wherein key functional motifs (such as carbonyl and hydroxyl groups) derived from the native compounds are incorporated into the surface chemistry of the CDs during hydrothermal synthesis, thereby programming their therapeutic function. This work meticulously details their synthesis, thorough physicochemical characterization, and evaluation of their multifaceted bioactivities. Furthermore, through targeted surface chemical modifications, we elucidate the key functional groups responsible for their enzymatic activity. We demonstrate that PSM-CDs function as potent SOD-mimetic nanozymes with superior ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammatory capabilities, effectively protecting against oxidative damage and inflammation in cellular and animal models of hemolytic anemia. Transcriptomic insights further reveal their role in promoting erythropoiesis via the ROS-HIF-1 axis. Crucially, we confirm their excellent biosafety. This work establishes a sustainable “waste-to-therapeutics” paradigm for nanomedicine development.

Results

The synthesis and characteristics of PSM-CDs

SM-CDs were successfully synthesized via a one-pot hydrothermal carbonization protocol at 220°C for 8 h. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed highly monodisperse spherical nanoparticles with uniform dispersibility, exhibiting a narrow size distribution between 0.9 and 2.3 nm (mean diameter: 1.63 nm) (Figures 1A and 1B). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) further confirmed the crystalline nature of PSM-CDs, displaying distinct lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.23 nm, consistent with the graphitic (002) plane of carbon nanomaterials (Figure 1C). Moreover, X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns indicated two distinct diffraction peaks at 21.3° and 40.9° (Figure 1D), corresponding to the (002) and (100) crystal planes of natural carbon structures,28 respectively. Thus, these results indicate that the PSM-CDs were prepared successfully, yielding a product of outstanding purity.

To comprehensively characterize the chemical architecture of PSM-CDs, we employed a multi-spectroscopic approach combining FTIR spectroscopy and XPS analyses. In Figure 1E, the FTIR spectrum identified O-H/N-H stretching at 3417 cm−1, aliphatic C-H stretching at 2925 cm−1, carbonyl C=O vibration at 1648 cm−1, aromatic C=C at 1451 cm−1, and ether C-O-C (1036 cm−1) stretching. The survey of XPS spectrum (Figure 1F) confirmed the predominant presence of carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen in PSM-CDs. The high-resolution C 1s XPS spectrum (Figure 1G) was deconvoluted into three distinct components centered at 284.5 eV (C−C/C=C, 50.7% area), 286.0 eV (C−O, 32.7%), and 287.9 eV (C=O, 16.5%), confirming a carbon-dominated framework with significant oxygen functionalization.29 Nitrogen speciation analysis revealed two predominant configurations in the N 1s spectrum (Figure 1H): pyrrolic nitrogen (398.0 eV, 71.3% contribution) and graphitic nitrogen (399.3 eV, 28.6% contribution), indicating nitrogen doping through both edge-site and basal-plane incorporation. Moreover, oxygen functional group analysis (O 1s, Figure 1I) further resolved two major bonding states: carbonyl groups (C=O, 531.25 eV, 41.2%) and hydroxyl/ether moieties (C−OH/C−O−C, 530.4 eV, 58.7%). The abundance of hydrophilic functional groups, particularly the dominant O−H/N−H and C−OH moieties, accounts for the exceptional aqueous dispersibility of PSM-CDs (zeta potential: −17.2 ± 1.2 mV, Figure S1), a critical feature for biomedical applications requiring colloidal stability in physiological environments.

Additionally, the UV-Vis absorption spectrum of PSM-CDs (Figure 1J) exhibited a pronounced absorption peak at 290 nm, characteristic of the π-π∗ electronic transitions in sp2-hybridized carbon domains, confirming the presence of graphitic nanostructures. Notably, PSM-CDs demonstrated excitation-dependent fluorescence emission, with optimal excitation and emission maxima at 390 nm and 470 nm, respectively. The absolute quantum yield of the PSM-CDs was measured to be approximately 7.3% using an integrating sphere system, suggesting their potential as versatile fluorescent probes for bioimaging applications. To evaluate the practical applicability of PSM-CDs in complex biological systems, we systematically investigated their fluorescence stability under diverse environmental stressors. PSM-CDs maintained 98.6% ± 1.4% of their initial fluorescence intensity across a wide NaCl concentration gradient (0–1.0 M) (Figure S2). Notably, the stability of PSM-CDs’ fluorescence stayed constant after 35 days of exposure (Figure 1K). The fluorescence intensity also remained stable over a pH range of 4–11 (Figure 1L). This exceptional stability of PSM-CDs may be attributed to their negatively charged surface, which electrostatically repels ionic species and prevents aggregation. Moreover, the bioimaging potential of PSM-CDs was further validated by laser confocal microscopy, which revealed efficient cellular uptake and homogeneous cytoplasmic distribution of PSM-CDs in live cells (Figure S3).

Assessment of SOD-like properties for ROS neutralization of PSM-CDs

Emerging evidence highlights the enzyme-mimetic potential of carbon-based nanomaterials, particularly those with tailored surface functionalities. Motivated by the presence of redox-active moieties (-COOH, C-N, and -OH) in PSM-CDs and their negative surface charge, we systematically investigated their oxidase-like antioxidant properties. First, the antioxidant capacities of PSM-CDs were assayed using a ABTS method.30 As shown in Figure 2A, PSM-CDs exhibited a dose-dependent antioxidant activity and eliminated over 90% ABTS·+ at a dose of 100 μg/mL. Importantly, the ABTS·+ was eliminated by PSM-CDs in 0.2 min (Figure 2B), which is equivalent to the efficacy of 50 μM Trolox (vitamin E analogue), indicating the excellent antioxidant properties of PSM-CDs.

ROS, primarily comprising hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxide anion, are natural byproducts of aerobic metabolism.31 To systematically evaluate the ROS-scavenging properties of PSM-CDs, we first investigated their superoxide anion elimination capacity using nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) as a colorimetric indicator. Quantitative analysis revealed a dose-dependent O2·− scavenging efficiency, achieving 87% elimination at 50 μg/mL (Figure 2C). Given the critical role of SOD in catalyzing O2·- dismutation, we further assessed the SOD-mimetic activity of PSM-CDs through WST-1-based enzymatic assays. Remarkably, PSM-CDs demonstrated superior SOD-like activity (9865 U/mg) (Figure 2D), outperforming most biomass-derived nanozymes. Through fenton reaction-mediated ·OH generation (Fe2+/H2O2 system), we observed that PSM-CDs exhibited exceptional hydroxyl radical scavenging efficiency (85% at 200 μg/mL) (Figure 2E). In contrast, PSM-CDs showed negligible catalase-like activity (5.8 U/mg) with minimal H2O2 decomposition capacity.

To delineate the specific contributions of key surface functional groups to the SOD-like activity, we performed targeted chemical modifications to probe the catalytic mechanism. PSM-CDs were modified using two distinct strategies: PSM-CDs-A (hydroxyl/amide passivation with propane sultone) and PSM-CDs-B (carbonyl reduction with NaBH4 followed by passivation), as detailed in the STAR Methods section. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) and 1,3-propanesultone (PS) were employed to selectively reduce carbonyl groups and to passivate hydroxyl/amide groups,16 respectively (Figure 2F). FT-IR analysis confirmed these modifications: treatment with PS (PSM-CDs-PS) substantially diminished the O–H/N–H absorption band while enhancing signals for C–O and C–N, indicating successful reaction with hydroxyl and amide groups. Conversely, NaBH4 treatment (PSM-CDs-NaBH4) led to a pronounced increase in C–O absorption alongside a marked decrease in the C=O peak, confirming the reduction of carbonyls to hydroxyls (Figure 2G). Subsequent activity assays revealed that hydroxyl/amide passivation decreased SOD-like activity to 4291 U mg−1, whereas carbonyl reduction caused a more pronounced drop to 821 U mg−1 (Figure 2H). To confirm that the observed changes in SOD-like activity were due to specific functional group modifications rather than particle aggregation, we characterized the zeta potential and particle size of PSM-CDs before and after chemical treatments. Both modified samples retained similar size distributions (1.6–1.7 nm) and colloidal stability (zeta potential > −14 mV) compared to unmodified PSM-CDs (Table S1), confirming that the activity loss resulted from the elimination of specific surface functional groups rather than physical instability. These results demonstrate that while all three groups contribute, the carbonyl moiety plays a dominant role in the catalytic function. This aligns with prior findings that π-conjugated carbonyl groups are particularly critical for electron transfer in the nanozyme mechanism of CDs.32

The intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) in a H2O2 induced erythroid progenitor (HUDEP) cell model.33 As shown in Figure S4, H2O2 treated cells exhibited strong fluorescence, which was significantly attenuated by PSM-CDs treatment, demonstrating their potent ROS scavenging capacity. These findings collectively demonstrate that PSM-CDs function as multifunctional antioxidant nanozymes, primarily through SOD-mimetic activity and hydroxyl radical neutralization, rather than catalase-mediated pathways.

PSM-CDs exhibited SOD-like enzyme activity for protecting RBCs from oxidative damage

The body’s oxidative state is directly linked to the progression of hemolytic anemia, especially high ROS levels elevate the lysis of red blood cells.34 Given the demonstrated SOD-like enzymatic activity of PSM-CDs in ROS scavenging, we hypothesized their potential to mitigate oxidative stress in erythroid cells. Zebrafish, a widely recognized animal model, is extensively utilized in hematopoiesis research due to its rapid reproduction, genomic similarity, and suitability for in vivo imaging.35 To evaluate the protective efficacy of PSM-CDs against oxidative damage, we established a PHZ-induced zebrafish hemolytic model, leveraging the well-characterized capacity of PHZ to generate ROS and promote circulating red blood cell (RBC) lysis.36 In PHZ-challenged zebrafish models, PSM-CDs administration significantly attenuated ROS accumulation (Figures 3A and 3B). Notably, PSM-CDs treatment enhanced erythroblast counts significantly (Figure 3C), demonstrating the critical role of PSM-CDs in preventing ROS-mediated RBC damage.

To further investigate the protective impact of PSM-CDs on erythroblasts, we employed a H2O2 induced erythroid progenitor (HUDEP) cell model. As shown in Figure 3D, the elevated cellular ROS levels were substantially mitigated by PSM-CDs co-treatment. Given that the excessive ROS generation provokes the oxidative damage of erythrocytes and results in cell apoptosis,37 we then evaluated the anti-apoptotic effect of PSM-CDs on erythrocytes using annexin V staining. As depicted in Figures 3E and 3F, the administration of PSM-CDs significantly lessened the high apoptosis in erythroid cells caused by H2O2. Meanwhile, the H2O2 provoked disorder of membrane lipid peroxidation and cellular GPx activities of erythroid cells were reversed by co-treating PSM-CDs (Figures 3G and 3H), pointing to the protective benefits of PSM-CDs against oxidative damage. These results established that PSM-CDs protect against oxidative erythrocyte damage through ROS scavenging via SOD-mimetic activity, suggesting potential therapeutic applications for PSM-CDs in managing hemolytic anemia associated with oxidative stress.

PSM-CDs reduced inflammatory response via scavenging reactive oxygen species

Hemolytic byproducts, including free hemoglobin and heme, act as proinflammatory mediators, activating immune responses and stimulating macrophages to produce inflammatory cytokines, which aggravates the development of hemolytic anemia in turn.38,39 Given the notable SOD-mimetic activity observed in PSM-CDs, we hypothesized their dual capacity to mitigate both oxidative stress and subsequent inflammatory responses. To verify the anti-Inflammatory ability of PSM-CDs, a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated RAW 264.7 cell model was employed. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that PSM-CDs significantly decreased the LPS-elevated ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 4A and 4B), highlighting their potent ROS-scavenging capacity. Similarly, nitric oxide levels were reduced after 24 h of PSM-CDs treatment, with no cell toxicity observed (Figures 4C and S5). Quantitative immunoblot analysis further revealed concentration-dependent attenuation of key inflammatory mediators, including COX-2, iNOS, and NLRP3 protein expressions (Figures 4D and S6). Given that LPS triggers macrophage M1 polarization to initiate pathogen-combating cytokine cascades, we employed RT-qPCR to evaluate transcriptional regulation of pro-inflammatory markers. As expected, the treatment of PSM-CDs downregulated the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β mRNA levels (Figures 4E–4G), aligning with established anti-inflammatory paradigms.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying these observations, we focused on the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, which will be activated by the exposure of LPS.40 Western blot analysis demonstrated that PSM-CDs mediated the reversal of LPS-induced elevated phosphorylation of IKKβ and p65 (Figures 4H–4J), indicating the inhibition of NF-κB signaling pathway. These coordinated effects on upstream signaling components and downstream inflammatory effectors make PSM-CDs potent NF-κB pathway modulators, suggesting therapeutic utility for inflammatory and immune disorders.

Efficacy of PSM-CDs as therapeutic agent against hemolytic anemia in animal model

Inspired by the multiple effects of PSM-CDs due to their SOD-mimetic properties, including antioxidation and anti-inflammatory capacities, we investigated their therapeutic potential against hemolytic anemia in vivo. Initial investigations in a PHZ-induced zebrafish hemolytic anemia model revealed that co-administration of PSM-CDs significantly upregulated erythroid cell counts in a dose-dependent manner (Figures 5A and 5B), demonstrating therapeutic promise. Building on these findings, we established a murine hemolytic anemia model to further characterize PSM-CDs’ therapeutic mechanisms. Mice pretreated with a single dose of PSM-CDs (15 mg/kg) followed by PHZ challenge and bi-daily oral maintenance doses (Figure 5C). Despite comparable physiological parameters between groups (Figure S7), PSM-CDs treatment significantly restored red blood cell (RBC) counts, hemoglobin levels, and hematocrit values (Figure 5D). Notably, the increased serum levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were counteracted by PSM-CDs (Figure 5E).

Since erythropoiesis commonly takes place in bone marrow, a comprehensive exploration of the erythroid differentiation process was undertaken. Importantly, there was a marked increase in the percentage of TER119+ cells in the bone marrow of mice treated with PSM-CDs (Figure 5F). By categorizing erythroblasts into six groups using CD44 versus FSC, we found that PSM-CDs had no discernible impact on the erythroid differentiation in bone marrow (Figure 5G). Given the spleen’s recognized role as a compensatory erythropoietic organ during hematopoietic stress, we extended our analysis to splenic erythropoiesis. PSM-CDs remarkably alleviated PHZ-induced splenomegaly (Figure 5H), accompanied by a proportional surge in TER119+ erythroid cells (Figure 5I). Importantly, erythroblast subpopulation analysis via CD44/FSC gating demonstrated preserved differentiation kinetics in splenic erythroblasts (Figure 5J), mirroring BM observations. To investigate the specificity of PSM-CDs toward erythropoiesis, we further assessed their impact on non-erythroid hematopoietic lineages. Flow cytometric analysis using lineage-specific markers showed that PSM-CDs did not alter the distribution of B cells, T cells, or myeloid cells in either peripheral blood or bone marrow (Figure S8), indicating no discernible effect on the development or homeostasis of these immune cell populations. These findings suggest that the therapeutic effect of PSM-CDs in alleviating hemolytic anemia may be attributed to their ability to eliminate ROS and exert anti-inflammatory properties.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals modulation of ROS-HIF-1 axis in erythroid progenitors

Building on the demonstrated therapeutic efficacy of PSM-CDs in PHZ-induced hemolytic anemia, we further investigated whether they could also directly stimulate erythropoiesis. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism, RNA sequencing was performed on TER119+ cells sorted from the bone marrow of PHZ-model mice (day 3) with or without PSM-CDs (Figure 6A). Principal-component analysis confirmed high reproducibility and data quality among replicates (Figure S9A). Differential expression analysis revealed 132 upregulated and 47 downregulated genes in the PSM-CDs-treated group compared to controls (|log2FC| > 1, p < 0.05) (Figures 6B and S9B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant regulation of the HIF-1 signaling pathway (Figure 6C), a key regulator of erythroid proliferation.41 To gain further functional insight, we performed gene ontology (GO) analysis. The results showed that the most significantly enriched biological processes in PSM-CDs-treated cells were response to hypoxia, erythrocyte development, and cell cycle (Figure 6D), collectively pointing toward enhanced erythropoietic activity. This interpretation was strongly supported by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which confirmed a significant enrichment of the response to hypoxia gene set (Figure 6E).

Guided by this convergent transcriptomic evidence highlighting the hypoxia response pathway, we sought to validate its activation and assess the downstream erythroid program. Quantitative PCR analysis confirmed that PSM-CDs treatment not only significantly upregulated core hypoxia-response target genes (Hif1α, Egln1, Flt1, Timp1, and Hmox1) but also enhanced the expression of key erythroid development genes (Figure 6F). Specifically, we observed increased mRNA levels of the master transcription factor GATA1, the cell cycle regulator p21, and the hemoglobin subunits Hba and Hbb. Integrating these findings, we propose a coherent mechanism: PSM-CDs modulate intracellular ROS homeostasis via their SOD-mimetic activity. This reduction in oxidative stress activates the HIF-1-mediated hypoxia response pathway, which in turn promotes the proliferation of erythroid progenitors and upregulates key terminal differentiation genes, collectively compensating for hemolytic loss.

Biotoxicity analysis of PSM-CDs in vitro and in vivo

To address potential biosafety concerns critical for clinical translation, we conducted a comprehensive toxicity evaluation of PSM-CDs across multiple biological systems. Initial cytotoxicity screening was assayed by using human vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs) and normal colon epithelial cells (NCM460), which revealed no significant impact on viability after 48-h exposure to PSM-CDs (0–400 μg/mL), confirming their biocompatibility in vitro (Figures 7A and 7B). Quantitative hemolysis analysis revealed minimal erythrocyte damage (2.8% ± 0.3% hemolysis) following PSM-CDs exposure at 800 μg/mL, indicating excellent blood compatibility (Figure 7C). To evaluate systemic safety, parallel acute (7-day) and chronic (60-day) in vivo toxicity studies were performed by administering a one-time dose of PSM-CDs (300 mg/kg). No adverse effects were observed on body weight, survival, or behavior in any treatment group (Figure S10). Hematological analysis also showed normal peripheral blood profiles (Figure S11). Furthermore, critical serum biomarkers of hepatic and renal function, including AST, ALT, CREA, and CK, remained within physiological ranges, confirming the absence of hepatorenal toxicity (Figure 7D). Histopathological validation through H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) revealed intact tissue architecture with no evidence of inflammation, necrosis, or degenerative changes in any treatment group (Figure 7E).

To enable in vivo biodistribution tracking, PSM-CDs were conjugated with the near-infrared fluorophore Cy5 (PSM-CDs@CY5) and administered at a dose of 15 mg/kg, consistent with the therapeutic dose used throughout the study. Although unmodified PSM-CDs exhibit sufficient fluorescence for cellular imaging, their moderate quantum yield (7.3%) is suboptimal for deep-tissue imaging; thus, Cy5 labeling was employed for whole-animal fluorescence imaging. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence signals in whole-body, liver, and kidney regions was performed using ImageJ software, and the results are presented in Figure S12. The data revealed rapid accumulation of PSM-CDs@CY5 in the liver and spleen within the first hour, with signal intensity peaking at 12 h and gradually declining thereafter. By 48 h post-injection, fluorescence signals were nearly undetectable in all examined organs, indicating efficient systemic clearance without long-term retention (Figure 7F). Collectively, this multilayered safety assessment establishes that PSM-CDs exhibit favorable biosafety profiles at both tested doses, supporting their potential for therapeutic applications.

Discussion

This study establishes a scalable, circular economy model that effectively valorizes a substantial agricultural waste stream into a high-value therapeutic agent. The conversion of low-value PSM into bioactive CDs via a facile, one-pot hydrothermal process embodies the principles of green chemistry by utilizing water as a solvent and avoiding toxic reagents. With a notable production yield of 73 g PSM-CDs per kg of PSM, this approach presents an economically viable and immediately adoptable strategy for the oil peony industry, transforming an environmental liability into a strategic resource for nanomedicine development. From a circular economy perspective, the cost-effectiveness of PSM-CDs represents a key advantage over conventional therapeutic agents. Commercial antioxidants such as vitamin E are priced at $50–100 per gram,42 while noble-metal nanozymes (e.g., Pt, Au) require expensive precursors and complex synthesis, with estimated production costs exceeding $500 per gram.43,44 In contrast, PSM-CDs are synthesized from agricultural waste via a simple hydrothermal process using only water as solvent. Based on the production yield of 73 g/kg and the negligible cost of PSM (<$0.10 per kg as agricultural waste), the estimated material cost for PSM-CD production is approximately $0.01–0.05 per gram, orders of magnitude lower than commercial enzymes or noble-metal nanozymes.45 This “waste-to-therapeutics” pipeline offers a tangible solution to the challenge of biomass accumulation, aligning agricultural sustainability with advances in pharmaceutical sciences.

The exceptional bioactivity of PSM-CDs is a direct consequence of our strategic selection of a phytochemical-rich agricultural byproduct as a precursor. Unlike synthetically engineered nanomaterials, the structure and function of PSM-CDs are intrinsically linked to their biological origin. The resulting N/O-doped graphitic core and the abundance of redox-active surface functional groups (e.g., -OH, C=O, and pyrrolic N) are likely derived from the molecular constituents, such as flavonoids and stilbenes, present in the PSM precursor. While the original compounds undergo carbonization, key functional motifs appear to be translated into the surface chemistry of the CDs during synthesis.46,47 While direct molecular tracking would be required for definitive proof, the concordance between (1) the known antioxidant pharmacophores in PSM, (2) these specific surface moieties identified on the CDs, and (3) the resulting high enzymatic activity strongly supports the inference that key functional motifs are preserved during carbonization.

To directly probe the origin of this high enzymatic activity, we conducted targeted chemical modification experiments. These studies provided crucial mechanistic insight: selective reduction of carbonyl groups (C=O) or passivation of hydroxyl/amide groups significantly diminished the SOD-like activity of PSM-CDs, with the carbonyl moiety playing a dominant role. This evidence strongly suggests that the preserved carbonyl groups, potentially derived from precursor flavonoids and stilbenes, serve as crucial catalytic centers for electron transfer in the SOD-mimetic mechanism. This bioactivity-informed design is crucial,18 as it confers upon PSM-CDs their exceptionally high SOD-like activity (9,865 U/mg) and potent hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity. It is important to note that this performance is comparable to other state-of-the-art nanozymes, such as Pt-integrated CDs (10,200 U/mg) and red-emissive CDs (9,950 U/mg).30,48 Our findings align with recent studies demonstrating that nanozyme catalytic efficiency is critically dependent on surface chemical composition and the preservation of bioactive motifs from precursor materials.49,50 The significance of our work, therefore, lies not in surpassing a singular activity metric, but in achieving such high performance through a distinctively sustainable and advantageous paradigm: via a simple, metal-free, aqueous synthesis from a single waste precursor. This underscores a key advantage of our approach: the precursor not only provides carbon and heteroatoms but also delivers a pre-organized, bioactive architecture that enables high catalytic efficiency without the need for precious metals or complex synthetic steps.

Building on this mechanistic insight, the therapeutic efficacy of PSM-CDs in hemolytic anemia stems from their ability to simultaneously target the interconnected pathways of oxidative stress and inflammation, a multi-target approach that aligns with the sustainable design of more efficient and potentially safer therapeutics. Functioning primarily as a potent SOD-mimetic nanozyme, with its activity rooted in specific surface carbonyl groups, PSM-CDs directly neutralize the primary reactive oxygen species, O2·−, at the inception of the oxidative cascade. While PSM-CDs lack direct catalase-like activity, they demonstrably attenuate H2O2-induced damage by bolstering the endogenous antioxidant defenses of cells, as evidenced by the upregulation of cellular catalase-like activity in erythroblasts (Figure S13). This indirect, host-oriented mechanism represents a more biologically nuanced and potentially sustainable therapeutic strategy than direct, exhaustive enzymatic scavenging. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis revealed that by modulating redox homeostasis, PSM-CDs activate the ROS-HIF-1 axis, thereby promoting hypoxia-response signaling and enhancing erythropoietic gene expression to facilitate compensatory RBC production.

An intriguing finding from our transcriptomic analysis is the upregulation of the HIF-1 signaling pathway by PSM-CDs, despite their potent ROS-scavenging activity. While ROS are known to stabilize HIF-1α by inhibiting PHD enzymes,51 several mechanisms may explain this apparent paradox. First, PSM-CDs may exert spatiotemporally selective antioxidant effects, preserving mitochondrial ROS signals that promote HIF-1α stabilization while scavenging damaging cytosolic ROS.52,53 Second, HIF-1 activation may represent a compensatory erythropoietic response following relief from severe oxidative stress, reflecting a cellular recovery program aimed at restoring red blood cell production. Third, direct interactions between PSM-CD surface functional groups and components of the HIF-1 degradation machinery cannot be excluded and warrant further investigation.54 Notably, similar observations have been reported for other antioxidant nanozymes,16 suggesting that the relationship between nanomaterial-mediated redox modulation and hypoxic signaling is multifaceted and context-dependent.

Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory efficacy of PSM-CDs, achieved through the suppression of the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathways, is intrinsically linked to their antioxidant function. By scavenging heme-induced ROS that act as inflammasome triggers,55 PSM-CDs disrupt the vicious cycle of inflammation and hemolysis. This dual capability to target both the upstream oxidative triggers and downstream inflammatory effectors positions PSM-CDs as a superior and more sustainable alternative to conventional corticosteroids, which are associated with broad immunosuppression and significant side effects. Beyond these direct antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms demonstrated in our study, emerging evidence highlights the profound regulatory role of CDs in broader cell fate decisions and immune modulation. Recent studies have shown that rationally designed CDs can trigger immunogenic cell death, modulate ferroptosis pathways, and reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment.56,57,58 These findings underscore the versatility of CDs in engaging complex biological pathways beyond direct ROS scavenging. While our current work focuses on the protection of erythroid cells via ROS-p53 axis suppression and NF-κB/NLRP3 inhibition, it is plausible that PSM-CDs may also influence these additional pathways in other cell types within the hematopoietic niche. Future investigations could explore whether PSM-CDs similarly affect programmed cell death modalities in splenic macrophages or modulate the inflammatory milieu during hemolytic anemia, potentially revealing additional therapeutic dimensions of this sustainable nanomaterial.

When compared with conventional therapies for hemolytic anemia reported in the literature, PSM-CDs offer distinct advantages. Glucocorticoids such as prednisone achieve initial response rates of 70%–85% but are associated with significant side effects and high relapse rates (30%–50% within 2 years).25 Recombinant human erythropoietin (rhEPO) stimulates erythropoiesis but shows limited efficacy in patients with inadequate bone marrow reserve and carries thromboembolic risks.59 Conventional antioxidants have demonstrated only modest protective effects in preclinical models.60 In contrast, PSM-CDs at 15 mg/kg restored RBC counts to approximately 85% of normal levels within 7 days with no observable toxicity, while simultaneously targeting both oxidative stress and inflammation. This multi-target mechanism represents a fundamental advantage over single-agent therapies. Direct head-to-head comparative studies with prednisone and rhEPO are planned in our future investigations to definitively establish the relative advantages of PSM-CDs.

The translation of any nanomaterial from bench to bedside is predicated on its safety profile. Our comprehensive assessment confirms that PSM-CDs, derived from a natural and edible source, exhibit an excellent biosafety profile.61 Their biosafety profile is excellent, showing negligible cytotoxicity, minimal hemolytic activity, no organ toxicity at high acute doses (300 mg/kg), and no adverse effects in a 60-day chronic study. These are not merely add-on features but fundamental attributes of their sustainable design. Moreover, the SOD-like activity of PSM-CDs, their core therapeutic function, exhibited high stability by retaining >90% of its initial potency after 60-day storage at 4 °C (Figure S14), ensuring durable therapeutic efficacy. This combination of high biocompatibility and functional stability significantly reduces potential environmental and biological risks, reinforcing the promise of PSM-CDs as a readily translatable and storable nanotherapeutic candidate.

Beyond the laboratory, this work provides a replicable template for the sustainable management of horticultural and agricultural waste streams. The successful transformation of PSM into a high-performance nanomedicine demonstrates that value can be extracted from what was previously considered waste, reducing environmental burden while creating economic opportunity. Future work will focus on establishing structure-activity relationships across different peony cultivars, optimizing integrated farming-byproduct valorization systems, and exploring life cycle assessments to quantitatively validate the environmental benefits of this approach. This paradigm can be extended to other crop-processing residues, paving the way for a new generation of sustainable nanotherapeutics rooted in the principles of circular economy and green chemistry.

In summary, this study presents a sustainable “waste-to-therapeutics” strategy by converting peony seed meal into bioactive CDs (PSM-CDs) via a green hydrothermal process. The PSM-CDs exhibit potent SOD-mimetic activity, driven primarily by surface carbonyl groups as identified through chemical modification studies. In hemolytic anemia models, PSM-CDs function as a dual-pathway therapeutic, alleviating disease progression by scavenging ROS and suppressing NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammation. Transcriptomics further revealed their role in promoting erythropoiesis via the ROS-HIF-1 axis. Combined with excellent biosafety, this work establishes a scalable circular economy model that transforms agricultural waste into effective and safe nanomedicine, offering a template for sustainable therapeutic development aligned with green chemistry principles.

Limitations of the study

This study establishes a sustainable “waste-to-therapeutics” strategy converting peony seed meal into bioactive CDs for hemolytic anemia treatment, but its efficacy was only validated in preclinical animal models, the precise atomic-level molecular mechanisms remain unclear, and only short-term biosafety and laboratory-scale synthesis were evaluated.

Future research should focus on clinical translation, in-depth mechanistic studies, long-term in vivo safety assessment, industrial production optimization, and exploring therapeutic applications in other oxidative stress-related diseases.

Method details

Synthesis of PSM-CDs

The P. ostii (cultivar “Feng Dan”) seed meal used in this study was sourced from Heze Yaoshun Peony Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong Province, China). The raw material was pulverized using a pharmaceutical-grade grinder to obtain homogeneous powder (particle size <100 μm). For CD synthesis, 20 g of powdered material was mixed with 100 mL ultrapure water and underwent hydrothermal processing at 200°C for 8 h. The resultant dark brown solution was vacuum-filtered through 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membranes, followed by molecular weight fractionation using 1 kDa cutoff dialysis in water for 48 h. Then, the purified solution was lyophilized to yield PSM-CDs powder, which was stored desiccated at −80°C until use.

Characterization of PSM-CDs

The morphological characteristics of PSM-CDs were examined using a Talos F200S TEM (Thermo Scientific) operated at 200 kV accelerating voltage. Chemical bonding analysis was performed on a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Surface chemistry profiling utilized a K-Alpha system (Thermo Scientific) with monochromatic Al Kα radiation. Emission spectra were recorded on an F-7000 spectrophotometer (Hitachi) with 5 nm excitation/emission slit widths. Crystallinity analysis was performed on a X'Pert PRO diffractometer (PANalytical). Surface charge properties were evaluated through a Zetasizer system (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Experimental animals

AB strain zebrafish were maintained following established protocols.16 In brief, embryos at 2 days post-fertilization (dpf) were selected for experimental procedures. C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories and housed in controlled environments with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow. To establish anemia models, zebrafish embryos were exposed to 100 ng/mL PHZ while mice received intraperitoneal injections of PHZ at 150 mg/kg body weight. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with institutional ethical guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong First Medical University (Approval No. W202508180826).

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), NCM460 normal colonic epithelial cells, and RAW 264.7 murine macrophages were cultured in ATCC-formulated growth medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (APExBIO). Human umbilical cord blood-derived endothelial progenitor cells (HUDEP) were maintained in specialized culture medium according to established protocols.33

Western blotting

Protein analysis in cells was conducted following established protocols.62,63 Briefly, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred electrophoretically to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at 25°C, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies. After three TBST washes, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 25°C. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Antibody specifications are provided in Table S2.

Flow cytometric assays

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from bone marrow, splenic tissues, and cultured cells using established mechanical dissociation protocols.64 Tissues were gently homogenized through a 70-μm cell strainer in PBS containing 2% FBS, followed by red blood cell lysis using ACK buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS and 0.09% sodium azide). For immunostaining, cells were first incubated with Fc-block (anti-CD16/32, BioLegend) for 10 min on ice. Surface staining was performed using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Key antibodies included: anti-TER119, anti-CD44, anti-B220, anti-CD3ε, and anti-CD11b (all from BioLegend, used at manufacturer-recommended dilutions). After staining, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer before acquisition. Data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8). Gating included exclusion of doublets and dead cells (stained with viability dye). All staining conditions were optimized in preliminary titration experiments.

Quantitative real-time PCR

The procedures for RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR were carried out as previously detailed.65 In short, total RNA was extracted from cell lysates using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher) and subjected to cDNA synthesis using HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech) following manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR analysis was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The relative expression levels of the specified genes were analyzed using the comparative CT (ΔΔCT) method, with normalization to 18S rRNA. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S3.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from sorted TER119+ bone marrow cells (with or without PSM-CDs treatment) using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Strand-specific cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Gene expression levels were quantified using the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10), and differential expression analysis between PSM-CD-treated and control groups was performed with DESeq2. Genes with significant changes were further subjected to functional enrichment analysis. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC) under BioProject accession number NGDC BioProject: PRJCA057035 and will be publicly accessible upon publication.

The antioxidant capacity assay

The comprehensive antioxidant capacity of PSM-CDs was quantified using a T-AOC Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) following manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, ABTS·+ radical cation solutions were prepared in 25 mM PBS (pH 7.4) and monitored at 414 nm (Thermo Scientific). Real-time absorbance measurements were recorded for PSM-CDs concentrations ranging from 0 to 400 μg/mL, with full spectral scans (300–700 nm) performed post-reaction to validate radical scavenging specificity.

The nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction method was employed to confirm the scavenging effect of PSM-CDs on O2·-, where the reaction system included 25 mM PBS (pH 7.4), 0.05 mM NBT,13 mM L-methionine, 20 μM riboflavin. The samples were irradiated with white LED light (3000 lux, 3 min) followed by immediate absorbance measurement at 560 nm. Scavenging efficiency was calculated relative to light-exposed controls without PSM-CDs.

The assessment of PSM-CDs’ effectiveness against ·OH radicals was performed as previously described, with minor adjustments. The Fenton reaction-mediated TMB oxidation system contained 10 mM FeSO4·7H2O, 100 mM H2O2, 10 mM TMB, and NaAc-Hac buffer (pH 5.2). After 60 min dark incubation, ·OH generation was quantified via TMB oxidation product absorbance at 652 nm.

Measurement of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (AmBeed) following manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the cells were exposed to the probe in a medium without serum for 30 min. Following three PBS washes, fluorescence images were captured using an LSM980 confocal microscope, with the best excitation at 492 nm and emission at 517 nm.

Surface modification and fluorescent labeling of PSM-CDs

PSM-CDs-A (hydroxyl/amide passivation)

To passivate hydroxyl and amide groups, PSM-CDs (5–10 mg/mL in 20 mL H2O) were reacted with propane sultone (PS, 1–2 g) in the presence of triethylamine (TEA, 1–2 mL) within a 1,4-dioxane solution (10–20 mL) at 30°C–40 °C for 24 h under a N2 atmosphere. The crude product was concentrated by rotary evaporation, dialyzed sequentially against 0.1 M NaCl (24 h) and ultrapure water (72 h, 500 Da MWCO), and then hydrolyzed with 0.5 M NaOH (10–20 mL, 30°C–40 °C, 24 h). Final purification involved neutralization with 0.1 M HCl, dialysis against ultrapure water (24 h), and lyophilization.

PSM-CDs-B (carbonyl reduction followed by passivation)

PSM-CDs (20–40 mg) were first reduced using NaBH4 (0.5 M, 50 mL) at room temperature for 12 h. The resulting intermediate subsequently underwent an identical PS conjugation, hydrolysis, and purification sequence as described for PSM-CDs-A.

PSM-CDs@CY5 (fluorescent conjugate)

The fluorescent probe PSM-CDs@CY5 was synthesized via a coupling reaction mediated by DMT-MM. Briefly, PSM-CDs (15 mg) and DMT-MM (6.8 mg) were dissolved in methanol (15 mL) and stirred for 1 h at room temperature. CY5-amine (15 mg) was then added, and the reaction proceeded for 24 h. The conjugate was isolated by dialysis against ultrapure water (24 h) and obtained as a solid via lyophilization.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.1 software and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless explicitly noted otherwise. Parametric statistical comparisons were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, where below 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. All statistical methodologies are explicitly annotated in corresponding Figure legends.
