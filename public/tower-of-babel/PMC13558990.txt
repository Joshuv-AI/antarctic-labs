PROTOCOL: Experiences of Informal Caregivers of Dependent Older People on Interventions to Reduce Care Burden and Promote Their Biopsychosocial Well-Being
Authors: Mariana Loezar-Hernández, Alejandra-Ximena Araya, Claudia Miranda-Castillo, Catalina Ganga-León, Carolina Climent-Sanz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is predicted that by 2050, 22% of the world’s population will be over 60 years old. Informal caregivers of dependent older adults face a significant physical, psychological and social burden. However, interventions designed to support these caregivers have predominantly been evaluated through quantitative outcomes. Qualitative evidence on the experiences and perceptions of these interventions reported by caregivers themselves remains largely unsynthesised. This review aims to identify and synthesise qualitative evidence on how informal caregivers of dependent older people experience and perceive interventions designed to alleviate the burden of caregiving and enhance their biopsychosocial well-being. For this purpose, a systematic search will be conducted in the following databases: MEDLINE (PubMed), CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection and ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global. Publications in English and Spanish from 2014 onwards will be included. Grey literature will also be included. Search strategies will be developed with a research librarian and peer-reviewed using the PRESS checklist. Regarding selection criteria, primary qualitative studies and mixed-methods studies with accessible qualitative data exploring the experiences of informal caregivers of dependent older adults (60+) who have received interventions targeting burden reduction and biopsychosocial well-being will be included. Studies focused exclusively on palliative cancer care or on caregivers of people with cognitive impairment will be excluded. Data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers. Thematic synthesis will be conducted using Thomas and Harden’s method. The methodological quality will be assessed using the CASP Qualitative Checklist and the confidence in the findings will be evaluated using GRADE-CERQual.

Background

The Problem, Condition or Issue

By 2050, it is estimated that 22% of the world’s population will be over 60 years of age. This demographic transition, known as ‘population aging’, is taking place alongside various economic and cultural factors, including the increased participation of women in the workforce, changes in family structures, longer life expectancy, lower fertility rates, and reduced resources for social care of older adults (Ministerio de Desarrollo Social y Familia, 2024). Together with a rise in chronic diseases and multimorbidity, these factors are contributing to greater dependency and frailty, which in turn complicate meeting the healthcare needs of older people (Alves et al., 2021; Carvacho et al., 2023; Price et al., 2020). As a result, the demand for care and the costs of medical services are increasing, placing additional strain on an already overstretched healthcare system (Price et al., 2020; Verbakel, 2018). In this context, informal care has become a critical, and even preventive solution for delaying the need to institutionalize dependent persons and reduce health system costs (Yuda & Lee, 2016).

Informal care refers to the provision of unpaid assistance or support to others who need help or are unable to care for themselves due to long-term health conditions and/or disabilities (Triantafillou et al., 2010). This includes support with basic (ADLs) and instrumental (IADLs) activities of daily living as well as care-related tasks such as care coordination and health management. For instance, caregivers are increasingly taking on medical and care planning responsibilities across different healthcare settings (Kim et al., 2023). Forms of support that are exclusively emotional, without involvement in direct care activities, are not considered within the primary scope of this study.

The proposed research focuses on the care provided to dependent older adults, understood as those aged 60 years and over who, for health reasons in interaction with other factors of the environmental context, present a limitation to carry out basic and instrumental activities of daily living, needing the help of third parties to do so (Fernández & Jorquera, 2023). Thus, informal caregivers are defined as individuals, such as family members, friends or neighbors, who provide continuous, unpaid care in the home environment for people aged 60 and over in a situation of dependency (Fernández & Jorquera, 2023).

Traditionally, these care needs have been provided by family members, particularly women, in the home environment. Social norms and gender roles have designated women as the primary caregivers, resulting in a disproportionate burden that negatively affects their physical, emotional, and financial well-being compared to men (Arber & Ginn, 1995; Flores et al., 2009; Vitaliano et al., 2003). Moreover, informal caregiving is not consistently reflected in Gross Domestic Product (GDP) calculations, leading to its invisibility and underestimation, despite playing a vital role in generating economic value (Gazmuri & Velasco, 2021).

Care is complex, continuous and intensive, requiring specific skills and physical effort. It often entails personal and professional sacrifices, impacting caregivers’ participation in the workforce, social status, and social life, reducing leisure and self-care activities. As a consequence, informal caregivers commonly experience feelings of sadness, guilt and loneliness, and are at increased risk of depression and fatigue (X.-R. Zhu et al., 2019), leading to a level of overload that threatens their biopsychosocial well-being (Lindt et al., 2020; Zepeda-Álvarez & Muñoz-Mendoza, 2019). In this review, biopsychosocial well-being is understood through Engel’s integrated model (Engel, 1977), in which biological (e.g., physical health, immune function), psychological (e.g., emotions, stress, coping), and social (e.g., social support, family relationships) factors interact dynamically and mutually influence health and illness (Colvin & Bullock, 2016).

To address the needs of the aging population, the literature emphasizes the importance of comprehensive care approaches that consider the physical, social, psychological and environmental needs of each individual (World Health Organization, 2015). However, the specialised literature has mostly focused on the economic costs of pension and care systems, while the health perspective and informal caregivers’ lived experiences have received far less attention. This gap is reflected in the limited availability and affordability of structured support for informal caregivers, who often lack specific knowledge about care tasks and struggle with the physical, emotional and psychological burden of providing informal care. Interventions are a primary tool for delivering structured support. Therefore, understanding caregivers’ experiences and perceptions of these interventions is essential, particularly when they aim to alleviate burden and enhance biopsychosocial well-being. This understanding becomes the interest of this study.

Why Is It Important to do This Review?

Diverse intervention approaches have been developed to support informal caregivers of dependent older adults, and existing reviews have mainly documented quantitative outcomes such as their effectiveness and efficiency (Kraun et al., 2023; Marques et al., 2021; McCauley et al., 2021; Raemdonck et al., 2022). However, there is a lack of syntheses that focus on caregivers’ own experiences and perceptions in taking part in such interventions from a qualitative perspective. Addressing this gap through a qualitative evidence synthesis could generate insights to guide the design of more suitable and person-centred interventions, thereby informing both practice and policy.

From a healthcare perspective, existing research highlights the importance of providing support and resources for training and information for caregivers (Kirvalidze et al., 2023). Studies also emphasize the role of coordinated support systems and accessible services to reduce caregiver overload and improve quality of care (Brenna, 2021; Kim et al., 2023). Existing literature has proposed mixed models of care in which formal systems and informal caregivers share responsibilities, with the aim of reducing caregiver burden (Verbakel, 2018).

A range of interventions have become available for informal caregivers of dependent older adults. These include psychosocial interventions that promote self-care; psychoeducational interventions designed to enhance coping skills and communication (Kedia et al., 2020); and interventions focused on reducing stress through techniques such as meditation and yoga (Kedia et al., 2020; Lengacher et al., 2012). Multicomponent interventions addressing the diverse needs of caregivers have proven more effective than single-approach interventions (Kedia et al., 2020). Information and communication technologies (ICTs) also play a crucial role in providing accessible tools for managing care, reducing stress, and offering support (Adler & Mehta, 2014), ranging from internet-based interventions (Ploeg et al., 2017) to mobile health applications and other web-based platforms (Lorca-Cabrera et al., 2020).

Despite this variety, the effectiveness of these interventions in real-world settings appears variable, and their uptake by informal caregivers remains limited (Leichsenring, 2004). Studies consistently report underutilization of community and formal support services (Choi et al., 2024; Vipperman et al., 2023). Caregivers are often inadequately informed about available resources and tend to seek support only when their burden becomes overwhelming (Hong, 2010). Barriers such as system complexity, lack of information, financial and time constraints, and interventions that do not fully match caregivers’ needs contribute to delayed help-seeking and low uptake (Vipperman et al., 2023). Recent scoping and systematic reviews emphasize a persistent gap in how interventions are put into practice (an implementation gap): most caregiver interventions focus on efficacy, while implementation strategies, implementation outcomes, and contextual determinants remain under-reported (Christie et al., 2018; Ugalde et al., 2019; E. M. Zhu et al., 2023).

Furthermore, these studies often employ a barriers-and-facilitators approach, which tends to neglect a comprehensive incorporation of caregivers’ own lived experiences and perceptions of participating in such interventions. Little is known about how caregivers perceive these programmes, what makes interventions feel useful, acceptable or feasible in their daily lives, or what motivates their engagement in the interventions. This lack of attention to caregivers’ subjective perspectives constitutes a significant gap in need of scientific exploration. Therefore, synthesising qualitative evidence on caregivers’ experiences and perceptions of support interventions can deepen understanding of why some programmes are more acceptable and workable than others, thereby informing more responsive and practically useful caregiver support in routine care and policy design.

Previous Reviews

A preliminary search of the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Database of Systematic Reviews revealed that most of the reviews and meta-reviews on interventions for informal caregivers considered quantitative evidence (Huis In Het Veld et al., 2015), or did not distinguish between qualitative and quantitative evidence (Bjørnnes et al., 2019; Docherty et al., 2008). This reflects a lack of qualitative evidence syntheses addressing caregivers’ experiences, with comparatively fewer reviews analysing exclusively qualitative evidence in this area (Docherty et al., 2008; Lauritzen et al., 2015; Rannikko et al., 2023; Woodman et al., 2016; Yuan et al., 2021).

Additionally, the existing literature aimed at systematizing evidence (including quantitative, qualitative, and mixed studies) tends to focus on caregivers of older people with specific clinical conditions, particularly neurocognitive impairments such as dementia (Christie et al., 2018; Huis In Het Veld et al., 2015; Lauritzen et al., 2015; Rannikko et al., 2023), as well as on individuals in palliative care (Docherty et al., 2008; Yuan et al., 2021), and those with heart disease (Bjørnnes et al., 2019; Woodman et al., 2016). Such focus may hinder the understanding of caregiving experiences in broader contexts of dependency.

An umbrella review (Kirvalidze et al., 2023) of caregiver interventions found inconclusive quantitative evidence on health outcomes and highlighted that qualitative reviews usually describe practical, emotional and relational benefits in a broad way, without a focused synthesis of caregivers’ lived experiences of such interventions or factors shaping their engagement and implementation in real-world contexts. Recent reviews and studies on caregiver support highlight barriers and facilitators to participation and implementation, such as caregiving workload and burden, lack of support, time constraints, practical and contextual barriers, and misalignment between services and caregivers’ needs (Abreu et al., 2015; Choi et al., 2024; Coumoundouros et al., 2022; Rice et al., 2020).

However, these determinants are usually reported within individual primary studies, integrative or mixed-methods reviews focusing on specific service types and diverse caregiving situations, rather than being qualitatively synthesized with a specific focus on informal caregivers of dependent older adults (Abreu et al., 2015; Choi et al., 2024; Coumoundouros et al., 2022; Leocadie et al., 2018). The few existing qualitative evidence syntheses have predominantly focused on caregivers’ experiences of providing care, including their motivations, burden and identity negotiation often framed by gender and cultural norms (Cross et al., 2018; Zarzycki et al., 2023; Zygouri et al., 2021). Yet, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, qualitative evidence regarding caregivers’ experiences of participating in support interventions has not been systematically synthesized.

For all of the above, this qualitative evidence synthesis aims to identify and synthesize existing qualitative evidence on the experiences and perceptions of informal caregivers of dependent older people, focusing on interventions designed to alleviate burden and enhance the biopsychosocial well-being of caregivers.

Methods

Design

A qualitative evidence synthesis will be conducted as it provides a structured, rigorous approach to exploring the meanings attributed to phenomena, analyzing and interpreting findings from original qualitative studies to gain a broader understanding than individual studies offer (Barnett-Page & Thomas, 2009; Lavis et al., 2005). This method facilitates the identification of underexplored areas in research and generates evidence for the design, implementation, and assessment of health interventions (Higgins et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2012). This qualitative evidence synthesis will follow the guidelines proposed by the Cochrane Qualitative and Implementation Methods Group (Harden et al., 2018; Harris et al., 2018; Noyes, Booth, Cargo, et al., 2018; Noyes, Booth, Flemming, et al., 2018).

Data Sources and Search Strategy

The proposed search strategy will include both published and grey literature considering the guide to information retrieval for Campbell systematic reviews (MacDonald et al., 2024).

Databases to be searched will be: MEDLINE (via PubMed), CINAHL (via EBSCOhost), PsycINFO (via EBSCOhost), Web of Science Core Collection (including Social Sciences Citation Index and Science Citation Index Expanded, via Web of Science). To identify grey literature, the search will also integrate ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global (via ProQuest).

The search strategy will be developed in four stages: (i) the search will be planned guided by the research questions; (ii) a preliminary search in PubMed, limited to relevant MeSH headings, will be conducted to identify keywords and textual terms in the titles and abstracts of pertinent articles; (iii) with the support of a research librarian with experience in health and social sciences, a customized search strategy will be formulated for each database; (iv) the reference lists of all included studies will be reviewed to identify additional relevant articles. Authors of potentially relevant articles not available electronically will be contacted directly.

The results of the search and screening will be presented using a flow chart, following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist (Moher et al., 2015). The complete search strategy is provided in the Supplemental material. To ensure rigor and completeness of search, the PRESS (Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies) checklist (McGowan et al., 2016) will be used to verify all components of a structured search.

Study Selection

Rayyan will be used for all relevant tasks related to organizing the studies identified in the searches. Duplicate records will be removed, and the remaining references will be imported into the systematic review software (Shanaa, 2021). Titles and abstracts will be screened and assessed by two independent reviewers against predefined inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion of studies will be recorded in the full text of the final qualitative synthesis. Disagreements among the researchers regarding the selection of articles will be discussed among them to reach a consensus or other researchers will be consulted and a majority decision will be adopted.

Data Extraction and Management

Data from the selected studies will be extracted using a standardized descriptive data extraction form developed by the researchers. The form will include sections such as data on the first author, country, year, study design, objective(s) of the study, sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, participant recruitment methods, data collection techniques and data analysis methods. This process will be carried out independently by two researchers, then their results will be compared to ensure that the data extraction is performed correctly.

Data Synthesis

The RETREAT framework (which considers seven domains: Review Question, Epistemology, Time, Logistical Constraints, Resources, Expertise, Knowledge, Audience and Purpose, and Data Type) (Booth et al., 2018) was used to select the appropriate synthesis method for this review. Thematic synthesis was selected because it enables the systematic identification, organisation, and interpretation of patterns across qualitative studies, allowing the generation of higher-order analytical themes that go beyond individual study findings. This approach is well-suited to respond to the research aims, seeking to understand caregivers’ experiences and perceptions across diverse intervention settings.

This thematic synthesis will be carried out using the method described by Thomas and Harden (Thomas & Harden, 2008) in four stages: (i) line-by-line manual coding of the data extracted from the articles independently by the researchers (participants’ testimonies and authors’ interpretations in the results section); (ii) assessment of similarities and differences between codes and creation of a mutually agreed taxonomy of codes; (iii) grouping of codes into descriptive themes to be agreed by the team of researchers; (iv) formulation of analytical themes (from the descriptive themes) in group work sessions between the researchers. Although the initial analysis will be carried out by two researchers, the entire research team will actively engage in a process of reflection, iteration and discussion to determine the final themes and their supporting arguments.

Critical Appraisal of the Included Studies

Each included study will be appraised individually for its methodological quality using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Qualitative Studies Checklist. The CASP tool was selected because it is the most commonly used quality appraisal tool in health-related qualitative evidence syntheses and is endorsed by the Cochrane Qualitative and Implementation Methods Group (Long et al., 2020). It provides a clear structure across three broad domains: rigour, credibility, and relevance. Two authors will independently assess each study; disagreements will be resolved through discussion or by consulting a third author.

Following individual appraisal, the overall confidence in the synthesis findings will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research (GRADE-CERQual) approach (Lewin et al., 2015). The four GRADE-CERQual domains will be evaluated: methodological limitations (informed directly by the CASP appraisals), relevance, coherence, and adequacy of data. Two authors will independently rate each domain (no or minor concerns, moderate concerns, serious concerns, very serious concerns). Any discrepancies will be resolved through consultation with other researchers to reach a consensus.

Specifically: (i) Methodological limitations will be derived from the CASP appraisals; (ii) Relevance will be determined by whether the studies included align with the review’s context (informal caregivers of dependent older adults), classifying the level of relevance as direct, partial, indirect, or uncertain; (iii) Coherence will be assessed to determine whether the findings are well substantiated and documented in the primary qualitative studies; (iv) Data adequacy will be evaluated to define whether the primary studies supporting the findings are sufficient in terms of quantity and richness (e.g., use of data saturation principle).

To enhance transparency, results will be presented in a CERQual Evidence Profile and Summary of Qualitative Findings (SoQF) table, summarizing the confidence assessments for each synthesized finding.

Reflexivity

The researchers involved in this review will engage in both prospective and retrospective reflexivity throughout all stages of the research process. Reflexivity will address how researcher subjectivity may influence the different functions and decisions inherent to qualitative evidence synthesis. To ensure transparency and rigor, regular meetings will be held with the researchers to facilitate reflective exercises and foster open discussions about research decisions. Additionally, an audit trail will be maintained to document all methodological decisions and their justifications. This audit trail will serve to systematically question and evaluate the individual and collective influence of team members on the synthesis process, thus enhancing the trustworthiness and credibility of the findings (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Patient and Public Involvement

For pragmatic reasons related to time constraints and capacity resources of the research team, users were not involved in the development of this protocol and will not participate in the final qualitative synthesis. The authors acknowledge this as a limitation, as informal caregivers, patients, the public or stakeholders’ input could have enriched this study. However, resource and timeline constraints made meaningful involvement not feasible.

Ethics and Diffusion

The nature of this methodology does not require formal ethics committee approval, as this review will utilize published or publicly available data. The findings will be disseminated through publication in high-impact journals and presentations at international scientific conferences. An executive summary of the review will be developed and distributed to key stakeholders to ensure the findings reach relevant audiences effectively.

Reporting of Protocol and Systematic Review

We will report our synthesis of qualitative research following the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) statement (Tong et al., 2012).
