Barriers and enablers for publishing results of randomized trials: a cross-sectional survey of principal investigators in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Ameer Hohlfeld, Tamara Kredo, Michael Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate the views and experiences of principal investigators (PIs) based in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) regarding publication bias of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and strategies to mitigate it.DesignCross-sectional online survey, informed by previous reviews and prior surveys on publication bias. The survey consisted of closed-ended items with Likert scales and optional free text.SettingWe emailed invitations to African-based PIs that we identified through the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform.Participants6056 invitations were sent, 610 opened the survey, 433 were eligible to partake, and 409 consented and proceeded with the survey.Main outcome measuredAwareness and perceived magnitude of publication bias in SSA, experiences of timely publication, non-publication, delayed publication and their perceived effectiveness of mitigating strategies.ResultsAwareness that all RCTs are to be published was 62.7% (227/362). More than 90% agreed that PIs are responsible for ensuring that their RCTs are published (295/323), and 84% (272/323) within 24 months of completion. Publication bias in SSA scored a median of 71/100 (IQR: 50 – 84) for geographical region and 66 (IQR = 40 –79) for the respondent’s specific field of research. Of the respondents, 63.1% (219/347) published the findings of at least one RCT within 24 months, and 23.6% (82/347) were unable to publish at least one complete RCT. Notably, 74% (246/332) indicated not having had prior training in writing RCT-specific manuscripts. Of the 82 that were unable to publish, their reported reasons for non-publication included 30.5% (25/82) journal rejection, 29.3% (24/82) time constraints, 14.6% (12/82) non-significant or 13.4% (11/82) non-positive results, 13.4% (11/82) methodological concerns and 12.2% (10/82) lack of confidence in writing. 41.2% (143/347) of respondents reported delays greater than 24 months, 47.9% (69/347) attributed time constraint, 38.9% (56/347) time-consuming analysis, and 36.8% (53/347) journal rejection. Of the 332 participants that answered questions on mitigating strategies, the following were suggested to be very effective: 56.6%(188/332) dedicated writing time, 37.1% (123/332) guidance on writing a manuscript and 35.8% (119/332) peer-review prior to submission. Furthermore, 52.7% (175/332) rated the CONSORT checklist as effective/very effective, and 37.4% (124/332) noted artificial intelligence tools somewhat effective.ConclusionsSSA PIs note that publication bias is a problem, which is a result of barriers such as time constraints, analysis difficulties, journal rejection, and limited writing support. Enablers include mentorship, dedicated writing time, and structured reporting tools.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41073-026-00207-0.

What is already known on this topic

Timely and complete publication of randomised controlled trial (RCT) results is essential for evidence-based healthcare and to prevent significant research waste. The World Health Organization recommends that trial results be published within 24 months of study completion to ensure transparency. Most existing evidence regarding the causes of publication bias originates from high-income countries, leaving a critical gap in understanding the specific barriers faced by researchers in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).

What this study adds

This study investigates the self-reported publication barriers and enablers from the perspective of principal investigators (PIs) based in SSA. It identified that 74% of SSA PIs have had no formal training in writing RCT-specific manuscripts, with journal rejections and severe time constraints being the most significant barriers to dissemination. PIs identified dedicated writing time, academic mentorship, and structured reporting tools like the CONSORT checklist as the most effective strategies for increasing publication rates.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy

Policy: Findings support national and international efforts to mandate timely publication as a formal condition for ethical approval and research funding. Practice: Institutions should implement support systems such as writing retreats, mentorship programs, and protected time to help researchers overcome structural capacity constraints. Research and Publishing: This study calls for more equitable publishing practices, including article processing charges (APC) waivers and increased LMIC representation on editorial boards to reduce regional bias.

Introduction

Open Science, a global movement, emphasises timely, complete publication to make research accessible, transparent and reproducible [1]. This is particularly important in the health science field, which relies on results of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to inform advances for healthcare policy and practice. RCTs assess whether interventions work or not, and are central to informing evidence-based healthcare (EBHC) through their inclusion in systematic reviews and guidelines [2]. Timely, only accessible reporting of RCTs enables evidence synthesis reviewers to update their reviews, thus supporting better healthcare decisions promptly [3–5].

Furthermore, publishing as soon as possible is important so that healthcare practitioners, policymakers, funders, and patients who rely on the timely and complete publication of RCT results can make informed healthcare decisions [3]. However, a significant challenge persists in the form of publication bias, also known as dissemination or reporting bias. Publication bias occurs when published research does not include all completed studies [6, 7]. For this study, we defined ‘timely dissemination’ using the 24-month benchmark, consistent with the WHO public disclosure guidance [8]. The guidance encourages public availability of trial results as soon as possible and sets 24 months from completion as an outer timeframe for making results public. A recent Cochrane review of publication bias included 204 research reports and found that nearly half (47%) of the trials in those methodological studies were not published in full. The authors reported that it took a median of 25 months from trial completion to publication [9].

Publication bias is a global problem, with the under-reporting and delayed dissemination of findings occurring across settings and disciplines. Currently, most empirical evidence investigating the causes of non- and delayed publishing is from high-income countries and there is limited to no evidence describing the barriers and enablers faced by trialists in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Identifying gaps in SSA is important because these settings may have structural constraints that are less common in high income settings such as limited time and research support, fewer incentives and mentorship opportunities, higher relative costs to publish, and weaker enforcement of expectations to report results. Furthermore, incomplete dissemination may have disproportionate consequences on SSA health systems where resources are severely constrained.

Publishing RCTs is necessary to avert research waste [10, 11], which was estimated by Chalmers and Glasziou in 2009 to affect 85% of research investments [12]. The consequences of publication bias result in possible wasted investments in health research [12, 13], and can also lead to misinformed clinical decisions based on biased evidence about the balance of benefits and harms [14] and skewed systematic review findings that overestimate the likely benefits of newer interventions [15]. Reliable and timely information on an intervention's effectiveness, or, importantly, its lack of effectiveness, is necessary for prompt decision-making in rapidly progressing health crises, such as pandemics [16–19]. This is critical in low-resource settings, which are particularly afflicted by a high burden of diseases, scarce resources and fragile health systems. The Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and the World Health Organization (WHO) unequivocally state the need for principal investigators to publish all RCT results, irrespective of their findings [20].

Principal investigators play a crucial role in limiting publication bias by ensuring that their research findings are published [21]. Investigators commonly mention publication bias to occur as a result of time and capacity constraints, analysis delays, journal rejection, article processing charges, limited mentorship or writing support, language barriers, authorship or data ownership disputes, sponsor or intellectual-property restrictions, weak institutional incentives (especially for null results), and perceptions that locally focus studies lack novelty (especially when submitting to global journals). Lastly, lacking motivation to publish ‘negative’ or non-significant findings promotes non- or delayed publication [15, 22–29].

Despite growing understanding of the reasons for publication bias, there is a need to investigate further the perspectives of principal investigators on the barriers and enablers they face in publishing RCTs.

This survey aimed to investigate the perspectives of principal investigators based in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) about the causes of publication bias, defined as non- or delayed publication of RCT results. We also assessed barriers to, and enablers of, publishing RCTs and potential strategies to mitigate publication bias.

Methods

The reporting of this survey follows the consensus-based checklist for reporting of survey studies (CROSS) [30]. The completed CROSS checklist is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Survey design

This cross-sectional survey was informed by the findings from a scoping review of interventions to mitigate publication bias [31] and also adapted from earlier surveys on a similar topic by Malicki, Carroll, and colleagues [22, 24].

To reduce the time burden and minimise responder fatigue, we presented closed-ended categorical questions that used dichotomous or multiple-choice options, as well as Likert scales. We gave respondents the option to elaborate on their answers or provide additional information as free text. We included non-mandatory open-ended questions as part of the final section, which pertained to demographic information that was less relevant to the aims of this survey. We used skip logic so that respondents would only be shown questions that were relevant to them. The survey had five parts: (1) Opinions of respondents on publication bias, (2) Publication practices (reasons for not publishing, delayed publishing, publishing), (3) Strategies to increase the publication of RCT results and shorten the time to publication, (4) Abilities, perspectives and experience, and (5) Demographics information.

Publication bias was not assessed via a standalone knowledge item, such as asking respondents to define the term. Instead, we framed the study purpose in the participant information sheet and operationalised publication bias throughout the instrument as non- or delayed publication of the results of completed RCTs.

Survey development and data collection

Pilot testing

The survey was developed in English and underwent piloting by three experts who have been principal investigators of RCTs in SSA. They assessed the survey for face and content validity, providing feedback by answering questions about the survey [32, 33]. These questions pertained to the time taken to complete the survey, whether the length was appropriate, whether the questions made sense, and whether the instructions were easy to understand [34]. Lastly, the experts provided feedback on questions that could be removed, changed or rephrased, and suggested questions to be added. Where necessary, we discussed any unclear comments with the experts [34]. When consensus was reached with the experts, we tested the survey multiple times to ensure efficient usability. The survey was then translated into French and Portuguese, and the three language versions were targeted at those who speak these languages in the SSA region. We did not undertake back translation because the instrument comprised predominantly of closed-ended questions with fixed response options. None of the free-text sections was completed in French or Portuguese (Table S2).

Sample characteristics and sample size

The inclusion criteria were Africa-based principal or lead investigators who had completed one or more RCTs that were conducted in SSA. Eligibility was assessed at the respondent level. We did not extract or classify trial-level co-PI arrangements from registry records. Trials with co-PIs from non-SSA institutions were not treated differently because the survey aimed to capture the perspectives of eligible respondents based in Africa. We searched the WHO's International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) for RCTs registered since 2010 with at least one study site in SSA. The ICTRP contains records from WHO-recognised primary registries, including the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR). Our sampling frame was intentionally restricted to WHO-ICTRP recognised registries, and we did not undertake additional searches of any country-specific registries. We extracted the associated email addresses listed on the platform. This resulted in a sample of 6056 unique email addresses, which we used to invite potential respondents to participate in the survey.

Survey administration and management

We developed the survey using REDCap and distributed it through that platform, ensuring a seamless and efficient process [35]. We gave identified respondents one month to complete the survey from the first invitation. Responses were automatically stored on REDCap for analysis. Respondents could save their responses intermittently and modify their answers before submitting their completed survey.

The survey was sent out on April 2, 2025, and was closed for analysis on May 15, 2025. We sent an invitation to investigators with a valid email address, explaining the purpose of the survey. We noted from our search of the trial registries that clinical trial administrative staff are sometimes listed as the contact person instead of the investigator. Therefore, in the email invitation, we requested that the survey be forwarded to the relevant investigators to increase responses. Some of our email invitations failed to be delivered due to the following reasons: (1) the recipient's server automatically blocked them, or the email was not delivered because the recipient's server was unavailable or busy (in these cases, if we received a “bounce” notification, we sent individual personalised emails to the respondents); (2) we received an automated response stating that the intended recipient is not contactable due to them having moved institutions (in these cases, we sent an invitation to any alternative email address provided); and (3) we received an automated out-of-office response (in these cases, we re-emailed potential respondents on a date when they would be back in the office).

Respondents accessed the survey via a hyperlink in the email. Eligibility was checked, and informed consent was obtained on the landing page before starting the survey. For non-responders, we sent two follow-up reminder emails. The first was to be sent on the 10th working day, and the second on the 20th working day after the first survey request. In the follow-up emails, we requested that respondents who had already completed the survey disregard the follow-up email. We provided no incentives to take part in this survey, but offered to provide our results upon completion or a link to the published report [36].

Survey data were stored on password-protected systems that underwent routine scheduled backups. Only the study investigators had access to the restricted data, in line with institutional data protection policies.

Branching (skip) logic was implemented in REDCap to minimise respondent burden and ensure item relevance, which meant that some questions were not presented to all respondents, leading to differences in denominators for some of the responses. Respondents needed to select their preferred language and provide consent, and satisfy eligibility screening to start the survey. A question-by-question flow with skip patterns is found in Supplementary Table S2. At survey entry, we provided participants with a participant information sheet describing the rationale for the study and framing the problems of non- and delayed publication of RCT results.

Statistical analysis

We downloaded the anonymised survey results from REDCap and exported the numerical data to STATA 16 for cleaning, organising and analysis [37].. We conducted a descriptive analysis of the respondents, the type of variables, and the nature of the data to inform the development of a plan for appropriate cleaning and recoding (where applicable), as well as to assess the magnitude of missing data and the spread of key variables for this study. We inspected the quantitative data, such as continuous variables, for normality using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. We calculated means with standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile range (IQR) as appropriate. For discrete variables, we calculated the numbers and percentages. Where appropriate, we report categorical data as frequencies and percentages. Depending on the nature and distribution of the data, we employed chi-squared tests for categorical variables, Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and independent samples t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We assessed non-response bias by comparing completed with partially completed survey sections using chi-square tests.

We analysed open-ended responses using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis, which is a flexible method for identifying, analysing, and reporting themes within qualitative data [38]. This involves six steps: becoming familiar with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes among the codes, reviewing and refining those themes, defining and naming them, and finally interpreting the themes in relation to the research question. This approach can help to uncover insights into the experiences and perceptions of respondents [38]. For our analyses, we have avoided presenting data that would make RCTs identifiable or allow readers to identify the researchers or their RCTs.

Patient and public involvement

We did not consult patients or members of the public in designing, conducting, reporting, or disseminating this study. We focus on being principal investigators, which meant that the instrument was developed and piloted in consultation with principal investigators and trial methodologists from SSA to ensure technical accuracy and relevance. However, we will make the finding available in a plain language summary via Cochrane South Africa’s social media platforms and website.

Ethical considerations

The survey conforms to all relevant regulations and data privacy legislation by adhering to South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA). We assured that responses would remain anonymous and confidential, and no potentially identifying information would be analysed or reported. Furthermore, this study upholds beneficence (maximising potential benefits) and non-maleficence (minimising any potential harm). No remuneration or incentives were offered for participation. The study was conducted in line with ethical principles. The Stellenbosch University Health Research Ethics Committee reviewed and approved this study: project ID 24380 and the ethics reference number S22/06/108 (PhD).

Results

Respondent characteristics

We identified 14,757 email addresses from our search and removed 8701 duplicate emails. We sent invitations to 6056 people, of whom 610 opened the link to the REDCap platform. Among these, 576 selected a language, 463 indicated that they agreed with the first screening question regarding the statement on the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013, and 452 agreed to participate in the survey. However, the last screening question identified that only 433 of these had been an investigator on an RCT conducted in an SSA country and were therefore eligible for this study. Of them, 409 respondents had signed the consent form and proceeded with the survey. Figure 1 depicts the respondent flow. Our tests for bias showed no evidence of differential non-response; the tests indicated non-significant differences. Language selection was predominantly English (538/576), with smaller numbers selecting French (33/576) and Portuguese (5/576) (Fig. 1). Respondents rated the extent to which they perceived publication bias as a problem in their geographical region and specific field of research using a scale from 0 to 100 (with higher scores indicating a greater perceived problem). The median responses were 71 (IQR: 50 – 84) for geographical region and 66 (IQR = 40–79) for the respondent’s specific field of research. Respondents further commented on their experiences and opinions regarding publication bias, as presented in the supplementary table S3. The respondents were not asked to define ‘publication bias’. Publication bias was operationalised as non- or delayed publication in the participant information sheet that was attached to the survey invitation.

Publication practices ( N =347)

Three hundred and forty-seven respondents completed the second section of the survey, asking about their publication practices. They were asked about 1) Good publication practices, which enquired about whether they had published the findings of any RCT within 24 months of study completion; 2) Non-publication of RCT findings after trial completion, which enquired about their inability to publish findings of any RCT after trial completion; and 3) Delayed publication of RCT findings, which enquired about reasons for taking longer than 24 months to publish main findings after completion for any RCT.

Good publication practices

Among respondents who completed this section, 219/347 (63.1%) reported publishing results for at least one RCT within 24 months of trial completion, 103/347 (29.7%) had not done so, and 25/347 (7.2%) were unsure (Table S4). For the optional item on dissemination routes (multiple selections allowed; denominator = respondent answering the item), most indicated peer-reviewed journals 219/347 (63.1%), followed by trial registries 63/347 (18.2%), non-peer-reviewed journals 2/347 (0.6%), and other routes 8/347 (2.3%), including conference/faculty abstracts, self-published reports, sponsors’ websites and policy briefs.

From the list of reasons for publishing their findings in peer-reviewed journals, respondents selected various reasons, and they were able to choose more than one, for which 161/347 (46.4%) indicated that the RCT was methodologically sound and of good quality, 160/347 (46.1%) indicated that it was a responsibility to the public, and 157/347 (45.2%) indicated that the findings were important. To a lesser extent, 129/347 (37.2%) selected that it is an ethical imperative, 121/347 (34.9%) the study had been completed, 90/347 (25.9%) for their career progression, and 76/347 (21.9%) had a good author team. The least selected were 60/347 (17%), indicating that a journal rejected the manuscript, yet they still pursued publication, among which the median number of rejections was 1.5 (IQR: 1–2). Other reasons for publishing include contributing to scientific knowledge and advancing the broader evidence base, recognising the significance of a published trial, fulfilling academic requirements, leveraging the findings to support future grant applications, and ensuring scientific rigour by going through the peer-review process (see Table S5 for a detailed summary).

Non-publication of RCT findings after trial completion (N = 347)

Among respondents, 23.6% (82/347) reported being unable to publish the findings of at least one RCT after trial completion. Of these, 45/347 (45/34.9%) could not publish the findings of one RCT, and 28.1% (23/347 (28.1%) were unable to publish between 2 and 5 RCTs; 14/347.1% (14/347.1%) were unsure of the number. The reasons for non-publication given by the 82/347 respondents included that one or more journals rejected their submission 25/82 (30.5% (25/82), time constraints 24/82 (29.3% (24/82), findings not significant 14.6% (12/82 (14.6%), findings not positive 11/82 (13.4% (11/82), poor quality or methodological flaws in the RCT 11/82 (13.4% (11/82), and lack of confidence in writing the manuscript to a level that a journal would accept it 10/82.2% (12.2%). Respondents submitted their unpublished manuscripts to multiple journals, with a median of 2 (IQR 2–4). These findings are summarised in Table S6. Respondents provided reasons for journal rejection which pertained to methodological limitations, lack of prospective trial registration or ethical approval, not aligned with the journal’s fit or scope, lack of impact or novelty, editorial space or constraints, unsatisfactory reviewer feedback, or major revisions required. A detailed summary can be found in Supplementary Table S7. Other reasons unrelated to the journal’s decisions were retrospective or delayed trial registration, financial constraints (e.g., article processing charges), lack of time or competing priorities, insufficient institutional support or resources, academic or professional conflicts, low recruitment or underpowered studies, study-specific or contextual factors (e.g., staff turnover), and lack of motivation or interest in publishing. A detailed summary can be found in Supplementary Table S8.

Delayed publication of RCT findings (N = 347)

Among the respondents, 143/347 (41.2%) indicated that they had taken longer than 24 months to publish the main findings after completion of an RCT on at least one occasion. Of these, 69/347 (47.9%) attributed this to time constraints, busy schedule or the loss of interest, 56/143 (39.2%) indicated that data analysis was time-consuming, and 53/143 (37.1%) indicated that one or more journals rejected their manuscript (Table S9). There was a median of 3 (2–3) journal submissions prior to publication for the 53 respondents who indicated one or more journals rejected their manuscript.

Respondents provided reasons for journal rejection, which included lack of novelty or significance, misalignment with journal scope or priorities, methodological concerns, retrospective trial registration, journal prestige and fit, localised or niche interest, negative or non-significant results, editorial desk rejection without feedback, language or editorial issues, long review times, processing charges, and study type restrictions. A detailed summary can be found in Supplementary Table S10. Other reasons unrelated to the journal’s decisions were personal and health-related challenges, pandemic-related operational delays, limited experience or capacity, academic and supervisory constraints, institutional or administrative barriers, financial constraints, delays in the manuscript submission and peer review process, and study design or data-related complexities. A detailed summary can be found in Supplementary Table S11.

Strategies to increase the publication of RCT results and shorten the time to publication, and prior training on preparing RCT-specific manuscripts (n = 332)

The use of innovative tools such as the CONSORT checklists was rated by 79/332 (23.8%) of respondents as being very effective, and an additional 121/332 (28.9%) rated it as effective, while 93/332 (28.0%) said it could be somewhat effective. Comparably, 124/332 (37.4%) of respondents rated Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools as somewhat effective. Lastly, 125/332 (37.7%) respondents rated the use of previously published reports as writing templates as effective, or very effective, 60/332 (18.1%). As a follow-up question, the 332 respondents were asked to indicate whether they received training on preparing RCT-specific manuscripts for peer-reviewed journals; 246/332 (74%) had no prior training (Table S12). Some respondents provided information on training, tools, and strategies they have used or know of that may mitigate publication bias. They highlighted the following additional strategies: protocol transparency and trial registration, adhering to reporting guidelines, especially at the design and protocol stage, for improved transparency and reporting quality at the very end. They reinforced mentorship and peer support as important means to guide the manuscript preparation process and indicated that planning and writing some sections of the manuscript before RCT completion would mitigate potential time constraints at a later stage. The mandating of the publishing of results by ethics committees and funders was identified as a critical enabler. Furthermore, respondents felt that awareness raising and education on publication bias as an ethical matter would ensure timely publishing.

Publishing abilities, experience and perspectives (N = 323)

The respondents rated their level of agreement with statements on their publishing ability, experience and perspectives on publishing RCTs (Table S13), of 147/323 (45.5%) agreed and 136/323 (42.1%) strongly agreed that they felt confident and competent to report their RCT findings in a peer-reviewed journal. Similarly, most found it easy to report their findings, with 143/323 (44.3%) agreeing and 74/323 (22.9%) strongly agreeing.

The majority, 117/323 (36.2%), agreed, and 178/323 (55.1%) strongly agreed that the lead investigators are responsible for ensuring that the RCT findings are published in peer-reviewed journals. Similarly, almost all agreed or strongly agreed that it is a disservice to EBHC.

Although there appeared to be mixed views on whether non-publication is scientific misconduct, with 54/323 (16.7%) strongly disagreeing, and 84/323 (26.0%) disagreeing with this statement. However, 103/323 (31.9%) were undecided, and 82/323 (25.4%) favoured the statement.

Respondents’ RCT experiences, funding sources and demographics (N = 323)

Among the 323 respondents who provided profile data, 307/323 (95.0%) indicated being senior trialists, with experience for 116/323 (35.9%) ranging from 2–5 years; 91/323 (28.2%) ranging from 6–10 years; and 96/323 (29.7%) with more than 11 years. They indicated a wide range of funding sources, with universities selected as the most prevalent. Participants were distributed across SSA, with the largest number representing Nigeria, South Africa and Ethiopia. The full distributions are in Tables S14 and S15.

Discussion

Although publication bias and selective reporting also affect studies other than RCTs, RCTs are conducted within a distinctive ethical and regulatory context. They require prospective registration and transparent results dissemination. Therefore, although our findings are likely to be relevant beyond trials, they should be interpreted primarily in relation to RCTs.

This survey has gathered information on the experiences, challenges and perspectives of principal investigators who have conducted RCTs in SSA in relation to publishing RCT results. Although most recognised the importance of timely, peer-reviewed publication, a substantial proportion indicated their inability to do so [24]. They consistently attributed this to time constraints, rejection by journals, and insufficient writing support. Our findings provide insight into region-specific factors that perpetuate publication bias and identify ways that investigators propose for targeted strategies to reduce publication bias.

Awareness and perceptions of publication bias

Although most respondents were aware of the need to publish the results of RCTs, more than a third were unaware or unsure of this. This suggests that local policies are not reinforcing this requirement, or that investigators have limited exposure to these international standards [8, 39]. The term ‘publication bias’ was not presented as a stand-alone definition in the survey. Some respondents may have interpreted the concept differently, which highlights the importance of targeted sensitisation and training for trialists on dissemination obligations and the distinctions between publication bias, selective reporting, and other reporting biases [8]. In SSA, ethics and regulatory enforcement of results reporting may vary. Therefore, it is critical to improve dissemination practices and reduce research waste through consistent and harmonised requirements from ethics committees, funders and registries.

Respondents regarded publication bias in their research field and geographic regions as a moderate to serious concern. This regional-specific information is consistent with global data on under-reporting [28, 40–42]. Addressing it may require targeted strategies at the SSA country level to address barriers and provide support, and stronger regulations or policies in individual countries. Respondents suggested that ways of overcoming this could be awareness-raising by ethics committees and funders who could strengthen a sense of responsibility to publish and ensure dissemination as an ethical imperative and public duty.

Barriers to timely publication and solutions

The respondents’ most frequently reported reasons for non-publication were rejection of submitted manuscripts by journals, time constraints and lack of confidence in writing journal-ready manuscripts. Beyond the investigator and institutional level of determinants, the dissemination of the results of SSA-led RCTs is shaped by structural features of the scholarly publishing ecosystems. Historically, the Global North has largely set the publishing norms and acted as the editorial gatekeepers; with an analysis of a global health journal showing underrepresentation of editors based in LMICs [43, 44]. Geographical representation in reviewer pools influences an author's chances of publishing, and language barriers may further disadvantage non-English-speaking authors and those not in English-speaking settings [45, 46]. Furthermore, Global South researchers may encounter financial barriers to dissemination because of the cost of the article processing charges (APC) [47, 48]. These interplays suggest that mitigating publication bias should not solely be framed as improving “research compliance” or reducing “research waste”, but should also acknowledge inequities in producing scientific knowledge and its dissemination. It is important for journals to promote diverse editorial boards and reviewers, provide clearer support for non-native English authors, increase transparency in peer-review processes, and implement equitable open-access policies, such as fee waivers, to reduce the barriers caused by APCs. Notably, some journals already have such mechanisms in place.

Respondents noted the utility of novel practical approaches that may improve their chances of publishing, which included structured templates and AI tools, especially for individuals without access to prior formal training, networks, or mentors, thereby bridging these gaps.

The main causes reported for delayed publications were time constraints, repeated manuscript rejections and time needed to analyse the RCT data. Respondents indicated a high agreement with the use of writing templates and CONSORT checklists. Tools like these, complemented by digital reporting tools such as WebCONSORT and COBWEB, have been shown to improve the reporting of RCTs [49, 50]. These tools provide time-saving approaches by providing investigators with structured formats to follow, and are especially useful for respondents who require support with the sections of their report that require technical details [51–53]. Thus, making these available in settings with limited access to formal training or writing mentorship is identified as a facilitator, and it seems likely that these strategies would benefit both novice and clinician-researcher investigators.

Attitude, responsibilities and misconduct

Respondents strongly agreed that the lead investigator has a duty to publish their RCT results and that failing to do so is a disservice to EBHC [10, 54, 55]. They also noted that publication is a responsibility to the public. Although many respondents concurred that non-publication is misconduct, nearly one third, 103/323 (31.9%), remained undecided, and a quarter, 82/323 (25.4%), did not think so. This range of views may reflect a lack of institutional enforcement and difficulties in publishing. For example, one respondent wrote, “It is not that I didn’t want to publish, I couldn’t”. This might require better training opportunities, given that only 246/332 (74.1%) indicated that they had not received formal training in preparing RCT-specific manuscripts [8, 54, 56–58].

Generalisability

Our findings reflect the contactable principal investigators based in SSA from the ICTRP. The lack of survey completion by Franco and Lusophone-speaking participants indicates underrepresentation. Thus, results should be interpreted with caution. The low participation in the translated versions may reflect numerous possibilities, either due to non-delivery or non-response to the invitation, limited engagement with the survey link, or, because English is a widely spoken and written academic language, respondents may have preferred to complete the survey in English despite the availability of a translation. Nonetheless, our findings are similar to previous research on this topic, suggesting its applicability to the SSA research contexts [22, 25–29].

Our approach relied on the investigator contact details available from trial registries and may underrepresent multicentre trials conducted in Africa that are led by sponsor or contract research organisation with a primary contact outside of SSA.

Implications for policy, practice and research

These findings suggest that reducing publication bias in SSA requires strategies targeted at the individual and system levels. National and international policies should consider mandating timely publications aligned with WHO/ICMJE standards [8, 54, 56]. Interest holders, such as ethics committees and funders, could make publication obligatory as a condition of their approval and funding [10, 59].

At an individual and research team-level, respondents endorsed several strategies, including face-to-face scientific writing workshops, mentorship that has been well-documented in the literature [31]. Concurrently, they noted that structured templates and reporting guidelines may reduce non-publication; however, these were not identified by the recent evidence synthesis [31]. Through this study, we identified a gap in the research to assess the perceptions of effectiveness of these innovative evidence-informed interventions.

As a next step, we will convene an interested stakeholder consultation with SSA-based trialists to explore these findings in the form of a focus group discussion. The Focus group will help us gain deeper insights into mitigation strategies. Ultimately, aiming to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges and, importantly, identify targeted approaches to mitigate publication bias in the SSA context.

Limitations

As with many unsolicited online surveys, the main limitation of this survey is the low response rate. In this case, this may be attributed in part to the failure of our email invitation to reach potential respondents [29]. Furthermore, we do not know how different the experiences and opinions of those who chose to respond to this survey may be from those of the invitees who did not respond. Lastly, non-Anglophone respondents may have been underrepresented and caution is required when extrapolating these findings to Francophone- and Lusophone-speaking SSA contexts.

Conclusion

This up-to-date survey of principal investigators of SSA-based RCTs has identified common barriers to timely publications of RCT findings and suggested solutions to this problem. Publication bias is a recognised concern that can be mitigated through multi-pronged approaches, which include traditional activities such as formalised training, writing retreats, and peer support, as well as more innovative approaches that might overcome structural constraints, especially for principal investigators with limited time and resources. Our next step is to discuss these findings in a meeting of principal investigators, to help develop proposals for strategies to contribute to ongoing efforts to mitigate publication bias and to offer recommendations for overcoming barriers so that those using reports of RCTs to make decisions about health and social care have easy access to up-to-date, unbiased evidence.
