Multiscale rational construction strategy for polyphenol self-assembled delivery systems: from nanoscale to microscale
Authors: Qi Xie, Haoyu Zhang, Yuan Gao, Hongbing Lan, Ling Tang, Jinying Lv, Conglian Yang, Li Kong, Dong Liu, Zhiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural polyphenols have garnered significant attention in pharmaceuticals, food, and cosmetics due to their unique bioactive functions such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and antibacterial properties. The multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups in polyphenol molecules (catechol and galloyl moieties) enable dynamic complexation with various substances through non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions and coordination bonding. The excellent self-assembly and interfacial properties of polyphenols enable the construction of multiscale and multifunctional delivery systems, ranging from nanoscale to microscale. Microscale delivery systems play an irreplaceable role in disease treatment, and research on polyphenol-based microscale delivery systems is gaining momentum. This review systematically describes various polyphenol-based microscale delivery systems, including coacervate droplets, Pickering emulsions, microcapsules, microparticles, and polyphenol-modified microbes and cells, and discusses their self-assembly mechanisms, preparation methods, functional characteristics and therapeutic applications. Finally, we outline the research advances in polyphenol self-assembled delivery systems and discuss future directions for the development of next-generation polyphenol-based biomaterials.Keywords: Polyphenols, Self-assembly, Coacervate droplets, Microscale, Drug delivery

Introduction

Polyphenols are a class of important bioactive substances in nature, occurring in various organisms such as plants, fungi, bacteria and animals. Plants are the most significant source of polyphenols, which are distributed in various plant tissues [[1], [2], [3]]. Structurally, polyphenols represent a diverse group of organic molecules characterized by the presence of multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups attached to aromatic rings. They are chemically active and usually exist as mixtures of homologues with similar properties. Thousands of polyphenol structures have been discovered and identified. Polyphenols can be classified into five major types based on their structural features: phenolic acids, flavonoids, tannins, stilbenes, and lignans [4]. Common polyphenol molecules with well-defined structures, high purity and wide availability include tannic acid (TA), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), protocatechualdehyde (PA), proanthocyanidin (PC), quercetin, ellagic acid (EA), epigallocatechin (EGC), gallic acid (GA), epicatechin (EC), catechin, curcumin, gossypol, resveratrol, caffeic acid and rutin, representing a structurally diverse array of bioactive molecules [5].

As ubiquitous secondary metabolites in plants, polyphenols function as essential structural constituents and critical components of the innate defense mechanisms in flora. The interaction between polyphenols and salivary proteins induces astringency, a defensive adaptation that deters excessive herbivory by animals and insects, while concurrently shielding plant tissues from ultraviolet radiation damage. Beyond their role as natural protectants for plants, polyphenols constitute a significant dietary source for humans and are vital bioactive compounds that contribute to health maintenance and longevity [6,7]. Historically integral to human civilization, polyphenols are prominently featured in globally consumed beverages such as tea, coffee, and wine, and are abundant in diverse food sources. They serve as key active constituents in herbal medicines and, with advancing technology, have gained escalating importance in daily life through applications in food, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals, underscoring their relevance to human health [8]. Polyphenols provide a robust foundation for modern functional material engineering, acting as natural molecular building blocks with extensive application potential across sectors including pharmaceutics, cosmetics, food science, daily chemicals, leather, petroleum, timber, water treatment, and industrial processes. In recent years, polyphenols have emerged as a focal point in biomedical research due to their multifunctional bioactivities, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, anti-allergic, antitumor, antiviral, and immunomodulatory properties [9]. Notably, TA and other polyphenols have been translated into clinical applications, emerging as therapeutic agents and formulations that leverage their unique biological properties [10]. Through multi-target mechanisms, polyphenols modulate diverse signaling pathways, exhibiting significant therapeutic efficacy in the management of various conditions such as infectious diseases, allergic disorders, cancer, aging, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases.

Physicochemical properties of polyphenols

Polyphenols exhibit amphiphilic characteristics, which arise from the presence of both hydrophobic aromatic benzene ring domains and hydrophilic hydroxyl groups in their structures [11,12]. The aromatic ring units in polyphenol molecules can interact hydrophobically with other molecules and can also form strong bonds with other aromatic ring structures through π-π stacking. The abundant hydroxyl groups give them excellent hydrophilicity and the ability to form hydrogen bond networks [13]. When two or three adjacent hydroxyl groups are present on the same benzene ring, these structures are called catechol or galloyl structures. Therefore, polyphenols can also form complexes with metal ions, forming five-membered ring coordination bonds with different metal ions, and their metal complexation properties are closely related to the substitution pattern of the hydroxyl groups. This process relies on the deprotonation of the hydroxyl groups, so the pH value is crucial for the complexation of polyphenols with metals. The alkaline environment is more likely to allow the hydroxyl groups to release protons, facilitating the binding of polyphenols with metal ions through coordination bonds. The structure of the galloyl group has an additional hydroxyl group, which can reduce the pKa of the adjacent two hydroxyl groups through electronic effects, so the galloyl structure is more effective in complexing metal ions than the catechol structure, and can achieve efficient complexation over a wider pH range. The conjugated aromatic ring system of polyphenols confers characteristic absorption in the ultraviolet (UV) region. The hydroxyl groups attached to the aromatic rings induce a red shift of the maximum absorption peak from approximately 254 nm (typical of benzene rings) to around 280 nm (characteristic of phenols). This structural feature endows polyphenolic compounds with superior UV absorption capacity, enabling them to effectively prevent UV radiation-induced DNA damages within cells [[14], [15], [16]]. Leveraging this photochemical property, polyphenols have been demonstrated to serve as natural photoprotective agents.

The widespread application of polyphenols is attributed to their high sensitivity to oxidation, which stems from the chemical reactivity of the phenolic hydroxyl bonds on the benzene ring [[17], [18], [19]]. The bond dissociation energy of phenolic hydroxyl groups is relatively low, making them prone to cleavage and generating highly reactive semiquinone or quinone radicals. These radicals can further initiate intermolecular or intramolecular reactions, forming a series of complex oxidation products [20]. In the presence of certain metal ions, catechol or galloyl structures can bind to metals, triggering redox reactions that reduce the metal ions [21]. Meanwhile, the polyphenols themselves are oxidized into semiquinone forms. Once generated, the semiquinone radicals can reduce another metal ion, continuing the oxidation process to form quinones. This oxidation reaction becomes more pronounced under alkaline conditions or in the presence of specific enzymes. Therefore, polyphenols exhibit a dual anti-oxidant function: on one hand, they can reduce metal ions and inhibit reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation pathways; on the other hand, they act as electron donors, exerting antioxidant effects by terminating free radical chain reactions. This distinctive chemical structure and reactivity render polyphenols excellent natural antioxidants.

Assembly principle of polyphenol-based delivery systems

Structural basis

Catechol and galloyl moieties, as the most important functional moieties of polyphenols, serve as the structural basis for polyphenol self-assembly behavior [22]. The ortho-dihydroxyl groups of catechol moieties possess moderate hydrogen bond donor density and reversible metal coordination ability. In metal-free systems, catechol moieties primarily drive self-assembly through intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking with aromatic rings, tending to form aggregates with relatively small size and flexible structure. The three adjacent phenolic hydroxyl groups of galloyl moieties significantly increase hydrogen bond donor density and molecular polarity, enabling the formation of stronger multiple hydrogen bond networks, while the larger conjugated aromatic systems enhance π-π stacking interactions [23]. Consequently, polyphenols containing galloyl moieties more readily assemble into aggregates with larger size, compact structure, and greater rigidity. Moreover, the galloyl moieties exhibits much higher affinity for metal ions than catechol moieties, favoring the formation of tridentate crosslinking networks. The multiple hydrogen bonds and π-π stacking of galloyl moieties enable stronger binding to drug molecules, thereby achieving higher drug loading capacity and greater resistance to dissociation. The catechol moieties, due to fewer binding sites, result in relatively lower drug loading, but avoid the potential drug release issues caused by excessive binding.

The intrinsic molecular structure of polyphenols endow them with unique interfacial interactions [24]. Polyphenols can effectively bind to a wide range of substances through non-covalent interactions such as hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, π-π interactions, and cation-π interactions [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. They can interact not only with inorganic and organic materials, but also with biological macromolecules (nucleic acids, proteins, etc.) and living organisms [[29], [30], [31]]. This versatile binding capacity allows polyphenols to serve both as modular building blocks for constructing delivery systems and as controllable agents for surface modification (Fig. 1).

Self-assembly mechanisms

Different intermolecular interactions indeed play distinct roles in polyphenol self-assembled delivery systems (Table 1). Polyphenols can chelate with various metal ions to form metal-polyphenol networks (MPNs). The core mechanism lies in the unique chelating property of polyphenol molecules. When the phenolic hydroxyl groups are deprotonated in an alkaline environment, the lone pair of electrons on the oxygen atoms forms highly stable five-membered chelation rings with metal ions [[32], [33], [34]]. Two hydroxyl groups in the polyphenol structure form the main coordination bonds with metal ions, while the third hydroxyl group reduces the pKa value of the adjacent hydroxyl group through an electronic effect. This coordination mode ensures the structural stability of metal-polyphenol nanoparticles while retaining the functional characteristics of metal active sites. By taking advantage of this metal-binding ability, polyphenols can be utilized for diverse applications such as therapy and imaging [35].

The interaction between polyphenols and biomolecules constitutes the fundamental mechanism underlying their biological activity and functional versatility. The binding of polyphenols to proteins is driven by a synergistic mechanism involving hydrophobic interactions and multiple hydrogen bonds. Proteins contain numerous aliphatic, aromatic, and other hydrophobic amino acid residues that collectively form hydrophobic pockets [36]. In the initial phase, polyphenols insert into these hydrophobic regions, enabling primary association through hydrophobic interactions. In the subsequent phase, the phenolic hydroxyl groups form multiple hydrogen bonds with the polar groups such as hydroxyl, amide and carboxyl of protein, further enhancing the stability of the complex. This dual-mode interaction leads to the formation of stable yet dynamically reversible polyphenol-protein complexes. Nucleotides, the building blocks of nucleic acids, consist of three essential components: nitrogenous heterocyclic bases, pentose sugars, and phosphate groups [37]. During molecular self-assembly, phosphate groups and pentose sugars are linked via condensation reactions to form the polynucleotide backbone, which features a phosphate-sugar framework densely populated with oxygen atoms bearing lone pairs. The abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups in polyphenols serve as potent hydrogen bond donors, forming strong hydrogen bonds with these electron-rich sites in the phosphate backbone. Furthermore, the purine and pyrimidine bases in nucleic acids possess hydrophobic aromatic rings that engage in π-π stacking interactions with the benzene rings of polyphenols, reinforcing the overall binding affinity [[38], [39], [40]]. This multifaceted interplay provides a robust molecular foundation for the rational design of multifunctional materials.

Synergistic effects

The inherent biological functions of polyphenols (e.g., anti-cancer, anti-oxidant, anti-microbial, and anti-inflammatory activities) can produce synergistic therapeutic effects with loaded drugs, enabling them to function not only as delivery carriers but also as therapeutic agents, thereby overcoming the limitations of monotonous function associated with conventional carriers [41]. Furthermore, the redox sensitivity and pH responsiveness of polyphenols provide inherent advantages for developing intelligent delivery systems. By integrating these features, polyphenol-based delivery systems exhibit diverse intermolecular interactions, bioactivity, and environmental responsiveness, making them suitable for applications in drug delivery, disease diagnosis, cell surface engineering, and tissue engineering. In recent years, polyphenols have been combined with various metal ions, small molecule drugs, nucleic acids, proteins, and polymers to construct diverse polyphenol-based delivery systems, such as nanoparticles, microparticles, microcapsules, and coacervate droplets. Polyphenols can serve either as primary components or as surface modifying components. These systems not only surpass traditional carriers in functionality, drug-loading capacity, stability, and biocompatibility but also advance the application of polyphenols in drug delivery. From nanoscale to microscale delivery systems, polyphenols demonstrate their unique strengths across different dimensions of delivery platforms (Fig. 2).

Polyphenol-based nanoscale delivery systems

Nanoparticles have emerged as pivotal delivery platforms in biomedicine owing to their unique size-dependent properties and tunable functional designability [42]. Through the synergistic effect of dynamic covalent bonds (such as borate ester bonds) and non-covalent interactions (such as hydrophobic interactions and π-π interactions), polyphenols can form stable nanoparticles with small molecule drugs [43]. Furthermore, the structural integrity and stability of these nanocarriers can be reinforced through additional mechanisms, including metal coordination and hydrogen bonding networks. These carrier-free delivery systems formed by the self-assembly of polyphenols and drug molecules offer multiple significant advantages [44]. They completely eliminate the need for traditional carriers, thereby avoiding immunogenicity and toxicity arising from carriers themselves. The preparation process is simple and efficient, requiring only mild conditions to drive self-assembly without complex steps [45]. Moreover, the carrier-free delivery systems achieve extremely high drug loading capacity, overcoming the limitation of large amounts of inert components typically present in conventional carriers [[46], [47], [48]]. The nanoparticles composed of small molecule drugs and polyphenols not only significantly improve the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs but also reduce systemic toxicity through environment-responsive release, demonstrating important application value in tumor targeted therapy, inflammation regulation, and related biomedical fields.

Polymers are a class of functional materials that play a significant role in fields such as sustained and controlled drug release and targeted delivery [49]. However, traditional polymer carriers still face challenges such as insufficient stability (premature drug release) and limited functionality (lack of biological activity). The introduction of polyphenols offers an innovative approach to address these issues [50]. The phenolic hydroxyl groups of polyphenols can interact with polymer molecules through dynamic non-covalent interactions. This molecular adhesion property and dynamic cross-linking mechanism not only enhance the structural stability of the carrier but also endow it with environmental responsiveness. Moreover, the inherent biological activity of polyphenols further confers synergistic therapeutic functions.

Polyphenols can also self-assemble with biomacromolecules (nucleic acids and proteins) to form functional nanoparticles through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic forces, and electrostatic forces. This strategy enables the efficient encapsulation and controlled release of therapeutic biomacromolecules while simultaneously conferring additional functionalities [[51], [52], [53]]. By virtue of their dynamic and reversible intermolecular interactions, nanoparticles composed of biomacromolecules and polyphenols combine three critical advantages: excellent biocompatibility, precise targeting capability, and robust preservation of bioactivity [[54], [55], [56]]. These systems have been successfully implemented in gene therapy and protein or peptide delivery, thereby paving the way for the clinical translation of biomacromolecule therapeutics.

The formation of MPNs via coordination bonding between polyphenols and metal ions not only facilitates the self-assembly of nanoparticles but also enables polyphenols to form stable surface coatings on a wide range of carriers owing to their strong binding affinity, thereby overcoming the limitations of conventional surface modification techniques [57]. Given the structural and functional diversity of both polyphenols and metal ions, MPNs with tailored functionalities can be engineered through rational selection of specific polyphenol and metal ions [[58], [59], [60]]. The dynamic and reversible nature of coordination bonds imparts environmental responsiveness to MPNs, allowing for stimuli-responsive assembly and disassembly in response to variations in pH, the presence of chelating agents, or exposure to light [61]. This tunable behavior highlights their potential for intelligent drug delivery systems with spatiotemporally precise control.

Polyphenol-based microscale delivery systems

Microscale drug delivery systems refer to particulate carriers with diameters in the micrometer range, designed to encapsulate therapeutic agents and regulate the release kinetics. They represent a paradigm of interdisciplinary integration between modern pharmaceutics and biomaterials science [[62], [63], [64], [65], [66]]. Through precisely engineered microstructures, these systems enable intelligent regulation of drug delivery in terms of timing, spatial location, and dosage [67,68]. The main characteristics of micron-scale delivery systems include (1) high drug loading capacity and co-delivery capability: compared with nanoscale delivery systems, their larger size allows encapsulation of substantial amounts of active ingredients and simultaneous loading of drugs with different properties for synergistic therapy; (2) controlled release kinetics: through carrier material degradation or structural diffusion mechanisms, sustained or controlled drug release can be achieved, prolonging therapeutic effects and significantly improving patient compliance; (3) local targeting and retention ability: particularly suitable for mucosal or local administration, these systems can be retained in specific cavities or tissues to exert long-lasting effects; (4) good biocompatibility and modifiability: commonly used materials such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), chitosan, and gelatin exhibit excellent biosafety, and their surfaces can be easily functionalized to enhance adhesion or responsiveness.

The fabrication of microscale delivery systems is well-established, benefiting from mature and scalable processing techniques. Their distinct particle size enables selective retention within target tissues, thereby conferring substantial biomedical application potential [[69], [70], [71], [72]]. In respiratory disease therapy, for instance, microscale systems can be delivered directly to the lungs via inhalation, achieving regional distribution and deposition. This targeted delivery significantly enhances local drug concentrations at pulmonary lesion sites while minimizing systemic exposure, thus improving therapeutic efficacy and safety [[73], [74], [75]]. In immunotherapy, microscale systems serve as highly efficient carriers for immune cell modulation. Notably, deformable microscale particles exhibit an enlarged contact interface with immune cells, facilitating efficient cellular uptake through multivalent interactions [76]. This capability allows for precise regulation of specific immune responses, highlighting their promising potential in infectious disease prevention and anti-tumor immune modulation.

Polyphenols can not only self-assemble into microscale carriers but also modify various microscale carriers as well as living carriers such as microorganisms and cells. Polyphenol-based microscale drug delivery systems enhance therapeutic efficacy and safety by maintaining stable plasma drug concentrations and reducing concentration fluctuations, thereby lowering toxicity and improving treatment outcomes. They also expand administration routes and broaden indications, enabling effective delivery of drugs with extremely low oral bioavailability and macromolecular biologics through novel pathways (Fig. 3). Furthermore, they facilitate personalized medicine by allowing the design of carriers with different release rates and responsive properties, providing a technological foundation for precision medicine. As a key platform in formulation science, polyphenol-based microscale drug delivery systems continue to advance drug therapy toward greater efficiency, prolonged effects, reduced toxicity, and intelligent responsiveness (Table 2).

Polyphenol coacervate droplets

Coacervate refer to dynamic droplet-like substances formed by the liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) of macromolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids, similar to membraneless organelles [[77], [78], [79]]. Essentially, they are concentrated liquid phase compartments spontaneously formed by non-covalent interactions among biological macromolecules under specific conditions, resembling spherical droplets [[80], [81], [82]]. These coacervate droplets are widely present in cells, such as nucleoli, stress granules, and P-bodies, and their formation depends on multivalent interactions among molecules and the regulation of external environments (such as pH, ion concentration, and temperature). Coacervate droplets can rapidly assemble or disassemble in response to cellular signals, enabling precise spatiotemporal regulation of essential biological processes, including gene expression, signal transduction, and metabolism [[83], [84], [85]]. The study of coacervate droplets provides a breakthrough for understanding disease mechanisms (abnormal assembly or disassembly of coacervate droplets) and opening new avenues for drug discovery. Coacervate droplets constructed through chemical methods can be used for drug delivery, biosensing, functional material synthesis, and the study of protocell models [[86], [87], [88], [89], [90]].

Coacervate droplets possess internal viscoelasticity and molecular crowding characteristics, which can recruit and enrich molecules with different physicochemical properties, thus having the potential to serve as delivery carriers [[91], [92], [93]]. Various non-covalent interactions between molecules (such as electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, cation-π interactions, etc.) can drive the formation of coacervate droplets [[94], [95], [96]]. Coacervate droplets consisting of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes are primarily driven by electrostatic interactions [97]. Polyelectrolyte-based coacervate droplets tend to disassemble in salt solutions, whereas polyphenol-based coacervate droplets remain relatively stable under the same conditions. Moreover, due to the inherent strong charges of polyelectrolytes, these raw materials exhibit a certain level of toxicity, potentially leading to risk factors such as hemolysis and inflammatory responses. In contrast, polyphenol-based coacervate droplets possess excellent biocompatibility and meet biosafety standards. Overall, polyphenol-based coacervate droplets are safer and more suitable for biomedical applications than polyelectrolyte-based coacervate droplets.

Polyphenols alone can form simple coacervate droplets, and they can also form complex coacervate droplets with other materials (Fig. 4). The unique chemical structure of polyphenols enables them to undergo LLPS through hydrogen bonding, coordination bonding, or hydrophobic interactions (Table 3). Furthermore, the size and morphology of coacervate droplets can be modulated by adjusting the concentration and ratio. Therefore, using polyphenols to construct coacervate droplets represents a promising new strategy.

The lipophilic curcumin underwent autonomous LLPS to form coacervate droplets when it was diluted from organic solvents into aqueous solutions [98]. The interior of curcumin coacervate droplets could encapsulate various biomacromolecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids, enabling their efficient delivery into the cytoplasm. Polyphenols, such as TA and EGCG, act as hydrogen bond donors, while polymers like polyethylene glycol (PEG) and Pluronic F68 serve as hydrogen bond acceptors [99,102]. Their pairing induces LLPS, leading to the formation of coacervate droplets. These droplets exhibit growth characteristics, with their size continuously increasing over time. Professor Cui's team [100] has demonstrated that PEG and TA could form polyphenol-based coacervate droplets in an aqueous environment. Adjusting the ratio and concentration of PEG to TA could modulate the coacervation and determine the morphology and size of resulting coacervates. The concentration and proportion of the constituent materials were crucial factors influencing the LLPS process. Increasing the concentration and proportion of TA transforms the initially transparent solution into isotropic polyphenol coacervate droplets. Wang et al. [104] employed EGCG, PEG, and europium ions (Eu3+) to form coacervate droplets, which were then rapidly transformed into an adhesive glue via centrifugation. This glue exhibited robust bio-adhesion, ensuring tight interfacial contact and enabling the sustained release of polyphenol coacervate droplets. Consequently, it could reduce inflammation at the site of damage, prevent bacterial infection, accelerate mucosal recovery, thereby achieving an effective therapeutic approach for chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis. Furthermore, both polyphenols themselves and MPNs could be used to modify the surface of various coacervate droplets, enhancing their stability and imparting additional functionalities [106,107]. For instance, MPNs formed by iron ions (Fe3+) and TA could modify the surfaces of peptide coacervate droplets to control their size [105].

Due to their unique microscale dimensions, flexibility, and internal fluidity, coacervate droplets have become an ideal model for studying protocells. Professor Chen's team [103] utilized coacervate droplets formed by TA, PEG, and polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) as templates to develop fluidic membrane-bound protocells (FMPs). The chemical structure of TA contained multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups, which played a key role in forming hydrogen-bond networks. Upon the introduction of PVP molecules, a dense hydrogen-bond network formed on the surface of the coacervate droplets, resulting in FMPs with a stable fluidic membrane (Fig. 5A). This process altered the balance of hydrogen-bond interactions, prompting the surface components of the coacervate droplets to respond to osmotic pressure changes and undergo redistribution. The fluidic membrane not only effectively prevented the aggregation of protocells but also exhibited membrane fluidity, which was achieved through the diffusive movement of molecules within the membrane (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, FMPs possessed high flexibility and could spontaneously functionalize themselves by attracting functional nanomaterials to their surfaces via electrostatic interactions. Then, nanozymes were loaded onto the surface of the protocells, enabling cascade catalytic reactions. This successfully achieved the degradation of uric acid and the elimination of its byproduct, H2O2, offering a new potential strategy for the treatment of uric acid-related diseases. These FMPs, possessing structural stability, membrane fluidity, and functionalization capabilities, demonstrated broad prospects for biomedical applications. Xie et al. [101] formed microscale polyphenol-based coacervate droplets via the self-assembly of TA and tetra-armed PEG (4-arm-PEG), serving as protocell cytoplasm. These droplets exhibited cytoplasm-like properties that enabled efficient enrichment of biomolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids. By coating the surfaces of droplets with liposomes, structurally integrated protocells were constructed, presenting the fluidity similar to cell membranes. Under gravity or centrifugal force, these protocells further aggregated into macroscale prototissues, allowing for living cell encapsulation, injectable molding, and shape editing, precisely matching the mechanical properties of soft tissues. These prototissues demonstrated bioadhesion, promotion of wound healing and long-term retention. Through this multilevel strategy encompassing molecular engineering, membrane system reconstruction, and intelligent tissue assembly, this approach deeply integrated the physicochemical characteristics of polyphenols with biomimetic structures. The microscale coacervate droplets mimicked the molecular enrichment function of cytoplasm, the liposome layers enabled interfacial regulation, and the macroscale prototissues integrated bioactivity via three-dimensional modulation. This engineered biomimetic platform offered promising potential for living cell delivery, organoid construction and regenerative medicine.

Polyphenol pickering emulsions

Emulsions, leveraging their unique biphasic structure (typically consisting of aqueous and oil phases), can efficiently encapsulate drugs with diverse physicochemical properties, demonstrating significant advantages in the field of drug delivery, particularly in transdermal absorption formulations [[109], [110], [111]]. Composed of two immiscible phases, one of which is dispersed as droplets within the other, emulsions allow various drugs to be encapsulated in either the aqueous or oil phase [[112], [113], [114]]. This not only enhances drug loading capacity, solubility, and bioavailability but also enables controlled drug release [115]. Furthermore, due to their high affinity for the skin, emulsions exhibit distinct advantages in improving the permeability and diffusion of active ingredients [116]. However, most emulsions suffer from insufficient stability and adhesion. Traditional emulsions typically rely on emulsifiers to enhance system stability, utilizing non-ionic surfactants (such as Tween and Span) or polymeric emulsifiers (like gelatin and gum arabic) to maintain stability. Nevertheless, such chemically synthetic surfactants are prone to causing biological toxicity, and their thermodynamic instability makes traditional emulsions highly susceptible to environmental influences, often failing to meet the demands of drug delivery.

Pickering emulsions, which employ solid particles in place of traditional surfactants, offer advantages such as low toxicity and environmental friendliness [[117], [118], [119]]. Compared to conventional emulsions, the formation of a dense physical protective layer by solid particles at the oil-water interface endows Pickering emulsions with exceptional physical stability, effectively preventing flocculation and coalescence of the emulsion [120]. The stabilization mechanism of Pickering emulsions lies in the wettability of solid particles toward the oil and water phases, where rigid solid particles adsorb at the interface between two phases to act as stabilizing layers [121]. However, currently used solid particles such as silica nanoparticles and polymer nanoparticles exhibit poor emulsifying ability and relatively limited functionality. In recent years, natural polyphenols have demonstrated significant advantages in the construction and functionalization of Pickering emulsions due to their unique physicochemical properties and interfacial characteristics. Polyphenols interact with the oil phase through hydrophobic aromatic rings, while hydrophilic phenolic hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bond networks with the water phase, leading to self-assembly into dense particulate layers at the oil-water interface. Through multiple interactions, polyphenols achieve long-term stabilization of Pickering emulsions, and the dynamic particulate layers further enhance the sustained release capability. Compared with conventional solid particles, polyphenol-stabilized Pickering emulsions offer multiple advantages, including stability, safety, and low cost.

Lipophilic polyphenol (such as curcumin and quercetin) nanoparticles with contact angles greater than 90° are typically dispersed in the oil phase [122]. For instance, during the stabilization of the oil-water interface by curcumin nanocrystal dispersions, the particles initially tended to dissolve in the oil phase. Upon reaching saturation, the remaining curcumin nanocrystals formed a shell-like structure that encapsulated the oil droplets [123]. In contrast, hydrophilic polyphenol nanoparticles with contact angles less than 90° are first wetted by the aqueous phase. These particles are usually dispersed in the aqueous phase as the continuous phase, which are then emulsified with the oil phase to form stable oil-in-water (O/W) Pickering emulsions. For example, when rutin particles were dispersed in the aqueous phase to prepare an O/W emulsion, they effectively resisted droplet coalescence, resulting in uniform droplet size and long-term stability [124].

Polyphenols can adsorb at the oil-water interface in the form of crystalline particles or self-assembled nanoparticles to achieve interfacial stabilization. In terms of rheological regulation, polyphenol-stabilized Pickering emulsions demonstrate precisely tunable potential, offering unique advantages for local drug delivery. Hydrophilic tea polyphenol (TP) and hydrophobic curcumin are used to prepare self-assembled nanoparticles (TC NPs). During the homogenization process, these TC NPs could adsorb onto the surface of thyme essential oil (TEO) droplets to form stable Pickering emulsions [108]. As functional solid particles, TC NPs created a nucleus-shell structure that stabilized the Pickering emulsions and encapsulates volatile TEO. The injectable Pickering emulsions (TEO/TC PE) were utilized for infected wound treatment (Fig. 5C). They not only eradicated bacteria and biofilms but also promoted angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and skin regeneration, leading to effective wound healing. TEO/TC PE were entirely composed of plant-derived active ingredients and represented promising biosafe wound dressings.

Polyphenol microparticles or microspheres

Microparticles refer to regular and uniform particles with diameters ranging from 1 to 1000 μm, typically fabricated from natural or synthetic polymeric materials. They can encapsulate various active ingredients (including small molecule drugs, proteins, peptides, nucleic acids, etc.) within their core or adsorb them onto their surface [[125], [126], [127]]. By altering the composition, particle size, and structure of microparticles, precise control over drug loading, protection, and release behavior can be achieved, thereby optimizing therapeutic efficacy and reducing side effects [128]. The key features of microparticles lie in their exceptional sustained and controlled release capabilities. Through gradual degradation of the carrier material or drug diffusion mechanisms, they can release drugs continuously over periods ranging from several days to months, significantly improving medication compliance [129]. Their microscale size facilitates administration via inhalation or injection and enables passive or active targeting through local retention effects or surface functionalization, thereby increasing drug concentration at the disease site [130]. Furthermore, the microparticle matrix effectively shields encapsulated sensitive drugs (particularly biomacromolecules) from degradation, preserving their bioactivity.

Microparticle technology has been extensively applied in disease treatment [63]. In tumor chemotherapy, drug-loaded microspheres administered via arterial embolization can directly occlude tumor-feeding blood vessels while enabling sustained drug release, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy [131]. For chronic disease management, sustained-release microspheres, such as risperidone microspheres for schizophrenia and exenatide microspheres for diabetes, reduce dosing frequency and enhance the convenience and stability of long-term patient care [132]. In vaccination, microparticles serving as adjuvant carriers can mimic pathogen size, facilitating more efficient uptake by immune cells, and enable pulsatile antigen release to simulate booster immunization, thereby enhancing immune responses. Furthermore, in local treatments such as postoperative anti-adhesion, intravitreal injection for ophthalmic diseases and intraarticular drug delivery, microparticles can also provide prolonged local drug exposure while minimizing systemic adverse effects [[133], [134], [135]]. In summary, with their tailorable drug release kinetics and diverse administration routes, microparticles have become a vital bridge in modern pharmaceutics, connecting drug development with clinical needs [136]. They continue to evolve toward more intelligent and efficient formulations in alignment with the trends of personalized and precision medicine.

Polyphenols serve as basic building blocks for microparticles. TA, EGCG or quercetin could be formulated into microparticles via spray drying [[137], [138], [139]], while TA could be processed into size-tunable particles through cryo-gelation [140]. Zhao et al. [141] developed CAPN microspheres using GA and calcium ions (Ca2+), which effectively mitigated inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, and inhibited abdominal adhesion, thereby demonstrating significant potential in preventing postoperative adhesions (Fig. 6A). Moreover, polyphenols can be employed to modify the surface diverse microparticles, enhancing colloidal stability and imparting functionality [146]. Microparticles within the 1-5 μm size range are particularly suitable for pulmonary delivery in the treatment of respiratory diseases [147]. This optimal aerodynamic diameter enables efficient transport into the lower airways with minimal deposition in the upper respiratory tract, resulting in enhanced retention in bronchioles and alveoli. Consequently, specific local drug delivery minimizes systemic exposure and maximizes drug concentration at pathological sites, thus improving therapeutic outcomes. Professor Deng's research team [142] developed TA-functionalized hybrid microparticles (MZT), in which polyphenol-mediated interactions with bacterial membranes significantly enhanced bacterial capture efficiency. The synergistic action of pathogen sequestration and physical penetration enabled effective eradication of bacteria and biofilms. After inhalation administration, MZT precisely targeted the lung tissues infected with Klebsiella pneumoniae, reducing the bacterial load while simultaneously remodeling the immune microenvironment. This offered a novel biomimetic material strategy for the treatment of antibiotic-resistant pneumonia (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, MPNs could be coated onto various microparticles, enabling local applications in hemostasis and wound healing [148,149]. Intraarticular administration of MPN-coated microparticles has also shown promise in alleviating symptoms of osteoarthritis [150].

Polyphenol microcapsules

Microcapsules are hollow-structured capsules typically composed of semi-permeable or impermeable shells. The unique architectures enable the protection of encapsulated drugs from adverse external conditions while facilitating targeted and controlled drug release [[151], [152], [153], [154]]. Consequently, microencapsulation technology offers multiple advantages, including enhanced stability, masking of undesirable odors, reduced toxicity, and improved targeting capabilities, leading to widespread application in drug delivery, food industry, biotechnology, and cosmetics [155]. Microcapsules are usually fabricated through sequential deposition of diverse materials onto template surfaces [156]. Leveraging their distinctive molecular structures, polyphenols can bind to various substances such as small molecules, polymers, proteins and nucleic acids through dynamic non-covalent interactions. This robust binding capacity allows polyphenols to stably modify the surfaces of diverse templates and substrates, thereby broadening the application potential of microcapsules (Table 4).

Polyphenols can self-assemble into microcapsules via the template removal method. Wei et al. [157] fabricated a type of sustained-release capsules composed of pure polyphenols (PC), which autonomously formed microcapsules through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. These PC microcapsules were capable of scavenging ROS, suppressing the secretion of inflammatory factors, and remodeling the osteoarthritis microenvironment. In both mouse and canine models of osteoarthritis, the PC microcapsules demonstrated curative efficacy with a single dose. MPNs formed through coordination could be deposited onto various template surfaces, subsequently, these templates could be dissolved and removed via chemical methods, yielding a wide array of microcapsules [158]. Given the extensive variety and functionalities of both metal ions and polyphenols, microcapsules with tailored properties can be fabricated by selecting appropriate building blocks. The dynamic characteristics of metal-phenolic coordination bonding endow the microcapsules with environmental responsiveness, enabling their controlled assembly and disassembly in response to external stimuli such as pH changes, the addition of chelating agents, or light exposure. EGCG could form metal-phenolic microcapsules with either copper ions (Cu2+) or zinc ions (Zn2+) [159]. For instance, in a mouse model of hindlimb ischemia, Cu-EGCG microcapsules enabled sustained release of Cu2+, which promoted angiogenesis, scavenged excessive ROS, and downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby facilitating the restoration of blood flow and temperature in the ischemic limb [164]. This approach effectively prevented ischemia-induced limb gangrene and demonstrated significant potential for the treatment of peripheral arterial disease.

Polyphenols and polymers can also form stable and homogeneous microcapsules through hydrogen bonding. For example, Chen et al. [160] fabricated microcapsules composed of TA and PVP. The hydrogen bonds could be disrupted by ultrasonic treatment, triggering instantaneous release of the encapsulated drugs. Professor Caruso's group [143] proposed a simple and versatile strategy to optimize the delivery of proteins (Fig. 6C and D). They constructed a series of protein-polyphenol microcapsules by supramolecular assembly of various proteins with TA on calcium carbonate (CaCO3) particles, followed by removal of CaCO3 templates using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Cui et al. [161] employed ultrasonication to promote protein adsorption at the interface between perfluorohexane (PFH) droplets and water. Subsequently, polyphenols were introduced to cross-link with proteins, and following evaporation of volatile PFH, the protein-polyphenol microcapsules were successfully obtained. Nucleic acids and polyphenols can also form microcapsules with diverse structures and functions through non-covalent interactions. Professor Caruso's group [162] also fabricated supramolecular microcapsules using TA and DNA. Intramuscular administration of these TA-DNA microcapsules in mice elicited antigen-specific antibody production and robust T-cell responses without notable toxicity, highlighting their potential for enhancing immune responses via the co-delivery of antigen and adjuvant. These results underscored the potential of TA-DNA microcapsules as a versatile platform for vaccine delivery. Microcapsules formed by polyphenols and biomacromolecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids, not only preserve their bioactivity but also serve as efficient delivery carriers, offering innovative solutions for the targeted delivery and therapeutic application of biomacromolecules.

During the template-based preparation of microcapsules, it is inevitable to encounter issues of residual templates and damaged capsule shells when removing the templates. Professor Ge's team [144] proposed a one-step assembly method of MPNs on bovine serum albumin (BSA) microbubbles, enabling the construction of functional hollow MPNs microcapsules without dissolving the substrates (Fig. 6E). This approach fundamentally resolved a series of problems caused by removal of templates. Furthermore, the microcapsules prepared via this microbubble method simultaneously allowed efficient drug loading during the assembly process. For instance, microcapsules loaded with the chemotherapeutic drug doxorubicin could be further functionalized and applied in combined chemo-immunotherapy for cancer [163]. Polyphenols and polymers can also form microcapsules via template-free methods. Kong et al. [145] utilized a microfluidic approach to assemble TA and polyethylene oxide (PEO) into solvent-free microcapsules (Fig. 6F). The size and dispersity of the microcapsules could be precisely controlled by optimizing the vibrational frequency and flow rate.

Polyphenol-modified cells

Cell therapy is an innovative medical technology that utilizes living cells as the core therapeutic tool [165]. By extracting, modifying, expanding, or transplanting functional cells, it leverages their inherent biological capabilities (such as regeneration, differentiation, killing, or regulatory functions) to directly repair damaged tissues, modulate the immune systems, or precisely eliminate pathological cells [166]. This approach enables fundamental treatment and intervention for diseases. The primary advantages include precise targeting of abnormal cells and enhancement of immune function or regenerative capacity through gene editing technology, overcoming the limitations of conventional drugs [167]. In response to the multiple mechanisms of complex and refractory diseases, cell therapy exhibits multidimensional synergistic intervention capabilities that enable comprehensive modulation of pathological processes [168]. Living cells possess a distinctive homing capability, enabling their active migration to pathological sites, specific recognition of target cells, and dynamic responsiveness to biochemical signals within the disease microenvironment [169]. The targeting ability and functional specificity of engineered living cells enable precise therapeutic interventions for diseases [[170], [171], [172]]. Furthermore, these cells can survive in the body and exert sustained biological effects, functioning as a living drug reservoir with durable efficacy and even curative potential [173]. Cell therapy also offers distinct advantages in personalization and customization, allowing for the genetic modification of autologous cells to achieve tailored precision medicine [71,174,175]. With groundbreaking progress in this field, multiple products such as chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells and stem cells have been approved globally and are widely applied in areas including cancer, autoimmune diseases, genetic disorders, tissue engineering, and regenerative medicine [[176], [177], [178], [179]].

Currently, cell therapy primarily utilizes a range of therapeutic cells, including mammalian cells. In contrast to traditional drugs, therapeutic cells are inherently fragile and unstable, posing substantial challenges for their effective delivery [180]. Cell surface engineering plays a pivotal role in modulating cellular behaviors and biological processes, as well as overcoming intrinsic functional limitations [181]. Genetic engineering approaches are typically complex and costly, whereas chemical covalent modifications risk compromising cell viability. Consequently, surface modification of cells using biomaterials represents a more favorable alternative [[182], [183], [184]]. For example, cell surface engineering enables living cells to acquire additional biological functions while simultaneously constructing protective barriers against adverse physiological conditions [185].

The dense catechol or pyrogallol moieties in the molecular structure of polyphenols confer them excellent cytoadhesive properties and mild assembly processes, which make polyphenols ideal materials for cell encapsulation and surface functionalization. Professor Guo's team [186] proposed a polyphenol molecular engineering strategy in which polyphenols could bind various functional materials (such as small molecule drugs, enzymes, imaging probes, or nanoparticles) to form a series of cytoadhesive polyphenol-functionalized nanoarchitectures (cytoPNAs). Serving as a versatile toolbox for cell surface engineering, cytoPNAs offered the dual advantages of dynamic reversibility and modular design. Without compromising cell viability, they enabled the rapid anchoring of functional molecules onto cell surfaces, thereby equipping cells with novel capabilities. They also allowed for the regulation of cellular behaviors to meet diverse demands, facilitating the precise design and preparation of therapeutic cells tailored to specific disease characteristics. As a highly flexible strategy, it provides a simple and universal platform for rapidly constructing sophisticated cell delivery systems. To address the issue that cell therapies rely on time-consuming genetic engineering processes, Zhao et al. [187] established a universal platform for assembling nanocomplexes (bioactive substances such as proteins, DNA, RNA, and even viruses) on cell surfaces, constructing cell-based hybrid systems (referred to as Cellnex). This strategy could modify various cell types, including red blood cells, T cells, natural kill cells, and macrophages, for adoptive cell therapy (Fig. 7A). The engineered red blood cells (Erythrocytenex) achieved an 11-fold increased efficiency in delivering target proteins to the lungs in vivo. The engineered macrophages (Macrophagenex) enhanced the delivery efficiency of the checkpoint inhibitor antibody to tumor sites, significantly improving tumor killing effects. Similarly, regulatory T cells (Tregs) were functionalized on their surfaces to obtain Tregnex, which was used for treating alveolar bone defects in periodontitis. Tregnex enabled the precise release of therapeutics within the inflammatory microenvironment, exerting combined therapeutic effects [190]. They modulated macrophage phenotypes and promoted the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, and ultimately regulating the periodontal immune microenvironment and repairing alveolar bone.

Engineering modified cells using MPNs is a simple and efficient strategy that not only encapsulates and protects cells, but also enables disease microenvironment-responsive release of drugs and cells [191,192]. Professor Guo's team encapsulated tumor cells with MPNs composed of manganese ions (Mn2+) and TA to construct whole-cell vaccines, which prevented antigen loss and retained all tumor-associated antigens in the vaccine [193]. Simultaneously, the release of Mn2+ synergistically promoted the maturation of dendritic cells, further presenting tumor antigen signals to T cells. These vaccines enhanced the antitumor immune responses and suppressed tumor growth. MPNs can also encapsulate cell spheroids composed of multiple cells. For instance, when periodontal ligament stem cell spheroids were encapsulated with MPNs, a protective barrier with antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities formed on the surfaces of cell spheroids [194]. This approach not only preserved the stemness of stem cells but also effectively remodeled the inflammatory microenvironment, thereby providing a promising and efficient delivery strategy for repairing periodontal defects under inflammatory conditions.

Polyphenol-modified microbes

Bacteria, typically ranging in size from 0.5 to 5 μm, have emerged as a promising bio-derived delivery platform, offering innovative approaches for modern disease therapy by harnessing their unique biological characteristics (particularly the autonomous and intelligent behaviors inherent in living systems). With natural chemotactic abilities, bacteria can directionally migrate toward and accumulate in specific pathological sites, such as hypoxic tumor microenvironments or inflamed tissues [195]. Once localized, they are capable of colonizing and proliferating within the lesion sites, enabling sustained in situ drug release over time. Bacterial delivery systems offer multiple advantages over traditional nanoscale systems [196]. They can actively penetrate physiological barriers and migrate into deep lesion sites, thereby significantly enhancing local drug concentrations while reducing systemic toxicity [197]. Moreover, certain probiotics possess intrinsic immunomodulatory properties or the capacity to restore microenvironmental homeostasis, enabling synergistic therapeutic effects in combination with other therapeutics. The application of bacteria as smart living pharmaceutical factories in precision medicine is becoming increasingly promising, with the field advancing from fundamental proof-of-concept studies to engineered design and preclinical evaluation [[198], [199], [200]]. In tumor therapy, these bacteria can selectively target tumor tissues, kill cancer cells through toxin release or immune activation, and ameliorate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [201]. For intestinal diseases, probiotics enable direct drug delivery to the intestinal mucosa, offering a viable therapeutic strategy for inflammatory bowel diseases [[202], [203], [204]]. Additionally, they show considerable promise in vaccine delivery, as well as in the treatment of metabolic and infectious diseases.

Orally administered probiotics face significant challenges in traversing the harsh gastrointestinal environment due to gastric acid, bile acid, and other adverse conditions, which can impede their survival and prevent successful colonization at target sites [205]. In recent years, researchers have developed various strategies that encapsulate probiotics with protective layers, enhancing their resilience under unfavorable gastrointestinal conditions [206]. These engineered coatings enable probiotics to safely reach the intestines and promote effective colonization [207]. MPNs have been widely employed to modify various bioactive interfaces owing to their excellent adhesiveness, pH responsiveness, controllable size and rigidity, and straightforward fabrication. Professor Liu's team [208] developed a protective network on the surface of probiotics by TA and Fe3+, forming the layer of MPNs on Escherichia coli. The strong adhesive properties of TA could protect probiotics against adverse conditions induced by intestinal peristalsis, maintain their biological activity, and facilitate attachment to target colonization sites, thereby prolonging their residence time. These TA-based MPNs not only enhanced intestinal colonization of probiotics but also demonstrated robust adherence across diverse biological interfaces such as skin, nasal mucosa, trachea and vagina, enabling precise control over bacterial localization. Furthermore, these sticky coatings enabled probiotics to load various drugs. When orally administered, these drug-loaded probiotics demonstrated synergistic therapeutic effects in models of intestinal infections and colitis. The MPNs formed by TA and Fe3+ could also serve as nanoarmor, protecting probiotics from antibiotic damage [188]. These armored probiotics successfully colonized the gastrointestinal tract and significantly alleviate levofloxacin-induced antibiotic-associated diarrhea (Fig. 7B). Moreover, due to the flexible surface modification of MPNs, different coatings could be utilized to encode additional biological functions in bacteria, such as cell communication, population recognition, and microbiome modulation [209,210]. Professor Zhang's team employed gallium ions (Ga3+) and EGCG to coat probiotics. After oral administration, Ga3+ eradicated harmful microbiota in the tumor microenvironment, modulating microbiota-immune interactions to enhance immunotherapy for pancreatic cancer [211].

Various polyphenol nanoparticles can also modify probiotics [212]. Professor Guo's team [189] constructed the polyphenol-based supraparticle-enabled live bacterial biotherapeutics (supraLBT) by assembling EGCG with food-grade milk proteins into nanoparticles, forming a protective polyphenol particle layer on the surface of individual probiotic. This unique structural design allowed probiotics to remain viable under chemotherapeutic drug conditions, effectively addressing the critical challenge of low survival rates in traditional probiotic delivery for chemotherapy patients (Fig. 7C). These nanotechnology-engineered probiotics not only maintained intestinal microbiota balance but also significantly boosted the antitumor immune responses, offering a novel strategy to improve chemotherapy efficacy.

Microalgae are a group of protists that typically contain chloroplasts and possess photosynthetic capabilities, enabling them to utilize light energy to drive metabolism and synthesize target products. They are easily amenable to chemical modification, achieving active targeting. They can serve as bioreactors for disease treatment. Microalgae exhibit several notable advantages: self-sustenance and sustainability, as photosynthesis provides energy for their growth and synthesis; oxygen-producing capacity, which can ameliorate hypoxic microenvironments such as tumor tissues and enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy. Li et al. [213] utilized MPNs to construct pH-responsive ultrathin shells on the surface of microalgae. By mimicking spores, this approach enhanced the resilience of microalgae and enabled artificial regulation of their division and motility behaviors. Microalgae held promise as green living therapeutic systems.

Fungi possess robust yet readily modifiable cell walls with abundant moieties on the surfaces, making them suitable as scaffolds for drug adsorption or anchoring [215]. This allows for efficient loading of nucleic acids, proteins, and small molecule drugs. Their relatively large cell size (typically 5-10 μm) provides a higher drug-loading capacity, along with excellent safety and biodegradability [216]. Notably, yeast stands as the longest-established, extensively utilized, and highly industrialized fungal species in the food and pharmaceutical fields. Park et al. [214] coated individual Saccharomyces cerevisiae with an artificial shell composed of MPNs. This shell provided a protective barrier without compromising biological activity, shielding the yeast from ultraviolet radiation, enzymatic lysis, and invasion by external agents. The MPNs on the yeast could be controllably degraded on demand. Although cell division was inhibited in the presence of the shell, it fully resumed upon shell degradation. This method extended the therapeutic applications of yeast.

Summary and perspectives

Natural polyphenols, as an important class of bioactive molecules, have demonstrated significant potential in drug delivery, including traditional small-molecule drugs, proteins and peptides, nucleic acids, bioimaging agents, and therapeutic cells [[217], [218], [219]]. Through non-covalent interactions or dynamic covalent bonding, polyphenols can serve as structural and functional synthons for constructing functional biomaterials, thereby forming diverse delivery carriers ranging from the nanoscale to the microscale and even macroscale. These carriers not only efficiently load various drug molecules but also acquire additional biological functions due to the inherent anticancer, antioxidant, and antibacterial activities of polyphenols. Moreover, the introduction of dynamic bonds such as metal-phenol coordination bonding or catechol-boronic ester bonding into these systems allows on-demand regulation of carrier stability and responsive behaviors, offering strong support for developing smart nanomaterials with multimodal imaging, multi-stimulus responsiveness, and combined therapeutic functions [220]. Furthermore, polyphenols can self-assemble with small molecule drugs, proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids through covalent or non-covalent interactions, forming supramolecular delivery systems with diverse structures, providing a universal and flexible approach for constructing intelligent drug delivery platform [221].

Despite a series of encouraging research advances, polyphenol-based drug delivery systems remain in their early developmental stage. Several critical issues must be resolved before they can advance toward clinical translation.

(1)Storage stability: polyphenols are prone to oxidation in air or under alkaline conditions, forming oligomers or polymers. This instability poses challenges for carrier preparation, activity preservation, and long-term storage, which must be carefully addressed in material design and formulation processes.

In summary, the unique chemical properties and bioactivities of natural polyphenols hold significant promise for developing novel drug delivery systems. Future research should focus on overcoming current bottlenecks related to stability, specificity, and raw material sourcing, and systematically elucidating the structure-activity relationships between polyphenol structures and biological functions. This will guide the rational design and clinical translation of next-generation intelligent delivery systems. In terms of carrier design, emerging design strategies and interdisciplinary approaches should be integrated [222]. Smart carriers responsive to the pathological microenvironment can be constructed by combining dynamic covalent chemistry and supramolecular chemistry. Molecular dynamics simulation and machine learning can accelerate carrier screening, while synthetic biology approaches enable high-yield production of specific polyphenol molecules. Furthermore, microfluidics, 3D bioprinting, and artificial intelligence platforms can be employed to advance personalized, standardized, and large-scale production of these systems [[223], [224], [225]]. In terms of translational application, the use of polyphenol-based microscale delivery systems should be extended to cutting-edge areas such as mRNA vaccines, neurotherapeutic drugs, implantable drug depots, and wearable bioelectronic devices [[226], [227], [228]]. Furthermore, low-cost extraction and purification processes for polyphenols derived from plant waste residues should be developed for the production of nutraceuticals or therapeutic drugs, thereby meeting practical industrial demands while embracing the concept of circular economy. Through these explicit and forward-looking research blueprints, natural polyphenols are poised to build a solid bridge between fundamental research and clinical translation, thereby fully unleashing their immense potential in biomedical field.
