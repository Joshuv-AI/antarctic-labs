Quantitative reconstruction and sedimentary response of a Jurassic paleodrainage system
Authors: Na Li, Lizhi Shi, Ke Ren, Jun Matsushima, Xiao Hu, Jinbiao Zhu, Longlong Liu, Zhongqiang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluvial facies are excellent reservoirs for fluids, critical for both carbon sequestration and hydrocarbon exploration. This study focuses on the Lower Jurassic fluvial deposits on the eastern flank of the Chepaizi Uplift in the Junggar Basin, addressing two key questions: (1) How to reconstruct the morphology of ancient river channels? (2) How to calculate the parameters of these channels (widths, lengths, and distribution ranges)? To achieve these objectives, we developed a quantitative paleodrainage reconstruction model guided by seismic sedimentology and GIS analyses, integrating 3D seismic data and a modern fluvial analog from Daqing Mountain region in Inner Mongolia. Using this method, we reconstructed the Lower Jurassic paleodrainage system and estimated key paleochannel parameters. Key results include: (1) V-shaped valleys are associated with fluvial erosion and high-energy depositional environments, whereas U-shaped and W-shaped valleys develop in low-energy settings. (2) The Lower Jurassic strata are divided into source, transport, and sink units, further subdivided into four sub-source-to-sink zones (P1-P4). (3) Quantitative analysis reveals that the widths of the paleochannels in the study area range from 330 to 1520 m, with lengths ranging from 2 to 9 km. The southern system features wider paleochannels and a greater sediment transport capacity. The areas of the piedmont alluvial fans in P1-P4 are 25.63 km2, 37.69 km2, 17.79 km2, and 41.43 km2, respectively. U-shaped or W-shaped channels are associated with larger depositional areas in these alluvial fans. This study provides practical guidance for hydrocarbon exploration in unexplored Jurassic areas of the Chepaizi Uplift. Meanwhile, the methodology can be applied to reconstructing paleodrainage systems in other terrestrial basins.

Introduction

Strata, as recorders of Earth’s history, preserve various important geological bodies such as channels, deltas, and alluvial fans1,2. These geological bodies all belong to the fluvial sedimentary system1–3, which holds significant research value due to its excellent reservoir properties4. Paleodrainage reconstruction is crucial for analyzing fluvial depositional systems1,2,3,5 (Syvitski et al. 200738). This process is fundamentally underpinned by the source-to-sink (S2S) framework, which is used to study the origin, transport, and deposition of sediments from source-to-sink regions6–9. In S2S systems, materials weathered from source areas are transported via rivers, glaciers, or ocean currents to sink regions, providing critical insights into depositional processes, surface evolution, and the influences of tectonics and climate10–14. In petroleum exploration, S2S analyses are also indispensable for reconstructing paleogeography, identifying sediment pathways, and predicting reservoir distributions4,15.

In this study, the S2S method is applied to reconstruct ancient Jurassic river channels in the Chepaizi Uplift. The Chepaizi Uplift in the Junggar Basin represents a significant hydrocarbon province. While Carboniferous strata serve as the primary source rocks and Paleozoic units host important reservoirs16, the majority of commercial discoveries, including the Chunfeng oilfield with reserves exceeding 100 million tons, occur in Cretaceous and younger reservoirs, particularly in the Lower Cretaceous Qingshuihe and Hutubihe formations17–19. However, as hydrocarbon resources in these shallower Cretaceous reservoirs become depleted, exploration has increasingly targeted the deeper Jurassic formations. Although previous studies on the Jurassic system of the Chepaizi Uplift have yielded substantial results17,20,21,22, several critical issues remain unresolved. This study focuses mainly on two key issues. The first is how to reconstruct ancient river channels. In this study, a process for reconstructing ancient river channels using modern visually identifiable river channels is established. The second key goal is to calculate the parameters of ancient river channels in the Jurassic strata of the study area, including channel widths, lengths, and distribution ranges.

Section "Geological setting" describes the geological setting of the study area, which includes the Chepaizi Uplift (an old landform) and the modern river systems. With respect to stratigraphic data that reveal old landforms, we detail the geographical locations, stratigraphic characteristics, climatic features, and seismic data of the Jurassic formations. For modern river systems, we describe their locations and selection criteria. Section "Materials and methods" provides data information and describes the methodology used in the study. Section "Analysis of contemporary fluvial systems" focuses on the analysis of sedimentary characteristics in modern river systems, including source areas, transport channels, and depositional fans, culminating in the development of an integrated methodology for paleodrainage reconstruction. On the basis of these results, we propose practical guidelines for reservoir predictions and the optimization of exploration strategies.

Geological setting

Geological setting of the chepaizi uplift

The study area for paleofluvial investigation is located on the eastern flank of the Chepaizi Uplift (Fig 1A), which is located along the western margin of the Junggar Basin in Xinjiang23,24. Geomorphologically, this region exhibits a distinct inverted triangular configuration (Fig 1B)16,17. The Chepaizi Uplift represents a tectonic uplift that formed during the Late Jurassic to Early Cretaceous25. The predominant rock types in this area include conglomerate, sandstone, siltstone, mudstone, and tuff. Specifically, the Jurassic strata in the study area are characterized by conglomerate, sandstone, mudstone, calcareous sandstone, argillaceous sandstone, siltstone, and fine sandstone. Within the Junggar Basin, the Jurassic system can be stratigraphically divided into three subunits: Lower, Middle, and Upper Jurassic, with an unconformable contact between the Jurassic and overlying Cretaceous systems. Although the Chepaizi Uplift exhibits a stratigraphic hiatus, it contains multiple formations, including the Lower Jurassic Badaowan Formation, Lower Jurassic Sangonghe Formation, Middle Jurassic Xishanyao Formation, Lower Cretaceous Qingshuihe Formation, Lower Cretaceous Hutubihe Formation, and Lower Cretaceous Shengjinkou Formation26. However, the eastern flank of the Chepaizi Uplift has a more pronounced Jurassic stratigraphic absence, with the Lower Jurassic Badaowan Formation being the most completely preserved unit in this area (Fig 1C).

Given the necessity of selecting modern sedimentary analogs for subsequent research, climatic consistency emerges as a critical selection criterion. Consequently, we conducted an extensive literature review on the paleoclimate of the study area. The review results28 indicate that the Xinjiang region experienced distinct climatic conditions during the Early and Late Jurassic: a warm and humid climate during the Lower Jurassic and a transition to an arid or semiarid climate during the Late Jurassic. This climatic dichotomy has left significant imprints in the geological record, directly influencing the evolution of sedimentary environments in the region. The warm and humid conditions of the Lower Jurassic likely resulted in higher precipitation rates and extensive vegetation cover, potentially reflected in the sedimentary record through water-rich deposits such as mudstones and fine sandstones. Conversely, the arid to semiarid climate of the Late Jurassic may have led to reduced precipitation and sparse vegetation, potentially manifested in the stratigraphic record through drier deposits such as sandstones and conglomerates. In selecting modern sedimentary analogs, we prioritize regions with climatic conditions that are analogous to those of the Early and Late Jurassic periods. This approach ensures that the selected modern cases are more representative and better aligned with the paleogeological history of the study area. These carefully chosen analogs provide deeper insights into the study, facilitating the understanding of the impacts of paleoclimate on sedimentary processes and rock characteristics and thereby contributing to a more comprehensive interpretation of various aspects of geological evolution.

Overview of contemporary fluvial systems

The necessity of conducting in-depth research on modern sediments can be articulated through four key aspects. First, the investigation of modern river systems facilitates a better understanding of ancient fluvial evolution processes. Modern rivers serve as natural analogs, and through comprehensive studies of their sedimentary characteristics, fluvial dynamics, and geomorphological features, we can gain profound insights into river behaviors and sedimentary processes. These insights provide crucial references for interpreting the information recorded in ancient fluvial strata, enabling more comprehensive reconstructions of paleofluvial morphology and evolutionary history. Second, studies of modern sediments allow for the verification of river systems that are identified through seismic data against natural principles. Field observations of modern rivers provide empirical data that, compared with river characteristics identified in seismic data, enable validation of geological models, thereby improving the interpretation accuracy of ancient sedimentary structures. Third, modern river observations can be conveniently conducted through satellite imagery, supplemented by field investigations when necessary. This direct observational approach offers intuitive information about sedimentary environments, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of modern sedimentary processes. Such first-hand observations provide valuable insights into fluvial morphology and geological characteristics. Fourth, the study of modern rivers provides empirical evidence for interpreting Jurassic fluvial systems in the study area. A detailed analysis of modern sediments yields data concerning sedimentary environments, sediment types, and stratigraphic features, thereby establishing a more reliable foundation for interpreting ancient sedimentary records. In conclusion, in-depth investigations of modern sediments enable us to profoundly understand ancient fluvial evolution, validate geological models, directly observe sedimentary environments, and provide empirical evidence, thereby advancing the comprehensive development of this research project.

The application of these principles ensures that the selected modern fluvial sediment analogs exhibit multidimensional similarity with the study area, thereby providing a more reliable and robust foundation for subsequent research. Through a comprehensive literature review and preliminary interpretation of seismic and geological data, we established five criteria specific to the Jurassic system of the Chepaizi Uplift area. As discussed in Section 2.1.1, the Early and Late Jurassic in the Chepaizi Uplift experienced different climatic conditions28. With respect to depositional environments, the presence of braided fan (alluvial fan) systems is supported by both core samples and seismic data. This evidence indicates the development of alluvial fan depositional systems, which are likely formed by rivers entering plains or lacustrine environments. In terms of hydrological variations, field observations of outcrops reveal significant fluctuations in water bodies rather than stable conditions. These variations, potentially involving periodic changes and water level fluctuations, significantly influenced ancient sedimentary processes. Evidence from seismic profiles suggests that the fluvial systems possessed distinct gradients. These flow characteristics, related to fluvial dynamic processes, may be associated with the formation and evolution of alluvial fans. Lithological studies indicate that the Jurassic strata in the Chepaizi Uplift primarily consist of sandstones and poorly rounded conglomerates. These rock types provide crucial insights into depositional environments, as they reflect fluvial transport and sedimentation characteristics. The presence of poorly rounded conglomerates in the Jurassic strata, characterized by large clast sizes and angular to sub-angular grain morphologies, implies short transport distances under high-energy, episodic flow conditions. This is consistent with flash flood deposition in alluvial fan environments, where sediment transport occurs during brief, intense discharge events29,30.

Guided by the five established selection principles, our methodology for identifying modern fluvial analogs commenced with river type classification using satellite imagery, with a focus on potential sites across China, Mongolia, Japan, and India. The geomorphological and sedimentological characteristics of the Daqing Mountain region present a complex and dynamic landscape shaped by diverse geological processes30. The area has a prominent mountain-basin topography characterized by steeply dipping fault-controlled mountain fronts transitioning into alluvial fan systems and fluvial plains29. Sedimentological analysis reveals a well-preserved stratigraphic sequence that consists of conglomerates, sandstones, and mudstones, reflecting alternating periods of tectonic activity and sedimentary deposition29,30. The geomorphology is dominated by deeply incised valleys, elevated plateaus, and extensive erosional surfaces, suggesting prolonged exposure to weathering and fluvial processes29. Sediment provenance studies indicate multiple source areas, with distinct facies associations corresponding to different depositional environments ranging from proximal alluvial fans to distal lacustrine settings29,30.

Materials and methods

Description of seismic data

In this study, active-source seismic data acquired through three-dimensional (3D) seismic exploration technology covering a total area of approximately 780 km2 are utilized. The data were provided by the Sinopec Xinjiang Xinchun Petroleum Development Co., Ltd. The seismic data were used for multiple purposes, including elevation data extractions, paleochannel analyses, source-to-sink parameter derivations, and sedimentary center area estimations. The 3D seismic data feature a time sampling interval of 2 ms and a bin size of 25 m * 25 m. The dataset consists of two adjacent survey areas, designated P1 and P6 (Fig 2B). The effective frequency bandwidth of the seismic data ranges from 15 to 75 Hz. The dominant frequencies are 25 Hz for the P1 dataset and 40 Hz for the P6 dataset (Fig 3A). Spectral analysis of the seismic data volumes yielded vertical resolutions of 23 m for the P1 block and 14.3 m for the P6 block, which were calculated on the basis of an average formation velocity of 2300 m/s in the target interval. These medium- to high-resolution seismic data sets are of sufficient quality for paleochannel identification and analysis. Methods

Our innovative methodology integrates modern fluvial analog studies with advanced paleodrainage reconstruction techniques, focusing on five key parameters, channel morphologies, slope gradients, mountain front positions, alluvial fan dimensions, and comprehensive depositional patterns, to elucidate the controlling factors of reservoir development. The research framework incorporates two distinct analytical models: (1) a simplified model based on the well-constrained modern fluvial system in the Daqing Mountain region, Inner Mongolia, which provides clearly measurable drainage parameters and well-preserved channel morphologies; and (2) a complex geological model derived from integrating Jurassic outcrops and subsurface data in the Chepaizi Uplift area of the Junggar Basin, accounting for variable sedimentation rates, diverse channel geometries, and complex lithological distributions.

Through comprehensive analysis, we successfully identified various channel morphologies and their associated sedimentary characteristics and elucidated the critical role of channel shapes and slope gradients in controlling reservoir distribution patterns. The application of source-to-sink analysis enables quantitative assessments of depositional scales and detailed reconstructions of the evolution of paleodrainage systems through space and time. These findings offer significant new perspectives on the formation mechanisms of reservoir development in sweet spots, with particular emphasis on channel incision zones and channel-lobe transition areas, which represent crucial targets for hydrocarbon exploration.

Feasibility of paleodrainage reconstruction using GIS technology

In paleo-hydrological studies, Geographic Information System Technology (GIS) offers powerful spatial analysis tools. Through the application of ArcGIS’s terrain analysis tools and geomorphometric algorithms, researchers can effectively identify and reconstruct the morphologies and spatial distributions of paleodrainage systems 31, (Hunegnaw et al., 202136).

The analytical process involves three primary data categories: geological data, hydrological data, and remote sensing data. Geological data play a crucial role in the analysis of paleodrainage systems. By integrating geological maps and stratigraphic data using GIS, researchers can determine the distributions and morphologies of paleodrainage systems and identify sediment types, depositional facies, and sedimentary environments, thereby inferring the characteristics and evolution of ancient hydrological systems32,33. Hydrological data constitute another essential data source for paleodrainage studies. With the use of hydrological tools provided by ArcGIS, researchers can delineate paleochannel locations and flow paths, providing insights into the dynamic characteristics of ancient drainage systems34. Furthermore, remote sensing data play a pivotal role in paleodrainage research. Through the analysis of satellite imagery, researchers can extract surface water features and geomorphological characteristics relevant to paleo-hydrological reconstruction32,35.

ArcGIS has emerged as an indispensable tool in paleodrainage reconstruction research, offering a comprehensive suite of geospatial analysis capabilities that enable researchers to effectively integrate, process, and visualize diverse datasets related to ancient hydrological systems31,32,36. These advanced functionalities significantly enhance our ability to reconstruct paleogeomorphological characteristics, decipher hydrological processes, and elucidate the evolutionary history of drainage systems, thereby contributing substantially to the advancement of paleo-hydrological studies35.

In the present study, GIS was employed primarily for the digital reconstruction and geomorphological feature extraction of both modern and paleoriver systems. Our methodological approach involves a systematic analytical workflow, which includes (1) pit filling analysis to remove topographic depressions, (2) flow direction analysis to determine water movement patterns, (3) flow accumulation analysis to quantify upstream contributing areas, (4) map algebra analysis for advanced spatial calculations, (5) stream link analysis to identify individual channel segments, (6) stream order classification based on Strahler’s method, (7) raster-to-vector conversion of drainage networks for precise feature representation, (8) basin analysis to delineate watershed boundaries, and (9) catchment area analysis to determine contributing zones. This integrated analytical process culminates in the generation of a comprehensive paleodrainage system distribution map, providing valuable insights into ancient hydrological patterns.

Under consistent source conditions, the outlet morphology significantly influences the areal extent of piedmont fan systems. Different outlet shapes lead to variations in fan morphology and dimensions. Notably, V-shaped outlets tend to form elongated fan systems, whereas U-shaped outlets are more conducive to the development of broader fan systems; however, this conclusion requires further validation through statistical analysis (Table 1). This phenomenon can be attributed to the differential impact of outlet morphology on flow velocity and sediment transport, which subsequently affects fan formation and expansion.

Furthermore, outlet types influence the sediment loads carried by fluvial systems. Certain outlet configurations may facilitate increased sediment transport, whereas others may result in reduced sediment accumulations in piedmont fans. Consequently, variations in outlet morphology affect both the sediment volume and thickness within fan systems, ultimately determining their areal extent.

Notably, in addition to the outlet morphology, several other factors influence the morphology and dimensions of piedmont fan systems, including the catchment area, slope gradient, and aspect (discussed in Section 3.1.2). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of fan formation and development requires the consideration of multiple factors. This study focuses primarily on the relationships between outlet types and piedmont fan characteristics. Source-to-sink system coupling analysis

When conducting source-to-sink system analyses, it is essential to consider additional parameters beyond valley morphologies, such as the slope gradients, geomorphological characteristics, and fluvial planform patterns. These hydrological parameters significantly influence the source-to-sink system dynamics in the study area, necessitating comprehensive statistical analysis.

The slope analysis focused on the gradients of selected river outlets. We generated a slope distribution map of the study area and classified the slopes into five categories using the Natural Breaks (Jenks) optimization algorithm, which minimizes within-class variance and maximizes between-class differences (Fig 10): 0.01°-6.55°, 6.55°-14.9°, 14.9°-23.4°, 23.4°-33.4°, and 33.4-79.5°. In this study, a slope of 14.9° served as the threshold, with slopes exceeding this value classified as steep and those below as gentle. This threshold is supported by geomorphological studies indicating that slopes >15° correspond to high-energy fluvial regimes with enhanced sediment bypass and incision37. Applying this classification to the 11 selected outlets revealed that five outlets (e.g., D1-1, D3-1, D4-1, D7-1, and D7-2) exhibit steep slopes, while the remaining outlets demonstrate gentle slopes (<14.9°). However, the outlet morphology significantly influences the piedmont depositional area. Under consistent source and channel conditions, wider U-shaped outlets demonstrate greater sediment transport capacities, resulting in larger piedmont fan areas and more expansive morphologies. We note that sediment transport capacity is a function of multiple interacting parameters including flow velocity, channel gradient, sediment grain size, and water discharge, not channel width alone. Investigations of source-channel-sink systems have significant implications for petroleum geology, geological resource assessment, environmental change studies, and paleoenvironmental reconstruction. To advance these research objectives, this study establishes a comprehensive paleodrainage reconstruction workflow through a detailed analysis of modern source-channel-sink systems. This workflow can be directly applied to four-dimensional (spatial and temporal) reconstruction of the Jurassic paleodrainage system in the Chepaizi Uplift.

Results and discussion for Jurassic drainage system analysis

Given the absence of satellite data for the ancient strata, we obtained depth information through stratigraphic interpretations and time-depth conversions. Time-depth conversions were performed using interval velocities derived from the 3D seismic processing workflow, with an average formation velocity of 2,300 m/s in the Jurassic target interval, as detailed in Section "Description of seismic data". These depth data were processed as inputs for GIS tools and were subsequently transformed into utilizable DEM data. The DEM for the Jurassic paleosurface was constructed using interpreted seismic horizons corresponding to the top of the Lower Jurassic Badaowan Formation, a regionally correlatable stratigraphic marker. Horizon picking was validated against well ties at five borehole locations within the study area, ensuring stratigraphic consistency. Depth conversion was performed using a velocity model calibrated to check-shot survey data from available wells. We note that the Badaowan Formation top represents a depositional surface that was subsequently buried without significant tectonic deformation, as evidenced by the parallel-stratified overlying Sangonghe Formation26. By utilizing the derived DEM data, we conducted a comprehensive paleodrainage analysis of the Jurassic strata in the study area and successfully reconstructed the paleodrainage system. The reconstructed paleo-hydrological parameters include the flow morphology (e.g., paleo-water depth, paleo-slope, paleo-geomorphology, and planform patterns) and hydrodynamic characteristics (sediment transport modes). Analysis of these parameters has provided profound insights into the evolutionary processes of the paleodrainage system.

Before presenting the Jurassic paleodrainage results, we note several important considerations when comparing modern observable fluvial systems with ancient, seismically-imaged counterparts. First, modern channel morphologies observed in the Daqing Mountain system represent single-event or short-timescale channel geometries, whereas seismically-imaged ancient channels often represent composite channel belts where multiple channel stories are merged at seismic resolution (~14-23 m vertically). Second, the geological record preserves a biased subset of the original fluvial architecture, with only the coarser channel-fill and abandonment facies typically retained2. Third, post-depositional compaction reduces apparent channel dimensions and slope angles in ancient systems compared to modern analogs8. Fourth, DEM resolution differs between modern satellite-derived data (12.5 m ALOS PALSAR) and seismic-derived paleo-DEM (25 m bin). These factors introduce systematic uncertainties that must be considered when extrapolating modern parameters to ancient reconstructions.

Method validation: comparison with modern analogs

To verify the reliability of our paleodrainage reconstruction methodology, we compared key parameters between the Jurassic paleochannels and the modern Daqing Mountain analogs. Channel sinuosity values are comparable between the two systems (Jurassic: 1.04-1.25; modern: 1.06-1.47), both indicating low-sinuosity fluvial systems. The ratio of fan area to source area (S2/S1) shows consistent scaling relationships: Jurassic values range from 0.70 to 2.54 (mean 1.44), while modern values range from 0.30 to 1.58 (mean 0.83). The association between W-shaped and U-shaped outlets with larger fan areas is consistent across both datasets. These quantitative consistencies support the validity of the modern analog approach for reconstructing ancient fluvial system parameters.

Uncertainty analysis and applicability boundaries

We assessed uncertainties in the modern analog approach for each Jurassic source-to-sink zone (P1-P4): (1) Seismic resolution uncertainty introduces vertical errors of ±12 m (P6 block, 40 Hz dominant frequency) to ±23 m (P1 block, 25 Hz), affecting paleochannel depth estimates. (2) Paleoslope calculations carry an estimated uncertainty of ±0.5° due to velocity model variability. (3) The preservation bias in ancient systems means that channel widths inferred from seismic data represent minimum estimates, as finer-grained channel margin facies may fall below seismic resolution. (4) Zone-specific confidence ratings are as follows: P2 and P4 (high confidence, well-imaged, multiple-outlet systems with clear fan geometries); P1 (moderate confidence, single outlet, steep slopes); P3 (lower confidence, limited seismic coverage, smaller fan area). These uncertainties should be considered when applying the reconstructed parameters to reservoir prediction and well placement decisions.

Conclusion

We reconstructed the Jurassic paleodrainage system of the Chepaizi Uplift by integrating source-to-sink system analysis with paleogeomorphological analysis, augmented by GIS and sedimentological methods. Valley morphologies (V-, U-, or W-shaped) and slope parameters serve as key indicators of depositional environments and reservoir types: V-shaped valleys record fluvial erosion in high-energy settings, whereas U- and W-shaped valleys develop in low energy environments and form more extensive piedmont fans. The Jurassic paleoalluvial landscape spans 17.79-41.43 km2; the Lower Jurassic hosts dense, well-developed paleochannel systems sourced from western and northwestern mountains, terminating in alluvial fans at the southeastern survey boundary with no seismic evidence of deltaic clinoforms. Channel incision zones and transition areas between channels and lobes represent reservoir sweet spots, and this systematic framework enables prediction of reservoir spatial distribution, connectivity, and thickness variations to guide exploration and well placement. The methodology is most applicable to tectonically stable basin margins with clearly preserved channel fill geometries and analogous modern and ancient climatic conditions; extension to highly deformed fold and thrust belts or deep burial settings requires further adaptation. Future work should integrate diverse geological data to build coupled models of paleodrainage, paleoenvironment, and tectonic evolution for more precise reservoir prediction.
