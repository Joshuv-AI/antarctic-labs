Governing autonomous AI in clinical care: the window is narrowing
Authors: Katherine W Eisenberg, Kavita K Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence systems in clinical care are moving beyond decision support into autonomous action—scheduling visits, initiating orders, and influencing insurance decisions such as prior authorization—yet, the governance infrastructure to deploy them safely is lagging. Primary care, where guideline-adherent care for a typical panel would require physicians to work more hours than there are in a day, is a natural deployment target and exemplifies this tension. While ample opportunities for artificial intelligence exist in almost every aspect of medicine, governance structures have not kept pace: accountability mechanisms, performance standards, and independent post-deployment monitoring remain weak, and the ability to oversee these systems often mirrors existing resource inequities. We propose that health systems and policymakers should risk-stratify autonomous actions by clinical consequence of error, tie procurement and value-based payment participation to minimum evaluation standards, and build shared governance infrastructure with sustainable funding so autonomous artificial intelligence narrows, rather than widens, existing care and equity gaps.

Medicine has spent much of the generative artificial intelligence (AI) era debating whether AI can outreason a physician. That question is now beside the point: AI is already acting, from billing to scheduling to medication refills, and companies are building entire business models around AI-enabled care. What matters is whether medicine has built the governance to deploy these systems safely as autonomy grows. It has not, and the window to do so is narrowing: increasingly sophisticated AI is becoming entrenched in deployed workflows and multi-year procurement contracts faster than federal rulemaking can respond, and each cycle that passes without conditioning payment on governance raises the cost of course correction.

The case for AI in primary care

Primary care exemplifies this tension. Shortages impact communities nationwide.1 Guideline-adherent care for a typical 2500-patient panel would require 26.7 hours per day.2 Physicians spend fewer than half their working hours on direct patient care because of administrative burden.3 Primary care also lends itself to AI-addressable workflows—screening, medication reconciliation, follow-up coordination—and is the wedge for virtual-first companies entering with AI-enabled triage, intake, and navigation. What looks administrative may, in practice, be clinical—scheduling can determine urgency, access, and triage. Medicine has long adopted new care models, from team-based care to telehealth, through iterative implementation, not pre-deployment evidence. What distinguishes autonomous AI is its scale, opacity, and speed of model change. Without defined benchmarks or post-deployment monitoring, clinicians and institutions have no reliable way to determine whether these systems meet existing standards.

The governance gap

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has established pathways for software that informs clinical decision-making, but the harder challenge is now emerging: how to oversee AI systems that initiate actions, adapt after deployment, and span clinical and administrative workflows. Accountability, error detection, and transparency into autonomous decisions remain unsettled.

A recent prospective feasibility study illustrates this.4 A conversational diagnostic AI performed pre-visit history-taking and differential generation in an ambulatory primary care clinic comparably to physicians but within a single academic medical center, leaving open how performance scales across populations, acuity, and practice contexts that define primary care. The standard of care is defined by clinical outcomes and professional consensus, not by the tools used to achieve them; without mature benchmarks, clinicians and institutions cannot reliably evaluate whether autonomous AI meets it.

The elements of effective AI governance have been described.5 Yet, pre-generative AI frameworks do not map cleanly onto autonomous systems: frontier models evolve faster than the evaluation literature can assess them, evidence for one version may not transfer to its successor, and post-deployment monitoring, when present, mostly relies on vendor self-reporting. Work through the Coalition for Health AI (CHAI), the Digital Medicine Society (DiMe), and others is defining best practices, but who funds evaluation and monitoring remains unclear. The U.S. government's current posture is not a comprehensive AI regulatory stance, but a more innovation-forward, sector-by-sector approach. FDA is advancing lifecycle expectations for AI-enabled medical devices; yet, broader federal policy is focused on reducing barriers to AI development and adoption, potentially leaving oversight uneven across agencies, states, health systems, and private standard-setters. The European Union has taken a different path: under the AI Act, many clinical AI applications may be treated as high-risk systems, with more explicit obligations for evaluation, transparency, human oversight, and post-market monitoring.

The governance gap is also an equity gap

Weak oversight risks widening existing disparities in who benefits from AI—and who bears its harms. Algorithmic bias is already embedded in clinical tools,6 and large language models alter clinical recommendations based on sociodemographic characteristics alone,7 dynamics likely to intensify under autonomous AI deployment. Health systems with strong informatics capacity can build internal oversight, but this capacity is unevenly distributed.5 Without deliberate policy intervention, AI governance capacity will track existing disparities, leaving the most vulnerable patients most exposed. Patients are largely absent from current AI governance, though precedents on institutional review boards (IRBs), hospital quality committees, and grantmaking panels offer models for incorporating community perspectives into oversight.

Near-term recommendations

First, risk-stratify autonomous AI actions by clinical consequence of error, not by surface category. A more durable approach asks what happens when the system errs. Actions with easily reversible, clinically minor errors—appointment reminders, drafting prior authorizations for human review, surfacing patients due for routine outreach—can tolerate higher autonomy. Actions whose errors propagate quickly, alter clinical trajectory, or are hard to detect should require a defined human checkpoint, even when nominally administrative, until governance matures. This is a starting point, not a definitive boundary.

Second, tie procurement and payment participation to minimum evaluation standards. Health systems should require performance data across diverse populations, audit trails, and accountability for system-initiated actions before purchase. But diverse data require deployment in diverse populations—a chicken-and-egg problem familiar from medical device regulation. A workable path is phased deployment: registry-based surveillance, IRB-overseen pilot rollouts, and tiered procurement requirements that scale with evidence. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology should consider conditioning value-based payment participation on baseline AI governance: pre-deployment evaluation, bias assessment, and post-deployment monitoring.

Third, build shared governance infrastructure with sustainable funding. Post-deployment monitoring and equity-focused evaluation cannot rest on vendor self-reporting or individual systems' informatics capacity. Drug and device post-market surveillance offers a starting point, not a complete template. FDA post-market activities are funded through a mix of federal appropriations and manufacturer user fees, but that system was designed for more bounded products and has well-known weaknesses, including uneven real-world monitoring, potential dependance on industry funding, and delays in detecting safety signals. AI systems pose a harder challenge because they can adapt over time, operate across clinical and administrative workflows, and diffuse quickly across health systems. A realistic AI governance model will therefore likely need hybrid financing: public investment, vendor fees scaled by risk and deployment, and pooled healthcare stakeholder contributions. In a federal environment oriented toward AI innovation and lighter-touch oversight, shared infrastructure should be built now by health systems, professional societies, states, and standards bodies rather than deferred until a comprehensive federal regime emerges.

The lesson of the EHR era

The electronic health record era showed what happens when clinical systems are deployed without adequate governance: tools meant to improve safety and efficiency became drivers of clinician burnout and workflow fragmentation, problems we now try to remedy with another technology layer. Autonomous AI is arriving faster, with less regulatory friction and higher stakes. The question is not whether to adopt it, but who shapes deployment. Clinical leaders, health systems, and policymakers must define responsible deployment before the market does.
