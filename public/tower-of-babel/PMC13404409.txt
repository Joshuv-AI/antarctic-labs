Intrinsic tumor cell line immunogenicity may drive CAR-independent T cell responses and confound CAR T cell preclinical modeling
Authors: Michael K Sheng, Wahed A Firoz, Kelly Loi, Logan V Vick, Craig P Collins, Ryan N Nielsen, Jessica L Schmeling, Shuchi Gulati, Sean J Judge, Robert J Canter, Arta M Monjazeb, Anthony E Zamora, William J Murphy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells are highly potent therapies approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for several hematological malignancies. However, efficacy remains variable due to resistance, antigen modulation, systemic toxicities, and relapse. Developing improved CAR T cell therapies relies on preclinical models that accurately predict clinical outcomes. For this purpose, immortalized tumor cell lines, such as the Burkitt’s lymphoma line Raji, are commonly used.MethodsCD19-targeting CAR (CAR19) and non-transduced (NT) human T cells were co-cultured with target cell lines in vitro to assess T cell cytotoxicity, cytokine production, activation, and proliferation. Major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and II blocking antibodies and TCR knockout (KO) T cells were used to validate T cell receptor interactions. In vivo, Raji wild-type (wt) or CD19 KO tumor-bearing mice were treated with CAR19 or NT T cells, and survival, tumor burden, serum cytokines, and T cell phenotype in tissues were analyzed. Statistical analyses using either paired/paired ratio/unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests, two-way analysis of variance, and log-rank (Mantel-Cox) tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.ResultsWe report that T cells generated from multiple different donors exhibit not only strong CAR-mediated cytotoxicity against CD19-expressing Raji WT cells but also, unexpectedly, CAR-independent reactivity against Raji CD19 KO cells. This reactivity was observed both in vitro and in vivo, and was associated with pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion and upregulation of T cell activation markers, consistent with endogenous T cell receptor (TCR) engagement. NT T cells displayed similar activation, cytotoxic, and cytokine responses against Raji cells. These effects failed to occur using other B cell leukemia/lymphoma or solid tumor cell lines in vitro. Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations contributed to this alloreactive response, which could be attenuated by blockade of MHC class I or II, highlighting a TCR:MHC-dependent mechanism.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that certain tumor lines can elicit strong allogeneic T cell responses independent of CAR specificity, revealing an important limitation of commonly used preclinical models and highlighting the potential for false-positive assessments of CAR T cell efficacy. Furthermore, these results highlight the importance of validating tumor models for alloreactivity prior to use in preclinical testing.

Alloreactive T cell responses are not normally considered when interpreting CAR T cell efficacy data.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that the human Burkitt’s lymphoma cell line, Raji, possesses intrinsic characteristics that elicit broad donor T cell alloreactivity independent of CAR specificity. This alloreactivity was demonstrated by both CD19-targeting CAR (CAR19) and non-transduced T cells exhibiting cytotoxicity, cytokine production, activation, and proliferation against Raji CD19 KO cells in vitro and in vivo, with greater alloreactivity in the CAR19-positive population. Furthermore, this observed anti-tumor activity was mediated by major histocompatibility complexes class I and II interactions with CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, respectively.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings highlight a key limitation in applying Raji-based preclinical models, as they may lead to false-positive or overexaggerated favorable assessments of CAR T cell efficacy. Therefore, this study underscores the need for careful selection and proper validation of tumor models in CAR T cell and immunotherapy research to account for TCR-mediated alloreactivity.

Introduction

In chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy, T cells are typically collected autologously from a patient and genetically modified to express an artificial receptor, termed a ‘CAR’, that enables antigen-specific recognition and subsequent activation and cytotoxicity against target tumor cells. This approach has profoundly revolutionized cancer immunotherapy, achieving unprecedented clinical success in hematological malignancies, and providing a highly curative alternative to conventional cancer treatments. Large-scale international clinical trials, including ELIANA/ENSIGN, ELARA, and ZUMA-1, have routinely reported objective response rates eclipsing 80%, complete response rates exceeding 60%, and 12-month survival rates surpassing 50% in patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.1–4

Despite clinical success, CAR T cell therapy still faces significant limitations. Not all patients respond favorably due to key challenges such as systemic toxicities like cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, antigen modulation, poor T cell persistence, and an immunosuppressive and hypoxic tumor microenvironment that characterizes many cancers.5–7 Also, CAR T cells have demonstrated limited efficacy in solid tumors.8 These limitations have required the development of novel next-generation CAR T cell therapies with enhanced persistence and anti-tumor activity in hostile tumor environments, such as armored CAR T cells and T cells redirected for universal cytokine-mediated killing.9 10 However, these therapies must be validated rigorously in preclinical testing prior to clinical translation.

Preclinical evaluation of CAR T cell efficacy typically relies heavily on using antigen-expressing tumor cell lines as targets for in vitro and in vivo studies.11 In studies of CAR T cells directed against lymphoma, the Burkitt’s lymphoma cell line Raji is commonly used due to its distinction of being the first continuous hematopoietic cell line and high expression of several CAR-targeted antigens such as CD19, CD20, and CD22.12 13

In our studies using CD19-targeting CAR (CAR19) T cells, we unexpectedly observed CAR-independent T cell reactivity against Raji cells lacking CD19 expression (Raji CD19 KO). These effects were also seen in non-transduced (NT) T cells against Raji cells regardless of CD19 expression. These responses bore similarity to T cell receptor (TCR)-mediated activation, raising the possibility that Raji cells possess intrinsic properties that elicit broad T cell alloreactive responses. Such responses could confound the interpretation of CAR T cell preclinical efficacy data. In this study, we investigate the extent to which these allogeneic responses occur against Raji cells in vitro and in vivo, determine whether this phenomenon is observed with other tumor cell lines, and explore the potential molecular pathways involved. Our findings highlight the need for careful consideration when using Raji cells to evaluate CAR T cell function in preclinical modeling.

Methods

Tumor cell lines

The firefly luciferase (ffLuc) expressing wild-type (WT) and CD19 knockout (KO) Raji and NALM6 lines were gifted by Caring Cross. Raji CD19 KO cells were validated by staining with FITC anti-human CD19 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 302206) and analyzing with flow cytometry. Nuclight Green (Sartorius, Cat. #: 4475) expression was generated by lentivirus transduction (multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3) with 5 µg/mL polybrene and selected in media containing 1 µg/mL puromycin (Gibco, Cat. #: A1113803). K562 (ATCC, Cat. #: CCL-243) and A-673 (ATCC, Cat. #: CRL-1598) cells were purchased from ATCC. SKMEL-28 cells were provided by Dr. Jose Guevara (Moffitt Cancer Center). Granta-519, Ramos, and REH cells were received from Dr. Joseph Tuscano (UC Davis School of Medicine). Raji, K562, Granta-519, NALM6, Ramos, and REH cell lines were cultured in 90% RPMI 1640 without glutamine (Gibco, Cat. #: 21870084), 10% Nu-Serum (Corning, Cat. #: 355100), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, Cat. #: 25030081), 1x MEM non-essential amino acids (Gibco, Cat. #: 11140050), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, Cat. #: 11360070), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Cat. #: 15140122), 1 mM HEPES (Gibco, Cat. #: 15630080). SKMEL-28 and A-673 cells were cultured in 90% Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Cat. #: 11995081), 10% Nu-Serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1 mM HEPES. Cells were passaged twice a week to maintain viability (>90%).

Tumor cell line phenotyping

For phenotyping, tumor cell lines were incubated with Zombie NIR viability dye, Fc blocked with a 1:20 dilution of human TruStain FcX (BioLegend, Cat. #: 422302), surface stained (FITC anti-human CD19, PE anti-human HLA-A, B, C (BioLegend, Cat. #: 311406), APC anti-human HLA-DR, DP, DQ (BioLegend, Cat. #: 361714), PE/Cyanine7 anti-human CD70 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 355112), Brilliant Violet 711 anti-human CD80 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 305236), PE anti-human CD86 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 305438), APC anti-human CD137L (BioLegend, Cat. #: 311506), FITC Mouse IgG1, κ Isotype Ctrl Antibody (BioLegend, Cat. #: 400108), PE Mouse IgG2a, κ Isotype Ctrl (BioLegend, Cat. #: 400214), APC Mouse IgG2a, κ Isotype Ctrl (BioLegend, Cat. #: 400220), PE-Cy7 Mouse IgG1 κ Isotype Control (BD, Cat. #: 557872), Brilliant Violet 711 Mouse IgG1, κ Isotype Ctrl (BioLegend, Cat. #: 400168), PE Mouse IgG2b, κ Isotype Control (BD, Cat. #: 559529), APC Mouse IgG1, κ Isotype Ctrl (BioLegend, Cat. #: 400122)) and analyzed by flow cytometry. For IFN-γ pretreatment, tumor cell lines were incubated in respective culture media for 48 hours with 100 ng/mL of recombinant human IFN-γ (R&D, Cat. #: 285-IF).

Mice

Female NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice (Jackson Laboratory, Cat. #005557) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the UC Davis Institute for Regenerative Cures, an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care accredited facility. Animals were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food (NIH-31 chow) and water.

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cell/T cell isolation

Leukocyte reduction chambers from anonymous healthy adult donors were procured from Vitalant. Available donor demographic information is listed in online supplemental tables 1 and 2. Blood was diluted 1:1 with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS) (Gibco, Cat. #: 14190144) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Cat. #: 26140079), placed into 50 mL SepMate peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation tubes (Stemcell Technologies, Cat. #: 85460) prefilled with lymphocyte separation media (Corning, Cat. #: 25-072 CV), and centrifuged at 1200 x g for 10 min at room temperature. The top layer containing PBMCs was poured into a new 50 mL conical tube and washed with D-PBS containing 2% FBS and treated with 1 x RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend, Cat. #: 422401). T cells were isolated from PBMCs using a MagniSort human T cell enrichment kit (Invitrogen, Catalog #: 8804-6810-74) and cryopreserved in 90% FBS and 10% DMSO at −80°C or liquid nitrogen.

Human CAR and NT T cell generation, expansion, and validation

Isolated T cells were thawed on day 0, resuspended to 1×10∧6 cells/mL in TexMACS GMP medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. #: 170-076-307) containing 200 IU/mL rhIL2 (Teceleukin), and activated with a 1:20 dilution of TransAct (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. #: 200-076-204). For CAR19 transduction, on day 1, T cells were transduced with lentivirus encoding a CAR targeting hCD19 (from Caring Cross) at an MOI of 10. This construct consists of a CD19-targeting single-chain variable fragment, a CD8 hinge domain, a CD8 transmembrane domain along with 4-1BB and CD3ζ intracellular signaling domains. On day 3, T cells were collected, centrifuged at 1200 RPM for 5 min, media decanted, and resuspended to a concentration of 0.25×10∧6 cells/mL in TexMACS containing 200 IU/mL rhIL2. On days 6, 8, and 10, T cells were resuspended to 0.5×10∧6 cells/mL in TexMACS containing 200 IU/mL rhIL2. Transduction efficiency was validated by primary staining and flow cytometry with recombinant human CD19-Fc protein (R&D Systems, Cat. #: 9269 CD-050) followed by secondary staining with Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647) anti-human Fc (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat. #: 109-606-098) and used for experiments between days 10 and 12. For TYRP1 CAR, T cells were activated in TexMACS containing 200 IU/mL rhIL2 and a 1:20 dilution of TransAct at 1×10∧6 cells/mL. On day 2, 1×10∧6 T cells and 1 mL of lentivirus encoding TYRP1-targeting CAR (from Dr. Guevara) were added to a 6 cm dish (Corning, Cat. #: 430166) coated overnight at 4° with 30 µg of RetroNectin (Takara, Cat. #: T100B) in 1 mL PBS and centrifuged at 2000×g for 60 min. On day 3, 850 µL was removed from each dish, another 1 mL of lentivirus added, followed by centrifuging at 2000xg for 60 min. On day 4, cells were washed and resuspended to 0.25×10∧6 cells/mL in TexMACS containing 200 IU/mL rhIL2 and after every 2 days, new media was added to maintain a concentration of 0.5×10∧6 cells/mL. Transduction efficiency was measured by GFP presence using flow cytometry.

Human NT T cell CRISPR KO and validation

3 days post-activation with TransAct, NT human T cells were collected and resuspended to a concentration of 20×10∧6 cells/mL in Buffer R (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #: MPK10096B). CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) mixtures were prepared per 2.0×10∧6 human CAR T cells by incubating 100 pmol of TRBC (Thermo Fisher, sequence: GGAGAATGACGAGTGGACCC) gRNA with 12.5 µg of TrueCut Cas9 Protein v2 (Thermo Fisher, Cat. #: A36498) and 50 µL of Buffer R for 20 min at room temperature. Afterwards, 50 µL of each RNP mixture was added to 100 µL of CAR T cells, collected in a 100 µL Neon transfection system pipette and electroporated using a Neon Electroporation System set to the conditions 1600 V/10 ms/3 pulses. After electroporation, cells were added back to prewarmed TexMACS medium containing 200 IU/mL rhIL2 and cultured as described above. To validate knockout efficiency, cells were stained with PE anti-human α/β T Cell Receptor (BioLegend, Cat. #: 306708) and analyzed with flow cytometry.

Cell sorting

T cells were surface stained (FITC anti-human CD3 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 317306), PE/Dazzle 594 anti-human CD45RA (BioLegend, Cat. #: 304146), APC anti-human CD45RO (BioLegend, Cat. #: 304210)) and sorted with a BD FACSAria II into CD3+CD45RA+CD45RO- (naïve) and CD3+CD45RA-CD45RO+ (memory) populations.

Human CAR and NT T cell in vitro co-culture studies

In 96-well round bottom plates, CAR or NT T cells were co-cultured with irradiated (10 000 cGy) target cells or a 1:20 dilution of TransAct for 72 hours at a 1:1 effector-to-target (E:T) ratio in TexMACS. Supernatants were collected after 72 hours and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) levels were measured by cytokine bead array (CBA) (BD, Cat. #: 558270; BD, Cat. #: 558269). Cells were incubated with Zombie NIR viability dye, Fc blocked with a 1:20 dilution of human TruStain FcX, surface stained (anti-human CD19-Fc+AF647 anti-human Fc, BV711 anti-human CD3 (BD Biosciences, Cat. #: 563725), BV785 anti-human CD4 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 317442), BUV737 anti-human CD8 (BD Biosciences, Cat. #: 749367), BV421 anti-human CD25 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 302630), BV605 anti-human CD69 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 310938), BV510 anti-HLA-DR (BioLegend, Cat. #: 307646), PE anti-human PD-1 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 329906), or APC anti-human PD-1 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 329908)), intracellular stained when applicable (PE/Cyanine7 anti-human Granzyme B (BioLegend, Cat. #: 372214) or FITC anti-human Granzyme B (BioLegend, Cat. #: 372206), PE/Dazzle 594 anti-human Ki67 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 350534), BV605 anti-human CD107a (BioLegend, Cat. #: 328634)), and analyzed by flow cytometry. Incucyte studies were done by plating CAR or NT T cells with target cells at different E:T ratios in 96-well flat bottom plates (Corning, Cat. #: 353072) in an Incucyte Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius) for 72 hours and analyzing using Incucyte analysis software (Sartorius). For major histocompatibility complex (MHC) blocking studies, purified anti-human HLA-A, B, C (BioLegend, Cat. #: 311402), purified anti-human HLA-DR, DP, DQ (BioLegend, Cat. #: 361702), and purified mouse IgG2a, κ Isotype Ctrl (BioLegend, Cat. #: 401502) were used.

Human CAR19 and NT T cell treatment of tumor-bearing mice

Female NSG mice (6 –12 weeks old) were injected intravenously with 1×10∧6 Raji WT or CD19 KO ffLuc cells on day 0 and intravenously with 5×10∧6 CAR or NT T cells on day 7. Blood was collected in Microtainer Collection Tubes (BD Biosciences, Cat. #: 365967), spun at 8000 x g for 10 min; serum was frozen at −80°C. Spleens and bone marrow were mechanically processed in D-PBS+2% FBS, filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer, treated with 5 mL of 1 x RBC lysis buffer, washed in D-PBS+2% FBS, and counted. Cells were incubated with Zombie NIR viability dye, Fc blocked with both a 1:20 dilution of human TruStain FcX and 1:20 dilution of TruStain FcX anti-mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 101320), surface stained (anti-human CD19-Fc+AF647 anti-human Fc, BV711 anti-human CD3 (BD Biosciences, Cat. #: 563725), BV785 anti-human CD4 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 317442), BUV737 anti-human CD8 (BD Biosciences, Cat. #: 749367), BV421 anti-human CD25 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 302630), BV605 anti-human CD69 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 310938), BV510 anti-HLA-DR (BioLegend, Cat. #: 307646), PE anti-human PD-1 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 329906)), intracellular stained when applicable (PE/Cyanine7 anti-human Granzyme B (BioLegend, Cat. #: 372214), PE/Dazzle 594 anti-human Ki67 (BioLegend, Cat. #: 350534)), and analyzed by flow cytometry. Serum human IFN-γ levels were measured using CBA. Mice tumor burden was assessed by i.p. injection of 4.5 mg D-luciferin (Revvity, Cat. #: 122799) followed by bioluminescent imaging using the IVIS Lumina III system.

Cell counting

After processing, spleen and bone marrow samples were counted using a Z1 Coulter Particle Counter (Beckman Coulter). For in vitro experiments, trypan blue (Gibco, Cat. #: 15250061) exclusion was performed with a BioRad TC20 (BioRad, Cat. #: 1450102) or a Countess 3 (Invitrogen, Cat. #: AMQAX2000).

Flow cytometry

For CD107a staining, cells were incubated for 5 hours with a 1:1000 dilution of GolgiPlug (BD, Cat. #: 555029), a 1:1500 dilution of GolgiStop (BD, Cat. #: 554724), and anti-human CD107a antibody prior to surface staining. Viability dye was prepared in D-PBS. Surface antibody dilutions and washing were performed in staining buffer (D-PBS+2% FBS). Cells were incubated on ice for 30 min per surface staining step and washed once in staining buffer. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Biosciences, Cat. #554722) for 1 hour at 4°C and washed with BD Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences, Cat. #554723). Intracellular antibody dilutions were prepared in BD Perm/Wash buffer. Cells were incubated with intracellular antibody dilutions for 30 min at room temperature and washed once in BD Perm/Wash buffer. Samples were run on a BD LSRFortessa and data were analyzed using FlowJo.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses using either paired/paired ratio/unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and log-rank (Mantel-Cox) tests were performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.

Results

CAR19 T cells demonstrate CAR-independent cytotoxicity and TCR-mediated reactivity toward Raji cells

We generated CAR19 T cells from two donors using a standard workflow involving T cell enrichment from PBMCs, activation with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 agonist antibodies (TransAct), transduction with CAR-encoding lentivirus, and expansion in media containing recombinant human IL-2 (rhIL-2) (online supplemental figure 1A). CAR19 T cells were co-cultured with either Raji WT cells expressing endogenous CD19 or Raji CD19 knockout (KO) cells. Raji CD19 KO cells were confirmed to be devoid of CD19 cell surface expression by flow cytometry using an antibody against human CD19 (clone HIB19) (online supplemental figure 1B).

As expected, Incucyte live-cell imaging demonstrated robust CAR19 T cell-mediated killing of Raji WT cells. Surprisingly, CAR19 T cells also showed measurable cytotoxicity against Raji CD19 KO cells although at a reduced magnitude of specific lysis (figure 1A–C). CAR19 T cells also displayed CD107a degranulation after 72 hours of co-culture with either target cell population, with lower levels observed in response to Raji CD19 KO cells (online supplemental figures 1C, D). Cytokine measurements from co-culture supernatants revealed increased IFN-γ secretion following co-culture with both Raji WT and CD19 KO cells, again with higher levels induced by CD19-expressing Raji WT cells (figure 1D). CAR19 T cells also underwent proliferation in response to both Raji WT and CD19 KO cells, with greater expansion observed in the presence of Raji WT cells (figure 1E). Additionally, through 72 hours of co-culture with both Raji WT and CD19 KO cells, phenotypic profiling showed upregulation of markers associated with T cell activation, including CD25, CD69, granzyme B, HLA-DR, Ki-67, and PD-1, on CAR19 T cells (figure 1F, G, online supplemental figure 1E). This activation profile resembled that induced by TCR stimulation with TransAct, suggesting that CAR19 T cell reactivity against Raji CD19 KO cells may involve TCR-mediated alloreactivity (online supplemental figures 1F, G). In contrast, co-culture with K562 cells, which engage T cells by NKG2D-mediated recognition of stress ligands, resulted in effective target killing but did not elicit upregulation of these activation markers (online supplemental figure 1I–K).

Previous studies have reported that naïve T cells are the primary mediators of allogeneic responses due to their diverse T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire and capacity to cause graft-versus-host disease (GvHD).14–16 However, memory T cells, including virus-specific clones, have also been shown to recognize mismatched HLA and mediate cross-reactive responses.17–19 To investigate whether our observed CD19-independent activation was driven preferentially by naïve or memory T cell subsets, we generated CAR19 T cells from sorted naïve (CD45RA+CD45RO⁻) and memory (CD45RA⁻CD45RO+) populations (online supplemental figure 2A) from two donors. After 72 hours of co-culture with Raji CD19 KO target cells, CAR19 T cells derived from both naïve and memory subsets upregulated activation markers associated with TCR stimulation, including CD25, CD69, granzyme B, HLA-DR, Ki-67, and PD-1 (online supplemental figure 2B, C). These findings indicate that TCR-mediated alloreactivity toward Raji cells is not restricted to naïve-derived CAR T cells and that memory-derived CAR T cells are also capable of recognizing CD19 KO Raji cells through their TCRs.

Both CAR19-Expressing and NT T cells display marked alloreactivity toward CD19-negative Raji cells: CAR expression augments this activity

To determine whether CD19-independent reactivity was specific to or enhanced in CAR19-positive T cells, CAR19 T cells were generated from five donors (~70%–80% transduction efficiency) and co-cultured for 72 hours with Raji WT or CD19 KO cells. CAR19-positive and CAR19-negative populations were gated by flow cytometry, and T cell activation markers as described above were assessed for both populations (online supplemental figures 3A–C).

CAR19-positive T cells upregulated activation marker expression after co-culture with both Raji WT and CD19 KO cells, with significantly higher levels observed in response to Raji WT cells, reflecting CAR-mediated recognition of CD19 (figure 2A, online supplemental figure 4A). CAR19-negative T cells also displayed alloreactivity, as indicated by increased activation marker expression. Unlike CAR19-positive T cells, however, the level of activation was similar between Raji WT and CD19 KO targets (except for Ki-67) (figure 2B, online supplemental figure 4B). When comparing responses to Raji CD19 KO cells to reflect non-CAR-driven reactivity, CAR19-positive T cells consistently showed higher activation marker expression than CAR19-negative T cells suggesting that TCR-driven alloreactivity may be enhanced in CAR19-positive T cells. This could be due to CAR tonic signaling although baseline differences in activation marker expression may also contribute (figure 2C, online supplemental figure 4C). This phenomenon was not unique to CAR19-expressing T cells. Using TYRP-1-targeting CAR T cells from a single donor, CAR-positive populations similarly showed elevated activation marker expression following co-culture compared with CAR-negative counterparts (online supplemental figure 4D).

To confirm CAR-independent alloreactivity, we generated NT T cells from two donors using the same production workflow as CAR19 T cells, but without lentiviral transduction (online supplemental figure 1A). Incucyte live-cell imaging demonstrated that, like CAR19 T cells, NT T cells were capable of killing both Raji WT and CD19 KO cells, although cytotoxicity against Raji WT cells was reduced compared with CAR19 T cells owing to lack of CAR antigen targeting (figure 2D, E). Supernatant cytokine analysis of NT T cell co-cultures revealed increased IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion in response to both target populations (online supplemental figure 5A). In contrast to CAR19 T cells, which preferentially expanded while in the presence of Raji WT cells, NT T cells underwent comparable proliferation when co-cultured with either Raji WT or CD19 KO cells (online supplemental figure 5B). Phenotypic profiling by flow cytometry after 72 hours showed upregulation of T cell activation markers when NT T cells were cultured with either Raji WT or CD19 KO cells (online supplemental figure 5C).

To rule out these effects were caused by non-specific stimulation due to potential LPS or media contamination, a new batch of Raji WT cells was thawed and cultured in TexMACS for 2 months. NT T cells co-cultured with these Raji cells still showed activation marker upregulation, confirming that this response was biologically driven and not a technical artifact (online supplemental figure 5D, E). Finally, in a side-by-side Incucyte cytotoxicity assay against Raji CD19 KO cells, NT T cells exhibited reduced killing compared with CAR19 T cells, providing further evidence that CAR tonic signaling may augment alloreactivity (figure 2F, G).

CAR alloreactivity against tumor cell lines appears to be limited to the Raji lymphoma cell line

To assess whether this observed alloreactivity extends to other leukemia or lymphoma cell lines, CAR19 or NT T cells were generated from five donors and co-cultured for 72 hours with Raji WT/CD19 KO, Granta-519, NALM6, Ramos, or REH cells. These additional cell lines express varying levels of MHC class I and II molecules, which could be further upregulated with IFN-γ treatment in all lines except NALM6 (online supplemental figures 6A–E). Moreover, these cell lines possess distinct profiles of HLA class I and II molecules compared with Raji cells, demonstrating strong haplotype diversity among tumor cell lines tested, as well as endogenous CD19 on their cell surface (online supplemental table 3, online supplemental figure 7A).

As expected, NT T cells upregulated activation markers when co-cultured with Raji WT or CD19 KO cells, consistent with the alloreactivity described above (figure 3A). CAR19 T cells also upregulated activation markers in response to all four cell lines, presumably due to CAR-mediated recognition of CD19 (online supplemental figures 7B–E). However, NT T cells did not exhibit activation marker upregulation when co-cultured with any of these other leukemia or lymphoma cell lines (figure 3B–E).

Furthermore, T cell alloreactivity was assessed against two solid tumor lines—SKMEL28 and A673. SKMEL28 cells express both MHC class I and II molecules, while A673 cells demonstrated surface expression of only MHC class I molecules, although expression of MHC molecules on both cell lines could be further upregulated by IFN-γ (online supplemental figures 6F, G, online supplemental table 3). Similar to other non-Raji leukemia or lymphoma cell lines, NT T cells did not upregulate activation markers when co-cultured with SKMEL28 and A673 cells (online supplemental figure 7F). Importantly, pretreatment of all non-Raji tumor cell lines, both hematologic and solid, with IFN-γ to enhance MHC expression did not induce activation marker upregulation in NT T cells (online supplemental figures 8A–G). Collectively, these data indicate that this strong observed alloreactivity appears largely restricted to the Raji cell line, although other tumor lines not included in this study should be assessed.

Both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells mediate Raji alloreactivity, with MHC blockade attenuating this response

To further investigate the mechanism underlying this alloreactivity toward Raji cells and confirm that it is TCR-driven, we examined the contribution from both major T cell subsets, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, to this response. After co-culturing NT T cells from five donors with both Raji WT and CD19 KO cells, flow cytometry gating on CD4+ and CD8+ subsets revealed that both populations upregulated the previously described T cell activation markers, demonstrating that this alloreactivity is not lineage specific (figure 4A, B).

We next assessed whether this activation was dependent on TCR engagement of peptide:MHC complexes. Since CD8+T cells recognize peptides presented on MHC class I molecules, we co-cultured NT T cells from two donors with Raji CD19 KO cells in the presence or absence of a pan-human MHC I-blocking antibody (clone W6/32). MHC I blockade significantly reduced T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against Raji targets (figure 4C). A similar reduction in cytotoxicity was observed when CAR19 T cells were co-cultured with Raji CD19 KO cells in the presence of MHC I blockade, demonstrating that CAR-independent activation against Raji cells requires MHC I engagement (online supplemental figure 9A). Flow cytometry phenotyping further confirmed that MHC I blockade reduced activation marker upregulation in CD8+T cells from both NT and CAR19 T cell populations (figure 4D, online supplemental figure 9B). CD4+T cells recognize antigenic peptides presented on MHC class II molecules. As expected, the addition of a pan-human MHC II blocking antibody (clone Tü39) did not impact target-cell killing, which is primarily mediated by CD8+T effector cells (figure 4E). However, MHC II blockade significantly diminished activation marker expression on CD4+T cells (figure 4F). These findings demonstrate that both MHC I and MHC II peptide complexes contribute to the TCR-driven alloreactivity against Raji cells and that both CD8+and CD4+ T cells respond through their respective MHC-restricted pathways. To further confirm this TCR-driven mechanism, we performed CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of the TCR on NT T cells (online supplemental figure 10A) from a single donor. The resulting mixed population of TCR-positive and TCR-negative NT T cells was co-cultured with Raji cells and following co-culture, upregulation of activation markers was observed only in the TCR-positive population while TCR-negative NT T cells failed to do so (online supplemental figures 10B–E).

CAR19 T cells exhibit significant anti-tumor effects against Raji CD19 KO cells in vivo

To determine whether the CAR-independent alloreactivity we observed against Raji in vitro occurs in vivo, immunodeficient NSG mice were engrafted intravenously with either Raji WT or Raji CD19 KO cells on day 0. On day 7 mice were treated with human CAR19 T cells or NT T cells generated from four donors with tumor burden and survival monitored over time and select cohorts euthanized for serum cytokine analysis and flow cytometric evaluation of T cell engraftment and activation phenotype (figure 5A). As expected, all (except one) of the Raji WT-bearing mice treated with CAR19 T cells (n=12) survived until our experimental endpoint at day 50, data consistent with robust CAR-mediated tumor clearance. However, CAR19 T cells also conferred a survival benefit in Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice (n=15), with approximately half surviving up to day 50 indicating a CAR-independent anti-tumor effect in vivo that correlated with our in vitro results (figure 5B). The strength of this CAR-independent anti-tumor effect varied across donors (online supplemental figures 11A). In one donor, both CAR19 and NT T cells mediated substantial control of Raji CD19 KO tumors, extending survival to day 70 (figure 5C, D, online suplemental figures 11B, C). NT T cells also exhibited in vivo reactivity against Raji WT tumors, although treatment of Raji WT-bearing mice with NT T cells only modestly prolonged survival, possibly reflecting the fast and more aggressive growth kinetics of Raji WT cells compared with their CD19 KO counterparts (online supplemental figures 11D–E). Consistent with CAR-independent T cell activation, serum analysis revealed the presence of human IFN-γ in both Raji WT and CD19 KO-bearing mice treated with either CAR19 or NT T cells (figure 5E, online supplemental figure 12A).

T cell engraftment and activation phenotype were assessed by flow cytometry (online supplemental figures 13A, B). At day 14, 7 days post-CAR19 T cell infusion, T cell frequencies in the bone marrow were higher in Raji WT-bearing mice compared with Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice, indicating CAR antigen-driven expansion due to the presence of CD19 (figure 5F). Among the engrafted T cells, a greater proportion of them were CAR-expressing in Raji WT-bearing mice relative to Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice (figure 5G). Despite having lower overall engraftment, CAR19 T cells recovered from the bone marrow of Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice displayed TCR-mediated activation in vivo. At day 14, these cells expressed activation markers including CD25, granzyme B, and PD-1 above pre-infusion levels, with expression of these markers higher than in CAR19 T cells recovered from Raji WT-bearing mice, likely due to the continuous presence of residual tumor in treated Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice whereas Raji WT-bearing mice demonstrated near-complete tumor clearance (figure 5H, C, D). By day 28, both the bone marrow and spleen of Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice treated with CAR19 T cells contained increased numbers of human T cells compared with Raji WT-bearing mice (figure 5I, J). CAR19 T cells isolated from the bone marrow of Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice also exhibited higher expression of granzyme B and HLA-DR, but not PD-1, compared with those from Raji WT-bearing mice, indicating sustained effector activity (figure 5K). T cell engraftment and activation were observed in NT T cells recovered from both the bone marrow and spleen of Raji WT- and Raji CD19 KO-bearing mice, further supporting the presence of CAR-independent, TCR-mediated reactivity toward Raji tumors in vivo (online supplemental figures 12B–G).

Overall, both in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrate that T cells from multiple donors, without CAR expression, can mediate anti-tumor activity and TCR triggering against Raji cells. This could lead to false-positive or overexaggerated favorable results when using Raji cells as a preclinical lymphoma model of CAR efficacy.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that Raji cells stimulate CAR T cells from multiple different donors without CAR antigen triggering. As expected, CAR19 T cells demonstrated strong cytotoxic activity against CD19-expressing Raji cells but also unexpectedly exhibited cytotoxicity against CD19 KO Raji cells, although at a reduced magnitude. This CAR-independent reactivity against Raji cells was observed in vitro and in vivo with both CAR19 and NT T cells and was accompanied by proliferation, CD107a degranulation, elevated cytokine (IFN-γ, IL-2) production, and an upregulation of T cell activation markers—CD25, CD69, granzyme B, HLA-DR, Ki-67, and PD-1—all indicative of polyclonal TCR stimulation and engagement of endogenous TCRs in the absence of CAR antigen CD19. Importantly, both CAR19 and NT T cells extended survival in NSG mice bearing CD19 KO Raji tumors, demonstrating that this alloreactive response can contribute meaningfully to the extent of anti-tumor activity observed, highlighting the need to exercise caution when interpreting efficacy data derived from Raji-based assays as exclusively CAR-dependent.

It is important to note that in vivo, the parental Raji WT line expanded much faster than the CD19 KO line, which may account for why we failed to detect significant anti-tumor effects of the NT T cells toward the parental line. Therefore, the rapid growth and progression of parental Raji tumors may mask potential effects of endogenous T cell alloreactivity in vivo although this could possibly overcome by increasing T cell dosage.

We considered the possibility of sex-mismatched minor histocompatibility antigens (such as H-Y antigens) for the observed alloreactivity, as Raji cells are derived from a male patient. However, since both male and female donors were included in this study (online supplemental tables 1 and 2) and Raji alloreactive responses were observed across all donors, sex-linked minor antigens are unlikely to fully account for this Raji-specific alloreactivity.

The ability of CAR T cells to mediate alloreactive responses is consistent with prior reports regarding the development of GvHD and other T cell-mediated effects as a result of antigen presentation rather than intrinsic CAR specificity.20–23 In fact, CAR expression itself has been shown to induce tonic signaling, resulting in increased basal T cell activation even in the absence of antigen.24 25 Tonic signaling may lower the activation threshold of endogenous TCRs, amplifying what would otherwise be subthreshold TCR:MHC interactions. This mechanism may explain the heightened activation and cytotoxicity observed in CAR-positive T cells compared with their counterpart NT T cells in response to CD19 KO Raji cells.

Previous work has demonstrated that humanized immune system mice (HIS), immunodeficient mice reconstituted with human CD34+hematopoietic stem cells, could spontaneously clear subcutaneously implanted Raji tumor cells and form antigen-specific memory responses.26 Our study extends these observations by demonstrating that in the context of preclinical modeling of CAR T cell efficacy, the use of Raji cells can generate misleading and false positive efficacy data due to endogenous TCR-mediated reactivity in both CAR-engineered and NT T cells. Furthermore, while their study reported attenuation of cytokine production, notably IFN-γ, following MHC class II blockade, our findings reveal that MHC class I blockade significantly reduces T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.26 Together, these results indicate that both CD4+and CD8+ T cell responses contribute to the observed anti-tumor activity against Raji cells through classical TCR:peptide–MHC recognition.

Interestingly, this T cell alloreactivity appears to be specific to Raji cells as neither other B cell leukemia/lymphoma lines (Granta-519, NALM6, Ramos, and REH) nor solid tumor lines (SKMEL28 and A673) were capable of inducing a similar activation response even after co-culture with IFN-γ to increase MHC expression. Spontaneous clearance has been reported for HT29 cells in HIS mouse models, however.27

Mechanistically, the antibody blockade experiments demonstrate that MHC class I engagement is necessary for CD8+T cell activation and cytotoxicity against Raji cells in both NT and CAR19 T cell populations. While MHC class II blockade reduced CD4+T cell activation, it did not impair target-cell killing, consistent with the dogma of CD8+ T cells serving as the effector populations for direct cytotoxic effects and CD4+T cells providing cytokine support. Furthermore, when TCRs were knocked out in a representative donor, NT T cells failed to generate an alloreactive response when co-cultured with Raji cells, supporting the role of TCR-dependent recognition in this process. These findings corroborate similar research demonstrating that knocking out TCRs in human T cells mitigated xenogeneic GvHD and allogeneic responses in mixed lymphocyte reactions.28

Our data suggest that Raji cells may possess unique tumor-intrinsic characteristics that allow them to elicit robust TCR-driven T cell activation independent of CAR expression. For example, they may possess distinct peptide repertoires derived from EBV-associated antigens and presented by MHC, rendering these cells unusually immunogenic.29 Raji cells are transformed with EBV and harbor approximately 50–60 complete copies of the EBV genome per cell.29 30 This viral integration may result in the expression and presentation of highly immunogenic viral peptides onto MHC molecules, which may result in prolonged expression and presentation of viral antigens capable of engaging alloreactive TCRs. Furthermore, they express high levels of T cell co-stimulatory ligands, such as 41BB-L, CD70, CD80, and CD86, which may contribute to their capacity to provide strong co-stimulation signals to the T cell (online supplemental figure 14).31 32 While knocking out TCR expression in NT T cells from our representative donor abrogated activation marker upregulation, it remains to be determined if, due to high expression of these co-stimulatory ligands, Raji cells might activate other donor T cells independent of TCR:MHC-peptide interactions, as seen in the case of CD28 super agonists.33 34 Alternatively, the Raji lymphoma line has been demonstrated to readily present superantigens, such as toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 and Staphylococcal enterotoxin A in vitro, and this may also possibly account for the global responses observed.35 36 Future experiments could determine whether EBV transformation, a widely employed technique used to generate immortalized lymphoblast cell lines, can drive T cell immunogenicity.37 38

Collectively, our results support a model in which Raji cells present immunogenic peptides on both MHC class I and II molecules and drive polyclonal TCR-mediated activation and proliferation, effects that can be further amplified by CAR tonic signaling. While EBV-derived antigens represent a plausible contributor to this response, we acknowledge that the precise peptides responsible remain unidentified. Comprehensive peptide:MHC profiling, including mass spectrometry-based approaches, is essential to resolve and determine the basis of this phenomenon. Elucidating these mechanisms is critical to improve the fidelity of CAR T cell preclinical models and minimize the risks of false-positive efficacy signals that arise from tumor-intrinsic immunogenicity rather than CAR specificity and signaling.
