Quantum (non)equivalence of dual massive p p -form gauge theories
Christian Canete, Elden Loomes

Abstract
Gauge theories of massive p p -forms are connected by various dualities, which hold classically but may be broken at the quantum level. One example is the B ​ F BF theory of topologically coupled p p - and ( d − p − 1 ) (d-p-1) -forms in d d dimensions, where the coupling between forms results in a manifestly gauge invariant mass term for either form when the other is integrated out classically. We perform the path integral quantisation of this theory; by integrating out one of the forms, the resulting determinants are sensitive to the topology of spacetime, and counterterms must be introduced to renormalise their divergences. We compute these determinants in terms of the topological numbers of spacetime, showing explicitly how the quantum duality of the massive theories is broken on topologically non-trivial backgrounds. This is directly related to the quantum breaking of the massless duality between the form that was integrated out and the longitudinal modes of its partner. In particular, the difference of counterterms is proportional to the Euler characteristic of spacetime. The existence of gravitational instantons suggests that these dualities may be broken even in Minkowski space in the presence of topological fluctuations.

Duality of massless
p

First, we show the duality between a massless

Suppose we have a massless

A\rightarrow A+\mathrm{d}\omega

\omega

(p-1)
-form. The gauge invariant Euclidean action for the massless

\displaystyle S_{p}[A]=\frac{1}{2}\int F\wedge\star F=\frac{1}{2}\langle F,F\rangle~,

(2)

where

is the field strength tensor of the

-form. Varying the action with respect to

\displaystyle\delta_{A}S_{p}[A]=\int\mathrm{d}\delta_{A}A\wedge\star F=\int\mathrm{d}\left(\delta_{A}A\wedge\star F\right)+(-1)^{p+1}\int\delta_{A}A\wedge\mathrm{d}\star F~.

(3)

Demanding that the field variation of the action is zero, we obtain the equation of motion
\mathrm{d}\star F=0
, along with the boundary condition that variations of the
p
-form vanish when projected on the boundary,
\delta_{A}A|_{\mathrm{bdry}}=0
(other prescriptions are possible, e.g. see

23

). The Bianchi identity for the
p
-form,
\mathrm{d}F=0
, is automatically satisfied since

is locally exact.

Alternatively, we could begin by imposing the Bianchi identity, in which case the local exactness of the field strength defines
A

(p+1)
de Rham cohomology group
H^{p+1}(M;\mathbb{Z})

M

A
. The most famous example of this non-exactness is that of the Wu–Yang monopole
[
71
,
72
]
, where
H^{2}(M;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
labels the discrete magnetic charges supported on a monopole defect. Hodge theory tells us that there is exactly one harmonic form,
\omega

\Box\omega=0
, representing each cohomology class
[
8
]
.

Now, the equation of motion tells us that the Hodge dual of
F
is also closed, and we can write
\star F=G
where
G
is a closed
(d-p-1)
-form. Like
F

is then locally the derivative of some
(d-p-2)
-form

C\rightarrow C+\mathrm{d}\theta
. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\star F=G=\mathrm{d}C~.

(4)

The equation of motion and the Bianchi identity of the

-form in terms of the

-form are now
\mathrm{d}G=0
and
\mathrm{d}\star G=0
, respectively. One can see that the duality transformation interchanges the equation of motion and Bianchi identity of the two massless theories.

Substituting Eq. (
4

2

(d-p-2)
-form

\displaystyle S_{d-p-2}[C]=\frac{1}{2}\int G\wedge\star G=\frac{1}{2}\langle G,G\rangle~.

(5)

Thus, a massless
p
-form and a
(d-p-2)
-form are classically equivalent due to the duality of the theories on-shell.

As sanity check, we can also check the energy-momentum tensors of the theories and show that the two are equivalent. Indeed, the Euclidean energy-momentum tensor for the massless
p
-form is (briefly going back to component form)

\displaystyle T_{\rho\sigma}[A]=\frac{1}{p!}F_{\rho\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}{F_{\sigma}}^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}-\frac{1}{2(p+1)!}g_{\rho\sigma}F_{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}F^{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}~.

(6)

In the dual theory, where
\star F=G
, the equation can be written in component form as

\displaystyle F^{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}=\frac{1}{(r+1)!}\epsilon^{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}G_{\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}~,\quad G_{\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}=(r+1)\partial_{[\alpha_{0}}C_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}]}~,

(7)

where

, and

denotes normalised antisymmetrisation. By direct substitution it is possible to show that

\displaystyle T_{\rho\sigma}[A]=\frac{1}{r!}G_{\rho\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}{G_{\sigma}}^{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}-\frac{1}{2(r+1)!}g_{\rho\sigma}G_{\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}G^{\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{r}}=T_{\rho\sigma}[C]~,

(8)

proving their equivalency.

As another sanity check, we verify the duality of the massless
p
-forms by counting the number of propagating degrees of freedom. First, note that the equations of motion for a

-form in component form is

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu_{0}}\left(\sqrt{-g}\,\partial^{[\mu_{0}}A^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]}\right)=0~.

Pick a time direction, indexed by zero and use Latin letters to denote spatial indices. We deduce that the time components of the gauge field

. They have gauge redundancy
A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{p}}\rightarrow A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{p}}+\partial_{[i_{1}}\omega_{i_{2}\cdots i_{p}]}^{(p-1)}
for

a
(p-1)

\omega^{(p-1)}_{i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}\rightarrow\omega^{(p-2)}_{i_{2}\cdots i_{p}}+\partial_{[i_{2}}\omega^{(p-2)}_{i_{3}\cdots i_{p}]}
for
\omega_{i_{3}\cdots i_{p}}^{(p-2)}

3
Duality of massive
p
-forms using a topological
B\wedge F
term

Naïvely, a mass term of the form
\langle A,A\rangle
seems to be prohibited by the gauge redundancy of a
p
-form theory. There are several ways to overcome this, all of which use some additional degree of freedom to compensate for this violation. In the Standard Model, the Higgs mechanism provides a mass term for the

\displaystyle S[A,\Omega]

\displaystyle=S_{p}[A]+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\int\Big(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\Big)\wedge\star\Big(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\Big)~,

(22)

for a
(p-1)
-form Stueckelberg field
\Omega
, which transforms as
\Omega\rightarrow\Omega+m\omega
while
A\rightarrow A+\mathrm{d}\omega
, thus preserving the gauge invariance of the theory.

To explore the duality between massive theories, we introduce a mass in a different way. Consider a theory with a
p
-form
A
and a
q
-form
B
, with field strengths
F=\mathrm{d}A
and
H=\mathrm{d}B
respectively. If

\displaystyle im\int B\wedge F=(-1)^{(d-p)(p+1)}im\int A\wedge H+(-1)^{d-p-1}im\int\mathrm{d}(B\wedge A)~,

(23)

where
m
is a parameter with dimensions of mass controlling the coupling strength between the two fields. In the Euclidean theory the term is imaginary, much like the topological
\theta
-term. When we integrate out one of the forms, the resulting theory depends only on
m^{2}
, so the difference in signs on either side can be absorbed. We will see below that the boundary term vanishes on-shell. The coupling thus behaves symmetrically between the
p
- and

-forms.

The full action of this ‘

\displaystyle S_{BF}[A,B]=S_{p}[A]+S_{q}[B]+im\int B\wedge F~,

(24)

where

is the action for a massless

-form as defined in Eq. (
2
). The theory possesses two independent gauge redundancies of the form
A\rightarrow A+\mathrm{d}\omega
and
B\rightarrow B+\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle S_{BF}[A,B]\rightarrow S_{BF}[A,B]+im\int\mathrm{d}\left(\theta\wedge F\right)~.

(25)

This boundary term is zero if we impose ‘small’ gauge transformations vanishing at the boundary, which represent the true gauge redundancy of the theory. Thus, the action is completely invariant under both gauge transformations of
A

Varying the action with respect to
A

\displaystyle\delta_{A}S_{BF}[A,B]=(-1)^{p+1}\int\delta_{A}A\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}\star F+(-1)^{q(p+1)}im\mathrm{d}B\right)+\int\mathrm{d}\left[\delta_{A}A\wedge\left(\star F+(-1)^{q(p+1)}imB\right)\right]~,

(26)

whilst varying with respect to
B

\displaystyle\delta_{B}S_{BF}[A,B]=(-1)^{q+1}\int\delta_{B}B\wedge\left(\mathrm{d}\star H+(-1)^{q+1}imF\right)+\int\mathrm{d}\left(\delta_{B}B\wedge\star H\right)~.

Thus, the equations of motion of this theory are

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\star F+(-1)^{q(p+1)}imH=0~,\quad\mathrm{d}\star H+(-1)^{q+1}imF=0~,

(28)

supplemented by the boundary conditions
\delta_{A}A|_{\mathrm{bdry}}=\delta_{B}B|_{\mathrm{bdry}}=0

The second equation of motion can be factorised, locally, as
\mathrm{d}\left(\star H+(-1)^{q+1}mA\right)=0

\displaystyle\star H=(-1)^{q}im\left(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\right)\implies H=(-1)^{q+p(q+1)}im\star\left(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\right)~,

We see that

\displaystyle S[A,\Omega]=S_{p}[A]+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\int\left(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\right)\wedge\star\left(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\right)-m^{2}\int\mathrm{d}\Omega\wedge\star\left(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega\right)~,

In fact, the relationship between the

Unlike, for example, massless
p
-form gauge theory or the Abelian Higgs mechanism (at finite breaking scale
v
) for 1-forms, the Stueckelberg theory does not support configurations where
F
is in a non-trivial cohomology class, even on topologically non-trivial backgrounds. Consider for example a
1
-form gauge theory on the background of flat four dimensional spacetime punctured by a ’t Hooft line,
\mathbb{R}^{4}\backslash\mathbb{R}\cong S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The field strength is classified by the 2nd cohomology classes
H^{2}(S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})\cong\mathbb{Z}
, which correspond to the discrete magnetic charges supported by the ’t Hooft line. If the 1-form gains a mass via the Higgs mechanism, the Meissner effect causes the magnetic field to condense into a flux tube. In the centre of the tube, the Higgs field sits at the origin and the symmetry is unbroken. As the breaking scale
v
is taken to infinity, the flux tube becomes an infinitely thin defect, and the action of these solutions diverges as
S\sim v^{2}
. This suppresses the charged states. This limit is equivalent to the 1-form Stueckelberg theory up to the insertion of these defects.

The suppression is similarly complete in the general
p
-form Stueckelberg theory. Notice that the field strength,
F=\mathrm{d}A
, can equally be written as
F=\mathrm{d}(A-\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{d}\Omega)
. While the former suggests that some non-trivial patching may be possible, the latter is the derivative of a gauge invariant combination. This combination must be globally defined, so
F
is always exact and in the trivial class.

In the
BF

B

F\propto\mathrm{d}\star H
, so
F
is likewise exact. The reciprocal conclusion for
H

A

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{U^{i}}\mathrm{d}(\delta_{A}A\wedge B^{i})=\sum_{i<j}\int_{\partial U^{i}\cap(-\partial U^{j})}\delta_{A}A\wedge(B^{i}-B^{j})

(40)

which just fixes
B
to be smooth across patches, and thus forces
H
in the trivial cohomology class.

This topological triviality on-shell is essential for the classical duality of these theories, otherwise there would exist topologically non-trivial states in one theory which may have no equivalent in its dual. We will show that while this remains true for the field strength in the quantum
BF
theory, the same cannot be said for the ghost modes. This is what breaks the duality.

4
Quantisation of massive
p
-forms

Now that we have demonstrated the equivalence of
p
-forms in the classical limit, we will show that the equivalence does not necessarily hold when one considers quantum corrections. Let us begin with the generating functional of our theory

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathcal{D}A\mathcal{D}B\Big|_{\mathrm{g.f.}}\,e^{-S_{BF}[A,B]}~,

(41)

where
S_{BF}[A,B]
is our Euclidean
BF
action in Eq. (
24

p
-form by integrating out the
q
-form
B
in the path integral. Indeed, we would like to write
Z
in the form

\displaystyle Z=X\int\mathcal{D}A^{\prime}\,e^{-S_{p}^{\mathrm{mass.}}[A^{\prime}]}=Y\int\mathcal{D}B^{\prime}\,e^{-S_{q}^{\mathrm{mass.}}[B^{\prime}]}~,

(42)

where
S_{p}^{\mathrm{mass}}[A^{\prime}]
is the action for a massive
p
-form, as Eq. (
33
). We will show that
X
and
Y
consist of determinants which produce different counterterms breaking the duality between the theories. In fact, these factors will simply correspond to the failure to dualise the form that is integrated out to a longitudinal degree of freedom for the remaining form.

To begin, we will complete the path integral over
B
, which we expect to contain a mass term for
A
. This path integral is just the generating functional of a free
q
-form gauge theory with a source term
\star J_{B}\equiv mF

\displaystyle Z_{q}[A]\equiv\int\mathcal{D}B\Big|_{\mathrm{g.f.}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathrm{d}B,\mathrm{d}B\rangle-im\int B\wedge F\right)~.

(43)

We quantise purely in the sector where
H
is in the trivial cohomology class; thus we can take
B
to be global and no boundary terms arise from patching. This is the sector where the classical theory lives, and the only sector where we expect the theories to be dual. In the other sectors, which possess no classical solutions, the non-trivial cohomology of
H
seems to source the
A
field in such a way as to break the duality directly—we defer these to later study. We will see that imposing trivial cohomology for
H
dynamically recovers the trivial topology for
F
as in the classical theory.

We will assume that the spacetime is Euclidean and geodesically complete, ensuring that the Poincaré duality survives
[
19
]
and there are no boundary terms for normalisable forms. See Appendix
C
for some more discussion.

To solve this path integral, we need to to impose some gauge-fixing procedure on
B
to avoid overcounting gauge-equivalent paths. The path integral contains an infinite volume contribution from the gauge direction, which we need to factorise out and absorb into the normalisation of
Z_{q}[A]
. Rather than taking the traditional Faddeev–Popov approach, where ghost counting is perilous (e.g.
[
68
]
), we follow an approach similar to
[
64
]
. We write the gauge transformations of the
q

B[\theta]\equiv B+\mathrm{d}\theta
. Suppose that there exists a functional
G_{q}[B[\theta]]
such that

\displaystyle 1=\int\mathcal{D}\theta\,G_{q}[B[\theta]]\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta B[\theta],\delta B[\theta]\rangle\right)~,

(44)

where
\delta
is the codifferential. Inserting this identity into Eq. (
43

\delta
z gauge result for the gauge-fixed action

\displaystyle Z_{q}[A]=\int\mathcal{D}\theta\int\mathcal{D}B\,G_{q}[B]\,\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle B,\Box B\rangle-im\int B\wedge F\right)~,

(45)

where
\Box=-(\delta\mathrm{d}+\mathrm{d}\delta)
is the Hodge Laplacian. The remaining integral over
\theta
is an overall factor of the gauge group volume that may be absorbed into the normalisation of
Z_{q}[A]
. Here we see that
G_{q}[B]
encodes the gauge-fixing condition of the theory. In Appendix

, we show that
G_{q}[B]
is independent of

and that

\displaystyle G_{q}[B]=V_{\mathrm{gauge}}\mathcal{G}_{q}\equiv V_{\mathrm{gauge}}\prod_{k=1}^{q}\big(\det\Box_{q-k}\big)^{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)/2}=V_{\mathrm{gauge}}\det\Box_{q-1}^{+1}\det\Box_{q-2}^{-3/2}\cdots\det\Box_{0}^{(-1)^{q+1}(q+1)/2}~,

(46)

where

is an infinite gauge volume factor that we can absorb into the normalisation of the path integral and

contains the tower of ghost fields associated with the
q

21

\displaystyle Z_{q}[A]=\bigg[\prod_{k=1}^{q}\big(\det\Box_{q-k}\big)^{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)/2}\bigg]\int\mathcal{D}B\,\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle B,\Box B\rangle-im\int B\wedge F\right)~.

(47)

Taking

\displaystyle Z_{q}^{0}\equiv\mathcal{G}_{q}\det\Box^{-1/2}_{q}=\prod_{k=0}^{q}\big(\det\Box_{q-k}\big)^{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)/2}~,

(48)

For non-zero

, in the

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int B\wedge\star\Box B-im\int B\wedge F

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\int(B+(-1)^{\eta}im\Box^{-1}\star F)\wedge\star(\Box B+(-1)^{\eta}im\star F)

(49)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt-im\int(B+(-1)^{\eta}im\Box^{-1}\star F)\wedge F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle B,\Box B\rangle+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\langle\Box^{-1}\star F,\star F\rangle~.

(50)

Then using the fact that

(51)

Now we can freely integrate out

\displaystyle Z_{q}[A]=\mathcal{G}_{q}\det\Box_{q}^{-1/2}\exp\!\Big(\frac{m^{2}}{2}\langle F,\Box^{-1}F\rangle\Big)=Z_{q}^{0}\exp\!\Big(\frac{m^{2}}{2}\langle F,\Box^{-1}F\rangle\Big)~,

Where

\displaystyle Z=\frac{Z^{0}_{p}}{Z_{q-1}^{0}}\int\mathcal{D}B^{\prime}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle H,H\rangle-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\langle B^{\prime},B^{\prime}\rangle\right)=\frac{Z_{p}^{0}}{Z_{q-1}^{0}}Z^{m}_{q}~.

Now we see that the (non)equivalence of the quantum theories is given exactly by the (non)equivalence of the dualised longitudinal modes,
{Z_{q}^{0}}/{Z_{p-1}^{0}}\neq{Z_{p}^{0}}/{Z_{q-1}^{0}}

5
Zero modes and counterterms

As discussed by Duff and Nieuwenhuizen
[
21
]
, zero modes of
\det\Box_{k}^{n/2}
produce divergences which must be absorbed by a local counterterm
\Delta\mathcal{L}=-\frac{\sqrt{g}}{\varepsilon}nb_{k}
, along with appropriate boundary contributions—regularised in
d+\varepsilon
dimensions at one loop—such that the action counts the corresponding
normalisable
Betti number

\displaystyle B_{k}=\int\mathrm{d}^{d}x\sqrt{g}\,b_{k}+\text{(boundary terms)}~.

(65)

By definition, this is the number of normalisable zero modes of
\Box_{k}
, i.e. normalisable harmonic
k
-forms (see Appendix
C
).
After integration, the counterterms in the action are
\Delta S=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}nB_{k}
.
Using the general form for
Z_{p}^{0}
, the total counterterm is

\displaystyle\Delta S_{p}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(B_{p}-2B_{p-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{p}(p+1)B_{0})~.

(66)

The counterterm for the numerator of Eq. (
64
) is then

\displaystyle\Delta S_{p}+\Delta S_{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[(B_{p}-2B_{p-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{p}(p+1)B_{0})+(B_{p-1}-2B_{p-2}+\cdots+(-1)^{p-1}pB_{0})\Big]

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[B_{p}-B_{p-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{p}B_{0}\Big]~,

(68)

and likewise the denominator

\displaystyle\Delta S_{q}+\Delta S_{q-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[B_{q}-B_{q-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{q}B_{0}\Big]~.

(69)

Thus the counterterms in
p
-form and
q
-form theories differ by

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{p\leftrightarrow q}

\displaystyle=\Delta S_{p}+\Delta S_{p-1}-\Delta S_{q}-\Delta S_{q-1}

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[(B_{p}-B_{p-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{p}B_{0})-(B_{d-p-1}-B_{d-p-2}+\cdots+(-1)^{d-p-1}B_{0})\Big]~,

(71)

then using the symmetry of normalisable Betti numbers on geodesically complete manifolds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big[(B_{p}-B_{p-1}+\cdots+(-1)^{p}B_{0})-(B_{p+1}-B_{p+2}+\cdots+(-1)^{d-p-1}B_{d})\Big]

(72)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{\varepsilon}\Big[B_{0}-B_{1}+B_{2}-\cdots+(-1)^{d}B_{d}\Big]=\frac{(-1)^{p}}{\varepsilon}\chi^{(2)}~,

(73)

where
\chi^{(2)}=\chi
is the Euler characteristic of a compact space. More care must be taken in the case of non-compact spaces, where the
\chi^{(2)}
may only equal the full Euler characteristic up to boundary corrections—here the alternating sum of
normalisable
Betti numbers gives the right expression. In either case, the duality between the theories is broken by a topological invariant.

Explicit local Lagrangian counterterms may be computed in terms of the Riemann tensor and its traces, using, for example, heat kernel methods
[
15
]
.

This result is directly comparable to the breaking of the duality between massless
p
- and
(q=d-p-2)
-form theories studied by Duff and Nieuwenhuizen in
d=4

[
21
]
, who concluded that the duality is broken by counterterms proportional to
\chi
. Indeed, our result can be seen as a direct consequence, as the breaking is seen to come only from the inequivalent dualisations of the integrated fields to the massless longitudinal degree of freedom of their partner.

Examples of quantum non-equivalence

We will now give some explicit examples in
4

0
-forms

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\quad\text{$3$-forms}

(74)

1
-forms

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\quad\text{$2$-forms}~,

(75)

for which we have

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{0\leftrightarrow 3}

\displaystyle=\frac{+1}{\varepsilon}(B_{0}-B_{1}+B_{2}-B_{3}+B_{4})

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{\varepsilon}(B_{0}-B_{1}+B_{2}-B_{3}+B_{4})~.

The local counterterms are proportional to

\displaystyle b_{0}-b_{1}+b_{2}-b_{3}+b_{4}=\frac{1}{32\pi^{2}}(R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}-4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+R^{2})~,

which is a total divergence, and integrates to the Euler characteristic (up to boundary corrections) by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem

In flat Euclidean space, all the normalisable Betti numbers vanish, so the theories are dual. We will look at three particular non-trivial cases in detail: the Euclidean Schwarzschild spacetime, the Euclidean de Sitter spacetime, and the Eguchi–Hansen instanton.

6.1
Euclidean Schwarzschild spacetime

The Euclidean Schwarzschild spacetime
M
describes the thermal Hawking radiation of non-rotating neutral black holes
[
42
]
. It is described by the metric

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\Big(1-\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}}{r}\Big)\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}+\Big(1-\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}}{r}\Big)^{-1}\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^{2})\,,

(79)

covering the whole manifold except the 2-sphere at

, the event horizon.
To make the spacetime everywhere regular (so the spacetime can be interpreted as an smooth continuation of its Lorentzian counterpart) the imaginary time coordinate
\tau

4\pi r_{\mathrm{h}}=1/T_{\mathrm{H}}
, and loops in

This spacetime is homotopic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{2}
, where

and
\tau
act as the radial and polar coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively, and
S^{2}
is spanned by the usual angular coordinates,
\theta
and
\phi
. Because

is asymptotically locally flat, normalisable

-forms on

2

[
46
]
. This changes the relevant topology to
M\cup\{\infty\}\cong S^{2}\times S^{2}
. As described in
[
56
]
, this topology supports harmonic 2-forms
\omega_{(n,k)}
wrapped around each 2-sphere, labelled by integer electric and magnetic charges,
(n,k)\in\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\omega_{(n,k)}=\frac{n}{2r^{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau\wedge\mathrm{d}r+\frac{k}{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\wedge\mathrm{d}\phi~.

(80)

There are no other normalisable harmonic
p
-forms on

[
34
]
, so

\displaystyle B_{k}=\begin{cases}2\,,&k=2\\
0\,,&\text{else}\end{cases}\quad.

(81)

This reproduces the Euler characteristic for the non-compactified spacetime,
\chi=\sum_{k}(-1)^{k}B_{k}=2
(c.f.
[
47

Thus

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{0\leftrightarrow 3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}

(82)

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}~,

(83)

so both dualities are broken.

6.2
Euclidean de Sitter

Because the Lorentzian de Sitter space lacks a global timelike Killing vector, an ordinary theory of scattering amplitudes is badly defined
[
29

70

\displaystyle B_{k}=\begin{cases}1\,,&k=0,d\\

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{0\leftrightarrow 3}

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}

6.3
Eguchi–Hanson instantons

The Eguchi–Hanson instantons
[
30

]
are the asymptotically locally Euclidean spacetimes with metric

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\Big(1-\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}\Big)^{-1}\mathrm{d}r^{2}+r^{2}(\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2})+r^{2}\Big(1-\frac{a^{4}}{r^{4}}\Big)\sigma_{z}^{2}\,,

\mathrm{d}\sigma_{i}=\varepsilon_{ijk}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{j}\wedge\mathrm{d}\sigma_{k}

The unique

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sigma_{x}\wedge\sigma_{y}-\frac{1}{r^{3}}\mathrm{d}r\wedge\sigma_{z}\,,

(88)

which is locally the exterior derivative of

\displaystyle B_{k}=\begin{cases}1\,,&k=2\\
0\,,&\text{else}\end{cases}\quad.

(89)

Note that the Euler characteristic receives a boundary correction, such that

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{0\leftrightarrow 3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

(90)

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mathrm{mass.}}_{1\leftrightarrow 2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}~,

(91)

so both dualities are broken yet again.

This case is of particular interest, even for observers in flat space; if these spacetimes do represent instantons in quantum gravity as expected, then these differing topological numbers demonstrate that the duality between massive
p
-form theories is broken by quantum gravity even in the vacuum.

7
Conclusion

In this paper we have demonstrated that the classical duality between massive
p
- and
(d-p-1)
-form gauge theories is broken by quantum corrections on topologically non-trivial spacetime backgrounds. The breaking arises from the mismatch of zero modes, which demand counterterms differing by a topological invariant. This invariant is the Euler characteristic of the spacetime, up to possible boundary corrections on non-compact manifolds.

The challenge of quantising a
p
-form gauge theory comes from the treatment of the gauge invariance, both in producing an appropriately gauge fixed path integral and gauge invariant mass term. For the former, we exhibit a novel gauge fixing functional. We achieve the latter with using the
BF
theory, where the manifestly gauge invariant topological coupling of a
p
- and
(d-p-1)
-form produces a mass term for one of the forms when the other is integrated out. In this formulation the non-equivalence is related to the quantum breaking of the massless
p
-form duality through the dualisation of the integrated-out form to a Stueckelberg field for its partner.

Considering specific examples in four dimensions, we have shown that the duality is broken in the thermal theories on Schwarzschild and de Sitter spaces. We have also shown that the duality is broken on the Eguchi–Hansen spacetime, which is thought to describe instantons in quantum gravity. These results suggest that the duality between massive theories may be broken even in the gravitational vacuum.

The essential topological structure of
p
-form gauge theories is that of de Rham cohomology. We have emphasised that, in both the classical and quantum cases, it is essential that the field strengths for dual massive
p
-form theories live in the trivial cohomology classes. In contrast to the massless theory, where the duality connects fields strengths in dual degrees,
\star F=G
, fields strengths for the massive theories in non-trivial cohomology classes would immediately violate the duality on backgrounds with non-isomorphic cohomology groups in the
p
-th and
(p-d-1)
-th degrees. In the classical theory this is enforced by the dynamics on-shell automatically. In the quantum
BF
theory the situations is less clear: choosing to quantise one field in the trivial sector automatically ensures that the other is trivial as well; on the other hand, the (purely quantum) theories in the non-trivial sectors require further study.

Unlike the field strengths, the cohomology classes of the
p
-form potential and ghost fields may be non-trivial. For each dimension of the cohomology group there is a corresponding zero mode of the Hodge Laplacian. These zero modes have zero action, so they contribute divergences that must be renormalised. The resulting counterterms break the duality in the quantum theory. This result, in essence, is a consequence of the topology of the gauge structure.

Our results are applicable to geodesically complete spacetimes, where the space of normalisable zero modes is sufficiently well-behaved. On spacetimes with a non-asymptotic boundary, boundary corrections will likely become more important; here a treatment of edge modes (see, for example,
[
6
]
) would be necessary.

Appendix A
Normalisation and components of
p
-forms

In this appendix we fix conventions relating differential forms and their components in a basis, as well as the normalisation of the field strength and kinetic terms of
p
-form gauge theories.

Let
\omega
and
\chi
be
p
-forms over a Riemannian manifold. Given a basis, a form and its components are related by

\displaystyle\omega=\frac{1}{p!}\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p}}~,

(A-1)

where
\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}
is totally antisymmetric. We work in Euclidean signature, in
d
dimensions. Given a metric
g_{\mu\nu}
with determinant
g
, the Hodge dual
\star
acts by

\displaystyle\star\omega=\frac{1}{(d-p)!}(\star\omega)_{\mu_{p+1}\cdots\mu_{d}}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p+1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{d}}=\frac{1}{p!(d-p)!}\varepsilon_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{d}}\omega^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p+1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{d}}\,,

(A-2)

with components

\displaystyle(\star\omega)_{\mu_{p+1}\cdots\mu_{d}}=\frac{1}{p!}\varepsilon_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{d}}\omega^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}~,

(A-3)

where
\varepsilon_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{d}}
is the covariant Levi-Civita tensor, with
\varepsilon_{12\cdots d}=\sqrt{g}
. The Hodge dual squares to
\star^{2}\omega=(-1)^{p(d-p)}\omega
acting on
p
-forms.

The Hodge dual defines a natural inner product on
p
-forms over a manifold

\displaystyle\langle\omega,\chi\rangle\equiv\int\omega\wedge\star\chi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p!}\int\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\chi^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\sqrt{g}\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x~,

(A-4)

where
\mathrm{d}^{d}x\equiv\mathrm{d}x^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{d}
is the volume form. The inner product is symmetric and duality invariant,
\langle\star\omega,\star\chi\rangle=\langle\omega,\chi\rangle
. A
p
-form
\omega
is said to
L^{2}
normalisable if
\langle\omega,\omega\rangle<\infty
.

The exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}
maps
p
-forms to
(p+1)
-forms, with

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega=\frac{1}{p!}\partial_{\mu_{0}}\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{0}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p}}=\frac{1}{p!}\partial_{[\mu_{0}}\omega_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{0}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p}}~,

(A-5)

where
[\,\cdots]
denotes the
normalised
antisymmetrisation of indexes.
The components are then

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}\omega)_{\mu_{0}\cdots\mu_{p}}=(p+1)\partial_{[\mu_{0}}\omega_{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{p}]}~.

(A-6)

Most importantly, this normalises the field strength for a
p
-form

\displaystyle F=\mathrm{d}A=\frac{1}{p!}\partial_{[\mu_{0}}A_{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{p}]}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{0}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{\mu_{p}}\,,\qquad F_{\mu_{0}\cdots\mu_{p}}=(p+1)\partial_{[\mu_{0}}A_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]}~.

(A-7)

The kinetic term in the action is then normalised

\displaystyle S_{p}[A]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int F\wedge\star F=\frac{1}{2(p+1)!}\int F_{\mu_{0}\cdots\mu_{p}}F^{\mu_{0}\cdots\mu_{p}}\sqrt{g}\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x~,

(A-8)

or in terms of the potential

\displaystyle=\frac{(p+1)}{2p!}\int\partial_{[\mu_{0}}A_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]}\partial^{[\mu_{0}}A^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{p}]}\sqrt{g}\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x~.

(A-9)

By expanding the antisymmetrisation, one can check that this is the canonical normalisation for each independent degree of freedom.

The codifferential
\delta
is defined as the adjoint of
\mathrm{d}
such that
\langle\mathrm{d}\omega,\chi\rangle=\langle\omega,\delta\chi\rangle
up to boundary terms. Using the definition of the inner product and Stokes theorem, we find that
\delta\omega=(-1)^{k}\star^{-1}\mathrm{d}\star\omega=(-1)^{(d-k-1)(k+1)+k}\star\mathrm{d}\star\omega
when acting on a
p
-form.

We denote by
\Box\equiv-(\delta\mathrm{d}+\mathrm{d}\delta)
the Hodge Laplacian (a.k.a. the Laplace–de Rham operator). This sign is opposite to that of much of the mathematical literature, but aligns with the usual Laplacian on scalars.
\Box
is essentially self-adjoint for normalisable
p
-forms on complete manifolds
[
14
]
and negative semi-definite, i.e. has only negative and zero eigenvalues. When necessary, we mark the operator with a subscript to indicate its restriction to a particular degree, such as when writing its determinant.

Appendix B
Derivation of gauge-fixing functional

In this section we provide a detailed derivation of the gauge-fixing method performed in Sec.
4
when quantising the massive
p
-form gauge theory. Firstly, we show that the gauge-fixing functional
G_{q}[B[\theta_{q-1}]]
defined in Eq. (
44
) is independent of
B
.

Suppose we have two
q
-forms
B
and
\tilde{B}
, with Hodge decompositions
B\equiv\mathrm{d}\alpha+\delta\beta+\gamma
and
\tilde{B}=\mathrm{d}\tilde{\alpha}+\delta\tilde{\beta}+\tilde{\gamma}
where
\gamma
,
\tilde{\gamma}
are harmonic forms. The gauge-transformed forms under this decomposition are simply
B[\theta_{q-1}]=\mathrm{d}(\alpha+\theta_{q-1})+\delta\beta+\gamma
and similar for
\tilde{B}[\theta_{q-1}]
. The identity Eq. (
44
) in terms of
B
can be written as

\displaystyle 1=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}\,G_{q}[B[\theta_{q-1}]]\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta\mathrm{d}(\alpha+\theta_{q-1}),\delta\mathrm{d}(\alpha+\theta_{q-1})\rangle\right)~.

(A-10)

We then perform a change of variables
\theta_{q-1}\to\theta_{q-1}^{\prime}=\alpha+\theta_{q-1}
, leaving the functional measure invariant, giving

\displaystyle 1=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}^{\prime}\,G_{q}[B[\theta_{q-1}]]\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}^{\prime},\delta\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}^{\prime}\rangle\right)~.

(A-11)

We can repeat the same steps for
\tilde{B}
and get a similar expression as above. Taking the difference between the two results gives

\displaystyle 0=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}^{\prime}\,\left(G_{q}[B[\theta_{q-1}]]-G_{q}[\tilde{B}[\theta_{q-1}]]\right)\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}^{\prime},\delta\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}^{\prime}\rangle\right)~.

(A-12)

This implies that the integrand must vanish, so
G_{q}[B[\theta_{q-1}]]=G_{q}[\tilde{B}[\theta_{q-1}]]
and the gauge-fixing functional
G_{q}
is independent of
B
(and hence
\theta_{q-1}
). We can thus factor it out of the integral and take its inverse to find

\displaystyle G_{q}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta B[\theta_{q-1}],\delta B[\theta_{q-1}]\rangle\right)

(A-13)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}\,\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\delta B,\delta B\rangle-2\langle\delta B,\Box\theta_{q-1}\rangle-\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1},\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}\rangle\right)\right]

(A-14)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}\,\exp\left(+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1},\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}\rangle\right)~,

(A-15)

where in the last equality, we have set
B=0
for convenience, knowing that both sides are independent of
B
anyway. If
q>1
then
G_{q}^{-1}
itself has a gauge redundancy
\theta_{q-1}\to\theta_{q-1}[\theta_{q-2}]=\theta_{q-1}+\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-2}
which we must also account for. Thus, we need to fix the gauge of our gauge-fixing function.

Seeing that Eq. (
A-15
) resembles the original path integral with an additional factor of
-\Box
, we define the gauge-for-gauge-fixing functional

\displaystyle G^{-1}_{q-1}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-2}\,\exp\!\left(+\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta\theta_{q-1}[\theta_{q-2}],\Box\delta\theta_{q-1}[\theta_{q-2}]\rangle\right)

(A-16)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-2}\,\exp\!\left(+\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\delta\theta_{q-1},\Box\delta\theta_{q-1}\rangle-2\langle\delta\Box\theta_{q-1},\Box\theta_{q-2}\rangle-\langle\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-2},\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-2}\rangle\Big]\right)~,

(A-17)

which is similarly independent of
\theta_{q-1}
. We insert
G_{q-1}^{-1}G_{q-1}=1
into Eq. (
A-15
) and, just like in the path integral, we factor out an overall gauge-equivalent volume. More explicitly, we start with

\displaystyle G_{q}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-2}\,G_{q-1}\exp\!\bigg(+\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1},\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}\rangle+\langle\delta\theta_{q-1},\Box\delta\theta_{q-1}\rangle

(A-18)

\displaystyle\hskip 156.49014pt-2\langle\delta\Box\theta_{q-1},\Box\theta_{q-2}\rangle-\langle\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-2},\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-2}\rangle\Big]\bigg)~.

This can be rewritten as

\displaystyle G_{q}^{-1}=G_{q-1}\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-1}\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-2}\,\exp\!\bigg(+\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}\big[\theta_{q-2}\big],\Box\mathrm{d}\theta_{q-1}\big[\theta_{q-2}\big]\rangle+\langle\delta\theta_{q-1}\big[\theta_{q-2}\big],\Box\delta\theta_{q-1}\big[\theta_{q-2}\big]\rangle\Big]\bigg)~.

(A-19)

Next we perform a change of variables
\theta_{q-1}\rightarrow\theta_{q-1}^{\prime}=\theta_{q-1}[\theta_{q-2}]
to get

\displaystyle G_{q}^{-1}

\displaystyle=G_{q-1}\int\mathcal{D}\theta^{\prime}_{q-1}\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-2}\,\exp\!\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\theta^{\prime}_{q-1},\Box^{2}\theta^{\prime}_{q-1}\rangle\bigg)

(A-20)

\displaystyle=\left(\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{q-2}\right)\left(\det\Box_{q-1}\right)^{-1}G_{q-1}~.

(A-21)

Inverting the above relation gives us an expression for
G_{q}
. If
q>2
, then again
G_{q-1}^{-1}
has its own gauge redundancy, and we must proceed recursively until we reach the scalar case
\theta_{0}
, which has no gauge invariance. For a general layer, we define

\displaystyle G_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-1}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\delta\theta_{n}[\theta_{n-1}],(-\Box)^{q-n}\delta\theta_{n}[\theta_{n-1}]\rangle\right)~,

(A-22)

for
n=1,\cdots,q
with
\theta_{q}\equiv B
and the terminating condition
G_{0}=1
. As before, we can expand this as

\displaystyle G_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-1}\,\exp\!\bigg((-1)^{q-n+1}\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\delta\theta_{n},\Box^{q-n}\delta\theta_{n}\rangle-2\langle\delta\theta_{n},\Box^{q-n+1}\theta_{n-1}\rangle-\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-1},\Box^{q-n+1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-1}\rangle\Big]\bigg)~,

(A-23)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-1}\,\exp\!\bigg((-1)^{q-n}\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-1},\Box^{q-n+1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-1}\rangle\Big]\bigg)~,

(A-24)

where in the last line we use the fact that
G_{n}
is independent of
\theta_{n}
.
Then using Eq. (
A-24
) and Eq. (
A-23
) for
G_{n}^{-1}
and
G_{n-1}G_{n-1}^{-1}=1
respectively, we find

\displaystyle G_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=G_{n-1}\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-1}\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-2}\,\exp\!\bigg((-1)^{q-n}\frac{1}{2}\Big[\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-1},\Box^{q-n+1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-1}\rangle+\langle\delta\theta_{n-1},\Box^{q-n+1}\delta\theta_{n-1}\rangle

(A-25)

\displaystyle\hskip 184.9429pt-2\langle\delta\theta_{n-1},\Box^{q-n+2}\theta_{n-2}\rangle-\langle\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-2},\Box^{q-n+2}\mathrm{d}\theta_{n-2}\rangle\Big]\bigg)~,

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle G_{n}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-2}\right)G_{n-1}\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-1}\,\exp\!\bigg((-1)^{q-n}\frac{1}{2}\langle\theta_{n-1},\Box^{q-n+2}\theta_{n-1}\rangle\bigg)

(A-26)

\displaystyle=\left(\int\mathcal{D}\theta_{n-2}\right)G_{n-1}(\det\Box_{n-1})^{(q-n+2)/2}~.

(A-27)

Unpacking this recursive relation, we find that

\displaystyle G_{q}=V_{\mathrm{gauge}}\prod_{k=1}^{q}\left(\det\Box_{q-k}\right)^{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)/2}~,

(A-28)

where

46

Appendix C
Hodge theory and
L^{2}
cohomology

Let
M
be a manifold. Recall that a
k
-form
\alpha
on
M
is
closed
if
\mathrm{d}\alpha=0
and
exact
if
\alpha=\mathrm{d}\beta
for some
(k-1)
-form
\beta
. Because
\mathrm{d}^{2}=0
, every exact form is closed, but not every closed form is exact. The failure of this exactness is measured by the
cohomology group
of degree
k
, quotient group

\displaystyle H^{k}(M)\equiv\frac{\{\text{closed $k$-forms}\}}{\{\text{exact $k$-forms}\}}~.

(A-29)

This is a homotopy invariant of
M
.

The Poincaré lemma states that
H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cong 0
, so any closed form is always exact on a topologically trivial local neighbourhood. In general, given a collection of topologically trivial local neighbourhoods
\{U^{i}\}
covering
M=\bigcup_{i}U^{i}
, a closed form
\alpha
is exact on each neighbourhood,
\alpha\big|_{U^{i}}=\mathrm{d}\beta^{i}\big|_{U^{i}}
, with each
\beta^{i}
differing by a closed form where they overlap.

When computing the determinant of the Hodge Laplacian
\Box_{k}
acting on
k
-forms, there is a divergent contribution from each zero mode appearing in the path integral. We call such
k
-forms
harmonic
, and denote the space of harmonic
k
-forms by
\mathcal{H}^{k}(M)
. To regulate this divergence, we must introduce counterterms proportional to the number of such zero modes.

On compact manifolds, the Hodge theorem
[
8
]
tells us that the number of such zero modes is counted by the Betti numbers, which equal the dimensions of the cohomology groups

\displaystyle B_{k}=\dim H^{k}(M)~.

(A-30)

See, for example, Christensen and Duff
[
15
]
. By the Poincaré duality, there is a symmetry between harmonic forms in dual degree,
B_{k}=B_{d-k}
.

On the other hand, on non-compact manifolds the path integral only receives contributions from zero modes
\omega
which are
L^{2}
normalisable, i.e. have finite
\langle\omega,\omega\rangle<\infty
. The theory of
L^{2}
harmonic forms is in general much more complicated—for example, the
L^{2}
cohomology is not homotopy invariant but instead depends on the quasi-isometry class of the metric
[
19
]
, and there is generally no symmetry between the
L^{2}
Betti numbers in dual degrees. Nonetheless, it has been shown that on geodesically complete manifolds there is an isomorphism between the ‘reduced’
L^{2}
cohomology
L^{2}\overline{H}{}^{k}(M)
and the
L^{2}
harmonic forms
{L^{2}}\mathcal{H}^{k}(M)
. This has been used to compute the
L^{2}
harmonic forms on (for example) the Eguchi–Hansen space
[
51
]
and the Euclidean Schwarzschild manifold
[
34
]
. In this case the
L^{2}
Betti numbers, which we defined to be the dimensions of
L^{2}\mathcal{H}^{k}(M)
, remain duality symmetric. It is these modes which enter into the computation of the
k
-form determinants.

We also rely on complete manifolds to ensure that potential boundary terms vanish. In particular, over
L^{2}
forms on complete manifolds there is no boundary term disrupting integration by parts,
\langle\mathrm{d}\omega,\chi\rangle=\langle\omega,\delta\chi\rangle

[
40
]
, and
\Box
is essentially self-adjoint
[
14
]
.
