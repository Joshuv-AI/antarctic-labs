Dynamic driver drowsiness detection with attention enhanced convolutional neural networks for real time monitoring and road safety applications
Authors: Samy Abd El-Nabi, Ahmed F. Ibrahim, Hosny. H. Abo Emira, Samah Alshathri, El-Sayed M. El-Rabaie, Ahmed Emam, Walid El-Shafai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Driver fatigue remains a major contributor to traffic accidents worldwide, underscoring the need for accurate and timely drowsiness detection systems. This work presents a real-time driver drowsiness detection framework utilizing deep learning-based facial analysis. The approach integrates pre-trained models (VGG19, VGG16, ResNet150, and DenseNet201) and a custom convolutional neural network (CNN) with an attention mechanism to classify drowsiness based on eye and mouth behavior. These models were fine-tuned and evaluated on the NTHU-DDD dataset. Experimental results show that the proposed CNN with attention mechanism achieves the highest performance, with an accuracy of 99.63% and perfect precision, recall, and F1-score. The attention mechanism further improves detection by emphasizing relevant facial regions. This framework demonstrates the potential for deployment in real-world driver monitoring systems and contributes to advancing driver-assistance technologies.

Introduction

Driver fatigue remains a pervasive and critical challenge to road safety, contributing significantly to the occurrence of vehicular collisions and fatalities across the globe. Despite substantial advancements in transportation infrastructure and the development of sophisticated safety technologies, the inability to effectively detect and mitigate driver drowsiness has left this issue largely unresolved. Fatigue impairs cognitive function, reduces attentiveness, and slows reaction times, significantly increasing the risk of accidents1,2. Professional drivers, particularly those subjected to strenuous schedules and extended working hours, are disproportionately affected, often operating under conditions that exacerbate fatigue-induced risks3,4.

Recent statistical analyses underscore the gravity of this issue. According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), driver fatigue is responsible for approximately 100,000 collisions annually in the United States, resulting in 71,000 injuries and economic damages exceeding $12.5 billion5. Furthermore, the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety attributes nearly 6,400 annual fatalities in the U.S. to drowsy driving6. Between 2017 and 2021, drowsiness accounted for nearly 30,000 fatalities, representing approximately 18% of all traffic-related deaths7. Globally, the prevalence of fatigue-related accidents is similarly concerning, with underreporting in official statistics likely masking the true extent of the problem. This alarming reality highlights an urgent need for innovative, scalable solutions capable of addressing the multifaceted risks associated with driver fatigue​.

The severity of the issue extends globally, with alarming statistics reflecting the widespread impact of driver fatigue. Studies estimate that drowsy driving contributes to 25–30% of all road accidents in the United Kingdom and is implicated in an astounding 85% of fatal traffic accidents in France8. In addition, between 2006 and 2010, fatigue-related driving was responsible for 18% of road deaths and 17% of fatal motor vehicle accidents globally9. However, the actual impact of drowsy driving is likely underestimated due to significant underreporting, a pervasive issue across all regions10. These figures emphasize the urgent necessity for multifaceted solutions, including public awareness campaigns to educate drivers, stricter legislation to enforce safer driving practices11, and cutting-edge monitoring technologies to address this preventable risk effectively.

While advancements in driver assistance technologies, such as automatic braking, lane departure warnings, and adaptive cruise control12, have substantially enhanced vehicle safety by reducing collisions and improving control13, these systems fall short of addressing driver drowsiness. This critical limitation persists despite drowsiness being a significant contributing factor in numerous road accidents14. Real-time identification of driver fatigue and timely intervention remain unachieved in many advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS), underscoring the pressing need for innovative, adaptive solutions15.

Deep learning emerges as a promising paradigm to address this challenge16. Unlike traditional machine learning techniques, deep learning models excel at processing and analyzing complex visual data17, including nuanced facial expressions, eye movements, and other subtle fatigue indicators18. By employing transfer learning techniques, researchers can fine-tune pre-trained models such as VGG19 and ResNet50V2 on domain-specific datasets to detect early signs of drowsiness in drivers with high accuracy and efficiency19,20. These models can not only identify fatigue symptoms in real-time but also generate timely alerts to prevent potential disasters. This approach has the potential to outperform traditional methods while offering a scalable, reliable framework to enhance road safety and mitigate the risks associated with drowsy driving.

Literature review

Because of the shortcomings of conventional monitoring systems, driver drowsiness detection has emerged as a crucial area of study in road safety. More complex methods for identifying weariness have been made possible by recent developments in computer vision, deep learning, and machine learning. Based on feature selection, algorithmic techniques, and practical applicability, this section classifies and critically evaluates earlier research, emphasizing the study’s advantages, disadvantages, and research gaps.

Feature-based approaches

Numerous research has depended on particular physiological or visual cues of sleepiness. For example23, used dashboard-mounted cameras and CNNs to identify yawning. CNNs were successful in localizing the mouth region, but their generalizability was constrained by their reliance on a single indicator (yawning) without quantitative assessment. In a similar vein24, employed facial landmarks to identify tiredness, reporting an overall accuracy of 83% with accuracies of 88% during the day and 85% during the night. Even with encouraging outcomes, these techniques had trouble capturing a variety of real-world situations where several fatigue signs coexist.

Other research looked at blink patterns and eye movement as important characteristics. The importance of combining spatial and temporal information was highlighted by the hybrid CNN–BiLSTM strategy in25, which increased accuracy from 85% (CNN just) to 94% (CNN + BiLSTM). Similarly26, used C-LSTM for eye movement analysis and obtained 95–97% accuracy; nevertheless, eye-only detection presents difficulties in complicated situations. In addition to visual cues27, presented EEG signals for the identification of tiredness. This approach provided a new neurophysiological viewpoint, but its practical usefulness was limited by its 73.22% accuracy.

Algorithmic innovations

In this field, deep learning architectures have undergone substantial development. On the NTHU-DDD dataset, a system that combined InceptionV3 with LSTM in28 obtained 93.69% training and 81.04% testing accuracy; nevertheless, performance was negatively impacted by its dependence on a small number of facial features. Similar to this29, used several CNN models (AlexNet, VGG-FaceNet, FlowImageNet, and ResNet) to categorise fatigue behaviours. They were able to achieve 85% accuracy at the expense of high computational complexity, which limited their ability to operate in real time.

ResNet and LSTM were merged in hierarchical and hybrid techniques, such the HDDD network in30,31, to analyse spatial and temporal features simultaneously, with an accuracy of 87.19%. Additionally, in order to assess driving states (normal, fatigued, inebriated, and irresponsible)32, developed emotion analysis with CNNs. This achieved 93% accuracy; however it mostly relied on predefined emotional categories. Some models, however, fared poorly:33 coupled CNNs and RNNs over 60-second image sequences, but only obtained 60% testing accuracy, indicating difficulties in accurately modelling temporal dependencies.

Real-time and mobile-oriented systems

There is still a significant research gap in practical applications. Using facial landmark detection, the Android-based system in34 demonstrated portability but lacked resilience for a range of situations, achieving 80% accuracy. Concerns regarding real-time applicability in embedded systems are also raised by the computationally demanding frameworks in29,30.

NTHU-DDD dataset

The NTHU-DDD dataset, a comprehensive and enhanced version of the original NTHU-DDD dataset, is an essential resource for advancing research in driver drowsiness detection. Developed by the Computer Vision Lab at National Tsing Hua University, this dataset includes meticulously annotated features that capture key fatigue indicators such as eye state, mouth movements, head positions, and overall drowsiness levels. These detailed annotations facilitate multi-task learning, enabling models to predict multiple behaviors simultaneously, thereby improving their efficiency and mitigating overfitting risks.

One of the most significant strengths of the dataset lies in its ability to reflect real-world driving scenarios. It incorporates diverse conditions, including variations in lighting, head poses, and facial expressions, ensuring robust model training and testing. This diversity equips deep learning algorithms with the capacity to detect drowsiness in dynamic environments, enhancing their applicability in real-time settings.

The dataset’s utility extends to supporting advanced deep learning architectures, such as Inception-ResNet, for efficient spatial feature analysis and accurate prediction of fatigue-related behaviors. Its rich annotations and comprehensive nature make it a foundational tool for developing reliable and scalable drowsiness detection systems. By addressing the critical challenges of generalization and real-time detection, the NTHU-DDD dataset has become instrumental in advancing road safety through cutting-edge driver-monitoring technologies.

Once the data has been acquired, it undergoes preprocessing to ensure that the input is standardized and optimized for training in deep learning models. The preprocessing stage involves several key operations:

A.Data resizing

A.Data resizing

Data resizing

In the preprocessing phase, the images extracted from video frames are resized to a uniform resolution of 128 × 128 pixels. This resizing step ensures consistency across all images in the dataset, which is essential for CNNs that require fixed input dimensions. Without uniform sizing, CNNs would encounter processing inconsistencies during training, potentially leading to inefficiencies and reduced learning capacity.

By standardizing the image dimensions, the model can efficiently process each frame without requiring additional configurations or separate models for varying image sizes. This uniformity simplifies the data pipeline and allows the network to focus on detecting patterns related to driver drowsiness. Furthermore, resizing ensures that computational resources are used effectively, striking a balance between preserving critical image details and reducing the input size for faster processing.

This preprocessing step plays a pivotal role in improving the model’s ability to learn patterns associated with fatigue-related behaviors, such as eyelid movements, yawning, and facial expressions, ensuring reliable and robust performance across the dataset.

B.Face, eye, and mouth detection with Dlib

Face, eye, and mouth detection with Dlib

To enhance the accuracy of driver drowsiness detection, the system employs the Dlib library for facial landmark detection. Dlib is a robust and widely adopted tool for face detection and analysis, capable of identifying critical facial features such as the eyes, mouth, and overall face shape. These features are instrumental in detecting subtle signs of fatigue, including drooping eyelids, frequent blinking, and yawning. By focusing on these specific facial cues, the system can effectively assess a driver’s state of alertness with greater precision.

Face detection isolates the most relevant region of the image, the driver’s face, where signs of drowsiness are most evident. Within this region, the eyes and mouth serve as primary indicators, enabling the system to monitor critical behaviors such as blinking frequency, squinting, and yawning. These behaviors provide valuable insights into fatigue-related states, ensuring a more comprehensive and nuanced analysis. By leveraging Dlib’s precise facial landmark detection capabilities, the system enhances its ability to track and interpret these key features, significantly improving the reliability and timeliness of drowsiness predictions.

This detailed analysis of facial features not only increases detection accuracy but also ensures prompt and dependable alerts, contributing to enhanced driver safety.

Data rescaling

This normalization step standardizes the input data, ensuring uniformity across all pixel values. Normalization plays a critical role in enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of the training process, as neural networks perform optimally when input features are scaled consistently.

By transforming pixel values into a uniform range, the model avoids being disproportionately influenced by extreme values within the dataset. This helps to stabilize the learning process and accelerates model convergence, allowing for faster and more reliable training. Additionally, rescaling ensures that all features contribute equally to the learning process, reducing the risk of numerical instabilities and improving the overall performance of the neural network.

This preprocessing step is crucial for building a robust and efficient driver drowsiness detection system, as it lays the foundation for stable and accurate model performance during training and inference.

A.VGG16

A.VGG16

VGG16

VGG16 is a widely recognized CNN architecture introduced by the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) at Oxford University in 2014. It comprises sixteen layers, including thirteen convolutional layers and three fully connected layers, with max-pooling layers strategically placed between convolutional layers to downsample the spatial dimensions of the input. What sets VGG16 apart is its simplicity and uniform design, as all convolutional layers employ 3 × 3 filters and max-pooling layers use a consistent 2 × 2 kernel. This straightforward structure makes VGG16 highly implementable while maintaining its robust feature extraction capabilities.

The depth of VGG16 plays a crucial role in its ability to capture hierarchical features. The initial layers detect fundamental patterns, such as edges and textures, while deeper layers capture more complex and abstract features, such as facial expressions. This hierarchical feature extraction is particularly beneficial for driver drowsiness detection, where recognizing subtle facial cues like blinking, eyelid closure, and head nodding is essential. While the computational cost of VGG16 is relatively high due to its depth, its precision and versatility make it an ideal candidate for tasks requiring detailed pattern recognition, such as detecting signs of driver fatigue.

When fine-tuned on domain-specific datasets like NTHU-DDD, VGG16 demonstrates exceptional adaptability in detecting facial features associated with driver fatigue. This fine-tuning allows the model to classify drivers’ states as alert or fatigued accurately, providing critical real-time alerts to enhance road safety and mitigate fatigue-induced risks. The model’s ability to generalize effectively to fatigue-specific patterns ensures its reliability in diverse driving conditions.

B.VGG19

B.VGG19

VGG19

VGG19, developed by the same team behind VGG16, is an extended version of the VGG16 architecture, consisting of nineteen layers in total. This deeper model includes sixteen convolutional layers and three fully connected layers, with additional convolutional layers allowing VGG19 to capture more complex and nuanced features. While maintaining the core design principles of VGG16, such as the use of 3 × 3 convolutional filters and 2 × 2 max-pooling layers, VGG19’s increased depth makes it more adept at detecting subtle and intricate patterns, which are essential in tasks like driver drowsiness detection.

The deeper architecture of VGG19 enables it to extract hierarchical features at varying levels of abstraction. While VGG16 is proficient in identifying prominent facial expressions, VGG19 refines these capabilities, excelling in detecting subtle cues such as slight blinking, gradual eyelid closures, or minor head nods. These subtle indicators are critical for accurately distinguishing between alert and fatigued drivers, making VGG19 an excellent choice for applications requiring high precision and sensitivity.

However, the enhanced feature extraction capabilities of VGG19 come at the cost of increased computational demands, including higher memory usage and longer processing times. Despite these challenges, VGG19 remains a popular choice in transfer learning due to its pretrained weights from large datasets like ImageNet. Fine-tuning VGG19 on domain-specific datasets such as NTHU-DDD allows the model to adapt to specialized tasks like facial expression recognition and drowsiness detection, ensuring robust and reliable performance in real-time driver monitoring systems. This fine-tuning process enables VGG19 to effectively capture fatigue-related behaviors, such as eye closures and yawning, ultimately improving road safety by delivering timely alerts to mitigate risks.

C.ResNet50

C.ResNet50

ResNet50

ResNet (Residual Networks), introduced by Kaiming He and colleagues in 2015, revolutionized deep learning by addressing the challenges of training very deep neural networks. The innovation of residual connections, which enable the network to bypass one or more layers during training, effectively solved the problem of vanishing gradients, a persistent issue in deep architectures. These residual connections facilitate the flow of gradients during backpropagation, allowing networks with hundreds or even thousands of layers to train efficiently without performance degradation. ResNet50, a fifty-layer architecture, has become one of the most widely used models for complex computer vision tasks, such as image classification, object detection, and facial recognition.

A defining feature of ResNet50 is its use of residual connections, which allow the network to skip specific layers if they are deemed redundant during training. This capability ensures stable gradient flow, enabling the network to learn complex features more effectively while maintaining efficient training. The architecture’s depth and ability to extract detailed hierarchical features make it particularly well-suited for applications like driver drowsiness detection, where identifying subtle facial cues such as blinking, eyelid closure, and head nodding is critical. Despite the computational demands of its deeper architecture, ResNet50 consistently outperforms many other models by efficiently capturing and processing intricate patterns.

In the context of driver drowsiness detection, ResNet50’s ability to detect fine-grained facial cues is invaluable. It excels in identifying micro-expressions, such as slight changes in eyelid movements or subtle head nods, which are key indicators of fatigue. These capabilities make it a powerful tool for real-time monitoring systems, where the early detection of fatigue-related behaviors can significantly enhance road safety by preventing accidents. By fine-tuning ResNet50 on specialized datasets like NTHU-DDD, the model adapts to domain-specific features, achieving high accuracy in classifying facial cues related to alertness and fatigue. This adaptability and precision establish ResNet50 as an essential component in the development of robust driver monitoring systems.

DenseNet201

DenseNet (Densely Connected Convolutional Networks), introduced by Gao Huang and colleagues in 2017, represents a significant advancement in deep learning architecture. DenseNet enhances traditional CNNs by introducing dense connections, where each layer receives input from all preceding layers. This approach contrasts with standard CNNs, where layers typically rely only on direct connections from the immediately prior layer. By enabling extensive feature reuse, DenseNet achieves more efficient learning and requires fewer parameters to deliver performance that matches or surpasses traditional models. DenseNet201, a specific variant of this architecture, consists of 201 layers and showcases the effectiveness of densely connected networks in handling complex tasks.

A defining characteristic of DenseNet201 is its dense connectivity. In this architecture, each layer benefits from the features learned by all earlier layers, fostering a richer representation of the data. This connectivity enables the network to capture intricate patterns, making it particularly well-suited for applications like facial expression recognition in driver drowsiness detection. For instance, dense connections allow the model to effectively learn subtle facial cues such as blinking patterns, yawning, and slight head movements, essential indicators of fatigue. This feature reuse mechanism not only enhances learning but also reduces the number of parameters required for training, making DenseNet201 more efficient in both training and optimization compared to traditional deep networks.

DenseNet201 also addresses the common problem of vanishing gradients in deep networks, similar to ResNet. By ensuring a strong flow of gradients throughout the network, DenseNet supports effective backpropagation, even in very deep architectures. This capability is critical for training on large datasets, as it prevents stagnation during learning and improves model performance over time. Furthermore, the feature reuse inherent in DenseNet201 enables it to achieve superior accuracy with fewer parameters, making it an ideal choice for real-time systems like driver drowsiness detection. Its computational efficiency ensures that DenseNet201 can be deployed in resource-constrained environments without compromising on speed or precision, making it a valuable tool for enhancing road safety.

Conclusion, limitations, and future studies

In conclusion, the proposed CNN with an attention mechanism establishes a new benchmark in driver drowsiness detection, achieving exceptional performance across all evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. The integration of the attention mechanism enables the model to dynamically focus on critical facial regions, such as the eyes and mouth, enhancing its ability to detect subtle fatigue indicators, including blinking, yawning, and eyelid closure. This capability makes the model not only highly efficient but also robust, paving the way for reliable and scalable real-time driver monitoring systems.

Despite these achievements, this work is not without its limitations. The model’s performance heavily relies on the NTHU-DDD dataset, which, while comprehensive, may not fully capture the diversity of real-world driving conditions, such as variations in lighting, weather, and cultural differences in facial features and behaviors. Additionally, the computational requirements for the attention mechanism and deep learning architecture may pose challenges for deployment in resource-constrained environments, such as edge devices in vehicles.
