Ecological and methodological insights from genetic and coprological profiling of gastrointestinal communities in wild howler monkeys
Authors: Silvia Carboni, Colin Macfarland, Saul Cheves Hernandez, Andre G. Buret, Susan Kutz, Amanda D. Melin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gastrointestinal tract hosts a complex community of microorganisms and helminth parasites that collectively contribute to host health and fitness. Analysis of these communities provides insight into diverse aspects of host dietary ecology, immunity, nutrition, and host-parasite interactions. However, research methodologies, such as sample preservation and sequencing approach, can influence how we understand and characterize these features. Here, we profiled the gastrointestinal microbial and helminth communities in different groups of wild Costa Rican mantled howler monkeys (Alouatta palliata palliata). We compared samples stored in ethanol versus directly flash frozen, and contrasted conclusions drawn from 16S versus shotgun sequencing approaches. Bacterial, archaeal, and eukaryotic taxa associated with the digestion of plant material dominated the GI communities. Storage and sequencing methods influenced microbial profiles: ethanol-stored samples exhibited higher diversity than frozen samples, and 16S sequencing detected lower diversity than shotgun. Helminths were detected via coprological microscopy in 71% of individuals, whereas metagenomic detection was inconsistent. This study provides new data on the microorganisms and their putative digestive functions in the gut of a folivorous primate, and highlights the pros and cons of different methodological choices when profiling host-microbiome and host-parasite interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57628-6.

Introduction

The gastrointestinal (GI) ecosystem is comprised of a complex and dynamic community of organisms that play essential roles in health and digestion1. In mammals, most research on the gut ecosystem has focused on the microbial community—the gut microbiome—and on parasitic helminths, both of which play key roles in shaping host behavior, health and physiology2–4. However, data on the gut ecosystem are still comparatively scarce in wild systems, especially regarding metagenomic characterization of microbial taxa and their putative functions5. Additionally, we know relatively little about sources of microbial and helminth variation in wild populations. For example, while wild species are increasingly exposed to changes in their environment, reported effects of anthropogenically disturbed habitats on microbial diversity and parasite communities vary widely6–11.

As interest in this topic continues to grow, researchers are inquiring how sample storage and processing methods influence conclusions about the reliability and accuracy of findings related to the structure, composition, and stability of gut communities12–14. Microbiome studies are perhaps especially sensitive to storage conditions, more so than helminth ones, because microbial communities can shift rapidly after collection through ongoing metabolic activity, rapid bacterial and fungal growth, and variability in taxa survival13,15–17. The gold standard storage method involves freezing the samples immediately after collection to limit these processes and preserve the original community composition18. However, immediate freezing is not always practical in non-clinical studies, and thus the use of preservatives such as RNAlater, FTA cards, and ethanol, has been proposed as an alternative16,19.

RNAlater is generally effective for preserving complex microbial communities, but may allow shifts if samples are stored at room temperature for extended periods19–21. FTA cards are accessible, inexpensive, and reliable under ambient conditions, although they may underrepresent certain bacterial taxa17,19–21. Ethanol is commonly used to study both helminth and microbial communities as it fixes DNA and inhibits the DNA-degrading enzymes13,15,22, but may not be as efficient in preserving all the taxa, possibly lowering microbial diversity. Although previous studies have examined how different storage methods can influence measures of microbial diversity e.g12,15–17,19.,, more research on wild populations is needed to determine which taxa are more affected, and how these effects may in turn impact ecological interpretations.

Sequencing approaches represent another critical source of methodological variation that can significantly shape analyses and results. Our understanding of the gut microbiome is largely based on analyses of bacterial and archaeal communities through 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (hereafter 16S). This widely used approach targets the conserved 16S rRNA gene in bacteria and archaea, using variable regions for taxonomic identification via established reference databases. It is cost-effective and well-documented in literature, allowing for cross-study comparisons. However, it has limitations, including low taxonomic resolution, often not reaching the species level, and it only captures bacterial and archaeal members of the community, omitting other taxa and potentially underestimating diversity14,23. Due to these limitations, there is growing interest in shallow shotgun metagenomic sequencing (hereafter shotgun), which captures the wider genetic content of a sample and enables finer taxonomic characterizations across all domains of life, as well as the detection of rare and less characterized taxa14,24. While this method offers greater resolution, it is costly, bioinformatically complex, still reliant on database choices, and thus data are still scarce, especially in wild populations and in larger scale studies24.

In this study, we expand existing datasets on the GI microbial and helminth communities and explore the effect of methodology on results derived from fecal samples of wild Costa Rican howler monkeys (Alouatta palliata palliata). We applied different storage and sequencing methods to evaluate impacts on metrics of diversity and composition, and to determine the most suitable approach for addressing specific research questions. Our first aim was to provide a comprehensive description of the GI microbial and helminth communities of wild mantled howler monkeys found in Costa Rican tropical dry forests, using: i) 16S sequencing for taxonomic profiling, ii) shotgun metagenomics taxonomic and functional profiling, and iii) coprological identification. Our second aim was to directly compare the core microbiota obtained from different storage and sequencing methods, and we predicted that: i) 16S sequencing of samples stored in ethanol would reveal lower diversity and differences in the relative abundance of core taxa compared to frozen samples, and ii) shotgun sequencing would detect higher core diversity than 16S sequencing.

Methods

Study species and sites

Howler monkeys are medium-sized and sexually dimorphic (males ca. 6.5 Kg; females ca. 4 Kg), arboreal, folivorous-frugivorous primates found in Mexico and throughout the tropical forests of Central and South America25–27. They are among the few primates that rely heavily to entirely on leaves as their primary food source27. Unlike many other leaf-eating mammals, they have a relatively simple stomach and depend heavily on microbial interactions to digest complex plant materials, including starches and fibers6,25,26,28. Howler monkeys are also known to carry helminths as part of their natural state, with the most commonly reported genera being Enterobius, Strongyloides, Trypanoxyuris, and Controrchis25,29. Additionally, these primates are found in a range of different environments, showing notable ecological adaptability30.

Our study subjects are wild mantled howler monkeys (Alouatta palliata palliata) from the Guanacaste province of northwestern Costa Rica. The region is characterized by large seasonal variation31, with distinct dry (mid-November to mid-May) and wet (mid-May to mid-November) seasons32,33. We collected samples from three locations: Sector Santa Rosa (“SSR”), a nearby ranch (“Ranch”), and an urban context (“Town”). SSR has a continuous tropical dry forest and is the least anthropogenically disturbed. It is one of the 18 sectors of the Área de Conservación Guanacaste, a UNESCO world heritage site, a biodiversity hotspot, and an example of a successful ecological recovery of one of the world’s most endangered habitats34,35. “Ranch” is about 40 km from SSR, on the border of an active cattle ranch and eco-tourist lodge located off a major continental highway. Here, the howler group lived in the trees planted around the border, primarily mango trees. “Town” is an urbanized site on the Nicoya peninsula. Howlers here traveled among a mosaic of natural and cultivated fruit trees located near a condominium complex and small residential houses.

Sample collection

We collected one fecal sample per individual for 21 howlers living in seven groups (Santa Rosa: 5 groups, 13 individuals; Town 1 group, 4 individuals; Ranch 1 group, 4 individuals; Supp. Table 1) immediately following defecation. Samples were collected within a 2-week period during the rainy season in July 2017 to minimize seasonal variation. Although some environmental contamination during collection is unavoidable in non-invasive fecal sampling, we attempted to minimize this risk using clean nitrile gloves, sterile tubes, and the extraction protocols described below. Upon collection, each fecal sample was divided into two portions: one was placed in 8 ml of pre-aliquoted 96% ethanol and stored at room temperature, while the second portion was placed in a 2 ml sterile cryotube and frozen upon arrival at camp in a liquid nitrogen dry shipper. All samples were subsequently transported to the University of Calgary. Ethanol samples were kept at room temperature away from direct light, and cryotubes were transferred to a -80 °C freezer for storage. The 21 samples stored in ethanol were used both for the coprological study to identify helminths, and to examine the bacterial composition through 16S sequencing (Santa Rosa n = 13, Ranch n = 4, Town n = 4). Of the 21 frozen samples, we used a portion for 16S sequencing, and another for shotgun sequencing. For shotgun sequencing, we analysed a majority subset: 19 of the 21 samples (Santa Rosa n =13, Ranch n = 3, Town n = 3) (Supp. Table 1).

Parasite flotation and identification

We identified parasites in the Kutz Research Laboratory at the University of Calgary through sucrose flotation and coprological microscopy following the Canadian Wildlife Health Cooperative’s standard operating procedures. Samples were homogenized by pulverizing and mixing in a disposable cup with a tongue depressor, during which we added approximately 12 ml of reverse osmosis water. This mixture was then filtered through a double layer of cheesecloth to separate large solids, and the cup was rinsed to wash any extra fecal slurry through the filter. The filtrate was then poured into a test tube, centrifuged for 10 minutes at 500 x g, and the supernatant was disposed of, leaving the precipitate of fecal slurry. Next, a small volume of Sheather’s sugar solution36 was added, and the tube was vortexed to mix it with the fecal slurry. The tubes were then filled with Sheather’s until a meniscus was visible above the test tube. A coverslip was set on top of each tube, and they were spun for another 10 minutes at 500 x g to allow any parasites to attach to the coverslip. The coverslips were then taken off and placed on a slide for microscopic analysis. We inspected the slides under 400x magnification, counted all eggs, cysts, and larvae, and took photos of suspected parasites for identification. Parasites were identified based on size, shape, and color, following published morphological criteria for different taxa4,13,37–40. We were not able to reliably assign helminths to the species level, and took a conservative approach to genus-level identification, labelling some specimens as “unidentified trematodes” to avoid potential misclassification. Parasite prevalence was calculated as the proportion of samples in which at least one of the parasites was detected. Parasite density was calculated as the average number of eggs/cysts/larvae found per milliliter of sample, using the original volume of feces collected, which was calculated based on the amount of displacement the sample had on the 8 ml of ethanol in the collection tubes.

DNA extraction, sequencing, and microbial identification

For the 16S sequencing, we extracted DNA from 21 ethanol fecal samples, 21 frozen samples, and 3 blanks each, while for shotgun sequencing, we extracted DNA from 19 fecal samples and 4 blanks. Blank controls contained molecular biology-grade water in place of fecal material. Extractions were performed between March and April 2018 at the genetic laboratory at the University of Calgary. We used a phenol-chloroform bead beating extraction protocol41, previously established and optimized in our laboratory42. Briefly, we added 500 μl of phenol-chloroform:IAA to approximately 200 μl of fecal sample with an equal proportion of zirconium beads. The standard bead-beating method of extraction was modified by adding 20 μl of Proteinase K to each sample and digesting them for 2 hours at 56 °C. After two rounds of successive bead beating and chilling on ice, the supernatant was then mixed with another aliquot of phenol-chloroform and centrifuged to leave a supernatant of solubilized DNA. This was left to precipitate overnight at −20 °C with isopropanol and sodium acetate. The following day, chilled samples were centrifuged to pellet DNA and washed with ethanol. Then, washed DNA was solubilized in TE buffer, and purified using a spin column purification process as per the Invitrogen Quick PCR Purification Kit procedure. The combination of phenol–chloroform extraction and isopropanol precipitation, together with mechanical processing steps, separates nucleic acids from proteins, organic material, and inhibitors, improving DNA purity but not eliminating exogenous DNA contamination. Purified DNA was then quantified using a Qubit 3 Fluorometer and stored at −20 °C.

We prepared 16S libraries for sequencing from both ethanol-preserved and frozen samples using a two-step amplification protocol, with primers Hyb515F_rRNA (5’-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTA -3) and Hyb806R_rRNA (5’-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) targeting the V4 region. Triplicate PCRs were performed to minimize amplification bias, followed by bead-based purification, indexing in a second PCR, and library pooling based on DNA quantification prior to sequencing (see Supp. Material, Protocol 1). After library preparation, the 250bp pair-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq Sequencer following the standard protocol and using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (500-cycles) kit. The frozen samples undergoing shotgun sequencing were processed with other fecal samples from another project on another primate species43. Shotgun metagenomic libraries were prepared at the University of Calgary Cumming School of Medicine Centre for Health Genomics and Informatics using the iGenomx Riptide library preparation kit. After library preparation, the 150bp paired-end sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq (S4, 300-cycle v1.5) on 8 lanes of sequencing, including a positive mock community from the ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard (D6300, Zymo-Research, Irvine, CA, USA).

The raw reads were polished and processed for taxonomic assignment using established bioinformatic pipelines, which differed according to the sequencing method employed. For 16S data, reads were quality filtered and demultiplexed using Illumina BaseSpace and cutadapt, then processed into amplicon sequence variants using DADA2, and taxonomically assigned using the SILVA database (see Supp. Material, Protocol 2). Shotgun metagenomic reads were first quality filtered, trimmed, and depleted of host sequences. We then performed taxonomic classification using Kraken2/Bracken, with additional screening for helminth sequences using a custom database. We identified microbial metabolic pathways and their abundance in each sample using Humann3.6 and normalizing the output in copies per million (see Supp. Material, Protocol 2). Subsequent analyses were performed in R software44. We used decontam (v1.24.0)45 to remove contaminants found prevalently in blank samples, and we filtered all ASVs / taxa to include only those belonging to samples with a read count of over 500. After quality filtering and contaminant removal, 16S sequence produced 59,871 and 61,404 reads per sample for ethanol-stored and flash frozen samples, respectively. Good’s coverage for both ranged between 90.8% and 99.7% suggesting that sequencing depth captured the majority of microbial diversity. Shotgun sequencing instead resulted in a mean sequencing depth of 22,677,794 reads per sample. We used the package phyloseq (v1.48)46 to arrange and organize data, as well as to calculate measures of alpha and beta diversity. To test our predictions, we calculated two measures of alpha diversity: i) the Observed richness (number of taxa), and ii) the Shannon index, which considers both richness and evenness (i.e., entropy) by quantifying the consistency of taxa abundance47. To measure beta diversity, we used the packages microbiome (v1.26) and vegan (2.6.6.1)48 to calculate the robust Aitchison distance metric from center-log transformed data, to account for the compositional nature of microbiome data49,50.

Statistical analysis

To meet our first aim of profiling the gastrointestinal microbial and helminth communities of wild howler monkeys, we tested differences in diversity measures across the three sampling locations by running ANOVA tests with log-transformed measures of alpha diversity as response variables. We checked the assumption of the model by running Shapiro tests to confirm the normal distribution of the residuals, and Levene’s tests to assess the homogeneity of variances across the three sites. For beta diversity, we ran a PERMANOVA test on the Aitchison distance metric48 to test the variation across the three sampling locations. We tested the differences in taxa abundance across locations using ANCOM-BC251, choosing the more conservative “Holm” method and only considering results that passed the sensitivity analysis to control for false positives. Finally, we tested differences in overall parasite prevalence and density (i.e., the average number of eggs/cysts/larvae found per milliliter of fecal sample, eggs/cysts/larvae/ml) across the three sampling locations using Fisher’s Exact tests and Kruskal-Wallis tests, respectively.

To test predictions from our second aim of evaluating how methods may affect the accuracy of conclusions in microbial studies, we first calculated the core microbiota for both sequencing and storage methods extracting only the taxa present in more than 80% of the samples and with minimal relative abundance of 0.01%52. We used Wilcoxon tests for alpha diversity measures, and PERMANOVA for beta diversity, to compare sequencing and storage methods, respectively. We then ran an ANCOM-BC2 test as described above to find the difference in abundance of taxa at the family level between storage methods, but not for sequencing method, due to differences in taxonomic resolution. To assess the relationship between taxa identified by shotgun and 16S, we first reduced dimensionality with sparse PCA, then performed regularized canonical correlation analysis (rCCA) from the mixOmics package53, identifying linear combinations of variables that maximized correlation between the two datasets. Finally, we calculated Spearman correlations between the canonical loadings of shotgun and 16S features to assess their concordance.

Results

Profile of the gastrointestinal microbial and helminth community

16S rRNA sequencing method

In the frozen samples sequenced with 16S method, we found a total of 1,436 ASVs (99% Bacteria, 0.9% Archaea). Among the bacterial ASVs, 11 could not be resolved at the phylum level, leaving 1,425 ASVs resolved to at least the phylum level, 71.5% resolved at the family level, and only 41.2% at the genus level. The community was dominated by bacteria, with a mean relative abundance of 97.7% (Fig. 1A). At the phylum level, Firmicutes were the most abundant (49.7%), followed by Actinobacteriota (33.4%), and Bacteroidota (12.3%). Sampling location did not affect alpha diversity measures of taxonomy (Observed: F = 0.639, p = 0.541; Shannon: F = 0.423, p = 0. 662), the variation in beta diversity explained by site was non-significant (R2 = 0.153, F = 1.44, p = 0.079) (Supp. Figure 2C, D), and the ANCOMB-BC2 test revealed no differences in taxonomic abundances.

Overall, reads assigned to nematodes had a higher mean relative abundance than those assigned to platyhelminths (78.3% vs 16.4%), and, at the species level, reads assigned to Haemonchus placei were the most abundant (3.3%).

Coprological microscopy method

We also looked for helminths using standard coprological methods. We identified helminths in 15 of the 21 samples screened (71%). The most prevalent helminth was the platyhelminth Controrchis sp. at 43% (9/21); the second most prevalent parasite was an unidentified trematode (29% - 6/21). We also detected Strongyloides sp. and Enterobius sp. (pinworms) with a prevalence of 19% (4/21) and 10% (2/21) (Fig. 3). Overall, parasite prevalence did not vary significantly across the sampled locations (Fisher’s Exact test: p = 0.789), nor did parasite density (Kruskal-Wallis test: H = 2.604, p = 0.272). Methodological impacts on the microbial community

Storage method: room temperature ethanol vs flash frozen in liquid nitrogen

We found that the core microbiota profiled with 16S sequencing on samples stored in ethanol included 399 ASVs, while we found a total of 302 ASVs in the samples frozen in liquid nitrogen (Supp. Figure 4). We found that ethanol-preserved samples had higher alpha diversity compared to frozen ones (Observed index: W = 441, p < 0.001; Shannon index: W = 367, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). The Aitchison distances were significantly different, and the storage method explained approximately 19.7% of the variance in the community composition (R2 = 0.197, F = 9.822, p = 0.001) (Fig. 4). Sequencing method: 16S vs shotgun

The core microbiota identified using shotgun sequencing comprised 2,525 taxa resolved to the species level, whereas the 16S dataset included 302 ASVs (Supp. Figure 4). We found that 16S sequencing method resulted in a dataset with lower alpha diversity compared to the one generated with shotgun sequencing (Observed index: W = 21, p < 0.001; Shannon index: W = 20, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5), and that the two datasets clustered away from each other, with sequencing method explaining the majority of the variance in the community (R2 = 0.561, F = 48.533, p = 0.001) (Fig. 5). The rCCA on the taxa selected through S-PCA showed a strong correlation between the two datasets (Spearman test on first canonical variates: ρ = 0.979, p < 0.001). Discussion

We report the first shotgun metagenomics dataset from wild Costa Rican mantled howlers, characterizing their microbial communities and helminth parasites across habitats of varying quality. Across sequencing methods, we detected microbes and predicted microbial functions consistent with a folivorous-frugivorous diet. Additionally, this is one of the few studies comparing the effects of both sample storage and sequencing methods on the core microbiota of wild primates, revealing significant variation in composition and diversity. These findings suggest how methodological choices may influence the study of host–microbe–parasite interactions, emphasizing the need to understand each approach’s strengths and limitations in wildlife research.

Profile of the gastrointestinal microbial and helminth community

The GI microbiota using 16S and shotgun sequencing

The gut microbiota of wild howler monkeys was primarily composed of the bacterial phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota, similarly to other herbivorous mammals6,54–57. At higher taxonomic resolutions, we found bacterial taxa associated with leaf-rich diets and the degradation of hemicellulose and other complex carbohydrates, including members of Lachnospiraceae, Eggerthellaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Prevotellaceae, as well as Faecalibacterium and Oscillibacter28,30,58–61. Through shotgun sequencing, we also characterized archaeal and fungal taxa that may support bacterial digestion of plant material. We detected abundant methanogenic archaea (Methanobrevibacter and Methanosphaera), which consume bacterial fermentation by-products, enhancing fiber degradation and energy extraction62–64, as well as eukaryotes such as Fusarium, Zymoseptoria, Rhizophagus, and Saccharomyces, which can mediate gut balance and enhance fiber accessibility for bacterial communities65–69. We also detected bacteriophages, which may indirectly influence food digestion by regulating microbial community composition70, although further studies are needed to clarify the effects of viruses on the broader microbiome.

Given the paucity of metagenomic studies on the microbial functional potential of non-human primates, especially herbivorous species, our results provide new evidence consistent with a role of the gut microbiome in plant material degradation. In particular, the presence of pathways involved in the use of plant-derived carbohydrates, such as sucrose and rhamnose metabolism, as well as fermentation pathways, may suggest microbial processing of dietary plant substrates into energy metabolites. The overall functional profile observed in this study, including pathways related to carbohydrate, amino acid, protein, and nucleotide metabolism, is consistent with previous findings in snub-nosed monkeys (Rhinopithecus bieti), another herbivorous primate57. This is one of the few studies to profile the howler monkey gut microbiota across all microbial domains and functional potential, highlighting the importance of examining these groups together, as we observed a complex community in which bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes co-occur and likely interact to support digestion and nutrient absorption.

The helminth population through coprological inspection and shotgun sequencing

Coprological inspection revealed trematodes and nematodes, in line with other reports on howler monkeys4,29,38,71. The most prevalent helminth detected was Controrchis, likely acquired through the consumption of infected insects found on dietary plants72. Additionally, overlapping infections with Controrchis and Strongyloides in sympatric howler, capuchin, and spider monkeys suggest shared exposure to intermediate hosts4,13,73. The recurrence of similar helminth taxa across howler monkey species and sympatric primate species4,22,29,38,72,74 may indicate long-standing associations. Importantly, we only collected one sample per animal, which likely underestimated parasite detection in our study and limited our ability to draw conclusions about infection status across individuals. Although some consistency in fecal egg shedding across samples from the same individual has been found in other hosts75, future studies should incorporate repeated sampling to improve detection reliability and reduce the likelihood of false negatives. Additionally, more comprehensive molecular surveys could help clarify the ecological and evolutionary dynamics of host–parasite interactions and their implications for conservation and human–wildlife interfaces.

We investigated whether shotgun sequencing could serve as a molecular tool to address current gaps in helminth surveillance. Although we detected reads assigned to trematodes and nematodes, the most abundant helminth reads mapped to Haemonchus placei, a nematode mainly associated with cattle76, and likely a false positive in our population. The relatively high risk of false positives with this approach arises because taxonomic assignment methods for short-read sequencing are typically optimized for microbial genomes, which are generally much smaller than helminth genomes. Moreover, genome size influences the number of reads assigned to a taxon, so differences among helminths may bias relative abundance estimates, while incomplete and unevenly represented helminth reference genomes increase the risk of false positives77,78. Furthermore, our positive control (containing only bacteria and fungi) showed assignments to several helminths. Together, these results suggest that short-read shotgun-based approaches are at present still unreliable for helminth detection in wildlife samples, and targeted molecular approaches may provide more robust alternatives. However, improvements in reference databases and assembly-based methods represent promising next steps, potentially enabling the same datasets to be used for both microbial and helminth analyses.

Comments on the impact of anthropogenic disturbance

Previous studies on black howler monkeys (A. pigra) and red colobus monkeys (Procolobus gordonorum) reported reduced gut diversity in primates from disrupted habitats associated with a less diverse diet7,30. In our study, we collected a small number of samples from howler monkeys living near a ranch or town. We found no significant association between microbial diversity and sampling location, but we observed higher Prevotellaceae abundance in samples collected from the ranch group compared to the more pristine forest of Santa Rosa, although this pattern was only significant in the 16S dataset. Nonetheless, a previous study reported that captive primates, including A. palliata, exhibited higher abundance of Prevotella (family Prevotellaceae) compared to wild populations8. It is possible that this taxon may represent a microbial indicator of changes due to anthropogenic environmental disturbance, warranting further investigation. Additionally, we found no differences in helminth prevalence and density between anthropogenically disturbed and pristine habitats. However, helminths differ from one another in their life cycle and infective properties, and such differences may become more apparent when analyzed at the species level. Importantly, our low sample size is likely underpowered for drawing conclusions about differences across sampling sites, and parasite prevalence and density may be higher than what we found29. Nonetheless, our study contributes to profiling the GI microbial and helminth communities of wild monkeys living in habitats of different quality. Future research with larger sample sizes is warranted and will offer insights into the variability and stability of the gut microbial and parasitic communities of wild howler monkeys.

Methodological impacts on the core microbial community

Storage method: room temperature ethanol vs flash frozen in liquid nitrogen

Storage conditions affected both diversity and taxa abundances, with implications for ecological interpretations. In line with our predictions and previous studies12,15,18, samples stored at room temperature in ethanol clustered separately from frozen samples. Contrary to our prediction, ethanol-stored samples showed higher overall diversity, suggesting that ethanol may not immediately halt enzymatic activity, thereby altering diversity. Previous studies found contrasting results, with frozen ethanol-stored samples showing either lower alpha diversity19 or no significant difference15,16,79 compared to raw-frozen samples. This difference may reflect a temperature rather than a preservative effect, since our ethanol-stored samples were kept at room temperature. We also found that frozen samples were enriched in anaerobic bacteria and oxygen-sensitive archaea, suggesting that liquid nitrogen more effectively maintained anaerobic conditions. Interestingly, Carruthers et al.12 found the opposite trend, with ethanol storage favouring the anaerobic community, though the study was conducted in clinical rather than wild conditions. Overall, our findings underscore the complexity of storage effects on microbiome data, as there is no consistency in their impact on diversity and composition.

Sequencing method: 16S vs shotgun

We found that the sequencing method significantly influenced the core microbial composition, with shotgun metagenomics revealing higher diversity than 16S sequencing. This is consistent with previous studies and reflects shotgun’s higher taxonomic resolution and broader community coverage, which enables detection of rare taxa14,23. While 16S reliably detects common bacterial taxa, it often cannot distinguish closely related species and may over- or underrepresent specific taxa, as it is strongly influenced by the choice of primers and targeted variable region, potentially resulting in coverage gaps80. In contrast, shotgun captures the full diversity of complex gut communities, including non-bacterial taxa, and it is free from primer amplification biases, even though results may vary substantially depending on sequencing effort and coverage14,23.

Despite these differences, the rCCA analysis suggested a strong correlation between shotgun and 16S datasets, suggesting that both methods capture similar multivariate patterns in core community structure and variation. These patterns were driven by taxa associated with the gut microbiota of folivorous-frugivorous mammals, including Bacteroidaceae, Prevotellaceae, and Lachnospiraceae. However, we found differences at finer taxonomic levels, likely reflecting variation in taxonomic resolution between approaches. Some observed differences may arise from inconsistencies across reference databases and methodological characteristics: 16S provides high coverage of a single gene, whereas shotgun represents the entire DNA content23,81. Similar findings were reported in feral horse gut microbiomes, where shotgun provided more accurate relative abundance estimates81. However, shotgun can introduce higher levels of host contamination and false positives for rare taxa, particularly in poorly characterized microbial communities24. Therefore, the best sequencing method depends on the objectives of the study. Shotgun is advantageous when the aim is to expand microbial databases, especially in underrepresented wild systems. It also enables us to explore microbial roles through functional and fine-scale taxonomic analyses. Instead, 16S is preferable for broader bacterial community composition, ensuring comparability with existing studies, and reducing false positives in poorly characterized microbiomes.

Conclusions and limitations

Overall, we provide new insights into the microbes, helminths, and their relationships in the gut microbiota of wild howler monkeys. We contribute valuable data on wild populations living under varying degrees of human disturbance, although our ability for fine-grained analysis and conclusions was limited as sample sizes in disturbed areas were small and we did not examine variables such as foraging behavior or direct metrics of habitat quality (e.g., tree transects or phenology or habitat size) in different environments. Further research of these habitats should increase sampling effort and incorporate observations of fruit and vegetation availability, along with foraging activity, to better resolve variability across locations and assess how these factors influence the microbiome. As in many studies of wild primates, our overall sample size was limited, a constraint acknowledged throughout the manuscript. However, the application of multiple methodologies to the same samples remains relatively rare in the literature, representing a valuable contribution to methodological assessment. Variation in results attributable to different methodologies is particularly understudied in wild systems. By analyzing the same samples under different storage and sequencing conditions, we identify factors that can affect microbial composition and the interpretation of community structure. We hope that these insights can help other researchers make informed decisions when selecting appropriate methods according to their research objectives and logistical constraints.
