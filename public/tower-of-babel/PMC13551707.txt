Genome-wide association study and KASP development for growth and leaf traits in Populus deltoides
Authors: Jiawei Yan, Chunxiao Liu, Zhongxu Zhang, Kailian Long, Yang Shen, Shengqing Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulus deltoides is a valuable timber species of considerable importance in the study of forest genetic breeding. However, its genetic improvement continues to rely predominantly on conventional selection and hybridization strategies hampered by long breeding cycles and limited efficiency.ResultsA total of 209 P. deltoides accessions were genotyped using a 60K SNP (Single nucleotide polymorphism) liquid array. Following quality control, 46,031 high-quality SNPs were screened and analyzed alongside 15 phenotypic traits in a genome-wide association study (GWAS), which identified 219 SNPs significantly associated with the traits. After further screening and annotation, a final set of 57 target SNPs and 77 candidate genes was obtained. Using kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) assays, we successfully developed 48 polymorphic KASP markers. Of these, 25 markers exhibited significant phenotypic differences (p < 0.05) across genotype groups.ConclusionsThese 25 KASP markers can serve as reliable and practical tools for phenotype-assisted selection, providing efficient molecular resources for accelerating genetic improvement and marker-assisted breeding in poplar.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13101-2.

Introduction

Populus deltoides, a representative species of Section Aegeiros within the genus Populus (Salicaceae) [1], is distinguished for its rapid growth, early maturation, and versatile wood utility compared to other poplar species [2]. Thus, it is widely used in global poplar breeding programs [3]. P. deltoides is a diploid species with a somatic chromosome number of 2n = 38. The recent studies have achieved chromosome-level de novo assemblies of this species using third-generation sequencing (Nanopore/PacBio) combined with Hi-C chromatin conformation capture technology [4]. However, breeding efforts for P. deltoides continue to rely primarily on conventional selection and hybrid breeding methods, which are hindered by long breeding cycles and low selection efficiency [5]. While molecular marker-assisted selection (MAS) offers a promising solution by significantly reducing these cycles and improving selection accuracy [6]. Nevertheless, the success of MAS depends on the availability of molecular markers that are strongly linked to the target traits. Studies in P. deltoides to date has largely focused on the development of SSR (Simple Sequence Repeat) markers for analyses of genetic diversity and population structure [7, 8]. Therefore, the development of trait-associated markers remains a priority for the practical application of MAS in the poplar breeding.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are preferable to traditional markers due to their greater genetic stability, specificity, and abundance across the genome [9]. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) is a powerful tool that leverages these SNPs to unravel the genetic architecture of complex traits by identifying SNPs associated with phenotypic variation [10, 11]. With the advancements in sequencing and genotyping technologies, GWAS has become a widely used approach for analyzing phenotypic variation at the genomic level [12]. Studies in P. tomentosa have identified SNPs linked to microfibril angle, fiber width, and diameter at breast height [13], while those in P. trichocarpa have correlated SNPs with traits such as leaf carbon and nitrogen content, lignin composition, tree height, leaf area, and chlorophyll levels [14, 15]. Similarly, SNPs associated with protein-coding and transcriptional regulation have been reported in P. euphratica [16]. Additionally, GWAS has been successfully used to identify hundreds of candidate genes for adventitious rooting in P. trichocarpa [17]; 4 and 20 associated genes for leaf by single- and multi-trait analyses, respectively [15]; five genes linked to nitrogen-use efficiency [18] and 394 genes associated with leaf size [19] in P. cathayana. In P. deltoides, studies have identified 69 significant SNPs associated with wood characteristics, leaf morphology, and disease resistance, along with 130 candidate genes [20]; five SNPs significantly associated with tree height and leaf area, as well as five trait-related genes [21]. However, studies on single trait and multi-environment analyses of growth and physiological traits remain scarce in P. deltoides. This points the necessary for GWAS to enable the development of KASP markers closely linked to the target traits.

Based on the fundamental DNA amplification technique of polymerase chain reaction (PCR), Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR (KASP) uses allele-specific amplification and high-sensitivity fluorescence detection, offering a cost-effective, high-throughput solution for precise genotyping of SNPs and Indels (Insertions and deletions) [22]. It supports a wide range of applications, including germplasm identification and allele mining [23]. KASP has been employed for sex identification in Pistacia [24] and large-scale genetic diversity assessments in olive [25]. Cui et al. [26] uses KASP to develop trait-linked markers for needle and branch in Larix olgensis, facilitating the conversion of GWAS-identified SNPs into validated tools for trait improvement. In P. deltoides, three KASP markers are found to be closely linked with leaf area and plant height, offering potential for genetic improvement and elite germplasm screening [21]. Furthermore, eight core KASP markers have been shown to accurately discriminate 34 distinct genotypes [27], highlighting the utility of KASP markers. However, it remains limited for the development of functional markers specifically targeting growth and pigment‑related phenotypes in P. deltoides, despite pigment content being a key determinant of photosynthetic efficiency.

This study performed GWAS utilizing single‑trait and multi‑environment joint analyses of 15 phenotypic traits from 209 P. deltoides clones. The resulting KASP markers are efficient tools for the prediction of phenotypic variation, offering a valuable molecular resource for accelerating the breeding selection and enhancing the genetic improvement in P. deltoides.

Materials and methods

Materials

This study was conducted at the Shishou National Poplar Elite Variety Base, Jingzhou, Hubei Province, China, which has a typical subtropical monsoon climate with an annual mean temperature of 16.5 °C and an annual rainfall of approximately 1200 mm. We collected 209 P. deltoides clones, which were sourced from 19 collection sites across four regions in the United States: Texas (TX), Mississippi (MS), Louisiana (LA), and the Lower Mississippi River (L-MR) region. A priority was given to the collected clones from distinct regions and those exhibiting phenotypic differences. The provenance information of genotypes from unknown collection regions and sites was as shown in Table S1. In March 2023, one-year-old branches were collected from all 209 clones, and pruned into 10–15 cm cuttings with three leaf buds retained per cutting. After land preparation and plastic film mulching, the cuttings were then propagated at a spacing of 30 cm between rows and 15 cm within rows in the field, with all cuttings managed under uniform conditions. Due to the limited number of cuttings collected from some samples and mortality during planting, 23 samples ultimately had fewer than three replicates. Thus, each clone consisted of 1–10 single-plant replicates.

Trait data acquisition

Dynamic plant growth measurements were initiated in mid-June (Table S2). Ground diameter (GD) was recorded at five timepoints, resulting in four incremental values (GDI1–GDI4). Height (H) was measured at three timepoints with corresponding incremental values (HI1 and HI2). Internode number (NI) was recorded at two timepoints (NI0 and NI1), yielding one incremental value (NII1). By early August, fully expanded mature leaves (7th–9th from the stem apex) were collected from each clones for the measurement of leaf thickness (LT), water content (WC), and photosynthetic pigment. Genotyping and quality control

In July 2023, mature leaves were collected from 209 P. deltoides genotypes and transported to the laboratory on dry ice. DNA was extracted using the magnetic bead method [29]. Genotyping was performed using a P. deltoides 60K liquid SNP array (unpublished). The genotyping data underwent quality control using PLINK v1.9.0 [30], excluding SNPs with a missing rate greater than 10% (--geno 0.1) or a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.05 (--maf 0.05), resulting in a high-quality set of SNPs for subsequent analysis.

Population structure analysis

Genetic relatedness and population structure were analyzed using the filtered SNPs. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in PLINK v1.9.0 [30], with the first 20 principal components (PCs) calculated and their eigenvalues extracted. An identity‑by‑state (IBS) matrix was constructed to assess genetic relatedness among individuals, from which a phylogenetic tree was inferred using the Neighbor‑Joining (NJ) method as implemented in the “ape” package in R [31]. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the IBS genetic distance matrix and the cophenetic distance matrix of the NJ tree was calculated, and a Mantel test with 1,000 random permutations was performed. Individual ancestry coefficients were estimated using ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 [32]. The number of ancestral clusters (K) was explored from 1 to 20, with 10 independent replicates per K-value, using a randomized seed. The optimal K-value for population subdivision was determined by selecting the model with the lowest cross-validation (CV) error after evaluating all replicates. Visualization of the population structure results was generated using the “ggplot2” package in R [33].

Genome-wide association studies of phenotypic data

GWAS was performed using the “IIIVmrMLM” package in R [34]. The ‘Single_env’ method was applied for single‑trait GWAS on 15 phenotypes, while the ‘Multi_env’ method was used for multi‑environment GWAS on the phenotypic groups GD (GDI1–GDI4), H (HI1–HI2), and NI (NI0–NII1). In all cases, Bonferroni correction was applied to control false positives, and the IBS matrix and the PCs were included as covariates. Manhattan and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots were generated using the “CMplot” package [35]. The Manhattan plot displays the genome-wide distribution of SNPs significantly associated with target traits, while the Q-Q plot illustrates the observed versus expected distribution of P-values, helping assess the reliability of the association tests.

Linkage disequilibrium analysis and candidate gene annotation

Trait-associated SNPs were annotated based on the P. deltoides reference genome (GCA_015852605.2) [4] using SnpEff v4.3 [36], focusing on those located in gene regions. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks were then identified using the “solid spine of LD” method in Haploview v4.2 [37, 38]. After removing SNPs that deviated from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE; p<0.001), had a successful genotyping rate <75%, or possessed a minor allele frequency (MAF) <0.001, LD blocks were defined for SNPs located within 500 kb of each other. LD blocks and independent SNPs (not forming blocks) that contain 3′UTR, 5′UTR, or missense variants were screened out, and candidate genes were identified based on the positions of these blocks and SNPs. Among them, the trait-associated SNPs were selected as candidate SNPs for KASP marker development.

For the annotation, the identified genes were first aligned against the non-redundant (NR) protein database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool for Proteins (BLASTP) available at NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). The effective annotation with the highest percent identity was selected, and the corresponding homologous genes in P. trichocarpa were retrieved, which were then submitted to the BioMart V13 tool within Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/), using the P. trichocarpa V4.1 genome as a reference.

Development and verification of KASP markers

Following the extraction of 150-bp flanking sequences upstream and downstream and the removal of multicopy regions, high GC content, and highly repetitive elements, KASP primers were designed for the candidate SNPs, consisting of two allele‑specific primers and one common primer, labeled with 6‑Carboxyfluorescein (FAM) and Hexachlorofluorescein (HEX) fluorophores, respectively. The validation of KASP markers was carried out on 94 samples using the Douglas Scientific Array Tape system (LGC Biosearch Technologies, Hoddesdon, UK). The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 15 min, followed by 10 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, and annealing/extension at 65–56 °C for 60 s (with a 0.8 °C temperature decrement per cycle). This was followed by 30 additional cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s and extension at 57 °C for 1 min (Table S3). Post-PCR fluorescence signal scanning was performed using the ARAYA system (LGC), and data analysis and genotyping were conducted using the INTELLICS software (LGC).

Statistical analysis

Phenotypic data were processed using Microsoft Excel 2021, and the mean value of all replicates for each genotype was taken as the phenotypic value of that genotype. Descriptive statistics and coefficients of variation (CoV) were calculated using IBM SPSS Statistics (v 26.0). Due to missing phenotypic data for some genotypes, a subset of 134 genotypes with complete data was used for phenotypic correlation analysis. Frequency histograms and non-linear regression curves were generated in R, and phenotypic correlation matrices were visualized using the “corrplot” package [39].

Results

Phenotypic trait variation in P. deltoides population

The initial statistics for the trait data are presented in Table S4. The CoV for all traits ranged from 8.0% to 42.1% (Table 1). Traits GDI1, GDI2, GDI3, and GDI4 exhibited CoVs greater than 25.0%, HI1 and HI2 were above 20.0%, and NI0 and NII1 exceeded 14.0%. The CoVs for the photosynthetic pigment traits (CHLA, CHLB, CAR, and TCHL) were also above 20.0%. In contrast, LT, WC, and SPAD showed CoVs below 15.0%, indicating lower genetic variation and less variability in these traits. The Shapiro-Wilk test revealed that GDI2, HI1, HI2, LT, and WC deviated from normality (p < 0.05), whereas the other traits did not show significant deviation (p > 0.05). Frequency histograms revealed that all phenotypes exhibited continuous distribution and generally approximated a normal distribution (Fig. 1). Overall, these results confirmed the suitability of the phenotypic data for GWAS analysis.

Genotyping and population structure

A total of 60,944 SNPs were obtained from genotyping 209 P. deltoides accessions. After quality control, 46,031 high-quality SNPs were retained. The distribution density of the filtered SNPs across chromosomes was shown in Fig. S1. PCA indicated that the first five PCs explained the following percentages of the total variance: PC1 (17.5%), PC2 (13.2%), PC3 (7.8%), PC4 (5.8%), and PC5 (5.1%), together accounting for 49.46% of the total variance (Fig. S2). A scatter plot of PC1 and PC2 showed no clear clustering based on geographic origin (Fig. 3A), suggesting the absence of a macroscale population structure organized by geography. ADMIXTURE analysis revealed that the minimum CV error occurred at K = 14 (Fig. S3), whereas K values beyond 14 resulted in higher CV errors, confirming K = 14 as the optimal number of groups (Fig. 3B). These results indicate a pattern of genetic subdivision within the population that is independent of geographic origin.

The IBS heatmap displayed generally low genetic similarity (IBS ≈ 0.6) among most genotype pairs, indicating high population-wide diversity (Fig. 3C). However, a subset of genotypes showed moderate relatedness (IBS ≈ 0.8). The Mantel test yielded a cophenetic correlation coefficient of r = 0.822 (p = 0.00099), indicating a strong and significant fit between the NJ tree and the original genetic distances. Furthermore, based on the 14 populations inferred by ADMIXTURE, we re‑plotted the PCA and the NJ tree, as shown in Fig. S4 and Fig. 3D, respectively. In the NJ tree, only a few ancestries showed clear clustering, while most remained highly admixed (Fig. 3D). To minimize false-positive associations in the GWAS, the first five PCs (PC1–PC5) and the IBS-based kinship matrix were included as covariates in the analytical model.

Significant association SNPs of traits

Manhattan and QQ plots illustrating the association results were presented in Fig. S5 and Fig. S6. We identified 145 SNPs associated with growth-related traits, including 68 for ground diameter growth increments (GD: 32, GDI1: 10, GDI2: 12, GDI3: 8, GDI4: 6), 31 for height growth increments (H: 20, HI1: 11), and 38 for internode number growth increments (NI0: 8, NI: 25, NII1: 5). A total of 78 SNPs were associated with photosynthetic pigment-related traits: 19 for SPAD value, 14 for CHLA, 15 for CHLB, 16 for CAR, and 14 for TCHL. Additionally, 12 SNPs were associated with LT and 11 with WC. Ultimately, we obtained 219 significant associated SNPs, of which 43 were significantly associated with multiple traits and 176 with a single trait (Table S5).

LD blocks and functional annotation of candidate genes

Functional annotation of the 219 SNPs revealed that 38 were intergenic, and 98 were located in upstream/downstream regulatory regions. The remaining 83 SNPs were within genes: 20 in the 3′UTR, 9 in the 5′UTR, 19 in introns, and 34 in the coding sequence (CDS). Among those in the CDS, 21 were synonymous, and 13 were missense variants (Table S6).

LD blocks analysis revealed that 50 of 83 SNPs were distributed across 48 independent LD blocks (Table S7), with significant variation in LD decay ranges. Thirty-three SNPs did not form LD blocks with surrounding loci and were therefore considered independent (Table S8). Some blocks, such as those containing Chr02_7493180, Chr04_17910829, and Chr06_20833705, were shorter than 1 kb, while others, such as those containing Chr04_22976213, Chr05_15868942, and Chr15_14379349, exceeded 10 kb. Of the identified LD blocks, 15 contained multiple genes (e.g., the block containing Chr04_22976213) (Fig. 4A), while the remaining blocks each contained only a single gene (e.g., the block harboring Chr18_14070439) (Fig. 4B). Based on the identified LD blocks, all SNPs within these blocks were extracted, and 203 SNPs were selected for further analysis.

Annotation of the 203 selected SNPs showed the distribution as follows: 10 intergenic variants, 43 intronic variants, 21 variants in the 3′UTR, 6 in the 5′UTR, 17 upstream variants, 11 downstream variants, one stop-gain variant, 52 synonymous variants, and 39 missense variants, 2 splice_region_variant & intron_variant, and 1 splice_region_variant & synonymous_variant (Table S7). Finally, 57 trait-associated SNPs were selected for subsequent marker development, based on their locations in 3′/5′UTR, missense variants in the CDS, or within haplotype blocks containing such variants. Thirty-five SNPs were located in LD blocks and 22 were independent SNPs (Table S9).

Gene screening of 57 selected SNPs identified 77 candidate genes. Thirty-two genes were associated with photosynthetic pigment-related traits, such as dynactin subunit P25 (DCTN5), hakai-interacting zinc finger protein 2 (HIZ2), and RELA/SPOT homolog 3 (RSH3). Thirty-nine genes were linked to growth-related traits, such as autophagy-related protein 18 F (ATG18F), dephospho-CoA kinase (DPCK), and purine permease 1 (PUP1). Additionally, three genes were related to LT, 2 to WC, and 1 was annotated as a protein of unknown function (Table S10).

Development and validation of KASP markers

From the 57 candidate SNPs, 48 primer pairs were successfully developed and synthesized (Table S11), which were used to genotype 94 P. deltoides accessions (Table S12). The genotyping data successfully clustered 92 individuals into distinct genotypic groups, with the exception of 2 samples with unknown genotypes (N/N) (Fig. S7).

Using the phenotypic data corresponding to the 48 developed markers, 59 box plots were constructed to visualize the relationships between genotypes and phenotypes (Table S13). Among these, 25 markers exhibited significant phenotypic differences among genotypes, while 23 markers showed no significant differences (Fig. S8). Eighteen markers displayed genotypes associated with significantly higher phenotypic values (p < 0.05) (Table 2), suggesting their potential for selecting individuals with high phenotypic performance (Fig. S9). Seven markers showed genotypes with significantly lower phenotypic values (p < 0.05), making them suitable for identifying low-performing genotypes (Fig. S10).

Discussion

In this study, a range of seedling-stage traits in 209 geographically diverse P. deltoides clones was evaluated, covering dynamic growth increments (GDI, HI, NI), leaf properties (LT, WC), and photosynthetic pigment-related phenotypes. The results revealed significant phenotypic variation across all traits. Specifically, the CoV for increments in GD, H, and NI all exceeded 14.0%, with a peak of 42.1%. LT, a trait linked to drought resistance due to its role in minimizing water loss [40, 41], exhibited a CoV of 11.6% and a relatively high mean value, indicating that this population is a promising candidate for the selection of drought-resistance clones. Photosynthetic pigments, including chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids, are critical for light absorption and energy conversion [42]. In this study, the CoV of all the pigments exceeded 20%. In contrast, SPAD values exhibited lower variability, with a CoV of 8.0%. Correlation analysis showed a significant association between SPAD values and the measured pigment concentrations, consistent with the finding by Xiang et al. [43]. The considerable phenotypic diversity within this P. deltoides population, as reflected in the high CoV, suggests not only enhanced environmental adaptability [44] but also underlying genetic diversity. This is in line with previous genetic studies on Populus traits [45–47]. The extensive genetic heterogeneity indicated by this diversity highlights the population’s potential for selective breeding.

Population genetic structure analysis revealed a complex genetic architecture of the P. deltoides population in this study. Although no distinct clustering based on geographic origin was observed, fine-scale genetic differentiation was evident. PCA showed no clear geographic clustering, likely due to the fact that the samples were mainly collected from multiple regions, including Texas and the Mississippi River basin. The frequent gene flow across these regions has led to genetic homogenization and high genetic similarity among individuals. This result aligns with findings from Fahrenkrog et al. [48, 49], Chen et al. [7], and Liu et al. [21]. ADMIXTURE analysis revealed an optimal genetic grouping at K = 14, a grouping that did not strictly correspond to the original 19 collection sites, indicating that the genetic structure is more intricate than simple geographic categorization. Fine-scale differentiation, indicated in the IBS analysis and phylogenetic tree, may reflect the effects of selective pressures acting on certain groups, as suggested by Chen et al. [7]. Moreover, individuals from different geographic origins may have exhibited phenotypic convergence due to adaptation to similar environmental conditions, leading to their clustering into the same genetic groups, as also noted by Chen et al. [7] and Liu et al. [21].

LD analysis using Haploview identified 48 haplotype blocks, of which 41 were smaller than 10 kb, while 7 blocks exceeded 10 kb. The smallest block spanned just 3 bp, whereas the largest extended over 43 kb. The number of SNPs within these blocks ranged from 2 to 20 with some blocks, like the 43-kb region, containing only 4 SNPs. These results are consistent with previous studies that report rapid LD decay in Populus species, where LD dissipates over short physical distances [48, 50]. Interestingly, physically distant SNPs can occasionally exhibit high LD values [45], suggesting that the definition of larger blocks should be interpreted with caution. The algorithm used by Haploview and similar tools to define LD blocks is heavily influenced by marker density, which affects both the number and size of blocks detected [51]. As marker density decreases, the reliability of LD block definition also declines [52]. Low marker density can hinder the algorithm’s ability to accurately partition the genome, leading to the erroneous inclusion of physically distant SNPs with spuriously strong LD into the same block, which may result in an overestimation of block size [53, 54]. Thus, some of the low-density blocks identified in this study, such as the 43-kb block, may be more reflective of analytical limitations than of true, biologically meaningful haplotype structures. These regions should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with higher marker density will be needed to validate the true LD structure.

To further understand the genetic mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation, this study focused on markers likely to have direct functional effects. Four markers annotated as missense mutations (Po900152, Po900157, Po900162, and Po900164) were selected as candidate markers for further analysis. The biological roles of the genes associated with these markers were investigated to understand how these specific variants might influence phenotype.

Marker Po900152, which is associated with chlorophyll B content, is located within the gene H0E87_010454, encoding the Dynactin subunit P25. Dynactin is a multi-subunit complex that enhances the processivity of cytoplasmic dynein, playing a dual role in cargo binding and complex regulation [55, 56]. As components of the dynein-dynactin complex, dynactin light chains (DLC) have been implicated in various plant developmental processes [57]. However, the specific regulatory role of the Dynactin subunit P25 in plants remains largely uncharacterized. Given the association between the C/C genotype and elevated chlorophyll B content, it is possible that the C > T variant might affect normal gene function, potentially destabilizing the P25 subunit and interfering with chlorophyll biosynthesis, which could in turn contribute to reduced chlorophyll B accumulation.

Marker Po900157, associated with chlorophyll B content, is located within the gene H0E87_026265, which encodes the HAKAI-interacting zinc finger protein 2 (HIZ2). In Arabidopsis, HIZ2 serves as a key component of the m6A writer complex [58]. The m6A modification is the most prevalent internal chemical modification in eukaryotic mRNA and plays a pivotal role in gene expression regulation. It is extensively involved in controlling plant growth and development [59]. Deficiency in HIZ2 results in decreased m6A levels and subsequent developmental abnormalities in plants [58]. Additionally, disruption of m6A machinery components is linked to impaired chloroplast function and reduced photosynthetic efficiency [60]. Plants with generally low m6A levels exhibit various growth defects [61]. Thus, it is hypothesized that the observed A > C variant may compromise HIZ2 function, impair m6A deposition, and ultimately reduce photosynthetic efficiency. However, phenotypic data reveal that the C/C genotype is associated with significantly higher chlorophyll B content compared to the C/A genotype, which appears to be at odds with a predicted reduction in overall photosynthetic efficiency. This observation might suggest that the relationship between chlorophyll B content and photosynthetic efficiency is not straightforward, and it is possible that the A > C variant influences these two traits via distinct pathways. Further investigation would be needed to clarify the underlying mechanism.

Marker Po900162, associated with GD increment, is located within the gene H0E87_004492, which is annotated as a purine transmembrane transporter belonging to the purine permease (PUP) family. Members of this family facilitate the transport of nucleobases and their derivatives, including cytokinins. In A. thaliana, PUPs mediate the transmembrane transport of cytokinins [62, 63]. Studies have shown that PUP8 and PUP14, residing in the plasma membrane, along with PUP7 and PUP21 located in the vacuolar membrane, coordinate the regulation of cytokinin levels in the apoplast and cytoplasm, thereby modulating signal perception by downstream receptors [64, 65]. Cytokinin regulates various plant developmental processes and plays a major role in cambial activity [66]. Notably, reduced cytokinin levels impair radial growth in transgenic poplar, highlighting its critical role in this process [67]. Based on these findings, it is plausible that the C > A variant identified in this study might affect PUP activity, which could in turn disrupt cytokinin transport and homeostasis, potentially leading to inhibition of stem radial growth in P. deltoides during the observed period. This interpretation is consistent with the significantly greater GDI3 observed for the C/C genotype compared to other genotypes.

Marker Po900164, associated with ground diameter increment, is located within the gene H0E87_030777, annotated as the A. thaliana AT3G47390 ortholog PHOTOSENSITIVE 1 (PHS1), which is involved in the riboflavin (vitamin B2) biosynthesis pathway. Previous study has shown that simultaneous loss of PHS1 and cytosolic disproportionating enzyme 2 (DPE2) in Arabidopsis impairs starch grain regulation and results in a dwarf phenotype [68]. Furthermore, PHS1 is defined as a negative regulator of abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, suppressing or attenuating ABA signal transduction [69]. ABA has dual positive and negative effects on plant growth and development [70]. Variants in specific ABA receptor genes in rice result in more robust growth compared to wild-type plants [71], while ABA-deficient Arabidopsis mutants exhibit increased leaf number [72]. ABA also inhibits plant cell division [73]. Given the significantly greater ground diameter increment observed in the G/G genotype, this raises the possibility that the T > G variant might influence PHS1’s inhibitory effect on ABA signaling. If so, this could contribute to reduced ABA levels, thereby potentially alleviating growth repression and promoting enhanced stem radial development during the specific growth period.

The remaining 21 markers were identified as variants in non-coding regions or synonymous variants. Although these variants did not directly alter the amino acid sequence of proteins, their potential regulatory roles warrant further investigation. The 5′ untranslated region (UTR) plays a pivotal role in ribosome assembly and translation initiation [74] and contains various regulatory elements, including upstream open reading frames (uORFs) and microRNA binding sites, which are vital for regulating gene translation and expression [75]. Similarly, the 3′UTR encompasses multiple specific sequences, structures, and cis-regulatory elements [74], which are involved in post-transcriptional regulation, influencing mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and subcellular localization [76]. Although synonymous variants do not directly affect protein function, they can modulate protein production and folding [77], they may cause changes in protein structure [78]. LD analysis further revealed that 14 of these 21 markers are strongly linked with missense variant sites within their respective LD blocks. Consequently, these markers may serve as indirect selection indicators for favorable alleles, playing a significant role in marker-assisted breeding.

Admittedly, our phenotypic analysis had two main limitations: (1) variable replicate numbers (1 − 10 per genotype) and lack of a randomized block design; (2) not using best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) to correct for environmental variation, nor having time effects or genotype‑by‑time interactions in the statistical model. These issues arose mainly from limited cutting availability and field mortality commonly occurring in long‑term clonal trials. Nevertheless, our approach remains reasonable: we used the arithmetic mean for multiple-replicate genotypes and excluded extreme outliers for single‑replicate genotypes. The key associated SNPs were further supported by variant annotation and phenotypic box plot differences, indicating that the results are reasonably robust. Future studies will adopt BLUEs and time‑interaction models in an optimally designed field trial.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated dynamic growth and leaf traits across 209 P. deltoides accessions, and all phenotypes showed approximately normal distribution. Subsequently, GWAS and KASP analyses identified 219 significantly associated SNPs, along with 77 candidate genes and 48 polymorphic KASP markers. These markers could be directly applied for MAS, enabling early selection of elite genotypes and reducing breeding cycles. Our findings offer insights into the genetic basis of seedling growth traits in P. deltoides. Future work should focus on the functional validation of the identified SNPs and exploration of the molecular mechanisms regulating these traits.
