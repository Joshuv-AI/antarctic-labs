 ABSTRACT
 Accelerated ion irradiation provides essential data for qualifying
additively manufactured nuclear materials, but differences in dose rate and
particle spectrum require physics-based models to interpret the observed
microstructures in the context of neutron irradiation. During fiscal year 2026,
complementary mean-field cluster dynamics (CD) and spatially resolved
mesoscale calculations were used to investigate irradiation-induced defects,
composition, and cavity evolution in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316
stainless steel using representative material and microstructural conditions. The
CD predictions of self-interstitial atom (SIA)-loop and void number densities
and mean diameters were compared with transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) measurements of experimentally resolvable populations under ion
irradiation at 300 and 600 °C. Parameter studies assessed how dislocation
sink strength, carbon-dependent vacancy mobility, and dose rate influence the
evolution of sub-resolution clusters and experimentally observable loops and
voids. The mesoscale models coupled void nucleation with growth to relate the
nucleation-derived void number density to the vacancy supply available to
individual cavities, while two-dimensional calculations demonstrated that local
sink conditions can favor cavity growth at either cell walls or cell interiors.
The balance between biased dislocation density and unbiased sink density was
identified as a critical parameter governing cavity growth and its preferred
location. Under representative prescribed sink conditions, the temperature
associated with the largest predicted 10-dpa cell-wall void radius shifted to
lower values as the dose rate decreased. Nucleation-informed growth-regime
mapping and sensitivity studies assessed the effects of irradiation conditions,
carbon, initial helium pressure, and radiation-induced segregation (RIS) on
cavity evolution. Future integration of the CD and mesoscale models, validation
against ion and neutron irradiation data, and uncertainty quantification will
support quantitative predictions of cavity swelling and irradiation-induced
microstructure evolution under in-reactor conditions.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The accelerated deployment of nuclear energy increases the need for reliable and economical
manufacturing routes for advanced reactor components. The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing
Technologies (AMMT) program addresses this need through rapid qualification approaches that combine
accelerated testing, advanced characterization, and physics-based predictive modeling to evaluate material
performance across nuclear environments [1]. additively manufactured (AM) alloys undergo rapid and
repeated solidification, which produces unique microstructures. In laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 316
stainless steel (SS), these features include heterogeneous cellular dislocation structures, solidification-induced
microsegregation, and variability in minor alloying elements such as carbon. These features affect
the production, transport, clustering, and absorption of irradiation-induced defects and can produce
radiation-damage responses that differ from those of conventionally manufactured 316 SS. Ion irradiation
enables accelerated testing to displacement-damage doses up to 10 dpa, but differences in dose rate
and particle spectrum make a direct one-to-one correspondence with neutron irradiation challenging [2].
Accordingly, the 2025 AMMT roadmap defines an irradiation challenge problem that combines ion
irradiation over 0.2–10 dpa, lower-dose neutron irradiation in the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) and High
Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR), and physics-based modeling to predict irradiation-damage evolution under
neutron irradiation while accounting for carbon content and microstructural variability [1].
 Previous fiscal years progressively established the experimental and computational capabilities needed to
address this challenge. The fiscal year 2023 work introduced the combined use of ion irradiation and modeling
to investigate how alloy chemistry, microsegregation, and dislocation-cell structures affect radiation-driven
microstructure evolution [3]. In fiscal year 2024, the irradiation challenge problem was further developed
through coordinated experimental and modeling activities across Argonne, Idaho, and Oak Ridge National
Laboratories, while atomistic, cluster dynamics (CD), and spatially resolved calculations were applied to
defect production, transport, clustering, dislocation evolution, and radiation-induced segregation (RIS) [4, 5].
The fiscal year 2025 work further exercised these multiscale predictive descriptions using atomistic point-
defect information and assessed their application across ion- and neutron-relevant irradiation conditions [6].
Together, these efforts provided the experimental benchmarks, material-specific inputs, and computational
capabilities required for systematic model verification, parameter assessment and calibration, comparison
with irradiation data, and targeted application in fiscal year 2026.
 In fiscal year 2026, this work adopted, refined, and exercised these capabilities. The CD calculations are
parameterized using atomistic point-defect information and compared with transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) measurements of self-interstitial atom (SIA)-loop and void number densities and mean diameters [7].
The calculations evaluate how dislocation density, carbon content, and dose rate affect defect-cluster evolution
accessible to experimental characterization. The spatially resolved mesoscale models are applied to nucleation-
informed effective-volume cavity-growth calculations within heterogeneous dislocation-cell structures.
Parameter studies examine how biased dislocation density, unbiased sink density, carbon content, RIS,
and preliminary helium effects influence cavity evolution. Calculations across targeted ion- and neutron-
relevant temperatures and dose rates are used to construct a map identifying cavity-growth regimes as
functions of irradiation conditions and microstructural sinks. The resulting predictions provide future inputs
for void/cavity-swelling calculations, while the predicted RIS fields provide local-composition inputs for
subsequent assessments of precipitation, secondary-phase formation, and their effects on irradiation-creep
performance. Collectively, the fiscal year 2026 work identifies microstructural and irradiation factors that
influence loop populations, heterogeneous void formation, and local composition redistribution in irradiated
LPBF 316 SS. It also establishes a framework for further multiscale integration and validation against
ion-irradiation data and ATR and HFIR neutron-irradiation data.

 2. MEAN-FIELD CLUSTER DYNAMICS MODELING
 CD simulations were used to model defect evolution in ion-irradiated AM 316 SSs under low-dose
irradiation conditions. The model describes the nucleation and growth of SIA loops and voids through the
absorption and emission of mobile point defects [6, 8]. Model predictions were evaluated against experimental
data for ion-irradiated AM 316H steels and showed good agreement with the reported average void size and
average SIA loop size. This methodology was then used to evaluate the effects of dislocation density on
the size and density of irradiation-induced defects, assess how carbon content influences defect growth, and
perform exploratory simulations of how lower dose rates–representative of neutron irradiation conditions–may
affect defect evolution.
 The CD framework provides a mechanistic description of defect populations that are difficult to resolve
directly in mesoscale microstructure models. In this approach, irradiation produces mobile vacancies and
SIAs that can recombine, migrate to sinks, or aggregate into larger defect clusters. The resulting population
balance is represented through coupled rate equations that track the density of defect clusters as a function
of size, irradiation dose, temperature, dose rate, and microstructural sink strength. This makes the method
well suited for evaluating how AM-relevant features, particularly dislocation density and associated sink
strengths, influence void swelling and loop evolution before these defect populations are incorporated into
higher-length-scale modeling [9].

 2.1. Cluster Dynamics Model Formulation
 The CD model used in this work was developed to simulate the evolution of irradiation-induced point
defects, dislocation loops, and voids in austenitic SSs [6]. The evolution of defects over time is modeled
using mean-field reaction-rate theory, in which the concentration of each defect species is evolved by solving
a coupled system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) [9]. This allows us to simulate the evolution of
defects including SIAs, vacancies, small defect clusters, SIA dislocation loops, and spherical voids.
 The CD model represents irradiation-induced defect evolution as a population-balance problem. Irradiation
introduces Frenkel pairs and small defect clusters through source terms associated with displacement cascades.
Following their production, mobile point defects may recombine, aggregate into clusters, be absorbed by
existing clusters, or be removed at microstructural sinks [6, 8]. Defect clusters may also shrink through the
thermal emission of point defects. Accordingly, the rate of change of each defect population can be written

2.2.1. Carbon-modified vacancy diffusion
 The present work requires an effective vacancy diffusion coefficient over the temperature and carbon-
composition ranges relevant to face-centered cubic (FCC) SSs. Carbon is not evolved as an independent
species in the present CD model; instead, its influence is incorporated through a carbon- and temperature-
dependent effective vacancy diffusivity. Interstitial carbon can modify vacancy transport through two coupled
effects. First, attractive carbon–vacancy interactions increase the probability that a vacancy is located in
the vicinity of a carbon atom. Second, the local carbon environment modifies the vacancy migration barrier
[19, 20]. A complete atomistic treatment would require all symmetry-distinct vacancy jumps, carbon–vacancy
association and dissociation events, and correlations between successive jumps. A complete treatment
of all distinct atomistic configurations, dissociation, and jumps would make the problem computationally
intractable. Instead, the present model adopts a simple and consistent assumption that interstitial carbon
diffuses sufficiently fast compared with vacancies such that the local carbon distribution around a vacancy
can be approximated by thermal equilibrium.

2.2.3. Demonstration Case: Effect of Carbon Content
 CD simulations were performed over a range of carbon contents to demonstrate the effect of carbon on
void growth in 316 SS. Simulations were performed at a temperature of 300◦ C, a dose rate of 10−3 dpa/s, and
a dislocation density of 5 × 1014 m−2 . Carbon contents ranging from 0.00 wt% to 0.08 wt% were considered.
In the present model, carbon influences void evolution through the reduction in effective vacancy diffusivity
described in Section 2.2.1. The experimentally measured average dislocation density of 5 × 1014 m−2 was
used as the reference value for this sensitivity study.
 The simulation results in figures 7 and 8 show that increasing the carbon content results in a higher
total void number density while reducing the average void diameter for doses greater than approximately
2 × 10−3 dpa. This trend indicates that reducing vacancy mobility slows the growth of individual voids while
allowing a larger population of smaller vacancy clusters to remain in the system. The reduction in vacancy
mobility also increases both the predicted SIA loop density and average loop diameter.
 The combination of a higher total void density and slower void growth also produces a counterintuitive
trend when only TEM-observable defects are considered. When a minimum observable diameter of 1.5 nm is
applied to the simulated defect population, the observable void density initially decreases with increasing
carbon content because a larger fraction of the vacancy-cluster population remains below the TEM detection
threshold. As the irradiation dose increases, these smaller clusters gradually grow beyond the detection
threshold, causing the trend in observable void density to reverse. Similar differences in the apparent onset
of TEM-observable voids have been reported when comparing irradiated 316L and 316H steels [7]. The
present simulations provide a possible mechanistic explanation for this behavior by showing that increasing
carbon can increase the total vacancy-cluster population while simultaneously delaying the growth of these
clusters into the experimentally observable size range. More broadly, this result highlights the important role
of CD modeling in interpreting irradiation experiments by tracking the evolution of sub-resolution defect

2.2.4. Demonstration Case: Effect of Dose Rate
 One of the major differences between ion and neutron irradiation is the substantially lower dose rate
associated with neutron irradiation. The AMMT program has performed neutron irradiations of LPBF 316H
SS in the HFIR and ATR, with the initial low-dose specimens reaching approximately 2 dpa at irradiation
temperatures between 400◦ C and 600◦ C [31]. To evaluate the effect of decreasing dose rate under conditions
relevant to these experiments, CD simulations were performed at 400◦ C over dose rates ranging from 10−3
to 10−6 dpa/s. The lowest dose rates approach those relevant to the neutron-irradiation conditions considered
being tested by the AMMT program. The upper end of this range corresponds to the ion-irradiation conditions,
while the lower dose rates were selected to approach neutron-irradiation conditions. All other material and
microstructural parameters were held at the reference values for the 0.04 wt% carbon case in Section 2.2.3.
Decreasing the dose rate increases the physical irradiation time required to reach a given dose and substantially
increases the computational cost of the CD calculations. Consequently, the simulations with dose rates below
10−3 dpa/s did not reach the same maximum dose as the highest-dose-rate calculations, and the results from
these simulations should be considered preliminary.
 Figures 9 and 10 show that decreasing the dose rate substantially reduces the predicted number densities
of both voids and SIA loops over the simulated dose range. The effect of dose rate on defect size is more
complex. As shown in Figure 11, decreasing the dose rate from 10−3 to 10−5 dpa/s generally increases the

 2.3. Discussion On Cluster Dynamics Simulations
 The CD simulations show that variations in local AM dislocation structure can significantly affect
irradiation-induced defect evolution. Increasing the prescribed dislocation density increased the sink strength
for mobile point defects, delaying SIA-loop growth and reducing the predicted loop number density. The
closest agreement with the measured loop diameters was obtained near 5 × 1015 m−2 , although the model
underpredicted the measured loop density. This result suggests that the model captures the effect of dislocation
sink strength on loop growth but may not include all mechanisms affecting the absolute densities of defect
clusters.
 The carbon-content CD simulations show that reduced vacancy mobility due to carbon-vacancy trapping
can also strongly change the predicted defect population. Increasing carbon content produced a larger
population of smaller vacancy clusters and reduced the average void diameter. Because more of these
clusters remained below the 1.5 nm TEM detection threshold, the predicted TEM observable void density
initially decreased with increasing carbon content even as vacancy-cluster density for cluster of any size
increased. This trend provides a possible mechanism for the delayed appearance of TEM observable voids in
higher-carbon 316 SSs.
 The dose-rate simulations further demonstrate that defect evolution can change significantly when moving
from ion-irradiation conditions toward the lower dose rates associated with neutron irradiation. Decreasing
the dose rate substantially reduced the predicted number densities of both voids and SIA loops, while generally
increasing the size of the defects large enough to be observed using TEM. The response of the total cluster
population was more complex, indicating that dose rate changes both the number of surviving clusters

 17

and the distribution of defects among cluster sizes. These lower-dose-rate calculations remain preliminary
because the longer physical irradiation times substantially increase the computational cost of the current
CD implementation. We are currently exploring options with the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling
and Simulation (NEAMS) program to move our CD model to a higher-performance, more computationally
efficient code base, being developed by NEAMS, in the future. This change is expected to make simulations
notably faster, enabling calculations at higher temperatures and lower dose rates, which will facilitate more
direct comparisons with neutron-irradiation experiments.

 2.4. Collaboration With NEAMS: Improving CD Performance
 The collaboration with the NEAMS program has already produced its first concrete result. As part of this
effort, a new CD solver based on the SUNDIALS time-integration library was developed to model concurrent
evolution of loops and voids under irradiation. The NEAMS-developed code is based on the previous CD
work by [33, 34] for radiation-enhanced precipitation while incorporating defect dynamics (e.g., dislocation
loops and voids). The grouping scheme by Golubov et al. [35] was incorporated and the new solver was
benchmarked against the code with the PETSc solver and used to extend the calculations reported here to
higher doses and temperatures. The grouping method is particularly crucial in this task due to the size of
voids. For example, a 50-nm-diameter void, which can form during high-temperature irradiation, contains
5.6 × 106 vacancies and is numerically challenging to model without grouping. The NEAMS-developed
CD code reproduces the previous results at 300 and 400 ◦ C while reaching 10 dpa in less than 0.1% of the
computational time. This significant improvement in computational efficiency enables the higher-temperature
and high-dose CD calculations needed for closer comparison with neutron-irradiation experiments in the
future.
 The expanded SUNDIALS capability provides a computational foundation for the planned collaboration
between AMMT and NEAMS in Fiscal Year 2027. This collaboration will use irradiation and characterization
data generated through AMMT (e.g., ion irradiation and neutron irradiation data) to guide continued
development and calibration of the NEAMS CD model, including improved treatments of mobile interstitial
clusters, evolving defect sinks, as well as other environmental factors associated with neutron irradiation.
The ability to complete calculations to 10 dpa at both temperatures supports the extension of these studies
over a broader range of irradiation conditions.

2.4.1. Summary of cluster dynamics Modeling
 CD modeling was used to evaluate how dislocation density, carbon content, and irradiation dose rate
affect the evolution of irradiation-induced defects in AM 316 SS. The simulations showed that increasing
dislocation density increases the removal of mobile point defects and reduces SIA-loop growth and number
density, while increasing carbon content reduces vacancy mobility and produces a larger population of smaller
vacancy clusters. The carbon effects simulations also showed that changes in the population of defects
below the TEM detection limit can produce an apparent delay in the formation of experimentally observable
voids. Decreasing the irradiation dose rate substantially reduced the predicted void and SIA loop number
densities and altered the resulting cluster size distributions, demonstrating that dose rate must be considered
when comparing ion and neutron irradiation conditions. Overall, these results demonstrate that both the
heterogeneous AM microstructure and irradiation conditions can strongly influence the defect populations
predicted by the CD model.

 3. SPATIALLY-RESOLVED CAVITY EVOLUTION MODELING
 Under irradiation, energetic particles produce vacancies and SIAs that migrate, recombine, cluster,
and are absorbed at microstructural sinks. The resulting microstructural evolution spans initial point-defect
production and reactions over nanometer length scales and nanosecond timescales to RIS and cavity nucleation,
growth, and swelling over micrometers and years. The LPBF process used to fabricate 316 SS introduces
additional spatial complexity through its heterogeneous dislocation-cell structure and solidification-induced
microsegregation. Ion-irradiation experiments have revealed spatially heterogeneous defect and composition
evolution and void swelling within the LPBF microstructure [7]. To interpret these observations, we use
spatially resolved, multiscale modeling to connect the point-defect and cluster populations predicted by
lower-length-scale models with experimentally observable local segregation and cavity evolution within the
distinctive dislocation and composition fields of the AM microstructure. Our approach combines a classical
void-nucleation model with sharp-interface and phase-field descriptions of cavity growth. This framework
accounts for vacancy and SIA production, recombination, biased absorption at dislocations, and unbiased
absorption at other defect sinks such as vacancy clusters. We investigate how biased and unbiased sinks
produce spatially distinct cavity evolution at dislocation cell walls and within cell interiors. Temperature-
and dose-rate-dependent calculations assess how irradiation conditions shift the boundaries among cavity
dissolution, growth, and saturation. We also investigate selected composition-dependent effects on void
evolution, including Fe–Cr–Ni RIS, carbon-induced reduction of vacancy diffusivity, and helium-induced
cavity stabilization through internal pressure.

 3.1. Spatially-resolved Cavity-growth Model Formulations And
 Methodology
 The modeling approach utilized here combines descriptions of the irradiation response across multiple
length scales. A coupled homogeneous rate-theory and classical nucleation calculation is used to first estimate
the void-nucleation rate and corresponding number density of cavities. The predicted cavity density is
then used to define the representative material volume associated with each cavity in subsequent dynamic
growth simulations. Spatially resolved mesoscale modeling is utilized to simulate cavity evolution within
homogeneous or prescribed heterogeneous dislocation and composition fields. Sharp-interface and phase-
field descriptions of the same point-defect transport and cavity-surface motion are cross-verified under
equivalent conditions; either formulation is utilized depending on the geometry, computational cost, and
interface resolution required for the application. The sharp-interface formulation is used for computationally
efficient representative-cell calculations and broad parametric studies, whereas the phase-field formulation
provides a general framework for treating complex cavity morphologies and interactions with heterogeneous
microstructural features. Wherever possible, the formulations utilize consistent point-defect production,
recombination, sink-loss, transport, and thermodynamic parameters while retaining the interface treatment
required by each method.

3.1.4. Composition effects on cavity growth
 A comprehensive treatment of composition-dependent cavity evolution would require the coupled
description of solute redistribution, impurity–defect trapping, gas production and transport, and their feedback
on point-defect and interface kinetics. For simplicity, the present work isolates three well-established
mechanisms to assess their effects: carbon–vacancy trapping through a reduced effective vacancy diffusivity,

 3.3. Effects Of Sink Bias And Spatial Sink Heterogeneity
3.3.1. Void growth at representative cell-wall and cell-interior
 The verified phase field model was used to examine the effect of homogeneous sink densities (biased
ρb and unbiased ρu ) on coupled cavity nucleation and growth. Figure 16 shows the vacancy concentration
surrounding representative voids at the cell wall and within the cell interior at 600 °C and K0 = 10−3 dpa/s.
The initial cavity radius was 1 nm, the unbiased sink density was 1014 m−2 , and the biased dislocation
densities were 1014 and 1012 m−2 at the cell wall and cell interior, respectively. These values were selected to
represent high-temperature ion-irradiation, while the two-order-of-magnitude difference in biased dislocation
density provides an idealized representation of dislocation-rich cell walls and comparatively dislocation-
poor cell interiors of the LPBF microstructure. The spatially uniform unbiased sink density, represents a
background population of vacancy clusters and provides a common sink-strength baseline in both regions,
allowing the effect of the heterogeneous biased dislocation density to be isolated. The nucleation calculation
gives representative domain radii of 2570 nm at the wall and greater than 5000 nm in the interior. The wall
cavity grows from 1 nm to 24.2 nm by 10 dpa, whereas the interior cavity decreases in size and dissolves.
The different void growth behavior at wall and interior results from the relative contributions of biased and
unbiased sinks. Dislocations absorb SIAs more efficiently than vacancies for ZI > ZV . A sufficiently large

 28

biased dislocation sink removes more SIAs from the matrix and leaves a net vacancy flux available for cavity
growth. The unbiased sinks remove vacancies and SIAs equally and reduce the vacancy supersaturation
without producing an absorption bias. The calculation therefore predicts preferential cavity growth at the
cell wall and suppression or dissolution within the cell interior, consistent with the heterogeneous cavity
distributions observed after ion irradiation of LPBF 316 SS at 600 °C [7].

3.3.2. Heterogeneous void growth in a dislocation-cell structure
 The preceding spherical void calculations, performed using the phase-field implementation, identified
biased and unbiased sink densities that promote void growth, stability, or shrinkage in representative, but
homogeneous, cell-wall and cell-interior environments. Here, we utilize two-dimensional calculations to
test whether two initially identical voids can evolve differently when placed within the same heterogeneous
dislocation-cell structure. The sink densities required in the two-dimensional calculations differ from those in
the spherical calculations due to the change in effective matrix-to-void volume ratio. This difference changes
the competition between point-defect removal at bulk sinks and point-defect flux to the void surfaces. A
1000 nm × 1000 nm domain is employed, with one 8-nm-radius void positioned in the cell wall and another
of the same size in the cell interior. Two sink-density distributions are examined: one favors cell-wall growth,
the second favors cell-interior growth. Each heterogeneous calculation is compared with two homogeneous
references containing voids at the same positions: one uses the cell-wall sink densities uniformly throughout
the domain, and the other uses the cell-interior sink densities uniformly throughout the domain.
 The cell-wall-favored case is shown in Figure 17. The cell interior is assigned (ρb , ρu ) = (1012 , 3 ×
10 ) m−2 , whereas the cell wall is assigned (ρb , ρu ) = (1014 , 1014 ) m−2 . In the homogeneous cell-

approximately 28.4 nm by 10 dpa. In the homogeneous cell-interior reference, applying ρb = 1012 m−2 and
ρu = 3 × 1015 m−2 causes both voids to shrink to approximately 7.82 nm. Assigning these sink densities to
their respective regions within the heterogeneous domain preserves the qualitative behavior predicted by the

interact through their shared vacancy and SIA fields.

 3.4. Effect Of Irradiation Parameters On Void Evolution
 The effects of irradiation conditions are evaluated using the coupled void-nucleation and spherical
void-growth calculations described in Sections 3.1.1 and 3.1.2. For each temperature and dose rate, the
homogeneous point-defect concentrations obtained from Equation (23) are used to evaluate the reference
nucleation rate Jnuc,ref from Equation (26). The nucleation rate determines the void density Nv = Jnuc,ref tnuc

and corresponding representative-domain radius Rout = [3/(4πNv )]1/3 . A single seed void of initial radius
R0 = 1 nm is then evolved within this representative volume. This coupling retains the effect of the predicted
void population on the matrix volume available to each void while resolving the radial vacancy and SIA fields
during growth.

3.4.1. Temperature and dose-rate dependence
 The combined effects of irradiation temperature and dose rate are evaluated for representative, but
homogeneous, cell-wall and cell-interior environments using the spherical growth model. Each calculation
contains one seed void within a spherical representative volume whose radius is determined by the
 Yang
corresponding nucleation calculation. The two environments use DI = 0.2DI and ρu = 1014 m−2 , with
biased dislocation densities of ρb = 1014 and 1012 m−2 for the cell-wall and cell-interior environments,
respectively. A bell-shaped temperature dependence is obtained in Figure 19 for the representative cell-wall
void. At 10−3 dpa/s, the maximum void radius occurs at approximately 600 °C. Reducing the dose rate to
10−4 , 10−5 , and 10−6 dpa/s shifts the maximum to approximately 550, 500, and 425 °C, respectively. This
trend is consistent with the Mansur invariance relation: comparable irradiation behavior requires preservation
of the balance between thermally activated point-defect transport and defect production, so increasing the
dose rate shifts the response to higher temperatures [51]. The cell-interior calculations produce substantially
smaller voids for the selected sink densities. At 10−3 dpa/s, the largest interior-void radius is approximately
2.36 nm, compared with 25.6 nm at the cell wall.

3.4.2. Nucleation-informed growth-regime map
 The range of predicted responses is summarized in the growth-regime map shown in Figure 20. The
horizontal coordinate is the sink-strength bias, (kI2 − kV2 )/kI2 , while the corresponding ratio ρu /ρb is shown
on the upper axis. The vertical coordinate is the representative-cell void density, Nv , obtained from the
nucleation calculation. The right axis reports K0 /DV (T ), which compares the rate of irradiation-induced
defect production with the rate of vacancy migration. Consistent with the Mansur invariance relation,
temperature–dose-rate combinations having similar values of K0 /DV (T ) are expected to produce similar
point-defect conditions when the other material and sink parameters are fixed. Each marker represents the
predicted void radius at 10 dpa for one combination of temperature, dose rate, biased-dislocation density, and
unbiased-sink density. The map separates four characteristic responses. Growth occurs when the sink-strength
bias maintains a net vacancy flux and the void density leaves an adequate representative volume for each
void. Dissolution occurs when the defect-flux bias is insufficient to sustain vacancy absorption against SIA
absorption and vacancy emission. Representative-cell saturation occurs at high void density, where the small
volume assigned to each void limits its available vacancy supply. Sink suppression occurs when removal
at the prescribed sinks reduces the point-defect concentrations sufficiently to suppress the nucleation rate.
Increasing (kI2 − kV2 )/kI2 generally favors growth, whereas increasing ρu /ρb reduces the relative absorption
bias and shifts the behavior toward stability, dissolution, or sink suppression. The map uses parametrically
prescribed values of ρb and ρu together with the void density obtained from the nucleation calculation. It
therefore identifies regimes associated with the represented mechanisms rather than predicting the evolution of
an explicit defect-cluster population. Future coupling to cluster-dynamics calculations will provide dose- and
irradiation-condition-dependent void densities and sink strengths in place of these parametrically prescribed
inputs.
 For the AMMT program, this mapping framework provides a computationally efficient means of screening
how irradiation conditions and microstructural sink populations promote or suppress cavity growth in
LPBF 316H SS and assessing sensitivity to additional factors, including carbon-dependent vacancy mobility
(Section 3.5.1), RIS, and helium pressure. Future coupling to cluster-dynamics calculations will replace the
prescribed inputs with dose- and irradiation-condition-dependent void densities and sink strengths, enabling
the framework to guide targeted validation and support interpretation of accelerated ion-irradiation results
under neutron-relevant conditions.

3.5.2. Helium pressure sensitivity on cavity growth
 Figure 22 compares three initial helium gas-pressure states at 500 °C and K0 = 10−6 dpa/s for a cell-
interior condition with ρu = 1013 m−2 and R0 = 1 nm. The empty cavity (χ0 = 0) and the χ0 = 0.01
cavity, corresponding to approximately 9 He atoms, both shrink. By contrast, the χ0 = 0.5 cavity, which
corresponds to approximately 180 He atoms and an initial pressure of 1.20 GPa, grows. Although this case
is mechanically underpressurized, it lowers the initial surface vacancy concentration from 2.03 × 10−10 to
5.26 × 10−11 at 500 °C. This reduction in the surface vacancy concentration allows the irradiation-generated
vacancy concentration outside the cavity to exceed csV and reverses the vacancy flux relative to the χ0 = 0 and
χ0 = 0.01 cases. The cavity reaches 40.12 nm at 10 dpa. The effect of He here is primarily the suppression
of vacancy emission during the early stage when the cavity is small. As the cavity grows, the pressure ratio
decreases to χ = 1.18 × 10−4 at 10 dpa because the helium inventory is conserved inside the single cavity;
however, the JV − JI flux remains sufficient to overcome the capillary contribution. This calculation of the
He effect on cavity growth is a sensitivity study because it prescribes the initial helium inventory and does not
include helium production, matrix diffusion, trapping, partitioning, emission, or helium-dependent nucleation
density. These processes are required before χ0 can be mapped uniquely to a bulk He-appm/dpa condition.
This study provides the AMMT program with a mechanistic foundation for incorporating particle-spectrum-
dependent helium production into future ion-to-neutron comparisons and predictions of cavity evolution and
swelling under reactor conditions.

 3.6. Summary Of Spatially-resolved Cavity Evolution Modeling
 Spatially resolved mesoscale models for cavity evolution driven by vacancy and SIA supersaturations
were verified through comparisons of predicted cavity-radius evolution and point-defect concentration profiles
under controlled supersaturation and irradiation-driven conditions. The resulting agreement supports their use
for investigating irradiation-induced cavity evolution within heterogeneous AM microstructures. Coupled
void-nucleation and growth calculations demonstrated that the balance between biased dislocation density and
unbiased sink density governs whether voids grow or shrink and whether growth is favored at representative
cell walls or in cell interiors. Two-dimensional simulations resolving a representative dislocation-cell
structure demonstrated preferential cavity growth at cell walls and shrinkage in cell interiors. Assigning
a lower unbiased sink density to the cell interior than to the cell wall reversed this response and favored
growth in the cell interior. Temperature and damage-rate studies showed that maximum cell-wall void growth
shifted to lower temperatures as the damage rate decreased, consistent with the expected difference between
ion- and neutron-relevant irradiation conditions. Sensitivity calculations further assessed selected effects
of carbon, initial helium pressure, and Fe–Cr–Ni RIS on vacancy transport and cavity evolution. Together,
these calculations establish a framework for constructing mechanistic maps of cavity growth across ion- and
neutron-relevant irradiation conditions while accounting for the heterogeneous dislocation and composition
fields characteristic of LPBF microstructures. For the AMMT program, this framework provides a physical
explanation for experimentally observed heterogeneous void formation in LPBF 316 SS: spatial variations
in cell-wall and cell-interior sink populations redistribute vacancies and SIAs, thereby determining where
cavities grow or dissolve. Relating these mechanisms to irradiation temperature and damage rate supports
physics-based interpretation of accelerated ion-irradiation data and future predictions of dimensional swelling
under neutron-relevant conditions.

 4. CONCLUSIONS
 During fiscal year 2026, this work supported the AMMT program goal of accelerating the qualification of
AM nuclear materials by exercising and extending complementary mean-field and spatially resolved models
for irradiation-induced microstructure evolution in LPBF 316 SS. Mean-field cluster dynamics models of
defect-cluster evolution and spatially resolved sharp-interface and phase-field models of cavity growth and
radiation-induced segregation were applied across ion- and neutron-relevant temperatures and dose rates.
Atomistic point-defect information and relevant parameters from the literature were used to parameterize
the calculations. By resolving dislocation-cell heterogeneity and assessing selected effects of carbon, local
composition, and initial helium pressure, this work provides components required for multiscale integration
and for interpreting accelerated ion irradiation experiments in the context of neutron irradiation.
 The CD work provided the population-level connection between point-defect production and
experimentally observable defect populations. The updated assessment compares predicted SIA-loop and
void number densities and mean diameters with TEM measurements at 2, 5, and 10 dpa for irradiation at
300 and 600 °C. At 300 °C, increasing carbon across the 0.00–0.08 wt.% range reduced vacancy mobility
and shifted the vacancy-cluster population toward smaller sizes, delaying the appearance of clusters above
the assumed 1.5-nm analysis cutoff used in the sensitivity calculations. At 400 °C, decreasing the dose
rate from 10−3 to 10−5 dpa/s reduced the predicted void and SIA-loop number densities while generally
increasing the sizes of defects above the analysis cutoff. These results demonstrate why ion-to-neutron
translation must account for changes in both cluster-size distributions and the fraction of defects accessible to
characterization. Future work, to be supported by the NEAMS program, will extend the calculations to lower
dose rates and incorporate dose-dependent dislocation evolution, loop-to-network-dislocation conversion,
and helium–defect interactions.
 The spatially-resolved modeling work provided the framework needed for future integration of defect
populations with the heterogeneous dislocation and composition fields of the LPBF microstructure.
Verification calculations established consistent cavity-radius evolution and point-defect concentration
profiles under controlled supersaturation and irradiation-driven conditions. Coupling void nucleation with
growth related the predicted nucleation-derived void number density to the vacancy supply available to
individual cavities, while two-dimensional calculations demonstrated that local sink conditions can favor
cavity growth at either cell walls or cell interiors. For representative prescribed cell-wall sink conditions,
the temperature associated with the largest predicted 10-dpa void radius shifted to lower values as the
dose rate decreased. A growth-regime mapping framework was established to identify void-nucleation
and growth regimes as functions of irradiation conditions and microstructural sink strengths. Sensitivity
studies of carbon, initial helium pressure, and RIS identified mechanisms that influence vacancy transport,
cavity survival, and subsequent growth. The calculations showed that carbon generally suppressed cavity
growth, whereas increasing initial helium pressure promoted cavity growth. The locally RIS-coupled
calculations produced void-growth responses distinct from the defect-only calculations, indicating that the
prescribed sink densities and diffusivities must be recalibrated or replaced with properties informed by
CD or lower-length-scale calculations. These results establish a mechanistic framework for interpreting
heterogeneous cavity evolution while identifying sink strengths and material parameters that require
calibration before quantitative predictions are made.
 The spatially-resolved calculations identified biased dislocation density and unbiased sink density as
critical parameters for heterogeneous void evolution. Because these quantities evolve with dose and irradiation
conditions, their identification provides the basis for integrating the CD and mesoscale models. In an integrated
workflow, dose-dependent nuclei densities and cluster sink strengths predicted by the CD model would be
transferred to the spatially resolved calculations instead of being prescribed independently. The resulting
dose-dependent cavity number densities and size distributions, including their spatial variation between
cell-wall and cell-interior regions, can be used to calculate the evolving cavity volume fraction and thereby

 39

provide quantitative void-swelling predictions. These predictions could be further validated against available
ion irradiation measurements and the limited neutron irradiation data available from the ATR and HFIR.
Uncertainty quantification would enable uncertainties in point-defect transport, dislocation and cluster sink
strengths, carbon and helium effects, and experimental measurements to be propagated through both modeling
scales. The predicted RIS fields would also provide local-composition inputs for assessing irradiation-induced
precipitation and secondary-phase formation, whose effects would be important for subsequent predictions
of irradiation swelling and creep performance. Such an integrated and validated framework would provide
predictions of heterogeneous defect and composition evolution and swelling, together with the microstructural
inputs needed to assess irradiation performance under neutron-irradiation conditions relevant to in-reactor
operation.
