Spectral perturbation theory for wall-admittance effects on compressible boundary-layer instability
Jiguang Yu, Louis Shuo Wang, Ye Liang

Abstract
Thin wall treatments modify high-speed boundary-layer instability through the pressure they admit or absorb at the wall. This paper develops a unified admittance formulation for such effects on trapped compressible Rayleigh modes. For a simple rigid-wall eigenpair, we prove the spectral sensitivity law c ⁡ ( A ) = c 0 + K ​ A + 𝒪 ⁡ ( | A | 2 ) , δ ​ σ = α ​ Im ⁡ ( K ​ A ) + 𝒪 ⁡ ( | A | 2 ) , c(A)=c_{0}+KA+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2}),\qquad\delta\sigma=\alpha\operatorname{Im}(KA)+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2}), where A A is the wall admittance and K K is an explicit functional of the rigid-wall eigenfunction. The formula separates wall physics from outer-mode physics and yields a phase criterion for stabilisation. Matched asymptotics show that viscous and thermal wall layers, blind-pore coatings and shallow non-separating roughness all reduce to this same boundary condition, with additive leading admittances. Mach-4.5 computations validate the sensitivity coefficient and demonstrate porous damping, viscous-wall damping and sign-changing reactive roughness effects.

Introduction and main results

The growth of instability waves in laminar high-speed boundary layers
controls laminar–turbulent transition and thereby surface heating and drag
on supersonic and hypersonic vehicles
(
Mack 1984
;
Fedorov 2011
;
Wang and Yu 2025
;
Schuabb et al. 2025
;
Zhuang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Wu 2023
;
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Cao et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2022
)
.
At the Mach numbers of interest the dominant instabilities are the first
(inflectional) mode, rendered inviscidly unstable by the generalised
inflection point
(
Yu et al. 2026
;
Lees and Lin 1946
;
Liang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Marxen et al. 2014
;
Dong and Zhao 2021
)
, and the second (Mack) mode, a trapped
acoustic mode of the waveguide formed between the wall and the sonic line

(
Mack 1984
;
Dong et al. 2020
;
Lu et al. 2025
)
. Because these modes are inviscid at leading
order, with
\mathcal{O}(1)
pressure fluctuations at the wall, they are sensitive to
what the wall does with that pressure: to the transpiration admitted by a
porous coating
(
Cai et al. 2026
;
Fedorov et al. 2001
;
Liang et al. 2025
;
Rasheed et al. 2002
;
Fedorov 2011
)
, to the
displacement of the effective impermeable plane by distributed roughness

(
Fujii 2006
;
Fransson et al. 2006
;
Chuvakhov and Fedorov 2016
;
Wang and Yu 2026
)
, and to the mass flux pumped by
the thin viscous Stokes and thermal layers that survive at the wall even
when the mode itself is inviscid
(
Mack 1984
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Gaponov 1975
)
.

Each of these wall effects possesses a substantial literature and its own
formalism: viscous corrections are computed from full Orr–Sommerfeld-type
systems or from triple-deck theory
(
Smith 1979
;
Yu and Wang 2026
;
Skorokhodov 2007
;
Gallagher et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2026
;
Landahl 1990
;
Huang and Inger 1983
;
Rizzetta et al. 1978
)
; coatings are inserted
as semi-empirical acoustic impedances
(
Fedorov et al. 2001
;
Brès et al. 2013
;
Tritarelli et al. 2015
)
; roughness is treated by homogenisation

(
Bechert and Bartenwerfer 1989
;
Yu et al. 2026
;
Luchini et al. 1991
)
coupled to receptivity or stability
analyses. The novelty of the present paper is not the separate existence of
these reductions.
The contribution is the asymptotic and spectral
synthesis
: under explicit ordering assumptions, all three mechanisms —
and their combinations — reduce to the same admittance boundary condition
on the same outer compressible Rayleigh problem, and the leading effect of
any of them on a trapped eigenmode is determined by one sensitivity
coefficient computable from the rigid-wall eigenfunction. This separates
wall physics, represented by a complex admittance
A
, from outer-mode
physics, represented by a complex coefficient
K
, and gives an explicit
phase criterion for stabilisation. The admittance description itself has a
long history for compliant walls in incompressible flow

(
Benjamin 1960
;
Landahl 1962
;
Wang et al. 2026
;
Carpenter and Garrad 1985
;
Koseki et al. 2025
;
Malik et al. 2018
)
and for liner acoustics

(
Zwikker and Kosten 1949
;
Yu et al. 2026
;
Melling 1973
)
; its use here as the common endpoint
of matched asymptotics for viscous, porous and rough walls, together with
the sensitivity calculus, is, to our knowledge, new.

The main results are as follows. Let
\hat{p}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha(x-ct)}
be a
pressure disturbance of the compressible Rayleigh problem
(§
2
) on a boundary layer with profiles
U(y),T(y)
at Mach
number
M
, and let the wall enter only through the admittance

A(\alpha,c)=-\,\frac{\hat{v}(0^{+})}{\hat{p}(0^{+})},

(1)

the ratio of outward wall-normal velocity to pressure seen by the outer flow
at the wall (
A=0
: rigid;
\operatorname{Re}A\geq 0
: passive). Then:

(i)

(
Theorem
1
, §
3
.
) If

(\hat{p}_{0},c_{0})
is a simple, trapped, rigid-wall eigenpair, the perturbed
eigenvalue is

c(A)=c_{0}+KA+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2}),\qquad K=\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha\,\hat{p}_{0}(0)^{2}}{c_{0}\,I},

(2)

with

) of the rigid-wall
eigenfunction; consequently the growth rate
\sigma=\alpha c_{i}
shifts by

\delta\sigma=\alpha\operatorname{Im}(KA)+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2})
, and an admittance of phase

(Corollary

).

(ii)

(

) Under the thin-layer ordering

),
(

), (

) and (

), additive in combination.

(iii)

(
§
5
.
) For a Mach-4.5 adiabatic

) to relative errors below

(table
1
) and confirm the phase criterion in

crosses

.

The objective is deliberately limited. We do not seek a replacement for
full viscous stability theory, direct simulation, or spatial transition
prediction. We identify the common asymptotic structure of several thin
wall mechanisms, prove the spectral perturbation result that makes the
reduction useful, and demonstrate both at a representative hypersonic
condition. The Mach-4.5 calculations are a proof of concept: they validate
the sensitivity formula against direct eigenvalue computations and show how
porous absorption, viscous wall layers and shallow roughness occupy
different regions of the same complex-admittance plane. Amplitude-dependent
and acoustic admittances can be inserted into the same boundary condition;
these extensions are outlined, deliberately briefly, in Appendix
A
.

The paper is organised as follows. Section

formulates

3
states the spectral
setting, proves Theorem

and its phase corollary, and

5

2
The compressible Rayleigh problem with wall admittance

Velocities are scaled by the free-stream speed
U_{\infty}^{*}
, temperature and
density by their free-stream values, pressure perturbations by

\rho_{\infty}^{*}U_{\infty}^{*2}
, and lengths by the local Blasius scale

L^{*}=(2\nu_{\infty}^{*}x^{*}/U_{\infty}^{*})^{1/2}
, so that

R=U_{\infty}^{*}L^{*}/\nu_{\infty}^{*}=(2Re_{x})^{1/2}\gg 1
. The base flow is a
compressible boundary layer
U(y),T(y)
of a perfect gas
(
\gamma=1.4
); the numerical illustrations use the compressible Blasius
solution with the Chapman law
\mu=T
, unit base-flow Prandtl number and an
adiabatic wall at
M=4.5
, for which
T_{w}=5.05
,

\lambda_{w}\equiv U^{\prime}(0)=0.0930
and
\delta_{99}=10.27

(figure
5
a). Disturbances take the locally parallel form

(\hat{u},\hat{v},\hat{p},\hat{\theta})(y)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha(x-ct)}+\mathrm{c.c.}

with
\alpha
real and
c=c_{r}+\mathrm{i}c_{i}
complex; the temporal growth rate is

\sigma=\alpha c_{i}
.

Outside all wall layers the pressure obeys the compressible Rayleigh
equation

\hat{p}^{\prime\prime}-\left(\frac{2U^{\prime}}{U-c}-\frac{T^{\prime}}{T}\right)\hat{p}^{\prime}-\alpha^{2}\!\left(1-\frac{M^{2}(U-c)^{2}}{T}\right)\hat{p}=0,

(3)

with the wall-normal velocity recovered from the
y
-momentum equation

\mathrm{i}\alpha\bar{\rho}(U-c)\hat{v}=-\hat{p}^{\prime}
,
\bar{\rho}=1/T
. Equation
(
3
) admits the self-adjoint form

\big(\Phi\,\hat{p}^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}=\alpha^{2}\big(\Phi-M^{2}\big)\hat{p},\qquad\Phi(y;c)=\frac{T}{(U-c)^{2}}.

In the uniform stream the decaying solution is

\hat{p}\sim\exp[-\gamma\,(y-y_{b})],\qquad\gamma=\alpha\big[1-M^{2}(1-c)^{2}\big]^{1/2},\quad\operatorname{Re}\gamma>0,

(4)

applied at the edge
y_{b}
of the computational domain; modes with

\operatorname{Re}\gamma>0
are trapped.

All wall physics is condensed into the admittance (
1
),
where
0^{+}
denotes the bottom of the outer region after the wall sublayers
have been absorbed. Combining (
1
) with the
y
-momentum
equation at
y=0
(where
U=0
) gives the outer wall condition in pressure
form,

\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)=-\,\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha c\,A}{T_{w}}\,\hat{p}(0).

(5)

Here positive
\hat{v}
is taken in the outward normal direction, from the
wall into the fluid, so that
-\overline{p^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\,|_{y=0}
is the acoustic
power per unit area entering the wall; for a neutral wave it equals

\tfrac{1}{2}|\hat{p}_{w}|^{2}\operatorname{Re}A
, whence a
passive
wall — one that can
only absorb disturbance energy — has
\operatorname{Re}A\ \geq\ 0
, with the standard generalisation for
c_{i}\neq 0
. Passivity serves
repeatedly below as a sign check on the asymptotics; it does
not
by
itself imply stabilisation, which is governed by the phase of
A
relative
to that of the mode’s sensitivity coefficient (Corollary
1
).
We present two extensions in Appendix
A
to illustrate the reach of the boundary condition
(
5
) beyond linear, trapped dynamics; both are presented as
outlines, not as developed theories.

3
The spectral sensitivity theorem

3.1
Dispersion function and analytic eigenvalue branch

We first make explicit the analytic eigenvalue branch to which the
sensitivity calculation applies. Fix
\alpha
and choose the branch of

\gamma(c)=\alpha\big[1-M^{2}(1-c)^{2}\big]^{1/2}

with
\operatorname{Re}\gamma(c_{0})>0
. In a neighbourhood of a trapped eigenvalue

c_{0}
, away from the sonic branch points and from critical singularities
on the real
y
-axis, the coefficients of the Rayleigh equation
(
3
) and the far-field exponent
\gamma(c)
are analytic
functions of
c
. Let
P(y;c)
denote the solution of
(
3
) obtained by shooting from
y=y_{b}
with the normalisation

P(y_{b};c)=1,\qquad P^{\prime}(y_{b};c)=-\gamma(c).

Standard analytic dependence of solutions of linear ordinary differential
equations on parameters implies that
P(0;c)
and
P^{\prime}(0;c)
are analytic
functions of
c
in this neighbourhood.

For a wall admittance
A
, define the scalar dispersion function

D(c,A)=P^{\prime}(0;c)+\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha cA}{T_{w}}P(0;c).

The eigenvalues of the Rayleigh problem with admittance boundary condition
(
5
) are precisely the zeros of
D(c,A)
. In particular,
the rigid-wall eigenvalue satisfies

D(c_{0},0)=P^{\prime}(0;c_{0})=0
.
We call
c_{0}
simple if

D_{c}(c_{0},0)\neq 0.

(6)

Equivalently, the zero of the rigid-wall dispersion relation

D(c,0)=P^{\prime}(0;c)
at
c=c_{0}
is simple. Under this condition, the
implicit function theorem gives a unique analytic eigenvalue branch

c=c(A)
, for
|A|
sufficiently small, such that

D(c(A),A)=0
and
c(0)=c_{0}
.
Differentiating
D(c(A),A)=0
at
A=0
gives

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{D_{A}(c_{0},0)}{D_{c}(c_{0},0)}
.
The remainder of the proof of Theorem
1
evaluates this quotient
by Green’s identity and shows that it is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha\hat{p}_{0}(0)^{2}}{c_{0}I}
.

Assumption 1

(Spectral setting)
.

The rigid-wall eigenvalue
c_{0}
is a trapped eigenvalue of the dispersion
relation
D(c,0)=0
, with
\operatorname{Re}\gamma(c_{0})>0
, and is simple in the
sense of (
6
). The chosen neighbourhood of
c_{0}

does not contain sonic branch points or critical singularities on the real

y
-axis; if a critical point is present, the standard causal indentation
is used. The admittance perturbation is measured by
A(\alpha,c_{0})
. If
the physical wall law has the form
A=\varepsilon\mathcal{A}(\alpha,c)
,
with
\varepsilon\ll 1
and
\mathcal{A}
analytic near
c_{0}
, then the

c
-dependence of
\mathcal{A}
contributes only to the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

term in the eigenvalue expansion.

Lemma 1

(The denominator as a dispersion derivative)
.

Let
P(y;c)
be the shooting solution used in
§
3.1
, normalised by

P(y_{b};c)=1,\qquad P^{\prime}(y_{b};c)=-\gamma(c),

and let
D_{0}(c):=D(c,0)=P^{\prime}(0;c)

be the rigid-wall dispersion function. Suppose that

D_{0}(c_{0})=0
, and set

\hat{p}_{0}(y)=P(y;c_{0})
.
Then
\displaystyle D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0})=-\,\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{T_{w}\hat{p}_{0}(0)}\,I
,
where
I
is the denominator defined in (
10
). In particular,

I=0
if and only if

D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0})=0
.
Thus, if
c_{0}
is a simple zero of
D_{0}
, then
I\neq 0
.

Proof.

Differentiate the self-adjoint Rayleigh equation

(\Phi\hat{p}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\alpha^{2}(\Phi-M^{2})\hat{p},\qquad\Phi(y;c)=\frac{T}{(U-c)^{2}},

with respect to
c
at
c=c_{0}
. Write

q(y)=\frac{\partial P}{\partial c}(y;c_{0})
and

\Phi_{0}(y)=\Phi(y;c_{0})
.
Since
\hat{p}_{0}=P(\cdot;c_{0})
, the differentiated equation is

(\Phi_{0}q^{\prime})^{\prime}-\alpha^{2}(\Phi_{0}-M^{2})q=-\left[\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}-\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}\hat{p}_{0}\right].

(7)

Multiply (
7
) by
\hat{p}_{0}
, integrate over

(0,y_{b})
, and integrate by parts on both sides, using the homogeneous equation
satisfied by
\hat{p}_{0}
. This yields the exact Green’s identity for the parameter derivative:

\Big[\Phi_{0}\big(\hat{p}_{0}q^{\prime}-\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}q\big)+\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}\hat{p}_{0}\,\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}\Big]_{0}^{y_{b}}=\int_{0}^{y_{b}}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}\left(\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}+\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{0}^{\,2}\right)\mathrm{d}y.

(8)

We now evaluate the endpoint terms on the left-hand side. At the rigid wall,

\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0,\qquad q^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\partial}{\partial c}P^{\prime}(0;c)\Big|_{c=c_{0}}=D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0}).

Because
\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
, the term proportional to
\partial\Phi/\partial c
vanishes identically, hence the lower endpoint contributes

\displaystyle-\Phi_{0}(0)\hat{p}_{0}(0)q^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{T_{w}}{c_{0}^{2}}\hat{p}_{0}(0)D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0})
.

At
y_{b}
, the shooting normalisation gives
P(y_{b};c)=1
, so

\hat{p}_{0}(y_{b})=1
and
q(y_{b})=0
. Moreover
P^{\prime}(y_{b};c)=-\gamma(c)
, so

\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(y_{b})=-\gamma_{0}
and
q^{\prime}(y_{b})=-\gamma^{\prime}(c_{0})
.
Direct substitution into the upper boundary term yields exactly
-I_{\infty}
:

-\Phi_{0}(y_{b})\gamma^{\prime}(c_{0})-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}(y_{b})\gamma_{0}=-\frac{1}{(1-c_{0})^{2}}\frac{\alpha^{2}-\gamma_{0}^{2}}{\gamma_{0}(1-c_{0})}-\frac{2}{(1-c_{0})^{3}}\gamma_{0}=-\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}+\alpha^{2}}{\gamma_{0}(1-c_{0})^{3}}=-I_{\infty}.

Moving this upper endpoint contribution
-I_{\infty}
to the right-hand side of (
8
) completes the exact definition of
I
.
Therefore (
8
) reduces to

\displaystyle-\frac{T_{w}}{c_{0}^{2}}\hat{p}_{0}(0)D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0})=I
.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0})=-\,\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{T_{w}\hat{p}_{0}(0)}\,I
.
If instead the pressure eigenfunction is normalised by the wall condition

\hat{p}_{0}(0)=1
, the same calculation gives the same equivalence with the
corresponding non-zero normalisation factor. In either normalisation,

I=0
if and only if

D_{0}^{\prime}(c_{0})=0
.
Thus a simple zero of the rigid dispersion relation has
I\neq 0
.

Theorem 1

(Admittance sensitivity of a compressible Rayleigh mode)
.

Let
(\hat{p}_{0},c_{0})
be a rigid-wall eigenpair of
(
3
), (
4
) satisfying
\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
, under
Assumption
1
, and let the wall condition be perturbed to
(
5
). Then, for sufficiently small
|A|
,

c(A)=c_{0}+KA+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2}),\qquad K=\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha\,\hat{p}_{0}(0)^{2}}{c_{0}\,I},

(9)

where

I=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2T}{(U-c_{0})^{3}}\Big(\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}+\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{0}^{\,2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y\;+\;I_{\infty},\qquad I_{\infty}=\frac{2}{(1-c_{0})^{3}}\,\frac{(\gamma_{0}^{2}+\alpha^{2})\,\hat{p}_{0}(y_{b})^{2}}{2\gamma_{0}},

(10)

the integral being taken over the boundary layer up to
y_{b}
and the tail
term
I_{\infty}
accounting exactly for the uniform-stream continuation
(
4
). Consequently

\delta\sigma=\alpha\,\operatorname{Im}(KA)+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2}).

(11)

Proof.

By §
3.1
, the rigid-wall eigenvalue is a simple zero of
the analytic dispersion function,

D(c_{0},0)=0,\qquad D_{c}(c_{0},0)\neq 0.

Hence the implicit function theorem gives a unique analytic eigenvalue
branch
c=c(A)
, for
|A|
sufficiently small, satisfying

D(c(A),A)=0
and
c(0)=c_{0}
.
It remains to compute the first derivative of this branch at
A=0
.
We do this by Green’s identity, in a form independent of the normalisation
of the eigenfunction.

Let
\hat{p}=\hat{p}_{0}+\delta\hat{p}
and

c=c_{0}+\delta c
,
where
(\hat{p},c)
lies on the analytic branch. Write

\Phi_{0}(y)=\Phi(y;c_{0}),\qquad\Phi(y)=\Phi(y;c)=\frac{T(y)}{(U(y)-c)^{2}}.

The rigid and perturbed eigenfunctions satisfy

(\Phi_{0}\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\alpha^{2}(\Phi_{0}-M^{2})\hat{p}_{0},\qquad(\Phi\hat{p}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\alpha^{2}(\Phi-M^{2})\hat{p}.

(12)

Multiply the first equation in (
12
) by
\hat{p}
, the second by

\hat{p}_{0}
, subtract, and integrate from
0
to
y_{b}
. Expanding the integrand, keeping terms up to first order in the perturbations
\delta c
and
\delta\hat{p}
, and carefully retaining all first-order boundary variations gives

\Big[\Phi_{0}\big(\hat{p}_{0}\,\delta\hat{p}^{\prime}-\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}\,\delta\hat{p}\big)+\delta\Phi\,\hat{p}_{0}\,\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}\Big]_{0}^{y_{b}}=\delta c\int_{0}^{y_{b}}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}\left(\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}+\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{0}^{\,2}\right)\mathrm{d}y+\mathcal{O}(|\delta c|^{2}+|\delta c|\,\|\delta\hat{p}\|).

(13)

Here
\displaystyle\delta\Phi=\delta c\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\big|_{c_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(|\delta c|^{2})
with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}=\frac{2T}{(U-c_{0})^{3}}
,
and products such as
\hat{p}\hat{p}_{0}
and
\hat{p}^{\prime}\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}
have been
replaced by
\hat{p}_{0}^{2}
and
\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}
to first order.

We now evaluate the boundary terms. Set

B(y)=\Phi_{0}\big(\hat{p}_{0}\,\delta\hat{p}^{\prime}-\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}\,\delta\hat{p}\big)+\delta\Phi\,\hat{p}_{0}\,\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime},

so that the left-hand side of (
13
) is
B(y_{b})-B(0)
.
At the wall,
\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
. The perturbed wall condition
(
5
) gives

\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha cA}{T_{w}}\hat{p}(0).

Since
\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)=\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)+\delta\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)
,
\hat{p}(0)=\hat{p}_{0}(0)+\delta\hat{p}(0)
, and
c=c_{0}+\delta c
, expanding the boundary condition yields

\delta\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha c_{0}A}{T_{w}}\hat{p}_{0}(0)+\mathcal{O}(|A|\,|\delta c|+|A|\,|\delta\hat{p}(0)|).

Consequently, noting that the
\delta\Phi
term in
B(0)
vanishes identically because
\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
, we have

-B(0)=-\Phi_{0}(0)\big(\hat{p}_{0}(0)\delta\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)-\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\delta\hat{p}(0)\big)=\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha A}{c_{0}}\hat{p}_{0}(0)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(|A|\,|\delta c|+|A|\,|\delta\hat{p}(0)|),

because
U(0)=0
and hence
\Phi_{0}(0)=T_{w}/c_{0}^{2}
.

It remains to account for the upper endpoint. At
y=y_{b}
the solution is
matched to the uniform-stream tail

\hat{p}_{0}(y)=\hat{p}_{0}(y_{b})\exp[-\gamma_{0}(y-y_{b})],\qquad\gamma_{0}=\alpha[1-M^{2}(1-c_{0})^{2}]^{1/2},\qquad\operatorname{Re}\gamma_{0}>0.

The variation of the profile
\Phi(y;c)
and the decay exponent
\gamma(c)
contributes to the boundary term at
y_{b}
. Explicit evaluation of
B(y_{b})
by matching with the perturbed tail exactly yields
B(y_{b})=-\delta c\,I_{\infty}+\mathcal{O}(|\delta c|^{2})
. Equivalently, and more invariantly, this total boundary contribution is identical to the closed-form continuation of the integral in (
13
) over the uniform stream
y>y_{b}
. Since
U=T=1
there,

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}=\frac{2}{(1-c_{0})^{3}},\qquad\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}+\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{0}^{\,2}=(\gamma_{0}^{2}+\alpha^{2})\hat{p}_{0}(y_{b})^{2}\exp[-2\gamma_{0}(y-y_{b})].

Thus

\int_{y_{b}}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial c}\Big|_{c_{0}}\left(\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}+\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{0}^{\,2}\right)\mathrm{d}y=\frac{2}{(1-c_{0})^{3}}\frac{(\gamma_{0}^{2}+\alpha^{2})\hat{p}_{0}(y_{b})^{2}}{2\gamma_{0}}=I_{\infty}.

Substituting the wall contribution
B(0)
and the tail contribution

\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha A}{c_{0}}\hat{p}_{0}(0)^{2}=\delta c\left[\int_{0}^{y_{b}}\frac{2T}{(U-c_{0})^{3}}\left(\hat{p}_{0}^{\prime\,2}+\alpha^{2}\hat{p}_{0}^{\,2}\right)\mathrm{d}y+I_{\infty}\right]+\mathcal{O}(|\delta c|^{2}+|\delta c|\,\|\delta\hat{p}\|+|A|\,|\delta c|+|A|\,|\delta\hat{p}(0)|).

By the analytic branch construction, both

where

Finally,

Corollary 1

(Phase criterion)
.

Write
A=|A|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}
and
K=|K|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{K}}
. Then, to first
order,
\delta\sigma=\alpha|K||A|\sin(\varphi+\varphi_{K})
: the admittance
stabilises the mode if and only if
\operatorname{Im}(K\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi})<0
, i.e. if
and only if its phase lies in the open half-plane

\varphi\in(-\pi-\varphi_{K},\,-\varphi_{K})
(mod
2\pi
).

As demonstrated in Figure
1
, the spectral sensitivity theorem elegantly decouples the outer mode physics from the inner wall mechanisms (Section
4
) via a simple inner product.
Proposition
1
rewrites this condition in Cartesian
form and gives the special cases used below for resistive, positive-reactive
and negative-reactive wall responses.

Proposition 1

(Geometry of the stabilising phase band)
.

Let
K=K_{R}+\mathrm{i}K_{I}\neq 0
, and let

A=A_{R}+\mathrm{i}A_{I}=|A|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}
.
To first order in
|A|
,

\delta\sigma=\alpha\operatorname{Im}(KA)=\alpha(K_{R}A_{I}+K_{I}A_{R})
.
Hence the stabilising admittances form the open half-plane

K_{R}A_{I}+K_{I}A_{R}<0.

(14)

In particular:

\text{purely resistive walls }(A>0)\text{ stabilise if and only if }K_{I}<0,

\text{positive reactance }(A=\mathrm{i}a,\ a>0)\text{ stabilises if and only if }K_{R}<0,

and

\text{negative reactance }(A=-\mathrm{i}a,\ a>0)\text{ stabilises if and only if }K_{R}>0.

For passive walls,
\operatorname{Re}A\geq 0
, or equivalently

\varphi\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, the passive sector has angular width
\pi
.
Therefore, except in the degenerate case in which one boundary ray is
neutral, no open stabilising half-plane can contain the entire passive
sector. Passive walls are consequently not generically stabilising: a
passive wall may damp or amplify a given mode depending on the phase of its
admittance relative to
K
.

Proof.

The first identity follows directly from Corollary
1
:

\operatorname{Im}(KA)=\operatorname{Im}\!\left[(K_{R}+\mathrm{i}K_{I})(A_{R}+\mathrm{i}A_{I})\right]=K_{R}A_{I}+K_{I}A_{R}.

The stabilising set is therefore the open half-plane
(
14
). If
A>0
is purely resistive, then

\operatorname{Im}(KA)=AK_{I}
,
so stabilisation is equivalent to
K_{I}<0
. If
A=\mathrm{i}a
,
a>0
, then

\operatorname{Im}(KA)=aK_{R}
,
so positive reactance stabilises if and only if
K_{R}<0
. If

A=-\mathrm{i}a
,
a>0
, then

\operatorname{Im}(KA)=-aK_{R}
,
so negative reactance stabilises if and only if
K_{R}>0
.

Finally, the passive sector
\operatorname{Re}A\geq 0
is the closed right half-plane
in the
A
-plane and has angular width
\pi
. The stabilising set is
also a half-plane through the origin. An open half-plane cannot strictly
contain another closed half-plane of the same angular width; at most their
boundary rays can coincide, in which case one passive boundary direction is
neutral. Thus passivity alone does not imply stabilisation.

4
Effective admittances of thin wall mechanisms

Assumption 2

(Thin wall-layer reduction)
.

Each wall mechanism occupies a region of thickness
\delta_{m}
satisfying

\delta_{m}\ll\delta_{BL}=\mathcal{O}(1),\qquad\alpha\,\delta_{m}\ll 1,

(15)

so that the disturbance pressure is uniform across the sublayer to leading
order,
\hat{p}\simeq\hat{p}_{w}
, and the sublayer returns to the outer flow a
wall-normal flux proportional to this common forcing. Cross-interactions
between distinct mechanisms — orifice end corrections, modification of the
Stokes layer by coating transpiration, roughness-induced local separation
— are higher order in the ratios of the layer scales or quadratic in the
individual admittances, so the admittances of coexisting mechanisms are
additive at leading order:

A=A_{v}+A_{T}+A_{p}+A_{r}+\mathcal{O}(\textnormal{products}).

(16)

Proposition 2

(Thin-layer admittance reduction)
.

Let a wall mechanism occupy an inner region of dimensional thickness

\ell_{m}
, and write

\delta_{m}=\frac{\ell_{m}}{L^{*}}\ll 1,\qquad\alpha\delta_{m}\ll 1.

Introduce the stretched normal coordinate
Y=y/\delta_{m}
. Suppose that,
after nondimensionalisation, the inner disturbance problem has the linear
form

\mathcal{L}_{m}(\alpha,c;\delta_{m})\,{\bf U}_{m}=\mathcal{F}_{m}(\alpha,c;\delta_{m})\,\hat{p}_{w}
,
where
\hat{p}_{w}=\hat{p}(0^{+})
is the outer wall pressure, and that the
solution defines an outward inner flux

q_{m}=\mathcal{Q}_{m}{\bf U}_{m}
.
Assume that the inner problem is well posed uniformly for
c
in the
neighbourhood considered in Assumption
1
, and that its flux
admits the expansion

q_{m}=-A_{m}(\alpha,c)\hat{p}_{w}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{w}|+\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})|\right).

(17)

Then the outer problem satisfies the effective admittance condition

\hat{v}(0^{+})=-A_{m}(\alpha,c)\hat{p}(0^{+})+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}(0^{+})|+\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})|\right).

(18)

Equivalently, if the remainder is absorbed into the boundary condition, the
wall admittance is

A=A_{m}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha\delta_{m}+\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})|/|\hat{p}(0^{+})|).

If
N
independent thin mechanisms are forced by the same leading-order
outer pressure and their fluxes are additive, then

\hat{v}(0^{+})=-\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}A_{m}\right)\hat{p}(0^{+})+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\alpha\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}(0^{+})|+\sum_{m=1}^{N}\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})|+\sum_{m\neq n}|A_{m}A_{n}|\,|\hat{p}(0^{+})|\right),

(19)

and therefore

A=\sum_{m=1}^{N}A_{m}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\alpha\delta_{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{N}\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})|/|\hat{p}(0^{+})|+\sum_{m\neq n}|A_{m}A_{n}|\right).

(20)

Proof.

The outer pressure has the Taylor expansion, in the inner coordinate

Y=y/\delta_{m}
,

\hat{p}(\delta_{m}Y)=\hat{p}(0^{+})+\delta_{m}Y\,\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})+\mathcal{O}(\delta_{m}^{2}Y^{2}).

Moreover, the streamwise phase factor varies across the layer by

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha x}\quad\hbox{with normal correction of size}\quad\mathcal{O}(\alpha\delta_{m}),

so the leading forcing of the inner problem is the constant pressure

\hat{p}_{w}=\hat{p}(0^{+})
. By the assumed uniform well-posedness and the
linear flux law (
17
), the inner solution returns the
outward flux

q_{m}=-A_{m}(\alpha,c)\hat{p}(0^{+})+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\alpha\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}(0^{+})|+\delta_{m}\,|\hat{p}_{y}(0^{+})|\right).

Matching of normal velocity between the inner and outer regions gives

\hat{v}(0^{+})=q_{m}
,
which proves (
18
). For several mechanisms occupying
asymptotically separated or independently homogenised thin regions, the
leading pressure forcing is common to all of them, while conservation of
mass makes the returned normal flux the sum of the individual fluxes:

\hat{v}(0^{+})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}q_{m}.

Adding the single-mechanism expansions gives the first two error terms in
(
19
). The product terms
A_{m}A_{n}
represent the first
neglected interactions: for example, modification of one sublayer by the
transpiration induced by another, or the displacement of one effective
boundary by another. These interactions are beyond the leading matching
order retained here. This gives (
19
) and hence
(
20
).
∎

Proposition
2
formalises the pressure-driven,
velocity-returned matching structure used below. The rest of this section
computes the leading coefficients
A_{m}
for the particular mechanisms of
interest.

4.1
The viscous Stokes and thermal sublayers

For an inviscidly controlled mode of frequency
\omega=\alpha c
, viscosity
acts at the wall in a Stokes layer of thickness

\delta_{v}=(\nu_{w}/\omega)^{1/2}=T_{w}(\omega R)^{-1/2}
, with

\nu_{w}=T_{w}^{2}/R
under the Chapman law; the ordering
(
15
) holds with
\delta_{m}=\delta_{v}
. Provided

\omega\gg\alpha\lambda_{w}\delta_{v}
— comfortably satisfied for the second
mode; the uniformly valid Airy-function (Tietjens) form, and its
triple-deck connection in the opposite limit, are given in
Appendix
B
— the base shear may be neglected across the
layer. The Stokes form is the high-frequency limit of the shear-affected wall
layer. To see the ordering explicitly, compare the change of base velocity
across the Stokes layer with the phase speed:

\epsilon_{A}=\frac{\alpha\lambda_{w}\delta_{v}}{\omega}=\frac{\lambda_{w}\delta_{v}}{c},\qquad\delta_{v}=\frac{T_{w}}{(\alpha cR)^{1/2}},\qquad\omega=\alpha c.

Thus
\displaystyle\epsilon_{A}=\frac{\lambda_{w}T_{w}}{c(\alpha cR)^{1/2}}
.
When
|\epsilon_{A}|\ll 1
, the base shear is a perturbation across the
Stokes layer and the uniformly valid Airy-function admittance of
Appendix
B
reduces to

A_{v}^{\rm Airy}=A_{v}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{A})\right)
.
At the reference second-mode point

\alpha=0.31,\qquad c_{0}=0.9082+0.0295\mathrm{i},\qquad R=2000,\qquad\lambda_{w}=0.0930,\qquad T_{w}=5.05,

we obtain

|\epsilon_{A}|=\left|\frac{\lambda_{w}T_{w}}{c_{0}(\alpha c_{0}R)^{1/2}}\right|\simeq 2.18\times 10^{-2}.

The use of the Stokes admittance (
21
) for the second mode therefore
has an expected shear-correction error of only a few per cent. For lower
frequencies or near the first-mode lower branch, where
|\epsilon_{A}|
is
not small, the Airy admittance (
29
) should be used instead.

The outer solution possesses the wall slip velocity

\hat{u}_{s}=T_{w}\hat{p}_{w}/c
(from the inviscid
x
-momentum equation at

y\to 0
); the Stokes-layer solution satisfying no slip and matching it is

\hat{u}=\hat{u}_{s}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-my})
with

m=(\omega/\nu_{w})^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}
, and continuity converts the
mass-flux deficit into an outward velocity

\hat{v}(0^{+})=\mathrm{i}\alpha\hat{u}_{s}/m
, whence

A_{v}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\,T_{w}^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{Rc^{3}}\right)^{1/2}.

(21)

Independently, the outer flow carries an isentropic temperature fluctuation

\hat{\theta}_{s}=(\gamma-1)M^{2}T_{w}\hat{p}_{w}
at the wall, whereas the solid wall
is isothermal to fluctuations; the resulting thermal layer of decay rate

m\sqrt{Pr}
stores and releases mass through the equation of state, giving

A_{T}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\,\frac{(\gamma-1)M^{2}T_{w}}{\sqrt{Pr}}\left(\frac{\alpha c}{R}\right)^{1/2}.

(22)

Both carry the Stokes phase
-45^{\circ}
; their passivity follows from the
inner dissipation identities in Proposition
3
. Although
asymptotically
\mathcal{O}(R^{-1/2})
, their numerical prefactor is large at high
Mach number because of the factors
T_{w}^{2}
and
(\gamma-1)M^{2}T_{w}
: at

R=2000
,
\alpha=0.31
,
c=c_{0}
we find
A_{v}+A_{T}=0.66-0.66\mathrm{i}
—

not
a small perturbation, and treated accordingly
(Remark
2
).
Viscosity, in this description, is simply another
wall impedance, whose phase locates it in the admittance plane alongside
coatings and roughness (figure
5
b).

4.2
Porous coatings of blind pores

Consider a coating of blind cylindrical pores of radius
r_{p}
, depth
h
and
surface porosity
\phi
, with
r_{p},\delta_{v}\lesssim 1\ll 2\pi/\alpha
, so
that (
15
) holds at the pore scale and the flow in each pore
is one-dimensional and driven by
\hat{p}_{w}
. Solving the oscillatory
pipe-flow (Womersley) and conduction problems across the pore
(Appendix
C
) yields the classical Zwikker–Kosten effective
medium
(
Zwikker and Kosten 1949
)
: with

\zeta=r_{p}(\omega/\nu_{w})^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4}
and
H(z)=2J_{1}(z)/[zJ_{0}(z)]
,

\rho_{d}=\frac{\bar{\rho}_{w}}{1-H(\zeta)},\qquad C_{d}=M^{2}\Big[1+(\gamma-1)H\big(\zeta\sqrt{Pr}\big)\Big],

(23)

the dynamic density and (volumetric) compressibility,

\hat{\rho}/\bar{\rho}=C_{d}\hat{p}
, interpolating between adiabatic
(
H\to 0
) and isothermal (
H\to 1
) sound in the pore gas. With pore
wavenumber and characteristic admittance

\Lambda=\omega(\rho_{d}C_{d})^{1/2}
,
Y_{0}=(C_{d}/\rho_{d})^{1/2}
, the input
admittance of a blind pore of depth
h
, times the porosity, is

A_{p}=-\mathrm{i}\,\phi\,Y_{0}\tan(\Lambda h).

(24)

Viscothermal damping selects the decaying pore branch; the corresponding positive-real property of
A_{p}
is verified energetically in Proposition
3
. The shallow limit is the cavity
compliance
A_{p}\simeq-\mathrm{i}\omega\phi C_{d}h
; strongly damped (narrow) pores
saturate at the semi-infinite-absorber value
A_{p}\to\phi Y_{0}
for

h\gtrsim 1.5
; between these limits lie quarter-wave resonances

\operatorname{Re}\Lambda\,h\approx(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi
at which
|A_{p}|
peaks —
prominent for wide pores, washed out for narrow ones
(§
5.5
). At the reference conditions
(
\phi=0.3
,
r_{p}=0.2
,
h=2
,
R=2000
) the coating has

A_{p}=0.905-0.740\mathrm{i}
, again
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

4.3
Shallow, dense, non-separating roughness

We next consider distributed roughness whose height and spacing are small
compared with the outer boundary-layer scale and whose local flow remains
attached. Let
b
denote the typical roughness amplitude,
k_{r}
a
representative roughness wavenumber, and
y_{e}
the effective protrusion
height measured from the chosen reference plane. The regime considered here
is

k_{r}b\ll 1,\qquad b\ll\delta_{v},\qquad\alpha b\ll 1,\qquad Re_{b}\equiv\frac{\rho_{w}U_{\rm rel}b}{\mu_{w}}\ll 1,

(25)

where
U_{\rm rel}
is the characteristic oscillatory velocity seen by the
texture. These assumptions express small slope, confinement inside the
viscous wall layer, small size relative to the instability wavelength, and
absence of local inertial separation. Thus the result below does not apply
to isolated roughness elements, finite-height trips, separated cavities, or
roughness-induced receptivity mechanisms.

In the regime (
25
), the classical homogenisation
description of fine riblets and grooves replaces the physical wall by an
equivalent smooth impermeability plane displaced by the protrusion height

y_{e}

(
Bechert and Bartenwerfer 1989
;
Luchini et al. 1991
)
. We take
y_{e}>0
when the
effective impermeability plane is displaced toward the fluid, and
y_{e}<0

for groove-dominated geometries referenced to the land surface. For a
small-amplitude sinusoidal texture, for example,
y_{e}=\mathcal{O}(k_{r}b^{2})
. The
outer no-penetration condition is therefore imposed at
y=y_{e}
:

\hat{v}(y_{e})=0
.
Transferring this condition to the reference plane gives

\hat{v}(0^{+})=-y_{e}\hat{v}^{\prime}(0^{+})+\mathcal{O}(y_{e}^{2}\hat{v}^{\prime\prime}(0^{+})).

(26)

It remains to evaluate
\hat{v}^{\prime}(0^{+})
from the outer inviscid equations.
From the wall-normal momentum equation,

\mathrm{i}\alpha\bar{\rho}(U-c)\hat{v}=-\hat{p}^{\prime}
and

\bar{\rho}=T^{-1}
.
At the rigid wall
U(0)=0
,
\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)=0
, and differentiating the
wall-normal momentum relation gives

-\mathrm{i}\alpha\frac{c}{T_{w}}\hat{v}^{\prime}(0)=-\hat{p}^{\prime\prime}(0).

The Rayleigh equation evaluated at the wall, again using

\hat{p}^{\prime}(0)=0
, gives

\hat{p}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\alpha^{2}\left(1-\frac{M^{2}c^{2}}{T_{w}}\right)\hat{p}(0).

Hence

\hat{v}^{\prime}(0)=-\mathrm{i}\,\frac{\alpha T_{w}}{c}\left(1-\frac{M^{2}c^{2}}{T_{w}}\right)\hat{p}(0).

Substitution into (
26
), and comparison with

\hat{v}(0^{+})=-A_{r}\hat{p}(0^{+})
, yields

A_{r}=-\mathrm{i}\,y_{e}\,\frac{\alpha T_{w}}{c}\left(1-\frac{M^{2}c^{2}}{T_{w}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}y_{e}^{2})+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{b}{\delta_{v}}A_{v}\right).

The first remainder is the next Taylor term in the displacement of the
impermeability condition from
y=y_{e}
to the reference plane. The second
is the leading dissipative correction associated with the extra Stokes-layer
dissipation over the rough wetted area; it is smaller than the leading
reactive protrusion-height term when
b/\delta_{v}\ll 1
. Retaining only the
leading term gives the roughness admittance used below:

A_{r}=-\mathrm{i}\,y_{e}\,\frac{\alpha T_{w}}{c}\left(1-\frac{M^{2}c^{2}}{T_{w}}\right).

(27)

The bracket in (
27
) is the wall-normal acoustic
parameter
1-M_{w}^{2}
, where
M_{w}=Mc/\sqrt{T_{w}}
is the relative Mach
number of the phase speed measured with the wall sound speed. For the
second Mack mode at
M=4.5
,
M_{w}^{2}\approx 3.3>1
. Thus protrusion-type
roughness,
y_{e}>0
, gives
A_{r}=+\mathrm{i}|a_{r}|
to leading order, whereas
groove-type roughness,
y_{e}<0
, gives the opposite reactance. By
Corollary
1
,

\delta\sigma=\alpha\operatorname{Im}(KA_{r})=\pm\alpha|a_{r}|\operatorname{Re}K
,
so the sign of the roughness effect is not universal: it is determined by
the sign of
\operatorname{Re}K
for the mode and wavenumber under consideration.
Consequently the roughness effect must reverse along any branch on which

\arg K
crosses
\mp\pi/2
, a parameter-free prediction tested in
§
5.5
. The result should therefore be read as a
leading reactive homogenised-wall correction, not as a theory of arbitrary
surface roughness.

4.4
Passivity of the effective admittances

The sign convention in (
1
) was chosen so that a passive
wall has
\operatorname{Re}A\geq 0
. We now verify this property directly for the
effective admittances derived above. The calculation is also a useful check
on the signs in (
21
)–(
27
). For a harmonic disturbance,
the time-averaged power per unit plan area entering the wall is

\mathcal{P}_{w}=-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\{\hat{p}_{w}\hat{v}_{w}^{*}\}=\frac{1}{2}|\hat{p}_{w}|^{2}\operatorname{Re}A,

because
\hat{v}_{w}=-A\hat{p}_{w}
. Thus
\operatorname{Re}A\geq 0
is equivalent to
non-negative time-averaged energy absorption by the wall.

Proposition 3

(Passivity of the wall-layer admittances)
.

For the sign convention
\hat{v}_{w}=-A\hat{p}_{w}
, the viscous and thermal
wall-layer admittances satisfy

\operatorname{Re}A_{v}\geq 0
,
\operatorname{Re}A_{T}\geq 0
,
with strict inequality for non-zero wall pressure. The blind-pore coating
admittance satisfies

\operatorname{Re}A_{p}\geq 0

whenever the pore walls are passive and the branch of the pore wavenumber is
chosen with viscothermal decay into the coating. The shallow roughness
admittance is lossless:

\operatorname{Re}A_{r}=0
.

Proof.

First consider the viscous Stokes layer. Let

\hat{u}=\hat{u}_{s}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-my}),\qquad m=\left(\frac{\omega}{\nu_{w}}\right)^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4},\qquad\hat{u}_{s}=\frac{T_{w}}{c}\hat{p}_{w}.

The harmonic kinetic-energy balance for the inner streamwise momentum
equation gives

\frac{1}{2}|\hat{p}_{w}|^{2}\operatorname{Re}A_{v}=\frac{1}{2R}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mu_{w}|\hat{u}_{y}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y,

up to the nondimensional constants already absorbed in the definition of

A_{v}
. The right-hand side is non-negative and is positive unless the
slip velocity vanishes. Equivalently, inserting (
21
) directly
gives

A_{v}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}T_{w}^{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{Rc^{3}}\right)^{1/2},

which has positive real part for the trapped growing modes considered here.
The thermal layer is identical in
structure: multiplying the inner heat equation by the complex conjugate of
the temperature fluctuation and taking the real part gives

\frac{1}{2}|\hat{p}_{w}|^{2}\operatorname{Re}A_{T}=\frac{1}{2RPr}\int_{0}^{\infty}k_{w}|\hat{\theta}_{y}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq 0,

again up to the nondimensional prefactor used in (
22
). Hence

\operatorname{Re}A_{T}\geq 0
.

For the pore coating, let
\Omega_{p}
denote one pore and let

\langle\hat{u}\rangle
be the cross-sectionally averaged axial velocity.
The averaged acoustic energy balance for the Womersley–thermal pore problem
gives

\frac{1}{2}|\hat{p}_{w}|^{2}\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{A_{p}}{\phi}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{p}}\left[\mu_{w}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+\frac{k_{w}}{T_{w}}|\nabla\hat{\theta}|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}V\geq 0,

with the precise nondimensional constants depending only on the pressure,
velocity and temperature scalings. Multiplication by the positive porosity

\phi
preserves the sign, so
\operatorname{Re}A_{p}\geq 0
. This is the energetic
counterpart of the Zwikker–Kosten formula (
24
): viscous and
thermal diffusion in the pore make the input admittance positive real for
the decaying branch of the pore transmission line.

Finally, the roughness admittance (
27
) is

A_{r}=-\mathrm{i}y_{e}\frac{\alpha T_{w}}{c}\left(1-\frac{M^{2}c^{2}}{T_{w}}\right).

At leading order the homogenised roughness model only displaces the
effective impermeability plane and contains no dissipative inner mechanism.
For a neutral mode, or for the real-frequency passivity check obtained by
setting
c_{i}=0
, this admittance is purely imaginary; hence

\operatorname{Re}A_{r}=0
.
It is therefore a reactive, lossless wall response. Any positive resistance
associated with real roughness arises at the next order through enhanced
Stokes-layer dissipation over the wetted area, and is not part of the
leading protrusion-height admittance (
27
).

6
Discussion

The contribution of this paper is one theorem and one mechanics
demonstration. Theorem
1
is an explicit spectral perturbation
result for the compressible Rayleigh operator under boundary perturbation:
the first-order response of a simple trapped eigenvalue to
any
wall
admittance is
KA
, with
K
a closed-form functional of the rigid-wall
eigenfunction, and with the phase criterion of Corollary
1
as
its immediate consequence. Section
4
shows that the
admittance is precisely the object that thin-wall asymptotics delivers —
for viscous sublayers, blind-pore coatings and shallow non-separating
roughness, under the explicit ordering of Assumption
2
, with
leading-order additivity — and §
5
validates both the
coefficient and its phase structure (the
sign-reversal of the roughness effect at
\arg K=-\pi/2
) at Mach 4.5.
The framework gives a leading-order spectral and asymptotic description of
how thin wall mechanisms modify trapped compressible boundary-layer modes;
it is intended as a diagnostic and design calculus complementary to, not a
replacement for, full viscous stability theory, direct simulation or
spatial transition prediction.

Limitations delimit the claims. The analysis is locally parallel and
two-dimensional; oblique and non-parallel extensions require only the
corresponding rigid-wall eigenfunctions in (
9
), the admittances
being unchanged. Assumption
1
excludes nearly degenerate
eigenvalues: in synchronisation regions the linear range shrinks and a
two-mode (degenerate) perturbation theory would be required.
Assumption
2
excludes isolated or high roughness, separated
flow over textures, and coating regimes with strong pore–pore or
pore–mode interaction; resonant reinforcement and coating-induced modes
documented at other conditions
(
Brès et al. 2013
;
Fedorov 2011
)
lie outside
the attenuative regime computed here. The quantitative results retain the
full admittance boundary condition; the first-order formula is exact only
in the limit, and at
\mathcal{O}(1)
admittance it is used as a phase diagnostic
(Remark
2
). A different perturbation problem arises near synchronisation, where two
rigid-wall eigenvalues are separated by a gap comparable with the admittance
perturbation. Then the scalar expansion
c(A)=c_{0}+KA+\mathcal{O}(|A|^{2})
must be
replaced by a finite-dimensional perturbation on the nearly resonant
eigenspace. Formally, if two rigid-wall modes
c_{1},c_{2}
are retained, the
leading approximation has the form

\det\left[\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&0\\[2.0pt]
0&c_{2}\end{pmatrix}+A\begin{pmatrix}K_{11}&K_{12}\\[2.0pt]
K_{21}&K_{22}\end{pmatrix}-cI\right]=0,

where the diagonal entries reduce to the scalar sensitivities away from the
interaction, while the off-diagonal entries are adjoint-mode boundary
bilinear forms coupling the two branches. Developing and validating this
two-mode theory is beyond the scope of the present paper; here we restrict
Theorem
1
and its validation to isolated simple eigenvalues.

Appendix A
Extensions to amplitude-dependent and acoustic admittances

Two extensions illustrate the reach of the boundary condition
(
5
) beyond linear, trapped dynamics; both are presented as
outlines, not as developed theories.

Amplitude dependence.
Oscillatory flow through pore orifices
acquires, at finite amplitude, the quadratic resistance of nonlinear liner
acoustics
(
Ingard and Ising 1967
;
Melling 1973
;
Cummings 1986
)
. In the
describing-function (first-harmonic) approximation the coating impedance
becomes
Z_{\rm NL}=A_{p}^{-1}+\Theta|\hat{v}_{w}|
with

\Theta=(1-\phi^{2})/(2C_{D}^{2}\phi^{2}T_{w})
, yielding a scalar fixed point for

A_{\rm NL}(\varepsilon)
at wall-pressure amplitude
\varepsilon
, and a
quasi-linear growth rate
\sigma(\varepsilon)
from the same eigenvalue
problem. Since
A_{\rm NL}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to\infty
, the coating
benefit erodes universally with the scaled amplitude

x=\Theta|A_{p}|\varepsilon
, like
x^{-1/2}
at large
x
; computations at

\alpha=0.31
(figure
10
) give half-effectiveness amplitudes

\varepsilon_{1/2}\approx 3
, far above the linear-disturbance regime, so
the reference coating is amplitude-robust over the range in which linear
theory itself applies. For configurations that are linearly stabilised
(
\sigma(0)<0<\sigma_{\rm rigid}
) the construction yields a

describing-function erosion threshold

\varepsilon^{\ast}
with

\sigma(\varepsilon^{\ast})=0
(figure
10
a). This threshold is not
a full nonlinear transition threshold — no amplitude equation for the
outer wave is derived — it is the amplitude at which the
amplitude-dependent wall admittance no longer offsets the linear rigid-wall
growth rate.

Acoustic admittances.
The far-field exponent (
4
)
bounds the trapped regime; if wall or flow modifications push the phase
speed across
c_{r}<1-1/M
, the eigenvalue continues onto a leaky sheet and
the mode radiates a Mach wave at

\theta_{M}=\arccos\{1/[M(1-c_{r})]\}
, the wall admittance entering the leaky
dispersion relation through the same condition (
5
)

(
Chuvakhov and Fedorov 2016
)
. For the trapped modes computed here, sound is
generated where wall homogeneity is broken (coating junctions);
in the Born approximation the receptivity of a junction with admittance jump

\Delta A
to sound incident at angle
\theta
is proportional to the
wall-response function
W(\theta)=\hat{p}(0)/\hat{p}_{\rm inc}
of the
signalling problem — the same Rayleigh operator and wall condition with
incident and outgoing far-field branches — weighted by the junction
Fourier transform, and flow-acoustic reciprocity implies the same kernel
controls the emission directivity.
We do not develop the adjoint machinery
required to make the emission statement rigorous — for the receptivity
half this is the programme carried through asymptotically by

Dong et al. 2020
, see also
Fedorov and Khokhlov 2001
;
Ma and Zhong 2003
— and accordingly
present
W(\theta)
as an acoustic diagnostic of wall treatments. Computed
at the second-mode peak frequency (figure
11
), the fast-wave
response is broad, peaking at
|W|=2.44
near
\theta=43^{\circ}
for the
rigid wall and reduced at
all
angles by the coating (peak
-9\%
):
the admittance that damps the trapped mode also softens the wall response
to fast incident sound. Slow waves are critical-layer-shielded
(
|W|\lesssim 0.2
away from the grazing degeneracy). A time-domain
impedance formulation
(
Tam and Auriault 1996
)
would be required to carry these
admittances into direct simulation.

Appendix B
The shear-affected wall layer: Airy form and the triple-deck
connection

When
\omega\not\gg\alpha\lambda_{w}\delta_{v}
, the base shear

U\simeq\lambda_{w}y
must be retained in the wall layer. With

\eta=y/\delta_{A}
,
\delta_{A}=(\nu_{w}/(\alpha\lambda_{w}))^{1/3}
, the shear
perturbation
\tau=\mathrm{d}\hat{u}/\mathrm{d}\eta
obeys Airy’s equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\tau}{\mathrm{d}\eta^{2}}-\mathrm{i}(\eta-\eta_{0})\tau=0,\qquad\eta_{0}=\frac{c}{\lambda_{w}\delta_{A}},

with decaying solution

\tau\propto\mathrm{Ai}(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/6}(\eta-\eta_{0}))
. Imposing no slip, matching
the outer slip velocity
\hat{u}_{s}=T_{w}\hat{p}_{w}/c
, and integrating
continuity across the layer gives the uniformly valid admittance

A_{v}^{\rm Airy}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha T_{w}\delta_{A}}{c}\,\frac{\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{Ai}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/6}\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(\xi_{0})},\qquad\xi_{0}=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/6}\eta_{0},

(29)

the Tietjens-function form of asymptotic stability theory. As

|\eta_{0}|\to\infty
,
\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(\xi_{0})/\!\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\infty}\mathrm{Ai}\to(-\xi_{0})^{1/2}
collapses (
29
) onto the Stokes result
(
21
); in the opposite limit
\delta_{A}
becomes the lower-deck
thickness and (
29
) reproduces the lower-deck displacement
response of triple-deck theory
(
Smith 1979
)
— the
Tollmien–Schlichting wave is, in this language, an eigenoscillation of the
inviscid upper flow closed by the wall admittance (
29
), the
impedance interpretation advocated for receptivity by
Dong et al. 2020
.
For the second mode at
R=2000
,

|\eta_{0}|\gg 1
and (
21
)–(
22
) are accurate to a few per
cent.

Appendix C
Pore-scale derivation of the Zwikker–Kosten coefficients

In a pore (
r<r_{p}
,
-h<z<0
) driven at its mouth by

\hat{p}_{w}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}
, with
r_{p}
small compared with the pore
acoustic wavelength and the external scales, the pressure is uniform over
the cross-section and the axial momentum and energy equations reduce to

-\mathrm{i}\omega\bar{\rho}_{w}\hat{u}=-\hat{p}_{z}+\mu_{w}\frac{1}{r}(r\hat{u}_{r})_{r},\qquad-\mathrm{i}\omega\bar{\rho}_{w}c_{p}\hat{\theta}=-\mathrm{i}\omega\hat{p}+\frac{\mu_{w}c_{p}}{Pr}\frac{1}{r}(r\hat{\theta}_{r})_{r},

with
\hat{u}=\hat{\theta}=0
at
r=r_{p}
. Both are Womersley problems; with

k=(\mathrm{i}\omega/\nu_{w})^{1/2}
the cross-sectional averages are

\langle\hat{u}\rangle=\frac{\hat{p}_{z}}{\mathrm{i}\omega\bar{\rho}_{w}}[1-H(kr_{p})],\qquad\langle\hat{\theta}\rangle=(\gamma-1)M^{2}T_{w}\,\hat{p}\,[1-H(kr_{p}\sqrt{Pr})],

with
H(z)=2J_{1}(z)/[zJ_{0}(z)]
. The first defines
\rho_{d}
of
(
23
); substituting the second into the linearised state and
continuity equations gives
C_{d}
. Averaged continuity and momentum form
the transmission line
\hat{p}_{z}=\mathrm{i}\omega\rho_{d}\langle\hat{u}\rangle
,

\langle\hat{u}\rangle_{z}=\mathrm{i}\omega C_{d}\hat{p}
, whose solution with

\langle\hat{u}\rangle(-h)=0
yields the input admittance

-\mathrm{i}Y_{0}\tan(\Lambda h)
and, after multiplication by the porosity,
(
24
). End corrections and interaction with the overlying Stokes
layer are
\mathcal{O}(r_{p}/\delta_{v},\phi A_{v})
, consistent with
Assumption
2
.
