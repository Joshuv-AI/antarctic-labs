Interferometrically Enhanced Asymmetry in Strong-field Ionization with Bright Squeezed Vacuum
G. Singh, T. Rook, J. Rivera-Dean, C. Figueira de Morisson Faria

Abstract
We demonstrate that quantum light statistics can be used to control strong-field ionization at the tunneling step. Using a bichromatic linearly polarized field composed of a strong coherent driver and a weak bright squeezed vacuum (BSV), we show through simulation that photoelectron momentum distributions (PMDs) exhibit asymmetries that exceed those obtained with classical fields of comparable intensity by orders of magnitude. This enhancement is uniquely linked to the nonclassical statistics of the BSV field. A semiclassical analysis based on the strong-field approximation (SFA) reveals that the effect originates from fluctuations in the instantaneous field amplitude, which strongly modify the tunneling ionization probability while leaving the electron’s continuum dynamics essentially unchanged. This selective control enables reconstruction of ionization pathways and provides a robust route to extract sub-cycle dynamics from strong-field observables.

Often, it is desirable to maximize the effects of spatio-temporal asymmetry in strong-field observables and minimize the disruption to the electron’s continuum dynamics. With that in mind, adding a weak second color to a strong driving field introduces subtle, but measurable distortions in the PMDs. As the strength of the second wave increases, the electron’s propagation in the continuum can change significantly and hinder the ability to reconstruct ionization pathways. This invites the question of whether ionization can be substantially modified, with dramatic consequences for the PMDs, without strongly perturbing the continuum dynamics.

In summary, the present results show that
adding a weak bright squeezed vacuum (BSV) field of frequency
\omega
to a strong coherent wave of frequency
2\omega
may significantly amplify asymmetries in the resulting ATI photoelectron momentum distributions, in comparison with classical, coherent or thermal fields. These asymmetries occur for values of the relative phase between the two waves, where the reflection symmetry regarding the field extrema breaks down. They can be traced back to the tunneling probabilities associated with each ionization event, which are unequally influenced by the field statistics. Systematic comparison between the skewness of PMDs generated by squeezed and coherent light, respectively, as well as a direct inspection of the ionization probability for specific events has confirmed that this effect is specific to BSV light statistics and increases with the squeezing parameter. Further studies, presented in the supplementary material, have shown that, for the parameter range considered in this article, there is no effective photon statistic force altering the electron’s propagation in the continuum. Thus, the effect reported here is exclusively associated with the tunneling dynamics, mapping sub-cycle emission times onto measurable asymmetries in photoelectron spectra.

In principle, the asymmetry amplification reported in this work enables the precise extraction of tunneling times, quantum phases, and pathway-resolved dynamics. This is in stark contrast with strong-field tunneling with classical fields, for which the relevant information, such as timing, phase, or electron pathway selection, is often encoded in very small differences buried under symmetric backgrounds. This calls for intricate schemes such as the phase-of-the-phase spectroscopy, or observables like the asymmetry parameter, to extract this information. Here, we have demonstrated that even a weak additional BSV driver increases the asymmetry by orders of magnitude and turns a weak differential signal into a robust observable.

Appendix A
Theoretical framework

A.1
A coherent-state-based formulation

In this work, we are interested in describing direct ATI processes under the presence of quantum bichromatic light. More specifically, we consider the case where the driving field consists of a coherent
2\omega
field and a BSV
\omega
field, i.e.,

\ket{\Phi(t_0)}=\big[\hat{S}_{\omega}(\xi)\otimes\hat{D}_{2\omega}(\alpha_{2\omega})\big]\ket{\bar{0}}

(5)

where
\hat{D}_{\omega}(\alpha)=\exp[\alpha_{\omega}\hat{a}_{\omega}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}_{\omega}\hat{a}_{\omega}]
and
\hat{S}_{\omega}(\xi)=\exp[\tfrac{1}{2}(\xi^{*}\hat{a}_{\omega}^{2}-\xi\hat{a}_{\omega}^{\dagger 2})]
are the displacement and squeezing operators, respectively, acting on the optical mode with frequency
\omega

\hat{a}^{(\dagger)}
denotes the annihilation (creation) operator and
\ket{\bar{0}}
the vacuum state of all modes. Since the coherent state basis is overcomplete, with closure relation
\mathbbm{1}=\pi^{-1}\int\differential^{2}\alpha\outerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha}
(

5

\omega

(6)

\!c(\alpha_{\omega})\!=\!\dfrac{1}{\pi\cosh(r)}\!\exp[\!-\dfrac{\absolutevalue{\alpha_\omega}^{2}}{2}\!-\!\dfrac{1}{2}e^{i\phi}\tanh(r)\alpha_{\omega}^{*2}].

A.2

\bm{p}

\displaystyle Y(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to\infty\end{subarray}}\tr[\big(\outerproduct{\boldsymbol{p}}{\boldsymbol{p}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\big)\outerproduct{\Psi(t)}{\Psi(t)}]

(16)

(17)

\displaystyle Y(\bm{p})\approx\!\!\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to\infty\\
t_{0}\to-\infty\end{subarray}}\!

\displaystyle\int\!\differential^{2}\alpha_{\omega}\!\int\!\differential^{2}\beta_{\omega}\!\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\!\differential t_{1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\!\differential t_{2}\ c(\alpha_{\omega})c^{*}(\beta_{\omega})

\displaystyle\times b_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p},t,t_{1})b^{*}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{p},t,t_{2})\innerproduct{\beta_\omega}{\alpha_{\omega}}.

(18)

One of the main differences between the above expression and previous approaches (see, e.g., Refs.
Wang and Lai 2023
;
Lyu et al. 2025

Stammer et al. 2025b

\alpha_{\omega}

g(\omega)\sim 10^{-8}\ll 1

Rivera-Dean et al. 2022
;
Rivera-Dean 2024

To address this question, let us examine how the factor
c(\alpha)c^{*}(\beta)\innerproduct{\beta}{\alpha}
in the integrand behaves as we move away from the diagonal contributions, i.e., when setting

\displaystyle c(\alpha)c(\beta^{*})\innerproduct{\beta}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\exp[-\dfrac{\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{\absolutevalue{\alpha+\delta}^{2}}{2}]

(19)

\displaystyle\times\exp[-\dfrac{\tanh(r)}{2}\big[\alpha^{*^{2}}+(\alpha+\delta)^{2}\big]]

\displaystyle\times\exp[-\dfrac{\absolutevalue{\delta}^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{\alpha\delta^{*}-\alpha^{*}\delta}{2}].

From this exponential, we are primarily interested in the real part of the exponent, as it determines the weight at which each contribution of the integrand behaves as
\delta
varies. Writing
\alpha=\alpha_{x}+i\alpha_{y}
and
\delta=\delta_{x}+i\delta_{y}
in terms of their real imaginary components, and denoting
\mathsf{Exp}(\alpha,\beta)=\ln[c(\alpha)c(\beta^{*})\innerproduct{\beta}{\alpha}]

\displaystyle\text{Re}[\mathsf{Exp}(\alpha,\alpha+\delta)]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\delta_{x}^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{\delta_{y}^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{\alpha_{x}^{2}}{2}\big(1+\tanh(r)\big)

(20)

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\alpha_{y}^{2}}{2}\big(1-\tanh(r)\big)

\displaystyle-\big(\alpha_{x}+\delta_{x})^{2}\big(1+\tanh(r)\big)

\displaystyle-\big(\alpha_{y}+\delta_{y})^{2}\big(1-\tanh(r)\big)\leq 0,

which is manifestly non-positive. Thus, we confirm that the off-diagonal elements contribution get exponentially suppressed as we move away from the diagonal.

To gain intuition for the practical scale of this suppression, let us rewrite our integrals in terms of the electric field strength
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
, such that
\text{Re}[\mathsf{Exp}(\alpha,\alpha+\delta)]\to(4g(\omega)^{2})^{-1}\text{Re}[\mathsf{Exp}(\varepsilon_{\alpha},\varepsilon_{\alpha}+\varepsilon_{\delta})]

4(g(\omega))^{2}\approx 10^{-8}
a.u.—a variation too small to produce any appreciable effect on the direct ATI probability amplitude, which requires field strengths on the order of

a.u. to become significant.

We can thus approximately rewrite Eq. (

\displaystyle Y(\bm{p})

\displaystyle\approx\int\differential^{2}\alpha_{\omega}Y_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p})\int\differential^{2}\beta_{\omega}\ c(\alpha_{\omega})c^{*}(\beta_{\omega})\innerproduct{\beta_\omega}{\alpha_\omega}

(21)

\displaystyle=\int\differential^{2}\alpha_{\omega}\ Q(\alpha_{\omega})Y_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p}),

where

t_{0}\to-\infty\end{subarray}}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}b_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p},t,t^{\prime})\right|^{2}
is the semiclassical ATI probability amplitude for a field with coherent state amplitudes determined by
\bm{\alpha}
(see Eq.
13

\pi\absolutevalue{c(\alpha_\omega)}^{2}=\pi^{-1}\absolutevalue{\braket{\alpha_\omega}{\Phi_{\omega}(t_0)}}^{2}\equiv Q(\alpha_{\omega})

\omega

Appendix B
About the photon statistics force

With this background in mind, we can now obtain some practical insight into the role of a photon statistics force. In Refs.
Even Tzur et al. 2023
;
Rivera-Dean et al. 2025
, the influence of squeezing on electron trajectories leading to strong-field phenomena—specifically high-harmonic generation—was interpreted as the action of an effective force arising from the photon statistics of BSV light: the so-called
photon statistics force
. Its derivation is based on evaluating the Fourier transform of the expectation value of the dipole operator under the saddle-point approximation with respect to all integration variables, including those defining the electric field amplitude. Yet, this quantity determines the HHG response
Lewenstein et al. 1994
;
Amini et al. 2019
; because ATI spectra is not determined by dipole expectation values, that derivation cannot be directly transplanted to describe the influence of a photon-statistics force in ATI processes.

In standard semiclassical ATI theory, the saddle-point approximation is instead applied to the probability amplitudes themselves. Accordingly, in our case we introduce the saddle-point approximation directly at the level of Eq. (
17
). To do so, we first expand the coherent state
\ket{\alpha_\omega}
in the Fock basis so that all dependence on
\alpha
and
t^{\prime}
appears explicitly in the probability amplitudes
Rivera-Dean 2024
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi(\boldsymbol{p},t)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int\differential^{2}\alpha\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\differential t^{\prime}c(\alpha)b_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p},t,t^{\prime})e^{-\frac{1}{2}\absolutevalue{\alpha}^{2}}

(28)

\displaystyle\times\dfrac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n_{\omega}}\otimes\ket{\alpha_{2\omega}}.

Note that we renamed the integration variable
\alpha_{\omega}
to
\alpha
. From the above equation, we can extract an effective quantum optical action

\displaystyle S^{(\text{QO})}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p},t,t^{\prime})=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int^{t}_{t^{\prime}}\differential\tau\big[\bm{p}+\bm{A}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\tau)\big]^{2}+I_{p}(t^{\prime}-t_{0})

(29)

\displaystyle+i\bigg[\dfrac{\alpha_{x}^{2}}{e^{-r}\cosh(r)}+\dfrac{\alpha_{y}^{2}}{e^{r}\cosh(r)}+\dfrac{\alpha_{x}^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{\alpha_{y}^{2}}{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\alpha_{x}\alpha_{y}\tanh(r),

consisting of the semiclassical action in Eq. (
14
) and additional terms coming from
c(\alpha)
and the Fock state expansion.

Analogously to Refs.
Even Tzur et al. 2023
;
Rivera-Dean et al. 2025
, we apply the saddle-point approximation to the integration variables
(\alpha_{x},\alpha_{y},t^{\prime})
, which leads to the following set of saddle-point equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t^{\prime}}S^{(\text{QO})}_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\Rightarrow\dfrac{[\bm{p}+\bm{A}_{\bm{\alpha}}(t^{\prime})]^{2}}{2}+I_{p}=0,

(30)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha_{x}}S^{(\text{QO})}_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\Rightarrow-\dfrac{g(\omega)}{\omega}\int^{t}_{t^{\prime}}\differential\tau\big[\bm{p}+\bm{A}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\tau)\big]\cdot\bm{\epsilon}_{\mu}\cos(\omega\tau)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+i\alpha_{x}\bigg[\dfrac{2}{e^{-r}\cosh(r)}+1\bigg]+\alpha_{y}\tanh(r)=0,

(31)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha_{y}}S^{(\text{QO})}_{\bm{\alpha}}=0\Rightarrow-\dfrac{g(\omega)}{\omega}\int^{t}_{t^{\prime}}\differential\tau\big[\bm{p}+\bm{A}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\tau)\big]\cdot\bm{\epsilon}_{\mu}\sin(\omega\tau)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+i\alpha_{y}\bigg[\dfrac{2}{e^{r}\cosh(r)}+1\bigg]+\alpha_{x}\tanh(r)=0,

(32)

where we have taken into account that

(33)

30

A

To gain insight into how these expressions scale with

(34)

By inverting this relation, we obtain

(35)

where we have restricted ourselves to real-valued squeezing amplitudes. Using the additional relations

\displaystyle e^{\pm\sinh[-1](x)}=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}\pm x,

\displaystyle\cosh(\sinh[-1](x))=\sqrt{x^{2}+1},

we find

\displaystyle e^{-r}\cosh(r)=\dfrac{1}{g(\omega)^{2}}\Big[\sqrt{I_{\text{squ}}+g(\omega)^{2}}-\sqrt{I_{\text{squ}}}\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\sqrt{I_{\text{squ}}+g(\omega)^{2}},

(37)

\displaystyle e^{r}\cosh(r)=\dfrac{1}{g(\omega)^{2}}\Big[\sqrt{I_{\text{squ}}+g(\omega)^{2}}+\sqrt{I_{\text{squ}}}\Big]

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\sqrt{I_{\text{squ}}+g(\omega)^{2}}.

(38)

\displaystyle e^{-r}\cosh(r)\simeq\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{g(\omega)^{2}}{8I_{\text{squ}}}\approx\dfrac{1}{2},\quad e^{r}\cosh(r)\simeq\dfrac{2I_{\text{squ}}}{g(\omega)^{2}},

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+i5\alpha_{x}+\alpha_{y}=0,

\bm{A}_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)
(see Eq. (
A.3
)), in particular,
Y_{\alpha}(\bm{p},\theta+\Delta\theta)=Y_{\alpha e^{i\Delta\theta}}(\bm{p},\theta)
. So, the total PMD when the squeezing angle is changed by
\Delta\phi
is given by

\displaystyle Y(\bm{p},\theta,\phi

\displaystyle+\Delta\phi)=\int\differential^{2}\alpha\,Q_{\text{BSV}}(\alpha;r,\phi+\Delta\phi)Y_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p},\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\differential^{2}\alpha\,Q_{\text{BSV}}(\alpha e^{-i\Delta\phi/2};r,\phi)Y_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{p},\theta).

(46)

Now, we make the substitution
\beta=\alpha e^{-i\Delta\phi/2}
. The Jacobian :
\differential^{2}\beta=\differential^{2}\alpha
. Thus,

\displaystyle Y(\bm{p},\theta,\phi

\displaystyle+\Delta\phi)=\int\differential^{2}\beta\,Q_{\text{BSV}}(\beta;r,\phi)Y_{\bm{\beta}e^{i\Delta\phi/2}}(\bm{p},\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\differential^{2}\beta\,Q_{\text{BSV}}(\beta;r,\phi)Y_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{p},\theta+\Delta\phi/2),

(47)

\displaystyle=Y(\bm{p},\theta+\Delta\phi/2,\phi).

(48)

Hence, a change of squeezing angle by
\Delta\phi
in the squeezing angle corresponds to a change of
\Delta\phi/2
in the relative phase between the two superposed fields.

In this Appendix, we analyze the saddle point ionization times (obtained from solving Eq. (
22
)) as a function of the longitudinal momentum
p_{x}
and the complex amplitudes
\alpha
as sampled from the Husimi distribution of different driving fields. This analysis complements the discussion of the ionization probabilities near field extrema (see Fig.
3

in the main body of the paper), but is slightly more technical.

From Eq. (
22
), it can be seen that the saddle point solution
t_{sp}=t_{r}+it_{i}
depends on the momentum
p_{x}
and complex variable
\alpha
. For each value of
p_{x}
and
\alpha
, we obtain the saddle point solution and plot the imaginary part of the ionization time in Fig.
6
. The solutions are weighted by the Husimi function through their transparency. The imaginary parts
\mathrm{Im}[t_{sp}]
provide qualitative information about the instantaneous tunneling probability at
t_{sp}
: the smaller
\mathrm{Im}[t_{sp}]

\exp[-2\mathrm{Im}(S)]
and the dominant term in the action being linear with
t_{sp}
. This explains why the minimum of the imaginary part occurs near
p_{x}=0
, corresponding to the peak of the field.

For all phases, the figure shows that introducing squeezing leads to an uncertainty in
\mathrm{Im}[t_{sp}]
, in comparison to the results obtained for coherent bichromatic field (see the black solid and dashed lines in the figure). For
\phi=-\pi/2
[Figs.
6
(a) and (b)], the imaginary parts are reflection-symmetric with regard to
p_{x}=0

.
This means that the tunneling probability and, consequently, the PMD, are reflection-symmetric about
p_{x}\rightarrow-p_{x}
. However, in panel (b) which corresponds to just the ionization events
I_{2}
and
I_{4}
in Fig.
3
(d),
\mathrm{Im}[t_{sp}]
has a larger degree of uncertainty, extending towards lower values. This will result in a larger probability amplitude, in comparison to the contributions from panel (a) which depicts ionization events
I_{1}
and
I_{3}
in Fig.
3
(d). The corresponding
\mathrm{Im}(t_{sp})
for the ionization events associated with the coherent bichromatic field with relative temporal phase
\theta=\pi/2
[Fig.

(a) and Fig.
6
(c)] and
\theta=\pi/4
[Fig.
3
(b) and Fig.
6
(a,b)] are shown as black lines and act as reference markers for the noisy imaginary-time solutions arising from the BSV field. (d)]. For

(50)
