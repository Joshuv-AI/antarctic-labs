1. Project Overview and Objectives
The overarching objectives of the SciDAC‐5 FASTMath Institute are to:
 • develop robust mathematical techniques and numerical algorithms to reliably address the challenges of
 large‐scale simulation of complex physical phenomena;
 • deliver highly performant software with strong software engineering to run efficiently and scalably on current
 and next‐generation advanced computer architectures at the DOE Office of Science’s three major computing
 facilities;
 • work closely with domain scientists to leverage our mathematical and machine learning expertise and deploy
 our software in large‐scale modeling and simulation codes; and
 • build and support the broader computational mathematics and computational science communities across
 the DOE complex.
The MIT component of the FASTMath SciDAC Institute advanced mathematical algorithms and software for large-
scale Bayesian inference, uncertainty quantification, and optimal data acquisition with computationally intensive
models. Its central scientific objective was to make rigorous Bayesian computation practical for DOE-relevant
models whose forward simulations are expensive, high-dimensional, or governed by partial differential equations.
The effort focused on hybrid approaches that combine local surrogate models, global approximation methods,
dimension reduction, and transport-based representations of probability measures.
This work also supported the broader FASTMath mission of delivering performant, well-engineered mathematical
software for the DOE computational science community. In particular, the MIT team developed capabilities within
MUQ, the MIT Uncertainty Quantification Library, and advanced interfaces between MUQ and complementary
FASTMath and partner codes, specifically Sandia National Laboratories’ DAKOTA library. The project also created
new interfaces between MUQ and the hIPPYlib software framework for PDE-constrained inverse problems. Over the
project period, the technical scope expanded from MCMC and surrogate acceleration to modern transport-based
variational and simulation-based inference, connecting tools from scientific machine learning with uncertainty
quantification for complex predictive models.

2. Major Technical Accomplishments
Surrogate-accelerated Bayesian inference and LA-MCMC.
A major accomplishment was the development and implementation of rate-optimal refinement strategies for local
approximation Markov chain Monte Carlo (LA-MCMC). These methods replace repeated evaluations of an expensive
target density with local surrogate approximations built from carefully selected model evaluations, while continually
refining the approximation to preserve asymptotic exactness. The project established a strategy for balancing
approximation bias against MCMC variance, proved near-standard Monte Carlo convergence rates for posterior
expectations, and demonstrated the approach on a computationally intensive Bayesian inverse problem in
groundwater hydrology. The resulting LA-MCMC algorithms were implemented in MUQ and provided a concrete
example of using mathematically controlled approximations to reduce the cost of inference without sacrificing UQ
guarantees.
Optimal experimental design and greedy algorithms.
The project produced new theory and algorithms for optimal Bayesian experimental design, where the goal is to
select observations or experiments that most effectively reduce uncertainty. For cardinality-constrained observation
selection, the MIT team developed and analyzed batch greedy methods for non-submodular, non-decreasing set
functions, with performance guarantees expressed through submodularity and supermodularity ratios. This work
clarified why greedy and batch-greedy heuristics can be effective even when classical submodularity assumptions
fail, and connected modular bounds, mutual information objectives, leverage-score ideas, volume sampling, and

example. A later Acta Numerica survey synthesized modern formulations and computational methods for optimal
experimental design, from classical design theory through Bayesian decision-theoretic criteria, nonlinear and non-

MUQ sampling algorithms and interfaces to DAKOTA and UQTk.
The project expanded MUQ’s capabilities for advanced MCMC and Bayesian computation. Development emphasized
parallel-chain and multilevel samplers, multi-index MCMC, parallel tempering, derivative-informed methods such as
MALA, dimension-independent likelihood-informed (DILI) MCMC, and preconditioned Langevin algorithms using low-
rank Hessian information. A key software abstraction was a common interface for sampling estimators, allowing
advanced samplers to use statistically efficient estimators rather than simple empirical averages and enabling
convergence diagnostics for samplers beyond classical Markov chains. These capabilities strengthened MUQ as both
a research platform and a deployable UQ library.
A continuing thrust was integration of MUQ with Sandia’s DAKOTA package (and to a lesser extent, UQTk). The
DAKOTA-MUQ work focused on installation and dependency management, expansion of the DAKOTA Bayesian
calibration interface, and improved MUQ2 documentation and examples. The result exposes DAKOTA users to MUQ
algorithms for derivative-enhanced MCMC, subspace MCMC, and hierarchical multilevel or multi-index models,
while taking advantage of DAKOTA’s strengths in model ensembles and workflow management.
hIPPYlib-MUQ for PDE-constrained Bayesian inverse problems.
The team completed an important interface between MUQ and hIPPYlib, combining hIPPYlib's adjoint-based
derivative and Hessian-action capabilities for PDE-constrained deterministic inverse problems with MUQ's Bayesian
inversion and MCMC algorithms. This integration addresses a central obstacle in large-scale Bayesian inverse
problems: black-box MCMC is often infeasible for PDE-governed models, while high-performance derivative-
informed MCMC needs gradients, Hessian actions, and low-rank structure. The hIPPYlib-MUQ framework brought
these capabilities together in an extensible software framework and demonstrated substantial speedups by
exploiting posterior geometry and intrinsic low-dimensional structure.
MParT and transport-based inference.
Another major software product was the Monotone Parameterization Toolkit (MParT), an efficient software library for
multivariate monotone function parameterizations. These parameterizations are central to transport maps and
normalizing-flow-like representations of probability measures. MParT provides performance-portable C++
implementations, with bindings to Python, Julia, and MATLAB, and uses Kokkos to target shared-memory CPU and
GPU execution. The toolkit enables fast construction and evaluation of monotone triangular maps for density
estimation, variational inference, simulation-based inference, and transport-accelerated MCMC. Reported
performance improvements over earlier transport-map implementations, together with GPU acceleration, make
MParT a practical vehicle for deploying transport methods in computationally demanding UQ settings.
During the later reporting years, the transport thrust broadened to include new quantization methods based on
gradient flow over the space of probability measures. This research asks whether it is possible to create small,
weighted point sets—essentially quadrature rules—to summarize complex datasets. The quality of the summary is
measured according to maximum mean discrepancy (MMD), which controls error in computing expectations. The MIT
team introduced new algorithms for creating weighted quantizations of empirical measure and demonstrated
superior performance over previous state-of-the-art algorithms for this task.
Statistical foundations for modern distribution learning and dimension reduction.
The project also contributed to the mathematical foundations of modern distribution-learning methods relevant to
scientific machine learning and UQ. Work on neural differential equations established nonparametric convergence
theory for learning probability distributions through ODE-induced flow maps trained by likelihood maximization,
including nearly minimax-optimal rates for smooth target densities and neural ODE parameterizations. Related work
on detecting low-dimensional structure in probability measures introduced sharper gradient-based dimension-
reduction criteria using dimensional logarithmic Sobolev and Poincare inequalities. Together, these results provide
theoretical guidance for learning, simplifying, and sampling high-dimensional probability measures that arise in
Bayesian inverse problems and uncertainty quantification.

3. Software, Products, and Dissemination
The project delivered both reusable software and peer-reviewed research products. Software outputs included new
and improved capabilities in MUQ, the MParT library, the hIPPYlib-MUQ interface, and ongoing interfaces between
MUQ and DAKOTA/UQTk. These products expose advanced Bayesian inference algorithms to users who work with
complex forward models, including models for which derivative information, multilevel model hierarchies, or
transport maps can be exploited.
Research results were disseminated through journal articles, open-source software papers, and presentations at
venues including SIAM UQ, SIAM CSE, AMS sectional meetings, JuliaCon, the Sandia UQ seminar series, and

4. Training and Workforce Development
The project contributed to the training of students, postdoctoral researchers, and research staff in computational
mathematics, uncertainty quantification, Bayesian computation, and scientific software development. Personnel
supported or involved in the effort included Andrew Davis, Jayanth Jagalur-Mohan, Robert (Zhi) Ren, Daniel Sharp,
Panos Tsimpos, Paul-Baptiste Rubio, Ricardo Baptista, Michael Brennan, Henning Bonart, Matthew Parno, and other
collaborators in the MUQ and MParT ecosystem. Training outcomes included graduate research in inference
algorithms and transport methods, experience in performance-portable software engineering, and direct interaction
with DOE laboratories and Sandia collaborators. Daniel Sharp’s Sandia CSRI internship and the project's engagement
with DAKOTA developers provided particularly direct exposure to DOE computational science practice.

5. Impact Summary
The MIT FASTMath effort produced a coherent set of mathematical and software advances for Bayesian inference
with expensive models. Its algorithmic contributions improved the efficiency and reliability of surrogate-based
MCMC, derivative-informed MCMC, optimal experimental design, transport-map inference, and low-dimensional
structure detection. Its software contributions made these ideas available through reusable open-source libraries
and interfaces that are relevant to the DOE computational science ecosystem.
The broader impact of the project lies in reducing the barrier between advanced Bayesian methodology and real
scientific applications. By combining theory, algorithms, and software, the project enabled more efficient use of
complex predictive models under uncertainty. The resulting capabilities support DOE needs in uncertainty
