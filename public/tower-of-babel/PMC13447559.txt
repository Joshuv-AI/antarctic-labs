Incidental finding of a complex right fused supernumerary kidney with vascular and ureteral anomalies following abdominal trauma: A case report
Authors: Minmei Zou, Guangxiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fused supernumerary kidney (fused SNK) is an extremely rare congenital malformation of the urinary system. This article reports a 59-year-old male patient who was admitted to the emergency department because of severe right flank pain and gross hematuria 4 days after a fall. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a complex combination of variations: the absence of the left kidney and left renal vessels and 3 partially fused supernumerary kidneys (upper pole, middle, and lower pole units) in the right renal fossa with 3 independent collection systems. The vascular and ureteral anatomy was highly anomalous, and an incidental congenital arteriovenous fistula (AVF) directly communicating with the inferior vena cava was identified in the upper pole kidney. Imaging confirmed an AAST Grade IV laceration in the lower pole kidney with active contrast extravasation and a large perirenal hematoma. The patient underwent emergency superselective transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) using N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA). In accordance with the “damage control” principle in the context of hemorrhagic shock, only active hemorrhage was treated, while asymptomatic AVF was left for conservative surveillance. Follow-up at 6 months revealed stable renal function. This case emphasizes the critical role of multiphase CTA in mapping complex anomalies and demonstrates that TAE is a safe, parenchyma-preserving intervention, for managing trauma in patients with rare renal malformations.

Introduction

Supernumerary kidney (SNK) is an exceedingly rare congenital developmental anomaly of the upper urinary tract; fewer than 1 hundred cases have been documented in the global literature [1]. It is defined as an additional distinct kidney structure, independent of the 2 normal kidneys, possessing its own independent capsule, renal parenchyma, and blood supply. When a supernumerary kidney is partially or completely fused with the ipsilateral normal kidney, it is termed a fused supernumerary kidney (fused SNK) [2,3].

Clinically, these anomalies in renal morphology are frequently accompanied by complex ectopic vascular networks. However, the coexistence of unilateral renal agenesis and a contralateral fused supernumerary kidney supplied by crossover vessels from the contralateral iliac artery is highly unusual [4]. Lacking normal anatomical positioning and the protection of surrounding retroperitoneal fat, these anomalous kidneys may face an increased risk of rupture and massive hemorrhage when subjected to blunt abdominal trauma. This article reports a complex case of right tripartite fused SNK complicated by a congenital AVF and acute trauma-induced hemorrhage. We discuss the rare imaging features, potential embryological origins, and therapeutic value of parenchyma-preserving superselective TAE.

Case presentation

Patient history, physical examination, and vital signs

A 59-year-old male (height: 160 cm; weight: 65 kg) presented to our emergency department complaining of agonizing right flank pain, persistent gross hematuria, and dysuria. Symptoms followed an accidental fall from a height of approximately 2 meters 4 days prior. He had initially visited a local clinic where a basic abdominal CT suggested right renal laceration; he was managed conservatively with hemostatic agents (carbazochrome sodium sulfonate), prophylactic antibiotics (cefotaxime), and fluid resuscitation. However, on the day of admission to our center, he drained approximately 1000 ml of bright red urine, accompanied by worsening right flank pain and progressive abdominal distension. On physical examination, the patient was conscious but appeared pale, distressed, and mildly diaphoretic. He vital signs at admission were as follows: pulse, 65 beats per minute; respiratory rate, 20 breaths per minute; and blood pressure, 98/65 mmHg. Emergency laboratory indices revealed a hemoglobin (Hb) concentration of 72 g/L, indicating early-stage hemorrhagic shock. His serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels were within normal limits. The abdomen was flat but exhibited significant right-sided tenderness and muscle guarding; however, no rebound tenderness or palpable masses were noted.

Imaging findings

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed the absence of the left kidney and its arteriovenous vessels. Developmental variation of the right kidney was noted: 3 sets of pelvicalyceal and ureteral systems were visible on the right, and the 3 renal parenchymas were partially fused with each other (for descriptive convenience, on the basis of the position of the renal pelvis, the 3 kidneys are referred to as the upper pole kidney, middle kidney, and lower pole kidney). The right renal artery originated from the left common iliac artery and gave off branches to supply the upper pole and middle kidneys. An accessory renal artery arose from the abdominal aorta to supply the upper pole kidney, and another accessory renal artery originated from the left common iliac artery to supply the lower pole kidney. Furthermore, a large vessel emerged from the upper pole kidney and drained directly into the inferior vena cava, suggesting an arteriovenous fistula (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The ureters originating from the middle and lower pole kidneys merged at their upper segments and inserted into the left posterior wall of the bladder, whereas the ureter from the upper pole kidney inserted directly into the right lateral wall of the bladder (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The capsule of the lower pole kidney was discontinuous, with large areas of low-density nonenhancing shadows and scattered nodular high-density hemorrhagic foci observed. The capsule of the middle kidney was locally irregular, with slightly reduced enhancement of the renal parenchyma. These manifestations suggested renal contusions and lacerations. In terms of acute traumatic injury, the capsule of the lower pole kidney was discontinuous, with scattered nodular high-density hemorrhagic foci. A massive perirenal hematoma was noted in the right retroperitoneal space, extending into the abdominal and pelvic cavities. These findings indicated severe renal laceration (AAST grade IV) of the lower pole kidney accompanied by active arterial contrast extravasation.

Treatment and prognosis

Under local infiltration anesthesia, the patient underwent emergency digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and superselective TAE via a femoral approach. Selective catheterization confirmed active contrast extravasation from a distal branch of the accessory renal artery supplying the lower pole kidney. Approximately 1 mL of N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) diluted with iodized oil was slowly injected to embolize the bleeding vessel. Postembolization angiography demonstrated complete cessation of hemorrhage with no further contrast extravasation. Adhering to the “damage control” principle in the context of hemorrhagic shock, a deliberate clinical decision was made to perform targeted life-saving embolization of the actively bleeding lower pole only, with the asymptomatic congenital AVF remaining for conservative surveillance. Attempting to embolize the high-flow AVF during the same acute session could have unnecessarily prolonged the procedure, increased the contrast medium burden, and elevated the risk of nontargeted embolization of healthy functioning parenchyma. Following the procedure, the gross hematuria was cleared within 48 hours, the urinary catheter was removed on postoperative day 4, and his hemoglobin level stabilized after the transfusion of 2 units of packed red blood cells. He was discharged in stable condition on postoperative day 6.

Discussion

Embryology and differential diagnosis

Supernumerary kidneys and fusion anomalies arise from abnormal interactions between the ureteric bud and the metanephric blastema during early gestation. In this case, the absence of the left kidney suggests early developmental arrest of the left ureteric bud. Conversely, the presence of 3 partially fused renal units with independent collecting systems on the right side suggests that the right mesonephric duct abnormally gave rise to multiple ureteric buds, which likely induced local fusion of the surrounding nephrogenic tissue during cranial migration [[5], [6], [7]]. It is clinically important to differentiate this entity from cross-fused renal ectopia. Although the patient’s lower fused ureter inserts into the left posterior wall of the bladder (a feature sometimes seen in crossed ectopia), the presence of 3 distinct functional moieties, each enveloped in an individual capsule and supplied by a highly complex, independent arterial network, aligns more closely with a supernumerary kidney complex [8,9]. Vascular variations and trauma management

During normal embryonic development, ascending kidneys recruit transient vascular networks from adjacent major vessels [10,11]. The unusual crossover of the pelvic blood supply (originating from the left common iliac artery) observed in this case might be explained by a “vascular stealing” hypothesis. Under this theory, the massive, developing right fused renal complex recruited embryonic vascular networks originally designed for the unutilized left side [12,13]. Furthermore, the congenital AVF identified in the upper pole kidney could be attributed to localized developmental arrest of the embryonic arteriovenous capillary bed, resulting in persistent direct communication [8,14]. Anomalous and ectopic kidneys often lack the protective cushion provided by normal deep perirenal fat and occupy atypical anatomical positions. When subjected to blunt external forces, they are highly vulnerable to severe parenchymal disruption—often described as the “anvil effect”—as the anomalous kidney is compressed against the rigid vertebral column [15,16]. In the management of acute renal trauma accompanied by complex vascular malformations, multiphase CTA provides a vital preoperative roadmap [9,17]. With respect to treatment, superselective TAE is widely considered a primary intervention for high-grade renal trauma in hemodynamically stable patients [15,18]. Guided by the CTA roadmap, this approach facilitated rapid hemostasis and maximal preservation of functional renal reserve using NBCA tissue glue, avoiding the morbidity associated with emergency nephrectomy.

Limitations and follow-up

We acknowledge certain limitations regarding the long-term radiological follow-up in this report. The patient resides in a remote rural region with limited access to tertiary healthcare infrastructure. Following the complete resolution of his clinical symptoms, the patient declined repeat contrast-enhanced CT examinations because of socioeconomic constraints and concerns regarding cumulative radiation exposure. Telephone follow-ups conducted at 3 and 6 months postdischarge indicated that the patient had returned to full physical activity, and routine serum renal function tests obtained at a local primary clinic remained stable. Although formal follow-up imaging was not performed, immediate successful emergency intervention offers important educational value for managing similar complex anatomical variants under acute stress.

Conclusion

The coexistence of unilateral renal agenesis, a contralateral tripartite fused supernumerary kidney, crossover iliac artery blood supply, and a congenital AVF represents a highly complex urological malformation. Multiphase CTA plays an essential role in accurately delineating anomalous vascular and collection systems under emergency conditions. For traumatic hemorrhage occurring in such malformed kidneys, superselective TAE offers precise hemostasis and maximal preservation of functioning renal tissue. In accordance with damage-control principles, targeted embolization serves as a safe and effective minimally invasive treatment modality.
