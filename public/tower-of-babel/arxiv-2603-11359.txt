The zeta function of regular trees, their special values and functional equations
Dylan Müller

Abstract
We determine the special values at positive integers of the spectral zeta function associated with the combinatorial Laplacian on the regular tree. These values admit explicit formulas in terms of certain polynomials, which we show to be palindromic and to have non-negative integer coefficients with a combinatorial interpretation. Along the way, we uncover unexpected symmetries between the values of the zeta function at negative and positive integers, expressed at the level of their generating functions. Using these symmetries, we ultimately establish a functional equation of the type s ⟷ 1 − s s\longleftrightarrow 1-s for a natural completion of the zeta function.



1
Introduction

In 1735, Euler famously found the exact value of the series

\zeta(2k)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{-2k}=\frac{(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2k)!}(-1)^{k+1}B_{2k},

(1)

where
B_{2k}
denotes the Bernoulli numbers.
Later, he computed the values at negative odd integers

\zeta(1-2k)=-\frac{B_{2k}}{2k},

and emphasized the striking symmetry between positive and negative special values.
This phenomenon ultimately led to the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function, later established by Riemann in his seminal 1859 memoir, where the zeta function was defined over the complex plane and its fundamental analytic properties were proven.

The general philosophy that symmetries manifest themselves through special values motivates the present work. We investigate the values at integers of the spectral zeta function associated with the regular tree
T_{q+1}
of degree
q+1
, which is the universal cover of every
(q+1)
-regular graph. We find that these special values enjoy unexpectedly rich algebraic and combinatorial structure, together with two remarkable symmetries that ultimately lead to a functional equation for the zeta function of the tree.

Spectral zeta function of the tree

In full generality, the spectral zeta function of a space
X
equipped with a Laplacian is defined as the Mellin transform of the trace of the heat kernel

\zeta_{X}(s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Tr}(K_{X}(t))\,t^{s}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t},

discarding the
0
-eigenvalue when it belongs to the point spectrum of the Laplacian. For instance, for the circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, whose spectrum is given by
\lambda=4\pi^{2}n^{2}
, with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, one obtains

\zeta_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\lambda^{-s}=2(2\pi)^{-2s}\zeta(2s).

Another fundamental example is the spectral zeta function of the discrete line
\mathbb{Z}
:

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}I_{0}(2t)\,t^{s}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}=\frac{1}{2^{2s}\pi^{1/2}}\frac{\Gamma(1/2-s)}{\Gamma(1-s)}=\binom{-2s}{-s}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-s/k)^{2}}{(1-2s/k)}.

Its special values at negative integers are central binomial coefficients and are directly related to Catalan numbers. Quite remarkably, it admits a functional equation of the type
s\leftrightarrow 1-s
, in complete analogy with other zeta functions in the continuous setting, and shares with them many other properties; see
[
FK17
,
KP23
]
.

As a more general graph, the regular tree
T_{q+1}
of degree
q+1
is fundamental among regular graphs. Through the spectral measure, its spectral zeta function is given by

\zeta_{q}(s)=\frac{q+1}{2\pi}\int_{-2\sqrt{q}}^{2\sqrt{q}}(q+1-\lambda)^{-s}\frac{\sqrt{4q-\lambda^{2}}}{(q+1)^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda.

(2)

It is a special case of a hypergeometric function; see
[
FK17
, Theorem 1.4]
. For
q>1
,

\zeta_{q}(s)=\frac{q(q+1)}{(q-1)^{2}(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2s}}F_{1}(3/2,s+1,1,3;u,v),

(3)

with
u=-4\sqrt{q}/(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2}
,
v=4\sqrt{q}/(\sqrt{q}+1)^{2}
,
and where
F_{1}
is one of Appell’s hypergeometric functions.

Main results

One of our main results is the determination of the special values at positive integers of
\zeta_{q}
through a family of polynomials with remarkable algebraic and combinatorial properties.

Theorem 1.1
.

For all integers
n\geq 1
and
q\geq 2
, we have

\zeta_{q}(n)=\frac{q}{(q-1)^{2n-1}(q+1)^{n}}P_{n}(q),

(4)

where
P_{n}
is a palindromic and monic polynomial of degree
2n-2
with integer coefficients. Moreover, the coefficients of
P_{n}
count weighted
2
-coloured Dyck words, and hence are non-negative.

The first polynomials can be computed by hand:

P_{n}
is given. The first
P_{n}

P_{n}
has no rational root. The combinatorial aspects of
P_{n}
are treated separately in Section

and can be used to show that, except for
P_{2}
, all coefficients of
P_{n}
are positive.

In the particular case
q=1
, the spectral zeta function
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}
has zeros at positive integers, as follows for instance from the infinite product above.

Concerning the special values at negative integers of
\zeta_{q}
, they were computed in
[
CJKS25

; for
q\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
,

\zeta_{q}(-m)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}^{2}\,q^{\,m-k}\;-\;(q-1)\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\;\sum_{k=0}^{m-2j}\binom{m}{k}\binom{m}{2j+k}\,q^{\,m-2j-k}.

(5)

When viewed as functions of
q
, these are monic polynomials of degree
m
, with non-negative integer coefficients, although not palindromic.

At first sight, the formulas above do not suggest any direct relation between the values at positive and negative integers. In fact,
Theorem

1.1
will arise as a consequence of an unexpected symmetry between the special values at positive and negative integers, expressed entirely at the level of their respective generating functions. More precisely, define the generating functions of positive and negative special values by

G_{+}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\zeta_{q}(n)z^{n},\qquad G_{-}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\zeta_{q}(-n)z^{n}.

(6)

Let
\Omega_{q}^{+}\coloneqq[(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2},(\sqrt{q}+1)^{2}]
be the spectrum of the combinatorial Laplacian, and let
\Omega_{q}^{-}
be the image of
\Omega_{q}^{+}
under the inversion
z\mapsto 1/z
. Writing
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
for the Riemann sphere, we obtain:

Theorem 1.2
.

For
q>1
, the functions
G_{\pm}
admit analytic continuations to
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\setminus\Omega_{q}^{\pm}
, respectively. Moreover, for
z
outside
\Omega_{q}^{+}
, the following identity holds:

G_{+}(z)+G_{-}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)=0.

(7)

This symmetry allows us to deduce
G_{+}
from
G_{-}
, the generating function of the special values (
5
). In turn,
G_{-}
is itself deduced from the generating function of the probability of return after
n
steps on
T_{q+1}
, already present in
[
Kes59
]
. A closed formula for
G_{+}
is therefore obtained, and
Theorem

1.1
follows directly.

A more structural question, raised by Karlsson in
[
K20
, p. 9]
, is whether
\zeta_{q}
admits a functional equation of the type
s\leftrightarrow 1-s
. This problem was also raised in
[
FK17
]
. We give a positive answer to this question.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
q>1
, define the entire function

\xi_{q}(s)\coloneqq(q-1)^{s}(2(q+1)\zeta_{q}(s)-\zeta_{q}(s-1)).

Then, for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

\xi_{q}(1-s)=\xi_{q}(s).

As we will see, this result arises from a second symmetry at the level of the generating functions, namely
Proposition

5.1
. This also explains the somewhat unexpected normalization of
\zeta_{q}
appearing in
Theorem

1.3
.

More conceptually, this result shows that a symmetry at the level of generating functions of special values can be transferred to the zeta function itself.

The connection with Sato–Tate and
q\to\infty

The notation
T_{q+1}
is motivated by its connection with number theory and, for instance, with
q
-adic groups; see
[
Ser02
]
. The on-diagonal spectral measure
\mu_{q}
of the adjacency operator on
T_{q+1}
is known as the
Kesten–McKay law

[
Kes59
,
McK81
]
, and it is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure:

\mathrm{d}\mu_{q}(x)=\frac{q+1}{2\pi}\frac{\sqrt{4q-x^{2}}}{(q+1)^{2}-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The study of
\mu_{q}
through harmonic analysis goes back to
[
Ca72
]
. It is involved in the “vertical” Sato–Tate problem. Fix a prime
q
; the
q
-Hecke operator preserves the space of cusp forms of a given level and weight. When properly rescaled, its eigenvalues asymptotically distribute according to
\mu_{q}
as the level/ weight tends to infinity; see
[
Ser97
]
.

When
q\to\infty
, the measure
\mu_{q}
, under the rescaling
x=\sqrt{q}\,u
, converges weakly to the
Sato–Tate measure

\mu_{\infty}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u.

The Sato–Tate conjecture — now proven in great generality
[
BGLHT11
]
— states that, under the appropriate rescaling, the Hecke eigenvalues of a fixed newform for primes
q\leq N
asymptotically distribute according to
\mu_{\infty}
as
N\to\infty
.

The Sato–Tate measure is called the Wigner semicircle law in the context of statistical physics. Both the semicircle and the Kesten–McKay laws play fundamental roles in random regular graph models, and determining precisely how
\mu_{q}
converges (locally) toward
\mu_{\infty}
is of particular interest in this context; see
[
BKY17
]
.

From this perspective, it is natural to let
q\to\infty
in our setting as well. Karlsson pointed out to me that the zeta function of the Sato–Tate measure, corresponding to
q=\infty
, has a functional equation of the type
s\leftrightarrow 1-s
. More precisely, by defining

\zeta_{\infty}(s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-2}^{2}(2-u)^{-s}\sqrt{4-u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u,

he observed that it admits a functional equation of the desired type.

Proposition 1.4

(
[
K25
]
)
.

For
\re s<1/2
, let

\xi_{\infty}(s)\coloneqq(2-s)\cdot 2^{s}\cos(\pi s/2)\zeta_{\infty}(1+s/2).

(8)

Then
\xi_{\infty}
can be analytically continued to an entire function and the following identity holds for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
:

\xi_{\infty}(1-s)=\xi_{\infty}(s).

The moments of the Sato–Tate measure are Catalan numbers and, in fact, the zeta function
\zeta_{\infty}
can be viewed as an analytic continuation of the Catalan numbers. Considering the formula

\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s)=\binom{-2s}{-s},

it is not surprising that
\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\zeta_{\infty}
are related through the identity

\zeta_{\infty}(1+s)=\frac{1}{1-s}\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}}(s).

This is another way of seeing that
\zeta_{\infty}
admits a functional equation.

Therefore, both
q=1
and
q=\infty
admit a functional equation in complete analogy with the Riemann case. Our
Theorem

1.3
completes the picture for all intermediate
1<q<\infty
.

Further perspectives

The method developed in the present work suggests a broader framework for studying spectral zeta functions on transitive non-amenable graphs. The fundamental symmetry at the level of generating functions of
Theorem

1.2
relies only on the presence of a spectral gap, and therefore persists on any transitive non-amenable graph. Moreover, the functional equation of
Theorem

1.3
is a consequence of algebraic relations at the level of the generating functions. It appears that these algebraic relations are specific to the tree, and with it the closed formulas and combinatorial structure of the special values. It is known that the Green function of virtually free group is algebraic, as a consequence of Muller-Schupp theorem
[
MS83
]
. For example, it would be interesting to investigate algebraic relations between generating functions of special values — and therefore functional equations for zeta functions — in this context, as well as computing the special values.

The heat kernel and the spectral zeta function on
T_{q+1}

Let
q\geq 1
be an integer. The combinatorial Laplacian on the regular tree
T_{q+1}
is defined by
\Delta_{q}=(q+1)-A
, where
A
is the adjacency operator. It acts on functions
f\colon T_{q+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

(\Delta_{q}f)(x)\coloneqq\sum_{y\sim x}\bigl(f(x)-f(y)\bigr).

It is a bounded self-adjoint operator on
\ell^{2}(T_{q+1})
with spectrum
\Omega_{q}^{+}=[(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2},(\sqrt{q}+1)^{2}]
. The heat kernel is defined as the one-parameter family of operators

K_{q}(t)\coloneqq\exp(-t\Delta_q).

It acts by convolution on
\ell^{2}(T_{q+1})
against the function

K_{q}(t,x,y)=\langle K_{q}(t)\delta_{x}\mid\delta_{y}\rangle.

Precisely, for
f\in\ell^{2}(T_{q+1})
we have

(K_{q}(t)f)(x)=\sum_{y\in T_{q+1}}K_{q}(t,x,y)f(y),

and the function
K_{q}(t,x,y)
is often also called the heat kernel.

For infinite graphs such as
T_{q+1}
, the operator
K_{q}(t)
is not trace class. To remedy this, we first observe that for transitive graphs
\Gamma
, the function
t\mapsto K_{\Gamma}(t,x,x)
is independent of the vertex
x\in\Gamma
, and second that if
\Gamma
is finite, then

\mathrm{Tr}\,K_{\Gamma}(t)=K_{\Gamma}(t,x,x)\cdot|\Gamma|.

Therefore, for infinite transitive graphs such as
T_{q+1}
, the trace is often replaced by the map
t\mapsto K_{\Gamma}(t,x,x)
, which is independent of the choice of vertex
x\in T_{q+1}
. Fix a vertex
\mathbf{0}\in T_{q+1}
. The (local) spectral zeta function at
\mathbf{0}
is defined by taking the Mellin transform of this localized trace, namely

\zeta_{q}(s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}K_{q}(t,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})\,t^{s}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

The heat kernel on
T_{q+1}
has been computed in numerous places; see, for example,
[
CY99
]
for a direct computation requiring no prior knowledge. Using the on-diagonal spectral measure
\mu_{q}
, we have

K_{q}(t)\coloneqq K_{q}(t,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})=\int e^{-t(q+1-x)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{q}(x).

When
q=1
, the tree
T_{q+1}
is the discrete line
\mathbb{Z}
, and the heat kernel is given by

K_{\mathbb{Z}}(t)=e^{-2t}I_{0}(2t),

where
I_{n}
is the
n
-th modified Bessel function of the first kind; see
[
CJKS25
]
for a recent overview of
K_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

When
q>1
, the spectrum of
\Delta_{q}
does not contain
0
. Therefore, for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the map
x\mapsto x^{-s}
is continuous on
\sigma(\Delta_{q})=\Omega_{q}^{+}
, which implies that the operator
\Delta_{q}^{-s}
is well defined and bounded by the spectral theorem. From this viewpoint, the local spectral zeta function at
\mathbf{0}
is the entire function

\zeta_{q}(s)=\int(q+1-\lambda)^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{q}(\lambda)=\langle\Delta_{q}^{-s}\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\mid\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\rangle.

3
The fundamental symmetry and explicit formulas for
G_{\pm}

The aim of this section is to prove the symmetry stated in
Theorem

1.2
. The idea of the proof is that the expansions of the Laplace transform of the heat kernel near
z=0
and near
z=\infty
are directly linked with the generating functions
G_{+}
and
G_{-}
defined in (
6
). Heuristically,
Theorem

1.2
may be summarized by the formal identity

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\zeta_{q}(n)z^{n}=0.

We then recall Kesten’s computation of the generating function of the probability of return after
n
steps and use it to deduce closed formulas for
G_{\pm}
.

3.1
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

The Laplace transform of the heat kernel is defined by

\mathcal{L}[K_{q}](s)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{q}(t)e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t.

It is analytic for
\re s>0
, and in that region

\mathcal{L}[K_{q}](s)=\int\frac{1}{q+1-\lambda+s}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{q}(\lambda),

showing in fact that it is analytic outside
-\Omega_{q}^{+}
. Near
s=0
, more precisely for
|s|<(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2}
, we have

s\,\mathcal{L}[K_{q}](-s)=G_{+}(s),

(9)

showing that the Laplace transform of the heat kernel provides the analytic continuation of
G_{+}
to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega_{q}^{+}
. Moreover, near
s=\infty
, more precisely when
|s|>(\sqrt{q}+1)^{2}
, we have

-\mathcal{L}[K_{q}](-s)=\frac{1}{s}G_{-}\left(\frac{1}{s}\right).

(10)

In summary, we have shown that through the Laplace transform of the heat kernel, the functions
G_{\pm}
admit analytic continuations outside the spectral cuts
\Omega_{q}^{\pm}
, respectively. The map
z\mapsto 1/z
exchanges their domains. Moreover, by combining (
9
) and (
10
), we obtain the desired relation: outside
\Omega_{q}^{+}
,

G_{+}(z)=z\,\mathcal{L}[K_{q}](-z)=-G_{-}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right),

as claimed.

\square

Remark 3.1
.

The proof displayed above relies only on the fact that
\Delta_{q}
is positive definite and bounded. Indeed, it is well known that the Laplace transform of the heat kernel is essentially the resolvent of the underlying operator. The same argument applies to the resolvent of any such operator.

Consequently, for any transitive graph of finite degree with a spectral gap, this symmetry applies.

3.2
Kesten’s generating function and a formula for
G_{-}

The Kesten–McKay law
\mu_{q}
, i.e. the spectral measure of the adjacency operator
A
at the root, was computed by Kesten in
[
Kes59
]
by studying the probability of return after
n
steps, or equivalently the numbers

c_{q}(n)\coloneqq\langle A^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\mid\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\rangle

of closed walks of length
n
from the root. These are the moments of
\mu_{q}
:

c_{q}(n)=\int\lambda^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{q}(\lambda).

Kesten computed their generating function

F(z)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{q}(n)z^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{(q+1)\sqrt{1-4qz^{2}}-(q-1)}{1-(q+1)^{2}z^{2}},

where the branch is chosen so that
F(0)=1
.
Since
\Delta_{q}=(q+1)-A
, the functions
F
and
G_{-}
satisfy the relation

G_{-}(z)=\frac{1}{1-(q+1)z}F\left(\frac{z}{(q+1)z-1}\right),

and, at the level of the coefficients,

\zeta_{q}(-m)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(-1)^{j}c_{q}(j)(q+1)^{m-j}.

The formula for
F
implies that the
c_{q}(n)
are polynomials in
q
with integer coefficients, which in turn implies that for negative integers
-m\leq 0
, the special values
\zeta_{q}(-m)
are polynomials in
q
with integer coefficients. Using the relation between
G_{-}
and
F
, we obtain

G_{-}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{(q+1)\sqrt{1-2(q+1)z+(q-1)^{2}z^{2}}+z(q^{2}-1)-(q-1)}{1-2(q+1)z}.

(11)

Extracting from this expression a closed formula for the special values at negative integers is cumbersome. However, using the hypergeometric nature of
\zeta_{q}
and a completely different method, such a closed formula was obtained in
[
CJKS25
]
; see (
5
) in the introduction.

3.3
The formula for
G_{+}

By combining (
11
) and
Theorem

1.2
, we obtain an elementary expression for
G_{+}
, the generating function of the positive special values of
\zeta_{q}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
q>1
, the following equivalent statements hold:

(i)

For
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega_{q}^{+}
, we have

G_{+}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{(q+1)\sqrt{(q-1)^{2}-2z(q+1)+z^{2}}+z(q-1)-(q^{2}-1)}{z-2(q+1)}.

(12)

(ii)

The generating function
G_{+}
satisfies the quadratic equation

(2(q+1)-z)G_{+}^{2}+(q-1)(z-(q+1))G_{+}+zq=0.

(13)

(iii)

The sequence of real numbers defined by
a_{n}\coloneqq\zeta_{q}(n)
satisfies, for
n\geq 2
,

(q^{2}-1)a_{n}=2(q+1)\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}a_{j}a_{n-j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}a_{j}a_{n-1-j}+(q-1)a_{n-1},

(14)

with
a_{0}=1
and
a_{1}=\tfrac{q}{q^{2}-1}
.

Proof.

The first statement follows by a direct substitution. The only delicate point is the choice of branch for the square root, but the condition
G_{+}(0)=0
determines it uniquely. The remaining statements are equivalent reformulations of (
12
) and follow directly.
∎

4
Proof of
Theorem

1.1
and a recursive relation for the
P_{n}
’s

In this section, using the explicit expression for
G_{+}
obtained in
Lemma

3.2
, we prove the first part of
Theorem

1.1
, leaving the combinatorial interpretation to the final section. We then translate the recursive relation (
14
) into a recursive relation for the
P_{n}
’s.

4.1
Proof of
Theorem

1.1

By (
12
), we write

G_{+}(z(q+1))=\frac{q-1}{2}+\frac{q-1}{2(z-2)}\left[1+\sqrt{1+k^{2}z(z-2)}\right],

with
k=\tfrac{q+1}{q-1}
. Observe that
k^{2}
is invariant under the inversion
q\leftrightarrow 1/q
. Expanding the square root near
z=0
, one obtains that for
n\geq 1
,

(q+1)^{n}\zeta_{q}(n)=(q-1)R_{n}(k^{2}),

where
R_{n}
is a polynomial with rational coefficients of degree
n
satisfying
R_{n}(1)=0
. Translating back in terms of
q
, we write

R_{n}(k^{2})=\frac{1}{(q-1)^{2n}}\,qP_{n}(q),

where
P_{n}(q)
is a rational polynomial satisfying
q^{2n-2}P_{n}(1/q)=P_{n}(q)
.

Now recall that the spectral radius of
A
is
2\sqrt{q}
, implying that for fixed
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the convergence in operator norm

\|(q+1)^{s}\Delta_{q}^{-s}-I_{q}\|\underset{q\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0

holds, where
I_{q}
is the identity operator on
\ell^{2}(T_{q+1})
. Thus

(q+1)^{s}\zeta_{q}(s)\underset{q\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1

for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
. It follows that
P_{n}
is monic of degree
2n-2
. In summary, we have shown that for
n\geq 1
,

\zeta_{q}(n)=\frac{q}{(q-1)^{2n-1}(q+1)^{n}}P_{n}(q),

(15)

with
P_{n}
a palindromic and monic polynomial of degree
2n-2
.

To conclude the proof, it remains to show that the coefficients of
P_{n}
are integers. In view of (
15
), this is equivalent to showing that for all
n\geq 0
,
\zeta_{q}(n)
belongs to the ring
\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
. This follows by induction from the recursive relation (
14
).

\square

Remark 4.1
.

The symmetry exhibited by the palindromic property of the polynomials
P_{n}
in
Theorem

1.1
is already present at the level of the spectral measure
\mu_{q}
. Indeed, through the change of variable
\lambda=(q+1)\,x
in (
2
), namely by considering the normalized Laplacian instead of the combinatorial one, the function
\zeta_{q}
can be written as

(q+1)^{s-1}\zeta_{q}(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}(1-x)^{-s}\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}-x^{2}}}{1-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(16)

where
\rho
is the spectral radius of the transition operator of the random walk on
T_{q+1}
,

\rho^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{q}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\right).

However, it is curious that, although the right-hand side of (
16
) is invariant under
q\leftrightarrow 1/q
, the left-hand side is not strictly invariant under this inversion. For example, the special values at negative integers (
5
) do not have any palindromic structure. Even at positive integers, the special values change sign under
q\leftrightarrow 1/q
. This breaking of symmetry comes from the fact that for fixed
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the right-hand side of (
16
) — when viewed as a function of
q
— is not analytic at
q=1
. The value
q=1
is the only value for which
0
belongs to the spectrum of
\Delta_{q}
.

We also note that the algebraic structure of the special values in
Theorem

1.1
ultimately relies on the algebraic nature of
G_{+}
. We deduce it from the fundamental symmetry between the generating functions of special values, together with Kesten’s previous work. This derivation can also be achieved independently through the
spectral angle representation
of
\zeta_{q}
:

\zeta_{q}(s)=\frac{2}{\pi}q(q+1)\int_{0}^{\pi}(q+1-2\sqrt{q}\cos\theta)^{-s}\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{(q+1)^{2}-4q\cos^{2}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

By directly computing the generating function and the resulting integral, one obtains
Lemma

3.2
by an alternative method.

However, we believe that our approach is more conceptual and technically less demanding. The symmetry of
Theorem

1.2
is the structural mechanism that explains why the generating function
G_{+}
admits an algebraic closed form and, consequently, why the polynomials
P_{n}
inherit palindromic and integrality properties. By contrast, the angle parametrization leads to a longer and more technical derivation, and makes the conceptual origin of the algebraic expression less transparent.

4.2
A recursive relation for the
P_{n}
’s

The first values of
P_{n}
can be computed by hand:

\displaystyle P_{1}(q)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle P_{2}(q)

\displaystyle=q^{2}+1,

\displaystyle P_{3}(q)

\displaystyle=q^{4}+q^{3}+4q^{2}+q+1,

\displaystyle P_{4}(q)

\displaystyle=q^{6}+3q^{5}+11q^{4}+10q^{3}+11q^{2}+3q+1,

\displaystyle P_{5}(q)

\displaystyle=q^{8}+6q^{7}+26q^{6}+46q^{5}+66q^{4}+46q^{3}+26q^{2}+6q+1.

Remarkably, all coefficients appearing above are non-negative. In fact, this phenomenon holds for every polynomial
P_{n}
, as will be proved in the next section, where we give a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients. Before doing so, we translate
Lemma

3.2
to obtain a recursive relation for the
P_{n}
’s and a quadratic equation for their generating function.

Corollary 4.2

(of
Lemma

3.2
)
.

Let
T
be the generating function of the family of polynomials
P_{n}
, namely

T(z)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 1}P_{n}(q)z^{n-1}.

It satisfies the quadratic equation

qz\bigl(2-z(q-1)^{2}\bigr)T^{2}+\bigl(z(q-1)^{2}-1\bigr)T+1=0,

and consequently the family
P_{n}
satisfies the recursive relation, for
n\geq 1
,

P_{n+1}=2q\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}P_{n+1-j}-q(q-1)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}P_{j}P_{n-j}+(q-1)^{2}P_{n},

(17)

with
P_{0}=1
.

Observe that, by working directly with the recursive relation, one can also deduce
Theorem

1.1
.

5
The functional equation

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.3
.

The first step toward this result is yet another relation between the generating functions
G_{\pm}
—defined in the introduction—obtained by taking advantage of their algebraic expressions.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
q>1
,
k\coloneqq\tfrac{q+1}{q-1}
, and define

\mathcal{E}(z)\coloneqq G_{-}\left(\frac{z}{q-1}\right)\cdot(1-2kz)+G_{+}((q-1)z)\cdot(2k-z).

Then
\mathcal{E}
can be analytically continued to an entire function and satisfies

\mathcal{E}(z)=z+1.

(18)

Proof.

From (
11
) and (
12
), around
z=0
, we have

G_{+}((q-1)z)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(q+1)\sqrt{1-2zk+z^{2}}+z(q-1)-(q+1)}{z-2k},

and

G_{-}\left(\frac{z}{q-1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(q+1)\sqrt{1-2kz+z^{2}}+(q+1)z-(q-1)}{1-2kz},

which shows that, for
z
small enough,

\mathcal{E}(z)=z+1.

∎

Consequently, by comparing coefficients in the generating series, we obtain the following recursive relation.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
q>1
and
a_{n}\coloneqq\zeta_{q}(n)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have the two-step recursive relation

a_{-n}-2(q+1)a_{1-n}=(q-1)^{2n-1}\left[a_{n-1}-2(q+1)a_{n}\right].

(19)

Heuristically, the symmetry (
18
) at the level of generating functions should induce a corresponding symmetry at the level of the zeta function. This is consistent with the general principle that the generating function, the heat kernel, and the spectral zeta function are related through Laplace – and Mellin – transform.

One is therefore led to consider the Mellin transform of (
18
), with the aim of recovering an analytic continuation of (
19
), and hence the desired functional equation. The main difficulty is that the spectral cuts lie on the natural path of integration. A Hankel contour could be used to bypass the cut, but in the presence of a spectral gap one can choose a much more convenient contour.

Recall that for
B
a self-adjoint operator with spectrum
\sigma(B)
, the resolvent of
B
is defined to be the holomorphic map

\sigma(B)\not\ni z\longmapsto R_{B}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{z-B}.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
B
be a bounded self-adjoint operator with spectrum
\sigma(B)
. Let
U\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set containing
\sigma(B)
, and let
\gamma
be a piecewise smooth positively oriented Jordan curve inside
U
, containing
\sigma(B)
in its interior. Then for any holomorphic function
h\colon U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}h(z)R_{B}(z)^{n}\,\mathrm{d}z=\begin{cases}h(B)&\text{if }n=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

This is a straightforward application of the spectral theorem and its functional calculus. Indeed, let
\mathbb{P}_{B}
be the projection-valued measure of
B
provided by the spectral theorem. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}h(z)R_{B}(z)^{n}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\int_{\sigma(B)}\frac{h(z)}{(z-\lambda)^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{B}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\sigma(B)}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{h(z)}{(z-\lambda)^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{B}(\lambda).

By Cauchy’s integral formula, the inner integral is
0
if
n\neq 1
, and
h(\lambda)
otherwise. Therefore, for
n=1
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}h(z)R_{B}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\sigma(B)}h(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{B}(\lambda)=h(B).\qed

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Consider the operator

and observe that its spectrum is
\sigma(B)=[\beta,\beta^{-1}]

In particular,

has a spectral gap and its inverse

has the same spectrum as

. Recall also that

\xi_{q}(s)\coloneqq(q-1)^{s}(2(q+1)\zeta_{q}(s)-\zeta_{q}(s-1))=(q-1)\langle(2kB^{-s}-B^{1-s})\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\mid\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\rangle,

where

From the definition of

, we have

\displaystyle G_{+}((q-1)z)

\displaystyle=\langle-zR_{B}(z)\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\mid\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\rangle,

\displaystyle G_{-}\left(\frac{z}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\langle(1-zR_{B^{-1}}(z))\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\mid\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\rangle.

Take
\gamma

. By combining

2

2n-2
. The first hint in this direction is the observation that by substituting

into the quadratic equation satisfied by the generating function

, we obtain

\sum_{n\geq 1}P_{n}(1)z^{n}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-8z}}{4z},

the generating function of the numbers
2^{n}C_{n}
, where
C_{n}
is the
n
-th Catalan number. It is well known that
C_{n}
counts the number of
Dyck paths
of length
2n
. A Dyck path is a path starting at
(0,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that ends on — and always stays above — the horizontal axis, with allowed steps in the set
\{(1,1),(1,-1)\}
; see

1
below.

.

A
2
-
coloured Dyck path
is a Dyck path in which the downward steps
(1,-1)
are coloured in two colours; see

2

.

The identity
P_{n}(1)=2^{n-1}C_{n-1}
strongly suggests that the coefficients of
P_{n}
count different types of
2
-coloured Dyck paths.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Sigma^{*}_{3}
be the free monoid on the set
\{U,B,R\}
. To each word
w\in\Sigma^{*}_{3}
we associate a
coloured path
, read from left to right, where
U
corresponds to the upward movement
(1,1)
, while
R
and
B
correspond to downward movements coloured in red and blue, respectively. The set of
2
-
coloured Dyck words
is the subset
\mathbb{D}\subset\Sigma^{*}_{3}
of those elements
w
such that the corresponding coloured path is a
2
-coloured Dyck path. The number of
2
-coloured Dyck words of length
2n
is
2^{n}C_{n}
.

Let
w\in\Sigma_{3}^{*}
. A
block of colour

\beta\in\{U,B,R\}
in
w
is a maximal and non-empty subword of
w
composed solely of the letter
\beta
. Denote by
r_{\beta}(w)
the number of blocks of colour
\beta
in
w
.

We define a weight function on
2
-coloured Dyck words by taking the number of
U
’s, adding the number of blue blocks, and subtracting the number of red blocks. Precisely, for
w\in\mathbb{D}
of length
2n
, let

h(w)\coloneqq n+r_{B}(w)-r_{R}(w).

Next we define the polynomials associated with
h
: for
n\geq 0
,

Q_{n}(t)=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{D}_{n}}t^{h(w)}=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\#\{w\in\mathbb{D}_{n}\mid h(w)=k\}t^{k},

where
\mathbb{D}_{n}
is the set of
w\in\mathbb{D}
of length
2n
.
It is straightforward to see that the
Q_{n}
are monic, palindromic, and have non-negative integer coefficients. The first values of
Q_{n}
can be computed by hand:

\displaystyle Q_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle Q_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{2}+1,

\displaystyle Q_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{4}+t^{3}+4t^{2}+t+1,

\displaystyle Q_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{6}+3t^{5}+11t^{4}+10t^{3}+11t^{2}+3t+1,

\displaystyle Q_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=t^{8}+6t^{7}+26t^{6}+46t^{5}+66t^{4}+46t^{3}+26t^{2}+6t+1.

Remarkably, for all
n=0,\ldots,4
, we have
Q_{n}=P_{n+1}
; in fact, we shall show that this always holds.

Theorem 6.2
.

For each
n\geq 0
, we have
Q_{n}=P_{n+1}
.

The proof rests on the fact that the weight
h
behaves well recursively. It yields a quadratic equation for the generating function of the
Q_{n}
’s which coincides with the one for the generating function
T
of the
P_{n}
’s, as described in
Corollary

4.2
.

Proof.

The generating function
H(t,z)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}Q_{n}(t)z^{n}
can be written as

H=\sum_{w\in\mathbb{D}}t^{h(w)}z^{l(w)},

where
l(w)
is half the length of
w
. Let us define the auxiliary generating functions

E_{B}\coloneqq\sum_{w\in\mathbb{D}(B)}t^{h(w)}z^{l(w)},\quad\text{and}\quad E_{R}\coloneqq\sum_{w\in\mathbb{D}(R)}t^{h(w)}z^{l(w)},

where
\mathbb{D}(\beta)
denotes the subset of words in
\mathbb{D}
that end with
\beta
. Then clearly,

H=1+E_{B}+E_{R}.

(20)

Let us investigate the recursive behaviour of
h
. Observe first that every non-empty
w\in\mathbb{D}
can be written uniquely as

w_{1}Uw_{2}\beta,

where
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
(possibly empty), and
\beta\in\{B,R\}
. Then

h(w_{1}Uw_{2}\beta)=h(w_{1})+1+h(w_{2})+\varepsilon(w_{2}\beta),

where

\varepsilon(w_{2}\beta)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }w_{2}\text{ ends with the letter }\beta,\\
\mathrm{sign}(\beta)&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and where
\mathrm{sign}(B)=1
and
\mathrm{sign}(R)=-1
.
Now let us compute

\displaystyle E_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1}Uw_{2}B}t^{h(w_{1})+1+h(w_{2})+\varepsilon(w_{2}B)}z^{l(w_{1})+l(w_{2})+1}

\displaystyle=tz\cdot H\cdot\sum_{w_{2}\in\mathbb{D}}t^{h(w_{2})+\varepsilon(w_{2}B)}z^{l(w_{2})}

\displaystyle=tz\cdot H\cdot(E_{B}+t(1+E_{R})).

Similarly, we obtain

E_{R}=H\cdot tz\cdot(E_{R}+t^{-1}(1+E_{B})).

By isolating
E_{B}
and
E_{R}
in each equation and by setting

u\coloneqq\frac{H\cdot tz}{1-H\cdot tz},

we obtain

E_{B}=\frac{u(u+t)}{1-u^{2}},\quad\text{and}\quad E_{R}=\frac{u(u+t^{-1})}{1-u^{2}}.

Finally, substituting into (
20
), we can write

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=1+E_{B}+E_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{(u+t)(u+t^{-1})}{1-u^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-tz^{2}(t-1)^{2}H^{2}+z(t-1)^{2}H+1}{1-2tzH}.

Consequently,
H
satisfies the quadratic equation

tz\bigl(2-z(t-1)^{2}\bigr)H^{2}+\bigl(z(t-1)^{2}-1\bigr)H+1=0.

(21)

This is the same quadratic equation as the one satisfied by
T
; see
Corollary

4.2
. Since both satisfy
T(0)=1=H(0)
, we deduce that
H=T
, and consequently the result follows.
∎

As an immediate consequence, the coefficients of
P_{n}
are non-negative. In fact,
Theorem

1.1
is a corollary of
Theorem

6.2
. Using this combinatorial interpretation, it can be shown that, except for
P_{2}
, all the coefficients of
P_{n}
are positive.
