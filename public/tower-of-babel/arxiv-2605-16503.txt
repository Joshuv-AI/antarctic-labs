From bulk to interface dynamics, in and out of equilibrium
Lila Sarfati, Julien Tailleur, Frédéric van Wijland

Abstract
We study the dynamics of weakly deformed interfaces separating two stable phases, starting from the fluctuating hydrodynamics of the phase-separating fields. Using a well-chosen definition for the interface and the dynamical-action formalism to represent path probabilities, we derive the linear relaxation of the interface and the fluctuations around it for a large class of models. Our method applies to equilibrium dynamics, where it recovers and complements existing results, but also extends to their non-equilibrium counterparts. We explain how non-linear terms can be systematically computed and illustrate their derivations in the case of (active) model A. We highlight the danger of a popular ansatz used to derive interface dynamics, which was rigorously established in equilibrium but is uncontrolled for active field theories.

II
From bulk dynamics to the definition of an interface

II.1
Bulk dynamics

For concreteness, we first consider systems described by a single
fluctuating scalar order-parameter field
\phi
. We assume that, at
least at the mean-field level, the evolution of
\phi
leads to a
smooth planar interface located at
z=0
, separating two spatially
stable phases along some direction
z
. Translational invariance is
maintained along the remaining
d-1
directions, hereafter denoted by

r
. We start from a stochastic partial differential equation for

\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)
in the form
42
,

\partial_{t}\phi=-\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+w[\phi]+\sqrt{2T}\eta(\text{\bf r},z,t)\;,

(1)

where
\eta
is a Gaussian white noise with correlations
\langle\eta(\text{\bf r},z,t)\eta(\text{\bf r}^{\prime},z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta(z-z^{\prime})\delta^{(d-1)}(\text{\bf r}-\text{\bf r}^{\prime})
. The functional
F
is a Landau free energy of the form

F[\phi]=\int\text{d}^{d-1}r\,\text{d}z\Big[\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\nabla}\phi)^{2}+f(\phi)\Big]\;,

(2)

where
f
exhibits two degenerate minima and
w
is the nonconservative contribution to the evolution. This functional Helmholtz decomposition has been discussed in a series of recent works
85
;
84
;
83
. The goal is to start from dynamics (
1
) and derive the fluctuating dynamics for a properly-defined interface between the coexisting phases. The extension of the formalism to other cases will be discussed further down, including the possibility of conservation laws and the coupling to auxiliary fields (that may themselves be conserved or not).

In Eq. (
1
), the parameter
T
refers to the amplitude of the noise experienced by the field
\phi
. This is a temperature only if
w=0
and the system is in thermal equilibrium. We denote by
m_{c}(z)
the phase-separated steady-state mean-field profile, which solves

0=-\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}[m_{c}]+w[m_{c}]\;.

(3)

Let us now discuss how to define the deformation field of the interface in the presence of fluctuations.

II.2
Interface definition

Figure
1
shows a cartoon of the field and of the
interface. We denote
h(\text{\bf r},t)
the amplitude of the deviation between
the fluctuating interface and its flat average. In what follows we
assume that the fluctuations of the interface occur over length scales
large with respect to its width
\xi
, that the amplitudes of
fluctuations remain small compared to the jump of the order parameter
across the interface,
\Delta m_{c}=m_{c}(+\infty)-m_{c}(-\infty)
, and that
the interface remains single valued (there are no overhangs).

To make progress, we need an operational definition of
h
starting from the field
\phi
, beyond the cartoon. A natural definition is that the interface
h(\text{\bf r},t)
is the set of
z
coordinates where the field
\phi
takes a prescribed value
\phi_{0}
(be it half the step size,
m_{c}(0)
,
etc
):
\phi(\text{\bf r},h(\text{\bf r},t),t)=\phi_{0}
. For reasons that will become clear below, it is useful to recast this as the following equation for
h(\text{\bf r},t)
:

\int\text{d}z[\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)-\phi_{0}]\delta(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))=0\;.

(4)

Even though prescribing a value to
\phi(\text{\bf r},h(\text{\bf r},t),t)
is an appealing definition for
h
, it is also very sensitive to initial condition, noise, and, for a given
r
, there might exist several values of
z
where the field takes the value
\phi_{0}
. This provides the incentive to search for a smoother definition of the interface, insensitive to such caveats.

Instead of using the fluctuating field, a possibility is to resort to its mean-field profile
m_{c}
and to define
h
as the solution of:

\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)=m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))\;.

(5)

This however assumes that
\phi
is simply a shift of the mean-field
profile along
\hat{\bf z}
, whose amplitude defines
h(\text{\bf r},t)
. This
is indeed a smoother definition of
h
, but it does not account for
the possibility of fluctuations within the bulk of each phase. This is
the typical approach adopted in the theory of capillary
waves
25
;
105
, and its use has
been justified when (local) equilibrium
holds
77
. This is also the basis of recent works
on interfaces in nonequilibrium systems such as
sheared
12
, driven
26
, or
active
fluids
30
;
6
;
63
;
16
;
101
;
71
;
13
. We
shall see in Sec.
III

Inspired by the quantum-field-theory literature on solitons, instantons, and extended states

;
35

34

;
61

\int\text{d}z\,\chi(\text{\bf r},z-h(\text{\bf r},t),t)\partial_{z}m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))=0

(6)

where we have introduced the auxiliary field

\chi(\text{\bf r},z-h(\text{\bf r},t),t)=\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)-m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))\;.

(7)

Comparing Eq. (

Comparing Eq. (

In this limit, the definition of

) and (

The interface definition through Eq. (

III
An appealing ansatz and its shortcomings

Let us now show how, when the definition of the interface relies on
the mean-field ansatz of Eq. (
5
), inconsistencies
arise. Since the goal is to obtain an evolution equation for
h
, we
can substitute
\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)=m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))
into
Eq. (
1
). Space and time derivatives can then be
computed as

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{z}m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))\hat{\text{\bf z}}

\displaystyle\;-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h(\text{\bf r},t)\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}h(\text{\bf r},t)\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z-h(\text{\bf r},t))\;,

(9)

and similarly for higher-order spatial derivatives. In the simplest case of equilibrium model A
42

\phi

\partial_{t}\phi=\Delta\phi+\xi^{-2}(\phi-\phi^{3})+\sqrt{2}\eta(\text{\bf r},z,t)\;,

(10)

which leads to

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}h\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)=

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)+(\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h)^{2}\,\partial_{z}^{2}m_{c}(z)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}{\eta}(\text{\bf r},z+h(\text{\bf r},t),t)\;.

(11)

We then need to eliminate the
z
-dependence to get closed dynamics for
h(\text{\bf r},t)
. This can, however, be achieved in many ways.

For instance we can follow a first protocol:

1

Multiply Eq. (
III
) by
\partial_{z}m_{c}(z+1)
and integrate over
z
, which yields

\partial_{t}h=\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma_{1}}(\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h)^{2}+\sqrt{2\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}}\tilde{\eta}(\text{\bf r},t)\;,

(12)

where
\tilde{\eta}
is a Gaussian white noise with correlations
\delta^{(d-1)}(\text{\bf r}-\text{\bf r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and we have introduced

\begin{split}\sigma=&\int\text{d}z\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)^{2}\\
\sigma_{1}=&\int\text{d}z\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z+1)\\
\alpha=&\int\text{d}z\,\partial_{z}^{2}m_{c}(z)\,\partial_{z}m_{c}(z+1)\;.\end{split}

(13)

The mean-field ansatz Eq. (
5
) can thus be used to predict that the interface of an equilibrium Ising model evolves according to the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation

Of course, this cannot be correct. In fact, in Eq. (
III
), the unknown function
h
depends solely on
r
and
t
, yet the resulting equation is valid for all

III

2

Multiply Eq. (
III

\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)
and integrate over

to get:

\partial_{t}h=\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sigma}}\tilde{\eta}(\text{\bf r},t)\;.

(14)

We know that Eq. (
14

First, if the dependence of

IV
Deriving the interface dynamics

For the sake of concreteness and simplicity, we first present our approach in the case of Eq. (
1
).

IV.1
Path-integral formalism

As discussed in Section
II
, the definition we adopt for the interface is Eq. (
6
).
Beyond its physical appeal, this definition also has a geometric interpretation that will serve as the basis of our approach.
We introduce the scalar product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int\text{d}zf(z)g(z)

(15)

in the space of
z
-dependent functions, such that the condition
Eq. (
6
) translates into

\langle\chi(\text{\bf r},z,t),u_{0}(z)\rangle=0\;,

(16)

with
u_{0}(z)=\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)
. The interface
h
is thus defined in such a
way that
\chi
is orthogonal to the function
u_{0}
.
Equation (
16
) serves several purposes. First, it
removes an ambiguity in Eq. (
7
), where a shift of
h

can be absorbed into a redefinition of
\chi
. Then, this geometric
formulation as a scalar product will prove useful to integrate out
the
\chi
degrees of freedom and obtain an effective dynamics for the
field
h
only. The precise choice of
u_{0}
among all the possible
peaked functions with support localized near the interface will then
be made model by model to facilitate the algebra.

To proceed, we start from

\mathbb{P}[h]=\langle\delta[h-h[\phi]]\rangle_{\phi}\equiv\int{\mathcal{D}}\phi\,\delta[h-h[\phi]]\mathbb{P}[\phi]\;,

(17)

where
\mathbb{P}[h]
and
\mathbb{P}[\phi]
are the path probabilities of the fields
h(\text{\bf r},t)
and
\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)
, respectively, and
h[\phi]
is defined by Eqs. (
16
) and (
7
).
To make progress, we then introduce the Janssen-De Dominicis action functional
44
;
28
,

Z=\int{\mathscr{D}}\bar{\phi}\,{\mathscr{D}}\phi\,\text{e}^{-\mathrm{S}[\bar{\phi},\phi]}

(18)

with the Itō-discretized action

\mathrm{S}[\bar{\phi},\phi]=\int\text{d}t\,\text{d}z\,\text{d}^{d-1}r\left[\bar{\phi}\left(\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-w\right)-T\bar{\phi}^{2}\right]\;,

(19)

so that:

\mathbb{P}[h]=\frac{1}{Z}\int{\mathscr{D}}\bar{\phi}\,{\mathscr{D}}\phi\,\delta[h-h[\phi]]\,\text{e}^{-\mathrm{S}[\bar{\phi},\phi]}\;.

(20)

We then use the definition Eq. (
16
) to rewrite

\delta[h-h[\phi]]=\delta\left[\langle\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)-m_{c}(z-h),u_{0}(z-h)\rangle\right]|J|\;,

(21)

where the functional Jacobian
J
is given by

J=-\left<\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t),\partial_{z}u_{0}(z-h)\right>\;.

(22)

To enforce the
\delta
constraint explicitly, we introduce the projector

\Pi=\mathds{1}-\frac{\left|\partial_{z}m_{c}\right>\left<u_{0}\right|}{\left<u_{0},\partial_{z}m_{c}\right>}\;.

(23)

The projector
\Pi
is well defined as long as
u_{0}
and
\partial_{z}m_{c}
have a non-zero scalar product,
which will prove a necessary condition for the choice of
u_{0}
. It verifies

\displaystyle\Pi\partial_{z}m_{c}=0\;,

(24)

\displaystyle\Pi^{\dagger}u_{0}=0\;,

where
\dagger
stands for the adjoint operator with respect to the scalar product defined in Eq. (
15
). Note that
\Pi
is not an orthogonal projector in general, except if
u_{0}\propto\partial_{z}m_{c}
. We can then split the fluctuation
\chi
, defined in Eq. (
7
), between its longitudinal and transverse components, using

\chi=\frac{\left<u_{0},\chi\right>}{\left<u_{0},\partial_{z}m_{c}\right>}\partial_{z}m_{c}+\chi^{\perp},\;\text{where}\;\chi^{\perp}=\Pi\chi\;.

(25)

Our choice of interface definition, which translates into the functional constraint in Eq. (
16
), thus enforces the vanishing of the longitudinal component of
\chi
.

This leads to an expression for
\mathbb{P}[h]
in terms of a path integral

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[h]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\int{\mathscr{D}}\bar{\phi}\,{\mathscr{D}}\phi\,\big\{\delta\left[\langle\phi-m_{c}(z-h),u_{0}(z-h)\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\text{e}^{-S[h,\bar{\phi},\phi]}\big\}\;,

(26)

where
S[h,\bar{\phi},\phi]=\mathrm{S}[\bar{\phi},\phi]-\int_{t,\text{\bf r}}\ln|J|
. Using Eq. (
7
) and defining
T\bar{\phi}(\text{\bf r},z,t)=\bar{\chi}(\text{\bf r},z-h,t)
then leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[h]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\int{\mathscr{D}}\bar{\chi}\,{\mathscr{D}}\chi\,\delta\left[\langle\chi,u_{0}\rangle\right]\text{e}^{-S[h,\bar{\chi},m_{c}+\chi]}

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\int{\mathscr{D}}\bar{\chi}\,{\mathscr{D}}\chi^{\perp}\,\text{e}^{-S[h,\bar{\chi},m_{c}+\chi^{\perp}]}\;,

(28)

where the
\delta
constraint of the first line restricts the path integration over
\chi
to functions
\chi^{\perp}
orthogonal to
u_{0}
. After some algebra, the action takes the form

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{T}

\displaystyle\int_{t,\text{\bf r},z}\Big\{\bar{\chi}\Big[-\partial_{t}h(\partial_{z}m_{c}+\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp})+\partial_{t}\chi^{\perp}

\displaystyle\;+\left.\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-w\right)\right|_{m_{c}+\chi^{\perp}}\Big]-\bar{\chi}^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle\;-\int_{t,\text{\bf r}}\ln\left|\left<\partial_{z}u_{0},m_{c}\right>+\left<\partial_{z}u_{0},\chi^{\perp}\right>\right|\;,

(29)

where the notation
(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-w)|_{m_{c}+\chi^{\perp}}
means that we first evaluate the functional derivative in
\phi(\text{\bf r},z,t)=m_{c}(z-h)+\chi^{\perp}(\text{\bf r},z-h,t)
and then do the shift
z\to z-h
. In order to find the explicit expression of this action, we use that, for
i=t,\text{\bf r}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\phi=-\partial_{i}h[\partial_{z}

\displaystyle m_{c}(z-h)+\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp}(\text{\bf r},z-h,t)]

\displaystyle+\partial_{i}\chi^{\perp}(\text{\bf r},z-h,t)\;.

(30)

Mirroring Eq. (
25
) for
\chi
, we decompose
\bar{\chi}
as:

\bar{\chi}=-\bar{h}u_{0}+\bar{\chi}^{\perp},\;\text{with}\;\bar{\chi}^{\perp}=\Pi^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}\;,

(31)

where

\bar{h}(\text{\bf r},t)=-\frac{\left<\partial_{z}m_{c}(z),\bar{\chi}(\text{\bf r},z,t)\right>}{\left<\partial_{z}m_{c}(z),u_{0}(z)\right>}

(32)

captures the component of
\bar{\chi}
that we anticipate
will drive the fluctuations of
h
. Note that
\chi^{\perp}
is orthogonal to
u_{0}
while
\bar{\chi}^{\perp}
is orthogonal to
\partial_{z}m_{c}
.

Our goal will now be to integrate out the fields
\bar{\chi}^{\perp}
and
\chi^{\perp}
to obtain an action for
\bar{h}
and

is even, as in Eq. (

), the speed

vanishes. Otherwise, the fluctuation-driven motion of the interface thus only occurs when the degenerate minima lie at the bottom of wells with distinct curvatures, in agreement with

For an asymmetric

with degenerate minima, assuming that the operator
\Omega_{0}
has similar spectral properties as for the standard
\phi^{4}
theory, the sum over eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
is dominated by the integral over the continuous part of the spectrum.
At high values of

\tilde{e}_{k}
to behave like plane waves and the eigenvalues to grow as
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}\sim k^{2}
.
Integration over
q
and

will therefore lead to a logarithmic divergence which can be regularized by introducing a short-wavelength cutoff

.

Note that, in three dimensions and more, the gradient terms in Eq. (

) do not generically cancel out.
One can then use the explicit expression of
\left<(\chi^{\perp})^{2}\right>_{0}^{\prime}
in terms of the eigenvectors of
\Omega_{0}
to find an estimate for the interface velocity.

As a final remark, we would like to point out that a phase-separated system, whose potential is asymmetric and has degenerate minima, is out of equilibrium, since a drift velocity is generated. In this case, one expects to see the KPZ nonlinearity arise. Showing this in detail goes beyond the scope of this paper. It would require going to higher order in
\sqrt{T}
in the expansion (

) and studying the additional contributions that enter the cumulant expansion. Among these terms, we can already identify one that yields a KPZ term:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T^{2}}\int_{\text{\bf r},t}

\displaystyle-\bar{h}(\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h)^{2}\int_{\text{\bf r}^{\prime},z,z^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(m_{c})}{2\sigma}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\left<\partial_{z}^{2}\chi^{\perp}(\text{\bf r},z,t)[\bar{\chi}^{\perp}(\chi^{\perp})^{2}](\text{\bf r}^{\prime},z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>^{\prime}_{c,0}\;.

(78)

As expected, substituting the explicit expression of the average in terms of the eigenmodes of

shows that this term vanishes for a symmetric potential

Note that, if we were to continue the expansion leading to Eq. (

This concludes the presentation of our framework for equilibrium model A. We now turn to more complex models, starting with model B.

V.2
Conserved relaxational dynamics: Model B

V.2.1
Early results on interface dynamics in Model B

We now study model B, whose dynamics is conserved and reads:

\partial_{t}\phi=\bm{\nabla}^{2}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}+\sqrt{2T}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\eta}(\text{\bf r},z,t)\;,

(79)

where the components of
\bm{\eta}
are independent white noises that obey
\left<\eta_{i}(\text{\bf r},z,t)\eta_{j}(\text{\bf r}^{\prime},z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>=\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta(z-z^{\prime})\delta^{(d-1)}(\text{\bf r}-\text{\bf r}^{\prime})

The Edwards-Wilkinson dispersion relation
\omega\propto k^{2}
found in model A does not hold anymore in model B. Instead, a dispersion relation
\omega\propto k^{3}
was predicted first using a phenomenological, hydrodynamic approach
62
.
A statistical-mechanical derivation, directly based on model B, was developed soon after
57
.
This provided an expression for the various coefficients entering the interface evolution equation.
Their better-known expressions in Fourier space where derived subsequently
58
;
77
.
Alternative derivations for the dispersion relation were proposed later, using variational
46
or spectral
94
approaches.

We now apply our field-theoretical framework to model B, mirroring the derivation employed for model A.

V.2.2

-V_{c}^{\prime}&1\end{pmatrix}=\mathbb{O}_{0}+\begin{pmatrix}-\nabla_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(119)

Here,
V_{c}^{\prime}
is a short-hand notation for
V^{\prime}(m_{c})
,
\Omega=-\partial_{z}^{2}+f^{\prime\prime}(m_{c})+V_{c}^{\prime 2}
and we stress that the Hermitian operator
\mathbb{O}_{0}
satisfies
\mathbb{O}_{0}\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}=0
. We Fourier transform in
t
and
r
, and introduce

\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}=i\omega\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\mathbb{O}_{\text{\bf q}}\;,

(120)

which plays for models C and D the role of
\Gamma
in Eq. (
83
) for model B. This
leads to:

\displaystyle S=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q},z}

\displaystyle\Big[\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{T}\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}(-h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\,\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}+\chi^{\perp}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})

\displaystyle-\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{T}\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\bar{\chi}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\Big]\;.

(121)

In the same spirit as in Eq. (
84
), we decompose

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}=-\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}U_{0}+(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{\perp},\;\text{with}

\displaystyle(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{\perp}=\Pi^{\dagger}\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\;,

(122)

which turns the action into

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Big\{\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}+\left<(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{\perp},\chi^{\perp}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\right>

\displaystyle-\Big<\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\left(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}(-\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}U_{0}+(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{\perp}),

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{G}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}(-\tilde{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}U_{0}+(\mathbb{G}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega})^{\perp})\Big>\Big\}\;,

(123)

where we have used that
\left<U_{0},\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}\right>=1
.
Performing the integration over
\chi^{\perp}
yields a
\delta
constraint in the space of functions orthogonal to
U_{0}
, which implies
(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{\perp}=a_{\text{\bf q},\omega}U_{0}

a_{\text{\bf q},\omega}
an unknown proportionality constant.
Since by definition
\left<(\mathbb{G}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}^{\dagger}\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{\perp},\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}\right>=0
, we must have
a_{\text{\bf q},\omega}=0
. This leads to an action for
\tilde{h}
and
h
only:

\begin{split}S_{h}&[\tilde{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Big[\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\\
&-\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\tilde{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\left<\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}(\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{-1}U_{0},(\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega})^{-1}U_{0}\right>\Big]\;.\end{split}

(124)

We respectively denote
L_{k}(q)
and
R_{k}(q)
the left and right eigenvectors of
\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\mathbb{O}_{\text{\bf q}}
, and
\Lambda_{k}(q)
the corresponding eigenvalue.
From Eq. (
120
), the leading contribution to
(G^{\dagger}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{-1}
(namely the slowest), once integrated over
\omega

\Lambda_{k}(q)
of
\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\mathbb{O}_{\text{\bf q}}
whose real part is closest to zero, which we hereafter refer to as
\Lambda_{0}(q)
. The scalar product in Eq. (
124
) can then be rewritten as

\begin{split}\langle\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}(\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{-1}&U_{0},(\mathbb{G}^{\dagger}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega})^{-1}U_{0}\rangle\simeq\frac{1}{|i\omega+\Lambda_{0}(q)|^{2}}\\
&\left<L_{0}(q),\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}L_{0}(q)\right>\left<R_{0}(q),U_{0}\right>^{2}\;,\end{split}

(125)

where
L_{0}(q)
and
R_{0}(q)
have been normalized such that
\left<L_{0}(q),R_{0}(q)\right>=1
.
Using
\left<\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}\right|\mathbb{O}_{0}=0
and the definition Eq. (
119
), the eigenvalue
\Lambda_{0}
satisfies

\Lambda_{0}(q)=\frac{q^{2}\left<\partial_{z}m_{c},(R_{0}(q))_{1}\right>}{\left<\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\partial_{z}\Phi_{c},R_{0}(q)\right>}\;,

(126)

where
(R_{0}(q))_{1}
refers to the first component of the vector
R_{0}(q)
.

For long wavelengths, we assume that
\Lambda_{0}(q)
is obtained as a perturbation of the ground state eigenvalue
\Lambda_{0}(0)=0
of
\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\mathbb{O}_{0}
, which is the operator where
q
has been set to zero in
\mathbb{O}_{\text{\bf q}}
, but not in
\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}
. Then, the lowest orders of the eigenvectors are given by their unperturbed expressions:
R_{0}(0)=\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}
and
L_{0}(0)=\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}
, normalized by their scalar product. As for model B, we choose to work with
\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}=(i\omega+\Lambda_{0}(q))^{-1}\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}
, so that the action reads

\begin{split}S_{h}[\bar{h}&,h]=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Big[\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}(i\omega+\Lambda_{0}(q))h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\\
&-\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\bar{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\left<\partial_{z}\Phi_{c},\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\partial_{z}\Phi_{c}\right>^{-1}\Big]\;.\end{split}

(127)

At this point, we need to discuss models C and D separately.

For model C,
\mathcal{L}=1
and

\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}D^{-1}&0\\
0&\Lambda^{-1}\,\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\;,

(128)

where the inverse Laplacian
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}
is already given in Eq. (
93
).
Defining
B(q)=2q\left<\partial_{z}V_{c},\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\partial_{z}V_{c}\right>
and using Eq. (
126
), we find

\begin{split}S_{h}[\bar{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}&\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Bigg[\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\left(i\omega+\frac{Dq^{2}}{1+\frac{D}{2\Lambda\sigma q}B(q)}\right)h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\\
&-\frac{D}{\sigma}\frac{1}{1+\frac{D}{2\Lambda\sigma q}B(q)}\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\bar{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\Bigg]\;.\end{split}

(129)

As pointed out in
110
;
3
, there are two different regimes depending on the symmetries of the auxiliary field
\rho_{c}
, which, we remind, satisfies
\rho_{c}=V(m_{c})
. The function
B
in Eq. (
129
) heavily depends on whether the difference of
\rho_{c}
in the two phases is
\Delta\rho_{c}\neq 0
, or
\Delta\rho_{c}=0
.

If
\Delta\rho_{c}=0
, to lowest order in
q
,
B(q)=-B_{1}q
with
B_{1}=\iint\text{d}z\,\text{d}z^{\prime}|z-z^{\prime}|\partial_{z}V_{c}\,\partial_{z}^{\prime}V_{c}
and the action takes the form

\begin{split}S_{h}[\bar{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}&\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Bigg[\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\left(i\omega+\frac{Dq^{2}}{1-\frac{DB_{1}}{2\Lambda\sigma}}\right)h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\\
&-\frac{D}{\sigma}\frac{1}{1-\frac{DB_{1}}{2\Lambda\sigma}}\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\bar{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\Bigg]\;.\end{split}

(130)

Thus at the special point
\Delta\rho_{c}=0
, the interface follows Edwards-Wilkinson dynamics.

If instead
\Delta\rho_{c}\neq 0
,
B(q)\simeq(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}
at long wavelengths, and we find

\begin{split}S_{h}[\bar{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}&\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Bigg[\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\left(i\omega+\frac{2\Lambda\sigma q^{3}}{(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}}\right)h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\\
&-\frac{2\Lambda q}{(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}}\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\bar{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\Bigg]\;.\end{split}

(131)

Hence in this generic case, the interface exhibits conserved dynamics. A discussion of this surprising behavior was presented in
110
.

To our knowledge, our derivation of the noise in model C is new. We can compare our dispersion relations to those obtained previously. In the
\Delta\rho_{c}\neq 0
case, our dispersion relation matches that of
77
;
110
;
3
:

i\omega=-\frac{2\Lambda\sigma}{(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}}q^{3}\;.

(132)

In the symmetric case, the prefactor of
q^{2}
differs from the one reported in
77
, but agrees with
3
:

i\omega=-\frac{Dq^{2}}{1-\frac{DB_{1}}{2\Lambda\sigma}}\;.

(133)

We believe that there is a possibility that the
q\to 0
behavior was improperly kept track of in
77
. On the numerical side, results consistent with both the
q^{2}
and
q^{3}
scaling regimes have been observed
48
;
41
.

In the symmetric case, a more rigorous derivation for the
q^{2}
prefactor would consist in establishing the absence of level crossing in the spectrum of
\mathbb{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\mathbb{O}_{\text{\bf q}}
as
q
departs from
0
. This goes beyond the scope of this work.

Regarding model D in which both fields are conserved, we have instead
\mathcal{L}=-\bm{\nabla}^{2}
and the action takes the form

\begin{split}S_{h}[\bar{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}&\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}\Bigg[\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\left(i\omega+\frac{2\sigma q^{3}}{\frac{(\Delta m_{c})^{2}}{D}+\frac{(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}}{\Lambda}}\right)h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\\
&-\frac{2q}{\frac{(\Delta m_{c})^{2}}{D}+\frac{(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}}{\Lambda}}\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\bar{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\Bigg]\;.\end{split}

(134)

This corresponds to the dispersion relation

i\omega=-\frac{2\sigma q^{3}}{\frac{(\Delta m_{c})^{2}}{D}+\frac{(\Delta\rho_{c})^{2}}{\Lambda}}\;.

(135)

Given that both fields appear on equal footing, the
q^{3}
behavior is insensitive to
V(m_{c})
being symmetric or not. This result, to the best of our knowledge, appears here for the first time in the literature. On the numerical side, the coarsening
67

shows a
t^{1/3}
growth law that we believe is consistent with the
q^{3}
scaling of the relaxation spectrum.

V.4
Coupling to a momentum conserving fluid: Model H

In a system of particles advected by a surrounding fluid in
d\geq 3
dimensions, the order parameter
\phi
couples to the velocity field
{\bf v}
of the solvent. The resulting stochastic coupled dynamics for
\phi
and
\bf v
goes by the name of Model H
59
;
39
;
42
;
22
. It reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\text{\bf v}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi=

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\mu+\sqrt{2T}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\xi}

(136)

\displaystyle\rho\,(\partial_{t}\text{\bf v}+\text{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\text{\bf v})=

\displaystyle\eta\nabla^{2}\text{\bf v}-\phi\bm{\nabla}\mu

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}P+\sqrt{2T\eta}\,\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\Sigma}

(137)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\text{\bf v}=

\displaystyle 0\;,

(138)

with
\mu=\delta F/\delta\phi
the chemical potential,
\rho
and
P
the density and pressure of the fluid, and
\eta
its viscosity (sending
\eta
to infinity brings us back to model B). Denoting again
\text{\bf x}=(\text{\bf r},z)
, the Gaussian white noises have correlations

\displaystyle\left<\xi_{i}(\text{\bf x},t)\xi_{j}(\text{\bf x},t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}\delta^{(d)}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(139)

\displaystyle\left<\Sigma_{ij}(\text{\bf x},t)\Sigma_{kl}(\text{\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{jk}\delta_{il})\times

\displaystyle\delta^{(d)}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;.

(140)

In the limit of low Reynolds number, one neglects the left hand side of Eq. (
137
). Using the incompressibility condition (
138
), one can solve the Stokes equation for
v
as a convolution:

v_{i}=-\mathrm{T}_{ij}*(\phi\,\partial_{j}\mu-\sqrt{2T\eta}\,\partial_{k}\Sigma_{jk})\;,

(141)

where
{\mathrm{T}}_{ij}
is the Oseen tensor, whose Fourier components read

T_{ij}(\text{\bf k})=\frac{1}{\eta\text{\bf k}^{2}}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{\text{\bf k}^{2}}\right)\;,

(142)

where
k
is the Fourier wavevector corresponding to the full space
\text{\bf x}=(\text{\bf r},z)
.
Then, upon eliminating the velocity field, the effective dynamics of the order parameter is given by

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\phi=&\nabla^{2}\mu+\sqrt{2T}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\xi}\\
&+\partial_{i}\phi(\text{\bf x})\int_{\text{\bf x}^{\prime}}\mathrm{T}_{ij}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\phi(\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\partial_{j}\mu(\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\\
&-\sqrt{2T\eta}\,\partial_{i}\phi(\text{\bf x})\int_{\text{\bf x}^{\prime}}\mathrm{T}_{ij}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\partial_{k}\Sigma_{jk}(\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\;.\end{split}

(143)

The interface dynamics in model H was previously studied in
45
;
57
;
58
;
77
;
80
;
96
;
12
, where the dispersion relation was derived using a variety of approaches. Here, we not only re-derive it consistently, but our approach also allows us to derive the noise term explicitly.

The dynamical action takes the form

\begin{split}\mathrm{S}[\bar{\phi}&,\phi]=\int_{t,\text{\bf x},\text{\bf x}^{\prime}}\Big\{\bar{\phi}(\text{\bf x},t)\Big[\delta(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})(\partial_{t}\phi-\nabla^{2}\mu)(\text{\bf x},t)\\
&+\partial_{i}\phi(\text{\bf x},t)\mathrm{T}_{ij}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\partial_{j}\phi(\text{\bf x}^{\prime},t)\mu(\text{\bf x}^{\prime},t)\Big]\\
&+T\delta(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\bar{\phi}(\text{\bf x},t)\nabla^{2}\bar{\phi}(\text{\bf x},t)\\
&-T\bar{\phi}(\text{\bf x},t)\partial_{i}\phi(\text{\bf x},t)\mathrm{T}_{ij}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})\partial_{j}\phi(\text{\bf x}^{\prime},t)\bar{\phi}(\text{\bf x}^{\prime},t)\Big\}\;,\end{split}

(144)

where we have used two properties of the Oseen tensor, namely
\partial_{i}\mathrm{T}_{ij}(\text{\bf x}-\text{\bf x}^{\prime})=0
and
\mathrm{T}_{ij}(\text{\bf k})\mathrm{T}_{jl}(-\text{\bf k})=\frac{1}{\eta\text{\bf k}^{2}}\mathrm{T}_{il}(\text{\bf k})
.

Defining
h(\text{\bf r},t)
and
\chi(\text{\bf r},z,t)
by Eqs. (
16
) and (
7
) and going to Fourier space in
r
and
t
, we obtain the following quadratic action:

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q},z,z^{\prime}}\Big\{\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}(z)\,\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}(z,z^{\prime})\bar{\chi}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\bar{\chi}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}(z)\Big[i\omega\delta(z-z^{\prime})-\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}(z,z^{\prime})(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{0}(z^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad\times(-h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\partial_{z}m_{c}(z^{\prime})+\chi^{\perp}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}(z^{\prime}))\Big]\Big\}\;,

(145)

with

\begin{split}\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}(z,z^{\prime})=&\delta(z-z^{\prime})(\partial_{z}^{\prime 2}-\text{\bf q}^{2})\\
&-\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)\mathrm{T}_{zz}(\text{\bf q},z-z^{\prime})\partial_{z}m_{c}(z^{\prime})\;.\end{split}

(146)

The operator that determines the behavior of the interface is
-\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{0})
: its smallest eigenvalue controls the dispersion relation. Using the explicit form of
\mathrm{T}_{zz}(\text{\bf q},z-z^{\prime})

96

\mathrm{T}_{zz}(\text{\bf q},z-z^{\prime})=\frac{1}{4\eta q}\left(1+q|z-z^{\prime}|\right)\text{e}^{-q|z-z^{\prime}|}\;,

(147)

the leading term in
\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}(z,z^{\prime})
at large wavelengths is
-\frac{1}{4\eta q}\partial_{z}m_{c}(z)\partial_{z}m_{c}(z^{\prime})
.
It is tempting to proceed in the same vein as before in Eq. (
126
) and to consider the
\text{\bf q}^{2}
term in
-\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{0})
as a perturbation of
-\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}\Omega_{0}
.
In
96
, Shinozaki warns that this procedure, which works well for model B, seems to be numerically challenged in model H, as he observes that the branch continuing the ground state of
-\mathrm{K}_{\text{\bf q}}\Omega_{0}
is crossed by the continuous spectrum at some wavevector that is a decreasing function of the viscosity.
For a large viscosity, the ground-state branch appears to be the lowest, and the ground-state eigenvalue controls the interface dynamics.
In this regime we find the eigenvalue to be
\frac{\sigma q}{4\eta}
to lowest order in
q
(see App.
E
for details). Introducing
\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}=(i\omega+\frac{\sigma q}{4\eta})^{-1}\tilde{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}
, the action for the interface height reads

\displaystyle S_{h}[\bar{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\omega,\text{\bf q}}

\displaystyle\Big[\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\Big(i\omega+\frac{\sigma q}{4\eta}\big)h_{\text{\bf q},\omega}

(148)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\eta q}\bar{h}_{\text{\bf q},\omega}\bar{h}_{-\text{\bf q},-\omega}\Big]\;,

which leads to a dispersion relation
i\omega\sim q
. Shinozaki argues that the correction to the
q
behavior goes as
q^{3}

96
;
95
. At small viscosity however, the continuous spectrum interferes with the ground state and no analytical prediction seems to be available.

VI
Out of equilibrium

We now turn to studying how nonequilibrium driving impacts interface dynamics, and begin with one the simplest instances of a nonequilibrium field theory, namely active model A
15
;
74
.

VI.1
Active model A

VI.1.1
Deriving the Edwards-Wilkinson equation

The dynamics of active model A (AMA) is defined by Eq. (
1
) with a nonequilibrium drive
w[\phi]=-\lambda(\bm{\nabla}\phi)^{2}
, leading to:

\partial_{t}\phi=-\frac{\delta F}{\delta\phi}-\lambda(\bm{\nabla}\phi)^{2}+\sqrt{2T}\eta(\text{\bf r},z,t)\;,

(149)

where
F[\phi]
remains given by Eq. (
2
). Note that we keep the notation
T
for the amplitude of the noise, even though
T
is not, thermodynamically speaking, a temperature. The steady-state mean-field profile
m_{c}(z)
solves the new equation

0=\partial_{z}^{2}m_{c}-f^{\prime}(m_{c})-\lambda(\partial_{z}m_{c})^{2}\;.

(150)

We introduce the linear operator
\Omega_{\lambda}
defined by

\Omega_{\lambda}=-\partial_{z}^{2}+f^{\prime\prime}(m_{c})+2\lambda(\partial_{z}m_{c})\,\partial_{z}\;.

(151)

The additional contribution
2\lambda(\partial_{z}m_{c})\,\partial_{z}
with respect to
\Omega_{0}
in Eq. (
40
) renders
\Omega_{\lambda}
non Hermitian. Using
\Omega_{\lambda}
we can write the Janssen-De Dominicis action as

\displaystyle S=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{t,\text{\bf r},z}\Big\{\bar{\chi}(\partial_{t}-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}+\Omega_{\lambda})(-h\partial_{z}m_{c}+\chi^{\perp})-\bar{\chi}^{2}

\displaystyle+\bar{\chi}\Big[-(\partial_{t}h-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h)\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp}-(\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h)^{2}\left[\partial_{z}^{2}m_{c}-\lambda(\partial_{z}m_{c})^{2}\right]+2\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp}

\displaystyle+\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(m_{c})}{2}(\chi^{\perp})^{2}+\lambda(\bm{\nabla}\chi^{\perp})^{2}-2\lambda\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}\chi^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h\,\partial_{z}m_{c}

\displaystyle-(\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h)^{2}\left[\partial_{z}^{2}-2\lambda\partial_{z}m_{c}\partial_{z}\right]\chi^{\perp}-2\lambda\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}\chi^{\perp}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h\,\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp}

\displaystyle+\lambda(\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}h)^{2}(\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp})^{2}+f^{\prime}(m_{c}+\chi^{\perp})-f^{\prime}(m_{c})-f^{\prime\prime}(m_{c})\chi^{\perp}-\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(m_{c})}{2}{\chi^{\perp}}^{2}\Big]\Big\}

\displaystyle-\int_{t,\text{\bf r}}\ln\left|\left<\partial_{z}m_{c},u_{0}\right>+{\left<\partial_{z}\chi^{\perp},u_{0}\right>}\right|\;.

(152)

The Gaussian part of this action, which controls the leading fluctuations, reads

\begin{split}S_{0}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{t,\text{\bf r},z}&\Big[\bar{\chi}[-(\partial_{t}h-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h)\partial_{z}m_{c}\\
&+(\partial_{t}-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}+\Omega_{\lambda})\chi^{\perp}]-\bar{\chi}^{2}\Big]\;.\end{split}

(153)

Comparing Eq. (
153
) with Eq. (
41
), we realize that the actions for model A and active model A are the same up to the replacement of
\Omega_{0}
with
\Omega_{\lambda}
. Introducing
\Gamma=\partial_{t}-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}+\Omega_{\lambda}
, and decomposing
\bar{\chi}^{\perp}
as in Eq. (
31
) then leads to

\displaystyle S_{0}=\frac{1}{T}

\displaystyle\int_{t,\text{\bf r}}\Big\{\bar{h}(\partial_{t}h-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h)\left<\partial_{z}m_{c},u_{0}\right>-\bar{h}^{2}\left<u_{0},u_{0}\right>

\displaystyle+\left<\bar{\chi}^{\perp},\Gamma\chi^{\perp}\right>-\left<\bar{\chi}^{\perp},\bar{\chi}^{\perp}\right>

\displaystyle-\bar{h}\left<u_{0},\Omega_{\lambda}\chi^{\perp}\right>+2\bar{h}\left<u_{0},\bar{\chi}^{\perp}\right>\Big\}\;.

(154)

where we have used
\langle\bar{\chi}^{\perp},\partial_{z}m_{c}\rangle=\left<\chi^{\perp},u_{0}\right>=0
and that
u_{0}(z)
is independent of
t,\text{\bf r}
.
As in model A, we have some freedom to choose
u_{0}
, which we shall use to cancel the terms coupling the bulk fluctuations to the interface deformation on the third line of Eq. (
154
). Canceling the second one could be achieved by choosing
u_{0}\propto\partial_{z}m_{c}
, but since
\Omega_{\lambda}
is not Hermitian,
\partial_{z}m_{c}
is not an eigenstate of
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
and the first term does not vanish. Instead, we choose to cancel the first term (which drives a deterministic contribution of bulk fluctuations to the evolution of the interface deformation) using for
u_{0}
an eigenvector of
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
. We now explain which one to pick.

We assume that the property that
0
is an isolated eigenvalue of
\Omega_{\lambda}
still holds when
\lambda\neq 0
(this is certainly true for
\lambda
not too large).
We introduce
\ell_{i}
and
r_{i}
the eigenvectors of
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
and
\Omega_{\lambda}
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, respectively. The vector
r_{0}=\partial_{z}m_{c}
is the ground state of
\Omega_{\lambda}
. In general, for
i\neq j
the orthogonality of
\ell_{i}
and
r_{j}
is not guaranteed, but
\ell_{0}
and
r_{0}

and

(155)

(156)

\Sigma=\left<\text{e}^{-2\lambda m_{c}}\partial_{z}m_{c},\text{e}^{-2\lambda m_{c}}\partial_{z}m_{c}\right>

At this stage, we note an important difference between the action

in Eq. (

The action for

can be directly read in Eq. (

\begin{split}S_{0,h}[\bar{h},h]=\frac{1}{T}&\int_{t,\text{\bf r}}\Big[\bar{h}(\partial_{t}-\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2})h-\frac{\Sigma}{\sigma_{A}^{2}}\bar{h}^{2}\Big]\;.\end{split}

(159)

The corresponding Langevin equation is

\partial_{t}h(\text{\bf r},t)=\bm{\nabla}_{\text{\bf r}}^{2}h(\text{\bf r},t)+\sqrt{\frac{2T\Sigma}{\sigma_{A}^{2}}}\tilde{\eta}(\text{\bf r},t)\;.

(160)

The linear interface dynamics in active model A still evolves according to an Edwards-Wilkinson equation, as for model A, albeit with a renormalized noise amplitude.

To interpret this renormalization, we first note that, in equilibrium, the interface dynamics must yield the same surface tension as that stemming from a purely static approach based,
e.g.
, on the free energy cost of small deformations. For active systems, it is known that, on the contrary, different definitions of surface tension that coincide in equilibrium take different values

;
103
;
108
;
20
;
109
;

Equation (

T

\sigma_{A}^{2}/\Sigma
. Alternatively, one can interpret this dynamics as stemming from a surface tension
\sigma_{A}

.

As in Sec.

, our derivation above shows that there is a specific protocol that allows deriving the linearized interface dynamics for active model A using the simplified ansatz (

Indeed, injecting the exact decomposition

\Omega_{0}
replaced by
\Omega_{\lambda}
.
Projecting this equation onto
\ell_{0}
would have eliminated
\chi
, thereby yielding the linear dynamics of the interface.
This is exactly what would have been obtained starting from the ansatz of Eq. (
5
) and specifically projecting onto
\ell_{0}
: As in model A,
the simplified procedure works in active model A, except that one has to use
\ell_{0}
to eliminate the

\partial_{z}m_{c}
.

As a final remark, we note that the choice

echoes a similar one made by Kuramoto in the study of propagating fronts

\displaystyle\Omega_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\partial_{z}R(m_{c})=0

To first order in

A contribution from the second cumulant is

\displaystyle-\frac{4\lambda}{T^{2}}

It is instructive to probe the extent to which a KPZ term could arise from the ansatz of Eq. (

, the coefficient

VI.2.2

The mean-field steady-state profile of the active model B+ satisfies

\displaystyle S_{0}=\frac{1}{T}

\Gamma_{\text{\bf q},\omega}=i\omega+\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{\alpha})-2\zeta\text{\bf q}^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}m_{c}\;,

VII
Prospects

We have introduced a method, that is both well-controlled and versatile,
to derive stochastic interface dynamics between two stable phases starting from a field theory.
Instead of the popular shortcut assuming that the fluctuations of the order parameter can be described by a simple shift of the mean-field profile, we account for the coupling between interface deformations and bulk fluctuations.
We show that interface and bulk fluctuations are of the same order in
\sqrt{T}
, so that neglecting the latter is not justified
a priori
.
To obtain the effective dynamics of the interface, one then has to eliminate bulk fluctuations, which can be done in a dynamical-action formalism, by integrating them out, together with their associated response fields.
Our formalism first systematically recovers known equilibrium results, regardless of the presence of a conservation law or coupling to other fields. In addition, we derive by the same token both the relaxation rate and the noise amplitude. Providing a derivation for the noise in addition to the dispersion relation is a significant improvement, as both quantities are required to define the capillary surface tension, even in equilibrium.

Bulk fluctuations generically relax faster than the interface. It may therefore come as a surprise that a naive adiabatic approximation may lead to inconsistent results. This is because, by working at fixed interface deformation, bulk fluctuations do relax faster, but not to zero. Instead they relax to a constrained interface-dependent expression. No guidelines had been established for the use of this shortcut in locally out-of-equilibrium systems, hence the necessity for a well-controlled approach.

Upon adapting the definition of the interface to the new properties of the system, the formalism we introduced consistently applies out of equilibrium, such as to active versions of models A and B. For active model B+, we provide a consistent derivation of the results of
30
, previously obtained with the simplified ansatz. The method we propose is to our knowledge the first well-controlled derivation of interface dynamics in an active system.
We emphasize that the use of the shortcut is dangerous if no prior proof exists, which establishes the applicability of the ansatz in a given model together with the specific path to follow.

Another important contribution of the path-integral formulation is that it allows to systematically derive nonlinear orders in the low-temperature limit, which have attracted a lot of interest in the active community
6
;
104
;
16
;
72
.
In passive model A, we immediately recover the expression for the fluctuation-induced drift (and its divergence with the microscopic cutoff) in the case of an asymmetric potential with degenerate minima, as well as the curvature contribution.
For active model A, we also identify a fluctuation-induced velocity and KPZ nonlinearities, which are already at the bare level. All these predictions are out of reach from the simplified ansatz.
An important next step will be to derive the bare KPZ-like terms of active model B+ and to discuss whether they renormalize to the proposed ‘
|\text{\bf q}|
KPZ’ nonlinearity.

Beyond the models that we have considered in this article, it would be interesting to investigate polar or nematic systems described by Landau-Lifschitz-Gilbert-like models
70
. We speculate that their analysis will bring us back to theories similar to models C and D.
Activity is one important way to drive a system out of equilibrium.
However, interfacial dynamics arise in a variety of other nonequilibrium systems.
For instance, in sheared phase-separated systems, we expect to recover the predictions of
12
to linear order, but nonlinear corrections might differ from the ones they predicted using the simplified ansatz.
In a similar vein, it would be interesting to put our formalism to work in the presence of propagating pushed fronts into unstable phases, a topic that has attracted attention recently
9
. Finally, equilibrium systems with quenched disorder have been studied using the simplified ansatz in
17
. There, it is simply not known which predictions are affected by accounting for all fluctuations in the system, especially when the relaxation of bulk deformations are hindered by the disorder.

C.1

\Omega_{0}
when using the potential

from Eq. (

\begin{split}\mathrm{H}_{\text{\bf q}}\partial_{z}m_{c}=&\frac{A(q)}{2\sigma q^{3}}\partial_{z}m_{c}+\frac{1}{q^{2}+\lambda_{1}}\left<e_{1},\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\partial_{z}m_{c}\right>e_{1}\\

(208)

For the first two eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}=0

\displaystyle e_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\partial_{z}m_{c}

(209)

\displaystyle e_{1}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{2}}}\frac{\text{sinh}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{2}}z\right)}{\text{cosh}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{2}}z\right)^{2}}\;.

In the continuum part of the spectrum we have
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}=\tau(2+k^{2})

34

78

\begin{split}\tilde{e}_{k}(z)=\frac{e^{i\,k\sqrt{\tau}z}}{N_{k}}\Big[&2k^{2}+1-3\tanh\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\tau}{2}}z\Big)^{2}\\

With periodic boundary conditions between

and
L

\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2q}\frac{1}{1-e^{-2qL}}\left(e^{-2qL}e^{q|z-z^{\prime}|}+e^{-q|z-z^{\prime}|}\right)\;.

(212)

We are interested in the

\left<f,\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}g\right>

In the main text, we introduced

as

\Omega_{0}
that appear. The first two eigenfunctions

and

For scalar products involving the

Scalar products involving the well-behaved part and another exponentially decreasing function can easily be upper bounded,

\displaystyle\Big|2q\langle\tilde{e}_{k}^{wb}

\displaystyle,\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}e_{0}\rangle\Big|\leq\frac{1}{1-e^{-2qL}}\times

\displaystyle 2q\left<\tilde{e}_{k}^{c},\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}e_{1}\right>

\displaystyle 2q\left<\tilde{e}_{k}^{c},\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}\tilde{e}_{l}^{wb}\right>

C.2
Intuition for next order correction

With the expansion above, we obtain the leading order in
q
of the dispersion relation:

i\omega=\frac{2\sigma q^{3}}{A(0)}

(230)

For higher orders in
q
, the eigenvectors other than
\partial_{z}m_{c}
start playing a role. We can however provide the reader with some intuition on the behavior of the dispersion relation to the next order. We begin by identifying the key operator
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{0})
, for a generic double-well potential
f
. We denote its eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(q)
and the corresponding right and left eigenvectors
R_{i}(q)
and
L_{i}(q)
respectively. Upon integrating over
\omega
, the leading contribution to
(\Gamma^{\dagger}_{\text{\bf q},\omega})^{-1}
,
i.e.
the slowest mode that controls the interface relaxation, comes from the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}(q)
whose real part is closest to zero. This eigenvalue can be expressed as

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}(q)&=q^{2}\frac{\left<L_{0}(0),\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}R_{0}(q)\right>}{\left<L_{0}(0),R_{0}(q)\right>}\\
&=2q^{3}\frac{\int_{z}e_{0}(z)\,R_{0}(q)(z)}{\int_{z,z^{\prime}}e_{0}(z)\,\text{e}^{-q|z-z^{\prime}|}R_{0}(q)(z^{\prime})}\;,\end{split}

(231)

where
R_{0}(q)(z)
is the right eigenvector of the operator
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{0})
for the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}(q)
and
L_{0}(0)=\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}^{-1}e_{0}
is the left eigenvector of the operator
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\Omega_{0}
for the eigenvalue
0
. The notation
R_{0}(q)
is admittedly a little bit misleading:
R_{0}(0)
is the eigenvector of
{\mathrm{L}}_{\text{\bf q}}\Omega_{0}
, and as such it still features a
q
dependence. Hence the
q
dependence in
R_{0}(q)
labels the added contribution of
q^{2}
in
{\mathrm{L}}_{\text{\bf q}}(\text{\bf q}^{2}+\Omega_{0})
. In general,
R_{0}(q)
is unknown, but Shinozaki and Oono
94
showed that
\lambda_{0}(q)
can be obtained by perturbing the zero eigenvalue of
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\Omega_{0}
, for which
R_{0}(0)=e_{0}
. Therefore in the
q\to 0
limit, the fraction (
231
) has a finite limit:
R_{0}(q)\to R_{0}(0)=e_{0}
and one recovers the relation
i\omega=\frac{2\sigma q^{3}}{A(0)}
. However for non-vanishing
q
, the study of the dispersion relation requires detailed knowledge of
R_{0}(q)
. Since the dispersion relation is obtained by perturbing
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}\Omega_{0}
by
q^{2}\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}
, it seems reasonable to assume that the correction to
R_{0}(0)
at low
q
is of order
q^{2}
. However, the presence of
q
in the operator
\mathrm{L}_{\text{\bf q}}=q^{2}-\partial_{z}^{2}
makes proving this statement analytically non-trivial and beyond the scope of this work. To proceed further, Shinozaki and Oono observed numerically that
R_{0}(q)(z)=e_{0}(z)+o(q)
. This strongly suggests that the dispersion relation is valid also to the next order:

i\omega=\frac{2\sigma q^{3}}{A(q)}(1+o(q))

(232)

Numerically, by direct simulation of the partial differential equation (
79
), we not only confirm the theoretical prediction for the leading order behavior, but we also find that the next-to-leading order is correctly given by expanding
A(q)
, in spite of us not having strong analytical support for this (see Fig.
2
).

This means that the expression obtained from the ansatz of Eq. (
5
) in
30
for
\lambda=\zeta=0
,
i\omega=\frac{2\sigma q^{3}}{A(q)}
also holds to order
q^{4}
. Beyond that order, we know from Eq. (
231
) that there are several
O(q^{5})
corrections to the dispersion relation that are not captured by
A(q)
.

Appendix D
Numerical methods

In this section we present the numerical scheme we used to obtain the relaxation of interfaces in models B and AMB. We integrate the stochastic PDEs (
79
), and (
172
) with
\zeta=0
, at zero temperature in a two-dimensional system, using a pseudo-spectral code with periodic boundary conditions and semi-implicit time-stepping. We use the symmetric
\phi^{4}
potential

f(\phi)=0.25\left(-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\phi^{4}\right)\;.

(233)

Using existing results on the generalized thermodynamics of motility-induced phase separation
98
;
97
, we can numerically solve for the binodals at different values of
\lambda
.
We then use the binodal values to construct the initial condition, with two bands of equal sizes for the dense and dilute phases. We then let this singular profile relax to obtain the steady-state solution. From the resulting profile
m_{c}
, we compute the quantities that appear in the theoretical prediction for the relaxation time,
\sigma_{A}
and
A_{A}(q)
. (In the passive case, we compute them from the explicit expression of the mean-field profile (
39
)).

Starting from the steady state, we then perturb one of the interfaces by a cosine and track its relaxation for different excitation wavelengths.
For this purpose, we use the local definition for the interface height given by Eq. (
4
), which is acceptable in the absence of noise.
We take
\phi_{0}
to be the average of the two bulk densities, and identify the interface by linear interpolation.

We simulated systems of size
L_{r}=L_{z}=256
for model B and
L_{r}=L_{z}=512
for active model B, with a spatial discretization of
1
in both directions. With these parameters and a
3/2
anti-aliasing procedure, we have about fifteen points to resolve the interface. Simulations were run for
\lambda=0,1,2
with a timestep of
0.04
for a total duration of
\Delta t_{\rm tot}(q_{1})=4.8e+6
for the mode
q_{1}=2\pi/256
, adapting the duration such that
q^{3}\Delta t_{\rm tot}(q)
remains constant for the various excited modes.

The theory predicts that, in the linear regime,
i.e.
for small enough initial perturbations, the interface relaxes as
\sim\text{exp}[-t/\tau(q)]
, with a relaxation time
\tau(q)
depending on the excited mode. In Fig.
4
, the amplitudes (the moduli) of the excited Fourier modes of a model B interface are shown as functions of
q^{3}t
on a logarithmic scale, confirming that the relaxation is exponential.
We extract the numerical value for
\tau(q)
by fitting the decay by an exponential in a time range where the instantaneous relaxation time is roughly constant.

Relaxation of a one-dimensional interface in a two-dimensional model B, starting from an initial condition
\frac{0.1}{q}\cos(qr)
, as a function of
q^{3}t
. The dots correspond to the measured Fourier amplitudes of the initially excited modes. The lines, in good agreement with the measurements, are the exponential fits.
