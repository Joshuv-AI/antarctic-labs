CD1D knockout swine are permissive to African swine fever virus genotype II infection
Authors: Daniel Madden, Jessie D. Trujillo, Shanmugasundaram Elango, Isaac Fitz, Chester D. McDowell, Patricia Assato, Eulim Lyoo, Taeyong Kwon, Konner Cool, Yonghai Li, Franco Matias Ferreyra, Natasha N. Gaudreault, Igor Morozov, Kiho Lee, John Driver, Juergen A. Richt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAfrican swine fever virus (ASFV) is an important pathogen of domestic and wild suids and the causative agent of African swine fever (ASF). ASFV displays a tropism for myeloid cells, predominately of the monocyte/macrophage lineage, which is critical for ASFV pathogenesis. However, the mechanisms that govern the cellular tropism of ASFV are complex and incompletely understood. ASFV can enter susceptible cells through several mechanisms. One way is by clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) via interaction with the host glycoprotein CD1d, encoded by the CD1D gene, and disruption of CD1D expression significantly reduces ASFV replication in cell culture. In order to evaluate the role of CD1d in the replication and pathogenesis of ASF in vivo, CD1D knockout (KO) pigs, generated using a CRISPR-Cas9 system, were challenged with the highly virulent genotype II ASFV isolate MNG19. Notably, CD1D KO pigs were highly permissive to ASFV infection and developed severe acute ASF similar to age-matched wildtype (WT) controls. No significant differences in disease severity or mortality between ASFV-infected WT and KO pigs were observed. ASFV DNA levels in blood or in the majority of visceral and lymphoid tissues were also not significantly different between ASFV-infected wild-type and KO pigs. In addition, pathological changes were similar between both groups and typical of acute ASF, such as fibrinous polyserositis, haemorrhagic lymphadenopathy, and systemic coagulopathy. This work demonstrates that porcine CD1d is not critical for genotype II ASFV replication and virulence in vivo, and that CD1D KO piglets possess no resistance to virulent genotype II ASFV infection.

Introduction

African swine fever virus (ASFV), the causative agent of African swine fever (ASF), is one of the most significant and devastating viral pathogens of swine globally. Domestic pigs and the Eurasian wild boar are highly susceptible to ASFV infections, and outbreaks of ASF are associated with high mortality rates in affected swine herds and severe economic losses [1]. Progress towards the development of a safe and efficacious ASFV vaccine has been hindered by a variety of factors including the biological complexity of the virus and an incomplete understanding of protective immunity against ASFV [2]. With the exception of two modified live virus (MLV) preparations licenced for use in Vietnam, which have raised concerns regarding safety and efficacy, no vaccines are commercially available for ASFV [3,4].

ASFV possesses a tropism for cells of the monocyte/macrophage lineage, and this is an important contributor to viral pathogenesis [5,6]. Infection of these cells promotes their activation and stimulates a massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNFα which are critical mediators of clinical ASF [5–9]. While the mechanisms governing this tropism are not completely understood, ASFV has been shown to enter susceptible cells through both receptor-mediated and receptor-independent mechanisms. Clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) is commonly exploited by viruses for receptor-mediated entry into cells, and ASFV internalization by CME has been described in multiple studies [10–13]. However, to date, no specific cellular receptor has been identified as essential for ASFV internalization, though specific macrophage cellular factors, including CD163, dynamin, clathrin, EGFR, actin, sodium/proton exchanger (Na/H), PKC, PI3 K, phosphorylated PI3 K, Pak1, tyrosine kinase, microtubules, Rab7, CD45, CD203a, MHC II, and activation of Rho-GTPase Rac1, have been shown to be involved in virion adsorption and internalization [14,15]. ASFV can also infect cells through receptor-independent macropinocytosis, a process routinely performed by myeloid-derived antigen presenting cells (APCs) as part of their normal biological functions in immune surveillance [11,16]. Phagocytosis of apoptotic cellular debris containing ASFV virions can also cause productive infection of cells, and ASFV utilizes apoptotic mimicry to facilitate uptake by macrophages [17,18]. Reports of antibody-mediated enhancement of disease in ASFV vaccine studies support Fc receptor-mediated uptake of antibody-coated ASFV virions as another potential pathway for ASFV entry, though investigational studies providing evidence of this entry mechanism are limited [19–21].

The glycoprotein CD1d is a nonclassical MHC protein encoded by the CD1D gene that is expressed on the surface of APCs and presents lipid antigens to natural killer T (NKT) cells and gamma delta T cells [22]. Recent research has demonstrated that CD1d facilitates ASFV entry into MA104 cells and primary porcine macrophages by CME through its interaction with the viral protein p72 and host protein EPS15, and that silencing of cellular CD1d expression or knockout (KO) of the CD1D gene significantly reduces replication of a genotype II HLJ/18 ASFV in vitro [23]. These observations lead to the hypothesis that CD1d could be an important host factor for ASFV replication and pathogenesis in vivo. To test this hypothesis, CD1D knockout (KO) pigs, generated using a CRISPR/Cas9 system, and wildtype (WT) control piglets were infected with the highly virulent genotype II ASFV strain MNG19 to compare disease progression and virus replication between the two groups. This study is the first to evaluate the role of host CD1d in ASFV replication and pathogenesis in vivo.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

The virulent genotype II ASFV strain MNG19 was isolated from an infected pig during the 2019 ASFV outbreak in Mongolia, and its pathogenesis in domestic swine has been previously investigated [24,25]. MNG19 was propagated and titrated on primary porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) obtained by bronchoalveolar lavage from healthy pigs as previously described [26,27]. PAMs were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Atlanta Biologicals, Flowery Branch, GA, USA), 1x MEM nonessential amino acids (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), and 1x antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco) and incubated in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 atmosphere. Virus stocks were titrated by hemadsorption (HAD) on 96-well plates, quantified as HAD50/mL, and stored at −80 °C until use. All laboratory work involving the handling of infectious ASFV was carried out at the Biosecurity Research Institute at Kansas State University under BSL-3 conditions.

Generation of CD1D knockout pigs

The creation and characterization of the CD1D KO pigt line (RRID: NSRRC 0038) used for this study has been previously described [28]. Briefly, in vitro transcribed mRNA encoding a CRISPR-Cas9 vector construct with a gRNA sequence targeting the porcine CD1D primary transcript was injected into zygotes created by in vitro fertilization using gametes derived from WT commercial outbred pigs. Zygotes were cultured for five days, followed by embryo transfer to surrogate sows for gestation. Embryos and offspring were genotyped using CD1D-specific PCR followed by Sanger DNA sequencing. Heterozygous offspring were crossbred to create homozygous founder pigs possessing a 1,599 base pair (bp) deletion in the CD1D gene, including removal of the start codon in exon 3 and deletions of exons 4 and 5 [28]. The circulating leukocytes of previous CD1D KO animals possessing this specific genotype have been characterized by flow cytometry, which demonstrated that CD1D KO pigs fail to develop NKT cells, while other immune cell populations, including conventional, regulatory, and γδ T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, monocytes, and granulocytes, do not differ from WT pigs [29]. Founder pigs were bred to produce homozygous CD1D KO piglets used for the study. All pigs were confirmed to be homozygous for the CD1D deletion by gel-based PCR (Supplementary Figure 1).

Animal experiment

Ten 5-week-old CD1D KO piglets obtained from the National Swine Resource and Research Center at the University of Missouri and six 5-week-old conventional outbred WT piglets sourced from Midwest Research Swine (Glencoe, MN, USA) were used for MNG19 infection (Figure 1). Baseline samples, including EDTA and heparinized blood, serum, and oropharyngeal (OP) swabs, were obtained the day before ASFV challenge. On the day of challenge, two CD1D KO piglets were randomly selected and euthanized to serve as negative controls for baseline postmortem evaluation and sample collection; the remaining animals were infected with 180 HAD50 ASFV MNG19 administered by intramuscular injection. Animals were evaluated for clinical symptoms and rectal temperature twice daily and were assigned clinical scores using standardized criteria, which have been previously described [25, 27]; moribund animals meeting clinical criteria for humane euthanasia were euthanized by intravenous pentobarbital administration. EDTA blood and oropharyngeal (OP) swabs were scheduled for collection from animals at –1, 1, 3 , 5 , 7 , 10 , and 14 days post-challenge (DPC), while serum samples were scheduled for collection at –1, 5, 7, 10, and 14 DPC. Necropsy, gross pathological evaluation, and postmortem tissue collection were performed at the time of death/euthanasia, with any animals surviving ASFV infection scheduled to be necropsied at 14 DPC. Gross and histological pathology

Necropsy and gross pathological evaluation of infected animals were performed by veterinary pathologists with prior experience working with necropsies and tissues of ASFV-infected pigs. Gross pathological evaluation and lesion scoring were performed as previously described [21,25]. Gross lesions for each animal were quantitatively assessed systematically by organ system including body condition (0–3), integument (0–3), cardiovascular (0–6), liver (0–6), lung (0–12), kidney (0–3), spleen (0–6), and gastrointestinal (GI) tract (0–11). Additional components of the lymphoid system that were scored included the tonsil (0–6), mandibular lymph node (ManLN) (0–6), prescapular lymph node (SCLN) (0–6), cranial mediastinal lymph node (CMLN) (0–6), renal lymph node (RLN) (0–6), gastrohepatic lymph node (GHLN) (0–6), ileocecal lymph node (ICLN) (0–6), and mesenteric lymph node (MesLN) (0–6). Base scores included 0 as indicating no lesions, with increasing scores reflecting increasing lesion severity. Specific criteria for tissue lesion scoring and results for individual animals are listed in Supplementary File 1. Total gross pathology scores for each animal were calculated as the sum of scores for every tissue. Disease severity for each animal was classified based on total gross lesion scores as mild (<20), moderate (21–47), or severe (>47).

Tissues collected at necropsy were processed for routine histopathology using standard methodology following a validated tissue fixation procedure for ASFV inactivation. Histological evaluation was performed blindly by a veterinary pathologist. Histological lesions were scored quantitatively using previously described methodology, with a score of 0 indicating no lesion and higher numbers indicating increasing lesion severity [21,25]. Tissues collected, lesion categories assessed for each tissue, and scoring results for all animals are listed in Supplementary File 2. Total histological scores for each animal were calculated as the sum of all individual tissue lesion scores and were classified as mild (0–30), moderate (31–60), severe (61–80), and marked (>80).

Viral DNA extractions and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Antemortem samples collected from pigs included EDTA blood, heparinized blood, serum, and OP swabs. White blood cells were isolated from heparinized blood and stored at –80 °C. To generate lysates from blood and swab samples for DNA purification, blood or swab media were mixed 1:1 with lysis Buffer AL and heat inactivated for 10 min at 70 oC prior to processing for DNA extraction. Postmortem samples collected for quantitative qPCR of viral DNA loads included lung, liver, heart, kidney, pooled GI tissue, faeces, bone marrow (BM), spleen, tonsil, ManLN, SCLN, CMLN, GHLN, MesLN, RLN, and ICLN. For postmortem samples, a 10% tissue homogenate was made in Buffer ATL containing 40 µl of proteinase K using a 5 mm stainless steel bead. Tissues were homogenized on the QIAGEN LT homogenizer (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) at 30 hertz for 30 s intervals, followed by digestion at 56 oC for one hour. Clarified homogenates were then diluted 1:1 in Buffer AL and heat inactivated for 10 min at 70 oC prior to processing for DNA extraction.

DNA was purified from lysates using an automated magnetic bead-based system (Taco-Mini, Genereach, Taichung City, Taiwan) and reagents for total nucleic acid extraction (taco™ DNA/RNA Extraction Kit; Genereach), with minor modifications. To detect and quantify ASFV DNA, TaqMan probe-based qPCR targeting the viral B646L (p72) gene was performed using primer and probe sequences developed by Zsak et al., with minor modifications as previously described [21,25,30]. Briefly, qPCR was performed with PerfeCTA FastMix II (QuantiBio, Beverly, MA, USA) in 20 µL reaction volumes using 5 µL of extracted DNA per reaction. Thermocycling conditions included an initial 95 °C denaturation for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Reactions were performed using a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and data analysis was performed using the Bio-Rad CFX Manager (Version 2.3) software. A standard curve based on serial dilutions of plasmid encoding the B646L target sequence from genotype II ASFV strain Armenia 2007 was used to calculate the concentration of ASFV DNA within samples as p72 copy number per mL (CN/mL). This plasmid was also used for extraction and PCR positive controls.

Statistical analysis and images

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Normality of individual datasets was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Analyses of post-challenge temperature, blood qPCR, and OP swab qPCR results were performed using multiple Welch’s t tests with correction for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Šídák method, while clinical scores were compared using multiple Mann Whitney tests with correction for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Šídák method. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were compared using the Mantel–Cox test. Tissue qPCR values, gross lesion scores, and histological lesion scores were compared using the Mann–Whitney test for non-normal datasets or Welch’s t test for normal datasets. All graphs were created using GraphPad Prism v9.5.1, with error bars indicating the standard error of the mean where applicable. The diagram of the planned timeline for the animal experiment was created using BioRender (Toronto, Canada). Gross pathological images were attained using a Canon G7X mark II camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan) in a waterproof case. Histological images were captured using a BX46 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with a DP 28 digital camera (Olympus) and cellSens standard software (Olympus).

Results

Post-infection clinical progression

Following ASFV infection, all animals in both the WT and CD1D KO groups developed clinical illness consistent with acute ASF that rapidly progressed to death or humane euthanasia endpoints (Figure 2). CD1D KO pigs had slightly higher baseline body temperatures than WT pigs; however, these temperatures are within normal limits for 5-week-old pigs (Figure 2A). CD1D KO pigs became febrile 24 h earlier than WT pigs, and body temperature for the CD1D KO group was significantly higher than the WT group at 3 DPC (Figure 2A). No other significant differences in body temperature were observed, with animals in both groups showing declining body temperatures at 6 DPC consistent with advancing ASF hypothermia (Figure 2A). Clinical scores began to rise above baseline at 3 DPC for CD1D KO pigs and 4 DPC for WT pigs; however, this difference was not significant, and clinical scores were not significantly different between groups at any time post-infection (Figure 2B). Animals in both groups began to reach humane endpoints at 5 DPC, and all animals were humanely euthanized or found dead by 6 DPC. Viral DNA levels in blood, swabs, and tissues

EDTA blood and OP swabs for quantification of ASFV DNA by qPCR were collected pre-challenge and at 1, 3 and 5 DPC. ASFV DNA in blood was detected as early as 1 DPC in 3/8 CD1D KO pigs and 1/6 WT pigs, and by 3 DPC all pigs in both groups were positive for ASFV DNA in blood, with maximum qPCR values occurring at 5 DPC (Supplementary File 3). No significant differences in blood ASFV DNA levels between WT and CD1D KO groups were observed at any time post-infection (Figure 3A). OP swabs became positive for ASFV DNA beginning at 3 DPC for 7/8 CD1D KO pigs and 4/6 WT pigs, and swabs from all animals in both groups were positive at 5 DPC (Supplementary File 3). ASFV DNA levels in OP swabs were not significantly different between groups at any timepoint (Figure 3B).

ASFV DNA was detected in all postmortem samples evaluated. While mean ASFV DNA levels in most postmortem samples tended to be slightly higher for the CD1D KO group compared to WT controls, a few significant differences were noted (Figure 4, Figure 5). Levels of ASFV DNA were significantly higher in the pooled GI tissue of CD1D KO pigs compared to WT controls, but no other significant differences were found for any other non-lymphoid postmortem samples including liver, lung, heart, kidney, and faeces (Figure 4). For lymphoid tissues, CD1D KO pigs had significantly higher levels of ASFV DNA in the RLN and spleen, but no significant differences were found for tonsil, ManLN, SCLN, CMLN, GHLN, MesLN, and bone marrow (Figure 5). Gross pathology and histology

Gross necropsy findings for both groups were consistent with acute ASF resulting from virulent ASFV infection. Gross pathology lesion scoring for all tissues and animals is detailed in Supplementary File 1, and all animals had total lesion scores classified as moderate or severe. Decreased body condition was observed in all animals, and a majority in both groups displayed skin lesions including erythema and cyanosis of varying severity. All animals showed congestion and haemorrhage in lymphoid tissues that are typical of virulent ASFV. Pulmonary congestion, oedema, and consolidation were universally present, as were pathologies in liver and GI tissues. Congestion and epicarditis were found in the hearts of all pigs in both groups. Abdominal effusion was also consistently found. All animals had total gross lesion scores classified as moderate or severe, with 7/8 CD1D KO pigs and 5/6 WT pigs having severe gross lesion scores (Supplementary File 1). While gross cardiovascular lesion scores were significantly greater for CD1D KO pigs, no other significant differences were present in any organ system evaluated (Figure 6). Discussion

The ASFV major capsid protein p72 can directly interact with the porcine membrane glycoprotein CD1d to initiate clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) of extracellular virions, and the disruption of cellular CD1d expression significantly reduces in vitro ASFV replication in both primary porcine macrophages and Chlorocebus sabaeus renal epithelium-derived MA104 cells [23]. However, the relevance of CD1d for ASFV infection and disease progression in vivo has never been evaluated. Therefore, we infected CD1D KO pigs generated using a CRISPR-Cas9 approach with an epidemiologically relevant highly virulent ASFV genotype II isolate (MNG19) to assess whether disruption of CD1D in pigs resulted in any effects on virus replication, viral tissue distribution, and disease severity. Disruption of CD1D in these animals has been shown to inhibit development of NKT cells, immune cells which recognize glycolipid antigens presented by the CD1d MHC-like protein, while having minimal impact on other leukocyte populations. This makes these pigs well suited for dissecting the significance of CD1d-ASFV interactions in vivo [29].

Our results clearly demonstrate that host CD1d is not essential for ASFV invasion, replication and virulence in domestic pigs, and that pigs lacking CD1d are not resistant to lethal infection with ASFV. Following ASFV infection, the disease course was similar for WT and CD1D KO pigs. While CD1D KO pigs developed fever 24 h prior to WT pigs, overall disease progression was not different from WT-infected pigs, and no significant differences in clinical score or survival between groups were found at any time post-infection (Figure 2). Disruption of porcine CD1D had a minimal effect on ASFV replication in vivo. ASFV DNA levels in the blood and OP swabs of CD1D KO and WT pigs were not significantly different at any time post-challenge, indicating efficient virus replication within CD1D KO animals (Figure 3). Similarly, the majority of postmortem samples showed no significant difference in viral DNA levels between the two groups. While viral DNA levels were significantly higher in the GI pool, spleen, and renal lymph nodes of CD1D KO pigs, no significant differences were present in faeces or in samples from seven other lymphoid tissues and four other major organs (Figure 4, Figure 5). Consistent with clinical findings and qPCR results, gross and histological lesions found at necropsy were also largely comparable between both groups. Gross pathological lesions were more severe in cardiovascular tissue of CD1D KO animals, but no other organ systems or tissues evaluated showed a significant difference in gross lesion scores; and total gross lesion scores were similar between both groups (Figure 6, Figure 7A). Except for more pronounced pulmonary histological lesions in WT animals, no significant differences in histological lesions were found, and total histological scores were not significantly different between WT and CD1D KO animals (Figure 7B, Figure 8). All animals in both groups developed moderate to severe histopathological changes consistent with acute ASF (Supplementary File 1, Supplementary File 2). These results show that the disruption of the CD1D gene in domestic pigs has a minimal impact on ASFV invasion, replication, tissue distribution, and pathogenesis in vivo. We recognize that subtle differences in ASFV replication and pathogenesis within the CD1D KO pigs may not have been apparent due to the limited number of animals included in this study (eight CD1D KO pigs and six WT pigs), and different experimental conditions such as inoculation route, infectious dose, or infection with other ASFV strains or genotypes could have affected the outcomes we observed in this study. The data presented here highlight the complex biology of ASFV’s cellular tropism and cell entry. ASFV displays an in vivo tropism for myeloid-derived monocytes and macrophages, though it can also be detected in other cell types, including lymphocytes, megakaryocytes, and neutrophils; it is also capable of replicating in cell lines derived from Chlorocebus sabaeus (green monkey) kidney epithelial cells (5,26). A receptor-mediated entry mechanism exploiting host cell molecules expressed on both monocytes and macrophages would provide a simple explanation for the observed in vivo cellular tropism of ASFV; it is well established that ASFV can utilize CME, a process exploited by a variety of viruses for receptor-mediated entry to infected host cells [10–13,31]. However, to date, no specific cellular receptor for ASFV entry has been identified [14]. CD163, a marker specific for cells of the monocyte/macrophage lineage, which is most abundantly expressed on terminally differentiated macrophages and tissue dendritic cells, confers susceptibility to porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) and was previously hypothesized to be a receptor necessary for ASFV infection. This was based on experimental data showing increased susceptibility in monocytes/macrophages with higher levels of CD163 expression, as well as the ability of an anti-CD163 monoclonal antibody to inhibit ASFV binding and infection of PAMs in a dose-dependent manner [32–34]. However, CD163 KO pigs generated using a CRISPR-Cas9 system showed no resistance to infection with ASFV genotype II strain Georgia 2007/1, and virus replication in macrophage cultures derived from these animals showed no difference compared to WT primary cells, indicating that the CD163 protein by itself is not essential for ASFV replication [35]. Multiple ASFV proteins have previously been identified to function in virus binding to susceptible cells, including p12, p54, and p72 [36–39]. However, antibodies targeting these proteins are unable to completely neutralize ASFV in vitro, suggesting virus attachment and entry can occur through mechanisms independent of the interaction between these viral proteins and their respective cellular ligands [37–39].

The current consensus is that ASFV can utilize multiple distinct pathways for cell entry, including receptor-dependent CME as well as receptor-independent processes involving phagocytosis and macropinocytosis [13,14,16–18]. An important corollary that can be drawn from this model of ASFV entry is that, while receptor-mediated mechanisms may facilitate ASFV infection, they may not be an essential requirement. Furthermore, fundamental differences between in vitro and in vivo infection might indicate that receptor-independent entry mechanisms seem more important for ASFV replication in vivo. Both of these conclusions are compatible with previously described observations of CD1d facilitating ASFV entry and replication in vitro and the current data showing disruption of porcine CD1D has a minimal impact on ASFV invasion, replication and virulence in vivo. CD1D KO pigs fail to develop invariant NKT (iNKT) cells, and it is plausible this would impact cytokine production and cellular immune responses to ASFV infection. However, the detailed characterization of the immune responses of ASFV-infected CD1D KO pigs compared to ASFV-infected WT pigs was beyond the scope of this study. Follow-up research is needed to characterize immune responses to ASFV infection in CD1D KO pigs and assess whether the lack of NKT-mediated immune responses influences disease progression. It is also possible that specific ASFV entry mechanisms may have variable importance among different ASFV genotypes or strains. ASFV can modulate host immune responses through multiple mechanisms including suppression of type I interferon responses, apoptosis, and blocking NF-κB- mediated cytokine induction, and this ability is an important determinant of ASFV virulence [15]. Importantly, a significant amount of variability in genome content and virulence exists among high-, moderate-, and low-virulence or attenuated ASFV isolates, and ASFV studies should be interpreted in the context of the specific virus strains used for infection [5,40]. However, it is unknown whether this variability has an impact on the interaction between ASFV and host CD1d. Additionally, it is possible that highly virulent ASFVs, such as the strain used in this study, may induce death of infected animals before small differences in viral replication and tissue distribution could manifest at a detectable level, and that host CD1d may have greater importance during infection with low-virulence ASFV isolates. Consequently, additional challenge studies using different ASFV isolates, including strains of moderate and low virulence, are necessary to determine whether these results are specific for the highly virulent MNG19 strain or more broadly applicable to other ASFVs.

Overall, our study demonstrates that CD1d is not essential to the invasion, replication and virulence of a genotype II ASFV in vivo, and the lack of CD1d expression does not significantly alter the pathogenesis of ASFV infection in domestic pigs. Further studies with different ASFV isolates of other genotypes as well as moderate and low virulence are necessary to determine whether this phenomenon is specific to virulent genotype II ASFVs or is more broadly applicable.
