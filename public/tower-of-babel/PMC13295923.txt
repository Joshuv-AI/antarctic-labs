Immunoglobulin GM (γ marker) allotypes expressed on IgG1 as potential genetic markers of response to immune checkpoint blockade therapy
Authors: Janardan P Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe results of a recent comprehensive investigation—confirmed in multiple cohorts—show that patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, who favorably responded to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy, were enriched in IgG1-producing plasma cells. The clonally expanded cells in responders also showed upregulation of IGHG1, the gene encoding the IgG1 antibodies. IgG1 expresses four GM (γ marker) allotypes: GM 1/a, GM 2/x, GM 3/f and GM 17/z. There are well-established associations between these allotypes and IgG subclass concentrations. Additionally, these determinants influence autoantibody responses to tumor-associated antigens and contribute to the antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity of tumors. Based on these and other observations, I hypothesize that GM allotypes expressed on IgG1 could potentially serve as genetic markers of response to anti-PD-1 and other immune checkpoint blockade therapies characterized by the skewed expansion of IgG1 plasma cells.Keywords: Antibody, B cell, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor

Immunoglobulin GM allotypes are encoded by three closely linked genes—IGHG1, IGHG2, and IGHG3—on chromosome 14q32. They are localized on the constant region of γ1, γ2, and γ3 chains. So far, 18 serologically testable GM allotypes have been identified.1 2 There is strong—almost absolute—linkage disequilibrium between particular GM alleles within a racial/ethnic group; as a result, GM alleles are transmitted in groups—that is, haplotypes. Each major racial group has a distinct array of several GM haplotypes. GM allele frequencies vary widely among racial/ethnic groups. There are qualitative differences as well. For instance, GM 3/f is commonly not found in people of African descent or GM 6/c3 in those of European descent.

GM allotypes have been shown to be associated with susceptibility to many malignant diseases. They are also associated with antibody responses to several tumor-associated antigens.1 None of these associations, however, have been confirmed or refuted by genome-wide association studies (GWAS) because such studies do not evaluate GM allotypes. Exclusion of GM alleles from the GWAS may be attributable to the highly homologous nature of the GM allotype-encoding IGHG loci, and the technical difficulty in distinguishing the allelic determinants which may have originated by gene conversion.3 4

Strong associations between GM allotypes and IgG subclass concentrations have been reported by several studies. Most of these studies have been done in people of European descent. The following associations are fairly well established: GM 1/a with high and GM 3/f with low levels of IgG1; GM 23/n+ with high and GM 23/n− with low levels of IgG2; GM 5/b1 with high and GM 21/g with low levels of IgG3.5 The mechanisms underpinning these associations are not completely understood. It has been speculated that allotype-specific quantitative differences may be due to the varying efficiency of switch regions associated with particular GM alleles.5 6

An elegant series of experiments has firmly established that skewed clonal expansion of IgG1+plasma cells contributes to a favorable immune environment in responders to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).7 The antibodies generated by these plasma cells were directed against cancer/testis antigens. These antigen-specific humoral responses were linked to tumor-reactive T-cell responses, underscoring the coordinated effects of the two arms of the immune system on antitumor immunity. The IGHG1 gene, which encodes the IgG1 subclass, was the top upregulated gene in responders to this therapy. The non-responders were characterized by a variety of immunoglobulin isotypes (IgM, IgA, and IgD) and no clonal expansion of IgG1+plasma cells. High IGHG1 expression was associated with improved overall survival in patients treated with immunotherapy, but not in those treated with chemotherapy. Using multiple datasets from immunotherapy clinical trials, the authors have extended their findings in HCC to other cancers (eg, melanoma) and other forms of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, such as vascular endothelial growth factor A blockade.

Based on the established associations between GM allotypes and IgG subclass concentrations discussed above, I hypothesize that GM allotypes are genetic markers of response to ICB therapy and that IgG1 allotype GM 1/a contributes to a favorable clinical response and GM 3/f to an unfavorable response. These predictions are for patients of European descent only. Since the expression of GM alleles is racially associated/restricted,1 2 different GM alleles are likely to be associated with response to ICB therapy in other racial groups. This hypothesis could be tested by genotyping subjects from a large ICB therapy trial involving patients of European descent. GM 1/a allotype would be expected to be predominant in responders and GM 3/f in non-responders. For people of African descent, first the relationship between GM allotypes expressed exclusively in this group (eg, GM 6/c3) and IgG subclass concentration needs to be delineated in large-scale studies. The results of such studies are likely to lead to testable hypothesis concerning GM alleles and response to ICB therapy in this group. Although—because of extensive homology in the coding region—GM allotyping has not been adapted to high-throughput genotyping technology, validated TaqMan genotyping and direct DNA sequencing methods are available for GM genotyping.

Since skewing towards IgG1 is present at baseline, GM allotypes could also help in treatment stratification. Association between high IGHG1 expression and improved overall survival7 suggests that IGHG1-encoded GM allotypes could potentially serve as genetic markers of overall survival following ICB therapy.

It has been suggested that clonally expanded IgG1+plasma cells in responders to ICB therapy may have effector functions.7 Since the antibodies generated by these cells have been shown to be directed against cancer/testis antigen NY-ESO-1, one putative effector function that could account for their beneficial effect would be antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) of NY-ESO-1-expressing cancer cells. GM allotypes have been shown to be associated with antibody responses to several tumor-associated antigens, such as MUC1, EGFR, cyclin B1, cancer/testis antigen XAGE-1b,18,10 but no studies have been undertaken to determine their role in immunity to NY-ESO-1. It would be of interest to determine if GM allotypes are associated with humoral immunity to NY-ESO-1, and if they contribute to the ADCC of NY-ESO-1-expressing tumors, analogous to their contribution to the ADCC of MUC1-expressing, HER2-expressing, and GD2-expressing tumors.1

Conclusion

If the hypothesis presented here is confirmed by large-scale multiethnic studies, it would provide a much-needed genetic tool for treatment stratification as well as a genetic marker of overall survival following ICB therapy. It would also spawn studies aimed at delineating possible effector functions, such as ADCC and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), mediated by antitumor IgG1 antibodies expressing particular GM allotypes, which could lead to more effective—and possibly individualized—immunotherapeutic strategies. Finally, the results could be instrumental in the proper evaluation of ICB efficacy trials. Thus, some patients could be allotypically/naturally high responders to ICB therapy, while others could be low responders. This possibility should be considered and used as an effect modifier in data analyses, to circumvent its potential confounding effect on the evaluation of ICB efficacy trials.
