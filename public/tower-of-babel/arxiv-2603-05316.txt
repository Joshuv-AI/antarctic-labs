Dyson Brownian motion on a Jordan curve
Vladislav Guskov, Mingchang Liu, Fredrik Viklund

Abstract
Zabrodin recently proposed a generalization of Dyson Brownian motion to a setting where the particles are confined to a smooth Jordan curve in the plane. In this paper, we discuss a rigorous construction of such a process on a rectifiable Jordan curve and study some of its basic properties. Under further smoothness assumptions, we derive the associated Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation, prove convergence towards the stationary Coulomb gas distribution, study large deviations at low temperature, and derive the limiting mean-field McKean–Vlasov equation in the many-particle limit.

Introduction

1.1
Background

In an important 1962 paper
[
15
]
, Dyson introduced a model of “Brownian motion gas” now known as Dyson Brownian motion. Roughly speaking, this is a stochastic process that describes
N<\infty
repelling Brownian particles living either on the unit circle or on the real line, interacting pairwise via the logarithmic potential,

\displaystyle-\beta\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\log|z_{i}-z_{j}|,\quad\beta>0.

(1.1)

Here
\beta
is the inverse temperature parameter. Dyson’s process has received considerable attention in both physics and mathematics, in part because of links to random matrix theory. For example, on the real line it describes the dynamics of eigenvalues of a Hermitian random matrix whose entries evolve as independent Brownian motions. See, e.g.,
[
9
,
10
]
and the references therein.

Recently, Zabrodin
[
29
]
proposed a generalization of Dyson Brownian motion to a setting where the particles are confined to a general smooth Jordan curve
\Gamma
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. In this generality, one does not expect a direct link to random matrix dynamics but it was predicted in
[
29
]
that the stationary law of the process equals the density of a planar Coulomb gas confined to the curve. That is, the probability measure on
\Gamma^{N}=\Gamma\times\dots\times\Gamma
given by the density

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)N!}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{\beta/2},\qquad z_{i}\in\Gamma,\qquad\beta>0,

(1.2)

were
Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)
is the Coulomb gas partition function,

\displaystyle Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{N!}\int_{\Gamma^{N}}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{\beta/2}\prod_{i=1}^{N}|dz_{i}|.

(1.3)

This fact, which we prove in Theorem
1.12
, generalizes a similar property for classical Dyson Brownian motion on the unit circle and could, in a sense, be taken as a defining property of the process.

The purpose of this paper is to discuss a rigorous construction of Zabrodin’s process on a Jordan curve satisfying minimal regularity assumptions and to study some of its basic properties. In addition to being a rather natural object of (we feel) intrinsic interest, our motivation comes from recent work
[
28
,
23
,
24
,
12
]
on confined planar Coulomb gases and the distribution of Fekete points; the model we study here is the natural dynamical version of a Coulomb gas on the curve. We will treat Dyson Brownian motion on a Jordan arc in a forthcoming paper
[
19
]
.

1.2
Main results

Existence

Let
\Gamma
be a rectifiable Jordan curve. We shall always assume that
\Gamma
is a curve in
\mathbb{C}
. By Rademacher’s theorem,
\Gamma
admits a parametrization by arc-length and we write
(\gamma(s))_{s\in[0,l)}
for this parametrization, where
l=l(\Gamma)
is the length of
\Gamma
. It will be convenient to extend the domain of
\gamma
to
\mathbb{R}/l(\Gamma)\mathbb{Z}
, making
\gamma
an
l
-periodic function on the real line.
Let

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta,N}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod\limits_{i\neq j}|\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})|^{\beta/2},\quad x_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\beta>0,

(1.4)

where
Z_{\beta,N}
is as in (
1.3
) and we are suppressing the additional condition that
1\leq i,j\leq N
in the notation.

Definition 1.1

(Parametrization process)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a rectifiable Jordan curve and fix the inverse temperature parameter
\beta>0
. The parametrization process for Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
is a continuous strong Markov process
X=(X_{1}(t),\dots,X_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with diffusion matrix equal to the identity and drift given by the weak gradient of
\frac{1}{2}\log\rho_{\beta,N}
, where
\rho_{\beta,N}
is as in (
1.4
), and such that for all
t\geq 0
, almost surely,

X_{1}(t)\leq X_{2}(t)\leq\dots\leq X_{N}(t)\leq X_{1}(t)+l.

Remark 1.2
.

Let

\displaystyle D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{N}<x_{1}+l\}.

(1.5)

The ordering condition in Definition
1.1
can be stated as: the
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued process
X
never leaves the closure of
D
.

Remark 1.3
.

We sometimes write
X=X^{x}
to emphasize the starting point
x\in D
.

Given the parametrization process, we define Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
by transplanting using the arc-length parametrization.

Definition 1.4

(Dyson Brownian motion on a Jordan curve)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a rectifiable Jordan curve. Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
is the continuous strong Markov process
\boldsymbol{Z}=(\gamma(X_{1}),\dots,\gamma(X_{N}))
, taking values in
\Gamma^{N}
, where
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
is the parametrization process of Definition
1.1
.

It is not obvious a priori that a process as in Definition
1.4
exists. The following result gives existence in the non-colliding regime.

Theorem 1.5

(Existence for
\beta\geq 1
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a rectifiable Jordan curve. For any
\beta\geq 1
and any collection
\boldsymbol{z}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}
of points on
\Gamma
, ordered counterclockwise, such that
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
, there exists a parametrization process
X=(X_{1}(t),\dots,X_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, with
z_{i}=\gamma(X_{i}(0))
, for Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
.

This process is the unique strong solution of the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

dX(t)=dB(t)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho_{\beta,N}(X(t))dt,

where
B=(B_{1}(t),\ldots,B_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is
N
-dimensional standard Brownian motion. Moreover, for all
t\geq 0
, almost surely,

X_{1}(t)<X_{2}(t)<\dots<X_{N}(t)<X_{1}(t)+l.

Setting

\boldsymbol{Z}=(\gamma(X_{1}(t)),\dots,\gamma(X_{N}(t)))_{t\geq 0}

defines Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
.

Theorem
1.5
is proved in Section
2
.

Remark 1.6
.

The choice
\gamma(s)=e^{is}
reproduces the angle process
\theta=(\theta_{1}(t),\dots,\theta_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
of classical Dyson Brownian motion on the unit circle:

d\theta_{i}(t)=dB_{i}(t)+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\frac{1}{2}\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{i}(t)-\theta_{j}(t)}{2}\right)dt.

The choice
\gamma(s)=s
reproduces classical Dyson Brownian motion on the real line:

dX_{i}(t)=dB_{i}(t)+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\frac{1}{X_{i}(t)-X_{j}(t)}dt.

Remark 1.7
.

If
\gamma\in C^{2}
, we can apply Itô’s formula to
Z(t)=\gamma(X(t))
, to see that our definition agrees with Zabrodin’s process in
[
29
]
, see (
1.11
) below.

Remark 1.8
.

Applying a deterministic time-change
t\to\beta t/2
and using the scaling property of Brownian motion, one may equivalently consider the SDE

dX(t)=\sqrt{\kappa}dB(t)-\nabla V(X(t))dt,

where

\displaystyle V(x)=-\sum_{i<j}\log|\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})|,

(1.6)

in order to construct the parametrization process. The inverse temperature
\beta
and the diffusion parameter
\kappa
are related via

\kappa=\frac{2}{\beta}.

We shall use this parametrization when discussing large deviations below.

Remark 1.9
.

The existence of the parametrization process in the regime
0<\beta<1
is technically more difficult to establish as one has to take into account collisions between particles. That is, the process
X^{x}
hits the boundary
\partial D
(where
D
is as in (
1.5
)) in finite time and must reflect back into the domain
D
. Under the general assumption that the curve
\Gamma
is rectifiable, one has to solve an SDE with reflecting boundary conditions in the non-smooth domain
D
with a measurable drift which is singular at the boundary. This is an interesting problem but we will not address it here, see
[
18
]
for further discussion.

FPK equation and Coulomb gas density

We next discuss the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) equation corresponding to the process constructed in Theorem
1.5
. Set

b_{\beta,N}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho_{\beta,N}.

We define the differential operator

Lf=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f+b_{\beta,N}\cdot\nabla f,\qquad f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D),

(1.7)

and denote by
L^{*}
its formal adjoint,

L^{*}f=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f-\nabla\cdot\left(b_{\beta,N}f\right).

(1.8)

Theorem 1.10

(Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a rectifiable Jordan curve. The transition probability function
P(x,t,dy)
of the parametrization process
X=X^{x}
with the state space
D
, defined in (
1.5
), has a positive locally Hölder continuous density
p(x,t,y)
for every
t>0
. The density satisfies the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation with reflecting boundary conditions, that is,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(x,t,\bullet)=L^{*}p(x,t,\bullet)\text{ weakly in }D,

\displaystyle n\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\beta,N}\frac{\nabla p(x,t,\bullet)}{\rho_{\beta,N}}\right)=0\text{ weakly on }\partial D.

where
n
is the unit inward-pointing normal vector.

Theorem
1.10
is proved in Section
3.1
.

Remark 1.11
.

Under the additional smoothness assumption that
\gamma\in C^{2}
, we show in Proposition
3.9
that the parametrization process
X=X^{x}
is a diffusion process in the sense of Kolmogorov, that is,

(1.)

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{B(x,\delta)^{c}}p(x,h,y)dy=0,

(2.)

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{B(x,\delta)}(y-x)p(x,h,y)dy=b_{\beta,N}(x),

(3.)

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{B(x,\delta)}((y-x)\cdot z)^{2}p(x,h,y)dy=|z|^{2}.

The mapping
g:D\to\Gamma^{N}
given by

g(x)=\left(\gamma(x_{1}),\dots,\gamma(x_{N})\right)

transplants the parametrization process
X
to Dyson Brownian motion
\boldsymbol{Z}
on the curve
\Gamma
. By Theorem
1.5
, both
X
and
\boldsymbol{Z}
are continuous strong Markov processes. Let
P(x,t,dy)
and
Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,|d\boldsymbol{w}|)
denote the transition probability functions of these processes correspondingly. If
f(x)=\boldsymbol{z}
, then the transition probability functions are related via

Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,\mathcal{V})=P(x,t,g^{-1}(\mathcal{V})),\qquad\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{B}_{S},

where
\mathcal{B}_{S}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
S
.

The next result concerns convergence towards the stationary distribution. This theorem motivates calling the process of Theorem
1.5
“Dyson Brownian motion”.

Theorem 1.12

(Convergence towards Coulomb gas density)
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{\infty}
. Let
Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,|d\boldsymbol{w}|)
be the transition probability function of the Dyson Brownian motion started at
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
. Then, as
t\to+\infty
, the measure
Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,|d\boldsymbol{w}|)
converges weakly, exponentially fast, to the unique stationary distribution on
(S,\mathcal{B}_{S})
given by the Coulomb gas density

\varrho_{\beta,N}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod_{i\neq j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{\beta/2},\qquad\boldsymbol{z}\in S.

(1.9)

We prove this result in Section
4
.

Large deviations

In the next statement we consider the time-changed process, with parameter
\kappa=2/\beta
, as in Remark
1.8
. As
\kappa\to 0+
, equivalently
\beta\to+\infty
, the system converges to a deterministic gradient flow and we are interested in large deviations for this limit. In order to state the result, let
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1}(t),\dots,Z_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
be (time-reparametrized) Dyson Brownian motion started at
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
. The process is given by
Z_{i}(t)=\gamma(X_{i}(t))
, where the parametrization process
X=(X_{1}(t),\dots,X_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a strong solution to the SDE

dX(t)=\sqrt{\kappa}dB(t)-\nabla V(X(t))dt,

where
V(x)
is as in (
1.6
). Recall that
S=f(D)\subset\Gamma^{N}
. Let
C_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},S)
be the space of continuous functions started at
\boldsymbol{z}\in S
. We equip this space with the topology of locally uniform convergence.

Theorem 1.13

(Large deviations as
\kappa\to 0+
)
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{3}
. The process
\boldsymbol{Z}
satisfies a large deviation principle in
C_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},S)
, as
\kappa\to 0+
, with rate
1/\kappa
and good rate function

J(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\left(\dot{w}_{i}-\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\frac{w_{i}-w_{j}}{|w_{i}-w_{j}|^{2}}\right)\overline{\tau(w_{i})}\right\}\right|^{2}dt,

for absolutely continuous
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, and set to
+\infty
otherwise.

The proof of Theorem
1.13
follows directly from the contraction principle, see
[
13
]
, given a large deviation principle for the parametrization process. The main result of
[
1
]
applies in our setting, and gives a large deviation principle for the parametrization process restricted to any compact time interval
[0,T]
. In Theorem
5.4
we extend the large deviation principle to the infinite time interval
[0,+\infty)
. More precisely, we have the following.

Theorem 1.14
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{3}
. The time-reparametrized parametrization process
X^{x}
, started at
x\in D
, satisfies a large deviation principle in
C_{x}(\mathbb{R}_{+},D)
, with rate
1/\kappa
and good rate function

I(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|\dot{u}_{i}(t)-\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(u_{i}(t))}{\gamma(u_{i}(t))-\gamma(u_{j}(t))}\right\}\right|^{2}dt,

for absolutely continuous
u=(u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and set to
+\infty
otherwise.

Remark 1.15
.

The large deviations result implies that

\inf\limits_{u\in C_{x}(\mathbb{R}_{+},D)}I(u)=0.

In particular,
u=(u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfies
I(u)=0
if it is a solution to the gradient system

\dot{u}(t)=-\nabla V(u(t)).

This gradient flow is related to the distribution of Fekete points on the curve. See Section
1.3
.

Hydrodynamical limit

In order to state our next result, we need some additional notation. Let
{\boldsymbol{Z}}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N})
be Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
as in Theorem
1.5
. For
t\geq 0
, denote by

\mu_{t}^{(N)}(dz)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Z_{i}(t/N)}(dz)

the empirical probability measure on
\Gamma
which can be viewed as a measure on
\Gamma\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We allow the initial condition
\boldsymbol{Z}(0)=(Z_{1}(0),\dots,Z_{N}(0))
to be chosen randomly, so that
\mu_{0}^{(N)}
is a random probability measure on
\Gamma
. We shall assume that there exist some limiting distribution
\mu_{0}
such that
\mu_{0}^{(N)}\to\mu_{0}
weakly, as the number of particles
N\to+\infty
. The most natural choice is perhaps to start the process
\boldsymbol{Z}
according to the stationary Coulomb gas distribution, so that
\mu^{(N)}_{0}
converges in the limit to the electrostatic equilibrium measure on
\Gamma
. Let
\tau:\Gamma\to\partial\mathbb{D}
be the tangent map, that is,
\tau(z)
is the unit tangent vector to
\Gamma
at the point
z
, oriented counterclockwise. The following theorem shows that the system approaches a mean-field McKean-Vlasov equation depending on the curve, in the many-particle limit,
N\to+\infty
.

Theorem 1.16

(Hydrodynamical limit)
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{2}
. Let
\mu_{0}
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
and suppose that

\mu_{0}^{(N)}\to\mu_{0}
in the weak sense, as
N\to+\infty
. Then, the family
\{(\mu_{t}^{(N)})_{t\geq 0}\}_{N\geq 1}
is tight, and any subsequential limit
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies

\displaystyle d\mu_{t}(f)=\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right)\mu_{t}(dz)\mu_{t}(dw)dt,

for all test functions
f
, smooth in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
.

We prove Theorem
1.16
in Section
6
.

1.3
Discussion

Zabrodin’s approach

Let us briefly describe the main results of
[
29
]
, which inspired the present paper. Let
\gamma\in C^{2}
with
\tau:\Gamma\to\partial\mathbb{D}
the tangent map. Let
\nu=-i\tau:\Gamma\to\partial D
be the outward pointing unit normal map. Finally,
k:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
is the curvature map of
\Gamma
, that is,
k(z)
is the curvature of
\Gamma
at the point
z
defined as

\displaystyle k(z)=\frac{d\arg\tau(z)}{d|z|}.

(1.10)

Via these quantities Zabrodin
[
29
]
proposed to construct a process
\boldsymbol{Z}
as a solution to the system of SDEs

\displaystyle dZ_{i}=\sqrt{\kappa}\tau(Z_{i})dB_{i}+\left(\tau(Z_{i})(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{Z})-\frac{\kappa}{2}\nu(Z_{i})k(Z_{i})\right)dt,\quad i=1,\ldots,N.

(1.11)

Here
B=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{N})
is
N
-dimensional standard Brownian motion, and
E:\Gamma^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is the real-valued energy functional on the configuration
\boldsymbol{Z}
of particles defined by

\displaystyle E(\boldsymbol{z})=2\sum_{i<j}\log|z_{i}-z_{j}|+\sum_{i}W(z_{i}),

(1.12)

where
W:\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
a real-valued external potential defined in a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)
of the curve. The external potential does not appear in our setup; in that way Zabrodin’s setup is slightly more general. The quantity
\partial_{s_{i}}E
refers to the tangential derivative of the energy functional with respect to the
z_{i}
variable.

For
f:\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}
defined in a neighborhood of the curve the tangential derivative is

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}f)(z)=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\to 0\\
h\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\frac{f(z+\tau(z)h)-f(z)}{h}=2\operatorname{Re}\,\left(\tau(z)\partial_{z}f(z)\right),\quad z\in\Gamma.

(1.13)

For a function
f(\boldsymbol{z})=f(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
on
\Gamma^{N}
,
\partial_{s_{i}}f
refers to the tangential derivative in the
i^{th}
variable, while
\partial_{z}f(z)
refers to the holomorphic derivative.

For
E
given by (
1.12
) the tangential derivative is

(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{z})=\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\sum_{j:j\neq i}\frac{\tau(z_{i})}{z_{i}-z_{j}}\right\}+2\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\tau(z_{i})(\partial W)(z_{i})\right\}.

Given this setup,
[
29
]
proposes to define the
Dyson diffusion process

\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N})
on a smooth Jordan curve
\Gamma
as a solution to the system of SDEs (
1.11
). Next if
\kappa=2/\beta
, then, the function
u:\Gamma^{N}\to[0,\infty)
defined by

u(\boldsymbol{z})=\prod_{i\neq j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{\beta}\prod_{i}e^{\beta W(z_{i})}.

is claimed to be a solution to the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation
L^{*}u=0
, where
L^{*}
is the (formal) adjoint of the generator

\displaystyle L=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}\partial_{s_{i}}^{2}+(\partial_{s_{i}}E)\partial_{s_{i}}\right).

No mathematical proofs of these statements were given in
[
29
]
.

Note that the notation of
[
29
]
differs from ours: Zabrodin’s
\beta
-parameter equals
1/2
-times our
\beta
-parameter. We have chosen to use the convention of
[
24
,
12
]
, for which the determinantal model corresponds to
\beta=2
.

Let us check that our construction matches the just described one. For the arc-length parameterization
\gamma
that traces out the curve
\Gamma
in a counterclockwise manner we recall the basic expressions of tangent vectors and curvature for plane curves:

\tau(\gamma(t))=\gamma^{\prime}(t),\quad-k(\gamma(t))\nu(\gamma(t))=\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t).

Itô’s formula, which can be applied since we assume
\gamma\in C^{2}
, shows that for a real-valued process
X_{i}
we have

d\gamma(X_{i}(t))=\gamma^{\prime}(X_{i}(t))dX_{i}(t)+\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(X_{i}(t))d\langle X_{i},X_{i}\rangle(t),

and by the equations above this can be rewritten in terms of
Z_{i}(t)=\gamma(X_{i}(t))
as

dZ_{i}(t)=\tau(Z_{i})dX_{i}(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}k(Z_{i})\nu(Z_{i})d\langle X_{i},X_{i}\rangle(t).

In order to match (
1.11
) we see that we should take

dX_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\kappa}\,dB_{i}(t)+(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{Z})\,dt.

Up to a linear time change of the process, and with
W\equiv 0
, this choice in turn matches (
1.1
) since

\partial_{x_{i}}\log\rho_{\beta,N}(X)=\beta\sum_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(X_{i})}{\gamma(X_{i})-\gamma(X_{j})}\right)=\beta\sum_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left(\frac{\tau(Z_{i})}{Z_{i}-Z_{j}}\right).

Gradient flow and Fekete points

For
u:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, consider the gradient system

\begin{cases}\dot{u}(t)=-\nabla V(u(t)),\quad t>0\\
u(0)=u_{0}\in D,\end{cases}

(1.14)

where
V
is as in (
1.6
).
The potential
V(x)\to+\infty
as
x\to\partial D
. A solution to the gradient system (
1.14
) does not hit the boundary
\partial D
since
t\to V(u(t))
is a non-increasing function:

\frac{d}{dt}V(u(t))=\nabla V(u(t))\cdot\frac{du(t)}{dt}=-|\nabla V(u(t))|^{2}\leq 0.

In particular, it stays inside the set
\{x\in D:V(x)\leq V(u_{0})\}
. Local boundedness and
l
-periodicity of
V
along
\hat{e}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}
imply that a solution does not blow up in finite time. Stationary solutions satisfy

\nabla V(u(t))\equiv 0,\qquad t\geq 0.

A particularly interesting class of stationary solutions are the Fekete points. Recall that the
N
:th level Fekete points of
\Gamma
is an
N
-tuple
\{z_{1}^{*},\dots,z_{N}^{*}\}
of points that maximizes the discriminant, equivalently minimizes the logarithmic energy,

\Delta_{N}(\Gamma):=\prod_{k\neq\ell}|z_{k}^{*}-z_{\ell}^{*}|=\max_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\in\Gamma}\prod_{k\neq\ell}|z_{k}-z_{\ell}|.

The simplest example is when
\Gamma=\mathbb{T}=\partial\mathbb{D}
. In this case any
N
-tuple of equidistant points form Fekete points, which are consequently not unique. Moreover,
\Delta_{N}(\mathbb{T})=N^{N}
. It is a classical result that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Delta_{N}(\Gamma)^{1/(N(N-1))}=\textrm{cap}(\Gamma),

that is, the transfinite diameter equals the logarithmic capacity of
\Gamma
. It is known in a general setting (for compact sets with infinitely many points) that that the empirical measure of Fekete points converges to the electrostatic equilibrium measure, as
N\to+\infty
. See, e.g.,
[
20
, p. 285]
and
[
27
]
for further information.

2
Existence

The objective of this section is to establish existence of a process as in Definition
1.4
. We show that in the regime
\beta\geq 1
there exists a parametrization process, constructed as a unique strong solution to a stochastic differential equation, which after transplanting it to the curve
\Gamma
with the arc-length parametrization yields a continuous strong Markov process. That is, we prove Theorem
1.5
. However, the existence of Dyson Brownian motion in the regime
0<\beta<1
remains an open problem.

2.1
Strong solution

First, we establish existence and uniqueness of the parametrization process as a strong solution to the system of SDEs:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX(t)=dB(t)+b(X(t))dt,\ t\geq 0,\\
X(0)=x\in D.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Here

D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{N}<x_{1}+l\},

is an open, unbounded, convex domain, with smooth boundary except at intersections of the hyperplanes which form its boundary.

B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{N})
is
N
-dimensional standard Brownian motion. The drift term

b=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho_{\beta,N}

is given by the logarithmic gradient of the density

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta,N}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod_{i\neq j}|\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})|^{\beta/2},\quad\beta>0.

The gradient is understood in the weak sense. The
i
:th component of the drift is given almost everywhere by

\displaystyle b_{i}(x)=\frac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}.

For a general rectifiable Jordan curve
\Gamma
the drift
b
is a locally bounded measurable function in
D
, and
|b(x)|\to+\infty
as
x\to\partial D
. Indeed
|\gamma^{\prime}|=1
where defined.

2.1.1
Strong local solution

Using results from
[
25
]
we can construct a unique strong solution up to the time when the process reaches the boundary of the domain
D
, that is, up to the first collision time of any two particles on the curve. Denote the exit time of the domain
D
by

\tau_{\partial D}(X)=\inf\{t\geq 0:X(t)\notin D\}.

In order to define a process for all
t\geq 0
, we can employ Alexandrov compactification of the state space
D
. Let
*\notin D
be some point, define its neighborhoods to be complements in
D
of closed bounded subsets of
D
. Then,
D^{\prime}=D\cup\{*\}
is a compact topological space.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq s},\mathbb{P})
be a complete filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions (the filtration is right-continuous and
\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all null sets). Let
B=(B(t))_{t\geq 0}
be
N
-dimensional standard Brownian motion adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

By
[
25
, Theorems 2.1, 2.5]
, for any
x\in D
and
s\geq 0
, there exists a continuous
D^{\prime}
-valued process
X\equiv X^{s,x}=(X^{s,x}(t))_{t\geq s}
, with
X^{s,x}(s)=x
, such that

(1.)

X
is adapted to the filtration;

(2.)

For any
t\geq s
on the event
\{t\geq\tau_{\partial D}(X)\}

X(t)=*\text{ a.s.;}

(3.)

For any
t\geq s
on the event
\{t<\tau_{\partial D}(X)\}

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}|b(X(r))|^{2}dr<\infty\quad\text{ a.s.}

and

\displaystyle dX(t)=dB(t)+b(X(t))dt\quad\text{ a.s.;}

(4.)

Pathwise uniqueness holds;

(5.)

The law of
X
on
C([s,\infty),D^{\prime})
is uniquely determined by the drift
b
;

(6.)

Let
\mathbb{P}_{s,x}
be the probability distribution of
X^{s,x}
on
C([s,\infty),D^{\prime})
. Define the filtration

\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma\left\{(\omega(r))_{r\in[s,t]}:\omega\in C([s,\infty),D^{\prime})\right\}.

The process

\mathbb{M}_{s,x}=(C([s,\infty),D^{\prime}),(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq s},(X(t))_{t\geq s},\mathbb{P}_{s,x})

is a strong Markov process;

(7.)

For any Borel bounded function
f
on
D^{\prime}
and
T>0

(s,x)\to\mathbb{E}\left[f(X^{s,x}(T-s))\right]

is continuous on
[0,T]\times D
.

2.1.2
Strong global solution

As stated above, there exists a unique strong solution to
dX(t)=dB(t)+b(X(t))dt,\ X(0)=x\in D
, defined up to the exit time of the domain
D
. However, the drift has the property that
|b(x)|\to+\infty
as
x\to\partial D
, which tends to keep the process away from the boundary. In the case of the real line and the circle, for any
\beta>0
the process never leaves
\overline{D}
, and for
\beta\geq 1
it never hits
\partial D
, see
[
9
,
10
]
. For a general rectifiable curve
\Gamma
we do not know of a similar result that covers the whole range of
\beta>0
. There are nonetheless sufficient criteria, imposed on the density, ensuring that the process never reaches the boundary. In this regime, a local solution becomes a global one.

Remark 2.1
.

Already in
[
25
]
a sufficient condition on the drift
b
for the process to never hit the boundary
\partial D
was given, in the case of gradient drift
b=-\nabla\psi
. It is sufficient that the potential function
\psi
satisfies the inequality
\Delta\psi\leq he^{\varepsilon\psi}
for some
\varepsilon\in[0,2)
and a continuous function
h:D\to\mathbb{R}
that, for all
\sigma>0
, is
L^{r}(D,e^{-\sigma|x|^{2}}dx)
-integrable for some
r=r(\sigma)>1
. Applied to our setting, this criterion gives non-collision of particles almost surely if
\gamma\in C^{2}
and
\beta>1
and consequently a strong global solution under these assumptions, which would cover the case considered in
[
29
]
.

Let
D^{\prime}=D\cup\{*\}
be the Alexandrov compactification of
D
.
Let
x\in D
and
X^{x}
be a unique strong local solution with initial condition
X^{x}(0)=x
. Denote by
\mathbb{P}_{x}
the distribution of
X^{x}
on

\tilde{\Omega}:=\{\omega\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+},D^{\prime}):\omega(t)=*\text{ for }t\geq\zeta(\omega)\},

where
\zeta(\omega)=\inf\{t\geq 0:\omega(t)\notin D\}
is the lifetime of the process.

\mathbb{M}=(\tilde{\Omega},\mathcal{G},(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0},(X(t))_{t\geq 0},(\mathbb{P}_{x})_{x\in D})

is a strong Markov process. This is
[
25
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.5]
.

We want to show that for all
x\in D
the process
X^{x}
does not hit the boundary
\partial D
almost surely. In other words, for

\tilde{\Omega}_{0}=\{\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}:\omega(0)\in D,\ \zeta(\omega)=\infty\}

we want to prove that

\mathbb{P}_{x}[\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]=1\text{ for all }x\in D.

Following
[
3
, Section 4]
, we can find a diffusion process, via the Dirichlet form theory, that posses this property, which then can be transferred to the strong local solution as explained below.

Dirichlet form

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a non-negative definite, symmetric, bilinear form defined by

\mathcal{E}(f,g):=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\int_{D}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}\rho_{\beta,N}(x)dx,\qquad f,g\in C^{\infty}_{0}(D),

where
C^{\infty}_{0}(D)
denotes the space of compactly supported smooth function in
D
.
C^{\infty}_{0}(D)
is a dense linear subspace of
L^{2}(D,\mu)
.

The form
\mathcal{E}
is closable if for any sequence
(f_{n})
in
C^{\infty}_{0}(D)
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}||f_{n}||_{L^{2}(D,\mu)}
and
\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(f_{n}-f_{m},f_{n}-f_{m})=0
it follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(f_{n},f_{n})=0
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The bilinear form
(\mathcal{E},C^{\infty}_{0}(D))
closable on
L^{2}(D,\mu)
.

Proof.

Since
D=\{\rho_{\beta,N}>0\}
and
x\to\rho_{\beta,N}(x)
in continuous, the lemma follows from the derivation in
[
26
, Section II.2.a]
.
∎

For the form
\mathcal{E}
to be closable is a necessary and sufficient condition to have a closed extension, see
[
17
, Chapter]
. Denote by
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
the closure of
(\mathcal{E},C^{\infty}_{0}(D))
on
L^{2}(D,\mu)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product
\mathcal{E}(f,g)+(f,g)_{L^{2}(D,\mu)}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
is symmetric, strongly local, regular Dirichlet form on
L^{2}(D,\mu)
.

Proof.

See the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 4.1.4]
or

[
5
, Proposition 4.2]
and references therein.
∎

Capacity

The capacity of a set with respect to the form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
is defined, for an open set
A\subset D
, by

\text{Cap}_{\mathcal{E}}(A):=\inf\{\mathcal{E}(f,f)+(f,f)_{L^{2}}:f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}),\ f\geq 1\ \mu\text{-a.e.}\},

and, for any set
B\subset D
, by

\text{Cap}_{\mathcal{E}}(B):=\inf\{\text{Cap}_{\mathcal{E}}(A):A\text{ is open and }B\subset A\}.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
\beta\geq 1
,
\text{Cap}_{\mathcal{E}}(\{\rho_{\beta,N}(x)=0\})=0
.

Proof.

The density
\rho_{\beta,N}(x)
is bounded by

\rho_{\beta,N}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod\limits_{i\neq j}|\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})|^{\beta/2}\leq\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod\limits_{i<j}h(|x_{i}-x_{j}|),

where
h(r):=\min(r,l-r)^{\beta}
,
r\in[0,l]
. The function
h
satisfies the following integrability conditions: for any
\beta\geq 1
,

\int_{0+}\frac{1}{h(r)}dr=+\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\int^{l-}\frac{1}{h(r)}dr=+\infty.

This integrability condition on the density forces the capacity (with respect to the Dirichlet form) of the set
\{\rho_{\beta,N}=0\}
to be zero. The derivation of this claim follows, with minimal changes, the proof of
[
22
, Proposition 2.1]
.
∎

Recall

\begin{split}&\tilde{\Omega}:=\{\omega\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+},D^{\prime}):\omega(t)=*\text{ for }t\geq\zeta(\omega)\},\\
&\tilde{\Omega}_{0}:=\{\omega\in\tilde{\Omega}:\omega(0)\in D,\ \zeta(\omega)=\infty\},\end{split}

where
\zeta(\omega)=\inf\{t\geq 0:\omega(t)\notin D\}
is the lifetime of the process.
The theory of regular Dirichlet forms provides an existence of a diffusion process, associated with
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
,

\mathbb{M}_{x}=\{\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{G}},(\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{t})_{t\geq 0},(X(t))_{t\geq 0},\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\}

on
D^{\prime}
for quasi-every starting point
x\in D
, i.e., points in
D
outside of an exceptional set. The exceptional set is fairly small — it has zero capacity with respect to the Dirichlet form — but is usually not explicit. See
[
17
,
26
]
for more on Dirichlet form techniques.

Remark 2.5
.

The theory of regular Dirichlet forms provides distributions
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for starting points
x\in D
outside of an exceptional set. The exceptional set is fairly small — it has zero capacity with respect to the Dirichlet form (see below) — but is usually not explicit. However, the measure
\mu(dx)=\rho_{\beta,N}(x)dx
does not charge sets of zero capacity. This allows the process associated with
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
to start from a random point

For
\mu(dx)=\rho_{\beta,N}(x)dx
, define

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}:=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{x}\ \mu(dx)\text{ and }\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}:=\int_{D}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}\ \mu(dx).

By Proposition
2.4
and
[
26
]
,

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}[\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]=1.

Lemma 2.6
.

\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}=\mathbb{P}_{\mu}.

Proof.

The proof follows
[
3
, Lemma 4.2]
. Let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the transition semigroup of
\mathbb{M}
. Since
\tilde{\mathbb{M}}
is associated to the Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}))
, its transition semigroup
(\tilde{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is related to
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by:
\tilde{P}_{t}f
is a
\mu
-version of
P_{t}f
for
f\in L^{2}(D,\mu)
, see
[
26
, Remark 2.5 (ii)]
.

Let
A_{i}
,
i=0,\dots,n
, be Borel-measurable set in
D
. Let
0<s_{1}<\dots<s_{n}
.
By Markov property, cf.
[
17
, Lemma 4.1.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}[X(0)\in A_{0},X(s_{1})\in A_{1},\dots X(s_{n})\in A_{n}]

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[X(0)\in A_{0},X(s_{1})\in A_{1},\dots X(s_{n})\in A_{n}]\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathds{1}_{A_{0}}(x)(\tilde{P}_{s_{1}}\mathds{1}_{A_{1}}\dots\mathds{1}_{A_{n-1}}(\tilde{P}_{s_{n}-s_{n-1}}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}))(x)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\mathds{1}_{A_{0}}(x)(P_{s_{1}}\mathds{1}_{A_{1}}\dots\mathds{1}_{A_{n-1}}(P_{s_{n}-s_{n-1}}\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}))(x)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}[X(0)\in A_{0},X(s_{1})\in A_{1},\dots X(s_{n})\in A_{n}].\qed

Lemma 2.7
.

\mathbb{P}_{x}[\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]=1
for all
x\in D=\{\rho>0\}
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}[\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]=\int_{D}{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]\mu(dx)=0
and
\mu(dx)=\rho_{\beta,N}(x)dx
with
\rho_{\beta,N}>0
on
D
, it holds that
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}[\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]=0
almost everywhere on
D
. We show that it holds everywhere.

Let
\theta_{s}:\tilde{\Omega}\to\tilde{\Omega}
denote the translation operator
\theta_{s}(\omega):=(\omega(t+s))_{t\geq 0}
. Then,
\theta^{-1}_{s}\tilde{\Omega}=\tilde{\Omega}
and
\theta^{-1}_{s}\tilde{\Omega}_{0}=\tilde{\Omega}_{0}
. Fix
x\in D
. By the telescopic property of conditional expectation,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0})&=\mathbb{P}_{x}(\theta^{-1}_{s}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}))\\
&=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\mathds{1}_{\{\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}\}}(\theta_{s}\omega)\mathbb{P}_{x}(d\omega)\\
&=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}\}}(\theta_{s}\tilde{\omega})\Big|\mathcal{G}_{s}\right)(\omega)\mathbb{P}_{x}(d\omega).\end{split}

By the Markov property,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}\}}(\theta_{s}\tilde{\omega})\Big|\mathcal{G}_{s}\right)(\omega)=\mathbb{P}_{\omega(s)}\left(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0}\right),

where
\omega(0)=x
. The transition probability function of
X^{x}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure; we prove this fact in Proposition
3.7
. This leads to the identity

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0})&=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\mathbb{P}_{\omega(s)}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0})\mathbb{P}_{x}(d\omega)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}_{X^{x}(s)}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0})\right]\\
&=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0})P(x,s,dy)\\
&=\int_{D}\mathbb{P}_{y}(\tilde{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{0})p(x,s,y)dy\\
&=0.\end{split}

Since
x\in D
was an arbitrary fix point, it follows that
\mathbb{P}_{x}[\tilde{\Omega}_{0}]=1
for every
x\in D
.
∎

This completes the proof of existence of a unique strong solution to the SDE

dX(t)=dB(t)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho_{\beta,N}(X(t))dt,

for any initial point
x\in D
, in the regime
\beta\geq 1
.

2.2
Back to the curve

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
be a complete filtered probability space on which the parametrization process
X^{s,x}=(X^{s,x}(t))_{t\geq s}
is defined, with the starting point
X^{s,x}_{s}=x\in D
. Denote by
\mathcal{B}_{D}
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
D
.
X^{s,x}
is a continuous
D
-valued time-homogeneous strong Markov process with the transition probability functions

\displaystyle P(s,x,t,B)=\mathbb{P}\left[X^{s,x}(t)\in B\right],\ t\geq s,\ B\in\mathcal{B}_{D},

which are determined by

\displaystyle P(x,t,B)=P(0,x,t,B),\ t\geq 0,\ B\in\mathcal{B}_{D}.

Denote by
X^{x}=X^{0,x}
. Then,
(X^{x},\mathbb{P})
is a time-homogeneous strong Markov process in the state space
(D,\mathcal{B}_{D})
, where

D=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{1}<\dots<x_{N}<x_{1}+l\right\},

with the transition probability function
P(x,t,B)
.

Quotient parametrization process

The parametrization process is defined on an unbounded domain
D
. In this section, we redefine the process on a compact "cylinder". Although this middle step is not required to transplant the process to the curve, we will make use of this construction in Section
4
.

Let
f:D\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a mapping defined by

f(x)=x-(x_{1}-(|x_{1}|\text{ mod }l))\hat{e},

(2.2)

where
\hat{e}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}
. The image of
D
under
f
is

E=f(D)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:0\leq x_{1}<\dots<x_{N}<x_{1}+l<2l\}.

One can identify the points on two parts of the boundary
\partial E\cap\{x_{1}=0\}
and
\partial E\cap\{x_{1}=l\}
by

\begin{pmatrix}0\\
x_{2}\\
\dots\\
x_{N}\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}l\\
x_{2}+l\\
\dots\\
x_{N}+l\end{pmatrix},

making the space
E
, equipped with the quotient topology, into a “cylinder”. Then,
f:D\to E
is a continuous surjective mapping.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the sets
\{E+kl\hat{e}\}=\{x+kl\hat{e}:x\in E\}
form a decomposition of
D
:

D=\bigcup\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\{E+kl\hat{e}\}.

For a subset
V\subset E
, we have

f^{-1}(V)=\bigcup\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\{V+kl\hat{e}\}.

Strong uniqueness of the parametrization process implies that the paths of
(X^{x}+kl\hat{e})_{t\geq 0}
and
(X^{x+kl\hat{e}})_{t\geq 0}
coincide almost surely. In particular, for
V\in\mathcal{B}_{E}

\mathbb{P}\left[X^{x+kl\hat{e}}(t)\in f^{-1}(V)\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[X^{x}(t)\in\{f^{-1}(V)-kl\hat{e}\}\right].

Due to decomposition property above the preimage
f^{-1}(V)
is invariant under translations along the vector
l\hat{e}
, that is,
f^{-1}(V)-kl\hat{e}=f^{-1}(V)
as sets. We obtain the following relation for the transition probability function of the process
X
: For all
V\in\mathcal{B}_{E}
, and
x,\tilde{x}\in\overline{D}
such that
f(x)=f(\tilde{x})

P(x,t,f^{-1}(V))=P(\tilde{x},t,f^{-1}(V)).

(2.3)

Definition 2.8

(Quotient parametrization process)
.

A quotient parametrization process started at
x\in E
is a process in
E
given by

\tilde{X}^{x}(t)=f(X^{x}(t)),

where
f
is as in (
2.2
) and
X^{x}
is a parametrization process started at

.

By
[
14

, one can construct a filtered probability space

such that

is a strong Markov-Feller process in the state space
(E,\mathcal{B}_{E})
, started at
x\in E
, with the transition probability function given by

\tilde{P}(x,t,V)=P(x,t,f^{-1}(V))=\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}P(x,t,V+kl\hat{e}).

The process on the curve

Let
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
denote a point in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. Dyson Brownian motion on the curve is the process
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1}(t),\dots,Z_{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, given by

Z_{i}(t)=\gamma(\tilde{X}_{i}^{x}(t)),

in the state space

S=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\in\Gamma^{N}:z_{i}=\gamma(x_{i}),\quad x\in E\}.

Denote by

the mapping

g(x)=(\gamma(x_{1}),\dots,\gamma(x_{N})).

If
S=g(E)
, then
g:E\to S
is a continuous bijective mapping. By
[

, Theorem 10.13]
, one can construct a filtered probability space
(\Omega,(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{Q})
such that

is a strong Markov-Feller process in the state space
(S,\mathcal{B}_{S})
, started at
\boldsymbol{z}=f(x)\in S
, with the transition probability function given by

Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,\mathcal{\mathcal{V}})=\tilde{P}(x,t,g^{-1}(\mathcal{V}))=P(x,t,f^{-1}(g^{-1}(\mathcal{V}))),\quad\mathcal{V}\in\mathcal{B}_{S}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

3.1
Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a domain,
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{N})
be a Borel vector field on
\Omega
,
A=(a_{ij})
be matrix-valued mapping on
\Omega
such that
a_{ij}
are Borel measurable,
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
, and
A\geq 0
. Define
L_{A,b}
as the differential operator that acts on
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
by

\displaystyle L_{A,b}\varphi=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}},

and denote by
L^{*}_{A,b}
its formal adjoint.

Definition 3.1
.

(a) A locally finite Borel measure
\mu
on the domain
(0,T)\times\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
satisfies Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation
\partial_{t}\mu=L^{*}_{A,b}\mu

if
a_{ij},b_{i}\in L^{1}_{loc}(|\mu|)
and

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega}(\partial_{t}\varphi+L_{A,b}\varphi)d\mu=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(b) A locally finite Borel measure
\mu
on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation
L^{*}_{A,b}\mu=0

if
a_{ij},b_{i}\in L^{1}_{loc}(|\mu|)
and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(L_{A,b}\varphi)(x)\mu(dx)=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

In the rest, we choose
A=\frac{1}{2}I
and denote by
L=L_{\frac{1}{2}I,b}
, where
b=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho
. The differential operator
L
is given by

\displaystyle Lf=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f+b\cdot\nabla f,\qquad f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega),

and
L^{*}
denotes its formal adjoint

\displaystyle L^{*}f=\frac{1}{2}\Delta f-\nabla\cdot(bf).

In the special case
d\mu=\mu_{t}(dy)dt
, one can consider a Cauchy problem.

Definition 3.2

(Cauchy problem)
.

Let
\nu
be a locally bounded measure on a domain
\Omega
. A measure
d\mu=\mu_{t}(dy)dt
solves a Cauchy problem with the initial condition
\mu_{0}=\nu
if

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{t}\varphi+L\varphi)\mu_{t}(dy)dt=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T)\times\Omega)

(3.1)

and

\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x)\nu(dx)=\lim\limits_{t\to 0+,t\in I_{\varphi}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x)\mu_{t}(dx)\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),

(3.2)

where the set
I_{\varphi}\subset(0,T)
has full measure
|I_{\varphi}|=T

Remark 3.3
.

In general,
I_{\varphi}
depends on the test function. However, when
t\to\int_{\Omega}\varphi d\mu_{t}
is continuous for all test functions, which is the case when
\mu_{t}(dy)
is a transition probability of a Feller process, one can take
I_{\varphi}=(0,T)
.

Remark 3.4
.

An equivalent definition of the Cauchy problem (
3.1
), (
3.2
) is

\int_{\Omega}\varphi d\mu_{t}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi d\nu+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}L\varphi d\mu_{s}ds\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),

which for the initial distribution
\nu=\delta_{x}
, i.e., the point mass at
x\in\Omega
, becomes

\int_{\Omega}\varphi d\mu_{t}=\varphi(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}L\varphi d\mu_{s}ds\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).

(3.3)

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\rho\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega)
, then the measure
\mu(dx)=\rho(x)dx
satisfies the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation
L^{*}\mu=0
with the drift
b=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho
, where the gradient is understood in the weak sense and
b(x)=0
whenever
\rho(x)=0
.

Proof.

Indeed, since
\rho\in W_{loc}^{1,1}(\Omega)
, for any compact subset
K\subset\Omega
we have

\int_{K}|b(x)||\mu|(dx)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{K}|\nabla\rho|dx<\infty,

that is,
b^{i}\in L^{1}_{loc}(|\mu|)
for all
i=1,\dots,N
. By integration by parts, for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta\varphi+b\cdot\nabla\varphi\right)\rho\ dx=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\rho\ dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla\rho}{2\rho}\cdot\nabla\varphi\rho\ dx=0.\qed

Corollary 3.5.1
.

For any rectifiable Jordan curve
\Gamma
, the measure
\mu(dx)=\rho_{\beta,N}(x)dx
on any domain
\Omega\subseteq D
, with the density

\rho_{\beta,N}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod\limits_{i\neq j}|\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})|^{\beta/2},

is a solution to the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation
L^{*}\mu=0
with the drift

b_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2\rho_{\beta,N}}\frac{\partial\rho_{\beta,N}}{\partial x_{i}}(x)=\frac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\},

where the derivative of the parametrization is understood in the weak sense since it exists almost everywhere.

Remark 3.6
.

Note that in general for gradient drifts there could exist infinitely many solutions to the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation even in the class of probability measures. See an example in
[
7
, p.790]
. In Proposition
A.1
of the appendix we show that, at least when the density
\rho
is smooth in
D
, any positive solution to the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation must equal
\rho
times a constant.

3.2
Parametrization process

Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation

In the following proposition we show that the transition probability function of the parametrization process has a density and satisfies the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation.

Proposition 3.7
.

The transition probability function
P(x,t,dy)
of the parametrization process
X^{x}
solves the Cauchy problem (
3.3
) with
\rho=\rho_{\beta,N}
. Moreover, the transition probability measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure

\displaystyle P(x,t,dy)=p(x,t,y)dy,

(t,y)\to p(x,t,y)
is positive and locally Hölder continuous on
(0,+\infty)\times D
, and the density satisfies the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation with reflecting boundary conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(x,t,\bullet)=L^{*}p(x,t,\bullet)\text{ weakly in }D,

\displaystyle n\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\beta,N}\nabla\frac{p}{\rho_{\beta,N}}\right)=0\text{ weakly on }\partial D.

In the proof of Proposition
3.7
we will use the following result about existence of densities for measure-valued solutions to the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation.

Lemma 3.8

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
d\mu=\mu_{t}(dy)dt
be a locally finite non-negative Borel measure on
(0,T)\times\Omega
which solves the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation
\partial_{t}\mu=L^{*}\mu
, with
b_{i}\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\Omega)
. Then,

d\mu=p(t,y)dydt
, where
p\in L^{r}_{loc}((0,T)\times\Omega)
for any
r\in[1,1+\frac{1}{N+1}]
. The density
p
is a non-negative locally Hölder continuous function on
(0,T)\times\Omega
.

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

1^{\circ}.
The drift
b=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho_{\beta,N}
is locally bounded in
D
, so in particular
b_{i}\in L^{1}_{loc}(P(x,t,dy))
. By Itô’s formula, for any
\varphi\in C^{2}({D})
, almost surely

\displaystyle\varphi(X^{x}(t))=\varphi(x)+\int_{0}^{t}(L\varphi)(X^{x}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\varphi(X^{x}(s))\cdot dB(s).

Taking the expectation shows that
P(x,t,dy)
solves the Cauchy problem

\int_{D}\varphi(y)P(x,t,dy)=\varphi(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{D}(L\varphi)(y)P(x,t,dy).

In particular, differentiating with respect to time variable gives the weak formulation of the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{D}\varphi(y)P(x,t,dy)=\int_{D}(L\varphi)(y)P(x,t,dy).

2^{\circ}.
By Lemma
3.8
the measure
P(x,t,dy)dt
has a density with respect to
dydt
, which is positive and locally Hölder continuous on
(0,T)\times D
. This implies that
P(x,t,dy)\ll dy
for almost every
t\in(0,T)
. Since the process is Feller, the function
t\to P(x,t,B)
is right-continuous for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{D}
. Hence,
P(x,t,dy)\ll dy
for all
t\in(0,T)
, and, as it holds for any
T>0
, the transition probability
P(x,t,dy)
has a density for all
t>0
:

P(x,t,dy)=p(x,t,y)dt.

3^{\circ}.

L^{*}p(x,t,\bullet)
is defined in the weak sense by the identity

\displaystyle\int_{D}\varphi(L^{*}p)dy=\int_{D}(L\varphi)pdy\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{2}_{0}(D).

Thus, it follows from
1^{\circ}.
that
\partial_{t}p=L^{*}p
weakly in
D
.
Let
K
be a compact subset of
D
with Lipschitz boundary. By Green’s identity

\displaystyle\int_{K}(Lf)gdy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial K}(n\cdot\nabla f)gdS=\int_{K}f(L^{*}g)dy+\int_{\partial K}fn\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\nabla g+\frac{\nabla\rho}{2\rho}g\right)d\sigma\quad\forall g,f\in C^{2}(K).

In general,
J=-\frac{1}{2}\rho\nabla\frac{p}{\rho}
might not make sense pointwise, but
n\cdot J|_{\partial K}
can be defined weakly by

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}f(n\cdot J)d\sigma=\int_{K}(Lf)pdy-\int_{K}f(L^{*}p)dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial K}(n\cdot\nabla f)pd\sigma\quad\forall f\in C^{2}(K).

Let
f\in C^{2}(\overline{D})
and
n\cdot\nabla f=0
on
\partial D
. Then, taking into account that
\partial_{t}p=L^{*}p
weakly in
D
, in the limit
K\uparrow D

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{K\uparrow D}\int_{\partial K}f(n\cdot J)d\sigma=\int_{D}(Lf)pdy-\int_{D}f(\partial_{t}p)dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial D}(n\cdot\nabla f)pd\sigma.

The first two terms cancel each other due to
1^{\circ}.
, and the last term is zero because of the choice of the test function. Hence, the reflecting condition
n\cdot J|_{\partial D}=0
is understood as

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{K\uparrow D}\int_{\partial K}f(n\cdot J)d\sigma=0\quad\forall f\in C^{2}(\overline{D}).

∎

Kolmogorov type diffusion

In the section we demonstrate that the parametrization process satisfies Kolmogorov’s definition of diffusion, i.e., a Markov process with transition probability function
P(x,t,dy)
such that there exit a function
b:D\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, called drift coefficient, and a matrix-valued function
a(x,t)
, called diffusion coefficient, and for all
\delta>0,x,z\in D
such that
\overline{B(x,\delta)}\subset D
it holds that

(1.)

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{B(x,\delta)^{c}}p(x,h,y)dy=0,

(2.)

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{B(x,\delta)}(y-x)p(x,h,y)dy=b(x,t),

(3.)

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{B(x,\delta)}((y-x)\cdot z)^{2}p(x,h,y)dy=a(x,t)z\cdot z.

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{2}
.
Then, the parametrization process
X^{x}
is a diffusion process in the sense of Kolmogorov with identity diffusion matrix and the drift coefficient given by
b=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\log\rho_{\beta,N}
.

Proof.

1^{\circ}.
Let
f\in C^{\infty}(D)
. The time derivative of
(P_{t}f)(x)=\mathbb{E}[X^{x}(t)]
at
t=0
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{D}f(y)P(x,t,dy)\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\left(\int_{D}f(y)P(x,h,dy)-\int_{D}f(y^{\prime})P(x,0,dy^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\left(\int_{D}f(y)P(x,h,dy)-f(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{D}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right)P(x,h,dy).

On the other hand, as in the proof of Proposition
3.7
, from Itô’s formula we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{D}f(y)P(x,t,dy)\Big|_{t=0}=\int_{D}(Lf)(y)P(x,t,dy)\Big|_{t=0}=(Lf)(x).

We obtain the identity

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{D}\left(f(y)-f(x)\right)P(x,h,dy)=(Lf)(x)\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}(D).

Taking
f_{k}(y)=y_{k}
yields

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{D}\left(y_{k}-x_{k}\right)P(x,h,dy)=(Lf_{k})(x)=b_{k}(x).

Taking
f_{ij}(y)=(y_{i}-x_{i})(y_{j}-x_{j})z_{i}z_{j}
yields

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\int_{D}(y_{i}-x_{i})(y_{j}-x_{j})z_{i}z_{j}P(x,h,dy)=(Lf_{ij})(x)=z_{i}^{2}\delta_{ij}.

2^{\circ}.
Next we show stochastic continuity of the transition probability function, that is, for all
x\in D
and
\delta>0
such that
\overline{B(x,\delta)}\subset D
the following limit holds

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}P(x,h,B(x,\delta)^{c})=0.

By Itô’s formula, for
p>2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(t)-x|^{p}\right]=

\displaystyle p\frac{N+p-2}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(s)-x|^{p-2}\right]ds

\displaystyle+p\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(s)-x|^{p-2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}(X^{x}(s))((X^{x}(s))_{i}-x_{i})\right]ds.

The sum in the second term can be written as

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}(y)(y_{i}-x_{i})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(b_{i}(y)-b_{i}(x))(y_{i}-x_{i})+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-x_{i})b_{i}(x).

Now we will show that the first term on the right hand side is bounded from above.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(b_{i}(y)-b_{i}(x))(y_{i}-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-x_{i})\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{(y_{i}-x_{i})\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})-(y_{j}-x_{j})\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{(x_{i}-y_{i})\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})-(x_{j}-y_{j})\gamma^{\prime}(x_{j})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}.

Now we take a closer look at the first term, which can be factored as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{(y_{i}-x_{i})\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})-(y_{j}-x_{j})\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=(y_{i}-x_{i})\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})-\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}+\left(1-\frac{x_{j}-x_{i}}{y_{j}-y_{i}}\right)\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})\frac{y_{i}-y_{j}}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}.

(3.5)

Since
x,y\in D
, it holds that
x_{i}<x_{j}
and
y_{i}<y_{j}
for
i<j
, and the term
\frac{x_{i}-x_{j}}{y_{i}-y_{j}}
is always positive. If
y_{j}-y_{i}\to 0+
, the expression (
3.5
) can only go to
-\infty
, because under the assumption
\gamma\in C^{2}

\lim\limits_{y_{j}-y_{i}\to 0+}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})\frac{y_{i}-y_{j}}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}=1

and

\lim\limits_{y_{j}-y_{i}\to 0+}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})-\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}=\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}\right\}=\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(y_{j})\overline{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}\right\}=0.

The same conclusion holds when
y_{j}-y_{i}\to l
since the expression (
3.5
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{(y_{i}-x_{i})\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})-(y_{j}-x_{j})\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}

\displaystyle=(y_{i}-x_{i})\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{i})-\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}+\left(1-\frac{l+x_{i}-x_{j}}{l+y_{i}-y_{j}}\right)\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\gamma^{\prime}(y_{j})\frac{l+y_{i}-y_{j}}{\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})}\right\}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{y\to\partial D}(b(x)-b(y))\cdot(x-y)=-\infty,

and similarly for
x\to\partial D
. There exist a constant
C=C(\gamma,N)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(b_{i}(y)-b_{i}(x))(y_{i}-x_{i})<C(\gamma,N).

Hence, there are positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
, that depend on
N,p,C(\gamma,N),|b(x)|,\beta
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(h)-x|^{p}\right]\leq c_{1}\int_{0}^{h}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(s)-x|^{p-2}\right]ds+c_{2}\int_{0}^{h}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(s)-x|^{p-1}\right]ds.

This implies that

\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(h)-x|^{p}\right]=0.

By Chebyshev’s inequality

P(x,h,B(x,\delta)^{c})=\mathbb{P}[|X^{x}(h)-x|\geq\delta]\leq\delta^{-p}\mathbb{E}\left[|X^{x}(h)-x|^{p}\right].

Hence, for all
x\in D
and
\delta>0
such that
\overline{B(x,\delta)}\subset D
we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}P(x,h,B(x,\delta)^{c})=0.

Consequently, combining the stochastic continuity with the results of
1^{\circ}.
gives items
1.
and
2.
of the proposition. The fact that the equations

can be written in terms of densities of the transition probabilities

follows from Proposition

.
∎

.

The transition probability function

4
Convergence to stationary distribution

In this section we prove Theorem
1.12
, that is, the transition probability of Dyson Brownian motion converges exponentially fast, as
t\to+\infty
, to the stationary distribution given by the Coulomb gas density
\rho_{\beta,N}
. First, we show that the transition probability of the quotient parametrization process
\tilde{X}^{x}
, defined in Section

4.1
Poincaré type inequality

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\gamma
be
C^{1}
and let
E
be as in (
3.6
). Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\beta,\gamma,N)>0
such that for any
f\in C^{1}(E)
such that
\int_{E}f(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy=0
the following Poincaré type inequality holds

\int_{E}f^{2}(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy\leq C\int_{E}\left|\nabla f(t)\right|^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy.

Proof.

By Jensen inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{E}f(y)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\left(f(y)-\int_{E}f(\tilde{y})\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y})d\tilde{y}\right)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{E\times E}(f(y)-f(\tilde{y}))^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y})dyd\tilde{y}

\displaystyle=2\iint_{E\times E}(f(y)-f(\tilde{y}))^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y})\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}<\tilde{y}_{1}\}}dyd\tilde{y},

\displaystyle f(y)-f(\tilde{y})=

\displaystyle f(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})-f(\tilde{y}_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{N})

\displaystyle+f(\tilde{y}_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{N})-f(\tilde{y}_{1},\tilde{y}_{2},y_{3},\dots,y_{N})

\displaystyle+\hskip 88.2037pt\dots

\displaystyle+f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{N-1},y_{N})-f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{N-1},\tilde{y}_{N}).

For
i=1,\dots,N
, denote

\displaystyle a_{i}:

\displaystyle=f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},y_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})-f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{i-1},\tilde{y}_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})

\displaystyle=\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})dr_{i}.

Then,

\displaystyle\int_{E}f(y)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy\leq 2N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\iint_{E\times E}(a_{i})^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y})\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}<\tilde{y}_{1}\}}dyd\tilde{y}

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle(a_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}\partial_{r_{i}}f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})dr_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}|\nabla f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})|^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})dr_{i}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt\times\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}\frac{1}{\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r^{\prime}_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})}dr^{\prime}_{i}.

J(y,\tilde{y},r_{i},r^{\prime}_{i}):=\frac{|\nabla f(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})|^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})}{\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots\tilde{y}_{i-1},r^{\prime}_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}).

Taking into account that
y_{1}<\tilde{y}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\iint_{E\times E}(a_{i})^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y})\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}<\tilde{y}_{1}\}}dyd\tilde{y}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{l}dy_{1}\int_{y_{1}}^{l}d\tilde{y}_{1}\dots\int_{y_{i-1}}^{y_{1}+l}dy_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i-1}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}dr_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}d\tilde{r}_{i}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\int_{y_{i}}^{y_{1}+l}dy_{i+1}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{i+1}\dots\int_{y_{N-1}}^{y_{1}+l}dy_{N}\int_{\tilde{y}_{N-1}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{N}J(y,\tilde{y},r_{i},r^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{l}dy_{1}\int_{y_{1}}^{l}d\tilde{y}_{1}\dots\int_{y_{i-1}}^{y_{1}+l}dy_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i-1}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{i}\int_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}_{i-1}}}^{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}}dr_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}d\tilde{r}_{i}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\int_{\boldsymbol{r_{i}}}^{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}}dy_{i+1}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{i+1}\dots\int_{y_{N-1}}^{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}}dy_{N}\int_{\tilde{y}_{N-1}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{N}J(y,\tilde{y},r_{i},r^{\prime}_{i}).

Denote
v=(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{i-1},r^{\prime}_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})
.

\displaystyle\int_{E}dv|\nabla f(v)|^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(v)\times

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{l}dy_{1}\dots\int_{y_{i-1}}^{y_{1}+l}dy_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i-1}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{y_{i}}d\tilde{r}_{i}\int_{\tilde{y}_{i}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{i+1}\dots\int_{\tilde{y}_{N-1}}^{\tilde{y}_{1}+l}d\tilde{y}_{N}\frac{\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{N})\rho_{\beta,N}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})}{\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{i-1},r^{\prime}_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})}.

The inner integrand equals

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{N})\rho_{\beta,N}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})}{\rho_{\beta,N}(\tilde{y}_{1},\dots,\tilde{y}_{i-1},r^{\prime}_{i},y_{i+1},\dots,y_{N})}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\frac{\prod_{k:k\neq i}|\gamma(\tilde{y}_{i})-\gamma(\tilde{y}_{k})|^{\beta}\prod_{l:l\neq i}|\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{l})|^{\beta}}{\prod_{p:p<i}|\gamma(r^{\prime}_{i})-\gamma(\tilde{y}_{p})|^{\beta}\prod_{q:q>i}|\gamma(r^{\prime}_{i})-\gamma(y_{q})|^{\beta}}\times

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i<\alpha<\eta}|\gamma(\tilde{y}_{\alpha})-\gamma(\tilde{y}_{\eta})|^{\beta}\prod_{\zeta<\theta<i}|\gamma(y_{\zeta})-\gamma(y_{\theta})|^{\beta}.

For any
\beta>0
, the integral with respect to

is uniformly bounded with respect to the remaining variables by some constant

. Hence,

\int_{E}f(y)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy\leq 2N^{2}c(\beta,\gamma,N)\int_{E}|\nabla f(v)|dv^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(v).\qed

4.2
Exponential convergence rate

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{\infty}
. Let
\rho_{t}(x,y)
be the density of the transition probability function of the quotient parametrization process
\tilde{X}^{x}
in
E
. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(\beta,\gamma,N)>0
such that

\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}(x,y)}{\rho_{\beta,N}(y)}-1\right)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t}{C}\right).

(4.1)

In particular, the transition probability function
\tilde{P}(x,t,dy)
converges weakly, as
t\to+\infty
, to the stationary distribution
\rho_{\beta,N}(y)dy
on
(E,\mathcal{B}_{E})
given by the density

\rho_{\beta,N}(y)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod_{i\neq j}|\gamma(y_{i})-\gamma(y_{j})|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}.

Proof.

In the course of the proof
\nabla\rho_{t}
refers to taking the gradient with respect to the second spatial variable. Differentiating the left-hand side of the inequality (
4.1
) with respect to time gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}dy

\displaystyle=2\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)\partial_{t}\rho_{t}dy

\displaystyle=-\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)\nabla\cdot\left(\rho_{\beta,N}\nabla\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}\right)dy,

where in the last equality we used that the density
\rho_{t}
satisfies the FPK equation pointwise in
E
. Integrating by parts, the right-hand side equals

-\frac{1}{2}\int_{E}\left|\nabla\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}\right|^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)n\cdot\rho_{\beta,N}\nabla\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}d\sigma.

The boundary term vanishes because
n\cdot\rho_{\beta,N}\nabla\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}
is zero on that part of the boundary
\partial E
that intersects with
\partial D
due to reflecting boundary conditions, while on the remaining part of the boundary
\{x_{1}=0\}\cap\partial E
and
\{x_{1}=l\}\cap\partial E
the integrand takes opposite values due to
l
-periodicity of
\rho_{t}
and
\rho_{\beta,N}
along
\hat{e}
, and the fact that
n(x)=(1,0,\dots,0)
for
x\in\{x_{1}=0\}
and
n(x)=(-1,0,\dots,0)
for
x\in\{x_{1}=l\}
.

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}dy=-\int_{E}\left|\nabla\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}\right|^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}dy.

The Poincaré inequality from Lemma
4.1
implies

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}dy\leq-\frac{1}{C}\int_{E}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\rho_{\beta,N}}-1\right)^{2}\rho_{\beta,N}dy,

which after integration yields the claim of the proposition.
∎

This result is immediately transferred to the transition probability function
Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,|d\boldsymbol{w}|)
of Dyson Brownian motion, which is given by the density

\varrho_{t}(z,w)=\rho_{t}\left(\gamma^{-1}(z_{1}),\dots,\gamma^{-1}(z_{N}),\gamma^{-1}(w_{1}),\dots,\gamma^{-1}(w_{N})\right).

Corollary 4.2.1
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{\infty}
. There exists a constant
C=C(\beta,\gamma,N)>0
such that

\int_{S}\left(\frac{\varrho_{t}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})}{\varrho_{\beta,N}(\boldsymbol{w})}-1\right)^{2}\varrho_{\beta,N}(\boldsymbol{w})|d\boldsymbol{w}|\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t}{C}\right).

In particular, the transition probability function
Q(\boldsymbol{z},t,|d\boldsymbol{w}|)
converges weakly, as
t\to+\infty
, to the stationary distribution
\varrho_{\beta,N}(\boldsymbol{w})|d\boldsymbol{w}|
on
(S,\mathcal{B}_{S})
given by the density

\varrho_{\beta,N}(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,N}(\gamma)}\prod_{i\neq j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}.

Proof.

Proposition
4.2
and the change of variable formula, using
\gamma^{\prime}=1
, immediately imply the corollary.∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.12
.

5

We will now study the behavior of Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
as the inverse temperature parameter
\beta\to+\infty
. Since the process on the curve is a continuous image of the parametrization process, it is sufficient to study the system

dX(t)=dB(t)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\rho_{\beta,N}(X(t))dt.

After a deterministic time-change, this becomes

dX(t)=\sqrt{\kappa}dB(t)+\nabla\rho_{1,N}(X(t))dt

with the small parameter
\kappa>0
which is related to
\beta>0
via

\kappa=\frac{2}{\beta}.

We are interested in large deviations as
\kappa\to 0
.
The recent article
[
1
]
establishes a large deviation principle for diffusions of “Dyson-type” with particles living on the unit circle, and the process is restricted to any compact time interval
[0,T]
. As we will see, this includes the parametrization process under suitable regularity assumptions on the curve
\Gamma
. First, we recall the definition of Dyson-type potential from
[
1
]
and show that, if one assumes the curve
\gamma\in C^{3}
, the parametrization process
X
defined in (
2.1
), after a deterministic time-change, is a Dyson-type diffusion. Then, we state the large deviation principle for the (time-changed) parametrization process
X^{x}=(X^{x}(t))_{t\geq 0}
in the space
C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
, of continuous
D
-valued functions started at
x
, equipped with the topology of locally uniform convergence.

5.1
Dyson-type diffusion

Definition 5.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

A function
U\in C^{2}(D,\mathbb{R})
is said to be a Dyson-type potential if there exist constants
a>0
and
B>0
such that for all
x\in D

-B\leq a\Delta U\leq|\nabla U|^{2}+B.

(5.1)

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{3}
. Then, the function

V(x)=-\log\rho_{1,N}(x)=-\sum_{i<j}\log|\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})|

is a Dyson-type potential, with some
B=B(\gamma,N)>0
and any
a\leq 1
.

Proof.

In the course of the proof we set
\rho\equiv\rho_{1,N}
. The Laplacian of
V
equals

\Delta V=-\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}+\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}.

In terms of
\rho
the condition (
5.1
) reads

-\frac{B}{a}+\frac{a-1}{a}\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\leq\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}+\frac{B}{a}.

First, we compute the square of the logarithmic derivative:

\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i}^{N}\left(\sum\limits_{j:j\neq i}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}\right)^{2}.

Then, the expression for
\Delta\rho/\rho
can written down as

\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}=\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}-\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right)^{2}\right\}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}.

1^{\circ}.
Upper bound.

Let us define the following constants that depend of the curve:

\displaystyle C_{1}(\gamma):=\sup\limits_{x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{1}+l}\left|\operatorname{Im}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{1})}{\gamma(x_{1})-\gamma(x_{2})}\right\}\right|,

\displaystyle C_{2}(\gamma):=\sup\limits_{x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{1}+l}\left|\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})-\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{2})}{\gamma(x_{1})-\gamma(x_{2})}\right\}\right|.

Then,
\Delta\rho/\rho
has the upper bound

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Im}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}+N^{2}(C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}).

2^{\circ}.
Lower bound.

We would like to determine the values of
a>0
such that the two middle terms on the right hand side of

\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}=\frac{a-1}{a}\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{a}\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}-\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\left(\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right)^{2}\right\}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}.

are bounded from below, that is, for some
A>0

\frac{1}{a}\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}-\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}^{2}\geq-\frac{A}{a},

which yields the condition

\frac{1}{a}\geq\sup_{x\in D}\frac{\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}^{2}-\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Im}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}^{2}}{\sum\limits_{i\neq j}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j\neq k}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{j})}\right\}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{i})}{\gamma(x_{i})-\gamma(x_{k})}\right\}+A}.

Define the following constant that depends on the curve:

\displaystyle C_{3}(\gamma):=\sup\limits_{x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{3}<x_{1}+l}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{1})}{\gamma(x_{1})-\gamma(x_{2})}\right\}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{1})}{\gamma(x_{1})-\gamma(x_{3})}\right\}

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{2})}{\gamma(x_{2})-\gamma(x_{1})}\right\}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{2})}{\gamma(x_{2})-\gamma(x_{3})}\right\}

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{3})}{\gamma(x_{3})-\gamma(x_{1})}\right\}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(x_{3})}{\gamma(x_{3})-\gamma(x_{2})}\right\}\Bigg|.

Taking
A=N^{3}C_{3}
we deduce that
a>0
is allowed to be
a\leq 1
and, consequently, the following inequality holds:

\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\geq\frac{a-1}{a}\left|\frac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}-N^{3}C_{3}-N^{2}C_{2}.

3^{\circ}.
For
\gamma\in C^{3}
, all the constants
C_{i}(\gamma)
,
i=1,2,3
, are finite, and the claim holds with
B=\max\{N^{2}(C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}),N^{3}C_{3}\}
and any
0<a\leq 1
.
∎

5.2
Extension to the time interval
[0,+\infty)

Let
C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
be the space of continuous
D
-valued functions started at
x\in D
, equipped with the topology of locally uniform convergence, that is, uniform convergence on
[0,T]
for every

.
Define
I:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to[0,+\infty]
by

I(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|\dot{f}_{i}(t)+\partial_{i}V(f(t))\right|^{2}dt

if
f
is absolutely continuous and set to
+\infty
otherwise. Define also a truncated

given by

I_{T}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|\dot{f}_{i}(t)+\partial_{i}V(f(t))\right|^{2}dt

if
f
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
and set to
+\infty
otherwise.

Lemma 5.3
.

The functional
I:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to[0,+\infty]
is a good rate function.

Proof.

For
f\in C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
, by the proof of
[
1
, Corollary 2.13]
, the following inequality holds

I(f)\geq\mathcal{D}(f)-V(x),

where
\mathcal{D}(f)
is the Dirichlet energy of the function
f
given by

\mathcal{D}(f)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\dot{f}_{i}(t)^{2}dt

if
f
is absolutely continuous and set to
+\infty
otherwise.

Let
c>0
. The above inequality implies that the subset
\{I(f)\leq c\}\subset C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
is contained in
\{\mathcal{D}(f)\leq c+V(x)\}
. The latter is compact since the Dirichlet energy
\mathcal{D}:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to[0,+\infty]
is a good rate function, see
[
13
, Chapter ??]
. Thus, any sequence
(f_{n})\subset\{I(f)\leq c\}
has a convergent subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})
since it is also contained in the compact set
\{\mathcal{D}(f)\leq c+V(x)\}
; denote by
f^{*}
its subsequential limit.

Since the truncated functional
I_{T}:C_{x}([0,T],D)\to[0,+\infty]
is a good rate function, see
[
1
, Lemma 2.15]
, it is lower semi-continuous.

I(f^{*})=\lim\limits_{T\to+\infty}I_{T}(f^{*})\leq\lim\limits_{T\to+\infty}\lim\limits_{k\to+\infty}I_{T}(f_{n_{k}})\leq c.

Hence,
f^{*}\in\{I(f)\leq c\}
. It follows that the level set
\{I(f)\leq c\}
is a compact subset of
C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
for every
c>0
, and therefore the functional
I
is a good rate function.
∎

Define the probability measure on
C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
by

\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa}(E)=\mathbb{P}[X^{x}\in E],\quad E\subset C_{x}([0,+\infty),D).

For
T>0
, the family
(\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa}^{T})_{\kappa>0}
of probability measures on
C_{x}([0,T],D)
, given by
\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa}^{T}(E)=\mathbb{P}[(X^{x}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}\in E]
, satisfies the large deviation principle, as
\kappa\to 0+
, with a good rate function given by the functional
I_{T}:C_{x}([0,T],D)\to[0,+\infty]
, see
[
1
, Theorem 1.8]
.

Theorem 5.4
.

The family
(\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa})_{\kappa>0}
of probability measures on
C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
, equipped with the topology of locally uniform convergence, satisfies the large deviation principle with a good rate function
I:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to[0,+\infty]
.

Proof.

For
T>0
, let
C_{x}([0,T],D)
be the space of continuous
D
-valued functions started at
x\in D
, equipped with the topology of uniform convergence. For
T^{\prime}\geq T
let
\pi_{T^{\prime},T}:C_{x}([0,T^{\prime}],D)\to C_{x}([0,T],D)
be the restriction map, that is,
\pi_{T^{\prime},T}((f(t))_{t\in[0,T^{\prime}]})=(f(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
. The map
\pi_{T^{\prime},T}
is continuous and surjective,
\pi_{T,T}
is the identity, and
\pi_{T^{\prime\prime},T}=\pi_{T^{\prime},T}\circ\pi_{T^{\prime\prime},T^{\prime}}
for
T\leq T^{\prime}\leq T^{\prime\prime}
. Let
\pi_{T}:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to C_{x}([0,T],D)
be the restriction map. Then
\pi_{T}=\pi_{T^{\prime},T}\circ\pi_{T^{\prime}}
for
T\leq T^{\prime}
. The topology of locally uniform convergence is the weakest topology when the maps
\pi_{T}
are continuous. The collection
(C_{x}([0,T],D),\pi_{T^{\prime},T})_{T\leq T^{\prime}}
defines a projective system.

By
[
2
, Theorem 4]
, the LDP of
(\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa}^{T})_{\kappa>0}
extends to
(\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa})_{\kappa>0}
, if

(1.)

For every
T>0
, the family
\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa}^{T}=\mathbb{Q}_{\kappa}\circ\pi_{T}^{-1}
satisfies the LDP on
C_{x}([0,T],D)
with the rate function
I_{T}:C_{x}([0,T],D)\to[0,+\infty]
.

(2.)

There exists a functional
I:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to[0,+\infty]
such that it has compact level sets and satisfies for all
T>0
and
f^{T}\in C_{x}([0,T],D)
the identity

I_{T}(f^{T})=\inf\{I(f):f\in\pi_{T}^{-1}(f^{T})\}.

The first item is the content of
[
1
, Theorem 1.8]
. The second item is given by the functional
I:C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)\to[0,+\infty]
:

I(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|\dot{f}_{i}(t)+\partial_{i}V(f(t))\right|^{2}dt

if
f
is absolutely continuous and set
+\infty
otherwise. Lemma
5.3
shows that it has compact level sets.

Now we show the required identity. Any
f^{T}\in C_{x}([0,T],D)
can be extended to a function in
C_{x}([0,+\infty),D)
by

f(t)=f^{T}(t)\mathds{1}_{[0,T)}(t)+\hat{f}^{T}(t-T)\mathds{1}_{[T,+\infty)}(t),

where
\hat{f}^{T}
is a solution the gradient system

\begin{cases}\dot{u}(t)=-\nabla V(u(t)),\quad t>0,\\
u(0)=f^{T}(T).\end{cases}

The local existence of a solution to the gradient system follows from the Peano existence theorem. The global existence follows from two facts: (1)
t\to V(g(t))
is non-increasing since

\frac{d}{dt}V(u(t))=\nabla V(u(t))\cdot\dot{u}(t)=-|\nabla V(u(t))|^{2}\leq 0.

V(x)\to+\infty
as
x\to\partial D
, hence a solution to the gradient system with initial value
u(0)=x\in D
stays inside
D_{V(x)}=\{y\in D:V(y)\leq V(x)\}
, in particular it never hits the boundary
\partial D
. (2) The gradient
\nabla V
is bounded on
D_{V(x)}
, since it is continuous and
l
-periodic in the direction
\hat{e}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}
, which implies the linear growth bound

|u(t)|\leq|x|+\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla V(u(s))|ds\leq|x|+(\sup_{y\in D_{V(x)}}|\nabla V(y)|)t.

The function
f
, extended in such a way, satisfies
I(f)=I_{T}(f^{T})+I(\hat{f}^{T})
, where the second term

I(\hat{f}^{T})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|\partial_{t}\hat{f}^{T}_{i}(t)+\partial_{i}V(\hat{f}^{T}(t))\right|^{2}dt

vanishes due to
\partial_{t}\hat{f}^{T}=-\nabla V(\hat{f}^{T})
. Thus, we obtain the identity

I_{T}(f^{T})=I(f)=\inf\{I(\tilde{f}):\tilde{f}\in\pi_{T}^{-1}(f^{T})\}.\qed

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.14
.

6
Hydrodynamic limit

We will now study a particular many-particle limit of the process.
Let
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})
be Dyson Brownian motion on
\Gamma
according to Definition
1.4
. Consider the empirical measure of the particles on the curve

\mu_{t}^{(N)}(dz)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Z_{i}(t/N)}(dz),

and denote

\mu_{t}^{(N)}(f)=\int_{\Gamma}f(z)\mu_{t}^{(N)}(dz)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{t}{N})).

Theorem 6.1

(Tightness and hydrodynamic limit)
.

Suppose
\gamma\in C^{2}
. Let
\mu_{0}
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
and suppose that the initial distribution of particles is chosen so that
\mu_{0}^{(N)}\to\mu_{0}
, weakly as
N\to+\infty
. Then, the family
\{(\mu_{t}^{(N)})_{t\geq 0}\}_{N\geq 1}
is tight, and any subsequential limit
\mu=(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(f)=\mu_{0}(f)+\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right)\mu_{t}(dz)\mu_{t}(dw)dt,

(6.1)

for all functions
f
smooth in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
.

Proof.

The proof adapts the arguments in
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
21
, Theorem 3.6]
to our setting.
Let
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
be the space of probability measures on
\Gamma
equipped with the topology of weak convergence; moreover,
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
is metrizable. Let
\mathcal{M}_{T}{}=C([0,T],\mathcal{P}(\Gamma))
be the space of continuous measure-valued processes
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
equipped with the topology of uniform convergence. In the course of the proof
\mu_{\bullet}^{(N)}
refers to the family
(\mu_{t}^{(N)})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

We show that the random family
\{\mu_{\bullet}^{(N)}\}_{N\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{M}_{T}
is tight for every
T>0
. This follows from the tightness of random variables
\left\{\mu^{(N)}_{\bullet}(f)\right\}_{N\geq 1}
in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for every real-valued
f\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}(\Gamma))
.

Denote
E(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{2}\log\varrho_{\beta,N}(\boldsymbol{z})
, where the density
\varrho_{\beta,N}
is defined in (
1.9
). For any real-valued
f\in C^{2}(\mathcal{N}(\Gamma))
, it follows from Itô’s formula, applied to the function
f\circ\gamma
, that

f(Z_{i}(t))=f(Z_{i}(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{s}f(Z_{i}(s))+(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{Z}(s))\partial_{s}f(Z_{i}(s))\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}f(Z_{i}(s))dB_{i}(s).

In turn, by a change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{(N)}(f)=\mu^{(N)}_{0}(f)+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}^{2}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N}))+(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{Z}(\tfrac{s}{N}))\partial_{s}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N}))\right)ds+M^{(N)}(t),

(6.2)

where

M^{(N)}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N}))dB_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N})

is a continuous martingale with

\mathbb{E}[|M^{(N)}(t)|^{2}]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[|\partial_{s}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N}))|^{2}]ds.

For the term with the second tangential derivative in (
6.2
) we have the following deterministic bound

\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}^{2}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N}))ds\right|\leq\frac{T}{2N}\max_{z\in\Gamma}\left|\partial_{s}^{2}f(z)\right|.

(6.3)

The term with the interaction potential
E
in (
6.2
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{Z})\partial_{s}f(Z_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\partial_{s}f(Z_{i})\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\tau(Z_{i})}{Z_{i}-Z_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{4N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\partial_{s}f(Z_{i})\left(\partial_{s}f(Z_{i})\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\tau(Z_{i})}{Z_{i}-Z_{j}}\right\}-\partial_{s}f(Z_{j})\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\tau(Z_{j})}{Z_{i}-Z_{j}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{4}\iint_{(\Gamma\times\Gamma)\smallsetminus\{z=w\}}\left(\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}\right\}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right\}\right)\mu^{(N)}_{\cdot}(dz)\mu^{(N)}_{\cdot}(dw).

For
z,w\in\Gamma
and
w\to z
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{w\to z}\left(\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right)

(6.4)

\displaystyle=\lim_{w\to z}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\tau(z)\frac{\partial_{s}f(z)-\partial_{s}f(w)}{z-w}\right\}+\lim_{w\to z}\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\frac{\tau(z)-\tau(w)}{z-w}\partial_{s}f(w)\right\}

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{\tau(z)\partial_{z}\partial_{s}f(z)\right\}+\operatorname{Re}\,\left\{-ik(z)\partial_{s}f(z)\right\}

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\partial_{s}^{2}f(z),

(6.7)

where
k(z)
is the curvature of the curve at the point
z\in\Gamma
.
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{s_{i}}E)(\boldsymbol{Z})\partial_{s}f(Z_{i})

(6.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right)\mu_{\cdot}^{(N)}(dz)\mu_{\cdot}^{(N)}(dw)

(6.9)

\displaystyle\hskip 256.0748pt-\frac{\beta}{4N}\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{s}^{2}f(z)\mu^{(N)}_{\cdot}(dz)

(6.10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta}{4}\sup_{(z,w)\in\Gamma\times\Gamma}\left|\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right|+\frac{\beta}{4N}\max_{z\in\Gamma}|\partial_{s}^{2}f(z)|.

(6.11)

Because
f\in C^{2}
and (
6.4
)-(
6.7
), the following constants are finite:

\displaystyle C_{1}(\Gamma,f):=\sup_{(z,w)\in\Gamma\times\Gamma}\left|\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right|,

\displaystyle C_{2}(\Gamma,f):=\max_{z\in\Gamma}|\partial_{s}^{2}f(z)|.

Combining (
6.2
)–(
6.8
) together, we obtain

\displaystyle|\mu^{(N)}_{t}(f)-\mu^{(N)}_{s}(f)|\leq(t-s)\frac{\beta C_{1}(\Gamma,f)}{4}+\frac{T(1+\beta/2)C_{2}(\Gamma,f)}{2N}+2\max_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{(N)}(t)|,

and

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}|\mu^{(N)}_{t}(f)-\mu^{(N)}_{s}(f)|>\eta\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{(N)}(t)|>\frac{\eta}{2}-\frac{\delta\beta C_{1}(\Gamma,f)}{8}-\frac{T(1+\beta/2)C_{2}(\Gamma,f)}{4N}\right].\end{split}

For every
\varepsilon>0
, by Doob’s martingale inequality, we can choose
N_{0}=N_{0}(\varepsilon)
large enough such that for all
N\geq N_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\left|M^{(N)}(t)\right|\geq\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|M^{(N)}(T)\right|^{2}\right]

(6.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|\partial_{s}f(Z_{i}(\tfrac{s}{N}))|^{2}ds\right]

(6.13)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T\max_{z\in\Gamma}|\partial_{s}f(z)|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}N}

(6.14)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

(6.15)

It follows that for every
\varepsilon,\eta>0
there exist
\delta>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{|t-s|<\delta}|\mu^{(N)}_{t}(f)-\mu^{(N)}_{s}(f)|>\eta\right]\leq\varepsilon\qquad\forall N\geq N_{0}.

By the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, see
[
6
, Theorem 7.3]
, the sequence
\{\mu_{\bullet}^{(N)}(f)\}_{N\geq 1}
of random processes in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R})
is tight, and therefore the sequence
\{\mu_{\bullet}^{(N)}\}_{N\geq 1}
in
\mathcal{M}_{T}
is tight for any
T>0
.

Moreover, the proof shows that

\begin{split}\mu_{t}^{(N)}(f)=&\mu_{0}^{(N)}(f)\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\beta}{4}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\left(\partial_{s}f(z)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(z)}{z-w}-\partial_{s}f(w)\operatorname{Re}\,\frac{\tau(w)}{z-w}\right)\mu^{(N)}_{s}(dz)\mu^{(N)}_{s}(dw)ds\\
&+R^{(N)}(t),\end{split}

where
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|R^{(N)}(t)|\to 0
, as
N\to+\infty
, almost surely for any
T>0
. Consequently, any subsequential limit
\mu_{\bullet}
is deterministic (since the initial condition is deterministic) and satisfies (
6.1
).
∎

Appendix A
Stationary FPK equation with gradient drift

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\rho\in C^{\infty}(D)
and
\rho>0
on
D
. If
u:\overline{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-negative solution to the stationary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation

\displaystyle\text{div}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla u-u\frac{\nabla\rho}{2\rho}\right)=0

in
D
, with reflecting boundary conditions, then there exist a constant
c>0
such that

u=c\rho
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

1^{\circ}
The assumptions on
\rho
imply that
\frac{\nabla\rho}{2\rho}\in C^{\infty}(D)
. Let
u
be a solution to
L^{*}u=0
which can be rewritten as

\text{div}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla u-u\frac{\nabla\rho}{2\rho}\right)=0.

Since the coefficients of this equation are smooth in
D
Weyl’s regularity theorem, see
[
8
, Chapter I]
, implies that a solution
u
must be
C^{\infty}(D)
. In the weak form it is written as

\int_{D}\nabla\phi\cdot\rho\nabla\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)dx=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D).

Let
f(t)=\frac{-1}{1+t}
,
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(D)
and
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then we can take
\varphi=f(\frac{u}{\rho})\psi\xi
as a test function.

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}f^{\prime}\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\psi\rho dx=-\int_{D}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\cdot\xi\nabla\psi dx-\int_{D}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\cdot\psi\nabla\xi dx.

2^{\circ}.
Let
\xi=\xi_{R}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be given by
\xi_{R}(x)=\eta(\frac{x}{R})
for some
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
0\leq\eta\leq 1
and
\eta(x)=1
for
|x|<R
. Let
D_{\delta}=\{x\in D:\text{dist}(x,\partial D)\geq\delta\}
,
K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}=D_{\delta}\cap\overline{B_{R^{\prime}}}
, where
B_{R^{\prime}}
is a ball of radius
R^{\prime}
around the origin.
K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}
is a compact subset of
D
with Lipschitz boundary. Let
\psi=\psi_{\varepsilon}=\mathds{1}_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}*\alpha_{\varepsilon}
be a mollification of the indicator function.

The sequence
\psi_{\varepsilon}
approximates the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}
. The measure
\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}dx
converges weakly to the surface
-nd\sigma
measure on
\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}
, where
n
is the inward-pointing normal unit vector,
[
16
, Chapter 5]
:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int g\cdot\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}dx=-\int_{\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}g\cdot ndS\quad\forall g\in C_{b}(U(\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}})),

where
U(\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}})
is an open neighborhood of
\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}
. The function
f\left(u/\rho\right)\rho\nabla\left(u/\rho\right)\xi_{R}
is smooth on
D
, so it leads to

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\int_{D}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\cdot\xi\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}dx=-\int_{\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\rho n\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\xi_{R}d\sigma.

and the equality

\displaystyle\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}\rho dx=\int_{\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)n\cdot\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\xi_{R}d\sigma-\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\cdot\nabla\xi_{R}dx.

The last term can be estimated by

\displaystyle\left|\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\cdot\nabla\xi_{R}dx\right|\leq\frac{||\eta||_{\infty}}{R}\left(\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}\rho dx\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}\rho dx\right)^{1/2}.

The integral
\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}\rho dx
is order of
R^{\prime}
. Taking
R^{\prime}=cR
for some
c>1
we can find a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}\rho dx\leq\left|\int_{\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)n\cdot\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\xi_{R}d\sigma\right|+\frac{C}{\sqrt{R}}\left(\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}\rho dx\right)^{1/2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}\rho dx\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{C}{\sqrt{R}}+\sqrt{\frac{C^{2}}{R}+4\left|\int_{\partial K_{\delta,R^{\prime}}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)n\cdot\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}d\sigma\right|}\right)^{2}.

Taking the limit
R\to\infty

\displaystyle\int_{D_{\delta}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{2}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}\rho dx\leq\left|\int_{\partial D_{\delta}}f\left(\frac{u}{\rho}\right)n\cdot\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}d\sigma\right|.

3^{\circ}.
Reflecting boundary condition

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{K\uparrow D}\int_{\partial K}\varphi\left(n\cdot\rho\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right)d\sigma=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{D})

leads to

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|\nabla\frac{u}{\rho}\right|^{2}\frac{1}{(1+u/\rho)^{2}}\rho dx=0.

Thus,
u/\rho
must be a constant on
D
.
∎
