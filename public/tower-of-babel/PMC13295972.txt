Personalised functional-connectivity–guided neuronavigated high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation for peripheral neuropathic pain: protocol for a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, controlled trial
Authors: Han Li, Zhimin Huang, Jiang Lin, Yunze Li, Xianwei Che, Zhiying Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeripheral neuropathic pain (PNP) is common, disabling and frequently refractory to pharmacotherapy. High-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) can reduce neuropathic pain but response rates remain variable. Personalised target selection based on functional connectivity may improve analgesic efficacy and help elucidate neurobiological mechanisms.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, multicentre, randomised, double-blind, controlled superiority trial recruiting 152 adults with PNP (eg, trigeminal neuralgia, postherpetic neuralgia and diabetic neuropathy) across four hospitals. Participants will be randomised 1:1 to (1) personalised functional connectivity guided rTMS delivered with MRI-based neuronavigation or (2) conventional rTMS target selection (traditional ‘hotspot’ approach) with identical procedures to maintain blinding. The intervention consists of 10 sessions over 2 weeks, 18 min per session, 10 Hz and at 90% resting motor threshold. The primary endpoint is change in pain intensity (visual analogue scale, 0–100 mm) from baseline to the day of the 10th session. Secondary outcomes include Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs, Brief Pain Inventory, Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire, Patient Global Impression of Change, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale and EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire, assessed at the end of the treatment and at 1-week and 1-month follow-up. Mechanistic measures include blood biomarkers (eg, Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), β-endorphin, prostaglandins, Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA) and glutamate), stool 16S rRNA profiling and resting-state functional MRI. Statistical analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Between-group differences in the primary and secondary endpoints will be evaluated using the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and linear mixed-effects models.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the lead centre, the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine ((2025C) IIT Ethics Approval No. 027), and the participating site, Hangzhou Normal University Affiliated Hospital (2024-E2-HS-008). Introduction

Neuropathic pain is defined as pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system and is clinically characterised by a constellation of positive and negative sensory symptoms.1 Patients commonly report spontaneous pain (burning, electric shock-like sensations), dysesthesia or paresthesia, and stimulus-evoked phenomena such as hyperalgesia and allodynia, which together contribute to marked functional impairment, sleep disturbance and psychological distress. Despite the widespread use of first-line pharmacotherapies (eg, gabapentinoids, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors and tricyclic antidepressants), a substantial proportion of individuals achieve inadequate analgesia or discontinue treatment because of dose-limiting adverse effects.2 This unmet need is reflected in contemporary guidelines that increasingly promote multimodal management and highlight the importance of non-pharmacological interventions, particularly for patients with refractory symptoms, polypharmacy or contraindications to systemic medications.3 4

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that delivers focal magnetic pulses to induce electric currents in cortical tissue, thereby modulating neuronal excitability and synaptic plasticity within targeted networks.5 6 Accumulating evidence indicates that high-frequency stimulation (typically ≥5 Hz) can produce analgesic effects in various neuropathic pain conditions, most consistently when applied to cortical regions involved in pain modulation such as the primary motor cortex and prefrontal areas.5 Proposed mechanisms include normalisation of maladaptive cortical excitability, engagement of descending inhibitory pathways and network-level reconfiguration of connectivity among regions implicated in sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational dimensions of pain. However, therapeutic outcomes remain heterogeneous across patients and syndromes, and the magnitude and durability of benefit can vary substantially. This inter-individual variability likely reflects both biological heterogeneity (eg, lesion location, chronicity and network disruption) and methodological factors (eg, coil positioning, dose parameters and target selection), underscoring the need for strategies that improve precision and align stimulation with each patient’s pain-relevant neurobiology.7 8

A major limitation of conventional rTMS protocols is that stimulation targets are often defined using coarse anatomical landmarks or ‘one-size-fits-all’ coordinates that may not correspond to the most therapeutically relevant network node for a given individual.9 10 In neuropathic pain, converging neuroimaging work suggests that pain is sustained not only by focal abnormalities but also by dysregulated large-scale networks, including corticostriatal circuits, salience and default mode networks and frontoparietal control systems. Functional connectivity (FC) derived from resting-state or task-based MRI offers an individualised marker of these network interactions and can be leveraged to identify patient-specific targets that are optimally connected to downstream regions implicated in pain perception and modulation. Neuronavigation further enables accurate and reproducible coil placement based on each participant’s anatomical MRI, reducing spatial targeting error and enhancing between-session consistency—two factors that may critically influence clinical response and interpretability of results.11 12

Beyond central mechanisms, chronic pain is increasingly being recognised as a systemic disorder involving the microbiota–gut–brain axis. Evidence suggests that top-down neuromodulation can influence gut homeostasis through autonomic and neuroendocrine pathways.13 14 Consequently, we use stool 16S rRNA sequencing—a validated methodological standard.15 16 This allows us to evaluate the gut microbiome as both a baseline predictor of rTMS efficacy and a responsive dependent variable reflecting treatment-induced systemic shifts. By integrating these measures, we aim to explore whether central analgesia is accompanied by a convergent restoration of microbial homeostasis, providing a holistic view of rTMS-mediated recovery.

Study objectives

Accordingly, the present protocol evaluates an individualised rTMS approach in which stimulation targets are selected using MRI-derived FC metrics and delivered under neuronavigation guidance to enhance targeting precision and standardise dose delivery across sessions. By integrating network-informed target definition with image-guided stimulation, this trial seeks to improve response rates relative to conventional targeting while also enabling mechanistic inference regarding how changes in FC relate to clinical analgesia. In addition to assessing efficacy and safety, the study is designed to explore whether baseline connectivity patterns predict treatment response and whether rTMS induces measurable, treatment-relevant modulation of pain-related networks. In this trial, clinical outcomes are assessed at the end of a standard 2-week induction course (10 sessions) to capture the peak cumulative analgesic effect. Subsequent follow-ups at 1 week and 1 month were selected to evaluate the short to medium term durability of pain relief, which is a critical indicator of the clinical usefulness of personalised rTMS compared with conventional protocols. This precision-neuromodulation framework has the potential to advance both clinical translation—through better patient outcomes and more rational target selection—and scientific understanding of network mechanisms underlying neuropathic pain and its modulation by rTMS.12 17

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study will be a prospective, multicentre, randomised, double-blind, controlled, superiority trial with two parallel arms (1:1 allocation): personalised FC-guided neuronavigated rTMS (Arm 1) versus conventional targeting rTMS (Arm 2). Sample size calculation

The trial is powered to test superiority between groups using the primary endpoint, defined as the change in visual analogue scale (VAS) pain intensity from baseline to the end of the treatment period. Sample size was estimated using G*Power (V.3.1.9.2): Developed by Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany based on an a priori power analysis. Based on outcome data from a previous neuromodulation study,18 the expected effect size for the difference in pain improvement between the individualised targeting group and the conventional group was estimated at Cohen’s d=0.60.

Because our primary hypothesis specifically posits that personalised FC-guided rTMS will yield superior analgesic effects compared with conventional targeting, an independent samples t-test framework on the change scores with a one-tailed assumption was specified. The following parameters were applied in G*Power: effect size Cohen’s d=0.60, one-sided α=0.05, power (1−β) = 0.95, and a 1:1 allocation ratio between study arms. Under these assumptions, the required sample size was 122 participants (61 per group) to adequately detect a between-group difference in VAS change at the end of the treatment. To account for an anticipated 20% attrition (eg, withdrawal, loss to follow-up or incomplete outcome assessments), the total planned enrolment was inflated to 152 participants, corresponding to 76 participants per group.19

Recruitment, enrolment and randomisation procedures

Recruitment

Participants will be consecutively recruited from the Pain Management Department of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hangzhou, China) and the Affiliated Hospital of Hangzhou Normal University (Hangzhou, China). Recruitment strategies will include hospital-based and community-based posters, leaflets distributed in outpatient and community settings, and online advertisements posted through institution-approved channels. Potentially eligible individuals who express interest will be contacted by trained study staff and provided with a standardised explanation of the study rationale, procedures, potential risks and benefits, time commitment and confidentiality protections. Individuals will be encouraged to ask questions and will be given adequate time to consider participation.

Recruitment will commence on 1 July 2025 and will continue until the target sample size (n=152) is achieved.

Enrolment and baseline assessment

Interested candidates will undergo a two-stage screening process. First, preliminary eligibility will be assessed using brief clinical history and basic demographic information. Second, potentially eligible candidates will be invited for an onsite screening visit where full inclusion/exclusion criteria will be verified. After confirming eligibility, written informed consent will be obtained prior to any study-specific procedures. Following consent, participants will be enrolled and assigned a unique study identification code. Baseline assessments will then be completed according to the study schedule before randomisation, to ensure that pretreatment measurements are collected without influence from group assignment.

Randomisation and allocation

A total of 152 eligible participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to one of two parallel study arms. Within each stratum, participants will be allocated to the two intervention groups using a computer-generated random sequence. This random allocation sequence will be generated using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA) by an independent research assistant who will have no contact with participants prior to assignment and will not be involved in outcome assessment or data collection. Allocation will be implemented only after completion of baseline assessments. Group assignments will then be communicated to the intervention team responsible for treatment delivery, while maintaining separation between personnel involved in intervention delivery and those involved in outcome assessment wherever feasible.

To minimise selection bias, the allocation process will follow a pre-specified randomisation list, and randomisation will occur in chronological order of enrolment within each stratum.

Blinding strategy for target-based comparison (active–active)

To preserve double-blinding despite different target selection logic, both groups should undergo identical preparatory steps (including neuronavigation procedures where feasible), with group-specific target coordinates managed by a concealed allocation system accessible only to an unblinded technologist not involved in assessments. This approach aligns with the protocol intent that participants, investigators, sponsors and study team remain blinded during the double-blind treatment phase.

Concomitant medications, prohibited treatments and rescue therapy

Allowed concomitant treatments

Concomitant medications for stable comorbid conditions are permitted if not expected to influence pain outcomes, and certain Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitor and short-acting hypnotics may continue if doses are stable for ≥28 days prior to stimulation. Neuropathic pain medications (eg, carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine, gabapentin/pregabalin, lamotrigine, clonazepam and baclofen; and neurotrophic vitamins) may be continued if stable at baseline; dose reduction is allowed at investigator discretion, but escalation or reintroduction is restricted during the study.

Prohibited treatments

Treatments likely to confound pain relief evaluation (eg, acupuncture, physical therapy modalities, laser therapy, nerve blocks, spinal cord stimulation and pulsed radiofrequency neuromodulation) are prohibited within 2 weeks before first stimulation and during the treatment period. Surgical interventions for pain (eg, percutaneous trigeminal procedures) require withdrawal if deemed necessary.

Rescue analgesia

If intolerable pain occurs (VAS ≥70 mm and ≥20 mm worse than baseline), participants will contact investigators promptly and may receive oxycodone/acetaminophen as rescue analgesia; all rescue use will be recorded in the electronic case report form (eCRF) for analysis.

Interventions

Participants will undergo 10 high-frequency rTMS sessions distributed over a 2-week period. Stimulation will be administered using a standard clinical stimulator (Magstim Rapid2, Whitland, UK) equipped with a figure-of-eight coil. The specific high-frequency protocol consists of 10 Hz stimulation delivered in 5 s trains with 25 s inter-train intervals. Each session comprises 36 trains, yielding a total dose of 1800 pulses per treatment. The therapeutic intervention will be delivered at 90% of the individualised resting motor threshold (RMT).

Arm 1: personalised FC-guided neuronavigated rTMS

Participants allocated to Arm 1 will receive individualised rTMS delivered under MRI-based neuronavigation. Briefly, each participant will undergo structural and functional MRI (fMRI) to (1) localise the primary motor cortex (M1) and (2) derive patient-specific FC metrics that inform the optimal stimulation target within M1 (or an M1 subregion most strongly connected to pain-relevant networks). The individualised target coordinates will then be imported into a frameless neuronavigation system to guide coil positioning and maintain accurate, reproducible stimulation over the personalised M1 site throughout the treatment course. To ensure participant safety and procedural consistency, all participants will receive standardised pre-stimulation instructions, and the staff will explain the procedure and its purpose in detail prior to the first session. The intervention team will follow a prespecified operating manual, including routine equipment checks and calibration/quality control of the stimulation and navigation systems. To enable mechanistic evaluation, fMRI will be acquired before the first rTMS session and after completion of the intervention, using the same acquisition parameters and preprocessing pipeline, to quantify treatment-associated changes in FC and related neuroimaging markers. Imaging outcomes will be analysed by trained personnel who are not involved in treatment delivery wherever feasible, and all imaging and clinical assessments will follow standardised protocols to reduce measurement bias and enhance reproducibility.12 17

Arm 2: conventional targeting rTMS

Participants allocated to Arm 2 will receive rTMS using a conventional target-selection approach based on the standard ‘hotspot’ method (ie, identification of the optimal scalp position over the primary motor cortex that elicits the most reliable motor-evoked response in the contralateral target muscle). The stimulation protocol—including device model, coil type, stimulation parameters, session duration, number of sessions and patient–staff interaction time—will be matched to the personalised FC-guided arm to minimise performance and expectancy effects, with the only difference being the targeting strategy. To ensure procedural consistency and participant safety, the intervention will be delivered according to a prespecified operating manual, with routine equipment checks and calibration/quality-control procedures performed throughout the trial. Before the first treatment session, all participants will receive a standardised explanation of the procedure and its purpose. A brief familiarisation/orientation will be provided to enhance comfort and reduce anxiety, and participants will be positioned using a standardised setup to maintain stable head posture and minimise motion during stimulation. For mechanistic assessment, a resting-state fMRI will be acquired before initiation of rTMS and after completion of the treatment course using the same imaging parameters and preprocessing pipeline as in Arm 1, enabling within-subject evaluation of treatment-related changes in FC and other exploratory neuroimaging markers. Imaging analyses and outcome assessments will be performed by trained personnel who are not involved in treatment delivery wherever feasible, in order to reduce measurement bias and improve reproducibility.11

MRI data acquisition and processing

MRI data acquisition

Scans were performed on a 3T GE (GE Healthcare Technologies, Chicago, Illinois, USA) Architect scanner. We acquired high-resolution T1-weighted structural images (1 mm slice thickness, Field of View (FOV)=256 mm) and resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI; Repetition Time (TR)=1000 ms, Echo Time (TE)=30 ms, flip angle=60°, FOV=216 mm, 3 mm slice thickness, multiband factor=3, 600 volumes). A multiband sequence with a short TR of 1000 ms was specifically chosen over the traditional 2000 ms TR to enhance signal quality. This approach increases the Nyquist frequency to 0.5 Hz, preventing higher-frequency physiological artefacts (eg, respiration) from aliasing into the low-frequency Blood Oxygen Level-Dependent signal, while doubling the temporal df for robust FC estimates.21 22 B0 field maps (TR=400 ms, TE1=4.9 ms, TE2=7.4 ms) were additionally acquired for Echo Planar Imaging distortion correction.

Imaging preprocessing

Data were preprocessed using FMRIB Software Library (V.6.0.0) and MATLAB. Steps included removal of the first 10 volumes, distortion and motion correction, boundary-based registration to T1 images, non-linear normalisation to Montreal Neurological Institute152 space, temporal filtering and Independent Component Analysis-based Feature Extraction and Classification automated denoising. Mean white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals were regressed out, but global signal regression and frame scrubbing were omitted. The exclusion criterion for excessive head motion was set at >0.4 mm mean relative displacement; all participants met this standard. Data were smoothed with a 4 mm Full Width at Half Maximum Gaussian kernel.

Target computation and functional connectivity

Network definitions for the cingulo-opercular network (CON) and somato-cognitive action network (SCAN) used the Human Connectome Project templates.23 The CON time series was extracted via weighted spatial averaging to maximise the signal-to-noise ratio. To identify the individualised rTMS target, we applied an iterative clustering algorithm within the SCAN mask to locate the peak functional cluster (≥26 contiguous voxels).24 The centre of gravity of this cluster was reverse-transformed to native T1 space and imported into the Brainsight (Rogue Research, Montreal, Canada) neuronavigation system for real-time structural guidance. Finally, overall SCAN-CON FC—used as a mechanistic index—was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient between their respective mean time series.

Outcomes

Assessments will be conducted at prespecified time points by independent physiotherapists who are blinded to group allocation. At baseline (week 0), demographic and clinical characteristics—including age, sex, symptom profile, disease severity, symptom duration, previous treatments and current medications—will be collected using a standardised questionnaire. Baseline patient-reported outcomes will additionally include anxiety and depression scales, the EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) and the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI). To investigate biological and mechanistic correlates of treatment response, blood samples will be obtained before and after the intervention to quantify circulating biomarkers, including Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), β-endorphin, prostaglandins, Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA) and glutamate. Stool samples will be collected for 16S rRNA-based microbiome profiling. Resting-state fMRI will be performed pre-treatment and post-treatment to characterise treatment-associated changes in brain function and network connectivity. Follow-up clinical outcomes (including the above symptom and quality-of-life measures) will be reassessed on the day of treatment completion and at 1 week and 1 month post-treatment. All AEs will be recorded prospectively throughout the study in real time.

All clinical outcomes will be captured using electronic patient-reported outcomes, including VAS, Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS), BPI, Short-Form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ), Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC), HAMD, HAMA, and EQ-5D-5L. Data will be entered into an eCRF.

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint will be the change in pain intensity assessed using a 100-mm VAS from baseline to the 10th rTMS session (end of treatment). VAS pain intensity will also be collected at 1 week and 1 month post-treatment to evaluate the persistence of analgesic effects. These follow-up intervals are based on established evidence that rTMS-induced neuroplasticity and therapeutic benefits can outlast the stimulation period for several weeks, making the 1-month mark a critical window for assessing clinical durability in neuropathic pain.5 25 The VAS consists of a 10 cm horizontal line anchored by ‘no pain’ (0 mm) and ‘worst imaginable pain’ (100 mm); participants will be instructed to rate pain at the affected site by marking the point that best represents their average pain intensity over the predefined recall period. In line with prior neuropathic pain trials, the clinical importance of treatment effects will be interpreted using established minimal clinically important difference minimal clinically important difference thresholds for VAS, considering both between-group differences and within-group change.26 27

Safety assessment and adverse event reporting

AEs will be assessed at each visit and documented in the eCRFs. The protocol includes AE/serious AE definitions, grading, causality assessment, reporting and follow-up procedures. The rTMS-related risks (eg, headache, scalp discomfort and rare seizure) will be explained during informed consent and standard TMS safety screening will be applied, consistent with contraindication criteria.

Data management

Clinical data will be collected via eCRFs and entered by trained site personnel. To promote data quality, the eCRF system incorporates automated range checks for valid values and logic checks for internal consistency. All data will be securely stored on an encrypted, password-protected server hosted by the coordinating centre, using anonymised participant codes. Electronic records will be retained for at least 10 years post-publication.

Data monitoring and interim analyses

Given the short duration of the intervention (2 weeks) and the established safety profile of rTMS, no interim analyses for efficacy or futility are planned. However, the trial may be prematurely terminated or temporarily suspended if unacceptable safety risks (eg, an unexpectedly high incidence of severe AEs, such as seizures) are identified. The principal investigator, in consultation with the ethics committee, will have access to the unblinded safety data if necessary and holds the final authority to make the decision to terminate the trial.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses will be conducted according to a pre-specified statistical analysis plan and performed using Statistical Analysis System (V.9.4 or later). Unless otherwise stated, tests will be two-sided with a significance level of α=0.05.

Analysis populations and general principles

The primary analytic approach will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all randomised participants in the groups to which they were allocated, regardless of adherence to the intervention. A per-protocol (PP) analysis (including participants with adequate treatment exposure and no major protocol deviations) will be conducted as a sensitivity analysis to assess robustness. Baseline characteristics will be summarised by group but not formally compared.

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables will be summarised as mean±SD when approximately normally distributed, or as median (IQR) when distributions are skewed. Categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages.

Primary analysis

The primary endpoint is the change in VAS pain intensity from baseline to the end of treatment (day of the 10th rTMS session, 1 week and 1 month after the treatment). The between-group comparison for the primary outcome will be conducted using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) framework, with end-of-treatment VAS (or VAS change score) as the dependent variable, treatment group as the main factor, and baseline VAS as a covariate. Additional prespecified covariates (eg, age, sex, disease duration/severity, medication use and/or imaging-related stratification factors if applicable) may be included to improve precision. Estimated treatment effects will be reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs. Because pain is assessed longitudinally (baseline, end of treatment and follow-ups), a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) will also be used as a complementary primary framework or supportive analysis, incorporating fixed effects for group, time, and group×time interaction and a subject-level random intercept (and random slope if supported by the data). This approach leverages all available repeated measurements and provides valid inference under missing-at-random (MAR) assumptions. No multiplicity adjustment will be applied for the primary endpoint. Any multiplicity procedures (eg, Bonferroni) will be reserved for exploratory post hoc comparisons among multiple secondary endpoints/time points as appropriate.

Secondary analyses

For outcomes with non-normal distributions, appropriate transformations or non-parametric methods (eg, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for between-group comparisons at specific time points) will be considered.

Handling of missing data

Primary analyses under mixed-effects frameworks inherently accommodate incomplete repeated-measures data under MAR assumptions. For the ITT set, missing outcome data due to early withdrawal will be additionally imputed using last observation carried forward (LOCF) as a conservative approach consistent with the protocol. To evaluate robustness, sensitivity analyses will be conducted using multiple imputation and/or alternative model-based approaches (eg, MMRM without LOCF), and results will be compared across methods.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, several pre-planned additional analyses will be conducted:

Sensitivity analyses: a PP analysis, which includes only participants who received adequate treatment exposure (eg, completed at least 8 of 10 sessions) and had no major protocol deviations, will be performed and compared with the primary ITT results. Furthermore, to address the potential impact of missing data, sensitivity analyses will be conducted using multiple imputation methods and compared against the primary mixed-effects model results under the MAR assumption.

Subgroup analyses: exploratory subgroup analyses will be performed to assess whether the treatment effect varies across pre-defined patient characteristics. Key subgroup variables will include primary neuropathic pain aetiology (eg, trigeminal neuralgia vs postherpetic neuralgia vs diabetic neuropathy). Interaction terms (treatment group×subgroup factor) will be included in the models to formally test for subgroup effects.

Post hoc comparisons and reporting

If multiple group comparisons are required at individual time points, post hoc pairwise comparisons will be conducted with multiplicity correction (eg, Bonferroni) when appropriate. Effect estimates will be reported alongside 95% CIs and exact p values, and model assumptions (normality and homoscedasticity) will be assessed using residual diagnostics.

Patient and public involvement

In this study, patients and the public were not involved as partners in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of the research. All involvement was limited to their roles as research participants/sources of data.

Discussion

This multicentre, double-blind, randomised controlled trial is designed to determine whether MRI-based FC-guided, neuronavigated rTMS yields superior analgesic benefits compared with conventional ‘hotspot’ targeting, while keeping stimulation parameters constant (10 Hz, 90% RMT and 10 sessions over 2 weeks). Although neuropathic and chronic pain conditions are often initiated or maintained by peripheral pathology, converging evidence indicates that symptom severity and persistence are strongly shaped by central nervous system mechanisms, including maladaptive cortical excitability and network-level dysregulation within sensorimotor, salience and affective–cognitive circuits. Against this background, individualised targeting based on each participant’s FC profile offers a rational strategy to reduce inter-individual variability and enhance treatment precision, thereby potentially improving both response rate and interpretability compared with anatomical or landmark-based approaches.

Beyond establishing clinical efficacy, the present protocol is deliberately mechanistic. By integrating pre–post resting-state MRI (to quantify activity, function and connectivity changes), blood biomarkers and metabolomics (including BDNF, β-endorphin, prostaglandins, GABA, glutamate and related metabolic features) and gut microbiome profiling (16S rRNA sequencing), the study will examine convergent biological pathways that may mediate analgesia and explain heterogeneity in treatment response. Specifically, we will explore whether baseline network features predict clinical improvement and whether treatment-related changes in FC and neurochemical signatures correlate with reductions in pain intensity and pain interference. This multi-layered design enables a more comprehensive understanding of the ‘brain–body’ mechanisms underlying pain modulation, including potential brain–gut axis relationships and candidate neuroplasticity markers linked to neuromodulation-induced benefit.12 13 35

Several methodological features strengthen the validity of the trial. First, the randomised, double-blind design and standardised stimulation dosing minimise performance and expectancy effects, allowing a focused test of whether targeting strategy itself contributes meaningfully to outcomes. Second, combining clinical endpoints with mechanistic measures provides an opportunity to identify biomarkers of response and to generate hypotheses for future precision-medicine implementation of rTMS. Third, prespecified correlation analyses between primary clinical outcomes (eg, VAS) and mechanistic measures (eg, FC indices and biological markers) will help bridge clinical efficacy with plausible neurobiological substrates.

We also acknowledge several anticipated limitations. Despite rigorous procedures, multicentre implementation can introduce site-related variability (eg, minor differences in scanning hardware/software or procedural workflow), which will be addressed through harmonised protocols, standardised training, and statistical adjustment where appropriate. In addition, while repeated follow-up assessments capture short-term durability, longer-term outcomes beyond 1 month are not evaluated in this protocol and may warrant future extension studies. Finally, mechanistic analyses involving high-dimensional imaging and omics data are exploratory by nature; findings will therefore be interpreted cautiously, with appropriate control of multiple comparisons in exploratory settings and emphasis on effect estimates and reproducibility. Furthermore, we acknowledge that fMRI-derived FC has inherent limitations. As an indirect statistical measure sensitive to preprocessing variations, it cannot be considered purely objective. Although we used traditional spatial averaging for time-series extraction to maximise reproducibility, these metrics should be interpreted strictly as quantitative surrogates.

In summary, this trial will provide high-quality evidence on whether FC-guided, neuronavigated rTMS improves analgesic outcomes relative to conventional targeting under identical stimulation dosing. By coupling clinical efficacy testing with functional neuroimaging, circulating biomarkers/metabolomics, and microbiome profiling, the study is positioned to clarify candidate mechanisms of action and to identify predictors of response—thereby informing the development of more precise and biologically grounded neuromodulation strategies for chronic pain management.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is conducted as a two-centre clinical trial. The study protocol was approved by the primary Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine ((2025C) IIT Ethics Approval No. 027) and the Institutional Review Board of Hangzhou Normal University Affiliated Hospital (2024-E2-HS-008). The trial is registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry for both participating centres: the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (ChiCTR2500104679, registered on 20 June 2025) and Hangzhou Normal University Affiliated Hospital (ChiCTR2400094568, registered on 24 December 2024). The trial will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Any protocol amendments will be communicated to the relevant ethics committees and the trial registries.

Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrolment (a model consent form is provided as online supplemental file 1). Participation is voluntary, and subjects may withdraw at any time. Data of all the participants will be pseudonymised using unique identification codes, stored on encrypted servers and accessed only by authorised research personnel to ensure strict confidentiality.

Safety screening (using the TMS Adult Safety Screen questionnaire) will be performed to exclude contraindications. AEs, such as scalp discomfort or headache, will be monitored and managed throughout the study. Emergency protocols are in place to address rare serious risks, such as seizures. All AEs will be documented and reported to the ethics committee.

Findings will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at conferences following Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. De-identified individual participant data will be made available to researchers on reasonable request to the corresponding author, subject to data access agreements.

Study status

This trial is currently ongoing. Participant recruitment began in June 2025 and is anticipated to be completed by December 2026. The present protocol represents the final version, which was completed in November 2024.
