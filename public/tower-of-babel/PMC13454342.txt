Hydroxyl chemistry regulation of cellulose biopolymers for aqueous zinc battery binders
Authors: Rongfu Xu, Ningxin Chen, Zichan Yuan, Zihan Guo, Jie Deng, En Chen, Yinglin Yan, Xianhui Wang, Chaoji Chen, Zhaohui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAqueous batteries offer inherent safety and low cost, but the inherently fixed physicochemical functionality and poor interfacial compatibility of existing binders limit their ability to boost positive electrode performance. Here we present a hydroxyl-chemistry regulation strategy that transforms cellulose, an abundant biopolymer on Earth, into a high-affinity, interfacially compatible binder that stabilizes high-capacity, shuttling-prone positive electrodes. By selectively opening the rigid cellulose ring structure within its molecular chains, we enhance dipole polarity, chain flexibility, and intermolecular hydrogen-bond density, thereby increasing functional-group accessibility and interfacial adhesion. These molecular-level modifications drive the formation of a dense nanofibrous network that conformally encapsulates active particles and anchors polyiodides, accelerating redox kinetics and reinforcing electrode stability. Iodine positive electrode incorporating the engineered binder deliver a reversible capacity of 150.9 mAh g−1 at 20 A g−1, with pouch cells retaining 98.07% capacity after 3000 h at 0.2 A g−1. The binder also enables layer-by-layer fabrication of high mass loading electrodes, and benefits other dissolution-prone positive electrodes such as V2O5. Overall, this work establishes a general and sustainable route for the molecular engineering of biopolymer binders, expanding their role in the design of advanced aqueous batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Bioinspired materials, Batteries

Introduction

The demand for sustainable, safe, low-cost, and scalable energy storage has intensified the search for rechargeable battery chemistries beyond lithium-ion systems1,2. Among these, aqueous zinc batteries are particularly attractive due to their use of non-flammable aqueous electrolytes, natural abundance, and the high theoretical capacity of zinc metal3–5. In these systems, the performance of the positive electrode is one of the key factors limiting the energy density, underscoring the importance of positive electrode materials and electrode design. High-capacity positive electrodes, such as iodine (I2), manganese oxides, and vanadium oxides, despite their distinct redox mechanisms, face shared challenges including sluggish charge-transfer kinetics at the positive electrode/electrolyte interface, and the dissolution of active species into the electrolyte (e.g., I3−/I−, Mn2+, VO2+)6–8. These issues originate within the electrode microstructure and are exacerbated under poor electronic/ionic conduction, causing capacity degradation and poor cycling efficiency9–11.

An ideal electrode for aqueous zinc batteries should integrate a continuous electron/ion conduction network, immobilize soluble redox species, and maintain structural integrity during cycling12. However, conventional binders, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), an overlooked yet vital component of electrodes, fall short of these requirements. Using I2 as a representative positive electrode, PVDF exhibits poor interfacial compatibility with both I2-loaded hosts (e.g., activated carbon (AC) @I2) and conductive additives (Fig. 1a), leading to materials segregation and discontinuous charge transport pathways13. This inhomogeneous material distribution exposes AC@I2 domains and creates interfacial voids that serve as leakage channels for polyiodide species (e.g., I3−, I5−). Meanwhile, the hydrophobic and functionally inert PVDF backbone lacks affinity for polyiodides, allowing their unimpeded migration and subsequent parasitic reactions at the zinc negative electrode14. These coupled physical and chemical deficiencies underscore the underappreciated yet pivotal role of binder chemistry in determining aqueous battery performance.

Recent studies have explored functional binders bearing polar groups, such as amide (polyacrylonitrile copolymer-based binder, LA13315), sulfonic acid (sodium lignosulfonate16), or hydroxyl and amino moieties (chitosan17), to enhance I2 positive electrode performance. These binders offer improved polyiodide-affinitive via hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, and also promote Zn2+ transport by modulating the local solvation environment. Yet, despite their advantages over PVDF, electrodes incorporating these binders often exhibit similar morphological defects, including interfacial voids and incomplete encapsulation of active particles15,17–19, which disrupt charge transport, degrade particle-electrolyte contact, and permit continued polyiodide leakage. More critically, their density of active functional groups is inherently constrained by the polymer backbone and challenging to increase without compromising processability or mechanical robustness. These intertwined limitations-low accessible binding site density and incomplete interfacial coverage-highlight a critical yet underexplored constraint in current binder design strategies.

Here, we present a hydroxyl-chemistry regulation strategy for cellulose biopolymers to overcome the chemical and interfacial limitations of state-of-the-art binders in aqueous zinc batteries (Fig. 1b). Cellulose, an abundant biopolymer on Earth, comprises repeating anhydroglucose units (AGUs) rich in hydroxyl groups, one of the ubiquitous and tunable functional motifs in natural and synthetic polymers20–23, yet their potential for engineering binder functionality remains underexplored. We show that selective disruption of the cyclic conformation of AGUs in the cellulose backbone redistributes the electron cloud, increasing the dipole moment and reactivity of hydroxyl groups, thereby unlocking their ability to regulate interfacial reactions and stabilize electrode architectures. This molecular-level modification enhances interchain flexibility and generates a dense network of accessible hydrogen-bonding sites, enabling strong electrostatic interactions with soluble active species, such as polyiodides. The increased chain flexibility and polarity also improve interfacial compatibility with both active materials and conductive additives, producing a uniform, conformal encapsulation of active particles and a compact, void-free electrode structure. These coupled chemical-structural improvements promote charge transfer at the electrode-electrolyte interface, accelerate I2/I− conversion kinetics, and enhance electrode integrity upon use. Consequently, I2 positive electrode incorporating the engineered binder achieve a high-rate capability of 150.9 mA h g−1 at 20 A g−1, and capacity retention of 98.07% over 3000 h in a practical pouch cell. The binder also improves performance in V2O5 positive electrode, demonstrating its broader applicability. This work bridges molecular engineering and binder chemistry of renewable biopolymers, offering a viable pathway toward sustainable, high-performance aqueous batteries.

Results

Regulating hydroxyl chemistry of cellulose binder

We employed a periodate-borohydride treatment to open the cyclic conformation of the AGUs in pristine cellulose (CNF), producing an oxidized cellulose binder (ONF) with a disrupted ring structure under an optimized NaIO4/AGU molar ratio of 1:1 (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and Supplementary Table 1). Attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2a) revealed that the intensities of C-O-C stretching vibrations at 1159 and 899 cm−1 were significantly reduced in ONF, indicating cleavage of glycosidic bond during oxidation-reduction24. In contrast to CNF, ONF exhibited enhanced -CH stretching vibrations at 2896 cm−1 and weakened in-plane AGU ring stretching vibrations at 1107 cm−1 25,26, confirming structural modifications of the AGU rings. Solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Fig. 2b) further confirmed these structural changes. Compared to the sharper peaks of CNF, ONF showed significant peak broadening, reflecting increased disorder. Notably, the C6 peak of ONF exhibited upfield splitting, while a new signal envelope emerged between 60 and 68 ppm, suggesting that the chemical environments of C2 and C3 became more similar to C627–29. These findings corroborate the successful opening of AGU rings.

13C NMR spectra also revealed a more pronounced left shoulder in the C4 signal of CNF, characteristic of crystalline domains30, whereas ONF exhibited a stronger right shoulder, indicative of enhanced amorphous character, reflecting reduced crystallinity upon ring opening of AGUs. This trend aligns with grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) patterns (Fig. 2c), where ONF exhibited attenuated intensities for the dominant (11¯0) and (200) peaks, alongside an enhanced (004) reflection, suggesting reduced long-range order, localized structural disorder, and a broader distribution of crystal orientations resulting from the increased molecular flexibility conferred by AGU ring disruption.

Theoretical calculations (Supplementary Fig. 3) further supported these observations by showing a loss of molecular symmetry in ONF and enhanced mobility of hydroxyl groups, facilitating the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Raman mapping (Fig. 2d) showed a significantly stronger -OH signal in ONF compared to CNF, aligning with the intensified -OH stretching (~3300 cm−1)19 observed in ATR-FTIR spectra (Fig. 2a). Moreover, the -OH signal in ONF shifted to lower wavenumbers. Deconvolution analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4) revealed an increase in intermolecular hydrogen bonding (from 52.28% in CNF to 57.16% in ONF) and enlarged hydrogen bond energy (from 28.51 to 29.24 kJ mol−1). Molecular dynamics simulations (Supplementary Fig. 5) further confirm that ONF forms a greater number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds and exhibits enhanced resistance to perturbation31–33, evidencing a more robust hydrogen-bonding network.

Differential charge density analysis (Fig. 2e) revealed charge accumulation near the oxygen atoms of -OH groups in CNF, accompanied by depletion around the hydrogen atoms on neighboring chains, indicative of directional O-H···O hydrogen bonds28. Notably, ONF exhibited a broader and more intensely charge redistribution region, suggesting stronger and more extensive hydrogen bonding networks. This enhancement is attributed to additional hydrogen bonds formed between newly exposed C6-hydroxyl groups and the C2/C3-hydroxyls of adjacent chains upon AGU ring opening (Supplementary Fig. 6), which promotes enhanced supramolecular cohesion and chain entanglement. Moreover, C2–C3 bond cleavage induces a cooperative reorganization of the cellulose chain conformation, giving rise to a more compact helical structure with preferential outward orientation of hydroxyl groups and enhanced spatial cooperativity (Supplementary Fig. 7)17. This conformational reorganization is expected to facilitate more effective physical confinement and chemical interaction with polyiodide species (Supplementary Note 2)34,35. These modifications also led to significant spatial charge redistribution, as evidenced by molecular electrostatic potential (ESP) maps (Fig. 2f). Compared to CNF, ONF displayed markedly increased positive potential (red-shaded regions) around hydrogen atoms, indicative of strengthened electrostatic affinity toward charged species, such as polyiodides16. This charge redistribution is consistent with Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 2g), where ONF exhibited a less negative surface potential (−7.24 mV) than CNF (−18.37 mV), reflecting reduced inter-fibrillar repulsion and improved colloidal cohesion24.

Owing to the combined effects of chain cleavage and reduced interchain repulsion, the ONF dispersion exhibited substantially lower absorbance than that of CNF across the 200–1000 nm range in the Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra (Fig. 2h), indicating better optical transparency. The transmittance of ONF reached 89.54% in the visible range (400–760 nm, Supplementary Fig. 8), compared to 79.43% for CNF, reflecting reduced turbidity. This improvement was visually evident in optical images (Fig. 2h, inset) and further validated by the long-term dispersion stability of ONF, which remained homogeneous after three months of storage (Supplementary Fig. 9). Contact angle measurements (Supplementary Fig. 10) showed that ONF dispersions spread more readily (48.4°) than CNF (58.5°), suggesting better wetting behavior. The calculated wetting free energy (ΔG, Fig. 2i) for ONF was more negative (−2136.8 J mol−1) than that of CNF (−1601.7 J mol−1), and thus the ONF can form stronger interactions with electrode components, such as active materials and conductive additives36.

Hydroxyl chemistry directed uniform coverage electrodes

To assess how the improved colloidal and interfacial properties of ONF translate into electrode-level morphology, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted on CNF and ONF cellulose papers fabricated via a conventional paper-making process (Supplementary Fig. 11). Both cellulose papers exhibited entangled nanofibrous networks; however, ONF displayed significantly more fused nanofiber junctions, whereas CNF retained discrete fiber boundaries with minimal interconnection. This morphological densification in ONF is accompanied by a markedly higher bulk density (0.98 g cm−3 for ONF vs. 0.68 g cm−3 for CNF as shown in Fig. 3a) and enhanced optical transparency (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicative of tight chain packing. These features arise from the synergistic effects of reduced inter-fibrillar electrostatic repulsion and intensified hydrogen bonding, enabling closer nanofiber association and minimization of interstitial voids21.

The compact and interconnected ONF network also imparts good mechanical integrity to the resulting cellulose paper, as evidenced by tensile strengths of 75.6 MPa for ONF and 48.8 MPa for CNF (Supplementary Fig. 13). Importantly, these morphological and structural features are well preserved in composite paper electrodes fabricated from dispersions containing active materials and conductive carbon additives (Supplementary Fig. 14). During the aqueous dispersion step (Fig. 3b), cellulose chains acted as spatial modulators: hydroxyl groups formed hydrogen bonds with water molecules, enabling nanocellulose to create uniform gaps between active particles, while also preventing agglomeration of conductive additives through steric hindrance mediated by hydroxyl-rich surfaces33,37. Benefiting from the dual functions of enhanced dispersibility and interfacial compatibility provided by the opened AGU structure, ONF enables more efficiently disperse carbon additives and crowd active material particles densely and uniformly. Upon unidirectional vacuum filtration, radial compression transforms the colloidal suspension into a lamellar architecture, yielding a freestanding, uniform coverage electrode in which ONF nanofibers tightly wrap around the active material.

To demonstrate the practical implications of ONF binder for electrode assembly, freestanding and flexible I2 paper electrodes were fabricated using AC@I2 as the active material (Fig. 3c). These current-collector-free electrodes are notably more lightweight than conventional slurry-cast counterparts utilizing PVDF binders. Surface morphology comparisons (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 15, 16) revealed that PVDF-based positive electrode exposed numerous AC@I2 particles on the electrode surface, whereas both CNF- and ONF-based positive electrodes exhibited a continuous nanofibrous coverage layer that covered the active materials. However, while the CNF-based positive electrode showed surface irregularities and uneven encapsulation, the ONF-based positive electrode displayed greater surface uniformity and flatness (Fig. 3e). Cross-sectional SEM imaging (Fig. 3f) further confirmed that AC@I₂ particles were uniformly embedded within a continuous lamellar network formed by interpenetrating nanocellulose fibers. Notably, the ONF binder yielded a denser and thinner coverage layer compared to the thicker and looser coating observed with CNF, consistent with the conformal lamellar coating architecture proposed in Supplementary Fig. 17. These observations underscore the capacity of ONF to construct electrodes with high degrees of active material coverage and spatial uniformity.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 18) show that, compared to CNF, the ONF-based host exhibits a pore-size distribution shifted toward smaller mesopores, indicating higher packing density and stronger interchain entanglement. Electrical conductivity measurements (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 2) showed that the ONF-based positive electrode achieved a conductivity of 0.50 S cm−1, which is approximately 50% higher than that of the CNF-based counterpart (0.33 S cm−1). Mechanical testing revealed that the ONF-based positive electrode also exhibited 19% higher tensile strength and 44% greater elongation than the CNF electrode (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 19). Enhanced wet-state mechanical robustness (Supplementary Fig. 20) together with suppressed thickness variation after electrolyte soaking (Supplementary Fig. 21) indicates that the densified and cohesive ONF binder network enables mechanical integrity even in aqueous electrolyte environment38,39. Meanwhile, electrolyte wettability tests (Supplementary Fig. 22) showed that the ONF-based positive electrode had a contact angle of 33.2°, lower than that of the CNF-based positive electrode (40.7°) and substantially lower than the PVDF-based positive electrode (118.2°). The enhanced electrode conductivity, mechanical strength, and wettability underscore the intimate interplay between hydroxyl chemistry and electrode architecture enabled by the ONF binder.

Electrochemical performance

To assess the structural and electrochemical advantages imparted by the ONF binder, we evaluated the performance of various I2 positive electrodes in aqueous Zn||I2 battery configurations. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 23) revealed the characteristic redox peaks corresponding to the reversible two-electron conversion reaction (I2 ↔ 2I−)40. The CNF-based positive electrode exhibited higher redox peak currents (2.84/2.91 A g−1) than the PVDF-based positive electrode (2.49/2.70 A g−1) at 1.0 mV s−1, albeit with a larger polarization voltage (0.16 V). In contrast, the ONF-based positive electrode demonstrated both the strongest redox responses (3.76/4.18 A g−1) and the smallest polarization voltage (0.07 V), indicative of enhanced electrochemical kinetics.

Kinetic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 24) showed that all three positive electrodes exhibited mixed pseudocapacitive and diffusion-controlled behavior. Notably, the ONF-based positive electrode achieved a pseudocapacitive contribution of 90.42% (Fig. 4b), exceeding those of the CNF (78.66%) and PVDF (79.04%) electrodes. This enhanced pseudocapacitive behavior reflects faster iodine redox kinetics for ONF-based positive electrodes41–43. The energy barriers analysis (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Note 3, and Supplementary Fig. 25) further supported this conclusion, showing that the ONF-based positive electrode exhibited the lowest kinetic barriers for both I2 reduction and I− oxidation, with activation energy reductions of 4.58 and 2.09 kJ mol−1, respectively. These reductions confirm that ONF catalytically promotes iodine conversion, rather than simply enabling physical adsorption. Galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 26, and Supplementary Table 3) were used to evaluate the overall polarization behavior and apparent reaction kinetics of the I2 positive electrode. The reduced voltage relaxation and smaller polarization in the ONF-based system indicate faster redox equilibration and lower internal resistance, demonstrating that the ONF binder effectively mitigates concentration polarization and facilitates rapid iodine redox conversion43–45.

In full-cell testing at 0.2 A g−1, the ONF-based positive electrode delivered a high discharge capacity of 222.9 mA h g−1 with a Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 99.35%, surpassing both the CNF-based positive electrode (218.1 mA h g−1, 97.86%) and the PVDF-based positive electrode (212.8 mA h g−1, 95.91%) (Fig. 4e). The ONF-based positive electrode also exhibited better rate performance than the other two, following the trend ONF > CNF > PVDF (Fig. 4f), delivering discharge capacities of 212.7, 199.1, 194.2, 185.5, 173.1, and 150.9 mA h g−1 at 0.2, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 A g−1, respectively. Notably, ONF consistently maintained the lowest polarization voltage across all specific currents (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 27), achieving a minimal overpotential of 0.28 V at 20 A g−1, which is 23.49% and 85.62% lower than the CNF- and PVDF-based positive electrodes, respectively. The ONF-based positive electrode also demonstrated low self-discharge during rest periods (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 28), maintaining 96.45%, 94.08%, and 93.26% of its initial capacity after 24, 48, and 96 h of resting, respectively. Meanwhile, the ONF-based positive electrode exhibited greater cycling stability, retaining 95.16% of its initial capacity after 10,000 cycles at 10 A g−1, corresponding to a per-cycle capacity decay rate as low as 0.00048% (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 29). In contrast, the PVDF- and CNF-based electrode failed after approximately 5000 cycles. Shuttle current measurements (Supplementary Fig. 30) further confirmed the ONF binder’s suppression of polyiodide migration, with the ONF-based positive electrode exhibiting the lowest shuttle current (0.49 μA g−1), nearly one-third lower than those of the other positive electrodes, confirming its effective suppression of polyiodide shuttling during dynamic cycling. Therefore, the ONF-based positive electrode is capable of stable operation even at a low rate of 0.2 A g−1, unlike other electrodes, which undergo rapid failure (Supplementary Fig. 31).

The synergistically achieved high-rate capacity (150.9 mA h g−1 at 20 A g−1) and long longevity (10,000 cycles at 10 A g−1 with minimal degradation) are competitive with some recent relevant reports on Zn||I2 batteries (Fig. 4j, Supplementary Fig. 32, and Supplementary Table 4), including those employing advanced I2 hosts, electrolyte modifications, ion-selective separators, or functional binder chemistries. These results position the ONF binder as a competitive solution in Zn||I2 battery technology, highlighting the potential of hydroxyl chemistry regulation of cellulose biopolymer.

Hydroxyl chemistry in Zn||I2 chemistry

To correlate the electrochemical performance of the ONF-based positive electrode with the underlying binder chemistry, a combination of ex situ/in situ spectroscopic analyses and theoretical calculations was conducted. The UV–vis spectra reveals that the characteristic adsorption bands of I3− at 288 and 354 cm−1 46 in a blank polyiodide solution decreased to ~8% of the original intensity after heating at 60 °C for 2 h (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Note 4, and Supplementary Fig. 33). In contrast, cellulose-containing systems showed higher polyiodide retention, with CNF retaining 56% and ONF achieving 67% of the initial intensity. The polyiodide confinement capability of ONF was corroborated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra. After polyiodide adsorption, the ONF binder exhibited stronger I 3d intensity than that of CNF (Supplementary Fig. 34). Notably, O 1s spectra (Fig. 5b) revealed that the ONF binder had a substantially higher proportion of intermolecular hydrogen bonding (C-OH···O at 532.6 eV47) prior to polyiodide adsorption, reaching 81% compared to 57% for CNF. Following polyiodide adsorption, ONF experienced a more pronounced reduction in hydrogen bonding peak intensity, attributed to the formation of strong I···H-O intramolecular halogen bond.

The in situ UV–vis results (Fig. 5c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 35) revealed that during open-circuit discharge to 0.6 V, the PVDF-based positive electrode exhibited rapid accumulation of I3−, and significant I3− concentration persisted even after charging, indicating severe shuttle effects and incomplete iodine redox conversion. In stark contrast, both CNF- and ONF-based positive electrodes (Fig. 5e) displayed negligible I3− signals, with the ONF-based positive electrode showed an almost complete absence of I3− accumulation throughout the entire cycle. These trends were reinforced by in situ Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 5f, the PVDF-based positive electrode displayed growing I5− signal at 160 cm−148 during charging beyond 1.3 V, these polyiodide signals persisted during subsequent discharge. In contrast, cellulose-based positive electrodes exhibited low I3−/I5− signal intensities across the entire potential window. Notably, such signals were nearly undetectable for the ONF-based positive electrode, demonstrating more effective immobilization and accelerated catalytic conversion of iodine intermediates.

Density functional theory calculations (Fig. 5g) showed ONF exhibited significantly stronger binding energies toward both I3− (−0.45 eV) and I5− (−0.36 eV), in contrast to CNF with moderate interactions (I3−: −0.3 eV; I5−: −0.23 eV) and PVDF with much weaker interactions (I3-: -0.21 eV; I5−: −0.17 eV). Complementary Gibbs free energy calculations of the I2 reduction pathway (Fig. 5h) demonstrated that while the conversion of I2 to I− (ΔG < 0) is thermodynamically favorable in all three systems, ONF further reduced the overall reaction free energy, suggesting that it facilitates faster reaction kinetics by lowering the activation barriers associated with polyiodide redox transitions.

The performance advantage of ONF binder was further corroborated by in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) coupled with distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Figs. 36, 37). DRT contour plots of CNF- and ONF-based positive electrodes reveal distinct differences across all relaxation domains (P1–P549,50, Supplementary Fig. 37). Notably, ONF exhibited a significantly lower P1 peak, reflecting enhanced electronic connectivity due to improved interparticle contact and binder uniformity. More critically, the ONF-based positive electrode showed markedly reduced P2–P5 intensities compared to both CNF and PVDF electrodes, reflecting its multifaceted interfacial and transport advantages. Specifically, the conformal ONF coating: i, physically isolates AC@I2 from direct electrolyte contact, reducing electric double-layer capacitance (P2); (ii) provides a dense, hydroxyl-rich interface that facilitates continuous and rapid Zn2+ migration, lowering interfacial ion transport resistance (P3, P5); and (iii) establishes a skin-like polyiodide-blocking layer that enhances redox reaction kinetics (P4).

Practical and extended applicability

Post-cycling morphological analysis validated the structural advantages conferred by the ONF binder. After prolonged cycling, the PVDF-based positive electrode exhibited severe structural degradation, including extensive cracking across both the electrode matrix and individual AC@I2 particles (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Figs. 38, 39). In contrast, the cellulose-based electrodes retained overall electrode integrity with no visible fractures (Fig. 6b, c). However, the CNF-based positive electrode showed regions of exposed AC@I2 particles after cycling (Fig. 6b), while the ONF-based positive electrode (Fig. 6c) preserved a fully high coverage surface with uniformly distributed nanofiber. Post-cycling XRD and ATR-FTIR analyses (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 40) further reveal that, unlike CNF, which undergoes partial oxidative degradation and molecular chain cleavage23,51, ONF remains chemically and structurally stable under prolonged cycling, confirming its resistance to electro-degradation even under dynamic electrochemical conditions, thus creating a polyiodide-shuttling-free barrier layer, which in turn protects the zinc negative electrode. Morphological and compositional analysis of cycled zinc negative electrodes further corroborated the stabilizing effect of the ONF binder (Supplementary Figs. 41–43). The zinc negative electrode paired with the ONF-based positive electrode exhibited a smooth surface with no detectable dendrites or byproducts, in sharp contrast to the roughened, byproduct-covered surfaces observed with the CNF-based and, more prominently, the PVDF-based counterparts.

These combined spectroscopic, theoretical, impedance, and morphology analyses confirm that ONF binder functions as an advanced interfacial regulator, enhancing polyiodide confinement and conversion kinetics at the positive electrode/electrolyte interface while buffering electrochemical stress and preserving structural integrity, as illustrated in Fig. 1b and consistent with the stable cycling performance of high-capacity ONF-based cells shown in Fig. 4f, g. These attributes translate into strong practical viability, as evidenced by Zn||I2 pouch cells (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Figs. 44, 45) employing ONF-based positive electrodes, which deliver an initial discharge capacity of 7.24 mA h at 0.2 A g−1 and maintained 98.07% capacity retention over 1250 cycles (3000 h). Notably, a representative pouch cell was able to power a light-emitting diode, unequivocally reflecting the binder’s capability to support long-term cycling under realistic operating conditions.

The good mechanical strength and cohesive hydrogen-bonding network of ONF also enable practical, scalable approaches to electrode integration. As shown in Fig. 6e, multiple ONF-based I2 positive electrode sheets can be stacked into thick electrodes via a simple drop-casting of water followed by mild pressing. Tensile tests (Fig. 6f) confirmed good interlayer adhesion, with the connection strength at 1 cm × 1 cm overlap regions exceeding the intrinsic tensile strength of a single electrode layer, indicating effective mechanical integration. Using this method, we fabricated a four-layer ONF-based positive electrode with an active mass loading of 16 mg cm−2 (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 46), which exhibited stable cycling performance over 500 cycles at 1 A g−1. Furthermore, a self-supporting I2 positive electrode (400 μm) was also fabricated via a one-step vacuum filtration process (Supplementary Fig. 47)49,52. By integrating multiple uniformly sized high loading I2 positive electrodes onto a single current collector, a larger-format pouch cell with a steady discharge capacity of 0.17 A h was fabricated (Supplementary Fig. 48). These results demonstrate the scalability and practical utility of the ONF binder in constructing thick electrodes with high mechanical integrity and continuous ion/electron transport pathways across the thickness direction.

Beyond I2 positive electrodes, the versatility of the ONF binder was further demonstrated in high-capacity V2O5 positive electrodes. SEM and XPS analyses (Supplementary Figs. 49–51) revealed that ONF forms a conformal nanofibrous network that uniformly encapsulates V2O5 particles, and it maintains structural integrity and morphology after cycling. In contrast, PVDF-based electrodes exhibited pronounced cracking and structural degradation, a failure mode analogous to that observed in I2 positive electrodes. Both CNF- and ONF-based V2O5 positive electrodes delivered high initial capacities (~430 mA h g−1 at 0.5 A g−1; Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 52), substantially outperforming the PVDF-based electrode (301 mA h g−1). Notably, ONF outperformed CNF under high-rate conditions, delivering 220.6 mA h g−1 at 5 A g−1 versus 179.8 mA h g−1, and maintained better cycling stability at 2 A g−1 (Fig. 6i and Supplementary Fig. 53). ONF also effectively suppresses self-discharge in Zn||V2O5 batteries, as evidenced by the higher retention of initial capacity during self-discharge tests (Supplementary Fig. 54).

Elemental mapping by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Fig. 55) on cycled zinc negative electrodes revealed minimal vanadium crossover (0.10 wt%) in the ONF system, significantly lower than that in CNF (0.61 wt%) and PVDF (2.19 wt%) cells. The broad applicability of this strategy was further demonstrated using LiMn2O4 positive electrodes, where ONF again enabled enhanced electrochemical performance (Supplementary Notes 6, 7, and Supplementary Figs. 56, 57). Taken together, the ONF binder consistently promotes uniform electrode morphology, ensures efficient ion/electron conduction, and effectively regulates soluble active species through confinement and conversion at the electrode level (Fig. 6k and Supplementary Note 8), enabling aqueous batteries with improved cycling stability across different positive electrode chemistries.

Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated a hydroxyl chemistry regulation strategy that transforms natural cellulose into a high-performance binder for aqueous zinc ion batteries (Supplementary Note 9). By unlocking the rigid AGU ring structure, the ONF binder features enhanced hydroxyl polarity, chain flexibility, and high density interchain hydrogen bonding network, enabling strong polyiodide anchoring and a dense, conformal encapsulation of active materials particles with fast charge transport at the electrode/electrolyte interface. The ONF-structured I2 positive electrode delivers a high reversible capacity of 150.9 mA h g−1 at 20 A g−1, a low capacity decay of 0.00048% per cycle over 10,000 cycles, and long-term retention of 98.07% after 3000 h in practical pouch cells. The water-mediated, layer-by-layer stacking of ONF-based positive electrode sheets also enables the construction of mechanically robust, high-mass-loading thick electrodes. Beyond iodine systems, the ONF binder demonstrates broad applicability to other high-capacity positive electrodes, such as V2O5, offering uniform electrode coverage, improved mechanical integrity, and effective suppression of soluble species crossover. This work establishes a benchmark for sustainable binder design and highlights the broader potential of natural polymer engineering in advancing high-performance aqueous battery technologies.

Methods

Materials

The CNF employed in this study was derived from nanocellulose of Cladophora algae; sodium periodate (NaIO4, Aladdin, CP); sodium chloride (NaCl, MACKLIN, GR); sodium borohydride (NaBH4, Kermel, AR); I2 (MACKLIN, GR); AC (FoShan Porous Carbon Tech Co., Ltd.); vanadium pentoxide (V2O5, MACKLIN, AR); aniline (C6H7N, MACKLIN, GC); hydrochloric acid (HCl, SINOPHARM, AR); ethanol (C2H5OH, Tianjin Yongda Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., AR); Lithium manganese oxide (LiMn2O4, CRYSTALGEN); graphitized multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, MACKLIN, 99.9%); PVDF (Canrd); N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Canrd); zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H2O, MACKLIN, 99.5%); glass fiber (GF, Whatman, Cat. No. 1822-090, 260 μm thick, cut-off particle size: 1.2 μm); titanium foil (Ti, Seongsuk, 20 μm thick); zinc foil (Zn, Seongsuk, 99.99%, 30 μm thick). All chemicals were used as received without further purification, with aqueous solutions prepared using deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ cm resistivity).

Preparation of ONF

ONF was synthesized by the oxidation of CNF according to previous reported methods with minor modification28,29. Especially, 0.5 g CNF was dispersed in 60 mL DI water followed by addition of 0.6 g NaIO4 and 2 g NaCl for stirring 2 days dark reaction. The oxidized product was dialyzed (JielePu, molecular weight cut-off value of 8–14 kDa) against DI water until no change in resistivity, then redispersed in DI water (60 mL), followed by reduction with 0.25 g NaBH4 for 24 h. The reduced product was dialyzed (JielePu, molecular weight cut-off value of 8–14 kDa) against DI water until no change in resistivity, then freeze-dried, and the obtained product was denoted as ONF, which was reconstituted as aqueous dispersion (~1.5 mg mL−1). An appropriate mass of cellulose (CNF or ONF) dispersion was filtrated through a filter membrane (Nantong Longjin Membrane Technology Co., Ltd. 0.1 μm PTFE, 160 μm thick) by vacuum filtration, and the resulting filter cake was allowed to stand at 25 ± 1.5 °C overnight before being transferred to a 60 °C oven for 12 h of drying, then get ONF or CNF paper. No iodine‑related signals were detected in the ONF paper (Supplementary Fig. 58).

Electrodes fabrication

The AC@I2 composite was prepared by mixing AC and I2 at a mass ratio of 2:3, sealing the mixture in a Teflon-lined autoclave, and heating at 120 °C for 12 h, followed by open-air treatment at 60 °C to remove surface-adsorbed I2. V2O5 powder was obtained by dispersing commercial V2O5 (0.72 g) in DI water (120 mL) under vigorous stirring for 10 min, adding aniline (240 μL), adjusting the pH to 3, and stirring for a further 30 min. The mixture was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave (100 mL volume) and hydrothermally treated at 140 °C for 24 h. The precipitate was collected by vacuum filtration (Nantong Longjin Membrane Technology Co., Ltd. 0.1 μm PTFE, 160 μm thick), thoroughly washed with DI water and ethanol, and dried at 60 °C.

For CNF- or ONF-based freestanding electrodes, the active material (AC@I2, V2O5, or LiMn2O4), binder (CNF or ONF), and MWCNTs were dispersed in DI water and homogenized (Ultrasonic homogenizer, SCIENTZ, JY92-IIN, 250 W, 40 min), followed by vacuum filtration (Nantong Longjin Membrane Technology Co., Ltd., 0.1 μm PTFE, 160 μm thick). The filter cakes were left at 25 ± 1.5 °C overnight and then dried in a vacuum oven (40 °C for I2 positive electrodes; 60 °C for V2O5 and LiMn2O4) for 18 h. The component mass ratios and active-material loadings were: AC@I2, 8:1:1, 4.0 mg cm−2; V2O5, 7:2:1, 2.0 mg cm−2; LiMn2O4, 8:1:1, 6.6 mg cm−2. For PVDF-based control electrodes, the active material and MWCNTs were ground together and added to the PVDF/NMP solution at the same mass ratios as above. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 12 h to form a slurry, then coated on Ti foil, and dried in a vacuum oven (60 °C for AC@I2; 120 °C for V2O5 and LiMn2O4) for 12 h. The resulting active mass loadings were 1.3 mg cm−2 (I2), 1.8 mg cm−2 (V2O5), and 3.9 mg cm−2 (LiMn2O4).

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were carried out on a battery testing system (NEWARE, CT-4008T) and an electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, CHI760E) using standard CR2025 coin cells comprising a prepared positive electrode (12 mm diameter), GF separator (16 mm diameter), 60 μL of aqueous electrolyte (2 M ZnSO4 for I2 and V2O5 positive electrodes; 2 M ZnSO4 + 1 M MnSO4 for LiMn2O4 positive electrodes), and zinc negative electrode (12 mm diameter). The pouch cell configuration consists of the following components: ONF-based positive electrode (5.0 cm × 4.0 cm, I2 loading: 34.2 mg), GF separator (5.1 cm × 4.1 cm), zinc negative electrode (5.0 cm × 4.0 cm), 1 mL of 2 M ZnSO4 aqueous electrolyte. Galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) tests were conducted within a voltage window of 0.6–1.6 V (vs. Zn2+/Zn) to determine specific capacity, CE, rate capability, cycling stability, and rest capacity retention. CV measurements in the same potential range provided insights into the redox reaction mechanisms. EIS was performed across a frequency range of 105–10−1 Hz with a voltage amplitude of 5 mV and a total of 60 data points, with in situ EIS measurements executed via custom macro commands coupled with DRT analysis for detailed interfacial process deconvolution. The GITT was utilized to evaluate the overall reaction kinetics and polarization behavior of the electrodes, the current pulse duration was set to 1800s followed by a relaxation period of 3600 s at a specific current of 0.01 A g−1. The data acquisition interval for GITT was set to 30 s. The shuttle current test was performed as follows: after five activation cycles at 0.2 A g−1 for the assembled Zn||I2 battery, the cells were discharged to 0.6 V and then charged to 1.37 V. The testing mode was then switched to potentiostatic polarization, maintaining the voltage at 1.37 V for several hours while recording the current-time response. All tests were conducted at 25 ± 1.5 °C.

Material characterizations

UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu spectrophotometer (UV mini-1280). In situ UV–vis measurements were performed with a cuvette-type electrochemical cell coupled to the UV–vis spectrophotometer and an electrochemical workstation. ATR-FTIR spectra were collected on a Thermo Scientific spectrometer (NICOLET iS20). Solid-state 13C NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker spectrometer (Avance Neo 400WB). GIXRD patterns were collected from 5° to 80° with a scanning speed of 5° min−1 using a Bruker diffractometer (D8 Advance) equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). XPS measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific system (Escalab 250Xi) to analyze elemental valence state changes before and after polyiodide adsorption. Binding energy calibration was referenced to adventitious C 1s at 284.8 eV. The zeta potentials of dispersions were determined using a Malvern analyzer (Nano-ZS). Raman spectra covering hydroxyl groups and polyiodides were acquired with a Renishaw confocal Raman microscope (inVia Qontor) at 532 nm excitation. In situ Raman characterization was conducted using a specially designed electrochemical cell featuring sapphire windows, integrated with the Raman spectrometer (inVia Qontor) and electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, CHI760E). Contact angle measurements were conducted on a SINDIN goniometer (SDC-100). The surface morphologies of papers and electrodes were characterized using SEM (JEOL, JSM-7610FPlus) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Olympus, LEXT OLS4000). The electrical conductivity of electrodes was measured with a four-point probe system (4Probes Tech, FT-201). Mechanical properties, including tensile strength and elongation, were evaluated using a universal testing machine (Jinan Fengzhi Instrument, WDW-01D) with a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm min−1.

Computational methods

The computational simulations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) to investigate the interchain interactions of cellulose, reaction Gibbs free energies, and polyiodide adsorption energies. All calculations employed the GGA-PBE functional with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 450 eV, energy convergence criterion of 10−6 eV, and force convergence threshold of −0.05 eV/Å2. Periodic boundary conditions were applied with sufficient vacuum layers in all directions to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images. Single-point energy calculations were conducted for thermodynamic property evaluation. For molecular structure optimization and surface electrostatic potential analysis, the DMol3 module was utilized with the GGA-BLYP functional. The calculation parameters and configurations for charge density difference, electrostatic surface potential, binding energy, and Gibbs free energy are provided in Supplementary Data 1–19. All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the GROMACS 2025.2 software package. All molecules were subjected to GAFF force fields. The initial configurations of the systems were constructed using the PACKMOL software package. The simulation boxes were cubic periodic cells with a side length of 5.0 nm. The simulation pipeline began with an energy minimization step to relax the initial structure. Subsequently, the system was run for 500 ps in an NVT (isothermal and isochoric) ensemble to reach temperature equilibrium, with a time step of 1.0 fs. The temperature was controlled by a Nosé-Hoover thermostat. A subsequent 5 ns NPT simulation with a step size of 1 fs was performed to bring the system pressure to equilibrium at 1 bar. After equilibrium was reached, a 10.0 ns production run was performed with a time step of 1.0 fs. Trajectories were saved every 1000 steps for subsequent analysis. The calculation parameters and configurations for molecular dynamics are provided in Supplementary Data 20–23.
