Introduction

Turán’s theorem
[
38
]
states that the balanced complete
s
-partite Turán graph
T_{s}(n)
has the maximum edge number among all
K_{s+1}
-free
n
-vertex graphs. These Turán graphs have rigid structures, particularly independent sets of linear size in
n

K_{s+1}
-free
n
-vertex graphs without such rigid structures. First introduced by Sós
[
13

in 1969, such problems form the core of Ramsey-Turán theory.

Let

denote the size of the largest independent set in a graph

(where
\overline{G}
is the complement of

is the largest

.

Definition 1.1
.

The

.

For

, we abbreviate this to

.

Definition 1.2
.

For an

-uniform hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
and function

, the
Ramsey-Turán number

\operatorname{RT}(n,\mathcal{H},f(n))
is the maximum number of edges in an

-vertex

-free hypergraph with independence number at most

.

It was motivated by the classical theorems of Ramsey and Turán and their connections to geometry, analysis, and number theory. Ramsey-Turán theory has attracted a great deal of attention over 60 years; see the nice survey by Simonovits and Sós
[

and some papers
[

,

,

,

,

Recall that the

is the power set of

equipped with the inclusion relation

, and the

Let

For nonnegative sequences

1.1
Our results

In this paper we study Ramsey-Turán type extremal functions for posets.

(1) Weak and strong Boolean Ramsey-Turán numbers for
t
-chains.

In Section
3
, we study the weak and strong Boolean Ramsey-Turán numbers for
t
-chains. Theorem
3.3
establishes the universal inequality
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
for any poset
P
. For the
k
-element chain
C_{k}
, Theorem
3.4
shows that weak and strong embeddings are equivalent, hence
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l,t)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l,t)
. For the core case
t=1
, Theorem
3.5
gives the exact value

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l)=(k-1)(l-1)

whenever
l-1\leq\binom{n-k+2}{\lfloor(n-k+2)/2\rfloor}
; in particular, this holds whenever
n\geq k+l-3
.

(2) An upper bound for poset Ramsey-Turán numbers.

In Section
4
, we use Dilworth’s theorem to derive a universal upper bound for Boolean Ramsey-Turán numbers. This purely combinatorial bound is valid for all finite posets
P
and does not use any special forbidden-poset structure.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P
be a finite poset and
l
a positive integer. For any fixed positive integer
t
, we have

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }l=1,\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}\binom{i(n+1)}{t}&\text{if }l\geq 2.\end{cases}

As
n\to\infty
(with fixed
t,l
), the asymptotic upper bound holds:

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\left(\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}i^{t}\right)n^{t}+O(n^{t-1}).

For the special case
t=1
, this reduces to the universal asymptotic bound:

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l)\leq(l-1)\cdot n+O(1)
.

Lemma
4.2
characterizes strong
A_{k}
-freeness via width (
w(Q)\leq k-1
). Combining Theorem
1.1
with a disjoint chain construction, Corollary
4.1
gives matching-order two-sided bounds for
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)
, with
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)=\Theta(n^{t})
for fixed
k,l,t
and
L=\min\{l-1,k-1\}\geq 1
. We also point out that the weak antichain case is different: weak
A_{k}
-freeness is equivalent to having fewer than
k
elements.

(3) Lower bounds for poset Ramsey-Turán numbers.

In Section
5
we prove a general lower bound for non-chain posets:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
P
be a finite poset with
w(P)=s\geq 2
. Let
l\geq 2
and put

L=\min\{l-1,s-1\}.

If
n\geq L
and
1\leq t\leq n-L+1
, then

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\geq L\binom{n-L+1}{t}.

Consequently, for every fixed non-chain poset
P
and fixed integers

with
l\geq 2
, we have

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)=\Theta(n^{t})

whenever
\min\{l-1,w(P)-1\}\geq 1
.

With the above structural tools and the Chernoff bound for binomial variables, we now restate our main result on the asymptotic lower bound for the Boolean Ramsey-Turán number
\mathrm{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
for general

, which leverages the structural properties from Assumption
1.1
:

Assumption 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the sequence of
n
-dimensional Boolean lattices, where
B_{n}=2^{[n]}
is equipped with the inclusion partial order. Let
P
be a finite poset with height
h(P)=r\geq 2
(by definition,
P
contains an
r
-element chain and no longer chains), and let
t
be an integer with
1\leq t<r
. Let
M_{n}=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
(Sperner number), which satisfies
M_{n}=\Theta(2^{n}/\sqrt{n})
by Stirling’s approximation. For fixed
0<\alpha<1
and all sufficiently large
n
, the following hold:

(A1)

For any fixed
\gamma>0
,
M_{n}^{\gamma}=\omega(n^{c})
for all fixed
c>0
.

(A2)

There exists
\mathcal{L}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\lfloor M_{n}^{\alpha}\rfloor}C_{i}\subseteq B_{n}
where each
C_{i}
is an
(r-1)
-element chain, all elements of
C_{i}
are incomparable with all elements of
C_{j}
for
i\neq j
, and

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(\mathcal{L})|=\lfloor M_{n}^{\alpha}\rfloor\binom{r-1}{t}.

This construction is feasible for all sufficiently large

: since the middle layer of
B_{n-r+2}
has size
\Theta(M_{n})
and
0<\alpha<1
, it contains at least
\lfloor M_{n}^{\alpha}\rfloor
elements. Take pairwise incomparable distinct subsets
A_{i}
from this middle layer (an antichain by Sperner’s theorem). Set
S_{0}=\emptyset
and
S_{j}=\{n,n-1,\dots,n-j+1\}
for
1\leq j\leq r-2
, and let

C_{i}=\{A_{i}\cup S_{j}:j=0,1,\dots,r-2\}.

For any
i\neq j
,
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are incomparable subsets of
[n-r+2]
, so for any
S,T\subseteq\{n-r+3,\dots,n\}
,

and
A_{j}\cup T
are incomparable (inclusion would require
A_{i}\subseteq A_{j}
or
A_{j}\subseteq A_{i}
, a contradiction).

(A3)

\Delta_{t}=\max_{x\in\mathcal{L}}|\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{t}(\mathcal{L}):x\in C\}|=\binom{r-2}{t-1}
(constant, independent of
n
).

(A4)

All subsets of
\mathcal{L}
are weakly
P
-free: every weak embedding maps chains of
P
to chains in
\mathcal{L}
, while the maximum chain length in
\mathcal{L}
is

; hence no weak embedding of
P
into any subset of
\mathcal{L}
exists.

Theorem 1.3
.

Under Assumption
1.1
, for any integer-valued function
l(n)
satisfying
2\leq l(n)\leq\lfloor M_{n}^{\alpha}\rfloor
:

1.

For fixed

,
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\geq\binom{r-1}{t}(l-1)
for all sufficiently large

;

2.

For

as

,
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)\geq(1-o(1))\binom{r-1}{t}l(n)
for sufficiently large

, where the

term tends to

as

.

In particular:

•

For

in the above range with
l(n)=\omega(n^{c})
for any fixed

,
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)=\omega(n^{c})
;

•

For

,
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)=\Omega(2^{\beta n}n^{-\beta/2})
.

Moreover, the same lower bounds hold for the strong Boolean Ramsey-Turán number with the corresponding parameter

or

, since every subset constructed in Assumption
1.1
(A2) has height at most

and hence contains no strong copy of
P
.

Preliminary

Ramsey theory
is a branch of mathematics that studies the conditions of when a combinatorial object necessarily contains some smaller given objects; see
[
34
]
.
Ramsey theory has important applications in algebra, geometry, logic, ergodic theory, theoretical computer science, information theory, number theory, and set theory; see the book
[
15
]
and survey paper
[
35
]
.

A
partially-ordered set
, or a
poset
, is a pair
(P,\leq)
,
where
P
is a set and
\leq
is a relation on
P

that is reflexive, anti-symmetric, and transitive.
When the relation
\leq
is clear from the context, we simply write
P
as a poset.
A pair
x,y\in P
is
comparable
if
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
,
and a
t
-chain
is a set of
t
distinct pairwise comparable elements. The
n
-antichain
, denoted
A_{n}
,
is the poset in which any two elements are not comparable.
Let
(P,\leq_{P})
and
(Q,\leq_{Q})
be posets. We say
Q\subseteq P
(i.e.,
Q
is a subposet of
P
) if
Q
is a subset of the underlying set of
P
(set-theoretic inclusion) and for all
x,y\in Q
,
x\leq_{Q}y
if and only if
x\leq_{P}y
; this corresponds to the special case of a strong embedding via the identity map. For posets
P
and
Q
, an injection
f:P\to Q
is a
weak embedding
if
f(x)\leq f(y)
whenever
x\leq y
, in which case
f(P)
is called a copy of
P
in
Q
. An injection
f:P\to Q
is a
strong embedding
if
f(x)\leq f(y)
if and only if
x\leq y
, and
f(P)
is then an
induced copy
of
P
in
Q
. A poset
Q
is
weakly
P
-free
if it contains no weak copy of
P
, and
strongly
P
-free
if it contains no induced copy of
P
.

A
Boolean lattice of dimension
n
, denoted
B_{n}
, is the
power set of an
n
-element ground set
X
equipped with inclusion relation.
In this paper,
unless otherwise noted,
we use the set
[n]=\{1,2,...,n\}

as a ground set of the Boolean lattice
B_{n}
of dimension
n
.
The
k
-th level
of
B_{n}
is the collection of all
k
-subsets of
[n]
, denoted by
{[n]\choose k}
.
Let
P
be a poset.
Let
|P|
denote the number of elements in
P
.
The
height

h(P)
of
P
is
the maximum number
t
such that
P
contains a
t
-chain. The
width

w(P)
of
P
is the number
w
of elements in a
largest set
\{a_{1},...,a_{w}\}
where no two elements are comparable.
We write
e(P)
for the largest
m

such that
P
cannot be embedded into any
m
consecutive layers of
B_{n}

for any
n
.

By a standard inclusion-exclusion count, the number of
t
-chains in a Boolean lattice has the following form.

Proposition 2.1
.

For positive integers
p
and
t
, the number of
t
-chains in
B_{p}
is

h_{p}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}(-1)^{t-1-i}\binom{t-1}{i}(i+2)^{p}.

In particular, for fixed
t
, we have
h_{p}(t)=\Theta((t+1)^{p})
as

.

Let
g(m,N)
denote the number of strong embeddings of
B_{m}
into
B_{N}
, and let
a(m)
denote the number of antichains in
B_{m}
. Axenovich and Walzer
[
1
]
gave the following estimate.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
, Count of Strong Embeddings)
.

Let

be two integers with

. Then

\frac{N!}{(N-m)!}(a(m)-m)^{N-m}\leq g(m,N)\leq\frac{N!}{(N-m)!}a(m)^{N-m}.

Here
a(m)=2^{{m\choose\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}(1+O(\log_{2}m/m))}
by the estimate of Kleitman and Markowsky
[
29
]
.

In this paper, we consider a
k
-coloring of
t
-chains,
which is an assignment of
k
colors,
say

,
to the

-chains of a given poset.
If

-coloring of elements of the poset.
A colored poset

is

if all of its
t
-chains
share the same color.

Ramsey theory on posets was initiated by Nešetřil and
Rödl
[
33
]
focusing on induced copies of posets.
Kierstead and Trotter
[
28
]
considered arbitrary hosts

studied Ramsey type problems in terms

For more details, we refer to
[

,
28
,
32
,
37
]
.
Recently, some

[

,
10
,
24
,
25
,
30
]
focused specifically on
finding induced copies of posets in the Boolean lattice.

A related extremal-set-theoretic line of work studies how many
t
-chains a poset-free family can contain. Katona
[
26
]
determined the maximum number of
2
-chains in a

-free family, and Gerbner and Patkós
[

]
extended this to

-free families for all

. This was later generalized to counting copies of one poset

in families avoiding another poset

[
20
]
. For chain copies, Gerbner, Methuku, Nagy, Patkós, and Vizer
[

]
proved a height-based dichotomy: when

, while when

it is smaller. Balogh, Martin, Nagy, and Patkós
[

]
further studied height-two forbidden posets for

. Unlike these weak, non-induced results, the present paper takes a Ramsey-coloring viewpoint and focuses on strong induced Boolean-lattice copies.

In 1984, Griggs, Stahl, and Trotter
[

initiated the study of

In

Definition 2.1
.

For a family

We use the simplified notation

.
When

, we abbreviate this to

Similarly, we define the

.

\operatorname{R}_{k}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{Q}\,|\,P_{1},P_{2},\dots,P_{k})
,

Let

Then

Similarly,

We now present several established findings regarding poset Ramsey numbers.
In the case of a 2-coloring scheme (i.e.,

) applied element-wise (i.e.,
t=1
) to an underlying poset,
the Ramsey number
\operatorname{R}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B}\,|\,B_{n},B_{m})

corresponding to Boolean lattices
B_{n}
and
B_{m}

has been investigated by
Axenovich and Walzer
[
1
]
, Lu and Thompson
[
30
]
,
Grósz, Methuku, and Tompkins
[
19
]
, Bohman and Peng
[
7
]
, and Walzer
[
39
]
.
Axenovich and Winter
[
2
,
3
]
as well as Winter
[
40
,
41
]

explored the characteristics of the poset Ramsey number
\operatorname{R}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B}\,|P,B_{n})
for a fixed
poset
P
and an
n
-dimensional Boolean lattice
B_{n}
, in the case where
n
tends to be sufficiently large.
Falgas-Ravry et al.
[

examined Ramsey properties associated with random posets.

Cox and Stolee
[

studied the case of
k
-coloring of

-chains in a Boolean lattice
with

and

,

.
Katona et al.
[

gave several lower and upper bounds on

-chains.

3
Relation between the strong and weak cases

A fundamental result connecting antichains and chain decompositions in finite posets is Dilworth’s theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Dilworth’s Theorem,
[
11
]
)
.

For every finite poset, the size of a largest antichain equals the minimum number of pairwise disjoint chains whose union is the whole poset.

We will also use Sperner’s theorem for the Boolean lattice.

Theorem 3.2

(Sperner’s Theorem,
[
16
, Theorem 1]
)
.

If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
is an antichain, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
. Moreover, equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{F}=\binom{[n]}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
or
\mathcal{F}=\binom{[n]}{\lceil n/2\rceil}
.

To simplify the notation, for a poset
P
and integers
n,l\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\geq 1
, we define

S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)=\{Q\subseteq B_{n}:\ Q\text{ is weakly }P\text{-free and }w(Q)<l\}

and

S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)=\{Q\subseteq B_{n}:\ Q\text{ is strongly }P\text{-free and }w(Q)<l\}.

When
n
and
l
are clear from the context, we simply write
S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P)
and
S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P)
.

Using Definition
1.3
, we now establish the basic relation between the weak and strong Boolean Ramsey–Turán numbers for
t
-chains. As before, in the special case
t=1
we abbreviate
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,1)
and
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,1)
to
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l)
and
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l)
, respectively.

Theorem 3.3
.

For any poset
P
and integers
n,l,t\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t).

Moreover, equality holds if and only if there exists a poset
Q_{0}\subseteq B_{n}
such that
Q_{0}
is weakly
P
-free,
w(Q_{0})<l
, and
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q_{0})|=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
.

Proof.

We first prove that
S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)\subseteq S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)
. Let
Q\in S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)
. Then
Q\subseteq B_{n}
,
w(Q)<l
, and
Q
is weakly
P
-free. Suppose, for a contradiction, that
Q\notin S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)
. Since the conditions
Q\subseteq B_{n}
and
w(Q)<l
are already satisfied, it follows that
Q
is not strongly
P
-free. Hence there exists a strong embedding
f:P\to Q
. By definition, a strong embedding is in particular order-preserving, so whenever
x\leq_{P}y
we have
f(x)\leq_{Q}f(y)
. Therefore
f
is also a weak embedding of
P
into
Q
, contradicting the assumption that
Q
is weakly
P
-free. This proves that every member of
S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)
also belongs to
S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)
.

Since
B_{n}
is finite, it has only finitely many subposets. Consequently, both
S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)
and
S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)
are finite families of finite posets, and the maxima appearing in the definitions of
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
and
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
are well defined. Because
S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)\subseteq S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)
and both Ramsey–Turán numbers maximize the same quantity
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|
, we obtain

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)=\max_{Q\in S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)}|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|\leq\max_{Q\in S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)}|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t).

We next characterize the equality case. Assume first that
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
. By the definition of
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
, there exists some
Q_{0}\in S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)
such that
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q_{0})|=\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
. Since the weak and strong Ramsey–Turán numbers are assumed to be equal, it follows that
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q_{0})|=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
. Hence
Q_{0}
is weakly
P
-free, satisfies
w(Q_{0})<l
, and attains the maximum defining the strong Boolean Ramsey–Turán number.

Conversely, suppose that there exists a poset
Q_{0}\subseteq B_{n}
such that
Q_{0}
is weakly
P
-free,
w(Q_{0})<l
, and
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q_{0})|=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
. Then
Q_{0}\in S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)
, and therefore the definition of
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
gives

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\geq|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q_{0})|=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t).

Combining this with the inequality already proved, we conclude that
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Let
C_{k}=(\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}\},\leq)
be the
k
-element chain with

a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{k}
, where
k\geq 1
.

We now show that for chain posets the weak and strong Boolean Ramsey–Turán numbers coincide.

Theorem 3.4
.

For any integers
k,n,l,t\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 1
and
l\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l,t)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l,t).

Proof.

We first show that, for every subposet
Q\subseteq B_{n}
, a map
f:C_{k}\to Q
is a weak embedding if and only if it is a strong embedding.

Indeed, every strong embedding is, by definition, a weak embedding. Conversely, let
f:C_{k}\to Q
be a weak embedding. We only need to prove the reverse implication in the order preservation. Assume that
f(a_{i})\leq_{Q}f(a_{j})
. If
a_{i}\nleq a_{j}
, then since
C_{k}
is a chain, we must have
a_{j}\leq a_{i}
. As
f
is weakly order-preserving, this implies
f(a_{j})\leq_{Q}f(a_{i})
. Hence both
f(a_{i})\leq_{Q}f(a_{j})
and
f(a_{j})\leq_{Q}f(a_{i})
hold, so by antisymmetry in
Q
we get
f(a_{i})=f(a_{j})
. Since
f
is injective, this is impossible unless
i=j
. Therefore
a_{i}\leq a_{j}
. It follows that
f
is a strong embedding. Thus weak and strong embeddings of
C_{k}
are the same.

We next deduce that

S_{\mathrm{weak}}(C_{k};n,l)=S_{\mathrm{strong}}(C_{k};n,l)
.
As shown in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, one always has

S_{\mathrm{weak}}(P;n,l)\subseteq S_{\mathrm{strong}}(P;n,l)
for every poset
P
.
For the reverse inclusion, let
Q\in S_{\mathrm{strong}}(C_{k};n,l)
. Then
Q\subseteq B_{n}
,
w(Q)<l
, and
Q
is strongly
C_{k}
-free. If
Q
were not weakly
C_{k}
-free, there would exist a weak embedding of
C_{k}
into
Q
. By the equivalence proved above, this embedding would in fact be a strong embedding, contradicting the assumption that
Q
is strongly
C_{k}
-free. Hence
Q
is weakly
C_{k}
-free, and therefore
Q\in S_{\mathrm{weak}}(C_{k};n,l)
. This proves the reverse inclusion.

Since the two admissible families are identical and both Ramsey–Turán numbers maximize the same quantity
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|
, we conclude that

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l,t)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l,t).

This completes the proof.
∎

Recall that
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,1)
. By Theorem
3.4
, the weak and strong Boolean Ramsey–Turán numbers coincide for chain posets, so it suffices to consider the strong case. When
t=1
, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{1}(Q)|=|Q|
, and hence the Ramsey–Turán number is simply the maximum size of an admissible subposet of
B_{n}
. We now determine this quantity exactly.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\geq 1
, let
k\geq 2
, and let
n\geq k-2
. If

l-1\leq\binom{n-k+2}{\left\lfloor(n-k+2)/2\right\rfloor},

then

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l)=\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l)=(k-1)(l-1).

In particular, the same conclusion holds whenever
n\geq k+l-3
.

Proof.

If
l=1
, then
w(Q)<1
forces every admissible poset
Q
to be empty, and the claimed value is
0
. Hence we may assume
l\geq 2
.

We first prove the upper bound. Let
Q\subseteq B_{n}
be strongly
C_{k}
-free with
w(Q)<l
. By Dilworth’s theorem,
Q
can be partitioned into at most
l-1
pairwise disjoint chains, say
D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
, where
m\leq l-1
. Since
Q
is strongly
C_{k}
-free, no chain in
Q
can have size at least
k
, for otherwise such a chain would contain a strong copy of
C_{k}
. Thus each
D_{i}
has size at most
k-1
. Therefore

|Q|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|D_{i}|\leq m(k-1)\leq(l-1)(k-1).

Since
Q
was arbitrary, it follows that
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l)\leq(k-1)(l-1)
.

For the lower bound, put
m=n-k+2
. By the assumption on
n
and
l
, the middle layer of
B_{m}
contains at least
l-1
elements. Choose distinct sets

A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{l-1}\in\binom{[m]}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}.

These sets are pairwise incomparable. Let
S_{0}=\emptyset
, and for
1\leq j\leq k-2
let

S_{j}=\{m+1,m+2,\dots,m+j\}\subseteq[n].

For each
i\in[l-1]
, define

C_{i}=\{A_{i}\cup S_{j}:j=0,1,\dots,k-2\}.

Each
C_{i}
is a chain of length
k-1
. Moreover, if
i\neq j
, then every element of
C_{i}
is incomparable with every element of
C_{j}
: indeed, an inclusion between
A_{i}\cup S_{a}
and
A_{j}\cup S_{b}
would imply
A_{i}\subseteq A_{j}
or
A_{j}\subseteq A_{i}
, contradicting the choice of
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
from one layer.

Set
Q=\bigcup_{i=1}^{l-1}C_{i}
. Then
Q
is the disjoint union of
l-1
pairwise incomparable chains, each of size
k-1
. Since no chain in
Q
has size
k
, the poset
Q
is strongly
C_{k}
-free. Also, any antichain in
Q
contains at most one element from each
C_{i}
, and choosing one element from each
C_{i}
gives an antichain of size
l-1
. Hence
w(Q)=l-1<l
. Finally,
|Q|=(l-1)(k-1)
, so

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,C_{k},l)\geq(k-1)(l-1)
.
Combining the upper and lower bounds gives the desired strong equality, and Theorem
3.4
gives the weak equality.

Finally, if
n\geq k+l-3
, then
m=n-k+2\geq l-1
, and hence

\binom{m}{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\geq m\geq l-1

for
l\geq 2
. Thus the displayed condition is satisfied. This completes the proof.
∎

4
Upper bounds for the poset Ramsey-Turán number

We begin with a simple monotonicity property of strong embeddings, which will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
P
be a finite poset, and let
f:P\to Q
be a strong embedding of
P
into a poset
Q
. Then
w(Q)\geq w(P)
and
h(Q)\geq h(P)
.

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq P
be an antichain of maximum size, so
|A|=w(P)
. Since
f
is a strong embedding, incomparable elements of
P
are mapped to incomparable elements of
Q
. Hence
f(A)
is an antichain in
Q
of size
w(P)
, and therefore
w(Q)\geq w(P)
.

Similarly, let
C\subseteq P
be a chain of maximum size, so
|C|=h(P)
. Since
f
is order-preserving, the image
f(C)
is a chain in
Q
of size
h(P)
. Thus
h(Q)\geq h(P)
.
∎

We next record a universal chain decomposition for all admissible subposets of the Boolean lattice. This is an immediate consequence of Dilworth’s theorem.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
P
be a finite poset and let
l
be a positive integer. If
Q\subseteq B_{n}
is a poset with
w(Q)<l
, then there exist pairwise disjoint chains
D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{l-1}
, some of which may be empty, such that

Q=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\cdots\cup D_{l-1},

and each
D_{i}
satisfies
|D_{i}|\leq n+1
.

Proof.

Since
w(Q)<l
and
w(Q)
is a nonnegative integer, we have
w(Q)\leq l-1
. By Dilworth’s theorem,
Q
can be partitioned into
w(Q)
pairwise disjoint chains. Adding
(l-1)-w(Q)
empty chains if necessary, we obtain pairwise disjoint chains
D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{l-1}
whose union is
Q
.

Finally, each
D_{i}
is a chain in
B_{n}
, and every chain in
B_{n}
has size at most
n+1
. Hence
|D_{i}|\leq n+1
for every
i
.
∎

Using the universal chain decomposition from Lemma
4.1
, we now derive a universal upper bound for the strong Boolean Ramsey–Turán number.

Theorem
1.1

(Restated)
.

Let
P
be a finite poset and let
l
be a positive integer. For every fixed positive integer
t
, we have

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }l=1,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}\binom{i(n+1)}{t},&\text{if }l\geq 2.\end{cases}

Moreover, as
n\to\infty
with
t
and
l
fixed,

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\leq\left(\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}i^{t}\right)n^{t}+O(n^{t-1}).

In particular, when
t=1
, this becomes

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l)\leq(l-1)n+O(1).

Proof.

Let
Q\subseteq B_{n}
be strongly
P
-free with
w(Q)<l
. If
l=1
, then
w(Q)<1
forces
Q=\emptyset
, and hence
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=0
. This proves the first case.

Assume now that
l\geq 2
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exist pairwise disjoint chains
D_{1},D_{2},\dots,D_{l-1}
, some of which may be empty, such that
Q=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\cdots\cup D_{l-1}
and
|D_{j}|\leq n+1
for every
j
.

For each
t
-chain
T\in\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)
, let

s(T)=\bigl|\{\,j\in[l-1]:T\cap D_{j}\neq\emptyset\,\}\bigr|

be the number of chains among
D_{1},\dots,D_{l-1}
that meet
T
. Since
T
has exactly
t
elements, we have
1\leq s(T)\leq\min(t,l-1)
.

For each integer
i
with
1\leq i\leq\min(t,l-1)
, define

\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}(Q)=\{\,T\in\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q):s(T)=i\,\}.

Then the families
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}(Q)
are pairwise disjoint and their union is
\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)
.

Fix such an
i
. To form a
t
-chain
T\in\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}(Q)
, one must first choose the
i
chains among
D_{1},\dots,D_{l-1}
that intersect
T
, and there are
\binom{l-1}{i}
ways to do this. Once these
i
chains are chosen, the chain
T
is a
t
-element subset of their union. Since each selected chain has size at most
n+1
, that union has size at most
i(n+1)
. Hence the number of possibilities for
T
is at most
\binom{i(n+1)}{t}
. Therefore

|\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}(Q)|\leq\binom{l-1}{i}\binom{i(n+1)}{t}.

Summing over all admissible values of
i
, we obtain

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}|\mathcal{C}_{t}^{(i)}(Q)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}\binom{i(n+1)}{t}.

Since
Q
was an arbitrary admissible poset in the definition of
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
, this proves the claimed upper bound for the strong number. The corresponding upper bound for the weak number follows from Theorem
3.3
.

For the asymptotic estimate, note that for each fixed
i
and fixed
t
,

\binom{i(n+1)}{t}=\frac{i^{t}}{t!}n^{t}+O(n^{t-1})\qquad(n\to\infty).

Substituting this into the previous bound gives

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}\left(\frac{i^{t}}{t!}n^{t}+O(n^{t-1})\right)=\left(\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,l-1)}\binom{l-1}{i}i^{t}\right)n^{t}+O(n^{t-1}),

which yields the asymptotic upper bound for
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
.

Finally, if
t=1
, then only the term
i=1
appears, and so

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l)\leq\binom{l-1}{1}\binom{n+1}{1}=(l-1)(n+1)=(l-1)n+O(1).

This completes the proof.
∎

We next characterize strong embeddings for antichains.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A_{k}
be the
k
-element antichain, and let
Q\subseteq B_{n}
be a poset. Then
Q
is strongly
A_{k}
-free if and only if
w(Q)\leq k-1
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
Q
is not strongly
A_{k}
-free. Then there exists a strong embedding
f:A_{k}\to Q
. Since any two distinct elements of
A_{k}
are incomparable, the definition of a strong embedding implies that any two distinct elements of
f(A_{k})
are also incomparable in
Q
. Hence
f(A_{k})
is an antichain of size
k
in
Q
, and therefore
w(Q)\geq k
.

Conversely, suppose that
w(Q)\geq k
. Then
Q
contains an antichain
\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k}\}
of size
k
. Let
A_{k}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}\}
, and define
f:A_{k}\to Q
by
f(a_{i})=s_{i}
for each
i\in[k]
. The map
f
is injective, and for any
i,j\in[k]
, we have
a_{i}\leq a_{j}
if and only if
i=j
, while
f(a_{i})\subseteq f(a_{j})
if and only if
i=j
, because
\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k}\}
is an antichain. Thus
f
is a strong embedding of
A_{k}
into
Q
, so
Q
is not strongly
A_{k}
-free.

Therefore,
Q
is strongly
A_{k}
-free if and only if
w(Q)\leq k-1
.
∎

Combining Theorem
1.1
with Lemma
4.2
, we obtain matching asymptotic bounds for antichains.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
P=A_{k}
be the
k
-element antichain, and let
L=\min\{l-1,k-1\}
. Then

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)\leq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }L=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,L)}\binom{L}{i}\binom{i(n+1)}{t},&\text{if }L\geq 1,\end{cases}

and, whenever
n\geq L
,

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)\geq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }L=0\text{ or }t>n-L+1,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle L\binom{n-L+1}{t},&\text{if }L\geq 1\text{ and }t\leq n-L+1.\end{cases}

In particular, for fixed
k,l,t
and
n\to\infty
, if
L\geq 1
, then
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)=\Theta(n^{t})
, and when
t=1
we have
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l)=Ln+O(1)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, a poset
Q\subseteq B_{n}
is strongly
A_{k}
-free if and only if
w(Q)\leq k-1
. Together with the condition
w(Q)<l
, this shows that every admissible
Q
satisfies
w(Q)\leq L
.

Applying the same counting argument as in Theorem
1.1
, but with
L
in place of
l-1
, we obtain

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)\leq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }L=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\min(t,L)}\binom{L}{i}\binom{i(n+1)}{t},&\text{if }L\geq 1.\end{cases}

For the lower bound, assume that
L\geq 1
and
n\geq L
. For each
i\in[L]
, define

C_{i}=\bigl\{\{i\}\cup\{L+1,L+2,\dots,L+s\}:s=0,1,\dots,n-L\bigr\}\subseteq B_{n},

and let
Q=\bigcup_{i=1}^{L}C_{i}
. Each
C_{i}
is a chain of size
n-L+1
, and if
X\in C_{i}
and
Y\in C_{j}
with
i\neq j
, then
i\in X\setminus Y
and
j\in Y\setminus X
, so
X
and
Y
are incomparable. Hence elements from different chains are incomparable, and thus every antichain in
Q
contains at most one element from each
C_{i}
. Conversely, choosing one element from each chain gives an antichain of size
L
, so
w(Q)=L\leq k-1
. By Lemma
4.2
,
Q
is strongly
A_{k}
-free, and since also
L\leq l-1
, we have
w(Q)<l
. Therefore
Q
is admissible in the sense of Definition
1.3
.

Moreover, every
t
-chain in
Q
lies entirely inside one of the chains
C_{i}
, while each
C_{i}
contains exactly
\binom{n-L+1}{t}
such
t
-chains. It follows that
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=L\binom{n-L+1}{t}
, which proves the lower bound.

For fixed
k,l,t
and
L\geq 1
, the lower bound satisfies
L\binom{n-L+1}{t}=\frac{L}{t!}n^{t}+O(n^{t-1})=\Omega(n^{t})
, whereas the upper bound is clearly
O(n^{t})
. Hence
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)=\Theta(n^{t})
. When
t=1
, the upper bound is at most
L(n+1)
and the lower bound is at least
L(n-L+1)
; their difference is the constant
L^{2}
, so
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l)=Ln+O(1)
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

The disjoint-chain construction used in Corollary
4.1
is asymptotically optimal in order for every fixed
t
, and it is tight up to an additive constant when
t=1
. For
t\geq 2
, the gap between the upper and lower bounds is caused by the universal upper bound allowing potential cross-chain contributions, whereas in our construction every
t
-chain lies inside a single chain.

Remark 4.2
.

The weak antichain case is quite different from the strong one. Since
A_{k}
has no nontrivial order relations, every
k
distinct elements of an arbitrary poset form a weak copy of
A_{k}
. Hence a poset
Q
is weakly
A_{k}
-free if and only if
|Q|\leq k-1
. Consequently, if
l\geq 2
and
n\geq k-2
, then

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,A_{k},l,t)=\binom{k-1}{t},

with the usual convention that
\binom{k-1}{t}=0
when
t>k-1
. For
l=1
, the value is
0
.

5
Structured lower bounds for Boolean Ramsey-Turán numbers

We now prove the lower-bound results stated in the introduction. The first one is a direct disjoint-chain construction for all non-chain posets.

Theorem
1.2

(Restated)
.

Let
P
be a finite poset with
w(P)=s\geq 2
. Let
l\geq 2
and put

L=\min\{l-1,s-1\}
.
If
n\geq L
and
1\leq t\leq n-L+1
, then

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\geq L\binom{n-L+1}{t}.

Consequently, for every fixed non-chain poset
P
and fixed integers
l,t
with
l\geq 2
, we have

\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)=\Theta(n^{t})

whenever
\min\{l-1,w(P)-1\}\geq 1
.

Proof.

For each
i\in[L]
, define

C_{i}=\bigl\{\{i\}\cup\{L+1,L+2,\dots,L+j\}:j=0,1,\dots,n-L\bigr\}\subseteq B_{n},

and set
Q=\bigcup_{i=1}^{L}C_{i}
.

Each
C_{i}
is a chain of size
n-L+1
. If
X\in C_{i}
and
Y\in C_{j}
with
i\neq j
, then
i\in X\setminus Y
and
j\in Y\setminus X
, so
X
and
Y
are incomparable. Hence
Q
is the disjoint union of
L
pairwise incomparable chains. It follows that
w(Q)=L<l
.

Since
L\leq s-1<w(P)
, Proposition
4.1
implies that
Q
contains no strong copy of
P
; otherwise such a copy would have width at least
w(P)=s
, whereas
Q
has width
L\leq s-1
. Thus
Q
is strongly
P
-free and is admissible in the definition of
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
.

Every
t
-chain of
Q
lies entirely inside one of the chains
C_{i}
, because elements from different chains are incomparable. Each chain
C_{i}
contains exactly
\binom{n-L+1}{t}

t
-chains. Therefore

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=L\binom{n-L+1}{t},

which proves the claimed lower bound.

For fixed
P,l,t
, the quantity
L
is a positive constant, and hence

L\binom{n-L+1}{t}=\frac{L}{t!}n^{t}+O(n^{t-1})=\Omega(n^{t}).

The universal upper bound in Theorem
1.1
gives
O(n^{t})
. Therefore
\operatorname{RT}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)=\Theta(n^{t})
. This completes the proof.
∎

For bounding the probability that the number of selected parent chains deviates from its expectation, we use the following Chernoff bound.

Theorem 5.1

(Chernoff Bound,
[
18
]
)
.

If
X
is a binomial random variable with expectation
\mu
, and
\epsilon>0
, then

\Pr[X\leq(1-\epsilon)\mu]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\mu}{2}\right),

and

\Pr[X\geq(1+\epsilon)\mu]\leq\left(\frac{e^{\epsilon}}{(1+\epsilon)^{1+\epsilon}}\right)^{\mu}.

Building on the structural construction of Boolean lattices in Assumption
1.1
, we restate our core result on the asymptotic lower bound for the Boolean Ramsey-Turán number
\mathrm{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)
as follows.

Theorem
1.3

(Restated)
.

Under Assumption
1.1
, for any integer-valued function
l(n)
satisfying
2\leq l(n)\leq\lfloor M_{n}^{\alpha}\rfloor
:

(i)

For fixed
l\geq 2
and all sufficiently large
n
,

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l,t)\geq\binom{r-1}{t}(l-1).

(1)

(ii)

For
l(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)\geq(1-o(1))\binom{r-1}{t}\,l(n).

(2)

In particular:

•

For
l(n)
in the above range with
l(n)=\omega(n^{c})
for any fixed
c>0
,
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)=\omega(n^{c})
.

•

For
l(n)=\lfloor M_{n}^{\beta}\rfloor
with
0<\beta\leq\alpha
,

\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)=\Omega(2^{\beta n}n^{-\beta/2}).

(3)

Moreover, the same lower bounds hold for the strong Boolean Ramsey-Turán number with the corresponding parameter
l
or
l(n)
.

Proof.

By Assumption
1.1
(A4), every subset of
\mathcal{L}
is weakly
P
-free. Since
\mathcal{L}\subseteq B_{n}
, any subset
Q\subseteq\mathcal{L}
with
w(Q)<l(n)
is admissible in the definition of
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)
. Thus it suffices to construct suitable subsets of
\mathcal{L}
and count their
t
-chains.

Write

M=\lfloor M_{n}^{\alpha}\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad c_{t}=\binom{r-1}{t}.

(4)

By Assumption
1.1
(A2), the family
\mathcal{L}
is the disjoint union of
M
chains, namely
\mathcal{L}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}C_{i}
, where each
C_{i}
has exactly
r-1
elements, the chains are pairwise incomparable, and each
C_{i}
contains exactly
c_{t}=\binom{r-1}{t}
distinct
t
-chains (consistent with (
4
)).

We consider two cases.

Case 1
.

Fixed
l\geq 2
.

For all sufficiently large
n
, we have
M\geq l-1
. Choose any
l-1
distinct chains
C_{i_{1}},\dots,C_{i_{l-1}}
from
\mathcal{L}
, and set
Q=\bigcup_{j=1}^{l-1}C_{i_{j}}
. Since different parent chains are pairwise incomparable by Assumption
1.1
(A2), any antichain in
Q
contains at most one element from each chosen chain. On the other hand, choosing one element from each of the
l-1
chains gives an antichain of size
l-1
. Hence
w(Q)=l-1<l
. By Assumption
1.1
(A4), the poset
Q
is weakly
P
-free.

Moreover, every
t
-chain of
Q
lies entirely inside one of the chosen chains, and each chosen chain contributes exactly
c_{t}=\binom{r-1}{t}

t
-chains. Therefore
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=(l-1)\binom{r-1}{t}
. Since
Q
is admissible, it follows that (
1
) holds.

Case 2
.

l(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

We now use a random selection of whole parent chains. Let

\delta_{n}=l(n)^{-1/3}
.
Then
\delta_{n}\to 0
and
\delta_{n}^{2}l(n)=l(n)^{1/3}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Since
l(n)\leq M
(from (
4
)), for all sufficiently large
n
we may define

p_{n}=\frac{(1-\delta_{n})l(n)}{M}\in(0,1].

Construct a random subset
\widetilde{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
by independently including each whole chain
C_{i}
with probability
p_{n}
, and excluding it otherwise. Let
N
denote the number of selected parent chains. Then
N\sim\operatorname{Bin}(M,p_{n})
and

\mathbb{E}[N]=Mp_{n}=(1-\delta_{n})l(n)
.

Because distinct parent chains are pairwise incomparable, every
t
-chain of
\widetilde{Q}
lies entirely inside a single selected parent chain. Since each selected parent chain contributes exactly
c_{t}=\binom{r-1}{t}

t
-chains, we have
|\mathcal{C}_{t}(\widetilde{Q})|=c_{t}\,N
. Also, any antichain in
\widetilde{Q}
contains at most one element from each selected parent chain, while choosing one element from each selected chain gives an antichain of size
N
. Hence
w(\widetilde{Q})=N
.

We claim that with positive probability,
(1-2\delta_{n})l(n)\leq N<l(n)
. Indeed, since
\mathbb{E}[N]=(1-\delta_{n})l(n)
, the upper-tail event
N\geq l(n)
can be written as

N\geq\left(1+\frac{\delta_{n}}{1-\delta_{n}}\right)\mathbb{E}[N].

Applying the Chernoff bound (Theorem
5.1
) with
\epsilon_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n}}{1-\delta_{n}}>0
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(N\geq l(n))\leq\left(\frac{e^{\epsilon_{n}}}{(1+\epsilon_{n})^{1+\epsilon_{n}}}\right)^{\mathbb{E}[N]}.

(5)

Since
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
, there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,

\left(\frac{e^{\epsilon_{n}}}{(1+\epsilon_{n})^{1+\epsilon_{n}}}\right)\leq\exp(-c\epsilon_{n}^{2}),

and therefore (
5
) implies

\mathbb{P}(N\geq l(n))\leq\exp\!\big(-c\epsilon_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}[N]\big).

Because
\epsilon_{n}=\Theta(\delta_{n})
and
\mathbb{E}[N]=\Theta(l(n))
, it follows that

\epsilon_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}[N]=\Theta(\delta_{n}^{2}l(n))=l(n)^{1/3}\to\infty,

and hence
\mathbb{P}(N\geq l(n))=o(1)
.

Similarly, the lower-tail event
N<(1-2\delta_{n})l(n)
can be rewritten as

N\leq\left(1-\frac{\delta_{n}}{1-\delta_{n}}\right)\mathbb{E}[N].

Applying the lower-tail form of the Chernoff bound with the same parameter
\epsilon_{n}=\delta_{n}/(1-\delta_{n})
, we get

\mathbb{P}\bigl(N<(1-2\delta_{n})l(n)\bigr)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{\epsilon_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}[N]}{2}\right).

(6)

By the same reasoning as above, the right-hand side of (
6
) is
o(1)
.

Therefore, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big((1-2\delta_{n})l(n)\leq N<l(n)\Big)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\bigl(N<(1-2\delta_{n})l(n)\bigr)-\mathbb{P}(N\geq l(n))=1-o(1)>0

for all sufficiently large
n
. Hence there exists a deterministic subset
Q^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
such that
(1-2\delta_{n})l(n)\leq w(Q^{*})<l(n)

and

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q^{*})|=c_{t}\,N\geq(1-2\delta_{n})c_{t}\,l(n)=(1-2\delta_{n})\binom{r-1}{t}l(n).

By Assumption
1.1
(A4), the subset
Q^{*}
is weakly
P
-free. Therefore
Q^{*}
is admissible in the definition of
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)
, and so
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)\geq(1-2\delta_{n})\binom{r-1}{t}l(n)
. Since
\delta_{n}=l(n)^{-1/3}\to 0
, we conclude that (
2
) holds.
This proves parts (i) and (ii).

For the first consequence, if
l(n)
is in the above range and
l(n)=\omega(n^{c})
for any fixed
c>0
, then by (
2
),
(1-o(1))\binom{r-1}{t}l(n)=\omega(n^{c})
, and hence
\operatorname{RT}(\mathcal{B};n,P,l(n),t)=\omega(n^{c})
.

For the second consequence, if
l(n)=\lfloor M_{n}^{\beta}\rfloor
with
0<\beta\leq\alpha
, then by Stirling’s approximation,

M_{n}=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\Theta\!\left(\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Therefore, we have

l(n)=\lfloor M_{n}^{\beta}\rfloor=\Theta\!\left(\left(\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{\beta}\right)=\Theta\!\left(2^{\beta n}n^{-\beta/2}\right),

and combining with (
2
) we conclude that (
3
) holds.

Finally, the same lower bounds hold for the corresponding strong Boolean Ramsey-Turán number. Indeed, every subset of
\mathcal{L}
has height at most
r-1<h(P)
, so it contains neither a weak copy nor a strong copy of
P
. Hence all subsets constructed above are also admissible for the strong Boolean Ramsey-Turán number. This completes the proof.
∎

Having established the asymptotic lower bound above, we next show that this construction is asymptotically tight up to a constant factor inside the structured family
\mathcal{L}
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Under Assumption
1.1
, for any
Q\subseteq\mathcal{L}
with
w(Q)<l(n)
, we have

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|\leq\binom{r-1}{t}(l(n)-1).

Proof.

Let
S=\{\,i:C_{i}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\,\}
be the set of parent chains with non-empty intersection with
Q
.

By Assumption
1.1
(A2), elements from different parent chains are pairwise incomparable. Hence any antichain in
Q
contains at most one element from each parent chain in
S
, so
w(Q)\leq|S|
. Conversely, for each
i\in S
, choose one element of
C_{i}\cap Q
. Since these chosen elements lie in distinct parent chains, they are pairwise incomparable, and therefore form an antichain of size
|S|
. Thus
w(Q)\geq|S|
. Therefore
w(Q)=|S|
.
Since
w(Q)<l(n)
, it follows that
|S|=w(Q)\leq l(n)-1
.

Now every
t
-chain of
Q
lies inside a single parent chain, because different parent chains are pairwise incomparable. Also, each parent chain
C_{i}
has exactly
r-1
elements, so any subset of
C_{i}
contains at most
\binom{r-1}{t}

t
-chains. Hence

|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q)|=\sum_{i\in S}|\mathcal{C}_{t}(Q\cap C_{i})|\leq\sum_{i\in S}\binom{r-1}{t}=\binom{r-1}{t}|S|\leq\binom{r-1}{t}(l(n)-1).

This completes the proof.
∎
