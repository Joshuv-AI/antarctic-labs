Infinitesimal Minkowskianity for manifolds with continuous Lorentzian metrics
Vanessa Ryborz

Abstract
We prove that any metric measure spacetime arising from a smooth manifold M M endowed with a continuous Lorentzian metric g g is infinitesimally Minkowskian, under the assumption that ( M , g ) (M,g) is causally simple. † † footnotetext: MSC2020: 53C50, 53C23, 53B30 Keywords . Lorentzian manifolds of low regularity, metric measure spacetimes, Lorentzian length spaces, causal functions

Introduction

The geometry of metric (measure) spaces is an active field of study across analysis, metric and differential geometry, and probability.
Metric measure spaces can be seen as a generalisation of smooth Riemannian manifolds, which turn out to carry enough information to allow generalisations of several core notions from differential geometry. These include upper and lower sectional curvature bounds, which can be defined on length spaces (see for instance
[
2
]
), the celebrated curvature dimension condition
\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
based on optimal transport introduced by Lott-Villani
[
34
,
33
]
and Sturm
[
46
,
47
]
(a synthetic definition of lower Ricci curvature and upper dimension bounds), and a generalised first order calculus (see
[
4
]
).

Analogous approaches were recently started in Lorentzian signature: After the pioneering work by Kronheimer and Penrose
[
28
]
and Busemann
[
15
]
, Kunzinger and Sämann
[
31
]
proposed the notion of Lorentzian (pre-)length spaces, a synthetic approach to Lorentzian geometry. They also studied timelike sectional curvature bounds through timelike triangle comparison.

A burst of activity in the field of non-smooth Lorentzian geometry followed:
Cavalletti and Mondino
[
19
]
developed optimal transport tools and a theory of synthetic timelike lower Ricci curvature bounds for Lorentzian length spaces (building on
[
36
]
and
[
42
]
that treated the case of smooth Lorentzian manifolds), together with a synthetic Hawking singularity theorem. Mondino and Suhr proposed a synthetic definition of upper timelike Ricci curvature bounds leading to a synthetic formulation of the Einstein equations in
[
42
]
.
In the flavour of the celebrated singularity theorems on smooth spacetimes, main developments in the non-smooth case include the study of cones and related singularity theorems
[
1
]
, the relationship between spacetime inextendability and synthetic curvature blow-up established in
[
26
]
, singularity theorems under variable timelike lower Ricci curvature bounds
[
9
]
, and an extension of the Penrose singularity theorem to continuous spacetimes satisfying a synthetic null energy condition
[
17
]
.
Further developments include a Lorentzian splitting theorem for Lorentzian length spaces with non-negative timelike sectional curvature
[
8
]
, a definition of Lorentzian Hausdorff measures and dimension
[
35
]
, the study of time functions on Lorentzian length spaces
[
13
]
, a definition of timelike lower Ricci curvature bounds on Finsler spacetimes
[
10
]
, and a new Lorentzian isoperimetric-type inequality under timelike lower Ricci curvature bounds
[
18
]
.

In
[
7
]
, the authors introduce the notion of metric measure spacetimes, a framework largely consistent with the works by Kunzinger-Sämann, Cavalletti-Mondino, and Mondino-Suhr. In this setting, they prove a d’Alembertian comparison (see also
[
11
]
) and develop a Lorentzian differential first order calculus.
Based on that, they define a synthetic criterion, called
infinitesimal Minkowskianity
, to distinguish those metric measure spacetimes whose time separation arises from a Lorentzian inner product from Lorentz-Finsler structures. This criterion can be seen as a Lorentzian counterpart of infinitesimal Hilbertianity, which was introduced in
[
5
,
24
]
for metric measure spaces and which provided a natural framework for remarkable structural results
[
40
,
12
]
when combined with Lott-Sturm-Villani’s
\mathsf{CD}(K,N)
condition.

A vast class of metric measure spacetimes arises from manifolds
M
endowed with non-smooth Lorentzian metrics
g
.
They have been studied in
[
21
,
45
,
25
,
16
]
. Such manifolds serve to describe various phenomena from general relativity, examples being the inside or outside of a star, spacetimes with conical singularities, and cosmic strings (
[
48
,
49
]
).
In this paper, we study the case that
g
is continuous. A physically relevant example is provided by Penrose’s impulsive gravitational waves (see for instance
[
43
]
and
[
27
, Chapter 20]
).

Both metric measure spacetimes and manifolds endowed with continuous Lorentzian metrics can be seen as generalisations of smooth Lorentzian manifolds, hence a naturally emerging question is whether these two generalisations are compatible. In Riemannian signature, such compatibility questions were studied in
[
14
,
29
,
41
,
30
,
22
]
.
The goal of this paper is to compare the classical first order calculus on manifolds with the first order calculus on metric measure spacetimes developed in
[
7
]
. This work can be seen as a Lorentzian counterpart to
[
14
]
and
[
41
, Section 4]
.

The main result of this paper is:

Theorem 1
.

Let
M
be a
(d+1)
-dimensional manifold and
g
be a continuous Lorentzian metric on
M
such that
(M,g)
is a causally simple spacetime.
Then the metric measure spacetime
(M,\ell_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
is infinitesimally Minkowskian.

Here, the causal simplicity is an assumption of technical nature as we merely use strong causality in our proofs, see Remark
4.6
.

The proof of Theorem
1
essentially consists of two steps:

•

We verify that for any sufficiently regular causal curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
, and almost every
t\in[0,1]
, the causal speed
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
which is almost everywhere given by
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|=\lim_{s\searrow t}\frac{\ell(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{s})}{|s-t|}
essentially coincides with the Lorentzian length of the derivative
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|_{g}=\sqrt{-g(\dot{\gamma}_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t})}
. This step is contained in Corollary
4.16
.

•

We prove that, for a causal function
f
, the maximal weak subslope is given by
|\nabla_{g}f|_{g}
, wherever
f
is finite and differentiable, and
+\infty
wherever
f
is infinite or not differentiable. The idea of the proof is similar to the proof in the Riemannian signature
[
41
, Proposition 4.24]
but in this setting an extra difficulty arises as we may work with functions that are not
C^{1}
. We use that causal functions are of bounded variation (see
[
7
]
), so the distributional derivative of
f
is given by a measure
\mathcal{D}f
, which potentially has a singular part
\mathcal{D}f^{s}
. Using the Besicovitch covering theorem allows us to suitably localise away from the singular part
\mathcal{D}f^{s}
(Lemma
3.2
) and then perform a contradiction argument in Lemma
4.22
.

The paper is organised as follows: In Section
2
, we recall the synthetic Lorentzian theory from
[
7
]
. Thereafter, in Section
3
, we establish relevant measure theoretic results that will be needed in the proof of Theorem
1
. Finally, in Section
4
, we recall how a manifold with a continuous Lorentzian metric enters the class of metric measure spacetimes and then compare the synthetic first order calculus to the classical manifold calculus to prove Theorem
1
.

Synthetic Lorentzian spaces and causal functions

We will briefly summarise relevant aspects of the theory on metric (measure) spacetimes following
[
7
]
. We will adapt the infinity conventions from
[
7
]
which are given by

\displaystyle+\infty-(+\infty):=+\infty,

\displaystyle-\infty-(-\infty):=+\infty,

\displaystyle\pm\infty+z:=\pm\infty

\displaystyle\quad\mathrm{for}\ z\in[-\infty,+\infty],

\displaystyle(\pm\infty)\cdot 0:=0.

Given a set
M
and a function
\ell\colon M\times M\to\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty]
, we call
\ell
a
time separation
if for all
x,y,z\in M
it holds

\displaystyle\ell(x,x)=0,\quad\min(\ell(x,y)+\ell(y,z),\ell(y,z)+\ell(x,y))\leq\ell(x,z).

Define the transitive relations chronology
\ll
and causality
\leq
via

\displaystyle\ll:=\ell^{-1}((0,+\infty]),\ \leq:=\ell^{-1}([0,+\infty]).

We call a subset
A
of
M

achronal
, if no two points in
A
are related by
\ll
.

Definition 2.1
.

We call
(M,\ell)
a
metric spacetime
if
\ell
is a time separation and
\leq
is a partial order. We call
(M,\tau,\ell)
a
Polish metric spacetime
if
\tau
is a Polish topology on the metric spacetime
(M,\ell)
and if
\{\ell\geq 0\}
is a closed set in
M^{2}
.

Definition 2.2

(
[
7
]
, Definition 3.1)
.

A function
f\colon M\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is called a
causal function
if
f
is causally order-preserving, i.e., for all points
x,y\in M
with
x\leq y
it holds
f(x)\leq f(y)
. We denote by
\mathrm{Dom}(f)
the domain of
f
, i.e., the set of all
x\in M
such that
f(x)
is a real number.

Definition 2.3

(
[
7
]
, Definition 2.26)
.

A map
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
is called a
left continuous causal path
if it is causal, i.e., for
s\leq t
it holds
\gamma_{s}\leq\gamma_{t}
, and left continuous, i.e., for all
t\in(0,1]
the limit
\gamma_{t-}:=\lim_{s\nearrow t}\gamma_{s}
exists and equals
\gamma_{t}
. The space of all left continuous causal paths is denoted
\mathrm{LCC}([0,1],M)
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
7
]
, Lemma 3.2)
.

Every causal function
f
has a representative that is continuous outside a countable union of achronal sets.

Definition 2.5

(
[
7
]
, Theorem 2.23 - Definition 2.25)
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
be a causal path. Then there exists a maximal Borel measure
|\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|
on
[0,1]
such that for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
, it holds
|\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|([s,t])\leq\ell(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})
. Denote its Lebesgue decomposition with respect to

as
|\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|=|\dot{\gamma}|\mathcal{L}^{1}+|\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}|^{s}
.
Then, the function
|\dot{\gamma}|
is called the
causal speed
of
\gamma
. It is for almost every
t\in[0,1]
given by

\displaystyle|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|=\lim_{s\nearrow t}\frac{\ell(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})}{t-s}=\lim_{s\searrow t}\frac{\ell(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{s})}{s-t}.

(2.1)

Definition 2.6

(
[
7
]
, Definition 2.48)
.

A
metric measure spacetime
is a quadruple
(M,\tau,\ell,\mathfrak{m})
, where
(M,\tau,\ell)
is a Polish metric spacetime and
\mathfrak{m}
a non-negative and non-zero Radon measure on
M
.

Given a topological space
X
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(X)
the space of Borel probability measures on
X
. Given two topological spaces
X,Y
, a Borel map
T:X\to Y
, and a Borel measure
\mu
on
X
, the pushforward
T_{\#}\mu
of
\mu
by
T
is defined via
T_{\#}\mu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))
, for every Borel set
B\subset Y
.
For
t\in[0,1]
, the evaluation map
e_{t}\colon\mathrm{LCC}([0,1],M)\to M
is defined via
e_{t}(\gamma):=\gamma_{t}
.

Definition 2.7

(
[
7
]
, Definition 3.7)
.

A measure
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{LCC}([0,1],M))
is called a
test plan
if it has bounded compression, i.e., if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
t\in[0,1]
it holds

\displaystyle(e_{t})_{\#}\boldsymbol{\pi}\leq C\mathfrak{m}.

Definition 2.8

(
[
7
]
, Definition 3.11)
.

Let
(M,\tau,\ell,\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure spacetime and
f\colon M\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
a causal function. A Borel function
G\colon M\to[0,\infty]
is called a
weak subslope
of

if for every test plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{LCC}([0,1],M))
, it holds

\displaystyle\int[f(\gamma_{1})-f(\gamma_{0})]\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma)\geq\int\int_{0}^{1}G(\gamma_{t})|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\,{\rm d}t{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma).

(2.2)

By Theorem 3.14 in
[
7
]
, there exists a unique weak subslope
G
that is no smaller than any other weak subslope. We will call it the

and denote it by
|{\rm d}f|
.

Definition 2.9

(
[
7
]
, Defintion 1.4)
.

We call
(M,\tau,\ell,\mathfrak{m})

infinitesimally Minkowskian
if for any two causal functions
f,g\colon M\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, it holds

\displaystyle 2|{\rm d}(f+g)|^{2}+2|{\rm d}f|^{2}=|{\rm d}g|^{2}+|{\rm d}(2f+g)|^{2}\ \mathfrak{m}-\mathrm{a.e.}

(2.3)

Given a test plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, we denote by
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
the density of the push-forward of the measure
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\pi}(\gamma){\rm d}t
under the “full” evaluation map

. Define
\mathrm{Vis}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\{\rho_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}>0\}
. We define
\mathrm{Vis}(M)
as the essential union of the sets
\mathrm{Vis}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
, as
\boldsymbol{\pi}
varies over all test plans (see
[
23
, Proposition 3.1.9]
).
We will use that a property holds
\mathfrak{m}
-almost everywhere on
\mathrm{Vis}(M)
if and only if it holds
\mathfrak{m}
-almost everywhere on
\mathrm{Vis}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
for every test plan
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
Finally denote
\mathrm{Invis}(M):=M\setminus\mathrm{Vis}(M)
.

Lemma 2.10

(
[
7
]
, Lemma 3.19)
.

Let
(M,\tau,\ell,\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure spacetime and
A\subset M
be an achronal set. Then
\mathfrak{m}(A\setminus\mathrm{Invis}(M))=0
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
(M,\tau,\ell,\mathfrak{m})
be a metric measure spacetime and
f
be a causal function. Then for
\mathfrak{m}
-almost every
p\in\{|f|<\infty\}\cap\mathrm{Vis}(M)
, there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
p
such that
\mathfrak{m}(U\setminus\{|f|<\infty\})=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, we can find a representative
\tilde{f}
of
f
and achronal sets
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\tilde{f}
is continuous outside
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
. We will from now on write
f
for
\tilde{f}
. Now Lemma
2.10
yields that
\mathfrak{m}
-almost every point in
\{|f|<\infty\}\cap\mathrm{Vis}(M)
is in
F:=\{|f|<\infty\}\cap\mathrm{Vis}(M)\setminus\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}
. Pick a point

. As

is continuous and finite at

, we get that there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of

such that
f
is finite on
U
.
∎

3
Preliminary results from measure theory

The following measure theoretic results will be useful in Section
4
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and let
\mu
be a locally finite Radon measure on
\Omega
.
For
\delta>0
define the set
\Omega_{\delta}:=\{p\in\Omega:\overline{B_{\delta}(p)}\subset\Omega\}
.
Then for every
\delta>0
, the map
E_{\mu,\delta}\colon\Omega_{\delta}\to[0,\infty),\,p\mapsto\mu(\overline{B_{\delta}(p)})
is
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-measurable and locally bounded.

Proof.

Fix a
\delta>0
.
Fix a compact set
K_{1}\subset\Omega_{\delta}
and a compact set
K_{2}\subset\Omega
such that
\overline{B_{\delta}(K_{1})}\subset K_{2}
. Now for each
p\in K_{1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mu(\overline{B_{\delta}(p)})\leq\mu(K_{2})<\infty.

This proves the local boundedness.

For
i=0,\ldots,m-1
, define the sets
P_{i}
and the Radon measures
\mu_{i+1},\nu_{i}
such that

•

P_{i}
is a countable union of
i
-dimensional spheres, where
i
-dimensional spheres are given by
\{q\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:|q-p|=r,q-p\in V\}
, where
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
r>0
and
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an
i
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. If
i=0
,
P_{0}
is going to be a countable set of points.

•

\nu_{i}=\mu_{i}{\mathchoice{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to7.0pt{\hrule width=7.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to7.0pt{\hrule width=7.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to5.0pt{\hrule width=5.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to3.35pt{\hrule width=3.35pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}}P_{i}
.

•

\mu_{i+1}=\mu_{i}-\nu_{i}
is zero on spheres of dimension at most
i
.

Define
\mu_{0}:=\mu
.
Now, define the set

\displaystyle P_{0}:=\{p\in K_{2}:\mu(\{p\})>0\}.

This set has to be countable, as
P_{0}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{p\in K_{2}:\mu(\{p\})>\frac{1}{n}\}
, and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
\#\{p\in K_{2}:\mu(\{p\})>\frac{1}{n}\}\leq n\mu(K_{2})<\infty
.
Now define the measures

\displaystyle\nu_{0}:=\mu{\mathchoice{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to7.0pt{\hrule width=7.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to7.0pt{\hrule width=7.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to5.0pt{\hrule width=5.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to3.35pt{\hrule width=3.35pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}}P_{0},\ \mu_{1}:=\mu-\nu_{0}.

(3.1)

Now for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
, define inductively the measures
\nu_{i},\mu_{i+1}
as follows:
Let

\displaystyle P_{i}:=\{S:i\mathrm{-dimensional\ sphere},\ S\subset K_{2},\ \mu_{i}(S)>0\}.

(3.2)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle\#\{S:i\mathrm{-dimensional\ sphere},\ S\subset K_{2},\ \mu_{i}(S)>1/n\}<n\mu_{i}(K_{2})\leq n\mu(K_{2})<\infty.

(3.3)

Indeed, suppose that this was not true. Then there exist two distinct
i
-dimensional spheres
S_{\alpha},S_{\beta}\in P_{i}
such that
\mu_{i}(S_{\alpha}\cap S_{\beta})>0
.
Now, we have that for any two distinct
i
-dimensional spheres, their intersection is the finite union of spheres of dimension at most
i-1
. By our assumption, the measure
\mu_{i}
is zero on spheres of dimension at most
i-1
, which yields a contradiction and hence verifies (
3.3
).
This proves that
P_{i}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{S:i\mathrm{-dimensional\ sphere},\ S\subset K_{2},\ \mu_{i}(S)>1/n\}
is countable.
Now we define

\displaystyle\nu_{i}:=\mu_{i}{\mathchoice{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to7.0pt{\hrule width=7.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to7.0pt{\hrule width=7.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to5.0pt{\hrule width=5.0pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}{\,\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt\vbox to3.35pt{\hrule width=3.35pt,height=0.2pt}}\,}}P_{i},\ \mu_{i+1}:=\mu_{i}-\nu_{i}.

(3.4)

In total, this gives us a decomposition of the measure

\displaystyle\mu=\mu_{m}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\nu_{i},

(3.5)

where the
\nu_{i}
are supported on countably many
i
-dimensional spheres (collected in
P_{i}
) and
\mu_{m}
is zero on spheres of dimension smaller than
m
.

For each
i\leq m-1
, and any
i
-dimensional sphere
S
, we have that the set

\displaystyle D_{S,\delta}:=\{p:S\subset\partial B_{\delta}(p)\}

is a sphere of dimension
m-i-1
. Any such sphere is a Borel
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-zero set.
Then, the set

\displaystyle D:=\bigcup_{i=0}^{m-1}\bigcup_{S\in P_{i}}D_{S,\delta}

(3.6)

is a Borel
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-zero set, which is a countable union of spheres of dimension at most
m-1
.
For any point
p\in K_{1}\setminus D
, we have that

\displaystyle\mu(\partial B_{\delta}(p))=\mu_{m}(\partial B_{\delta}(p))=0.

Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(B_{\delta+2^{-n}}(p)\setminus B_{\delta-2^{-n}}(p))=\mu(\partial B_{\delta}(p))=0.

(3.7)

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu(B_{\delta+2^{-n}}(p)\setminus B_{\delta-2^{-n}}(p))\leq\varepsilon
.
Then we have that for each
q\in B_{2^{-n-1}}(p)
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\mu(\overline{B_{\delta}(p)})-\mu(\overline{B_{\delta}(q)})|

\displaystyle\leq\mu((\overline{B_{\delta}(p)}\setminus\overline{B_{\delta}(q)})\cup(\overline{B_{\delta}(q)}\setminus\overline{B_{\delta}(p)}))\leq\mu(B_{\delta+2^{-n}}(p)\setminus B_{\delta-2^{-n}}(p))\leq\varepsilon.

(3.8)

Hence,
E_{\mu,\delta}
is continuous at
p
. This holds for
p\in K_{1}\setminus D
, so the map is continuous
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-almost everywhere. Hence,
E_{\mu,\delta}
is
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-measurable when restricted to
K_{1}
. As
K_{1}\subset\Omega_{\delta}
was arbitrary, this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mu
be a locally finite Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\mu\perp\mathcal{L}^{m}
.
Then for
\mathcal{L}^{m}
-almost every point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mu(B_{\delta}(p))}{\delta^{m}}=0.

(3.9)

Proof.

Suppose this was not the case. Then there exist an
\varepsilon>0
and a bounded set
A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\mathcal{L}^{m}(A^{\prime})>0
such that for each
p\in A^{\prime}
it holds

\displaystyle\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mu(B_{\delta}(p))}{\delta^{m}}\geq 2\varepsilon>0.

(3.10)

Note that by Lemma
3.1
, we have that the set of all points satisfying (
3.10
) is Lebesgue measurable.
As
\mu\perp\mathcal{L}^{m}
, there exists a decomposition of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
into disjoint Borel sets
L,S
such that
\mathcal{L}^{m}(S)=0=\mu(L)
and
L\cup S=\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Define
A:=A^{\prime}\setminus S
. Then
\mathcal{L}^{m}(A^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}^{m}(A)>0
. Pick an open set
\tilde{O}\supset A
. Then for each point
p\in A
, there exists a
\delta_{p}>0
such that
B_{\delta_{p}}(p)\subset\tilde{O}
and such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(B_{\delta_{p}}(p))}{(\delta_{p})^{m}}\geq\varepsilon.

(3.11)

Then

\displaystyle A\subset\bigcup_{p\in A}B_{\delta_{p}}(p)\subset\tilde{O}.

By the Besicovitch covering theorem, there exists a constant
c_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
such that there exist
c_{m}
countable families
T_{1},\ldots,T_{c_{m}}
of points
p\in A
such that

\displaystyle A\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{c_{m}}\bigcup_{p\in T_{i}}B_{\delta_{p}}(p)=:O\subset\tilde{O},

and such that for each
i
and
p,p^{\prime}\in T_{i}
, it holds

\displaystyle B_{\delta_{p^{\prime}}}(p^{\prime})\cap B_{\delta_{p}}(p)=\emptyset.

(3.12)

By the pigeonhole principle, we get that there exists an
i\in\{1,\ldots,c_{m}\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{m}\Big(\bigcup_{p\in T_{i}}B_{\delta_{p}}(p)\Big)\geq\frac{1}{c_{m}}\mathcal{L}^{m}(A).

(3.13)

But now (
3.12
) together with (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) yields that

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\bigcup_{p\in T_{i}}B_{\delta_{p}}(p)\Big)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{L}^{m}(B_{1}(0))}\sum_{p\in T_{i}}\mathcal{L}^{m}(B_{\delta_{p}}(p))\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{m}\mathcal{L}^{m}(B_{1}(0))}\mathcal{L}^{m}(A).

(3.14)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu(\tilde{O})\geq\mu(O)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{m}\mathcal{L}^{m}(B_{1}(0))}\mathcal{L}^{m}(A).

As
\tilde{O}
is an arbitrary open set containing
A
, the outer regularity of Radon measures on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
yields that

\displaystyle\mu(A)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{m}\mathcal{L}^{m}(B_{1}(0))}\mathcal{L}^{m}(A)>0.

(3.15)

But
A\subset L
and
\mu(L)=0
by assumption. This is a contradiction and finishes the proof.
∎

For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we denote by
A^{c}
its complement, i.e.,
A^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus A
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be Lebesgue measurable and
P,Q\subset A
such that
\mathcal{L}^{m}(A\cap P^{c})=0
. Assume that for each
p\in P
there exists a radius
r_{p}>0
such that
\mathcal{L}^{m}(Q^{c}\cap B_{r_{p}}(p))=0
. Then,
\mathcal{L}^{m}(A\cap Q^{c})=0
. In particular,
Q
is measurable.

Proof.

We may assume
A
to be bounded.
Then
A\cap P\subset\bigcup_{p\in P}B_{r_{p}}(p)
. The Besicovitch covering theorem yields that there exists a countable set
(p_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset P
such that

\displaystyle A\cap P\subset\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}B_{r_{p_{i}}}(p_{i}).

Denote by
\mathcal{L}^{m*}
the outer
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Now we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{m*}(A\cap Q^{c})\leq\mathcal{L}^{m*}(A\cap P^{c})+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{L}^{m*}(B_{r_{p_{i}}}(p_{i})\cap Q^{c})=0.

Now null sets are Lebesgue measurable, which proves the lemma.
∎

4
Application to manifolds with non-smooth Lorentzian metrics

We want to study how the theory on causal functions as it has been developed in
[
7
]
applies to the setting of a Lorentzian manifold with a metric of low regularity.
From now on the setting will be a smooth
(d+1)
-manifold
M
, for
d\geq 1
, equipped with a continuous Lorentzian metric
g
, i.e., a continuous symmetric bilinear
(0,2)
-tensor of signature
(-,+,\ldots,+)
.
We say that a vector
v\in TM
is
timelike
if
g(v,v)<0
,
null
or
lightlike
if
g(v,v)=0
and
v\neq 0
,
spacelike
if
v=0
or
g(v,v)>0
, and
causal
if it is timelike or null.
We assume that
g
is
time orientable
, i.e., there exists a continuous timelike vector field
X
which tells us at each point, which cone is the future cone and which cone is the past cone. More precisely, we say that a causal vector
v\in TM
is future directed, if
g(v,X)<0
, otherwise we say that it is past directed. Time orientable Lorentzian manifolds are called
spacetimes
.

For causal vectors
v\in TM
, we define
|v|_{g}:=\sqrt{-g(v,v)}
. Recall that for
p\in M
and
v,w\in T_{p}M
causal, the reverse Cauchy-Schwartz inequality holds, i.e.,

\displaystyle|g(v,w)|\geq|v|_{g}|w|_{g},

(4.1)

with equality if and only if
v=\alpha w
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

A spacetime arising from a manifold with a continuous Lorentzian metric is naturally associated with a topology, a time separation, and a measure as follows:

•

Any complete Riemannian metric
h
on
M
induces a distance
\mathsf{d}_{h}
on
M
such that
(M,\mathsf{d}_{h})
is a Polish metric space. Fix some such
h
and denote by
\tau_{h}
the topology induced by
\mathsf{d}_{h}
. Note that
\tau_{h}
equals the topology induced by charts, so from now on we will not always mention the topology explicitly.

•

The metric
g
induces a volume measure locally given by
{\rm d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\sqrt{-\det g}\,{\rm d}\mathcal{L}^{d+1}
.

•

We say that an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
is
classically causal
(c.c.) and future directed (past directed) if
g(\dot{\gamma}_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t})\leq 0
and
\dot{\gamma}_{t}
is future directed (past directed) or zero for almost every
t\in[0,1]
.

•

The time separation
\ell_{g}:M^{2}\to\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty]
is defined as

\displaystyle\ell_{g}(x,y)=\sup\Big\{L_{g}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{-g(\dot{\gamma}_{t},\dot{\gamma}_{t})}\,{\rm d}t\Big|\ \gamma\in C^{0,1}([0,1],M)\ \mathrm{c.c.,\ future\ dir.},\ \gamma_{0}=x,\gamma_{1}=y.\Big\}.

(4.2)

We then define the chronological future and past
I^{+},I^{-}
of a point
p\in M
via

\displaystyle I^{+}(p)=\{q\in M:p\ll q\},\ I^{-}(p)=\{q\in M:q\ll p\},

and the causal future and past
J^{+},J^{-}
of a point
p\in M
via

\displaystyle J^{+}(p)=\{q\in M:p\leq q\},\ J^{-}(p)=\{q\in M:q\leq p\},

where the chronological relation
\ll:=\ll_{g}
is defined via
\ll_{g}:=\ell_{g}^{-1}((0,\infty])\subset M^{2}
and the causal relation
\leq:=\leq_{g}
is defined via
\leq_{g}:=\ell_{g}^{-1}([0,\infty])\subset M^{2}
. From now on when speaking of classically causal curves, we always refer to future directed classically causal curves, even if not explicitly mentioned.

Remark 4.1
.

Notice that in the literature, it is sometimes employed to consider only those classically causal curves whose tangent vectors are non-zero almost everywhere (e.g.,
[
45
]
). In the context of Lorentzian length spaces, causal curves are commonly defined to be non-constant
[
31
,
19
]
. However, our definition allows classically causal curves with constant segments, as this ensures that
\ell_{g}(x,x)=0
for
x\in M
, and better suits the definition of causal paths in
[
7
]
. In particular, Lemma
4.3
shows that our choice to allow classically causal curves with zero derivatives at a positive measure set of times for the definition of
\ell_{g}
does not change the values of the time separation
\ell_{g}(x,z)
for
x\neq z
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
g
be a continuous Lorentzian metric on
M
and let
p\in M
. Then there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
p
and coordinates
y=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{d})
for
U
such that the following holds: For any points
p^{1}\leq_{g}p^{2}\in U
that can be joined by a
g
-classically causal curve that is contained in
U
, it holds that
y_{i}(p^{1})\leq y_{i}(p^{2})
for all
i=0,\ldots,d
.

Proof.

This proof uses a similar idea as in
[
7
, Theorem A.1]
. Fix a neighbourhood
W
around
p
that is contained in one coordinate patch and assume that
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Moreover, we may assume the coordinates on
W
to be chosen in a way that
g(p)=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)
.
We call the coordinates
(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and suppose that
\partial_{t}
is future directed.
Now there exists a neighbourhood
U\subset W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
of
p
such that
g\prec\mathrm{diag}(-2,1,\ldots,1)=:m_{2}
on
U
. Possibly shrinking
U
further, we may assume that
U\ni p
is
m_{2}
-globally hyperbolic and that it can be written as
I_{m_{2}}^{+}(p^{-})\cap I_{m_{2}}^{-}(p^{+})
, for some
p^{-},p^{+}\in W
.
Now there exists a linear transform into coordinates
(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
such that the future cone of
m_{2}
is contained in the set

\displaystyle C:=\big\{\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}|\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\geq 0\big\}.

As
m_{2}\succ g
in
U
, we get that the future cone of
g
is contained in
C
at every point
q\in U
.
Now pick two points
p^{1},p^{2}\in U
such that they can be joined by a
g
-classically causal curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma([0,1])\subset U
.
Suppose that there exists an
i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
such that
y_{i}(p^{1})>y_{i}(p^{2})
.
Then
w:=p^{2}-p^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus C
.
But by assumption, we have that
\gamma
is a
m_{2}
-classically causal curve from
p^{1}
to
p^{2}
, hence as
m_{2}
is constant, we get that
p^{2}-p^{1}\in C
which yields a contradiction. Hence, we have that for all
i
, it holds
y_{i}(p^{1})\leq y_{i}(p^{2})
. As
p^{1},p^{2}
were arbitrary, this proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
x\neq z\in M
. Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
be a Lipschitz continuous classically causal curve from
x
to
z
. Then there exists a Lipschitz continuous and classically causal curve
\tilde{\gamma}\colon[0,1]\to M
with
\tilde{\gamma}_{0}=x,\tilde{\gamma}_{1}=z
, such that for almost all
t\in[0,1]
it holds
\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}\neq 0
, and such that

\displaystyle L_{g}(\gamma)=L_{g}(\tilde{\gamma}).

Proof.

Lemma
4.2
implies that for every point
p\in M
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
p
and local coordinates
(y_{0},\ldots,y_{d})
for
U
such that the following holds: For any points
p^{1}\leq_{g}p^{2}\in U
that can be joined by a
g
-classically causal curve that is contained in
U
, it holds that
y_{i}(p^{1})\leq y_{i}(p^{2})
for all
i=0,\ldots,d
.
We can cover
\gamma([0,1])
with finitely many of such neighbourhoods and may hence assume that
\gamma([0,1])
is contained in one such
U
. From now on, we work in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with the coordinates
y
and for
p\in U
and
i=0,\ldots,d
we write
p_{i}
for
y_{i}(p)
. We may assume that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d}z_{i}-x_{i}=1.

(4.3)

Define the function
f\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
via

\displaystyle f(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=0}^{d}(\dot{\gamma}_{s})_{i}\,{\rm d}s.

Note that
f
is Lipschitz continuous as
\gamma
is Lipschitz continuous. Moreover, our choice of coordinates together with (
4.3
) yields that
f
is increasing and surjective. In particular, it holds

\displaystyle f(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(\gamma_{t})_{i}-(\gamma_{0})_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}|(\gamma_{t})_{i}-(\gamma_{0})_{i}|.

We now define the curve
\tilde{\gamma}\colon[0,1]\to U
via

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(\tau):=\gamma(\sup\{\sigma:f(\sigma)\leq\tau\}).

(4.4)

We immediately get that
\gamma_{0}=\tilde{\gamma}_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}=\tilde{\gamma}_{1}
.
Now let
0\leq\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}\leq 1
and for
j=1,2
define
\sigma_{j}:=\sup\{\sigma:f(\sigma)\leq\tau_{j}\}
. Then note that as
f
is increasing it holds
\sigma_{2}\geq\sigma_{1}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(\tau_{1})=\gamma(\sigma_{1})\leq_{g}\gamma(\sigma_{2})=\tilde{\gamma}(\tau_{2}).

(4.5)

Moreover, as
f
is continuous, we get that
f(\sigma_{j})=\tau_{j}
for
j=1,2
. In particular, this yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d}|(\tilde{\gamma}(\tau_{2}))_{i}-(\tilde{\gamma}(\tau_{1}))_{i}|=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(\gamma(\sigma_{2}))_{i}-(\gamma(\sigma_{1}))_{i}=f(\sigma_{2})-f(\sigma_{1})=\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}.

It follows that
\tilde{\gamma}
has unit
\mathsf{d}_{l^{1}}
-speed in the
y
-coordinates, where
\mathsf{d}_{l^{1}}(p,q):=\sum_{i=0}^{d}|p_{i}-q_{i}|
for
p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. As all norms on the Euclidean space are equivalent, it follows that
\tilde{\gamma}
is
\mathsf{d}_{euc}
-bi-Lipschitz and in particular it holds that
\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}\neq 0
for almost every
t\in[0,1]
.

Claim 1

\gamma=\tilde{\gamma}\circ f
.

Proof of claim 1.
Let
t\in[0,1]
. Define
t^{*}:=\sup\{\sigma:f(\sigma)\leq f(t)\}
.
As
f
is continuous it follows that
f(t)=f(t^{*})
. By the definition of
f
, it follows that
\gamma(t)=\gamma(t^{*})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(f(t))=\gamma(t^{*})=\gamma(t).

This proves claim 1.

Claim 2.

\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}
is causal and future directed for almost every
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof of claim 2.

By our previous observations we have that
\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}
exists and is non-zero for almost every
t\in[0,1]
so we may fix one such
t
.
Denote
p:=\tilde{\gamma}_{t}\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Define the constant Lorentzian metric
g_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
via
g_{\varepsilon}(w,w):=g(p)(w,w)-\varepsilon g(p)(X(p),w)^{2}
. Then
g_{\varepsilon}\succ g(p)
and hence there exists a neighbourhood
U_{\varepsilon}\subset U
of
p
such that
g_{\varepsilon}\succ g
on
U_{\varepsilon}
.
We argue by contradiction.
Suppose that
v:=\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\cong T_{p}M
was spacelike or causal and past directed. Then
g(p)(v,v)>0
or
g(p)(v,X(p))>0
.

Note that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}g_{\varepsilon}=g(p)
, hence there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that
g_{\varepsilon}(v,v)>0
or
g_{\varepsilon}(v,X(p))>0
. Fix such an
\varepsilon
and possibly shrink
U_{\varepsilon}\ni p
such that the vector
X(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is
g
-timelike everywhere in
U_{\varepsilon}
.
Now let
h_{*}>0
be small enough such that
\tilde{\gamma}([t,t+h_{*}])\subset U_{\varepsilon}
. Pick
h\in(0,h_{*})
.
Denote by
\sigma_{0}:=\sup\{\sigma:f(\sigma)\leq t\}
. Then
\gamma_{\sigma_{0}}=p
. Moreover, denote
\sigma_{h}:=\sup\{\sigma:f(\sigma)\leq t+h\}
. Then
\gamma_{\sigma_{h}}=\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}
.
Note that by claim 1, we get that
\gamma([\sigma_{0},\sigma_{h}])=\tilde{\gamma}([t,t+h])\subset U_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, we can reparametrise
\gamma|_{[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{h}]}
to obtain a
g
-classically causal curve
\check{\gamma}:[0,1]\to U_{\varepsilon}
that connects
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}
with
\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}
. By our choice of
U_{\varepsilon}
, we get that
\check{\gamma}
is
g_{\varepsilon}
-classically causal. However, as
g_{\varepsilon}
is constant and its future cone is a convex set, we get that

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}-\tilde{\gamma}_{t},\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}-\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\leq 0\ \mathrm{and}\ g_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}-\tilde{\gamma}_{t},X(p))\leq 0,

i.e.,
\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}-\tilde{\gamma}_{t}
as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is
g_{\varepsilon}
-causal and future directed.
Note that
v=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{t+h}-\tilde{\gamma}_{t}}{h}
, so we get that
g_{\varepsilon}(v,v)\leq 0
and
g_{\varepsilon}(v,X(p))\leq 0
, which contradicts our assumption. Hence,
v
has to be causal and future directed.
This proves claim 2.

Note that this shows that
\tilde{\gamma}
is
g
-classically causal.

Now, both
\tilde{\gamma}
and
f
are Lipschitz continuous, hence we may apply the chain rule and get that

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{t}=f^{\prime}(t)\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{f(t)}\ \mathrm{almost\ everywhere}.

It follows that

\displaystyle L_{g}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{g({\gamma}_{t})(\dot{{\gamma}}_{t},\dot{{\gamma}}_{t})}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)\sqrt{g(\tilde{\gamma}(f(t)))(\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{f(t)},\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{f(t)})}\,{\rm d}t.

However, as
f
is monotone and absolutely continuous, we may apply substitution (see for instance
[
32
, Theorem 5.42]
) and get that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)\sqrt{g(\tilde{\gamma}(f(t)))(\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{f(t)},\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{f(t)})}\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{g(\tilde{\gamma}_{\tau})(\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\tau},\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\tau})}\,{\rm d}\tau=L_{g}(\tilde{\gamma}),

which yields
L_{g}(\gamma)=L_{g}(\tilde{\gamma})
and hence proves the lemma.
∎

Recall that the spacetime
(M,g)
is said to be

•

strongly causal
if for every
p\in M
and any neighbourhood
U\subset M
of
p
, there exists a neighbourhood
V\subset U
of
p
such that every classically causal and future directed curve with endpoints in
V
is contained in
U
.

•

causally simple
if there are no non-constant closed classically causal and future directed curves and if
\leq
is closed in the manifold topology.

•

globally hyperbolic
if it is strongly causal and if for any two points
p,q\in M
the set
J^{+}(p)\cap J^{-}(q)
is compact.

The next result is known for smooth Lorentzian spacetimes (see for instance
[
39
]
) and the proof works similarly. Alternatively, Minguzzi’s result on closed cone structures
[
38
, Theorem 2.47]
implies the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
(M,g)
is causally simple then
(M,g)
is strongly causal.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that
(M,g)
is not strongly causal. Then there exists a
p\in M
and an open set
U\subset M
such that
p\in U
and such that for every open set
V\in M
with
p\in V
there exist
q^{-},q^{+}\in V
and a classically causal and future directed curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma_{0}=q^{-}
and
\gamma_{1}=q^{+}
that leaves
U
.
By possibly shrinking
U
we may assume that
U\ni p
is relatively compact. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, fix open sets
V_{n}
such that
p\in V_{n+1}\subset V_{n}\subset U
and such that
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}V_{n}=\{p\}
.
Now, by our assumption, there exist
q_{n}^{-},q_{n}^{+}\in V_{n}
and a causal curve
\gamma_{n}:[0,1]\to M
from
q_{n}^{-}
to
q_{n}^{+}
that leaves
U
. Denote

\displaystyle q_{n}^{0}:=\gamma\big(\min\{t\in[0,1]:\gamma_{t}\notin U\}\big)\in\partial U.

By potentially passing to a subsequence, the relative compactness of
U
and the choice of the
V_{n}
yields that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}^{-}=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}^{+}=p,

and that there exists a
q\in\partial U
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}^{0}=q.

Moreover, we have that
q_{n}^{-}\leq_{g}q_{n}^{0}\leq_{g}q_{n}^{+}
, hence the fact that
\leq_{g}
is closed yields that

\displaystyle p\leq_{g}q\leq_{g}p.

However,
p\notin\partial U
, hence
q\neq p
. Thus, there exists a non-constant closed classically causal curve, namely starting at
p
and passing through
q
. This contradicts the causal simplicity and hence proves the lemma.
∎

Assumption 4.5
.

From now on we will assume
(M,g)
to be causally simple.

Remark 4.6
.

The assumption of causal simplicity is of technical nature, as causal simplicity is necessary to enter the framework of a Polish metric measure spacetime as in
[
7
]
. Indeed, it is needed for
\{\ell_{g}\geq 0\}
to be closed in the manifold topology. However, strong causality is implied by Lemma
4.4
and suffices for our proofs. By
[
45
, Proposition 3.3]
, any globally hyperbolic continuous spacetime is causally simple and strongly causal.

From now on, we will consider the metric measure spacetime
(M,\tau_{h},\ell_{g},\mathrm{vol}_{g})
. When speaking of causal paths we refer to maps
\gamma:[0,1]\to M
such that for each
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s\leq t
, it holds
\gamma_{s}\leq_{g}\gamma_{t}
(these are the future directed and causal paths as defined in
[
7
]
). Note that the notion of a causal path uses the definition of
\ell_{g}
based on classically causal curves. When speaking of a causal curve, we refer to continuous causal paths.

A priori, all we know is that the class of classically causal curves is contained in the class of causal paths. We quickly notice the following useful fact.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(M,g)
be a spacetime and
V\subset U\subset M
neighbourhoods such that every classically causal curve with endpoints in
V
is contained in
U
. Then, every causal path with endpoints in
V
is contained in
U
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists a causal path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
with
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in V
and such that there is a
t\in(0,1)
with
\gamma_{t}\notin U
. By the definition of causal paths, we have that
\gamma_{0}\leq_{g}\gamma_{t}\leq_{g}\gamma_{1}
. Hence, there exist Lipschitz continuous classically causal curves
\lambda^{1},\lambda^{2}\colon[0,1]\to M
such that
\lambda^{1}_{0}=\gamma_{0}
,
\lambda^{1}_{1}=\lambda^{2}_{0}=\gamma_{t}
, and
\lambda^{2}_{1}=\gamma_{1}
. Then, the concatenation
\lambda
of
\lambda^{1}
and
\lambda^{2}
is a classically causal curve with endpoints in
V
that leaves
U
. This is a contradiction and proves the lemma.
∎

This shows that the strong causality assumption also holds for causal paths, not only for classically causal curves. Similarly, the causal simplicity assumption holds for causal paths as well.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
p\in M
and
0\neq w\in T_{p}M
be spacelike. Then there exist timelike vectors
u,v\in T_{p}M
such that one of them is future directed and the other one past directed and such that
g(u,w),g(v,w)>0
.

Proof.

As this is a statement about the tangent space, we may assume to be working in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\cong T_{p}M
. Furthermore, we may assume the coordinates to be chosen in a way that
g(p)=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)
.
Write
w=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
By applying a rotation, we may assume that
w_{2}=\ldots=w_{d}=0
. This reduces the problem to the case
d=1
and
g=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)
.

Now as
w
is spacelike, we get that
w_{1}^{2}>w_{0}^{2}
.
Define the vectors
\vartheta:=(w_{1},w_{0}),\vartheta^{\prime}:=-\vartheta=(-w_{1},-w_{0})
and note that
\vartheta,\vartheta^{\prime}
are both timelike but one is future directed and the other one past directed. Moreover, it holds that
g(w,\vartheta)=g(w,-\vartheta)=0
. Fix an
\alpha>0
such that
\vartheta+[-\alpha,\alpha]e_{1}
is timelike and hence in the same cone (future or past) as
\vartheta
and such that similarly
\vartheta^{\prime}+[-\alpha,\alpha]e_{1}
is timelike and hence in the same cone as
\vartheta^{\prime}
.
Now we distinguish cases:

Case 1.

w_{1}>0
.

Then
u=\vartheta+\alpha e_{1}
and
v=\vartheta^{\prime}+\alpha e_{1}
yield the desired. Indeed,

\displaystyle g(w,u)=g(w,\vartheta)+\alpha w_{1}=\alpha w_{1}>0,

\displaystyle g(w,v)=g(w,\vartheta^{\prime})+\alpha w_{1}=\alpha w_{1}>0.

Case 2.

w_{1}<0
.

In this case,
u=\vartheta-\alpha e_{1}
and
v=\vartheta^{\prime}-\alpha e_{1}
yield the desired.
This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
p\in M
and fix a local trivialisation that identifies
T_{p}M
with
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Let
w\in T_{p}M
be causal and past directed and let
\varepsilon>0
and
\beta>0
. Then there exists a timelike and future directed vector

such that
\beta\leq|v|_{euc}\leq 2\beta
and such that

\displaystyle\langle v,w\rangle_{g}\leq\varepsilon|v|_{g}+|v|_{g}|w|_{g}.

Proof.

If
w
is timelike then for any
\lambda>0
, we have that
v_{\lambda}:=-\lambda w
is timelike and future directed and

\displaystyle\langle v_{\lambda},w\rangle_{g}=|v_{\lambda}|_{g}|w|_{g}.

Now we can pick
\lambda
such that
2\beta=|v_{\lambda}|_{euc}
.

Now assume that
w
is null. Write
w=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Applying linear change
L
of coordinates, we may again assume that (in the new coordinates after applying
L
)
g(p)=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)
and that
w_{2}=\ldots=w_{d}=0
.
We may furthermore assume that
e_{0}
is future directed and that
w=(-a,-a,0,\ldots,0)
for some
a>0
.
Fix some
b>0
such that
|L^{-1}(b,b,0,\ldots,0)|_{euc}=3\beta/2
.
Define the timelike and future directed vector
v_{\delta}:=(b+\delta,b,0,\ldots,0)
for
\delta>0
. We can compute that

\displaystyle g(v_{\delta},v_{\delta})=-2b\delta-\delta^{2}\ \mathrm{and}\ |v_{\delta}|_{g}=\sqrt{2b\delta+\delta^{2}}.

Furthermore, we get that

\displaystyle\langle v_{\delta},w\rangle_{g}=a\delta.

There exists a
\delta_{0}>0
such that for
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
, it holds

\displaystyle\langle v_{\delta},w\rangle_{g}=a\delta\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{2b\delta+\delta^{2}}=\varepsilon|v_{\delta}|_{g}=\varepsilon|v_{\delta}|_{g}+|w|_{g}|v_{\delta}|_{g}.

, it holds that

∎

Many of the following arguments will be local, so we will often work in small neighbourhoods
U\subset M
that may be assumed to be contained in one coordinate patch. To keep notation short, we will oftentimes assume
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
without explicitly mentioning (pushforwards by) a chart
\psi:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
be a causal path. Then for each
t\in[0,1]
, at which
\gamma
is differentiable, the derivative
\dot{\gamma}_{t}\in T_{\gamma_{t}}M
is either
0
or causal and future directed.

Proof.

Fix a
t\in[0,1]
such that
\gamma
is differentiable at
t
and denote
p:=\gamma_{t}
and
v:=\dot{\gamma}_{t}
. We may pick local coordinates for an open set
U\ni p
such that
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and such that
g(p)=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, define the Lorentzian metric
m_{\varepsilon}:=\mathrm{diag}(-1-\varepsilon,1,\ldots,1)
on
U
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have that there exists an open neighbourhood
U_{\varepsilon}\subset U
such that

and such that
m_{\varepsilon}\succ g
on
U_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, by the strong causality of
(M,g)
, there exists a neighbourhood

such that every

-causal path with endpoints in
V_{\varepsilon}
is contained in
U_{\varepsilon}
.

We argue by contradiction and assume that

is non-zero and spacelike or causal and past directed. Then
g(p)(v,v)>0
or
g(p)(X(p),v)>0
. As
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}m_{\varepsilon}=g(p)
, we have that there exists an
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that
m_{\varepsilon}(v,v)>0
or
m_{\varepsilon}(X(p),v)>0
. Fix such an
\varepsilon>0
. Note that for
h_{*}>0

\gamma_{t+h}\in V_{\varepsilon}
for any

\displaystyle v_{h}:=\frac{\gamma_{t+h}-\gamma_{t}}{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

As
\gamma
is causal, we have that

, hence there exists a Lipschitz continuous,

-classically causal curve

such that

,
\lambda_{1}=\gamma_{t+h}
. But then

. Hence,
\lambda
is
m_{\varepsilon}
-classically causal.
But
m_{\varepsilon}
is constant on
U_{\varepsilon}
and the causal future cone of

is a convex cone in

, hence, we get that
v_{h}
is
m_{\varepsilon}
-causal and future directed for

.
Now, we have that

as

-causal and future directed. This contradicts our assumption and hence proves the lemma.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

Lemma
4.10
shows that in the setting of a manifold with a continuous Lorentzian metric the class of causal paths is a sensible extension of the class of classically causal curves, in the sense that the derivatives of these maps are causal or zero wherever they exist. This observation is in the flavour of
[
31
, Proposition 5.9]
.

A similar observation as the following one can be found in
[
7
]
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
f
be a causal function on
M
. Then for each point
p\in M
at which
f
is differentiable, the gradient
\nabla_{g}f(p)\in T_{p}M
is either zero or causal and past directed.

Proof.

Take any timelike and future directed vector
v\in T_{p}M
. Working in a small neighbourhood
U
around
p
, we may assume that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and identify the tangent spaces with
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
Then, by the continuity of
g
, there exists a neighbourhood
V
around
p
such that for each
q\in V
,
g(q)(v,v)<0
, where
v
is seen as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Hence, there exists an
s>0
such that the curve
\gamma:[-s,s]\to M,t\mapsto p+tv
is a classically causal and future directed curve.
Note that
\gamma
is differentiable at
t=0
.
Then, the chain rule yields that

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(\gamma_{h})-f(\gamma_{0})}{h}={\rm d}f_{p}(v)=\langle\nabla_{g}f,v\rangle_{g}(p).

The causality of
f
and
\gamma
yields that

\displaystyle\frac{f(\gamma_{h})-f(\gamma_{0})}{h}\geq 0,\ \forall 0\neq h\in[-s,s].

Hence,
\langle\nabla_{g}f,v\rangle_{g}(p)\geq 0
. As
v
was chosen arbitrarily, we get that for all timelike and future directed vectors

it holds

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{g}f,v\rangle_{g}(p)\geq 0.

Now assume
\nabla_{g}f(p)
was non-zero and spacelike. Then by Lemma
4.8
, there exists a past directed timelike vector
u\in T_{p}M
such that
\langle\nabla_{g}f,u\rangle_{g}(p)>0
. But then the vector
v:=-u
is timelike and future directed and satisfies
\langle\nabla_{g}f,v\rangle_{g}(p)<0
, which is a contradiction. Similarly, considering
v=X(p)
, we get that
\nabla_{g}f(p)
cannot be causal and future directed.
∎

Lemma 4.13

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to M
be a left continuous causal path. Then

has at most countably many discontinuities.

The proof of this lemma follows the same strategy as the proof of

[

]
, working in a so-called forward metric spacetime.

Proof.

Recall that we fixed a complete Riemannian metric

the induced Polish distance on

\displaystyle B:=\{t\in[0,1]:\limsup_{s\to t}\mathsf{d}_{h}(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})>c\}.

If we can prove that

is countable for any

, the result follows. Assume for a contradiction that there exists a

such that

is uncountable.

Claim:
There exists a

such that for all
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

is uncountable.

Proof of the claim.
We argue by contradiction. If the claim was false, then for every
T\in(0,1]
there exists an
\varepsilon(T)>0
such that

Proof.

\displaystyle\{|f_{1}+f_{2}|<\infty\}=\{|2f_{1}+f_{2}|<\infty\}=\{|f_{1}|<\infty\}\cap\{|f_{2}|<\infty\}.

We aim to prove

Define
