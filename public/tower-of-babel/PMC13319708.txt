Exploring drug repurposing for monkeypox virus: A structural and computational approach
Authors: Somia Gul, Faiza Akhtar, Iqra Rehman, Muhammad Shah, Umer Rashid, Abdur Rauf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe increasing global threat posed by viral infections that are transmitted to humans from animals has intensified interest in emerging pathogens such as monkeypox virus (MPV). This study investigated the potential of drug repurposing as an efficient therapeutic strategy against MPV, focusing on its close genetic relationships with vaccinia and variola viruses.MethodsDue to the structural and evolutionary similarities among these orthopoxviruses, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved smallpox drug tecovirimat was selected as a reference drug. A descriptive-analytical approach was used to assess the binding energies of 38 FDA-approved drugs from four therapeutic classes. Molecular docking was performed against key target proteins from the genus Orthopoxvirus.ResultsSeveral compounds exhibited stronger binding energies than the reference drug tecovirimat, indicating their potential repurposing as anti-MPV agents. To further validate the stability and interaction dynamics of the top performing drug–protein complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted, which supported their potential as viable candidates for future therapeutic development.ConclusionThese findings provide promising leads for the development of new therapeutic applications, thereby enhancing both the public health response and commercial value of repurposed treatments. MD simulations also supported the stability and feasibility of these promising drug–protein complexes.

Introduction

In the 21st century, zoonotic diseases have become a significant concern due to outbreaks with increased morbidity and mortality rates. Zoonotic diseases are infections that are transmitted between animals and human beings.1 Outbreaks of SARS-CoV (2002–2003), MERS (2012), Ebola (2014–2016), and Nipah virus in recent decades highlight the increasing frequency and global threat posed by zoonotic infections.2,3 The emergence of monkeypox in the first quarter of 2022 is a prime example of the rise of zoonotic diseases. This outbreak demonstrates the global threat posed by these viruses, which were previously considered endemic to specific regions, particularly Central and West Africa. However, recent outbreaks in the USA and Europe underscore the potential of zoonotic diseases to transcend geographical boundaries, evolving from endemic to pandemic threats.2,3 Endemic and pandemic diseases are distinguished based on their spread and impact, where endemic diseases are consistently present in specific regions, whereas pandemic diseases can spread across multiple countries and continents, often causing widespread health crises. The outbreak of COVID-19 in the pandemic caused by SARS-CoV-2 virus is a recent example of how zoonotic diseases can escalate into a global health emergency, emphasizing the need for proactive measures to combat these threats.4

The monkeypox virus (MPV), vaccinia virus, and variola virus belong to the genus Orthopoxvirus, and share significant structural and genetic similarities. The vaccinia virus, historically used as a vaccine against smallpox, provides immunity against the variola virus, demonstrating the potential for cross protection among orthopoxviruses.5 Monkeypox, a DNA virus, exhibits similar clinical symptoms to smallpox, including cutaneous lesions, fever, and lymphadenopathy, which distinguish it from other poxviruses.6 The transmission of monkeypox occurs through animal bites, direct contact with infected animal fluids, or respiratory droplets, making it a significant public health concern.6 Effective prevention of these infectious diseases involves vaccination and the treatment or control of closely related viruses, including smallpox. At present, no drug has been approved specifically for the treatment of monkeypox. Nevertheless, drug repurposing has developed as a promising new strategy to fill this gap. In drug repurposing, existing drugs are repurposed for new uses by identifying their therapeutic applications based on established safety profiles and pharmacological properties, allowing their development as treatments for emerging diseases.7

Drug repurposing is particularly advantageous for combating emerging infectious diseases such as monkeypox because it offers reduced timelines, lower risk of failure, and decreased costs,. For example, tecovirimat (TECO), an antiviral first developed for smallpox, was repurposed by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and World Health Organization8,9 for treating monkeypox under emergency use authorization. Similar to brincidofovir, its relative cidofovir exhibits antiviral activity against monkeypox infections, as shown by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.10 Vaccinia immune globulin was adopted in KSA to treat monkeypox as a repurposed therapy, but its effectiveness has yet to be demonstrated.11 Remdesivir was developed by Beigel et al.12 to inhibit SARs-CoV-2 replication and was subsequently given emergency authorization for use in several countries.

Thalidomide, which was initially developed as a sedative and later withdrawn due to its teratogenic effects, was repurposed for the treatment of multiple myeloma, where its anti-angiogenic and immunomodulatory characteristics make it a valuable newly developed therapeutic for this type of cancer.13 Ivermectin, an antiparasitic treatment for river blindness and other parasitic infections, has been investigated as a possible antiviral in an infectious disease context. Despite the controversial clinical effectiveness of ivermectin against COVID-19, it obtained promising results in the treatment of dengue and Zika.14 Thus, the successful repurposing of drugs from existing pharmacopeias is highlighted by the usefulness of implementing repurposed drug strategies during public health emergencies.

Recently, computational methods have revolutionized drug discovery and repurposing efforts. In silico techniques, such as molecular docking and virtual screening, enable researchers to predict drug–receptor interactions with high accuracy, significantly reducing the time and cost associated with experimental validation.15 For example, computational studies have identified several FDA-approved drugs, such as antibacterial and anticancer agents, as potential candidates for drug repurposing.16, 17, 18 Combining computational and experimental methods can accelerate the development of effective treatments for emerging zoonotic diseases and minimize their impacts on global health.

Thus, drug repurposing is a fundamental basis for addressing the most urgent critical therapeutic requirements for new zoonotic diseases, including monkeypox. Drug repurposing is a viable solution to the problem of emerging infectious diseases, ultimately reducing the time required to develop efficient treatments through the best use of information about known drugs, as well as using innovative technologies and emerging zoonotic treatments, thereby preserving public health.

The present study explored the critical role of drug repurposing as a rapid and cost effective strategy for combating emerging zoonotic diseases, particularly by focusing on monkeypox. By utilizing computational advances in repurposed therapeutics, we aimed to highlight how leveraging existing pharmacopeias can accelerate drug development, mitigate global health crises, and establish a robust framework for addressing future outbreaks. Ultimately, in this study, we aimed to contribute to the evolving landscape of infectious diseases management by promoting innovative idea-driven approaches to drug discovery and deployment.

Materials and methods

Software for molecular docking

In silico investigations were conducted by utilizing an Intel Core i7 processor with 2.90 Ghz, 8 GB RAM, and the Windows 10 Pro operating system. Molecular docking studies were performed using AutoDock (version 4.2.6) and AutoDock tools (version 1.5.7). The resulting docked complexes were evaluated with PyMOL (version 2.3.2). ChemDraw professional (version 17.1) was employed for depicting ligand structures. Ligand energy minimization was conducted by using CHEM 3D Ultra. Furthermore, the visualization and preparation of proteins were facilitated by BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted by using GROMACS software.

Methods

A descriptive-analytical approach was employed to investigate the molecular interactions between selected FDA-approved compounds and target proteins belonging to the genus Orthopoxvirus within the Poxviridae family.19 In total, 38 FDA-approved drugs were chosen for evaluation, representing four pharmacological categories, i.e., antivirals, anthracyclines, antiretroviral HIV-1 protease inhibitors, and hepatitis C virus (HCV) NS3/4A protease inhibitors, for repurposing in MPV treatment.

Target proteins were selected based on their relevance to the pathogenicity of Orthopoxvirus and structural availability in Protein Data Bank (PDB). The viral target proteins included: thymidylate kinase from vaccinia virus (PDB ID: 2V54), A42R protein from MPV (PDB ID: 4QWO), a structural protein from vaccinia virus (PDB ID: 7SEZ), and methyl transferase VP39 from MPV (PDB ID: 8B07).

Given the close genetic and structural similarities between MPV and variola virus, an FDA-approved antiviral agent for smallpox, TECO, was included as a reference compound for comparative analysis.20

Retrieval of targets and ligands from databases

The PubChem database was used to obtain structural information for selected compounds (ligands).21 The structures were retrieved in SDF format and then converted into PDB format. The three-dimensional structures of selected target proteins were retrieved from PDB.22 The target proteins were selected based on the close structural associations of MPV with vaccinia virus and variola virus. Hence, the first protein target was the crystal structure of vaccinia virus with thymidylate kinase bound to thymidine diphosphate (PDB ID: 2V54, resolution: 2.4 Å).23 The second target protein was the crystal structure of MPV methyltransferase VP39 in complex with sinefungin (PDB ID: 8B07, resolution: 2.05 Å).24 The third target protein retrieved from PDB was the crystal structure of vaccinia virus decapping enzyme D9 in complex with 7-Methylguanosine 5'-diphosphate (7GDP) (PDB ID: 7SEZ, resolution: 1.70 Å).25 The final target protein was the crystal structure of A42R profilin-like protein from MPV Zaire-96-1-16 (PDB ID: 4QWO, resolution: 1.52 Å).26 The target protein structures were prepared by using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer, which involved various steps such as deleting all water molecules, hetero atoms, and adding hydrogen atoms. The new files were saved in PDB format for docking analysis.

Docking by virtual screening

To explore the molecular interactions between different selected compounds and the target proteins of MPV, molecular docking by virtual screening was conducted with AutoDock Vina,27 which involved generating a virtual combinatorial library of clinically approved compounds. Energy minimization was performed for each compound in the virtual combinatorial library to optimize its geometry by utilizing MMFF94 in CHEM 3D Ultra.28 After the addition of charges and polar hydrogen, all of the PDB files for ligands and targets were converted into PDBQT files. Centroid grid boxes were generated to specify the active sites. The docking parameters, including orientation and torsion angles (7.5), number of generations (27,000), and energy evaluations (250,000), were set to the default values. The predicted binding energies and best conformations of the docked structures were utilized to rank the complexes formed by ligands and target proteins.

Screening criteria

The aim of the current study was to identify the most suitable ligand candidates capable of forming stable and strong interactions with target proteins from the genus Orthopoxvirus, particularly by focusing on MPV. Docking was conducted by using AutoDock for screening the binding energies and interaction profiles of selected FDA-approved drugs with the viral targets. PyMOL and Discovery Studio Visualizer tools were utilized to visualize and analyze the outcomes of molecular docking.

MD simulations of ligand–protein complexes

After docking analysis, the selected complexes were further investigated by conducting MD simulations using GROMACS software. The stabilities of proteins before and after complexation, structural dynamics, and interactions with ligands were investigated under natural body conditions through MD simulations. The stability of each ligand with the active site was also analyzed. The MD simulations involved generating the cubic shape box, followed by solvation with the TIP3P water model. After solvation, the protein was neutralized using sodium and chlorine salt. The system was minimized in 5000 steps, followed by two steps of NPT (constant Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature) and NVT (constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature) equilibration at the optimum level (1 atm, 310.15 K). After applying natural body conditions, the system was simulated up to 100 ns. Post-MD treatment was executed by conducting three types of analysis: root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and hydrogen bonding (HB) histogram interaction analysis.

Results and discussion

The ongoing spread of MPV demands the immediate development of an effective therapeutic solution. New antiviral drug development is a lengthy and resource-intensive process, so repurposing existing FDA-approved drugs is a suitable strategy for obtaining practical and time-efficient solutions. Molecular docking methods were used to analyze 38 approved therapeutic drugs as potential MPV protein inhibitors that target thymidylate kinase (PDB ID: 2V54), methyltransferase VP39 (PDB ID: 8B07), decapping enzyme D9 (PDB ID: 7SEZ), and profilin-like protein A42R (PDB ID: 4QWO). Molecular connectivity data regarding drug interactions with MPV proteins were successfully obtained to highlight their potential inhibitory effects and suitability for further investigation.

Docking results with FDA-approved drugs for repurposing against MPV.

The docking results for vaccinia virus thymidylate kinase (PDB ID: 2V54) indicated that the binding affinities varied among the 38 FDA-approved drugs from four pharmacological classes: antivirals, anthracyclines (anticancer agents), antiretroviral HIV-1 protease inhibitors, and HCV NS3/4A protease inhibitors. TECO, an FDA-approved antiviral, was used as a reference in this study and it had a binding energy of −7.5 kcal/mol, and it was applied as a standard for evaluating the performance of other ligands.

The strongest binding interactions were observed with compounds from the antiretroviral HIV-1 protease inhibitor class, where saquinavir (SAQU) had the lowest binding energy of −9.5 kcal/mol, followed closely by indinavir (INDI) with −9.4 kcal/mol. These values were significantly lower than that for TECO, suggesting their superior binding energy and stronger interaction with the 2V54 target. Other strong binding energies in this class included lopinavir (LOPI) with −8.6 kcal/mol, tipranavir (TIPR) with −8.2 kcal/mol, and darunavir (DARU) with −7.9 kcal/mol.

The HCV NS3/4A protease inhibitors also yielded promising results, including glecaprevir (GLEC) with −8.9 kcal/mol, simeprevir (SIME) with −8.7 kcal/mol, and paritaprevir (PARI) with −8.6 kcal/mol, which all outperformed TECO. In addition, asunaprevir (ASUN) with −7.7 kcal/mol and telaprevir (TELA) with −7.2 kcal/mol had moderate binding energies compared with the reference.

Among the anthracycline class of anticancer agents, doxorubicin (DOXO) and epirubicin (EPI) both had binding energies of −7.1 kcal/mol, which were slightly less than that for TECO but still indicative of potentially useful interactions. Idarubicin (IDAR) with −7.0 kcal/mol and daunorubicin (DANU) with −6.7 kcal/mol also had similar binding energies.

By contrast, most of the antiviral agents had binding energies in the range of −4.5 to −6.3 kcal/mol, and weaker compared with TECO.

Notably, SAQU, INDI, GLEC, and SIME emerged as strong candidates for repurposing against MPV based on the results against the target vaccinia virus thymidylate kinase (PDB ID: 2V54).

Against the MPV VP39 protein (PDB ID: 8B07), PARI had the strongest binding energy of −9.0 kcal/mol, and those for other drugs included GLEC with −8.7 kcal/mol, IDAR with −8.6 kcal/mol, sofosbuvir (SOFO) with −8.4 kcal/mol, and SIME with −8.3 kcal/mol. These drugs are mainly HCV inhibitors and anthracyclines, and they may have high potential for repurposing against MPV.29

Against vaccinia virus protein (PDB ID: 7SEZ), the compounds with the best binding energies were DARU with −10.6 kcal/mol, TIPR with −10.1 kcal/mol, and SAQU, LOPI, and nelfinavir (NELF) with −10.0 kcal/mol. Atazanavir (ATAZ) and SIME both had binding energies of −9.7 kcal/mol. These HIV-1 and HCV protease inhibitors had better binding energies than the standard TECO with −7.5 kcal/mol, thereby highlighting their strong binding potential for repurposing against MPV.

For the MPV A42R protein (PDB ID: 4QWO), several drugs had stronger binding energies than the reference drug TECO with 9.3 kcal/mol. Among the HCV NS3/4A protease inhibitors, PARI with −10.4 kcal/mol and SIME with −10.3 kcal/mol had the highest binding energies, indicating their excellent potential for repurposing within this class. GLEC with −9.3 kcal/mol had the same binding energy as TECO, as shown in Table 1.

Molecular docking analysis provided useful insights into the binding energies of selected drug candidates with four target proteins: 2V54, 8B07, 7SEZ, and 4QWO. Among all of the drug candidates, DARU achieved the highest binding energy with −10.6 kcal/mol against 7SEZ, followed by SIME with −10.3 kcal/mol and TIPR with −10.1 kcal/mol. The drug candidates INDI and SAQU had stronger binding energies of −9.5 kcal/mol and −9.4 kcal/mol, respectively, against 2V54 compared with the reference drug TECO (−7.5 kcal/mol). PARI had a binding energy of −9.0 kcal/mol against 8B07, followed by IDAR with −8.6 kcal/mol. Against 4QWO, SIME had a binding energy of −10.3 kcal/mol and GLEC had a binding energy of −9.3 kcal/mol.

The standard drug TECO had moderate binding energies with all targets ranging between −7.5 and −9.3 kcal/mol. The results indicated that PARI, SAQU, and GLEC had high binding energies, making them potential candidates for further evaluation. Thus, our results suggest that these compounds have potential uses as inhibitors for multiple target proteins in MPV as they exhibited selective behavior against certain targets. Binding interactions with hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues

In the present study, we identified key amino acids that played strong roles in facilitating drug–protein binding by forming hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, as shown in Table S1. Against the target protein (PDB ID: 2V54), SAQU formed strong hydrogen bonds at ARG-41(2.1 Å) and ASP-13 (2.3 Å), as well as forming hydrophobic bonds with MET-22 (3.6 Å) and GLN-40 (3.3 Å). The strength of thymidylate kinase inhibition by this compound increased its possible function as a potent antiviral agent through these protein–drug interactions. Similarly, against the target protein (PDB ID: 2V54), INDI formed two hydrogen bonds at ARG-137 (2.5 Å) and ARG-41 (2.3 Å), as well as hydrophobic interactions at GLY-16 (3.4 Å) and TYR-35 (2.6 Å) (Figure 1). These molecular interactions between INDI and the target protein demonstrate that it can effectively inhibit MPV replication by targeting fundamental enzymatic sites.

TECO established stable bonding interactions between TYR-189 and the target protein 8B07 to form two hydrogen bonds with bond lengths of 2.6 Å and 3.6 Å, and further stabilized its binding with hydrophobic contacts with PHE-115 at 4.2 Å and ARG-97 at 4.1 Å. With the same protein target, PARI engaged in strong binding through PHE-293 (3.8 Å) and ASN-273 (4.1 Å), and formed hydrophobic interactions with LYS-274 (4.0 Å) Fig. S1. These interactions demonstrate the significant inhibitory potential of TECO and PARI against their designated targets.

Against the target protein 7SEZ, the DARU complex formed numerous interactions with four hydrogen bonds at ARG-50 (2.4 Å), HIS-33 (3.0 Å), GLU-130 (2.6 Å), and LYS-198 (2.2 Å), as well as an additional hydrophobic interaction at TYR-158(2.3 Å). The hydrogen bonds in TIPR (PDB ID: 7SEZ) connected HIS-33 with bond lengths of 2.3 Å and 2.6 Å, and another hydrogen bond was observed with ARG-50 (3.1 Å). The drug TIPR also stabilized through hydrophobic bond interactions at GLN-57 (2.5 Å) Fig. S2. These interactions show that DARU and TIPR can successfully disrupt the viral protease functions that are required for the virus to survive.

The binding interactions between SIME and the target protein 4QWO included hydrogen bonds with LYS-26 (2.1 Å, 2.4 Å) and LYS-29 (2.5 Å), and a hydrophobic bond with HIS-100 (3.2 Å). Against the same target protein, GLEC formed hydrogen bonds through SER-12 with bond lengths of 2.4 Å and 2.5 Å, as well as another hydrogen bond with ARG-38 (2.8 Å) and hydrophobic interactions with ILE-35 (3.6 Å) Fig. S3. The drug–protein interactions contributed to their binding strength and reinforced their ability to function as effective inhibitors. The ability of these drugs to establish multiple hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions enhances their stability within viral active sites, possibly playing crucial roles in disrupting the key processes essential for MPV replication.17

Based on the docking analysis results, the drug candidates that can potentially be repurposed against MPV (see Table 2) were further tested in MD simulations. The drugs were selected based on their binding energies as well as steady hydrogen bond interactions and hydrophobic bonds within the active sites. We prioritized drugs that formed robust molecular bonds with key amino acid positions in the active site. The drugs that passed the selection stage had the best docking scores when assessed against all four proteins, and they also belonged to different drug classes. MD simulations

DARU complex

Following docking analysis, the selected protein–ligand DARU complex was further studied through MD simulations using GROMACS software. The structural and dynamic changes in the protein were analyzed based on RMSD plots, RMSF plots, and HB histograms. The results showed that RMSD was within 0.15–0.35 nm. MD simulations were performed for the protein–ligand docking complexes to investigate the binding stability and intermolecular interactions within a reference period. The MD trajectories were retrieved, and the simulation results were assessed using RMSD, RMSF, and HB analysis.

In the MD simulations, the RMSD was used to calculate the average distance obtained by atom displacement for a given reference time frame. It was possible to detect whether the simulation had equilibrated based on the RMSD result. The backbone deviation (N, Cα, C) of the protein was derived using the RMSD value obtained from the MD simulation. The complex structure exhibited minor fluctuations (1–2%) at 1.6–2.2 nm for 53–75 ns until reaching equilibrium. The average RMSD for the protein–ligand complex was 1.3 nm, and the fluctuations were also within acceptable limits. The protein fluctuated at 2.5 nm for 41 ns until reaching equilibrium. The protein reached equilibrium at 1.6 nm for 43 ns and continued up to 100 ns. The average RMSD value for the protein was 1.6 nm, which is acceptable. Fluctuations between 1 and 3 nm within a reference protein structure are entirely normal; however, considerably greater values show that the protein has undergone significant conformational change and that the system is no longer stable.

A third deviation was observed in the region of 180.

The simulations indicated the formation of hydrogen bonds with sufficient frequencies throughout the simulation. HB analysis is very important for understanding protein–ligand interactions. Conclusion

The resurgence of MPV is a global health concern that highlights the critical need to rapidly identify effective antiviral therapeutics. In this study, we systematically employed molecular docking and MD simulations to evaluate the inhibitory potential of 38 FDA-approved drugs from various classes, i.e., HCV NS3/4A protease inhibitors, antiretroviral HIV-1 protease inhibitors, anticancer drugs, and antivirals, against four key orthopoxvirus protein targets. The results showed that DARU, SIME, TIPR, INDI, SAQU, PARI, IDAR, and GLEC had superior docking scores, indicating their potential use as better broad-spectrum antiviral candidates compared with the reference drug TECO. These findings support the feasibility of drug repurposing for MPV treatment. MD simulations conducted under physiological conditions further validated the docking results by confirming the structural stability and sustained interactions. The MD simulations showed that the ligands remained stably bound within the active sites of their targets, and they exhibited consistent HB and favorable dynamic behavior over time. These results supported the reliability of the docking predictions and highlight the potential repurposing of the selected compounds as stable and effective inhibitors of MPV proteins.
