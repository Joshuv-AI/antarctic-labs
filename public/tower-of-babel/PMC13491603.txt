Structural barriers and facilitators of accessing rehabilitation among adults aged 50 years and older with low back pain: a scoping review of the literature
Authors: Jessica J. Wong, Lauren Ead, Alanna Veitch, Nora Bakaa, Kennedy Latour, Jen Rinaldi, Ginny Brunton, Heather Shearer, Michelle Hewitt, Kent Murnaghan, Pierre Côté
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults with low back pain (LBP) experience challenges when seeking rehabilitation; a comprehensive view of structural factors to access is needed to inform rehabilitation delivery. We aimed to systematically map the experiences regarding structural barriers and facilitators to accessing rehabilitation among adults aged 50 years and older with LBP; and explore whether experiences vary by social factors.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review based on Joanna Briggs Institute methodology. Eligible studies were qualitative and mixed-method studies (qualitative component) on structural barriers or facilitators to rehabilitation access among adults ≥ 50 years with LBP. Structural factors included socioeconomic and political contexts, governance, policies, and cultural/societal values. We searched databases from inception to February 2025. Paired reviewers independently screened citations and extracted data. We conducted a descriptive synthesis to map the structural barriers/facilitators and identify knowledge gaps. An Advisory Committee including people with lived experience, family members, and healthcare practitioners provided critical input across stages.ResultsOf the 8550 citations screened, 16 studies were relevant (3 from middle-income countries, 12 from high-income countries, 1 covering both; 12 studies on LBP, 4 studies on lumbar spinal stenosis). Common domains of structural barriers/facilitators to access were mapped for LBP and lumbar spinal stenosis in middle- or high-income countries: (1) finances (e.g., costs, insurance coverage, financial support from family); (2) location, transportation, inaccessibility or service unavailability (e.g., inadequate public transportation, wait-times, close proximity to services and resources); (3) lack of knowledge, perceptions, or stigma with certain rehabilitation approaches (e.g., limited education of treatment options provided to patients). Some older adults described ageism from providers (e.g., advised to accept back pain as part of aging) or stigma with certain rehabilitation approaches (e.g., mobility aids) as barriers. Studies in high-income countries also explored healthcare-related factors (e.g., referrals, collaboration across providers) as structural barriers/facilitators to access. Findings varied by rurality, socioeconomic strata, and disabilities.ConclusionsIn middle- and high-income countries, older adults with LBP or lumbar spinal stenosis experience many structural barriers to rehabilitation access that vary across social factors. Structural factors relate to finances, location/transportation, availability of services, healthcare provider-related factors, ageism, and stigma. Findings inform future work towards strategies for equitable rehabilitation delivery in middle- and high-income countries. Research is needed in low-income countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14868-6.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines rehabilitation as a set of interventions aimed to optimize functioning in persons with health conditions when interacting with their environment [1]. At least one in three (2.4 billion) people globally will require rehabilitation at some point in their life, but many do not receive these services, representing unmet needs [2]. Consequently, the WHO called for stronger delivery of rehabilitation across health systems worldwide [3, 4]. Rehabilitation is considered an important strategy for achieving the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal of healthy lives and well-being at all ages [5].

Low back pain (LBP) is the leading cause of years lived with disability, and the main reason for requiring rehabilitation globally [2, 6]. This condition affects approximately one in every four older adults, and prevalence associated with disability increases with age [7–9]. LBP can present with or without leg pain, and more commonly among older adults, symptoms affecting the legs may arise from lumbar spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication. The growing need for rehabilitation services particularly affects older adults with LBP [2, 10, 11]. Despite their need, this population encounters considerable barriers to accessing rehabilitation services due to structurally generated inequalities [10, 11]. Structurally generated inequalities represent disparities that are socially produced and systematic in their distribution across the population [12, 13]. Namely, rehabilitation access is largely driven by a range of non-medical factors, including occupational and class status, income security, educational opportunities, and social inclusion, distributed in ways that produce systemic disadvantage [12–14]. Barriers to accessing rehabilitation for LBP can have tremendous psychosocial consequences for older adults [10, 11, 15]. In turn, unmet needs in older adults greatly affect participation and daily activities, and may contribute to social isolation [2, 10, 11]. Structural barriers to care often lead people with LBP to experience worse functioning and complex rehabilitation needs [15]. Overall, the physical and psychosocial impact of structural barriers faced by older adults has negative implications for care and well-being.

Previous studies have explored the lived experiences of older adults with LBP seeking rehabilitation services [16–28]. However, this body of literature needs to be systematically mapped to comprehensively identify structural factors to rehabilitation access and knowledge gaps in this area. To our knowledge, no scoping review has explored the structural barriers and facilitators to accessing rehabilitation among older adults with LBP. It is also critical to explore whether these experiences vary across social factors (e.g., gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic strata), as diverse communities may have unique experiences and challenges to accessing rehabilitation services. These findings are needed to inform future research towards building a deeper understanding of diverse lived experiences when seeking rehabilitation, longer-term impact on functioning and well-being, and tailored strategies to improve equitable access to rehabilitation.

Therefore, the objectives of our scoping review are to: (1) systematically map the experiences regarding structural barriers and facilitators to accessing rehabilitation among adults aged 50 years and older with LBP; and (2) explore whether these experiences with structural barriers or facilitators to rehabilitation access vary across social factors (e.g., gender, race, ethnicity, place of residence, religion, occupation, education, socioeconomic strata, social capital/class).

Methods

Protocol and registration

Our scoping review methods follow guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [29], with reporting based on Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis Extension for Scoping Reviews [30]. JBI guidance further refines scoping review methodology [31], building on Arksey and O’Malley’s framework for scoping reviews that was advanced and extended by Levac and colleagues [32, 33]. Experts in scoping reviews and evidence synthesis, including JBI/JBI Collaboration working group members, developed the PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist to be consistent with JBI scoping review methodology [30, 31]. Our protocol was registered with Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/t89ug). This registered protocol covers both LBP and osteoarthritis; the scoping review focused on osteoarthritis will be published in a separate manuscript. Our team is also conducting a scoping review with similar methods focused on post-surgical rehabilitation for LBP, as described in a published protocol [34].

Eligibility criteria

Population

Our review included older adults (aged ≥ 50 years) with LBP. We intended to include the wide range of age thresholds used to define older adults globally. The United Nations defines an older person as aged ≥ 60 years, but notes that people in their 50s may be considered older in places where life expectancy is lower [35]. LBP is defined as pain localized between the costal margin and inferior gluteal folds with or without leg pain, without serious underlying pathology (e.g., fractures, dislocations, tumours, malignancies, osteoporosis, inflammatory arthritis) [36]. We included studies focused on lumbar spinal stenosis. Lumbar spinal stenosis is the narrowing of the spinal canal, lateral recesses, or intervertebral foramen in the lumbar spine with compression of the neurovascular elements, which can present with neurogenic claudication [37]. Studies with mixed samples had to stratify results for adults aged ≥ 50 years with LBP to be included.

We defined functioning as an umbrella term for body function, body structures, activities and participation when interacting with the environment, based on the WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health [38]. We included studies that described limitations in functioning among older adults with LBP, and studies focused on older adults seeking or receiving rehabilitation.

Concept

We included studies that explored the perspectives of older adults with LBP, as well as those of family members, caregivers, healthcare providers, and decision-makers towards rehabilitation access for this population. The WHO refers to rehabilitation as a set of interventions aimed to optimize functioning in individuals with health conditions when interacting with their environment [1]. We focused on the health care context, and excluded post-surgical rehabilitation or single interventions only. Based on the WHO Framework for Action on Social Determinants of Health, structural barriers/facilitators include socioeconomic and political context; governance; macroeconomic, social or public policies, and cultural and societal values/norms [13].

Context

We investigated whether these experiences of rehabilitation access vary across social factors, which were informed by the WHO Framework for Action on Social Determinants of Health and Cochrane Equity PROGRESS-Plus [13, 39]. Examples of social factors include, but are not limited to, gender, race, ethnicity, place of residence, religion, occupation, education, socioeconomic strata, social capital/class. Across the stated concept and context, we focused on structural and social factors of health inequities as defined by the WHO Framework for Action on Social Determinants of Health [13].

In addition to the stated definitions, eligible citations met the following criteria: (1) qualitative or mixed methods research study (where qualitative data could be extracted); systematic or scoping review of qualitative or mixed methods studies; (2) published in English or French language (to increase feasibility of conducting this scoping review). We also included books and book chapters, theses and dissertations, and full papers provided as conference proceedings. We excluded: (1) guidelines, letters, editorials, commentaries, government reports, abstracts, lectures, consensus development statements, studies without methods reported; and (2) literature reviews and quantitative research studies.

Information sources and search strategy

We collaborated with an experienced health science librarian to search MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), PsycINFO (Ovid), and Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest) from database inception to February 7, 2025 (Appendix I). A second librarian reviewed the search using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist [40, 41]. We did not restrict the search by language; instead, we listed possibly relevant articles published in other languages in the supplementary file for transparency. We also searched reference lists of relevant reviews for additional citations. We contacted authors, if needed, for additional information or clarification during screening or data extraction. We used EndNote (version 20.0.1) for reference management and EPPI-Reviewer (version 4.14.2.0) for screening.

Selection of studies and sources of evidence

After de-duplication using EndNote, independent reviewers screened citations based on eligibility criteria in two phases. First, paired independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts and discussed disagreements to reach consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer if needed. This was preceded by pilot testing 50 randomly selected citations with two independent reviewers who discussed disagreements to reach consensus. If the agreement was less than 80% during pilot screening, we discussed the inclusion and exclusion criteria and modified if needed before formal screening. All citations considered possibly eligible based on titles/abstracts went to full text screening. Paired independent reviewers screened full text citations and documented the reasons for exclusion. Paired reviewers discussed disagreements to reach consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer if needed. Similar to phase I, we conducted pilot testing on 25 randomly selected full texts to assess for at least 80% agreement.

Data charting and data items

We extracted data from included studies into a predefined table (informed by JBI guidance [42]), which outlined the sample characteristics and context (e.g., sociodemographics, description of LBP, or rehabilitation need, setting), methods (e.g., data collection, analysis), and main findings (e.g., structural barriers/facilitators to rehabilitation access, intersecting social factors). We pilot tested the form on five studies before formal data extraction. In data extraction, one reviewer extracted all data, and a second reviewer verified the extracted data. Disagreements were discussed between reviewers to reach consensus, with involvement of a third reviewer if needed.

Synthesis

In the context of answering our research question, we summarized the relevant data based on sample characteristics and context, methods, and main findings. After data extraction, one reviewer first read through all content from the extracted data. The reviewer then grouped the structural barriers and facilitators into domains based on similar content emerging from the data. We conducted the synthesis inductively and did not use an a priori framework to derive the domains. These proposed domains were discussed with the Advisory Committee of knowledge users for critical input to guide the alignment of the mapping process to the final domains. We mapped structural barriers or facilitators to rehabilitation access in this population, stratified by setting (low-, middle-, or high-income classified by World Bank [43]) and condition (LBP versus lumbar spinal stenosis). We identified knowledge gaps to guide future research, such as limited information on certain structural barriers/facilitators or in certain settings.

Critical input from knowledge users

We worked alongside an Advisory Committee of knowledge users (five members) that included people with lived experience (people living with musculoskeletal conditions), family members, and clinicians. Specifically, the knowledge users consisted of three people with lived experience, one family member (who also was a rehabilitation provider), and one rehabilitation clinician. Members of our research team have worked with these knowledge users on other projects and have built long-standing partnerships to support sharing of their diverse perspectives. This Advisory Committee provided critical input on the scoping review methods and interpretation of results to ensure our research was meaningful and relevant.

Results

Study selection

The search retrieved 8986 citations (Fig. 1). After removing 436 duplicates, we screened 8550 titles and abstracts, of which 505 were screened in full text. We identified 17 relevant articles reporting on 16 studies (14 unique study samples) after full-text screening. The primary reasons for exclusion during full-text screening were ineligible population, ineligible concept or context, and ineligible study design. The percentage agreement between independent reviewers was 93% for title/abstract screening and 77% for full-text screening. All disagreements were discussed between independent reviewers to reach consensus.

Study characteristics

All relevant studies were qualitative studies [16–28, 44–47] (Table 1; Appendix II). None of the studies were conducted in low-income countries. Three studies were conducted in middle-income countries (Nigeria, China, Brazil) [16–18], twelve studies were conducted in high-income countries (United States, Australia, Scotland, Netherlands, United Kingdom) [19–27, 44–47] and one study was conducted in both middle- and high-income countries (China and Switzerland) [28]. There were 12 studies focused on LBP among older adults in community settings, with the majority of studies focused on chronic LBP. There were four studies focused on older adults with lumbar spinal stenosis in community settings [24–27]. Among studies that focused on older adults with LBP, ages ranged from 57 to 92 years and 45–100% were women. For studies that focused on older adults with lumbar spinal stenosis, ages ranged from 61 to 92 years and 40–75% were women. Rehabilitation services described by participants varied across studies, with combinations of treatments including assistive devices, education and self-management, exercise, manual therapy, acupuncture, electrotherapy, peer mentoring with self-management (including education and exercise), or more broadly, rehabilitation services provided by different providers, including physiotherapists, general practitioners, chiropractors, physiatrists, and Chinese medicine practitioners.

Structural barriers and facilitators to accessing rehabilitation

We stratified the identified structural barriers and facilitators to accessing rehabilitation by condition (lumbar spinal stenosis versus LBP) and setting (middle-income versus high-income countries). The structural barriers/facilitators to rehabilitation access described in the studies were mapped onto four domains, with factors concerning: (1) finances; (2) location, transportation, inaccessibility or unavailability of services; (3) lack of knowledge, perceptions, or stigma regarding health condition or related care; (4) healthcare provider-related (referrals, collaboration, or continuity of care) (Table 2; Fig. 2). As described below, social factors intersected with structural barriers/facilitators to access, including rurality, socioeconomic strata, and disabilities (mobility limitations). As guided by the WHO Framework for Action on Social Determinants of Health [13], we describe below the structural and social factors of health inequities as they emerged from the data. None of the studies were conducted in low-income countries, which represents an important knowledge gap.

Low back pain

Middle-income countries

Overall, results from four studies conducted in middle-income countries mapped onto three domains [16–18, 28]: finances (3 studies); location, transportation, inaccessibility or unavailability of services (3 studies); lack of knowledge, perceptions, or stigma regarding health condition or related care (2 studies) (Table 2).

Financial factors were described as barriers/facilitators to rehabilitation access [17, 18, 28] (Table 2, Appendix III). This included the unaffordability of treatment or limited insurance coverage for treatment that acted as a barrier to accessing rehabilitation, whereas financial support from family acted as a facilitator. Long wait-times or limited scheduling for rehabilitation services also acted as barriers [16, 18]. In particular, services in public facilities providing rehabilitation in middle-income countries had limited resources, and at times, older adults with LBP had to access private clinics instead [16, 18]. It was noted that some older adults with LBP had the privilege of accessing public facilities for rehabilitation through personal connections to a worker or staff member in these facilities [16]. On the other hand, availability of rehabilitation services in community settings (e.g., a community centre for older adults) facilitated access to care [17].

Participants of one study described lack of knowledge as a barrier (i.e., lacking knowledge of rehabilitation options or of the types of healthcare providers available to provide rehabilitation) [17]. One study described that some healthcare providers appeared uninterested in helping older adults with LBP, which also acted as a barrier to access [18]. Overall, these structural barriers to access intersected with socioeconomic strata as a social factor.

High-income countries

Overall, results from nine studies mapped onto four domains [19–23, 28, 44, 46, 47]: finances (4 studies); location, transportation, inaccessibility or unavailability of services (4 studies); lack of knowledge, perceptions, or stigma regarding health condition or related care (6 studies); healthcare provider-related (referrals, collaboration, or continuity of care) (4 studies) (Table 2).

Financial factors were described barriers to rehabilitation access [20, 23, 28, 47] (Table 2, Appendix III). This included unaffordability of treatment, including if multiple visits across different healthcare providers were required, limited insurance coverage for treatment, and limited income among some older adults. Barriers to accessing rehabilitation also involved transportation or location-related factors [20–23]; barriers included the need to use public transportation, someone else to drive them to the clinic, or special travel arrangements for older adults living in senior housing. Conversely, a facilitator to access would be living in close proximity to the clinic, but some older adults noted the challenges of accessing rehabilitation when their regular healthcare provider moved/relocated [21].

Participants of one study described the challenges of living in rural areas where there was often lack of funding for certain rehabilitation programs, limited public transportation, and weather-related challenges during travel to clinics [22]. In this study, virtual care was explored as an option to overcome these location-related challenges, but some older adults had concerns about online security risk when using the internet, and some preferred personal contact when receiving care [22].

Referrals to other healthcare providers influenced access to rehabilitation services [20, 23], while recommendations for certain healthcare providers provided by family or friends acted as a facilitator [21]. Some older adults had a regular healthcare provider with whom they had a trusting relationship, which facilitated ongoing access to care [19]. When seeking rehabilitation services across different healthcare providers, collaboration, communication, and co-management between providers also facilitated access to care [20].

Not being provided with education on the health condition or treatment options for LBP served as barriers to access [20, 21, 23, 44]. Having previous experiences with a certain healthcare provider, a positive perception of the provider, or knowledge of the provider’s expertise or credentials served as facilitators [21, 23]. Some healthcare providers prioritized other health conditions over LBP, which acted as barriers to accessing care for LBP [20, 23, 44].

Dismissive or ageist comments (e.g., back pain is simply a normal part of aging) acted as barriers to accessing care [20, 23, 44–46]. In addition, some older adults with LBP felt that ableist stigmatization of assistive devices (e.g., walking aids, back braces) were barriers to their use [45, 46]. Some older adults with LBP described the use of assistive devices as a visual reminder of their functional limitations and vulnerability to stigma resulting from systemic ableism. Some participants were reluctant to use walking aids or back braces due to fear of age-related stigma or pity from others. Some participants with LBP would confront/reframe their view of the walking aid (e.g., avoiding the term “cane” and instead using “walking stick”) to reduce this fear of stigma [46]. Overall, these structural factors to access intersected with socioeconomic strata, rurality, and disabilities as social factors.

Lumbar spinal stenosis

High-income countries

Overall, results from four studies mapped onto four domains [24–27]: finances (2 studies); location, transportation, inaccessibility or unavailability of services (3 studies); lack of knowledge, perceptions, or stigma regarding health condition or related care (4 studies); healthcare provider-related (referrals, collaboration, or continuity of care) (1 study) (Table 2).

Financial factors, specifically unaffordable treatment costs, were described as barriers to accessing rehabilitation [25, 26] (Table 2, Appendix III). This barrier was related to the time commitment required to attend multiple treatment visits, which also acted as a barrier to access [25, 27]. A potential facilitator to overcome these financial or time-related barriers is to focus on education with self-management strategies [25, 26]. Another barrier for older adults with lumbar spinal stenosis was the transportation required to attend clinic visits for rehabilitation services, which included challenges with public transportation, lack of public parking, or the need for someone to drive them [24, 25]. Potential facilitators to overcome these barriers were services available locally and flexibility in scheduling [24, 25].

Lack of referrals to other healthcare providers for rehabilitation acted as a barrier to accessing these services [27]. Mistrust of healthcare providers also acted as a barrier [25, 26], which included some older adults perceiving that healthcare providers were ineffective or would withhold treatment such as removing certain interventions when other interventions were used (e.g., withholding pain medication if physical therapy was used) [26]. Some older adults with lumbar spinal stenosis wanted more education on the health condition and the range of rehabilitation options that were available [24, 25, 27].

Older adults with lumbar spinal stenosis in one study described the use of assistive devices as stigmatizing, which acted as a barrier to their use [24]. In this study, some participants described feeling strong negative emotions when considering or discussing walking aids, as some associated these devices with frailty and dependency. Although participants generally felt that walking aids could help older adults, some did not want to use them due to the stigma attached to appearing vulnerable. Some participants overcame their reluctance to use walking aids by personalizing or styling the device to reframe the perception of walking aids [24]. Overall, these structural factors to accessing rehabilitation intersected with socioeconomic strata and disabilities as social factors.

Across middle- and high-income countries for LBP and lumbar spinal stenosis, the stated structural factors to rehabilitation access intersected with socioeconomic strata, place of residence (e.g., rurality), and disabilities. The included studies did not explore other social factors such as gender, race, ethnicity, or occupation influencing structural factors to rehabilitation access. This represents an important knowledge gap for future research.

Discussion

For older adults with LBP or lumbar spinal stenosis in middle- or high-income countries, common barriers to accessing rehabilitation included the following domains: (1) finances (e.g., costs, insurance coverage); (2) location, transportation, inaccessibility or unavailability of services (e.g., inadequate public transportation, proximity to local services and resources, wait times); and (3) lack of knowledge, perceptions, or stigma with certain rehabilitation approaches (e.g., limited education of treatment options provided to the patient). Studies in high-income countries also explored healthcare-related factors (e.g., referrals, collaboration across different providers) as structural factors to access. For both LBP and lumbar spinal stenosis, some older adults described ageism from providers (e.g., advised to accept back pain as part of aging) or stigma with certain rehabilitation approaches as barriers to access. In particular, some older adults with LBP or lumbar spinal stenosis found the use of mobility aids stigmatizing, a barrier that required them to reframe/confront ableist perceptions of mobility aids. Findings varied by rurality, socioeconomic strata, and disabilities (mobility limitations). We did not identify any studies conducted in low-income countries, representing an important area for future research.

An important finding from our review is that some older adults reported ageist comments from their healthcare providers that acted as barriers to accessing care. These comments tended to frame LBP as a normal part of aging and felt dismissive to these older adults. Ableist stigma around assistive devices also limited their use, as some older adults felt that others view the assistive devices as markers of decline, drawing pity or judgement. These structural barriers are critical for further investigation to better understand their drivers and inform tailored training, resources, and other strategies that support inclusive, shared decision-making and reduce negative societal views. In addition, our findings suggest that virtual care may address location-related barriers to rehabilitation, though some older adults reported security concerns and preferences for in-person care [22]. While virtual care may improve access, inequities in access and design can widen disparities; the Principles for Equitable and Inclusive Digital Health Co-Design framework offers guidance for inclusive engagement and may inform future virtual care research [48].

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to systematically map the literature on structural barriers and facilitators to accessing rehabilitation among older adults with LBP. A previous systematic review examined exercise adherence among people with chronic LBP, reporting that accessibility to resources, including geographical location, transportation, and finances, influenced exercise adherence [49]. While these factors align with our findings, this previous review did not examine rehabilitation more broadly and was not focused on older adults with LBP. More broadly, a previous scoping review published in 2019 explored barriers to accessing rehabilitation services for non-communicable diseases and injuries [14]. The authors reported that main barriers to rehabilitation access were concerning limited or unequal geographical distribution of services, lack of transportation, and service unaffordability, and highlighted the important focus on low- and middle-income countries [14]. These findings align with the results of our scoping review. As added contributions to the literature, our review provides a comprehensive and updated view of the range of structural factors to rehabilitation access specific to older adults with LBP. We acknowledge that the four emergent domains of structural factors to accessing rehabilitation do not explicitly connect back to the WHO Framework for Action on Social Determinants of Health [13]. The domain concerning knowledge, perceptions, or stigma aligns with societal views, whereas the other three domains (finances; location, transportation, inaccessibility, services unavailability; healthcare provider-related factors) reflect issues in governance and policies. Of note, our review only identified 16 relevant studies, which constrains our ability to fully understand the breadth of structural and social factors affecting access to rehabilitation. Further research is needed in this area.

Our findings inform future research to guide advancements in rehabilitation access and health systems tailored to older adults with LBP or lumbar spinal stenosis in middle- and high-income countries. This aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, namely Goal 3 (to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages), and Goal 10 (to reduce inequality within and among countries) [50]. Our findings also help to support the United Nations Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030), which is a global collaboration to improve the lives of older people, their families, and the communities in which they live [51]. Understanding structural barriers and facilitators to rehabilitation access informs strategies to overcome barriers and improve care access to optimize functioning and quality of life for older adults with LBP.

Moreover, it is important for further research to support system-level changes aligned with the WHO Rehabilitation 2030 initiative [3]. This includes integrating rehabilitation into all levels of care and strengthening services in primary care and at the community level. Additional work is needed to improve rehabilitation availability, accessibility, and transportation access; address ageism and stigma around certain rehabilitation approaches, including assistive devices; and increase education on LBP, lumbar spinal stenosis, and related rehabilitation options among these communities. In particular, further research is needed to build deeper understanding of the negative views around back pain and certain rehabilitation approaches (e.g., mobility aids) in this area, including ageism and stigma from systemic ableism. Future studies can consider involving patient- and community partners in co-designing solutions to improve rehabilitation access for this population. Future research in these areas is warranted, particularly in low-income countries given the critical knowledge gap that we have identified. Further research is also needed to explicitly explore the influence of a wide range of social factors on rehabilitation access in this population.

Strengths and limitations

There are strengths to our scoping review. First, our protocol was pre-registered on Open Science Framework. We conducted the scoping review based on JBI guidance and reported it based on PRISMA-Scoping Reviews [30, 52]. Second, structural barriers/facilitators to accessing rehabilitation were informed by the WHO Framework for Action on Social Determinants of Health, while social factors were informed by Cochrane Equity PROGRESS-Plus [13, 39]. Third, a second librarian reviewed our search strategy using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies Checklist [40]. Fourth, we conducted pilot screening for training and calibration of independent reviewers before conducting formal screening. As a notable strength, we worked with an Advisory Committee of knowledge users, including people with lived experience, family members/caregivers, and healthcare providers, throughout all research stages. The Advisory Committee provided critical input on the scoping review methods and interpretation of results to ensure relevance and meaningfulness.

Our scoping review has limitations. First, we only included articles in English or French language to increase feasibility. We identified three possibly relevant articles published in other languages and included these references in the appendix for transparency (Appendix IV). Second, we only searched for unpublished literature in the form of theses/dissertations and full papers provided as conference proceedings. We did not search other sources of grey literature, so we may have missed other relevant studies that have not been published.

Conclusions

Older adults with LBP or lumbar spinal stenosis in middle- and high-income countries experience many structural barriers to rehabilitation access that vary across social factors. Structural factors influencing access to rehabilitation include financial barriers, location/transportation challenges, availability of services, healthcare provider-related factors, ageism, and stigma associated with certain rehabilitation strategies, including use of mobility aids. Findings inform future work towards strategies for equitable rehabilitation delivery in middle- and high-income countries. Research in this area is needed in low-income countries, as we identified this as an important knowledge gap.
