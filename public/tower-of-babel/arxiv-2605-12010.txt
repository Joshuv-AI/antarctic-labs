Limits of Learning Linear Dynamics from Experiments
Aybüke Ulusarslan, Niki Kilbertus, Nora Schneider

Abstract
Learning governing dynamics from data is a common goal across the sciences, yet it is only well-posed when the underlying mechanisms are identifiable. In practice, many data-driven methods implicitly assume identifiability; when this assumption fails, estimated models can yield spurious predictions and invalid mechanistic conclusions. Classical identifiability guarantees for controlled linear time-invariant (LTI) systems provide sufficient conditions– controllability and persistent excitation– but leave open whether identifiability holds when these conditions fail, and which parts of the system remain identifiable without full identifiability. We show that the experimental setup, i.e., the realized initial state and control input, dictates a fundamental limit on the information recoverable from the observed trajectory. We develop a geometric characterization of this limit and derive a closed-form description of all systems consistent with the experimental setup. Crucially, we prove that even when the full system is not identifiable, the restricted dynamics on the subspace reachable by the experiment remain uniquely determined.

This leads to our main contributions: (i) a partial identifiability theorem characterizing the subsystem uniquely determined by a given experiment,
(ii) an explicit, closed-form parametrization of all systems consistent with the experiment, and
(iii) algebraic criteria that quantify proximity to non-identifiability. Finally, we validate these results empirically, demonstrating their relevance for sparse systems.

3
Theoretical Analysis

This section provides an
experiment-conditional
characterization of identifiability from a single experiment
\mathrm{e}=(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{u})
. We proceed in four logical steps:
First, we introduce the
visible subspace

V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
– the geometric limit of what an experiment class
\mathcal{E}
can probe.
Second, we link the experiment class to a single experiment and demonstrate that a single persistently exciting control input maximizes information content in a single trajectory, forcing the experiment-consistent set to collapse to the design-consistent set, i.e.,
[A,B]_{\mathrm{e}}=[A,B]_{\mathcal{E}}
. Third, we develop a computable, closed-form parameterization of
[A,B]_{\mathrm{e}}
that characterizes the identifiable subsystem for an informative experiment
\mathrm{e}
. Finally, we propose two explicit, algebraic criteria that quantitatively describe a system’s proximity to structural non-identifiability for an informative experiment.

The Visible Subspace.
Excitation enters the system through two primary channels: the “initial excitation” injected by the state
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and the control inputs
u\in\mathcal{U}
filtered through the map
B
. The excitation propagates through the state space via the repeated action of the transition matrix
A
, defining a structural “information ceiling”—the smallest
A-
invariant subspace that contains all potential sources of excitation.

Definition 3.1

(Visible subspace)
.

For any system
(A,B)\in\Omega
and initial state
\mathrm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the
visible subspace
as

\displaystyle V(\mathrm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\;\coloneqq\;\mathcal{K}_{n}\!\big(A,\,[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;\;B]\big)

(7)

\displaystyle\;=\;\operatorname{span}\Big\{[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;\;B],\,A[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;\;B],\,\dots,\,A^{n-1}[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;\;B]\Big\},

where
\mathcal{K}_{n}
denotes the
n-
th order Krylov span generated by
[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+1)}
, the column-wise-stacked initial state vector and control matrix. Equivalently,
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
is the smallest
A
-invariant (i.e.,
AV(\mathrm{x}_{0})\subseteq V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
) subspace containing the initial state and the input image,
\{\mathrm{x}_{0}\}\cup\mathrm{Im}(B)
. (The formal proof is provided in
Appendix

A
).

The visible subspace imposes a strict boundary:
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
contains all directions of excitation and is invariant under
A
, therefore the trajectory induced by any experiment
\mathrm{e}
is mathematically locked within the visible subspace. Regardless of the input’s complexity or richness, the system cannot develop components in the “blind spot” outside
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
. We formalize this restriction as
orbit confinement
:

Lemma 3.2

(Orbit confinement)
.

For any system
(A,B)\in\Omega
, initial state
\mathrm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and admissible input
\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
, the resulting state trajectory is strictly confined to the visible subspace:
\varphi(t|A,B,\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{u})\in V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
.

Orbit confinement allows us to separate the state space into the
visible

V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
, and an arbitrary complementary subspace

of unexcited “blind” directions. Using a basis adapted to the direct sum
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})\oplus W=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can explicitly isolate the dynamics on

– which, as we will show, are uniquely determined by the experiment– from the unconstrained dynamics on

Lemma 3.3

(Block form in a
(V,W)

For any system
(A,B)\in\Omega
and initial state
\mathrm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

be as in

V\coloneqq V(\mathrm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle T^{-1}AT

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}A_{V}&A_{*}\\[2.0pt]

\displaystyle\;\;T^{-1}B=\begin{bmatrix}B_{V}\\[2.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle,\;\;T^{-1}\mathrm{x}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{x}_{0,V}\\[2.0pt]
0\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

In particular, any realized trajectory under

,

depends only on

This lemma yields the structural consequences of orbit confinement (

can affect the observed trajectory.
In coordinates adapted to

and is independent of the blocks

determines the design-consistent set

From Design Limits to Experiment Limits.

The results above are
structural
: they characterize the maximal information that is, in principle, recoverable from the experiment design
\mathcal{E}
. In practice, however, the practitioner often conducts only a single experiment
\mathrm{e}=(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{u})
and thus faces the larger experiment-consistent set
[A,B]_{\mathrm{e}}
. In the following, we analyze under which conditions this larger set collapses to the structural limit
[A,B]_{\mathcal{E}}
. In particular, we show that this depends on the
information content of the observed trajectory
: even when
(A,B)
is structurally identifiable within
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
, an uninformative control input may fail to excite all in-principle identifiable directions. This motivates characterizing the informativeness of an observed trajectory with respect to the visible subspace.

Definition 3.4

(Informative Trajectory)
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{[0,T]}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}z(t)z(t)^{\top}dt

(PE Transfer)

Consider the restricted dynamics on the visible subspace

where

Next, we formalize the link between the experiment-consistent set and the design-consistent set. Intuitively, if a single experiment is sufficiently informative, it exhaustively sweeps the visible subspace, leaving us with the same uncertainty as if we had performed all possible experiments
\{(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathrm{u}):\mathrm{u}\in\mathcal{U}\}

Theorem 3.6

(The Bridge)
.

Let
(A,B)\in\Omega
and let
\mathcal{E}=(\mathrm{x}_{0},\mathcal{U})
be an experiment design. If the realized trajectory generated by

is informative (Def.

), then the single-trajectory consistency set collapses to the design-level consistency set:
[A,B]_{\mathrm{e}}=[A,B]_{\mathcal{E}}

Theorem

3.6
guarantees that a single informative experiment suffices to reach the design-level information ceiling.

Experiment-Conditional Identifiability.

Next, we characterize the structure of the experiment-consistent set under an informative experiment by combining
Theorem

3.6
and
Lemma

3.3
. Since

3.6
guarantees

\mathcal{E}

V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
, while they may differ arbitrarily on the complement

Tests for Full Identifiability.
To test if a system is fully identifiable from a single experiment
\mathrm{e}
, we need to evaluate two conditions. First, we need to test if the induced experimental trajectory is informative (informative trajectory requirement). Trajectory informativeness can be ensured by using a persistently exciting input
\mathrm{u}
of order at least
k+1
(
Definition

2.1
). Second, we need to test if the visible subspace spans the full state space, i.e.,
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
(full-visibility condition). We propose two algebraic criteria to test the latter condition.
These criteria build on a controllability test of the augmented system pair
(A,[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B])
. This is motivated by the fact that
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
is precisely the Kalman reachable subspace of the augmented pair
(A,[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B])
.

(i)

(Left-eigenvector test).
Full identifiability fails if and only if there exists a left eigenpair
(\lambda,w)
of
A
, with
w^{\top}A=\lambda w^{\top}
, such that
w^{\top}\mathrm{x}_{0}=0
and
w^{\top}B=\mathbf{0}
(equivalently,
w^{\top}[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B]=\mathbf{0}
). To quantify the proximity to this failure, for each left eigenpair
(\lambda_{i},w_{i})

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(A,B\mid\mathrm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\|w_{i}^{\top}[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;\;B]\|_{2}}{\|w_{i}\|_{2}\|[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B]\|_{2}},\;\;

\displaystyle\mu_{\min}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{i}\mu_{i}\in[0,1].

Under a sufficiently rich control input, the system is fully identifiable if and only if
\mu_{\min}>0
.

(ii)

(Fixed-experiment PBH test).
The classical Popov–Belevitch–Hautus (PBH) criterion measures distance to uncontrollability via the matrix pencil
[\lambda I-A,B]
. Because
\mathrm{x}_{0}
is a fixed property of the executed experiment and not a system parameter subject to uncertainty, any unobservable mode must be strictly orthogonal to the exact nominal
\mathrm{x}_{0}
.
Define
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{\perp}\coloneqq\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:w^{\top}\mathrm{x}_{0}=0\}
and let

have orthonormal columns spanning
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{\perp}
(take
Q=I_{n}
if
\mathrm{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
). Let
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)\coloneqq[\lambda I-A,\ B]
. We define the fixed-experiment PBH margin as:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{PBH}}(A,B\mid\mathrm{x}_{0})\coloneqq\inf_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\sigma_{\min}\big(Q^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\lambda)\big),

(11)

where
\sigma_{\text{min}}(\cdot)
denotes the smallest singular value. Under a sufficiently rich control input, the system is fully identifiable if and only if
d_{\mathrm{PBH}}(A,B\mid\mathrm{x}_{0})>0
.

Both quantities,
\mu_{i}(A,B\mid\mathrm{x}_{0})
and
d_{\mathrm{PBH}}(A,B\mid\mathrm{x}_{0})
, can also be interpreted as
margins
to non-identifiability (provided that the informative trajectory requirement is satisfied). Specifically, when the margins are very small, the inverse problem might be numerically ill-conditioned: an estimator that inverts a near-rank-deficient regressor might struggle with identifying the true system, even though the system is theoretically fully identifiable.

5
Related Work

Learning LTI systems from data has received considerable attention
[
9
,
22
,
20
]
. These works derive finite-time, non-asymptotic bounds on the error of least-squares and gradient-based estimators, characterizing how quickly the system matrices can be recovered as a function of trajectory length and noise level. A common feature is that identifiability is guaranteed by design: the system is driven by i.i.d. process/observation noise (or random inputs) that ensures the state visits all directions, making the estimation well-posed. We address the complementary, logically prior question: when the experiment does not provide such sufficient excitation, which parameters are recoverable at all?

Classical identifiability for controlled LTIs
[
12
,
1
]
establishes that controllability plus PE inputs suffice for identifiability.
Identifiability for autonomous systems has been studied geometrically by
25
, who link global identifiability from an initial state to whether the orbit is confined to a proper
A-
invariant subspace, and by
17
, who derive closed-form descriptions of experiment-consistent systems via Jordan decomposition. Our work extends this geometric perspective from autonomous to controlled systems. In this setting, persistent excitation becomes essential for identifiability. Moreover, rather than using a Jordan-decomposition characterization as in
17
, we formulate the equivalence class through Krylov subspaces– an approach which connects directly to controllability and yields simple algebraic tests.

Recent work studies identifiability under hidden confounders
[
26
]
and via multi-environment intervention diversity
[
18
]
. These address different ambiguities– unobserved latent variables and representation non-uniqueness under Gaussianity – rather than the geometric limits imposed by a single experiment. Closest to our setting is the work by
29
, who show that data-driven predictive control remains equivalent to model-predictive control when controllability fails. Their focus is which
control tasks
remain solvable without controllability; ours is which
parameters
remain identifiable, a logically prior question that characterizes the experiment-induced epistemic uncertainty.

6
Discussion

We characterize experiment-conditional identifiability for controlled LTI systems without assuming controllability– a setting that classical theory
[
12
,
27
]
and the autonomous-system literature
[
25
,
17
]
both leave unresolved. We derive the visible subspace, the state space that an experiment can probe. If the visible subspace is a proper subspace of the state space, the system is only partially identifiable.
In that case, any system identification method that fits the observed trajectory is blind to the dynamics outside of the visible subspace, and conclusions about those directions are unwarranted regardless of training fit.
Empirically, we show that sparse systems are often uncontrollable, but system identification methods still successfully recover the identifiable subsystem.

Proof (

\mathrm{x}_{0}
.

Proof.

Let

g(\lambda)\neq 0
for all
\lambda\in\sigma_{r}(A,B)
, so
g(A)
is invertible on

\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}:=\operatorname{span}\{A^{k}B\}
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{span}\{A^{k}B_{d}\}=\operatorname{span}\{A^{k}g(A)B\}=\operatorname{span}\{A^{k}B\}=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct},

giving
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{dt}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}
.
For the reverse inclusion, restrict attention to
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}
.
On this subspace, the eigenvalues of
A
are precisely
\sigma_{r}(A,B)
, which
remain distinct under
z\mapsto e^{\Delta tz}
by the non-pathological sampling assumption.
Since
f(z)=e^{\Delta tz}
is analytic with
f^{\prime}(\lambda)=\Delta te^{\lambda\Delta t}\neq 0
,
the Jordan block sizes for
A
and
A_{d}=f(A)
on
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}
coincide
(
[
10
, §3]
). Consequently, for every polynomial
p
there exists a polynomial
q

such that
p(A)\big|_{\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}}\;=\;q(A_{d})\big|_{\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}}
.
Applying both sides to
B_{d}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}
yields

\operatorname{span}\{A^{k}B_{d}\}\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{A_{d}^{k}B_{d}\}
, hence

\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{0}^{dt}
.
Combining the two inclusions gives
\mathcal{R}_{0}^{dt}=\mathcal{R}_{0}^{ct}

(see also
[
8
,
13
,
2
]
for closely related criteria).

3. Visible subspace is preserved.

From
\operatorname{span}\{A_{d}^{k}B_{d}\}\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{A^{k}B_{d}\}
with
w\in\{x_{0}\}\cup\operatorname{col}(B)
and
B_{d}=g(A)B
we get the easy direction

V_{dt}(x_{0})\subseteq V_{ct}(x_{0})
.

For the reverse inclusion on the
autonomous
part
\mathcal{K}_{n}(A,x_{0})
,
assume
\lambda\mapsto e^{\lambda\Delta t}
is injective on all of
\sigma(A)
.
By the same analytic/Jordan-block argument as above (now on the full space),
for each polynomial
p
there is a
q
with
p(A)x_{0}=q(A_{d})x_{0}
, hence

\mathcal{K}_{n}(A,x_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{n}(A_{d},x_{0})
.
Together with (ii), which gives
\mathcal{K}_{n}(A,B)=\mathcal{K}_{n}(A_{d},B_{d})
,
we conclude
V_{ct}(x_{0})\subseteq V_{dt}(x_{0})
, and thus equality.

4. Informativeness under ZOH.
To bridge the continuous-time informativeness to ZOH inputs, we rely on the exact discrete-time dynamics on the visible subspace
V(\mathrm{x}_{0})
:

\displaystyle\xi[j+1]=A_{d,V}\xi[j]+B_{d,V}\mathrm{u}[j],

where
A_{d,V}=e^{A_{V}\Delta t}
and
B_{d,V}=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}e^{A_{V}s}B_{V}\;ds
. By Step 3, if
\Delta t
is non-pathological,
(A_{d,V},[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;\;B_{d,V}])
is a controllable pair on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(where
k=\operatorname{dim}(V(\mathrm{x}_{0}))
).

For discrete-time systems, an input sequence
\mathrm{u}[0],\dots,\mathrm{u}[N-1]
is persistently exciting of order
r
if its Hankel matrix
\mathcal{H}_{r}(\mathrm{u})
has full row rank:

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{u}[0]&\mathrm{u}[1]&\dots&\mathrm{u}[N-r]\\
\mathrm{u}[1]&\mathrm{u}[2]&\dots&\mathrm{u}[N-r+1]\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{u}[r-1]&\mathrm{u}[r]&\dots&\mathrm{u}[N-1]\end{bmatrix}=rm.

Let
z[j]:=[\xi[j]^{\top},\mathrm{u}[j]^{\top}]^{\top}
. We want to show that if
\mathrm{u}
is PE of order
k+1
, the discrete Gramian
\mathcal{G}_{d}=\sum_{j}^{N-1}z[j]z[j]^{\top}
is positive definite.

Assume for contradiction that
\mathcal{G}_{d}
is singular. Then, there exists a nonzero
w=[\nu^{\top},\eta^{\top}]^{\top}
such that
w^{\top}z[j]=0
for all
j
, meaning
\nu^{\top}\xi[j]+\eta^{\top}\mathrm{u}[j]=0
. Using the discrete-time transition equation
\xi[j+1]=A_{d,V}\xi[j]+B_{d,V}\mathrm{u}[j]
, we can express future states purely in terms of the initial state and inputs. Substituting
\xi[j]
into the annihilator and shifting forward in time generates a system of linear equations identical in structure to the continuous-time derivative annihilator in
Equation

15
. By applying the Cayley-Hamilton theorem to
A_{d,V}
, we can linearly combine
k+1
consecutive time shifts to eliminate the state
\xi[j]
entirely. This leaves a linear combination of strictly the inputs
\mathrm{u}[j],\dots,\mathrm{u}[j+k]
equating to zero for all
j
. However, because the input is PE of order
k+1
, the rows of the Hankel matrix are linearly independent, meaning no such non-trivial linear combination can exist. This forces
\nu=0
and
\eta=0
, contradicting
w\neq 0
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}_{d}\succ 0
, and the trajectory is informative.

∎

We simulate

1

Beyond the high-level experimental design in §4, we fix the following implementation-level choices for the baseline estimators. DMDc was implemented as the standard least-squares estimator of

NODEs (§4.2).
Neural ODEs
[
5
]
use a parameterized function

to approximate the underlying ODE of the system in
Equation

1
. While

is typically implemented as a neural network, in our sparse linear setting we parameterize it directly by a matrix pair

Appendix D
Example

(experiment-consistency).

We illustrate the theory with the following example.

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
0&2&1\\
0&0&3\end{bmatrix},\;B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
2&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

and the initial states

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(a)}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\quad(\text{``good''}),\qquad\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\quad(\text{``bad''}).

A convenient left–eigenbasis of
A
is

\displaystyle w_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\
-2\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad w_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-1\\
1\end{bmatrix},\quad w_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix},

for which

\displaystyle w_{1}^{\top}B=[-2,\,-2],\ \ w_{2}^{\top}B=[-2,\,-1],\ \ w_{3}^{\top}B=[0,\,0],\qquad w_{3}^{\top}\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(a)}=1\neq 0,\ \ w_{3}^{\top}\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}=0.

Thus the third left–eigenvector is completely unexcited by
B
, exactly mirroring the
m{=}1
base case.

Visible/hidden decomposition and the secondary system.

Let
V\coloneqq\mathcal{K}(A,\,[\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}\ \ B])
and choose a complementary
W
so that
\mathbb{R}^{3}=V\oplus W
.
Because
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}
has zero third component and the third row of
B
vanishes, one checks
V=\{(x_{1},x_{2},0):x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}
and
W=\mathrm{span}\{e_{3}\}
. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{A}\ \coloneqq\ \begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
0&2&0\\
0&0&4\end{bmatrix},\qquad\tilde{B}\ \coloneqq\ B.

Then
\tilde{A}|_{V}=A|_{V}
and
V
is
\tilde{A}
–invariant. Consequently, for any input and any trajectory staying in
V
(in particular, those from
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}
), the responses of
(A,B)
and
(\tilde{A},\tilde{B})
coincide; while for initial conditions with a
W
–component (e.g.
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(a)}
) the responses differ.

Identifiability scores.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{3}\!\coloneqq\!K\!\big(A,[\mathrm{x}_{0}\ B]\big)
and let
\mu_{i}(A,B\hskip 1.42271pt|\hskip 1.42271pt\mathrm{x}_{0})\coloneqq\|w_{i}^{\top}[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B]\|_{2}\big/\bigl(\|w_{i}\|_{2}\;\|[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B]\|_{2}\bigr)
with
\mu_{\min}=\min_{i}\mu_{i}
.
For our two initial states:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}\text{initial state}&\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{K}_{3})&\mu_{\min}&\text{PBH margin}&\lambda_{\min}\big(W_{T}([\mathrm{x}_{0}\ B])\big)\\
\hline\cr\text{good }\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(a)}&3&\approx 0.41&\approx 0.77&\approx 8307.33\\
\text{bad }\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}&2&0&=0&=0\end{array}

Here "PBH margin" denotes
d_{\mathrm{PBH}}(A,B\mid\mathrm{x}_{0})
as defined in
Equation

11
, and
W_{T}
is the finite-horizon controllability Gramian of the augmented pair
(A,[\mathrm{x}_{0}\;B])
.

How
(\tilde{A},\tilde{B})
is picked.

The construction enforces
\tilde{B}=B
and
\tilde{A}|_{V}=A|_{V}
on the
visible
subspace

\displaystyle V=\mathcal{K}\!\big(A,\,[\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}\ \ B]\big)=\{(x_{1},x_{2},0)\},

while freely modifying the
hidden
block on
W=\mathrm{span}\{e_{3}\}
(here: moving the eigenvalue
3\to 4
and zeroing the
V\!\to\!W
/
W\!\to\!V
couplings). This preserves all single-trajectory data from
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(b)}
yet changes the responses from
\mathrm{x}_{0}^{(a)}
, illustrating the equivalence-class mechanism.
