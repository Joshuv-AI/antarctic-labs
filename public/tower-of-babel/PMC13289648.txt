Differential Regulation of Granule Release in Platelets and Immune Cells
Authors: Qing Xia, Jiandao Hu, Hongcun Sun, Zhixiang Zhu, Wenbo Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatelets and certain immune cells contain releasable granules that play essential roles in hemostasis and immune regulation. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing degranulation of platelets versus immune cells remain incompletely understood.ObjectivesThis study aimed to elucidate the distinct regulatory mechanisms underlying granule release in platelets and immune cells.MethodsMouse platelets were freshly isolated from peripheral blood, neutrophils were purified from bone marrow, and mast cells were generated from bone marrow progenitors by in vitro differentiation. The effects of phorbol ester (PMA), ionomycin, and their combination on degranulation of platelets, neutrophils, and mast cells were examined. In addition, pharmacological inhibitors targeting key components of multiple signaling pathways were used to investigate the molecular mechanisms regulating degranulation of these three cell types.ResultsPMA and ionomycin strongly induced α-granule release of platelets, whereas only moderately stimulating dense-granule secretion. For neutrophils, PMA and ionomycin each triggered moderate azurophilic-granule release, whereas their combination markedly enhanced degranulation. In contrast, PMA alone failed to induce mast cell degranulation, ionomycin robustly stimulated granule release, and their combination further amplified ionomycin-induced response. Mechanistically, thrombin-induced platelet degranulation was inhibited exclusively by phospholipase C (PLC) and protein kinase C (PKC) inhibitors. Zymosan-induced neutrophil degranulation was suppressed by inhibitors of phosphoinositide 3 kinase, spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK), Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK), mitogen-activated protein kinase 1/2 (MEK1/2) and P38 but was enhanced by PLC and PKC inhibition. Mast cell degranulation induced by IgE and antigen was significantly inhibited by the inhibitors targeting SYK, BTK, PLC, and PKC.ConclusionThese findings reveal substantial differences in the regulatory pathways controlling degranulation in platelets and immune cells. Such distinctions highlight the opportunities for the development of cell type-selective inhibitors to modulate degranulation, providing potential therapeutic strategies for thrombotic diseases and immune-related diseases.

Introduction

Platelets are a type of blood cell and play important roles in hemostasis. In platelets, there are two major classes of releasable granules that are named as α-granules and dense granules. Alpha-granules store large thrombogenic proteins, including fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor, fibronectin, and thrombospondin, whereas dense granules contain small nonprotein molecules, such as adenosine diphosphate, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), serotonin, and calcium. 1 2 3 Upon exposure to thrombin or subendothelial collagen at sites of vascular injury, circulating platelets undergo activation and release the contents of these granules, a process essential for further platelet recruitment and activation, ultimately promoting platelet aggregation and hemostasis. However, excessive platelet activation can result in pathological thrombosis, leading to conditions such as myocardial infarction and stroke. 4 To develop new inhibitors that target granule release of platelets and subsequent activation for the prevention and treatment of thrombotic diseases, it is necessary to deeply reveal the regulatory mechanisms governing platelet degranulation.

Similarly, many immune cells contain distinct types of granules and the release of the content in granules in response to stimuli plays key roles in regulating immune function. In neutrophils, azurophilic granules, specific granules, tertiary granules, and secretory vesicles are generated during differentiation and maturation. The contents of these granules can be released in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns, chemoattractants, and cross-linking of FcγRs. 5 6 In addition, mast cells contain releasable granules that are critical mediators of allergic diseases. Upon antigen binding to IgE associated with FcεRI on mast cells, granule-stored mediators such as histamine, leukotriene, and prostaglandins are rapidly secreted. 7 8 Exploring the regulatory mechanisms governing degranulation of neutrophils and mast cells is meaningful for developing strategies to control inflammatory and allergic diseases.

Accumulating evidence highlights coordinated interactions between platelets and immune cells in thrombotic, inflammatory, and allergic diseases. Platelet factor 4 (PF4) released from activated platelets promotes immune cell chemotaxis, whereas platelet-activating factor derived from immune cells induces platelet activation. 9 10 Dissecting the mechanistic differences underlying platelet and immune cell regulation is therefore essential for selectively targeting these systems, particularly in granule release. Some studies have reported that protein kinase C (PKC) and calcium play important regulatory roles in the degranulation of multiple cells. However, their respective contributions to degranulation of platelets, neutrophils, and mast cells remain incompletely understood. The different regulatory effects of PKC and calcium on platelets versus immune cells also require further clarification. In addition, multiple signaling pathways have been implicated in regulating degranulation of these cells. 11 12 13 Elucidating how these pathways orchestrate cell type-specific degranulation will facilitate the development of targeted therapies for thrombotic, inflammatory, and allergic diseases.

In the present study, we investigated the different effects of phorbol ester (PMA), ionomycin, and their combination on the degranulation of platelets, neutrophils, and mast cells. In addition, we explored how inhibitors targeting key components of multiple signaling pathways regulate degranulation of these three cell types.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Prostaglandin E 1 (PGE 1 ) were purchased from Aladdin Industrial (Shanghai, China). Thrombin from bovine plasma (T6634), adenosine 5′-triphosphate disodium salt (ATP), luciferase, 1,4-dithiothreitol (DTT), coenzyme A sodium salt (CoA), bovine serum albumin (BSA), zymosan, anti-dinitrophenyl (DNP) IgE, DNP-BSA, hydrogen peroxide, 4-methylumbelliferyl-β-D-glucuronide hydrate, and p-nitrophenyl-N-acetyl-β-D-glucosamide were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). D-luciferin was obtained from J&K Scientific (Beijing, China). OxiRed probe was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Magnetic bead isolation kit for mouse neutrophils was purchased from Miltenyl Biotec (Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Recombinant mouse stem cell factor (SCF) and interleukin-3 (IL-3) were obtained from PeproTech (Rocky Hill, New Jersey, United States). Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) conjugated to APC, FITC, APC, PE, FITC, FITC, and APC and specific for CD41, CD62P, Ly-6G, CD11b, CD63, c-Kit, and FcεRI), as well as 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) were purchased from Becton, Dickinson and Company (Franklin Lake, New Jersey, United States). Phorbol ester, ionomycin, LY294002 (phosphoinositide 3 kinase (PI3K inhibitor), fostamatinib (spleen tyrosine kinase [SYK] inhibitor), ibrutinib (Bruton's tyrosine kinase [BTK] inhibitor), U73122 (phospholipase C [PLC] inhibitor), Go 6983 (PKC inhibitor), U0126 (mitogen-activated protein kinase 1/2 [MEK1/2] inhibitor), and SB203580 (P38 inhibitor) were purchased from AbMole Bioscience (Houston, Texas, United States).

Animals

Fifty-four male BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old, weighing 25 ± 2 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhejiang University (Hangzhou, China), and bred in specific pathogen-free and climate-controlled facilities of Ningbo University. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethical Committee on Animal Research in Ningbo University (number: 11827) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in the University.

Preparation of Washed Platelets

Washed platelets were prepared as previously described. 14 Briefly, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (60 mg/kg), and blood was collected into tubes containing sodium citrate as an anticoagulant. Immediately after collection, the citrated blood was centrifuged at 200 g for 10 minutes at room temperature to obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP). The PRP was then diluted with CGS buffer (18.3 mM trisodium citrate, 1.7 mM citric acid, 10 mM glucose, 120 mM sodium chloride, pH 6.5) containing 2 μM PGE 1 . Platelets were isolated from the diluted PRP by centrifugation at 800 g for 10 minutes and washed again with CGS buffer without PGE 1 . The final washed platelets were resuspended in modified Tyrode's buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.9 mM KCl, 0.34 mM Na 2 HPO 4 , 12 mM NaHCO 3 , 5 mM HEPES, 5 mM glucose, 1 mM MgCl 2 , and 1 mM CaCl 2 , pH 7.3). Mice were subsequently sacrificed by cervical dislocation.

Preparation of Neutrophils and Mast Cells

Neutrophils were isolated from bone marrow of BALB/c mice. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium and subsequently sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Bone marrow cells were collected from the femurs and tibias, and red blood cells were removed using lysis buffer. The remaining cells were resuspended in magnetic activated cell-sorting buffer (PBS, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% BSA) and neutrophils were isolated using a negative-selection magnetic bead kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cell purity was analyzed by flow cytometry on BD LSRFortessa (Becton, Dickinson and Company), with Ly-6G + neutrophils exceeding 90%. Purified neutrophils were maintained in IMDM supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C under 5% CO 2 .

Bone marrow-derived mast cells were generated by culturing bone marrow cells in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 50 ng/mL SCF and 20 ng/mL IL-3 for 4 weeks. After cytokine induction, more than 95% of the cells were identified as c-Kit + FcεRI + mast cells by flow cytometry.

Flow Cytometry of Alpha Granule Release of Platelets

Mouse platelets (1 × 10 7 /mL in modified Tyrode's buffer) were stimulated with phorbol ester, ionomycin, or their combination for 10 minutes at 37°C. The platelet suspension was then incubated with anti-mouse CD41-APC and anti-mouse CD62P-FITC for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. The expression of CD62P on CD41 + platelets was subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

In some experiments, mouse platelets were pretreated with or without serial dilutions of inhibitors targeting PI3K, SYK, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, or P38, and then activated with 0.1 U/mL thrombin for 10 minutes at 37°C. The platelet suspension was incubated with anti-mouse CD41-APC and anti-mouse CD62P-FITC for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. The expression of CD62P on CD41 + platelets was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Bioluminescence Analysis of ATP in Dense Granules Released by Platelets

Mouse platelets (1 × 10 7 /mL in modified Tyrode's buffer) were added into a white 96-well plate and stimulated with phorbol ester, ionomycin, or their combination for 10 minutes at 37°C. Subsequently, the luciferase-luciferin reagent (1.25 μg/mL luciferase, 250 μM luciferin, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 15 μM coenzyme A, and 0.025% BSA in modified Tyrode's buffer) was added, and ATP released from dense granules was measured by bioluminescence analysis on an EnSpire Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States).

In some experiments, platelets were pretreated with or without serial dilutions of inhibitors targeting PI3K, SYK, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, or P38 for 5 minutes, followed by stimulation with 0.1 U/mL thrombin for 10 minutes at 37°C. ATP release was then quantified by bioluminescence analysis.

Flow Cytometry of Degranulation of Neutrophils

Purified neutrophils (5 × 10 5 /well) were inoculated into 48-well plates and stimulated with phorbol ester, ionomycin, or their combination for 1.5 hours at 37°C. In some experiments, the neutrophils were pretreated with or without serial dilutions of inhibitors targeting PI3K, SYK, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, or P38, and then activated with 10 μg/mL zymosan for 1.5 hours at 37°C. Cells were subsequently collected, resuspended in PBS containing 0.2% BSA, and incubated with anti-mouse Ly-6G-APC, anti-mouse CD63-FITC, anti-mouse CD11b-PE, and 7-AAD for 30 minutes on ice. The expression of CD63 and CD11b on 7-AAD - Ly-6G + neutrophils was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Activity Analysis of Myeloperoxidase and Beta-Glucuronidase Released by Neutrophils

Purified neutrophils (2.5 × 10 5 /well) were seeded into 96-well plates and stimulated with phorbol ester, ionomycin, or their combination for 1.5 hours at 37°C. The activity of released myeloperoxidase was measured by incubating 50 μL of supernatant with 50 μL of 200 μM hydrogen peroxide and 100 μM OxiRed probe in 0.1 M potassium phosphate (pH: 7.0) at 37°C for 20 minutes. Fluorescence was recorded at an excitation wavelength of 535 nm and an emission wavelength of 587 nm. In addition, the activity of released β-glucuronidase was determined by incubating 50 μL of supernatant with 50 μL of 2 mM 4-methylumbelliferyl-β-D-glucuronide hydrate in 0.05 M sodium citrate (pH: 4.5) at 37°C for 1 hour. The reaction was terminated by adding 200 μL of 0.05 M carbonate buffer (pH: 10), and fluorescence was measured immediately at an excitation wavelength of 365 nm and an emission wavelength of 460 nm. 15

Activity Analysis of Beta Hexosaminidase Released by Mast Cells

Mast cells (1.5 × 10 5 /well) were inoculated into 96-well plates and stimulated with phorbol ester, ionomycin, or their combination for 1 hour at 37°C. β-hexosaminidase release was quantified by incubating 50 μL of supernatant with 50 μL of 1 mM p-nitrophenyl-N-acetyl-β-D-glucosamide in 0.05 M sodium citrate buffer (pH: 4.5) at 37°C for 1 hour. The reaction was terminated by adding 200 μL of 0.05 M sodium carbonate buffer (pH: 10) and absorbance was measured at 405 nm.

In some experiments, mast cells were sensitized overnight with 1 μg/mL anti-DNP IgE. After washing to remove unbound IgE, cells were pretreated with or without serial dilutions of inhibitors targeting PI3K, SYK, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, or P38, and then stimulated with 1 μg/mL DNP-BSA for 1 hour at 37°C. β-hexosaminidase release was subsequently quantified as described above.

Statistical Analysis

All data were obtained from three independent experiments, each including three biological replicates (mice). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation ( n = 3 experiments). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc test was used to analyze statistical significance, with a threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

The Effects of PMA and Ionomycin on Platelet Degranulation

To investigate the effects of PMA and ionomycin on platelet degranulation, we determined the release of α-granules and dense granules with flow cytometry and bioluminescence analysis. As shown in Fig. 1A and B , resting platelets exhibited low basal CD62P expression. Stimulation with PMA, ionomycin, or their combination significantly increased CD62P expression on platelets. The percentages of CD62P + platelets were 70.6 ± 3.4% for PMA, 83.5 ± 0.6% for ionomycin, and 86.6 ± 2.1% for the combination. CD62P expression in the ionomycin and combination groups was higher than in PMA group; however, the combination did not significantly exceed ionomycin alone. These results indicate that both PMA and ionomycin strongly induce α-granule release of platelets.

Effects of PMA and ionomycin on platelet degranulation. Washed platelets resuspended in modified Tyrode's buffer were transferred into 1.5-mL centrifuge tubes and stimulated with PMA, ionomycin, or their combination at 37°C for 10 minutes. After stimulation, anti-mouse CD41-APC and anti-mouse CD62P-FITC were added and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by fixation with paraformaldehyde. CD62P expression on CD41 + platelets was analyzed by flow cytometry ( A ) and ( B ). In some experiments, washed mouse platelets were seeded into white 96-well plates and stimulated with PMA, ionomycin, or their combination at 37°C for 10 minutes. Luciferase-luciferin reagent was then added, and ATP release was quantified by bioluminescence analysis ( C ). # p < 0.05 versus control; & p < 0.05 versus PMA; * p < 0.05 versus ionomycin.

Interestingly, both PMA and ionomycin moderately induced ATP release from platelets, whereas their combination triggered a robust increase in ATP release ( Fig. 1C ). These results suggest that neither PMA nor ionomycin alone is sufficient to fully induce dense-granule release, but their combination can achieve complete degranulation.

The Regulatory Effects of Multiple Inhibitors on Thrombin-Induced Degranulation of Platelets

Thrombin-induced platelet degranulation involves in multiple signaling pathways. To reveal the roles of specific pathway components, we assessed the effects of inhibitors targeting key enzymes. The results showed that inhibitors of PI3K, SYK, BTK, MEK1/2, and P38 had no significant effect on thrombin-induced CD62P expression or ATP release. In contrast, PLC and PKC inhibitors markedly suppressed both CD62P upregulation and ATP release ( Fig. 2 ), indicating that PLC and PKC are critical regulators of thrombin-induced platelet degranulation.

After stimulation, anti-mouse CD41-APC and anti-mouse CD62P-FITC were added and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by fixation with paraformaldehyde. The Effects of PMA and Ionomycin on Neutrophil Degranulation

Neutrophils contain multiple granule types formed during differentiation and maturation. The contents of these granules are released in response to multiple endogenous and exogenous stimuli. In this study, we first investigated the effects of PMA and ionomycin on neutrophil degranulation by flow cytometry. The results showed that both PMA and ionomycin strongly upregulated CD63 expression, but their combination did not enhance this effect ( Fig. 3A and B ). PMA moderately increased CD11b expression, whereas ionomycin induced a robust upregulation; intriguingly, their combination reduced this effect ( Fig. 3A and C ). Subsequently, we analyzed the effects of PMA and ionomycin on neutrophil degranulation by determining the release of myeloperoxidase and β-glucuronidase. It was observed that both PMA and ionomycin mildly induced the release of myeloperoxidase and β-glucuronidase, whereas their combination markedly enhanced the release of myeloperoxidase and β-glucuronidase ( Fig. 3D and E ).

Effects of PMA and ionomycin on neutrophil degranulation. Neutrophils were seeded in 48-well plates and stimulated with PMA, ionomycin, or their combination at 37°C for 1.5 hours. Cells were then harvested and stained with 7-AAD and fluorescence-conjugated anti-CD63, anti-CD11b, and anti-Ly-6G antibodies. The expression of CD63 and CD11b on 7-AAD − Ly-6G + neutrophils was analyzed by flow cytometry ( A – C ). In some experiments, neutrophils were seeded in 96-well plates and stimulated under the same conditions for 1.5 hours, after which the activities of released myeloperoxidase and β-glucuronidase were measured by fluorescence analysis ( D ) and ( E ). # p < 0.05 versus control; & p < 0.05 versus PMA; * p < 0.05 versus ionomycin.

The Regulatory Effects of Multiple Inhibitors on Zymosan-Induced Degranulation of Neutrophils

To reveal the regulatory mechanisms of neutrophil degranulation, we stimulated neutrophils with zymosan and assessed the regulatory effects of various inhibitors. As shown in Fig. 4A–G , inhibitors of SYK, BTK, MEK1/2, and P38 significantly suppressed zymosan-induced CD63 upregulation, while PI3K inhibition caused a modest reduction. Interestingly, 10 μM PLC inhibitor and 0.4 μM PKC inhibitor enhanced zymosan-induced CD63 expression. Similar patterns were observed for CD11b expression in response to these inhibitors ( Fig. 4H–N ).

The regulatory effects of multiple inhibitors on neutrophil degranulation. Neutrophils were seeded in 48-well plates and pretreated with serial dilutions of inhibitors for 30 minutes, followed by stimulation with zymosan (10 μg/mL) at 37°C for 1.5 hours. Cells were then harvested and stained with 7-AAD and fluorescence-conjugated anti-CD63, anti-CD11b, and anti-Ly-6G antibodies. The expression of CD63 ( A – G ) and CD11b ( H – N ) on Ly-6G + neutrophils was analyzed by flow cytometry. # p < 0.05 versus control; * p < 0.05 versus Zymosan.

The Effects of PMA and Ionomycin on Mast Cell Degranulation

Mast cells are critical effector cell in allergic responses, releasing granule-stored mediators upon antigen binding to IgE associated with FcεRI. We first examined the effects of PMA and ionomycin on mast cell degranulation. The results showed that PMA alone did not trigger β-hexosaminidase release, whereas ionomycin strongly stimulated it. Notably, the combination of PMA and ionomycin further enhanced ionomycin-induced release of β-hexosaminidase ( Fig. 5 ).

The activity of released β-hexosaminidase was subsequently measured by colorimetric analysis. # p < 0.05 versus control; & p < 0.05 versus PMA; * p < 0.05 versus ionomycin.

The Regulatory Effects of Multiple Inhibitors on DNP-BSA-Induced Degranulation of Mast Cells

To investigate the roles of key signaling components in mast cell degranulation, we also assessed the effects of multiple enzyme inhibitors on antigen-induced β-hexosaminidase release. Inhibitors of SYK, BTK, PLC, and PKC markedly suppressed antigen-induced degranulation ( Fig. 6B–E ). In contrast, PI3K and P38 inhibitors only mildly reduced β-hexosaminidase release ( Fig. 6A and G ), and MEK1/2 inhibitor had no significant effect ( Fig. 6F ).

Discussion

PKC agonist and calcium ionophore are commonly used to activate platelets, but the distinct regulatory effects of PKC activation, calcium influx, and their combination on platelet degranulation remain unclear. In this study, we found that both PMA and ionomycin strongly induced α-granule release of platelets, indicating that PKC and calcium can independently trigger α-granule secretion. In contrast, ATP release from dense granules was only moderate upon treatment with PMA or ionomycin alone, whereas their combination completely induced ATP release. These findings suggest that complete dense-granule secretion in platelets requires the coordinated action of both PKC and calcium.

Platelet activation is regulated by multiple signaling pathways. For example, PI3K, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, and P38 have been reported to play important roles in thrombin-induced platelet responses. 16 17 18 19 In particular, Gilligan et al. reported that PKC plays a key role in platelet activation induced by PMA, A23187 (an analogue of ionomycin), and thrombin, which was further supported by pharmacological inhibition using the PKC inhibitor staurosporine. However, staurosporine has relatively low selectivity and can inhibit multiple kinases. 20 In this study, we used specific inhibitors of these enzymes to examine their roles in thrombin-induced platelet degranulation. The highly selective inhibitors of PLC and PKC significantly suppressed α-granule and dense-granule release, whereas inhibition of PI3K, BTK, MEK1/2, or P38 had no effect. In many cell types, PLC is directly activated by agonist receptors or indirectly via PI3K-BTK or SYK-BTK signaling pathway, subsequently activating PKC through diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol triphosphate (IP 3 ). In our previous study, we observed that thrombin mainly induces phosphorylation of PLCγ2 and PKCδ. 21 Therefore, our data suggest that thrombin might primarily induce platelet degranulation through direct activation of the PLCγ2-PKCδ signaling pathway. We will further validate this by immunoblotting or phosphoproteomic analysis in subsequent studies. It is worth noting that mouse platelets predominantly express protease-activated receptor 4 (PAR4), whereas human platelets express PAR1 and PAR4, with PAR1 being more sensitive to thrombin stimulation. The differences in signaling pathways regulating thrombin-induced degranulation between mouse and human platelets also warrant further investigation.

PMA is well known to induce the respiratory burst and promote reactive oxygen species production in neutrophils, 22 23 but its effects on neutrophil degranulation have not been fully clarified. Moreover, the impact of calcium ionophore and its combination with PMA on neutrophil degranulation remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the effects of PMA, ionomycin, and their combination on azurophilic granule release of neutrophils by analyzing CD63 surface expression and the releases of MPO and β-glucuronidase. Both PMA and ionomycin strongly upregulated CD63, and their combination did not further enhance this effect. However, each stimulus only moderately induced MPO and β-glucuronidase release, whereas their combination markedly amplified the secretion of these enzymes. These findings suggest that CD63 translocation to the plasma membrane does not necessarily indicate complete discharge of azurophilic granules. PKC activation and calcium elevation can independently trigger partial azurophilic granule release, while their combined action promotes more complete granule exocytosis. Interestingly, we also examined CD11b expression that is mainly derived from specific granules and secretory vesicles. 24 25 PMA moderately increased CD11b expression, ionomycin intensively enhanced it, but their combination unexpectedly suppressed these stimulatory effects. These results indicate that although PKC activation and calcium elevation can individually induce the release of specific granules and secretory vesicles, their combination may interfere with this process. The underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Zymosan, a TLR2 ligand, activates neutrophils and mimics the stimulatory effects of fungi. 26 27 28 29 In this study, we used selective inhibitors of PI3K, SYK, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, and P38 to examine their regulatory roles in zymosan-induced neutrophil degranulation. The results showed that inhibition of PI3K, SYK, BTK, MEK1/2, or P38 significantly suppressed zymosan-induced CD63 upregulation. In contrast, 10 μM PLC inhibitor and 0.4 μM PKC inhibitor unexpectedly enhanced CD63 expression. The data suggest that PI3K, SYK, BTK, PLC, PKC, MEK1/2, and P38 positively regulate zymosan-induced the release of azurophilic granules, whereas PLC and PKC may exert negative regulatory effects in this context. A similar pattern was observed for the regulation of CD11b expression, which reflects specific-granule release. Inhibitors of PI3K, SYK, BTK, MEK1/2, and P38 suppressed zymosan-induced CD11b upregulation, whereas PLC and PKC inhibitors enhanced it. These results indicate that PI3K, SYK, BTK, MEK1/2, and P38 promote zymosan-induced specific-granule exocytosis, while PLC and PKC negatively modulate this process.

Calcium is a crucial factor for mast cell function, primarily triggering granule release through exocytosis. In addition, several studies have reported that calcium can contribute to the stabilization of the mast cell membrane. 30 31 To clarify the roles that PKC activation and calcium influx in mast cell degranulation, we also investigated the effects of PMA, ionomycin, and their combination on β-hexosaminidase release. PMA alone failed to induce degranulation, whereas ionomycin elicited a strong release of β-hexosaminidase. Notably, the combination of PMA and ionomycin further enhanced ionomycin-induced degranulation. These findings suggest that calcium plays a particular critical role in mast cell degranulation, while PKC activation contributes only a secondary, supportive effect. Furthermore, membrane stabilization of mast cells is just partly enhanced by extracellular calcium under resting conditions. The influx of extracellular calcium together with the release of intracellular calcium stores induced by ionomycin can rapidly induce mast cell degranulation.

Under physiological conditions, mast cell degranulation is primarily triggered by antigen binding to IgE already bound to FcεRI on the cell surface. 32 33 34 35 In this study, mast cells were sensitized with DNP-specific IgE and subsequently stimulated with DNP-BSA to induce degranulation. We then evaluated the regulatory effects of the aforementioned inhibitors on mast cell degranulation by measuring β-hexosaminidase release. The results demonstrated that inhibitors of SYK, BTK, PLC, and PKC strongly suppressed antigen-induced degranulation of mast cells, whereas PI3K and P38 inhibitors produced only mild inhibitory effects. Given that SYK functions directly downstream of FcεRI in mast cells and SYK inhibitor is clinically applied in the treatment of anaphylactic diseases, 36 our findings further suggest that PLC and PKC activation in antigen-induced mast cell degranulation is largely mediated through SYK and BTK signaling.

Collectively, our findings reveal substantial differences in the regulatory mechanisms governing degranulation in platelets and immune cells. PKC and calcium make distinct contributions to the degranulation processes of these cell types. Moreover, thrombin-induced platelet degranulation engages signaling pathways that differ markedly from those activated during zymosan-induced neutrophil degranulation and antigen-induced mast cell degranulation. These results highlight the potential for developing selective inhibitors that specifically target degranulation in platelets or immune cells, offering promising strategies for treating thrombotic disorders and immune dysfunctions.
