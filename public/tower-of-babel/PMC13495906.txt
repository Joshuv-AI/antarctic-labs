MOF‐Derived Bi2WO6/MXene Ternary Heterojunction as a Highly Efficient Piezocatalyst for Ultrasound‐Driven Hydrogen Evolution and Oxygen Evolution Reaction
Authors: Atul Verma, Esakkinaveen Dhanaraman, Yen‐Pei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA Bi‐MOF (CAU‐17)‐derived, Bi‐rich Bi2WO6 composite (MBW0.25) is rationally designed and integrated with Ti2C MXene to realize an efficient piezo‐catalyst for ultrasound‐driven hydrogen evolution and piezo‐catalytic water splitting. Controlled Na2WO4 incorporation induces a dual‐phase Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 structure with lattice distortion and enhanced polarization, while partially retaining MOF domains. The resulting disordered Bi2WO6 framework exhibits distinct structural and electronic features compared to conventionally synthesized Bi2WO6, favoring piezo‐induced charge separation. Coupling MBW0.25 with 5 wt.% MXene yields an unprecedented MOF‐derived oxide/MXene ternary heterojunction for piezo‐catalysis with excellent efficiency. The optimized MBW0.25/MX‐5 achieves a high piezo‐hydrogen evolution rate of 1450.1 µmol g−1 h−1, representing ∼36, ∼21, and ∼7‐fold enhancements over pristine Bi‐MOF, Bi2WO6, and Ti2C, respectively. Radical trapping experiments reveal a non‐conventional •OH‐mediated hydrogen evolution pathway, distinguishing this system from typical electron‐driven piezocatalytic processes. The catalyst maintains relatively stable activity in tap water and shows appreciable performance in seawater. Piezo‐electrochemical oxygen evolution reaction measurements further confirm efficient mechanical‐to‐electrical energy conversion, evidenced by reduced overpotentials, increased piezo‐currents, and lowered charge‐transfer resistance under vibration.

Introduction

Hydrogen is increasingly recognized as a clean energy source, offering high energy density and simple combustion products (water), and thus represents the transition towards sustainable energy. Recent investigations emphasize green hydrogen production via direct water splitting or mechanical energy harvesting as a means to reduce carbon emissions in transport, industrial feedstocks, and long‐duration energy storage [1]. Piezo‐catalysis, wherein mechanical deformation drives charge separation and subsequent redox reactions, is gaining traction as a complementary pathway to conventional photocatalysis. Reviews highlight this mode of energy conversion as a promising yet underexplored avenue for hydrogen production. Significantly, piezo‐catalytic systems have demonstrated synergistic coupling with wastewater remediation, demonstrating that the mechanical‐energy‐harvesting route can yield hydrogen even in challenging aqueous environments [2]. Within the family of piezo‐catalytic materials, layered bismuth tungstate (Bi2WO6) is particularly noteworthy for its favorable structural and electronic properties. A recent review on Bi2WO6 summarizes its Aurivillius‐layered structure, internal polarity, suitable band edges, and capacity for defect engineering, all of which contribute to enhanced charge separation and catalytic activity [3]. Previously, Bi2WO6 nanoplates have been demonstrated to promote hydrogen evolution under ultrasonic vibration by exploiting their inherent piezoelectricity and strong redox properties. These findings underscore the significance of Bi2WO6 as a suitable material for piezo‐catalytic hydrogen evolution [4]. The design of composite catalysts combining Bi2WO6 with porous metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs) offers further advantages. MOFs can act as templates to control morphology, increase surface area, enhance active site exposure, and introduce heterointerfaces that facilitate charge separation. Meanwhile, two‐dimensional transition‐metal carbides, such as Ti2C (MXenes), serve as excellent electron acceptors, providing rapid electron transport, abundant conductive surfaces, and strong interfacial contact [5, 6, 7]. Furthermore, the formation of Schottky junctions between MXenes and Bi2WO6 has been shown to significantly improve piezo‐catalytic hydrogen evolution by facilitating efficient electron transfer, improving piezoelectric response, and suppressing recombination [8]. In combination, these catalysts synergize the piezoactivity of the oxide with the high conductivity/charge extraction capacity of MXene, enhancing piezo‐catalytic output. In the recent decade, applications of Bi2WO6 are plentiful in photocatalytic pollutant and dye removal [9, 10, 11, 12], CO2 reduction [13, 14, 15, 16], and NH3 production [17, 18, 19, 20]. Similarly, Bi‐MOF‐derived Bi2WO6 composite photocatalysts have emerged in recent times [21, 22, 23]. In addition, Bi2WO6 is well known as a candidate for piezo‐catalysis; however, the piezo‐performance of Bi‐MOF is still underexplored. Recent literature has explored Bi‐MOF‐based piezo catalysis for pollutant removal; however, there is no specific target for piezo‐HER. This makes it relatively novel to explore such possibilities [24]. In addition, to explore the dual functionality, piezo‐electrocatalytic water splitting was achieved via the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). OER is a critical component of water splitting for hydrogen evolution, as it is the rate‐determining step. It is well known that OER is characterized by sluggish kinetics, prompting further research into catalyst development to reduce its overpotential [25]. Moreover, an additional piezo force can enhance OER kinetics and further reduce the OER overpotential. Use of piezo in such scenarios has rarely been reported, as most of the literature prefers light irradiation to mechanical vibration. In this work, we report the synthesis of a Bi‐MOF (CAU‐17) derived series of Bi2WO6/Bi‐MOF composites produced by controlled Na2WO4 addition (0.25, 0.50, 0.75 mmol). The 0.25 mmol sample (MBW0.25) exhibited the highest piezocatalytic H2 production and was subsequently hybridized with Ti2C MXene at 2, 5, and 8 wt.%, with 5 wt.% yielding the highest performance. We analyzed structure‐property relations, piezo‐induced charge dynamics, and the role of MXene loading in boosting H2 evolution and electrocatalytic water splitting, and compared our findings to recent advances in MOF‐derived Bi‐oxides and MXene‐based piezo‐catalytic systems.

Results and Discussion

Material Formation and Morphological Analysis

Figure 1a depicts the XRD pattern of the synthesized catalysts. Interestingly, in MBW0.25, additional shoulder peaks were observed at 27.86°, 32.27°, 46.27°, and 54.88°, adjacent to the main Bi2WO6 (JCPDS 39‐0256) reflections (28.34°, 32.81°, 47.15°, and 55.90°). These positions correspond closely to those reported for Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 (JCPDS 43‐0447), indicating the formation of a Bi‐rich intermediate phase alongside Bi2WO6. The existence of Bi‐rich Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 was most likely attributed to the excess amount of Bi3+ and a lower amount (0.25 mmoles) during the hydrothermal reaction. As shown in Figure 1b, with the increase in the Na2WO4 precursor from 0.25 to 0.75 mmoles, the Bi2WO6 peaks shift to lower 2θ values, reflecting an expansion of the lattice, probably due to the formation of oxygen vacancies and residual stress from defects [26, 27]. Figure S2 shows the XRD pattern of comparison samples Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 and Bi2WO6 (N), clearly depicting their distinct peak positions. Moreover, the presence of residual stress and percentage of Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 in MBW0.25 can also be understood via Rietveld refinement [28] performed on MBW0.25, MBW0.5, and MBW0.75. All the refined parameter data are shown in Table 1, and the fitting is shown in Figure S3. The XRD data were fit using standard COD files 1528730 (Bi3.84W0.16O6.24) and 2108252 (Bi2WO6), with Rwp and GOF values indicating reliable, accurate fits. In the MBW0.25 fitting, the relative percentages of Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 and Bi2WO6 were calculated to be 19.27 % and 80.73%, respectively. A gradual increase in lattice parameters and unit cell volume (from 487.69 to 489.13 Å3) was observed with increasing Na2WO4 concentration, indicating subtle lattice expansion and structural distortion during the MOF‐derived phase evolution. The crystallite size (131) decreased from 30.68 nm for MBW0.25 (only for the Bi2WO6 phase) to 17.7 nm for MBW0.75, suggesting suppression of crystallite growth at higher Na2WO4 concentrations. These findings suggest that Na2WO4 concentration strongly influences nucleation kinetics, defect formation, and structural ordering in the MOF‐derived Bi2WO6 framework.

FTIR spectra (Figure 1d) also exhibit peak broadening of the W─O peak at 728 cm−1, supporting the presence of structural disorder and defect sites. Furthermore, the absence of W─O─W vibrational bands in the Bi2WO6 derived from the MOF confirms disruption of the W–O–W chain network, further evidencing defect formation within the Bi2WO6 lattice. In Figure 1c, the Bi‐MOF bands at 533, 731, and 825 cm‐1 could be attributed to metal oxygen Bi–O and out‐of‐plane C–H bending [29, 30]. Whereas, bands at 1382, 1543, and 1614 cm−1 are symmetric and unsymmetric carboxylate stretching in the MOF. Bi‐MOF bands are present in MBW0.25 but missing in the MBW0.5 and MBW0.75 samples, indicating a complete conversion of MOF into defective Bi2WO6. Bi‐MOF comprises thick rod structures with a size of around ∼3 µm (Figure 1e), which is reduced to ∼0.6 µm in MBW0.25 (Figure 1f), confirming the conversion of MOF into Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6. The structural duality observed in XRD of MBW0.25 also explains the dual morphology observed in the FESEM image, as the Bi‐rich phase often crystallizes as irregular nanoparticles (Bi3.84W0.16O6.24) within the Bi2WO6 hierarchical structure composed of nanoplates assembled into larger micro‐sheets, as reported previously [31, 32]. Additionally, Figure S4a,b shows 1 µm‐sized disk structures of MBW0.75 (MOF‐derived Bi2WO6) and pristine Ti2C with a layer thickness of 375 nm. Figure 1g depicts the MBW0.25/MX‐5 catalyst showcasing the presence of all components of the composite, namely Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6, Bi‐MOF, and Ti2C, forming a synergistic intimate junction. The elemental distribution and EDS spectra of MBW0.25/MX‐5 clearly reveal the presence of Bi, W, O, Ti, and C, as shown in Figure 1h. The role of Bi‐MOF is primarily as a structure‐directing precursor that enables the formation of a non‐stoichiometric Bi‐rich phase and defect‐rich lattice, which are not typically observed in conventionally synthesized Bi2WO6 systems. Moreover, there is a clear enhancement in the surface area from 9.56 m2g−1 for Bi2WO6(N)‐derived from nitrate to 11.97 m2g−1 for MBW0.25, as shown in Figure S4c.

The TEM image of MBW0.25/MX‐5 (Figure 2a) reveals the micro‐sized Bi2WO6 flower‐shaped structure, which is made up of nanosheets. Dark spots ∼100 nm in size could potentially be the Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 phase, as confirmed by FESEM analysis. Clear lattice fringes with spacings of 2.77 and 2.79 Å, indexed to the (200) planes of Bi2WO6, confirm the presence of Bi2WO6. The corresponding SAED pattern shown in Figure 2b displays well‐defined polycrystalline diffraction rings attributed to the (200), (202), and (331) planes of Bi2WO, as confirmed by XRD data. HAADF‐STEM and elemental mapping (Figure 2c) show a uniform distribution of Bi, W, O, Ti, C, and N across the sample, demonstrating homogeneous dispersion of all the elements. As shown in Figure S5, TEM was recorded at another place depicting the Ti2C stacked structure with d = 2.69 Å corresponding to the (101) plane, analogous to published reports [33, 34], along with smaller nano‐sized flakes of Bi2WO6 confirmed via d = 2.74 Å of the (200) plane. This confirms the formation of a heterointerface between the Ti2C and Bi2WO6 in MBW0.25/MX‐5.

In Figure 3b, the XPS spectra of Bi 4f for the MBW0.25/MX‐5 composite shows two main spin‐orbit pairs at 159.18, 164.43 eV and 160.73, 165.83 eV, assigned to the Bi 4f7/2 and Bi 4f5/2 components, respectively. The latter pair appears at a slightly higher binding energy relative to Bi2WO6, attributed to Bi3+ species showcasing a change in the local environment, likely arising from the presence of a secondary Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 phase or strong interfacial interaction with Ti2C MXene. Similar splitting and chemical shifts have been observed in studies involving non‐stoichiometric or defective Bi2WO6‐based systems and heterostructures in previous reports. The W 4f XPS spectra (Figure 3c) of MBW0.25/MX‐5 reveal two prominent peaks at 35.11 and 37.31 eV, corresponding to W 4f7/2 and W 4f5/2 of W6+ in Bi2WO6, with additional features at higher binding energies (35.37 and 39.12 eV). These results are similar to the ones observed in Bi 4f spectra [35, 36]. Figure 3d depicts High‐resolution O 1s XPS spectra revealing multiple oxygen environments across the samples. Bi‐MOF shows a Bi–O component at 530.76 eV with higher BE components at 531.72 and 533 eV from surface hydroxyls/Chemisorbed oxygen (Oc) and adsorbed water molecules. MBW0.75 displays oxide lattice O at 530.78 eV and an adsorbed hydroxyl component at 532.48 eV, which could emanate from conversion to Bi2WO6 from Bi‐MOF with residual surface species. Ti2C exhibits characteristic O–Ti based surface oxide at 530.39 eV and Ti–OH terminations around 531.99 eV [37, 38]. The MBW0.25/MX‐5 composite shows a distinct lattice oxygen at 529.36 eV (lower B.E. than other catalysts), together with a second lattice component at 530.6 eV attributable to lattice oxygen from Bi2WO6, Bi3.84W0.16O6.24, and Ti–O (Ti2C). The presence of the Bi‐rich Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 non‐stochiometric phase can create unique chemical environments, herein increased electron density leading to a lower B.E. peak at 529.36 eV. This observation is consistent with previous reports [39, 40]. The higher B.E. components at 531.93 and 533.82 eV are likely due to the hydroxyl/Chemisorbed oxygen (Oc) and adsorbed H2O components, respectively. The C 1s XPS spectrum (Figure 3e) for MBW0.25/MX‐5 displays distinct peaks at 281.79, 284.80, 286.45, and 288.59 eV. The lowest binding energy component at 281.79 eV is attributed to Ti–C bonds characteristic of the MXene core structure, while the peak at 284.8 eV is ascribed to adventitious or graphitic carbon. Peaks at 286.45 and 288.59 eV are assigned to oxygenated carbon species, such as C–O and O–C═O, likely arising from surface terminations and interfacial interactions between the MXene and Bi2WO6 components [41]. Figure 3f shows the Ti 2p spectra, in which Ti2C reveals peaks at 455.73 and 459.03 eV, which are direct evidence of Ti–C and Ti–F bonds. Moreover, the surface oxide Ti–O shows are larger peak at 461.53 and 464.76 eV. The broad peaks in MBW0.25/MX‐5 are quite tedious to deconvolute since Ti 2p overlaps with Bi 4d from Bi2WO6. Based on previous reports, involving similar catalytic systems, Ti–C peak was marked at 462.36 eV, and Ti–F was at 459.01 eV [8].

Piezoforce Microscopy (PFM) Analysis

The PFM amplitude images in Figure 4a,e,i reveals the spatial distribution of electromechanical response. The MBW0.25/MX‐5 composite exhibits the strongest contrast and highest signal intensity, indicating enhanced local piezoresponse compared to Bi‐MOF and MBW0.25 catalysts. Moreover, the phase images in Figure 4b,f,j show clear contrast differences, confirming the presence of polarization domains in all catalysts. As shown in Figure 4c,g,k, composite MBW0.25/MX‐5 shows a lower amplitude than Bi‐MOF and MBW0.25 catalysts. The enhanced amplitude of MBW0.25 could result from its strong lattice polarization and good deformation capability under piezo environment. Hence, it could be regarded as an efficient piezocharge generator. In MBW0.25/MX‐5, the piezo amplitude decreases because charges generated by MBW0.25 are quickly dissipated by the highly conductive Ti2C MXene, thereby reducing the overall local electric field. The composite exhibits a shift in switching voltage toward higher bias (∼4 V), indicating increased field. This behavior could be attributed to the formation of a built‐in electric field at the heterointerface and to enhanced charge screening provided by the conductive MXene component. In Figure 4d,h,i, phase switching behaviour is nearly ∼180°, signifying the presence of polarization reversal.

Piezo‐Catalytic H2 Production

Figure 5a,b presents the time‐resolved H2 evolution profiles and H2 production rate, respectively, for all the synthesized catalysts. The estimations were based on calibrated H2 data as shown in Figures S6 and S7. Bare Bi‐MOF exhibits a very low activity (40 µmol g−1 h−1), whereas MBW0.25 and MBW0.75 demonstrate significant improvements up to 557.1 and 455.2 µmol g−1 h−1, respectively. Obviously, integration of MXene (Ti2C) in MBW0.25 dramatically boosts H2 evolution, with MBW0.25/MX‐2 and MBW0.25/MX‐5 achieving outstanding evolution rates up to 1410.3 and 1450.1 µmol g−1 h−1, respectively, signifying a synergistic effect between Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6/Bi‐MOF and MXene components. The formation of a non‐stoichiometric Bi‐rich phase induces defect‐mediated lattice distortion and oxygen vacancies, which act as polarization centers, enhancing piezoelectric charge generation beyond conventional stoichiometric Bi2WO6 systems. The presence of these vacancies was conclusively proved via EPR, as shown in Figure S8. The EPR signal at g = 1.99 in MBW0.25/MX‐5 is attributed to electrons trapped at oxygen vacancy sites, predominantly originating from the nonstoichiometric Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 phase. The incorporation of Ti2C facilitates electron delocalization and stabilizes these defect states, leading to an enhanced EPR response [42, 43]. MXene loading beyond the optimal point, MBW0.25/MX‐8, results in decreased activity (371.3 µmol g−1 h−1), suggesting excessive MXene may impede electron transfer or reduce catalytic surface area. Control experiments with 10% methanol (no catalyst), Ti2C, and no piezo confirm their weak piezo‐catalytic activity, with H2 evolution rates of 23.9, 223.9, and 0 µmol g−1 h−1, respectively, underscoring the crucial role of composite engineering. Moreover, BiNO3‐derived Bi2WO6 (N) yielded a mere 70.7 µmol g−1 h−1 H2, much lower than the Bi‐MOF‐derived MBW0.75 (completely converted Bi2WO6), which yielded 455.2 µmol g−1 h−1 of H2 (Figure S9). This indicates the superiority of MOF‐derived components, owing to structural distortions and defects that may have enabled efficient adsorption and desorption of intermediates. An interesting result is shown by pristine Bi3.84W0.16O6.24, yielding 308.8 µmol g−1 h−1 of H2, which, although higher than nitrate‐derived Bi2WO6 (N), falls short compared with MOF‐derived MBW0.75. Moreover, TOF values, as shown in Table S1, indicate that MBW0.25/MX‐5 shows a TOF of 101.16 × 10−2 h−1, which is 60 and 2.6 times higher than Bi‐MOF and MBW0.25, respectively. Figure 5c demonstrates the robustness of the MBW0.25/MX‐5 composite across different aqueous environments. The catalyst achieves nearly identical maximal H2 yields in tap water (1102.3 µmol g−1 h−1), with lower production in real seawater (307.9 µmol g−1 h−1) and simulated seawater (547.7 µmol g−1 h−1). These results highlight the inhibitory effect of ionic species in seawater, likely due to competition for surface‐active sites or screening of the piezoelectric field. Nevertheless, the composite maintains appreciable activity even in challenging real‐world conditions, supporting its practical potential for diverse water sources. Radical scavenging experiments (Figure 5d) were performed to identify the active species involved, wherein isopropanol (IPA), AgNO3, and Ascorbic acid (AA) were used as scavengers for •OH radicals, electrons, and •O2¯, respectively. In conventional hydrogen evolution systems, the addition of electron scavengers significantly reduces H2 production, indicating that electrons are the dominant reducing species. However, in the present work, AgNO3 caused only a minor suppression (24%), suggesting that direct electron‐driven proton reduction is not the sole pathway. In contrast, the addition of IPA significantly suppressed H2 evolution (63.8%), indicating the crucial role of hydroxyl radicals. This means that there is an indirect •OH‐based pathway to produce H2 rather than a direct reaction between H+ and electrons. •OH radicals produced during piezocatalysis may participate with CH3OH to produce carbon‐based radicals such as •CH2OH, which are further oxidized to HCHO and surface‐bound H*. Finally, these H* combine to produce H2 [44, 45, 46]. AA (usually used as a •O2¯ scavenger) also showed significant H2 suppression by 55.2%. However, in our experiments, O2 was removed from the solution by purging with N2 prior to each experiment. In such cases, AA most likely scavenges holes (h+) and •OH [47]. Therefore, it can interfere with the previously mentioned •OH reaction pathway and hence suppress H2 production. Post‐Catalytic Analysis

Figure 6a evaluates the recyclability of MBW0.25/MX‐5 over four consecutive cycles, revealing a gradual decline in H2 evolution from 1450.1 to 620.8 µmol g−1 h−1. This indicates moderate durability and suggests further optimization to reduce deactivation, which may arise from surface fouling or structural degradation. Post‐catalytic and XPS were employed to study the moderate deactivation of MBW0.25/MX‐5. As shown in Figure 6b, XRD of used MBW0.25/MX‐5 clearly validates the appearance of Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 as a relatively dominant phase as opposed to Bi2WO6. Moreover, as shown in Figure 6c,d, new features are observed at 157.63 (Bi 4f) and 34.08 (W 4f), which could suggest reduced Bi and W environments, most likely from Bi(3−x)+/Bi0 and W5+, respectively. Additionally, the other Bi and W peaks have lower binding energies, indicating a strong reduction of Bi and W centers during piezocatalysis, which might also point to the formation of excessive oxygen vacancies [15, 53]. Figure 6e shows the changes in the O 1s environment, which is also quite significant. The OL peaks in the pristine sample are now shifted to higher binding energies, from 529.36 and 530.58 eV to 529.88 and 531.05 eV, indicating electron removal from the oxygen environment and the removal of oxygen atoms during piezoelectrolysis, again reiterating the formation of excessive oxygen vacancies [54, 55]. Also, the loss of OH‐ and H2O‐related peaks suggests depletion of active sites for H+ reduction on the catalyst surface. As shown in Figure 6f, the disappearance of the Ti–C peak and C–O components indicates degradation of the MXene surface chemistry and loss of oxygen‐containing functional groups. This is consistent with the O 1s results, which show depletion of hydroxyl and adsorbed water species. Overall, composite phase transformation, over‐reduction, leads to the loss of active sites, resulting in interface collapse, which clearly reflects in the performance of H2 production over successive cycles.

(a) Recycle experiments for MBW0.25/MX‐5, comparison of pristine and used MBW0.25/MX‐5 (b) XRD, XPS (c) Bi 4f, (d) W 4f, (e) O 1s, and (f) C 1s.

To elucidate the working mechanism of the MBW0.25/MX‐5 composite, UV‐DRS, XPS valence band (VB) spectra, Mott–Schottky, and KPFM measurements were examined. As shown in Figure 7a–f, MBW0.25/MX‐5 exhibits a strong surface potential of 148.7 mV, compared to MBW0.25 (138.1 mV) and Bi‐MOF (84.5 mV). This indicates it possesses a robust electric field built in, providing better charge separation for H2 production [56]. The fitted TRPL spectra of Bi‐MOF, MBW0.25, and MBW0.25/MX‐5 are shown in Figure S10a–c. The composite sample exhibited the longest average lifetime (τav = 4.30 ns) compared to Bi‐MOF (2.44 ns) and pristine MBW0.25 (2.83 ns), indicating suppressed charge‐ carrier recombination after Ti2C incorporation. Moreover, the appearance of a long‐lived decay component (τ3 = 55.66 ns) in MBW0.25/MX‐5 suggests efficient interfacial charge separation and prolonged carrier migration. Moreover, the contribution of the faster decay components τ1 from 67.42% (Bi‐MOF), 63.97% (MBW0.25), to 56.64% (MBW0.25/MX‐5) further confirms inhibition of rapid electron‐hole recombination. The enhanced carrier lifetime can be attributed to the excellent electrical conductivity and electron‐trapping capability of Ti2C MXene, which facilitates interfacial electron transfer and accelerates charge separation [57, 58]. As shown in Figure S10d, PL emissions were collected at two excitations, namely 250 and 405 nm. MBW0.25 shows a significant quenching of emission compared to Bi‐MOF; however, the composite MBW0.25/MX‐5 exhibits higher emission than MBW0.25. Despite this, the prolonged TRPL lifetime indicates more efficient interfacial charge separation and delayed recombination after Ti2C incorporation. To further strengthen the interfacial charge separation, the photocurrent response was evaluated as shown in Figure S11. MBW0.25/MX‐5 depicted the highest response as compared to Bi‐MOF and MBW0.25, confirming the facile movement of photogenerated charge carriers throughout the heterostructure, catering to its superior activity [59]. The presence of such a heterointerface can also be inferred from the shift in VB‐XPS, as shown in Figure S12. The VB of MBW0.25/MX‐5 is significantly up‐shifted closer to Fermi (Ef = 0) at 1.74 eV compared to Bi‐MOF (2.45 eV), MBW0.75 (2.4 eV), and Ti2C (2.78 eV). This shift indicates the formation of strong electron coupling, possibly chemical bonding at the heterojunction interface [60]. Based on the UV‐DRS (Figure S13a), band gaps were calculated as shown in Figure S13b, which were 3.35 eV for Bi‐MOF and 2.52 eV for MBW0.75. Herein, for precise calculations, we have considered MBW0.75, a Bi2WO6 obtained from the complete conversion of Bi‐MOF. The VB‐XPS values represent the energy separation between the flat‐band potential and the valence band of each material. The EVB‐XPS values were determined to be 2.45 and 2.40 eV for Bi‐MOF and MBW0.75, respectively, as displayed in Figure S13c. In addition, the Mott‐Schottky plots exhibited n‐type behavior with positive slopes, and the extracted flat‐band potentials (Efb) were −0.25 and −0.15 V versus RHE for Bi‐MOF and MBW0.75, respectively, as shown in Figure S13d. The valence band positions were then calculated by subtracting EVB‐XPS from the corresponding Efb values, yielding VB levels of 2.20 eV (Bi‐MOF) and 2.25 eV (MBW0.75). Using these VB values along with the optical band gaps obtained from UV‐DRS, 3.35 eV for Bi‐MOF and 2.51 eV for MBW0.75, the conduction band (CB) positions were subsequently derived. The resulting CB values were estimated to be −1.15 eV for Bi‐MOF and −0.27 eV for MBW0.75. A complete band alignment diagram based on these calculated parameters is illustrated in Scheme 1a. When an external piezo‐force is applied, polarization charges get aligned on either side of the Bi‐MOF and Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6 parts of the composite owing to the piezoelectric effect. This gives rise to an inbuilt piezo‐potential Pz, also producing band bending in both the piezo‐catalysts, allowing the Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6 to reach the water oxidation potential at 0 V (NHE) [51, 61]. As shown in Scheme 1b, in the case of MBW0.25/MX‐5, the piezo‐field drives the electrons from Bi‐MOF to Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6 towards the Ti2C sink, which plays a crucial role in electron capture and transfer. Direction of electron flow was also confirmed via XPS, where W 4f of MBW0.25/MX‐5 depicted a shift to lower binding energy as opposed to MBW0.75, indicating enrichment of electrons on Bi3.84W0.16O6.24/Bi2WO6. This gives rise to an efficient piezo‐system with well‐separated charge carriers, enabling them to participate in redox reactions. At the Bi‐MOF holes, methanol oxidation takes place via •OH mediated pathway producing •CH2OH and surface bound H* (confirmed via scavenging experiments), and other oxidation products such as HCHO and/or CO2 [62]. The H* finally combine on the catalyst surface to produce hydrogen.

Piezo‐Electrocatalytic Oxygen Evolution Reaction

The electrocatalytic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performance of Bi‐MOF, MBW0.25, and MBW0.25/MX‐5 was evaluated in the absence and presence of ultrasonic vibration to explain the influence of piezoelectric excitation on catalytic activity. LSV curve Figure 8a reveals that all prepared catalysts exhibit higher current density and lower onset potential under ultrasonic vibration, confirming that piezoelectric excitation significantly enhances water splitting performance. This improvement demonstrates that piezoelectric excitation can effectively couple mechanical energy into the electrochemical system to promote water splitting. Among them, MBW0.25/MX‐5 exhibits the highest current density and lowest onset potential, which can be attributed to the strong coupling between the piezoelectric active MBW0.25 component and highly conductive Mxene sheets. The overpotential was calculated with a benchmark current density of 10 mA/cm2. A clear drop in overpotential was observed from 445 → 410 mV for MBW0.25, 443 → 380 mV for MBW0.25/MX‐5 (Figure 8b), demonstrating the effective utilization of piezoelectric energy to lower the thermodynamic barrier for water splitting. In MBW0.25 and MBW0.25/MX‐5, dual‐phase Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 derived from Bi‐MOF introduces piezoelectric domains generating the electric field under vibration. These polarization fields facilitate charge separation and migration, thereby accelerating surface adsorption and deprotonation of hydroxide ions. However, for Bi‐MOF, the effect was reversed; its overpotential increased from 446 → 482 mV, accompanied by a decrease in current density. This shows that the organic ligand network of Bi‐MOF wasn't able to effectively convert mechanical energy into performance as other catalysts. Meanwhile, the elevated overpotential of the Bi‐MOF further confirmed that only piezoactive Bi2WO6 and a conductive Mxene composite can effectively harness mechanical energy. The piezo current behavior was further analyzed using chronoamperometry (i‐t curve) under chopped ultrasonic vibration. As shown in Figure 8c, the current density jumps at the onset of ultrasonic vibration and returns to its original baseline once the ultrasonic field is switched off, confirming the reversibility of the piezo‐electrocatalytic process. Moreover, Bi‐MOF and MBW0.25 depict a sudden current drop and a shorter decay time when the ultrasonic is off; however, MBW0.25/MX‐5 shows a slower decay in piezo‐current even when the vibration is off, owing to the conductive Mxene and its synergistic coupling with MBW0.25. The current increment (Δjpz = jon—joff) quantifies the piezoelectrically induced enhancement in the catalytic activity. For Bi‐MOF, MBW0.25, and MBW0.25/MX‐5, the average Δjpz values are 3.62, 4.65, and 51.17 µA cm−2, respectively. These values are closely related to overpotential reduction, in which MBW0.25/MX‐5 is the most effective at harvesting mechanical energy to accelerate the electrochemical reaction. Figure 8d shows the Tafel slope without vibration, where all materials exhibit similar slope values ranging from 110‐114 mV dec−1. Under ultrasonic vibration, smaller Tafel slope values are obtained, further confirming the kinetic improvement induced by vibration as shown in Figure 8e. MBW0.25/MX‐5 depicted a drop from 109 to 86 mV dec−1, indicating faster charge transfer kinetics and a more favorable reaction pathway facilitated by the piezoelectric effect. The mechanical energy generated by ultrasonic vibration enhances the adsorption and desorption of intermediates (OH*, O*, OOH*) on the catalyst surface, reducing the energy barrier for the rate‐determining step [63]. The lower Tafel slope and reduced overpotential indicate improved electrochemical kinetics, meaning that mechanical energy can effectively enhance the rate‐determining step of water splitting. This phenomenon is attributed to the effective coupling between the piezoelectric Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 phase and the high electrical conductivity of Mxene, ensuring a rapid charge excitation and reduction in recombination losses. This coupling effect results in a stable piezoelectric field that assists electron‐hole separation and enhances charge transport across the catalyst‐electrolyte interface. To evaluate the mechanical to electrical energy conversion efficiency, the apparent piezo‐potential was calculated using the Tafel slope and steady state current ratio (j on, j off) derived from i–t curve by the given Equation Δφpz = blog10(jon/joff), where, b is tafel slope, jon/joff is ratio of steaty state current [64]. MBW0.25/MX‐5 apparent piezo potential is 147.36, as opposed to 71.41 for MBW0.25. The higher apparent piezo potential indicates that ultrasonic mechanical energy is effectively converted into a strong internal electric field, which assists in improving the charge carrier separation and reducing the energy barrier of water splitting by promoting the rate‐determining O─O bond formation. To understand the dynamic stability and reversible electrochemical response, multi‐step current measurements were performed over a range of current densities from 5 to 50 mA cm−2 at 50‐second time intervals, with and without ultrasonication, as shown in Figure 8f.

Under vibration, all catalysts exhibit lower potentials (except Bi‐MOF), confirming that mechanical energy lowers the energy barrier to water splitting at various current densities. MBW0.25/MX‐5 required 1.77 V versus RHE to achieve 50 mA cm−2, consistent with its LSV profile. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements of MBW0.25/MX‐5 were conducted in the non‐faradaic region at scan rates ranging from 10 to 100 mV s−1 under ultrasonication (Figure 8g). The ECSA was evaluated via double‐layer capacitance obtained from the non‐faradaic CV region. Figure 8h shows the Cdl values of MBW0.25 and MBW0.25/MX‐5 under ultrasonication. the Cdl value of 2.2 and 3.0 mF cm−2 corresponding to ECSA of 55 and 75 cm2 for MBW0.25 and MBW0.25/MX‐5 respectively. The significant increase in Cdl and the larger ECSA for MBW0.25/MX‐5 reveal a higher number of active sites for water oxidation. The EIS Nyquist plot of MBW0.25 and MBW0.25/MX‐5 is shown in Figure 8i. Among the series, MBW0.25/MX‐5 exhibits the lowest charge‐transfer resistance under both vibration and non‐vibration conditions, indicating efficient electron migration through the conductive Mxene framework and improved electrolyte accessibility to the active Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 site. The piezoelectrically induced local electric field promotes dynamic charge redistribution, lowering the activation barrier for OER intermediates. The overall performance of the piezo‐electrochemical OER trend follows the order Bi‐MOF < MBW0.25 < MBW0.25/MX‐5, clearly indicating the beneficial roles of both MOFs in oxide conversion and Mxene integration.

Mechanism for Piezo‐Electrochemical OER

Under ultrasonic vibration, alternating compressive and tensile strains in the Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 lattice generate oscillating dipoles and a transient potential due to its intrinsic piezoelectric properties [65]. This piezoelectric field modulates the local charge density at the electrode surface, effectively enhancing the electric potential that drives charge transfer during the water splitting process. Herein, the applied piezo energy acts as an additional bias that supplements the externally applied potential, thereby enhancing the reaction rate without increasing the energy input. Under vibration, mechanical energy alters the local band bending, resulting in a reduced energy barrier for the formation and desorption of key intermediates [66]. In all these, Ti2C Mxene acts as a highly conductive electron highways that efficiently extract piezo electrons from Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 and stabilize holes at the MBW0.25 interface, which minimizes recombination with surface holes and maintains the polarization field for a longer duration. These rapid interfacial charge‐transport accelerates the oxidation of surface‐adsorbed hydroxyl species. Therefore, the triple‐phase interface between Bi‐MOF, Bi2WO6/Bi3.84W0.16O6.24, and Mxene thus enables efficient coupling of mechanical, electrical, and electrochemical processes. In addition to the intrinsic piezoelectric contribution, ultrasound hydrodynamics further enhance the catalytic activity by reducing diffusion layer thickness, improving the OH– ion transport, and promoting water splitting [67, 68].

Conclusions

A multifunctional piezocatalyst was successfully developed by deriving Bi‐rich Bi2WO6/ Bi3.84W0.16O6.24, comprising dual morphology, from CAU‐17 MOF and synergistically integrating it with Ti2C MXene. The controlled formation of a dual‐phase oxide with defect‐rich lattices and residual MOF domains yielded a structurally flexible, polarization‐responsive framework highly suitable for piezo‐driven reactions. Ti2C MXene provided a highly conductive electron network, accelerating piezo‐induced charge separation and strengthening the internal piezoelectric field. Owing to this, the MBW0.25/MX‐5 composite achieved a remarkably high H2 evolution rate of 1450.1 µmol g−1 h−1, superior to both pure Bi‐MOF and pristine Ti2C. Unlike conventional piezocatalytic systems dominated by direct electron‐driven proton reduction, the present system follows a radical‐mediated pathway where •OH‐derived intermediates govern H2 evolution. The material also showed good activity in various water matrices. Simultaneously, MBW0.25/MX‐5 also displayed promising potential for piezo‐electrochemical OER, including reduced overpotential, higher ECSA, and faster interfacial charge transfer. Overall, this work demonstrates that Bi‐MOF‐derived Bi‐rich Bi2WO6 coupled with MXene electron highways offers a robust and generalizable design pathway for next‐generation piezo‐catalytic hydrogen evolution and electrochemical OER systems. In contrast to conventional Bi2WO6/MXene systems, the present catalyst introduces an intrinsic Bi‐rich/Bi2WO6 dual‐phase junction, enabling internal charge redistribution prior to extraction by MXene.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Bi‐MOF, MBW (Bi‐MOF/Bi2WO6), MBW/MX (Bi‐MOF/Bi2WO6/MXene), and Bi3.84W0.16O6.24

In a typical synthesis, 2 mmoles of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (970 mg) and 4 mmoles of trimesic acid (H3BTC, 840.5 mg) were dissolved in 60 mL of methanol under stirring. The resulting solution was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon‐lined stainless‐steel autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 120°C for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting white precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed several times with methanol, and then dried at 60°C overnight to obtain the Bi‐MOF. 200 mg of Bi‐MOF was dispersed in 30 mL of DI water and ultrasonicated for 15 min to obtain a homogeneous suspension. In a separate beaker, Na2WO4·2H2O (82.4 mg, 0.25 mmol) was dissolved in 30 mL of DI water. The Na2WO4·2H2O solution was then added dropwise into the Bi‐MOF suspension under continuous stirring for 30 min. The mixture was transferred into a Teflon‐lined autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 180°C for 12 h. The obtained white solid was washed thoroughly with DI water and ethanol, and dried at 60°C overnight to yield Bi‐MOF/Bi2WO6‐0.25 (MBW0.25). The overall synthesis procedure is depicted in Scheme 2. Similarly, by varying the Na2WO4·2H2O amount to 0.5 mmol and 0.75 mmol, the corresponding composites MBW0.5 and MBW0.75 were synthesized under identical conditions. Since MBW0.75 was completely converted to Bi2WO6, it was considered as a standalone Bi2WO6 for further characterizations and studies. The optimized composite, MBW0.25, was further hybridized with varying mass percentages of Ti2C MXene (2, 5, and 8 wt.%). Calculated amounts of MBW0.25 and Ti2C MXene were dispersed in 50 mL DI water via ultrasonication for 15 min, and the solution was stirred for 1 h at 450 rpm. After mixing and drying, the resulting composites were vacuum‐dried at 70°C for 12 h and labelled as MBW0.25/MX‐2, MBW0.25/MX‐5, and MBW0.25/MX‐8, respectively. Also, a separate Bi2WO6 was synthesized using 1 mmole of BiNO3.5H2O and 0.5 mmole of Na2WO4 in similar hydrothermal conditions at 180°C for 12 h and designated as Bi2WO6 (N). For comparison, Bi3.84W0.16O6.24 was synthesized using the same procedure as for Bi2WO6 (N), except that the solution pH was adjusted to 12 with 1 M NaOH prior to hydrothermal conditions.

Materials Characterization

X‐ray diffraction patterns were recorded using a Rigaku diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å), operated at 30 kV and 50 mA. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 10°–80° with a scan rate of 4°min−1. The surface morphology of the photocatalysts was analyzed by field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM, JEOL JSM‐7000F). UV‐vis diffuse reflectance spectra (UV‐DRS) were obtained using a JASCO V‐650 spectrophotometer. X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; VGS Thermo K‐Alpha) was performed using Al Kα radiation as the excitation source to determine the chemical composition of the hybrids. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was performed using a FEI (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Talos F200XG2 instrument. PFM (piezo force microscopy) and KPFM (Kelvin probe force microscopy) were performed on a Bruker Dimension ICON scanning probe microscope system.

Piezo‐Catalytic H2 Evolution

30 mg of the catalyst was added to 50 mL of a solution composed of DI water and 10% methanol (MeOH). The reaction vessel was sealed and flushed with nitrogen at a flow rate of 100 sccm for 20 min, after which it was placed in an ultrasonic bath (40 kHz, 120 W) to begin the H2 generation process. The amount of water in the ultrasonic bath was always maintained at 1000 mL to achieve similar conditions for all tests. The temperature of water in the ultrasonic bath was maintained around 24°C–26°C using low‐temperature (10°C) water circulating in a hollow aluminum bar as shown in Figure S1. Gas samples of 0.5 mL were taken from the system every 30 min and analyzed offline using a GC‐9800 gas chromatograph equipped with a 5A molecular sieve column and a TCD detector, with argon (Ar) serving as the carrier gas. The quantity of H2 produced was determined using a calibration curve derived from known H2 standards. For water media tests, DI water was replaced with tap water, real seawater (filtered before use with filter paper), and simulated seawater (DI water with 3.5% NaCl). Scavenger experiments were performed under the same setup (45 mL DI H2O + 5ml CH3OH) with the addition of 0.05 mmoles of AgNO3 (e¯), 0.05 mmoles of Ascorbic acid (•O2¯), and 0.5 mL Iso propyl alcohol (•OH).

Piezo‐Electrochemical OER Measurement

All piezo‐electrochemical OER experiments were carried out on a CHI 627D electrochemical workstation using a three‐electrode configuration, with a standard Ag/AgCl reference electrode, a Platinum plate as the counter electrode, and the as‐prepared samples as the working electrode. Piezoelectric stimulation was applied using an ultrasonic bath (40 kHz, 120 W), with samples immersed and subjected to vibration cycles to evaluate piezo‐electrochemical water splitting. A 1 M aqueous KOH solution was used as the electrolyte in all measurements. For electrode fabrication, 10 mg of the as‐prepared catalyst and 1 mg of PVDF were thoroughly mixed in a mortar, followed by the addition of 25 µL of N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP) to obtain a uniform paste. The paste was coated onto Ni foam (1cm2) using the doctor‐blade method and subsequently dried at 80°C under vacuum for 12h. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was estimated in the non‐faradaic region from cyclic voltammograms (CV) recorded at various scan rates under vibration. For piezo‐electrochemical measurements, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1, and Tafel plots were derived from LSV data obtained with and without vibration. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted at open circuit voltage over a frequency range of 100kHz to 0.01Hz.
